Harnessing the potential of γδ T cells through engineering and combination treatment for cancer therapies
Authors: Jonathan Fisher, Yin Wu, Daniel Abate-Daga, Marta Barisa, Lawrence Lamb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractγδT cells comprise 1-10% of circulating T cells, are further enriched in barrier tissues such as the gut and skin, and also form a critical component of lymphoid stress surveillance. γδ T cells have been gaining prominence as an alternative to αβ T cells for cancer immunotherapy. Such popularity may be attributed to multiple factors including the MHC-independent nature of γδ T activation, which differentiates them from αβ T cells and underlies their lack of alloreactivity. In this Review, we describe efforts to optimize the potential of γδ T cells as a cancer immunotherapeutic. We discuss the impact of disease burden on efficacy and the contexts in which unmodified γδ T cells have succeeded or failed to yield durable responses. Finally, we explore options for enhancing γδ T cell efficacy including combinations with other treatments and engineering strategies to capitalize on their potency.Subject terms: Immunotherapy, Cancer immunotherapy, Gammadelta T cells, Cellular immunity

Introduction

Along with B cells and αβ T cells, γδ T cells are the third lineage of adaptive lymphocytes bearing somatically recombined antigen receptors1–3. γδ T cells express T cell receptors (TCR) composed of gamma and delta chains, as opposed to alpha and beta chains found on αβ T cells. This difference underpins some of the fundamental differences between γδ T and αβ T immunobiology. Whilst some elements of canonical T cell biology apply to γδ T cells, others do not – most prominently the requirements to achieve γδ TCR activation.

Cancer immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (CPI), have revolutionized treatment for advanced disease and now offer realistic chances of cure for some patients4–7. Nonetheless, the outlook for most patients that do not respond to current standard-of-care (SoC) immunotherapies remains poor. Current SoC therapies predominantly harness antigen-specific αβ T cells8, so resistance to treatment is often associated with an absence of these cells from the tumor microenvironment (TME) as well as reduced functionality owing to impaired cancer-associated antigen presentation on major histocompatibility complex (MHC)9–11. In the context of cellular immunotherapy, recognition of non-self MHC by αβ T cells is one of the main drivers of graft versus host disease (GvHD). Alloreactivty has therefore constrained engineered αβ T cells to autologous manufacture, or imposed additional engineering requirements to overcome alloreactivity. In these respects, harnessing γδ T cells presents several key advantages. Unlike αβ T cell receptors, γδ T cell receptors respond to ligands in a manner independent of classical peptide-MHC (pMHC) restriction12,13 and are free of the requirement for co-receptor (CD4 or CD8) engagement14. γδ T cells therefore do not display TCR-driven alloreactivity, making them an attractive chassis for cellular immunotherapy that could be manufactured a-priori from healthy donor material15.

Key features of γδ T cells

In humans, γδ T cells are often identified by their TCR delta chain—for example Vγ9Vδ2T cells are frequently referred to as Vδ2. From a functional perspective, separation of human γδ T cells into Vδ2 and non-Vδ2 subsets allows grouping of cells with broadly similar biological properties, although the differences between Vδ1+ and Vδ3+ cells should not be ignored3. Tissue residency, for example, is predominantly segregated by γδ T subset: Vγ9Vδ2 predominate in the blood, whereas non-Vδ2 γδT reside in barrier tissues from which most solid cancers arise2,16,17. In parallel, Vγ9Vδ2, Vδ1 and Vδ3 subsets of γδ T cells exhibit distinct adaptation and stress-surveillance programs. These differences have implications for γδ T cell therapeutic use and engineering strategies18,19.

As alluded to above, γδ TCR activation is independent of classical MHC-restricted peptide presentation. Ligands for γδ TCRs vary depending on the combination of gamma and delta chains, including those recognized via MHC/MHC-like interactions, via immunoglobulin-like interactions, and via B7 family-like proteins and phosphoantigen recognition14. Examples of γδ TCR ligands are shown in Box 1. One of the best characterized γδ TCR ligand/receptor interactions is that between the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR and butyrophilins (BTN) 2A1 and 3A1, but these represent only a small portion of endogenous γδ TCR ligands, the list of which is still expanding.

In addition to expressing γδ TCRs, γδ T cells also express a wide range of stress-sensing stimulatory receptors including NK receptors such as NKp30, NKp46, and NKG2D, as well as receptors such as CD16 governing interaction with other arms of the immune system. This empowers γδ T cells to detect late-stage tumors that commonly suppress neoantigen presentation to evade αβ T cell surveillance20,21. γδ T also express receptors that suppress their activity when activated, including NKG2A, KIRs, PD-1 and TIGIT. Thus, γδT activation is governed by a balance of stimulatory and suppressive signals, so that human γδ T cells may display both anti-tumor18,22–28 and pro-tumor29,30 effector functions in vitro, evoking a functional dichotomy that has been well established in mouse models of cancer31–37. Despite these apparent similarities, incomplete cross-species conservation of γδ T cells has hindered direct translation to human disease. Meanwhile, presence of γδ T cells within tissues and tumors predominantly correlates with improved clinical outcomes, implying a net anti-cancer contribution in humans18,22–26,28,38,39. γδ T cells in innate responses and immune surveillance

The concept of cancer immunosurveillance presupposes that the immune system recognizes premalignant cells and, in most cases, eliminates them prior to development of overt disease. The importance of this phenomenon is demonstrated in animals that possess naturally occurring or experimentally induced defects of adaptive immunity, including the loss of recombination-activating gene 2 (RAG2)43, αβ T cells, γδ T cells37,44, invariant NKT cells, interferon-γ (IFNγ) receptor 145; signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1), perforin, or tumor-necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL)46. All these animals are more susceptible to spontaneous development of cancer or carcinogenic stimuli.

γδ T cells play a critical role in tumor immunosurveillance37,47–49 and in the immune response to cancer50–54, a feature demonstrated in both mice37,47,55,56 and humans57,58. Indeed, Beck59 showed mobilization of peripheral γδ T cells into the circulation shortly after intracranial placement of syngeneic GL261 glioma cells in WT C57BL/6 mice several days prior to the development of neurologic symptoms. Long term survival data from patients who developed a spontaneous increase in γδ T cells during the first year following bone marrow transplantation (BMT) for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) or acute myeloid leukemia (AML) show a significant improvement in disease-free survival (DFS) when compared with similar risk patients60,61. Despite these γδ T activity as ’first responders‘, successful evolution of a tumor mass indicates a failure of γδ T cell immunosurveillance, potentially due to a combination of suppression, activation-induced cell death (AICD) and rapid tumor growth outpaces γδ T cell elimination capacity.

Exploiting γδ T cell immune surveillance by “resetting the clock”

As failure of immune surveillance is often characterized by evolution of unfavorable imbalance between tumor proliferation and immune control, it may thus be possible to treat cancer by first returning or “resetting” (via chemotherapy and/or surgical resection etc.) the primary malignancy to small pockets of residual disease that are devoid of a suppressive microenvironment, so that sensitivity to innate immune effectors can be regained. Initial disease reduction is often achievable with conventional approaches such as surgery accompanied by chemotherapy, and/or radiotherapy. However, these approaches leave the immune system suppressed and in need of reconstitution. In this setting, γδ T cell-based therapies could be most effective against residual disease. For example, delivery of ex-vivo expanded γδ T cells could be one way to optimize effector:target ratios in a recently ablated disease state. Animal studies and indirect evidence from human allogeneic bone marrow transplant (BMT) studies suggest that γδ T cells can also facilitate bone marrow engraftment62–67, are not primary initiators of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD)63,67, and are not substantially activated in the in vitro allogeneic mixed lymphocyte culture68,69, thereby implicating allogenic γδ T transfer as a potential treatment or adjuvant.

An ideal setting to examine the homeostatic recovery and durability of ex vivo expanded and activated γδ T cells following infusion is in the context of haploidentical BMT for leukemia. This treatment is associated with profound lymphodepletion, has a high incidence of relapse, and typically requires ongoing immuno-suppression to reduce GvHD. In a Phase I study, adult patients with newly diagnosed leukemia were given a reduced-intensity conditioning regimen followed by a BMT from a haploidentical donor before infusion of donor-derived, ex-vivo expanded γδ T cells within 7 days of neutrophil engraftment. There, γδ T cells proliferated, persisted and eliminated residual leukemia cells in BMT patients who received standard of care70. After 1 year, patients given BMT + γδ T cell infusion showed improved progression-free and overall survival compared with reported outcomes for patients given BMT and post-transplant cyclophosphamide (100% versus 52.7% relapse-free survival)71, and after 19 months, 7 of 10 showed complete response72.

Similar approaches may also be feasible in the context of solid tumors where surgical removal of the tumor comprises a mainstay of treatment. Brain tumors such as glioblastoma are typically treated with surgery to remove as much tumor as possible, followed by chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy. Infusion of γδ T cells to the surgical cavity via a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) access device has been used to provide high, localized effector:target ratios, promoting tumor control. Specific elements of glioblastoma therapy, combined with γδ T cell engineering, were exploited to achieve this. In this regard, temozolomide (TMZ) is an alkylating agent chemotherapy that forms part of SoC treatment after surgery for glioblastoma, causing cancer cell death by DNA methylation. Via a separate mechanism, TMZ also restores expression of NKG2D ligands (NKG2DL) on the surface of glioblastoma cells73,74. This is important because NKG2DL expression is frequently downregulated in the hypoxic microenvironment of the tumor and this downregulation is associated with resistance to γδ T cell-mediated recognition and lysis55. The effect of TMZ in restoring NKG2DL expression is, however, transient, requiring five consecutive daily doses of TMZ that simultaneously impairs γδ T cell function73. This problem was addressed by genetically modifying γδ T cells with a P140K-MGMT-expressing lentiviral vector that confers resistance to TMZ. MGMT-engineered γδ T cells proved to be safe and effective in vitro and in patient-derived xenograft mouse models75, preserving the in vivo expansion of γδ T cells in the presence of TMZ and allowing high effector:target ratios at the site of solid tumor resection. A trial involving infusion of TMZ-resistant γδ T cells following surgical resection of high-grade gliomas and intravenous TMZ chemotherapy showed that γδT cells could target upregulated NKG2D ligands on tumor cells despite alkylating chemotherapy exposure. 83% of subjects exceeded median 7-month progression free survival, with manageable toxicity, and one patient remains progression free after more than 35 months76. γδT as orchestrators of adaptive responses

Following an initial response arising from innate immunity, tumor-associated antigens (TAA) are presented to αβ T cells via MHC class I or II on antigen presenting cells (APC), triggering αβ T cell activation, expression of co-stimulatory molecules and secretion of chemokines and cytokines. Clonal expansion of TAA-specific αβ T cells then occurs as well as that of other immune effector cells. In this regard, the Vγ9Vδ2 subset of γδ T cells can respond early to infection or transformation and recruit adaptive responses from CD4+ and CD8+ T cells by internalizing antigens, processing them and presenting the pMHC complexes on their cell surface77,78. As professional antigen presenting cells, γδ T cells express equivalent levels of costimulatory molecules79 and CCR7, home to lymph nodes80 and are equally potent as dendritic cells78,79 at promoting proliferative responses in αβ T cells81. This suggests that γδ T cells may have roles beyond those of first responders in immune surveillance, acting to orchestrate a subsequent adaptive immune response.

Challenges to overcome

A major challenge that drove the development of engineered cell therapy for cancer is the highly diverse and MHC-dependent targeting of polyclonal αβ T cell populations. Chimeric antigen receptors (CAR) and TCR-transfer technologies all seek to redirect this polyclonal response against single antigen targets. The success of CD19 or BCMA targeting CAR-αβ T cells against lymphoid malignancies is, however, emerging as the exception rather than the rule. Especially in the context of solid cancers, directing to a single target of the tumor/immune interaction are demonstrably less effective than those targeting multiple targets82,83. While adoptive cell therapy with MAGE-A4-specific αβ TCR T cells for HLA-A*02-expressing advanced synovial sarcoma was recently approved by the FDA84, and anti-GD2 CAR-T show early efficacy against neuroblastoma85, CAR T cell approaches that target a single TAA had for some time been generally disappointing in solid tumors86–88, although more recent breakthroughs suggest that this therapeutic bottleneck is easing88–91. Notwithstanding these successes, and in line with the hypothesis that simultaneous targeting of multiple tumor/immune axes is more effective, the use of effector cells possessing innate and heterogeneous anti-tumor reactivity, such as γδ T cells, is an attractive option.

Ex-vivo expanded populations of γδ T cells of both Vδ1 and Vδ2 subsets have demonstrable anti-tumor activity in their own right70,92,93. If a higher effector:target ratio is sought after, the potential for more heterogeneous targeting can be better capitalized by minimizing disease burden prior to γδ T administration. Approaches that seek to restore effector:target ratios through pre-treatment or surgical de-bulking, as well as repeated administration of the cell therapy product, are likely to further improve the success of these strategies.

Strategies to alter or augment γδ T cell function

Influence of cytokines

γδ T cell expansion protocols typically involve the use of one or more Th1-type cytokines such as IL-2, IL-15, or IL-2192,94–96. There is increasing evidence that γδ T cells develop dependence on these cytokines as they expand in vitro, leading to their demise if cytokines are withdrawn97,98. This correlates with the relatively short lifespan and disappointing efficacy of infused γδ T cells observed in autologous trials of ex-vivo expanded γδ T cells, although clinical reporting of this effect in peer-reviewed format is sparse97,99,100. Early γδ T cell trials explored infusion of autologous Vγ9Vδ2T cells together with Th1 cytokines101–103, demonstrating that the approach was safe but yielded disappointing efficacy. This would suggest that Vγ9Vδ2 cytotoxicity requires additional cytokine augmentation, either by engineering the cells or by combining them with more agents as discussed in this review.

There is also emerging preclinical evidence that certain cytokine combinations used in γδ T cell expansion may favour the generation of pro-tumorigenic phenotypes. An example is the complex cytokine cocktail proposed for Vδ1 γδ T cell expansion (Delta One T cells – DOT Cell protocol) which includes IL-1β, IL-21, IL-4, and IL-1595. IL-1β was found to induce significant upregulation of amphiregulin secretion by γδ T cells, promoting migration and growth of colorectal cancer lines by γδ T cells that acquire a “wound healing” phenotype29. Whether these preclinical findings impact clinical efficacy remains to be seen and may be answered by an ongoing first-in-class trial of DOT cells (NCT05886491) if recruitment re-starts. IL-4 has also been associated with the induction of tolerogenic γδ T cell phenotypes. Specifically, whilst IL-4 promoted Vδ1T cell growth, these expanded cells acquired a regulatory-like phenotype, secreting IL-10 and inhibiting Vδ2 γδ T cell activity104. A simplified protocol containing IL-15 and IL-18 abrogated the increase in amphiregulin expression, with IL-4 and IL-1β shown to be non-essential for Vδ1T cell expansion96. Clinical interventions using IL-4 and IL-10 have not yielded γδ T cell-specific readouts. Lastly, more classical tolerogenic cytokines such as TGFβ, which is also a master regulator of epithelial-mesenchymal transition in tumors, have shown complex effects on γδ T cells. TGFβ can be used to generate FOXP3+ γδ Treg cells from both Vδ1 and Vδ2 subsets, overriding the influence of IL-15105,106, and yet others have used continuous exposure to TGFβ1 to enhance γδ T cell expansion and generate a more expansive and cytotoxic γδ T cell product that is resistant to the immunosuppressive influence of intratumoral TGFβ and PGE2107. These seemingly contradictory reports remain to be reconciled.

Pharmacological modulation of γδ T cells

Achieving a high effector:target ratio at the site of a solid tumour relies on two things: reduction in tumour bulk to drive down targer cell numbers, and introduction of large numbers of effectors to the tumor site. Such approaches are therefore best used in situations where γδ T effectors can be infused directly to the resection bed and are not required to traffic to the tumour via the bloodstream. An alternative option is to enhance γδ T cell potency, such that lower effector:target ratios deliver a comparative response. Recent advances in our understanding of γδ T cell biology have opened up new opportunities for harnessing γδ T cells in combination with other therapies by implicating pharmacological intervention to enhance γδ T cell activity. Fig. 2 illustrates some of the potential routes for augmenting γδ T cell activity. Reviewed in detail by Kabelitz and colleagues108, the immunosuppressive feedback loops employed by tumors to suppress T cell activation typically arise from tumor exposure to inflammatory cytokines. Interfering in these feedback mechanisms to tip the balance away from immune tolerance is therefore a strategy worth exploring, as discussed below.

Aminobisphosphonates

The best-known means of augmenting Vγ9Vδ2T cell cytotoxicity is the use of aminobisphosphonates such as zoledronate and pamidronate. These compounds, which typically bind to hydroxyapatite in bone, have a proven clinical safety record and form part of many in vitro Vγ9Vδ2 expansion protocols. Importantly, aminobisphosphonates have little direct impact on γδ T cells in isolation—instead acting via monocytes or tumor cells exposed to the drug, to which Vγ9Vδ2 cells then respond109–111. Mechanistically, aminobisphosphonates sensitise targets to killing by Vγ9Vδ2T cells by inhibiting the enzyme farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase, which leads to an accumulation of the phosphoantigen metabolite isopentenyl-5-pyrophosphate (IPP), which interacts with butyrophilins (BTN) 2A1 and 3A1112–116 causing a conformational change in the butyrophilin complex rendering it recognisable by the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR.

Previous studies have therefore focused on using aminobisphosphonates to stimulate Vγ9Vδ2T cells. Importantly, preclinical data demonstrating efficacy of bisphosphonates as a γδ T cell adjunct in immunodeficient mice bearing human tumor xenografts is inherently biased by this complex mechanism, because only the human tumor cells will undergo the necessary changes in BTN conformation to activate the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR97,117–119. Nevertheless, several γδ T cell clinical trials have examined aminobisphosphonate in combination with cytokines such as IL-2 as a means of activating and expanding Vγ9Vδ2T cells in vivo103,120–122. These studies have been extensively reviewed already123–125 and in summary yielded transient clinical responses at best. Whilst aminobisphosphonates still form part of many preclinical regimens that target Vγ9Vδ2T cells97,119, there are other adjuncts that are showing promise.

TRAIL and cyclo-oxygenase

Although the potential targets of cyclo-oxygenase (COX) and tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) are likely not limited to γδ T cells, experimental work has confirmed effects in γδ T cell biology. At present, however, no published clinical studies have examined the influence of TRAIL or COX modulation on γδ T cell mediated anti-tumor responses.

TRAIL expression is a effector mechanism for inducing apoptosis in TRAIL receptor (TRAIL-R)-expressing target cells which is shared by αβ T cells, γδ T cells and NK cells. Ligation of TRAIL-R1 and TRAIL-R2 induces apoptosis of tumor cells, whereas TRAIL-R3 and TRAIL-R4 lack the pro-apoptotic death domain and act as decoy receptors by binding TRAIL or forming hetero-complexes with TRAIL-R1 or TRAIL-R2. Whilst these pathways are relatively poorly understood in γδ T cells compared to αβ T cells, enhancement of TRAIL-R1/R2 expression in osteosarcoma using celastrol (a bioactive compound derived from Tripterygium wilfordii, a herb used in Chinese medicine126) enhanced Vδ1 γδ T cell-mediated tumor cell lysis127, and tumoral TRAIL-R4 expression can regulate cytotoxicity of both Vδ1 and Vδ2 γδ T cells. Still, there appears to be some redundancy in this system that may create challenges for therapeutic intervention: Vδ1T cell-mediated cytotoxicity was not impaired by knockdown of TRAIL-R1/R2 in models of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), whereas, when TRAIL-R4 was overexpressed on γδ T cells, there was a substantial negative impact on Vγ9Vδ2 γδ T cell cytotoxicity, with concomitant increases in tumoral prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) secretion128.

In addition to being downstream of TRAIL, PGE2 is also an immune suppressive factor present into the tumor microenvironment and therefore an attractive direct target for therapy. Inhibitors of cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, which convert arachidonic acid to prostacyclins, prostaglandins and thromboxanes, are in common clinical use as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. There are two predominant isoforms of COX: COX-1 and COX-2. Both COX isoforms can be expressed both by tumors and microenvironmental components including mesenchymal stem cells and fibroblasts129,130, leading to accumulation of PGE2 with subsequent regulatory T cell (Treg) recruitment to the tumor. PGE2 negatively influences TCR-mediated Vγ9Vδ2 γδ T cell activation129,131,132, participating in an immunosuppressive feedback loop, where increases in COX-2 expression and resulting PGE2 production occur in response to γδ T cell derived TNF132. Blockade of tumor PGE2 synthesis using a selective and irreversible COX-2 inhibitor valeryl salicylate (DuP697) enhanced the lysis of γδ T cell-resistant Colo357 PDAC cells by Vγ9Vδ2T cells stimulated with the synthetic phosphoantigen bromohydrin pyrophosphate (BrHPP). Combining PGE2 blockade with a bi-specific tri-body [(Her2)2×Vγ9], which binds the Vγ9 TCR and HER-2 on PDAC cells, resulted in more effective γδ T cell-mediated tumor lysis than stimulation with BrHPP132.

IDO inhibition

Metabolites in the tumor microenvironment can also be immunosuppressive for T cells including γδ T cells108. For example, metabolites of the amino acid tryptophan (Trp), in particular kynurenine (Kyn), promote regulatory CD4+ T cells, impair T cell cytotoxicity and induce T cell cycle arrest133. The Trp/Kyn balance is controlled by the activity of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which is upregulated in inflammatory responses including those from IFNγ-producing Vγ9Vδ2T cells134. Interference in this process is a sought-after target for therapeutic immunomodulation, leading to the development of IDO1 inhibitors such as navoximod and epacadostat, although the latter showed no additive clinical benefit compared to the anti-PD1 antibody pembolizumab in metastatic melanoma135. Inhibition of IDO enhanced γδ T cell lysis of PDAC cell lines, but the effect was not universal134.

Galectin modulation

Galectins, also known as S-type lectins, are soluble proteins that bind β-galactoside-containing glycans and are expressed in all tissues. Galectin overexpression is associated with tumor progression, invasion and metastasis, in part due to its tolerogenic effects including the recruitment of immune-suppressive Treg cells and myeloid cells, as well as the reduction of Th1 cell effector responses136,137. Galectin-3 secreted by PDAC suppressed Vδ2 γδ T cell proliferation but not their cytotoxicity, with minimal galectin-3 release by the γδ T cells themselves138. Substantial increases in galectin-9 expression have been observed in murine γδ T cells that infiltrate PDAC, and are associated with concomitant rises in γδ T cell PD-L1 and FoxP3 expression that are absent in splenic γδ T cells from the same animals. Similar increases in galectin-9 expression have been observed on human tumor--infiltrating γδ T cells, and this axis is potentially amenable to targeting with the intention of removing this immunosuppressive effect.

Repeated intratumoral injection of galectin-1-targeting antibodies enhanced the ability of patient-derived ex vivo expanded tumor-infiltrating γδ T cells to control cervical cancer xenografts in SCID mice, although in these experiments the antibody alone had some effects, suggesting that this effect is not entirely γδ T cell-dependent139. Neutralization of galectin-9 or PD-L1 in wild type mice was sufficient to protect them from tumor formation following the challenge with orthotopic Pdx1Cre;KrasG12D;Tp53R172H PDAC-derived cell line-derived tumors, an effect that was not observed in TCRδ-deficient mice32, suggesting that galectin-9 functionally represses murine γδ T cells. A suppressive effect of galactin-1 was also seen in the context of tumor-promoting inflammation in transplantable sarcomas generated from autocthonous p53/K-ras tumors and implanted into wild type (WT) mice. In this study, wild-type mice, but not Tlr5-/- mice, showed increased galectin-1 expression by γδ T cells, an increase abrogated by antibiotic treatment, thereby suggesting an axis between microbial TLR5 engagement and γδ T cell production of galectin-1140.

In the clinical context, galectin-3 inhibition using belapectin (GR-MD-02) in combination with pembrolizumab led to objective responses in 7 out of 14 melanoma patients (4 of whom had previously progressed on prior treatment); the responses were associated with reduced numbers of intra-tumoral monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells141. Antibody directed at galectin-9 (LYT-200) is also showing some promise in patients with solid tumors, although the most recently reported data suggests a higher response rate when used in combination with tislelizumab for PD-1 blockade142. Whilst these approaches have not yet been combined with γδ T cells, they demonstrate the clinical feasibility of combining galectin-based therapies with other immunotherapeutic strategies.

Mitochondrial complex I

The hypoxic nature of the tumor microenvironment can also impair T cell function. In murine models of high-grade glioma, exposure of tumor-infiltrating γδ T cells to hypoxia stimulates apoptosis, with γδ T cell Hif1a expression correlating with the apoptotic marker Bax. Reduction of tumoral oxygen consumption using metformin had no direct effect on tumor viability in vitro but led to reductions in tumor size in vivo in a γδ T cell-dependent manner. Further analysis demonstrated that increased oxygen tension alleviated suppression of γδ T cells, restoring NKG2D expression and cytotoxicity by Vδ2+ γδ T cells143. Intriguingly, a detailed analysis of γδ T cell metabolism revealed that cytolytic IFNγ+ γδ T cells were almost entirely dependent on glycolysis, whereas pro-tumorigenic IL-17+ γδ T cells strongly engaged oxidative metabolism showing both increased mitochondrial mass, lipid uptake and storage144. It is unclear whether this dichotomy exists in humans, but the association of γδ T cell metabolic states with immune functions remains an attractive target, especially given that metformin, which inhibits mitochondrial complex I145 and is commonly prescribed to treat type 2 diabetes, may therefore favour a more glycolytic, cytotoxic γδ T cell phenotype146.

These results add to the growing body of evidence suggesting that simultaneous targeting of multiple components in the γδ T cell/tumor interaction would be beneficial and account for potential heterogeneity within target cell populations.

Antibodies and bi-specific T-cell engagers

γδ TCR stimulating biologics

As mentioned above, in vivo γδ TCR stimulation has mainly been achieved using aminobisphosphonates or synthetic phosphoantigens such as BrHPP in combination with IL-2. Efficacy of in vivo use of the bisphosphonate zoledronic acid and IL-2 was disappointing103,120–122. Alternative approaches include antibodies that maintain butyrophilin 3A in a conformation that stimulates the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR147, and bi-specific T cell engagers (bsTCE or BiTE) that bring together the tumor cell and γδ T cell as well as stimulating the T cells themselves148. Recent results from a combination trial (NCT04243499) involving the anti-BTN3A monoclonal antibody ICT01 in combination with pembrolizumab (anti-PD1) indicated tolerable safety and some indications of efficacy in melanoma patients previously exposed to immune checkpoint inhibitors147,149. Importantly, patients were selected for inclusion in this trial on the basis of higher numbers of Vγ9Vδ2T cells at baseline, highlighting a key limitation of this therapeutic class that relies on the effector capacity of a rare cell type. The same limitation has been noted for BiTE-based approaches that use Vδ2T cells. Results from these studies is still pending, but unfortunately one trial (LAVA-051/NCT04887259, which used a bi-specific engager targetting CD1d on tumor cells and the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR) was discontinued prior to completing recruitment due to a strategic realignment by the sponsor. Similarly, evaluation of an anti-PSMA/Vγ9Vδ2 bi-specific engager (LAVA-1207) was terminated at Phase I. Whilst safety of anti-Vδ2/anti-prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) bsTCEs appeared tolerable, infusion of the product led to rapid reduction in circulating Vγ9Vδ2T cell numbers. Only three out of eight evaluable patients in early reports from LAVA-1207 showed stable disease at 8 weeks150. Due to this poor efficacy, further assessment including combination with pembrolizumab with or without IL-2 for the same indication (NCT05369000)151 has now been halted despite recruiting 71 patients.

In parallel, a series of preclinical γδ TCR–engaging BiTEs have been developed, which broadens the range of target antigens while still relies on endogenous Vγ9Vδ2T cells. A CD1d–Vδ2 bsTCE, built from single-domain VHHs against CD1d and the Vδ2TCR, was shown to co-engage Vγ9Vδ2 T cells and type I NKT cells, driving potent cytokine production and cytotoxicity against CD1d⁺ chronic lymphoblastic leukaemia (CLL) 152, multiple myeloma (MM) and acute meloid leukaema (AML) samples and prolonging survival in xenograft models. A mechanistically related anti-TRGV9/anti-CD123 bi-specific antibody selectively redirects Vγ9⁺ γδ T cells, rather than bulk CD3⁺ T cells, to CD123⁺ AML blasts and demonstrates robust γδ T cell activation and in vivo antileukemic activity in xenografts153. To more closely mimic the physiological BTN2A1–BTN3A1 ligand complex for the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR, a heterodimeric BTN2A1/BTN3A1-Fc–CD19 scFv fusion protein has been engineered, which provides signal 1 through the γδ TCR and redirects activated Vγ9Vδ2 T cells to CD19⁺ lymphoma cells, thereby enhancing granzyme B–mediated killing in vitro154. Beyond hematologic targets, an EGFR–Vδ2 bsTCE has been shown to activate Vγ9Vδ2 T cells in blood and tumor specimens, mediate lysis of EGFR⁺ solid tumor samples, and suppress tumor growth in xenograft models, thus supporting the feasibility of this approach in epithelial malignancies155.

More recently, next-generation γδ TCR engagers (TCE) have begun to combine checkpoint modulation with T cell redirection. A PD-L1×Vδ2 bsTCE, again based on VHH domains, simultaneously blocks PD-1/PD-L1 interactions and engages Vγ9Vδ2 T cells to PD-L1⁺ tumor and myeloid cells, resulting in direct tumor lysis, depletion or reprogramming of PD-L1⁺ myeloid populations, and broader remodeling of the T-cell infiltrate in murine models156. Others have sought to harness the specificity of the γδ TCR for metabolically perturbed cells as a means of enhancing αβ T cytotoxicity. Bispecific constructs linking elements of the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR to CD3-stimulating scFvs enabled targeting of metabolically stressed targets by polyclonal αβ T cells, as an evolution of the conventional bi-specific antibody paradigm157.

Collectively, these preclinical data demonstrate that direct engagement of the Vγ9Vδ2 TCR via bispecific engager formats can elicit potent antitumor responses while, at least in vitro and in vivo models, maintaining relatively favorable cytokine profiles compared with conventional CD3-targeted bsTCEs. However, all these approaches still fundamentally depend on sufficient numbers and fitness of endogenous Vγ9Vδ2 T cells in patients, and none has yet shown clear, durable efficacy in the clinic beyond early-phase signals, underlining the need to integrate γδ-engagers with strategies that expand, sustain or supplement the γδ T cell compartment in vivo.

Recruitment of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity

Engagement of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by Vδ2 T cells, owing to their expression of the low-affinity FcγR CD16, has generated substantial preclinical data in both mice and non-human primates92,117,158. These data have led to a clinical trial involving infusion of allogeneic ex vivo expanded Vδ2 T cells in conjunction with anti-GD2 antibody dinutuximab and zoledronic acid to treat relapsed neuroblastoma and osteosarcoma (NCT05400603). Deeper investigation of the role of CD16 in γδ T cell biology has suggested that CD16 is a marker for heightened cytotoxic potential97,159 and could therefore be used as a biomarker for allogeneic donor selection, even if ADCC is not the clinical goal. In an approach that combines these two features into one platform, Vδ2 γδ T cells that secrete synthetic opsonins have demonstrated enhanced efficacy against orthotopic patient-derived osteosarcoma xenografts97, also enabling recruitment of antibody-engaging effectors from other immune lineages.

Immune checkpoint blockade

γδ T cells express several immune checkpoint receptors including BTLA, TIGIT, PD-1, TIM-3, CD39, LAG-3 and NKG2A in the context of both solid and hematological cancers18,23,24,26,28,160,161. CPIs are highly efficacious in enhancing γδ T cell activity against various cancers, including colorectal cancer18. This was particularly evident in cancers with defective HLA expression162, where the influence of CPI on tumor-resident γδ T cells could represent a means of overcoming tumor evasion of HLA-mediated immunity. Similar results have been observed in melanoma with low mutational burden22, which is generally thought to be less sensitive to CPI therapy. To date, these observations have been the by-products of larger immune subset analysis performed during CPI studies. Clinical trials testing CPIs combined with adoptive transfer of γδ T cells or with γδ T cell-stimulating agents such as bisphosphonates are in progress, including (i) NCT06212388, which involves allogeneic unmodified γδ T cells + anti-PD-1 mAb or IFN-α for melanoma; and (ii) NCT06404281, which involves allogeneic γδ T cells that are genetically engineered to secrete anti-PD-1 mAb for advanced solid tumors163.

Engineering γδ T cells

Owing to their lack of MHC-restricted activation and relative safety in the allogeneic setting99, γδ T cells are an attractive chassis for engineered adoptive cell therapies. Moreover, the inherent tissue adaptation of γδ T cells may confer benefits for adoptive cell approaches such as improved tissue homing, retention and survival. Indeed, a recent longitudinal study of patients receiving autologous chimeric antigen receptor engineered T (CAR T) cell therapies revealed that CAR γδ T cells persist for many years after treatment164. So far γδ T cells have been engineered in two main ways, to enhance their cytotoxicity or to gain an advantage in the tumor microenvironment by providing TMZ resistance. Several previous reviews have discussed options for engineering γδ T cells148,165; here we will discuss how these strategies have evolved. Chimeric antigen receptors containing CD3ζ signaling domains

Canonical CAR comprising an antigen recognition domain, a trans-membrane, and a CD3ζ endodomain in series with one or more costimulatory endodomains have been successfully expressed in γδ T cells and transitioned to clinic165. In this regard, γδ T cells share signaling and activation dynamics with both αβ T cells and NK cells. For example, both γδ T cells and NK cells synthesize inputs from multiple activating receptors for optimal activation, combining signals from immune tyrosine activation motifs (ITAM) in receptors such as FcγRIII and NKp3097 as well as with those from orthogonal detectors of cellular stress, such as NKG2D and DNAM-1. NKG2D uses the YINM-bearing DAP10 adaptor protein to transmit signals, whereas DNAM-1 contains an immune-tyrosine like tail motif. Both receptors signal via the AKT/PI3-K pathways, and additionally engage SLP-76 and ERK166,167. However, unlike NK cells, γδ T cells also receive activating signals through their γδ TCR. Indeed, the theoretical strength of signals generated through the 10 γδ TCR ITAMs outweighs those derived from stress receptors such as FcγRIII and NKp30, which have 6 ITAMs each168. This, combined with the broader stress ligand-mediated activation of both Vδ1 and Vδ2 γδ TCRs, presents a choice to cellular engineers.

Availability of shared activation pathways between αβ T and γδ T cells means that CD3ζ-containing CARs can elicit effector responses in γδ T cells. The nature and quality of these effector responses differ between cell types, however. CAR-expressing Vγ9Vδ2 γδ T cells have been reported to display equivalent cytotoxicity but lower levels of antigen-specific proliferation and cytokine production169 than CAR-αβ T cells170, with this reduction potentially impacting their efficacy in hematological cancers where CAR-T cell proliferation and longevity is particularly important. On the other hand, long-term follow-up data from patients treated with anti-CD19 CAR-T products showed persistence of CAR expressing γδ T cells 3 years after CAR-T infusion164. Intriguingly, most of the CAR-γδ T cells that persisted were Vδ1T cells. Together with preclinical evidence of CAR-Vδ1 functional durability171, these results support further efforts to use this lesser studied subset of γδ T cells for CAR-T cell therapy. In contrast to CAR-αβ T cells, CAR-γδ T cells retain their innate anti-cancer reactivity, and therefore have the potential to act against tumor cells that lack the antigen targeted by the CAR, thereby providing a means of mitigating immune escape by antigen loss. This ability to recognise targets independently of a CAR has been harnessed to good effect in the context of bone-resident metastatic models from prostate cancer by combining anti-PSCA CAR-Vδ2 with zoledronic acid to further sensitise the tumor119.

Alternative CAR designs

Both αβ and γδ TCRs transduce their signals via ITAMs found on CD3. In αβ T cells, MHC/epitope restriction creates an absolute requirement for synthetic CD3 signals, which canonical CARs were developed to provide in the presence of a chosen antigen. Whilst potent, the signaling derived from CARs has limitations, including target-autonomous “tonic” activation and triggering by a single antigen input, which can cause on-target off-tumor toxicity.

Tonic signaling arises from target-autonomous clustering of CAR molecules, and is now well recognized to impair CAR-T cell function167,172. Phosphoproteomic analysis showed that the majority of tonic signaling in CAR-αβ T was CD3-derived167, making the suppression of tonic signaling challenging as CD3 is essential for CAR-αβ functions. By contrast, a key feature of γδ T cells is that their TCRs have the potential to serve as detectors of cellular stress-related motifs, independent of peptide-MHC interactions. This enables endogenous γδ TCR signaling to contribute to activation in the context of cancer, removing the necessity to introduce synthetic CD3 signals via a CAR. In this regard, Vγ9Vδ2T cells expressing CARs which lacked CD3 signaling domains showed lower levels of tonic signaling, had reduced expression of exhaustion markers, and retained a degree of antigen-specific functional enhancement167,173.

On-target off-tumor toxicity is a consequence of the fact that CAR-T cells have been developed to target proteins that are not truly tumor-specific. In the context of targets such as CD19, B cell aplasia is relatively well tolerated and, indeed, has recently been harnessed in the treatment of autoimmunity. Regardless, the lack of cancer specificity has caused significant toxicities in other contexts174,175 and has led to appropriate caution in developing novel therapies. CAR γδ T cells retain the endogenous MHC-unrestricted responsiveness of γδ TCRs, thus removing the requirement for CD3ζ in their synthetic signaling apparatus. Chimeric receptors lacking CD3ζ but containing a range of signaling endodomains including DAP10 and CD28 provided functional benefit to Vδ2 T cells in the presence of tumor cell lines but not in the presence of healthy cells with equivalent target antigen expression167,173. An alternative approach, using chimeric receptors that recognise NKG2D ligands but signal via a CD3ζ endodomain176, is also under clinical evaluation (NCT05303037). This approach harnesses the ability of NKG2D to respond to stress ligands from multiple tumor types and results in both a CD3ζ (synthetic) and DAP10 (endogenous) signal when these are present, and also includes co-administration of zoledronic and IL-2 to further augment γδ T-mediated responses176.

Strategies involving secreted synthetic proteins

In addition to targeting autonomous tonic signalling, engineering strategies that rely on membrane-anchored, signal transducing molecules such as CAR are limited because only the engineered cells show enhanced activity. To overcome these limitations, γδ T cells have been engineered to deploy immune-active payloads. Because secreted payloads contain no synthetic signalling endodomains, they do not exhibit target autonomous signalling. Secreted payloads also have the potential to influence the behaviour of nearby un-engineered effector cells either via paracrine signals or by acting as opsonins upon binding to tumour cells. For example, Vδ2T cells expressing a nanobody-based CAR targeting HLA-G and secreting BiTEs targeting PD-L1 and CD3ε were effective at eliminating both HLA-G- and PD-L1-expressing tumors in preclinical models of triple-negative breast cancer. In this instance, the CAR endodomains included 4-1BB domains with an inserted TYK2-binding motif derived from the interferon-α/β receptor 1 (IFNAR1) and CD3ζ with an inserted DAP12-derived ITAM (denoted BBTyk-3z), which provided superior cytotoxicity and IFNγ release when compared to conventional 4-1BB-CD3ζ CARs. A suggested benefit of BiTE secretion was observed in the recruitment of stimulated bystander immune cells, as indicated by the enhanced tumor infiltration by human αβT cells in a partially humanised NSG mouse model177. In this regard, phase I studies of this technology are planned but have not yet commenced (NCT06150885).

Payload delivery by γδ T cells is not limited to BiTEs. Accompanied by a single dose of zoledronic acid, Vδ2 γδ T cells secreting an IL-15Rα-IL-15 fusion protein and scFv-Fc fusion proteins targeting GD2 showed enhanced efficacy against orthotopic, patient-derived xenograft models of osteosarcoma97. Cytotoxic enhancement was derived through the engagement of FcγRs on γδ T cells and other bystander immune cells including macrophages and NK cells. In this system, provision of IL-15 signals via the IL-15Rα-IL-15 fusion protein enhanced γδ T cell persistence and allowed recruitment of NK cells that were normally not recruited in the absence of this module. This study also raised an important point about the need for cytokine-armouring of γδ T cells to ensure persistence, and may explain some of the disappointing results observed using un-engineered γδ T cells. Furthermore, antibody payload secretion is not limited to ADCC-inducing opsonins: γδ T cells engineered to secrete humanized anti-PD1 antibodies showed superior control of subcutaneous OVCAR8 ovarian cancer xenografts in highly immunodeficient NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice when compared to unmodified γδ T cells. Importantly, both the HLA-G/BiTE and the anti-PD1-engineered γδ T cell regimens required weekly administration of γδ T cells163, whereas the model using IL-15Rα-IL-15 secretion required only one dose97.

Strategies harnessing γδ T cell biology in αβ T cell chassis

Growing understanding of the heterogeneity within specific γδ T cell subsets has highlighted challenges and opportunities in their use as immunotherapeutic effectors. For example, within expanded Vγ9Vδ2 populations, the cells’ baseline activation state has substantial impact on their functional properties178, and whilst the butyrophillin/Vγ9Vδ2 interaction is now widely accepted, Vγ9Vδ2 cells are polyclonal, hinting a potentially wide range of functional avidities influenced by TCR CDR3 sequences179. This has led to a collection of TCR transfer-based technologies where specific, Vγ9Vδ2 TCRs selected for optimal function are expressed in αβ T cells. Manufacturable at scale, αβ T cells expressing defined γδ TCRs (TEG cells)180 were efficacious against acute myeloid leukemia as well as patient-derived 3D tumor organoids181. Further development of the concept including the addition of chimeric co-receptors comprising NKG2D ectodomains lined to CD28 or 4-1BB endodomains182, or chimeric costimulatory receptors that also target BTN3A183, has demonstrated the impact of different signaling dynamics on effector proliferation and function between αβ and γδ TCRs. Importantly, however, the intracellular signaling events uncovered by these synthetic systems are housed within an αβ T cell chassis and are therefore free of some of the identified limitations of blood-derived Vγ9Vδ2T cells.

Clinical testing: past, present and future

The future of γδ T cell-based therapies has been influenced by recent changes. Since October 1st 2025, there has been a marked change in γδ T cell-based clinical trial activity. This change was in part prompted by Takeda halting all cell therapy programs using GDX012, an allogeneic Vδ1 γδ T cell-based therapy (e.g., NCT05886491) taken on as part of their acquisition of Gamma Delta Therapeutics. This has sadly deprived the field of data around the effectiveness of infused Vδ1+ γδ T cells, despite compelling preclinical data95,184.

Infusion of unmodified Vδ2 γδT cells has produced mixed results: they appear to be largely ineffective against solid tumors including breast cancer, pancreatic cancer and lung cancer (NCT03183206, NCT03180437, NCT02425748) but showing more promise against hematological malignancies. In the latter setting, the infusion protocols appear to govern effectiveness and highlights the importance of reducing disease burden prior to infusion. Thus, TCB-202-001 (NCT03790072), in which patients were infused up to 108 cells/kg of highly expanded haploidentical Vδ2T cells to pre-treated patients following fludarabine (25 mg/m2) and cyclophosphamide (500 mg/m2), yielded no benefit that could be definitively attributed to the cell product100. Conversely, within the context of prior HSCT, infusion of far fewer donor-derived cells (DL1 106/kg, DL2 3 × 106/kg) following haploidentical HSCT has led to median 15.3 month morphologic complete remission and 100% relapse-free survival, compared to historic 50% survival in similar patients receiving the same HSCT regimen (INB-100, NCT0353381670,72).

In 2024, the first reasonably sized dataset for an allogeneic CAR-Vδ1 product in patients with lymphoma became available, including 10 patients who received ADI-001 (CD20 CAR-Vδ1), with ORR 80%, CR 60% and a median duration of response of 17.5 months in patients with mantle cell lymphoma. This study was important as it demonstrated both safety and potential efficacy of a regimen involving multiple allogeneic CAR-γδ T infusions185. Sadly, despite showing early promising data, the trial of ADI-001 in B cell malignancies (NCT04735471) was terminated and the technology repurposed for testing in B cell-driven autoimmune diseases (NCT06375993). Recruitment to a another trial sponsored by the same company (ADI-270, comprising Vδ1 expressing an anti CD70 CAR and a dominant negative TGF-βRII) against renal cell carcinoma has also been halted (NCT06480565).

Despite these withdrawals, the number of actively recruiting γδ T cell-based trials increased from 10 cancer-focused studies in December 2024 to 22 studies in December 2025 covering both cancer (16 studies) and autoimmune indications (6 studies). These are summarized in Table 1 and include only two studies testing autologous products: NCT06193486, a PSCA CAR-Vδ2 product for metastatic prostate cancer, and NCT06196294, a combination of GPC3/Mesothelin CAR-γδ T ± immune checkpoint blockade antibody. Additionally, two studies continue the investigation of γδ T cells as a donor lymphocyte infusion after hematopoietic stem cell transplant (NCT03533816 and NCT05686538). All the other currently recruiting studies propose the use of allogeneic γδ T cells, reflecting the industry’s interest in the development of off-the-shelf treatments as a more cost-effective and scalable approach. A major contributor to the increased number of recruiting trials comes by substantial activity in China, with 10 actively recruiting studies all involving engineered γδ T cells. Outside the HSCT context, fludarabine and cyclophosphamide remain the commonest agents used for lymphodepletion prior to receipt of allogeneic or autologous γδ T cell-based therapies (9 out of 24 studies, 8 out of 24 do not specify). Other studies bypass the issue of lymphodepletion by infusing CAR-γδ T cells locally via CSF-access devices to target brain tumors. One product (INB-200), comprising autologous γδ T cells engineered to resist the effects of temozolomide maintenance chemotherapy substantially, extended mean progression-free survival (median PFS in 13 patients 16.1 months compared to typical median PFS 7 months for historical controls)76,186. Studies of CSF-delivered B7H3 CAR γδ T cells in patients with glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) or high-grade glioma and γδT with knockout of ARIH1 and BCL11b in patients GBM are currently ongoing (NCT06018363, NCT07144735).

Whilst allogeneic γδT cells do not cause GvHD, they are still susceptible to host-mediated rejection, thereby limiting their persistence of an infused allogeneic product. The encouraging results reported187, however, provide reasons for researchers to remain optimistic. A study of allogeneic, CD20-targeting CAR-Vδ1T cells showed persistence of the infused cells 28 days after delivery. In this study, the highest dose of CAR-Vδ1 yielded a mean 16,553 CAR copies/μl in patient blood at D + 28188, comparable with levels observed for autologous CAR-αβT, and clinical responses were observed189. The fact that the host immune system did not reject the allogeneic cells upon recovery from lymphodepletion indicates that there are many aspects of the immunobiology of γδ T cells that remain poorly understood. These findings highlight the relevance of conducting correlative studies on clinical samples to further our understanding of such processes, and to inform the development of improved γδ T cell products. Notwithstanding these successes, enhanced maintenance of allogeneic γδ T numbers has been approached in two ways, either by introducing gene editing to reduce HLA expression (NCT05554939) or by allowing multiple doses of an a-priori manufactured product to be given (9 out of24 studies currently recruiting specify this in the trials registry).

Combination of γδ T cells with adjunctive agents is also gaining in popularity. Prior administration of zoledronic acid is used as an adjunct in three studies, one harnessing autologous anti-PSCA CAR-Vδ2 in mCRPC (NCT06193486), one using an NKG2D ligand engaging CAR-γδ T product with IL-2 administration up to 2 h after cell infusion (NCT05302037), and one given in a chemoimmunotherapy strategy (irinotecan, temozolomide, dinutuximab and zoledronic acid); the last study (NCT05400603), where γδ T are infused after chemoimmunotherapy to patients with relapsed/refractory neuroblastoma or osteosarcoma, is innovative insomuch as the γδ T cells are given alongside a proven chemoimmunotherapy backbone that also removes the need for additional lymphodepletion190. In a different setting, combination with immune checkpoint-targeting antibodies has been tested in a trial of GPC3/Mesothelin CAR-γδT for lung, pancreatic or liver malignancies as well as mesothelioma (NCT06196294), in a trial of anti-EGFR conjugated γδT cells (NCT06415487), and in another study using HLA-G-CAR-γδ T cells that secrete an anti-PDL1/CD3ε bi-specific T cell engager. As these studies unfold, we will learn whether the increased potency provided by the combination agents, and/or by the expression of synthetic immune receptors, will boost the therapeutic efficacy of γδ T cells.

Due to the speed of innovation in γδ T engineering, several studies have only provided clinical proof-of-concept data to date, with further updates expected. An example is the ACE1831 trial (NCT05653271), which uses allogeneic Vδ2T cells chemically conjugated to anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies. The interim clinical outcomes for the first 5 patients enrolled indicate some responses even at the lowest dose levels (1 out of 5 complete response (CR), 3 out of 5 stable disease (SD) at 3 months) in patients with relapsed/refractory non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, while showing no ACE1831-related SAEs, dose-limiting toxicity, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or immune-effector cell associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS)191. Similarly, the KB-GDT-01 study, which combines repeated infusions of ex-vivo expanded γδ T cells with low dose localized radiotherapy (1Gy on D-2 and D-1, NCT06069570), has shown some initial responses in highly refractory metastatic NSCLC patients. Interim reports of 5 evaluable patients showed mean PFS of 4.8 m (range 2–8 months) with tumor shrinkage in 2 out of 5 patients and no dose-limiting toxicities. Again, this demonstrates the feasibility of multiple γδ T cell infusions, in this instance in combination with radiotherapy instead of conventional lymphodepletion.

Future directions

γδ T cells as a therapy for autoimmune disease

Whilst much of the evidence is from mouse data, γδ T cells can also regulate autoimmunity, providing a mechanistic rationale for exploiting them therapeutically. Related approaches build on the success of CAR-αβ T cells in autoimmunity192–194, but there are additional signs that γδ T cells themselves have a role in both the promotion and suppression of autoimmunity. In mice with experimental lupus, γδ T cell deficiency has been shown to exacerbate disease, while specific γδ subsets can restrain autoreactive αβ T cells, B cell help, and germinal centre responses, indicating an intrinsic capacity of γδ T cells to restore tolerance rather than simply promote inflammation195–198. In models of type I diabetes mellitus, CD8⁺ γδ “Treg” populations induced by aerosolised insulin can prevent transfer of disease in NOD mice in an IL-10–dependent fashion, and IL-17–producing γδ cells can similarly limit diabetogenic effector responses in a TGF-β–dependent manner199–201. These data, together with evidence that γδ T cells are numerically and phenotypically perturbed in human SLE and other rheumatic diseases202, underpin current efforts to either deplete pathogenic B lymphocyte compartments using γδ-based CAR T cells, or to reconstitute protective γδ subsets in vivo. 6 out of 24 of the currently open γδ T cell trials are employing them as a means of treating autoimmune diseases including systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic sclerosis (SSc) and Sjogren’s syndrome (SS). At present, all of the trials where sufficient detail is available aim to deplete B cell using CAR-γδ T targeting either CD19 (NCT06838832, NCT06361745, NCT06828041, NCT06106893) or CD20 (NCT06375993). It remains to be seen whether more nuanced approaches to harness γδ T cell biology will translate to the clinic, but it is conceivable that the tropism of Vδ2- γδ T populations for epithelial surfaces could offer a means of addressing diseases characterised by debilitating mucosal inflammation.

Steps to confidence in γδ T based immunotherapeutics

The rapid pace of development in advanced immunotherapy has, over the past 10 years, been characterised by surges in enthusiasm, which are typically then tempered by real world pressures such as high development costs and a need to provide rapid return on commercial investment. Thus, CD19 remains the commonest antigenic target for CAR-T trials203 because established and effective CD19-CAR-T therapies have widespread regulatory approval, most likely due to the favourable risk profile of CD19 targeting and the accrued confidence of clinicians and researchers in administering anti-CD19 therapies204,205. When broadening the range of targetable antigens and diseases, the risk of technological failure (due to non-efficacy or toxicity) comes from several places. Non-efficacy can arise from heterogenous target antigen expression, immune escape mechanisms or physical obstacles such as the currently debated blood/brain barrier 206, while toxicity can arise from off-tumor expression of target antigens or from inflammatory phenomena such as CRS or ICANS. Introduction of an additional unknown, through the use of a non-standard immune cell type such as γδ T cells, must therefore be strategically justified to funding bodies within the context of pre-existing risks. Given that γδ T cells can require complex expansion protocols95, and are hard to transduce with industry-standard lentivirus207, their use must be justified based on their particular advantages.

Discovery of the advantageous properties of γδ T cells and their transition to clinical use has been slowed by a lack of homology between mouse and human γδ T cell biology as well as the resultant lack of representative preclinical models. Whilst γδ T cells possess several characteristics, such as their innate anti-tumor activity and a lack of alloreactivity, that make them an excellent chassis for cellular cancer immunotherapy their clinical efficacy has not matched that of engineered αβ T cells. One may thus be tempted to dismiss this cellular subset as a suitable contender in the search for leading cancer therapeutics. To draw these conclusions would, however, be premature. Handled correctly, γδ T cells are as amenable to engineering as αβ T cells97,173,208,209, can be expanded to large numbers sufficient to treat multiple patients from a single production run100, and have the potential to be augmented by several compounds already in clinical use. As the demand for diverse cellular therapies transcends targeting of a limited range of antigens chosen due to tolerability of off-tumor toxicity, more nuanced approaches are needed. Simply using γδ T cells as a vehicle for cellular engineering designed for αβ T cells will not fully realize the potential of this subset. Instead, harnessing the innate reactivity of γδ T cells will offer an opportunity for multimodal immunotherapy, targeting several arms of the tumor/immune interaction to minimise the likelihood of disease escape. Having said that, it remains to be seen whether γδ T cell-based immunotherapies can deliver substantially better efficacy than αβ T based approaches, with the remaining challenges broadly categorised into biological and translational constraints.

From a biological perspective, γδ T are fundamentally more challenging as a starting material than αβ T cells. If circulating γδ T cells (Vδ2+ or Vδ2−) are used, their relative paucity in peripheral blood necessitates much greater expansion than αβ T cells to generate viable products. Meanwhile, using γδ T cells from alternative sources such as skin or umbilical cord blood may mitigate this problem, but may also be constrained by the availability of these tissues. In parallel, when using γδ T as a vector for immunotherapy, applying logic derived from αβ T biology may not be appropriate. For example, conventional 2nd generation CARs bypass the polyclonal αβ TCR, whereas similar engineering in γδ T must first acknowledge and integrate the influence of the γδ TCR to observe the existence of different functional lineages within γδ T subsets. The impact of tumoral adaptation must therefore also be considered: for example, productive Vγ9Vδ2 engagement requires coordinated BTN2A1-BTN3A1 organisation as well as sufficient intracellular phosphoantigen accumulation, with the disruption of either element reducing tumor sensitivity210. These constraints are particularly evident in solid tumors where spatially-imposed metabolic gradients and stromal buffering reduce the uniformity of target engagements and impose additional metabolic pressures on effector populations144,182. Added to this, high levels of inter- and intra-donor γδ T hererogeneity178, modest target-driven secondary expansion167,211 and the growing evidence that less ex-vivo expansion favors immunotherapeutic product efficacy212,213 all demand compelling justification for γδ T use. Addressing each of these challenges using engineered modules, such as cytokine armouring to support in-vivo persistence, is helpful but limited by cassette capacity.

From a translational perspective, a relative lack of detailed reporting of both γδ T cell trial protocols, GMP manufacturing protocols and clinical results has impeded iterative and consensus-based improvements in how γδ T cell can be used. Whilst the number of currently recruiting trials using engineered γδ T cells speaks to the feasibility of expanding and engineering them, published protocols can be complex95 and transduction challenging207. The likelihood is that these challenges will be set against the benefits of an allo-compatible cellular chassis with all the benefits of a-priori manufacture.

For advanced therapies involving CAR-T or other engineered immune cells, transition from academic funding to commercialization remains a key component of sustainable delivery. This is challenging for the γδ T cell field as a whole, as even well stablished cell therapies such as Kymriah, a CD19-targetted CAR-αβT cell therapy, have seen a 12% down-turn in sales in 2025214. Appetite for commercial investment into cell therapy has dampened substantially, with several companies involved in development of γδ T cell therapies closing their cell therapy programs or failing completely. This poses challenges, but also opportunities, for innovation within the γδ T cell field. Given the trend for retrenchment in the cell therapy space, future clinical trials involving γδ T cells should be designed in a way that respects the properties of this unique cell type. To fully realise this potential, publication of preclinical data, sharing or standardisation of manufacturing protocols, accruement of clinical biomarker data, and design of clinical trials to include detailed biological secondary endpoints are all warranted.
