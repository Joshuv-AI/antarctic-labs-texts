1.
Introduction

In this paper we study in detail a certain tiling of
\mathbb{N}^{2}
by
\mathbb{F}_{2}
that is obtained by simple geometric means from
the
number wall
of the Thue-Morse sequence and establish its automaticity in an explicit manner.
This tiling is tightly related to questions in Diophantine approximation
of quadratic Laurent series in the local field
\mathbb{F}_{2}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)
and to the properties of an orbit of the
full diagonal group in
\operatorname{PGL}_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)\times\mathbb{F}_{2}((t))\right)
acting on the homogeneous space

(1)

\operatorname{PGL}_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)\times\mathbb{F}_{2}((t))\right)/\operatorname{PGL}_{2}\left(\mathbb{F}_{2}\left[t,t^{-1}\right]\right).

Our analysis of the tiling allows us to disprove certain statements in the Diophantine side that were widely believed to hold and, in that respect, are surprising. Consequently, this paper may be of interest to three groups of mathematicians with different mathematical backgrounds: automatic tilings, Diophantine approximation, and homogeneous dynamics.

We split the introduction into two parts. We begin by explaining briefly the implication of the analysis carried out to Diophantine approximation and homogeneous dynamics. We then move to defining the necessary terminology in the theory of tilings and stating our main result.

1.1.
Partial escape of mass

Let
q
be a prime power. Every Laurent series
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)
has a unique continued fraction expansion

\theta=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cdots\vphantom{\cfrac{1}{1}}}}=:[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]\,,

where
a_{k}=a_{k}(\theta)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
are polynomials for every
k\geq 0
, and

(2)

d_{k}=d_{k}(\theta):=\mathrm{deg}\left(a_{k}\right)

for every
k\geq 1
satisfies
d_{k}\geq 1
. It also has a unique base-
t
expansion

\theta=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}b_{n}t^{-n}\,,

where
b_{n}=b_{n}(\theta)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and only finitely many of the coefficients of positive powers of
t
are nonzero. Multiplication by
t
changes the base-
t
expansion by a left-shift. Therefore, for every
j,n\in\mathbb{Z}
the base-
t
expansion of
t^{j}\theta
satisfies

(3)

b_{n}\left(t^{j}\theta\right)=b_{n+j}\left(\theta\right).

It is natural to ask how
a_{k}\left(t^{j}\theta\right)
vary as
k,j\rightarrow\infty
.

A very well studied scenario is when
\theta
is quadratic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
.
De Mathan and Teulié proved
[
5
, Theorem 4.5]
that

\limsup_{k,j\rightarrow\infty}d_{k}\left(t^{j}\theta\right)=\infty\,.

Paulin and Shapira
[
10
]
, relying on an earlier work with Kemarsky
[
7
]
,
improved this result by showing that, on average, a positive proportion of the degrees are unbounded, in the following sense:

Definition 1.1
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)
,
d>0
and
k\geq 1
, the
mass of
\theta
which lies above
d
up to the
k
th
partial quotient
is

(4)

e_{\theta}(d,k):=\frac{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\max\left\{d_{k^{\prime}}-d,0\right\}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}d_{k^{\prime}}}\,.

Their result
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
implies that if
q
is not a power of
2
and
\theta
is quadratic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
, then

(5)

\lim_{d\to\infty}\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}e_{t^{j}\theta}(d,k)>0\,.

From the homogeneous dynamics point of view which is described in
[
7
]
(and the analogous discussion in
[
2
]
in the real setup), every quadratic
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1}))
corresponds to a probability measure
\nu_{\alpha}
on the homogeneous space of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
-
lattices

X=\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1})))/\operatorname{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q}[t])
. The meaning of the limit in (
5
) being equal to
c
is that any weak* accumulation point
\nu
of the sequence of measures
\left\{\nu_{t^{j}\theta}\right\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfies
\nu(X)<1-c
. From this point of view, this paper is focused on the understanding of the measures
\left\{\nu_{t^{j}\theta}\right\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
.

Our analysis will allow the computation of the limit in (
5
) for a particular example of
\theta
which is of independent interest. Let
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
be two symbols and define a substitution
\mu:\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}\to\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}^{2}
by

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{a}&\mathbf{b}\end{array},\quad

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{b}&\mathbf{a}\end{array}.

Consider the sequence of finite strings of
\mathbf{a}
’s and
\mathbf{b}
’s obtained by starting with the string
\mathbf{a}
and applying
\mu
iteratively to it:

\mathbf{a}\overset{\mu}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\overset{\mu}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\overset{\mu}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\overset{\mu}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{a}\dots

The reader can easily convince themself by induction that for any
l\geq l^{\prime}\geq 0
, the first
2^{l^{\prime}}
symbols in the sequence
\mu^{l}(\mathbf{a})
do not depend on
l
. This means that there is a well defined limit of this process in
\left\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\right\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
This limit, which we denote by
\mu^{\infty}(\mathbf{a})
,
is
the
Thue-Morse sequence
.
Define a coding
\tau:\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}\to\{0,1\}
by

(8)

\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle=0\,,\quad

\displaystyle\tau(\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle=1\,.

The map
\tau
extends naturally to strings of
\mathbf{a}
’s and
\mathbf{b}
’s. Denote

(9)

\alpha=\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}:=\tau\left(\mu^{\infty}(\mathbf{a})\right)\,,

and use the same letter for the corresponding Laurent series in
\mathbb{F}_{2}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)

\alpha=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}t^{-n}\,.

It is
well-known that

\alpha
is quadratic over
\mathbb{F}_{2}(t)
(see
[
4
]
).
Our main application is
the following result, which disproves
[
7
, Conjecture 6]
, where it is predicted that the limit in (
10
) is
1
:

Theorem 1.2
.

(10)

\lim_{d\to\infty}\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{k\to\infty}e_{t^{j}\alpha}(d,k)=\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}\,.

1.2.
Tilings and diagonally-aligned number walls

We now introduce some terminology in order to state our main Theorem
1.3
, which
implies Theorem
1.2
. We refer the reader to
[
1
, Section 4]
for a more thorough exposition of this terminology.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite set and refer to its elements as
tiles
. A
tiling of a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by
\Sigma

is simply a map
T:S\to\Sigma
. We think of the tiling as a planar array of tiles where the tile
T(m,n)
is located at position
(m,n)
. Note that the way we arrange
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in the plane is
not
the standard way but the one consistent with matrix notation. That is, as the row index
m
increases the position advances
downwards
, and as the column index
n
grows the position advances
rightwards
.

Let

(11)

\theta=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}t^{-n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right).

It is well known
that many of the Diophantine properties of the Laurent series
\theta
are encoded in the tiling

(12)

\mathfrak{H}(\theta):=\left\{\mathfrak{H}_{m,n}(\theta)\right\}_{m=1,n=1}^{\infty},

where for any
m,n\geq 1
,

(13)

\mathfrak{H}_{m,n}(\theta):=\det\begin{pmatrix}b_{n}&b_{n+1}&\cdots&b_{n+m-1}\\
b_{n+1}&\iddots&\iddots&b_{n+m}\\
\vdots&\iddots&\iddots&\vdots\\
b_{n+m-1}&b_{n+m}&\cdots&b_{n+2m-2}\end{pmatrix}

is a determinant of a Hankel matrix which corresponds to the sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, computed in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
More explicitly, for any
k\geq 1
, denote

(14)

i_{k}=i_{k}(\theta):=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}d_{k^{\prime}}\,,

where
\left\{d_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are the degrees of the partial quotients of
\theta
defined in (
2
).
The integer
i_{k}
is the degree of the denominator of the
k
th
convergent of
\theta
. A standard fact in the theory of Diophantine approximation over function fields
[
11
, Setion 9]
is that

(15)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{i,1}(\theta)\neq 0

\displaystyle\iff\min_{M,N\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t],\;N\neq 0,\;\mathrm{deg}N<i}|N\theta-M|=q^{-i}

\displaystyle\iff i=i_{k}(\theta)\text{ for some }k\geq 1\,,

where the first equivalence follows by rewriting
|N\theta-M|=q^{-i}
as a system of linear equations. By (
3
) and (
13
), the same holds more generally for every
j\geq 0
with
\mathfrak{H}_{i,j+1}(\theta)
and
t^{j}\theta
instead of
\mathfrak{H}_{i,1}(\theta)
and
\theta
. It is evident from this and from Definition
1.1
that in order to understand the approximations of
\left\{t^{j}\theta\right\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
by rational functions in
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
in general and questions about escape of mass in particular, one can study the patterns of zeros in
\mathfrak{H}(\theta)
.

Figure 1.
Hankel determinants of the Thue-Morse sequence
\mathfrak{H}_{m,n}(\alpha)
for every
m,n\geq 1
such that
2m+n-2\leq 128
, where the
0
’s are white and the
1
’s are black.

For the Thue-Morse sequence over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, it is visually evident that
\mathfrak{H}(\alpha)
has some self-similarity (see Figure
1
). In their celebrated paper
[
3
]
, Allouche, Peyrière, Wen and Wen formalise this by proving that
\mathfrak{H}(\alpha)
is
automatic
, which loosely speaking means that it is obtained as a limit of a substitution and coding process similar to the one used above in the definition of
\alpha
(see
[
4
, Section 14]
for a treatment of
2
-dimensional automatic tilings).
However, in
[
3
]
no explicit substitution and coding that generate
\mathfrak{H}(\alpha)
are provided. This paper fills this gap. Apart from its importance to the subject of automatic tilings, this explicit structure is precisely what allows us to deduce Theorem
1.2
in §
4
.

Our main discovery is that the symmetries and structure of the tiling
\mathfrak{H}(\alpha)
, although still quite complex, become reasonably approachable
after applying a certain linear map to the plane.
In order to do that, for any
\theta
as in (
11
) we first extend the tiling
\mathfrak{H}(\theta)
to nonpositive
m
’s by setting

\mathfrak{H}_{m,n}(\theta)=\begin{cases}1&\textrm{for $m=0$ and all $n\geq 1$}\\
0&\textrm{for all $m<0$ and $n\geq 2|m|+1$}\end{cases}.

We then define

(16)

\mathfrak{F}_{m,n}(\theta):=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\tfrac{(m-n-1)(m-n+1)}{2}}\mathfrak{H}_{\frac{m-n+1}{2},n}(\theta)&m\not\equiv n(2)\\
0&m\equiv n(2)\end{cases}

for every
m\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
.

The array

(17)

\mathfrak{F}(\theta):=\left\{\mathfrak{F}_{m,n}(\theta)\right\}_{m=0,n=1}^{\infty}\,

is an example of a tiling we call
diagonally-aligned number wall
(see Section
2
for a definition).
The main discovery in this paper is that
\mathfrak{F}(\alpha)
has a remarkable structure which turns out to be simple enough in order to be explicitly described (see Figure
2
).

Figure 2.
The (diagonally-aligned) number wall of the Thue-Morse sequence
\mathfrak{F}_{m,n}(\alpha)
for
0\leq m\leq 129
and
1\leq n\leq 130
, where the
0
’s are white and the
1
’s are black.

Our main result Theorem
1.3
below identifies the tiling
\mathfrak{F}(\alpha)
as an explicit coding of a substitution tiling. The definition of the substitution and coding requires some preparations which we now turn our attention to.

1.3.
The main theorem

Our goal now is to define the necessary objects needed to state our main result. Unfortunately, we need to introduce quite a bit of structure in order for things to be understandable. Our plan is as follows:

•

We define a set
\Sigma
of
15

basic tiles
and a group
G
of
16
elements that acts on
\Sigma
.

•

We then extend the action of
G
to an action on square matrices
\operatorname{Mat}_{2^{l}}(\Sigma)
for any
l\geq 0
, that involves both the
G
-action on the entries as well as a geometric action as isometries of the matrix thought of as a square.

•

We then use this
G
-action to define a
2
-substitution
\sigma
on
\Sigma
that
commutes
with the
G
-actions defined. By choosing an appropriate tile
\mathbf{a}_{0}\in\Sigma
, we obtain a
2
-dimensional substitution tiling
\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})

with remarkable symmetries which are captured by the
G
-actions.

•

We then define a
4
-coding
\kappa_{1}:\Sigma\longrightarrow\operatorname{Mat}_{4}(\mathbb{F}_{2})
and obtain the
2
-dimensional tiling
\kappa_{1}\left(\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right)
of
\mathbb{N}^{2}
by
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

The following theorem is the main theorem of this paper. It will be restated again in the end of the current subsection after we carry out the plan laid above.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\alpha
be the Thue-Morse sequence as in (
9
).
The substitution
\sigma
and coding
\kappa_{1}
generate
\mathfrak{F}(\alpha)
. More precisely,

\left\{\mathfrak{F}_{m,n}(\alpha)\right\}_{m=1,n=1}^{\infty}=\kappa_{1}\left(\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right).

We now execute the above plan that will allow the reader to understand the automatic tiling
\kappa_{1}\left(\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right)
featuring in Theorem
1.3
.

is a

, the tiles

and

are fixed points for

.

To summarise, we have defined the set of basic tiles

\Sigma=\left\{\mathbf{a}_{i},\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{i}:0\leq i\leq 3\right\}\cup\left\{\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}},\mathbf{o}\right\}

which consists of
15
tiles, on which the dihedral group
D
acts and partition it into five orbits.

Next, we introduce an extra symmetry on
\Sigma
that flips between
\mathbf{a}_{i}
and
\mathbf{b}_{i}
for every
0\leq i\leq 3
and also flips between
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}
and
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}
. This extra symmetry is crucial for our analysis.
Let

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\left\langle\iota|\iota^{2}\right\rangle

be a group of order two.

Definition 1.4
.

The action of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
on
\Sigma
is defined by the following involution:

\displaystyle\iota\mathbf{o}=\mathbf{o}\,,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \iota\mathbf{c}_{i}&=\mathbf{c}_{i}\,,&\quad&\textrm{for all $0\leq i\leq 3$}\,,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \iota\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}&=\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}\,,&\textrm{ and }&&\iota\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}&=\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\,,&\quad&\\
\iota\mathbf{a}_{i}&=\mathbf{b}_{i}\,,&\textrm{ and }&&\iota\mathbf{b}_{i}&=\mathbf{a}_{i}\,,&\quad&\textrm{for all $0\leq i\leq 3$}\,.\end{aligned}

The action of
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
on
\Sigma
commutes with the action of
D
. Therefore, this gives an action of the group

G:=D\times\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}

on
\Sigma
, where
D
and
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
are viewed as subgroups of
G
. The decomposition of
\Sigma
into
G
-orbits is almost identical to the decomposition into
D
-orbits, just that now, the two
D
-orbits
\left\{\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\}_{i=0}^{3}
and
\left\{\mathbf{b}_{i}\right\}_{i=0}^{3}
become one
G
-orbit of
8
elements. We have

(18)

\Sigma=G\mathbf{a}_{0}\cup G\mathbf{c}_{0}\cup G\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\cup G\mathbf{o}\,.

1.3.2.
Actions on matrices

When one looks at the tiling
\mathfrak{F}(\alpha)
in different scales and stares at it enough, the graphic depictions of
the tiles in
\Sigma
emerge (see Figure
3
).

Figure 3.
The (diagonally-aligned) number wall of the Thue-Morse sequence
\mathfrak{F}_{m,n}(\alpha)
for
0\leq m\leq 2049
and
1\leq n\leq 2050
, where the
0
’s are white and the
1
’s are black.

In order to be able to exploit the
G
-action on these tiles for our cause, it is necessary to generalise this action to an action of
G
on
square matrices over
\Sigma
. There are two natural actions:

•

For
g\in G
and a matrix
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\Sigma)
, let
T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{g}M
denote the matrix obtained from
M
by acting on each of its entries by
g
. It is clear that this defines a
G
-action on
\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\Sigma)
for any
n\geq 1
.

•

For
g\in G
and a matrix
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\Sigma)
, let
T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{g}M
denote the matrix obtained from
M
by acting on it by the isometry of the square
\pi(g)
, where

\pi:G\to D

is the natural projection. For example,
T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\iota}
acts trivially,
T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\rho}M
rotates
M
by
90\degree
clockwise and
T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\eta}M
reflects
M
along
the anti-diagonal.
It should be clear that this defines an action of
G
on
\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\Sigma)
for any
n\geq 1
.

These two actions commute in the sense that for every
n\geq 1
and
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{n}(\Sigma)
, every
g,h\in G
satisfy

(19)

T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{g}T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{h}M=T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{h}T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{g}M\,.

In reality we will use a
twisted
combination of these two actions.
The twist is obtained from an automorphism
\psi:G\to G
, which we now introduce.

Definition 1.5
.

The function
\psi:G\to G
is the unique group automorphism which is defined on the generators of
G
by

(20)

\psi(\rho)=\iota\rho\,,\quad\psi(\eta)=\iota\eta\,,\quad\psi(\iota)=\iota\,.

The reader can verify that there exists a unique automorphism
\psi
of
G
that satisfies (
20
). Such a verification amounts to showing that the group generated by
\iota\rho
and
\iota\eta
is isomorphic to
D
and that it does not contain
\iota
.

We now define for any
l\geq 0
the
twisted action
of
G
on
\operatorname{Mat}_{2^{l}}(\Sigma)
. This action will extend the
G
-action described above on
\Sigma
which corresponds to the case
l=0
.

Definition 1.6
.

The action of
G
on
\operatorname{Mat}_{2^{l}}(\Sigma)
is as follows: for every
g\in G
and
M\in\operatorname{Mat}_{2^{l}}(\Sigma)
, the action of
g
on
M
is

(21)

gM:=T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{g}T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\psi^{l}(g)}M\,.

In words, the action of
g
on a matrix is defined by the geometric action of
\pi(g)
as an isometry on the matrix
M
thought of as a square and the action of
\psi^{l}(g)
on each of the entries of the matrix
M
.

Note that the commutation (
19
) implies that this is indeed an action of
G
on

\operatorname{Mat}_{2^{l}}(\Sigma)
for any
l\geq 0
. Also note, that for
l=0
we recover the action of
G
on
\Sigma
.

Example 1.7
.

It is instructive to work out explicitly what happens for
2\times 2
matrices (
l=1
):

\displaystyle\rho\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\rho)c&\psi(\rho)a\\
\psi(\rho)d&\psi(\rho)b\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\rho c&\iota\rho a\\
\iota\rho d&\iota\rho b\end{matrix}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\eta\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\eta)d&\psi(\eta)b\\
\psi(\eta)c&\psi(\eta)a\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\eta d&\iota\eta b\\
\iota\eta c&\iota\eta a\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\iota\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\iota)a&\psi(\iota)b\\
\psi(\iota)c&\psi(\iota)d\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota a&\iota b\\
\iota c&\iota d\end{matrix}\right).

The following lemma is essential for the extension of the definition of
\sigma
from
\Sigma_{0}
to all of
\Sigma
equivariantly.

Lemma 1.9
.

Every
s\in\Sigma_{0}=\left\{\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{c}_{0},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}},\mathbf{o}\right\}

satisfies

(23)

\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(s)\leq\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(\sigma(s))\,.

Proof.

The proof follows by checking (
23
) for every
s\in\Sigma_{0}
. Start with
s=\mathbf{a}_{0}
. Since
|G|=16
and
|G\mathbf{a}_{0}|=8
,
the stabilizer group
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(\mathbf{a}_{0})
has
2
elements. Since it contains the involution
\eta
, it must be equal to
\left\langle\eta\right\rangle
. Acting with
\eta
on
\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{0})
using the twisted
G
-action, equation (
22
) gives

\eta\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{0})=\eta\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{c}_{0}\\
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}&\mathbf{b}_{0}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\eta\mathbf{b}_{0}&\iota\eta\mathbf{c}_{0}\\
\iota\eta\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}&\iota\eta\mathbf{a}_{0}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{c}_{0}\\
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}&\mathbf{b}_{0}\end{matrix}\right),

so
\eta\in\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(\sigma(\mathbf{a}_{0}))
.

Proceed with
s=\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}
. The orbit
G\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}
has
2
elements and hence its stabilizer group is of order
8
. It clearly contains
\rho
and
\iota\eta
(since both
\iota
and
\eta
switch between
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}
and
\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}
). Since the group
\left\langle\rho,\iota\eta\right\rangle
has
8
elements, it is
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)
. Thus, in order to verify (
23
) it is enough to show that
\rho
and
\iota\eta
stabilize
\sigma\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)
.
Working with the definition of the twisted action on matrices (see (
22
)) gives

\displaystyle\rho\sigma\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{2}\\
\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{b}_{3}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\rho)\mathbf{a}_{0}&\psi(\rho)\mathbf{b}_{1}\\
\psi(\rho)\mathbf{b}_{3}&\psi(\rho)\mathbf{a}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\rho\mathbf{a}_{0}&\iota\rho\mathbf{b}_{1}\\
\iota\rho\mathbf{b}_{3}&\iota\rho\mathbf{a}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{2}\\
\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{b}_{3}\end{matrix}\right).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\eta\iota\sigma\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)

\displaystyle=\eta\iota\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{2}\\
\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{b}_{3}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\eta\iota)\mathbf{b}_{3}&\psi(\eta\iota)\mathbf{a}_{2}\\
\psi(\eta\iota)\mathbf{a}_{0}&\psi(\eta\iota)\mathbf{b}_{1}\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\eta\mathbf{b}_{3}&\eta\mathbf{a}_{2}\\
\eta\mathbf{a}_{0}&\eta\mathbf{b}_{1}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{b}_{1}&\mathbf{a}_{2}\\
\mathbf{a}_{0}&\mathbf{b}_{3}\end{matrix}\right).

Next, equation (
23
) is verified for
s=\mathbf{c}_{0}
. As for the previous cases, it is easy to see that since the
orbit has
4
elements, the stabilizer is of order
4
, so it must be
\left\langle\eta,\iota\right\rangle
. Therefore, it is enough to check that
\iota,\eta\in\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0}))
. Indeed,

\eta\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})=\eta\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{o}&\mathbf{o}\\
\mathbf{c}_{0}&\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\eta)\mathbf{o}&\psi(\eta)\mathbf{o}\\
\psi(\eta)\mathbf{c}_{0}&\psi(\eta)\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\eta\iota\mathbf{o}&\eta\iota\mathbf{o}\\
\eta\iota\mathbf{c}_{0}&\eta\iota\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{o}&\mathbf{o}\\
\mathbf{c}_{0}&\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right).

Similarly,

\iota\sigma(\mathbf{c}_{0})=\iota\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{o}&\mathbf{o}\\
\mathbf{c}_{0}&\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\psi(\iota)\mathbf{o}&\psi(\iota)\mathbf{o}\\
\psi(\iota)\mathbf{c}_{0}&\psi(\iota)\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\mathbf{o}&\iota\mathbf{o}\\
\iota\mathbf{c}_{0}&\iota\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{o}&\mathbf{o}\\
\mathbf{c}_{0}&\mathbf{o}\end{matrix}\right).

Finally, the verification of (
23
) for
s=\mathbf{o}
is left to the reader.
∎

Given Lemma
1.9
, we extend the definition of
\sigma
to all of
\Sigma
as follows:

Definition 1.10
.

For any
s\in\Sigma
, write
s=gs_{0}
for some
s_{0}\in\Sigma_{0}
and
g\in G
.
Then

\sigma(s)=\sigma(gs_{0}):=g\sigma(s_{0})\,,

where
\sigma(s_{0})
is as in Definition
1.8
.

This is well defined. Indeed,
if
s=g_{1}s_{1}=g_{2}s_{2}
for some
s_{1},s_{2}\in\Sigma_{0}
, then by construction
s_{1}=s_{2}
and
g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}\in\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(s_{1})
. So, by Lemma
1.9

g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}\in\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(\sigma(s_{1}))
, which implies that
g_{1}\sigma(s_{1})=g_{2}\sigma(s_{2})
, as needed.

As hinted above, the commutation relation
\sigma(gs)=g\sigma(s)
for
s\in\Sigma
will be a key feature in our ability to analyse this substitution. It is the seed that propagates and governs the symmetries in
\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})
.

Proposition 1.11
.

The
G
-actions on
\Sigma
and on
\operatorname{Mat}_{2}(\Sigma)
commute with
\sigma
. More explicitly, every
g\in G
and
s\in\Sigma
satisfy

(24)

\sigma(gs)=g\sigma(s)\,.

Proof.

By Definition
1.10
this is almost a tautology, but there is still something to check: given
g\in G
and
s\in\Sigma
, write
s=g_{0}s_{0}
for some
g_{0}\in G
and
s_{0}\in\Sigma_{0}
. Definition
1.10
gives

\sigma(gs)=\sigma(gg_{0}s_{0})=gg_{0}\sigma(s_{0})\,.

On the other hand, by the same definition,

g\sigma(s)=g\sigma(g_{0}s_{0})=gg_{0}\sigma(s_{0})\,,

which finishes the proof.
∎

Structure of the paper

The rest of the paper is organised as follows: Tilings like (

2.
The frame relations

This section is devoted to arithmetic relations that characterise arrays such as
(
17
). A version of this characterisation is studied by Lunnon
[
8
]
. It plays a central role in
[
1
]
, and it also plays a central role in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The following terminology is
standard
and convenient for introducing these relations. For simplicity, it is only phrased in the context of subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and functions to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
:

Definition 2.1
.

(1)

A
shape
is a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

(2)

A
pattern
is a function
P:S\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
S
is a shape. In this case
P
is a
pattern of shape
S
.

(3)

If
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
are shapes and
P
and
P^{\prime}
are patterns of shapes
S
and
S^{\prime}
, respectively, then
P
is contained in
P^{\prime}
if
\left.P^{\prime}\right|_{S}=P
.

We will mostly be dealing with rectangular shapes that are aligned with the standard axes of the plane or along axes which are rotated by
45\degree
. In order to describe the latter ones it is convenient to have a
45\degree
rotation of the plane with integer coefficients. Since we think of the coordinates of the plane as row vectors, it will be defined as a linear action on row vectors. Denote

(27)

R(x,y):=(x,y)\left(\begin{matrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{matrix}\right)=(x+y,y-x)\,.

Definition 2.2
.

(1)

An
axes-aligned rectangle
is a shape
S
for which there exist

x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
for which

S=\left([1,x]\times[1,y]+(x_{0},y_{0})\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,.

Its
height
and
width
are
x
and
y
. It is a
square
if
x=y
, and then its
size
is equal to its height and width.

(2)

A
diagonally-aligned rectangle
is a shape
S
for which there exist

x_{0},y_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
for which

S=\left(R([1,x]\times[1,y])+(x_{0},y_{0})\right)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,.

Its
height
and
width
are
x
and
y
.
It is a
diagonally-aligned square
if
x=y
, and then its
size
equals its height and width.

(3)

A pattern is
axes-aligned
(respectively,
diagonally-aligned
)
rectangular
if its shape is an axes-aligned (respectively, diagonally-aligned) rectangle. In this case, its
height
and
width
are those of its shape. Axes-aligned rectangular patterns are also referred to as matrices.

2.1.
Number walls

Definition 2.3
.

A
frame pattern
and the corresponding
frame relation
is one of the following three:

(1)

A
plus pattern
is a diagonally-aligned square pattern of size
2

\begin{array}[]{ccc}&A&\\
B&E&C\\
&D&\end{array}.

It satisfies the
plus relation
if

E^{2}=AD+BC\,.

(2)

An
inner frame pattern
is a pattern of the form

\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}&&&A&&&&\\
&&&0&&&&\\
&&\iddots&&\ddots&&&\\
B&0&&&&\ddots&&\\
&&\ddots&&&&0&C\\
&&&\ddots&&\iddots&&\\
&&&&0&&&\\
&&&&D&&&\\
\end{array}

where the
0
’s form a diagonally-aligned rectangle of height
k
and width
w
(the
\ell_{\infty}
-distances between the coordinates of
A
to
B
and
C
, respectively). It satisfies the
inner frame relation
if

AD=(-1)^{k(k+w-1)}BC\,,

(3)

An
outer frame pattern
is a pattern of the form

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}&&&&E&&&&&\\
&&&&A&A^{\prime}&&&&\\
&&&&0&&&&&\\
&&&\iddots&&\ddots&&&&\\
F&B&0&&&&\ddots&&C^{\prime}&\\
&B^{\prime}&&\ddots&&&&0&C&G\\
&&&&\ddots&&\iddots&&&\\
&&&&&0&&&&\\
&&&&D^{\prime}&D&&&&\\
&&&&&H&&&&\\
\end{array}

with
ABCD\neq 0
. The diagonally-aligned rectangular pattern whose corners are
A,B,C,D
is the corresponding inner frame pattern. It satisfies the
outer frame relation
if

\left(EB^{\prime}+\left(-1\right)^{k}FA^{\prime}\right)CD=\left(HC^{\prime}+\left(-1\right)^{k}GD^{\prime}\right)AB\,.

Definition 2.4
.

A
number wall
is a pattern which satisfies the frame relations of all patterns which it contains.

Note that the plus relations force any two diagonally adjacent zeros to propagate into a
2\times 2
square pattern of zeros. This implies that the zero patterns in number walls are arranged in rectangles.
The inner frame relations ensure that they are squares – because any diagonally-aligned rectangular pattern of
0
’s
P
propagates by the plus relation to the smallest square pattern containing
P
.

Number walls are tightly connected to Toeplitz determinants. This connection is the reason they are key to our discussion.
For any
\theta=\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathbb{N}}

denote

\mathfrak{T}\left(\theta\right):=\left\{\mathfrak{T}_{m,n}\left(\theta\right)\right\}_{m>-\infty,n\geq|m+1|},

where for any
n>m\geq 0
,

(28)

\mathfrak{T}_{m,n}\left(\theta\right):=\det\begin{pmatrix}b_{n}&b_{n+1}&\cdots&b_{n+m}\\
b_{n-1}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&b_{n+1}\\
b_{n-m}&\cdots&b_{n-1}&b_{n}\end{pmatrix},

and
\mathfrak{T}_{-1,n}=1
for all
n\geq 0
and
\mathfrak{T}_{m,n}=0
for all
m<-1
and
n\geq-m-1
.
The following theorem follows from
[
8
, page 11]
(see also
[
1
, Corollary 3.6]
):

Theorem 2.5
.

For every
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathbb{N}}
the array
\mathfrak{T}\left(\theta\right)
is a number wall. Conversely, if
\mathfrak{G}=\left\{\mathfrak{G}_{m,n}\right\}_{m>-\infty,n\geq|m+1|}
is a number wall which satisfies
\mathfrak{G}_{-1,n}=1
for all
n\geq 0
and
\mathfrak{G}_{m,n}=0
for all
m<-1
and
n>-m-1
, then the sequence
\theta=\left\{\mathfrak{G}_{0,n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfies
\mathfrak{T}\left(\theta\right)=\mathfrak{G}
.

The frame relations in Definition
2.3
connect the values on the corners of diagonally-aligned rectangular patterns. Therefore, if one is looking for a simple structure in a number wall, it is natural to look for it in directions parallel to axes of slope
\pm 1
. This point was already noticed by Lunnon
[
9
]
. An example of a number wall over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
with such a structure is suggested in
[
9
, Appendix C]
. Recently, this approach was employed by Garrett and Robertson
[
6
]
where Lunnon’s work is extended to fields of characteristic
5
,
7
and
11
.

A slightly different approach is taken in this paper. Instead of looking for a structure along lines of slopes
\pm 1
, the number wall itself is rotated by
45\degree
clockwise.
The original tiles fit points of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
for which all the
\ell_{1}
-distances are even, while the rest of the points are filled with zeros. Apart from making the structure axes-aligned, the resulting tiling is natural from a dynamical point of view. In short, after the rotation, the minimal
\ell_{1}
-distance to a nonzero entry is precisely the height in the homogeneous space (
1
). This point of view gives another reason to the zero values that are introduced in-between the tiles of the original number wall. This part of the story is not essential for the proofs of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. Hence, we choose not to elaborate further on it.

2.2.
Diagonally-aligned number walls

We now define a family of tilings which we call
diagonally-aligned number walls
and are obtained from number walls by a simple procedure of rotation, inflation and translation, which we now describe formally. If needed, and without a risk of confusion, number walls as in Section
2.1
will be referred to as axes-aligned number walls, and the term number wall will refer to both axes-aligned and diagonally-aligned ones according to the context. Recall the definition of the linear map
R
in (
27
).

Definition 2.6
.

The
inflated rotation of a shape
S
is the shape

S^{R}:=R(S)\sqcup\left\{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,|R(S)\cap\{v\pm(1,0),v\pm(0,1)\}|\geq 2\right\}.

For any
v_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the
inflated rotation of a pattern
P
translated by
v_{0}
is the pattern of shape
S^{R}+v_{0}
which is defined by

P^{R,v_{0}}(v)=\begin{cases}P\left(R^{-1}(v-v_{0})\right)&\mbox{ if }v\in R(S)+v_{0}\\
0&\mbox{ if }v\in\left(S^{R}\setminus R(S)\right)+v_{0}\end{cases}.

Since for any shape
S
the image
R(S)
is contained in
\left\{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\,:\,m\equiv n(2)\right\}
, and since the inverse map of
R
is

R^{-1}(x,y)=(x,y)\left(\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\[2.0pt]
-\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\frac{x-y}{2},\frac{x+y}{2}\right),

if
P
is a pattern over
S
then every
(m,n)\in S^{R}
satisfies

(29)

P^{R}(m,n)=\begin{cases}P\left(\frac{m-n}{2},\frac{m+n}{2}\right)&\mbox{ if }m\equiv n(2)\\
0&\mbox{ if }m\not\equiv n(2)\end{cases}.

Definition 2.7
.

A pattern
P
is a
diagonally-aligned number wall
if there exist a number wall
P^{\prime}
and a vector
v_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
such that
P=(P^{\prime})^{R,v_{0}}
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathbb{N}}
, define

\mathfrak{F}(\theta):=\mathfrak{T}(\theta)^{R,(1,0)}\,.

By (
29
), the explicit formula is

(30)

\mathfrak{F}_{m,n}\left(\theta\right)=\begin{cases}\mathfrak{T}_{\frac{m-n-1}{2},\frac{m+n-1}{2}}\left(\theta\right)&m\not\equiv n(2)\\
0&m\equiv n(2)\end{cases}

for every
m\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
.

It should be clear from the definition of the Hankel and Toeplitz determinants in (
13
) and (
28
) (and their extension to nonpositive and negative rows, respectively) that the relation between the Hankel and Toeplitz tilings of
\theta
is

\mathfrak{T}_{m,n}(\theta)=(-1)^{\tfrac{m(m+1)}{2}}\mathfrak{H}_{m+1,n-m}(\theta)\,.

Hence, equation (
30
) is consistent with the definition (
16
) of
\mathfrak{F}(\theta)
, which was already given in the introduction in terms of Hankel determinants.

The following analogue of Theorem
2.5
for diagonally-aligned number walls
is a direct corollary of it.

Theorem 2.8
.

For every
\theta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathbb{N}}
the array
\mathfrak{F}\left(\theta\right)
is a diagonally-aligned number wall. Conversely, if
\left\{\mathfrak{G}_{m,n}\right\}_{m\geq 0,n\geq 1}
is a diagonally-aligned number wall which satisfies
\mathfrak{G}_{n-1,n}=1
for all
n\geq 1
and
\mathfrak{G}_{m,n}=0
if for all
m\geq 0
and
n>m+1
, then the sequence
\theta=\left\{\mathfrak{G}_{n+1,n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfies
\mathfrak{F}\left(\theta\right)=\left\{\mathfrak{G}_{m,n}\right\}_{m\geq 0,n\geq 1}
.

When one takes Definition
2.3
and applies the linear map
R
as above, one obtains the following:

Definition 2.9
.

A
diagonally-aligned frame pattern
and the corresponding
frame relation
is one of the following four:

(1)

A
parity pattern
is a rectangular pattern of height and width
1
and
2
or
2
and
1
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{cc}B&A\end{array},

or

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}A\\
C\end{array}.

It satisfies the
parity relation
if
AB=0
or
AC=0
, respectively.

(2)

An
ex pattern
is a pattern of the form

\begin{array}[]{ccc}B&0&A\\
0&E&0\\
D&0&C\end{array}.

It satisfies the
ex relation
if

E^{2}=AD+BC\,.

(3)

An
inner frame pattern
is a rectangular pattern of the form

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}B&0&\cdots&0&A\\
0&\cdots&&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&&\cdots&0\\
D&0&\cdots&0&C\\
\end{array}.

It satisfies the
inner frame relation
if

AD=(-1)^{\frac{k-1}{2}\frac{k+z-4}{2}}BC\,,

where
z
and
k
are the height and width of the rectangle (the distances between the coordinates of
A
to
C
and
B
, respectively, plus
1
). Note that if
ABCD=0
then the sign term
can be ignored, while if
ABCD\neq 0
and additionally the parity relations as in Definition
2.9
(
1
) are satisfied, then
z
and
k
must be even.

(4)

An
outer frame pattern
is a rectangular pattern of the form

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}F&0&*&0&\cdots&0&*&0&E\\
0&B&0&\cdots&&\cdots&0&A&0\\
B^{\prime}&0&\cdots&&&&\cdots&0&A^{\prime}\\
0&\cdots&&&&&&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&&&&&&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&&&&&&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&&&&&&\cdots&0\\
D^{\prime}&0&&&&&&0&C^{\prime}\\
0&D&0&\cdots&&\cdots&0&C&0\\
H&0&*&0&\cdots&0&*&0&G\end{array}

with
ABCD\neq 0
. The rectangular pattern whose corners are
A,B,C,D
is the corresponding
inner frame pattern. It satisfies the
outer frame relation
if

\left(EB^{\prime}+\left(-1\right)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}FA^{\prime}\right)CD=\left(HC^{\prime}+\left(-1\right)^{\frac{k-1}{2}}GD^{\prime}\right)AB\,.

where
k
is the corresponding width of the inner frame.

Definition
2.9

allows us to directly check if a pattern is a diagonally-aligned number wall, by checking that all the frame patterns it contains satisfy the
frame relations.

A few remarks are in order.

•

The parity relations, which do not appear in the definition of axes-aligned number walls, simply guarantees that our pattern (at least as long as its shape is connected) agrees with a pattern of the form
P^{R,v_{0}}
for some pattern
P
and translation vector
v_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

•

The ex relations guarantee that in diagonally-aligned number walls the zeros appear in diagonally-aligned rectangles.

•

The inner frame relations make sure that the patterns of zeros are diagonally-aligned squares,
or an infinite increasing union of such.

•

Over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
the frame relations have a particularly simple form:

–

The ex relation is

E=AD+BC\,.

–

The inner frame relation is

(31)

AD=BC=0\,,\quad\textrm{ or }\quad A=B=C=D=1\,.

–

The outer frame relation is

(32)

E+F=G+H\,.

3.
The tiling and its structure

In this section the tilings
\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})
and
\kappa_{1}\left(\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right)
are analysed. This analysis is based on the symmetries in the definition of
\sigma
and
\kappa
and the extension of the
G
-action to large square patterns over
\Sigma
. The outcome of the analysis is a proof of Theorem
1.3
.

3.1.
The structure of
\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})

The following lemma and corollary explore further the
G
-action on large patterns.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
l\geq 1
and let

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{2^{l+1}}(\Sigma)
. Then,

(33)

\displaystyle\rho\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\rho C&\iota\rho A\\
\iota\rho D&\iota\rho B\end{matrix}\right),

(34)

\displaystyle\eta\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota\eta D&\iota\eta B\\
\iota\eta C&\iota\eta A\end{matrix}\right),

(35)

\displaystyle\iota\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota A&\iota B\\
\iota C&\iota D\end{matrix}\right).

Proof.

Start with (
35
).
Recall Definition
1.6
, that
T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\iota}
acts trivially and that
\psi^{k}(\iota)=\iota
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\iota\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\iota}\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\iota}A&T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\iota}B\\
T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\iota}C&T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\iota}D\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\iota A&\iota B\\
\iota C&\iota D\end{matrix}\right)

For (
33
), note that on the geometric level

T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\rho}\left(\begin{matrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\rho}C&T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\rho}A\\
T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\rho}D&T^{\operatorname{geo}}_{\rho}B\end{matrix}\right).

Also, since any

4.
Escape of mass of the Thue-Morse sequence

In this section we compute the escape of mass of
t^{j}\alpha
where
\alpha
is the Thue-Morse sequence thought of as a series in
\mathbb{F}_{2}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)
. Escape of mass is expressed in Definition
1.1
in terms of degrees of partial quotients of Laurent series. As we explain shortly, these degrees are tightly related to the length of consecutive zeros in number walls. However, in order to use the self-similarity of the number wall
\mathfrak{F}(\alpha)
, it is convenient to extend the definition of escape of mass to number walls in general.

4.1.
Motivational picture

We start by explaining an intuitive picture that motivates our discussion and terminology. Imagine a space
X
partitioned into a sequence of subsets
X=\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{\infty}X_{i}
. The function
\mathbf{h}:X\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
defined by
\mathbf{h}(x)=i\iff x\in X_{i}
will be thought of as a height function, and so we think of
X
as a tower with
X_{i}
being the
i
th
floor. Imagine that there is a map
T:X\to X
having the following two properties:

(1)

For all
x\in X
,
\mathbf{h}(T(x))\in\left\{\mathbf{h}(x)+1,\mathbf{h}(x)-1\right\}\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. That is, the height must change under the action of
T
and cannot change by more than one.

(2)

If
\mathbf{h}(x)=i>0
, and
\mathbf{h}(T(x))=i-1
, then for any
0\leq j\leq i
,
\mathbf{h}(T^{j}(x))=i-j
. That is, once an orbit starts going down, it will go down until it reaches floor
0
.

Fix
x\in X_{0}
and suppose we are given a sequence
P=\left\{P_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the property that

P_{i}\neq 0\iff T^{i}(x)\in X_{0}
. Then, we can recover the height of
T^{i}(x)
by studying the
0
’s in the sequence
P
: each time we see in the sequence
P
a pattern of the form
P_{l},0,\dots,0,P_{l+r}
, with
P_{l}\cdot P_{l+r}\neq 0
, then it follows that
r
is even and the height of
T^{l+i}(x)
satisfies

(49)

\mathbf{h}\left(T^{l+i}(x)\right)=\begin{cases}i&\mbox{ for }0\leq i\leq r/2\\
r/2-(i-r/2)&\mbox{ for }r/2+1\leq i\leq r.\end{cases}

This is exactly what happens behind the scenes when one restricts attention to a sequence that appears as a column (or a row) of a diagonally-aligned number wall.

4.2.
Escape of mass for sequences

In this section we develop the terminology which is necessary for the consideration of escape of mass in the context of general sequences over a finite field.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
P:[m_{1},m_{2}]\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
be any sequence over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
which is not identically zero. The
height of
P
is a function
h_{P}:[m_{1},m_{2}]\to\mathbb{N}
defined by

h_{P}(i):=\min_{P(i^{\prime})\neq 0}|i^{\prime}-i|\,.

The
escape of mass of
P
is a function
E_{P}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by

E_{P}(d):=\frac{\#\left\{m_{1}\leq i<m_{2}\,:\,\max\left\{h_{P}(i),h_{P}(i+1)\right\}>d\right\}}{m_{2}-m_{1}}\,.

We refer to
E_{P}(d)
as
the mass of
P
that lies above height
d
.

A basic tool that will be used several times later in this section is the ability to compute the escape of mass of a finite sequence by considering a partition of it into subsequences. In the statement of the next lemma we will use the square bracket notation that was introduced in Convention
3.12
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
P^{\prime}:[0,m^{\prime}]\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
P^{\prime\prime}:[0,m^{\prime}]\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
be sequences over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
P^{\prime}(m^{\prime})=P^{\prime\prime}(0)
, and set

P=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}P^{\prime}\\
P^{\prime\prime}\end{array}\right].

Assume that
P^{\prime}
ends with precisely
0\leq d^{\prime}<m^{\prime}
consecutive
0
’s while
P^{\prime\prime}
starts with precisely
0\leq d^{\prime\prime}<m^{\prime\prime}
consecutive
0
’s.
Then for every
d
which satisfies

(50)

\max\{d^{\prime},d^{\prime\prime}\}\leq d\,,

the mass of
P
which lies above
d
satisfies

E_{P}(d)=\frac{m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}E_{P^{\prime}}(d)+\frac{m^{\prime\prime}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}E_{P}(d)\,.

Proof.

For every
d
as in (
50
), the height functions satisfy

(51)

\displaystyle h_{P^{\prime}}(i)

\displaystyle>d\;\textrm{if and only if}\;h_{P}(i)>d\,,\;\textrm{for every}\;0\leq i<m^{\prime}\,,\;\textrm{and}

\displaystyle h_{P^{\prime\prime}}(i)

\displaystyle>d\;\textrm{if and only if}\;h_{P}(m^{\prime}+i)>d\,,\;\textrm{for every}\;0\leq i<m^{\prime\prime}\,.

The first line in (
51
) will be proved and the second line follows by symmetry. Since
P^{\prime}
is a subsequence of
P
, it satisfies
h_{P}(i)\leq h_{P^{\prime}}(i)
. Therefore, it is enough to show that if
h_{P^{\prime}}(i)>d
, then
h_{P}(i)>d
. By assumption,
P(m^{\prime}-d^{\prime})\neq 0
, so
h_{P}(i)=h_{P^{\prime}}(i)
for every
0\leq i\leq m^{\prime}-d^{\prime}
, and it is enough to consider
i>m^{\prime}-d^{\prime}
. Moreover, for any
m^{\prime}-d^{\prime}<i<m^{\prime}
, the height function satisfies

h_{P^{\prime}}(i)=i+1-(m^{\prime}-d^{\prime})\leq d^{\prime}\,.

Since
d^{\prime}\leq d
by (
50
), this finishes the argument for (
51
). Using (
51
) gives

\displaystyle E_{P}(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{\#\left\{0\leq i<m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}\,:\,\max\left\{h_{P}(i),h_{P}(i+1)\right\}>d\right\}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}\frac{\#\left\{0\leq i<m^{\prime}\,:\,\max\left\{h_{P^{\prime}}(i),h_{P^{\prime}}(i+1)\right\}>d\right\}}{m^{\prime}}+

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\frac{m^{\prime\prime}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}\frac{\#\left\{0\leq i<m^{\prime\prime}\,:\,\max\left\{h_{P^{\prime\prime}}(i),h_{P^{\prime\prime}}(i+1)\right\}>d\right\}}{m^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}E_{P^{\prime}}(d)+\frac{m^{\prime\prime}}{m^{\prime}+m^{\prime\prime}}E_{P^{\prime\prime}}(d)\,.

∎

Finally, we will also need the computation of the escape of mass for a basic building block which is composed out of a stretch of
0
’s between nonzero elements.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
r>0
let
P:[0,r]\to\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a sequence which satisfies
P(i)=0
if and only if
0<i<r
.
Then for every
d
, the escape of mass of
P
satisfies

(52)

E_{P}(d)=\frac{2\max\{r/2-d,0\}}{r}\,.

Proof.

As in (
49
), the height function of
P_{r}
satisfies

h_{P}(i)=\begin{cases}i&\mbox{ for }0\leq i\leq r/2\\
r-i&\mbox{ for }r/2<i\leq r.\end{cases}

Therefore, for every
d
,

\displaystyle E_{P}(d)

\displaystyle=\frac{\#\left\{0\leq i<r\,:\,h_{P}(i)>d\right\}}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\#\left\{0\leq i\leq r/2\,:\,h_{P}(i)>d\right\}}{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\max\{r/2-d,0\}}{r}\,.

as claimed.
∎

4.3.
Partial escape of mass

We will show now that for
\theta=\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
thought of also as the Laurent series
\theta=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}t^{-n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\left(\left(t^{-1}\right)\right)

. Recall that (

For any

The standard facts from Diophantine approximation of Laurent series quoted in (

\mathfrak{F}_{2i,1}(\theta)\neq 0\quad\iff\quad i=i_{k}\text{ for some }k\geq 0\,,

\displaystyle\#\left\{0\leq i<2i_{k}\,:\,\max\left\{h(i),h(i+1)\right\}>d\right\}

Therefore, the mass of

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\max\left\{d_{k^{\prime}}\left(\theta\right)-d,0\right\}}{2i_{k}}=E_{\left.\mathfrak{F}(\theta)\right|_{\left[0,2i_{k}\right]\times\{1\}}}(d)\,.

∎

Note that for every

in terms of Hankel or Toeplitz determinants. Therefore, Proposition

implies that for every

The rest of this section deals with the number wall of the Thue-Morse sequence
\mathfrak{F}=\mathfrak{F}(\alpha)
, which as we proved in Section

th

\kappa_{1}\left(\sigma^{\infty}(\mathbf{a}_{0})\right)

\sigma^{l-l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}\right)=\iota\sigma^{l-l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)=T^{\operatorname{ent}}_{\iota}\left(\sigma^{l-l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}}\right)\right),

\sigma^{l-l^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}\right)(m,n)\in\{\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{a}},\mathbf{d}_{\mathbf{b}}\}
, which finishes the proof.
∎
