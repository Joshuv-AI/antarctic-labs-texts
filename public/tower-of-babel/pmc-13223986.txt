Electroacupuncture for knee osteoarthritis: a multicentre randomised controlled trial assessing symptomatic and structural efficacy
Authors: Minghui Hang, Yubo Shao, Wang Lu, Xiaoyun Wang, Hao Xu, Shaohua Chen, Xiang Yu, Junjun Zhu, Xianggeng Luo, Honggang Yi, Jiabao Zhang, Yimeng Wang, Shiyong Xue, Shunchao Liu, Zheng Gong, Zhipeng Song, Lianbo Xiao, Zheng Xiang, Shiyan Yan, Ting Zhang, Ruoyu Yao, Tao Wang, Zhanying Tang, Lin Cong, Qiang Li, Haiyin Zhao, Weiwei Meng, Yibin Fan, Rongguang Ao, Bingli Liu, Liyuan Tao, Xiaohua Gu, Li Ding, Tao Wu, Yong Xu, Yongjun Wang, Qianqian Liang

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundWhile electroacupuncture (EA) alleviates pain in knee osteoarthritis (KOA), high-quality evidence from large trials is limited, and its potential to influence structural progression is unknown. We investigated whether EA confers structural benefits beyond symptom relief.MethodsIn this multicentre, randomised, parallel-group, sham-acupuncture (SA)-controlled trial at six Shanghai hospitals, eligible participants were assigned (1:1) to EA or SA, administered three times/week for six weeks, with 30-week follow-up. Patient enrolment occurred from November 30, 2021, to April 26, 2024, and the final follow-up was completed on November 22, 2024. The primary outcome was the change in the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) global score (0–240) at week 6. The minimal clinically important improvement (MCII) was predefined as a ≥12% relative improvement from baseline in the WOMAC global score. Key secondary outcomes included the WOMAC pain, stiffness, and function subscales, the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), the Lequesne index, the Lysholm score, and the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT). Exploratory outcomes included artificial intelligence (AI)-based knee magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measures of cartilage morphology and synovial inflammation. This trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000036292).FindingsOf 686 assessed, 480 participants were randomised (EA: n = 240; SA: n = 240). At week 6, the EA group showed a significantly greater reduction in WOMAC global score than the SA group (adjusted mean change 65.35 vs 24.76, mean difference [MD]: 40.56, 95% CI 36.35–44.77; P < 0.001; Cohen's d = 1.21), exceeding the prespecified MCII threshold. Consistent benefits were observed across all key secondary symptomatic outcomes, including WOMAC pain (MD: 11.98, P < 0.001), WOMAC stiffness (MD: 3.12, P < 0.001), WOMAC function (MD: 25.47, P < 0.001), VAS (MD: 2.04, P < 0.001), Lysholm score (MD: −7.36, P < 0.001), Lequesne index (MD: 1.23, P < 0.001), and 6MWT (MD: −41.95, P < 0.001). In exploratory MRI analyses at week 6, EA was associated with signals of reduced synovial inflammation and less cartilage loss in several compartments, with effect sizes ranging from very small to moderate (Cohen's d range −0.03 to 0.64). No serious adverse events occurred.InterpretationIn patients with KOA, a 6-week course of EA provided substantial and durable symptomatic relief, representing a clinically meaningful therapeutic benefit. Exploratory MRI findings suggest that EA might have anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effects, but these structural signals require confirmation in trials specifically designed and powered for imaging endpoints.FundingThis work was supported by the National Natural Science Foundation of China (Key Program, Grant No. 82530121; General Program, Grant No. 82474534), the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission (Health Youth Talent Project, Grant No. 2022YQ025), the Science and Technology Commission of Shanghai Municipality (Medical Innovation Research Special Project, Grant Nos. 25Y12800800 and 23Y31920100), the Shanghai Oriental Talents Program (Leading Talent Project), the Traditional Chinese Medicine Innovation Team and Talent Support Program-National Traditional Chinese Medicine Multidisciplinary Cross-Innovation Team Project (ZYYCXTD-D-202401), and the Shanghai Triennial Initiative for Promoting Clinical Proficiency and Innovation Capabilities in Municipal Hospitals (Grant No. SHDC2020CR1010A).Keywords: Acupuncture, Knee osteoarthritis, Articular cartilage, Magnetic resonance imaging, Artificial intelligence, Randomised controlled trial

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a chronic joint disease characterized by articular cartilage degeneration and synovial inflammation, significantly impairing patients' quality of life. To date, no approved disease-modifying drugs are available, and treatment primarily focuses on symptom relief. Electroacupuncture (EA), which combines traditional needle insertion with low-intensity electrical stimulation, has been shown in multiple studies to effectively alleviate KOA-related pain and improve joint function. We searched PubMed from January 1, 2000, to September 1, 2025, for English-language articles using the terms: (“electroacupuncture”) AND (“knee osteoarthritis” OR “knee OA”) AND (“randomized controlled trial”). The search yielded 37 results, of which 22 were randomized controlled trials. However, the overall quality of evidence remains variable, and larger, more rigorous trials are needed to confirm its efficacy. Whether EA has the potential to concurrently influence structural progression or local inflammation remains unclear.

Added value of this study

This multicentre, randomised, sham-controlled trial further confirms the symptomatic benefits of EA in KOA within a large cohort of 480 participants. At the primary endpoint (week 6), EA significantly improved pain and function compared to sham acupuncture, with a large effect size (Cohen's d= 1.21) for the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) global score, and durable benefits persisting through 30 weeks. Key secondary symptomatic outcomes (WOMAC subscales, Visual Analogue Scale, Lysholm score, Lequesne index, 6-Minute Walk Test) consistently favoured EA. In addition, this study provides exploratory, hypothesis-generating evidence from artificial intelligence (AI)-based quantitative knee magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggesting that EA may be associated with reduced synovial inflammation and less cartilage loss in several compartments.

Implications of all the available evidence

The findings of this study expand the evidence base for EA in the treatment of KOA by further confirming its durable symptomatic benefits. The exploratory MRI signals generate new hypotheses about potential structural and inflammatory effects, but these require confirmation in long-term randomised controlled trials powered for structural outcomes. Future studies should prioritise the integration of imaging biomarkers and patient-reported outcomes to comprehensively assess the multifaceted efficacy of therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a systemic joint disease characterized by structural changes in the articular cartilage, subchondral bone, ligaments, joint capsule, synovium, and surrounding musculature.1 Pain is the primary symptom and serves as a major driver of clinical decision-making and healthcare utilization.2 Its complex pathogenesis involves mechanical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors, ultimately leading to structural damage and failure of the synovial joint.3 There are currently no approved medications available that can alter the natural progression of KOA; the primary treatment goals are to alleviate pain and prevent structural deterioration.4,5

Electroacupuncture (EA), a modality that combines traditional needle insertion with low-intensity electrical stimulation, is thought to alleviate pain by modulating peripheral and central pain pathways, as well as by influencing local blood flow and inflammatory mediators.6,7 Randomized trials have suggested its effectiveness in reducing pain and improving function in KOA8, 9, 10; however, the overall quality of evidence remains variable, and larger, more rigorous trials are needed to confirm its efficacy.11 Additionally, whether EA can concurrently influence other important aspects of KOA, such as articular cartilage degradation,12 and inflammation of the infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP) and synovium,13 remains unclear.

Loss of articular cartilage is a recognized indicator of the structural progression of KOA, and currently, there are no approved therapeutic methods that have been proven to slow down disease progression.14 Animal studies have demonstrated protective effects of EA on articular cartilage in KOA models.15, 16, 17 A meta-analysis encompassing 35 studies involving 247 animals suggests that acupuncture may delay cartilage degeneration by mitigating the degradation of the extracellular matrix in chondrocytes.18 However, it remains unclear whether these potential structural benefits are applicable to humans. The IPFP and synovium, considered as a structural complex, are recognized as a major source of inflammation in KOA.19 However, whether EA can modulate inflammation within the IPFP and synovium remains unknown. Currently, artificial intelligence (AI)-based automatic segmentation and quantitative measurement of knee magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have demonstrated significant value in assessing joint cartilage and the IPFP20; however, these techniques have not been utilized to evaluate the potential structural effects of EA interventions.

Therefore, this large-scale, multicentre, randomised, sham-acupuncture (SA)-controlled trial aims to further confirm the efficacy of EA in relieving pain and improving function in KOA, and to generate exploratory evidence, using AI-based knee MRI, on whether EA may be associated with changes in cartilage and synovial inflammation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a multicentre, randomized, parallel-group, SA-controlled trial conducted across six clinical research centres in Shanghai, China, and it was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000036292; registered on 22 August 2020; available from: https://www.chictr.org.cn/hvshowproject.html?id=284774&v=1.6). The protocol and statistical analysis plan were available in the Supplementary Materials. This study was reviewed and approved by the medical ethics committees of all participating clinical research centres before the enrolment of participants, and was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was reported in compliance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 guidelines for randomized controlled trials (RCT).21 All patients provided written informed consent before study participation.

Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: age 50–75 years; diagnosis of knee osteoarthritis according to the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) 2019 guidelines with radiological confirmation (Kellgren–Lawrence grade II-III)22,23; a Lysholm swelling score indicating any degree of knee joint swelling; knee ultrasound showing a depth of the anechoic zone of the IPFP >3 mm; no acupuncture treatment in the past three months; at least six months of knee pain with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score >4 and ≤ 7 during the seven days before screening; and ability to understand and sign informed consent. Participants were excluded if they had any of the following: severe cardiac, cerebral, hepatic, renal or pulmonary dysfunction; indications for knee surgery or previous knee joint surgery; receipt of treatments for KOA that could affect outcome measures (including oral analgesics, intramuscular injections, joint injections, or soft-tissue corticosteroid injections); psychiatric disorders; coagulopathy, lower limb infections, lower limb vascular malformations or vascular dysfunction; history of opioid, sedative-hypnotic or alcohol abuse; indications for pelvic surgery or previous pelvic surgery; pregnancy or breastfeeding; or participation in another clinical trial within one month before enrolment.

Participant recruitment involved collaboration with general practitioners, rheumatologists, and orthopedic surgeons, as well as advertisements through local media channels and social media. All treatments and assessments were provided free of charge, and all participants received a transportation allowance upon study completion.

Randomisation and masking

This trial employed stratified block randomization. Stratification was based on study site, with a fixed block size of 4. Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the EA group or the SA group. The planned sample size was 480 participants (240 per group). Random allocation sequences were generated by professional statisticians using SAS software (version 9.4). Randomization was implemented via a centralized, online interactive web-response system (IWRS). Upon confirmation of eligibility and in the order of enrollment sequence, the site investigator submitted an enrollment request through the IWRS. The system then automatically assigned a unique random identifier and immediately allocated the participant to either the EA or SA group. Participants received the therapeutic intervention corresponding to their system-assigned group allocation.

Participants, outcome assessors, and statisticians were unaware of group assignments. Acupuncturists were not blinded, allowing them to fulfil their responsibilities in administering acupuncture treatment; however, they did not participate in efficacy evaluations or data analysis. To ensure the integrity of this design, both tiers of blinding were individually sealed and stored at the coordinating centre and the statistical unit, ensuring that no one could access group information prior to data locking and analysis.

Procedures

All treatments across the six participating hospitals in Shanghai were administered by registered acupuncturists with at least three years of clinical experience, who underwent standardized training and competency assessment prior to trial initiation (detailed information on trial sites and provider training is provided in the Supplementary Materials). The disposable sterile needles, control blunt-tip sham needles, and EA devices used in this trial were all manufactured by Changzhou Dayi Medical Equipment Co., Ltd. The disposable sterile needles selected were of the manufacturer's specification of 0.25 mm × 40 mm, while the custom blunt-tip needles were designed as disposable 0.25 mm × 25 mm. The EA devices used were of the Huatuo brand, model V. During the procedures, both groups utilized custom sterile circular sponge pads for needle fixation. The sponge pads featured a two-layer structure, with the bottom layer consisting of a sponge affixed with 3 M adhesive for securing the pad to the skin's surface, and the upper layer being a circular white sponge designed to stabilize the inserted needles and prevent them from falling out.

In the EA group, disposable sterile needles were used in conjunction with the EA device. Acupoint selection was based on clinical practice and expert consensus24: Xuehai (SP10); Liangqiu (ST34); Dubi (ST35); Neixiyan (EX-LE4); Zusanli (ST36); Yanglingquan (GB34); Sanyinjiao (SP6); Xuanzhong (GB39). After inserting the needles to a depth of 13–30 mm, manual manipulation (lifting, thrusting, and rotating) was performed 3–5 times to elicit de qi-a composite of sensations including soreness, numbness, distention, and heaviness, which is considered indicative of effective needling in traditional Chinese medicine. Following the achievement of de qi, the EA device was activated, applying a continuous wave at 2 Hz for 5 min, followed by a sparse-dense wave at 2 Hz for 20 min.

The SA group utilized specially designed blunt-tipped needles to simulate acupuncture in conjunction with the EA device, selecting the same acupuncture points as the EA group. The simulated acupuncture involved the use of non-sharp, flat-headed needles, which were applied to pads placed over the acupuncture points with slight pressure to elicit a sensation of needling without penetrating the skin. Manual manipulation was simulated by gently tapping and rotating the needle handle 3–5 times to mimic the sensation of de qi, although no actual needle insertion or tissue penetration occurred. Following this simulated manipulation, the EA device was activated with the same parameters as the EA group (continuous wave at 2 Hz for 5 min, followed by a sparse-dense wave at 2 Hz for 20 min).

Both groups received treatment three times per week for a total duration of six weeks. Participants were instructed to avoid any other treatments for knee pain during the trial. Celecoxib was permitted as rescue medication for intolerable pain, and its use was documented at each follow-up visit. Detailed information on the treatment protocol and concomitant care is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

MRI acquisition and AI analysis

MRI was performed at baseline and week 6 using scanners with field strength ≥1.5 T. The protocol included sagittal T1-weighted and fat-suppressed axial, coronal and sagittal sequences. Participants lay supine with the target knee stabilised in a dedicated coil. After acquisition, images were reconstructed into multiplanar views and reviewed by musculoskeletal radiologists.

Structural lesions were graded with a modified MRI Osteoarthritis Knee Score (MOAKS). Cartilage damage (15 subregions), bone-marrow lesions (15 subregions), osteophytes (12 locations), meniscal damage and joint effusion/synovitis were each scored on 0–3 scales. Baseline and week-6 scans were reviewed in pairs by three blinded radiologists; a fourth assessed 20% of scans. Inter-rater agreement was substantial to almost perfect (>0.61–1.00).

Deep-learning segmentation was performed using a hybrid Swin-Transformer and Res2Net architecture (STRR-Net).25 The model was trained on sagittal T2-weighted MR images annotated for nine knee structures. DICOM images were converted to 224 × 224 arrays, normalised, and split into training (80%), validation (10%) and test (10%) sets. Data augmentation (random flipping, scaling, cropping, RandAugment) yielded 5595 images. STRR-Net achieved an average Dice score of 81.7% across all tissues, outperforming U-Net and DeepLabV3+. Dice scores exceeded 93% for bone, 86% for the IPFP, and 64–69% for cartilage. Segmented images were used to derive quantitative biomarkers: cartilage thickness/volume, effusion area/volume, and fat pad signal/texture features normalised to subcutaneous fat. Comprehensive details on imaging, scoring, model implementation and biomarker derivation are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Assessments and outcomes

In accordance with the recommendations from the Rheumatology-Osteoarthritis Research Society International for clinical trials on outcome measures, we assessed outcome measures for pain, physical function, and global assessment. The primary outcome was the between-group difference (EA vs SA) in change from baseline in the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) global score (0–240 points) at Week 6 (i.e., the Week-6 treatment contrast). To aid in clinical interpretation, the minimal clinically important improvement (MCII) was defined a priori as a 12% improvement from baseline in the WOMAC global score.26,27 Key secondary outcomes reported in this paper included the WOMAC pain, stiffness, and function subscales, the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) (0–10 cm), the Lequesne index (0–24), the Lysholm score (0–100), and the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT). Exploratory outcomes included AI-based knee MRI measures (assessing cartilage morphology, IPFP signal intensity, and effusion-synovitis depth).25 These assessments were conducted at baseline and weeks 3, 6, 14, 22, and 30 (except for MRI, which was performed at baseline and week 6 only).

In addition to the outcomes reported above, the trial registry pre-specified a broader set of secondary outcomes aimed at providing comprehensive mechanistic and functional insights. These included gait analysis, isokinetic muscle strength testing, knee and inguinal region ultrasonography, multi-omics profiling (including peripheral blood metabolomics and proteomics), the SF-36 health survey, plain radiography and computed tomography (CT) of the knee, bone mineral density testing, and analysis of inflammatory cytokines in synovial fluid. Near-infrared indocyanine green (NIR-ICG) imaging was also initially planned but was not implemented. However, as detailed in the ‘Note on Additional Pre-specified Outcomes' section of the Results, operational challenges led to incomplete data collection for these measures (except for NIR-ICG, which was not performed), and thus they were not analyzed in this primary report.

Blinded assessments were conducted at week 6, during which all patients were asked to guess which treatment they received. All adverse events (AEs) were defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a participant, regardless of causality. AEs were collected via spontaneous reports and open-ended questioning at each visit. Severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe, and causality was assessed by the treating acupuncturist as related or unrelated to treatment.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was conducted for a statistical superiority test using a two-sample t-test framework at the prespecified primary endpoint (week 6). With a one-tailed significance level (α = 0.025) and 90% power (β = 0.1), we assumed an intergroup difference of 9 in the mean week 6 WOMAC global score and a common standard deviation of 25 for both groups at week 6 (based on preliminary RCT data). This calculation indicated that 328 participants (164 per group) were the minimum analyzable sample size. To ensure: a) robust power maintenance under the anticipated 20% dropout rate, b) operational resilience across the 6 competitive-enrollment sites, and c) conservative protection against parameter instability, we enrolled 480 participants (240 per group). The t-test-based approach was used because information on the within-subject correlation/variance-covariance structure required for repeated-measures planning was limited; the primary treatment effect was subsequently estimated using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) as the between-group difference in change at week 6. All secondary outcomes, including WOMAC subscales and MRI-derived measures, were not powered and are considered exploratory.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.4.3) software. All analyses were conducted based on the intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, including all randomized participants. For WOMAC global score, the linear MMRM was used, which included treatments, time (repeating factor), and their interaction as fixed effects, adjusted for the baseline values, clinical centre (fixed effect), sex, age, body mass index (BMI), and disease duration. MMRM analyses used repeated measures nested within participants (participant as the clustering unit), with visit treated as a categorical factor (baseline and weeks 3, 6, 14, 22, and 30), thereby allowing for non-linear trajectories over follow-up via the treatment-by-visit interaction, and within-participant correlation across visits modeled using an unstructured variance-covariance matrix. Time was specified as a visit factor rather than a continuous variable to avoid imposing a particular functional form and to accommodate the uneven spacing of assessments across follow-up. The prespecified primary treatment effect at Week 6 was estimated as the treatment-by-visit contrast at Week 6 in change from baseline.

Secondary patient-reported outcomes (WOMAC subscales, VAS, Lequesne, and Lysholm score) and the supportive objective functional outcome (6MWT) were analyzed longitudinally using the same MMRM framework, incorporating all scheduled visits (baseline and weeks 3, 6, 14, 22, and 30), with time treated as a categorical factor. MRI outcomes were assessed at baseline and week 6 only. Accordingly, MRI endpoints were analyzed as exploratory outcomes by comparing the between-group difference (EA vs SA) in change from baseline to week 6, adjusted for the baseline MRI value and the prespecified covariates, and reported as adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals. To address multiplicity arising from post hoc pairwise contrasts across multiple visits and outcomes, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc contrasts were applied for between-group comparisons at each visit (and corresponding within-group visit contrasts where reported). Outcome analyses were reported as adjusted least squares means and 95% confidence intervals, including between-group mean differences at Week 6 for the primary endpoint (with other time points presented descriptively/secondarily).

To complement adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals, we also reported standardized effect sizes using Cohen's d at week 6. Cohen's d was calculated as the between-group difference in mean change from baseline to week 6, standardized by the pooled baseline (pretest) standard deviation. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional thresholds (approximately 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, and 0.8 large), with the sign indicating the direction of benefit. To facilitate clinical interpretation of the primary outcome, we prespecified a MCII for WOMAC global score, defined as a relative improvement of ≥12% from baseline at the 6-week assessment.26,27 We additionally performed a responder analysis comparing the proportion of participants achieving this MCII threshold between groups.

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) data are presented as continuous plots illustrating numerical changes from baseline in WOMAC scores and other secondary outcomes on the horizontal axis, with the vertical axis representing the cumulative percentage of patients attaining at least that level of improvement. Separate curves were plotted to represent each group for each study visit. Primary analyses were conducted under the ITT principle using likelihood-based MMRM, which incorporates all available repeated outcome measurements and does not require explicit outcome imputation under a missing-at-random assumption. To assess the robustness of the primary findings, multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, unadjusted MMRM was applied without covariates to evaluate baseline treatment effects. Second, pattern mixture models (PMM) with SA-based pattern imputation were employed to address potential missing data biases, assuming missing outcomes followed patterns observed in the SA group. Third, Rubin's multiple imputation (m= 5) was performed under the missing-at-random assumption as an additional sensitivity analysis to handle incomplete data. These analyses aimed to verify the consistency of results across methodological approaches and mitigate potential confounding from missing data or model assumptions.

Post-hoc subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the treatment effects across different patient populations. Predefined subgroups included sex, age, BMI, and duration of disease. Forest plots were generated to visually assess whether the treatment effects of EA compared to SA were consistent within each subgroup. The presence of statistically significant interactions between treatment assignment and subgroup categories was also assessed to determine the robustness and generalizability of the observed effects. The James blinding Index (range 0–1) was utilized to assess blinding (0 = no blinding; 0.5 = completely random blinding; 1 = full blinding). Comparisons of binary outcomes were performed using chi-square tests. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P< 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients

Patient enrolment occurred from November 30, 2021, to April 26, 2024, during which a total of 686 patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for KOA. After excluding 206 patients, 480 eligible patients were enrolled in this study, among which 240 participants were randomly assigned to the EA group and 240 to the SA group. The final follow-up visit was completed on November 22, 2024. Safety monitoring for AEs was conducted throughout the treatment and follow-up periods, from enrolment until the last participant's final visit. Data for the primary outcome were available for all randomized participants. Regarding the secondary outcomes, three participants in the SA group were lost to follow-up after treatment, yielding a completion rate of 99.4% (477/480) at the 30-week assessment (Fig. 1; Tables S1 and S2). All participants completed the scheduled treatments as intended, delivered by trained acupuncturists, with no protocol deviations. No participant used the permitted rescue medication (celecoxib) during the trial. The mean age of the patients was 57 years, 76.25% were women, and the BMI of the entire cohort was 23.96 kg/m2. Baseline characteristics and clinical characteristics were generally comparable between groups (Table 1). Small numerical differences were observed for BMI, VAS, Lequesne index, and 6MWT; however, these differences were modest in magnitude and were not considered clinically meaningful.

Primary outcome

The EA group showed clinically meaningful improvements in the WOMAC global score compared with the SA group at the prespecified 6-week primary endpoint. The adjusted mean change from baseline in WOMAC global score was 65.35 points (95% CI, 61.00–69.71) for the EA group vs 24.76 points (95% CI, 20.41–29.12) for the SA group, yielding an adjusted between-group mean difference of 40.56 points (95% CI, 36.35–44.77; P < 0.001). These week-6 estimates represent the prespecified Week-6 treatment contrast derived from the MMRM and are reported in Table 2. Intergroup differences in WOMAC global score were evident as early as week 3 and persisted through the 30-week follow-up period, as shown by the longitudinal trajectories in Fig. 2. Moreover, the week-6 primary effect estimate from the primary MMRM was consistent in three prespecified sensitivity analyses addressing model specification and missing-data assumptions: (1) unadjusted MMRM; (2) a SA-based pattern mixture model under non-random missingness assumptions; and (3) Rubin's multiple imputation (m = 5) with missing-at-random assumptions (Tables S3–S7).

The clinical relevance of the primary finding was further supported by the responder analysis based on the prespecified MCII ≥12% relative improvement from baseline. At week 6, 223 of 240 participants (92.9%) in the EA group achieved MCII compared with 163 of 240 (67.9%) in the SA group, corresponding to an absolute risk difference of 25.0 percentage points (95% CI 18.3–31.7). In the covariate-adjusted analysis, EA was associated with a significantly greater likelihood of achieving MCII (adjusted RR 1.369, 95% CI 1.247–1.502; P < 0.001; Table S8), supporting the clinical relevance of EA beyond statistical evidence based on mean change alone.

Secondary outcomes

Key secondary

The key secondary patient-reported outcomes were consistent with the primary analysis and generally favoured EA over SA at the 6-week endpoint. Across the WOMAC subscales, the EA group showed greater improvements in pain, stiffness, and physical function than the SA group (Table 2; Fig. 2).

For WOMAC pain, the EA group achieved an adjusted mean reduction of 16.87 points (95% CI, 15.81–17.93) compared with 4.93 points (95% CI, 3.87–5.99) in the SA group, yielding a between-group mean difference of 11.98 points (95% CI, 10.95–13.02; P < 0.001) with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 1.73). For WOMAC stiffness, the adjusted mean reduction was 6.12 points (95% CI, 5.81–6.42) in the EA group and 3.00 points (95% CI, 2.69–3.30) in the SA group, yielding a mean difference of 3.12 points (95% CI, 2.82–3.41; P < 0.001; d = 1.10). For WOMAC physical function, the adjusted mean reduction was 42.37 points (95% CI, 38.57–46.17) in the EA group and 16.84 points (95% CI, 13.04–20.64) in the SA group, resulting in a mean difference of 25.47 points (95% CI, 21.79–29.14; P < 0.001; d = 1.07). Between-group differences in WOMAC pain and function were evident from week 3 onward (P < 0.01), whereas the difference in stiffness became apparent at week 6 (P < 0.05). All effects persisted through the final assessment (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Other major symptomatic outcomes showed a similar pattern. EA showed superior reductions in VAS pain score than SA, with a mean difference (MD) of 2.04 points (95% CI: 1.85–2.23; P < 0.0001; Cohen's d = 1.98). Improvements were also observed in the Lequesne index (MD: 1.23, 95% CI: 0.61–1.85; P= 0.0001; d = 0.42), and Lysholm scores (MD: −7.36, 95% CI: −8.18 to −6.54; P < 0.0001; d = −0.91), again favouring EA (Table 2). For the 6MWT, a supportive objective functional measure, the EA group also showed greater improvement than the SA group, with a between-group mean difference was −41.95 m (95% CI, −56.11 to −27.80; P < 0.0001), although the corresponding effect size was modest (d = −0.23) (Table 2).

Exploratory imaging outcomes

In exploratory analyses of MRI outcomes at week 6, the EA group exhibited a potential protective effect against articular cartilage degradation compared to the SA group (Table 2). Statistically significant MD were observed in several cartilage parameters: medial femoral cartilage thickness (−0.07 mm; 95% CI: −0.12 to −0.01; P = 0.013; Cohen's d = −0.03), lateral femoral cartilage volume (−13.84 mm3; 95% CI: −19.42 to −8.26; P < 0.0001; d = −0.07), medial tibial cartilage volume (−13.93 mm3; 95% CI: −19.85 to −8.01; P < 0.0001; d = −0.05), and lateral tibial cartilage thickness (−0.08 mm; 95% CI: −0.11 to −0.05; P < 0.0001; d = −0.06). However, standardized effect sizes for these cartilage morphology measures were small (Cohen's d ranging from −0.03 to −0.07), and given the short 6-week imaging window, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. No significant between-group differences were observed for the remaining cartilage parameters.

Additionally, the EA group demonstrated significant reductions in joint inflammation markers. There was a decrease in IPFP signal intensity (MD: 0.14; 95% CI: 0.11–0.16; P < 0.0001; d = 0.64) and effusion-synovitis depth (MD: 0.97 mm; 95% CI: 0.60–1.33; P < 0.0001; d = 0.40) compared to the SA group, suggesting an anti-inflammatory effect of EA in addition to its potential protective role in cartilage preservation (Table 2).

For WOMAC global score and its subscales, the CDF curves generally favoured EA over SA across the range of response levels (Fig. 3). Similar patterns were observed for VAS, Lysholm, Lequesne, and 6MWT outcomes (Figure S3). Exploratory CDF analyses of MRI-derived measures are presented in Figure S4.

Post-hoc subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, BMI, and disease duration were conducted as exploratory analyses to assess the consistency of treatment effects. Across subgroups, treatment effect estimates generally favoured EA over SA for the primary and major secondary outcomes. No significant subgroup-by-treatment interactions were observed (Figures S5 and S6). Given multiplicity, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating only.

Blinded assessments

Blinding assessments suggested no clear evidence of meaningful unblinding between groups. The Cohen's weighted Kappa coefficient between the two groups was 0.19 (95% CI: 0.13–0.25; P < 0.001) (Table S9). The James' blinding index was 0.53, and the upper bound of its 95% CI was 0.57 (>0.5), suggesting there was insufficient evidence to show unblinding between the two groups.

Adverse events

AEs were reported in 12 of 240 participants (5.0%) in the EA group and 3 of 240 (1.3%) in the SA group. In the EA group, 7 AEs were considered treatment-related, including subcutaneous hematoma (n = 4) and skin numbness (n = 3); the remaining 5 AEs in the EA group, including fever (n = 2) and dizziness (n = 3), were deemed unrelated to the intervention. All 3 AEs in the SA group, including fever (n = 2) and dizziness (n = 1), were assessed as unrelated to treatment. All reported AEs were mild in severity and resolved spontaneously without medical intervention. No serious AEs occurred, no participants withdrew due to AEs and no deaths were reported during the trial. No participants received any additional analgesic medication, including the permitted rescue medication (celecoxib).

Note on additional pre-specified outcomes

In addition to the outcomes reported above, the trial registry pre-specified a broader set of secondary outcomes. However, the complete collection of data for these outcomes was impeded by several significant challenges. These included operational difficulties across the six participating sites, such as intermittent equipment availability; the high participant burden inherent to the extensive assessment protocol, which involved substantial time commitments, procedural discomfort, and exposure to ionizing radiation; and, most notably, major disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-related restrictions and safety concerns led to the suspension of many non-essential research activities, significantly affecting follow-up compliance and the feasibility of conducting complex, in-person assessments.

Consequently, data collection for the following measures was incomplete: gait analysis, isokinetic muscle strength testing, knee and inguinal region ultrasonography, multi-omics analyses, the SF-36 health survey, plain radiography and CT of the knee, bone mineral density testing, and analysis of inflammatory cytokines in synovial fluid. Furthermore, the NIR-ICG imaging protocol could not be established and was not implemented.

The extent of missing data for these outcomes is substantial and likely non-random, precluding a valid ITT analysis and introducing a high risk of bias. Therefore, in line with guidelines for responsible reporting, we have decided not to present formal statistical comparisons for these outcomes in this primary results paper. A detailed accounting of data availability is provided in the Supplementary Materials Table S10. The data that were successfully collected will be conserved for future focused, hypothesis-generating analyses.

Discussion

This multicentre, randomized, parallel-group, SA-controlled trial provides robust evidence that a 6-week course of EA confers substantial and durable benefits for symptoms and function in KOA. At the powered primary endpoint (WOMAC global score at week 6), the magnitude of benefit was not only statistically supported but also clinically meaningful, with improvements exceeding the prespecified MCII threshold (≥12% relative improvement from baseline) and corresponding to a large standardized effect (Cohen's d). Beyond symptomatic outcomes, exploratory MRI analyses at week 6 suggested directionally favourable changes in cartilage and synovial inflammation measures, but these findings were not powered for definitive inference and should be interpreted cautiously.

Previous studies have demonstrated the short-term efficacy of acupuncture for KOA; however, its long-term effects remain a topic of debate.28 Additionally, there is contention regarding the treatment frequency, as acupuncture administered 3–5 times per week has been shown to be more effective than treatment administered 1–2 times per week.9 Therefore, patients received 6 weeks of treatment (3 sessions per week) and were followed for an additional 24 weeks. The effects of EA treatment persisted throughout the 30-week study period, which is consistent with the findings of Tu JF et al.8 Importantly, interpreting WOMAC changes using clinical thresholds rather than P-values alone, an improvement exceeding 12% from baseline in the WOMAC global score is commonly considered to represent the MCII.26,27 In our study, WOMAC global score demonstrated improvements well beyond this threshold at week 6, highlighting the clinical relevance of EA treatment.

Interestingly, the SA group also showed improvements exceeding the MCII, a finding consistent with previous studies reporting that SA itself is associated with larger effects than usual care for chronic pain conditions.29,30 This observation underscores that acupuncture is a complex intervention in which placebo effects-arising from the interaction between patient, practitioner, and therapeutic setting-are inevitably embedded.31 Crucially, however, these non-specific effects did not extend to structural outcomes: among all six structural parameters for which EA showed statistically significant protective effects compared to SA (Table 2), no such improvements were observed in the SA group. That symptomatic improvement in the SA group was unaccompanied by structural changes suggests that the potential cartilage-protective effects observed with EA are unlikely to be explained solely by non-specific effects, and may instead point to a specific therapeutic action of EA–a hypothesis that warrants confirmation in future studies specifically designed and powered to assess structural outcomes.

Our sample size calculation was based on a conservative estimate of a 9-point between-group difference in WOMAC scores from preliminary single-center data. The observed difference of 40.56 points substantially exceeded this estimate, indicating that: (a) The anticipated effect size (Cohen's d = 0.36) was underestimated relative to the actual large standardized effect size (Cohen's d = 1.21), likely due to the preliminary study's limited sample and single-center design; (b) The rigorous standardization of EA protocols across multiple centers enhanced treatment fidelity; (c) The SA control, while eliciting a clinically relevant response (adjusted mean change of 24.76 points), may have functioned as an active comparator rather than a pure placebo, amplifying the relative advantage of EA. This discrepancy highlights the challenges in predicting effect sizes for complex interventions like acupuncture.

In exploratory MRI analyses, EA was associated with signals of less cartilage deterioration and reduced synovial inflammation. The detection of such subtle structural differences within 6 weeks may be explained by the use of an AI-based segmentation tool (STRR-Net), which enabled precise quantification of very small variations that would likely be imperceptible to traditional measurement approaches. Given the short 6-week follow-up and the exploratory nature of these analyses, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary signals rather than evidence of disease modification. This exploratory pattern, if confirmed, would be notable given that some investigational agents have shown cartilage-protective effects without concomitant pain relief.32,33 EA, by contrast, may offer both symptomatic benefit and exploratory structural signals. However, longer-term studies are required to confirm whether these early signals translate into clinically meaningful effects.

This study has several strengths, including its multicentre randomized design, rigorous sham control, high retention rate, and the integration of patient-reported outcomes with objective AI-driven MRI measures. This combined methodology enabled a comprehensive assessment of both symptomatic and potential structural effects of EA. The high rate of data completeness for primary and secondary outcomes (Tables S1 and S2) may be attributed to the provision of free treatments and examinations, as well as transportation subsidies offered to participants upon study completion.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the main limitation of this study is the incomplete data for several pre-specified secondary outcomes that were not reported. While our primary findings are robust, we cannot draw conclusions regarding the intervention's effect on parameters such as gait, muscle strength, or systemic inflammation. This highlights the practical challenges of implementing a highly complex assessment protocol in a multicenter trial and underscores the importance of prioritizing a core set of feasible and critical endpoints in future study designs. Second, small numerical baseline differences were observed in symptom and function measures (e.g., VAS, Lequesne index, and 6MWT), which could in principle contribute to regression to the mean, particularly for subjective outcomes. However, because the primary MMRM adjusted for baseline values and key prognostic covariates and yielded consistent estimates across multiple sensitivity analyses (including unadjusted and missing-data approaches), the findings appear robust to baseline imbalance, although residual influence cannot be entirely excluded when interpreting subjective outcomes. Third, MRI outcomes were assessed over a relatively short duration (baseline to week 6) and the trial was not powered for structural endpoints. Therefore, despite statistically supported differences, standardized effect sizes for cartilage morphology were small; these MRI findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in longer-term trials powered for structural change. Fourth, visit was modeled categorically to obtain robust, visit-specific contrasts consistent with the observed non-linear course; continuous-time modeling could better reflect uneven visit spacing but would require a prespecified functional form and may be sensitive to model specification. Fifth, sample size was calculated using a two-sample t-test at Week 6 primary endpoint, whereas the primary analysis used an MMRM; the relative efficiency of MMRM depends on within-participant correlation and model specification, and may vary across settings; therefore, non-significant effects-especially for secondary and exploratory outcomes-should be interpreted cautiously.

In conclusion, in patients with KOA, a 6-week course of EA produced durable, clinically meaningful improvements in pain and function. Exploratory MRI findings suggested possible anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effects, which warrant confirmation in future trials designed for structural endpoints.
