Salt Intake and Kidney Outcomes in Older Adults With Chronic Kidney Disease: A Stage‐Specific Cohort Study
Authors: Hiroaki Miyauchi, Tatsuhiko Azegami, Takashin Nakayama, Chisato Mitsue, Hirotaka Shibata, Kaori Hayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimIn patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), salt restriction has been shown to lower blood pressure and proteinuria and to provide kidney protective effects. However, the benefit of salt restriction in older adults with CKD remains unclear, particularly in advanced stages.MethodsWe conducted a single‐center retrospective cohort study of patients aged ≥ 75 years with CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2). Salt intake was estimated from spot urine using the Tanaka formula and categorized into quartiles. The primary outcome was a composite kidney endpoint of ≥ 30% eGFR decline or initiation of kidney replacement therapy. Cox proportional hazards models and competing risk analyses were performed, stratified by CKD stage.ResultsA total of 462 patients (median age, 79 years; 68.6% male) were included. Of these, 305 had CKD Stage 3 and 157 had Stages 4–5. During a median follow‐up of 2.79 years, 121 patients (26.2%) experienced the composite outcome. In Stage 3 CKD, higher salt intake was significantly associated with increased risk (adjusted HR 2.70; 95% CI 1.02–7.12; highest vs. lowest quartile), and findings were consistent across sensitivity analyses. In contrast, no significant association was observed in Stages 4–5.ConclusionAmong older adults with CKD, excessive salt intake was associated with adverse kidney outcomes in CKD Stage 3 but not in Stages 4–5. These results suggest that salt restriction may be most beneficial in the early stages of CKD in this population.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects more than 850 million people worldwide, imposing substantial health and economic burdens. CKD increases the risk of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD) and cardiovascular disease [1]. Age‐related declines in glomerular filtration rate and increases in proteinuria contribute to the rising prevalence of CKD [2]; in Japan, CKD affects an estimated 25.3% of individuals aged ≥ 65 years and 34.6% of those aged ≥ 75 years [3].

Salt restriction is an established non‐pharmacological therapy for CKD, providing renoprotective effects by lowering blood pressure and proteinuria and enhancing the antiproteinuric effects of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibitors [4, 5, 6]. However, its direct effect on kidney function remains controversial [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Large‐scale studies have generally excluded older adults, particularly those aged ≥ 75 years, limiting evidence for salt restriction in this population [8, 11]. In older individuals, excessive salt restriction may cause nutritional deficiencies, frailty, and sarcopenia due to altered taste perception [12]. Previous studies in individuals aged ≥ 65 years have reported that salt intake below 6 g/day is associated with poorer kidney prognosis [10].

While salt restriction is beneficial in the early stages of CKD, its effect may be limited in advanced CKD (Stages 4–5) due to irreversible glomerulosclerosis and interstitial damage [7]. Therefore, the benefits and risks of salt restriction likely vary according to both age and CKD stage.

Against this background, we investigated the association between salt intake and kidney outcomes in patients aged ≥ 75 years with CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2). We performed a stratified analysis by CKD stage—Stage 3 (eGFR ≥ 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) and Stages 4–5 (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2)—to clarify the potential benefits of salt restriction in older adults.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This single‐center retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Hospital.

We included patients who met the following criteria: (1) aged ≥ 75 years; (2) had CKD with an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 at baseline; (3) visited our department between January 2017 and December 2024; and (4) had at least two measurements of urinary sodium (Na) and creatinine (Cr) within a six‐month period. For each patient, the earliest pair of eligible urinary sodium and creatinine measurements obtained within a six‐month period was identified, and the mean of the two estimated salt intake values was used as the baseline exposure. The date of the first urinary measurement was defined as baseline, and follow‐up for outcome events commenced on that date.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of dialysis or kidney transplantation; (2) acute kidney injury (AKI) at presentation; (3) absence of height or weight records; and (4) inability to complete at least one year of follow‐up.

Participants were stratified into CKD Stage 3 or Stages 4–5 according to eGFR at baseline, and kidney outcomes were compared according to salt intake within each stage. The study was approved by the University School of Medicine Ethics Committee (approval number: 2025‐1127) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained via an opt‐out method on the institutional website.

Baseline Characteristics

The following data were obtained from medical records at baseline: age, gender, blood pressure (BP), presence of diabetes, urine test results (Na, Cr, total protein), blood test results (haemoglobin, albumin, Cr), and medication history (diuretics, antihypertensive medications, sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors [SGLT‐2 inhibitors], statins, vitamin D preparations, and erythropoiesis‐stimulating agents [ESA]).

BP was measured at the hospital in the seated position during routine clinical visits. For the analysis, the average of two measurements obtained on the day of salt intake estimation was used. Diabetes was defined as a prior diagnosis or current use of hypoglycaemic agents. Antihypertensive drugs included angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARB), angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), calcium channel blockers (CCB), beta‐blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, central alpha‐2 receptor agonists, and alpha‐1 adrenoreceptor antagonists. Diuretics included loop diuretics, tolvaptan, and thiazides.

eGFR was calculated using the Japanese Society of Nephrology (JSN) equation, which is based on the Japanese population [13]: JSNeGFR=194×Crmg/dL−1.094×age−0.287×0.739for female.

Nutritional status was assessed using the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), calculated as follows [14]: GNRI=14.89×Albg/dL+41.7×body weightkg/ideal body weightkg. Ideal body weightkg=heightm2×22kg/m2.

The presumed underlying cause of CKD was determined from the clinical diagnosis documented in the medical records and classified as hypertensive nephrosclerosis, diabetic kidney disease, chronic glomerulonephritis, monoclonal gammopathy‐related kidney disease, urological or structural kidney disease, other causes, or unknown.

Estimation of Salt Intake

Salt intake was estimated from 24‐h urinary Na excretion, calculated using the Tanaka formula based on spot urine samples collected during outpatient visits [15]. The mean of two estimates obtained within a six‐month period was used. Height, weight, and age were retrieved from medical records.

The formulas were as follows: 24−hour urineCrmg/day=−2.04×age+14.89×weightkg+16.14×heightcm−2244.45, 24−hour urineNamEq/day=21.98×urineNamEq/L/urineCrmg/dL×10×24−hour urineCrmg/day0.392, Estimated salt intakeg/day=24−hour urineNamEq/day×58.443/1000.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite kidney outcome, defined as the occurrence of either: (1) a ≥ 30% decline in eGFR or (2) initiation of kidney replacement therapy (haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation). Dialysis was included only if initiated as maintenance therapy. Survival time was defined as the period from baseline to the occurrence of an event or the last follow‐up. As a secondary outcome, we evaluated the annual change in eGFR (ΔeGFR; mL/min/1.73 m2/year).

Statistical Analysis

Participants were classified into quartiles according to estimated salt intake: Q1 (< 6.9 g/day), Q2 (6.9–8.2 g/day), Q3 (8.2–9.5 g/day) and Q4 (> 9.5 g/day), and then stratified by CKD stage (Stage 3 and Stages 4–5). Baseline continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median (interquartile range, IQR), depending on their distribution. Group comparisons were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. Comparisons between CKD Stage 3 and Stages 4–5 were conducted using the t‐test or the Mann–Whitney U test.

For the primary outcome, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. To assess potential heterogeneity across CKD stages, an interaction term between salt intake quartiles and CKD stage (Stage 3 vs. Stages 4–5) was included in the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model. The risks for Q1 and Q4 were compared by subgroup. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were constructed to assess potential nonlinear associations, and changes in HRs were visualized. The annual change in eGFR was evaluated using a linear mixed‐effects model. To test for linear trend in eGFR decline across quartiles, quartile categories were treated as an ordinal variable (1–4) and included as an interaction term with time in the mixed‐effects model. In Model 1, we adjusted for age and gender. Model 2 was further adjusted for eGFR, proteinuria, GNRI, diabetes, systolic BP, and use of diuretics. We performed sensitivity analysis to test the robustness of the findings. First, estimated salt intake was recalculated using the Kawasaki method [16], and the Cox proportional hazards model was reapplied. Second, competing risk analysis was conducted using the Fine and Grey model, with death treated as a competing risk. Third, to evaluate the potential influence of diuretic use on estimated salt intake, Cox proportional hazards analyses were repeated after excluding patients receiving diuretics at baseline. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 and R version 4.5.1.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Based on the selection criteria, 462 patients were included in the analysis (Figure 1). The median age of the study population was 79 years (IQR, 75–85), and 68.6% were male. The median eGFR was 35.5 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR, 25.8–45.0), and the median proteinuria was 0.35 g/gCr (IQR, 0.11–1.59). A total of 81.6% of patients were taking antihypertensive medication (Table 1).

CKD Stage 3 was present in 305 patients (66.0%) and Stages 4–5 in 157 patients (34.0%). In CKD Stage 3, body mass index (BMI) (Q1: 23.4 vs. Q4: 24.4 kg/m2), systolic BP (Q1: 133 vs. Q4: 140 mmHg), proteinuria (Q1: 0.16 vs. Q4: 0.29 g/gCr) were higher in the high‐salt intake group. Additionally, diabetes (Q1: 13.7% vs. Q4: 38.3%) was more prevalent. GNRI tended to increase with salt intake, but the difference was not statistically significant. In CKD Stages 4–5, diabetes (Q1: 16.3% vs. Q4: 38.2%) and proteinuria (Q1: 0.43 vs. Q4: 2.61 g/gCr) were more prevalent in the high–salt intake group. No significant differences in eGFR were observed in either stage. Regarding medication use, in CKD Stage 3, the high–salt intake group had a higher frequency of beta‐blocker, diuretic, and statin use. In CKD Stages 4–5, GNRI was lower, proteinuria was higher, and the use of ACEi/ARB/ARNI was less frequent, whereas CCB use was more frequent compared with Stage 3 (Table S1).

Hypertensive nephrosclerosis and diabetic kidney disease were the most common presumed causes of CKD. The distribution of CKD etiologies was comparable between patients with CKD Stage 3 and those with Stages 4–5 (Table S1).

Primary Outcomes

During a median follow‐up of 2.79 years (IQR, 1.63–4.40), 121 of 462 patients (26.2%) reached the composite kidney outcome. This occurred in 54 patients (17.8%) with CKD Stage 3, and in 67 patients (42.7%) with Stages 4–5. Cox proportional hazards models were used to calculate HRs, with salt intake Q1 as the reference group (Table 2). In the overall analysis, no significant association was found between salt intake and kidney outcomes. However, among patients with CKD Stage 3, higher salt intake was significantly associated with increased risk. In Model 2, the HRs (95% CIs) for Q2, Q3, and Q4 versus Q1 were 2.06 (0.73–5.80), 2.74 (1.00–7.51), and 2.70 (1.02–7.12), respectively. By contrast, no significant associations were identified among patients with CKD Stages 4–5 (Model 2: HRs [95% CIs] for Q2–Q4 vs. Q1, 1.02 [0.50–2.09], 0.69 [0.35–1.39], and 0.85 [0.40–1.81]). Furthermore, a statistically significant interaction was observed between salt intake quartiles and CKD stage for the primary composite outcome (P for interaction = 0.049), confirming that the association between salt intake and kidney outcomes differed according to disease stage. RCS models were used to explore potential nonlinear associations (Figure 2). In CKD Stage 3, HRs showed an approximately linear increase with salt intake, whereas in Stages 4–5, HRs remained relatively stable.

Secondary Outcomes

In adjusted mixed‐effects models, higher salt intake was associated with slightly steeper eGFR decline overall, but the absolute differences were small and not statistically significant (Figure 3, Table S2).

Stratified analyses suggested stage‐specific patterns. Among patients with CKD Stage 3, eGFR slopes became progressively more negative across quartiles—Q1–1.15 (95% CI −1.90 to −0.40), Q2–1.54 (−2.25 to −0.82), Q3–1.89 (−2.63 to −1.16), and Q4–1.82 (−2.50 to −1.14) mL/min/1.73 m2/year (Model 2: p for trend = 0.160). In contrast, in CKD Stages 4–5, all quartiles showed similarly steep declines with no meaningful differences between quartiles (Model 2: p for trend = 0.694). The direction of effect was consistent with the primary outcome, but the magnitude for ΔeGFR was modest and not statistically significant.

Subgroup Analyses

In the overall analysis, risks between Q1 and Q4 were compared across the following subgroups: gender (male or female), BMI (≥ 25 or < 25 kg/m2), GNRI (> 98 or ≤ 98), proteinuria (≥ 0.5 or < 0.5 g/gCr), haemoglobin (≥ 12 or < 12 g/dL), and use of ACEi/ARB/ARNI (yes or no). The associations were consistent across sex, BMI, GNRI, haemoglobin, or use of ACEi/ARB/ARNI, but differed significantly according to proteinuria (Figure S1).

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis using the Kawasaki formula to estimate salt intake showed a similar trend in CKD Stage 3 (Model 2: HRs [95% CIs] for Q2–Q4 vs. Q1, 2.21 [0.80–6.11], 2.41 [0.87–6.66] and 2.80 [1.06–7.41]) (Table S3). In addition, in the competing risk analysis using the Fine and Grey model, higher salt intake was associated with an increased risk of kidney outcomes in CKD Stage 3 (Model 2: HRs [95% CIs] for Q2–Q4 vs. Q1, 1.97 [0.74–5.22], 2.63 [1.00–6.92] and 2.70 [1.07–6.83]) (Table S4). Finally, in the sensitivity analysis excluding patients receiving diuretics, the overall hazard ratio estimates and direction of associations were generally consistent with those of the primary analysis, although statistical significance was limited by the smaller sample size (Table S5).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study examined the association between salt intake and kidney outcomes in older adults with CKD aged ≥ 75 years. In the overall cohort, no significant association was identified. However, in patients with early‐stage CKD (Stage 3), higher salt intake was significantly associated with an increased risk of the composite kidney outcomes, whereas no such trend was observed in advanced CKD (Stages 4–5). These findings suggest that excessive salt intake may accelerate kidney function decline even in older adults with early‐stage CKD, whereas its impact appears to be attenuated in those with advanced CKD.

Previous large‐scale cohort studies, such as the KoreaN Cohort Study for Outcome in Patients With CKD (KNOW‐CKD) [11] and the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) study [8], have consistently reported that higher sodium excretion is associated with an increased risk of kidney function decline in CKD patients. However, the impact of salt intake in older adults has rarely been reported worldwide. Our study extends these findings by focusing on patients aged ≥ 75 years, who were not included in most prior studies. We observed that higher salt intake was significantly associated with composite kidney outcomes in CKD Stage 3, whereas no significant association was observed in Stages 4–5. Importantly, a formal test for interaction revealed a statistically significant stage‐dependent heterogeneity (P for interaction = 0.049), providing robust statistical support for separating early and advanced CKD when considering the clinical role of salt restriction in older adults. In early‐stage CK, salt sensitivity and intraglomerular hypertension remain key targets where dietary restriction can confer clear benefits. In contrast, in advanced stages, irreversible pathological changes such as glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis are already established [17], and age‐related nephrosclerosis and microvascular lesions may further attenuate the relative impact of dietary salt intake [18, 19].

To our knowledge, only one previous study has specifically examined older patients with CKD. That pooled analysis of four prospective cohorts compared salt intake and outcomes between patients aged ≤ 65 and > 65 years, and found that in older patients, a salt intake of < 6 g/day was associated with a higher risk of ESKD, whereas no corresponding increase in mortality was observed across intake levels. In younger patients, no significant association was detected [10]. However, assessing the relationship between salt intake and kidney outcomes using ESKD alone may be insufficient. In older adults with relatively preserved kidney function, the risk of death may compete with the risk of progression to ESKD [20]. Therefore, using ESKD as an endpoint may not fully capture the effect of salt intake especially in older patients with early‐stage CKD. Moreover, previous studies suggest that the impact of salt intake may vary according to CKD stage, highlighting the need for stage‐specific evaluations. Accordingly, in the present study, we addressed these limitations by using a composite outcome that included both eGFR decline and ESKD, and by conducting analyses stratified by CKD stage.

The potential adverse effects of salt restriction in older individuals should also be considered. Age‐related declines in taste and olfactory perception frequently reduce appetite and overall dietary intake in older adults [12]. Therefore, excessively strict salt restriction may further impair food palatability and decrease food intake, potentially leading to malnutrition, sarcopenia, and an increased risk of frailty and falls [21, 22]. In addition, age‐related decline in the kidney's autoregulatory function may predispose to pre‐renal acute kidney injury when salt intake is too low, due to reduced renal blood flow [23, 24]. Recovery from such acute kidney injury can be challenging in older patients [25]. Therefore, the degree of salt restriction in older CKD patients should be individualized, taking into account residual kidney function, nutritional status, and overall frailty. Our findings suggest that avoiding excessive salt intake may help preserve kidney function in relatively early‐stage CKD, whereas the benefits of salt restriction are limited in advanced CKD.

The strengths of this study include clarification of the stage‐specific significance of salt restriction in older patients with CKD, an area that has not been sufficiently investigated, and verification of the robustness of our results through competing risk analysis for death. However, this study has several limitations. First, salt intake was estimated using spot urine and a predictive formula, which is less accurate than 24‐h urine collection. Second, although using the mean of two spot urine estimates within 6 months reduces random intra‐individual variability, the samples were not systematically collected across different seasons; thus, potential seasonal variations in dietary salt intake and urinary excretion could not be fully eliminated. Third, as a single‐centre retrospective cohort study, the generalizability of our findings is limited, and there remains the possibility of residual confounding inherent to the retrospective design. Fourth, although nutritional status was adjusted for using GNRI, direct measures of nutritional status (e.g., potassium, protein, and energy intake, as well as physical activity) were not assessed, and these factors may have influenced the outcomes. Fifth, BP measurements were obtained during routine outpatient care in a retrospective manner, which may have introduced subtle measurement variability compared with strict clinical trial protocols.

In conclusion, higher salt intake was associated with an increased risk of kidney outcomes in older adults with CKD Stage 3, underscoring the potential importance of salt restriction in the early stages of disease. In contrast, no such association was found in advanced CKD (Stages 4–5), suggesting that the benefits of salt restriction may be limited in advanced CKD. These findings highlight the importance of implementing individualized salt management early in the course of CKD, particularly before progression to advanced stages, taking into account residual kidney function, nutritional status, and overall frailty.
