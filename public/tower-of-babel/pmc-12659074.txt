Determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms among older adults living alone: longitudinal national evidence from Thailand, 2015–2022
Authors: Supa Pengpid, Karl Peltzer, André Hajek, Razak M Gyasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA few longitudinal studies on the life satisfaction and depressive symptoms of older people living alone have been carried out. The present study aimed to investigate the determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms of older people living alone in Thailand.MethodsWe used data from Thailand’s Health, Aging, and Retirement research conducted in 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022. The community-based analytic sample included 1735 observations of older adults living alone. The average age of the pooled sample was 74.0 years (SD = 8.5, range: 60–107 years). Life satisfaction and depressive symptoms were measured using established metrics. Linear random-effects models evaluated determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms.ResultsRegressions found that higher education, higher subjective economic status, female sex, urban residence, social engagement, better self-rated health status, physical activity, chewing ability, wearing dentures, and participation in annual health check-ups were positively associated with higher life satisfaction. Being single, divorced, separated, or widowed, having depressive symptoms, and loneliness were negatively associated with life satisfaction. Furthermore, male sex, currently working, rural residence, functional limitations, and loneliness were associated with higher depressive symptoms, while self-rated physical health, physical activity, and wearing dentures were negatively associated with depressive symptoms.ConclusionsOverall, this study among older adults living alone showed that advantages and disadvantages in socioeconomic situation, health, and health behaviour were the determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms, respectively. Efforts to promote well-being among older adults living alone should reduce or promote the identified associated factors.Keywords: Life satisfaction, Depressive symptoms, Living alone, Longitudinal study, Older adults, Thailand

Introduction

Thailand’s population is aging quickly; between 2000 and 2015, the country’s life expectancy at 60 increased from 20.2% to 21.9% [1]. Thirteen million persons aged sixty and above made up roughly 19% of Thailand’s population in 2022; by 2042, that number is predicted to rise to 19 million, or 31.4% [2]. In Thailand, lower rates of the leading causes of death have been associated with longer life expectancies [3]. In Thailand, family size is decreasing in tandem with the country’s sharp rise in life expectancy [4], and based on Thailand’s 2017 Survey of Older People, 10.8% of persons 60 years and older were living alone [5]. There is mounting evidence that older persons who live alone have worse health and die younger than those who do not [6–8]. Among older adults, life satisfaction [9] and depressive symptoms [10] have been found to be determinants of mortality. Satisfaction with life and depressive symptoms are distinct dimensions of mental well-being. To better assess the determinants of mental well-being in older adults living alone longitudinal studies are needed. However, only one longitudinal study [11] in a high-income country, South Korea, investigated the determinants of well-being (life satisfaction) in older adults living alone. To fill this research gap, this study aimed at longitudinally assess the determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms of older people living alone in a middle-income country, Thailand.

In Thailand, solo living among older adults is influenced by a combination of cultural shifts, evolving government policies, and changes within the health system. The trend is driven primarily by urbanization, the breakdown of traditional family structures, and government initiatives that support “aging in place” [12, 13]. Cultural factors may include a decline in multi-generational households, e.g., younger people migrate to cities for work, leaving older adults in rural areas, a growing emphasis on independence and self-reliance and changing societal views on care for older adults, such as external care services. Policy factors may include the provision of government allowances and welfare, especially for those living below the poverty line, Aging in place” initiatives to allow seniors to remain in their homes and the promotion of elder employment increasing financial independence, and enabling solo living [2, 14]. Health system factors may include Long-Term Care (LTC) programme expansion providing home- and community-based care for dependent older persons, provision of free health promotion and disease prevention services, and provision of social services and support systems providing crucial medical and social services that support independent living [2, 14].

A framework for the determinants of mental well-being in older adults living alone may be conceptualized as a combination of sociodemographic factors, social integration, health status and lifestyle factors [13, 15, 16]. Sociodemographic factors associated with life satisfaction among older adults living alone include increasing age [11, 17], being widowed [11], male sex [18], higher education [11, 17, 18], higher economic level [11, 19], higher monthly allowance (Kim & Sok, 2013), being a homeowner [17], employed [11], not employed [18], better overall dwelling conditions [18]. Sociodemographic factors associated with depressive symptoms among older adults living alone include female sex [19], male sex [20], lower economic status [21], rural residence, and economic activity [22].

Regarding social integration factors associated with life satisfaction among older adults living alone included having at least one child [17], having daily contact with child [17], higher social support [19], higher social engagement [11], higher leisure activities [17], satisfaction with health services [19]. Living with at least one child is positively correlated with older people’s happiness in Thailand compared to living alone [23]. Social integration factors associated with depressive symptoms among older adults living alone include less frequent contact with relatives [21], social inactivity [19], no religious belief, lower social support [24], without more senior citizen facilities in a community, a larger percentage of older persons, and greater social networks of friends and family [20].

In terms of health status factors associated with life satisfaction among older adults living alone included better self-rated health status [17, 25], higher self-esteem [25], less functional limitations [17], less lonely [19], less depressive symptoms [11, 25], lower number of chronic conditions [11]. Health status factors associated with depressive symptoms among older adults living alone included poorer self-rated health status [19, 21, 22, 24], more functional limitations [19–22], active pain [19], and chronic illnesses [24].

Lifestyle factors associated with life satisfaction among older adults living alone included underweight and obesity compared to normal weight [11]. Compared to other household composition forms, older people living alone in Thailand were more likely to consume instant meals [26]. Lifestyle factors associated with depressive symptoms among older adults living alone included currently smoking and alcohol use abstinence [19].

The present study aimed to investigate the determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms of older people living alone in Thailand. Developing solutions to support Thailand’s ageing population living alone requires knowledge of the determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms among solo-living older Thais.

Methods

Participants and protocols

Four waves of the Health, Ageing, and Retirement Studies (HART) conducted in Thailand in 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022 were analyzed. To gather panel data on the multidisciplinary aspects of aging in Thai older adults, HART is a biannual household panel survey. It includes the following questions: (1) General Information; (2) Family and Support; (3) Health Status; (4) Work Status; (5) Income and Expenditure; (6) Asset and Debt; (7) Life Expectation; and (8) Exit Interview [27]. As the inclusion criterion, one adult (over 45) was chosen randomly from each household and interviewed in-home using a multi-stage probability sampling method being nationally representative of middle-aged and older adults in Thailand; further details are given elsewhere [28]. Only participants who were 60 years of age or older and living alone were included in the sample for this analysis; 1735 observations from four research assessments in 2015, 2017, 2020, and 2022 made up the analytic pooled sample. Participants who were lost to follow-up showed lower depression symptoms (p = 0.002) and higher life satisfaction (p = 0.003) than those who remained in the study. Participants provided signed informed consent, and the study’s protocol was approved by the National Institute for Development Administration’s Human Research Ethics Committee (ECNIDA 2020/00012). Every procedure followed the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with all applicable rules and regulations.

Measures

Outcome variables

Using a visual analogue scale, life satisfaction was measured with the item, “Overall, how satisfied are you with your quality of life?” reported on a “0 (= very poor) to 10 (= excellent)” scale [29]. Single-item life satisfaction measures are reliable and valid as multi-item measures [30, 31].

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D-10), excluding the loneliness item (total scores 0–27, with higher scores showing more depressive symptoms) [32]. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.67 in this study.

Independent variables

Sociodemographic factors

As a main inclusion criterion (not an independent variable in our analysis), living alone was defined as having just one household member regularly residing as opposed to having one or more other people regularly residing in the household.

Work status was assessed with the question, “Are you currently working for income?” (Yes/No). This commonly categorizes work status in large cohort studies [33]. Further, sociodemographic variables included age in years, sex, education (none, primary or more than primary), residence status (urban, rural), and subjective economic status were among the sociodemographic variables. “How satisfied are you with your economic situation?” was the question used to assess the latter. Higher economic status is indicated by higher scores ranging from 0 to 10.

Social integration factors included marital status (widowed/single/divorced/separated versus married/cohabiting), having living children, medical homecare utilization (past year), social engagement, and religious involvement.

Participation in at least one of six activities—social, athletic, musical, artistic, cultural, volunteer, and/or political—was considered social engagement (yes/no). It has a Cronbach’s α of 0.63.

Religious involvement was sourced from four questions, e.g., “Prayer in the morning/before going to bed.” [34]. Higher scores indicated stronger religious commitment; the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.81 in this study. Responses ranged from 0 = never to 3 = always, for a total range of 0–12.

Health status factors

Using a visual analogue scale, self-rated physical health status was generated from the item, “In general, how would you rate your physical health status?” reported on a “0 (= very poor) to 10 (= excellent)” scale [35].

Count score of chronic conditions (0–13), included diabetes, high blood pressure, bone disorders, heart disease, mental or emotional disorders, dementia, brain disorders, liver or gastrointestinal disorders, kidney illness, lung disorders, prostate disorders, conditions affecting the ovaries or uterus, glaucoma/cataract/pterygium, and any other diseases impacting your life as diagnosed by a doctor.

Functional limitations of Activities of Daily Living (ADL) were assessed with a modified 5-item ADL scale (dress, wash, eat, bathing or showering, and other activities) [36]. Response options were 0) ‘Can do every step myself without needing help’, 1) ‘Need help sometimes with some steps’, 2) ‘Need help with some steps’, 3) ‘Need help sometimes with every step’, and 4) ‘Need help every time with every step.’ Total scores ranged from 0 to 20, with higher scores representing greater functional limitations. Cronbach’s α was 0.91.

Loneliness was measured from the CES-D-10 item [32], “In the past week, how often did you experience feeling lonely?” defined as “almost always (5–7 days), often (3–4 days) or sometimes (1–2 days)”=1 and “very rarely (less than one day) or none”=0. The item was reverse-scored so that higher scores reflected higher loneliness. This loneliness measure performs similarly to other measures [37, 38].

Mastication or chewing ability was assessed with two questions: “What is your ability to chew sticky/hard/crispy things such as meat, guava, apple?” (a) wearing dentures and (b) not wearing dentures. Response options were “1) Very bad, 2) A bit bad, 3) Average, 4) Fairly good, and 5) Very good.” Scores (2–10) of the two questions were summed, with higher scores indicating better mastication [39].

Lifestyle factors

Substance use included current smoking (yes/no) and current alcohol use (yes/no).

In order to determine the frequency of physical activity or exercise, participants were asked, “How often do you exercise?” (“1 = 0 days, 2 = 1–2 days, 3 = 3–4 days, 4 = 5–6 days or 5 = 7 days per week”).

Meal skipping (0–6) was assessed with the question: “How many meals have you had in the last 2 days?” “Yesterday (breakfast, lunch, dinner; yes/no) and the day before yesterday (breakfast, lunch, dinner; yes/no).” [40].

Further questions included the use of dentures (Yes/No) and participation in annual health check-up (Yes/No).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample. Continuous outcomes (life satisfaction and depressive symptoms) were analysed with linear random-effects models to acccount for the repeated measures of participants across the four study waves from 2015 to 2022. The linear random-effect models were chosen based on the Hausman specification test between the fixed effects model and the random-effects model [for life satisfaction (14.29) p = 0.504, and for depressive symptoms 17.56) p = 0.175]. Additionally, cluster-robust standard errors were determined (Cameron & Trivedi, 2010). P < 0.05 was deemed significant, and only full instances (missing cases were < 3% in study variables) were examined. Survey weights were used to adjust for overrepresentation or underrepresentation of specific subgroups in the sample as well as to make up for differential selection probabilities [28]. StataSE 18.0 (College Station, TX, USA) was used for statistical analysis. When using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to check for collinearity, none was discovered (< 1.43).

Results

Participants

There were 1,735 observations of older adults (≥ 60 years) living alone in the combined analytical sample. The average age of the individuals in the pooled sample was 74.0 years (SD = 8.5, range 60–107 years), 39.9% were men, 71.7% were widowed, single, divorced, or separated, 8.7% lacked formal education, 51.6% lived in cities, 30.3% were working and 76.2% had living children. The average life satisfaction was 7.6 (SD = 1.7), and the average depressive symptoms were 4.7 (SD = 3.5). Further sample details are shown in Table 1.

Determinants of life satisfaction in older adults living alone

In terms of sociodemographic factors, higher education (β 0.21, SE = 0.09, p = 0.023), higher subjective economic status (β 0.26, SE = 0.02, p < 0.001), and urban residence (β 0.13, SE = 0.06, p = 0.045) were associated with higher life satisfaction, while male sex (β −0.16, SE = 0.07, p = 0.028) was negatively associated with life satisfaction. Regarding social integration, social engagement (β 0.17, SE = 0.07, p = 0.012) was positively associated with life satisfaction, and being single, divorced, separated, or widowed (β −0.31, SE = 0.08, p < 0.001) was negatively associated with life satisfaction. Self-rated physical health (β 0.22, SE = 0.02, p < 0.001) and chewing ability (β 0.08, SE = 0.03, p = 0.005) were positively associated, and depressive symptoms (β −0.08, SE = 0.01, p < 0.001) and loneliness (β −0.17, SE = 0.06, p = 0.005) were negatively associated with life satisfaction. Physical activity (β 0.05, SE = 0.02, p = 0.007), wearing dentures (β 0.23, SE = 0.08, p = 0.002), and participation in annual health check-up (β 0.13, SE = 0.07, p = 0.044) were positively associated with life satisfaction (see Table 2).

Discussion

The study used national, longitudinal data from 2015 to 2022 to quantify, for the first time, the longitudinal determinants of life satisfaction and depressive symptoms among Thailand’s ageing adults living alone. Regressions showed that higher education, higher subjective economic status, female sex, urban residence, social engagement, better self-rated health status, physical activity, chewing ability, wearing dentures, and participation in annual health check-ups were positively associated with higher life satisfaction. Being single, divorced, separated, or widowed, having depressive symptoms, and loneliness were negatively associated with life satisfaction. Furthermore, male sex, currently working, rural residence, functional limitations, and loneliness were associated with higher depressive symptoms, while self-rated physical health, physical activity, and wearing dentures were negatively associated with depressive symptoms.

Sociodemographic factors

Some previous studies among older adults living alone showed that increasing age was associated with life satisfaction [11, 17], while this study did not find significant age differences in relation to life satisfaction. It is possible that psychological factors like resilience and a sense of coherence are stronger predictors of life satisfaction than age, especially in the Thai context where cultural values foster independence and community support, overriding the effects of increasing age [41]. A study among older adults living alone in Malaysia found that male sex was associated with higher life satisfaction [18], while this study found that female sex was associated with higher life satisfaction. These differences may be explained by different gender norms in predominantly Muslim Malaysia and predominantly Buddhist Thailand. Gender roles have been shaped by the intersection of Islamic values and traditional Malay culture, which has historically placed emphasis on men as providers, while in Thai culture, influenced by Buddhism, also reflects a history of greater female economic participation and matrilocal residence patterns, where children and grandchildren live near or with the maternal household creating stronger support networks for older women [42, 43].

Consistent with several previous studies [11, 17–19, 25], this study showed that higher education, and higher subjective economic status were positively associated with life satisfaction. Higher socioeconomic status is linked to increased life satisfaction primarily through enhanced access to resources and opportunities, including better healthcare, education, employment, and housing. These factors contribute to improved physical and mental well-being, greater autonomy, and a sense of control over one’s life, ultimately leading to higher life satisfaction [44]. Former research found mixed results regarding employment status and life satisfaction [11, 18], and this study found no significant association between work status and life satisfaction. This study showed that being single, divorced, separated, or widowed was negatively associated with life satisfaction, which is consistent with a study among 45–65-year-olds living alone in South Korea [11]. The loss of the spouse may contribute to the decrease in life satisfaction [11].

Consistent with one study among older adults living alone in South Korea [20], this study showed that being male increased depressive symptoms. Given the absence of support they once received from their spouses, this may suggest that older men living alone in Thai culture are more susceptible than women [20]. Furthermore, this study showed no association between economic status and depressive symptoms, while in a study in China [21] and South Korea [20] lower economic status was associated with depressive symptoms. Consistent with another study in China [22] work activity and rural residence were associated with depressive symptoms. People who live alone may have less financial support, which makes them more stressed out by financial demands. Engaging in economic activities can also have a detrimental effect on their mental health [22, 45].

Social integrative factors

This study showed that higher social participation was positively associated with life satisfaction, which is consistent with several studies [11, 17, 19]. However, unlike some previous research [17], this study did not find an association between having children and life satisfaction. Unlike some previous research [19–21], that found an association between low social engagement and depressive symptoms, this study did not find a significant association.

Health status factors

In line with previous findings [11, 17, 19, 25], better self-rated health status, fewer depressive symptoms, and fewer loneliness increased life satisfaction. Unlike some previous studies [11, 17], this study did not find a significant association between fewer functional limitations, a lower number of chronic conditions, and life satisfaction. Consistent with previous research [19–22, 24], this study found that poorer self-rated health, functional limitations and loneliness were associated with higher depressive symptoms. However, the number of chronic conditions did not significantly increase depressive symptoms, unlike in a study in Taiwan [24].

Lifestyle factors

A previous study among older adults in Thailand did not find a significant association between denture use and life satisfaction [46]. Further, physical activity and wearing dentures were negatively associated with depressive symptoms in this study. Physical activity improves mental health and lessens depression symptoms, according to studies [47, 48]. A decrease in inflammatory markers linked to sadness, disability, and chronic illness may be one of the processes via which physical activity or exercise improves mental health [49]. A study among older adults in South Korea showed that denture use was associated with higher depressive symptoms [50], and in China denture use was associated with a decreased risk of depression [51]. Consistent with the China study [51], this study found that denture wearing was protective against depressive symptoms, which may be attributed to keeping people in good nutritional condition, and those wearing dentures having a healthier diet than those without dentures, dentures can help reduce depressive symptoms, and by preserving their prior position, denture use can help them cope with emotional stress and sadness [51, 52]. Other lifestyle factors, including currently smoking and alcohol use, were not significantly associated with depressive symptoms, while a study in South Korea found a positive association between smoking, alcohol use, and abstinence and depressive symptoms [19].

Implications for policy and practice

Overall, this study showed that advantages in socioeconomic situation (higher education, higher economic status) were determinants of life satisfaction and disadvantages in socioeconomic situation (currently working, and rural residence) were associated with depressive symptoms among older adults living alone. Policy implications may include the provision of government allowances and welfare, especially for those living below the poverty line, ‘Aging in place’ initiatives to allow seniors to remain in their homes and the promotion of elder employment increasing financial independence, and enabling solo living [2, 14]. Advantages in health (better self-rated physical health status, less depressive symptoms, less loneliness, and chewing ability) and health behaviours (physical activity, wearing dentures, and participation in annual health check-up) were the determinants of life satisfaction among older adults living alone, and disadvantages in health (poorer self-rated physical health status, loneliness, and functional limitations) and unhealthy behaviours (physical inactivity, not wearing dentures) were the determinants of depressive symptoms among older adults living alone. Health system policy implications may include Long-Term Care (LTC) programme expansion providing home- and community-based care for dependent older persons, provision of free health promotion and disease prevention services, and provision of social services and support systems providing crucial medical and social services that support independent living [2, 14, 53].

Study strengths and limitations and future research

The study incorporates known metrics from the Korean Longitudinal Study on Aging (KLOSA), for most sociodemographic, social integrative and health variables with adaptation to the Thai specific context, and the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), as well as a longitudinal, nationally representative community-dwelling population. The fact that all data were evaluated using self-report is one study drawback; objective measurements must be incorporated into future evaluations as well. Although single-item measures of life satisfaction have been found valid [30, 31], future research should include multi-item measures to better capture the complexity of this phenomenon. Future studies would also wish to examine the impact on mental health of the respondent’s children’s (if any) closeness to their place of residence [13, 42]. Although efforts have been made to control for a number of confounders, reverse causality cannot be ruled out.

Conclusions

Overall, this study showed that advantages in socioeconomic situation (higher education, higher economic status) and advantages in health (better self-rated physical health status, less depressive symptoms, less loneliness, and chewing ability) and health behaviours (physical activity, wearing dentures, and participation in annual health check-up) were the determinants of life satisfaction among older adults living alone. Furthermore, disadvantages in socioeconomic situation (currently working, and rural residence) and disadvantages in health (poorer self-rated physical health status, loneliness, and functional limitations) and unhealthy behaviours (physical inactivity, not wearing dentures) were the determinants of depressive symptoms among older adults living alone. Efforts to promote well-being among older adults living alone should reduce or promote the identified associated factors.
