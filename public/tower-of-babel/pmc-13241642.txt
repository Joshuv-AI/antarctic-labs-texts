Massive ascites and adnexal masses mimicking malignancy: A case report of Chlamydia trachomatis infection diagnosed by metagenomic next-generation sequencing
Authors: Yunlu Liu, Hong Xie, Zhenkun Song, Miaoyu Huang, Minge Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis) is the most commonly reported bacterial sexually transmitted infection among sexually active women. Although often asymptomatic or associated with non-specific clinical manifestations, it can cause inflammatory exudates and encapsulated fluid collections that are similar to adnexal masses on imaging. In rare cases, it may also present with massive ascites, a constellation of findings that may mimic ovarian malignancy.CaseWe report the case of a 32-year-old female with a nearly 3-month history of abdominal pain, adnexal masses and massive ascites. The initial workup did not reveal obvious evidence of infection and cytological examination showed no malignant cells. However, advanced gynecological originated cancer could not be fully excluded. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), which detected C. trachomatis in ascitic fluid, facilitated the diagnosis. The patient showed satisfactory clinical improvement following doxycycline treatment.ConclusionFor young, sexually active women presenting with unexplained ascites and adnexal masses, after excluding malignancy and common infectious diseases such as tuberculosis, C. trachomatis infection should be considered. Modern etiological detection methods, such as mNGS, can be employed to facilitate the diagnosis.Keywords: Chlamydia trachomatis, Ascites, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Pelvic inflammatory disease

Introduction

Chlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis) is the most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection (STI) worldwide, accounting for an estimated 128 million new cases annually among individuals aged 15–49 years, with studies in China reporting a particularly high prevalence ranging from 4.7% to 10.2% [1], [2]. C. trachomatis infection can cause multiple sequelae in women, the most serious of which are pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, and infertility [3]. However, infected people are frequently asymptomatic and are not aware of their infection. Chlamydia screening programs have been demonstrated to reduce PID rates among women [4], [5]. Though rare, C. trachomatis has been found to cause significant ascites, pelvic masses and elevated tumor marker making it easily confused with ovarian cancer and influencing clinical judgement [6], [7]. Thus, it is key for clinicians to understand the clinical manifestations of this disease and to be more vigilant in testing for and making the diagnosis of C. trachomatis in high-risk patients. We report such a case in a young woman, in which metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of ascites fluid established the diagnosis and obviated unnecessary surgery.

Case

Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report. A 32-year-old G3P2A1 (gravida 3, para 2, abortion 1) woman was referred to our hospital’s gastroenterology department for recurrent abdominal pain, and massive ascites. Two months before admission, she began experiencing severe, persistent right upper quadrant colic pain during menstruation, without fever, nausea, or vomiting, which prompted her to present to a local hospital. Blood test at local hospital showed the HGB was 81 g/L, Neutrophil percentage was 83.3% and the white blood cell count was normal. There was no positive ultrasound finding. She received intravenous ceftazidime and metronidazole for five days with significant symptomatic improvement. However, she fell intermittent right upper quadrant dull pain shortly after the treatment. A subsequent computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis was conducted which found diffuse peritonitis and bilateral physiological ovarian cysts. Another course of antibiotics (intravenous piperacillin for five days, followed by oral cefuroxime axetil for one week) provided transient relief. However, she noticed abdominal distension that has been gradually worsening, accompanied by mild bloating. One month later, she returned for another medical visit. Ultrasound examination found moderate ascites, and intestinal obstruction and related issues were excluded by abdominal X-ray, prompting referral to our hospital for further evaluation.

She was sexually active. Her obstetric and gynecologic history was significant for one non‑elective cesarean section and one abortion. Her menstrual history was unremarkable, with no dysmenorrhea. Glucose‑6‑phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency was diagnosed during her previous pregnancy. Her medical history was otherwise unremarkable.

On presentation, she appeared anemic but was in good general condition. Her abdominal circumference was 86 cm. Her abdomen was soft but fluid wave thrill is suspiciously positive, and shifting dullness is present. No abdominal mass was palpable. There was no tenderness or rebound tenderness over the entire abdomen.

Laboratory findings revealed moderate anemia (hemoglobin: 67 g/L) and mild hypoproteinemia (serum albumin: 34 g/L). The anemia was characterized as iron deficiency, attributed to decreased serum ferritin and iron levels (3.8 ug/L, and 2.6μmol/L respectively), along with an elevated unsaturated iron-binding capacity (57.9 umol/L). The serum tumor marker CA125 was elevated (119 U/mL) while the other tumor markers were within normal limits. White blood cell and platelet counts, hepatic and renal function, electrolytes, and coagulation parameters were also within normal ranges. Blood tests for syphilis, HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C were all negative. On the second day after admission, ultrasound-guided ascites drainage with catheter placement was performed, with approximately 1000 mL of ascitic fluid drained daily. From the sixth day of hospitalization, the ascites volume began to decrease spontaneously. Ascites analysis revealed 1640 nucleated cells with a mononuclear predominance (57.5%), a positive Rivalta test, and a serum-ascites total protein ratio of 1.1. Cytologic examination revealed no malignant cells. Tests for Mycobacterium tuberculosis antibody, interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), and PPD test were all negative. Cultures of the ascitic fluid for other acid-fast bacilli (AFB), bacteria, and fungi were also negative. CT of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated massive ascites and bilateral mixed-density adnexal masses, predominantly on the left, with the largest measuring approximately 60 × 27 mm and exhibiting partial enhancement (Fig. 1). Additional findings included massive ascites, the absence of significant lymphadenopathy, and no identifiable hepatobiliary or gastrointestinal mass. Chest imaging was noncontributory. No significant abnormalities were detected on gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Abdominal and pelvic CT findings, (a) Massive perihepatic ascites labelled by arrow. (b, c) Bilateral adnexal masses labelled by arrows.

While findings were not consistent with a gastrointestinal malignancy or common infectious etiologies like tuberculosis, the presence of bilateral adnexal masses on CT necessitated a gynecological consultation. Pelvic examination revealed bilateral, cystic adnexal masses, each measuring approximately 4–5 cm, without associated tenderness, cervical motion tenderness, or abnormal vaginal discharge. While the presentation was atypical for advanced cancer, the severe ascites with negative routine microbiology was still concerning for a gynecological malignant process. Therefore, the patient was transferred to the gynecology department for further work-up and potential diagnostic laparoscopy. A subsequent, more comprehensive ultrasound examination was conducted by an senior gynecological radiologist. The examination identified bilateral hypoechoic adnexal masses, each measuring 5–6 cm in greatest dimension, which were hypervascular on Doppler signal (Fig. 2). Furthermore, an encapsulated fluid collection was noted in the left adnexal region. Cultures of cervical secretions for bacterial and fungal pathogens, along with Sexually Transmitted Disease (STD) testing for C. trachomatis antigen, Mycoplasma and Neisseria gonorrhoeae were all negative.

Gynecological ultrasound findings, (a, b) Bilateral sausage-shaped hypoechoic adnexal masses, measuring approximately 63 × 23 mm on the right and 56 × 25 mm on the left, with rigid morphology and abundant intralesional vascularity on Doppler imaging. (c) Free fluid in the pelvic cavity, with a maximum depth of 47 mm. (d) Adhesion of the left ovary to the sausage-shaped hypoechoic mass, with surrounding loculated fluid collection measuring approximately 43 × 12 mm, containing multiple cord-like hyperechoic strands.

Prior to proceeding with invasive laparoscopy, and in an effort to exhaust all diagnostic avenues given the persistent inflammatory profile, ascitic fluid was sent for mNGS to evaluate for uncommon pathogens. The result was unexpected and diagnostic. The mNGS report returned strongly positive for C. trachomatis, with a high sequence count of 579 and a relative abundance of 95.82%. No other pathogenic bacteria, viruses, parasites, or fungal elements were detected. The clinical picture was immediately reinterpreted as a systemic manifestation of C. trachomatis infection with PID, secondary inflammatory ascites and encapsulated fluid collection in the adnexal region.

Treatment

She was treated with oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily. One week after initiating therapy, a follow-up sonographic reevaluation demonstrated a reduction in both the size and Doppler vascular signals of the adnexal masses. She was subsequently discharged home with a prescription for an additional two-week course of doxycycline.

Follow-up

Laboratory findings showed improved anemia condition (hemoglobin: 90 g/L) and near-normal serum CA125 (24 U/mL) when she visited gynecological clinic for follow-up 24 days after therapy. Sonography revealed further improvement, with bilateral adnexal masses measuring 42 × 15 mm on the left and 30 × 11 mm on the right. The maximum depth of the ascites was 28 mm. Forty-eight days after initiating therapy, the patient's general condition had returned to normal, and her abdominal circumference had normalized. She reported no further abdominal pain or bloating. Laboratory tests revealed a hemoglobin level of 97 g/L and a normal serum CA-125 level.

Discussion

We report a rare case of C. trachomatis-associated ascites in a young woman, a condition that initially mimicked malignancy but was ultimately unraveled through a multidisciplinary diagnostic approach. Treatment with doxycycline resulted in resolution of ascites and adnexal mass.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of C. trachomatis infection presenting with moderate anemia that improved significantly following anti-infective therapy, suggesting that the anemia was likely attributable to inflammatory consumption. In patients with advanced malignancy, anemia is also commonly observed but typically exhibits a progressive downward trend and is rarely reversible with conventional treatment.

The early clinical manifestations of C. trachomatis infection often lack specificity. It may be associated with perihepatitis recognized as Fitz-Hugh-Curtis Syndrome (FHCS) [8], [9], and "upper abdominal pain" can be a primary symptom at the onset of the illness. Ascending infection can lead to PID, and in rare instances, may result in extensive inflammatory exudation and massive ascites [7], [10]. Severe adhesive disease may lead to the formation of inclusion cysts in the pelvis that may mimic an ovarian neoplasm [11]. An increased CA-125 tumor marker level could be attributed to peritoneal irritation. Thus, it may be easily misdiagnosed as malignant ascites or tuberculous peritonitis. Several case reports have documented similar scenarios in which C. trachomatis infection mimicked ovarian cancer or carcinomatous peritonitis [11], [12], [13]. This pitfall underscores the need for a broad differential diagnosis in young women with unexplained ascites.

Upon admission to our hospital, the 32-year-old was initially suspected to have gastrointestinal disease, and the possibility of advanced ovarian cancer could not be excluded. Based on further workup, benign conditions were still considered to be the most likely. Therefore, we did not carry out diagnostic laparoscopy immediately. First, the patient's general condition remained relatively stable during the nearly 3 months from the onset of symptoms to diagnosis, with no evidence of disease progression, new-onset hydrothorax, or severe hypoproteinemia, findings that argue against an advanced malignancy. Secondly, considering her young age within the reproductive years, benign conditions was more likely to be the cause rather than malignancies. Thirdly, mononuclear predominant cells were detected on cytological examination of the ascitic fluid instead of malignant cells, a finding that would be atypical in the context of massive malignant ascites. In addition, the ascites displayed a self-limiting tendency. Even thought conventional etiological examinations all yielded negative results, Ascites examination using mNGS suggested C. trachomatis infection, confirming the presence of the pathogen, without any invasive examinations. The lack of detectable malignancy, combined with the positive mNGS result, led to the diagnosis of C. trachomatis-associated PID and ascites. The decision to pursue mNGS prior to laparoscopy proved pivotal, as it not only established the diagnosis noninvasively but also spared the patient from unnecessary surgical intervention.

Both direct and indirect techniques are used in diagnostic procedures to find C. trachomatis infections. Assays for direct pathogen identification, such as culture, antigen tests, nucleic acid hybridization, and amplification tests, were often used to assess localized infections. Indirect techniques rely on the identification of antibodies against C. trachomatis [14]. Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), primarily polymerase chain reaction (PCR), are considered the most accurate method. Antigen testing, which was used in this case, has clinical value due to its lower cost and simpler technical requirements, despite its relatively lower sensitivity compared with PCR. PCR testing of the ascitic fluid was not further performed in this case, as the initial cervical swab had tested negative for C. trachomatis Antigen. The negative cervical antigen result in this case deserves further explanation, as several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, the patient had received two courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics prior to admission, which may have suppressed cervical shedding without eradicating the pelvic infection. Second, C. trachomatis can cause localized upper genital tract or peritoneal infection without sustained detectable cervical colonization. Third, antigen testing from cervical swabs may provide false negative results, particularly in chronic or partially treated infections. Studies have also found inconsistencies between upper and lower genital tract testing for C. trachomatis. The reported inconsistency varies across studies, likely due to differences in testing methods and sample types. A previous study involving 60 women with pelvic pain found that, among the 11 who tested positive for C. trachomatis antigen in peritoneal fluid from the pouch of Douglas obtained during laparoscopy, 10 had negative endocervical swabs (10/11) [15]. In another study, among 32 women who tested negative for C. trachomatis on preoperative cervical, vaginal, and urethral smears, 14 were found to have a positive result in fallopian tube samples obtained by laparoscopy [16]. Furthermore, a study enrolled 40 women with ectopic pregnancy and simultaneously tested for C. trachomatis in cervical, endometrial, and fallopian tube specimens. Although all 40 cervical swabs tested negative, the positive rate in the upper genital tract (endometrium or fallopian tube) was 12.5% [17].

The diagnostic breakthrough in this case came from mNGS of the ascitic fluid, which was performed for broad-spectrum pathogen screening including uncommon ones. mNGS allows for rapid, noninvasive pathogen detection within 24 h using easily accessible specimens. This makes it particularly well-suited for patients with suspected infections in whom conventional testing has been unrevealing. In this case, mNGS of ascitic fluid was particularly valuable because cervical swab testing for chlamydia antigen had been negative—a finding that does not exclude upper genital tract or peritoneal infection. mNGS bypasses this limitation by directly sampling the site of pathology. Although diagnostic laparoscopy, resection or biopsy serves as gold standard for diagnosis, this procedure presents several drawbacks, including operative trauma, complications and higher medical costs. Furthermore, surgical intervention is unnecessary for certain pathogens, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis or Chlamydia trachomatis, which respond well to antimicrobial therapy.

In conclusion, in young, sexually active women presenting with unexplained ascites with a mononuclear predominance, particularly when associated with adnexal findings and elevated CA125, C. trachomatis infection should be considered, even in the absence of classic symptoms of pelvic inflammatory disease, provided that malignancy has been excluded. The standard treatment is doxycycline. Based on previous cases, inadequate dosing may lead to disease recurrence. Therefore, a 2–3 week course would be more appropriate. mNGS can serve as an effective modern diagnostic tool in such clinical scenarios.
