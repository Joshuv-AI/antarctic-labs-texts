Post-glitch Recovery and the Neutron Star Structure: The Vela Pulsar
Himanshu Grover, Erbil Gügercinoğlu, Bhal Chandra Joshi, M.A. Krishnakumar, Shantanu Desai, P. Arumugam, Debades Bandyopadhyay

Abstract
Pulsar glitches, sudden changes in a neutron star’s rotation, offer a unique window into the extreme physics governing these celestial bodies. The post-glitch recovery phase, characterized by a gradual recovery, reveals insights into the superfluid dynamics within the star. The post-glitch recovery phase probes the coupling mechanisms between different stellar components, sheds light on the properties of neutron star matter at supra-nuclear densities and even allows us to predict the time to the next glitch. In this work, we present a detailed analysis of the Vela pulsar’s rotational behavior using around 100 months of observational data spanning from September 2016 to January 2025, during which four glitches were identified. Here, we demonstrate the post-glitch recovery of these glitches within the framework of the vortex creep model, providing new insights into the Vela pulsar’s internal structure. Notably, we present the first-ever investigation of vortex residuals – the discrepancy between observed values and those predicted by the vortex creep model – through the lens of the vortex bending model, marking a significant step forward in understanding neutron star physics through pulsar glitches.



I
Introduction

Pulsars are highly magnetized rotating dense and compact stars observed to have periodic radiation of pulses with the periodicity tied to its remarkably stable rotation. The slow-down in the rotation of a pulsar is influenced by two types of instabilities: glitches and timing noise
(
D’alessandro 1996
;
Haskell and Melatos 2015
;
Zhou et al. 2022
)
. Glitches are abrupt changes in the rotational frequency and spin-down rate of a pulsar, while timing noise is a stochastic wander in the spin parameters of pulsars. The occurrence of glitches, as well as their slow relaxations ranging from a few days to several years, reveal the presence of neutron superfluidity inside the inner crust of the neutron star. The most successful mechanism explaining glitches and their recoveries is based on the superfluid vortex pinning and creep model
(
Anderson and Itoh 1975
;
Alpar et al. 1984a
;
Alpar et al. 1984b
)
. This model suggests that neutron superfluidity in the interior of the star leads to a differential rotation, storing a significant amount of angular momentum. When this angular momentum is released, the crust spins up, resulting in a glitch. Pulsar glitches thus provide valuable insights into the superfluid dynamics within the star
(
Zhou et al. 2022
)
.

The post-glitch recovery is the process in which the pulsar gradually relaxes back towards its original pre-glitch rotational state. According to the vortex creep model, this process is driven by the repinning of superfluid vortices to the crustal nuclei and the re-establishment of the equilibrium lag between the rotational rates of the superfluid interior and the crust. Hence, the vortex dynamics play a crucial role in determining the recovery behavior. The post-glitch relaxation typically involves the contribution of angular momentum transfer from the neutron star interior to the surface, seen as an exponential recovery followed by a linear decay in the spin-down rate. Occasionally, some inward movement of vortices may also play a role
(
Akbal et al. 2015
)
where crustquakes initiate glitches. The linear type of relaxation in time originates from the tangling or bending of the vortices in the non-linear creep regions
(
Gügercinoğlu et al. 2023
)
. The post-glitch recovery can be utilized to probe the internal dynamics and structure of neutron stars
(
Gügercinoğlu et al. 2022
)
.

PSR J0835–4510, B0833–45 or the Vela pulsar, has shown 26 glitches, most of which are large glitches and detected after a regular, quasi-periodic inter-glitch time. The Vela pulsar is one of the most studied glitching pulsars. The first-ever glitch detection was recorded in the Vela pulsar
(
Radhakrishnan and Manchester 1969
;
Reichley and Downs 1969
)
. Moreover, the first-ever glitch detection caught in the act was also realized for the Vela pulsar
(
Palfreyman et al. 2018
)
.
The Vela pulsar is titled the most popular or most studied glitching pulsar among other glitching pulsars for various reasons: it is a bright source, young, and displays glitches at regular intervals. The recoveries of glitches also demonstrate strikingly similar behavior. A measure of a pulsar’s slow down over time is described by the braking index (
n
). It is defined by a power-law relation between the pulsar’s spin-down rate
\dot{\Omega}
and its rotational angular frequency (
\Omega
), given by
\dot{\Omega}=k\Omega^{n}
. The braking index provides insights into the pulsar’s rotational evolution, magnetic fields, and emission mechanisms.

Here, we present the observational behavior of the Vela pulsar from September 2016 to January 2025; during this period, four large glitches were observed in our monitoring campaign
(
Grover et al. 2024a
;
Basu et al. 2020
)
. Theoretical inferences from the post-glitch recovery phase are also presented. We have utilized the recovery behavior of four glitches in the Vela pulsar to estimate the following parameters: (a) the fractional moments of inertia (FMI) of various layers participating in a glitch, (b) the re-coupling timescale of the crustal superfluid, (c) theoretical prediction for the time to the next glitch, (d) braking index of the Vela pulsar. The paper’s outline is as follows: we describe the pulsar observations, the data reduction and the analysis processes in section
II
. A brief description of the vortex creep and bending models is given in Section
III
. In Section
IV
, we discuss the results. The conclusions and future work are given in Section
V
.

The rotational evolution of a pulsar during a glitch can be expressed as a Taylor series expansion,

\displaystyle\nu(t)=

\displaystyle\nu_{0}+\dot{\nu}_{0}(t-t_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\ddot{\nu}_{0}(t-t_{0})^{2}+\dots

(1)

where
\nu_{0}
is the spin frequency, and
\dot{\nu}_{0}
and
\ddot{\nu}_{0}
are the first and second derivatives of the frequency, respectively, at reference epoch
t_{0}
. The spin evolution of the Vela pulsar from September 2016 to January 2025 obtained after precision timing is given in Fig.
1
. Each measurement is made with the local timing of several epochs, typically within a 50-200 day data window, contingent on data availability. Exceptions include the first and last few points adjacent to glitches. The resulting measurements are reflected in the precision of error bars, which are smaller than the symbol used in the figure. By choosing multi-epoch windows, we achieve reliable rotational estimates with minimized error bars, providing a robust foundation for studying vortex residuals. This approach effectively averages out temporal effects caused by physical processes, such as ionized interstellar medium scattering-induced broadening, thereby validating the accuracy of measurements aligned with the star’s rotation and internal dynamics.

For each recovery, we utilized Bayesian statistics for model comparison and parameter estimation. Bayesian statistics uses Bayes’ theorem to enhance the probability of the hypothesis based on new evidence or data. For model comparison, the standard
Jeffreys 1961
scale is utilized, which suggests the preferred model based on the strength of evidence
(
Trotta 2008
)
. Bayesian inference is also used to predict the epoch of the next glitch using data from previous glitches.

The priors are selected based on two criteria: initial estimates from the weighted least-square fit and reasonable ranges for each parameter using literature knowledge. These reasonable ranges can be fixed for some parameters, like the fractional moment of inertia (typically positive and below 1) and may vary for others like the recoupling timescale, usually depends whether it is a 3 or 2 or 1 exponential component model. For the linear relaxation region, the prior for recoupling timescale is 10-300 days, and the offset time is 300 to 1200 or more for some recoveries with the initial guess as given by the weighted least-square technique. All the priors were considered as uniform priors. Since all the recoveries are unique hence this flexible approach to setting priors is necessary. The Gaussian likelihood is considered for the analysis. The MCMC Hammer: emcee
(
Foreman-Mackey et al. 2013
)
ensemble sampler is used for both parameter estimation and model comparison.

The post-glitch recoveries are studied within the framework of the vortex creep model and the vortex bending model
(
Gügercinoğlu et al. 2023
)
. We first model the frequency derivative measurements from the data using the vortex creep model. Then, we estimate the residuals obtained after subtracting the predicted value by the vortex creep model from the measured values. These residuals were investigated within the framework of the vortex bending model.
The generalized Lomb-Scargle periodogram technique
(
Lomb 1976
;
Scargle 1982
;
VanderPlas 2018
)
was employed to detect periodicities in the residuals. Peaks with false alarm probabilities (FAP) below 5% or Z-score above 3
\sigma
were considered significant for vortex bending studies. FAP estimation was performed using the Astropy library
(
Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013
)
, which implements the
Baluev 2008
approach. Alternative methods, including approximations based on effective independent frequencies and bootstrap resampling, yielded similar results. The Z-score significance is obtained from the tail of the one-sided Gaussian distribution corresponding to the observed FAP
(
Cowan et al. 2011
;
Ganguly and Desai 2017
)
. The formalism of the vortex creep and bending models is described in the next section.

III
The Vortex Creep and Bending Models

The Vortex Creep model describes the occurrence of glitches and the post-glitch recovery as a byproduct of the superfluid present inside the star. The important features of vortex creep for this paper are now described.

In the neutron star crust, vortex lines coexist with lattice nuclei, and it is energetically favorable for the vortices to pin to the crystal lattice nuclei. The distribution of vortices is non-uniform due to crustal inhomogeneities such as dislocations, cracks, and impurities formed after quakes. This results in formation of structures such that regions of high vortex density are surrounded by areas with vortex depletion regions collectively referred to as vortex traps
(
Cheng et al. 1988
)
. Glitches may arise due to collective vortex unpinning events induced in these vortex traps when the angular velocity lag between the superfluid and the crustal normal matter exceeds a threshold value.

After a glitch, the steady-state lag decreases, causing the cessation of creep in certain superfluid regions. The post-glitch recovery process involves the restoration of the creep until the steady-state conditions are re-established by the external braking torque. The vortex creep model describes the formation, recovery and other phenomena related to glitches as follows
(
Alpar et al. 1984a
;
Gügercinoğlu and Alpar 2020
;
Gügercinoğlu et al. 2022
)
:

•

A crustquake initiates the formation of vortex traps and triggers a vortex unpinning avalanche. The unpinned vortices transfer their excess angular momentum to the crust, causing it to spin up, which is observed as a glitch.

•

Superfluid regions in which unpinning occurs decouple from the external decelerating torque, resulting in a step increase in the spin-down rate.

•

Soon after the large-scale vortex unpinning avalanche, the excess angular momentum of the crustal superfluid is first transferred to the normal matter nuclei and electrons in the crust. This transfer causes a peak in the crustal rotation rate following a glitch.

•

This change in the crustal rotational state is then communicated to the core superfluid via electrons scattering off magnetized vortex lines
(
Alpar et al. 1984b
)
. Consequently, the initial peak in the observed crustal rotation rate relaxes on the crust-core coupling time scale, eventually reaching the post-glitch equilibrium rotation rate.

•

A peak in the rotation rate of the normal component will occur if the coupling between the crustal superfluid and the normal matter is stronger than the coupling between the crustal superfluid and the core superfluid
(
Pizzochero et al. 2020
)
.

•

The slow post-glitch evolution is the response of the crustal superfluid to the glitch-induced changes. On longer time scales, the core superfluid reaches equilibrium with the normal matter in the crust, and they behave as a single component. Consequently, the only component decoupled from the braking torque is the crustal superfluid.

•

Delayed spin-ups have been observed in the Crab pulsar’s glitches
(
Wong et al. 2001
;
Shaw et al. 2018
;
Basu et al. 2020
;
Ge et al. 2020
)
. Such events occur whenever vortex unpinning events also include an inward vortex motion due to platelet movements
(
Gügercinoğlu and Alpar 2019
)
.

•

The crust breaking can produce transient magnetospheric signatures that coincide with the glitch, such as changes in pulse shape, polarization, and emission. These changes are linked to the plastic motion of the magnetic field lines anchored to the fractured crustal platelets.

•

Damped sinusoidal-like oscillations in the spin-down rate can appear due to the bending of the vortex creep lines in the linear creep relaxation region, which is mainly generated by the shocks from the respective glitch and can also have an impact from the previous glitches
(
Gügercinoğlu et al. 2023
)
.

The post-glitch spin-down rate behavior mirrors the responses of the vortex creep regions in both linear and non-linear regimes to a glitch, with additional involvement of induced inward vortex motion in delayed spin-up events. In certain regions of the superfluid in the inner crust and the outer core, vortex creep exhibits a linear response to glitch-induced changes, leading to exponential relaxation
(
Alpar et al. 1989
;
Flanagan 1990
;
Gügercinoğlu 2017
)
as:

\Delta\dot{\nu}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}-\frac{I_{\text{ei}}}{I_{\text{c}}}\frac{\Delta\nu}{\tau_{\text{ei}}}e^{-t/\penalty\tau_{\text{ei}}}.

An external effect like a crustquake can add an inward vortex motion. This increases the superfluid rotation rate as the vortices pinned to the broken plate are induced to move towards the rotation axis along with the plate. When vortices move inward, the superfluid rotates faster. The lag increases from its steady-state value, making the creep more efficient than in steady state and resulting in an enhanced vortex creep current in the radially outward direction
(
Gügercinoğlu and Alpar 2019

5

•

Model 1: Only exponential recovery given by equation
2
.

–

Model 1a: Exponential recovery with only 1 exponential component.

–

Model 1b: Exponential recovery with 2 exponential components.

–

Model 1c: Exponential recovery with 3 exponential components.

•

Model 2: Only linear relaxation described by equation
5
.

•

Model 3: Exponential and linear recoveries, the sum of equations
2
and
5
.

–

Model 3a: The sum of linear recovery and exponential recovery with only 1 exponential component.

–

Model 3b: The sum of linear recovery and exponential recovery with 2 exponential components.

–

Model 3c: The sum of linear recovery and exponential recovery with 3 exponential components.

The evidence for these different alternatives was evaluated using Bayesian analysis of our post-glitch recovery measurements of the Vela pulsar to select the suitable model and estimate its parameters.

Apart from long-term post-glitch recovery, we see evidence for sinusoidal-like spin-down rate oscillations over and above the recovery predicted by the vortex creep model as described in Section
IV

(

and appear to be a common feature following glitches, as also observed from PSRs J1522–5735
(

)

.

•

Hypothesis II – Damped model consisting of a sinusoidal term and a damping factor,

•

The evidence for these hypotheses was also evaluated using Bayesian analysis to select the most suitable model and calculate its parameters. The bending of vortices remains a relatively unexplored domain, yet it can significantly impact simulations of neutron star glitches. Incorporating oscillation parameters associated with vortex bending, as reported in the next section, may provide a more comprehensive understanding of glitch behavior and boost the hydrodynamic simulation of the superfluid vortices
(
Howitt et al. 2020

Anantharaman and Bhattacharya 2025

IV
Results and Discussions

Our pulsar monitoring program with the ORT and the uGMRT started in 2015. The previous results were reported in
Basu et al. 2020
and
Grover et al. 2024a
. In this work, we present analysis of a new Vela glitch at MJD 60429.9. Further, we demonstrate the spin-down rate evolution for the Vela pulsar since the beginning of the monitoring program. Our primary focus is to provide the theoretical interpretation of glitches using the post-glitch recovery phase and to obtain measurements
for the re-coupling timescales and fractional moment of inertia for various layers that participate in a glitch. We have estimated the theoretical time to the next glitch through the vortex creep model and used Bayesian statistics to predict the time for the next glitch. All the predictions match well with the observed inter-glitch times.

IV.3
Correlations and Braking Index

To date, the Vela pulsar has exhibited 26 glitches, with 21 of them being with large magnitudes exceeding 10
-8
. Fig
11
presents these 21 events, revealing a pattern where glitch magnitudes exhibit an alternating increasing and decreasing trend: if glitch A has a lower magnitude, the successive glitch is observed with a higher magnitude, and vice versa. This trend is particularly evident in the last 10 glitches (or after MJD 50000).

Figure 11
:
Glitch amplitude vs. the detection epoch (in MJD).

We also utilized the data of these 21 events to investigate potential correlations between the glitch amplitude and the inter-glitch time. Most correlations were not statistically significant except a correlation between glitch amplitude and subsequent inter-glitch time, shown in the Fig
12
. Specifically, the amplitude of a glitch (e.g., glitch A) showed a positive correlation with the inter-glitch time following it (i.e., the time difference between glitch A+1 and glitch A). The correlation coefficients were: Pearson’s = 0.6582 (P–value = 0.0022), Spearman’s = 0.6266 (P–value = 0.0041), and Kendall’s = 0.4692 (P–value = 0.0051), indicating moderate to strong positive correlation. Such a tight correlation has only been found for PSR J0537–6910
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
.

Figure 12

We measured the Vela pulsar’s braking index by estimating the frequency second derivative through linear fitting of frequency first derivative measurements just before glitch epochs, yielding a braking index value of 2.94
\pm
0.55. As a caution, it may be noted that our estimate assumes almost a full recovery with stable values of the first and second spin derivatives. This value is expected to become more constrained with future observations as this would increase glitch numbers or dataset coverage. It is close to the prediction of the model that invokes the magnetic dipole radiation in a plasma-filled magnetosphere (e.g.
Ekşi et al. 2016
). Previous attempts to estimate the braking index for the Vela pulsar by different studies yielded qualitatively different results: a value of 1.4
\pm
0.2
(
Lyne et al. 2010
)
, 1.7
\pm
0.2
(
Espinoza et al. 2017
)
and 2.81
\pm
0.12
(
Akbal et al. 2017
)
, respectively. The values in each case were different potentially attributed to differences in techniques used to estimate spin parameters and their derivatives and assumptions.

V
Conclusion and Future Prospects

The investigation of post-glitch recovery offers valuable insights into neutron star internal structure, which is not fully understood yet, and illuminates the underlying mechanisms driving these sudden spin-up events. Focusing on the Vela pulsar’s last four glitches, spanning from MJD 57651 to 60700 (
\sim
100 months of observations), we employed the vortex creep model to extract key neutron star parameters. Bayesian analysis demonstrated that the recovery of the Vela glitches is well described by a hybrid model combining exponential and linear relaxations. Additionally, Bayesian inference closely predicted the time to the next glitch for all the glitches and suggested that the upcoming glitch is likely to occur around MJD 61377.7 (December 3, 2026), with a 68% credible interval spanning from MJD 61249 to 61506. The vortex creep model provided an excellent fit for the observed recoveries.

Furthermore, we analyzed the vortex residuals (the difference between the vortex creep model predictions and the measured spin-down rate) within the vortex bending model framework, conducting the first study of this kind for the Vela pulsar. Our analysis confirms damped quasi-periodic oscillations and yields precise determinations of the oscillatory parameters. Intriguingly, we also see an unexplained feature in the vortex residuals just preceding the subsequent glitch. These findings provide a foundational understanding of vortex residuals and motivate further enhancements in future both in theory and in similar follow-up investigations. Moreover, the measured parameters can improve the precision of hydrodynamical simulations of superfluid vortices, allowing for more accurate modeling and deeper insights into the neutron star’s dynamics and structural properties.

We explored several correlations and our analysis revealed a positive correlation between the glitch amplitude and the subsequent inter-glitch time, suggesting a potential link between the magnitude of glitches and the time elapsed before the next occurrence. Furthermore, we estimated the pulsar’s braking index as 2.94
\pm
0.55. As more glitches are observed, this estimate is likely to become more precise, yielding a more refined understanding of the pulsar’s rotational dynamics and offering deeper insights into its internal structure, magnetospheric conditions and evolutionary history.

Future research includes investigating the post-glitch recovery and internal structure of other notable glitching pulsars, such as the Crab and PSR J1740-3015. To streamline the process, we are developing automated rotational parameter estimation scripts that perform the timing analysis quickly, enabling rapid model investigation and facilitating timely results. Additionally, the measured recovery parameters provide a unique approach to constrain the neutron star equation of state, which will be investigated in a subsequent work in progress. Furthermore, determining the internal and surface temperatures of neutron stars and exploring correlations with glitch observables could yield valuable insights. Lastly, constraining internal magnetic field configurations will also be a key area of investigation, ultimately advancing our understanding of neutron star physics and glitch mechanisms.
