Sustainable ammonia synthesis from nitrate wastewater via graphdiyne Mo–Cu–C≡C interfaces
Authors: Zhaoyang Chen, Shuya Zhao, Qian Xiao, Yue Tian, Xiaofeng Lu, Yurui Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical green ammonia (NH3) production using renewable electricity is sustainable but suffers from selectivity-activity-stability trade-off under industrial conditions. Here we report an atomic interface engineering strategy enabling in-situ assembly and coupling of hexaethynylbenzenes on molybdenum-copper oxidesto form an sp-hybridized Mo/Cu-C≡C heterointerface. It features dual d-orbital hybridization (Mo 4d-C 2p and Cu 3d-C 2p) weakening N–O bond by 36.85% and lowering activation barrier by 0.43 eV, reversible electron-buffer facilitating proton-coupled electron transfer, and self-regulated charge compensation between metal atoms and -C≡C- atomic wires, collectively endowing high activity and near-complete hydrogen evolution reaction suppression. The resulting catalyst achieves high NH3 yielding rate (YNH3, 2.45 mmol h−1 cm−2) and Faradaic efficiency (~100%) under ambient conditions. A prototype flow electrolyzer operating with this catalyst sustains industrial-current densities of 500 mA cm−2 for 300 hours with <3% activity decay, yielding potable water from nitrate wastewater. The membrane electrode assembly (MEA) achieves 380 h stable operation at the same current density with YNH3 of 3.64 mmol h−1 cm−2.

Introduction

The electrification of the nitrogen cycle represents a dual-benefit strategy for ammonia (NH3) synthesis, aiming to decarbonize its production while remediating aquatic nitrogen pollution−two pressing challenges aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) on clean water (Goal 6) and climate action (Goal 13)1–3. Although the conventional Haber-Bosch process dominates global NH3 supply4, it accounts for ~1.44% global CO2 emissions and 1–2% of worldwide energy consumption due to the severe operating conditions (150–300 atm, 400–500 °C), contrast with the growing demand for green NH3 as fertilizers and hydrogen carriers5–7. Electrocatalytic nitrate-to-ammonia conversion (ENtAC) has recently emerged as a promising alternative, which not only valorizes nitrate-contaminated wastewater but also uses water as the proton source under mild conditions. ENtAC benefits from the higher aqueous solubility of nitrate (1 M for NO3− vs. 0.02 mM for N2 in H2O), a lower activation energy (N=O vs. N≡N: 204 vs. 941 kJ mol−1), alongside the potential mitigate eutrophication8–12.

However, practical implementation of ENtAC remains challenging due to a fundamental selectivity-efficiency trade-off, particularly at industrial current densities (>500 mA cm−2). Under such conditions, catalyst reconstruction and cumulative competition from the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) become severe–Faradaic efficiency often drops to below 50%11,13–15, and these issues are exacerbated in real wastewater containing competing anions (such as SO42−) that accelerate corrosion. Moreover, conventional electrocatalysts are fundamentally constrained by the scarcity of accessible active sites, leading to poor atom utilization efficiency. These challenges have motivated intensive efforts towards the precise engineering of active sites to achieve high selectivity, activity, and stability.

Theoretically, catalytic efficiency is closely correlated with the position of the d-band center relative to the Fermi level, which governs the adsorbate binding energies and thus determines activation barriers, reaction rates, and selectivity16,17. Ideal ENtAC electrocatalysts should enable precise d-band center modulation to facilitate N–O bond cleavage, multi-step protonation, and in situ charge replenishment while suppressing catalyst oxidation and HER. Such criteria call for new materials capable of simultaneously stabilizing active sites, ensuring efficient electron transport, and resisting corrosive environments.

Among reported materials, graphdiyne (GDY)18–20, a sp–sp2 co-hybridized two-dimensional carbon allotrope, provides a platform for atomic-level interface engineering. Its highly conjugated π-system and rich diyne bonds create an intrinsically uneven surface charge distribution21–23, which favors strong metal-support interactions and facilitates charge transfer24–26. More importantly, the triangular pores and out-of-plane sp-hybrid orbitals allow GDY to seize metal atoms and electronically couple with metal atoms, potentially enabling precise d-band tuning via the overlap of orbital electrons27–31. These inherent properties allow for the precise modulation of active site structures, optimizing the adsorption strength of intermediates and reducing the activation barriers32–34. Additionally, the dynamic charge self-buffering capability arising from the rich sp-carbon atoms enhances operational stability by countering overoxidation35–37.

Capitalizing on these attributes, we demonstrate the controlled synthesis of a Mo–Cu–C≡C atomic heterointerfaces via in situ assembly and coupling of hexaethynylbenzenes on MoCuOx surface (GDY-MCO). The formed sp-hybridized Mo/Cu–C≡C heterointerface induces dual orbital hybridization (Mo 4d-C 2p and Cu 3d-C 2p), which not only optimizes the reaction pathway for nitrate reduction but also serves as a dynamic electron buffer to maintain metal active sites in their favorable oxidation states. As a result, the GDY-MCO achieves high NH3 production rate and near-unity selectivity at room temperatures (25 °C) and ambient pressures (101 kPa). When integrated into a flow electrolyzer processing real agricultural runoff, the system continuously produces fertilizer-grade NH3, sustaining 500 mA cm−2 for 300 h with negligible decay, while purifying water to drinking standards. Besides, the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was established and operated over 380 h at 500 mA cm−2, with highest NH3 yield (3.64 mmol h−1 cm−2). This work showcases a practical technology for closing the nitrogen loop in a sustainable manner.

Results

Theoretical simulation of Mo/Cu–C≡C heterointerfaces

We began by employing density functional theory (DFT) calculations to probe the atomic and electronic structure of the proposed GDY-MCO interfaces. The established model reveals a well-defined interface characterized by dual d-orbital hybridization (Mo 4d-C 2p and Cu 3d-C 2p; Fig. 1a–c). This intimate contact suggests strong electronic coupling, which we hypothesized would critically influence the catalytic behavior. Charge density difference analysis (Fig. 1d) confirms significant electron redistribution across the interface, with an estimated charge depletion of 0.228 e−/Å2 at Mo/Cu sites and a corresponding accumulation of 0.079 e−/Å2 at the sp-hybridized acetylenic carbons of GDY (Supplementary Fig. 2). The electronic density of states (DOS) calculation reveals that the d-band centers of both Mo (dMo = −0.80 eV) and Cu (dCu = −2.09 eV) in GDY-MCO shift closer to the Fermi level compared to pristine MCO (dMo = −0.86 eV, dCu = −2.32 eV; Fig. 1f). This upshift is a direct consequence of the interfacial electronic modulation and typically optimizes the adsorption strength of reaction intermediates, the charge transfer ability and the reaction kinetics. As shown in Fig. 1g, the N–O bond in nitrate adsorbed on GDY-MCO is elongated, and crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP) analysis indicates a significant weakening of the N–O bond (Fig. 1h), indicating facilitated nitrate activation. Free-energy diagrams of the ENtAC pathway (Fig. 1i) demonstrate that GDY-MCO dramatically lowers the activation barrier for the nitrate reduction rate-determining step by 0.43 eV compared to MCO, and also enhances subsequent protonation steps. GDY-MCO has better favorability for nitrates activation and ammonia formation, with the process of NO3−-*NO3-*NO2−-*NO-*N-*NH2-NH3. The interfacial Mo/Cu–C≡C wires create a proton-electron transfer pathway, lowering the overall activation energy compared to bare MCO. This significant reduction in the energy barrier underscores the profound kinetic enhancement enabled by the tuned d-band centers. Moreover, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) barrier on GDY-MCO is substantially increased to 0.78 eV from −0.31 eV on MCO (Fig. 1j), effectively inhibiting the competing reaction and ensuring high ammonia selectivity (Fig. 1k). It should also be noted that the practical viability of GDY-MCO as an ENtAC catalyst still requires rigorous experimental validation, as idealized DFT models are inherently limited in replicating the true complexities of actual electrochemical conditions.

Fabrication and characterization of Mo/Cu–C≡C heterointerfaces

Guided by these theoretical predictions, we engineered the GDY-MCO heterointerface through a multi-step synthesis protocol designed to achieve atomic-level architecture control (Fig. 2a). The process initiates with (i) the spontaneous oxidation-growth of vertical Cu(OH)2 nanowires (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3) on Cu foam (Supplementary Fig. 4), serving as a scaffold; (ii) followed by the molybdenum ion-exchange alloying to transform Cu(OH)2 into MoCu(OH)x (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 5), (iii) the controlled thermal annealing (300 °C, Ar) to yield metastable MoCu oxides (MCO; Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 6), while preserving nanowire morphology; and (iv) the in situ self-assembly and cross-coupling of hexaethynylbenzene on MCO surfaces to form graphdiyne-MoCu heterointerfaces (GDY-MCO; Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 7). The corresponding evolution of structures from Cu(OH)2 to GDY-MCO is also confirmed by X-ray Diffraction (XRD) (Supplementary Fig. 8), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10) and Raman Spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12). It is noted that the Raman signals of typical GDY can be observed on the surface of GDY-MCO, while the signals below the GDY layer designated as typical metal oxide. Cross-sectional high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Supplementary Fig. 13) confirms the uniform distribution and atomic-scale integration of Mo, Cu, C, and O across the heterointerface, without phase segregation. Structural evolution analysis reveals that Cu(OH)2, MoCu(OH)x, and MCO have smooth surfaces, while GDY-MCO features an interlinked hierarchical porosity, including the intrinsic GDY micropores enabling molecular sieving and nanowires-induced mesopores facilitating mass transport. Such “pores-on-nanowire” architecture boosts electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) by 2.2, 3.7, and 9.1 times larger than bare MCO, MoCu(OH)x, and Cu(OH)2, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 14); and the hydrophilic surface (contact angle = 0° versus 12° of pure Cu foam substrates; Supplementary Fig. 15) enhances nitrate/reaction intermediates adsorption capacity and facilitates the mass transport at the interface. Structural evolution at the interface was characterized via HRTEM. While the MCO nanowire maintains its crystallinity with uniform lattice spacing of 0.25 nm from the surface to the inside regions (Fig. 2f–i) and exhibits smooth planar edges (Fig. 2g, h), GDY layer grows epitaxially with a lattice spacing of 0.41 nm (Fig. 2j, k, n–q), approximate 11% contracted compared to free-standing GDY (0.46 nm, Supplementary Fig. 16). Most notably, atomic-resolution mapping reveals a stepped interface configuration (Fig. 2k) where compressive strain propagates 3–5 atomic layers at the heterointerface reducing the interfacial metal-metal distance to 0.24 nm (Fig. 2l, m), a 4% contraction compared to the bulk MCO. This lattice distortion is a direct manifestation of the strong interaction between the interfacial metal atoms and the GDY, confirming the formation of the predicted Mo/Cu–C≡C interface. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns further verify the epitaxial relationship between GDY and MCO.

High-performance ammonia synthesis of GDY-MCO

We evaluated the catalytic performance of GDY-MCO in a flow-cell system under conditions relevant to industrial wastewater conditions (100 mM NO3−, pH 8.0 ± 0.3), eliminating concentration polarization through continuous electrolyte renewal (Supplementary Fig. 17). The products were quantified by ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 18–20). GDY-MCO exhibits the smallest onset potential and the largest current densities at the same potentials for NH3 production (Fig. 3a; Supplementary Fig. 21) than MCO and CuOx references (Supplementary Figs. 22–26). The ammonia yields (YNH3) increased with potential elevation (Fig. 3b), while Faraday efficiencies (FEs) have a typical volcano diagram style for all samples (GDY-MCO, MCO, and CuOx) with the increase in the applied potentials. GDY-MCO reaches the largest ammonia FE of 99.93% and large current density at −1.9 V versus SCE, indicating the complete suppression of the competitive HER and other undesired by-products. 15N isotopic labeling experiments using K15NO3 as the sole nitrogen source confirm that the NH3 originates exclusively from nitrate reduction (Fig. 3c). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), quasi in situ XPS, and in situ attenuated total reflection-infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) were conducted to elucidate the origin and mechanism of the high activity. C 1s XPS spectra (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 27) show the emergence of a metal-carbon (M–C) bond peak at 283.7 eV in the C 1s spectrum, alongside an upshift in the sp-C peak (by 0.3 eV) and downshifts in the Cu 2p (by 0.2 eV; Fig. 3f) and Mo 3d peaks (by 0.1 eV; Fig. 3g), collectively attest to the substantial charge transfer from MCO to GDY. Compared with pristine MCO, low-valent species of Cu+ and Mo4+ appeared in GDY-MCO, resulting the mixed valent states of Cu and Mo in GDY-MCO. There is +0.23 eV shift of Cu 3d and +0.06 eV shift of Mo 4d in PDOS of GDY-MCO (Fig. 1f), respectively. These values are in good agreement with our XPS results, which indicates the electrons coupling effect between the heterointerface of GDY and MCO. Such electron redistribution is consistent with the DFT predictions and underpins the formation of the electronic highway at the interface, which can act as the atomic-level charge transfer channels to reduce interfacial charge transfer resistance by 30.4% from MCO (11.2 Ω) to GDY-MCO (7.8 Ω; Supplementary Fig. 28). Quasi in situ XPS analysis reveals that while pristine MCO suffers from progressive reduction of Cu2+ under operation (electron deficiency; Cu+ content increased by 50.26% in 60 min; Fig. 3h), GDY-MCO maintains the oxidation states of Cu 2p (Fig. 3i). This electron homeostasis can be attributed to GDY’s π-conjugated system acting as an electron reservoir, dynamically donating or accepting electrons to maintain the active sites in their optimal state, thus achieving ~100% electron utilization efficiency (Fig. 3j). Similar phenomena are also observed at different applied potentials. For MCO, the reduction of Cu2+ occurs obviously with the negative shift of applied potential (Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30), while the oxidation states of metal center can be maintained in GDY-MCO (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). In situ attenuated total reflection-infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR; Fig. 3k, l) tracks the reaction intermediates, confirming the complete pathway from nitrate to ammonia with N–O bond cleavage and N–H formation (Fig. 3m). As shown in Fig. 3l, the IR peak shift from 1393 cm−1 to 1369 cm−1 corresponds to the cleavage of N–O bond of the adsorbed NO3−38. The new peaks at 1281 and 1096–1121 cm−1 indicate the formation of the key intermediates of *NO2− and *NH2, respectively38–42. The emergence of 1432 and 1641 cm−1 reveals the continuous production of NH3 with σ(N–H) bending39. The broadening of the peak at 3200–3600 cm−1 corresponds to the N–H stretching of surface NH4+ species40. To further validate the reaction mechanism and potential byproduct formation identified by in situ ATR-FTIR, in situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) was performed on both GDY-MCO and MCO (Supplementary Figs. 33–36). For GDY-MCO, characteristic signals corresponding to NO, N, NH, NH2, and NH3 were detected, confirming the electrocatalytic nitrate-to-ammonia conversion (ENtAC) pathway consistent with the ATR-FTIR findings. Notably, no signals associated with by-products such as H2, N2, N2O were observed, indicating a near 100% Faradaic selectivity towards NH3. In contrast, the MCO sample exhibited clear byproduct signals. Based on the combined in situ ATR-FTIR and DEMS results, the GDY nanosheets grown on the MCO nanowire surface do not alter the intrinsic ENtAC reaction pathway. Instead, the GDY layer functions as a dynamic electron buffer, efficiently directing the electrode-surface electrons towards the ENtAC process, thereby suppressing competitive side reactions and preventing the electrochemical reduction of the metal active sites. This is also confirmed by the results shown in Supplementary Fig. 37. These results are consistent with the theoretically predicted mechanism (Fig. 1i).

To demonstrate practical viability, we constructed a continuous-flow electrochemical reactor for ammonia synthesis (Fig. 4a). Significantly, the concentration and mass of the produced NH3 in the storage tank were determined at each 5-h interval for over 50 h and exhibited a linear relationship with the reaction time (Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39) and zero performance degradation in theYNH3 and FEs (Supplementary Fig. 40). The as-synthesized catalyst can efficiently convert nitrate in wastewater into fertilizer-grade ammonia products (Supplementary Fig. 41). After electrolysis, the residual NO3− concentration was measured as 47.70 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 42) while NO2− was essentially undetectable in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 43). For metal ion analysis, the electrolytes were determined by ICP-MS measurements and showed no detectable leaching of Cu or Mo from GDY-MCO after long-term operation, confirming the water purification capability of GDY-MCO that meets drinking standards (<50 ppm NO₃−) This performance is competitive among the noble-metal and other state-of-the-art catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 55 and Supplementary Table 1), underscoring the technological potential of our approach. The reactor achieves YNH3 of 2.45 mmol h−1 cm−2 and near 100% nitrate-to-ammonia conversion efficiency in simulated wastewater. The current density of this continuous-flow electrochemical reactor can reach around 500 mA cm−2 for over 300 h with negligible performance decay at this applied potential, which can initially meet the needs of industrialization (Fig. 4b). To further explore the performance of GDY-MCO under conditions closer to the real situation of NH3 production, a MEA was operated at 500 mA cm−2. As shown in Fig. 4c, the full cell voltage of the MEA was stable at about 3.4 V at 500 mA cm−2, and operated stably for more than 380 h. To accurately evaluate the performance of GDY-MCO in MEA, three typical time periods were selected, including 0–50 h (Supplementary Fig. 44), 150–200 h (Supplementary Fig. 45), and 300–350 h (Supplementary Fig. 46). YNH3 and FENH3 were calculated and presented in Fig. 4d. The conversion efficiency of nitrate-to-ammonia can still maintain above 90%, and the maximum can reach about 98%. According to this result, the costs of NH3 production and energy efficiency can be calculated as 45.97 kWh kgNH3−1 and 9.56%. The structural stability of GDY-MCO after the long-time electrolysis was also evaluated by characterization of morphology and structures. High-magnification SEM images show almost no structural changes in GDY-MCO after ENtAC (Supplementary Fig. 47). Besides, HRTEM images show the clear heterointerface of GDY nanosheets and MCO nanowires (Supplementary Fig. 48), and EDS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 49) shows that GDY nanosheets remain largely intact and uniformly coat the MCO nanowire surface, in consistent with the XRD patterns (Supplementary Figs. 50 and 51). Discussion

We have demonstrated a graphdiyne-enabled atomic interface engineering strategy to create a high-performance electrocatalyst for sustainable ammonia synthesis from nitrate wastewater. The in situ-formed sp-hybridized Mo/Cu–C≡C heterointerface induces dual d-p orbital hybridization, which (i) simultaneously weakens N–O bonds, lowers activation barriers, and optimizes the reaction pathway for nitrate reduction; (ii) acts as a dynamic electron reservoir to maintain catalytic activity and stability; and (iii) effectively suppresses competing side reactions. By integrating this catalyst into a flow reactor, we achieve simultaneous fertilizer production and water purification, showcasing a practical path towards circular nitrogen management. Based on our MEA operation at 500 mA cm−2 with an average cell voltage of 3.4 V, the energy consumption is calculated to be 45.97 kWh kgNH3−1, corresponding to an energy efficiency of 9.56%. Although this value is higher than that of the centralized Haber-Bosch process, our system operates under ambient conditions and directly valorizes nitrate-contaminated wastewater, thereby offering distinct advantages for decentralized applications. Furthermore, the 380-h stable operation at industrial current density demonstrates promising catalyst durability, which would substantially reduce catalyst replacement costs. In comparison with biological denitrification, our approach avoids the need for external carbon addition, sludge generation, and nutrient loss, rendering it particularly attractive for distributed settings where these factors are undesirable. Further reductions in cell voltage and extended catalyst lifetime remain essential for achieving practical competitiveness. This work not only provides a practicable and sustainable technology for nitrogen management but also establishes the construction of interfacial charge highways as a general design principle for advanced electrocatalysts targeting complex sustainable transformations.

Methods

Materials

Ammonium persulfate (H8N2O8S2, 98%, Energy Chemical), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥97%, Macklin), potassium sodium tartrate (C4H4O6KNa·4H2O, 99%, Macklin), sodium salicylate (C7H5NaO3, 99%, Energy Chemical), sodium hypochlorite (NaClO, 10% available chlorine, Energy Chemical), sodium nitroprusside dihydrate (Na2[Fe(CN)5NO]2H2O, 99%, Energy Chemical), tetrabutylammonium fluoride (C16H36FN, 1.0 mol/L in THF Energy Chemical), dichloromethane (CH2Cl2, 299.5%, Singpharm), pyridine (C5H5N, ≥99.5%, Singpharm), ethyl acetate (C4H8O2, 299.5%, Sinpharm), tetrahydrofuran (C4H8O, 299.5%, Sinepharm), N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, C3H7NO, ≥99.5%, Singpharm), sodium chloride (NaCl, ≥99.8%, Singpharm), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36–38%, Singpharm), ethanol (C2H6O, >99.5%, Singpharm), Argon (Ar, 99.999%, Deyang), were used without further purification unless otherwise stated. All water used was purified with a Millipore system (typically 18.2 MΩ cm resistivity).

Synthesis of Cu(OH)2

The Cu(OH)2 was synthesized according to reported literature. In brief, a piece of copper foam (1.0 cm × 1.0 cm) was subsequently washed with 3 M HCl, ultrapure water, and anhydrous ethanol, followed by being dried using nitrogen flow. The washed-copper foam was added to an aqueous solution of ammonium persulfate (0.05 M) and sodium hydroxide (1.0 M), and kept for 1 h. Then the copper foam was pulled out and washed with ultrapure water and anhydrous ethanol thoroughly, and dried.

Synthesis of MCO

The as-prepared Cu(OH)2 nanowires were immersed in sodium molybdate solution for 24 h, protected from light. After being thoroughly washed, the resulted MoCu(OH)x was placed in a tube furnace at 300 °C for 2 h under the protection of Ar atmosphere. After being naturally cooled to room temperature, the MoCuOx (MCO) sample was obtained.

Synthesis of GDY-MCO

The MCO sample was immersed into a solution of dichloromethane-pyridine (28:2) containing HEB (1 mg mL−1) under the protection of Ar atmosphere. The reaction vessel was then sealed and kept at room temperature for 3 days. The obtained samples were subsequently washed with anhydrous ethanol, DMF, and ultrapure water, followed by being dried under vacuum.

Electrochemical measurements

All the electrochemical measurements were performed on CHI 760D (there-electrode system; Shanghai CH. Instruments, China) in 0.5 mol L−1 K2SO4 + 0.1 mol L−1 KNO3 (pH = 8.0 ± 0.3) using H-type cell separated by Nafion 117 (N117, 3 × 3 cm2, 183 μm thick) membrane. Before use, the membrane was pretreated with 5 wt% H2O2 at 80 °C for 1 h, washed with deionized water, and then activated in 0.5 M H2SO4 at 80 °C for 1 h. Products analysis

Quantification of ammonia (NH3): The detection of NH3 is based on the indophenol blue method. Typically, 1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution (2 mL), including 5% salicylic acid and 5% trisodium citrate dehydrate, was added to 2 mL electrolyte sample. Subsequently, 0.05 M sodium hypochlorite (10–15%) solution (1 mL) was mixed into the above solution. Finally, 0.2 mL 1% sodium nitroprusside (Na2[Fe(CN)5NO] · 2H2O) was also added, and the solution was stored in the dark. After 1 h, the colored solutions formed had the UV-Vis absorption peak centered at 652.5 nm by UV spectrophotometer. The concentration-dependent absorption spectra were calibrated using standard ammonia chloride solutions with varying concentrations. The obtained value was then fitted to the calibration curve to acquire the corresponding ammonia concentration.

In situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) analysis

In situ DEMS signals were recorded on CIS-DEMS-DM, using specific electrochemical cell (CIS-DMS-EC-SD, Hefei In situ Technology Co., Ltd., China) (Supplementary Fig. 56). Electrochemical measurements were performed on CHI 600E (Shanghai CH. Instruments, China). In situ DEMS measurements were conducted using a vacuum-assisted sample introduction method. Prior to testing, Ar was purged through the electrolyte (0.5 mol L−1 K2SO4 + 0.1 mol L−1 KNO3) at a flow rate of 6 mL min−1 to remove residual air. A copper tape was attached to the edge of the GDY-MCO, which had been grown in situ on copper foam, to serve as the working electrode. An Ag/AgCl electrode and a platinum electrode were used as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. The pore size of the membrane was 20 nm, and the porosity was 50%. The electrolyte in the cell was circulated using a peristaltic pump at 5 mL min−1 to eliminate bubbles within the cell, after which the system was connected to the gas inlet on the left side of the mass spectrometer. The resistance was then measured, the experimental method was configured, and the baseline was recorded. Once the baseline became stable, chronoamperometric i–t measurements were performed under a constant applied potential of −1.855 V for 60 s. This potential was applied five times in total, with an interval of 600 s between each application. Finally, the data from both the electrochemical workstation and the mass spectrometer were saved.

Computational details

All of the calculations are performed in the framework of the spin-polarized density functional theory with the projector augmented plane-wave method, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)43,44. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) proposed by Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) is selected for the exchange-correlation potential45,46. The long-range van der Waals interaction is described by the DFT-D3 approach47. The cut-off energy for plane wave is set to 480 eV. The energy criterion is set to 10–5 eV in iterative solution of the Kohn-Sham equation. All the structures are relaxed until the residual forces on the atoms have declined to less than 0.02 eV/Å. Data analysis and visualization are carried out with the help of VASPKIT48 code and VESTA49. The atomic coordinates of the optimized computational models in Supplementary Data 1–4.
