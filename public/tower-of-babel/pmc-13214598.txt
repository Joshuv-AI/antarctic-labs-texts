Dose-Dependent Cytotoxic Effects of Lavandula angustifolia L. Oil on A549 Human Lung Carcinoma Cells
Authors: Duygu Zorlu, Nuray Bayar Muluk, Imran Özdemir, Muhammet Volkan Bulbul, İlknur Keskin, Semiha Mervenur Evren, Elvin Alaskarov, Cemal Cingi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe investigated the dose-dependent cytotoxic effects of Lavandula angustifolia L. (L. angustifolia L.) oil on A549 human lung carcinoma cells.Material/MethodsA549 human lung carcinoma cells (ATCC CCL-185) were acquired in cryopreserved form, with the catalog number CCL-185 that is commercially available, and cultured using F-12K medium (Kaighn’s modification of Ham’s F-12, ATCC 30-2004), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ATCC 30-2020) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco 15240062). Five working concentrations of L. angustifolia L. oil (10, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μg/mL) were prepared. The antiproliferative effect was assessed using the MTT cell viability assay.ResultsTreatment of A549 human lung carcinoma cells with varying concentrations of L. angustifolia L. oil resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in cell viability by MTT assay. L. angustifolia oil exerts significant antiproliferative activity, with higher doses producing more profound cytotoxicity. There was a marked reduction in cell density, and the formation of prominent intercellular gaps, indicative of extensive cell death, was detected. Loss of adhesion and cell rounding have been associated with cytotoxic effects.ConclusionsL. angustifolia L. oil has good potential to inhibit the viability of A549 human lung carcinoma cells. It exhibits moderate antiproliferative activity. L. angustifolia oil exerts significant antiproliferative activity against A549 cells. It affects cell morphology that is indicative of extensive cell death.

Introduction

The prevalence of lung cancer is increasing, making it the second most common cancer worldwide after breast cancer. Lung cancer accounted for about 12.4% of all cancers in 2022, with an estimated 2.5 million new cases diagnosed globally. In 2022, nearly 1.8 million people died from lung cancer [1]. In most countries, lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths. The rates are highest in industrialized regions such as Eastern Asia, North America, and Europe [2].

Many chemical compounds with diverse biological activities are found in aromatic medicinal plants [3]. One of the most well-known medicinal herbs in aromatherapy, L. angustifolia (Lamiaceae), has a long history of use as a sedative. In addition to reducing skeletal muscle tone in rats, the plant also possesses anti-inflammatory, anti-flatulent, antispasmodic, spasmolytic, and antimutagenic properties [4,5]. Lavender oil is used to treat several rheumatic, neurological, and gastrointestinal diseases [6]. Essential oil from lavender, or lavandin, exhibits antimicrobial, antifungal, and desensitizing properties [7]. A 0.25% (v/v) dosage of lavender oil was found to be cytotoxic to human skin cells in vitro [8]. Essential oils from L. angustifolia include compounds that help calm nerves and promote relaxation in both humans and animals [9–11].

The mint family, which includes L. angustifolia Mill. (Lamiaceae), originated in the western Mediterranean [12–14]. The L. angustifolia plant is used as a medicinal herb through oil extraction from its flowers and leaves [15]. Recent years have seen a growing body of scientific evidence suggesting that L. angustifolia extracts has antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer properties [15,16]. It has a history of use in relieving irritation, rheumatism, and gastrointestinal problems. Animal models of lung (A-549), breast (MCF-7), cervical (HeLa), and prostate (PC-3) cancers have shown cytostatic and apoptotic effects from distillation extracts of L. angustifolia [17]. Linalool has been shown to induce apoptosis and activate antitumor immunity in cell line studies, acting as an antiproliferative agent against SW 620, Hep G2, A-549, and T-47D cells [18].

This preliminary in vitro cytotoxicity screening study was conducted to investigate the dose-dependent cytotoxic effects of L. angustifolia L. oil on human lung cancer cells. We used A-549 cells for its prior use in essential oil screening [18].

Material and Methods

This study was conducted at the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, Faculty of Medicine, Eskisehir Osmangazi University, in collaboration with the Department of Histology and Embryology, School of Medicine, Istanbul Medipol University, Turkey. The A549 human lung carcinoma cell line (ATCC CCL-185) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). According to standard institutional practice, ethics approval is not required for in vitro-only studies conducted using established, commercially available cell lines.

Materials

The A549 human lung carcinoma cell line was purchased frozen from ATCC, with the catalog number CCL-185 (ATCC CCL-185).

Cell Culture

A549 human lung carcinoma cells (ATCC CCL-185) were obtained in cryopreserved form and cultured using F-12K medium (Kaighn’s modification of Ham’s F-12, ATCC 30-2004), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ATCC 30-2020) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Gibco 15240062). Frozen vials were thawed in a 37°C water bath for about 1 min, decontaminated with 70% ethanol, and handled under sterile conditions. Cells were transferred into 9 mL of complete medium, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of fresh medium. This suspension was seeded into T75 flasks (Corning #430641) containing 11 mL of complete medium and incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. When cells reached approximately 80% confluency, they were detached using trypsin-EDTA (ATCC 30-2101) after being washed with PBS. The detached cells were resuspended with medium, centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min, and resuspended in 5 mL of fresh medium. Cell counting was performed using a Scepter™ 2.0 Handheld Automated Cell Counter (Merck, C85360).

Preparation of Lavandula angustifolia L

Lavender essential oil (L. angustifolia L.) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Cat. No: 8000-28-0, Product No: 61718; appearance: colorless to yellow liquid; refractive index at 20°C: 1.450–1.470; 1H-NMR: conforms to structure). The stock solution (0.879 g/mL) was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to facilitate homogeneous dispersion and subsequently diluted with complete culture medium to obtain 5 working concentrations (10, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μg/mL).

The final DMSO concentration in all treatment wells was 0.1% (v/v) and was kept constant across concentrations. A vehicle control group containing 0.1% DMSO without essential oil was included and incorporated into all statistical analyses. Cells incubated only with complete medium served as the negative control, while cells treated with Triton X-100, a membrane-lysing detergent, served as the positive control.

To exclude optical interference, oil-containing wells without cells were prepared for each concentration and measured at 570 nm. These blank values were subtracted from corresponding experimental readings before normalization.

MTT Cell Viability Assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h to allow attachment. At approximately 70% confluence, medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the designated concentrations of L. angustifolia oil. Each concentration was tested in triplicate wells in 3 independent biological experiments.

After 24-h exposure, medium was removed and MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added at a 1: 20 ratio relative to total volume and incubated for 3 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. The solution was discarded and formazan crystals were solubilized using a 1: 1 mixture of DMSO and culture medium. Plates were shaken in the dark for 1 h and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer.

Absorbance values were first corrected by subtracting oil-only blanks and then normalized to the negative control. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated from normalized viability values using nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism (version 9.1). Additionally, morphological evaluation of cells exposed to the IC50 concentration for 24 h was performed using an inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss™ Axio Vert.A1), and representative images were recorded.

Statistical Evaluation

Dose–response relationships were analyzed using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA). Concentration–response curves were fitted using a 4-parameter logistic inhibitory model (4-parameter logistic, 4PL) with variable slope, defined as: Y = Bottom + Top − Bottom1 + 10 (LogIC50 − X) × HillSlope Y=Bottom+ 1+10 (LogIC 50 − X)×HillSlope Top–Bottom where: X represents log10 (concentration), Y represents normalized cell viability (%), Top and Bottom correspond to the upper and lower asymptotes of the curve. HillSlope describes the curve steepness curve, and fitting was performed using least-squares nonlinear regression without weighting (each concentration equally weighted). Top and Bottom parameters were not constrained and were estimated freely by the model. The analysis was performed using normalized viability percentages calculated relative to untreated control, rather than raw absorbance values. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was derived from the fitted curve. Estimated parameters: LogIC50=2.247 IC50=176.6 μM 95% CI for IC50=146.7–221.0 μM, Hill slope=−0.6618 (95% CI: −0.8131 to −0.5439) R2=0.9783, Degrees of freedom=19, Number of concentrations analyzed=21, Goodness of fit was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R2) and residual distribution. All experiments were performed in at least 3 independent replicates and data are presented as mean±SEM. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The positive control group (Triton X-100) was included as an independent group within the same ANOVA model as all treatment and negative control groups and was therefore incorporated into post hoc multiple comparison testing.

Results

The viability curve graph of A549 cells after 24 hs of exposure to Lavandula angustifolia L. oil is shown in Figure 1, showing that lavender oil inhibits the viability of A549 cells. The cytotoxic effect of L. angustifolia L. oil on the tested cells was measured using the MTT assay. The dose–response curve showed a concentration-dependent decrease in normalized absorbance, indicating reduced cell viability as oil concentrations increased. Nonlinear regression analysis produced an IC50 value of 176.6 μg/mL (LogIC50=2.247 μg/mL), with a high goodness of fit (R2=0.9783), suggesting a strong link between oil concentration and cytotoxic activity. At concentrations near and above the IC50, cell viability was significantly reduced, approaching near-complete inhibition at the highest doses tested. These results indicate that L. angustifolia L. has moderate antiproliferative activity under these conditions (Figure 1).

Cell viability (%) of A549 human lung carcinoma cells after 24-h exposure to various concentrations of L. angustifolia L. oil is presented in Table 1, and the corresponding statistical comparisons are illustrated in Figure 2. The effects of L. angustifolia L. oil on A549 cell viability demonstrated a clear concentration-dependent cytotoxic response as determined by the MTT assay.

Compared with the untreated control group (100.0±5.6%), treatment with 10, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μg/mL resulted in mean viabilities of 84.0±0.8%, 68.1±0.4%, 63.2±0.4%, 54.6±0.4%, and 44.4±2.4%, respectively. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was performed using biological replicate means (n=3 independent experiments). Within each experiment, measurements from triplicate wells were averaged prior to analysis and technical replicates were not treated as independent observations.

All tested concentrations significantly reduced cell viability compared with the negative control: 10 μg/mL: P=0.0048, 50 μg/mL: P<0.0001, 100 μg/mL: P<0.0001, 150 μg/mL: P<0.0001, 200 μg/mL: P<0.0001. Pairwise comparisons confirmed a progressive cytotoxic effect: 10 vs 50 μg/mL: P=0.0048, 10 vs 100 μg/mL: P=0.0004, 10 vs 150 μg/mL: P<0.0001, 10 vs 200 μg/mL: P<0.0001.

No statistically significant differences were observed between 50 and 100 μg/mL (P=0.7681), 100 and 150 μg/mL (P=0.2183), or 150 and 200 μg/mL (P=0.0997), indicating a plateau in cytotoxicity within this intermediate concentration range. However, 50 μg/mL differed from 150 μg/mL (P=0.0178) and 200 μg/mL (P<0.0001), and 100 μg/mL differed from 200 μg/mL (P=0.0011).

The positive control showed complete cytotoxicity and differed significantly from all treatment groups (all P<0.0001). The strongest inhibitory effect of L. angustifolia oil was observed at 200 μg/mL. The positive control (Triton X-100) was analyzed within the same statistical model as the treatment and negative control groups. Mean viability in the positive control group was 0.0±0.0% (n=3). Tukey’s post hoc analysis demonstrated that the positive control differed significantly from all experimental concentrations (all P<0.0001) as well as from the negative control (P<0.0001), confirming complete loss of cell viability and validating assay performance.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that L. angustifolia L. oil exerts significant antiproliferative effects against A549 cells in a concentration-dependent manner, with increasing cytotoxicity at higher doses and a partial response plateau at 50 to 150 μg/mL (Figure 2, Table 1).

Cell Morphology

Representative phase-contrast images of A549 cells are shown in Figure 3 to illustrate gross morphological differences between untreated cells (Figure 3A) and cells exposed to L. angustifolia L. oil at the IC50 concentration for 24 h (Figure 3B). Control A549 cells exhibited typical adherent growth with an epithelioid morphology, forming a confluent monolayer with uniform cell shape and close cell–cell contacts (Figure 3A). After 24-h exposure to L. angustifolia L. oil, cells showed reduced adhesion, increased rounding, and decreased cell density, with many areas of cell detachment and widened intercellular spaces (Figure 3B).

Discussion

The 2 most common types of lung cancer are small cell lung carcinoma (SCLC) and non-small cell lung carcinoma (NSCLC). About 85% of lung cancers are non-small cell lung tumors. Subtypes of NSCLC can be identified by histology as adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), or giant cell carcinoma [2].

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in both the United States and worldwide. In 2025, lung cancer is projected to cause approximately 125 000 deaths in the United States, exceeding deaths from other major cancers such as prostate, breast or colorectal cancers [19]. Over the past several decades, the types of lung cancer has shifted, with rates of adenocarcinoma increasing and rates of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) declining [2].

Research on rodents has shown that L. angustifolia has anti-inflammatory and antinociceptive effects [20,21]. Anticancer and antimutagenic effects have been found in various components of L. angustifolia essential oil [22–24]. Lavender oil contains many substances, including tannins, essential oil, coumarins, triterpenes, and flavonoids, as well as monoterpenoids such as linalool, linalyl acetate, perillylalcohol, 1,8-cineole, β-ocimene, limonene, terpinen-4-ol, and camphor [7]. In both human and hamster cell lines, perillyl alcohol inhibits the growth of pancreatic tumor cells in a concentration-dependent manner [25]. The growth of murine B16 melanoma cells is inhibited by perillyl alcohol, and it has cytotoxic effects on liver cancer, pancreatic tumors, and mammary cancers in rats [26]. Furthermore, terpinen-4-ol can limit the growth of human M14 melanoma cells [27], and 1,8-cineole can suppress the proliferation of leukemia cell lines [28].

The present study shows that lavender oil, scientifically known as L. angustifolia L., affects human lung cancer cells (A549) in a dose-dependent manner. Lavender oil decreased the viability of A549 cells, based on our results. We used the MTT assay to assess the cytotoxic effect of L. angustifolia L. oil on the tested cells. Reduced cell viability was indicated by a concentration-dependent decline in normalized absorbance, visible in the dose–response curve. Nonlinear regression analysis revealed a strong correlation between lavender oil concentration and its cytotoxic effect, with a high goodness of fit (R2=0.9783) and an IC50 value of 176.6 μg/mL (LogIC50=2.247 μg/mL). Cell viability was significantly decreased, and at the highest concentrations tested it was nearly completely inhibited, especially at concentrations near and above the IC50. These findings suggest that L. angustifolia L. has potent antiproliferative effects in laboratory conditions.

The MTT assay showed that the viability of A549 human lung cancer cells decreased in a dose-dependent manner after treatment with various doses of L. angustifolia L. oil. Compared to the negative control group, cell viability clearly declined at concentrations of 10, 50, 100, 150, and 200 μg/mL. The most significant reduction was observed at 200 μg/mL, with a notable decrease in viability relative to both negative and positive controls. No significant difference in cytotoxicity was seen among concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 μg/mL, indicating a plateau effect. Overall, these findings demonstrate that L. angustifolia oil markedly inhibits A549 cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, with greater cytotoxicity at higher concentrations.

Untreated control cells showed strong adherence to the culture substrate, maintaining their normal epithelioid shape and forming a continuous monolayer at high confluency. The distinct borders allowed for observation of cellular features, clearly distinguishing cytoplasmic and nuclear divisions. Without signs of degenerative changes such as membrane blebbing, cytoplasmic retraction, or nuclear disintegration, the plasma membrane appeared structurally intact, suggesting overall cellular integrity was preserved. In contrast, cells exposed to L. angustifolia L. oil exhibited significant morphological changes, suggesting cytotoxic effects. Over time, the epithelioid shape deteriorated, and cells became more spherical; many showed signs of partial or complete detachment from the substrate, with a number even entering suspension. This detachment was accompanied by a dramatic decrease in cell density and the formation of large intercellular spaces, both indicative of widespread cell death. Numerous nuclei displayed pyknosis, characterized by intense chromatin condensation, and the cytoplasmic volume appeared reduced.

Linalool and linalyl acetate are the primary components of L. angustifolia distillation products; these 2 compounds determine the flavor profile and medicinal effects of the plant [14,29]. Naturally occurring phytochemicals, including linalyl acetate and linalyl oleoside, have shown anti-inflammatory effects in rats with carrageenan-induced edema [30]. The anticancer effects of linalool are attributed to its ability to modulate oxidative stress, as demonstrated by an in vivo study using the sarcoma-180 solid tumor model [31]. Linalool shows very strong antibacterial activity against bacteria such as Staphylococcus epidermidis and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as Candida albicans [32]. The acetate derivative of linalool is known as linalyl acetate, which exhibits typical prodrug characteristics [30]. Linalyl acetate can alter melanogenesis in melanoma cells through mechanisms involving oxidative stress and the JNK and ERK signaling pathways [12,33].

Three different extracts of aerial components and the essential oil of L. angustifolia were tested for their cytotoxicity and mechanisms of cell death in both normal and malignant human cells in a study by Tayarani-Najaran [34]. Plant extracts were cultured with a range of cells, from human fibroblasts to cancerous HeLa and MCF-7 cell lines. Cell viability was assessed using the MTS test. Flow cytometry, through the detection of the sub-G1 peak by DNA fragmentation labeling with propidium iodide, was used to identify apoptotic cells. Western blotting confirmed the presence of molecules involved in apoptotic signaling, such as Bax and cleaved PARP. The essential oils, ethanol extracts, and n-hexane demonstrated strong cytotoxic effects on cancer cells while showing only mild cytotoxicity to human fibroblasts in vitro. Treated cells exhibited a sub-G1 peak in the flow cytometry histogram compared to the control group. Western blotting indicated that, relative to the control, ethyl alcohol and n-hexane extracts increased Bax expression and caused PARP cleavage in HeLa cells. Apoptosis is the likely mode of action for L. angustifolia, which exerts cytotoxic and apoptotic effects on HeLa and MCF-7 cell lines.

The prognosis is poor, since most lung carcinomas are first detected when they are already advanced. Lung cancer screening for high-risk patients has been driven by the clear need to detect the disease early, when it might still be treatable. Furthermore, smoking-related damage to the heart and lungs makes intensive surgical or multimodal therapy less feasible for most patients who develop lung cancer [2]. Radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and surgery are the main treatment options. Skilled palliative care is a key part of treating patients with NSCLC, as most lung malignancies cannot be effectively treated with current therapies [18]. Quitting smoking after an early-stage lung cancer diagnosis can improve prognosis, likely by slowing disease progression, according to a meta-analysis by Parsons et al. The estimated 5-year survival rate for 65-year-old patients with early-stage NSCLC who continued smoking was 33%, based on data from 9 trials; in contrast, those who quit had a rate of 70% [35].

Patients who smoked cigarettes at the time of diagnosis had a lower chance of survival compared to those who never smoked, according to an analysis of 4200 patients in the NSCLC Database Project of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network. Similarly, among younger patients with stage IV disease, current smokers had a worse chance of survival compared to former smokers who quit smoking more than 12 months prior to diagnosis [36].

A connection between NSCLC and daily use of 0.625 mg of conjugated equine estrogen (CEE) and 2.5 mg of medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) was found in a secondary analysis of the Women’s Health Initiative randomized, placebo-controlled study. More than 5 years of CEE plus MPA use was linked to an increased risk of NSCLC, and the mortality rate among CEE+MPA users diagnosed with NSCLC was higher than that of NSCLC patients who did not receive hormone therapy [37].

Patients with NSCLC stages I through IIIA are candidates for surgery as a treatment option [38]. Furthermore, adjuvant chemotherapy is administered to patients with resected lung cancer due to the high recurrence rate [39]. Chemotherapy and surgery are common treatment options for patients with stage IIIB and stage IV NSCLC. Treatment of advanced NSCLC is increasingly focused on molecular-targeted therapy [2].

There has been a surge of interest in using molecular-targeted therapy as a preoperative treatment for resectable early-stage NSCLC, based on its effectiveness in older patients [40]. The neoadjuvant use of nivolumab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor, combined with platinum-doublet chemotherapy, was authorized by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2022 for patients with resectable NSCLC (tumors ≥4 cm or node-positive). Patients with stage IB, II, or IIIA NSCLC participated in the CheckMate 816 trial, which led to this approval [2,41,42].

Skilled palliative care is a vital part of treatment for NSCLC, as most cases cannot be cured with current therapeutic methods. When advanced NSCLC is first diagnosed, there is increasing evidence supporting palliative care in addition to normal oncologic treatment [43].

Our results support previous research showing that L. angustifolia L. oil mainly causes cytotoxicity. Our results are consistent with previous studies indicating that L. angustifolia L. oil predominantly exerts cytotoxic effects. The observed cell rounding and loss of adhesion may be associated with cytoskeletal alterations, including changes in actin filament organization, as a general response to cytotoxic injury. These morphological findings further support that the antiproliferative effects of L. angustifolia L. oil are largely attributable to its cytotoxic effect on cancer cells.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the experiments were conducted using a single lung adenocarcinoma cell line (A549). Therefore, the observed cytotoxic response may not fully represent the heterogeneity of lung cancer subtypes or other tumor models. Second, cytotoxicity was primarily evaluated using the MTT assay and morphological observations. Although these methods reliably demonstrate loss of metabolic activity and cell viability, they do not distinguish among apoptosis, necrosis, and other forms of cell death. Confirmation of the underlying mechanism would require assessment of specific molecular markers such as caspase activation, annexin V binding, mitochondrial membrane potential, or DNA fragmentation. Third, no normal lung epithelial cell line was included; therefore, the selectivity of L. angustifolia oil toward malignant versus non-malignant cells could not be determined. Fourth, despite the use of vehicle controls and blank correction, essential oils are complex mixtures that can interact with tetrazolium-based assays. Thus, metabolic assays alone may not fully reflect clonogenic survival or long-term proliferative capacity. Finally, in vitro exposure conditions do not reproduce the pharmacokinetic and metabolic processes present in vivo; consequently, the reported IC50 values should be interpreted as demonstrating biological activity rather than therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that the oil of L. angustifolia L. can decrease the viability of human lung cancer cells (A549). The antiproliferative effect of L. angustifolia L. is quite strong. The concentration determines how much the oil inhibits A549 cell growth; higher doses lead to more significant cytotoxicity. It alters cell shape, resulting in a sharp decline in cell density and the formation of large gaps between cells, which are both signs of widespread cell death.
