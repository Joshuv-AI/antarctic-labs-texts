Correlation of sub-centennial-scale pulses of initial Central Atlantic Magmatic Province lavas and the end-Triassic extinctions
Authors: Dennis V. Kent, Paul E. Olsen, Huapei Wang, Morgan F. Schaller, Mohammed Et-Touhami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceDuring the initial phase of eruptions, Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) basalts spewed more than 500 times the amount of sulfur released during the Laki historic eruption in Iceland. The repeated injections of sulfate aerosols, constrained by paleosecular variation data, occurred in rapid succession. The resulting severe [albedo-induced] volcanic winters may have been the proximal cause for the well-resolved end-Triassic mass extinction in the continental realm. However, in the less temporally constrained marine realm, longer-term cumulative release of carbon dioxide from CAMP eruptive and intrusive activity may have played an important albeit somewhat diachronous role in the extinctions through ocean acidification and longer-term warming.

The end-Triassic extinction (ETE) along with the end-Permian and end-Cretaceous events are the most severe biotic extinctions in the past ~270 million years (Myr) (1). Each of these mass extinctions coincides with a huge continental flood basalt province (2, 3), but there is a wide disparity between the short duration estimated for the extinction event (on the order of <100 kyr) and the much longer duration of magmatism that typically lasts ~1 Myr or more (4). In the case of the end-Permian event, high-precision U-Pb geochronology links the extinction in the Meishan volcanic ash-bearing sedimentary section in South China (5) to the emplacement of the Siberian Trap sills that postdated initial lava eruptions by almost 1 Myr (4), supporting the view that liberation of thermogenic greenhouse gases, especially CO2, drove the mass extinction (e.g., ref. 6). The end-Cretaceous event is precisely associated with an Ir anomaly from the Chicxulub impact, the most probable proximal trigger for the extinctions (7), that has been dated in continental deposits in the Denver basin at 66.02±0.08 Ma (8). This timing is close to a U-Pb dated pulse of volcanism between ~66.0 to 66.1 Ma in the Poladpur Formation of the Deccan Traps, which might allow a Deccan contribution to the end-Cretaceous mass extinction (9).

Stratigraphic superposition provides more direct evidence for a link between the continental ETE (10) and the initial lavas of the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) (11) within an overall temporal framework provided by high-precision U-Pb dating (12, 13). However, the tetrapod and microflora ETE recorded in continental sediments of the Pangea rift basins of eastern North America, as exemplified in the Newark basin (14), actually underlies and hence precedes the local emplacement of earliest CAMP lavas. All CAMP lavas (and interbedded sediments) in the Newark Basin are of normal geomagnetic polarity (15) but are immediately preceded (along with the intervening ETE) by short reverse polarity Chron E23r (16, 17). The report of reverse polarity magnetizations within one of the thickest sequences of CAMP lavas (Tiourjdal section in the High Atlas of Morocco) and attributed to E23r (18, 19) thus pointed to a more direct coincidence of CAMP activity and the ETE. However, the putative reverse polarity magnetizations reported within the CAMP lava sequence in Morocco have been shown to be an artifact of complex remagnetization (20). Moreover, evidence of E23r has since been found in sediments of the Bigoudine Formation just below the oldest CAMP lavas of the Tasguint Formation in the Argana Basin of Morocco (21) as well as in the Blomidon Formation just below the North Mountain Basalt (NMB) in the Fundy Basin (22). The turnover in sporomorph taxa marking the terrestrial palynological ETE plus the interval of post-ETE strata found below the lowermost basalts in the Newark and Fundy basins (23, 24) demonstrate that CAMP basalts indeed postdate the ETE within these basins (13). A similar palynological turnover, however, is not observed in sediments below the lavas of the Argana Basin where Triassic-aspect sporomorphs are observed within sedimentary strata up to the base of the lowermost CAMP Tasguint Basalt (25), which would be permissive of a coincidence of the continental ETE with earliest CAMP lavas (21).

Relicts of CAMP, the aerially largest continental basalt province (26), occur over large areas of eastern North America, Africa, and South America, with more limited exposures in Europe for a total footprint of about 10 million square kilometers (Mkm2) (27, 28) when these continents were together as Pangea (Fig. 1). Most of the CAMP remnants are basaltic sills and dikes, but lavas are famously preserved interbedded with sediments in continental rift basins in the northern sector of the CAMP, principally the Culpeper, Newark, Hartford, and Fundy basins of eastern North America, and the Argana and Central High Atlas basins of Morocco. High-precision U-Pb zircon dates from the lavas and closely associated igneous intrusions combined with orbitally paced lacustrine cycles in interbedded sedimentary strata place age constraints on the ETE recorded in the same sections (13). The Amelal sill feeder for the 2nd Argana basalt (Alemzi Formation) of Morocco (201.564 ± 0.054 Ma; 2σ analytical uncertainties quoted throughout) and the East Ferry Member of the NMB in the Fundy Basin of Atlantic Canada (201.566 ± 0.031 Ma) provide some of the oldest age limits for CAMP lavas (13). The lowermost Argana basalt [Tasguint Formation (29)] has not been directly dated but immediately overlies sediments of the uppermost Bigoudine Formation with latest Triassic sporomorphs (e.g., Patinasporites–Enzonalisporites group) (18, 25) whose disappearance marks the ETE in the uppermost Blomidon Formation a few decimeters below the NMB of the Fundy Basin (13, 23, 24). The Orange Mountain Basalt (OMB) in the Newark Basin with a date of 201.520 ± 0.034 Ma for the correlative Palisade Sill feeder characterizes a somewhat younger phase of initial CAMP extrusive activity, which compared to the oldest dates (e.g., 201.566 ± 0.031 Ma for the NMB) thus extended over something like 46 kyr with a numerically comparable rms 2σ uncertainty of ±46 kyr (13).

A detailed record of the ETE (formerly identified with the Triassic–Jurassic boundary) occurs in the uppermost Passaic Formation about 11 m below the OMB in the Jacksonwald syncline of the Newark Basin and is marked by a fern spike, the disappearance of the Patinasporites–Enzonalisporites group, and a turnover from Brachychirotherium to Eubrontes-dominated dinosaur ichnotaxa (10). Based on an average sediment accumulation rate (32 cm/kyr) determined from the underlying three McLaughlin (405 kyr) cycles in the continuous Martinsville drill core, the ETE occurred about 34 kyr before eruption of the OMB and only ~20 kyr after Chron E23r, whose 8.3 m maximum thickness in the Martinsville core is estimated to represent about 26 kyr [(34); SI Appendix]. Chron E23r thus serves as a close temporal proxy for the palynological ETE boundary. A thick interval of strata immediately underlying Chron E23r is of uniform normal polarity (Chron E23n) that extends back to 202.49 Ma (Chron E22r) according to cycle stratigraphy in the Martinsville core. A long interval of uniform normal polarity (Chron E24n) also characterizes succeeding strata that encompasses the entire CAMP extrusive zone in the Newark Basin to well above the homotaxial and correlative CAMP lavas and interbedded sediments in the nearby Hartford Basin to an estimated age of 199.93 Ma (Chron E24r) according to the Newark–Hartford astrochronostratigraphic polarity time scale [N-H APTS (35) (Table 1). The 0.026 Myr-long reverse polarity Chron E23r thus punctuates a vastly longer (~2.5 Myr), well-resolved interval of otherwise normal polarity (E23n and E24n), providing a distinctive target for precise temporal correlation in close temporal proximity of the ETE on a global scale. We note that durations of intervals delineated in the sedimentary sections, such as Chron E23r, are based on an astrochronology in which the 405 kyr eccentricity cycle framework is corroborated by U-Pb geochronology (36) but do not depend on details of the U-Pb geochronology on CAMP igneous rocks for which we use data reported in ref. 13 for internal consistency (SI Appendix).

Here, we seek to tighten the relative age constraints for the regional onset of CAMP extrusive activity using paleosecular variation of geomagnetic field directions recorded in the ~210-m-thick NMB and in other CAMP lavas in eastern North America and comparing the directional groups (DGs) to those reported for the 260-m-thick Tiourjdal section in the Central High Atlas of Morocco (19), the most complete succession of early CAMP flows with characteristically evolved high-titanium quartz-normative (HTQ) chemistries (18, 37). Our primary focus is on stratigraphic sections containing evidence related to the ETE that can be linked by Chron E23r and where the temporal order with respect to CAMP lavas is unambiguously determined by superposition and the relative timing of the lava units constrained by their records of paleomagnetic secular variation in the overall context of astronomical cyclicity of the underlying sediments (35, 38). This allows us to postulate constraints on the duration and relative timing of initial pulses of CAMP eruptions on a centennial level of resolution to assess their causative agency from emissions of CO2 and especially SO2 for the ETE. We also attempt to extend the temporal analysis to records of the ETE in the marine stratigraphic realm in which the Triassic–Jurassic boundary is defined but which rarely include CAMP lavas or, thus far, a robust record of Chron E23r.

Results

Directional Groups in Camp Lavas.

The ~210-m-thick North Mountain Basalt (NMB) of the Fundy Basin of Atlantic Canada consists of three units distinguished by volcanological criteria (39): the basal >30- to 180-m-thick East Ferry Member (EFM) that is regarded as a single massive lava flow in which pegmatitic segregation veins in its middle part are the source of zircons for high-precision U-Pb dating (12, 13, 40); the ~15- to 150-m-thick Margaretsville Member (MM) that is composed of multiple relatively thin amygdaloidal lavas; and the overlying (locally absent) Brier Island Member (BIM) that represents a single coarse-grained lava flow locally up to ~150-m-thick. In what proved to be a futile search for Chron E23r in lavas of the NMB, we collected three to nine oriented samples for paleomagnetic study from 37 sites distributed through the NMB along the 200 km-long exposures on the south shore of the Bay of Fundy with 22 sites near Cape Blomidon and 15 sites near Digby (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1). Characteristic remanent magnetizations (ChRMs) were successfully isolated in 34 of the sampling sites using progressive alternating field and/or thermal demagnetization techniques and with an acceptance precision parameter, k > 50, for within-site directional scatter (SI Appendix, Table S1; see SI Appendix, SI Text and Figs. S2–S4 for description of measurement and analytical procedures and supporting rock magnetic data). All ChRM have normal polarity (SI Appendix, Fig. S5); no convincing evidence of reverse polarities was found even in densely sampled albeit azimuthally unoriented core GAV77-3 that recovered practically all the NMB (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and Table S2). The ChRM site-means are nonetheless distributed in two directional clusters reflecting secular variation: a northeasterly grouping around the EFM mean and a northwesterly grouping centered on the BIM mean (SI Appendix, Fig. S5, Top). The 19 accepted ChRM site-means from MM tend to fall close to either the EFM or BIM mean directions. The thin MM flows are most simply interpreted as either fed from cracks of the top of the inflating EFM-mega flow (DGNM1) or lobes from the advancing BIM flow (DGNM2), as opposed to originating from the eruptive fissure source (41) 87 km from Digby and 158 km from Cape Blomidon. We group MM sites within one angular SD (ASD) of the EFM mean as part of a lower directional group (DGNM1: D = 19.6°, I = 43.2°, a95=2.2°, N = 17 sites) and likewise those within one ASD of the BIM mean as part of an upper directional group (DGNM2: D = 345.8°, I = 40.3°, a95=4.9°, N = 17 sites) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5, Middle). The resulting DGNM1 and DGNM2 means have a total angular separation of 25.2 ± 5.4° and agree well with the lower and upper groups of directions for NMB reported earlier (42) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5, Bottom).

The pronounced change in direction between DGNM1 and DGNM2 in the NMB appears to be congruent with the distinctive paleomagnetic directional shift between DGT2 (lavas #8 to 22) and DGT3 (lavas #23 to 27) in the Tiourjdal section (19) (Fig. 2). Data from both localities show a counterclockwise shift of about 35° in declination with only ~5° shallowing of inclination. DGT3 and DGNM2 have similar thicknesses and are each overlain by sediments, the capping McCoy Brook Formation in the case of the North Mountain DGNM2 and the thin limestone layer that records what is regarded as a spurious magnetization (20) between lavas of Tiourjdal DGT3 and DGT5 (lava 28). To make a stricter comparison, we converted the DG directions at each locale to virtual geomagnetic poles (VGPs) and rotated those from the Tiourjdal section to North American coordinates using a standard reconstruction for northwest Africa prior to opening of the North Atlantic Ocean in the Jurassic (SI Appendix, Table S3). The Fundy Basin and the High Atlas region, presently about 46° arc-distance or ~5,000 km apart (Fig. 3), were at the time of CAMP emplacement less than 7° or ~750 km apart (Fig. 1). Similar patterns of directional change in both areas are thus most simply interpreted as simultaneous records of the same regional geomagnetic secular variation. The directional shift from DGNM1 to DGNM2 corresponds to an easterly change in the corresponding mean VGPs that is paralleled by the DGT2 to DGT3 shift in VGPs from the Tiourjdal section, each of about 30° of arc (Fig. 3). This correlation would imply that Tiourjdal DGT1 (lavas 1 to 7) has no correlative in the NMB. The equivalent of the lavas recording DGT1 at Tiourjdal in the High Atlas Basin (19) is the Tasguint Basalt in the Argana Basin (37) whose basal lava conformably overlies sediments of the uppermost Bigoudine Formation that preserve Chron E23r and have Triassic-aspect sporomorphs that immediately precede the ETE in the Fundy and Newark basins (21).

Tiourjdal DGT6 (lavas #29 to 34, which are undated) is separated from the DGT1, DGT2, and DGT3 sequence by the remagnetized limestone of unknown duration and a single lava assigned to a marginally defined directional group (DTT5) (19) (Fig. 2); the VGP for DGT6 has no known counterpart in other CAMP lavas (Fig. 3). Finally, a fifth directional group (DGOM) is represented by some of the youngest CAMP lavas of HTQ chemistry about 1,000 km south of the Fundy Basin in the OMB of the Newark Basin (Fig. 1). Five published paleomagnetic results from the OMB in the Newark Basin and the homotaxial Talcott Basalt in the Hartford Basin give a tightly grouped overall mean VGP for DGOM (64.3°N 105.2°E A95=1.9°, K = 1,591, N = 5) (Fig. 3) whose low dispersion is consistent with capturing a snapshot of secular variation, perhaps including those recorded by similar directions reported from the Palisade Sill and four other diabase intrusions in the Newark and nearby Gettysburg basins (SI Appendix, Table S4 and section S4).

Statistically indistinguishable paleomagnetic directions ranging over successive rapidly cooled lavas that constitute a DG can be interpreted as indicating an eruptive episode less than about a century in overall duration (e.g., ref. 44). The timing is based by analogy to the documented tempo and amplitude of geomagnetic secular variation in historic times, most directly by the long observatory records from London and Paris that go back about 400 y (43) and show approximately parallel shifts in direction of ~9° over the best-defined part of the records since 1800 CE (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Similar amplitudes and rates of directional change have been measured in archeomagnetic records of the past 3,000 y in western Europe (e.g., ref. 45) with a calculated modal rate of ~2° per century (44). Assuming that characteristics of secular variation were about the same in the geologic past, the total duration for each DG observed in volcanic sequences such as the Deccan Traps (44) would be only a few decades to a century. We apply a similar logic to the CAMP lavas and suggest that the directional groups in Morocco (19) and eastern North America defined here encompass less than a century each. Moreover, in the absence of interbedded sediments or signs of deeper weathering and erosion, the volcanic pulses might be separated by pauses of only a few thousand years as in the case of DGT2 to DGT3 and DGNM1 to DGNM2.

Camp Lavas and the Continental ETE.

Scaled by the relative thickness of HTQ lavas in the section at Tiourjdal (19), more than 2/3 of the volume of HTQ lavas in Morocco were rapidly emplaced during the DGT1, DGT2, and DGT3 directional group sequence (Fig. 2). Evidence for earliest DGT1 is confined to Morocco in lavas that occur in the Central High Atlas Basin and as the lower Tasguint Basalt in the Argana Basin approximately 175 km distant. Assuming these lavas initially covered ~130,000 km2 (46) and were 70-m-thick, the DGT1 eruptive phase would have had a nominal volume of around 9,000 km3. DGT2 (lavas #8 to 22 at Tiourjdal and extending to the upper part of the Tasguint Formation in the Argana basin) covered about the same geographic extent in Morocco and with a somewhat larger nominal thickness of ~100 m (19), would have had an estimated volume of 13,000 km3. According to our analysis of the secular variation data (Fig. 2), extrusive basalts correlative to DGT2 occur in the Fundy Basin of Nova Scotia as DGNM1, which includes the thick initial basalt flow (EFM) and some of the thinner flows of MM of the NMB. For a lateral extent on Pangea of around 800 km and a nominal regional thickness of 100 m, the estimated collective volume of extrusive basalts of DGT2 and DGNM1 correlatives in Morocco and Nova Scotia would be about 26,000 km3; DGT3 and DGNM2 correlatives also in Morocco and Nova Scotia may have had a comparable volume of ~26,000 km3. The geographic extents and volume of lavas corresponding to DGT6 in Morocco and DGOM in the Newark and Hartford basins are more difficult to assess but may have each had nominal volumes of ~10,000 km3.

The large and rapid initial pulses of CAMP volcanism point to the likelihood of significant environmental impacts from associated eruptive degassing of CO2 and especially SO2 that may bear on the ETE (e.g., refs. 47, 48). Based on a broad review (49) of petrological studies used to reconstruct emissions from subaerial basalt eruptions, we use two independent determinations of volatile contents and consequences for general scaling: 1) the mid-Miocene Roza lava unit in the Columbia River flood basalt province, which provided an early published indication of the potential atmospheric SO2 yield from a large flood basalt eruption (50); and 2) the observed environmental effects of aerosols from the 1783 CE Laki eruption of Iceland, one of the largest basaltic lava emplacements in historic times (51, 52). The Roza lava has an estimated volume of 1,300 km3 emplaced over about a decade and a half along a ~150 km-long vent system (50). The Laki eruption has a comparatively small 14.7 km3 volume emplaced over 8 months along a 27 km fissure (53). Despite the large difference in their volumes, the Roza and Laki eruptive bodies were emplaced at roughly comparable rates of ~0.8 km3/y per km of fissure length (54). This emplacement rate would yield a total of ~6,400 to 9,600 km3 of lava for eruptions of DGT1 if they extended over a fissure length of ~400 km (distance between Bigoudine to Oujda) for several decades, in general agreement with the volume (~9,000 km3) estimated from nominal surface area and thickness.

Basaltic magmas release about 14 Mt CO2 per km3 (55) corresponding to a nominal CO2 melt concentration of ~0.5 wt% (56) as also measured in CAMP bubble-bearing melt inclusions (57). Practically unique among large continental flood basalt provinces, the amount of CO2 released by major phases of CAMP activity associated with lava emplacements can be directly constrained from pedogenic carbonate data in interbedded sediments. According to paleobarometry measurements on sediments bracketing the OMB in the Newark Basin (58) and using a revised smaller value of Sz of 2,000 (59), the entire HTQ volcanic pulse is associated with an increase of atmospheric pCO2 from ~1,200 to 3,000 ppm; this is equivalent to a minimum addition of about 14,000 Gt CO2 to the atmosphere and does not take into account rapid drawdown by the oceans and continental silicate weathering (e.g., ref. 60). Known CAMP lavas apparently cannot account for even this amount of CO2 (but see ref. 46). Even doubling the estimated volume of basalt ascribed to the initial two eruptive phases of CAMP (DGT1 and DGT2/DGNM1: 9,000 + 26,000=35,000 × 2 = 70,000 km3) to roughly account for the final three eruptive phases of HTQ activity (i.e., DGT3/DGNM2, DGT6, and DGOM) and then increasing the volume estimate by ~50% to 100,000 km3 to factor in any HTQ lavas emplaced on what is now the Nova Scotia continental shelf, for example, in the Jeanne d’Arc and Flemish Pass basins (61), would yield only about 1,400 Gt CO2. It would require an extreme “broad terrane” scenario for CAMP extrusive basalts (27) that might invoke something like a 100-m-thick lava pulse emplaced over the entire 10 Mkm2 CAMP footprint for a total volume of ~1 Mkm3 of extrusive basalt to generate a mass of CO2 (14,000 Gt) closer to the bare minimum required. Not included in our estimates is the potentially huge inventory of lavas representing the seaward dipping reflectors along the continental margin of eastern North America (27, 62) due to the lack of direct geochronological constraints and because modeling of the associated East Coast Magnetic Anomaly indicates their emplacement may have taken as long as 31 Myr (63). Additional sources of CO2 therefore must include intrusive CAMP activity (40), including crystallization of pre-eruption intrusions (64) or thermal metamorphism of organic matter by sills (60). But a more fundamental problem with postulating CO2 emissions as an agent of environmental change directly responsible for the ETE is that the available albeit limited data allow for only a modest pCO2 increase just prior to or at the ETE (58).

Erupting basaltic magmas also release about ~7 Mt SO2 per km3 of lava (55), including measurements on CAMP rocks (65). The SO2 vented during explosive phases can be lofted in eruption columns high in the atmosphere where sulfate aerosols are produced and dispersed in plumes to distal regions (66). The strong albedo effect of sulfate aerosols is what can give even relatively small volcanic eruptions a strong environmental punch, for example, the 1991 CE eruption of Pinatubo (67). The 1783 CE Laki eruption is a larger case in point, being associated with a dry fog or haze in North America and Europe and widespread crop failures even though emissions constituted only an estimated ~100 Mt SO2 over the course of its 8-mo activity (53). Given the short (<5-y) suspension lifespans of sulfate aerosols in the atmosphere (68), eruptions of earliest CAMP lavas of DGT1 that occurred virtually coincident with the ETE level are of particular interest. With an estimated volume of ~9,000 km3, these DGT1 lavas may have injected ~63,000 Mt SO2 into the atmosphere over the course of 7 to 10 eruptive episodes spanning less than a century according to paleosecular variation constraints. The estimated total SO2 contribution may have been more than 500 times greater and with broader dispersal from tropical paleolatitudes (55) than documented for the 1783 CE Laki eruption from near-Arctic latitudes of Iceland. Modeling shows that outgassing of CO2 and SO2 during pulsed extrusive activity such as described for CAMP may have exacerbated environmental stresses from climate swings on decadal to millennial timescales (68–70).

ETE in the Marine Realm.

CAMP lavas in superposition with fossiliferous marine strata are rare, and the few occurrences have yet to be studied in any detail (38). The relative timing of CAMP activity in well-calibrated marine sections thus has been inferred using proxies such as stable carbon isotopes (71), or anomalous concentrations of mercury (72) or platinum-group elements (73). Moreover, it is also apparent that there is more than a single recorded episode of marine extinctions (e.g., ref. 74). For example, the section at St. Audrie’s Bay in southwest England is a widely used template for global correlation of end-Triassic events in the marine realm with an organic carbon isotope stratigraphy providing an iconic signature of environmental change over at least regional extent (31) (Fig. 4). Major changes in spore, pollen, and dinoflagellate fossil assemblages within the Cotham Member of the Lilstock Formation occur just above the prominent “initial” negative carbon isotope excursion (CIE) (31). Conodonts are present in the overlying Langport Member of the Lilstock Formation (75) where their eventual disappearance marks an important ETE component that occurs after the changes in local pollen and spore assemblages. However, the disappearance of conodonts at the Kuhjoch stratotype for the Tr-J boundary in Austria occurs just below what is identified as the CIE (Fig. 27 in ref. 76) and thus before changes in the pollen and spore assemblages.

The LO of Choristoceras crickmayi is a proxy of the ETE in the marine realm and has been dated at 201.51±0.15 Ma also in the Pucara Group of Peru (12, 78). The LO of C. crickmayi is projected into this section although the ammonite does not occur in the St. Audrie’s section (or anywhere in the United Kingdom). The palynological ETE in continental sediments has a similar estimated U-Pb-calibrated age of 215.5 Ma [(13); see text], in good agreement with projection to the pollen-spore-dinoflagellate turnover level at about the 12.5 m within the Cotham Member.

A caveat for global correlation in the general absence of a robust record of the short Chron E23r in marine sections is that the nominal ETE interval in the Tethyan realm is often accompanied by a marked facies change. Prominent examples are the exposure surfaces truncating large desiccation cracks at about the change in pollen and spore assemblages within the Cotham Member at St. Audrie’s Bay (31, 32), the 2-m-thick reddish silty clays of the Schattwald beds (79) deposited during the peak of a regression that abruptly overlie the thin (20 cm) gray-colored clays of the so-called T-bed in the uppermost Kössen Formation in the Kössen Basin of Austria (80), and the so-called Triletes beds, a meters-thick unit of claystones, sometimes partly red, rich mainly in fern spores in the uppermost Keuper (Exter Formation) of the Germanic Basin with characteristic latest Rhaetian pollen and dinoflagellate cysts (81). Local changes in the ecosystem structure of organisms with the shift in facies from shallow marine to nonmarine and back to marine plausibly influenced the local organic carbon isotope profile, with the consequence that the various CIEs may not be a global signal (82).

Although the Kuhjoch section is tectonized and remagnetized (76), a tentative magnetic polarity stratigraphy has been established for the St. Audrie’s Bay and East Quantoxhead sections (32, 77). Our working chronostratigraphic framework based on the N-H APTS (35) for the ETE and Tr-J boundary interval (Fig. 4) correlates the polarity change from magnetozone SA4Ar to SA5n in the upper Blue Anchor Formation to the Chron E21r/E22n boundary (203.41 Ma), magnetozone SA5n.1r in the Westbury Formation to Chron E22r (203.30 to 202.47 Ma), and magnetozone AQ1r in the Blue Lias Formation at 74.44 m above the base of the Williton Member with an onset age of 199.93 Ma (77) to Chron H24r. This scheme implies there are three reported magnetozones bracketing the Lilstock Formation that are candidates for Chron E23r: magnetozone SA5n.2r defined by one sampling level in the uppermost Westbury Formation, magnetozone SA5n.3r by one sampling level in the lowermost Cotham Member of the Lilstock Formation, and SA5r by two contiguous sampling levels in the lowermost Blue Lias Formation. Taking magnetozone SA5n.3r as a possible correlative of Chron E23r, this framework suggests an increase of sediment accumulation rate from less than 5 m/Myr in the Westbury and upper Blue Anchor formations to around 38 m/Myr in the Blue Lias Formation that is in good agreement with independent cycle stratigraphic analysis of this unit (77). The choice of which (if any) of the closely spaced candidate magnetozones reported from the challenging low-resolution St. Audrie’s Bay record might record Chron E23r does not appreciably affect the overall sediment accumulation rate pattern because the inflection stays broadly associated with the Lilstock Formation. The pattern also happens to parallel an inflection to higher sediment accumulation rates in the earliest Jurassic of the Newark and Hartford basins (35). As checks on the overall age model, we note that the last occurrence (LO) of the ammonite C. crickmayi as a proxy of the ETE in the marine realm has been dated at 201.51±0.15 Ma in the volcanic ash-bearing Pucara Group of Peru (12, 78). This is in general agreement with the palynological ETE in continental sediments and its projection to the pollen-spore-dinoflagellate turnover level at about the 12.5 m level within the Cotham Member (Fig. 4). We note that the first occurrence (FO) of P. planorbis, which approximates the definitional Tr-J boundary (76) at about the 20 m-level and for which a close proxy (FO P. spelaea) has been U-Pb dated at 201.36±0.17 Ma in the Pucara Group (12, 78), is also consistent with the age model.

There are ample complexities that make interpretation of the ETE in European marine sections problematic. Soft sediment deformation features regarded as seismites (83) are present just below the exposure (desiccation) surface and CIE in the Cotham Member at St. Audrie’s Bay (31). Similar features are found across a broad swath of Europe just beyond the northern extent of exposed CAMP rocks and attributed to intense earthquake activity accompanying magmatic intrusions during initial stages of distal CAMP emplacement (84). An associated ground swell from tectonic doming (85) has been proposed to explain apparent sea-level regressions (74, 86), which could have produced the exposure surface in the Cotham Member and deposition elsewhere of regressive facies such as the Schattwald Beds at Kuhjoch. The apparent persistence of conodonts into strata of the immediately overlying Langport Member at numerous localities and at two localities reported as basal Blue Lias where their last occurrence is recorded (87), thereby plausibly postdate the pollen-spore assemblage turnover near the exposure surface in the Cotham Member (31, 32) that could correspond to the ETE in the Newark and Fundy basins. At Kuhjoch, the last occurrences of conodonts and the guide ammonite Choristoceras marshi are in the so-called T-bed in the uppermost Kössen Formation (76) and close to the base of the Rhaetipollis–Porcellispora Zone of the immediately overlying Schattwald Beds (88). The apparent asynchronous disappearance of conodonts (89) may be due to preservation differences whereby CAMP-related tectonic shoaling at Kuhjoch may have been somewhat earlier (Schattwald Beds) than at St. Audrie’s Bay. However, lacking independent age constraints such as Chron E23r or high-resolution radioisotopic dates, the apparently conflicting marine biostratigraphic and exposure data cannot be currently resolved.

Discussion

We concur that the continental ETE occurs virtually at the contact with the initial pulse (DGT1) of CAMP lavas in Morocco (19) because the immediately underlying sediments do not record the characteristic fern spike yet do record Chron E23r (21). Our analysis of paleosecular variation data, moreover, suggests that the lavas of the lower part of the NMB (DGNM1) are correlative to the 2nd directional group of lavas in Morocco (DGT2), which together constitute a volumetrically dominant pulse of early CAMP volcanism. This and the immediately following pulse (North Mountain DGNM2 and correlative Morocco DGT3) demonstrably erupted after the terrestrial ETE recorded in sediments underlying the NMB in the Fundy Basin (23) that also record Chron E23r (22). Fundy NMB DGNM1 and Argana DGT3 (Alemzi Basalt and its feeder the Amelal sill) have some of the oldest CAMP lavas dated with high-precision U-Pb zircon geochronology [201.566 ± 0.031 Ma and 201.564 ± 0.030 Ma, respectively (13)], which together place a strong minimum age constraint (i.e., older than ~201.565 Ma) on the continental ETE in the underlying sediments. Lavas of DGT1 have not been dated, but the difference of ~21° arc distance in the mean VGP with respect to that of DGT2 could be accounted for by as little as ~1,000 y of secular variation.

Further constraints on the relative age of the ETE can be obtained from the orbitally paced cycle stratigraphy of the uppermost Passaic Formation in the Newark Basin using the thickness of Chron E23r (8.3 m) with respect to the thickness of the overlying strata to the ETE (6.3 m) (Fig. 5). The thickness ratio is 1.32, which for an estimated duration from cycle stratigraphy of 26 kyr for Chron E23r would imply the ETE occurred ~20 kyr afterward. In the Fundy Basin, Chron E23r is much thinner (from 1.45 to 0.6 m levels = 0.85 m below the OMB) but so is the stratum up to the ETE at the 0.2 m stratigraphic level (22), giving a thickness ratio (1.31) and hence relative timing (~20 kyr) that is practically identical to the Newark Basin. And in the Argana Basin, Chron E23r extends from 2.4 to 1 m (1.4 m thickness) below the contact with the initial CAMP lava and the presumed ETE, giving a ratio of 1.4 and hence a relative timing (~19 kyr younger) remarkably like the Newark and Fundy basins, as was already deduced and anticipated (21, 22). Scaled by the local thickness of Chron E23r, the 0.2 m stratigraphic difference in the Blomidon Formation between the ETE and the overlying NMB amounts to about 6,000 y with large relative uncertainty, suggesting that the Argana lavas of DGT1 are older than the North Mountain lavas of DGNM1 by roughly this amount. In the Newark Basin, there is a span of 11 m from the ETE to the OMB, which assuming the same accumulation rate (32 cm/kyr) we used for Chron E23r is equivalent to a ~34 kyr younger age for the OMB relative to the oldest dated CAMP lavas (i.e., 201.57 Ma adjusted age for Argana lavas of DGT1), which is compatible within quoted analytical uncertainties to the 201.520±0.030 Ma date for the OMB (13). However, this coherent chronology is in apparent conflict with recently adjusted U-Pb dates that purportedly decrease the NMB (DGNM1) date of 201.566 ± 0.031 Ma (13) by more than 100 kyr to 201.464 ± 0.017 Ma (90), making it significantly younger by 58 kyr than their adjusted date for the OMB (DGOM) at 201.522±0.033 Ma. This disagrees with the independent cycle-stratigraphic ordering of events. Similar “adjustments” make previously indistinguishable and independent ages of the Amalel sill from the same site (201.564 ± 0.054 vs. 201.569 ± 0.042 Ma) differ by more than a nominal 100 kyr (201.574 ± 0.054 vs. 201.462 ± 0.064 Ma) [(13) vs. (90)]. We prefer the internal consistency of the high-precision U-Pb dates of ref. 13 to estimate relative ages and calibrate the N-H APTS (35).

The general correspondence between the ETE and onset of CAMP extrusions established by superposition in the terrestrial realm supports widely held conjectures that the emplacements of large continental flood basalt provinces produce environmental perturbations that trigger major biotic crises usually considered to result from release of CO2 and other greenhouse gases (e.g., refs. 40, 46, 60, 91, 92) or, more provocatively, from sulfate (SO2) aerosols (e.g., refs. 2, 3, 6, 70). CO2 outgassing would need to be sufficiently large and sustained to significantly perturb the already CO2-rich ocean-atmosphere system that existed just prior to CAMP activity (93), whereas establishing a precise timing between the volcanism and record of extinctions is of the essence to demonstrate a plausible environmental forcing from SO2 outgassing given the very short residence time of albedo-enhancing sulfate aerosols in the atmosphere and lack of proxies (67, 94). Some of the most expanded sections of the end-Triassic interval occur in the Newark Basin, such as the Jacksonwald syncline (17). The continental ETE is recorded there as a brief, singular biostratigraphic and environmental perturbation that occurred before the locally earliest CAMP lavas of the Orange Mountain Basalt with no evidence of any other notable biotic events from at least the end of Chron E23r for 20 kyr to the ETE and then 34 kyr to the base of the OMB (Fig. 5). The same distinct palynofloral ETE occurs ~6 kyr before the earliest member of the North Mountain Basalt, whereas the initial CAMP extrusive pulse in Morocco coincides with the ETE (21). In the case of North Mountain Basalt pulses DGNM1 and DGNM2 in Nova Scotia (and correlatives DGT2 and DGT3 in Morocco), the palynofloral assemblage of the basal Scots Bay Member of the McCoy Brook Formation immediately above the NMB is very different than that of the Blomidon Formation immediately below the NMB, with only 2 of the 26 listed palynoflora species (24) in common and with a very low percentage of Classopollis. The palynofloral change has nonetheless been regarded as showing little difference in age between the post-ETE in the uppermost Blomidon Formation (see also ref. 23) and the McCoy Brook Formation with no mass extinction. This is because the same basic microfloral assemblage as seen in the post-ETE Blomidon assemblage, dominated by Classopollis, returns in strata above the basal Scots Bay Member of the McCoy Brook Formation (95, 96). A detailed comparison of palynofloras of the uppermost Passaic Formation and lowermost Feltville Formation might prove useful to determine whether the intervening and rapidly emplaced Orange Mountain Basalt produced any marked change in assemblages.

In terms of direct environmental effects of SO2 outgassing, the most vulnerable taxa may already have succumbed in the repeated extreme volcanic “winters” produced at the ETE by the earliest CAMP (DGT1) lavas in Morocco so that subsequent and sometimes even larger CAMP eruptive pulses such as DGT2 and DGT3 in Morocco, and correlatives DGNM1 and DGNM2 in Nova Scotia, did not leave a strong imprint on surviving continental biota. Our favored interpretation contrasts with those that have effects more directly attributable to CAMP CO2 outgassing as the favored agent of environmental change associated with the end-Triassic biotic events (e.g., refs. 40, 46, 57, 60, 91, 92). CO2 outgassing may more simply explain effects from cumulative rather than immediate introduction especially when the amount of which, unlike that of sulfate aerosols, can be constrained by measurable proxies. In this regard, stratigraphically constrained pedogenic carbonate data (58) indicate that the initial HTQ phase was associated with a minimum addition of about 14,000 Gt CO2. However, combined HTQ basalt lava volume (Morocco plus Fundy, Hartford, and Newark basins, plus basins on the Nova Scotia continental shelf) of roughly 100,000 km3 would yield only ~1,400 Gt CO2, about 10% of what is required even for an instantaneous atmospheric loading. Fluxes of CO2 may nevertheless have been high even if not sustained with the short timing of volcanic pulses. For example, an eruptive pulse of ~10,000 km3 of lavas (e.g., DGT1) spread over up to a century might result in a CO2 flux of ~1.4 GT CO2/y, which would be more than five times greater than the present-day background global magmatic flux of around 0.26 Gt CO2/y (97).

To account for the needed amount of CO2 to satisfy the pedogenic carbonate constraint, much of the required CO2 must have been generated by a large volume of CAMP intrusions and perhaps thermogenic devolatilization of sediments that outgassed over a more prolonged time interval bracketing the continental ETE. For example, a large-scale sill emplacement in Brazil was dated at 201.477 ± 0.062 Ma (91), whereas the Kakoulima layered mafic intrusive in Guinea now has one of the oldest reported high-precision U-Pb dates for CAMP magmatism at 201.635 ± 0.029 Ma (40) and joins other CAMP intrusions that could nominally predate the earliest dated CAMP eruptions, for example, the Messejana dike of Spain (201.585 ± 0.034 Ma) and Tarabuco sill of Bolivia (201.612 ± 0.046 Ma) (40). Moreover, the Messejana dike has two sampling sites with reverse polarity (98), suggestive of magnetization acquisition during cooling of these parts of the dike during Chron E23r (201.574 to 201.600 Ma; Table 1), the only confirmed reverse polarity interval within about a million years of the ETE.

Extinctions in the marine realm, such as conodonts, have yet to be shown to coincide with the palynological ETE and may have been more influenced by the cumulative and longer-term environmental effects of prolonged CO2 emissions from CAMP activity, such as marine anoxia and ocean acidification (74). Perhaps a case can even be made that adaptations to CO2 warming made biota more vulnerable to the shocks of onset and termination of cooling that abruptly ensued with SO2 emissions from the initial pulse of lavas that triggered the terrestrial ETE. The repeated and intense “volcanic winters” may have directly led to the ecological rise of feathered (insulated) dinosaurs that were already acclimated to cooler conditions at higher latitudes, whereas the tropics had been dominated by large uninsulated reptiles such as pseudosuchians, which would have had more limited options to survive incursions of CAMP volcanic winters (99) and thus been prone to extinction.

Conclusions

The initial (HTQ) phase of CAMP volcanism extended over nominally 46 kyr with comparable analytical uncertainty from U-Pb geochronology (i.e., NMB at 201.566 ± 0.031 Ma to OMB at 201.520 ± 0.030 Ma) but was emplaced in only four to five main pulses each lasting less than about a century according to published directional groupings of paleomagnetic secular variation data from Morocco and our analysis including data from eastern North America. The known close coincidence of the earliest CAMP pulse of volcanism with the ETE in Morocco points to intense volcanic winters from massive sulfate aerosols repeatedly injected into the atmosphere within a century as a plausible proximate cause of the end-Triassic mass extinction event in the continental realm. Published estimates of atmospheric pCO2 from pedogenic carbonate data in bounding sediments indicate that the increase associated with the initial HTQ phase of volcanism requires a minimum addition of CO2 that is more than an order of magnitude larger than can be attributed to even an instantaneous release from the estimated total HTQ basalt lava volume; moreover, the available albeit limited sample data do not show a resolvable increase in atmospheric pCO2 immediately prior to or at the continental ETE. The required volume of CO2 to account for the measured atmospheric increase across the initial (HTQ) phase of CAMP activity was more likely generated by CAMP intrusions and thermogenically devolatized sediments. The large and prolonged CO2 emissions likely produced oceanic acidification and anoxia as longer-term agents of environmental stress that contributed to extinctions in the marine realm but which were not necessarily closely coincident in timing with what appears to be a singular and abrupt continental extinction event. Widespread soft-sediment deformation features (seismites) in north-central Pangea have been associated with initial emplacement of CAMP magmas that may have produced sea-level regressions from tectonic doming culminating locally with exposure surfaces. Precise chronostratigraphic placement of marine extinctions in the European realm may thus be influenced by preservation and facies changes that could be usefully constrained by eventual detailed delineations of Chron E23r where its record has not been disturbed by seismites.

Based on superposition, the continental ETE occurs at the onset of the ~46 kyr-long initial phase of CAMP, in contrast to the end-Permian and end-Cretaceous extinction events that are dated to occur several 100 kyr after the onset of their respective hypothesized continental flood basalt triggers, the Siberian and Decan traps. The coincidence in timing with the emplacement of sills in the massive Siberian Traps has suggested that the end-Permian extinctions, especially those documented in the marine realm, were likely triggered by massive CO2 emissions. The end-Cretaceous extinction event, however, is also precisely linked with the huge Chicxulub asteroid impact crater with a massive ejection of dust and aerosols producing a deep and prolonged impact winter, perhaps not dissimilar to the net effect of rapidly repeated volcanic winters postulated from the onset of CAMP lavas triggering the ETE.

Materials and Methods

A total of 180 oriented samples were collected from 37 sites in natural outcrops; 6 sites were distributed in the lower unit (EFM), 11 sites in the upper unit (BIF), and 20 sites in what was mapped by Kontak (39) as the middle unit (MM). About one-half (18) of the sites were sampled using a 25-mm-diameter diamond bit in a hand-held gasoline-powered drill, whereas the remaining 19 sites were sampled with a 19-mm-diameter diamond bit in a cordless drill, both with water swivels. All samples were oriented with a magnetic compass; the magnetic variation was 18.5° W over the study area. A regional dip of 4° toward the NW (330°) was assumed for tilt correction. A total of 138 19-mm-diameter sample plugs were also taken perpendicular to the axis of Getty Mines azimuthally unoriented core GAV77-3 that contains a 210-m section of the North Mountain Basalt. Further sampling details and description of measurement and analytical procedures are described in SI Appendix.
