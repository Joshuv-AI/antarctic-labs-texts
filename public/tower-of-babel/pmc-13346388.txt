Association between ranitidine use and thyroid cancer based on FAERS and VigiAccess databases
Authors: Qingli Shen, Zhonghua Fu, Zhengchao Xia, Yinhui Qin, Chenglong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe potential carcinogenic risk of ranitidine has sparked widespread controversy, and its relationship with thyroid cancer remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the disproportionality signal for thyroid cancer reporting associated with ranitidine by comprehensively utilizing the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and VigiAccess databases.MethodsThe data were sourced from the FAERS database and the VigiAccess database. Initially, data mining and statistical analyses were performed on ranitidine-related thyroid cancer events within the FAERS database to detect and characterize disproportionality signals. Subsequently, the findings were further supplemented using the VigiAccess database to provide additional supportive evidence.ResultsA disproportionality signal was identified between ranitidine and thyroid cancer (ROR = 40.60, 95% CI: 38.80–42.49, P < 0.05), with VigiAccess providing supplementary corroboration. Time-to-onset analysis showed higher reporting frequency of thyroid cancer with longer ranitidine exposure duration.ConclusionDespite certain limitations, our findings indicate a disproportionality signal suggesting a potential association between ranitidine use and thyroid cancer reporting in pharmacovigilance databases. In light of these findings, the disproportionality signal suggests a potential risk requiring confirmation through prospective studies; pending such validation, clinicians should exercise caution when prescribing ranitidine for extended periods, with attention to thyroid monitoring.Keywords: Ranitidine, Thyroid cancer, FAERS, VigiAccess, Risk factor

Introduction

Ranitidine is an H2-receptor antagonist commonly used in the treatment of gastric acid-related disorders such as gastroesophageal reflux disease, gastric ulcers, and duodenal ulcers. It alleviates stomach discomfort by inhibiting the secretion of gastric acid. However, ranitidine has been recalled and banned in several countries worldwide in recent years due to its potential carcinogenic risk, particularly associated with N-Nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA) contamination, which has raised significant concerns [1–3].

The issue of the carcinogenic risk associated with ranitidine is highly controversial. Ranitidine can metabolize to produce the class 2 A carcinogen NDMA [4], but the actual extent of this risk for ranitidine users remains uncertain. Some studies have indicated that ranitidine may elevate the risk of cancers including gastrointestinal, bladder, liver, lung, and pancreatic cancers [5–7], while others have found no direct correlation between ranitidine use and cancer risk [8–10].

While these studies offer insights into the potential cancer risks associated with ranitidine, the relationship between ranitidine and thyroid cancer remains poorly understood. To thoroughly investigate the potential impact of ranitidine on thyroid cancer and to elucidate its safety profile, we conducted a comprehensive analysis using the FAERS and VigiAccess databases. The FAERS database, a critical pharmacovigilance tool, offers extensive real-world data that can be utilized to analyze reporting patterns and disproportionality signals between drugs and adverse events [11, 12]. VigiAccess is a publicly accessible database developed and managed by the World Health Organization. It provides aggregated data on adverse drug event (ADE) reports, aiming to enhance awareness of drug safety among the public and professionals [13]. By integrating these two databases, we aimed to provide a more comprehensive and robust understanding of the potential disproportionality signal for thyroid cancer reporting associated with ranitidine.

The objective of this study was to comprehensively analyze the disproportionality signal for thyroid cancer reporting associated with ranitidine using data from the FAERS and VigiAccess databases, in order to inform hypothesis generation for subsequent safety evaluation.

Materials and methods

Data source

The FAERS database, which is publicly accessible and downloadable from the official FDA website, was utilized in this study. Data spanning from the third quarter of 2014 to the first quarter of 2024 were leveraged for analysis. The chosen timeframe facilitates a thorough investigation into the potential associations between ranitidine and thyroid cancer. VigiAccess data can be accessed via https://www.vigiaccess.org, as retrieved on May 18, 2024. Users can directly enter the generic name of a drug to obtain relevant ADE reports.

FAERS data processing

This study utilized Structured Query Language (SQL) Server 2022 software for the quality control and analysis of FAERS data. We employed the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) version 26.1 to categorize and delineate ADE signals based on Preferred Terms (PTs). PTs serve as specialized terms denoting the precise expression of a singular medical concept, encompassing diseases, symptoms, diagnoses, indications, and medical, social, or family histories.

The data underwent a series of rigorous processing steps to ensure integrity and quality control. The data standardization procedures were conducted as follows [14]: (1) Data integration. The DEMO, DRUG, INDI, OUTC, REAC, and THER tables were integrated using “primaryid” as the primary key. (2) Standardization of PT. The PTs in the data contain errors such as spelling mistakes or being reported in Lowest Level Terms, necessitating normalization. (3) Normalization of age, gender, and weight. Inconsistencies in age units have been standardized to years to facilitate streamlined processing. Gender data have been categorized into three groups: Female, Male, and Unknown. Weight units have been normalized to kilograms. (4) Elimination of duplicate entries. To ensure data integrity, we implemented a three-step process for eliminating duplicates. First, all duplicate rows were removed from the dataset to prevent redundant counting of the same reports. Second, we retained reports with the latest date when the “caseid” column was identical. Third, we employed a group-based deduplication approach, progressively removing one element at a time to ensure comprehensive data cleaning. This approach ensures minimal duplication of ADE reports, thereby enhancing the quality and accuracy of the dataset.

Our focus centered on the primary suspect (PS) drugs, which represent the medications most strongly associated with ADEs, whether administered as monotherapy or as part of combination drug therapy. Then we filtered the ADEs related to ranitidine based on whether the “prod_ai” (active ingredient) column equals “Ranitidine”. We defined the PTs encompassing thyroid cancer as follows: anaplastic thyroid cancer, follicular thyroid cancer, huerthle cell carcinoma, medullary thyroid cancer, poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, papillary thyroid cancer, thyroid cancer metastatic, thyroid cancer (PT), thyroid cancer stage 0, thyroid cancer stage I, thyroid cancer stage II, thyroid cancer stage III, and thyroid cancer stage IV.

Statistical analysis of FAERS data

Positive ADE signals were screened by Reporting Odds Ratio (ROR), Proportional Reporting Ratio (PRR), Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN) and Multiple Item Empirical Bayesian Gamma Poisson Shrinker (MGPS) methods [15–18].The formulas and screening criteria for the four methods are presented in Table 1. An ADE signal was considered significant if it met the criteria of a ≥ 3 and was consistent across all four methods simultaneously. Higher values of ROR and PRR indicate a stronger signal, suggesting a more pronounced disproportionality signal between the target drug and the specified adverse event.

Subsequently, an analysis of the event timelines was conducted based on thyroid cancer events associated with ranitidine. Additionally, logistic regression analysis was performed to preliminarily explore the risk factors for ranitidine-related thyroid cancer events [19]. Statistical analysis and plotting were conducted using R version 4.4.0 programming software.

Supplementary signal assessment

The results supplementary assessment was conducted in two steps: (1) Monitoring the number of thyroid cancer events associated with ranitidine using the VigiAccess database. However, since VigiAccess and FAERS both include spontaneous reports and may share overlapping cases, the validation should be regarded as supplementary rather than independent confirmation. (2) Assessing whether similar drugs such as cimetidine, famotidine, nizatidine, roxatidine, and lafutidine are associated with thyroid cancer events using both the FAERS and VigiAccess databases, and calculating the combined ROR and PRR for ranitidine in comparison.

Results

The clinical information of ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events

A total of 3663 ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events were identified in this study, accounting for 1.93% of all ranitidine-associated ADEs (Table 2). In terms of gender, ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events were 1.83 times more common in women than in men (58.97% vs. 32.24%). Regarding age distribution, the majority of cases were concentrated in patients aged 45–65 years (48.89%) and 18–45 years (25.96%). Geographically, the reported cases were predominantly from the United States (96.56%). Regarding indications, the most frequent were dyspepsia, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and abdominal discomfort. In terms of clinical outcomes, the mortality rate for ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events was lower than that of all ranitidine-associated ADEs (2.24% vs. 8.72%).

Ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer signals

After mining and screening ADE signals, a total of 11 thyroid cancer signals were identified, excluding anaplastic thyroid cancer and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma, which had zero reported cases (Table 3; Fig. 1). Overall, the ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer signal was significant (ROR = 40.60, PRR = 39.83, P < 0.05). It is important to note that the reported events constituted 48.59% of all thyroid cancer cases within FAERS; however, this proportion should not be interpreted as reflecting the actual incidence in the general population. Rather, it likely reflects reporting bias and stimulated reporting following the ranitidine recall. A total of 6 positive signals were detected for thyroid cancer (PT), thyroid cancer stage 0, thyroid cancer stage I, thyroid cancer stage II, thyroid cancer stage III, and thyroid cancer stage IV. The proportion of reported events corresponding to each of these PT categories was notably high.

Preliminary exploration of risk factors

The results of the logistic regression analysis for ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events are presented in Table 4. The univariate regression analysis results indicated that women had a significantly higher risk of experiencing ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events compared to men with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.20 (P < 0.05). Regarding age, using the 45–65 age group, which had the highest number of thyroid cancer events, as the reference, the risk of thyroid cancer events was significantly higher in the 18–45 age group (OR = 2.14, P < 0.05). In contrast, the risk was significantly lower in the 65–85 and ≥ 85 age groups compared to the 45–65 age group (P < 0.05). Body weight showed a positive association with the risk of thyroid cancer events (OR = 1.01, P < 0.05). In terms of the route of administration, 632 thyroid cancer events were reported exclusively for the oral route.

The results demonstrated that the impact of gender on thyroid cancer was no longer statistically significant. The OR was 1.76 (P = 0.317) in the 18–45 age group and 0.70 (P = 0.579) in the 65–85 age group, while data for other age groups were not included in the multivariable analysis. Time to onset analysis of ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events

The time to onset analysis of ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events revealed that 2.16% of these events occurred within the first year of ranitidine use, with a gradual increase thereafter (Fig. 2). The percentage of thyroid cancer events increased to 8.74% within 1–3 years and 10.48% within 3–5 years, indicating a gradual rise over time. After 5 and 10 years of ranitidine use, the percentage of thyroid cancer events increased to 18.65% and 45.07%, respectively. This suggests that longer ranitidine exposure may be associated with a higher reporting frequency of thyroid cancer events. Combined ROR and PRR values

Thyroid cancer events associated with ranitidine analogs-cimetidine, famotidine, nizatidine, roxatidine, and lafutidine were monitored using both the FAERS and VigiAccess databases. However, no thyroid cancer events related to these analogs were detected in the FAERS database. The VigiAccess database identified two reports: one involving cimetidine associated with thyroid cancer III, and another involving famotidine associated with thyroid cancer (PT). The absence of signals for other H2RAs may reflect lower prescription volumes, shorter market duration, or differential reporting patterns rather than inherent safety differences. In the VigiAccess database, the combined ROR for ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer was 1158.78 (95% CI: 289.73–4634.50), with a PRR of 1116.96 (χ2 = 2291.75). In both databases, thyroid cancer events were reported more frequently in patients using ranitidine. The high combined ROR and PRR values indicate a significantly stronger disproportionality signal for ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer reporting compared to other similar drugs (P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study aimed to characterize disproportionality signals between ranitidine and thyroid cancer using FAERS and VigiAccess data. Our findings inform hypothesis generation but highlight methodological constraints that require prospective validation before any policy considerations.

Ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer signal

This study identified a disproportionality signal for thyroid cancer reporting associated with ranitidine using FAERS data (ROR = 40.60, PRR = 39.83, P < 0.05). The reported events constituted 48.59% of all thyroid cancer cases within FAERS; however, this proportion reflects reporting bias and stimulated reporting following the 2020 recall rather than true epidemiological incidence. It should be emphasized that ranitidine is not prescribed for thyroid cancer treatment; this study examines a potential safety signal for thyroid cancer reporting as an adverse outcome.

Six statistically significant positive signals were detected across all thyroid cancer stages (P < 0.05), with VigiAccess providing supplementary validation (note: potential case overlap between databases). The high ROR and PRR values compared to other H2RAs likely reflect ranitidine’s widespread use and subsequent recall generating stimulated reporting, rather than inherent differential risk.

However, this signal requires careful interpretation. You et al. [8] found no association in 1.2 million patients (HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.84–1.24); Kantor et al. [9] and Joung et al. [10] similarly reported no increased risk in UK Biobank and Korean population studies, respectively. This inconsistency likely reflects limitations of spontaneous reporting systems—including stimulated reporting and NDMA concerns—whereas well-designed cohort studies control for confounding. The signal may reflect NDMA contamination rather than ranitidine’s pharmacological action.

In conclusion, disproportionality analysis generates hypotheses requiring prospective confirmation. Clinicians should consider thyroid monitoring in long-term ranitidine users, recognizing that routine screening is not universally warranted pending validation.

Potential mechanistic pathways

This study identified a potential disproportionality signal for thyroid cancer reporting associated with ranitidine use. The underlying mechanism may involve chronic inflammation and nutritional metabolic disturbances. Cancer cells characteristically exhibit altered energy metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, creating a microenvironment conducive to genomic instability [20]. Inflammation and malnutrition synergistically shape a tumor-permissive microenvironment [21], while inflammatory markers and immune-nutritional indices serve as independent prognostic factors in malignancies [22, 23]. Ranitidine or its degradation product NDMA may indirectly alter thyroid cellular homeostasis via gut microbiota modulation, interference with fatty acid synthesis and nutrient absorption, and induction of chronic systemic inflammation [24–26]. Precise pathways require further elucidation through molecular epidemiological and experimental studies.

Preliminary exploration of risk factors

Our study conducted a preliminary investigation into the risk factors for thyroid cancer events associated with ranitidine use, utilizing logistic regression analysis. Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed several important findings. Firstly, gender emerged as a notable risk factor, with females showing a significantly higher risk of thyroid cancer compared to males (OR = 2.20, P < 0.05). This observation aligns with the broader epidemiological pattern where females typically exhibit a higher incidence of thyroid cancer [27]. Secondly, the risk of thyroid cancer was notably higher in the 18–45 age group compared to the 45–65 age group (OR = 2.14, P < 0.05), while it was significantly lower in the 65–85 age group and the ≥ 85 age group (P < 0.05). This variation may be attributed to physiological and hormonal differences, immune system function, and disparities in medical testing and diagnostics across different age cohorts. This age-dependent risk profile underscores the importance of accounting for age-specific vulnerabilities when evaluating cancer risk among ranitidine users. Weight showed a positive association with the reporting of thyroid cancer events, with an OR of 1.01 (P < 0.05), suggesting a potential link between higher body weight and increased reporting frequency of thyroid cancer events, warranting further investigation.

In multivariate logistic regression analyses, the associations of sex and age with thyroid cancer were no longer statistically significant. Body weight remained significantly associated with reporting frequency (OR = 1.02, P < 0.05), consistent with prior evidence linking body mass index to thyroid cancer risk [28, 29]. However, the multivariate analysis was based on a very limited sample (n = 17 events, 0.46% of cases), severely restricting statistical power and confounder adjustment capability. Moreover, unmeasured factors—including obesity severity, metabolic disorders, lifestyle habits, and environmental exposures—could not be captured in FAERS. Therefore, the observed association between body weight and reporting frequency should be interpreted cautiously as a potential risk indicator rather than a confirmed causal factor, requiring validation in large-scale cohort studies.

Time to onset analysis of ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer events

The temporal relationship between ranitidine exposure duration and thyroid cancer reporting frequency reveals a progressive increase in reported events with longer use. This pattern suggests a potential dose-time response signal, where prolonged exposure is associated with higher reporting frequency. However, these observations should be interpreted cautiously, as time-to-onset data in spontaneous reporting systems may be incomplete, inaccurately recorded, or subject to reporting delays and detection bias. The apparent trend may reflect stimulated reporting following the 2020 ranitidine recall rather than a true biological time‑response relationship. Given the detected disproportionality signal but absence of confirmed causality, clinicians should exercise individualized clinical judgment regarding thyroid monitoring in long-term ranitidine users. Patient-specific risk factors—including age, body weight, and underlying thyroid conditions—should guide monitoring decisions rather than routine universal screening. Prospective longitudinal studies with rigorous ascertainment of exposure duration and outcome timing are required to elucidate whether this temporal pattern reflects a true biological relationship or methodological artifacts inherent to spontaneous reporting systems.

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, FAERS is subject to selective and stimulated reporting, particularly following high-profile safety concerns such as the 2020 ranitidine recall. These reporting biases may artificially inflate case numbers and exaggerate the disproportionality signal. The small sample available for multivariable regression (n = 17 events, 0.46% of thyroid cancer cases) further limits statistical power and the reliability of confounder adjustment. Residual confounding by obesity, metabolic conditions, lifestyle factors, and environmental exposures—which are not captured in FAERS—cannot be excluded and may have influenced the observed associations. Second, the data were predominantly from the United States (96.56%), introducing geographic bias and limiting generalizability. The disproportionately high proportion of ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer cases in FAERS should not be interpreted as true epidemiological incidence but rather reflects reporting artifacts. Third, this study relied solely on disproportionality analysis of pharmacovigilance databases, which can generate safety signals but cannot establish causality. The consistency observed in VigiAccess provides supplementary rather than independent validation, as both databases contain overlapping spontaneous reports. Additionally, the observed thyroid cancer signal may be attributable to NDMA contamination rather than the pharmacological action of ranitidine itself. In summary, despite these limitations, this study innovatively combines FAERS and VigiAccess to provide new insights into ranitidine-associated thyroid cancer signals; however, independent validation through large-scale epidemiological studies is required to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

The combined analysis of FAERS and VigiAccess databases identified a disproportionality signal for thyroid cancer reporting associated with ranitidine use, underscoring the need for continued clinical vigilance in patients receiving long-term ranitidine therapy. Despite inherent limitations, these findings highlight the importance of continued vigilance in clinical practice and the need for large-scale epidemiological studies to validate this potential safety signal.
