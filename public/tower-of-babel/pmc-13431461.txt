Vision-Focused Nomogram for Assessing the Risk of Depression in Diabetic Retinopathy: Integrating Visual Function, Metabolic Factors and Psychosocial Status
Authors: Mengjiao Tang, Jia Wang, Yuting Yang, Ruiyan Cheng, Xiuqing Jiang, Jie Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and validate a comprehensive nomogram integrating visual function, metabolic factors, and psychosocial measures for assessing the risk of depression risk in patients with type 2 diabetic retinopathy (DR), using a robust machine learning-based feature selection approach.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 490 DR patients from January 2020 to March 2025. Participants were randomly allocated to a training cohort (n=343) and an internal validation cohort (n=147). We assessed nineteen candidate variables. To identify the most significant and consistent predictors, a multi-step feature selection was employed: 1) LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation, 2) Stepwise Forward Logistic Regression, and 3) Random Forest importance ranking. The intersection of key variables from all three methods was used to build the final nomogram via multivariate logistic regression. Model performance was evaluated using ROC curves, calibration plots, 10-fold cross-validation, decision curve analysis, and SHAP for interpretability. Temporal validation was performed on an independent cohort (n=104) from the same center (June 2025-December 2025).ResultsThe overall prevalence of depressive symptoms was 29.18% (143/490). The integrative feature selection identified four robust independent predictors: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (OR=1.716, 95% CI: 1.438–2.049), sleep quality index (PSQI score) (OR=1.306, 95% CI: 1.146–1.488), social support score (SSRS score) (OR=0.883, 95% CI: 0.824–0.947), and vision-related quality of life (VRQoL score) (OR=1.138, 95% CI: 1.094–1.183). The nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination in the training cohort (AUC=0.918, 95% CI: 0.886–0.950) and internal validation cohort (AUC=0.867, 95% CI: 0.797–0.936), with good calibration. The 10-fold cross-validation yielded a stable average AUC of 0.891. SHAP analysis confirmed VRQoL as the most influential predictor. In temporal validation, the model maintained good performance (AUC=0.875, 95% CI: 0.805–0.946).ConclusionThis vision-focused nomogram, developed through a robust multi-method feature selection process, provides an effective and interpretable tool for depression risk stratification in DR patients. It integrates key clinical and patient-reported outcomes, demonstrating good and stable discriminative performance, and can assist clinicians in early screening of high-risk individuals for timely intervention.Keywords: diabetic retinopathy, depression, nomogram, machine learning, risk factors

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), one of the severe microvascular complications of diabetes, has become a leading contributor to global vision loss.1 The rising prevalence of type 2 diabetes has inevitably increased the incidence of DR, imposing substantial challenges on healthcare systems.2 Although most investigations have concentrated on the biological features of DR, the strong connection between DR and psychological disorders, especially depression, has been insufficiently examined.3 Evidence from recent clinical studies, including a large NHANES analysis, indicates that individuals with DR experience depression at significantly higher frequencies (eg, a reported prevalence of approximately 40.5%) compared with those who only have diabetes or with healthy populations.4 This frequent coexistence highlights the profound psychological burden caused by DR and suggests possible shared mechanisms, such as dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, impaired insulin sensitivity, and long-term inflammatory activity; in addition, depressive states may in turn accelerate both the onset and advancement of DR through multiple pathways.5 Patients with depression frequently demonstrate diminished adherence to treatment, impaired capacity for self-management, and altered health behaviors, which collectively result in suboptimal glycemic control and accelerate the development of retinopathy.6 Importantly, the relationship between DR severity and depression risk presents a complex pattern; evidence indicates that the likelihood of depression is greatest in individuals with severe non-proliferative DR, whereas the risk appears to decline once progression to proliferative DR occurs.7 This non-linear association likely stems from a complex interplay where patients with severe NPDR retain more functional vision but face greater uncertainty about disease progression, whereas those with PDR may experience vision loss so severe that it paradoxically reduces the psychological burden of “fear of losing sight.” This complexity complicates clinical evaluation and intervention, highlighting the urgent need for more accurate tools for risk prediction.

Early detection of depression vulnerability among DR individuals yields considerable clinical utility alongside interventional advantages. Depressive conditions substantially undermine adherence to regimens and personal care practices in DR cases, consequently impairing glycemic management while hastening retinal deterioration. Evidence from studies indicates that diabetics experiencing depression demonstrate substantially reduced engagement rates for standard diabetes instruction, ocular fundus checks, and glycated hemoglobin evaluations, in contrast to markedly elevated rates of detrimental lifestyle choices.6 Secondly, the vision loss and impaired life functions caused by DR can exacerbate depressive symptoms, and depression, in turn, accelerates the progression of DR through physiological and behavioral mechanisms, forming a difficult-to-break negative cycle. Therefore, timely detection and intervention targeting depressive vulnerability in individuals with DR possess substantial clinical relevance. Nonetheless, contemporary protocols for gauging depressive risks among DR cases harbor various hurdles and limitations: clinicians in endocrinology and ophthalmology commonly emphasize retinal modifications alongside physiological metrics of DR, thus neglecting thorough mental health appraisals; additionally, despite demonstrating strong reliability and validity, the relevance and forecasting precision of prevailing depression screening instruments within the unique DR demographic warrant more rigorous confirmation.8,9 More importantly, relying solely on clinical manifestations and scale assessment is difficult to achieve individualized risk prediction and cannot meet the needs of precision medicine.

Development of tools for forecasting disease hazards in targeted demographics has been facilitated by the broad integration of prognostic frameworks across medical disciplines in contemporary times. Clinicians benefit from nomograms, which act as graphical prognostic devices by converting elaborate quantitative frameworks into accessible visual formats that support rapid evaluation of personalized patient vulnerabilities. For evaluating prognoses and anticipating illnesses, this method has evolved into a vital asset.10 Nomograms find effective use in diabetes research for estimating chances of cognitive frailty, diabetic retinopathy risk, and all-cause mortality, where they showcase robust prognostic efficacy along with suitability for medical settings.11 Although several studies have attempted to establish models for predicting depression risk in patients with DR, notable shortcomings remain. Most existing work has relied on conventional logistic regression or Cox proportional hazards models, which, despite their explanatory strengths, are inadequate for capturing complex nonlinear associations and multiple interactions. Furthermore, many current models are constructed primarily on single clinical or biological variables, without integrating data from diverse sources such as clinical features, laboratory measures, psychological assessments, and social determinants, thereby restricting improvements in predictive accuracy. Research specifically targeting predictive models for depression risk in DR populations is also relatively limited.12 Patients with DR present distinct clinical profiles and psychological vulnerabilities, making direct application of models developed in other populations prone to bias and reduced validity. In addition, the majority of available models fail to adequately incorporate the influence of DR severity and stage on the risk of depression.

Recent studies further demonstrate that machine learning applied to dynamic metabolic data—such as continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and OGTT-derived features—can predict incident diabetic retinopathy and key inflection points in disease progression, highlighting the potential added value of incorporating temporally rich glycemic markers beyond static clinical and psychosocial variables.13,14 While these advances represent a shift towards high-resolution monitoring, they often require specialized equipment and may overlook the patient’s subjective experience of disease burden. In contrast, our proposed model focuses on the integration of vision-related quality of life (VRQoL)—a dimension frequently neglected in purely metabolic or clinical models. By combining VRQoL with psychosocial and metabolic factors into a practical nomogram, we aim to provide a complementary, cost-effective tool that captures the holistic impact of DR, thereby offering a more comprehensive risk assessment for depression in clinical settings where dynamic monitoring may not be feasible.

To address the limitations of conventional single-method approaches, which may be susceptible to overfitting or fail to capture complex interactions, we employed an integrative machine learning-based feature selection strategy. This approach combines LASSO regression (for handling multicollinearity), stepwise regression (for statistical rigor), and Random Forest (for capturing non-linear relationships) to ensure the robustness and clinical translatability of the final nomogram by identifying only the most stable predictors across different algorithmic perspectives.

Based on the preceding research context and identified limitations, this study aimed to develop and validate a prognostic nomogram for predicting depression risk in patients with diabetic retinopathy. The model integrates multidimensional prognostic factors, including psychosocial status, DR characteristics, clinical indicators, and demographic features. An integrative machine learning-based feature selection approach was employed to identify robust predictors, thereby mitigating potential biases associated with conventional variable selection methods. The resulting graphical nomogram is designed to enable healthcare practitioners to rapidly and individually assess depression risk in DR patients, supporting early identification and personalized intervention.

Method

Study Population and Design

We executed a cross-sectional inquiry within one facility, sequentially enrolling subjects who sought care at the ophthalmology during the period spanning January 2020 through March 2025. Inclusion standards comprised: (1) participants with ages at or exceeding 18 years; (2) confirmation of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) followed the “Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (2020 Edition)”, incorporating glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C) ≥ 6.5%, or 2-hour postprandial blood glucose (2hPG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L along with standard diabetic indicators.15 Standards for exclusion included: (1) presence of lens opacity hindering fundus evaluation; (2) individuals who experienced intraocular surgery or fundus laser procedures; (3) cases involving angle-closure glaucoma inappropriate for mydriasis assessment.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third People’s Hospital of Changzhou (Ethics Number: 02A- A2025022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the data collection.

Assessment of depressive states in study subjects involved the integration of the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Featuring nine entries, the PHQ-9 includes four alternatives per entry, assigned values spanning zero through three. Categorization of depressive conditions relied on PHQ-9 aggregates as outlined: absence of depression corresponded to 0–4 units, potential mild depression aligned with 5–9 units, potential moderate depression matched 10–14 units, potential moderate to severe depression linked to 15–19 units, while potential severe depression pertained to 20–27 units. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the PHQ-9 was 0.857.16 Patients with a PHQ-9 score of ≥5 were further evaluated using the HAMD-17. The HAMD-17 consists of 17 questions, each with 3 to 5 options, scored from 0 to 4. Depression states were categorized according to the HAMD-17 totals, as detailed below: scores under 8 signified the absence of depressive symptoms, scores ranging from 8 to 17 indicated mild depression, scores between 18 and 23 represented moderate depression, and scores of 24 or higher indicated severe depression.17 In this study, a score of ≥8 on the HAMD-17 was considered indicative of depression. Both scales were administered by trained medical staff to all participants during question-and-answer sessions, with the final classification based on the patients’ scale scores. For patients who could communicate clearly but could not complete the scales independently, the medical staff assisted them in filling out the forms.

Determination of participant counts proceeded through a logistic regression framework mandating outcome occurrences to equate to no less than tenfold the quantity of explanatory factors (Events Per Variable, EPV principle). Moreover, the risk of bias assessment tool in conjunction with guidelines for predictive model study reporting underwent consultation, specifying that explanatory variables must number at least ten.18 In this study, depressive symptoms were defined as a combined positive screen on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17). Specifically, a PHQ-9 score ≥ 5 followed by a HAMD-17 score ≥ 8 was used as the cutoff for defining the outcome. Based on literature review, approximately 15 independent variables were anticipated for inclusion in the predictive model, and the prevalence of depression among patients with T2DM was estimated at ~30% (adjusted from 40% to match EPV). Accordingly, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 15×10÷30%=500. To account for potential ineligible entries, an extra 20% increment occurred, culminating in an overall participant volume of 490 subjects (adjusted to match actual enrollment). Allocation at random partitioned these cases into validation (n=147) and training (n=343) cohorts via a 3:7 division. Figure 1 depicts the evaluation protocol. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, as the proportion of missing values for all candidate variables was less than 5%. Endorsement for this inquiry came from the ethics panel at The Third People’s Hospital of Changzhou Hospital, alongside acquisition of signed informed consent from each participant. Temporal validation was performed on an independent cohort recruited from June 2025 to December 2025.

Data Collection

Basic Information Collection

This study adopted a standardized data collection process. Regarding demographic indicators, age data were obtained by verifying patients’ identification documents. Height and weight determinations relied on a tuned electronic scale combined with stadiometer, attaining precision levels of 0.1 kg for weight alongside 0.1 cm for height. Derivation of BMI proceeded via the equation: weight(kg)/(height (m))2.

Ophthalmic Specialized Examination

Comprehensive ocular assessments were performed on each participant by qualified eye specialists, and color fundus photographs served as the basis for DR staging and diagnosis. First, visual acuity and intraocular pressure were assessed, and the anterior segment was examined using a slit-lamp microscope. Pupil dilation to more than 6 mm was achieved with one or two drops of compound tropicamide eye drops. The fundus was then evaluated with a 90D lens. Two experienced ophthalmologists performed the diagnosis and staging of DR according to the unified standards recommended by the International Society of Diabetic Retinopathy in 2002. In cases of inconsistent staging, color fundus photographs were obtained with a professional digital retinal camera, and the staging was determined based on these images. When both eyes presented lesions, the more severe eye was recorded.

DR severity received categorization in line with the 2002 International Clinical Classification Standard for DR:19 (1) Lack of apparent retinal damage: post-mydriasis fundus evaluations uncovered no deviations; (2) Mild category: post-mydriasis fundus evaluations uncovered exclusively microaneurysms; (3) Moderate category: post-mydriasis fundus evaluations uncovered microaneurysms, although features appeared less pronounced relative to severe NPDR (non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy, NPDR); (4) Severe NPDR: post-mydriasis fundus evaluations uncovered no PDR (proliferative diabetic retinopathy, PDR) features, though one or more of these indicators surfaced: surpassing 20 intraretinal hemorrhages across any quadrant; over two quadrants showing venous beaded modifications; more than one quadrant revealing marked retinal microvascular irregularities; (5) PDR: one or more of these modifications surfaced: preretinal hemorrhage, vitreous hemorrhage, or neovascularization.

Inclusion Indicators

The standardized questionnaire was used to collect the following variables: age (continuous), gender (categorical), smoking history, history of alcohol abuse, family history of diabetes, BMI (continuous), HbA1c (continuous), FPG (continuous), duration of diabetes (continuous), TC, TG, LDL-C, DR grade (categorical), PSQI score (continuous), SSRS score (continuous), VRQoL score (continuous), where scores range from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating worse vision-related quality of life, SAS score (continuous), DDS score (continuous), and VAS score (continuous).

Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c): 2–3mL of blood was drawn from the elbow vein using an EDTA anticoagulant tube. The subjects were instructed to avoid vigorous exercise or extreme diet interference before blood collection. Centrifugation applied to the specimens enabled plasma/serum isolation, while segregation of non-glycated and glycated hemoglobin occurred via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) depending on disparities in charge.20

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG): The subjects were required to fast for 8–10 hours overnight and have their blood drawn between 7 and 9 a.m. the next morning. The blood was collected from the median cubital vein after disinfection with iodophor and placed in a vacuum blood collection tube. Serum/plasma segregation ensued from specimen centrifugation executed not beyond 2 hours following procurement. Utilization of the glucose oxidase method (GOD-POD) facilitated glucose level quantification, during which peroxide arising from enzymatic activities engages a color-producing reagent to permit colorimetric evaluation.21

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL, mmol/L), triglycerides (TG, mmol/L), and total cholesterol (TC, mmol/L): Fasting lasted between 8 and 12 hours among participants. Directives for the three days leading up to assessment encompassed eschewing vigorous exercise, alcohol, and high-fat foods alongside sustaining routine eating habits. A five-minute period of seated immobility preceded phlebotomy to mitigate postural impacts on lipid concentrations. Collection entailed withdrawing 3–5 mL of blood via the cubital vein into tubes lacking anticoagulants. Specimen processing for serum extraction involved centrifugation at 3000 revolutions per minute over a ten-minute duration, initiated 30 to 45 minutes after procurement. Level determinations proceeded through the cholesterol oxidase technique (CEH-CHOD-PAP), with all lipid parameters reported in mmol/L.22

The PSQI incorporates five items evaluated by others alongside nineteen rated by oneself, though eighteen of the self-assessed ones contribute to the actual computation. Division of those elements spans seven categories, featuring ratings per category that extend between zero and three. Aggregate PSQI values emerge from adding up ratings across components, spanning a spectrum of 0 through 21.23 Diminished sleep caliber becomes apparent with augmented ratings. Within this inquiry, sleep impairments were regarded as evident whenever total PSQI surpassed 5.

Ten elements constitute the SSRS, encompassing three centered on social support usage, three oriented toward objective support, alongside four aligned with subjective support. The total SSRS score can be divided into three levels: low support level (total score ≤22), medium support level (23–44), and high support level (≥45).24

The VRQoL scale assesses vision-related quality of life with scores ranging from 0 to 100, where higher scores indicate better quality of life.25

Nineteen constituent elements form the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS). Item ratings aggregate post-evaluation, undergo scaling via multiplication by 1.25, and then integer rounding to derive normalized SAS figures from 25 to 100. Escalating figures signal amplified anxiety intensity.26

The Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS) gauges psychological torments coupled with emotional burdens originating from the condition itself alongside diabetes handling within diabetic individuals, denoting adverse affective incidents associated with the disorder. In-depth comprehension of affliction roots from varied viewpoints arises through the four principal facets spanned by the 17 elements in the Chinese adaptation of DDS. Overall aggregates extend between 17 and 102, where amplified totals denote exacerbated mental strain connected to diabetes.27

The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) dedicated to pain serves as an instrument for personal discomfort appraisal. A line or ruler extending 10 centimeters forms its basis, featuring a left extremity denoted by “0” to indicate discomfort absence alongside a right extremity labeled “10” to denote excruciating intolerable agony. Indication of ache sensation occurs via participant markings along the segment, with quantification arising from the span separating the zero extremity from the designated spot. Conventional partitioning includes: 7–10 designating severe discomfort, 4–6 signifying moderate discomfort, 1–3 reflecting mild discomfort, and 0 denoting no discomfort.28

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To identify robust predictors, an integrative machine learning-based feature selection strategy was employed. This included: (1) applying LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation, where the optimal λ (lambda.1se=0.030) was selected based on the 1-standard-error rule from the cross-validation error curve, balancing model complexity and prediction accuracy (for variable selection via shrinkage); (2) performing Stepwise Forward Logistic Regression (for statistical interpretability); and (3) evaluating feature importance via Random Forest (for capturing non-linear relationships). The intersection of variables selected by all three methods was taken to ensure robustness and mitigate potential overfitting inherent in relying on a single algorithm. The intersection of key variables consistently selected by all three methods was retained to ensure robustness. These selected predictors were then incorporated into a multivariable logistic regression to derive odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Regression coefficients from the final model were used to construct a prognostic nomogram. Model performance was evaluated through a three-part validation: (1) the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% CI was calculated to assess discrimination; (2) calibration was examined using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and visualized with calibration plots; (3) clinical utility was evaluated across a range of threshold probabilities via decision curve analysis (DCA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (P25, P75) based on their distribution, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of Clinical Data Between the Non-Depression and Depression Groups in the Training Set

Table 2 presents a detailed comparison of baseline characteristics between the non-depression group (n=245) and the depression group (n=98) within the training cohort. The prevalence of depressive symptoms in the training set was 28.57% (98/343). The two groups were well-balanced in terms of demographic factors, with no statistically significant differences in gender distribution, age, body mass index, history of smoking or alcohol abuse, or family history of diabetes (P > 0.05 for all). However, significant disparities were observed in several clinical and psychosocial parameters. Machine Learning Based Feature Selection

The training cohort consisted of 343 patients diagnosed with diabetic retinopathy (DR). To identify the most significant predictors for depression, three distinct machine learning-based feature selection methods were employed on 19 candidate variables: age, gender, smoking history, alcohol abuse history, family history of diabetes, BMI, HbA1c, FPG, duration of diabetes, TC, TG, LDL-C, DR grade, PSQI score, SSRS score, VRQoL score, SAS score, DDS score, and VAS score. First, LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to select variables with non-zero coefficients, which identified six key predictors: HbA1c, duration of diabetes, PSQI score, SSRS score, VRQoL score, and SAS score, as illustrated in Figure 2. Second, Stepwise Forward Logistic Regression (SR-LR) was performed, which yielded another set of six significant predictors: HbA1c, TC, PSQI score, SSRS score, VRQoL score, and DDS score, as detailed in Table 3. Third, a Random Forest classifier was built to evaluate feature importance, with the results of variable importance ranking shown in Figure 3. To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the final model, the intersection of variables selected by all three methods was taken, resulting in the selection of four common predictors: PSQI score, SSRS score, HbA1c, and VRQoL score, which were subsequently used to construct the nomogram.

Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis of Depression Risk Factors

Table 4 presents the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis incorporating the four candidate variables selected by the machine learning feature screening process. The analysis identified HbA1c, PSQI score, SSRS score, and VRQoL score as significant independent predictors of depression in patients with DR, as detailed in Table 4. Specifically, higher levels of HbA1c (OR=1.716, 95% CI: 1.438–2.049, P<0.001) and poorer sleep quality (PSQI score) (OR=1.306, 95% CI: 1.146–1.488, P<0.001) were associated with an increased risk of depression. Conversely, higher levels of social support (SSRS score) (OR=0.883, 95% CI: 0.824–0.947, P<0.001) were protective factors against depression, while higher vision-related quality of life scores (VRQoL score) (OR=1.138, 95% CI: 1.094–1.183, P<0.001) were associated with an increased risk of depression, as higher VRQoL scores indicate worse vision-related quality of life. Notably, the 95% confidence intervals for the odds ratios (OR) of all four variables did not include 1, further confirming their statistical significance.

Construction and Evaluation of Nomogram Model for Depression in DR Patients

To facilitate individualized risk assessment for depression in patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR), a prognostic nomogram was developed using the four key independent predictors identified from the multivariate logistic regression analysis (HbA1c, PSQI score, SSRS score, and VRQoL score), as illustrated in Figure 4. The nomogram serves as a practical visual tool for predicting depression probability in clinical practice. To apply this model, the specific value for each predictor is located on its respective axis, the corresponding points are summed to obtain a total score, and this cumulative value is then projected onto the “Risk of Depression” axis to read the individualized predicted probability, enabling clinicians to quickly estimate a patient’s depression risk based on these accessible variables.

Internal Validation and Performance Assessment of the Nomogram Model

Based on the four predictor variables incorporated into the nomogram, internal validation of the model was rigorously performed. The Receiver Operating Characteristic curve analysis demonstrated the model’s strong discriminative capability for predicting depression in patients with diabetic retinopathy. The training set exhibited an AUC of 0.918 (95% CI: 0.886–0.950; refer to Figure 5A), while the internal validation set yielded an AUC of 0.867 (95% CI: 0.797–0.936; refer to Figure 5B). Calibration plots revealed excellent consistency between the predicted probabilities and the observed actual outcomes for both the training and validation sets, as depicted in Figure 6A and B, respectively. Quantitative assessment confirmed good calibration, with a calibration slope of 6.128 and intercept of −3.0701 in the training set, and a slope of 5.8393 and intercept of −2.8904 in the internal validation set. The Brier scores were 0.0987 and 0.1217, respectively. The 10-fold cross-validation further supported the model’s stability, yielding an average AUC of 0.8909 (95% CI: 0.8482–0.9335), as detailed in Figure 7. The clinical applicability of the model was appraised using Decision Curve Analysis, with the results visualized in Figure 6C and D. The clinical utility was further quantified by evaluating standard diagnostic metrics. In the training set, the model achieved a sensitivity of 78.6%, specificity of 91.0%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 77.8%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 91.4%. In the internal validation set, the corresponding values were 68.9%, 94.1%, 83.8%, and 87.3%, respectively.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was conducted to elucidate the model’s decision - making process. The SHAP scatter plot (Figure 8A) illustrates the distribution of SHAP values for each feature: higher VRQoL scores (orange - colored points) are predominantly associated with positive SHAP values (increasing the risk of depression), while lower VRQoL scores (purple - colored points) show negative SHAP values (decreasing the risk); HbA1c exhibits a mixed pattern with both positive and negative SHAP values across different score ranges, and SSRS shows that lower scores (yellow - colored points) tend to have positive SHAP values, while higher scores (purple - colored points) have negative ones. PSQI scores (mostly purple - colored points) are mainly associated with positive SHAP values (increasing the risk of depression). The SHAP feature importance bar plot (Figure 8B) indicates that VRQoL is the most influential predictor, contributing 44% to the model’s output, followed by HbA1c (29%), SSRS (14.6%), and PSQI (12.4%). Collectively, the SHAP analyses transparently illustrate the model’s internal logic, confirming that its predictions are driven by key clinical variables in an interpretable manner, with feature importance and directional effects clearly quantified.

Temporal Validation of the Nomogram Model at the Same Center

To evaluate the model’s generalizability and temporal stability, a temporal validation was conducted using a more recent, independent cohort of diabetic retinopathy patients from the same medical center. This validation cohort comprised patients enrolled from June 2025 to December 2025. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated the model’s sustained performance in this temporal validation set, achieving an AUC of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.805–0.946; refer to Figure 9A). The calibration plot (Figure 9B) revealed a high degree of consistency between the predicted probabilities and the observed actual outcomes in the temporal cohort. Quantitative calibration metrics for the temporal validation set were: slope = 5.369, intercept = −2.7701, and Brier score = 0.1302. The clinical applicability of the nomogram was further assessed using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA), as shown in Figure 9C. For the temporal validation cohort, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 96.9%, specificity of 66.7%, PPV of 56.4%, and NPV of 98.0%.

Discussion

We formulated a prognostic nomogram aimed at assessing the risk of depressive states within DR-afflicted subjects by employing an integrative machine learning-based feature selection approach to fuse key metrics across social, psychological, and biological realms (specifically, vision-related quality of life score, glycated hemoglobin, social support score, and sleep quality index). The nomogram demonstrated robust performance in the developmental (training) cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.918 (95% CI: 0.886–0.950). Internal validation confirmed its predictive capability, yielding an AUC of 0.867 (95% CI: 0.797–0.936). Pronounced congruence was observed between empirical incidences and forecasted likelihoods, as revealed through calibration graphing. Depressive forecasting among DR cases benefits from the framework’s robust and validated prognostic capability, enabling its deployment as an effective instrument by healthcare personnel outside psychiatry for identifying elevated-risk subjects.

To quantify the incremental value of incorporating psychosocial variables, we conducted a post-hoc analysis comparing the full model (with HbA1c, PSQI, SSRS, VRQoL) against a reduced model including only the metabolic predictor (HbA1c). The full model demonstrated a significantly higher AUC in the training set (0.918 vs 0.782, DeLong’s test P<0.001) and internal validation set (0.867 vs 0.745, P<0.001). This confirms that the integration of sleep quality, social support, and vision-related quality of life provides substantial incremental predictive power beyond glycemic control alone, reinforcing the necessity of a multidimensional assessment in DR patients.

Investigations preceding this one revealed elevated depressive rates among individuals exhibiting moderate-to-severe NPDR or PDR.7 Kho’s inquiry further established a robust association between DR severity and adverse psychosocial outcomes, noting the bidirectional relationship where depression may also exacerbate DR progression.29 In the univariate analysis of our training set, a longer duration of diabetes and higher HbA1c were significantly associated with depression, while DR grade (NPDR vs PDR) itself did not show a statistically significant difference (P>0.05, Table 2). Furthermore, during our integrative machine learning feature selection process, DR grade was not consistently selected as a key predictor across the three methodological approaches. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that the inclusion of more direct, patient-reported measures, such as vision-related quality of life (VRQoL), may more effectively capture the functional and psychological burden of the disease, thereby attenuating the independent predictive contribution of the clinical staging alone.30 The complex, potentially non-linear relationship between DR progression and depression risk underscores that clinicians should consider integrated multidimensional assessments, incorporating psychological and functional metrics, rather than relying solely on ophthalmologic staging for evaluation.

Findings herein reveal that the caliber of slumber emerges as a pivotal modulator affecting depressive indications within DR-afflicted subjects, demonstrating partial concordance alongside deviations from prior explorations. Elevated hazards for depression coupled with DR manifested in T2DM individuals enduring sleep spans shorter than five hours, as evidenced by inquiries targeting those aged 18 through 64, thereby mirroring discoveries in the current work.6 In contrast, Morjaria and associates documented comparable magnitudes of depressive and anxious expressions among DR cases versus unaffected controls, absent any disparities spanning assorted pathological intensities. Predominant superior ocular acuity within the dominant eye across recruited cohorts potentially accounts for these observations.31 Positive linkages surfaced between cumulative PSQI metrics and depressive conditions in a separate probe examining rest in DM cases, paralleled by affirmative ties connecting augmented HbA1c quantities to somnolence during daylight hours.32 Multivariate analysis in this study further confirmed an independent association between HbA1c and depressive symptoms, indicating that sleep disturbances may interact with metabolic and inflammatory factors in the pathogenesis of depression in DR patients. Sleep status was comprehensively evaluated using the PSQI, which covers subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, and sleep medication use, providing a more complete assessment of sleep problems than instruments limited to daytime sleepiness. Potential mechanisms linking sleep quality to depressive symptoms in DR include:33,34 (1) Bio-metabolic pathway: Sleep disorders disrupt hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis function, elevate cortisol secretion, induce insulin resistance, and impair glycemic control. Elevated HbA1c levels further exacerbate microvascular injury, creating a vicious cycle of sleep disturbance, metabolic dysregulation, and retinal lesions. (2) Inflammatory–immune pathway: Poor sleep quality activates the innate immune system and increases the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may penetrate the central nervous system through the blood–retinal barrier, altering neurotransmitter metabolism and neuroendocrine activity. (3) Psychological–behavioral pathway: Visual impairment reduces daytime activity and social participation, and inadequate sleep intensifies this social isolation. Sleep quality is also strongly associated with anxiety, suggesting a cascading sleep–anxiety–depression effect.

An integrative machine learning-based feature selection strategy (incorporating LASSO regression, stepwise forward regression, and Random Forest) was employed for variable selection. This multi-method intersection approach effectively enhanced the robustness of predictor identification and mitigated potential overfitting or selection bias inherent in relying on a single method. The nomogram model, built upon the four consensus predictors, enables clinicians to rapidly evaluate a patient’s sleep status along with other key metrics to assess depression risk. Nevertheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged, including the subjective nature of the PSQI, the cross-sectional design of the sleep assessment, and the absence of detailed analysis of individual PSQI components. Future studies should incorporate objective sleep monitoring, adopt longitudinal designs to elucidate the temporal relationship between sleep changes and depression onset, and evaluate the roles of specific components such as sleep efficiency. Furthermore, interventions targeting sleep in DR patients should be explored to determine whether improving sleep quality can indirectly alleviate depressive symptoms and slow retinopathy progression, thereby supporting individualized risk prediction and precise intervention.

Research by Gao et al indicated connections between social support and states of depression alongside anxiety within individuals affected by DR.35 Xu and associates observed that social support mitigates depressive and anxious conditions among DR cases, in contrast to stress which intensifies such conditions.9 In the present study, the Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) score was consistently identified as a key predictor through our integrative machine learning-based feature selection strategy and was subsequently validated as a significant independent protective factor in the multivariate logistic regression model (OR=0.883). This finding reinforces and extends the conclusions of prior research by quantitatively establishing SSRS as a robust, standalone modulator of depression risk in DR patients, derived from a consensus of multiple analytical methods. Social support serves as a vital shield for psychological welfare, delivering a principal mitigating function amid sufferers of persistent ailments, notably diabetic retinopathy instances.

This study integrates psychosocial factors into the prediction model, embodying the application of the bio-psycho-social medical framework. In contrast to a previous depression risk model for type 2 diabetes based on NHANES 2007–2014 data, which included alternative social support indicators such as socioeconomic status (PIR) and education level, that analysis did not employ a standardized social support scale.36 By directly incorporating the SSRS standardized instrument, the present study provides a more comprehensive and precise assessment of the multidimensional features of social support, including subjective support, objective support, and support utilization, representing a notable methodological improvement. Social support may mitigate depression risk through neuroendocrine pathways (eg, attenuating stress responses and lowering cortisol levels), behavioral pathways (eg, enhancing treatment adherence), and psychological pathways (eg, strengthening self-efficacy).37 Combining SSRS scores with clinical and laboratory indicators, this study establishes an integrated prediction model. Outcomes reveal that the prognostic nomogram integrating social support metrics displays strong differentiation capability across validation alongside training cohorts, signifying that embedding social support elements bolsters the framework’s competence for anticipating depressive hazards within DR-affected subjects. In clinical practice, the SSRS scale can be applied to rapidly identify DR patients with low social support, enabling the implementation of targeted interventions such as family-based interviews and patient mutual-aid groups. Future studies should further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying these associations and evaluate the effectiveness of related interventions.

Zafeiri et al reported that patients with diabetic peripheral neuropathy and comorbid depression exhibit elevated HbA1c levels.38 Similarly, Buchberger et al demonstrated in a diabetes population that HbA1c is associated with diabetes-related anxiety and depression.39 Consistent with these findings, the present study identifies HbA1c as a major determinant of depression in DR patients and highlights its substantial contribution within the constructed nomogram model. Elevated HbA1c is an independent predictor of depression in DR, with potential mechanisms including: chronic hyperglycemia leading to neuroendocrine disturbances, such as activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, increased cortisol secretion, insulin resistance, and dysfunction of the 5-hydroxytryptamine system; inflammation and oxidative stress, where hyperglycemia promotes the release of inflammatory mediators, damages vascular endothelial function, and disrupts neurotransmitter balance; and hormonal imbalance, such as reduced testosterone levels associated with elevated HbA1c and depressive symptoms.40 This study incorporated HbA1c into a nomogram prediction model for depression in DR, integrating variables such as HbA1c, disease duration, and retinopathy stage to enable individualized risk estimation and early identification of high-risk patients. Future research should further elucidate causal pathways linking HbA1c and depression, including the roles of inflammatory mediators and cortisol, and should evaluate the potential of routine depression risk screening in DR patients with elevated HbA1c, as well as the impact of hypoglycemic therapy on depressive symptoms. In addition, multicenter prospective studies are required to validate the generalizability of the model and to assess whether incorporating genetic markers or other biomarkers can improve predictive accuracy. In conclusion, HbA1c, as a long-term indicator of glycemic control, provides significant value for predicting depression in DR patients. The proposed model offers a practical clinical tool, although further validation and refinement are necessary. Future multicenter studies with larger, more diverse populations are warranted to validate the model on a broader scale and minimize potential selection biases.

Research by Upaphong shows that VRQoL is an independent factor influencing depression in DR patients, highlighting the significant impact of subjective visual function on mental health.30 Research by Bao et al indicates that depression in DR patients is not related to best corrected visual acuity but is related to VRQoL.7 In univariate analysis, the VRQoL score alone demonstrated moderate predictive efficacy for depression (AUC = 0.770). The high specificity (93.1%) at a cut-off value above 65 points suggests its value as a preliminary screening indicator, where higher scores indicate a higher risk of depression. Crucially, when integrated with HbA1c, PSQI, and SSRS into the final nomogram, the model achieved excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.918 in the training set). The high specificity (93.1%) suggests that when the VRQoL score is below 65 points, it can effectively identify DR patients who may have depression, which holds significant clinical screening value. As a comprehensive indicator reflecting patients’ subjective visual experience, this score can effectively capture functional impairments and psychological impacts that traditional vision tests cannot assess. In this study, VRQoL was consistently identified as a pivotal predictor through our integrative machine learning-based feature selection strategy and was further confirmed as a significant independent factor in the subsequent multivariate logistic regression. VRQoL may affect the risk of depression through multiple pathways:41 first, the functional limitation pathway, where decreased visual function leads to limitations in daily activity ability; second, the psychological stress pathway, where vision impairment triggers fear and anxiety of blindness; third, the neurobiological pathway, where reduced chronic visual stimulation may affect the function of brain regions involved in emotion regulation. This study incorporated the standardized VRQoL score into the depression risk prediction model for DR patients, covering multiple dimensions such as daily visual function, mobility, social participation, and emotional impact, and constructed a nomogram model with clinical application value. Despite limitations such as a cross-sectional design that cannot determine causality, subjective reporting bias, and failure to assess the impact of DR stages, future research can deepen this field through longitudinal design, combining objective visual function assessment, exploring the effects of low vision rehabilitation intervention, and developing digital monitoring tools. Based on these findings, it is recommended that VRQoL assessment be routinely conducted for all DR patients, depression screening be strengthened for those with low scores, and VRQoL improvement be included in comprehensive management goals to improve the physical and mental health outcomes of patients through multidisciplinary collaboration.

Limitation

We formulated a prognostic nomogram aimed at assessing the risk of depressive states in patients with type 2 diabetic retinopathy (DR) by employing an integrative machine learning-based feature selection strategy. This approach consolidated key measures across psychological, social, and biological spheres, specifically vision-related quality of life (VRQoL) score, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), social support (SSRS) score, and sleep quality index (PSQI score), into a practical tool for risk stratification. The nomogram demonstrated robust performance in the developmental (training) cohort (AUC=0.918), good performance in internal validation (AUC=0.867), and maintained efficacy in temporal validation (AUC=0.875). Pronounced congruence was observed between empirical incidences and forecasted likelihoods, as revealed through calibration graphing. This framework benefits healthcare personnel outside psychiatry for screening and identifying elevated-risk subjects.

Nevertheless, specific limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-center, cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships and limits observation of the dynamic interplay between psychological status and disease progression. Specifically, the directionality between risk factors and outcomes warrants caution. It is plausible that reverse causality explains part of our findings: rather than poor sleep (high PSQI) causing depression, it may be that existing depressive symptoms lead to sleep disturbances. Similarly, the relationship between HbA1c and depression could be bidirectional, where depression exacerbates glycemic dysregulation via HPA axis dysfunction and poor self-care, rather than hyperglycemia solely driving depression. This design also limits causal inference and precludes determining the temporal sequence between risk factors (eg, sleep quality,VRQoL) and depression. Second, although the sample size of 490 meets statistical requirements, recruitment from a single institution may introduce selection bias; additionally, the absence of explicit nonlinear modeling (eg, splines) limits the exploration of complex variable interactions. Future multicenter studies with larger, more diverse populations are warranted to validate the model on a broader scale, incorporate nonlinear modeling techniques, and minimize potential selection biases. Third, the assessment relied primarily on subjective instruments (eg, PSQI, VRQoL) without incorporating objective biomarkers such as IL-6, which constrains mechanistic interpretation. Fourth, the use of self-reported scales (PHQ-9, HAMD-17) in a visually impaired population may introduce measurement bias. Finally, although the model underwent internal and temporal validation, external validation across diverse populations and healthcare settings is needed to confirm its generalizability and clinical applicability, as internal/temporal validation alone cannot substitute for external validation in establishing model transportability. Consequently, future research should expand sample diversity, integrate objective biomarkers, and employ longitudinal designs to enhance the robustness and translational potential of the findings.

Conclusion

This investigation established and validated a nomogram for assessing the risk of depression in diabetic retinopathy (DR) patients through an integrative machine learning feature selection strategy. The model incorporates four key predictors across biological, psychological, and social domains: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), sleep quality (PSQI score), social support (SSRS score), and vision-related quality of life (VRQoL score). It demonstrated robust and consistent risk stratification performance in internal and temporal validations, accurately identifying patients at higher risk of current depressive symptoms. This practical tool enables non-psychiatric clinicians to screen high-risk DR patients efficiently, supporting timely mental health referral and optimized resource allocation. While the model shows significant potential for clinical application in DR management, its utility as a screening tool requires confirmation in future multicenter prospective studies.
