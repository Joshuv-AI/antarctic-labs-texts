Abstract
. We introduce a q q -deformation of the Fock space of holomorphic functions on ℂ \mathbb{C} , based on a geometric definition of q q -analyticity. This definition is inspired by a standard construction in complex differential geometry. Within this framework, we define q q -analytic monomials z q n z_{q}^{n} and construct the associated q q -Fock space as a Hilbert space with orthonormal basis { z q n / [ n ] q ! ] } n ⩾ 0 \{z_{q}^{n}/\sqrt{[n]_{q}!]}\}_{n\geqslant 0} . The reproducing kernel of this space is computed explicitly, and q q -position and q q -momentum operators are introduced, satisfying q q -deformed commutation relations. We show that the q q -monomials z q n z_{q}^{n} can be expanded in terms of complex Hermite polynomials, thereby providing a realization of the q q -Fock space as a subspace of L 2 ​ ( ℂ , e − | z | 2 ​ d ​ x ​ d ​ y ) L^{2}(\mathbb{C};e^{-|z|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y) . Finally, we define a q q -Bargmann transform that maps suitable q q -Hermite functions into our q q -Fock space and acts as a unitary isomorphism. Our construction offers a geometric and analytic approach to q q -function theory, complementing recent operator-theoretic models.

1.
Introduction

The classical Fock space of holomorphic functions on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, equipped with a Gaussian weight, plays a fundamental role in quantum mechanics, signal processing, and complex analysis. It provides a natural setting for the Bargmann transform and coherent states, allowing the interplay of analysis, geometry, and operator theory
[
4
]
.

In recent years, several attempts have been made to define
q
-analogues of analytic structures, motivated by quantum algebra, combinatorics, and
q
-special functions (see
[
8
,
13
,
19
]
). The notions of
q
-derivative, Jackson integral, and
q
-exponential have led to a renewed interest in the development of a deformation of classical analysis, known as
q
-calculus. Recently, observations regarding
q
-deformations in different contexts such as Clifford analysis and quaternionic analysis were made (see
[
1
,
6
,
15
,
24
]
).

In particular, in the recent work
[
1
]
, the authors study a family of
q
-deformed reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{q}
, which interpolate between the classical Hardy and Fock spaces, via kernels of the form

K_{q}(z,w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(zw)^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}.

Their construction focuses on operator-theoretic properties, including the adjointness relations between
q
-difference operators and multiplication, and provides structural identities such as

R_{q}M_{z}-qM_{z}R_{q}=I,

which characterize these spaces.

Turning back to holomorphicity, it is well known that holomorphic functions can be defined in several equivalent ways. However, when one tries to generalize this concept based on just one of these definitions, it often leads to the loss of some of the other equivalent characterizations. This issue becomes particularly evident in quaternionic (or hypercomplex) analysis (see, for example,
[
21
]
). A similar pattern can be observed in the context of
q
-calculus. To gain a deeper understanding of this subject, we chose to explore it from a more geometric perspective, i.e., by assuming a sort of complex differentiability.

In fact, in the standard setting, a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}U\subseteq\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is
holomorphic
if and only if its differential
\mathrm{d}f
commutes with the action of multiplication by
i
, meaning
\mathrm{d}f(i\mathbf{v})=i\,\mathrm{d}f(\mathbf{v})
for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This formulation emphasizes holomorphy as a condition not merely on partial derivatives but on the geometric structure of the differential as a linear map preserving the complex structure.

In our setting, we will impose the condition

\mathrm{d}_{q}f=\left(M_{q}^{y}D_{z}f\right)\mathrm{d}_{q}z,

where
M_{q}^{y}
is a suitable dilation operator.

Building upon this idea, we consider a
q
-deformation of the classical monomials
z^{n}
, denoted by
z_{q}^{n}
, which span a space of
q
-analytic functions. These functions form a basis of a
q
-Fock space defined by

\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})=\left\{f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}z_{q}^{n}\;\middle|\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}[n]_{q}!<\infty\right\}.

This space admits a natural inner product and a reproducing kernel structure analogous to the classical setting, yet deeply influenced by the
q
-deformation.

We further study
q
-position and
q
-momentum operators on
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
and prove that they satisfy
q
-commutation relations deforming the canonical ones. Moreover, although the monomials
z_{q}^{n}
are not holomorphic in the classical sense, each
z_{q}^{n}
admits an expansion in complex Hermite polynomials
H_{p,r}(z,\overline{z})
; this provides a concrete embedding (the
realization
in the title) of the
q
-Fock space into
L^{2}(\mathbb{C};e^{-|z|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)
.

Finally, we construct a
q
-Bargmann transform and we show that such a map is unitary. A two-variable tensor version yields an explicit coherent-state reproducing kernel and identifies the family
\{z_{q}^{k}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{\,h}\}_{k,h\geqslant 0}
and its span inside
L^{2}(\mathbb{C};e^{-|z|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)
.

Lastly, some few words on the relation between our paper and
[
1
]
.
While this paper and
[
1
]
, both deal with
q
-deformations of classical spaces and involve similar structures, our approach differs significantly in the starting idea and technical formulation. In particular,
[
1
]
builds on standard power series in
z
, while our work uses
q
-deformed monomials
z_{q}^{n}
based on geometric
q
-differentiability.
Moreover, we realize our
q
-Fock space inside
L^{2}(\mathbb{C},e^{-|z|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y)
via expansion in complex Hermite polynomials, whereas
[
1
]
considers weighted measures supported on discrete radial levels.
Finally, we introduce a
q
-Bargmann transform which maps
q
-Hermite functions into the space of
q
-analytic functions, similarly to the classical transform but adapted to the non-holomorphic
q
-setting.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we review Jackson
q
–calculus and introduce the complex
q
–derivatives
D_{z},D_{\overline{z}}
together with the

.
Then, in Section

–space on
[-\lambda,\lambda]
.
In Section

q
–Fock space
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
from the family

, compute its reproducing kernel

\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
inside

2.
Preliminaries on
q
-Calculus

Here we briefly recall the standard definitions in
q
-calculus (see
[
8
,
17
,
19
]
).
We start with the following general assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

From now until the end of the article, the number
q
is chosen to be in the open interval
(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
. Although many of the statements in the article also hold for other values of
q
(real or complex), this choice is made to ensure the good position of Jackson’s integral.

q
-Derivatives and
q
-Numbers

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be a set such that if
x\in\Omega
, then
qx\in\Omega
as well. Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be any function. The
q
-derivative
(or
q
-difference operator
) of
f
is defined by:

D_{x}^{q}f(x)=\frac{f(qx)-f(x)}{(q-1)x}.

As an example, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, and consider the function defined on the whole real line
f(x)=x^{\alpha}
. Then:

D_{x}^{q}x^{\alpha}=\frac{(qx)^{\alpha}-x^{\alpha}}{(q-1)x}=\frac{q^{\alpha}-1}{q-1}x^{\alpha-1}.

This motivates the following definition:

[\alpha]_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1-q^{\alpha}}{1-q},

so that
D_{x}^{q}x^{\alpha}=[\alpha]_{q}x^{\alpha-1}
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, for integer values
n\in\mathbb{N}
, this reduces to:

[n]_{q}=1+q+q^{2}+\dots+q^{n-1},

and the corresponding
q
-factorial is defined as

[n]_{q}!=[n]_{q}[n-1]_{q}\cdots[1]_{q},\quad[0]_{q}!\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1.

Using this, the
q
-binomial coefficients are given by:

\left[\begin{matrix}n\\
k\end{matrix}\right]_{q}=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[n-k]_{q}![k]_{q}!}.

An identity that will be useful in later sections is:

[n]_{q}-[n+1]_{q}=-q^{n}.

q
-Differentials

From the definition of the
q
-derivative, the corresponding
q
-differential can be naturally introduced.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be a set such that if
x\in\Omega
, then
qx\in\Omega
. Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be any function. The
q
-differential
of
f
is defined by:

\mathrm{d}_{q}f(x)=f(qx)-f(x)=\left(D_{x}f(x)\right)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}x,

where
\mathrm{d}_{q}x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(q-1)x
.

The operator
f(x)\mapsto f(qx)
is often called the dilation operator, denoted
M_{q}^{x}
, and acts as

M_{q}^{x}f(x)=f(qx).

Remark 2.4
.

In operator form, the dilation operator is sometimes denoted as
M_{q}^{x}=q^{x\frac{d}{dx}}
, resulting in the representation:

D_{x}^{q}=\frac{q^{x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{dx}}-1}{(q-1)x}.

In fact, the exponential operator
e^{a\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}
operates on a function
f(x)
through its formal Taylor series expansion:

e^{a\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{n}}{n!}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}f}{\mathrm{d}x^{n}}(x)=f(x+a),

which translates the function by
a
.

Similarly, the operator
q^{x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}
is defined by:

q^{x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=e^{(\ln q)x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\ln q)^{n}}{n!}\left(x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^{n},

and acts on functions by performing a dilation:

q^{x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}f(x)=f(qx).

This is because the operator
x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
acts diagonally on monomials
x^{n}
, with:

x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{n})=nx^{n},

and hence:

e^{(\ln q)x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}}x^{n}=q^{n}x^{n}=(qx)^{n}.

For any two functions
f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and any scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the operator
D_{x}
is linear:

D_{x}(\lambda f+g)(x)=\lambda D_{x}f(x)+D_{x}g(x).

Moreover, the
q
-derivative satisfies two distinct versions of the Leibniz product rule:

\displaystyle D_{x}(fg)(x)

\displaystyle=(D_{x}f(x))g(x)+f(qx)D_{x}g(x),

\displaystyle=(D_{x}f(x))g(qx)+f(x)D_{x}g(x).

However, a general chain rule does not hold in this framework.

2.1.
q
-Exponential Function

A natural extension of the classical exponential function within the framework of
q
-calculus is the Jackson exponential.

Definition 2.5
.

For
x\in\left(-\frac{1}{1-q},\frac{1}{1-q}\right)
, the
Jackson
q
-exponential
function is defined as

E_{q}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{[k]_{q}!}.

Remark 2.6
.

The series
E_{q}(x)
converges absolutely and uniformly for
|x|<\frac{1}{1-q}
when
0<q<1
, and it converges for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
when
q>1
(see
[
8
, Section 6.8]
).

A second
q
-exponential function is defined via an inverse relationship:

e_{q}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=E_{q}^{-1}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}q^{\frac{1}{2}j(j-1)}\frac{x^{j}}{[j]_{q}!}.

These functions satisfy the identity (see
[
7
]
)

E_{q}(t)\,e_{q}(-t)=1,

and their
q
-derivatives are given by:

D_{x}^{q}E_{q}(x)=E_{q}(x),\quad D_{x}^{q}e_{q}(x)=e_{q}(qx).

It is shown in
[
7
, Lemma 11]
that, for
0<q<1
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(1)

e_{q}\left(\frac{q^{-k}}{q-1}\right)=0.

2.2.
Jackson Integral

Jackson introduced a notion of integration that serves as an inverse operation to
q
-differentiation
[
18
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

For
0<q<1
, the
Jackson
q
-integral
over the interval
[0,a]
is defined as:

\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{0}^{a}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t=(1-q)a\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}f(aq^{k})q^{k}.

Integration over a general interval
[a,b]
is then defined by:

\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{a}^{b}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{0}^{b}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t-\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{0}^{a}f(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t,

and satisfies the
q
-analog of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{a}^{b}D_{x}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}x=f(b)-f(a).

Remark 2.8
.

The assumption
0<q<1
ensures the convergence of the infinite sum defining the Jackson integral. For
q>1
, this sum may diverge.

2.3.
q
-Analytic Functions

We introduce the concept of
q
-analyticity, drawing inspiration from
[
20
,
22
]
.
Prior to this definition, we establish a family of sets within which this notion becomes pertinent.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\Omega
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}
. We will say that
\Omega
is a
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant
set if, for any
(x,y)\in\Omega
, then
\{(qx,y),(x,q^{-1}y)\}\subset\Omega
.

The whole set
\mathbb{C}
is clearly a
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant
set. Other basic examples are the half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)>0\}
, as well as
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(z)>0,\,\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}(z)>0\}
.

Remark 2.10
.

We want to show how to construct non-trivial families of
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant set .
Consider
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\cong\mathbb{C}
. We denote
(x,y)
as the generating point of the set
\Omega_{(x,y)}
. By induction, we establish that the points
(qx,y)
and
(x,q^{-1}y)
are also members of
\Omega_{(x,y)}
. Consequently, we conclude that
\Omega_{(x,y)}=\{(q^{b_{1}}x,q^{-b_{2}}y),b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{(0,0)\}\}
. Therefore, the set
\Omega_{(x,y)}
is a grid generated by the point
(x,y)
and the parameter
q
. Evidently,
\Omega=\cup_{k=1}^{n}\Omega_{(x_{k},y_{k})}
satisfies the aforementioned condition.

In Figure
1
, the reader can observe a portion of a set generated from the set of points

S=\{(9.8,1.2),(10,1.2),(10.2,1.2),(9.8,1),(10,1),(10.2,1),(9.8,0.8),(10,0.8),(10.2,0.8)\}.

These are the nine points located in the bottom right corner of the image.

Figure 1
.
The set generated by
S
after
6
iterations, where
q=0.6
.

The
q
-differential of a function
f
is defined as:

\mathrm{d}_{q}f(x,y)=f(qx,qy)-f(x,y).

This can be expressed in terms of
q
-derivatives as:

\mathrm{d}_{q}f(x,y)=\left(M_{q}^{y}D_{x}^{q}f(x,y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}x+\left(D_{y}^{q}f(x,y)\right)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}y

In complex coordinates,
z=x+iy
,
\overline{z}=x-iy
, we have:

\mathrm{d}_{q}z=\mathrm{d}_{q}x+i\mathrm{d}_{q}y,\quad\mathrm{d}_{q}\overline{z}=\mathrm{d}_{q}x-i\mathrm{d}_{q}y.

Therefore, the total
q
-differential becomes:

\mathrm{d}_{q}f=\left(M_{q}^{y}D_{z}f\right)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}z+\left(M_{q}^{y}D_{\overline{z}}f\right)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}\overline{z},

where the complex
q
-derivatives are defined as:

D_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{x}^{q}-iM_{1/q}^{y}D_{y}^{q}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{x}^{q}-iD_{y}^{1/q}\right),\quad D_{\overline{z}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{x}^{q}+iM_{1/q}^{y}D_{y}^{q}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(D_{x}^{q}+iD_{y}^{1/q}\right)

and
D_{\chi}^{p}
denotes the
p
-derivative with respect to the variable
\chi
.

Remark 2.11
.

Notice that, in order for a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
to admit
D_{z}
and
D_{\overline{z}}
derivatives we must ask
\Omega
to be a
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant set.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant set.
A function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be
q
-analytic
if

D_{\overline{z}}f=0.

Therefore, if
f
is
q
-analytic, the
q
-differential simplifies to:

\mathrm{d}_{q}f=\left(M_{q}^{y}D_{z}f\right)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}z.

In the classical limit
q\to 1
, this reduces to the standard Cauchy-Riemann condition:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline{z}}=0.

Remark 2.13
.

In the classical holomorphic setting, a holomorphic function is uniquely determined up to a scalar. Conversely, a
q
-analytic function is uniquely determined up to a
(q,q^{-1})
-periodic function
A_{q}
, i.e., a function such that for any
(x,y)\in\Omega
, we have
A_{q}(x,y)=A_{q}(qx,y)=A_{q}(x,q^{-1}y)
.
These equalities imply that
A_{q}
is constant on any grid
\Omega_{(x,y)}
contained in
\Omega
.

Remark 2.14
.

Analogously to the classical case, a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
on a
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant set
\Omega
is said to be
q
-anti-analytic
if it satisfies

D_{z}f=0.

2.4.
q
-Analytic Polynomials and Power Series

As discussed in
[
3
]
, while the kernel of
D_{\overline{z}}
contains unconventional functions, there exists a class of polynomials and power series that closely resemble conventional holomorphic functions.

Definition 2.15
.

Given
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}
and
n\geq 2
, the
q
-analytic monomials
are defined recursively as follows:

z_{q}^{2}=(x+iy)(x+iqy),\quad z_{q}^{n}=z_{q}^{n-1}(x+iq^{n-1}y).

Remark 2.16
.

These monomials satisfy
D_{\overline{z}}z_{q}^{n}=0
, and moreover:

D_{z}z_{q}^{n}=[n]_{q}z_{q}^{n-1}.

Consequently, any linear combination of
z_{q}^{n}
is
q
-analytic.
Analogously, one can define the
q
-anti-analytic monomials
\overline{z}_{q}^{n}
. (Clearly,
\overline{z}_{q}^{n}=\overline{z_{q}^{n}}
.)

Note that, while
q
-analytic functions are not analytic functions (not even continuous functions
[
3
,
6
]
), the functions
z_{q}^{n}
are generalized analytic functions
[
20
]
in the sense of Vekua
[
23
]
.

Remark 2.17
.

For any positive integer
n
, it holds that
x^{2}+q^{2n}y^{2}\leq x^{2}+y^{2}
, which implies:

|z_{q}^{n}|\leq|z^{n}|.

Consequently, if a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{D}_{r}\to\mathbb{C}
is holomorphic with power series expansion
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z^{k}
, then the
q
-power series

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z_{q}^{k}

is well-defined and
q
-analytic in any
(q,q^{-1})
-invariant subset contained in the same disc
\mathbb{D}_{r}
. In particular, if
f
is an entire function, then
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}z_{q}^{k}
is
well-defined and
q
-analytic in the whole space
\mathbb{C}
.

3.
A Family of
q
-Deformed Hermite Polynomials

In this section, we recall a family of
q
-deformed Hermite polynomials introduced in
[
9
]
, and later studied in a different context in
[
7
]
. These polynomials will play a key role in the subsequent definition of the
q
-Bargmann transform.

3.1.
The
q
-Analog of the Gamma Function

We begin by introducing a
q
-analogue of the Gamma function. For
q<1
, it is defined by:

\Gamma_{q}(t)=\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{k})}{\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-q^{t+k})}(1-q)^{1-t},

and it satisfies the functional equation:

\Gamma_{q}(t+1)=[t]_{q}\Gamma_{q}(t),

see
[
13
]
.
Moreover,
\Gamma_{q}
admits the following
q
-integral representation:

\Gamma_{q}(z)=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{1-q}}\,t^{z-1}e_{q}(-qt)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t,

see
[
10
,
11
]
.
For
q<1
, and setting
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{1}{1-q^{2}}}
, the following identity holds:

\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}t^{\nu-1}e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t=\frac{2}{q+1}q^{\nu}\Gamma_{q^{2}}\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right).

3.2.
The
q
-Hermite Equation

Following
[
7
]
, we consider the
q
-Hermite equation originally studied by Exton
[
9
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
q
-Hermite polynomial

H_{k}^{q}
is defined as a polynomial of the form

H_{k}^{q}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}a_{k}^{j}t^{k-2j},

with initial coefficient
a_{k}^{0}=(q+1)^{k}
. These polynomials are eigenfunctions of the
q
-Hermite differential equation:

\left(\left(D_{t}^{q}\right)^{2}-(q+1)tD_{t}^{q}\right)f(t)=-(q+1)\lambda f(qt).

The corresponding eigenvalues are given by:

\lambda_{k}=[k]_{q}q^{-k}.

An explicit expression for
H_{k}^{q}
is:

H_{k}^{q}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}(q+1)^{k-j}\frac{[k]_{q}!}{[k-2j]_{q}![-2j]_{q}[-2j+2]_{q}\cdots[-2]_{q}}t^{k-2j}.

As usual, in the classical limit
q\to 1
, these reduce to the standard Hermite polynomials:

H_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}(-1)^{j}2^{k}\frac{k!}{(k-2j)!j!}t^{k-2j}.

3.3.
Recurrence Relation, Annihilation and Creation Operators

We now present the recurrence relation and annihilation operator satisfied by the
q
-Hermite polynomials. These can be derived by analyzing the structure of their expansion coefficients.

Theorem 3.2

(Theorem 6 in
[
7
]
)
.

The
q
-Hermite polynomials satisfy the recurrence relation:

H_{k+1}^{q}=(q+1)tH_{k}^{q}-(q+1)[k]_{q}q^{k}H_{k-1}^{q}\quad\text{for }k>0.

Moreover, the
q
-derivative acts as an annihilation operator:

D_{t}^{q}H_{k}^{q}(t)=(q+1)[k]_{q}H_{k-1}^{q}(t).

In the classical case, the creation operator can be derived from either the recurrence relation or directly from the Hermite differential equation. However, in the
q
-deformed setting, these two approaches yield distinct creation operators.

For the
q
-Hermite polynomials
H_{k}^{q}
, the following identities hold for
k>0
:

H_{k}^{q}(t)=\left((q+1)t-q^{k}D_{t}^{q}\right)H_{k-1}^{q}(t),\quad H_{k}^{q}(qt)=q^{k}\left((q+1)t-D_{t}^{q}\right)H_{k-1}^{q}(t).

3.4.
Exponential Function and Orthogonality

Recall the
q
-exponential function:

e_{q^{2}}(u)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{q^{j(j-1)}u^{j}}{[j]_{q}!}.

This function satisfies the identity:

D_{t}^{q}\left[e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})\right]=-(q+1)t\,e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2}).

Using this identity and the Leibniz rule, one obtains:

H_{k}^{q}(qt)\,e_{q^{2}}(-q^{2}t^{2})=-q^{k}D_{t}^{q}\left[H_{k-1}^{q}(t)\,e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})\right].

Theorem 8 in
[
7
]
establishes the orthogonality of the
q
-Hermite polynomials. Recall from Equation (
1
) that the convergence radius of
e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})
is
\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}
.

If
0<q<1
, the
q
-Hermite polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the inner product

(2)

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}f(t)\,g(t)\,e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t,

where
\lambda^{2}=\frac{1}{1-q^{2}}
. In particular, we have:

(3)

\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}H_{k}^{q}(t)\,H_{l}^{q}(t)\,e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t=\delta_{kl}\,2(q+1)^{k-1}\,q^{\frac{1}{2}(k+1)(k+2)}\,[k]_{q}!\,\Gamma_{q^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\Lambda\,\delta_{kl},

with

(4)

\Lambda=2(q+1)^{k-1}\,q^{\frac{1}{2}(k+1)(k+2)}\,[k]_{q}!\,\Gamma_{q^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right).

Remark 3.3
.

This inner product is positive definite only when the functions are restricted to the discrete set of points
\{\pm\lambda q^{j}\mid j\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

With the inner product in Formula (
2
), we define a weighted
L_{q}^{2}
-space:

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}
. The space

L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R},e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2}),\lambda)
is the closure of

\operatorname{span}\Big\{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}\,H_{k}^{q}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}k\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}

with respect to the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}f(t)\,g(t)\,e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t.

Remark 3.5
.

This definition resembles the situation in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),
where the Hermite functions are a basis.

4.
A
q
-Fock Space of
q
-analytic Functions

In this section, we will introduce and study a
q
-deformed version of the classical Fock space.
Inspired by the work
[
2
]
, we will define all the necessary concepts in terms of the coefficients of the functions involved in the space itself. Later, we will explore a method to reconcile the discrete and continuous approaches.
In the classical case
[
2
]
, the reproducing kernel of the Fock space typically follows from

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{n}(\xi)u_{n}(\overline{z})=\exp(\xi\overline{z})

with
u_{n}(z)=\frac{z^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}
.

In
[
20
]
the authors describe a
q
-wave function built from the functions

\psi_{n}(z_{q})=\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}=\frac{(x+iy)_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}.

Let us consider then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi_{n}(\xi_{q})\psi_{n}(\overline{z}_{q})
. We obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi_{n}(\xi_{q})\psi_{n}(\overline{z}_{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\xi_{1}+i\xi_{2})_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\cdot\frac{(z_{1}-iz_{2})_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\xi_{1}+i\xi_{2})_{q}^{n}(z_{1}-iz_{2})_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}.

It is not possible to combine this into a
q
-deformed exponential. However, for
q\rightarrow 1
, we recover
\exp(\xi\overline{z})
. We must also consider the convergence of this series. While defining the wave functions
\psi_{n}
from the
q
-coherent states, the authors of
[
20
]
argue that we need to restrict to the disc
|z|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{1-q}
for
|q|<1
. For
q>1
, the
q
-exponential function (Jackson’s exponential) converges without restriction.
We can still determine whether this is a reproducing kernel of our
q
-Fock space.

Set

K_{q}(z_{q},w_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}\overline{w}_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}.

We are now able to define our
q
-Fock space.

Definition 4.1
.

The
q
-Fock space
is defined as

\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})=\left\{f(z_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z_{q}^{n}a_{n}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{C},\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_{n}|^{2}[n]_{q}!<\infty\right\},

where
|q|<1
.

This
q
-Fock space can be endowed with the following inner product and the associated norm:

(5)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!a_{n}\overline{b_{n}},\qquad\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!|a_{n}|^{2}.

Now let us go back to our function
K_{q}
.

Remark 4.2
.

The inner product (
5
) on the
q
–Fock space can be realized as a (
q
-)Fischer inner product:

(6)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\text{F}}=f(D_{z})\,\overline{g}(0)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{[n]_{q}!}\,D_{z}^{\,n}f(0)\,\overline{D_{z}^{\,n}g(0)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!\,a_{n}\,\overline{b_{n}},

where
f(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}a_{n}z^{n}
,
g(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}b_{n}z^{n}
. In particular,

f(D_{z})\,\overline{f}(0)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}[n]_{q}!\,|a_{n}|^{2}>0
for
f\neq 0
.

Theorem 4.3
.

The function
K_{q}(z_{q},w_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}\overline{w}_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}
is the reproducing kernel of the
q
-Fock space
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha(w_{q})=\frac{\overline{w}_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}
. Then we have:

K_{q}(z_{q},w_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z_{q}^{n}\alpha(w_{q}).

Consider the norm:

\lVert K_{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!|\alpha(w_{q})|^{2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|w_{q}^{n}|\frac{1}{[n]_{q}!}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|w^{n}|\frac{1}{[n]_{q}!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|w|^{n}\frac{1}{[n]_{q}!}<\infty

if
q\in(0,1)
and
|w|<\frac{1}{1-q}
.
The inequality follows from Remark
2.17
.
Therefore,
K_{q}(z_{q},w_{q})\in\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
.
Next, we need to show that
e_{w}(f)=f(w_{q})
is a continuous linear functional and
|e_{w}(f)|\leq\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}\lVert K_{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}\,\forall f\in\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
. Consider an arbitrary sequence of coefficients
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{C}
.

\displaystyle|e_{w}(f)|=|f(w_{q})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|w_{q}^{n}|\cdot|a_{n}|=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}|w_{q}^{n}|\cdot|a_{n}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{[n]_{q}!}|a_{n}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!(|w_{q}^{n}|)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}\lVert K_{q}\rVert_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})},

where the last inequality follows, again, thanks to Remark
2.17
.

Finally we just need to verify that
K_{q}(z_{q},w_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}\overline{w}_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}
is a reproducing kernel of
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, i.e.
\langle f,K_{q}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}=f(w_{q})
.

Take
f(z_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z_{q}^{n}a_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
with
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{C}
and set
b_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\overline{w}_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}
.

\langle f,K_{q}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!a_{n}\overline{b_{n}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[n]_{q}!a_{n}\frac{w_{q}^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}w_{q}^{n}=f(w_{q}).

∎

Let us consider
q
-binomials
z_{q}^{n}
. Their inner product is then:

\langle z_{q}^{n},z_{q}^{m}\rangle_{\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})}=[n]_{q}!\delta_{n,m}

as we only have
a_{k}=b_{k}=1
if
m=n
.

Remark 4.4
.

The kernel
K_{q}(z_{q},w_{q})
is positive definite and Hermitian symmetric. By the Moore–Aronszajn theorem
[
5
]
, it defines a unique reproducing kernel Hilbert space. However, this space does not coincide exactly with our
q
-deformed Fock space
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
.

Indeed, while the
q
-complex binomials
z_{q}^{n}
form a linearly independent set that spans
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, they are not orthogonal with respect to the standard inner product. This lack of orthogonality arises because the monomials
z_{q}^{n}
generally depend on both
z
and
\overline{z}
, as will be further discussed in Section
4.2
.

For the same reason, the inner product in
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
cannot be defined using an integral formulation. Explicit computations show that
\langle z_{q}^{n},z_{q}^{m}\rangle\neq 0
even when
n\neq m
, whenever the product
z_{q}^{n}\cdot\overline{z}_{q}^{m}
retains mixed dependence on
z
and
\overline{z}
.

Nevertheless, we will attempt to construct an explicit realization of the
q
-Fock space within a proper Hilbert space framework in Section
4.2
.

4.1.
Position and Momentum Operators

In this subsection, we introduce the
q
-deformed analogues of the position and momentum operators and
we compute their commutator, which deforms the identity and tends to it as

q\to 1
.

We define the creation operator
a
and the annihilation operator
a^{\dagger}
based on the
q
-position operator
X_{q}=z\circ M_{q}^{y}
and the
q
-momentum operator
P_{q}=-iD_{z}
by:

a=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(X_{q}+iP_{q})\qquad a^{\dagger}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(X_{q}-iP_{q}),

where
z=x+iy
, and we recall that
M_{q}^{y}
denotes the dilation operator acting on the
y
-variable, i.e.,
M_{q}^{y}(f(x,y))=f(x,qy)
.
Note that this action satisfies:

(z\circ M_{q}^{y})(z_{q}^{n})=z_{q}^{n+1}.

Remark 4.5
.

Since

a=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(X_{q}+iP_{q})
, and
a^{\dagger}=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(X_{q}-iP_{q})
,
we have that

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\,[X_{q}+iP_{q},\,X_{q}-iP_{q}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\big([X_{q},-iP_{q}]+[iP_{q},X_{q}]\big)=-\,i[X_{q},P_{q}]

\displaystyle=-\,i[X_{q},-\,iD_{z}]=[D_{z},X_{q}].

Hence,
[a,a^{\dagger}]=[D_{z},X_{q}]
. In particular,

-i[X_{q},P_{q}]=[D_{z},X_{q}]
.

In the classical case, the commutator
[a,a^{\dagger}]
equals the identity operator. In our
q
-deformed setting, the commutator deforms accordingly and tends to the identity as
q\to 1
.

Lemma 4.6
.

The commutator of the creation and annihilation operators satisfy

[a,a^{\dagger}](z_{q}^{n})=q^{n}\,z_{q}^{n}\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant 0.

Proof.

Using
[a,a^{\dagger}]=[D_{z},X_{q}]
and the rules above,

\displaystyle(z_{q}^{n})

\displaystyle=D_{z}\big((z\circ M_{q}^{y})(z_{q}^{n})\big)-(z\circ M_{q}^{y})\big(D_{z}z_{q}^{n}\big)

\displaystyle=D_{z}(z_{q}^{n+1})-(z\circ M_{q}^{y})([n]_{q}z_{q}^{n-1})=[n+1]_{q}z_{q}^{n}-[n]_{q}z_{q}^{n}=q^{n}z_{q}^{n},

since
[n+1]_{q}-[n]_{q}=q^{n}
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

For any positive integer
n
we have that

-i[X_{q},P_{q}](z_{q}^{n})=q^{n}\,z_{q}^{n}.

Remark 4.8
.

Recall the Fischer inner product from Formula (
6
); then, for arbitrary polynomials

R(z)=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}r_{m}z_{q}^{m},\qquad Q(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}q_{n}z_{q}^{n},

we have

\displaystyle\langle D_{z}R,\,Q\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{m\geqslant 1}r_{m}[m]_{q}z_{q}^{m-1},\,\sum_{n\geqslant 0}q_{n}z_{q}^{n}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}r_{m}[m]_{q}\,\overline{q_{m-1}}\,\langle z_{q}^{m-1},z_{q}^{m-1}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}r_{m}[m]_{q}[m-1]_{q}!\,\overline{q_{m-1}}=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}r_{m}[m]_{q}!\,\overline{q_{m-1}}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\langle R,\,X_{q}Q\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\sum_{m\geqslant 0}r_{m}z_{q}^{m},\,\sum_{n\geqslant 0}q_{n}z_{q}^{n+1}\Big\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\overline{q_{n}}\,\langle z_{q}^{n+1},z_{q}^{n+1}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}\,r_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}r_{n+1}[n+1]_{q}!\,\overline{q_{n}}=\sum_{m\geqslant 1}r_{m}[m]_{q}!\,\overline{q_{m-1}},

which equals
\langle D_{z}R,\,Q\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}
. Hence
(D_{z})^{\ast}=X_{q}
and
(X_{q})^{\ast}=D_{z}

with respect to the Fischer inner product. (In contrast, the oscillator-style pair

a=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(X_{q}+iP_{q})
,
a^{\dagger}=\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}(X_{q}-iP_{q})

is not an adjoint pair for the Fischer product.)

4.2.
Realization of
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{C};e^{-z\overline{z}}dxdy)

The family of functions
\mathcal{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{z_{q}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not composed of holomorphic functions. Indeed, for any

L^{2}(\mathbb{C};e^{-z\overline{z}}\,dx\,dy)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\,|\,\int_{\mathbb{C}}||f||^{2}\,e^{-z\overline{z}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty\},

The set

Proof of Proposition

The space

N=2
,

z_{q}^{2}=z(A_{1}z+B_{1}\overline{z})=A_{1}z^{2}+B_{1}z\overline{z},\quad z_{q}\overline{z}_{q}=z\overline{z},\quad\overline{z}_{q}^{2}=\overline{z}(A_{1}\overline{z}+B_{1}z)=B_{1}z\overline{z}+A_{1}\overline{z}^{2}.

Suppose a linear combination
D_{2,0}z_{q}^{2}+D_{1,1}z_{q}\overline{z}_{q}+D_{0,2}\overline{z}_{q}^{2}=0
. Since
z^{2}
appears only in
z_{q}^{2}
, it follows that
D_{2,0}=0
; similarly
D_{0,2}=0
from the term
\overline{z}^{2}
. Therefore,
D_{1,1}=0
as well.

We now pass to the case
N=3
and then proceed for the general case.
We have

\begin{cases}z_{q}^{3}=z(A_{1}z+B_{1}\overline{z})(A_{2}z+B_{2}\overline{z})\\
z_{q}^{2}\overline{z}_{q}=z(A_{1}z+B_{1}\overline{z})\overline{z}\\
z_{q}\overline{z}_{q}^{2}=z\overline{z}(B_{1}z+A_{1}\overline{z})\\
\overline{z}_{q}^{3}=\overline{z}(B_{1}z+A_{1}\overline{z})(B_{2}z+A_{2}\overline{z}).\end{cases}

Hence, if we consider a linear combination

D_{3,0}z_{q}^{3}+D_{2,1}z_{q}^{2}\overline{z}_{q}+D_{1,2}z_{q}\overline{z}_{q}^{2}+D_{0,3}\overline{z}_{q}^{3}=0,

then the only summands containing
z^{3}
and
\overline{z}^{3}
are the first and the last, respectively.
Therefore,
D_{3,0}=D_{0,3}=0
and we are left with

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=D_{2,1}z_{q}^{2}\overline{z}_{q}+D_{1,2}z_{q}\overline{z}_{q}^{2}=z\overline{z}(D_{2,1}(A_{1}z+B_{1}\overline{z})+D_{1,2}(B_{1}z+A_{1}\overline{z}))

\displaystyle=z\overline{z}((D_{2,1}A_{1}+D_{1,2}B_{1})z+(D_{2,1}B_{1}+D_{1,2}A_{1})\overline{z}).

Now, since
A_{1}^{2}-B_{1}^{2}\neq 0
, then we get that
D_{2,1}=D_{1,2}=0
.

Consider now the family
\{z_{q}^{k}\overline{z}_{q}^{h}\}_{k+h=N+1}
and suppose:

\sum_{k+h=N+1}D_{k,h}z_{q}^{k}\overline{z}_{q}^{h}=0.

As before, the only terms containing
z^{N+1}
and
\overline{z}^{N+1}
appear in
z_{q}^{N+1}
and
\overline{z}_{q}^{N+1}
, respectively, so
D_{N+1,0}=D_{0,N+1}=0
. What remains is:

(9)

D_{N,1}z_{q}^{N}\overline{z}_{q}+D_{N-1,2}z_{q}^{N-1}\overline{z}_{q}^{2}+\dots+D_{1,N}z_{q}\overline{z}_{q}^{N}=0.

Now, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we can express

z_{q}^{k}=z_{q^{0}}z_{q^{1}}\dots z_{q^{k-1}},\quad\text{with }z_{q^{\ell}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x+iq^{\ell}y,

so
z_{q}^{k}=z\cdot(z_{q^{1}})_{q}^{k-1}
. Then equation (
9
) becomes:

(10)

z\overline{z}\left(D_{N,1}(z_{q^{1}})_{q}^{N-1}+D_{N-1,2}(z_{q^{1}})_{q}^{N-2}\overline{z}_{q^{1}}+\dots+D_{1,N}(\overline{z}_{q^{1}})_{q}^{N-1}\right)=0,

where
z_{q^{1}}=x+iqy
.

We now repeat the previous argument where
z
is replaced by the elliptic complex variable

w=z_{q^{1}}
(see Remark
4.10
). Therefore, Formula (
10
) can be written as

(11)

z\overline{z}\left(D_{N,1}w_{q}^{N-1}+D_{N-1,2}w_{q}^{N-2}\overline{w}_{q}+\dots+D_{1,N}\overline{w}_{q}^{N-1}\right)=0.

Similarly as before we have
(\overline{w}_{q})^{M}=\overline{w_{q}^{M}}
and,

w_{q}^{M}=w\left(A_{1}w+B_{1}\overline{w}\right)\left(A_{2}w+B_{2}\overline{w}\right)\dots\left(A_{M-1}w+B_{M-1}\overline{w}\right).

Hence, again the only instances of
w^{N-1}
and of
\overline{w}^{N-1}
are contained in the first and last summands of Equation (
11
), respectively.
Therefore, thanks to Lemma
4.11
,
D_{N,1}
and

D_{1,N}
must be equal to zero.

Again, as before, we are allowed to collect
w\overline{w}
from
the left hand side of Formula (
11
) and so we are left with

z\overline{z}w\overline{w}\left(D_{N-1,2}w_{q}^{N-3}+\dots+D_{2,N-1}\overline{w}_{q}^{N-3}\right)=0.

We now start an iteration process in which at each step we define a new elliptic complex variable
z_{q^{\ell}}
, so then the first and last coefficients
D_{N-\ell,\ell+1}
and

D_{\ell+1,N-\ell}
must vanish and we can factorize
z_{q^{\ell}}\overline{z}_{q^{\ell}}
again.

Eventually we will end up in one of the two cases
N=2
or
N=3
discussed before, depending whether the starting power
N+1
is even or odd.
∎

5.
A
q
-Bargmann transform

In this section we construct a
q
-Bargmann transform that identifies the
Jackson
L^{2}_{q}
-space on
[-\lambda,\lambda]
with the
q
–Fock space
endowed with the Fischer inner product. We introduce the kernel,
compute the image of the
q
–Hermite basis, and prove unitarity.

Fix
\lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}
. Let
\{H_{k}^{q}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
be the
q
–Hermite polynomials discussed in Section
3

and define the
q
–Hermite functions

\tilde{H}_{k}^{q}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}\,H_{k}^{q}(t)\,\sqrt{e_{q^{2}}(-t^{2})}\,,

where
\Lambda
is given in Formula (
4
).
By construction the family
\{\tilde{H}_{k}^{q}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
is orthonormal in

L_{q}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)
with respect to the Jackson inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}_{q}}=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}f(t)\,\overline{g(t)}\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t.

On the
q
–Fock space
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
we use the orthonormal basis

\big\{\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\big\}_{n\geqslant 0}
.

Definition 5.1
.

Define the kernel

(12)

A(z_{q},t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\;\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}(t),

and the linear map
\mathcal{B}_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L_{q}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)\to\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
by

(13)

(\mathcal{B}_{q}f)(z_{q})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}f(t)\,A(z_{q},t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t.

Proposition 5.2
.

For every
m\geqslant 0
,

\mathcal{B}_{q}(\tilde{H}_{m}^{q})(z_{q})=\frac{z_{q}^{m}}{\sqrt{[m]_{q}!}}.

Proof.

Using (
12
)–(
13
) and orthonormality of
\{\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}\}
,

\mathcal{B}_{q}(\tilde{H}_{m}^{q})=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\tilde{H}_{m}^{q}(t)\!\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}(t)\right)\!\mathrm{d}_{q}t.

Now consider the sequence:

\left(\varphi_{N}\right)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left(\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\tilde{H}_{m}^{q}(t)\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t\right)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}.

This sequence satisfies:

\varphi_{N}=0\quad\text{for }N<m,\quad\varphi_{N}=\frac{z_{q}^{m}}{\sqrt{[m]_{q}!}}\quad\text{for }N\geq m.

Therefore, it becomes constant for
N\geq m
and converges to that value. Using the orthonormality relation from Equation (
3
), we conclude:

\mathcal{B}_{q}(\tilde{H}_{m}^{q})(z_{q})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}(t)\tilde{H}_{m}^{q}(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\delta_{n,m}=\frac{z_{q}^{m}}{\sqrt{[m]_{q}!}}.

∎

Theorem 5.3
.

The map
\mathcal{B}_{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L_{q}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)\to\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
is unitary.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.2
,
\mathcal{B}_{q}
maps the orthonormal basis

\{\tilde{H}_{m}^{q}\}_{m\geqslant 0}
of
L_{q}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)
onto the orthonormal basis

\big\{\frac{z_{q}^{m}}{\sqrt{[m]_{q}!}}\big\}_{m\geqslant 0}
of
\mathcal{F}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
(with Fischer product).
Hence
\mathcal{B}_{q}
is an isometry on the finite span of the basis and extends by continuity
to a surjective isometry between the completions, i.e., a unitary operator.
Equivalently,

\big\langle\mathcal{B}_{q}\tilde{H}_{m}^{q},\mathcal{B}_{q}\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}\big\rangle_{\text{F}}=\delta_{mn}=\big\langle\tilde{H}_{m}^{q},\tilde{H}_{n}^{q}\big\rangle_{L^{2}_{q}},

hence
\mathcal{B}_{q}
is unitary.
∎

5.1.
Tensor Product of the Bargmann Transform and its conjugate

The transform introduced by V. Bargmann is bijective and isometric from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto the classical Fock space; in particular the Hermite functions are mapped to the normalized analytic monomials, which form an orthonormal basis of entire functions.
In our setting, while we found a
q
-Bargmann transform
\mathcal{B}_{q}
mapping the
q
-Hermite basis to the powers of
z_{q}
(Theorem
5.3
), the family
\{z_{q}^{k}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
is not a basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{C};e^{-z\overline{z}}\,dx\,dy)
.
Instead, Section
4.2
shows that the bidimensional family
\{z_{q}^{k}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{h}\}_{k,h\in\mathbb{N}}
is complete (Proposition
4.9
).
This motivates a “tensorial” construction, reminiscent of the classical tensor product of Bargmann transforms.

Define the bidimensional
q
-Fock space

\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{q}(\mathbb{C})\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ \overline{\mathrm{span}}\left\{\frac{z_{q}^{k}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{h}}{\sqrt{[k]_{q}!\,[h]_{q}!}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\ k,h\in\mathbb{N}\right\},\qquad\big\langle z_{q}^{k}\overline{z}_{q}^{h},\ z_{q}^{m}\overline{z}_{q}^{n}\big\rangle_{\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{q}}=[k]_{q}!\,[h]_{q}!\,\delta_{km}\delta_{hn}.

We introduce the factorized kernel

(14)

A^{(2)}(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q};\,t,s)\ \mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ A(z_{q},t)\,A(\overline{z}_{q},s)=\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sum_{k,h\geqslant 0}\frac{z_{q}^{k}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{h}}{\sqrt{[k]_{q}!\,[h]_{q}!}}\ \widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}(t)\,\widetilde{H}^{q}_{h}(s).

Since
\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}
are real–valued,
A^{(2)}(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q};\,t,s)
is Hermitian-symmetric in
(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q})
.

Notation 5.4
.

We write
H\widehat{\otimes}K
for the Hilbert tensor product, i.e., the completion of
the algebraic tensor product
H\otimes K
with respect to the inner product

\langle u_{1}\!\otimes\!v_{1},\,u_{2}\!\otimes\!v_{2}\rangle=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle_{H}\,\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle_{K}
.
In particular,

L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)\,\widehat{\otimes}\,L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)\cong L^{2}_{q}([-\lambda,\lambda]^{2},\lambda^{\otimes 2}),

and the orthonormal basis
\{\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
induces

\{\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}\otimes\widetilde{H}^{q}_{h}\}_{k,h\geqslant 0}
.

Definition 5.5
.

Define

\mathcal{B}_{q}^{(2)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)\ \widehat{\otimes}\ L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{q}(\mathbb{C}),\qquad(\mathcal{B}_{q}^{(2)}f)(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{[-\lambda,\lambda]^{2}}\!\!f(t,s)\,A^{(2)}(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q};\,t,s)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t\,\mathrm{d}_{q}s.

Theorem 5.6
.

The map
\mathcal{B}_{q}^{(2)}
is unitary. Moreover,

\mathcal{B}_{q}^{(2)}\!\big(\,\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}\otimes\widetilde{H}^{q}_{h}\,\big)(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q})=\frac{z_{q}^{k}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{h}}{\sqrt{[k]_{q}!\,[h]_{q}!}}\qquad(k,h\in\mathbb{N}).

Proof.

Using (
14
) and orthonormality of
\{\widetilde{H}^{q}_{n}\}
in
L^{2}_{q}(\mathbb{R},\lambda)
, we get

\mathcal{B}_{q}^{(2)}(\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}\!\otimes\!\widetilde{H}^{q}_{h})=\sum_{m,n\geqslant 0}\frac{z_{q}^{m}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[m]_{q}!\,[n]_{q}!}}\left(\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\!\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}(t)\widetilde{H}^{q}_{m}(t)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}t\right)\left(\mathcal{J}\mspace{-20mu}\int_{-\lambda}^{\lambda}\!\widetilde{H}^{q}_{h}(s)\widetilde{H}^{q}_{n}(s)\,\mathrm{d}_{q}s\right)=\frac{z_{q}^{k}\,\overline{z}_{q}^{h}}{\sqrt{[k]_{q}!\,[h]_{q}!}}.

Hence
\mathcal{B}_{q}^{(2)}
maps the orthonormal tensor basis
\{\widetilde{H}^{q}_{k}\otimes\widetilde{H}^{q}_{h}\}
onto the orthonormal basis

\{\frac{z_{q}^{k}\overline{z}_{q}^{h}}{\sqrt{[k]_{q}!\,[h]_{q}!}}\}
of
\mathcal{F}^{(2)}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, so it is an isometry on finite spans and extends by continuity to a surjective isometry between the completions.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

Setting

\Phi_{z_{q}}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A(z_{q},t)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\frac{z_{q}^{n}}{\sqrt{[n]_{q}!}}\ \widetilde{H}^{q}_{n}(t),

the kernel factorizes as
A^{(2)}(z_{q},\overline{z}_{q};\,t,s)=\Phi_{z_{q}}(t)\,\Phi_{\overline{z}_{q}}(s)
.
Thus
\{\Phi_{z_{q}}\}_{z\in\mathbb{C}}
may be regarded as a family of
q
–coherent state vectors for the analysis operator
\mathcal{B}_{q}
, and
A^{(2)}
provides the natural tensor product coherent kernel.
