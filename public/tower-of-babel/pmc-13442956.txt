Smart detection of juice adulteration: An approach based on ion mobility spectrometry and machine learning
Authors: José Luis P Calle, Marta Ferreiro‐González, Miguel Palma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDFruit juice quality is regulated by European Union legislation; however, adulteration by adding cheaper alternatives, such as white grape juice, remains a prevalent issue. This practice constitutes economic fraud and poses health risks to consumers, including potential allergic reactions to the adulterant. Addressing this problem requires reliable methods for detecting and quantifying adulteration in fruit juices.RESULTSThis study developed a method for automated data processing and quantification of adulteration levels in orange, pineapple, and apple juices adulterated with white grape juice. The approach combines headspace‐gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (HS‐GC–IMS) with machine learning (ML) algorithms. Regression models, including support vector regression (SVR), partial least squares (PLS), and random forest (RF) regression, were trained. The Boruta algorithm was applied to select relevant variables, enhancing model performance. Among the models, SVR achieved the best performance with a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.831 and a coefficient of determination (R 2) of 0.987. An interactive web application was also developed to facilitate data processing and make the methodology accessible to users.CONCLUSIONSThe results demonstrate the high accuracy and reliability of combining HS‐GC–IMS with ML algorithms for quantifying adulterants in fruit juices. This non‐targeted workflow, unlike traditional methods, provides an effective tool to prevent fraud and ensure consumer safety, while the web application enhances usability, allowing broader adoption in analytical settings. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Fruit juices are one of the most widely consumed beverages and are associated with different nutritional benefits. 1 In 2023, the total production of 100% fruit juices and nectars in the European Union (EU) reached about 6.8 billion liters, representing an estimated market value of 19.2 billion euros. 2 Considering their large consumption and economic importance, it is essential to ensure their quality and authenticity. Although their production is regulated by the 2012/12 EU directive, 3 fruit juices are among the most adulterated food products. 4 , 5 This illegal practice can affect nutritional quality, composition and even pose heath risk. Generally, these adulterations involve the addition of water and/or sugars. However, there is a growing trend of using cheaper or lower quality fruit juices as adulterants, which are more difficult to detect and may trigger allergic reactions in unaware consumers. 6 , 7

Conventional analytical methods, such us liquid chromatography (LC) or gas chromatography (GC) coupled to mass spectrometry (MS) have proven effective for detecting adulteration in fruit juices. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 However, these techniques, which are commonly based on the identification of target compounds, suffer from some drawbacks including long analysis times, the use of solvents, extensive sample preparation, and the need for skilled analysts. 12 In contrast, data‐driven approaches based on machine learning (ML) enable the direct prediction of sample authenticity, avoiding the need to identify individual compounds. This makes the process faster and easier to automate for routine quality control.

For this reason, global profiling techniques (non‐target techniques) are a promising alternative for automatic and objective quality control of foods including juices. Unlike identification of target compounds, these methodologies use all or most of the signals obtained, enabling the detection of subtle significant differences for characterization. 13 Studies using ultraviolet (UV)‐visible, near‐infrared (NIR), or Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) in combination with ML have been recently used for the detection of a great variety of adulterants in different types of fruit juices 7 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Nevertheless, it has been less used for detecting juice‐to‐juice adulterations. 14 , 20 , 21 , 22 Moreover, grape juice has specifically been used to adulterate other fruit juices, and its adulteration has been previously identified using Raman spectroscopy, 23 GC × GC–MS, 24 or fluorescence spectroscopy, 25 applying chemometrics to characterize the sample.

Among global profiling techniques, headspace‐gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (HS‐GC–IMS) is increasingly being used in food fraud as an electronic nose 26 by overriding the chromatographic separation. The resulting ion mobility sum spectrum (IMSS) 27 provides a global volatile profile that reflects the overall composition of the sample. This technique combines low detection limits (ppb), fast analysis, low cost, environmental friendliness, and its real‐time and portable operation is helpful for routine on‐site quality control analyses. 27 Although HS‐GC–IMS have been widely used in many different fields, 28 , 29 , 30 this technique has demonstrated to be a promising tool for the characterization and quality control of different foodstuff such as honey, 27 , 31 coffee 32 or oil. 33 , 34 , 35 However, its application in juice samples is still very scarce. It has been previously applied to study the evolution of volatile flavor compounds in pumpkin juice during storage, 36 to monitor the fermentation process of cherry juice 37 as well as to control the variations of volatile flavor compounds during storage of bayberry juice. 38 However, these studies relied on the identification of target compounds and not HS‐GC–IMS‐based global profiling methods. In contrast, Brendel et al. successfully applied IMS data for juice characterization using a global profile. 39 Similarly, in a previous study carried out by our research group, the IMSS was used to characterize various juices, optimizing the analysis conditions. 40 This demonstrated the potential of HS‐GC–IMS as a global profiling technique, positioning it as a promising alternative for quantifying adulterations in fruit juices.

The huge amount of data obtained from the IMSS makes data processing a key factor for the characterization of the samples. Recent advances in chemometric tools based on ML have driven the use of a global profiling technique that can automate data interpretation and decision‐making industrial quality control. In fruit juice analysis, the main chemometric tools commonly used are linear discriminant analysis (LDA) for classification problems and partial least squares (PLS) regression for the quantification of the level of adulteration. However, the use of other techniques, such as support vector regression (SVR) or random forest (RF) models, has also shown great potential, especially when coupled with global profiling techniques. 41

Therefore, the objective of this study is to develop a new method based on HS‐GC–IMS (IMSS) in combination with ML models (RF, SVR, and PLS) to demonstrate the potential of global volatile profiling for the automatic quantification of juice‐to‐juice adulteration at different levels in different fruit juices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

To ensure a diverse representation of fruit juices, three types of unadulterated fruit juices from different raw materials (pineapple, apple, and orange) were selected. These specific juices were chosen as they are among the most widely consumed in the European market, 42 making them highly relevant for authenticity studies. White grape juice was selected as an adulterant because of its low cost compared to other types of fruit juices. In addition, to capture a wide range of heterogeneity, the samples were obtained from local markets, encompassing multiple brands and batches for each type of juice. To preserve the heterogeneity throughout the adulteration process, new samples of each juice type were prepared by mixing the different brands and batches in equal proportions. These mixtures were deliberately prepared in duplicate to ensure reliability and consistency in the experimental approach. The adulterations were prepared by adding the adulterant (white grape juice) at different ratios (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 30%, 40% and 50%) to the mixture of each type of juice. This range was selected to cover both subtle adulterations and the composition of common commercial blends, providing a representative dataset of the real problem of the juice adulteration, additionally these samples allow for model development and validation. All prepared samples were stored in sealed, dark containers under refrigeration (4 °C) until analysis to preserve their composition. Samples were analyzed in duplicate. Therefore, the total number of samples was 96 (three types of fruit juice × two samples × eight adulteration percentages × two replicates). The samples were coded as follows: firstly, with the first initial of the raw material, that is ‘O’, ‘P’ or ‘A’ representing orange, pineapple and apple juice, respectively; secondly, the different sample number (1 or 2). Next, the percentage of adulteration (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40 and 50) and finally, the number of the replicate ‘R1’ or ‘R2’. Therefore, the first sample of 10% adulterated pineapple juice would be labeled as ‘P1_10_R1’ for the first replicate and ‘P1_10_R2’ for the second.

HS‐GC–IMS analysis acquisition

All samples were analyzed by HS‐GC–IMS, using a FlavourSpec system (G.A.S., Dortmund, Germany). The instrumental configuration and analytical conditions were those previously optimized by our research group. 40 Briefly, the system was equipped with an MCC OV‐5 multicapillary column (5%‐diphenyl, 95%‐dimethylpolysiloxane), using nitrogen (99.999%) as the drift and carrier gas. The conditions for creating headspace were a sample volume of 750 μL, an incubation temperature of 46.3 °C, and an incubation time of 5 min.

In the present study, the IMSS was used, specifically the drift time range of 1.020 to 1.900 with increments of 0.001 relative to the reaction ion peak (RIP), since all the compounds of interest are included in this range. The IMSS was obtained using Laboratory Analytical Viewer (LAV) software (G.A.S.), which automatically normalizes any driftime to the RIP signal. Finally, the collected data were organized and stored in two‐dimensional matrix (D n×p ), where ‘n’ represents the number of samples and ‘p’ denotes the number of variables (drift times – relative to RIP). The final complete array size was D 96×881, indicating 96 samples (32 of each type of juice in the range of 0% to 50% adulteration) and 881 drift times (relatives to RIP). This complete data matrix was split into two sets: 75% for training set and 25% for testing. Although the division into training and test sets was performed randomly, the duplicates always fell into the same set, ensuring that instrumental replicates do not constitute independent samples. Therefore, the effective number of independent samples was 48. Thus, the training set (D 72×881) was made up of 72 samples and was used for the creation and internal validation of the different regression models. The test set (D 24×881) contained the remaining 24 samples and was used for external validation of the developed models.

Data analysis and software

Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing is vital in non‐target data analysis for improving quality, reducing noise, and enhancing relevant features. This allows for more meaningful information to be extracted from raw data. Common techniques include derivatives and smoothing filters. 43 In this study, a Savitzky–Golay smoothing filter was used in combination with the first derivative for spectral pretreatment. This approach improved data resolution, addressed baseline shift and overlapping peaks and facilitated feature extraction and quantification while reducing noise and preserving key spectral features. This was achieved using a window size equal to 11 and a third‐order polynomial degree. These parameters selected based on preliminary testing with the third‐order polynomial effectively capturing the typical peak shapes without overfitting, consistent with approaches reported in previous studies. 21 This combined approach improves the overall interpretation of the data and at the same time removes edge information, reducing the number of drift times from 881 to 871. Therefore, the two matrices corresponding to the training and test set were preprocessed, reducing the matrices to D 72×881 and D 24×881, respectively.

Variable selection

Variable selection is crucial in training regression models, as it identifies the most relevant predictors and eliminates irrelevant or redundant ones. This step is especially important in non‐target data analysis, where high dimensionality and noisy variables are common. In particular, not all drift times in IMSS data contribute equally to predicting adulteration levels. Including irrelevant variables can lead to overfitting, reduced interpretability, and increased computational complexity. In this study, the Boruta algorithm was used and evaluated for variable selection. Therefore, a comparison has been established between the performance of the regression models with and without variable selection. The Boruta algorithm is a wrapper‐based method that evaluates the importance of each variable by comparing it to a set of random permuted copies. A variable is considered relevant and is selected for further analysis if its importance is significantly higher than that of the permuted variables. Boruta algorithm offers several advantages over spectra data because it can handle high‐dimensional data efficiently, reducing dimensionality. In addition, it is a robust algorithm to noise and missing values, can capture non‐linear relationships and interactions between variables as well as provide statistical significance. 44

Machine learning models

After an exploratory analysis based on visual inspection of the spectra and a hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), three different regression models were used: PLS, RF and SVR. These models were selected to represent both linear and non‐linear approaches commonly applied in chemometric analysis. PLS served as a reference linear method suitable for high‐dimensional and collinear data, while RF and SVR were included as non‐linear algorithms capable of capturing complex relationships. As these algorithms are based on fundamentally different learning principles, their combined use provides a broader and more comprehensive assessment of the data behavior and predictive performance. Both the complete IMSS and the variables extracted using the Boruta algorithm (all of them after applying the first derivative and the Savitsky–Golay smoothing filter) were used as inputs for these models. Consequently, a total of six models were constructed, all of them using the training set.

Briefly, PLS regression is a linear model that identifies latent variables (known as components) that capture the maximum covariance between the predictors and the response. It is useful for high‐dimensional data and multicollinearity issues. 45 RF regression builds an ensemble of decision trees, where each tree is trained on a random subset of the data and features, improving accuracy, handling complex relationships, and reducing overfitting. RF is also robust against outliers and missing values. 46 SVR model aims to find a hyperplane that maximizes the margin between the predicted values and the observed values, while allowing a specific tolerance for errors. By transforming the data into a higher‐dimensional feature space, SVR captures non‐linear relationships. In this study, the Gaussian kernel function was used since it is particularly effective in dealing with non‐linear relationships and high‐dimensional data. 47

Model optimization and validation

For all algorithms, hyperparameter optimization was performed within the training set using a leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (LOOCV) strategy. Model performance during this optimization process was evaluated based on root mean square error (RMSE), with the goal of minimizing this metric to identify the optimal combination of hyperparameters. The LOOCV procedure was used exclusively for internal parameter tuning, ensuring that the test set remained completely independent for external evaluation of model performance. Final model performance, both in training and test sets, was assessed using multiple complementary metrics, including RMSE and the coefficient of determination (R 2), as well as the ratio of performance to deviation (RPD) and the range error ratio (RER). RPD is defined as the ratio of the standard deviation of the reference values to the RMSE, while RER corresponds to the ratio of the total range of the reference values to the RMSE, providing additional insight into the predictive capacity and comparability of the models.

In the case of the PLS model, the optimal number of latent variables (LVs) was determined by applying this approach over a range of 1 to 20 components and selecting the number that minimized RMSE.

For the SVR model, two main hyperparameters were optimized: the cost (C) and gamma (γ). The hyperparameter C controls the trade‐off between maximizing the margin and minimizing training errors, while γ controls the behavior of the gaussian kernel. The values of γ and C were from log2 γ and log2 C within the range of [−10, 10], with increments of 0.5, resulting in 41 values for each parameter and 1681 unique combinations. This range allows for the efficient exploration of multiple orders of magnitude for both hyperparameters, enabling the evaluation of a wide spectrum of model complexities and helping to identify a suitable local optimum. Furthermore, there is a third hyperparameter, the learning rate (ε), which must be predetermined by the analyst. In this study, the ε value was maintained constant at a value of 0.1, corresponding to the default value in SVR implementations, 48 as preliminary tests showed that small variations did not significantly affect model performance.

Finally, for the RF model, two main hyperparameters were optimized. The first is the number of trees, which was set at 500 because it was observed that higher values did not increase accuracy, thus avoiding unnecessary computational complexity. The second hyperparameter (mtry) controls the number of variables randomly evaluated at each split during the training of individual trees, which introduces randomness and helps to reduce overfitting. For the complete matrix, a range of mtry values from 1 to 500 was tested. In contrast, for the reduced matrix with fewer variables, the search was limited to a range of 1 to 30.

Software

All data analyses were conducted using RStudio software version 4.2.2 (RStudio Team 2022, Boston, MA, USA). The pretreatment of the data consisting of the first derivative and the Savitsky–Golay smoothing filter was performed using the prospectr package. 49 For graphical representations, the ggplot2 package 50 was mainly used. The HCA was performed using the stats package. 51 Regression models (PLS, SVR, and RF) were created using the caret package. 48 Finally, the interactive web page was developed using the shiny package. 52

RESULTS

Exploratory analysis

Throughout the exploratory analysis, the complete data matrix (D 102×881), which includes additional samples of grape juice, was used. The objective is to determine if there is any pattern or tendency to group the samples based on the fruit juice used (orange, pineapple, apple and grape) as well as the percentage of adulteration (0–50%). First, the raw IMSS of all the samples was represented (Fig. 1(A)). Furthermore, to enhance their visual comprehension, the samples were color‐coded based on the type of juice employed (red for apple, yellow for pineapple, green for grape, and orange for orange juice). It is evident that the spectra exhibit undesirable phenomena, including baseline shift and wide bands. To overcome these issues and develop generalizable regression models, data preprocessing is essential. As mentioned earlier, the first derivative and a Savitzky–Golay smoothing filter (with a polynomial degree of 3 and a window size of 11) were employed to address these concerns. The resulting spectra after this preprocessing step are illustrated in Fig. 1(B), where information edges are eliminated. As a result, the matrix is reduced from 881 to 871 variables, spanning from drift time 1.025 to 1.895 (relative to RIP).

(A) Representation of the raw spectra (IMSS) obtained for all the fruit juice samples (D 102×881) analyzed by HS‐GC–IMS (B) IMSS for all the fruit juice samples (D 102×871) analyzed by HS‐GC–IMS after the application of the first derivative and a Savitzky–Golay filter. All juice samples are colored according to the fruit used: apple (colored in red), orange (colored in orange), pineapple (colored in yellow) and grape (colored in green).

To facilitate a more comprehensive analysis of the spectra, an interactive link containing these graphs has been published, available at the following link: https://rpubs.com/JoseLuisPCalle/1364487. This enables users to zoom in on specific spectrum regions and exclude groups from visualization by clicking on the legend. Additionally, hovering over the samples with the cursor allows the display of their corresponding names. These features enable a more detailed examination of the spectra. Regardless of the graph, it becomes evident that visual dissimilarities exist depending on the analyzed juice. For instance, when zooming in on the drift time range of 1.278 to 1.315 (relatives to RIP), there is a discernible difference in intensity for orange juice compared to the other samples. Therefore, this region allows differentiating the type of juice, however, a difference in intensity is not observed visually as a function of the percentage of adulteration.

Although spectra visualization is a crucial initial task for observing spectral characteristics, attempting to identify clustering trends or relationships through visual inspection is often challenging and, in some cases, impossible. For this reason, it becomes necessary to employ algorithms to obtain deeper understanding, discover hidden patterns and extract valuable insights from the data. In this case, a HCA was carried out, which allows grouping similar samples into clusters based on their similarity or dissimilarity. In this HCA, the inter‐individual similarity matrix was calculated using the Manhattan distance. Average method was specifically chosen as linkage method, based on the correlation between the cophenetic distance of the dendrogram and the original distance matrix. The obtained correlations were as follows: 0.8975 for the simple method, 0.8909 for the complete method, 0.9109 for the average method, 0.7673 for the Ward method, and 0.9019 for the centroid method. Thus, the average method obtained the highest correlation. The dendrogram resulting from the HCA is shown in Fig. 2. This dendrogram was represented in a circular manner and the samples were colored according to the raw material to facilitate its interpretation. Additionally, the five main clusters have been highlighted and referred with different letters (from A to E) to ease the discussion of the results.

Dendrogram resulting from the HCA using the Manhattan distance and the average method for all IMSS juices samples (D 96×871). The samples are colored according to raw material: pineapple (green), apple (red) and orange (orange).

Figure 2 shows a notable pattern to group the samples according to the raw material giving rise to five main clusters (A, B, C, D and E). As can be seen, all the orange samples appear in clusters B and C. Notably, cluster B exclusively contains ‘O1’ samples belonging to the first adulteration and, in general, this group exhibits higher percentages of adulteration. In turn, cluster C is divided into subclusters depending on the different adulterated sample (‘O1’ or ‘O2’) and, once again, there seems to be a slight tendency to group the samples based on the percentage of adulteration. Focusing on the apple samples, it is observed that all of them appear in cluster D. In this case, the subcluster division is not so clear, but they are generally divided according to the adulterated sample (‘A1’ or ‘A2’). Furthermore, it is noteworthy that the unadulterated samples (0%) tend to be consistently close to the lowest percentages of adulteration (5% and 10%). Finally, the pineapple samples comprise two clusters A and E. Cluster A is made up exclusively of unadulterated pineapple samples, which also display noticeable dissimilarity from the remaining clusters. The adulterated samples are found in cluster E, and, with some exceptions, the samples tend to be grouped by percentages of adulteration.

The primary clustering trend, as revealed by HCA, was strongly associated with the raw material. This indicates substantial variations in the composition of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that make up the IMSS (e.g., the predominance of terpenes in orange, esters in apple, or sulfur‐containing compounds in pineapple). Consequently, the intrinsic compositional variability among juice types exerts a stronger influence than the adulteration level, which may limit the generalization capacity of the models, as patterns driven by adulteration become less distinguishable. However, a partial grouping trend based on the percentage of adulteration was also observed, although this relationship was not always consistent, and some samples with high percentages of adulteration levels appeared close to those with low ones. Furthermore, the specific adulterated sample used (‘1’ or ‘2’) seems to have an impact, which makes the complexity of the data structure even more difficult and poses challenges in identifying patterns through unsupervised techniques.

Importantly, these results are in accordance with a previous study conducted by our research group, which reported a strong influence on the IMSS depending on the type of juice analyzed as well as the brand used. 40 Furthermore, it is important to highlight that other previous studies utilizing spectroscopic data have also reported a consistent trend of grouping the samples based on the type of juice analyzed. 21

Although HCA has provided satisfactory results, it is important to note that this method does not allow predicting future observations. Additionally, HCA is unable to accurately find differences based on the level of adulteration. It must be noted that the main volatiles from the adulterated samples can remain as the main volatiles also in the adulterated ones, if their levels are larger than in the adulterant. Therefore, using the whole data set instead of selecting specific variables can produce some confusion. This limits the model's ability to learn features that are exclusively and consistently related to the percentage of adulteration, regardless of the fruit matrix. Therefore, the use of supervised regression techniques becomes essential for adulterant quantification as they enable the development of predictive models.

Regression models

As mentioned earlier, the preprocessed data set (D 96×871 – first derivative and Savitsky–Golay filter) was randomly partitioned into a training set (split = 0.75) and a test set (split = 0.25). It is important to note that the variable selection process (utilizing the Boruta algorithm) was performed exclusively in the training set. This precaution was taken to prevent overfitting of the models and to obtain unbiased error estimations. Boruta algorithm classifies variables into three types: Confirmed, Tentative and Rejected. The result of applying the algorithm in the training set showed that 31 attributes were ‘confirmed’ and, therefore, useful for regression problems. The complete list of these selected variables (corresponding to specific drift times) is provided as Supporting Information (Table S1). The remaining variables (830 rejected and 10 tentative) were eliminated from both the training and test set.

Once the variables were selected, the different regression models (PLS, SVR and RF) were applied to both the complete data matrix (D 96×871) and the reduced matrix by Boruta algorithm (D 96×31).

Partial least squares (PLS) regression

The optimal number of LVs was determined to be 16 for the complete data matrix and 13 for the reduced matrix. For the model with the complete data matrix, the training set obtained an RMSE of 3.479 and an R 2 of 0.955. However, these performance metrics notably decreased when applied to the test set, yielding an RMSE of 9.537 and an R 2 of 0.695. This substantial performance drop is a clear indicator of overfitting, which can be primarily attributed to the high dimensionality and multicollinearity of the complete spectral data matrix. The model trained with the reduced matrix, showed RMSE values of 5.961 and 8.431 and, R 2 of 0.809 and 0.711 for the training and test set, respectively. Although a difference in performance metrics still exists, it is smaller, suggesting that feature selection mitigated overfitting by eliminating non‐informative variables.

The obtained results revealed a correlation between the response variable and the independent variables. Nonetheless, the model's ability to predict the percentage of adulteration was not satisfactory. Moreover, regardless of whether the full or reduced data matrix was used, there was a notable decline in performance on the test set (measured by R 2 and RMSE) compared to the training set. However, it was observed that the application of the Boruta algorithm mitigated excessive overfitting of the model, as the values obtained in both subsets (training and test set) were more similar. It is noteworthy that the error in the test set is the most reliable and unbiased, as these samples were never involved in the workflow or model creation process. Therefore, although the performance of the model in the training set was better when using the complete data matrix, it had a poor generalization to the test set. Hence, the optimal PLS model was the one derived from the reduced matrix, as it exhibited superior metrics in the test set.

Support vector regression (SVR)

The hyperparameters for the SVR model were optimized using the methodology described previously (Machine learning models section).

For the model trained on the complete matrix, the optimal hyperparameters were a C value of 42.25 and a γ of 9.765 × 10−4. Using these hyperparameters, the RMSE values were 1.531 and 7.192, and the corresponding R 2 values were 0.992 and 0.808 for the training and test sets, respectively. Once more, it is evident that the model experiences overfitting, leading to a decrease in performance metrics on the test set. However, this overfitting is less pronounced compared to the complete matrix PLS model. Furthermore, better values of RMSE and R 2 were observed in the test set compared to either of the two PLS models (reduced or complete). These results suggest that the data exhibits non‐linearity, and the SVR model seems to find more generalizable patterns compared to the PLS model.

The result for the reduced matrix indicated that the best combination of hyperparameters was a C value of 11.31 and a γ value of 0.0883. In the training set, an RMSE of 1.374 and an R 2 of 0.996 were obtained. In the case of the test set, the RMSE was 1.831 and the R2 was 0.987. This model was far superior to the results obtained by PLS. In addition, the low RMSE and high R 2 obtained, both for the training and test sets, allow a very accurate prediction of the percentage of adulteration. In this case, unlike the complete matrix SVR model, no overfitting was observed. As mentioned earlier, this phenomenon can be attributed to the inherent collinearity issues commonly present in spectra. Hence, reducing the number of predictors can effectively mitigate this problem.

Random Forest (RF) regression

For the complete matrix, a range of mtry values from 1 to 500 was tested. In contrast, for the reduced matrix with fewer variables, the search was limited to a range of 1 to 30.

The optimization of the RF models yielded distinct hyperparameters for each dataset. In the case of complete matrix, the best results were obtained with an mtry value of 356 which led to an R 2 of 0.967 and an RMSE of 4.426 in the training set. In the test set, overfitting was again suffered, increasing the RMSE to 9.683 and the R 2 decreased with a value of 0.716. Regarding the reduced matrix, the optimal outcome was achieved with an mtry value of 3. In both the training and test sets, the RMSE values were 3.784 and 6.003, respectively, while the R 2 values were 0.962 and 0.813.

Finally, it can be stated that the selection of variables using the Boruta algorithm is more appropriate for this dataset. Furthermore, excellent results have been obtained for the SVR model with an R 2 of 0.987 and an RMSE of 1.831 on the test set. When evaluating predictive ability and model comparability through RPD and RER, chemometric standards indicate that a model may be considered excellent for analytical quantification when RPD is greater than 3 and RER is greater than 9. 53 In this study, only the SVR model trained with the reduced matrix satisfied these criteria on the test set (RPD = 8.63, RER = 27.31). Even so, the absolute predictive error remains very low (RMSE = 1.831). Additionally, for this model, the limit of detection (LOD), calculated following one of the methods proposed by Ortiz et al., 54 was 3.92%.

The superior performance of SVR models aligns with previous studies conducted by our research group, which reported similar results when employing NIR spectroscopic data. In that study, the best performance was also obtained with the SVR model and the values of R 2 and RMSE were very similar to those obtained in this study. 21 In the case of using the FTIR data, all regression models reported a very high performance with R 2 values higher than 0.99. However, the SVR model obtains the best results followed by PLS and RF. 20 Other authors such as Qiu et al. used data obtained by an electronic nose and tongue system to quantify different parameters related to strawberry juice quality. In this case, the best results were obtained with the RF regression model, followed by SVR and PLS. 55 Therefore, it can be stated that there is no one model clearly superior to another, and the choice depends on the purpose and the type of data used. In this case, for IMSS, the best results have been obtained for the SVR models.

Application development

Finally, based on the promising results obtained using the reduce IMSS matrix in combination with SVR model, a Shiny web application has been developed. This application allows sharing the model previously trained for the quantification of adulterants in juices. This software can be accessed through the following link:

https://agr291.shinyapps.io/App_Zumos_IMS/

Its interface is quite simple, and users will only have to upload excel or csv files with the IMSS generated directly from the analysis of the sample using HS‐GC–IMS. This file is the IMSS data obtained directly from the software (detailed in HS‐GC–IMS analysis acquisition section). Note that a test file is also available for download, which can be used to verify the application's functionality. By uploading a file and clicking the ‘submit’ button, the application automatically performs the variable selection by using the Boruta algorithm and quantify the percentage of adulteration applying the SVR model. It should be noted that, in most cases, sharing these algorithms is uncommon, making it difficult to monitor the analyzed samples. For this reason, having these models available eliminates the need for other analysts to maintain their own databases and facilitates sample characterization for all users. Furthermore, as more samples are analyzed, the models can be retrained, thus constantly learning and updating to meet the needs of the food industry at any given time.

CONCLUSIONS

The IMSS in combination with ML models was evaluated for the quantification of adulterations in fruit juices. HCA showed a tendency to group the juice samples according to the raw material (type of fruit) and to a lesser extent depending on the sample used and the percentage of adulteration. For quantitative analysis, the best results were obtained using an SVR model trained with the reduced matrix after variable selection by the Boruta algorithm (RMSE of 1.831 and R 2 of 0.987 in the test set). In addition, this model is available to all users through the interactive website developed.

This study demonstrates the methodological potential of IMSS combined with ML for the automatic, rapid, and objective quantification of juice‐to‐juice adulterations. Given its low cost, minimal sample preparation, and high throughput, this approach could be readily implemented in routine laboratory analyses and industrial quality control systems. Moreover, this methodology contributes to improving consumer safety, supporting regulatory compliance, and strengthening the detection of economic food fraud. Future works should explore its applicability to a wider range of adulterants and juice types to broaden its industrial relevance and robustness. Importantly, to transition from a proof of concept to a fully operational tool for subtle fraud detection, future studies must specifically target narrower, low‐adulteration ranges using larger sample sets.
