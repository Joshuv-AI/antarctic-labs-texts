The retina-body axis: proteomic mechanisms linking oculomics and clinical traits in a female aging cohort
Authors: Selena Wei Zhang, Shunming Liu, Yanxian Chen, Jing Liu, Ying Yao, Xianwen Shang, Honghua Yu, Yu Huang, Mingguang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The retina provides a unique window into systemic health, yet molecular mechanisms linking retinal features (oculomics) to clinical traits in aging remain unclear. In this study, we leveraged the homogeneous Canton 70 s Alumni Cohort (N = 258 females aged ~70 years) to minimize socio-demographic confounders and extracted oculomic features from fundus images using AutoMorph. Linear mixed-effects models identified 129 significant associations between oculomic and clinical features (p < 0.05). Sparse canonical correlation analysis indicated a key phenotypic retina-body axis (r = 0.538, p = 0.047) primarily driven by central retinal venular equivalent (Hubbard, zone b) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration. Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance revealed that oculomic categories were significantly associated with systemic conditions like chest pain, dyslipidemia, and stroke. Age differentially impacted retinal features across clinical condition status (adjusted p for interaction < 0.2), with pronounced trends in individuals with health problems but relative stability in healthy controls. Plasma proteomics was integrated to explore potential molecular mechanisms. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis identified shared proteomic modules associated with both oculomic and clinical features. These modules were enriched in pathways including complement and coagulation cascades, cholesterol metabolism, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. This study establishes both phenotypic and molecular connections underlying the retina-body axis in a female aging cohort.

Introduction

The human retina is a unique anatomical site where microvascular and neural structures can be directly visualized in vivo, providing a rare, non-invasive opportunity to assess both vascular and neuronal health1,2. Advances in retinal imaging—such as fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and OCT angiography—have enabled the detailed extraction of retinal features. Concurrently, the integration of state-of-the-art artificial intelligence (AI) technologies has greatly facilitated the automated quantification of these features3. This rapidly evolving field, known as “oculomics”, harnesses high-throughput image analysis and AI to comprehensively characterize the retina and correlate its features with systemic health2–4. Specifically, the “oculomic features” analyzed in this study are quantitative measurements of retinal vascular and optic nerve head, which were derived automatically from fundus images using the deep learning-based AutoMorph pipeline5.

A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that oculomics are closely associated with a range of clinical parameters, reflecting systemic physiological and pathological states that are often accelerated or manifested with age6. For instance, retinal microvascular abnormalities have been significantly correlated with elevated blood pressure, impaired kidney function (e.g., reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate), and liver dysfunction markers such as triglycerides—conditions frequently prevalent among older adults7–11. Recent advances in oculomics have further demonstrated that retinal characteristics can predict a range of age-related conditions, including cardiovascular, neurodegenerative, renal, metabolic, and hepatobiliary diseases12–15. These associations highlight the value of oculomics as a non-invasive indicator of systemic health across the lifespan, and especially during aging.

Despite these advancements, most reported associations between retinal features and clinical biomarkers remain largely phenomenological or statistical16. The molecular mechanisms underlying the link between retinal alterations and clinical biomarkers—particularly in the context of biological aging—are often unclear, limiting the translational potential of oculomic biomarkers3,17. The integration of proteomic profiling to identify circulating proteins that may link retinal vascular traits and clinical biomarkers represents a promising approach to elucidate the biological pathways connecting ocular and systemic health18. This is especially relevant in aging research, where multimodal biomarkers are urgently needed to capture complex physiological decline.

In this study, we leveraged data from the Canton 70s Alumni Cohort (CAC), a cohort consisting of female participants aged around 70 years—a critical period for the emergence of multiple age-related conditions. We systematically investigated the relationships between oculomics and clinical traits at both macro (oculomic categories or systemic conditions) and micro (quantitative features) levels, and integrated proteomic profiling to explore shared proteomic pathways that may underlie these retina–systemic associations. By integrating advanced statistical modeling, multivariate analysis, and omics data, our study aims to elucidate phenotypic connections and explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the retina–body axis in female aging.

A total of 72 oculomic features and 35 clinical features were available across visits (Data S1 and S2), enabling multimodal investigation of retina–systemic associations. Multivariate oculomic–clinical associations

To assess large-scale correlations between oculomic and clinical features during aging, we performed sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA). In the body mass index (BMI)-adjusted sCCA model, the first pair of canonical variates demonstrated a statistically significant correlation (r = 0.538, p = 0.047; Fig. 2A, B), indicating a meaningful association between the oculomic and clinical datasets. Variables with non-zero canonical weights in the first pair were identified as key contributors to this association (Figs. 3 and 4). Among oculomic features, central retinal venular equivalent (Hubbard, zone b) exhibited the largest absolute weight ( | weight | = 0.423), suggesting its dominant contribution (Fig. 3). For clinical features, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration showed the highest absolute weight ( | weight | = 0.845), identifying it as the primary clinical contributor (Fig. 4). Associations between oculomic categories and systemic conditions

To systematically evaluate how different aspects of retinal vascular and neural structures are linked to systemic health, we clustered the oculomic features into distinct categories for analysis. We employed Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) to evaluate the association between these oculomic categories and major systemic conditions that are highly prevalent in older adults (Fig. 5). Several oculomic categories—such as cup/disk, arterial tortuosity, arterial density and arterial calibre—showed significant associations with systemic conditions, further supporting the relevance of oculomics in capturing systemic disease variation. For example, arterial density (R2 = 5.57%) and arterial calibre (R2 = 4.20%) were associated with exercise-induced left chest pain, arterial tortuosity (R2 = 2.91%) was associated with dyslipidemia, and cup/disk parameter had associations with stroke (R2 = 8.71%).Fig. 5Associations between oculomic categories and systemic conditions.Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to evaluate the associations between oculomic categories and major systemic conditions prevalent in older adults. Distinct aging patterns of oculomic features across clinical condition status

To explore whether age affects oculomic features differently depending on systemic conditions, participants were categorized into groups based on the presence or absence of key chronic conditions in our PERMANOVA analysis (Fig. 5). We modeled the interaction between age and systemic conditions using a linear mixed-effects framework to determine whether the rate and pattern of age-related changes in retinal features varied by clinical condition. Significant interactions (false discovery rate [FDR]–adjusted p for interaction <0.2) were identified between age and clinical condition status for multiple oculomic features (Fig. 6). Across the significant interactions, it was found that the aging trajectory pattern of oculomic parameters differs between diseased and healthy participants. Shared proteomic modules linking oculomic features to clinical features

Plasma proteomics was performed using data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). After quality filtering, a total of 3649 proteins were identified and used for downstream network analyses. To investigate the proteomic links potentially underlying the observed associations between retina and systemic conditions, we applied weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to the plasma proteomic dataset in relation to both oculomic and clinical features. WGCNA identified multiple protein co-expression modules, and correlations between module eigengenes and phenotypic features were subsequently examined (Fig. S3). After excluding the grey module, which represents proteins not assigned to any co-expression cluster, 15 modules were significantly associated with oculomic features (Fig. S3A), whereas 17 modules were associated with clinical features (Fig. S3B).

To further explore potential molecular links between retinal phenotypes and systemic features, we performed an integrative comparison of modules associated with both oculomic and clinical features. Shared proteomic modules were identified, and the top-ranked Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway within each module was used to represent its dominant biological function. These relationships were visualized using a Sankey diagram (Fig. 7), illustrating the connections between oculomic features, proteomic modules, and clinical features. Functional annotation of the shared modules revealed several biologically meaningful pathways potentially underlying the retina–systemic associations (Fig. 7). Multiple modules—including the brown, grey60, turquoise and yellow modules—were enriched for complement and coagulation cascades. This suggests a prominent role of inflammatory and hemostatic processes linking retinal neurovascular features with systemic immune and hematological traits. For example, these modules connected retinal vascular parameters such as vessel width, arteriovenous ratio, and fractal dimension with blood routine markers, including hematocrit, lymphocyte count, platelet count, and red cell distribution width.

In addition, several modules were associated with metabolic or immune-related pathways. The royalblue module was enriched for cholesterol metabolism, linking retinal structural features such as disc height, cup height and vessel density with hematological indices including mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration and platelet count. The lightgreen module was enriched for cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, indicating potential involvement of inflammatory signaling pathways connecting retinal tortuosity features with renal and hematological markers. The lightyellow module showed enrichment for systemic lupus erythematosus, while the midnightblue module was enriched for nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, suggesting possible links between immune regulation, metabolic pathways and retinal vascular morphology.

Other modules captured additional biological processes. The blue module was enriched for cornified envelope formation, while the red module showed enrichment for cardiac muscle contraction, potentially reflecting broader systemic physiological pathways influencing retinal microvascular features. For some modules, including cyan and salmon, no significant KEGG pathway enrichment was detected.

Discussion

Our study utilized a homogeneous cohort of older female participants, which minimized the confounding effects of socio-demographic factors and allowed focused insight into biological aging processes. Leveraging multimodal data of the CAC, we investigated the associations between oculomics and clinical traits, and explored the proteomic correlates potentially linking these associations. Our findings provide new evidence of the retina-body axis at both phenotypic and molecular levels in a female aging cohort.

We first demonstrated that oculomic features were significantly associated with a range of clinical traits, including individual features and systemic conditions commonly affected by aging. At the micro level, the results indicated that oculomic features correlated with blood pressure, renal/liver function, blood lipids, immune markers, and hematological traits. These findings align with previous studies, suggesting that multisystem physiological processes—including liver/kidney disorders, blood lipids, inflammation, and hematological parameters—exert influence on retinal vascular structure19–25. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because after multiple testing correction, no statistically significant correlations were identified. Given the substantial collinearity among features within each domain, strict multiple testing correction may be overly conservative and may not reflect the effective number of independent tests. The significant first canonical pair (r = 0.538, p = 0.047) of sCCA revealed a distinct dimension of association, characterized by specific biomarkers: central retinal venular equivalent (the strongest oculomic contributor) and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (primary clinical driver). Together with 42 weighted features (26 oculomic, 16 clinical), these core variables defined a retina-body axis linking inflammatory processes, hematological parameters, blood lipid profiles, and renal function to structural alterations in the retinal vasculature26–30.

At the macro level, oculomic categories were associated with systemic conditions that are prevalent in older adults. For example, arterial density and calibre were associated with left-sided chest pain when exercise; arterial tortuosity was associated with dyslipidemia; and cup/disc parameters had associations with stroke; these associations reinforced the retina’s role as a non-invasive window into systemic health and age-related physiological decline31, further substantiating the concept of the retina-body axis in older women. Furthermore, our interaction analysis revealed that systemic conditions modified the age-related trajectories of retinal features. Individuals with various health conditions exhibited greater age-associated changes in retinal vascular parameters, whereas healthy controls demonstrated relatively stable trajectories. These findings suggest that systemic disease burden may reshape microvascular aging patterns in the retina, consistent with the concept of vascular aging, potentially through mechanisms such as cumulative vascular damage, chronic inflammation, and oxidative stress32,33. In older women, post-menopausal estrogen decline may exacerbate key hallmarks of vascular aging, including endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation. Together with cumulative vascular injury, these processes may promote accelerated microvascular remodeling in the aging retina34,35.

To further explore potential biological mechanisms underlying the retina-body axis in females, we integrated plasma proteomics with oculomic and clinical features using a network-based approach. Functional annotation of the shared proteomic modules suggested that several systemic biological pathways may bridge retinal and systemic phenotypes. Notably, multiple modules were enriched for complement and coagulation cascades, highlighting the potential role of inflammatory and hemostatic processes in linking retinal microvascular features with immune and hematological traits. These pathways are known to be involved in vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular remodeling, which are key hallmarks of vascular aging36–41.

In addition, modules enriched for cholesterol metabolism and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction indicated that lipid metabolism and immune signaling may also contribute to retina–systemic coupling. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism has been associated with alterations in retinal vascular calibre and density42,43, and may also affect intraocular pressure homeostasis and optic nerve head structure, potentially contributing to variations in cup/disc parameters44. Additionally, chronic low-grade inflammation is a recognized driver of both systemic aging and microvascular changes45–48. Together, these findings suggest that systemic metabolic and inflammatory processes may partly underlie the associations between retinal structural features and systemic health observed in our cohort.

Although several biologically plausible pathways were identified, the functional significance of many module–feature relationships remains to be further investigated. Future studies integrating longitudinal proteomics, functional experiments, and larger cohorts will be important for clarifying the causal mechanisms linking systemic biological pathways with retinal aging.

There are still limitations in our study. Firstly, the homogeneity of our cohort—comprising older, educated females—while useful for reducing confounding, may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations with different age groups or demographics. Secondly, the observational design and cross-sectional nature of the proteomic data restricted our ability to infer causality between retinal features and systemic traits. Lastly, although we identified shared proteomic pathways, the functional relevance of many signals remains to be validated experimentally, especially given the potential influence of high-abundance background proteins such as immunoglobulins.

In conclusion, our findings substantiated the retina-body axis hypothesis and suggested that systemic proteomic signatures may represent shared biological mechanisms linking retinal integrity with systemic health in female aging. Integrating oculomics with proteomics holds promise for identifying causal pathways, improving risk stratification for age-related diseases, and discovering novel biomarkers or therapeutic targets aimed at promoting healthy aging. The use of a homogeneous cohort of older women enabled the examination of aging-related biological processes within a relatively stable hormonal and socio-demographic context. As women follow a distinct vascular aging trajectory—marked by post-menopausal estrogen decline, increased inflammation, and lipid changes—this cohort offers a unique opportunity to investigate female-specific retina–systemic interactions in later life. However, given the female-only composition of our cohort, future studies are warranted to examine whether similar retina-body axis mechanisms exist in males or more diverse populations.

Methods

Cohort information

A cohort of 258 female older adults born between 1946 and 1953 who graduated from Guangzhou Zhixin Middle School (Guangzhou, China) was recruited in the CAC study since 2021. The cohort comprised exclusively female participants, allowing focused investigation of aging processes in women while minimizing sex-related biological heterogeneity. The CAC is homogeneous in age, gender, educational background and living environment, ensuring a more controlled environment to minimize potential confounding variables. Prospective participants must meet the following criteria: (1) possess a comprehensive understanding of the study’s purpose, consent to participate, and provide a signed informed consent form; (2) commit to a five-year follow-up period; (3) be capable of actively engaging in the completion of questionnaires and in-depth interviews; and (4) be willing to cooperate in undergoing whole-body and eye examinations, as well as the extraction of biological samples.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (number: 2020-300H-12) and adhered to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. The study was conducted between 2021 and 2024, and duly informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical laboratory test records

Participants’ relevant medical records—including clinical visit notes, hospital records, and annual physical examination reports from 2021 to 2024—were collected. These examinations, conducted at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital or other healthcare centers, included measurements of blood pressure, blood routine, liver and kidney function, and blood lipid profiles—key health indicators that frequently change with advancing age. The detailed information on clinical features is listed in Data S1.

Oculomics

Participants underwent annual retinal fundus imaging at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital between 2021 and 2024. Oculomic features were extracted from the retinal fundus image using the publicly available AutoMorph pipeline5. First, an EfficientNet-B4-based convolutional neural network49 was employed to classify the image quality into three grades: good, usable, and reject. Only good and usable images were retained, and low-confidence images (mean probability < 0.75 or standard deviation > 0.1) were excluded to ensure reliable measurements. Next, anatomical segmentation was performed using a series of deep learning networks: an adversarial network for binary vessel segmentation50, an information fusion network for artery/vein classification51, and a coarse-to-fine network for optic disc localization52. Images were excluded if they lacked a detectable optic disc, had fewer than 6 detectable arteries or veins in a zone 0.5-1.0-disc diameter from the optic disc’s margin, or had fewer than 2 arteries or veins in the entire fundus. Finally, custom region-specific summarization and global/geometric parameters were used to measure the retinal vascular features, including cup/disk, complexity, tortuosity, density, and calibre for both retinal arteries and veins53,54. All deep learning modules have been previously validated against expert annotations, and the generalizability of the pipeline was confirmed via external validation5. A detailed list of oculomic features is provided in Data S2.

Proteomics data generation

Proteomics data was performed on plasma samples in 2024. A total of 10 µL plasma obtained from venous blood was first mixed with 240 µL methanol (MeOH, Fisher Chemical, 4 L A452-4) and 800 µL methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE, SIGMA, 1 L 650560-1 L), followed by the addition of 200 µL double-distilled water (ddH2O). After centrifugation, the precipitated protein settled at the bottom of the vial, where 80 µL SDT buffer (4% SDS and 100 mM Tris-HCl at pH 7.6) was introduced to the precipitated protein. The lysates underwent further processing through sonication and boiling for 15 minutes. Post-boiling, the mixture was centrifuged at 14,000× g for 40 min. The resulting supernatant was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China), with 15 µg of protein from each sample being combined with 5X loading buffer and boiled for an additional 5 min. The proteins were then separated on a 4%–20% SDS-PAGE gel under a constant voltage of 180 V for 45 min, with protein bands visualized through Coomassie Blue R-250 staining.

Next, dithiothreitol (Bio-Rad, US) was added to each sample to achieve a final concentration of 40 mM. The samples were mixed at 600 rpm for 1.5 h at 37 °C. Following cooling to room temperature, iodoacetamide (Bio-Rad, US) was introduced to the mixture at a final concentration of 20 mM to block reduced cysteine residues, and the samples were incubated for 30 min in darkness. The samples were then transferred to microcon units (10 kDa filters) individually. Each filter was washed three times with 100 µL of UA buffer (8 M urea and 150 mM Tris-HCl at pH 8.0) and twice with 100 µL of 25 mM NH4HCO3 buffer. Subsequently, trypsin was added to the samples at a trypsin to protein (wt/wt) ratio of 1:50 and incubated at 37 °C for 15–18 h. The resulting peptides were collected as a filtrate. The peptides from each sample were desalted using C18 Cartridges (Empore™ SPE Cartridges MCX, 30 µm, waters), concentrated via vacuum centrifugation, and reconstituted in 40 µL of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The peptide content was estimated through UV light spectral density at 280 nm. For DIA experiments, indexed retention time (iRT) calibration peptides were added to the sample. An equal portion of each sample was pooled for quality control (QC) purposes.

After that, the peptides from each sample were analyzed by OrbitrapTM AstralTM mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, US) connected to a Vanquish Neo system liquid chromatography (Thermo Scientific, US) in the DIA mode. Precursor Ions were scanned at a mass range of 380–980 m/z, MS1 resolution was 240,000 at 200 m/z, Normalized AGC Target: 500%, Maximum IT: 5 ms. 299 windows were set for DIA mode in MS2 scanning, Isolation Window: 2 m/z, HCD Collision Energy: 25 ev, Normalized AGC Target: 500%, Maximum IT: 3 ms.

DIA data was analyzed with DIA-NN 1.8.1. Main software parameters were set as follows: enzyme is trypsin, max missed cleavage is 1, fixed modification is carbamidomethyl(C), dynamic modification is oxidation(M) and acetyl (Protein N-term). All reported data were based on 99% confidence for protein identification as determined by FDR ≤ 1%.

Quality control and statistical analysis

A standardized QC pipeline was applied to all omic datasets. Subjects with >50% missing values were excluded. Subsequently, features with >30% missing values were removed.
