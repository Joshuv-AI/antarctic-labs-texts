Local feature filtering for scalable and well-conditioned Random Feature Methods
Jan Willem van Beek, Victorita Dolean, Ben Moseley

Abstract
In recent years, machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool for solving partial differential equations (PDEs), with randomized neural networks showing remarkable potential. These networks, typically shallow, differ from conventional approaches by randomizing all parameters except those in the final layer. Notably, by integrating techniques inspired by domain decomposition, they achieve improved local accuracy and computational efficiency. Following [ 3 ] , we refer to these approaches as random feature methods (RFM). Despite their promise, RFMs lead to highly ill-conditioned least squares systems, posing a significant computational challenge. To address this, we introduce a novel approach based on different strategies of local filtering. This technique provides two key advantages: first, it enables the selective elimination of nonexpressive features, reducing system size and improving the condition number without sacrificing accuracy; second, it facilitates the construction of an efficient preconditioner for the global system. Our numerical experiments demonstrate that this strategy can rival or even outperform existing preconditioning techniques, both in terms of condition number and the efficiency of iterative solvers. These results underscore the potential of our method as a powerful enhancement to machine learning-based PDE solvers.

Ultimately, randomized neural networks, the Random Feature Method, and reservoir computing share a common foundation: fixing most network parameters after random initialization and training only a subset, enabling efficient learning while preserving approximation properties an expressivity. However, these approaches lead to more complex linear algebra problems, due to the structure and ill-conditioning of the underlying least-squares system.
For this reason, it is desirable to develop efficient preconditioners for these systems. Recently, progress was made on this topic in
[

We propose a novel approach for addressing the ill-conditioning that arises in least squares formulations, particularly in the context of overlapping domain decomposition. Inspired by the recent work of
[
25
]

2

First, the RRQR factorization enables dimensionality reduction by selecting a subset of influential columns associated with large singular values thereby identifying physically or computationally informative parameters. Unlike SVD, which produces linear combinations involving all parameters, this sparsity-promoting selection can enhance interpretability and facilitate more efficient computations.

Second, we leverage these local factorizations to construct a subdomain-based preconditioner tailored directly to the least squares problem. This contrasts with the overlapping Schwarz preconditioner used in
[
25

The structure of the resulting preconditioned system is also of independent interest because of its particular form. The submatrices corresponding to individual subdomains have orthogonal columns, but there are overlaps in collocation points that lie in the intersection of different subdomains. For nonoverlapping subdomains the matrix is perfectly conditioned, and one would expect intuitively for the conditioning to degrade as the sizes of the overlaps increase.

To rigorously analyze this phenomenon, we model the subdomain-restricted matrices as submatrices of random orthogonal matrices, a well-established framework in random matrix theory

[

Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

A subdomain-based rank-revealing QR (RRQR) strategy that improves interpretability and enables principled parameter selection;

•

A new preconditioning method that operates directly on the least squares problem, thereby avoiding potential pitfalls associated with normal-equation-based approaches;

•

A novel application of random matrix theory to analyze the impact of subdomain overlap on conditioning, with theoretical support via asymptotic bounds.

2
Background

In this section we begin by formulating the Random Feature Method (RFM) for solving boundary value problems (BVPs), adopting the notation and structure used in
[
1
]
. The goal is to approximate the solution of a linear PDE using a neural ansatz that combines randomized feature maps with localized support. Specifically, we consider a PDE defined on a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, subject to given boundary conditions. The solution is represented as a sum of random features modulated by locally supported window functions, leading to a model inspired by both kernel methods and domain decomposition. By fixing the random feature parameters and optimizing only the output weights, we reduce the problem to a structured least squares formulation. This section details the mathematical setup, introduces the loss functional, and reformulates the learning problem in matrix form to set the stage for subsequent analysis of conditioning and preconditioning strategies.

2.1
RFM for BVP a.k.a. the ELM-FBPINN method

2.2
Preconditioning the RFM via domain decomposition

To address the ill-conditioning often encountered in Random Feature Methods (RFM), particularly in the presence of redundant features, several domain-decomposition-inspired preconditioning strategies have been proposed. In this section, we briefly review the preconditioner introduced in
[
25
]
, which is based on the additive Schwarz method applied to the normal equations.

Starting from the decomposition of the system matrix into localized contributions,

{\bf M}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf V}_{j}^{T}{\bf M}_{j}{\bf W}_{j},

where
{\bf M}_{j}
is the submatrix associated with subdomain
\Omega_{j}
and
{\bf V}_{j}
,
{\bf W}_{j}
are restriction operators as introduced in (
4
), the method proceeds by forming the Gramian matrix
\mathbf{A}={\bf M}^{T}{\bf M}
. From this, one defines local Gramian blocks

{\bf A}_{j}={\bf W}_{j}{\bf A}{\bf W}_{j}^{T}={\bf M}_{j}^{T}{\bf M}_{j},

which correspond to diagonal sub-blocks of
\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{M}
associated with each subdomain. These blocks are then inverted and assembled into the additive Schwarz preconditioner

N^{-1}_{\text{AS}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf W}_{j}{\bf A}_{j}^{-1}{\bf W}_{j}^{T}.

An alternative formulation includes weighting matrices
D_{j}
to form a partition of unity, leading to the restricted additive Schwarz (RAS) variant:

N^{-1}_{\text{RAS}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf W}_{j}{\bf D}_{j}{\bf A}_{j}^{-1}{\bf W}_{j}^{T},\qquad\text{where}\quad\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf W}_{j}{\bf D}_{j}{\bf W}_{j}^{T}=I.

While effective in improving convergence in certain settings, a key drawback of this approach is that it operates on the normal equations rather than the original least squares problem. Forming the Gramian matrix
{\bf M}^{T}{\bf M}
explicitly can lead to numerical difficulties: its condition number is the square of that of
{\bf M}
, i.e.,
\kappa({\bf M}^{T}{\bf M})=\kappa({\bf M})^{2}
, potentially exacerbating round-off errors and amplifying the effect of poorly scaled features.

To overcome the limitations associated with preconditioning the normal equations, we seek alternative strategies that operate directly on the original least squares system. Beyond numerical stability, this shift is also driven by computational considerations: forming and storing the Gramian matrix
\mathbf{M}^{T}\mathbf{M}
can be costly, both in time and memory, and it typically destroys the sparsity of
\mathbf{M}
. In contrast, the matrix
\mathbf{M}
itself, due to the local support of the window functions, is often highly sparse, and preserving this sparsity is crucial for scalability in high-dimensional settings.

To exploit this structure efficiently, we turn to the rank-revealing QR (RRQR) factorization as an algebraic tool for dimension reduction and feature selection within each subdomain. Compared to singular value decomposition (SVD), which is often used for similar purposes, RRQR offers a favorable trade-off: while both methods have
\mathcal{O}(mn^{2})
complexity for a
m\times n
matrix with
m\geq n
, RRQR is typically faster in practice, more amenable to sparse input, and returns actual columns of the matrix rather than linear combinations, enhancing interpretability and enabling localized filtering. In the next section, we introduce the formal definition of RRQR and outline how it forms the basis of our preconditioning approach.

Property 2.2

Let

More precisely, the algorithm of Gu and Eisenstat

Interestingly, the type of rank-revealing structure sought by the Gu-Eisenstat algorithm echoes phenomena encountered in high-dimensional probability. In particular, when the design matrix

Thus, the deterministic guarantees of Gu-Eisenstat align with the typical-case behavior of randomized models. This connection motivates using RRQR not just as a practical tool for feature selection, but also as a mechanism to uncover and control the implicit structure induced by randomization and locality.

2.4
Random Matrix Theory

This leads us to introducing tools from random matrix theory. Specifically, we model the subdomain-restricted matrices produced by RRQR as random semi-orthogonal matrices, reflecting their approximate local orthogonality and statistical independence due to randomized features. Proposition 2.6

Let
H\subset\mathcal{O}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be any measurable subset. Conditioning on a fixed realization
\mathcal{P}=P
, we have:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}\in H\mid\mathcal{P}=P)=\mathbb{P}(P\mathcal{Q}\in H)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in P^{-1}H),

where
P^{-1}H=\{Q\in\mathcal{O}_{n}\mid PQ\in H\}
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is Haar-distributed and
P
is fixed, we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in P^{-1}H)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in P^{T}H)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in H),

by left invariance of the Haar measure.

Integrating over all possible values of
\mathcal{P}
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}\in H)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Z}\in H\mid\mathcal{P}=P)\ d\mu(P)=\int_{\mathcal{O}_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in H)\ d\mu(P)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in H)\int_{\mathcal{O}_{n}}d\mu(P)=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{Q}\in H).

since
\mu
is a probability measure. Therefore,
\mathcal{Z}
is Haar-distributed.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Numerical intuition)
.

This result formalizes an intuitive property: if we multiply two orthogonal matrices, each randomly sampled in a systematic way (e.g., using the QR decomposition of Gaussian matrices), the result is still randomly distributed in the same way in the space of orthogonal matrices. This closure of the matrix matrix multiplication is a feature of compact groups like
\mathcal{O}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and ensures that the ”orthogonal randomness” is preserved under composition, an important property in randomized algorithms and preconditioner analysis. We say that the statistical properties of such matrices are preserved under orthogonal transformations and compositions and their use in iterative solvers or algorithms does not introduce additional bias or artifacts.

Building on the closure property of Haar-distributed orthogonal matrices under multiplication, we now explore how block structures of such matrices behave statistically - this will be key to modeling interactions between overlapping subdomains in our preconditioner analysis.

\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be independent random orthogonal matrices drawn from the Haar measure. We partition both matrices into conforming
2\times 2

\ell\times r
, i.e.,

.

Let
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}

This idea will play a role in approximating the spectral behavior of preconditioned systems with overlapping random subdomain interactions.

To quantify how large such blocks are in norm, we recall the following concentration bound from

3
Main contribution: QR based filtered RFM preconditioner and analysis using random matrices

This section develops our main theoretical contributions for addressing ill-conditioning in least squares problems arising in Random Feature Methods (RFM) with overlapping subdomains. We propose a subdomain-wise filtering strategy based on
\sigma
-rank revealing
QR
factorizations, which reduces feature redundancy locally while preserving sparsity. We begin by analyzing the structure of the system matrix in the case of two overlapping subdomains and identify two sources of ill-conditioning: feature redundancy within subdomains and interactions in the overlap. The proposed QR-based filtering mitigates the former, and the resulting factorizations form the basis of a global preconditioner.
To understand the effect of overlap, we model the preconditioned matrix using random matrix theory, treating the local QR factors as random orthogonal blocks. This allows us to derive an asymptotic bound on the expected condition number in terms of the overlap size.

3.1
QR
based filtering

In the remainder of this section, we present the theoretical foundation and algorithmic realization of our QR-based filtering method and the resulting preconditioner for ill-conditioned least squares problems. Our approach addresses two key challenges arising in Random Feature Methods (RFM) with overlapping subdomains: linear dependence within subdomains and the degradation of conditioning due to overlap. To motivate the design of our method, we begin by analyzing the structure of the system matrix in a simplified setting involving two overlapping subdomains
\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
in one dimension. The resulting matrix
{\bf M}
defined in
eq.

•

Local redundancy

•

Overlap interference

To address these issues, we adopt a subdomain-wise strategy and illustrate it in the general multi-domain case. A key numerical observation is that the matrices

3.2
Preconditioning via Filtered QR Factors

Having introduced the subdomain-wise filtering strategy based on the
\sigma
-rank revealing QR decomposition, we now explain how to combine these local factorizations into a global preconditioner for the original least squares system.

Recall from the domain decomposition identity (
4
) that the global matrix
{\bf M}
can be written as

{\bf M}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf V}_{j}^{T}{\bf M}_{j}{\bf W}_{j}.

After applying local QR factorizations to each subdomain, i.e.,
{\bf M}_{j}={\bf Q}_{j}{\bf R}_{j}
, we obtain the global filtered decomposition

{\bf M}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf V}_{j}^{T}{\bf Q}_{j}{\bf R}_{j}{\bf W}_{j}.

This suggests defining a preconditioner that operates directly on the original system and not on the normal equation without requiring full matrix assembly, hence being suitable for parallel computations. Specifically, we define the (right) preconditioner

{\bf S}^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf W}_{j}^{T}{\bf R}_{j}^{-1}{\bf W}_{j}.

Because each matrix
{\bf R}_{j}
is upper triangular, applying
{\bf S}^{-1}
involves only inexpensive back-substitution. Moreover, the preconditioner never needs to be explicitly formed, only local factors
{\bf R}_{j}
and selection matrices
{\bf W}_{j}
are needed.

Proposition 3.1
.

The right preconditioned matrix simplifies to

{\bf M}{\bf S}^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf V}_{j}^{T}{\bf Q}_{j}{\bf W}_{j}.

Proof.

Substituting the factorizations
{\bf M}_{j}={\bf Q}_{j}{\bf R}_{j}
into the definition of
{\bf M}
gives

{\bf M}{\bf S}^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf V}_{j}^{T}{\bf Q}_{j}{\bf R}_{j}{\bf W}_{j}{\bf W}_{j}^{T}{\bf R}_{j}^{-1}{\bf W}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}{\bf V}_{j}^{T}{\bf Q}_{j}{\bf W}_{j},

using the identity
{\bf R}_{j}{\bf R}_{j}^{-1}={\bf I}
on the range of
{\bf W}_{j}
.
∎

This formulation isolates the orthogonal components
{\bf Q}_{j}
, which are numerically stable, and removes the influence of the potentially small singular values in
{\bf R}_{j}
, which would otherwise degrade the conditioning of the system. As a result, the preconditioned system is better suited for iterative solvers.

3.3
Condition Number Estimate for Overlapping Subdomains

We now analyze the spectral properties of the preconditioned matrix introduced in the previous subsection. Our preconditioner isolates the orthogonal factors
{\bf Q}_{j}
obtained through local RRQR factorizations. This effectively projects the global system onto a subspace spanned by patched-together locally orthonormal blocks. The resulting matrix inherits a structure that depends critically on the degree of overlap between subdomains.

To understand how this affects conditioning, we consider the case of
J
overlapping subdomains seen as consecutive blocks in one dimension or that may arise from a strip-wise decomposition in higher dimensions.

Let
J
be the number of overlapping subdomains. Each subdomain contributes a block of
C
features. The matrix
{\bf Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times JC}
is composed of row blocks corresponding to physical regions (interior and overlap) and column blocks corresponding to subdomains.

We assume the following:

•

Each subdomain
j
contributes a local feature matrix
{\bf Q}_{jj}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{j}\times C}
for its interior,

•

Overlapping regions between adjacent domains
j
and
j+1
are represented by matrices
{\bf Q}_{j,j+1},{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{j}\times C}
,

•

All columns of
{\bf Q}
are orthonormal.

Then
{\bf Q}
has the block structure:

{\bf Q}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf Q}_{11}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
{\bf Q}_{21}&{\bf Q}_{12}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&{\bf Q}_{22}&{\bf Q}_{23}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&{\bf Q}_{J-1,J}&{\bf Q}_{JJ}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times JC}

Given that all columns of
{\bf Q}
are orthonormal and that rows from different subdomains are disjoint, we compute:

{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}={\bf I}_{JC}+{\bf E}

where
{\bf E}
contains off-diagonal interactions due to overlaps.

Specifically,
{\bf E}
is a block tridiagonal matrix with:

{\bf E}_{j,j+1}={\bf Q}_{j,j+1}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{j+1,j},\quad{\bf E}_{j+1,j}={\bf E}_{j,j+1}^{\top}

and all diagonal blocks are the identity:

({\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q})_{jj}={\bf I}_{C}\quad\text{for all }j.

So the matrix
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}
has the form:

{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}&{\bf A}_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
{\bf A}_{1}^{\top}&{\bf I}&{\bf A}_{2}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&{\bf A}_{2}^{\top}&{\bf I}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&{\bf A}_{J-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&{\bf A}_{J-1}^{\top}&{\bf I}\end{bmatrix}

with
{\bf A}_{j}:={\bf Q}_{j,j+1}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Condition Number Bound for Overlapping Block Matrix)
.

Let
{\bf Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times JC}
be a block-structured matrix constructed from
J
overlapping subdomains with orthonormal rows. Assume:

•

Each subdomain contributes
C
features;

•

Overlaps occur only between neighboring subdomains
j
and
j+1
;

•

The Gram matrix
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{JC\times JC}
satisfies:

{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}={\bf I}+{\bf E},

where
{\bf E}
is symmetric and block tridiagonal with off-diagonal blocks:

{\bf E}_{j,j+1}={\bf A}_{j}:={\bf Q}_{j,j+1}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{j+1,j},\quad{\bf E}_{j+1,j}={\bf A}_{j}^{\top}.

Assume further that:

\|{\bf A}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\alpha\quad\text{for all }j.

Then the condition number of
{\bf Q}
satisfies:

\kappa({\bf Q})\leq\left(\frac{1+2\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\right)^{1/2},\quad\text{provided }2\alpha<1.

Proof.

By the symmetry of
{\bf E}
, its eigenvalues are real. Using the definition, the submultiplicativity of the spectral norm and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can easily estimate norm of
{\bf E}
:

\|{\bf E}\|_{2}\leq 2\alpha.

Thus, the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}={\bf I}+{\bf E}
satisfy

1-2\alpha\leq\lambda\leq 1+2\alpha.

Therefore, the condition number of
{\bf Q}
in the spectral norm is

\kappa({\bf Q})=\sqrt{\kappa({\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q})}\leq\left(\frac{1+2\alpha}{1-2\alpha}\right)^{1/2},

as long as
1-2\alpha>0
, i.e.,
2\alpha<1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

The matrix
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}
is always symmetric and positive semidefinite, since it is a Gram matrix. All its eigenvalues are non-negative.

However, the bound above is only meaningful when
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}
is
strictly
positive definite and a stricter inequality holds.
Since we estimate
\lambda_{\max}({\bf E})\leq 2\alpha
, the condition
2\alpha<1
ensures invertibility of
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}
and validity of the condition number estimate.

The value
\alpha=\max_{j}\|{\bf Q}_{j,j+1}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}\|_{2}
measures the strength of coupling between adjacent subdomains. The smaller the overlap-induced interactions, the better conditioned the global system is — which is somewhat counterintuitive from the domain decomposition perspective.

Remark 3.4
.

In the special case of only two overlapping blocks (
J=2
), the matrix
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}
has the form:

{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}&{\bf A}_{1}\\
{\bf A}_{1}^{\top}&{\bf I}\end{bmatrix},

so the interaction matrix is:

{\bf E}=\begin{bmatrix}0&{\bf A}_{1}\\
{\bf A}_{1}^{\top}&0\end{bmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}
are then
1\pm\sigma_{i}({\bf A}_{1})
, and the condition number becomes:

\kappa({\bf Q})=\left(\frac{1+\|{\bf A}_{1}\|_{2}}{1-\|{\bf A}_{1}\|_{2}}\right)^{1/2},\quad\text{valid whenever }\|{\bf A}_{1}\|_{2}<1.

Hence, the condition for positive definiteness is simply
\|{\bf A}_{1}\|_{2}<1
, which is strictly weaker than
2\alpha<1
. This makes the two-block case less restrictive and ensures well-conditioning as long as we have positive definiteness
{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf Q}\succ 0
.

While the deterministic estimate above shows that the condition number of
{\bf Q}
is governed by the interaction norms
\|{\bf A}_{j}\|_{2}
, it remains to understand how these norms behave when the feature blocks are generated randomly. In our context,
{\bf Q}_{j,j+1}
and
{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}
are constructed from local RRQR filtering of randomized features, suggesting that their behavior can be fruitfully analyzed using tools from random matrix theory. This motivates a probabilistic approach to estimating these interaction norms.

3.4
Estimating block interactions via Random Matrix Theory

To carry out a probabilistic analysis, we model the interaction matrices
{\bf A}_{j}={\bf Q}_{j,j+1}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}
in terms of random feature blocks
{\bf Q}_{j,j+1},{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times C}
with orthonormal columns. When these blocks are obtained from randomized methods, it is natural to treat them as independent semi-orthogonal matrices drawn from Haar-distributed orthogonal matrices. This perspective allows us to apply spectral norm bounds from random matrix theory to estimate the size of
\|{\bf A}_{j}\|_{2}
.

In this case the product
{\bf A}_{j}={\bf Q}_{j,j+1}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{j+1,j}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times C}
behaves like a submatrix of a Haar-distributed orthogonal matrix.

A result of Tropp
[
27
]
provides the following bound on the spectral norm of a principal submatrix of a Haar orthogonal matrix
{\bf P}\in\mathcal{O}_{n}
:

\mathbb{E}\left\|{\bf P}_{11}\right\|_{2}\leq\left(1+\frac{r}{2n}\right)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{r}+\sqrt{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}},\quad{\bf P}_{11}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell\times r}.

In our context, with
r=C
and treating
n
as the effective embedding dimension, we interpret:

\mathbb{E}\|{\bf A}_{j}\|_{2}\lesssim\left(1+\frac{C}{2n}\right)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}.

This gives an explicit probabilistic estimate:

\alpha\approx\left(1+\frac{C}{2n}\right)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Hence, in the high-dimensional limit where
n\gg C,\ell
, the block interaction norm
\alpha
tends to zero, and the condition number of
{\bf Q}
improves accordingly. This quantifies the idea that weaker overlap leads to better conditioning.

Probabilistic Condition Number Estimate

Combining the deterministic bound on the condition number from Theorem 1 with the probabilistic estimate for the block interaction norm
\alpha
, we obtain the following expression.

Define

\epsilon_{\ell,C,n}:=\left(1+\frac{C}{2n}\right)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{C}+\sqrt{\ell}}{\sqrt{n}},

as the upper bound for
\mathbb{E}[\|{\bf A}_{j}\|_{2}]
for each overlap block, based on Tropp’s inequality. Then the expected condition number satisfies:

\mathbb{E}[\kappa({\bf Q})]\leq\left(\frac{1+2\epsilon_{\ell,C,n}}{1-2\epsilon_{\ell,C,n}}\right)^{1/2}.

Remark 3.5
.

This probabilistic bound provides a precise quantitative trade-off between:

•

C/n
: the relative feature complexity per block,

•

\ell/n
: the relative size of the overlap region.

To ensure good conditioning (i.e., small
\epsilon_{\ell,C,n}
), it suffices that:

\left(1+\frac{C}{2n}\right)\cdot(\sqrt{\frac{C}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{n}})\ll\frac{1}{2}.

hence both low feature complexity and small overlap ensure good conditioning. If one of the relative dimensions
\frac{C}{n}
or
\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{n}}
gets bigger the overlap will deteriorate.

Discussion.

The numerical results presented here validate the theoretical claims regarding the effectiveness of the proposed QR-based preconditioning strategy. Notably, the dramatic improvement in condition number—from
\kappa(M)\approx 2.25\times 10^{13}
to
\kappa(MS^{-1})\approx 5.18\times 10^{5}
—confirms the theoretical prediction that filtering local subdomain matrices via rank-revealing QR decompositions significantly reduces ill-conditioning. This is consistent with the spectral estimates derived in Section 4, where the overlap-induced condition number degradation is shown to scale with a parameter
\varepsilon_{\ell,C,n}
that diminishes in high-dimensional regimes or with moderate overlap. The sharper SVD decay of
MS^{-1}
in Figure
3
corroborates the theoretical assertion that the preconditioner isolates well-conditioned orthogonal components while filtering redundant directions. Additionally, the reduced iteration counts and lower
\ell^{1}
-errors for both CG and LSQR solvers indicate not only improved conditioning but also enhanced convergence and solution accuracy. These observations empirically support the claim that direct preconditioning of the least squares system—without forming normal equations—can be both numerically stable and computationally efficient in the RFM setting.
