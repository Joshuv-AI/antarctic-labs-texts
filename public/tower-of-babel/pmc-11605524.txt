A rare case of posttraumatic subdural hematoma and intratumoral hemorrhage in a left frontotemporal meningioma: a conservative approach with longitudinal follow-up. Illustrative case
Authors: Mansour Khaleel, Dana Sayyed Ahmad, Mohammed Ayyad, Amer Abu Al Ragheb
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDMeningiomas are common primary intracranial tumors, with associated intratumoral hemorrhage being a rare but serious complication requiring prompt diagnosis for optimal outcomes. Although spontaneous bleeding in benign meningiomas is rare, it can lead to subarachnoid hemorrhage, subdural hematoma, or intratumoral/intracerebral hemorrhage, significantly increasing morbidity and mortality. Advanced imaging and recognition of radiographic hemorrhage patterns are crucial for accurate diagnosis. Treatment is case specific, as surgical intervention is preferred for symptomatic patients, while observation might be adequate for asymptomatic cases. When surgery is not feasible, stereotactic radiotherapy or chemotherapy, which is used only as salvage therapy, is considered.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the case of a 78-year-old male with a history of recurrent falls. Imaging studies revealed a posttraumatic subdural hematoma and an intratumoral hemorrhage within a left frontotemporal meningioma. The patient was managed conservatively, showing significant improvement over 6 months. Notably, there was a marked reduction in both tumor size and intratumoral hemorrhage during follow-up.LESSONSThis case highlights the importance of adopting case-specific management strategies for hemorrhagic complications associated with meningiomas. Meningiomas are among the most prevalent primary intracranial tumors, accounting for almost 20% of the cases. However, they seldom undergo intratumoral hemorrhage.1 Compared to the estimated incidence of intracranial hemorrhage caused by other intracranial tumors, spontaneous bleeding from benign meningiomas is notably less common.2, 3 Remarkably, the occurrence of hemorrhagic transformation in a meningioma can be linked to a range of factors, all associated with the well-established vascular nature of meningiomas.1, 4, 5 It is crucial to recognize that tumoral bleeding is associated with higher morbidity and mortality rates. Moreover, mortality rates vary between different types of tumoral bleeding; however, the absence of statistically significant disparities underscores the necessity for additional research in this domain.6 This case highlights the unusual co-occurrence of posttraumatic subdural hematoma and intratumoral hemorrhage in a meningioma, emphasizing the significance of diligent surveillance and the assessment of potential hemorrhagic risk factors. Additionally, we explore the prognostic indicators associated with this condition and evaluate the merits of conservative versus surgical interventions in such scenarios.

Illustrative Case

A 78-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department after falling down and sustaining a head trauma resulting in a small scalp hematoma. There was no history of loss of consciousness, seizure activity, nausea, vomiting, visual disturbances, or limb symptoms. He was complaining of intense left-sided parieto-occipital pulsatile headaches that had started and progressively worsened over 2 weeks with minimal response to acetaminophen. It is noteworthy that the patient reported a history of recurrent falls spanning the past 4–5 years, with the most recent episode occurring just days before his presentation. His medical history included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, hypothyroidism, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Notably, he had a significant smoking history, with a daily consumption of 3 packs of cigarettes. His prescribed medications consisted of apixaban, metoprolol, atorvastatin, tamsulosin, and amiodarone. During the consultation, the patient mentioned recently visiting his cardiologist and undergoing a full cardiovascular assessment, which had not revealed any significant pathology to explain his recurrent falls. On examination, the patient was conscious, alert, and oriented to time, person, and place, with stable vital signs and a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 15. Neurologically, he had no cranial nerve, cerebellar, or limb motor or sensory deficits. Investigations, including screening body radiography and brain computed tomography (CT), were performed. The brain CT scan revealed a small linear nondisplaced fracture in the left temporal bone. Additionally, it indicated the presence of a subacute subdural hematoma in the left frontoparietal region. Moreover, there was a dura-based lesion measuring 1.7 cm at its maximum thickness in the left frontotemporal area, resembling a meningioma, and causing some mass effect on the frontal and temporal lobes. There was also a small scalp hematoma in the posterior right occipital region, with normal gray-white matter differentiation, normal posterior fossa structures, and age-related brain atrophic changes (Fig. 1). The patient was admitted to the neurosurgery department for further evaluation and management. Although it is not customary to perform routine electroencephalography (EEG) in patients with subdural hematomas, the presence of an intracranial dura-based lesion, coupled with the history of recurrent falls, warranted further investigations to rule out any potential underlying seizure activity. Accordingly, EEG was performed, revealing decreased activity in the anterior temporal regions on the left side. Subsequently, contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted. MRI displayed a left frontotemporal extra-axial dura-based enhancing mass, measuring 4.8 × 2 × 4 cm (anteroposterior [AP] × transverse [TR] × craniocaudal [CC]). Importantly, the mass was associated with a dural tail and exerted some mass effect on the frontal and temporal lobes, in keeping with a meningioma. A few internal foci of hyperintensities were observed on the T1-weighted images, likely indicating small areas of microbleeds. Furthermore, a left frontoparietal subdural hematoma was observed. It exhibited a maximum thickness of 11 mm and contained various blood products at different stages of aging, including older liquefied blood and smaller late subacute blood products, leading to minimal displacement of midline brain structures of approximately 3 mm to the contralateral side (Fig. 2). The consensus was that the patient’s recurrent falls could be attributed to vascular factors or potentially associated with epileptogenic activity in proximity to the meningioma. While the subdural hematoma was notably thick, it did not induce substantial compression on the brain. However, upon retrospective examination, it was evident that the meningioma had exhibited growth in comparison to MRI scans obtained 3 years previously, on which the extra-axial lesion was already discernible (Fig. 3). Interestingly, the pronounced blooming artifact observed on the susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) sequences was likely indicative of the existence of blood products, suggesting the presence of an intratumoral hemorrhage. It was reassuring that the patient’s examination did not elicit alarming neurological deficits. Yet, the risk of rebleeding and further brain compression was still possible. The patient’s condition and imaging findings were discussed with the patient and his family, along with the possible treatment options, including conservative management and surgical intervention. While a conservative management strategy involving sequential brain imaging and frequent neurological evaluations remained a reasonable choice, the combination of tumor enlargement, intratumoral hemorrhage, and subtle EEG alterations in addition to the patient’s recurrent falls favored excision. Following consultations with a neurologist, it was deemed that there was no immediate urgency for surgical intervention, and the option of initiating prophylactic levetiracetam to guard against potential seizure activity was considered. Regrettably, the patient declined this recommendation. To ensure proper monitoring and management of blood coagulation, a cardiology consultation was sought, and the decision was reached to withhold anticoagulation therapy. Eventually, the patient was discharged from the hospital in a stable condition, with a plan to repeat brain CT and examine him in the outpatient clinic after 2 weeks. Two weeks later, the patient presented to the clinic for follow-up, complaining only of occasional mild dizziness. His neurological examination was stable, with no detected deficits. The follow-up brain CT scan demonstrated a significant reduction in the previously noted subdural hematoma. Regarding the presumed meningioma, it remained unchanged in size at 1.7 cm, while a reduction in hyperdensity was noted (Fig. 4). The patient was reassured, and the decision was made to continue conservative management. Accordingly, the plan was to see the patient in 6 months with follow-up contrast-enhanced brain MRI, and strict instructions were given to him to visit the nearest emergency department if his condition deteriorated or he developed any new symptoms. Six months later, the patient returned to the clinic without any reported complaints. During the examination, his neurological functions were found to be intact, with no observed deficits. Contrast-enhanced brain MRI revealed a notable reduction in the size of the meningioma, with measurements showing 3.2 × 1.3 × 3 cm (AP × TR × CC), in addition to changes in the SWI sequence when compared to earlier scans (Fig. 5). This positive development provided reassurance. Consequently, the patient was scheduled for a follow-up clinic visit in 1 year, which will include another contrast-enhanced brain MRI study. Discussion

Meningiomas are neoplastic growths believed to originate from the arachnoid cap cells, which provide structural support to the brain and spinal cord.7 Despite representing nearly 20% of primary intracranial tumors, the occurrence of intratumoral hemorrhage in association with meningiomas is markedly rare.1 Spontaneous bleeding associated with benign meningiomas is significantly less frequent than intracerebral hemorrhage from other intracranial tumors, occurring at a rate of 0.5%–2.4%. The incidence of intracerebral hemorrhage from other intracranial tumors is known to range from 0.5% to 4.4%.2, 3 When it does occur, hemorrhage can present as a subarachnoid hemorrhage, subdural hematoma, or intratumoral hemorrhage and is consequently linked to a heightened mortality rate, with estimates ranging from 28% to 55%.2, 8, 9

Considering the acknowledged vascularity of these lesions, numerous potential mechanisms contributing to hemorrhage have been postulated, although they largely remain inconclusive. Suggested etiologies include compensatory hypertrophy of the vascular bed supplying the tumor, caused by accelerated and aberrant angiogenesis, which eventually leads to vascular wall fragility and weakening, making it highly susceptible to rupture. Furthermore, this could also be exacerbated by systemic factors such as hypertension and diabetes mellitus, direct tumor cell invasion into the vasculature, extensive intratumoral necrosis and infarction, the stretching and subsequent rupture of subdural veins, and minor trauma, all of which lead to vascular wall weakening.1, 4, 5 Moreover, recent studies have suggested that other modifiable and nonmodifiable factors could potentially contribute to the elevated risk of a hemorrhagic presentation. These factors include a patient’s age exceeding 70 or below 30 years, the tumor’s location within the intraventricular or convexity regions, a history of traumatic brain injury, the concurrent use of anticoagulant medications, treatment involving serotonin-modulating agents, high-dose estrogen replacement therapy, and the presence of malignant-appearing, translational, or angiomatous histopathological features.1, 6

Considering the various hypotheses that could elucidate the occurrence of hemorrhage in our patient, our assessment points toward the possibility that a blood dyscrasia linked to anticoagulation medication usage, coupled with the presence of multiple comorbidities, including hypertension and diabetes mellitus, along with a recent history of minor head trauma, could have contributed to the development of subdural and intratumoral hemorrhage in this particular case. It is noteworthy to mention that a scant number of case reports ascribe the origin of hemorrhagic meningiomas to minor head trauma, while the majority of studies accentuate nontraumatic risk factors. However, head trauma can still trigger an acute intratumoral hemorrhage in meningiomas, especially when the injury impacts an area near the tumor.9, 10

Dang et al. reported a case of intratumoral hemorrhage in a frontal parafalcine meningioma following a fall.10 The patient, a 75-year-old man with diabetes, hypertension, and alcohol use disorder, presented with a GCS score of 15, which declined to 12 after an unwitnessed fall. A brain CT scan revealed a large right frontal hemorrhage with early herniation and significant edema, suggesting an underlying neoplasm. There were no signs of bone involvement, while additional injuries included a subgaleal hematoma and soft tissue edema. The patient’s condition worsened, requiring intubation and hyperosmolar therapy before emergency surgery. Intraoperative findings included a right frontal hematoma with necrotic debris and a fibrous capsule. A laceration of the superior sagittal sinus was also repaired. A postoperative brain CT scan showed successful resection, and MRI confirmed no residual tumor but persistent edema. Pathology confirmed a World Health Organization (WHO) grade 1 meningioma with microcystic and angiomatous features, with no brain invasion and low Ki-67 staining. The postoperative neurological examination showed improvement, with a mild left facial droop as the only deficit. The patient was discharged to rehabilitation on postoperative day 4.10 On the other hand, the initial detection and diagnosis of meningiomas predominantly rely on the clinical presentation as well as imaging studies.11 Nevertheless, the definitive diagnosis necessitates biopsy or resection with histopathological examination.12 In our patient, SWI blooming suggested intratumoral hemorrhage; however, this signal could represent either calcification or blood products. Subsequent imaging demonstrated a reduction in both the size of the tumor and the associated hyperdensity, strongly suggesting that the hyperdensity was more likely due to intratumoral hemorrhage rather than calcification.

A thorough examination of the radiographic hemorrhage pattern at the initial presentation is crucial for an accurate differential diagnosis and timely treatment. Early diagnosis and appropriate treatment can significantly reduce mortality rates, potentially bringing them down to as low as 13.9% for cases involving hemorrhagic meningiomas.6 However, when substantial intratumoral hemorrhage complicates the identification of a potential underlying neoplasm, clinicians should conduct noncontrast CT to assess specific characteristics. These features include alterations in bony structures such as hyperostosis or erosive changes, distortion of focal cerebral and venous sinuses, perifocal edema, variations in signal density within the hemorrhagic region, a round morphology, and a well-defined demarcation between the hemorrhagic foci and the surrounding parenchyma. Such indicators aid in distinguishing intralesional hemorrhage from spontaneous intracranial hemorrhage.13 In our patient, the presence of varied density patterns on imaging raised concerns regarding the presence of an underlying neoplasm. However, our case presented a less urgent need for workup and immediate treatment, as the patient was stable. Subsequently, we opted for MRI to gain more comprehensive insights into the underlying meningioma, facilitating our long- and short-term treatment planning decisions.

Meningiomas encompass a diverse spectrum of pathological variations, necessitating a tailored treatment approach, particularly when assessing risk stratifications.14 While neurosurgical interventions have traditionally been the cornerstone of treatment, it is imperative to acknowledge that, notwithstanding their often-benign histological profile, these tumors are associated with notable clinical symptomatology, the potential for recurrence, and a less favorable prognosis.14 Contemporary treatment strategies are principally stratified based on the symptomatic or asymptomatic nature of the neoplasms. In the case of small, asymptomatic meningiomas, a strategy of close observation and follow-up is typically advocated, as was employed in our patient’s care. This approach entails a regimen of clinical surveillance and MRI screenings following the initial diagnosis. Patients who remain asymptomatic beyond a 5-year interval transition to annual observation-only protocols.12

Conversely, for symptomatic meningiomas, surgical intervention is usually preferred, with further stratification of symptomatic meningiomas based on the WHO grading system. Recurrence risk, survival rates, and morbidity intricately intertwine with WHO grades, rendering them pivotal determinants in the selection of the most appropriate treatment modality.15 Nevertheless, individuals who may not be deemed suitable candidates for surgical interventions, such as elderly or incapacitated patients, are offered stereotactic radiotherapy/stereotactic radiosurgery or chemotherapy as their primary treatment modalities.16 When considering the prognosis of these patients, it is crucial to recognize that hemorrhage related to tumors significantly increases the risk of both morbidity and mortality. Specifically, mortality rates for hemorrhagic meningiomas have been found to be 21.1% in the overall patient population and 9.5% among those who have undergone surgical intervention. Interestingly, there has not been a direct comparison between cases of pure intratumoral bleeding and those with other forms of tumor bleeding in the literature, highlighting the need for further research in this specific area.6

Observations

In this case, the main observations revolve around the rare occurrence of intratumoral hemorrhage within a meningioma, compounded by the presence of a subacute subdural hematoma. Notably, the patient’s clinical presentation, including recurrent falls and intense pulsatile headaches, along with his medical history and imaging findings, highlighted the significant yet subtle interplay between comorbidities, anticoagulant medication usage, and the potential contribution of minor head trauma to the development of hemorrhagic complications. Importantly, the gradual improvement observed over 6 months under conservative management, marked by significant reductions in both the subdural hematoma and intratumoral hemorrhage, highlights the effectiveness of a nonsurgical approach in select cases. While the notion of traumatic etiology in hemorrhagic meningiomas has been sporadically reported, our case adds to the existing body of literature by elucidating the complex pathophysiological mechanisms and treatment considerations in managing such presentations. Moreover, the patient’s stability throughout the course of treatment and the favorable response to conservative management provide valuable insights into the potential benefits of tailored treatment strategies in optimizing patient outcomes.

Lessons

The most significant lesson derived from this case highlights the nuanced management of hemorrhagic complications within meningiomas, emphasizing the importance of tailored treatment strategies and vigilant surveillance. While surgical intervention remains a cornerstone in managing symptomatic meningiomas, our case highlights the potential efficacy of conservative approaches in select patients, particularly those with stable clinical presentations and manageable intracranial hemorrhages. However, it is crucial to acknowledge the limitations of our findings, including the lack of a large-scale comparative analysis and the inherent challenges in extrapolating conclusions from a single case. Nonetheless, the relevance of these observations extends beyond the scope of our study, shedding light on the complex interplay among vascular factors, comorbidities, and treatment modalities in the context of hemorrhagic meningiomas. Thus, future research endeavors should aim to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving hemorrhagic presentations, explore the prognostic implications of conservative management strategies, and delineate optimal treatment algorithms tailored to individual patient profiles.

In conclusion, our case highlights the significance of recognizing meningiomas as potential sources of acute intratumoral hemorrhages, especially in patients with predisposing factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and antithrombotic medication use. Early identification and vigilant monitoring are paramount, even when symptoms directly attributable to the meningioma are absent, as they can lead to more favorable outcomes. Nonetheless, while these occurrences are exceedingly rare, their implications necessitate further research efforts aimed at better understanding and managing such cases.
