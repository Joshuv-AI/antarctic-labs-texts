Open quantum theory of magnetoresistance in mesoscopic magnetic materials
Xian-Peng Zhang, Xiangrong Wang, Yugui Yao

Abstract
Magnetoresistance (MR) in magnetic materials arises from spin-exchange coupling between local moments and itinerant electrons, representing a challenging many-body open-quantum problem. Here we develop a comprehensive microscopic theory of MR within an open-quantum-system framework by solving the Liouville–von Neumann equation for a hybrid system of free electrons and local moments using the time-convolutionless projection operator method. Our approach reveals both ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic MR as consequences of temperature- and field-dependent spin decoherence, encompassing spin relaxation and dephasing. In particular, the resistance associated with spin decoherence is governed by the order parameters of magnetic materials, such as the magnetization in ferromagnets and the Néel vector in antiferromagnets. This theory deepens the fundamental understanding of MR and offers guidance for interpreting and designing experiments on magnetic materials.

Before presenting our microscopic theory, we qualitatively describe the essence of the MR and the challenges it presents. Here, we investigate spin exchange in a composite system consisting of itinerant electrons and localized moments (see Fig.
1
). Thus, this is fundamentally a many-body problem. However, the composite system, in principle, will not reach a steady state on its own, as its dynamics are governed by a unitary Hamiltonian (
1
). During the spin exchange process, total spin is conserved; it merely transfers back and forth between the itinerant electrons and the localized moments, and thus no spin relaxation occurs. However, as is well known, such a composite system is not completely isolated - it is coupled to a huge environment and eventually reaches a steady state. Since our primary interest lies in the transport of the itinerant electrons, we assume that the local moments constitute a large system compared to the itinerant electrons, with their thermodynamic behavior determined by the external environment, such as the magnetic field and temperature. Therefore, the MR effect, by its nature, is an interacting and nonequilibrium problem in an open quantum system.

III
Open quantum theory

In this section, we present the details of the open quantum theory of a hybrid system consisting of free electrons and local moments, with the latter being treated as a quantum bath (or reservoir). The full dynamics of the many-body density matrix
\check{\varrho}(t)
, governed by the spin-exchange coupling in Eq. (
4
), are described by the Liouville-von Neumann equation in the interaction picture relative to the noninteracting Hamiltonian
\check{H}_{0}=\hat{H}_{e}+\widehat{H}_{m}
:

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\check{\varrho}(t)=\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[\check{\varrho}(t),\check{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{em}}(t)],

(7)

where the interaction-picture operator is defined as

\displaystyle\check{\mathcal{O}}(t)=e^{+i\check{H}_{0}t/\hbar}O(t)e^{-i\check{H}_{0}t/\hbar}.

(8)

We solve Eq. (
7
) approximately using a time-convolutionless projection operator technique. Under the second-order Born-Markov approximation
Breuer and Petruccione 2002
, we obtain the master equation for the reduced electron density matrix
\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)=\text{Tr}_{m}[\check{\varrho}(t)]
:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}\text{Tr}_{m}[\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)\widehat{\varrho}_{m}(t),\check{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{em}}(t)]

(9)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{d\tau}{\hbar^{2}}\text{Tr}_{m}[[\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)\widehat{\varrho}_{m}(t),\check{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{em}}(\tau)],\check{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathrm{em}}(t)].

Here,
\widehat{\varrho}_{m}(t)=\text{Tr}_{e}[\check{\varrho}(t)]
is the reduced density matrix of the local moments, and
\text{Tr}_{e/m}
traces out the free electron/local moment degree of freedom.
The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
9
) represents a correction to the effective Zeeman magnetic field experienced by the electrons due to the average spin expectation of the local moments. It can be compactly expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(t)\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)=-i\langle\widehat{L}_{n}(t)\rangle[\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t),\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n}(t)],

(10)

where we have combined the indices into a single label
n=(\nu,\imath,i)
for brevity. The second term in Eq. (
9
) acts as a collision integral,
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\hat{\varrho}_{e})
, which describes dissipation and decoherence. It is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\hat{\varrho}_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\left\{\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}n_{2}}(\tau-t)\left[\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{2}}(t),\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{1}}(\tau)\right]\right.

(11)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.\mathcal{D}_{n_{2}n_{1}}(t-\tau)\left[\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{2}}(t),\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{1}}(\tau)\hat{\varrho}_{e}(t)\right]\right\}.

Here,
\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}n_{2}}(t_{1}-t_{2})
is the two-time correlation function of the quantum reservoir (i.e., local moments), defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}n_{2}}(t_{1}-t_{2})=\langle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{1}}(t_{1})\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{n_{2}}(t_{2})\rangle.

(12)

For simplicity, we assume the magnetic order is determined solely by the Hamiltonian of the local moments, Eq. (
3
). This treatment neglects the back-action of the itinerant electrons (the small subsystem) on the magnetic configuration of the local moments (the large reservoir), as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Transforming into the Schrödinger picture, we obtain the master equation for the reduced electronic density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
of free electrons

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{\rho}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{H}_{e}\right]-\hat{B}_{1}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)=\hat{J}(\hat{\rho}).

(13)

Now, we can clearly see that the term
\hat{B}_{1}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)
is responsible for effective magnetic field experienced by the electrons due to the average spin expectation
\langle\widehat{L}_{n}(t)\rangle\propto\langle\widehat{S}_{n}(t)\rangle
of the local moments

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{1}(t)\hat{\rho}(t)=-i\langle\widehat{L}_{n}(t)\rangle[\hat{\rho}(t),\hat{V}^{n}],

(14)

The term
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\hat{\rho})
is a collision integral that describes spin-dependent electron scattering from the fluctuating local moments:

\displaystyle\hat{J}(\hat{\rho})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t-t_{0}}d\tau\left\{\mathcal{D}_{n_{2}n_{1}}(-\tau)\left[\hat{V}^{n_{1}},\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{2}}(-\tau)\right]\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\mathcal{D}_{n_{1}n_{2}}(+\tau)\left[\hat{V}^{n_{1}},\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{2}}(-\tau)\hat{\rho}(t)\right]\right\}.

(15)

with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}^{n_{2}}(-\tau)=e^{-i\hat{H}_{e}\tau/\hbar}\hat{V}^{n_{2}}e^{i\hat{H}_{e}\tau/\hbar}.

(16)

Here, the upper limit of the integral will be extended to infinity, consistent with the Markov approximation.

III.1
From many- to one-body master equation

In this subsection, we derive a single-particle master equation from the many-body master equation for the electron density matrix
\hat{\rho}(t)
, given by Eq. (
13
). This reduction is essential as most quantum transport observables—such as charge and spin densities and currents—are expressed in terms of single-particle operators. The central object is the single-particle density matrix, defined as

\varrho_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}(t)=\langle\alpha_{2}|\hat{\varrho}(t)|\alpha_{1}\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}_{e}\{\hat{f}^{+}_{\alpha_{1}}\hat{f}_{\alpha_{2}}\hat{\rho}(t)\}.

(17)

where
\alpha=(\boldsymbol{p},\sigma)
labels states in an arbitrary orthonormal basis
{|\alpha\rangle}
(e.g., spin-momentum states). Note that the index ordering in the single-particle density matrix is the conjugate of the ordering for the field operators inside the trace. The equation of motion for the single-particle density matrix is obtained by taking the trace of Eq. (
13

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\varrho_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\varrho(t),\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{e}_{0}]_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\varrho(t),\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{m}]_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}=\mathcal{J}_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}}(\hat{\varrho}).

(18)

The effective mean-field potential arising from the local moments is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}_{m}=-\frac{1}{2}N_{\imath}\mathcal{J}^{\nu}_{\imath}\langle\widehat{S}^{\nu}_{\imath}\rangle\hat{\sigma}^{\nu},

(19)

which represents a renormalization of the electronic Zeeman energy.

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{r}|\alpha\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}e^{\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}|\sigma\rangle.

(20)

\displaystyle g\mu_{B}\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\imath i}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\to\textsl{g}\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\imath i}\cdot\boldsymbol{H}_{\imath i}.

\boldsymbol{H}_{\imath i}=\boldsymbol{B}-\frac{1}{\textsl{g}\mu_{B}}J_{\imath i,\imath^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\langle\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}_{\imath^{\prime}i^{\prime}}\rangle.

This approximation dramatically simplifies the spin-spin correlations, reducing them to purely local terms:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\eta}_{n_{2}n_{1}}(\omega)\simeq\delta_{\boldsymbol{j}_{2}\boldsymbol{j}_{1}}\mathcal{D}^{\eta,\imath_{1}}_{\nu_{2}\nu_{1}}(\omega),

where

and on-site correlation function

The spin expectation value and its autocorrelation are then determined self-consistently by this Weiss field

\langle S^{\imath}_{\parallel}\rangle=-SB_{S}\left(\beta S\epsilon^{\imath}_{L}\right),

(32)

\displaystyle\langle S^{\imath}_{\parallel}S^{\imath}_{\parallel}\rangle=S^{\imath}(S^{\imath}+1)+\coth(\beta\epsilon^{\imath}_{L}/2)\langle S_{\parallel}\rangle,

where

,
B_{S}(x)
is the Brillouin function, and
S^{\imath}_{\|}
is the spin component in the direction of external the magnetic field
\boldsymbol{B}
. Crucially, the mean-field approximation renders the magnon dispersion flat, represented by a single effective Larmor energy
\epsilon^{\imath}_{L}=g\mu_{B}H_{\imath}
. Applying the local correlation approximation (
31
), the collision integral (
25
) simplifies significantly, factoring into a sum over independent sublattices:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\check{\varrho})

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}}{V}\left\{\left[\mathcal{D}^{-,\imath}_{\nu_{2}\nu_{1}}(\omega^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}\sigma})\sigma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\nu_{1}}\varrho^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\sigma_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}^{\nu_{2}}\bar{\varrho}^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right.\right.

(34)

\displaystyle-\left.\mathcal{D}^{-,\imath}_{\nu_{2}\nu_{1}}(\omega^{\boldsymbol{k}\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}})\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sigma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\nu_{2}}\bar{\varrho}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\sigma_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}^{\nu_{1}}\right]

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{D}^{+,\imath}_{\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}(\omega^{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}\sigma})\bar{\varrho}^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\sigma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\nu_{2}}\varrho^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\sigma_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}^{\nu_{1}}\right.

\displaystyle-\left.\left.\mathcal{D}^{+,\imath}_{\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}(\omega^{\boldsymbol{k}\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}})\sigma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\nu_{1}}\bar{\varrho}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\sigma_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}^{\nu_{2}}\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right]\right\},

where
n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}=N_{\imath}/V
is the density of local moments of
\imath
sublattice.

In summary, the collision integral in Eq. (
34
) is derived based on three key approximations: (i) the second-order Born-Markov approximation applied to the many-body master equation [Eq. (
9
)], (ii) the diagonal density matrix approximation for the electronic degrees of freedom [Eq. (
24
)], and (iii) the Weiss mean-field approximation for the local moments [Eq. (
29
)]. The mean-field treatment, in particular, greatly simplifies the many-body problem by reducing the spin-spin correlations to purely local functions [Eq. (
31
)]. It is important to note that the spin-spin correlation functions of the local moments, which enter the collision integral and are treated as a quantum bath, depend explicitly on temperature, external magnetic field strength, and the underlying magnetic configuration.

III.2
Anisotropic spin relaxation

In this subsection, we investigate the spin relaxation time for an arbitrary spin quantization axis, parameterized by the orientation angles
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
. To facilitate this analysis, we introduce a rotated spin basis defined by the transformation:

\displaystyle\left|\Uparrow\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\uparrow\right\rangle-e^{-i\phi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\downarrow\right\rangle,

(35)

\displaystyle\left|\Downarrow\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\downarrow\right\rangle+e^{+i\phi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\left|\uparrow\right\rangle,

(36)

where
\left|\uparrow\right\rangle
and
\left|\downarrow\right\rangle
are the eigenstates of the bare
\sigma_{z}
operator (aligned parallel and antiparallel to the external magnetic field, respectively). The states
\left|\Uparrow\right\rangle
and
\left|\Downarrow\right\rangle
are the eigenstates of the rotated Pauli operator

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{z}}=-\sin\theta\cos\phi\sigma_{x}+\sin\theta\sin\phi\sigma_{y}+\cos\theta\sigma_{z},

(37)

with eigenvalues
+1
and
-1
, respectively. This transformation corresponds to a rotation of the spin quantization axis, implemented by the matrix
R
which performs a rotation by angle
\phi
about the
z
-axis followed by a rotation by angle
\theta
about the
y
-axis. The spin operators for both the free electrons and the local moments transform under this rotation as:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}\\
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{y}}\\
\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}\end{bmatrix}=R\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\sigma}_{x}\\
\hat{\sigma}_{y}\\
\hat{\sigma}_{z}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{x}}\\
\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{y}}\\
\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{z}}\end{bmatrix}=R\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{L}^{i}_{x}\\
\widehat{L}^{i}_{y}\\
\widehat{L}^{i}_{z}\end{bmatrix},

(38)

where the rotation matrix is given by

\displaystyle R=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta\cos\phi&\mathtt{-}\cos\theta\sin\phi&\sin\theta\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi&0\\
\mathtt{-}\sin\theta\cos\phi&\sin\theta\sin\phi&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}.

(39)

Expressed in this rotated basis, the spin-exchange coupling term in Eq. (
4
) takes the form

\displaystyle\check{V}_{\mathrm{em}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left\{\widehat{L}_{\mathbf{+}}^{i}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{-}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})+\widehat{L}_{\mathbf{-}}^{i}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{+}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})+\widehat{L}_{\mathbf{z}}^{i}\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{z}}(\boldsymbol{r}_{i})\right\},

(40)

where the corresponding ladder operators are defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{+}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{x}}+i\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{y}},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{+}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{2}[\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})+i\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{r})],

(41)

\displaystyle\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{-}}

\displaystyle=\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{x}}-i\widehat{L}^{i}_{\mathbf{y}},\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{-}}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{1}{2}[\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{r})-i\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{r})].

(42)

We now define the spin density along the arbitrary quantization axis defined by Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
) as

\displaystyle N^{\sigma}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(43)

where
\sigma\in{\Uparrow,\Downarrow}
. The time evolution of this spin density is governed by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}N^{\sigma}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\check{\varrho}),

(44)

where
\mathcal{J}^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\varrho)
is the collision integral given in Eq. (
34
). We evaluate this expression in the
{\Uparrow,\Downarrow}
basis for convenience. Substituting the explicit form of the collision integral, the rate equation for the spin density becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}N^{\sigma}=W_{\sigma,\bar{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\varrho^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\bar{\varrho}^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-W_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k})\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\bar{\varrho}^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}},

(45)

where
\bar{\sigma}
denotes the spin state opposite to
\sigma
. The transition rate
W_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})
is given by

\displaystyle W_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{V^{2}}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\mathcal{D}^{\eta,\imath}_{\nu_{2}\nu_{1}}(\omega^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-\omega^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\sigma_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}^{\nu_{1}}\sigma_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}^{\nu_{2}}.

(46)

This form of the rate equation (
45
) has a clear physical interpretation: the first term represents the rate of scattering into state
|\boldsymbol{k}\sigma\rangle
from all other states
|\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}\bar{\sigma}\rangle
(gain term), while the second term represents scattering out of state
|\boldsymbol{k}\sigma\rangle
(loss term).

In thermal equilibrium, we assume the electronic system is described by a Fermi-Dirac distribution, such that the density matrix is diagonal and given by

\displaystyle\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}),

(47)

where the spin-dependent energy is
\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}+\epsilon^{\sigma}_{B}
. Here,
\epsilon^{\sigma}_{B}=\langle\sigma|\epsilon_{B}\hat{\sigma}_{z}|\sigma\rangle
represents the Zeeman energy shift for the spin states
\sigma={\Uparrow,\Downarrow}
in the rotated basis. A crucial element of this derivation is the fluctuation-dissipation theorem, which relates the spin-spin correlation functions at positive and negative frequencies:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{1}\nu_{2}}(\epsilon)=e^{\beta\epsilon}\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{2}\nu_{1}}(-\epsilon).

(48)

This relation directly implies that the scattering rates defined in Eq. (
46
) satisfy the principle of detailed balance:

\displaystyle W_{\sigma,\bar{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=e^{\beta\left(\epsilon^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\right)}W_{\bar{\sigma},\sigma}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k}).

(49)

When the equilibrium form of the density matrix and the detailed balance condition (
49
) are inserted into the rate equation (
45
), the gain and loss terms cancel exactly. Consequently, the time derivative of the spin density vanishes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}N^{\sigma}=0,

(50)

as required for a system in thermal equilibrium. This result serves as an important consistency check for our formalism.

To describe out-of-equilibrium spin dynamics, we employ an ansatz that incorporates a spin-dependent chemical potential shift:

\displaystyle\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\mu^{\sigma}).

(51)

The deviation of the distribution function from its equilibrium value is given by
\delta\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})
reads:

\delta\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\simeq-f^{\prime}(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\mu^{\sigma}=\beta f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\bar{f}(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\mu^{\sigma},

(52)

with
\bar{f}(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})=1-f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})
. The resulting deviation in spin density is then

\displaystyle\delta N^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\delta\varrho^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\beta f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\bar{f}(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\mu^{\sigma}=\nu^{\sigma}_{F}\mu^{\sigma},

(53)

which defines the effective density of states

\displaystyle\nu^{\sigma}_{F}=\int d\epsilon\nu(\epsilon)\beta f(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\bar{f}(\epsilon^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}}).

(54)

At zero temperature, this simplifies to
\nu^{\sigma}_{F}=\nu(\epsilon_{F}-\epsilon^{\sigma}_{B})
, the density of states at the spin-split Fermi energy. Using Eq. (
53
), we linearize the rate equation (
45
) to obtain the dynamics of the spin density deviation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta N^{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\beta W_{\sigma,\bar{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})f(\omega^{\bar{\sigma}}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})\bar{f}(\omega^{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{k}})(\mu^{\bar{\sigma}}-\mu^{\sigma}).

(55)

The out-of-equilibrium charge and spin densities along the quantization axis are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle\delta N^{o}

\displaystyle=\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}\mu^{\Uparrow}+\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F}\mu^{\Downarrow},

(56)

\displaystyle\delta N^{\|}

\displaystyle=\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}\mu^{\Uparrow}-\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F}\mu^{\Downarrow}.

(57)

The time evolution of the charge density is given by the sum of the spin-resolved rates

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta N^{o}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}N^{\Uparrow}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}N^{\Downarrow}=0,

(58)

which explicitly demonstrates charge conservation within our model.
The evolution of the spin density is governed by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta N^{\|}

\displaystyle=-2\beta W_{\Downarrow,\Uparrow}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k})f(\omega^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\bar{f}(\omega^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}})(\mu^{\Uparrow}-\mu^{\Downarrow}).

(59)

Expressing the chemical potential difference in terms of
\delta N^{c}
and
\delta N^{s}
using the inverted relations
\mu^{\Uparrow/\Downarrow}=(\delta N^{c}\pm\delta N^{s})/(\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}+\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F})
leads to a coupled equation of motion:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta N^{\|}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{T_{\|}}\delta N^{\|}-\frac{1}{T_{0}}\delta N^{0},

(60)

where the spin relaxation rate
(1/T_{\|})
and the charge-spin conversion rate
(1/T_{0})
are given by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{\|}}=\left(\frac{\beta}{\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}}+\frac{\beta}{\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F}}\right)W_{\Downarrow,\Uparrow}(\boldsymbol{k}_{3},\boldsymbol{k})f(\epsilon^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\bar{f}(\epsilon^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{3}}),

(61)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T_{0}}=\left(\frac{\beta}{\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}}-\frac{\beta}{\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F}}\right)W_{\Downarrow,\Uparrow}(\boldsymbol{k}_{3},\boldsymbol{k})f(\epsilon^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\bar{f}(\epsilon^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}_{3}}).

(62)

In the steady state (
\partial_{t}\delta N^{s}=0
), Eq. (
60
) yields a direct proportionality between the non-equilibrium spin density and charge density:

\displaystyle\delta N^{\|}=-\frac{T_{\|}}{T_{0}}\delta N^{0}.

(63)

This result signifies a mechanism for charge-to-spin conversion induced by the local moments, which is active whenever the spin-subband densities of states differ (
\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}\neq\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F}
), such as in a finite external magnetic field. In the following section, we will derive explicit expressions for the longitudinal and transverse spin relaxation times from the general result in Eq. (
61
).

III.2.1
Longitudinal spin relaxation time

We now evaluate the longitudinal spin relaxation time, which corresponds to the case where the quantization axis is parallel to the external magnetic field [
\theta=0
in Eq. (
39
)]. In this configuration, the rotated ladder operators for the local moments simplify to:

\displaystyle L^{\imath}_{\mathbf{+}}

\displaystyle=e^{+i\phi}\left[\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{+}+\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}-\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{-}\right],

(64)

\displaystyle L^{\imath}_{\mathbf{-}}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\phi}\left[\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}-\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{+}+\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{-}\right].

(65)

A crucial consequence of anisotropic spin-exchange coupling (
\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}\neq\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}
) is the breaking of spin conservation. The
\sigma_{+}L^{\imath}_{\mathbf{-}}
term, for instance, demonstrates that a spin-flip of the free electron (
\sigma_{+}
) can now be accompanied by a spin-flip of the local moment in the same direction (
S^{\imath}_{+}
), a process forbidden in the isotropic case. Substituting Eqs. (
64
) and (
65
) into the scattering rate (
46
) yields:

\displaystyle W_{\Downarrow,\Uparrow}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{8\hbar^{2}V^{2}}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}(\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})^{2}\left\langle S^{\imath}_{-}S^{\imath}_{+}\right\rangle(\omega^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\omega^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{L}^{\imath})

(66)

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{8\hbar^{2}V^{2}}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}(\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}-\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})^{2}\left\langle S^{\imath}_{+}S^{\imath}_{-}\right\rangle(\omega^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\omega^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{L}^{\imath}).

The first and second terms correspond to spin-conserving and non-conserving scattering processes, respectively. The spin-spin correlation functions governing these flip processes are determined by the parallel spin components:

\displaystyle\left\langle S^{\imath}_{-}S^{\imath}_{+}\right\rangle(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left[S^{\imath}(S^{\imath}+1)-\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle-\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle\right]\delta(\omega-\omega^{\imath}_{L}),

(67)

\displaystyle\langle S^{\imath}_{+}S^{\imath}_{-}\rangle(\omega)

\displaystyle=\left[S^{\imath}(S^{\imath}+1)-\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle+\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle\right]\delta(\omega+\omega^{\imath}_{L}),

(68)

where we have used the identity

\displaystyle S^{\imath}_{x}S^{\imath}_{x}+S^{\imath}_{y}S^{\imath}_{y}+S^{\imath}_{z}S^{\imath}_{z}=S^{\imath}(S^{\imath}+1).

(69)

The delta functions enforce energy conservation during the scattering. Within the Weiss mean-field framework, the final expression for the longitudinal spin relaxation rate is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{\parallel}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4\hbar}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\nu_{F}(\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})\beta\epsilon_{L}^{\imath}n_{B}\left(\epsilon^{\imath}_{L}\right)[S^{\imath}(S^{\imath}+1)-\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle-\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle]

(70)

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{4\hbar}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\nu_{F}(\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}-\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})^{2}\beta\epsilon_{L}^{\imath}[1+n_{B}(\epsilon_{L}^{\imath})][S^{\imath}(S^{\imath}+1)-\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle+\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle],

where the effective density of states is

\displaystyle\nu_{F}=\left(\frac{1}{2\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}}+\frac{1}{2\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F}}\right)\nu^{\Uparrow}_{F}\nu^{\Downarrow}_{F},

(71)

Here,
n_{B}(\epsilon^{\imath}_{L})=1/(e^{\beta\epsilon^{\imath}_{L}}-1)
is the Bose-Einstein distribution function. The appearance of
n_{B}
and
1+n_{B}
, derived microscopically from the many-body density matrix, reflects the bosonic nature of the local moments treated as a quantum bath. It is noteworthy that only spin-flip processes contribute to the longitudinal spin relaxation.

III.2.2
Transverse spin relaxation time

We now evaluate the transverse spin relaxation time, which corresponds to the quantization axis lying perpendicular to the external magnetic field [
\theta=\pi/2
in Eq. (
39
)]. In this configuration, the rotated ladder operators for the local moments are given by

\displaystyle L^{\imath}_{\mathbf{+}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{z}S^{\imath}_{z}+i\left[\frac{\sin\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}-i\cos\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{+}\right.

(72)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\sin\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+i\cos\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{-}\right],

\displaystyle L^{\imath}_{\mathbf{-}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{z}S^{\imath}_{z}-i\left[\frac{\sin\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}-i\cos\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{+}\right.

(73)

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{\sin\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+i\cos\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}}{2}S^{\imath}_{-}\right].

A key distinction from the longitudinal case (Eqs. (
64
) and (
65
)) is the presence of a spin-conserving term proportional to
S^{\imath}_{z}
. This term will contribute to spin dephasing. Substituting Eqs. (
72
) and (
73
) into the scattering rate (
46
) yields three distinct physical contributions:

\displaystyle W_{\Downarrow,\Uparrow}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{8\hbar^{2}V^{2}}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}(\sin^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\cos^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})\left\langle S^{\imath}_{-}S^{\imath}_{+}\right\rangle(\omega^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\omega^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-\omega_{L}^{\imath})

(74)

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{8\hbar^{2}V^{2}}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}(\sin^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\cos^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})\left\langle S^{\imath}_{+}S^{\imath}_{-}\right\rangle(\omega^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\omega^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}+\omega_{L}^{\imath})

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{2\hbar^{2}V^{2}}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{z}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{z}\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle(\omega^{\Uparrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\omega^{\Downarrow}_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}).

The first two terms represent spin-flip processes, while the third term represents a spin-conserving, dephasing process. The final expression for the transverse spin relaxation rate within the Weiss mean-field framework is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{\perp}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{4\hbar}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\nu_{F}(\sin^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\cos^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})\beta\epsilon_{L}^{\imath}n_{B}(\epsilon_{L}^{\imath})\left\langle S^{\imath}_{-}S^{\imath}_{+}\right\rangle

(75)

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{4\hbar}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\nu_{F}(\sin^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{x}+\cos^{2}\phi\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{y})\beta\epsilon_{L}^{\imath}[1+n_{B}(\epsilon_{L}^{\imath})]\left\langle S^{\imath}_{+}S^{\imath}_{-}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{\pi}{\hbar}n_{\text{S}}^{\imath}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{z}\mathcal{J}_{\imath}^{z}\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle.

In contrast to the longitudinal case, the transverse relaxation is governed by both spin-flip processes (the first two terms) and spin-conserving dephasing processes (the third term). The latter, which persists even in the absence of spin-flip scattering, is responsible for the pure dephasing of transverse spin components.

In summary, the analytical results presented in this work rely on several key approximations. First, to isolate the relaxation mechanisms stemming purely from the spin-exchange coupling, we have assumed a spin-independent density of states at the Fermi energy. This excludes contributions from spin-resolved band structure effects. Second, we have decoupled the spin-exchange coupling from spin-orbit coupling to specifically calculate the spin relaxation time arising from the local moments. Third, we disregard the feedback effect of the itinerant electrons on the thermodynamic equilibrium of the local moment system. The expressions for the spin relaxation times in Eqs. (
70
) and (
75
) are derived within the combined framework of the Born-Markov and Weiss mean-field approximations, yielding a compact and analytical form. Finally, we note that the spin expectation values and spin-spin correlation functions that enter these expressions are intrinsically dependent on the magnetic configuration of the local moments.

IV
Anisotropic spin diffusion equation

With the longitudinal and transverse spin relaxation times established in Eqs. (
70
) and (
75
), we can now construct the spin density continuity equation in the rotated basis. This equation describes the evolution of the spin chemical potential vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s}=(\mu^{\textbf{x}}_{s},\mu^{\textbf{y}}_{s},\mu^{\textbf{z}}_{s})

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{\textbf{x}}_{s}\\
\mu^{\textbf{y}}_{s}\\
\mu^{\textbf{z}}_{s}\end{bmatrix}+\nabla\cdot\begin{bmatrix}j^{\textbf{x}}_{s}\\
j^{\textbf{y}}_{s}\\
j^{\textbf{z}}_{s}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\tau^{-1}_{\perp}&0&-\omega_{L}\\
0&\tau^{-1}_{\perp}&+\omega_{L}\\
+\omega_{L}&-\omega_{L}&\tau^{-1}_{\|}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{\textbf{x}}_{s}\\
\mu^{\textbf{y}}_{s}\\
\mu^{\textbf{z}}_{s}\end{bmatrix}.

(76)

The spin current is governed by Fick’s law as follows

\displaystyle j^{\nu}_{s}=-\mathcal{D}\nabla\mu^{\nu}_{s},

(77)

where the diffusion coefficient
\mathcal{D}
is assumed to be isotropic for simplicity. As indicated by Eq. (
19
), the first-order correction from the spin-exchange coupling renormalizes the Hanle spin precession frequency. This introduces an effective spin-exchange field, shifting the frequency to

\displaystyle\omega_{L}=\omega_{B}-\frac{1}{\hbar}\sum_{\imath}n^{\imath}_{\mathrm{S}}\mathcal{J}^{\imath}_{sd}\langle S^{\imath}_{\|}\rangle.

(78)

To express the equations in the original laboratory frame, we apply the inverse of the rotation matrix
R
defined in Eq. (
39
). The spin chemical potential in the rotated basis relates to the original components via

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{\textbf{x}}_{s}\\
\mu^{\textbf{y}}_{s}\\
\mu^{\textbf{z}}_{s}\end{bmatrix}=R\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{x}_{s}\\
\mu^{y}_{s}\\
\mu^{z}_{s}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}*&*&*\\
&*&*\\
\hat{b}_{x}&\hat{b}_{y}&\hat{b}_{z}\end{array}\right]\begin{bmatrix}\mu^{x}_{s}\\
\mu^{y}_{s}\\
\mu^{z}_{s}\end{bmatrix},

where the third row of
R
is the unit vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}
, referring to the quantization axis, i.e.,
\hat{\boldsymbol{b}}=[\mathtt{-}\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta]
. Transforming the continuity equation back to the lab frame and considering the steady state (
d/dt=0
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot j^{\nu}_{s}=-\tau^{-1}_{\perp}\mu^{\nu}_{s}-(\tau^{-1}_{\|}-\tau^{-1}_{\perp})\hat{b}_{\nu}\hat{b}_{\kappa}\mu^{\delta}_{s}-\omega_{L}\epsilon_{\nu\kappa\delta}\mu^{\delta}_{s},

(82)

where
\nu,\kappa,\delta={x,y,z}
and
\epsilon_{\nu\kappa\delta}
is the Levi-Civita symbol. We now consider a mesoscopic magnetic material that is translationally invariant in the
\hat{x}
-
\hat{y}
plane, such that the spin accumulation
\mu_{s}^{\nu}
depends only on the coordinate
z
. Substituting the spin current expression (
77
) into Eq. (
82
) yields a diffusion equation with anisotropic spin relaxation

\partial_{z}^{2}\mu_{\mathrm{s}}^{\nu}=\ell_{\mathrm{\bot}}^{-2}\delta_{\nu\kappa}\mu_{\mathrm{s}}^{\kappa}+\left(\ell_{\mathrm{\parallel}}^{-2}-\ell_{\mathrm{\bot}}^{-2}\right)\hat{b}_{\nu}\hat{b}_{\kappa}\mu_{\mathrm{s}}^{\kappa}-\ell_{\mathrm{L}}^{-2}\epsilon_{\nu\kappa\delta}\hat{b}_{\delta}\mu_{\mathrm{s}}^{\delta},

(83)

The Hanle spin precession length is given by
\ell_{\mathrm{L}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}/\omega_{L}}
, while the longitudinal and transverse spin diffusion lengths are expressed as
\ell_{\mathrm{\bot}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\tau_{\mathrm{\bot}}}
and
\ell_{\mathrm{\parallel}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{D}\tau_{\mathrm{\parallel}}}
, respectively.

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}[U^{o}_{\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}-iU^{s}_{\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{k}\times\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})]

V.2
Antiferromagnetic MR

VI
Summary

We have developed a comprehensive microscopic theory of MR from an open-quantum-system perspective. In particular, we solve the Liouville–von Neumann equation for a hybrid system of itinerant electrons and local moments using the time-convolutionless projection-operator technique. Both ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic MR are described in terms of temperature- and field-dependent spin decoherence processes (spin relaxation and spin dephasing), where the resistance varies with the magnetization and the Néel vector, respectively. Our theory provides a deeper understanding of the fundamental mechanisms underlying MR and offers insights relevant to experiments on magnetic materials. Furthermore, the framework can be readily extended to a broader class of magnetic systems, including ferrimagnetic and antiferromagnetic metals and semiconductors, as well as normal metals decorated with magnetic impurities (see Appendix
C
), and furthermore our quantum spin decoherence theory can be applied to macroscopic magnetic materials

;
Feng et al. shed
