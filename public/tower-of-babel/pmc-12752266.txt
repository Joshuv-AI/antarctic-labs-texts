Subphenotype heterogeneity to guide predictive enrichment in acute kidney injury: insights from machine learning and target trial emulation
Authors: Jiayang Li, Mingyi Zhao, Qingnan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) exhibits substantial heterogeneity in clinical presentation. Previous studies used traditional clustering approaches to identify subphenotypes, often focusing on adverse clinical outcomes, while ignoring that the central goal of subphenotyping is to enable individualized care.ObjectiveIn this study, we aimed to identify distinct AKI subphenotypes and evaluate their heterogeneous associations with vasopressor choice and renal replacement therapy (RRT) strategies.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database and included 31,030 patients diagnosed with AKI within 48 h of admission. Generative topographic mapping, a probabilistic unsupervised machine-learning model, was used to identify clusters. We then applied a target trial emulation framework to emulate comparisons of norepinephrine versus vasopressin, RRT modalities, and continuous RRT initiation timing across subphenotypes.ResultsFour distinct subphenotypes were identified, with marked differences in clinical features, laboratory abnormalities, and outcomes: a large group with intermediate severity; a hyper-inflammatory subphenotype with marked liver dysfunction and severe AKI; a cardiorenal congestion subphenotype with high cardiovascular comorbidity; and a younger subphenotype enriched for postoperative or trauma patients. Vasopressin was associated with reduced mortality in Subphenotype 2. A more delayed continuous RRT initiation strategy was linked to lower 60-day mortality in Subphenotypes 1 and 2. These associations remained robust in sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsWe identified four clinically distinct AKI subphenotypes that demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in their mortality associations with vasopressor use and RRT strategies. These findings could improve prognostication and advance precision medicine in critical care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13040-025-00514-0.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a prevalent and significant syndrome in critically ill patients, affecting approximately 30% to 50% of those admitted to intensive care units and contributing to substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1–4]. Despite standardized staging by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria based on serum creatinine and urine output [5], AKI remains a clinically and biologically heterogeneous syndrome with diverse pathophysiologic cascades and therapeutic needs [6].

Consequently, there is growing interest in identifying AKI subphenotypes (patient subsets with shared clinical features, biomarker profiles, and outcomes) to enable better risk stratification and targeted interventions. Over the past decade, unsupervised machine-learning approaches have delineated such subphenotypes with distinct clinical characteristics and trajectories in AKI and critical care nephrology [7–9]. However, most existing subphenotyping pipelines rely on traditional clustering algorithms that are sensitive to initialization, make strong distributional assumptions and do not quantify uncertainty in cluster membership, which limits their robustness and bedside interpretability [10]. In addition, the majority of prior work has focused on prognostic enrichment, i.e. forecasting clinical outcomes within subgroups, with limited emphasis on predictive enrichment, namely identifying which patients are more or less likely to benefit from specific therapies [11, 12].

From a treatment perspective, three decisions are particularly consequential and time-sensitive in AKI care: the choice of vasopressor, the initial renal replacement therapy (RRT) modality (intermittent hemodialysis [IHD] vs. continuous RRT [CRRT]), and the timing of CRRT initiation. Norepinephrine and vasopressin differ in their physiologic mechanisms and hemodynamic profiles, raising the possibility of phenotype-dependent vasopressor responsiveness [13]. Similarly, both the comparative effectiveness of IHD versus CRRT and the optimal timing of CRRT initiation remain debated, with divergent findings across trials and practice patterns [14, 15]. To move beyond prognostic labeling toward predictive enrichment, we integrate subphenotyping with a causal-inference framework, target trial emulation (TTE), to estimate treatment effect heterogeneity. This design links unsupervised subphenotyping to actionable bedside guidance and targeted trial-enrichment strategies grounded in real-world data.

In this study, we aimed to (i) derive and validate AKI subphenotypes in critically ill patients using generative topographic mapping (GTM) and (ii) evaluate heterogeneity of treatment effects across these subphenotypes for vasopressor selection, RRT modality and the timing of CRRT initiation. To our knowledge, this is the first study to embed TTE within machine learning–derived subphenotypes to explicitly connect subphenotyping with precision therapy in ICU AKI.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted an retrospective cohort study using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, which contains de-identified ICU admissions from a tertiary academic center (2008–2019). A schematic of the analysis pipeline is provided as Fig. 1. The author Li has completed the Collaborative Institutional Training Program (CITP) exam (certification number: 13808420). Since the study was an analysis of a third-party anonymized publicly available database with pre-existing institutional review board (IRB) approval, IRB approval from our institution was exempted.

Study population and eligibility

Adults (≥ 18 years) with AKI within 48 h after admission were enrolled. Exclusions were: age > 89 years, ICU length of stay ≤ 24 h, end-stage kidney disease, or missing key baseline data precluding AKI ascertainment. For patients with multiple admissions, only the first ICU stay was included.

Definitions

AKI was defined by KDIGO criteria, based on serum creatinine (increase ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h, or ≥ 1.5× baseline within 7 days) and urine output (< 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 6 h) [16]. We then restricted the cohort to patients whose first KDIGO-defined AKI episode occurred within the first 48 h after ICU admission.

Exposures for emulation: (i) Vasopressor selection (norepinephrine vs. vasopressin as the first qualifying vasopressor strategy); (ii) IHD vs. CRRT; (iii) CRRT initiation timing (≤ 65 h vs. > 65 h from ICU admission) was determined based on the median time to initiation within the cohort.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes were mortality at 30, 60, and 100 days after ICU admission, final AKI stage during hospitalization, and length of ICU and hospital stay.

Variables

A total of 36 variables derived from early vital sign trajectories (e.g., temperature, heart rate) and laboratory test results (e.g., glucose, albumin) were extracted from the first 48 h of ICU admission for model development. These variables were chosen because they reflect early physiological status and are commonly available across ICU patients. Static demographic variables were excluded at this stage to ensure that clustering was driven primarily by acute biological signatures rather than baseline characteristics. For the TTE analyses, we incorporated an additional 18 variables that were not used in subphenotype construction. Instead, they served as covariates for confounding adjustment and to facilitate interpretation of subphenotype features. These included demographic data (e.g., sex, height, weight), severity scores such as the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), as well as comorbidities, ventilation status, and culture results.

Data preprocessing

First, the extracted dataset (a total of 59 variables) was aggregated by computing the mean for each feature. After excluding variables with more than 50% missing values (Figure S1), we standardized the remaining data using Z-score normalization to eliminate differences in measurement scales across features. In the primary analysis, this approach allowed us to retain a relatively broad set of physiologic and laboratory variables. However, because some of these variables may be measured selectively in sicker patients and thus exhibit informative patterns of missingness, we additionally performed a prespecified sensitivity analysis restricting the clustering feature set to variables with less than 30% missingness (see Additional and sensitivity analyses). To mitigate multicollinearity, we calculated pairwise Spearman rank correlation coefficients and removed six variables with correlation coefficients greater than 0.7 (Figure S2). For the other variables, multiple imputation was performed using classification and regression trees (CART). A total of 20 imputed datasets were generated, and the averaged dataset was used for subsequent analyses. Before further analysis, the dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets at an 8:2 ratio.

Clustering

As detailed elsewhere [10, 17], we applied GTM, a probabilistic latent variable model, to derive AKI subphenotypes. GTM constructs a low-dimensional latent space, typically a two-dimensional grid, and maps each point into the high-dimensional data space through a nonlinear function such as radial basis functions. These mapped points, called reference vectors, define the means of a constrained Gaussian mixture model. Model parameters are optimized using the expectation-maximization algorithm to approximate the observed data. Posterior distributions over the latent space are then used for clustering, such as hierarchical clustering, to identify distinct subphenotypes.

Unlike dimensionality reduction techniques such as Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), which maintain local and global structures without modeling distributions, GTM offers a probabilistic framework that allows for soft data assignments and explicit uncertainty modeling. This enhances robustness against noise and facilitates interpretable patient stratification based on posterior probabilities.

The optimal configuration was found to be an 18 × 18 latent grid, 17 × 17 RBFs, and a regularization coefficient of 1. This configuration was subsequently used to train the final GTM models on both the training and testing datasets. To obtain clinically meaningful AKI subphenotypes, we then performed agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Ward’s minimum variance method on the GTM reference vectors and selected a four-cluster solution based on inspection of the dendrogram and clinical interpretability of the resulting groups. Further details of GTM implementation and cluster selection are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Section S1: Clustering).

Predictive enrichment

To estimate the effects of alternative treatment strategies, each eligible participant was duplicated and assigned to both strategies. During follow-up, individuals whose observed treatment deviated from their assigned strategy were censored. To adjust for potential selection bias, we applied inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and truncated extreme weights at the 99th percentile to minimize their influence. Covariate balance was assessed using love plots, with standardized differences below 0.1 indicating adequate balance. Further details of each emulated target trial are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Section S2: Predictive Enrichment).

Statistical analysis

We presented continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and categorical variables as counts with percentages. The Kruskal–Wallis H test and the Pearson’s chi-squared test were used as appropriate to assess group differences. Kaplan–Meier analysis was conducted to estimate in-hospital mortality across subphenotypes, and differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. In the TTE analysis, we applied weighted Cox proportional hazards models and constructed weighted survival curves, reporting adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Results were considered statistically significant if the 95% CI for the hazard ratio did not include 1. All p-values were two-sided, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Additional and sensitivity analyses

Several additional and sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of our results. (i) We first repeated all primary analyses in the testing cohort and performed an exploratory cross-algorithm comparison using UMAP and k-means to quantify concordance with the GTM-derived subphenotypes. (ii) In exploratory, we examined simple process-of-care proxies for components of the KDIGO AKI bundle, including exposure to clearly nephrotoxic antibiotics and the number of serum creatinine measurements. (iii) We then restricted the clustering feature set to variables with less than 30% missingness, which led to the exclusion of nine variables (ALP, ALT, albumin, basophils, eosinophils, monocytes, total bilirubin, osmolarity, and LDH). (iv) For Trial A (vasopressin vs. norepinephrine), we repeated the vasopressor trial after excluding all patients whose first AKI episode occurred before ICU admission. (v) For Trial B (IHD vs. CRRT), we repeated the analysis while censoring follow-up at the time of any switch in modality between CRRT and IHD. (vi) For Trial C (delayed vs. more-delayed CRRT initiation), we evaluated alternative, clinically plausible thresholds of 48 and 72 h for defining the delayed and more-delayed strategies.

Software

Data extraction and preprocessing were performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) on Google Cloud BigQuery (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) and Navicat Premium 17 (PremiumSoft CyberTech Ltd., Hong Kong, China). Statistical analyses were conducted in R, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python, version 3.9 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). In R, we used the packages lubridate, data.table, tidyverse and tableone for data integration, exploration and summary, and mice for multiple imputation. Survival analyses were performed using the survival and survminer packages. GTM-based clustering was implemented using an open-source GTM library (Zenodo DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.12207621), and TTE was conducted using publicly available code adapted from Marti et al. (https://github.com/rmartigas/causal-inference-invasive-ventilation-MIMIC-IV). Python analyses were run in PyCharm 2020.1 (JetBrains s.r.o., Prague, Czech Republic).

Results

Characteristics of the study cohort

We identified 31,030 critically ill patients with AKI from the MIMIC-IV database. Among them, 24,824 patients were assigned to the derivation cohort, and 6,206 patients to the testing cohort for validation. The median age of the patients was 66 years (IQR [55–75]), and 58% (14,418) were male. The median SOFA score was 4 (IQR [2–7]). Approximately 84% (20,837) of the patients received mechanical ventilation, and 40% (9,883) had a positive blood or urine culture (Tables 1 and 2, Supplementary Table S1).

GTM to identify subphenotypes

Figure 2 illustrates the results of unsupervised clustering using GTM. A light gray-blue-green color scale was used to visualize the reference vectors. In the latent space (Fig. 2A), greener areas indicate higher variable values among participants in those clusters. We applied hierarchical clustering to define four distinct AKI subphenotypes, as shown in the dendrogram (Fig. 2B). The distribution of macroclusters (i.e., subphenotypes) is depicted in Fig. 2C, where the size of each microcluster reflects its population density. Lastly, for an individual patient randomly selected from the cohort, their cluster membership was determined based on the posterior probability distribution over the latent space (Fig. 2D).

Comparative characteristics across subphenotypes

The four identified AKI subphenotypes exhibited distinct clinical profiles (Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 3). Subphenotype 1 included the largest number of patients, with the highest proportion of males (87%) and a relatively high burden of malignancy (17%). Subphenotype 2 comprised the most severely ill patients, characterized by the highest blood urea nitrogen, lowest urine output, and signs suggestive of multi-organ dysfunction. These patients showed hemodynamic instability (lowest mean arterial pressure), metabolic acidosis (highest anion gap and lowest bicarbonate), and had the lowest albumin levels (2.79 [2.40–3.16]). They also exhibited markedly elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase (403.60 [276.67–734.00]) and lactate (2.40 [1.76–4.00]), along with prolonged prothrombin time (PT, 18.38 [14.90–24.53]) and activated partial thromboplastin time (PTT, 41.66 [33.10–56.76]), suggesting potential liver dysfunction. Notably, this group had the highest proportion of patients with elevated SOFA, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III (APS III), and Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) scores, indicating a greater overall disease burden. Subphenotype 3 included the oldest patients (72.00 [63.00–80.00]), with a predominance of females (94%) and higher rates of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cardiovascular comorbidities (73%). In contrast, Subphenotype 4 represented the youngest group, with the lowest proportion of patients receiving mechanical ventilation (74%) and the lowest rate of documented infections (32%).

The chord and Sankey diagrams (Fig. 3C, D) further illustrated the distinguishing features across subphenotypes. Subphenotype 2 was strongly associated with electrolyte disturbances (e.g., bicarbonate, anion gap, chloride, pH, phosphate), metabolic derangements (e.g., lactate dehydrogenase and glucose), and coagulation abnormalities (e.g., PT, PTT), supporting its classification as the most critically ill group. Subphenotype 3 was primarily driven by kidney function indicators such as serum creatinine. In contrast, Subphenotype 4 was characterized by high contributions from vital signs (e.g., systolic and diastolic blood pressure) and blood cell parameters (e.g., red blood cell count, mean corpuscular volume).

Validation of AKI subphenotypes

To evaluate the stability of the AKI subphenotypes derived from the training cohort, we applied the same GTM model to the testing group and obtained four clusters with similar characteristics (Supplementary Figure S4). We further performed an exploratory cross-algorithm comparison using UMAP and k-means, and found substantial concordance, with an adjusted Rand index (ARI) of 0.67 and normalized mutual information (NMI) of 0.61 (Supplementary Figure S5).

Clinical outcomes and recovery trajectories of AKI subphenotypes

At AKI onset, most patients across all subphenotypes were initially classified as stage 1. Over the subsequent ICU course, Subphenotype 2 demonstrated the greatest progression in AKI severity, with more than half of the patients (2,094 [53.0%]) reaching stage 3 as their maximum stage, whereas stage 2 was more prevalent as the peak stage in Subphenotypes 1, 3 and 4. Following treatment, more than 75% of patients in Subphenotypes 1, 3, and 4 recovered to stage 0, indicating resolution of renal injury. However, in Subphenotype 2, only 56.7% of patients recovered to stage 0, and 20.3% remained at stage 3, suggesting persistent renal dysfunction. These progression and recovery trajectories are illustrated in Fig. 3, with the full cross-tabulation provided in Supplementary Tables S2, S3.

In the derivation cohort, the clinical outcomes varied significantly across AKI subphenotypes. Subphenotype 2 had the longest median hospital stay, while Subphenotype 4 had the shortest (13.12 days vs. 8.31 days, P < 0.001, Supplementary Tables S4). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to estimate the cumulative risk of mortality within 100 days following AKI diagnosis. The survival curves showed that Subphenotype 2 had the lowest survival probability, whereas Subphenotype 4 had the highest (P < 0.001). Subphenotypes 1 and 3 had similar mortality trajectories (Supplementary Figure S6). Comparable patterns were observed in the testing cohort, supporting the stability of these findings (Supplementary Tables S4; Figure S6).

Treatment effect heterogeneity by AKI subphenotype

In the derivation cohort, IPTW was applied to adjust for confounding, and love plots showed that covariates were well balanced between groups before and after censoring. (Supplementary Figure S7). Among patients in Subphenotypes 2, 3, and 4, vasopressin use was associated with improved 100-day survival compared to norepinephrine, with aHRs of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.78–0.93), 0.77 (95% CI, 0.62–0.96), and 0.57 (95% CI, 0.46–0.70), respectively. In contrast, no significant difference was observed in Subphenotype 1 (aHR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.80–1.15) (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S6). These findings were supported in the testing cohort, where Subphenotype 2 showed a survival benefit associated with vasopressin (aHR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.65–0.98). Although hazard ratios for Subphenotypes 3 and 4 were directionally similar, they did not reach statistical significance in the testing set (Supplementary Table S9).

Among RRT recipients, 165, 686, 80, and 98 patients in subphenotypes 1–4 received RRT, respectively. The median time from ICU admission to RRT initiation was 65.5 h for Subphenotype 1, 56.4 h for Subphenotype 2, 87.5 h for Subphenotype 3, and 68.8 h for Subphenotype 4, with a modest but statistically significant difference across subphenotypes (Kruskal–Wallis χ² = 9.08, P = 0.028). We next used our TTE framework to compare 100-day survival between patients receiving IHD and CRRT. In the emulated trial, use of IHD was consistently associated with better adjusted 100-day survival compared with CRRT across all subphenotypes, suggesting that the survival benefit of dialysis modality did not vary by subphenotype (Supplementary Table S7).

We further examined the effect of initiation timing among patients who received CRRT. IPTW was applied, and any residual imbalanced covariates were included in the weighted Cox models. In Subphenotypes 1 and 2, a more delayed initiation of CRRT was associated with improved 60-day survival compared to early initiation, with adjusted hazard ratios of 0.47 (95% CI, 0.3–0.69) and 0.74 (95% CI, 0.63–0.88), respectively. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in Subphenotypes 3 and 4 (aHR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.40–1.12] and 1.14 [95% CI, 0.67–1.96], respectively) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table S8). These findings were consistent in the testing cohort (Supplementary Tables S9).

Sensitivity and additional analyses

Nephrotoxic antibiotic use differed markedly across subphenotypes (20.7%, 43.3%, 18.5% and 16.1% in Subphenotypes 1–4, respectively; global χ² test p < 0.001), with the hyper-inflammatory Subphenotype 2 receiving nephrotoxic agents most frequently. Likewise, the number of creatinine measurements within 48 h varied significantly (median [IQR] 3 [2–4], 4 [3–7], 2 [2–4] and 2 [2–4] in Subphenotypes 1–4; Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001), suggesting that more severe subphenotypes underwent more intensive biochemical monitoring (Supplementary Table S5).

After excluding variables with more than 30% missingness, the results remained consistent with our main findings (Supplementary Figure S8). Results were also similar after excluding participants whose first AKI episode occurred before ICU admission (Supplementary Table S10, Figure S9). Sensitivity analyses censoring follow-up at the time of any switch in modality between CRRT and IHD likewise yielded estimates that were consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S11). When defining delayed versus more-delayed CRRT initiation using alternative thresholds of 48–72 h, the direction and magnitude of the estimated effects remained broadly unchanged: with a 48-hour cutoff, the hazard ratio for more-delayed versus delayed CRRT in Subphenotype 2 was 0.86 (95% CI 0.72–1.01; P = 0.07), suggesting a similar magnitude of benefit but with slightly wider confidence intervals, and in Subphenotype 1 the point estimate again favoured more-delayed initiation without reaching statistical significance. With a 72-hour cutoff, the qualitative pattern of heterogeneous effects across subphenotypes was unchanged (Supplementary Table S12, Figure S10). These analyses support the robustness of our main findings.

Discussion

In this study, we applied an unsupervised machine learning approach to derive and validate AKI subphenotypes in critically ill patients. The four resulting subphenotypes exhibited distinct clinical features, laboratory profiles, and prognostic trajectories. Importantly, they showed differential associations with key therapeutic strategies, including vasopressor selection and renal replacement therapy timing, and these patterns were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses. Our findings highlight the potential of data-driven subphenotyping to enhance understanding of AKI heterogeneity and to inform more precise, individualized management strategies.

Compared with traditional clustering algorithms, GTM offers a probabilistic framework that captures local data structures through micro-clusters and aggregates them into macro-clusters via posterior distribution-based hierarchical clustering. This enhances robustness to uncertainty and noise in clinical data. The non-linear dimensionality reduction inherent in GTM also reduces reliance on strong model assumptions, improving flexibility and generalizability. Importantly, GTM provides intuitive visualization tools such as reference vector maps and responsibility plots that improve model interpretability. Because the clustering variables were aggregated over the first 48 h of ICU admission, the four subphenotypes are best interpreted as early ICU trajectory subphenotypes that reflect both initial organ dysfunction and the short-term impact of early management, rather than purely pre-treatment baseline profiles.

Crucially, our analysis did not stop at subphenotypic classification. We further employed a TTE framework to assess specific treatment associations across subphenotypes [18]. This combination of subphenotype identification and treatment effect evaluation enables both prognostic and predictive enrichment, advancing precision medicine in AKI management.

Notably, among all AKI subphenotypes, Subphenotype 1 comprised the largest number of patients, with a high proportion of males and a notable burden of comorbidities, including coronary artery disease, malignancy, and other common medical conditions.

Clinical and laboratory findings were intermediate compared to the other subphenotypes, indicating a moderate disease severity. Most patients progressed at most to KDIGO stage 2 and more than three-quarters recovered to stage 0 before discharge, suggesting that kidney injury in this group was often transient and reversible. Clinically, this cluster resembles a heterogeneous group of general medical ICU admissions, where AKI likely develops in the setting of mild physiological stress rather than severe systemic dysfunction.

Subphenotype 2 demonstrated a distinctly hyper-inflammatory and coagulopathic profile, which appears to be the dominant biological signal of this group. Patients exhibited marked neutrophilia, profoundly elevated lactate, a high anion gap, low bicarbonate, and prolonged coagulation times, indicating microcirculatory impairment and a systemic inflammatory response. Similar hyper-inflammatory patterns have been extensively reported in critical care research, including studies in sepsis, ARDS, and heterogeneous ICU cohorts, where this profile consistently corresponds to the highest illness severity, rapid clinical deterioration, and poor outcomes [19, 20]. In our cohort, these patients indeed had the greatest organ dysfunction burden and the highest mortality risk, and they also showed the poorest kidney recovery: more than half progressed to KDIGO stage 3 as the maximum stage, and only around half subsequently recovered to stage 0, with a sizeable minority remaining in stage 3 at discharge. Within this broader inflammatory state, we also observed cholestatic and hepatocellular abnormalities. However, these findings likely represent secondary consequences of systemic inflammatory and circulatory dysfunction, rather than a primary hepatic process. Prior studies have shown that inflammatory-driven hypoperfusion and microcirculatory derangement can induce transient liver injury and worsen kidney function, mimicking components of a hepatorenal-like physiology [21]. This hepatic involvement may partially contribute to the observed greater benefit of vasopressin over norepinephrine in this group, as vasopressin is known to improve splanchnic perfusion in settings of severe inflammation and circulatory collapse [22–24]. Consistent with this interpretation, the diagnostic spectrum of this subphenotype was dominated by sepsis, bloodstream infections, alcoholic cirrhosis, and other multi-organ failure syndromes, reinforcing that systemic inflammation is the defining feature.

Patients in Subphenotype 3 exhibited a cardiorenal–congestive profile. They were predominantly older females with a high prevalence of heart failure, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, and cerebrovascular disease. Laboratory profiles demonstrated impaired renal function, elevated serum creatinine, and metabolic markers consistent with venous congestion and reduced renal perfusion. This pattern mirrors classical cardiorenal syndrome [25], supported by the high burden of cardiovascular diagnoses and the presence of respiratory failure in many patients. Although these patients frequently reached higher AKI stages, the majority subsequently recovered to KDIGO stage 0, suggesting that congestion-driven AKI in this group retains substantial potential for renal recovery once hemodynamics are optimised and volume status is corrected. The constellation of findings indicates that AKI in this group likely arises from combined cardiac dysfunction and renal susceptibility, rather than isolated renal injury.

In contrast, Subphenotype 4 comprised the youngest patients and represented a surgical/trauma-associated profile. Vital signs were more stable compared with other subphenotypes, and mechanical ventilation and infection were less common. Laboratory patterns showed contributions from variables such as systolic blood pressure, red blood cell count, and mean corpuscular volume, suggesting a physiological stress response. Most patients in this group progressed only to mild or moderate AKI and had the highest probability of recovery to KDIGO stage 0 before discharge, consistent with a relatively resilient renal subphenotype in which AKI often reflects transient haemodynamic or perioperative insults.

The hyper-inflammatory Subphenotype 2 received nephrotoxic agents most frequently and underwent more frequent creatinine testing. These patterns are consistent with KDIGO recommendations that high-risk patients receive closer monitoring and careful review of potentially nephrotoxic medications [16]. However, our proxies captured only a narrow subset of bundle components, and the retrospective nature of the data precluded robust reconstruction of a full KDIGO care bundle.

In the management of critical illness, vasoactive agents and RRT play essential roles in hemodynamic support, organ perfusion, and electrolyte balance. In our study, we observed remarkable differences in fluid and electrolyte profiles across AKI subphenotypes, suggesting potential heterogeneity in therapeutic responsiveness.

This observation aligns with findings by Bhatraju et al., who reported that patients with impaired renal function and a higher prevalence of sepsis benefited more from vasopressin than from norepinephrine [26]. Our Subphenotype 2 shares similar clinical characteristics with this group. Although large trials such as VASST and VANISH did not show a significant mortality benefit with vasopressin, both studies reported wide confidence intervals, and subgroup analyses revealed potential advantages in selected patients [27, 28]. Russell and his colleagues also highlighted this point [29]. Therefore, additional post hoc analyses (e.g., clustering) should be performed within the defined populations to fully uncover underlying associations between patient characteristics and treatment responses.

We further evaluated the impact of RRT modality and CRRT initiation timing across different AKI subphenotypes. Across all subphenotypes, the emulated trial suggested that IHD was associated with better adjusted survival than CRRT, and this relative benefit did not materially differ by subphenotype. In Subphenotypes 1 and 2, more delayed CRRT initiation was associated with lower 60-day mortality, consistent with prior findings suggesting that initiation based on clinical trajectory rather than fixed criteria may optimize treatment outcomes [30–32]. The reduced benefit of early CRRT in these subphenotypes may reflect increased vulnerability to treatment-related complications, such as hemodynamic instability or electrolyte disturbances. In contrast, Subphenotypes 3 and 4, characterized by milder biochemical abnormalities or more resilient baseline organ function, showed no significant prognostic differences based on the timing of CRRT initiation.

We did not attempt to emulate an analogous “early vs. delayed” strategy for IHD. In our centre, and in line with current practice, CRRT is the predominant initial modality for hemodynamically unstable ICU patients with severe AKI, whereas IHD is usually reserved for more stable patients or as a step-down therapy during recovery [33, 34]. Consequently, the timing of IHD initiation in this cohort largely reflects clinician judgement, hemodynamic stability, recovery trajectory and organisational factors, resulting in limited covariate overlap between putative “early” and “delayed” IHD groups. Under these conditions, an observational comparison of IHD timing would be highly susceptible to residual confounding and model extrapolation.

There are several limitations in this study. First, some variables used in the clustering step were measured in a minority of patients and are likely ordered selectively in sicker individuals, which may introduce bias and complicate the interpretation of the resulting subphenotypes. To address this concern, we excluded variables with more than 30% missingness to conduct a sensitivity analysis, and the findings were qualitatively unchanged. Moreover, this was a single-centre analysis and we only performed internal validation within MIMIC-IV, so external validation in independent cohorts will be required to establish generalisability. Nevertheless, the large sample size and the consistency of findings across multiple sensitivity analyses enhance confidence in the robustness of our results. Second, because of irregular and sparse sampling of ICU variables, we summarised covariates over the first 48 h after ICU admission instead of modelling high-frequency time-series trajectories. This choice improves robustness across heterogeneous variables but means that the subphenotypes partly incorporate the very early impact of treatment and are best regarded as early ICU trajectory profiles rather than strictly pre-treatment baseline states. Third, in the TTE, using ICU day-1 covariates as baseline implies that some measurements in a small subset of patients may be partially influenced by very early treatment. In routine clinical practice, however, patients admitted to the ICU typically undergo an initial assessment of illness severity and organ dysfunction before major therapeutic decisions are made, so ICU day-1 variables are likely to reflect pre-treatment status for most individuals. Our emulated trials should therefore be interpreted as providing clinically informative, hypothesis-generating estimates rather than definitive causal effects. Lastly, the timing of CRRT initiation was defined relative to the median value in each group, rather than based on explicit clinical triggers such as complications or creatinine thresholds, and Trial C was restricted to patients who eventually received CRRT. This restriction inevitably introduces selection on treatment initiation and limits generalisability to the broader population of critically ill patients with AKI.

Conclusion

In summary, we identified four data-driven AKI subphenotypes that differed in underlying pathophysiology and associated mortality risk. These subphenotypes may inform individualized treatment strategies, particularly regarding the use of vasopressin and the timing of renal replacement therapy. Our findings support a precision medicine approach to AKI management by helping clinicians align therapeutic interventions with distinct patient biology, rather than relying solely on broad clinical criteria.
