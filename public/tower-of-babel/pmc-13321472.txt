Outcomes of acute kidney injury patients with and without chronic kidney disease in intensive care units: a retrospective contrast analysis
Authors: Yan Liang, Ji Zhang, Tianhao Weng, Wenxian Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a common complication among patients in the intensive care unit (ICU), however, the impact of pre-existing CKD on patient who were admitted to the ICU and diagnosed with acute kidney injury (AKI) remains controversial.MethodsCritically ill patients (18–90 years old) with AKI were enrolled as study participants from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-III database. Patients with repeated ICU admissions and a length of stay less than 48 h were excluded. In-ICU mortality was considered the main endpoint, and multivariable Cox regression analyses were performed. Moreover, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to adjust potential interference factors, and the three-year survival rate was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsThis study included 20440 patients, divided into the pure AKI group (18441 patients) and the acute-on-chronic kidney disease (ACKD) group (1999 patients). Multivariable Cox regression analyses revealed lower in-ICU mortality in ACKD group than pure AKI group (7.0% versus 7.7%, hazard ratio (HR) 0.83, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.69–0.99, P = 0.047), as well as after PSM (7.0% versus 9.9%, HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.63–0.99, P = 0.049). Statistically significant differences persisted when patients were stratified by AKI stages. The results demonstrated that preexisting CKD was associated with reduced in-ICU mortality among AKI stage 3 patients, regardless of the classification criteria used. However, the Kaplan–Meier method indicated a lower three-year survival in the ACKD group (P = 0.017).ConclusionAmong AKI patients in ICU, the ACKD group had lower ICU mortality compared to pure AKI group but experienced worse long-term survival.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03532-y.

Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common syndrome characterized by the rapid deterioration of kidney function with an increase in serum creatinine and/or oliguria. It is an important complication in patients admitted to the hospital (10%–15%) of all hospitalizations and in patients in the intensive care unit (ICU), where its prevalence sometimes exceeds 50% [1]. Moreover, AKI worsens the short- and long-term outcomes [2, 3]. Evidence from past years suggests that ICU patients with AKI carry a higher risk of accentuated progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and tend to have a worse prognosis following hospital discharge [4, 5]. The risk of development, progression, and adverse outcomes associated with AKI is contingent on a myriad of additional factors, including patient baseline susceptibility (genetic profile, age, multimorbidity, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and kidney reserve), illness understanding, non-kidney organ dysfunction, and underlying features of acute kidney injury [6]. In recent years, several novel inflammatory biomarkers have been discovered, such as the red blood cell distribution width-to-albumin ratio (RAR), which has been independently associated with short-term mortality in critically ill patients with acute kidney injury (AKI). RAR may serve as a clinically accessible tool for early prognostic assessment, including subgroups such as chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients [7]. Further, preliminary evidence suggests certain interventions, including amino acid infusion, may modify AKI progression [8].

CKD is defined by KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) as structural or functional abnormalities of the kidney that have been present for at least three months and have health implications. It is classified based on the cause, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) category (G1–G5), and albuminuria category (A1–A3). The burden of CKD is significant, with estimates suggesting that 11%–13% of the global population has CKD, and patients with CKD are at high risk for developing critical illness [9]. Preexisting CKD is associated with various adverse outcomes, which is probably the most crucial factor defining the susceptibility of AKI to nephrotoxic exposure [2, 10, 11]. However, the effect of preexisting CKD on outcomes of ICU patients with AKI has been variably described. Therefore, it is necessary to further identify whether preexisting CKD affects the prognosis of AKI in the ICU, which may influence clinical treatment. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between pre-existing CKD and the prognosis of patients with AKI admitted to the ICU. Furthermore, we investigated whether the associations varied among different AKI stages according to the KDIGO, UO (urinary output), and serum creatinine (SCr) criteria.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective study was conducted based on a freely accessible critical care database, the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III), which comprised clinical data of critically ill patients hospitalized in the ICU at the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between June 1, 2001, and October 31, 2012. The MIMIC-III database project was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of BIDMC and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. This study was conducted using an anonymous public database; therefore, obtaining institutional review board approval and written patient consent was unnecessary.

Definitions

In this study, the presence of AKI was determined based on the following: an increase in serum creatinine (SCr) to ≥ 1.5 times baseline within one week or a ≥ 0.3 mg/dL increase in SCr within 48 h, or urine volume < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h or more. A minimum of the SCr values available within seven days before ICU admission was used as the baseline SCr. However, missing baseline serum creatinine values were common in ICU patients [12]. When pre-admission SCr was unavailable, the first SCr measured at ICU admission was used as the baseline SCr. AKI stages were defined according to the KDIGO, SCr, and UO criteria. Patients were considered to have CKD when they were diagnosed with CKD, hypertensive nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy, gouty nephropathy, nephrotic syndrome, or glomerulonephritis. We excluded patients with acute glomerulonephritis and pregnancy-related renal conditions (including abortion-associated kidney injury). Pure AKI was defined as patients who developed AKI but without preexisting CKD, and acute-on-chronic kidney disease (ACKD) was defined as AKI that developed in those with preexisting CKD. Critically ill patients (18–90 years old) with AKI were enrolled as study participants from MIMIC-III. Repeated ICU admission patients and those with a length of stay of < 48 h were excluded. Patients were excluded due to the absence of SCr level. Patients who had already undergone a chronic hemodialysis program and were diagnosed with ESRD were also excluded. Data extraction and endpoints

The following variables of study participants were extracted from MIMIC III using structured query language (SQL): age, gender, ethnicity, admission type, mean arterial pressure (MAP), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) score, comorbidities, length of ICU stay, SCr level, use of invasive mechanical ventilation and renal replacement therapy (RRT) on the first day, and use of diuretics and vasopressors. The following results encompass mortality at 30-day and 90-day post-ICU admission, along with in-ICU mortality (defined as deaths occurring during the intensive care unit stay). Among them, in-ICU mortality was selected as the primary endpoint of the study, and 30-day and 90-day mortalities were considered secondary endpoints.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentage). Kruskal–Wallis and Chi-square (or Fisher’s exact) tests were used to compare the groups. The associations between preexisting CKD and endpoints were assessed using Cox regression models. We used the following two models to adjust for potential confounders: (1) Model 1 was adjusted by age, gender, and ethnicity; (2) Model 2 was adjusted by age (age ≥ 70, age < 70), gender, AKI stage, SCr initial, ethnicity, admission type, MAP, SAPS II, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), sepsis, cerebrovascular disease, invasive mechanical ventilation, RRT first day, vasopressor use, diuretic use, and length of ICU stay. Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed to adjust potential interference factors. Patients with or without CKD were matched in a 1:1 ratio based on age, gender, AKI stage, MAP, SAPS II, heart failure, sepsis, hypertension, invasive mechanical ventilation, RRT, and length of ICU stay. Kaplan–Meier methods were used to compare three-year survival among patients with AKI and with or without CKD. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 15.0.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The study included 20,440 patients, comprising 1,999 with pure AKI and 18,441 with ACKD. The demographic data and detailed clinical characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. The median age of the patients in ACKD group was significantly older than the pure AKI group (73.95 versus 66.21 years; P < 0.01). The ACKD group had a higher proportion of male patients compared to pure AKI (62.5% vs. 57.6%, respectively; P < 0.01). Significant differences were observed in both ethnicity and admission types between patients with pure AKI and those with ACKD. Compared with pure AKI group, the ACKD group exhibited a significantly greater number of comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, coronary heart disease, COPD, pneumonia, sepsis, cerebrovascular disease, and a higher severity of illness, which was manifested by a higher SAPS II score (SAPS-II 40 versus 34; P < 0.001) at the time of AKI diagnosis. Subjects with preexisting CKD exhibited a lower MAP than those with pure AKI. Regarding interventions, the pure AKI group differed from the ACKD group because of higher rates of invasive ventilation (32.9% versus 28.0%; P < 0.001), whereas the pure AKI group was less likely to use RRT (1.4% versus 4.4.0%; P < 0.001) on the first day after ICU admission. Moreover, vasopressor use was more common in the pure AKI group, while diuretic use was lower. The initiation and mean SCr levels were significantly higher in the ACKD group than in the pure AKI group. No statistically significant difference was observed in the length of ICU stay between the two groups.

Clinical outcomes of the study population

Multivariable Cox regression analyses were used to examine the difference in mortality outcomes between the two groups. In the pre-matched cohort, model 2 suggested the preexisting CKD attributable to lower in-ICU mortality [7.0% versus 7.7%, hazard ratio (HR) = 0.83, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.69–0.99, P = 0.047)]. The results for model 1 are presented in Table 2. In contrast, analyses of the other endpoint (90-day mortality) indicated that preexisting CKD increased the risk of 90-day mortality adjusted by model 1 [(22.9% versus 17.6%, HR = 1.2, 95% CI = 1.08–1.32, P < 0.01). The results of model 2 are presented in Table 2. However, no significant difference was observed between the two groups when 30-day mortality was analyzed, whether adjusted by model 1 or model 2. In PSM, 1976 patients from the pure AKI group were matched with 1976 patients from the ACKD group. The matched patient characteristics included age, gender, AKI stage, MAP, SAPS II, heart failure, sepsis, hypertension, invasive mechanical ventilation, RRT, and length of ICU stay. Consistent with findings in the pre-matched cohort, the ACKD group showed a significantly reduced risk of in-ICU mortality compared to pure AKI (7.0% vs. 9.9%; adjusted HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.63–0.99, P = 0.049), whereas no significant differences were observed in 30-day and 90-day mortalities between the two groups. The Kaplan–Meier survival curves in Fig. 2 presented three-year survival of patients with or without CKD, and the comparison between the two groups revealed significantly better survival of patients without CKD (P = 0.017).

Subgroup analysis

After stratification by AKI stage, estimates of the association between preexisting CKD and in-ICU mortality among patients after matching are presented in Table 3. The results revealed that preexisting CKD was associated with reduced in-ICU mortality in patients with AKI at stage 3 but not in those with stage 1 and 2, according to the KDIGO criteria. When the AKI stage was defined by the SCr criteria, a reduced outcome was only observed in stage 2 and 3. Furthermore, preexisting CKD also reduced in-ICU mortality in patients in stages 3, according to the UO criteria. Therefore, regardless of the criteria used for defining the AKI stage, preexisting CKD reduced the in-ICU mortality in patients with stage 3.

Discussion

This study selected 20440 patients with AKI admitted to the ICU to investigate the clinical outcomes of AKI patients with and without CKD. Overall, our results indicate that preexisting CKD decreased in-ICU mortality in patients with AKI, whether pre-matched or post-matched. In contrast, Kaplan–Meier survival curves revealed that preexisting CKD decreased the three-year survival of patients. Regarding the various AKI stages, the results indicated that preexisting CKD reduced in-ICU mortality among patients in stage 3.

To our knowledge, patients with AKI are at a higher risk of developing CKD [13]. Conversely, preexisting CKD significantly increases the risk of AKI owing to comorbidities [14]. Additionally, CKD is widely recognized to be associated with increased all-cause mortality [15, 16]. However, the influence of preexisting CKD on the prognosis of patients with AKI remains controversial. Several previous studies have reached different conclusions. A single-center study in Korea reported that CKD is an independent predictor of increased in-hospital mortality rates [17]. This difference may be due to various study subjects, which included geriatric patients (≥ 65 years old). Our finding that patients in ACKD group exhibited significantly decreased in-ICU mortality when compared with pure AKI patients is consistent with most previous studies [18, 19]. Khosla et al. reported that the in-hospital mortality rate was lower (31% versus 40%, P = 0.04) in patients with a prior history of CKD in a prospective observational cohort study that included 618 patients with AKI [20]. Another similar multicenter study that included 201 patients with AKI suggested that preexisting CKD was an independent protective factor against in-hospital mortality, and the prevalence of vasopressor use, ICU care, and mortality was significantly higher in pure AKI patients than in patients with preexisting CKD [18]. Compared with the above two studies, our study used different models and matched the subjects to adjust for potential confounders. Moreover, we examined mortality over a longer period (up to 90 days). Despite similar results, the specific data varied. The above two studies focused on in-hospital mortality, which may include ICU hospitalization, whereas we counted in-ICU mortality, which may explain why our mortality rate was lower than that of these two studies. Furthermore, this difference might be due to differences in the study design and subjects.

Despite carrying a greater burden of underlying comorbidities, patients in ACKD group demonstrated significantly lower in-ICU mortality compared to those with AKI alone in our study. Several possible reasons could be postulated to explain these findings. First, patients with pre-existing CKD demonstrate higher susceptibility to AKI progression, including increased risk of dialysis dependence [21]. Notably, at equivalent AKI stages, these patients exhibit less severe pathophysiological insult compared to pure AKI cases. This ultimately translates into lower ICU mortality rates in CKD patients relative to pure AKI patients. Second, delayed renal specialist consultation may lead to poor prognosis for patients with AKI, and compared with pure AKI patients, patients with CKD were more frequently managed by nephrologists [18, 22]. Third, ischemic preconditioning appears to be protective for AKI [23–25], and renal perfusion is always reduced in patients with CKD, which may explain the protective effect of previous CKD [24]. Some of the underlying mechanisms have been explored in some preliminary experiments, but more evidence is still needed [26]. Additionally, associated organ failure in the ICU, which can be reflected in the need for mechanical ventilation, is related to in-ICU mortality [20]. Consistent with this explanation, our study demonstrated that the proportion of invasive mechanical ventilation on the first day in pure AKI patients is significantly higher than that in patients with CKD (32.9% versus 28.0%, P < 0.001). Furthermore, regarding long-term mortality, our study data indicated that long-term survival (three-year survival) was significantly worse in patients with preexisting renal dysfunction than in those with pure AKI, which is consistent with almost all previous reports [27, 28]. Theoretically, complex underlying comorbidities are more likely to determine long-term outcomes.

Moreover, few studies have examined the influence of preexisting CKD across different AKI stages, despite significant variations in pathophysiology among these stages. Therefore, further investigation is needed to elucidate how preexisting CKD affects clinical outcomes in AKI subgroups. Notably, our analysis revealed that preexisting CKD was associated with reduced in-ICU mortality across all three diagnostic criteria, particularly in stage 3 AKI. This protective effect may reflect greater physiological adaptation to acute renal dysfunction in CKD patients, with the benefit becoming more pronounced as the severity of acute injury increases.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, we used the ICD code to identify CKD in the subjects. However, this approach may not capture all CKD subtypes, and a few patients lacked an ICD coding diagnosis of kidney disease, which may have led to this part of the error distribution in the pure AKI group. Secondly, excluding patients with repeated ICU admissions and those with an ICU stay of less than 48 h may have biased the results. Thirdly, although we used various models and subgroup analyses to determine the results, possible confounders cannot be completely excluded and may affect our findings. Finally, retrospective designs inherently limit causal inference. While propensity score matching reduces bias, residual confounding likely persists. Accordingly, additional prospective investigations are required to validate our conclusions.

Based on this study, we propose the following ICU management strategies: For patients with normal renal function upon ICU admission, clinicians should maintain high vigilance to prevent AKI. If AKI occur, prompt nephrology consultation should be considered. For patients with pre-existing CKD, in addition to nephrology consultation, management should focus on CKD-AKI interactions, and emphasize both acute intervention and chronic disease continuity of care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, preexisting CKD may increase in-ICU survival but decrease long-term survival among ICU patients with AKI. This retrospective study reveals significantly divergent in-ICU mortality between AKI patients with pre-existing CKD versus those without CKD. These findings are beneficial for making renoprotective and therapeutic maneuvers in patients with AKI.
