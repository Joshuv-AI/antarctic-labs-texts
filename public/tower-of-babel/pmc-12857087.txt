Obesity is a major modifiable factor associated with ER-negative breast cancer: epidemiological and mechanistic evidence from a high-risk cohort
Authors: Naser Elkum, Ali Saeed Al-Zahrani, Noura N. Alraouji, Taher AL-Tweigeri, Abdelilah Aboussekhra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe biological mechanisms linking modifiable risk factors to aggressive breast cancer subtypes remain poorly defined, particularly in underrepresented populations which experience a disproportionate burden. Regions with high obesity prevalence and early-onset disease provide a unique setting to investigate these drivers.MethodsIn a case–control study of 567 premenopausal breast cancer cases and 906 controls from a high-risk population, tumors were classified into molecular subtypes. Logistic regression estimated subtype-specific risks, and population-attributable fractions (PAF) were calculated. To provide mechanistic validation, we examined paracrine effects of fibroblasts from obese versus lean women on ER-α expression in breast epithelial cells using co-culture and qRT-PCR analysis.ResultsObesity was the strongest modifiable factor, with adjusted ORs ranging from 2.29 (luminal B) to 4.32 (TNBC), corresponding to a population-attributable fraction of approximately 43% for TNBC—an effect size exceeding most previous reports. Family history was independently associated with TNBC. Mechanistically, primary mammary fibroblasts from obese donors downregulated ER-α and HER2 in human mammary epithelial cells, providing a plausible explanation for obesity-driven ER/HER2-negative tumorigenesis.ConclusionsObesity is a major, modifiable factor strongly associated with TNBC, with its effect magnified in high-risk populations. The mechanistic link to ER and HER2 suppression suggests a plausible biological pathway, highlighting metabolic health as a critical target for prevention strategies worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02189-1.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide and is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Molecular classification has revealed distinct subtypes with divergent etiologies and prognoses, with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) representing an aggressive form associated with poorer outcomes and a higher incidence in younger women and certain underrepresented populations [2–5].

This challenge is exemplified in populations such as Arab women, who present with unique epidemiological features, including earlier onset and a disproportionately high burden of TNBC [6, 7]. In Saudi Arabia, for instance, more than one-third of breast cancer cases are diagnosed before age 50, with TNBC comprising ~ 21% of premenopausal cases—nearly double the global average [6, 8].

Obesity has emerged as a prime candidate driver of this disparity and is a leading modifiable risk factor for cancer development and progression [9–12]. Beyond its established role in postmenopausal breast cancer [13], accumulating evidence implicates obesity in the etiology of aggressive molecular subtypes, including TNBC [9, 10, 14–16]. Proposed mechanisms include chronic low-grade inflammation, adipokine dysregulation, and metabolic reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment [10, 12, 16].

However, the majority of this epidemiologic and mechanistic evidence originates from Western populations [15, 17]. This represents a critical gap in knowledge, as the drivers of aggressive disease may be obscured in lower-risk populations. Studying high-risk cohorts is therefore essential to fully characterize the potent effects of factors like obesity and to understand the underlying biology.

To address this, we conducted a comprehensive subtype-specific case-control study in a high-risk Middle Eastern cohort characterized by significant metabolic stress. We quantified the obesity-attributable risk across molecular subtypes and complemented these analyses with direct biological validation using primary breast fibroblasts. This dual-level approach provides robust evidence on the magnitude of obesity's effect and offers a biologically plausible mechanism with global relevance for targeted prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a case-control study including 567 premenopausal women diagnosed with breast cancer at King Abdullah Oncology Center, King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Centre (KFSHRC), and 906 healthy controls. Participants were recruited between 2000 and 2007, a period that preceded national screening programs and thus reflects baseline subtype distribution. All cases were histologically confirmed, and controls were frequency matched to cases by age (± 5 years) and region of residence. The study was approved by the institutional review board of KFSHRC (RAC#2,031,091), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data collection

Trained interviewers administered standardized questionnaires to obtain demographic, reproductive, and lifestyle information, including marital status, parity, breastfeeding practices, oral contraceptive (OC) use, and smoking history. Clinical records provided ER, PR, and HER2 status, with molecular subtyping performed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) following established guidelines. Anthropometric measurements were obtained by trained staff at enrollment using standardized protocols; BMI values were measured directly rather than self-reported.

Molecular subtyping was performed using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) following established guidelines contemporaneous with the recruitment period (2000–2007). Subtypes were defined as follows: Luminal A: ER-positive and/or PR-positive, HER2-negative; Luminal B: ER-positive and/or PR-positive, HER2-positive (IHC 3 + or FISH amplified); HER2-enriched: ER-negative, PR-negative, HER2-positive; Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC): ER-negative, PR-negative, HER2-negative. As Ki-67 staining was not routinely performed during this era, it was not used for subtyping.

Cells and cell culture

Primary fibroblasts were isolated from reduction mammoplasty specimens with informed consent and ethical approval (RAC#2,140,017) as previously described [18]. All the females (3 obese, BMI ≥ 30) and (3 BMI < 30) were pre-menopausal (22–35 years old). Fibroblasts were cultured in M199 medium (Gibco, 12,350–039) and Ham’s F12 (Gibco, 11,765–054) mixed 1:1 and supplemented with 20% Heat Inactivated Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Gibco, 10,082–139) and 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic 100X (Gibco, 15,240–062). MCF-7 cells were obtained from ATCC and were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, 11,875–093) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic. While human primary mammary luminal cells (NBL-10) were obtained as previously described [18], and were maintained in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, 11,320–033) supplemented with 0.5% Bovine Pituitary Extract (Gibco, 13,028–014) and 1% HuMEC supplement (Gibco, 12,754–016) in addition to 2% FBS and 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic.

RNA purification and quantitative RT-PCR

RNA was isolated using mRNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA (1 µg) was used for reverse transcription with Advantage RT-PCR kit (Clontech Laboratories, Mountain View, CA, USA). FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used for quantitative real-time PCR on the LightCycler® 96 Real‐time PCR detection system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Primer sequences are:

GAPDH FW: 5’-GAGTCCACTGGCGTCTTC-3’; RV: 5’-GGGGTGCTAAGCAGTTGGT-3’

ESR-1 FW: 5’-AGGAATACAAGGGGCACACA-3’; RV: 5’-GCTGCTCTCCATAGGTCCAT-3’

Cellular lysate preparation and immunoblotting

This has been performed as previously described [19]. Antibodies directed against ER-α and GAPDH were purchased from Cell Signaling, while Her2 was purchased from BD Biosciences.

Serum-free conditioned media preparation

Cells were cultured in serum-free medium (0.5% FBS). After 24 h, media were collected and briefly centrifuged. The resulting serum-free conditioned mediua waere frozen at −80 °C until use.

Statistical analysis

Unconditional logistic regression was applied to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for breast cancer risk factors by molecular subtype, using the common control group. Multivariable models adjusted for age group, BMI, parity, total pregnancies, breastfeeding, oral contraceptive use, marital status, family history of breast cancer, and region. A directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Figure S1) was constructed to define the minimally sufficient adjustment set—age, parity, oral contraceptive use, and family history—while avoiding adjustment for mediators along the obesity-inflammation—ER-loss pathway.

Population-attributable fractions (PAF) for obesity in TNBC were calculated using Levin's formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ PAF = \frac{{P_{e} \left( {OR - 1} \right)}}{{P_{e} \left( {OR - 1} \right) + 1}} $$\end{document}where Pe represents the proportion of obesity in the control population and OR is the adjusted odds ratio. The attributable fraction among the exposed (AFe) was calculated as (OR-1)/OR.

To assess the robustness of our findings, BMI was modeled both categorically and as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines (with knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles) to evaluate a non-linear dose-response relationship. A p-value for trend was calculated across the lean, overweight, and obese categories. Covariate selection was informed by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) reflecting known confounders of the obesity-breast cancer relationship. We performed sensitivity analyses by additionally adjusting for diabetes status, stratifying by parity, and testing for an interaction between obesity and family history of breast cancer.

Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3 and SAS version 9.4. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Reporting followed the STROBE guidelines for case-control studies. Statistical analysis for immunoblotting and qRT-PCR results were performed using Prism softwire. Data are presented as mean ± SD of independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, with P values of < 0.05 considered significant.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary findings. Modeling BMI as a continuous exposure and with restricted cubic splines showed a monotonic increase in TNBC risk with higher BMI and no evidence that the main conclusion depended on category cut-points. Additional adjustment for diabetes, stratification by parity, and testing the interaction between obesity and family history yielded estimates that were consistent in direction and magnitude with the primary models. Full results are provided in Supplementary Table S1 and Figure S2. These robustness checks were consistent with the minimally sufficient adjustment set defined by the DAG (Supplementary Figure S1).

Population-attributable fraction of obesity

Using the prevalence of obesity among controls with complete BMI data (23.1%) and the adjusted OR for TNBC (4.32), the population-attributable fraction (PAF) was estimated to be 43%. The attributable fraction among obese women (AFe) was 77%, indicating that over three-quarters of TNBC cases occurring in obese women may be attributed to obesity, assuming a causal relationship (Fig. 5). Fibroblasts from obese donors downregulate ESR1 and HER2 in human mammary epithelial cells

To explore mechanistic underpinnings, we examined the paracrine impact of mammary stromal fibroblasts from obese versus non-obese women on the expression of the major marker and the defining feature of TNBC (ER-α in MCF-7 breast cancer luminal cells. To this end, exponentially growing breast stromal fibroblasts from 3 non-obese females (BFL1-3) and 3 others from obese females (BFO1-3) were cultured at similar passages (4–8) in serum-free medium for 24 h, and then serum-free conditioned media (SFCM) were collected and used to treat MCF-7 cells for 24 h. Immunoblotting analysis shows that SFCM from 3 BFO fibroblasts reduced the level of the ER-α protein in MCF-7 cells, relative to the level of the protein in BFL-treated MCF-7 cells (Fig. 6A). These findings were confirmed at the mRNA level. Indeed, quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed a threefold reduction in ER-α transcript levels in MCF-7 cells exposed to BFO1-3 as compared to cells exposed to SFCM from fibroblasts generated from non-obese females BFL1-3 (p = 0.0227) (Fig. 6B). To confirm these results and investigate whether this effect extended to other key biomarkers, we examined the impact on the HER2 receptor, which also characteristically downregulated in TNBC. We have tested the effect of BFL1, BFL2 and BFO1, BFO2 fibroblasts on primary mammary luminal cells (NBL-10) through direct co-culturing experiment. The immunoblots and their quantifications show clear downregulation of both ER-α and Her2 in luminal cells treated with SFCM from BFO fibroblasts relative to those that were exposed to SFCM from BFL fibroblasts (Fig. 6C). Discussion

In the present study we provide clear evidence that obesity is strongly associated with estrogen receptor-negative breast carcinogenesis. The magnitude of this association, reflected by an adjusted OR of 4.32 and a population-attributable fraction of approximately 43%, underscores the substantial contribution of obesity to TNBC risk in this population. This value is higher than estimates reported in many Western cohorts [15, 17]. Several investigations, including meta-analyses, have shown that obesity is a general risk factor for TNBC in premenopausal women [20], with obese women having roughly a 20% higher risk compared with their normal-weight counterparts [15]. The Carolina Breast Cancer Study similarly reported that high BMI and waist–hip ratio increased TNBC risk in premenopausal African-American women [21]. Notably, our epidemiologic results are mechanistically supported by cellular evidence showing that breast stromal fibroblasts from obese women downregulate ER-α and HER2 expression in breast epithelial cells, thereby providing a biological link between obesity and the development of receptor-negative tumors. We and others have previously demonstrated that obesity activates breast stromal adipocytes and promotes their pro-carcinogenic effects through IL-8–dependent pathways [22], indicating that obesity alters multiple stromal components to create a tumor-promoting microenvironment. This aligns with global evidence showing that obesity drives tumorigenesis through inflammatory cytokines, metabolic reprogramming, and disrupted adipose–tumor cross-talk [9, 16, 23]. Collectively, these findings suggest that obesity, as reflected by elevated BMI, may exert its tumor-initiating effects via circulating inflammatory cytokines and adipokines that reprogram stromal fibroblasts and foster neoplastic transformation in adjacent luminal or progenitor cells.

In Arab women, breast cancer occurs predominantly in younger, premenopausal individuals (age < 50 years), and the TNBC subtype is particularly prevalent within this age group. These demographic and biological characteristics likely contribute to the strong obesity–TNBC association observed in our cohort. Notably, a higher incidence of TNBC among obese patients has also been reported in Caucasian women [24], suggesting that while the underlying biology is shared, the magnitude of effect may be amplified in populations with early-onset disease and higher baseline metabolic stress.

The public health implications of these findings are substantial and extend well beyond this cohort. Integrating obesity prevention into national cancer-control frameworks is a global priority [25]. Our data suggest that interventions targeting metabolic health could have a particularly profound impact on reducing the incidence of aggressive, ER-negative tumors across diverse populations. Beyond lifestyle modification, emerging pharmacologic strategies such as GLP-1 receptor agonists offer promising tools for precision prevention, supported by both real-world evidence and randomized trials [26, 27].

Family history was also independently associated with TNBC, underscoring the contribution of genetic predisposition—particularly BRCA1-related pathways—to this subtype [28, 29]. In populations with higher rates of inherited susceptibility and consanguinity, such as those in the Gulf region, genetic risk may compound obesity-driven molecular pathways, emphasizing the need for integrated prevention that combines genetic counseling with metabolic-risk management.

Although reproductive factors such as parity and breastfeeding showed protective effects in univariate models, they did not attenuate the obesity–TNBC relationship after adjustment. This indicates that obesity is a strong, independent predictor of TNBC risk in this population.

From a clinical and policy perspective, national frameworks should prioritize obesity prevention as an essential component of breast-cancer control strategies. Early identification of women at high metabolic risk could enable targeted interventions before malignancy develops. In practice, this entails community-based weight-management and nutrition programs, earlier metabolic-health screening, and culturally tailored education campaigns linking obesity to breast-cancer risk. Such efforts would simultaneously address the dual epidemic of cancer and metabolic disease. At the research level, translational studies of obesity-driven TNBC biology—such as the present fibroblast–epithelial interaction model—should be expanded to guide biomarker discovery and the evaluation of metabolic interventions. Novel agents, including GLP-1 receptor agonists, may represent a new frontier in precision prevention, aligning with Vision 2030 and global precision-medicine goals [26, 27].

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of this study lies in its large, well-characterized cohort of premenopausal Arab women, representing a population that is understudied yet disproportionately affected by aggressive breast cancer subtypes. The analysis integrates comprehensive epidemiologic data with molecular subtyping and experimental validation, providing a rare and coherent bridge between population-level and mechanistic evidence. Rigorous statistical modeling—including sensitivity analyses, spline modeling, and DAG-informed covariate selection—ensures the robustness of the observed associations.

The use of primary human mammary fibroblasts from obese and non-obese donors adds biological depth, directly linking epidemiologic findings to molecular mechanisms of ER/HER2 downregulation. This dual epidemiologic–mechanistic framework is a distinctive strength rarely achieved in single studies.

Several contextual limitations should be noted. The case-control design may introduce recall bias, and recruitment preceded widespread national screening, which could influence subtype distribution. However, this pre-screening era cohort offers a valuable baseline window into intrinsic disease biology, unaffected by detection bias. Subtype classification relied on ER, PR, and HER2 status without Ki-67, reflecting historical diagnostic standards during the recruitment period; while this may lead to minor misclassification between Luminal A and B, it does not affect the identification of ER-negative tumors central to our conclusions. Finally, the mechanistic experiments, though limited in sample size, were designed as proof-of-concept studies to illustrate biologically plausible pathways, not to define them exhaustively.

Taken together, the consistency between strong epidemiologic associations, coherent mechanistic evidence, and alignment with global data strengthens confidence in the validity and translational relevance of our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides robust epidemiologic and mechanistic evidence that obesity is a major modifiable factor associated with TNBC risk of aggressive, estrogen receptor–negative breast cancer in a high-risk, understudied population. The strength of the observed association (adjusted OR 4.32; PAF 43% for TNBC) underscores that metabolic health is a critical determinant of breast cancer risk in premenopausal women. By combining large-scale population data with direct biological validation using primary human fibroblasts, we demonstrate a coherent model linking metabolic dysfunction to ER-α and HER2 downregulation within the breast microenvironment.

These findings not only illuminate the biological basis of obesity-related TNBC but also identify actionable opportunities for prevention and risk stratification. Integrating metabolic screening, weight management, and novel interventions—such as GLP-1 receptor agonists—into breast cancer prevention programs could substantially reduce the burden of this aggressive subtype. Finally, our work highlights the scientific and public health value of studying high-risk regional populations, whose distinct metabolic and genetic profiles can reveal mechanisms of global relevance for precision prevention.

Age, parity, oral contraceptive use, and family history act as confounders that influence both BMI and breast cancer molecular subtype and therefore form the minimally sufficient adjustment set. The BMI → inflammation/ER-loss → subtype pathway represents a mediating biological process through which obesity may affect tumor phenotype; this mediator was intentionally not adjusted for to avoid over-control. This DAG informed the multivariable models and supports the robustness checks performed, including the addition of diabetes and stratification by parity.

Supplementary Figure S2. Restricted cubic spline for BMI and TNBC risk. Restricted cubic spline model (3 degrees of freedom) showing the continuous association between BMI and the odds of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), adjusted for age, parity, oral contraceptive use, and family history. The reference value is BMI = 22 kg/m2. The spline demonstrates a monotonic increase in TNBC risk across the BMI continuum with no evidence of deviation from linearity (p = 0.93). This figure supports the sensitivity analyses described in the manuscript, confirming that results were robust to alternative BMI modeling approaches. The shaded band represents the 95% confidence interval.
