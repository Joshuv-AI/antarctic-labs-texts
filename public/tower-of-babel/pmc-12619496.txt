Betaine alleviates letrozole-induced polycystic ovarian syndrome in rats by regulating oxidative stress and inflammation and restoring the proliferation-apoptosis balance
Authors: Esmaeel Babaeenezhad, Donya Farzane Yegane, Sahar Yarahmadi, Arshia Fakouri, Omid Dezfoulian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) is a common reproductive disorder that often leads to infertility. This study aimed to examine the potential beneficial effects of betaine (BTN) in a rat model of letrozole-induced PCOS.MethodsThirty-six adult female rats were allocated to six groups (n = 6): control, BTN 100 (100 mg/kg/day BTN), PCOS (1 mg/kg/day letrozole), PCOS + BTN 50 (1 mg/kg/day letrozole + 50 mg/kg/day BTN), PCOS + BTN 100 (1 mg/kg/day letrozole + 100 mg/kg/day BTN), and PCOS + Metformin (Met) (1 mg/kg/day letrozole + 300 mg/kg/day Met). Letrozole was administered orally for 21 days, followed by 14 days of oral BTN or Met treatment.ResultsOur findings indicated that BTN significantly ameliorated fasting blood glucose levels, insulin resistance, ovarian histopathological changes, and fibrosis in rats with PCOS. Reduced follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) concentration, elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) levels, and an increased LH/FSH ratio were all alleviated in the BTN-treated PCOS rats. Moreover, BTN significantly diminished ovarian oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated catalase and glutathione peroxidase activities, upregulated nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) expression, and reduced malondialdehyde levels. Betaine markedly suppressed PCOS-induced inflammation by downregulating phosphorylated p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) and nuclear factor-kappa B p65 (NF-κB p65). The disrupted proliferation-apoptosis balance was notably restored following BTN treatment through the regulation of p53, cleaved caspase-3, B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl2), and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA). Interestingly, the beneficial effects of 100 mg/kg BTN were more pronounced than those of 50 mg/kg BTN or Met.ConclusionsThis study elucidated the effectiveness of BTN in ameliorating letrozole-induced PCOS in rats by reducing oxidative stress, apoptosis, and inflammation, and modulating PCNA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01826-9.Keywords: Polycystic ovarian syndrome, Betaine, Metformin, Inflammation, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis

Background

Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) is the leading cause of female infertility, and a prevalent hormonal and metabolic condition affecting about 20% of women in the reproductive age [1, 2]. Although the detailed mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of PCOS are not fully understood, the condition is commonly characterized by hormonal imbalance, insulin resistance (IR), obesity, oxidative stress, and inflammation. A prominent feature of PCOS is biochemical hyperandrogenism, accompanied by abnormal secretion of luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) [2, 3]. As a result of these hormonal disturbances, individuals may experience severe metabolic, biochemical, and ovulatory abnormalities, such as cystic follicular development, oligo-ovulation, and anovulation [3].

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a redox-sensitive transcription factor that regulates the cellular antioxidant defense system by activating genes involved in maintaining redox homeostasis [4]. Under oxidative stress, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) and initiates antioxidant gene expression [4, 5]. In PCOS, impaired Nrf2 signaling may exacerbate oxidative damage, disrupt granulosa cell function, and impair folliculogenesis, thereby contributing to ovarian dysfunction [5–7].

Polycystic ovarian syndrome is associated with chronic low-grade inflammation and dysregulation of inflammatory biomarkers [8, 9]. Adipocyte hypertrophy in PCOS contributes to hypoperfusion of adipose tissue and local hypoxia, which in turn activate the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK)/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway [10–13]. This activation subsequently promotes the nuclear translocation of NF-κB and enhances the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby contributing to the chronic low-grade inflammation characteristic of PCOS [10, 12–14]. Chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of IR, which subsequently disrupts hormonal balance, leads to hyperandrogenism, and ultimately compromises reproductive functions [15]. In addition, hyperandrogenism exacerbates PCOS progression by inducing follicular apoptosis, inhibiting granulosa cell proliferation, and eventually leading to atresia of the developing follicles [16]. Normal ovarian function and follicular development depend on a tightly regulated balance between granulosa cell proliferation, mediated by proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), and apoptosis, modulated by B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl2) family proteins [2, 16–18]. Inflammation in PCOS negatively affects follicular and oocyte development by promoting apoptosis and oxidative stress in the cumulus cells [19].

Transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) is a pleiotropic cytokine that regulates various cellular processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [20]. Its dysregulation has been implicated in the pathogenesis of PCOS [20, 21]. Increased TGF-β expression in the ovarian tissue is associated with fibrotic remodeling, follicular arrest, and granulosa cell dysfunction [2, 20–22]. Therefore, targeting TGF-β signaling represents a promising therapeutic approach for mitigating ovarian dysfunction and its associated pathophysiological features in patients with PCOS.

Current pharmacological treatments, such as metformin (Met), do not offer a comprehensive solution for PCOS and are often associated with adverse effects. Metformin, a widely used insulin-sensitizing agent in women with PCOS and IR, promotes weight loss, reduces androgen levels, and lowers the risk of long-term PCOS complications, such as cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes [23]. However, the chronic use of Met is limited because of its potential side effects, including gastrointestinal disturbances, risk of lactic acidosis and hypoglycemia, and limited efficacy in certain individuals [24, 25]. Therefore, there is a growing need to explore alternative therapeutic strategies for PCOS. Natural bioactive compounds have garnered increasing interest in PCOS management owing to their availability, efficacy, and low incidence of adverse effects. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that these compounds significantly improve key metabolic and hormonal parameters in affected women [26].

Betaine (BTN) or trimethylglycine is synthesized in the inner mitochondrial membrane via choline oxidation, primarily in the liver and kidneys. Although BTN is not classified as a herbal compound, it is commonly extracted from plant-based sources, such as sugar beet, spinach, quinoa, and wheat bran [27]. Betaine serves as a methyl group donor and protects cells from osmotic stress. It also exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway [28]. Although the effects of BTN on ovarian function remain poorly understood, emerging evidence suggests that it may play a role in female fertility and reproduction [29]. Betaine mitigates the detrimental effects of hyperglycemia on ovarian granulosa cell function and steroidogenesis in rats [29]. Additionally, it has demonstrated potential for improving insulin sensitivity and lipid profiles in rats fed a high-fat diet (HFD) [30]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that BTN may exert beneficial effects on metabolic disturbances in PCOS by enhancing insulin sensitivity and reducing inflammation [31]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the potential protective effects of BTN on letrozole-induced PCOS in rats by assessing hormonal, histopathological, and molecular changes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Letrozole, metformin (Met), and betaine hydrochloride were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). The lysis buffer for western blotting was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc. (Cat. No. SC-24948, USA).

Animals

Healthy adult female Wistar rats weighing 180–220 g were obtained from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran) and housed in the Animal Care Facility of the Lorestan University of Medical Sciences (Lorestan, Iran). The estrous cycle was monitored daily for eight consecutive days using vaginal cytology. The rats were maintained under controlled environmental conditions: a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, a 12-hour light/dark cycle, relative humidity of 55 ± 5%, and free access to food and water. A one-week acclimatization period was provided before the experiment. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the university’s Ethics Committee and the guidelines of the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH, 1978). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Lorestan University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.LUMS.REC.1402.286).

Experimental groups

Thirty-six female rats were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 6 per group) as follows:

Control group: Healthy rats received 1% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) as the vehicle for letrozole, BTN, and Met for 35 days.

Control group: Healthy rats received 1% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) as the vehicle for letrozole, BTN, and Met for 35 days.

BTN 100 group: Healthy rats were administered 1% CMC for 21 days, followed by BTN (100 mg/kg/day) for 14 days.

BTN 100 group: Healthy rats were administered 1% CMC for 21 days, followed by BTN (100 mg/kg/day) for 14 days.

PCOS group: Animals were treated with letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) [32, 33] for 21 days, followed by 1% CMC for the remaining 14 days.

PCOS group: Animals were treated with letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) [32, 33] for 21 days, followed by 1% CMC for the remaining 14 days.

PCOS + BTN 50 group: Letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) was administered for 21 days, followed by BTN (50 mg/kg/day) [34, 35] for 14 days.

PCOS + BTN 50 group: Letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) was administered for 21 days, followed by BTN (50 mg/kg/day) [34, 35] for 14 days.

PCOS + BTN 100 group: Animals received letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) for 21 days and were then treated with BTN (100 mg/kg/day) [34, 36] for 14 days.

PCOS + BTN 100 group: Animals received letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) for 21 days and were then treated with BTN (100 mg/kg/day) [34, 36] for 14 days.

PCOS + Met group: Rats were treated with letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) for 21 days and Met (300 mg/kg/day) for 14 days [2, 37].

PCOS + Met group: Rats were treated with letrozole (1 mg/kg/day) for 21 days and Met (300 mg/kg/day) for 14 days [2, 37].

Letrozole was administered for 21 days to induce PCOS [32, 33]. To confirm PCOS induction, daily vaginal smears were collected to monitor the estrous cycle (Supplementary Fig. 1), and rats exhibiting irregular cycles were considered PCOS-positive [2]. After confirmation, the rats were treated with BTN or Met for 14 days (days 22–35). All compounds (letrozole, BTN, and Met) were administered orally via gavage. The doses were selected based on previous studies [34–36].

Blood and tissue collection

Twenty-four hours after the final gavage, the rats were anesthetized with ketamine (10 mg/kg) and xylazine (100 mg/kg) [38] via intraperitoneal injection after overnight fasting. Blood samples were obtained via cardiac puncture from the left ventricle using a syringe and centrifuged at 2000 × g (10 min) to separate the serum for biochemical and hormonal analyses. The isolated serum was stored at − 20 °C until analysis. The rats were subsequently euthanized, and their ovaries were excised for further analysis. The left ovary was immediately frozen and stored at − 80 °C for future analysis, whereas the right ovary was fixed in neutral-buffered formalin (10%) for histopathological and immunohistochemical investigations.

Measurement of serum biochemical and hormonal parameters

Sex hormone levels

Serum LH, FSH, and testosterone levels were evaluated using commercial ELISA kits (Cusabio Biotech, Wuhan, China). Hormone levels were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and absorbance was recorded at the appropriate wavelength using a microplate reader (Stat Fax 4700, USA).

Fasting blood glucose (FBG), insulin, and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)

Fasting blood glucose levels were determined using a colorimetric assay kit (Pars Azmoon, Iran) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum insulin concentrations were measured using a rat insulin ELISA kit (Mercodia® Corporation, Uppsala, Sweden). The level of HOMA-IR was calculated using the following formula [39]: HOMA-IR = [serum insulin (mIU/mL) × serum glucose (mg/dL)]/405.

Oxidative stress biomarkers

Commercial kits from Navand Salamat Company (Urumia, Iran) were used to assess the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPX; Nagpix™ Kit), catalase (CAT; Nactaz™ Kit), and malondialdehyde (MDA; Nalondi™ Kit) in ovarian tissues. The ovaries were homogenized using the provided lysis buffer, and the homogenates were centrifuged at 4 °C to obtain the supernatants.

Histopathological evaluations

Ovarian tissue samples were fixed in neutral-buffered formalin (10%), dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol, cleared with xylene, and immersed in paraffin. The prepared paraffin blocks were sliced into 5 μm-thick sections, deparaffinized, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Additional sections were stained using Masson’s trichrome (MT) to detect collagen fibrils. Stained ovarian sections were examined in a blinded manner by an experienced pathologist using a light microscope (Olympus CX-31, Philippines) to evaluate histopathological changes.

Western blot detection of Nrf2, phosphorylated p38 MAPK, nuclear factor-kappa B p65 (NF-κB p65), p53, procaspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, Bcl2, and PCNA

The protein levels of Nrf2, phosphorylated p38 MAPK, NF-κB p65, Bcl2, PCNA, procaspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, and p53 in the ovarian tissues were quantified using western blotting. Briefly, the ovarian tissues were homogenized using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors (SC-24948). Total protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay following centrifugation (13,000 rpm for 10 min). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated on 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked in a 3% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBS-T) buffer for 3 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting Nrf2 (1:1000, sc-365949), phosphorylated p38 MAPK (1:1000, sc-17852-R), NF-κB p65 (1:1000, sc-8008), Bcl2 (1:1000, sc-492), PCNA (1:1000, sc-53407), p53 (1:1000, sc-126), caspase-3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, #14220), and β-actin (1:1000, sc-517582). After washing, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, sc-516102, sc-2357) for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Cat. No. 1705061) and X-ray film. The intensity of the detected bands was quantified using the ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemical evaluation of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and TGF-β protein levels

Ovarian paraffin-embedded tissues were sectioned to a thickness of 3 μm, deparaffinized, and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed in Tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (Tris-EDTA) buffer (pH 9.0) by heating the slides at 98 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. To reduce non-specific binding, the sections were incubated with 5% normal rabbit serum. The slides were then exposed to primary antibodies: polyclonal rabbit anti-TGF-β (1:100, MBS462142, Mybiosource, USA) and TNF-α (1:100, ab6671, Abcam, UK) for 1 h. After rinsing, the sections were incubated with a prediluted goat anti-rabbit biotinylated secondary antibody (Biocare, USA) for 20 min, followed by a 20-min incubation with prediluted streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (sHRP; Biocare, USA). Immunoreactive sites were visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), and the sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Bio-Optica, Italy).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism Software (version 8.0; San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, was used for multiple group comparisons. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Effects of BTN and Met on ovarian histopathological changes and fibrosis in rats with PCOS

Ovarian sections stained with H&E from the control group revealed follicles at various stages of maturation, ranging from primordial to Graafian (Fig. 1A). In addition, MT-stained ovarian sections from the control group showed normal follicular development with proper collagen deposition limited to the ovarian hilus (Fig. 1a). In the BTN 100 group, multiple corpora lutea (CL) and a range of healthy follicles were observed (Fig. 1B), along with minimal and normally localized fibrotic tissue (Fig. 1b). In contrast, ovaries from the PCOS group showed numerous cystic follicles (CFs), few early stage follicles (Fig. 1C), and extensive collagen deposition throughout the stroma. Atretic follicles (AFs) and vacuolar spaces (VS; arrows) were also abundant (Fig. 1c). In the PCOS + BTN 50 group, only a few CFs were present among the developing follicles (Fig. 1D), and fibrous tissue formation was markedly reduced compared to that in the PCOS group (Fig. 1d), indicating partial restoration of folliculogenesis. Interestingly, the PCOS + BTN 100 group exhibited well-structured ovaries with no apparent abnormal follicles (Fig. 1E), and collagen expansion was minimal (Fig. 1e). Conversely, the PCOS + Met group displayed more CFs than the BTN-treated groups (Fig. 1F), although several normal follicular structures were also present. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed localized connective tissue expansion, primarily around CFs (Fig. 1f).

A quantitative assessment of ovarian follicular populations was performed to investigate the effects of BTN and Met on folliculogenesis (Table 1). The number of cystic and atretic follicles was significantly higher in the PCOS group than in the control group (p < 0.0001 for both). Betaine treatment (both 50 and 100 mg/kg) significantly reduced the number of CFs (p < 0.0001 and p = 0.003, respectively) and AFs (p = 0.0002 and p < 0.0001, respectively) compared to the PCOS group, with the most substantial reduction observed in the PCOS + BTN 100 group. Metformin administration to PCOS rats also significantly reduced AFs compared to the PCOS group (p < 0.0001), but did not significantly affect CFs (p = 0.17). The PCOS group exhibited a significantly lower number of Graafian follicles (p = 0.03) and CL (p = 0.04) than the control group. Only 100 mg/kg BTN significantly increased the number of Graafian follicles (p = 0.04) and CL (p = 0.01) compared to the PCOS group. Treatment with 50 mg/kg BTN or Met did not significantly affect the number of Graafian follicles (p = 0.43 and p = 0.35, respectively) and CL (p = 0.12 and p = 0.20, respectively).

Effects of BTN and Met on FBG, insulin, and HOMA-IR levels in rats with PCOS

Insulin resistance and hyperglycemia are common characteristics of PCOS, prompting us to examine the effects of BTN and Met on FBG levels and IR in rats with PCOS. As shown in Fig. 2A, FBG levels were significantly higher in the PCOS group than in the control group (p = 0.002). However, treatment with BTN (100 mg/kg) or Met significantly reduced FBG levels compared to the PCOS group (p = 0.013 and p = 0.037, respectively), with the most pronounced effect observed in PCOS rats treated with 100 mg/kg BTN (Fig. 2A). In contrast, FBG levels in the PCOS + BTN 50 group did not differ significantly from those in the PCOS group (p = 0.1; Fig. 2A).

Analysis of insulin and HOMA-IR levels showed marked differences among the experimental groups (Figs. 2B and C). The PCOS group exhibited significantly elevated insulin and HOMA-IR levels compared to those of the control group (p < 0.0001 for both). Interestingly, treatment with BTN at both 50 and 100 mg/kg reduced insulin (p = 0.057 and p < 0.0001, respectively) and HOMA-IR (p < 0.0001 for both) in a dose-dependent manner compared to the PCOS group. Similarly, Met-treated PCOS rats showed a significant decrease in both insulin and HOMA-IR levels compared to PCOS rats (p < 0.0001 for both). Notably, the greatest reduction in insulin and HOMA-IR levels was observed in the PCOS + BTN 100 group.

Effects of BTN and Met on serum LH, FSH, and testosterone levels in rats with PCOS

As hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are key indicators for diagnosing PCOS, serum concentrations of testosterone, LH, and FSH were measured in all experimental groups (Fig. 3). Administration of letrozole for 21 consecutive days led to a significant increase in serum testosterone and LH concentrations, as well as the LH/FSH ratio (p < 0.0001 for all) in the PCOS group compared to the control group (Fig. 3). Treatment with BTN at both 50 and 100 mg/kg significantly decreased serum testosterone (p < 0.0001), LH (p < 0.0001), and the LH/FSH ratio (p < 0.0001) compared to those in the PCOS group. Additionally, 14-day Met administration significantly reduced serum LH concentrations (p < 0.0001) and the LH/FSH ratio (p < 0.0001) compared to the PCOS group; however, the reduction in testosterone levels was not significant (p = 0.375).

Effects of BTN and Met on ovarian oxidative stress biomarkers in rats with PCOS

We assessed the effects of BTN and Met on oxidative stress in rats with PCOS by measuring the ovarian levels of MDA, activities of GPX and CAT, and expression of Nrf2 (Figs. 4 and 5B). The PCOS group exhibited significantly higher ovarian MDA levels than the control group (p < 0.0001; Fig. 4A). Treatment of PCOS rats with 100 mg/kg BTN significantly decreased MDA levels compared to those in untreated PCOS rats (p = 0.0008; Fig. 4A). Although the administration of 50 mg/kg BTN and Met reduced MDA levels, the reductions were not statistically significant (p = 0.317 and p = 0.094, respectively; Fig. 4A).

In contrast, ovarian GPX and CAT activities were significantly decreased in PCOS rats after 21 days of letrozole administration compared to healthy rats (p = 0.006 and p = 0.001, respectively; Figs. 4B and C). Betaine administration (100 mg/kg) markedly increased GPX and CAT activities relative to the PCOS group (p = 0.001 and p = 0.015, respectively; Figs. 4B and C). Furthermore, 21-day letrozole administration significantly downregulated Nrf2 expression in the ovarian tissue compared to the control group (p = 0.005; Figs. 5A and B). Rats treated with BTN (50 and 100 mg/kg) exhibited significantly higher Nrf2 expression than untreated PCOS rats (p = 0.041 and p = 0.027, respectively; Figs. 5A and B). In contrast, Met administration for 14 days significantly reduced Nrf2 expression in PCOS rats compared to that in untreated PCOS rats (p = 0.020; Figs. 5A and B).

Effects of BTN and Met on inflammatory signaling (phosphorylated p38 MAPK/NF-κB p65) and cytokines (TNF-α and TGF-β) in rats with PCOS

The protein expression of phosphorylated p38 MAPK was significantly upregulated after letrozole administration in the PCOS group compared with the control group (p < 0.0001; Figs. 5A and C). Likewise, NF-κB p65 expression slightly elevated in the PCOS group; however, the difference was not statistically significant compared to the control group (P > 0.05; Figs. 5A and D). Betaine treatment (50 and 100 mg/kg) induced a dose-dependent reduction in phosphorylated p38 MAPK expression in PCOS rats compared with untreated PCOS animals (p < 0.0001 for both doses; Figs. 5A and C). Interestingly, PCOS rats treated with 100 mg/kg BTN showed a significant decrease in NF-κB p65 levels (p = 0.0005; Figs. 5A and D), whereas treatment with 50 mg/kg BTN had no significant effect (p = 0.935; Figs. 5A and D). Similar to the effects of high-dose BTN, Met treatment also significantly reduced the expression of both phosphorylated p38 MAPK and NF-κB p65 in PCOS rats compared with untreated PCOS rats (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.001, respectively; Figs. 5A-D).

Immunohistochemical analysis of TNF-α and TGF-β expression revealed that the staining intensity in the stroma or follicles of BTN- and Met-treated rats was mildly elevated compared to that of the other groups (Fig. 6). However, quantitative analysis using ImageJ software showed no statistically significant differences between the groups.

To determine the effects of BTN and Met on ovarian apoptosis in rats with PCOS, we measured the expression of apoptotic biomarkers p53, Bcl2, procaspase-3, and cleaved caspase-3 in each experimental group. Both BTN doses (50 and 100 mg/kg) significantly reversed the PCOS-induced alterations in procaspase-3 (p < 0.0001 for both doses), cleaved caspase-3 (p = 0.04 for 50 mg/kg and p = 0.01 for 100 mg/kg), and the cleaved/procaspase-3 ratio (p < 0.0001 for both doses). Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the protective effects of BTN in a letrozole-induced PCOS model in female rats and to compare these effects with those of Met. Our findings indicate that BTN significantly mitigated the letrozole-induced abnormalities, including ovarian histopathological changes, hormonal imbalance, and IR. Moreover, BTN modulated key signaling pathways related to mitochondrial apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation. The effects of BTN were comparable to those of Met, and in certain aspects, even superior to this standard therapeutic agent commonly used for PCOS management.

Letrozole administration led to IR, as reflected by elevated FBG, insulin, and HOMA-IR levels. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that PCOS is characterized by hyperinsulinemia and hyperglycemia [15, 40]. Conversely, treatment with BTN, particularly at a dose of 100 mg/kg, markedly attenuated these metabolic disturbances and was more effective than treatment with Met or a lower dose of BTN (50 mg/kg). These findings align with those of previous studies demonstrating the efficacy of BTN in reducing IR in various pathological conditions [41, 42]. Although the mechanisms underlying IR in PCOS have not been fully elucidated, several factors have been identified. One proposed mechanism is androgen excess, which may impair insulin signaling by interfering with glucose transporter (GLUT) dysfunction [43]. In our model, letrozole-induced elevation of androgens may have contributed to the observed IR via these pathways [43], and BTN treatment reduced both androgens and IR. Although the detailed mechanisms by which BTN improves IR were not elucidated in our study, several pathways have been proposed in previous studies. The serine 307 residue in insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) is phosphorylated by the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathway, impairing insulin signaling [44]. Overactivation of this pathway promotes IR via IRS-1 phosphorylation. A previous study by Wan et al. demonstrated that BTN attenuates IR by suppressing the JNK pathway in a non-PCOS model of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [45]. Although our study did not investigate JNK activation, we hypothesized that a similar mechanism might contribute to the effects observed in our PCOS model. In addition, BTN has been reported to increase adiponectin levels, leading to AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation and enhanced glucose uptake [46], which may also play a role in reducing IR in this study. However, these proposed mechanisms remain speculative and warrant further investigation in the context of letrozole-induced PCOS.

Letrozole is frequently used to develop a rat model of PCOS that closely mimics human conditions [47]. Mechanistically, letrozole inhibits aromatase, an enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of androgens to estrogens, thereby increasing androgen levels and promoting CFs formation [2, 47]. In this study, letrozole administration elevated testosterone levels and altered the LH/FSH ratio, resulting in abnormal follicular development and ovarian cysts. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies reporting hyperandrogenism and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis in PCOS-induced animal models [48–50]. Treatment with BTN significantly decreased serum LH and testosterone levels and normalized the LH/FSH ratio, which was accompanied by a reduction in CFs and improved follicular development, more effectively than Met treatment. These findings are in accordance with those of previous reports showing that improvement in hormonal abnormalities is accompanied by amelioration of ovarian histopathological changes in PCOS models [37, 48]. Chronic low-grade inflammation is closely associated with androgen overproduction [6, 51]. Therefore, the reduction in androgen levels observed after BTN treatment may be attributed, at least in part, to its anti-inflammatory activity, as demonstrated in this study. A negative association between FSH and testosterone has been well documented in both animal and human studies, wherein increased testosterone levels are typically associated with decreased FSH levels [52, 53]. Thus, the increased FSH concentration in BTN-treated PCOS rats may have resulted from the reduction in testosterone levels.

Oxidative stress is a key contributor to PCOS pathogenesis [54, 55], affecting ovarian steroidogenesis and leading to hyperandrogenemia and CFs development [56]. Consistent with previous reports [40, 49], letrozole-induced PCOS rats exhibited elevated oxidative stress, as indicated by reduced ovarian GPX and CAT activities and increased ovarian MDA levels. Conversely, BTN administration, particularly at 100 mg/kg, significantly counteracted the effects of letrozole, promoting antioxidant enzyme activity and reducing lipid peroxidation. These effects were more pronounced with 100 mg/kg BTN than with 50 mg/kg BTN or Met, corroborating previous studies that demonstrated the antioxidant potential of BTN in various pathological conditions [34, 57]. The reduction in oxidative stress observed in BTN-treated PCOS rats may result from its capacity to elevate non-enzymatic antioxidants through the methionine–homocysteine cycle [58]. We also observed a significant increase in nuclear Nrf2 expression after BTN treatment in PCOS rats. Therefore, the antioxidant effects of BTN may be attributed to increased Nrf2 activity and/or enhanced levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants.

Polycystic ovarian syndrome is closely associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, which contributes to the development of IR and hyperandrogenemia [15]. In this context, NF-κB activation plays a major role in mediating inflammation, as the p65 subunit is released from its inhibitor IκB (inhibitor of kappa B), translocates to the nucleus, and promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine gene expression [14]. Interestingly, administration of BTN (100 mg/kg) or Met significantly downregulated ovarian NF-κB p65 expression in PCOS rats, consistent with previous reports demonstrating the anti-inflammatory effects of both agents via NF-κB suppression in inflammation-related conditions [59–61]. Activation of NF-κB is modulated by its upstream regulator, p38 MAPK [62]. In agreement with previous studies [63, 64], our results showed that BTN effectively suppressed p38 MAPK activation, suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effect of BTN may be mediated by the inhibition of the p38 MAPK/NF-κB signaling pathway. Although activation of this pathway would predict alterations in downstream cytokines, no significant differences in TNF-α expression were detected among the experimental groups. This unexpected finding may be explained by the limited sensitivity of the immunohistochemistry (IHC) technique, which is generally less sensitive than western blotting or ELISA in detecting subtle changes in protein expression levels. Additionally, the extent of molecular or histological differences among the groups may have been affected by the duration of the letrozole treatment. Although a 21-day regimen is widely accepted for PCOS induction [33, 48], longer exposure (e.g., 27–30 days) has been reported to cause more pronounced histopathological and inflammatory changes in animal models [65]. Therefore, the lack of significant TNF-α changes observed in this study might be due to both the methodological limitations of IHC and moderate inflammation severity during the 21-day induction period.

Follicular atresia is one the key features of PCOS and is primarily driven by apoptosis of granulosa cells and ovarian oocytes [66]. Granulosa cell apoptosis contributes to PCOS by disrupting the conversion of androgens to estrogens, leading to hyperandrogenemia [67–69]. Consistent with this mechanism, we observed increased expression of cleaved caspase-3 and p53, along with elevated testosterone levels, in PCOS rats. Intriguingly, treatment of PCOS rats with BTN (50 and 100 mg/kg) significantly downregulated p53 and cleaved caspase-3 expression and reduced testosterone levels, indicating its anti-apoptotic effects. These results align with those of previous studies demonstrating the anti-apoptotic properties of BTN in various disease models [70, 71]. Interestingly, PCOS induction also led to Bcl2 overexpression, possibly as a compensatory response to counteract apoptosis, as previously reported [72, 73]. Conversely, BTN administration reduced Bcl2 expression while simultaneously inactivating caspase-3, suggesting that BTN may exert its anti-apoptotic effects through a Bcl2–independent mechanism, potentially involving extrinsic apoptotic pathways, such as Fas/FasL/caspase-8 signaling [74]. Although Bcl2 was upregulated in the PCOS group, it did not prevent caspase-3 activation in the ovaries. This suggests that the increase in Bcl2 was insufficient to alter the Bax/Bcl2 ratio in a way that would inhibit caspase-3 activation, consistent with the findings of Huo et al. [72]. In PCOS, oxidative stress and NF-κB activation are major contributors to granulosa cell apoptosis [75, 76]. In this study, BTN treatment significantly alleviated oxidative stress and inhibited NF-κB activation in PCOS rats, implying that its anti-apoptotic effects may be mediated by the suppression of oxidative stress and/or NF-κB signaling.

Enhanced granulosa cell proliferation is another hallmark of PCOS, particularly in women with anovulatory PCOS, where proliferation rates are higher than in those with regular ovulation [77]. In line with this, PCNA, a cell proliferation biomarker, was upregulated in PCOS rats, consistent with reports from both human [78] and animal [73] studies. After 14 days of treatment, BTN and Met significantly downregulated PCNA expression in PCOS rats. Upadhyay et al. [73] showed that elevated PCNA levels in PCOS rats were associated with increased expression of 14-3-3β protein, which promotes both PCNA expression and androgen synthesis in ovarian tissues. The upregulation of PCNA in PCOS rats may reflect a compensatory response to increased apoptosis aimed at normalizing cell turnover, as its expression declined in parallel with reduced apoptosis following treatment with BTN or Met. Our results are consistent with those of a previous study by Cai et al., which showed BTN-mediated downregulation of PCNA in a testicular oxidative stress model [79]. The observed reduction in PCNA expression in BTN-treated PCOS rats may be related to the downregulation of 14-3-3β protein, which requires additional investigation.

We histopathologically evaluated ovarian fibrosis in various groups by assessing collagen deposition using MT staining. Consistent with previous studies [2, 22], ovarian sections from letrozole-treated rats showed marked fibrosis, characterized by extensive stromal collagen accumulation and numerous VS throughout the ovary. Administration of BTN or Met considerably mitigated fibrotic tissue formation, particularly in PCOS rats treated with 100 mg/kg BTN, where the expansion of fibrous connective tissue was limited. These results support previous reports of the anti-fibrotic effects of BTN in models of alcoholic liver fibrosis [80] and doxorubicin-induced nephrotoxicity [81]. Transforming growth factor-beta is believed to promote ovarian fibrosis in PCOS through multiple mechanisms [2]. It enhances fibroblast proliferation to enhance collagen accumulation [82], activates the TGF-β/Smad pathway to induce fibrogenic gene expression [83], and stimulates epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, resulting in extracellular matrix accumulation [83, 84]. Several studies have implicated oxidative stress-induced NF-κB activation as an upstream regulator of TGF-β signaling [20, 76]. Consistent with our findings, Singh et al. [60] reported that BTN ameliorated doxorubicin-mediated cardiomyopathy by reducing oxidative stress, NF-κB activation, and TGF-β expression. Interestingly, we found that BTN treatment attenuated ovarian fibrosis and mitigated oxidative stress and NF-κB activation in PCOS rats. However, our study did not reveal significant differences in TGF-β expression among the groups, which may be attributed to the limitations of the IHC method used for its detection.

This study had several limitations. First, the endogenous levels of BTN in the circulation and mitochondria were not assessed. Future studies using targeted metabolomics are needed to determine whether mitochondrial BTN depletion occurs in our PCOS model and whether the observed therapeutic effects are associated with the restoration of such depletion. Second, although animal models are valuable for understanding disease mechanisms, they may not fully replicate the spectrum of human PCOS pathophysiology. Finally, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect subtle but potentially meaningful differences between groups.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings indicate that BTN exerts significant therapeutic effects in a letrozole-induced PCOS rat model. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate and directly compare the beneficial effects of BTN and Met in this model. Betaine treatment not only ameliorated hormonal and histopathological abnormalities but also modulated key pathogenic mechanisms associated with PCOS, including oxidative stress (through enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity and Nrf2 upregulation), inflammation (via downregulation of the p38 MAPK/NF-κB p65 pathway), and apoptosis (via the regulation of p53, cleaved caspase-3, Bcl2, and PCNA). Notably, BTN exhibited greater efficacy than Met, a widely used therapeutic agent, across multiple parameters. The strengths of this study include the use of a validated animal model, comprehensive analysis of endocrine, histological, and molecular parameters, and mechanistic insights into the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects of BTN. These findings provide promising preclinical evidence to support further research on the therapeutic potential of BTN in the management of PCOS.
