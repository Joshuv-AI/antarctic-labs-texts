Experiences and preferences of Hispanic men in the National Diabetes Prevention Program: a qualitative study guided by the COM-B framework
Authors: Christopher J Gonzalez, Noelia G Hernandez, Clarence N Perez-Mejia, Hana Flaxman, Cara Stephenson-Hunter, Eric Gil, Taynara Formagini, Martin F Shapiro, Jeffrey S Gonzalez, Earle C Chambers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis qualitative study aims to explore the experiences and preferences of Hispanic men participating in the National Diabetes Prevention Program (NDPP), an intensive lifestyle change intervention that effectively reduces diabetes risk, considering Hispanic men experience diabetes disproportionately yet remain underrepresented in the NDPP.DesignIndividual semi-structured interviews were conducted over the phone in English or Spanish between June 2023 and February 2024. Transcripts were analysed using a framework analysis.Participants17 Hispanic men engaged in the NDPP for ≥4 sessions. The majority were foreign-born (n=11) and self-identified as English proficient (n=11).ResultsThrough three major themes, Hispanic men reflected on their experiences: (1) Going into the NDPP: despite not knowing what to expect from the NDPP, their fear of diabetes motivated them to enrol in the programme; (2) During the NDPP: they felt relief from gaining critical knowledge about diet, exercise and diabetes prevention; and finally (3) Impressions of the NDPP: they appreciated the NDPP’s informational resources, personalised coaching, group format and acknowledgement of traditional cultural diets and found men-only groups often offered additional emotional safety but had mixed feelings about the programme’s virtual format.ConclusionsFindings suggest that Hispanic men appreciate the knowledge and skills attained from the NDPP and value its resources, group format, culturally-tailored content and gender-tailored structure. Recruitment efforts may benefit from emphasising how the programme reduces uncertainty about prediabetes and from more clearly conveying the structure of the programme. Strategies to improve sustained engagement should consider how to feasibly offer delivery formats that accommodate diverse preferences.Keywords: Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2; Disease Management; Health Equity; QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

Diabetes affects 29.1 million people in the USA but is disproportionately prevalent, and more likely to contribute to severe morbidity, among Hispanic men compared with both non-Hispanic White men and to Hispanic women (12.3%, 7.9% and 11.3%, respectively).1 The National Diabetes Prevention Program (NDPP), a group-based intervention that promotes healthy dietary and exercise habits through an evidence-based curriculum facilitated by a lifestyle coach,2 can reduce the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (henceforth referred to as diabetes) by up to 58% among people with pre-diabetes.3,5 However, despite composing 8.6% of NDPP eligible individuals, Hispanic men compose only 2% of those enrolled in the NDPP, among whom only 53% complete the programme (vs 70.5% of non-Hispanic White men).6 7 These high attrition rates have substantial implications for the long-term outcomes of this population, given that the number of programme sessions attended is linearly associated with the likelihood of reaching the programme’s 5% wt loss goal, and each session contributes to a loss of 0.31% body weight.8

Prior research suggests that Hispanic men with pre-diabetes fear developing diabetes, but it remains unclear if and how this may motivate enacting lifestyle changes to prevent it.9 10 Moreover, numerous barriers, predominantly those related to capabilities and opportunities, restrict their participation and engagement in existing lifestyle interventions, including work obligations, transportation issues, financial limitations and inadequate knowledge of diabetes prevention.10 11 Several interventions tailored to the unique preferences of Hispanic populations, and even those of Hispanic men specifically, have aimed to overcome these barriers, with varying levels of success.12,14 Importantly, even culturally tailored programmes, which leverage social opportunities and motivations, and virtual programmes, which theoretically overcome some capability barriers but may also create others, often struggle to recruit and retain Hispanic men.12 15 Effectively addressing these limitations may require further adapting the content and delivery of existing interventions or alternatively developing new interventions that promote and support lifestyle change while addressing the unique needs and preferences of Hispanic men.16 17 While prior research, including our own, has identified influences limiting engagement and driving early attrition in the NDPP among Hispanic men, little is known about what those who have substantially engaged in the programme have valued from it.18

This qualitative study examined the experiences of Hispanic men who substantially engaged in the NDPP, with a focus on evaluating their capacity, opportunities and motivations to engage with the programme. Insight into Hispanic men’s experiences with the NDPP can inform methods to improve recruitment to and delivery of the programme so that it better meets the needs and preferences of this population. Addressing these needs and preferences could potentially increase NDPP engagement among Hispanic men and potentially reduce racial and ethnic disparities in the incidence and prevalence of diabetes in the USA.

Research design and methods

Study design

This qualitative study of Hispanic men in a remotely-delivered NDPP used a framework analysis to deductively code semi-structured interview transcripts guided by the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour Model (COM-B) and the Theoretical Domains Frameworks (TDF) and presents themes developed using an iterative inductive approach.

Setting, recruitment and study sample

Recruitment occurred from June 2023 to February 2024 and leveraged the protocol of the Power Up study, a randomised controlled trial testing the effectiveness of men-only vs mixed-gender remote NDPP groups, offered in English and Spanish, tailored for men from disadvantaged communities.17 19 Partnered with the hospital-affiliated Montefiore Health System and the New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene, the Power Up study used primary care sites’ electronic health records to screen for men at risk of diabetes and invited them to participate in the study. Eligibility criteria for the current separate study included self-identifying as a Hispanic man, aged >18 years, being at risk for developing diabetes (eg, HbA1c of 5.7%–6.4% within the past year) and having cognitive capacity to interview by phone. Those meeting criteria were invited to participate in the current study during the initial outreach phone calls for the Power Up study or following completion of all core sessions of the NDPP. Interested eligible individuals were then contacted by study staff by phone, provided study details, including potential risks, and read a scripted consent form. Eligible individuals were allowed to ask any questions about the study or opt out prior to providing verbal consent. Interviews were only conducted after verbal consent was obtained.

Hispanic men were purposefully sampled for diversity in NDPP engagement and stratified into those with low vs substantial engagement, defined as attending <4 or ≥4 core sessions, respectively. A comparative analysis of themes emerging uniquely among those in this sample with low engagement explored barriers to initial engagement in the NDPP and has been published; the current study focuses instead on evaluating the NDPP from the perspective of Hispanic men who meaningfully engaged in the programme.18 Although a relatively low threshold, prior studies of the NDPP and prior reimbursement thresholds defined meaningful engagement (ie, substantial engagement) as ≥4 sessions.8 20 Participants were also purposefully sampled for diversity in men-only (intervention) versus mixed-gender NDPP groups (control). The Institutional Review Board of Weill Cornell Medicine reviewed and approved the study protocol, and Albert Einstein College of Medicine approved the referral process. This report follows the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (online supplemental COREQ).

Of the 55 individuals invited, 32 completed interviews; of those 17 had substantial engagement in the programme and were included in a separate analysis for the current study. The remainder declined for lack of interest (n=12) or could not be reached after the initial invitation (n=11). Recruitment concluded when no new insights emerged from analysing three consecutive transcripts (ie, saturation).21

Data collection

A bilingual Hispanic man trained in qualitative interviewing performed one-on-one interviews (median: 26.4 min; range: 18.4–39.1 min) through phone calls in English or Spanish. Interviews, conducted and recorded through Microsoft Teams, used a semi-structured interview guide (online supplemental e-Table 1) informed by the TDF and COM-B. COM-B contextualises individual-level change through examination of capabilities, opportunities and motivation influence on behaviour.22 TDF further explores the barriers and facilitators of change and classifies them into 14 domains overlapping with COM-B.22 23 Given the study focus, the interview guide aimed to elicit participants’ experiences with engaging in the NDPP, with individual questions relating to their capabilities (eg, knowledge and skills), opportunities (eg, resources and social influences) and motivations (eg, social identity including ethnicity and gender, emotion and intentions) to engage with the programme. Recordings were deidentified and transcribed through Acolad, a professional translation and transcription service.

Analysis

Two research assistants with qualitative experience, one of whom was also the interviewer and another of whom is a bilingual Hispanic woman with formal qualitative research training and experience, independently coded transcripts using NVivo software.24 A third researcher (a Hispanic male physician-scientist and principal investigator of this study) reconciled codes, with the study team resolving discrepancies to create a codebook. Initially, a deductive thematic approach was used for analysis, with new codes arranged into the constructs of the COM-B and TDF.23 25 88 codes emerged, which were then grouped inductively, iteratively developing new themes and subthemes that ultimately captured the participant’s reflections on the NDPP retrospectively as they proceeded through the programme. Finally, subthemes are presented with the constructs of TDF and COM-B they reflect. Emerging codes and processes for theme development are presented in online supplemental e-Table 2. Participants did not review or provide feedback on findings. Participants’ quotes are presented with a unique identifier.

Patient and public involvement

The aim of the public and patient involvement in this study was to leverage there knowledge and experiences to identify their preferences for potentially adapting the NDPP. Patients were not involved in conducting interviews or reporting resutls, but were recruited and interviewed using standard qualitative methods

Results

Among 17 Hispanic men with substantial engagement in the NDPP (ie, engaged in more than four core sessions), the majority were not born in the USA (n=11), felt they were proficient in English (n=11), reported completing more than a high school education (n=10) and considered themselves to have good health literacy (n=13). Additionally, purposeful sampling ensured representation of participants from both mixed-gendered NDPP control (n=8) and men-only intervention arms (n=9). Going into the NDPP

Motivations to prevent diabetes (COM-B: motivation, opportunity; TDF: emotions, goals, intentions, social influences)

Participants expressed wanting to engage in lifestyle change and diabetes prevention for various reasons. As one participant recognised: “it is important to take care of oneself,” [#11]. They described wanting to learn information about eating healthily, losing weight, diabetes prevention and taking precautions for their health. Some participants described wanting to prevent diabetes because they had “family that suffers from the disease.”[#25] One described “hearing stories about family members [passing away from diabetes and], getting their legs cut off” [#6]. Participants also communicated their fear of having to treat diabetes long term: “I didn’t want to end up checking myself with a needle and taking insulin” and their fear of developing health complications, including blindness [#23].

Ideas about the NDPP prior to engaging (COM-B: motivations; TDF: beliefs about capabilities; goals and intentions)

Retrospectively describing their impressions prior to participating in the NDPP, participants described worrying that the programme would be “a boring group” and admittedly “had no idea” what to expect or what they would actually get out of the programme, other than general health information [#6 and 15]. Confidence and reasoning in joining the NDPP varied among participants: some believed that their participation would support them in preventing diabetes (“[I joined the DPP] to learn things that are helpful, learn what is harmful, what is good” #16); others reflected on their previous failed attempts to enact lifestyle change.

During the NDPP

Reactions to enacting lifestyle change (COM-B: motivations, capability; TDF: emotions, beliefs about capabilities, mental capacity and behavioural regulation)

Participants described feeling several emotions while attempting to implement the lifestyle changes learnt during their participation in the NDPP into their daily life. They described consistently choosing healthy diet options, including calorie counting, to be too much work, describing it as “uncomfortable” and “challenging.” [#25] Some participants found the act of taking responsibility for their health, including deciding what to eat and how to exercise, to be overwhelming, describing it simply as: “Everything is in my control” [#27]. They also reflected on how discouraging it was to not see improvements in their weight every week, and how at times it made them want to quit. One participant described feeling “inferior” to others who were seeing improvements [#27]. However, despite the discomfort and discouragement, participants often expressed feeling relieved that they had joined the NDPP. They described feeling comforted by the knowledge and peer support to manage these challenges.

Knowledge and skills learned (COM-B: capability; TDF: knowledge)

Participants described having very limited knowledge of pre-diabetes prior to engaging in the NDPP, despite having heard of diabetes before. One noted: “I never even knew I was prediabetic. I do know that many people have diabetes, but not that it starts with prediabetes” [#11]. While participants perceived developing diabetes to be serious and concerning and recognised the need for lifestyle changes like reducing sugar consumption, many reported a lack of knowledge about healthy diets and how to maintain them.

While reflecting on their experience in the NDPP, participants described the programme as an abundant source of specific information for diabetes prevention. One described “learning about working out and the proper foods to eat” [#14]. They discussed receiving education on moderating and controlling their diet, the seriousness of diabetes and understanding the balance of exercise and diet. They also described learning specific nutritional skills like calorie counting, label reading and cooking recipes. Lastly, they mentioned becoming comfortable with exercising and pacing themselves to lose weight, which they thought were valuable skills to implement in their daily lives.

Impressions of the NDPP

Utility of the NDPP structure (COM-B: opportunity, motivation; TDF: social influence, reinforcement)

Resources of the NDPP: Participants described how the plethora of available resources, including brochures, informational videos, scales and booklets, reinforced their engagement in the programme and lifestyle change, as well as boosted their motivation in engaging with the NDPP. Many participants described using the items sent to them and how effective they considered them to be, expressing: “They sent me a scale, so I was able to weigh myself and I was able to lose several pounds,” [#5] and “The book was telling us the calories that we were consuming etc., it was impressive.” [#4].

Cadence of the NDPP: Participants explained that having frequent meetings and check-ins with their NDPP coaches and peers created a personalised environment which allowed them to not only learn from each other but also serve as space to feel heard, which was missing from their primary care appointments. They further explained that having goal check-ins helped hold themselves accountable for setting lifestyle change goals and reaching them.

Group Dynamics of the NDPP: Participants elaborated that having a social environment with others who have pre-diabetes was beneficial for learning and finding support. They felt NDPPs offered spaces to receive advice and suggestions from peers. Additionally, they discussed how NDPP coaches contributed to their confidence and willingness to engage because of their professional and informative approach to teaching. Lastly, although few, some participants shared challenges with the group structure, stating that there could be too many people in the group or alternatively that single individuals could dominate the conversation, expressing ‘there’s always one person who needs to be the center of attention’. Participants praised the guides for facilitating conversations and suggested including social ties, such as friends and family, to improve their experiences with engaging in the NDPP.

Utility of adapting to gender and culture (COM-B: motivation; TDF: professional role and identity)

When asked about how they felt being in a men-only or mixed-gendered NDPP as well as the strengths and drawbacks to either version, participants expressed a variety of perspectives. Some participants expressed their preference for being in a male-only NDPP due to it being more comfortable, describing that the vulnerability and embarrassment tied to discussing health topics would have made it difficult to open up around women. They shared sentiments such as “it’s better when men could talk with men, because they could express themselves a little more… if a woman is around, men don’t actually open up,” [#7] and “from like a safe space perspective, I think that [the group format] was really good… some guys were sharing about sexual issues, performance issues, body image issues…” [#21].

On the other hand, some participants noted that having a men-only NDPP would have had no difference to their experience, or even a negative impact. These participants expressed sentiments that pre-diabetes was something that “affects men and women alike” and therefore did not play a role in the social aspect of the NDPP [#18]. Some even noted that being in a mixed-gendered NDPP allowed them to experience different perspectives, which they found to be beneficial: “I didn’t think I was going to be comfortable with the women, but it was good… because you hear different sides, women have good ideas too, and especially in the way they prepare the meals” [#6]. One participant felt that interactions in the men-only group were sometimes uncomfortable: “It was interesting to hear about the way that they would speak about women. I think I might personally have benefited more from something that was more gender neutral.” [#21]

Participants were also asked about their NDPP experience in relation to cultural tailoring. They mentioned how they felt the dieting recommendations and curriculum acknowledged the eating habits and culture of Hispanics and provided information on healthier alternatives: “Our eating habits is our culture. But there’s other recipes: there’s cauliflower rice… whole wheat pasta. We can still stay true to our culture, but in a more healthy way.” [#15]. Many answered that they felt that the NDPP was helpful for them as Hispanic men and also acknowledged the benefit of tailoring the NDPP for other cultures as well. Lastly, participants highlighted the benefits of participating in a NDPP delivered in Spanish, echoing how it made them comfortable and able to communicate their concerns better.

Challenges engaging with the NDPP (COM-B: capability, opportunity; TDF: skills, resources)

Participants described the virtual format of the NDPP as a challenging component to navigate, both with technological difficulties, such as internet connection problems, and social connection issues. Although the virtual style helped some attend remotely, many described that the impersonal nature of meeting online often felt weird and made it difficult to “establish a friendship in person” [#5]. Participants also noted that contacting the NDPP could be challenging, and that although they wanted to recommend friends or family to the programme, they did not know how others could enrol in the NDPP, particularly because participants had been enrolled through direct outreach from the study.

Outside of NDPP logistics, participants found it difficult to engage with the lifestyle change expected from the NDPP due to outside factors such as finances and schedules. Participants described how eating unhealthily, such as at fast food restaurants, is often more convenient as well as cheaper than eating healthy: “I can go to McDonald’s and get a full meal for like $5 bucks,” in addition to gym memberships being out of budget [#21]. Work schedules and locations also made it difficult to participate in NDPP meetings and limited time available for lifestyle behaviours such as finding time to exercise. Lastly, they explained that medical appointments could conflict with NDPP meetings, creating a conflict in their engagement.

Discussion

This qualitative study offers additional insight into the motivations and experiences of Hispanic men engaged in a virtually delivered NDPP. Their perspectives about the programme, including their a priori concerns about its structure and utility, their appreciation for the knowledge and skills attained from it, and their experiences interacting with others and with the virtual format reveal several opportunities to enhance recruitment, engagement and retention in this historically underrepresented population. Moreover, our findings highlight some of the factors that participants appreciated most from the programme, including the abundance of supplemental resources, safe and diverse opportunities to interact with others, guided coaching focusing on prevention, and content tailored to cultural preferences and linguistic needs. These insights inform how to potentially deliver the most vital and high-yield components of the NDPP to those who may not be able to attend this intensive intervention. A central theme that emerged regarding initially participating in the NDPP was the role the fear of diabetes had as a catalyst for inspiring interest in the programme. This echoes prior reports of motivations for engaging in diabetes prevention present among Hispanic men, including fear of having to inject insulin, which emerged in our study as well.10 11 However, despite enrolling in the NDPP, Hispanic men in our study expressed not knowing what to expect from the programme, underscoring a critical gap in NDPP marketing, particularly to Hispanic men. Current outreach may lack cultural resonance and may insufficiently provide a clear picture of what the programme entails. Advertising strategies should consider harnessing themes that emerged in our findings, such as the relief felt from learning how to prevent diabetes and the desire to want to incorporate family members, framing participation not just as disease prevention but as an act of caring for one’s household and self. For men, who often cite self-sufficiency as their reason for not engaging in weight management interventions, peer testimonials may further build relevance and trust to encourage engagement with the programme.18

Social connection within the NDPP groups emerged as a salient driver of engagement in the programme, underscoring the programme’s roots in social cognitive theory.13 26 Participants described a sense of accountability and motivation stemming from peer support and facilitator check-ins. Considering previous research shows that social influences are likely to influence lifestyle behavioural change, such as dieting and physical exercise, further facilitation of interactions within the diabetes prevention programme may assist in reinforcing continuous NDPP engagement.27,29 In addition to offering more structured opportunities for peer interaction, incorporating family and friends into NDPPs, as recommended by participants, is worth considering and is being evaluated in more recent trials.27 Leveraging novel and promising strategies, such as incorporating family or social units into the programme has shown promise in boosting retention in lifestyle change programmes among Hispanic men and their families, although these studies have primarily focused on the health outcomes of children.17 30 31 Future studies should aim to identify which specific family members or friends most effectively encourage programme engagement and facilitate behaviour change among Hispanic men.

Although the NDPP has incorporated some linguistic and cultural tailoring, which was perceived favourably by participants in this study, our findings highlight the potential benefits of expanding this tailoring approach beyond culture and beyond curriculum content. Specifically, there appear to be benefits to tailoring the NDPP group structure to meet the gender-related preferences of Hispanic men. Participants felt that men-only groups created crucial safe spaces for Hispanic men to talk about vulnerable weight-related health topics openly and increased collegiality within the group. Although they had some drawbacks, including limiting the diversity of perspectives shared, the men-only group format was generally well-received by Hispanic men in this study. Notably, when discussing the benefits of these groups, participants focused on the emotional safety of these groups but did not discuss gender differences in attitude and behaviours related to weight or weight management. An abundance of research suggests men have different perceptions of body image compared with women, and that they feel weight loss programmes are designed to address the needs of women and not of men.32,34 Together, these findings theoretically support tailoring of lifestyle interventions to meet the unique preferences of men, such as through men-only NDPP groups; however, future research should evaluate other ways that the group composition of the NDPP can be leveraged to effectively address the intersecting gender-related and cultural preferences of Hispanic men, such as through direct pairing of individuals in the group or by directly addressing cultural masculinity ideals.35

While men-only groups may offer unique benefits that potentially improve participation and improve the quality of engagement in the NDPP, this tailoring strategy offers several challenges related to its feasibility and generalisability. Hispanic men from the current study were recruited from a randomised controlled trial that attempted to contact 11 052 seemingly NDPP-eligible men and ultimately randomised 303 participants.19 Ultimately, it is unclear whether these efforts are practical outside of a research setting. Future research should aim to explore alternative ways to provide the benefits of men-only groups (ie, similar feelings of collegiality and emotional safety within NDPP groups) while maximising feasibility and sustainability. For example, traditional NDPP groups may be augmented with supplemental materials and resources that are tailored to individual preferences. NDPP participants can also be offered opportunities to interact with additional identity-specific affinity groups, through virtual formats or group messaging. These strategies may offer opportunities to interact with both diverse perspectives and with individuals with similar characteristics, while facilitating feasibility.

Technology-enabled platforms, including messaging apps or virtual check-ins, could support continual engagement in the programme, especially when in-person attendance is difficult. Hispanic men in this study felt that the mode of programme delivery shaped their engagement: while some appreciated the convenience of virtual formats, others struggled with technological barriers or yearned for more traditional in-person interactions. One approach to addressing these varied preferences may be offering hybrid or modular formats, with the ability to personalise frequency and modality of sessions (ie, in person vs virtual); this may improve accessibility without sacrificing the quality of interactions. The recent HOMBRE trial provided Hispanic men a choice among three options for engaging in a weight loss intervention: coach-facilitated group sessions using online video conferencing, coach-facilitated group sessions in person and prerecorded videos of group sessions available online.13 Although this flexibility did not yield a statistically significant difference in outcomes compared with a minimally intensive control group at 18 months, participants had relatively high education (79.7% had at least some college experience). There remains a need to assess whether such flexibility can still yield clinically meaningful outcomes in a more generalisable sample of Hispanic men.

Finally, participants’ reflections on their NDPP involvement revealed a need for sustained support beyond the core NDPP curriculum. Ongoing access to materials, reinforcement of learnt skills, and continued connection with programme staff or peers may help maintain healthy behaviours post-programme. Although the NDPP itself is a finite programme, community health workers or peer leaders could play a key role in behavioural sustainment, and have previously been found to be effective methods for engaging participants in health-related behaviour change.26 Nonetheless, future research would be needed to assess how to best integrate community health workers and peer leaders into diabetes prevention care. Participants also mentioned several barriers to enacting lifestyle changes, including the unavailability of low-cost convenient healthy food options and gym memberships, reflecting structural determinants of health such as economic stability and neighbourhood built environment; these barriers may need to be addressed through supplemental resources, such as through the provision of food vouchers or gym memberships.

Limitations

This study, while sampling individuals from a randomised controlled trial, is exploratory and not designed to quantify the relative importance of specific barriers or facilitators to engagement. Participants may not reflect the broader population of Hispanic men, specifically those not engaged in care, as recruitment centred in primary care sites part of a health system primarily located in New York City’s Bronx borough. Finally, socio-demographic or clinical characteristics, including BMI data and other health indicators, were not incorporated to contextualise participants’ responses, which may limit the depth of interpretation regarding how these factors influence engagement. Furthermore, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses.

Conclusion

This theory-informed qualitative study provides valuable insights into the beliefs, motivations, and experiences of Hispanic men engaged in the NDPP, and informs future development and optimisation of strategies to improve recruitment and retention within the programme. Potential strategies to enhance engagement include increasing access to programme materials and resources early on in the programme, fostering connection and encouraging interaction that addresses participants’ unique preferences, and increasing the cultural and gender relevance of recruitment and of programme content. These findings support the need for more person-centred adaptations and targeted recruitment efforts to better serve Hispanic men at risk for diabetes.
