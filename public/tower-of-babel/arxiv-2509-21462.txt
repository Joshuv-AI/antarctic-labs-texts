Entanglement sharing schemes
Zahra Khanian, Dongjin Lee, Debbie Leung, Zhi Li, Alex May, Takato Mori, Stanley Miao, Farzin Salek, Jinmin Yi, Beni Yoshida

Abstract
We ask how quantum correlations can be distributed among many subsystems. To address this, we define entanglement sharing schemes (ESS) where certain pairs of subsystems allow entanglement to be recovered via local operations, while other pairs must not. ESS schemes come in two variants, one where the partner system with which entanglement should be prepared is known, and one where it is not. In the case of known partners, we fully characterize the access structures realizable for ESS when using stabilizer states, and construct efficient schemes for threshold access structures, and give a conjecture for the access structures realizable with general states. In the unknown partner case, we again give a complete characterization in the stabilizer setting, additionally give a complete characterization of the case where there are no restrictions on unauthorized pairs, and we prove a set of necessary conditions on general schemes which we conjecture are also sufficient. Finally, we give an application of the theory of entanglement sharing to resolve an open problem related to the distribution of entanglement in response to time-sensitive requests in quantum networks.

To set the stage for our work, it is helpful to recall the framework of quantum secret sharing (QSS)

Secret sharing is a fundamental tool in both classical and quantum cryptography.
The setting is as follows.
A designated dealer is given an unknown quantum state
\ket{\psi}

The dealer then applies an encoding map that distributes

The encoding is required to respect an

\ket{\psi}

Beyond its cryptographic applications, quantum secret sharing can also be viewed more fundamentally as a framework for describing how quantum information is distributed across multiple subsystems.
It addresses the question: under what conditions can the same quantum information be stored within authorized collections of subsystems
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
while remaining inaccessible to unauthorized collections
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}\in\mathcal{U}
?

Building on this perspective, we ask how
quantum correlations
can be distributed among many subsystems. To formalize this idea, we introduce the notion of a
pair access structure
.
Such a structure consists of a set of authorized pairs
\mathcal{A}
and a set of unauthorized pairs
\mathcal{U}
. Each pair consists of two disjoint subsets
T_{i},T_{j}
of the full set of parties
S
.
For authorized pairs
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{A}
, there should exist local operations that recover a system
A
from
T_{i}
and a system
B
from
T_{j}
, such that
AB
is prepared in the desired quantum state
|\psi\rangle_{AB}
.
For unauthorized pairs, no such process should yield output systems
C
and
D
and a state that approximates
|\psi\rangle_{CD}
to high fidelity.

Within this framework, several natural variants arise.
First, the target state
\psi
(which has two subsystems) may either be known to all parties in advance or be an unknown state provided as input to the scheme.
In this work we focus on the case where the state is known, which we refer to as an
entanglement sharing scheme
(ESS).
Note that in quantum secret sharing the known-state case is trivial, since any subsets can always prepare the known state and hence no nontrivial unauthorized subsets can exist.
By contrast, in the setting of correlations the situation is more subtle: the monogamy of entanglement and the impossibility of generating entanglement between non-communicating parties together impose strong restrictions, enabling the existence of unauthorized pairs and permitting a wide variety of nontrivial pair access structures.

Another subtlety in the setting of pair access structures is whether parties know which other set of parties they are expected to share entanglement with.
As a simple example, consider three parties
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and
P_{3}
, where an EPR pair is distributed between
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
and a second EPR pair between
P_{1}
and
P_{3}
.
If the partner is known, we may realize an access structure such that both
\{\{P_{1}\},\{P_{2}\}\}\in\mathcal{A}
and
\{\{P_{1}\},\{P_{3}\}\}\in\mathcal{A}
, since once
P_{1}
is told which partner to be entangled with, it can simply return the corresponding subsystem.
By contrast, if the partner is unknown,
P_{1}
only knows that they must return a system entangled with someone, but not with whom.
The monogamy of entanglement then prevents
P_{1}
from succeeding, and thus this access structure cannot be realized in the unknown-partner setting.

In this work, we study the access structures achievable in entanglement sharing schemes under both the known-partner and unknown-partner settings.
We provide a set of necessary conditions in each case.
Within the class of stabilizer states, we fully characterize the realizable access structures, and we present an explicit example showing that non-stabilizer constructions enable additional structures to be realized.
In addition, we construct schemes that realize general access structures under a weakened notion of security, where unauthorized pairs may become nearly authorized in a sense that we make precise.
A complete understanding of the strong setting remains an open problem.
In particular, we leave for future work a full characterization of the non-stabilizer case under the strong notion of security.

To illustrate the quantum states underlying our constructions, consider the following example, in the known partner setting, of an entanglement sharing scheme.
The scheme distributes an entangled state among five parties, each holding a 5-dimensional qudit:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}_{ABCDE}=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{k,s}\ket{k}_{A}\ket{k+s}_{B}\ket{k+2s}_{C}\ket{k+3s}_{D}\ket{k+4s}_{E},

Background and preliminaries

2.1
Quantum information tools

We define the von Neumann entropy of a density matrix
\rho_{A}
as

\displaystyle S(A)_{\rho}=-\text{tr}(\rho_{A}\log\rho_{A}).

(4)

The conditional quantum mutual information is

\displaystyle I(A:C|B)_{\rho}=S(AB)_{\rho}+S(BC)_{\rho}-S(B)_{\rho}-S(ABC)_{\rho}.

(5)

The von Neumann entropy satisfies strong subadditivity, which means

\displaystyle I(A:C|B)_{\rho}\geq 0.

(6)

The squashed entanglement is defined as

E_{sq}(A:B)_{\rho}=\min_{\sigma_{ABC}:\Tr_{C}(\sigma)=\rho}\frac{1}{2}I(A:B|C).

(7)

It satisfies:

E_{D}(A:B)_{\rho}\leq E_{sq}(A:B)_{\rho}\leq\frac{1}{2}I(A:B)_{\rho}\leq S(A)_{\rho},

(8)

where
E_{D}
is the distillable entanglement.
Moreover, the squashed entanglement satisfies the monogamy inequality
[
6
]
: for any
\rho_{ABC}
,

E_{sq}(A:B)_{\rho}+E_{sq}(A:C)_{\rho}\leq E_{sq}(A:BC)_{\rho}.

(9)

The relative entropy of entanglement is defined as

\displaystyle E_{R}(A:B)_{\rho}=\min_{\sigma_{AB}\in SEP_{AB}}D(\rho_{AB}||\sigma_{AB}),

(10)

where
D(\rho||\sigma)=\Tr(\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma)
is the quantum relative entropy and
SEP_{AB}
is the set of separable states on
AB
.
The relative entropy of entanglement satisfies the following continuity property
[
7
]
,

\displaystyle E_{R}(AA^{\prime}:B)_{\rho}\leq E_{R}(A^{\prime}:B)_{\rho}+2\log d_{A}.

(11)

2.2
Quantum secret sharing

The setting we study can be viewed as a bipartite analogue of the traditional framework of quantum secret sharing, which also proves to be a useful tool in the bipartite case.
For clarity, we briefly recall the definition of quantum secret sharing and some basic facts about access structures.

Definition 1

Consider a set
\Gamma=\{S_{1},\cdots,S_{n}\}
of
n
parties.
An
access structure

\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})
on
\Gamma
consists of an authorized set
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}:A_{i}\subset\Gamma\}_{i}
and an unauthorized set
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{l}:U_{l}\subset\Gamma\}_{l}
. Each party
S_{j}
holds a system which we denote also by
S_{j}
and is called "a share".

A
threshold access structure
is a special case of particular practical interest.
Given an integer
1\leq k\leq n
, all subsets of
k
or more parties are required to be authorized, while all subsets of size less than
k
are required to be unauthorized.

Next, we define a quantum secret sharing scheme
[
4
]
.
Here and throughout the paper, for a quantum channel
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow BC}
, we denote
\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow B}:=\text{tr}_{C}\circ\mathcal{N}_{A\rightarrow BC}
.

Definition 2

A
quantum secret sharing scheme
realizing access structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})
on
n

\mathcal{E}_{Q\rightarrow S_{1}...S_{n}}
such that

•

For all

, there exists a decoding map
\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}\rightarrow Q}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{A_{i}\rightarrow Q}\circ\mathcal{E}_{Q\rightarrow A_{i}}=\mathcal{I}_{Q\rightarrow Q},

(12)

where
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity channel.

•

For all
U_{l}\in\mathcal{U}
, there exists a state preparation channel
P_{\emptyset\rightarrow Q}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{Q\rightarrow U_{l}}=P_{\emptyset\rightarrow Q}\circ{tr}_{Q}.

(13)

There are efficient constructions of threshold quantum secret sharing schemes
[
4
]
.
These constructions use shares whose dimension matches that of the secret, which is known to be optimal.
To see why, we review the following notion of a significant share.

Definition 3

A share
S_{j}
in an access structure
\mathcal{S}
is called a

if there exist an authorized set
A_{i}
and an unauthorized set
U_{l}
such that
U_{l\;}\cup\{S_{j}\}=A_{i}
.

We now state a lemma that constrains the size of significant shares. For completeness, we reproduce the proof here (see also
[
5
,
8
]
).

Lemma 4

Any significant share must have dimension at least that of the secret.

Proof.

Consider preparing the system
Q
in a maximally entangled state with reference
R
.
First, notice that for any authorized set, in particular
A_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle I(R:A_{i})=2n_{Q}

(14)

where
n_{Q}=\log\dim Q
.
This follows from
eq.

12
along with the data processing inequality: there is a decoder acting on
A_{i}
which recovers the maximally entangled state with
R
from
A_{i}
, so
A_{i}
already must be maximally correlated with
R
.
Then, notice that

\displaystyle I(R:U_{l})=0

(15)

since
eq.

13
requires
R
and
U_{l}
form a product state.
Then using the entropy inequality,

\displaystyle I(R:AB)\leq I(R:A)+2S(B)\leq I(R:A)+2\log\dim B

(16)

with
B=S_{j}
,

, we are done.

We call an access structure
valid
if there exists a secret sharing scheme that realizes it.
Not every access structure is valid. Two necessary conditions are:

•

Monotonicity:
If
A\subseteq X
and
A
is authorized, then
X
must also be authorized.

•

No-cloning:
If
A
and
A^{\prime}
are both authorized, then
A\cap A^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

The first condition is immediate: if recovery is possible from a smaller set, then it is also possible from any larger set by, for instance, discarding the additional systems and applying the recovery channel for the smaller set.
The second condition follows directly from the no-cloning theorem: if two disjoint authorized sets existed, each could independently reconstruct the secret, thereby producing two copies.

Remarkably, these two conditions are not only necessary but also sufficient for an access structure to be valid.
Explicit constructions of secret sharing schemes for arbitrary access structures can be found in
[
5
,
9
]
.
We also provide in
appendix

B
an alternative construction that establishes sufficiency.

2.3
Pair access structures

A (quantum) secret sharing scheme is characterized by a collection of authorized sets and a collection of unauthorized sets.
In the bipartite case, we need a somewhat more complicated object to characterize an access structure.

Definition 5

Consider a set
\Gamma=\{S_{1},\cdots,S_{n}\}
of
n
parties. A
pair access structure

\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})
on
n
parties consists of a set of authorized pairs
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{k}\}
, and a set of unauthorized pairs
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{\ell}\}
.
Each
A_{k}
or
U_{\ell}
is an unordered pair of two subsets,
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}
, with
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
subsets of
\Gamma
. We require that any pairing
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}
occurring as an authorized or unauthorized set be disjoint:
T_{i}\cap T_{j}=\emptyset
.

Further, we introduce a partial ordering on pairs as follows.

Definition 6

Given two pairs
X=\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
,
Y=\{T_{3},T_{4}\}
, we say
X\preceq Y
if at least one of the following is true:

•

T_{1}\subseteq T_{3}
and
T_{2}\subseteq T_{4}

•

T_{1}\subseteq T_{4}
and
T_{2}\subseteq T_{3}

We say
X\prec Y
if
X\preceq Y
and
X\neq Y
.

With this partial ordering in hand, we can give a definition of monotonicity of a pair access structure.

Definition 7

We say that an access structure
\mathcal{A}
satisfies
monotonicity
if for all pairs
A
and
A^{\prime}
, whenever
A\in\mathcal{A}
and
A\preceq A^{\prime}
we also have
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
.

A special case of a pair access structure is a threshold structure, which has the property that all pairs which are large enough are authorized.
To define this precisely, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 8

A pairing
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}
is said to have
type

\{q,p\}
if
\{|T_{i}|,|T_{j}|\}=\{p,q\}
.

Now we can define a threshold pair access structure.

Definition 9

A pair access structure
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})
is said to be a type-
((p,q,n))

threshold structure
if it is a pair access structure on
n
parties such that:

•

All pairs of type
\{p,q\}
are authorized.

•

All pairs

such that there exists a pairing
K
of type
\{p,q\}
with
K\preceq J
are authorized.

•

All pairs
J
such that there exists a
K
of type
\{p,q\}
with

are unauthorized.

As an example, a
((2,2,5))
pair access structure has all pairings of type
\{2,2\}
and
\{2,3\}
authorized, and all pairings of type
\{1,1\}
,
\{1,2\}
unauthorized.
Pairings of type
\{1,3\}
and
\{1,4\}
are incomparable to any of type
\{2,2\}
, so the structure does not place a requirement on them (they may be authorized or unauthorized).

We also use a few more definitions related to pair access structures.

Definition 10

Given a pair authorized set
\mathcal{A}
, its

\cup\mathcal{A}
is defined as:

\cup\mathcal{A}=\{T\,|\,\exists\,T^{\prime}\,\text{s.t. }\{T,T^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{A}\}.

(17)

In other words, we collect all subsets (of the party set)

such that

occurs in an authorized pair.

In some settings it will be helpful to view the authorized sets of an access structure as a graph, with the subsets

labelling the vertices and edges added for each authorized pairing.

Definition 11

The

is the undirected graph with vertex set

, and edge set

:
it includes a vertex

for each subset

that occurs in an

, and an edge
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
for each authorized pair
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 12

A graph has no odd cycles if and only if it is bipartite.

2.4
Stabilizer codes

We review some useful facts about stabilizer codes here, as well as the construction of a specific class of stabilizer codes known as Reed-Solomon codes.
We consider quantum systems consisting of
p
-dimensional qudits, where
p
is a prime number.
In this setting,
X
and
Z
operators are defined by

X\ket{j}=\ket{j+1},~~Z\ket{j}=\omega^{j}\ket{j},

(18)

where
\omega=e^{i2\pi/p}
is a
p
-th root of unity, and these operators satisfy
ZX=\omega XZ
.

In the stabilizer formalism, we begin with a set of mutually commuting Pauli operators that generate an Abelian group
S
, called the stabilizer group, such that
e^{i\phi}I\notin S
for any
e^{i\phi}\neq 1
.
The corresponding stabilizer state
\rho
is defined as the maximally mixed state supported on the common eigenspace of all stabilizers
g\in S
with eigenvalue
+1
.
We call the latter subspace the stabilizer code associate with
S
.

We will use a special class of stabilizer codes called Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) codes.
A CSS code is specified by two classical linear codes
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
satisfying
C_{2}^{\perp}\subseteq C_{1}
.
Denoting the parity check matrices of the classical codes as
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, the condition is equivalent to
H_{1}H_{2}^{T}=0
.
The stabilizer group of the CSS code is specified by the following symplectic representation via a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&H_{1}\\
H_{2}&0\end{array}\right).

More precisely, there are two types of stabilizer generators,
Z
-type and
X
-type, defined as:

\displaystyle S^{Z}_{i}=Z(H_{1,i})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(Z_{j})^{H_{1,ij}},

(21)

\displaystyle S^{X}_{i}=X(H_{2,i})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j})^{H_{2,ij}},

(22)

where the
H_{a,i}
in the middle denotes the
i
-th row of
H_{a}
;
Z_{j}
and
X_{j}
denote the Pauli
Z
and
X
matrices on qudit
j
, respectively.
The condition
H_{1}H_{2}^{T}=0
ensures that the
Z
- and
X
-type stabilizers commute.
Conversely, a collection of mutually commuting
X
- and
Z
-type stabilizers gives rise to a CSS code.

The dimension of the code subspace is given by
|C_{1}|/|C_{2}^{\perp}|
, with a basis given by the following coset states:

\displaystyle\ket{x+C_2^\perp}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C_{2}^{\perp}|}}\sum_{y\in C_{2}^{\perp}}\ket{x+y},

(23)

where
x\in C_{1}
.
They are indeed invariant under the
S^{Z}_{i}
and
S^{X}_{i}
defined above.
In fact, each term in the summation is a codeword of
C_{1}
, hence invariant under
S_{i}^{Z}
.
For

, it shifts each
\ket{x}
to
\ket{x+y_0}

is a fixed element in

, hence the summation is invariant under

.

There are two types of logical operators for a CSS code:
Z
-type and
X
-type.
They correspond to classical codewords
\bar{z}\in C_{2}\setminus C_{1}^{\perp}

\bar{x}\in C_{1}\setminus C_{2}^{\perp}

\displaystyle L^{Z}

\displaystyle=Z(\bar{z})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(Z_{j})^{\bar{z}_{j}},

(24)

\displaystyle L^{X}

\displaystyle=X(\bar{x})=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j})^{\bar{x}_{j}}.

(25)

The states defined in

23
are eigenstates of all
Z
-type logical operators.
On the other hand, the following state, obtained by superposing all the states in
eq.

23
, is an eigenstate of every
X
-type logical operator with eigenvalue
1
:

\displaystyle\ket{C_1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|C_{1}|}}\sum_{x\in C_{1}}\ket{x}.

(26)

In this paper we frequently use a special class of CSS codes, namely the quantum Reed–Solomon codes. Here we record only the definitions and properties that will be needed; a more detailed account, including proofs, is provided in
appendix

A
.

An
[[n,k]]_{p}
quantum Reed–Solomon code, assuming
p\geq n
, is specified by
n
distinct elements
x_{0},\cdots,x_{n-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
and a parameter
r
such that
1\leq r\leq n-k
.
Define a family of polynomials
f_{c,s}(x)
, where
c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}
and
s\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{k}
, as follows:

f_{c,s}(x)=c_{0}+c_{1}x+\cdots+c_{r-1}x^{r-1}+s_{0}x^{r}+\cdots+s_{k-1}x^{r+k-1}.

(27)

The code states are then defined as:

\ket{\bar{s}}=\frac{1}{p^{r/2}}\sum_{c\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{r}}\ket{f_{c,s}(x_0)}\otimes\ket{f_{c,s}(x_1)}\cdots\otimes\ket{f_{c,s}(x_{n-1})}.

(28)

For our purpose, we need the following result on its code distance.

Lemma 13

For the quantum Reed–Solomon code defined above,
any set of
r+1
qudits supports a nontrivial
Z
-type logical operator; no set of
r
qudits supports a
Z
-type logical operator or stabilizer operator.
Similarly, any set of
n-(r+k)+1
qudits supports a nontrivial
X
-type logical operator; no set of
n-(r+k)
qudits supports an
X
-type logical operator or stabilizer operator.

In particular, the code has the following code distance:

d=\min\{d_{X},d_{Z}\},~~d_{Z}=r+1,~~d_{X}=n-r-k+1.

(29)

3
Entanglement sharing schemes with a known partner

We first consider the setting with known partners.
Namely, given a pairing
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}
, the party holding
T_{i}
knows they are trying to prepare a state shared with
T_{j}
, and vice versa.
We focus on the case where the to-be-recovered state is a maximally entangled state, the EPR state, which we label
\Psi^{+}
.

3.1
Definition and first example

We define our setting more precisely below.

Definition 14

We say a state
\ket{\Psi}_{S_{1}...S_{n}}
is an
entanglement sharing scheme (ESS) with a known partner
and with pair access structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{U})
if:

1.

For each
A=\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{A}
, there is a channel
\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow ab}=\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{i}\rightarrow a}\otimes\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{j}\rightarrow b}

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow ab}(\Psi_{T_{i}T_{j}})=\Psi^{+}_{ab};

(30)

2.

For each
U=\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{U}
, for all
\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow ab}=\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{i}\rightarrow a}\otimes\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{j}\rightarrow b}
,

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{N}^{ij}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow ab}(\Psi_{T_{i}T_{j}}),\Psi^{+}_{ab})\leq 1-\delta

(31)

with
\delta>0
.

Here,
a
and
b
denote output systems, corresponding to the local operations applied by
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
, respectively.
We do not fix the dimensions of
a
and
b
in this definition, since they may vary in different scenarios.
The double index on the channels
\mathcal{N}^{ij}
indicates that each party is aware of the identity of its partner in the pairing, so their local operations can depend on the other party.

There are many possible variants of our definition, depending on how one chooses to formalize the notions of being authorized or unauthorized.
For instance, one could instead require that authorized pairs exhibit high entanglement according to a chosen entanglement measure, while unauthorized pairs are restricted to separable states.
This definition is not directly comparable to ours: our condition on authorized pairs is stronger, while our condition on unauthorized pairs is weaker.
Fortunately, several of our constructions are strong enough to satisfy the more stringent requirements as well: for threshold schemes, we explicitly construct examples where authorized pairs can generate a specific maximally entangled state, while unauthorized pairs remain separable.

As a first example, let us revisit the entanglement sharing scheme for the
((2,2,5))
threshold access structure described in the introduction.
This scheme is realized by the state

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}_{ABCDE}=\sum_{k,s}\ket{k}_{A}\ket{k+s}_{B}\ket{k+2s}_{C}\ket{k+3s}_{D}\ket{k+4s}_{E}

(32)

with local dimension
5
and addition and multiplication over
\mathbb{F}_{5}
.
We will omit some unimportant normalization for simplicity.
To verify that this realizes a
((2,2,5))
scheme, we first check that every pair of size
{2,2}
is authorized.
Suppose, for concreteness, that Alice holds subsystems
AB
and Bob holds
CD
.
By a local relabeling, they can map the state to

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}_{ABCDE}\to\sum_{k,s}\ket{s}_{A}\ket{k}_{B}\ket{s}_{C}\ket{k}_{D}\ket{k+4s}_{E}.

(33)

Then, since they both know the identity of the other subset in the pair, they know the
E
system is the one not involved.
This means they can act locally again to obtain

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle\to\sum_{k,s}\ket{s}_{A}\ket{k+4s}_{B}\ket{s}_{C}\ket{k+4s}_{D}\ket{k+4s}_{E}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}\ket{s}_{A}\ket{s}_{C}\sum_{k}\ket{k+4s}_{B}\ket{k+4s}_{D}\ket{k+4s}_{E}

\displaystyle=\ket{\Psi^+}_{AC}\ket{GHZ}_{BDE}.

(34)

In particular, Alice and Bob obtain a maximally entangled state between their subsystems.
An analogous procedure shows that all other pairs of type
{2,2}
are authorized as well.
We emphasize that the decoding operation producing the EPR pair necessarily depends on the identity of the excluded subsystem, as seen in the second step above.

We can also verify the type
\{1,2\}
pairs are unauthorized.
To do this it suffices to notice that since in that setting we trace out two systems, the environment learns both
k
and
s
, decohering the superposition in the state and preventing recovery of entanglement.
More explicitly, the marginal on (for example) ABC is

\displaystyle\Psi_{ABC}=\sum_{k,s}\outerproduct{k}{k}\otimes\outerproduct{k+s}{k+s}\otimes\outerproduct{k+2s}{k+2s}

(35)

which is separable across
A
and
BC
.
To make this more apparent, it is useful to have Bob apply a local unitary to obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{ABC}=\sum_{k,s}\outerproduct{k}{k}\otimes\outerproduct{k}{k}\otimes\outerproduct{s}{s},

(36)

so that Alice and Bob only share a bit of classical randomness.

3.2
Necessary conditions

The monotonicity property given as definition
7
is a necessary condition for an ESS scheme with a known partner.
We formalize this as a lemma.

Lemma 15
(monotonicity)

The access structure for an entanglement sharing scheme with a known partner must satisfy monotonicity.

The proof is almost trivial.
Assume
\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
is authorized and that
\{T_{1},T_{2}\}\preceq\{T_{3},T_{4}\}
. Without loss of generality, this means
T_{1}\subseteq T_{3}
and
T_{2}\subseteq T_{4}
.
Now Alice, who holds
T_{3}
, can simply restrict attention to the registers in
T_{1}
; Bob, who holds
T_{4}
, does the same with
T_{2}
.
Thus they can recover whenever
\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
can, and it follows immediately that
\{T_{3},T_{4}\}
is also authorized.

Let us emphasize that Alice and Bob need to agree on which smaller pairing to trace down to before recovering and to include this as part of the specification of the scheme, since they cannot coordinate their actions after the state for the ESS scheme is distributed.
To coordinate this, Alice and Bob also need to know the identity of the full pairing
\{T_{3},T_{4}\}
, not just their respective subsystems, so this proof only applies in the known partner case.
In fact, we will see that in the unknown partner case monotonicity no longer holds.

3.3
Threshold schemes

In this section we analyze special cases of pair access structures that admit efficient constructions.
We begin with
((r,r,3r-1))
schemes, which have particularly simple realizations, and then address the more general
((p,q,p+2q-1))
schemes where
p
and
q
need not coincide (we always take
p\geq q
).

(r,r,3r-1)
Entanglement sharing schemes

Our first threshold construction is inspired by an observation about the
((2,2,5))
scheme introduced earlier.
The state
eq.

32
may be viewed as a superposition over values in a
(2,5)
classical secret sharing scheme, which maps

\displaystyle(k,s)\rightarrow(k,k+s,k+2s,k+3s,k+4s).

(37)

Because any two shares can be used to recover
s
, Alice and Bob can each isolate a copy of
\ket{s}
.
Then to recover the EPR pair they additionally need to decouple the remaining systems from
s
.
This is possible because the particular secret sharing scheme above has the stronger property that any two shares determine not only the secret
s
but also the randomness
k
.

Using this idea, we can construct
((r,r,3r-1))
schemes for all
r\geq 1
.
Our construction is based on Shamir’s classical secret sharing scheme
[
10
]
, which yields a
(r,3r-1)
threshold structure such that the local dimension of the entangled state is the same as the local dimension of each share.

In Shamir’s scheme, we pick a degree
(r-1)
polynomial
3
3

3

Note that there is a notational mismatch between this equation and
eq.

37
. However, the computation is essentially equivalent.
,

\displaystyle F_{\vec{a}}(x)=a_{r-1}x^{r-1}+a_{r-2}x^{r-2}+...+a_{1}x+s.

(38)

We store the secret as
F_{\vec{a}}(0)=s
, and choose the other coefficients at random.
The arithmetic is performed in the field
GF(p)
for
p
any prime power larger than
3r-1
.
We then define the entangled state

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{a},s}\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(1)}...\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(3r-1)},

(39)

with each subsystem having local dimension
p
.
Now suppose that Alice gets
r
subsystems, and Bob gets another
r
subsystems.
For simplicity of notation we will take these to be the first and second set of
r
, but similar arguments work for any two subsets of size
r
.
Then they can act locally to produce the state

\displaystyle\sum_{\vec{a},s}\left(\ket{s}\ket{\vec{a}}\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(2r+1)}...\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(3r-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\otimes\left(\ket{s}\ket{\vec{a}}\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(2r+1)}...\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(3r-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\otimes\left(\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(2r+1)}...\ket{F_{\vec{a}}(3r-1)}\right).

This procedure amounts to coherently reconstructing
\vec{a}
and
s
from the
k
available shares, and then using them to evaluate the polynomial on the coordinates corresponding to the missing shares.
Since this process is reversible, Alice and Bob can implement it in superposition.
Then, notice that the systems storing
s
are in a tensor product state with the rest of the subsystems: the remaining registers hold only
(r-1)
distinct values of
F
, and hence store no information about
s
.
Thus the systems decouple, so that Alice and Bob hold a maximally entangled state.

To see why pairings of type
\{r-1,r\}
or smaller cannot recover an EPR pair, note that in this case the systems that are traced out contain
r
evaluations of the secret sharing polynomial.
From these, the environment can reconstruct both
\vec{a}
and
s
.
Consequently, the remaining systems are left in a classical mixture over the values of
F_{\vec{a},s}(i)
, yielding a separable state.
In particular, such states cannot be used to recover an EPR pair.

Connections with Reed-Solomon codes

The above constructed states are special codewords of quantum Reed-Solomon codes.
Indeed, let us set
r=k=1
in the polynomials
eq.

27
, we get

f_{c,s}(x)=c+sx.

(40)

Then setting
x_{i}=i
for
i=0,\cdots,n-1
, the codeword

26
becomes exactly
eq.

32
.

More generally, redefining
r=r^{\prime}-1
, and setting

and

, we get

f_{c,s}(x)=x^{r^{\prime}-1}F_{\vec{a}}\left(1/x\right),

(41)

where

is defined in
eq.

38
and
\vec{a}
is the reverse of

.
Despite the notational mismatch, the codewords
eq.

26
is still equivalent to
eq.

39
.

((p,q,p+2q-1))
threshold schemes

In the following, we will generalize the above perspective to construct a more general threshold scheme for the case when the size of two parties is not equal.
Namely, we construct a
((p,q,p+2q-1))
entanglement sharing scheme for arbitrary integers

and

.
We assume
p\geq q
.

Consider the Reed-Solomon code with parameters

,

and

.
Since

, we have only one
X
-type logical operator and one

-type logical operator up to equivalence.
We pick the code state

that is stabilized by the

-type logical operator.

Theorem 16

The above defined state
\ket{\Psi}
is a
((p,q,p+2q-1))
threshold scheme.

Proof.

Let us consider arbitrary two parties
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
such that
|T_{1}|=p
,
|T_{2}|=q
and
T_{1}\cap T_{2}=\emptyset
.
Since
|T_{1}\cup T_{2}|=p+q=n-r
, there exists a
X
-type logical operator supported on
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
due to
lemma

13
.
We denote it as
\bar{X}=\bar{X}_{T_{1}}\otimes\bar{X}_{T_{2}}
, where
\bar{X}_{T_{i}}
denotes a Pauli operator supported on
T_{i}
.
By construction, we have:

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\bar{X}|\Psi\rangle=\bar{X}_{T_{1}}\otimes\bar{X}_{T_{2}}|\Psi\rangle.

(42)

Similarly, since both
|T_{1}|,\ |T_{2}|\geq r+1
, both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
support
Z
-type logical operators from
lemma

13
.
We denote them
\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}
and
\bar{Z}_{T_{2}}
.
It follows that
\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}\otimes\bar{Z}_{T_{2}}^{\dagger}
is a stabilizer of the code, hence

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle=\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}\otimes\bar{Z}_{T_{2}}^{\dagger}|\Psi\rangle\ .

(43)

Since
\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}
is a logical operator, we have
\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}\bar{X}=\omega\bar{X}\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}
.
Therefore,

\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}\bar{X}_{T_{1}}=\omega\bar{X}_{T_{1}}\bar{Z}_{T_{1}}.

(44)

Similarly,
\bar{Z}_{T_{2}}^{\dagger}\bar{X}_{T_{2}}=\omega^{-1}\bar{X}_{T_{2}}\bar{Z}_{T_{2}}^{\dagger}
.
An EPR pair distillation strategy is given by a local Clifford unitary
U_{T_{1}}
and
U_{T_{2}}
that transform them to single qudits Pauli operators.

Let us now consider the case when
|T_{1}|+|T_{2}|<p+q
.
From
lemma

13
, they cannot support any
X
-type logical operators or stabilizer operators.
Now, notice that, when reducing the state
\ket{\Psi}
to

, the reduced state can be written as

\displaystyle\rho_{T_{1}T_{2}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{g\in S,\,\text{supp}(g)\in T_{1}\cup T_{2}}g,

(45)

where

is the total dimension of the Hilbert space.
In particular, only
Z
-type operators appear in the summation.
We can simultaneously diagonalize all the terms by working in the
Z
-basis.
Due to the positivity, the
\rho_{T_{1}T_{2}}
is therefore a summation of classical states, which is separable.

3.4
General access structures for stabilizer states

Throughout the section, we will assume that the distributed state is a stabilizer state over
p
dimensional qudits where
p
is a prime number.
Accordingly, we demand a
p
dimensional maximally entangled state is recoverable in the definition of ESS scheme.

While we have shown that monotonicity
lemma

15
is necessary for an ESS scheme, it is not sufficient to guarantee a stabilizer ESS scheme.
As a minimal counterexample, suppose we have three parties
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
and wish to construct a scheme with only the authorized pair
A=\{\{S_{1}\},\{S_{2},S_{3}\}\}
.
However, it is not hard to show that (following the proof of
lemma

18
), for a stabilizer state,
A
is authorized implies at least one of
\{\{S_{2}\},\{S_{1},S_{3}\}\}
and
\{\{S_{3}\},\{S_{1},S_{2}\}\}
must be authorized.
Therefore, we cannot have
\mathcal{A}=\{A\}
, although it satisfies monotonicity.

In the following we discuss an equivalent condition that fully characterize the access structures which can be realized in stabilizer constructions.
We begin with the following notion.

Definition 17

An authorized pair
\{T_{1},T_{2}\}\in\mathcal{A}
is said to be
minimal
if there does not exist another pair
\{T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{A}
such that

\{T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime}\}\prec\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
.

We denote the set of minimal authorized pairs as
\mathcal{A}_{\min}
.

For any minimal pair
A=\{T_{1},T_{2}\}\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
, we construct two matrices
M(A)
and
\tilde{M}(A)
as follows.
First, we consider
\mathcal{U}(A)
, a subset of the unauthorized sets
\mathcal{U}
, defined as:

\mathcal{U}(A)=\{\{T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{U}\,|\,T_{1}^{\prime}\cup T_{2}^{\prime}=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\}.

(46)

Denote
K_{A}=|\mathcal{U}(A)|
,
n_{A}=|T_{1}|+|T_{2}|
.
The matrix
M(A)
will be a
(K_{A}+1)\times n_{A}
matrix, whose row is indexed by elements of
\mathcal{U}(A)
and column is indexed by elements of
T_{1}\cup T_{2}
.
The matrix is defined as follows:

•

For each pair
U=\{T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{U}(A)
, we make it ordered as
(T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2}^{\prime})
in an arbitrary way;

•

For the first

M(A)_{U,s}=1
if and only if party
s
is contained in the first component of pair
U
;

M(A)_{U,s}=0
otherwise.

•

The last row of

is all 1’s.

The matrix
\tilde{M}(A)
will be a
(K_{A}+2)\times n_{A}
matrix, whose first

, and the last row corresponds to the pair

M(A)_{A,s}=1

is contained in

, the first component of pair

.

Lemma 18

\forall A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
we have
\rank M(A)<\rank\tilde{M}(A)
.
Here rank is over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.

Proof.

Since
A\in\mathcal{A}
, there exist two stabilizers

and

\displaystyle V_{T_{1}}W_{T_{1}}=\omega W_{T_{1}}V_{T_{1}}

\displaystyle,~~V_{T_{2}}W_{T_{2}}=\omega^{-1}W_{T_{2}}V_{T_{2}}.

(47)

Let us represent the stabilizer

as a vector

, indexed by elements of

, such that

e^{\frac{2\pi i}{p}x_{s}}=V_{s}W_{s}V_{s}^{-1}W_{s}^{-1},

(48)

where

is the tensor component of

z_{0}x=0,~~z_{A}x=1,

(49)

where

and

For any

In contrast to

\displaystyle V_{T_{1}^{\prime}}W_{T_{1}^{\prime}}=W_{T_{1}^{\prime}}V_{T_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle,~~V_{T_{2}^{\prime}}W_{T_{2}^{\prime}}=W_{T_{2}^{\prime}}V_{T_{2}^{\prime}}.

\displaystyle z_{0}x=0,\,\,\,z_{A}x\neq 0,

We view these additional qudits as organized into

in the

th set of qudits as
T_{1}^{i}
.
After completing this construction, every

is now authorized, since we can use the layer of qudits labelled by

to recover from
A

Furthermore, in each layer of qudits we have that the second line of equation

holds.
Since this is true for each stabilizer
V^{A}
,
W^{A}
, there is no way to construct a pair of stabilizers

W^{\prime}
from these that has the commutation property

\displaystyle V^{\prime}_{T_{1}^{\prime}}W^{\prime}_{T_{1}^{\prime}}=\omega^{\ell}W^{\prime}_{T_{1}^{\prime}}V^{\prime}_{T_{1}^{\prime}},\,\,\,\,V^{\prime}_{T_{2}^{\prime}}W^{\prime}_{T_{2}^{\prime}}=\omega^{-\ell}W^{\prime}_{T_{2}^{\prime}}V^{\prime}_{T_{2}^{\prime}}

Therefore we also have that for every

4
Entanglement sharing schemes with an unknown partner

4.1
Definition and first example

In this section we discuss entanglement sharing schemes in the setting where the partner is unknown.
In other words, a pairing
\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
now means that Alice obtains
T_{1}
but doesn’t know
T_{2}
.
Similarly, Bob obtains
T_{2}
but doesn’t know
T_{1}
.
We state this more carefully below.

Definition 20

We say a state
\Psi_{S_{1}...S_{n}}
is an
entanglement sharing scheme (ESS) with an unknown partner
and with access structure
\mathcal{S}
if the following hold.

1.

There exists a family of channels
\{\mathcal{N}_{T_{i}\rightarrow X_{i}}\}
such that for each
A_{k}=\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}X_{j}}(\Psi_{T_{i}T_{j}})=\Psi^{+}_{X_{i}X_{j}}

(57)

where we define
\mathcal{N}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}X_{j}}=\mathcal{N}_{T_{i}\rightarrow X_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{N}_{T_{j}\rightarrow X_{j}}
.

2.

For each
U_{k}=\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{U}
, all possible channels
\mathcal{M}^{k}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}X_{j}}=\mathcal{M}^{k}_{T_{i}\rightarrow X_{i}}\otimes\mathcal{M}^{k}_{T_{j}\rightarrow X_{j}}
we have

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{M}^{k}_{T_{i}T_{j}\rightarrow X_{i}X_{j}}(\Psi_{T_{i}T_{j}}),\Psi^{+}_{X_{i}X_{j}})\leq 1-\delta.

(58)

for
\delta>0
.

We can illustrate the distinction between entanglement sharing with a known and unknown partner with a simple example.
Consider the access structure with
\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
,
\{S_{1},S_{3}\}
, and
\{S_{1},\{S_{2},S_{3}\}\}
authorized.
This satisfies monotonicity, and it is easy to construct for it an entanglement sharing scheme with a known partner.
However in the context of entanglement summoning with an unknown partner the access structure is clearly impossible to realize: Alice, who holds
S_{1}
, must prepare a system
X_{1}
to return which must be maximally entangled with both
X_{2}
and
X_{3}
, the systems returned by
S_{2}
or
S_{3}
respectively, since she doesn’t know which one she is trying to entangle with.
But the monogamy of entanglement means this is impossible, so there can be no such scheme.

For other access structures, it is initially not clear if they can be realized as an entanglement sharing scheme with unknown partner.
For instance consider a
((2,2,5))
threshold access structure.
Suppose Alice receives shares
\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
.
Then, Bob could receive any of three possible subsets:
\{S_{3},S_{4}\}
,
\{S_{4},S_{5}\}
, or
\{S_{3},S_{5}\}
.
Now there is no obvious violation of monogamy, and in fact we can perform entanglement sharing with an unknown partner in at least these cases by using the following strategy: take a single EPR pair
\Psi_{AB}^{+}
, and encode
A
into a
((2,2))
scheme and
B
into a
((2,3))
scheme.
Then give the shares
A
is encoded into to
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, and the shares
B
is encoded into to
S_{3},S_{4}
and
S_{5}
.
While this realizes some of the authorized sets of the
((2,2,5))
scheme, it’s not clear if all of them can be realized simultaneously.

Before addressing the general question of which access structures can be realized for ESS with an unknown partner, let us first comment on an important subtlety.
In the context of unknown partners, the possibility of distilling an EPR pair from a given authorized pair can depend not just on the state which has been shared, but also on which other authorized pairs are included in the access structure
\mathcal{A}
.
In other words, given a state, the access structure may not be unique.
To illustrate this, consider a case with four parties
1,2,3,4
, and suppose we distribute the state

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^+}_{12}\otimes\ket{\Psi^+}_{34}.

(59)

For this state, we can have an authorized pair
\{\{1,3\},\{2\}\}
or an authorized pair
\{\{1,3\},\{4\}\}
, but we cannot include both in the pair access structure simultaneously.
If we do, someone holding
\{1,3\}
will be unsure if they should output qubit
1
or qubit
3
to prepare entanglement with the partner subsystem.
This means in particular that our access structures for unknown partner ESS may not be monotone.
For instance, suppose we have authorized pairs
\{\{1\},\{2\}\}
,
\{\{3\},\{4\}\}\in\mathcal{A}
.
By monotonicity we would be led to conclude that both
\{\{1,3\},\{2\}\}
and
\{\{1,3\},\{4\}\}
should be authorized as well, but in fact we can only have one of these two in
\mathcal{A}
given the above state.

4.2
Necessary conditions

In this section we develop an understanding of which pair access structures can be realized as entanglement sharing schemes with an unknown partner.
We start by developing a number of necessary conditions on these access structures.

There are two classes of necessary conditions.
First, there are conditions which involve only the pattern of authorized sets.
For example, we argued above that
\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
and
\{S_{1},S_{3}\}
cannot be both authorized if
S_{2}\cap S_{3}=\emptyset
, due to monogamy of entanglement.

Second, there are conditions that appear when we focus on access structures that are
maximal
, i.e., that cannot be extended further.
More precisely, an authorized set
\mathcal{A}
is maximal with respect to a state
\rho
and a valid family of channels
\{\mathcal{N}_{T}\}
, if there does not exist a proper superset
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{A}
and a family of channels
\{\mathcal{N}_{T}\,|\,T\in\cup A^{\prime}\}
that extends (or equals)
\{\mathcal{N}_{T}\}
such that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
and
\{\mathcal{N}_{T}\,|\,T\in\cup A^{\prime}\}
define an ESS scheme with unknown partner for
\rho
.
An analogous maximality condition already appears in quantum secret sharing, which leads to the monotonicity condition.
For ESS with unknown partner, we noted above that monotonicity fails.
Instead, maximality leads to more subtle conditions.

Before proving our necessary conditions, we give the following simple statements.

Lemma 21

Let
d\geq 2
.

1.

There does not exist a
U\in SU(d)
and a nonzero
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
VU\ket{\psi}=UV^{t}\ket{\psi},~\forall\,V\in SU(d)
.

2.

There does not exist a nonzero
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
(V\otimes 1)\ket{\psi}=(1\otimes V)\ket{\psi},~\forall\,V\in SU(d)
.

3.

If
\ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
satisfies
(V\otimes 1)\ket{\psi}=(1\otimes V^{t})\ket{\psi},~\forall\,V\in SU(d)
, then
\ket{\psi}
is proportional to the EPR state.

Proof.

(1) We prove the claim by contradiction. If the claim is true, we first show that we may assume without loss of generality that
U=1
.
We choose
V=WU^{*}

(where
U^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
U
) to obtain

\displaystyle(WU^{*})U\ket{\psi}=U(WU^{*})^{t}\ket{\psi}=UU^{\dagger}W^{t}\ket{\psi}=W^{t}\ket{\psi}.

(60)

We can also apply the claim for
V=U^{*}
in the following,

\displaystyle WU^{*}U\ket{\psi}=W(U^{*}U\ket{\psi})=W(U(U^{*})^{t}\ket{\psi})=W\ket{\psi}.

(61)

Therefore,
W^{*}W\ket{\psi}=\ket{\psi},~\forall\,W\in U(d)
.
Now choose
W=\frac{X+Y}{\sqrt{2}}\oplus\mathbb{1}_{d-2}
, which is indeed a unitary.
We have
W^{*}W=(iZ)\oplus\mathbb{1}_{d-2}
.
Therefore, the first two components of
\ket{\psi}
must be zero.
Repeating the argument for other components, we conclude that
\ket{\psi}=0
, which is a contradiction.

(2) Under the isomorphism between
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
M(d,\mathbb{C})
, the claim is equivalent to the nonexistence of

such that

for all

For

, and then the claim cannot hold for

For

, the claim is proved by inspecting each matrix element and reducing to the

(3) Let
A
be the matrix corresponding to
\ket{\psi}
under the isomorphism between
\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
M(d,\mathbb{C})
.
We have that

for all
V\in SU(d)
.
Since

linearly spans the whole

, which contains only the scalar matrices.
Taking the isomorphism back to

\ket{\psi}\propto\ket{EPR}

For necessary conditions of the first type, we have the following

We prove this by contradiction.

The following two necessary conditions are of the “maximality” type.

Lemma 24
(Transitivity)

If there is a path of edges

Proof.

This in turn implies

Lemma 25
(Weak monotonicity)

If

Assume the contrary: that we have constructed a scheme defined by state

Since also

For later reference, we record another consequence of maximally.

Lemma 26

If

Proof.

Otherwise, we can always add the pair

There is a subtlety in the definition of ESS with known partner: we could demand the players output a fixed EPR state

The next result says that they are in fact equivalent, so long as we require that for a given authorized pair the returned maximally entangled state is fixed.

Lemma 27
(Calibration)

Suppose a distributed state
\Phi_{S_{1}...S_{n}}
can be used to complete an ESS scheme with unknown partner, in the weak sense that the protocol returns a maximally entangled state
\Psi^{i}
for each authorized pair
A_{i}
, but which maximally entangled state may be different for each pair.
Then, the same distributed state
\Phi_{S_{1}...S_{n}}
can be used with a modified protocol to always return the state
\Psi^{+}
for every pairing
A_{i}
.

Proof.

For each connected component of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
, consider a spanning tree and pick a root, then label the vertices of that component by their depth.
We can unitarily conjugate the distillation channels according to the tree structure so that all edges in the tree are calibrated in the sense that they indeed give rise to the state
\ket{\Psi^+}
.
In particular, we leave the recovery channel corresponding to the root unchanged.
Then at the first level, if an edge
(j,k)
corresponds to a returned maximally entangled state
\ket{\Psi_{i,j}}_{T_{j}T_{k}}=U^{i,j}_{T_{j}}\ket{\Psi^+}_{T_{j}T_{k}}

T_{j}
from
\mathcal{N}_{T_{j}\rightarrow X_{j}}
to
U_{X_{j}}^{i,j}\mathcal{N}_{T_{j}\rightarrow X_{j}}(\cdot)(U_{X_{j}}^{i,j})^{\dagger}
, so that the returned state is now
\Psi^{+}
as needed.
In the next layer, if the returned state is initially
V_{T_{\ell}}\ket{\Psi^+}_{T_{j}T_{\ell}}
we conjugate the recovery channel by
V^{\dagger}_{X_{\ell}}U^{*}_{X_{\ell}}
to both undo the correction from the previous layer and make the needed correction for the current layer.
Proceeding in this way we can correctly return
\Psi^{+}
for every authorized pair corresponding to an edge in the spanning tree.

Since not all edges in the given connected component of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
are in the spanning tree, this has only calibrated a subset of the authorized pairs.
To handle the remaining authorized pairs, notice that the proof of
lemma

22
is valid in the weaker definition, so that
G_{\mathcal{A}}
must contain no odd cycles.
This also means
G_{\mathcal{A}}
is bipartite.
This means the edges missing from the spanning tree will never connect layers of the tree with the same parity.
Considering such an edge, there is an odd length path through the spanning tree with the same start and end points.
But then the proof of lemma
24
gives that these pairs are automatically authorized, and in fact authorized in that they return
\ket{\psi^+}
as needed.

4.3
Characterization without unauthorized pairs

In this section we give a characterization of ESS schemes with an unknown partner in the case where there are no unauthorized pair constraints: we only require there to be a set of authorized pairs.
Note that it can still be the case that many pairings are unauthorized, but we don’t put this in as a requirement.
For the known partner case, this setting is trivial: we can share an EPR pair between every authorized pair and make any collection of pairs authorized this way.
For the unknown partner case the setting is still non-trivial however.

In this setting, the maximality related conditions trivialize, since they specify that certain additional pairs must be authorized given a set of authorized pairs.
In contrast, the no-odd cycles and monogamy conditions remain non-trivial: these are conditions on collections of authorized pairs needed for those pairs to all be made authorized (not statements that additional pairs must be authorized).
This reasoning leads us to the following theorem.

Theorem 28

A pair access structure
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{A},\varnothing)
can be realized as an entanglement sharing scheme with an unknown partner if and only if the following conditions hold.

•

No odd cycles:
The graph
G_{\mathcal{A}}
must not contain any odd cycles.

•

Monogamy:
Suppose there is an even length path
\{T_{1},T_{2}\}
,
\{T_{2},T_{3}\}
, …,
\{T_{k-1},T_{k}\}
in
G_{\mathcal{A}}
. Then
T_{1}\cap T_{k}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Necessity has been proved as lemmas
22
and
23
.
It remains to prove sufficiency.
To do this, consider the graph
G_{\mathcal{A}}
and split it into its connected components.
We construct a state by first analyzing a single connected component and then combining the constructions.

Consider a subgraph
G_{\mathcal{A}}^{a}
which is connected, where the index
a
runs over connected components of
G_{\mathcal{A}}
.
The no odd cycles condition and lemma
12
imply the graph
G_{\mathcal{A}}^{a}
is bipartite.
Call the two vertex sets induced by the bipartite structure
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{a}
and
\mathcal{V}_{R}^{a}
.
Our strategy will be to take a single copy of
\ket{\Psi^+}_{A_{L}A_{R}}
, and share
A_{L}
among the vertices in
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{a}
and
A_{R}
among the vertices in
\mathcal{V}_{R}^{a}
.
More concretely, we first claim that
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{a}
satisfies the no-cloning property.
To see this, consider
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}^{a}
.
Because we have limited ourselves to a single connected component, there is at least one path between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
.
Since the graph is bipartite and
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{V}_{L}^{a}
, this path must have even length.
But then monogamy implies
T_{1}\cap T_{2}\neq\emptyset
, as needed.
Now we consider
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{L}^{a}
, the minimal set that satisfies monotonicity and includes
\mathcal{V}_{L}^{a}
.
It is clear that
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{L}^{a}
also satisfies no-cloning.
Therefore,
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{L}^{a}
, viewed as authorized sets for a quantum secret sharing scheme, forms a valid access structure, and hence we can use a QSS to share
A_{L}
among
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{L}^{a}
.
Similarly we use a QSS to share
A_{R}
among
\bar{\mathcal{V}}_{R}^{a}
.

We repeat this procedure for each connected component and then include all of the constructed states together to obtain the final construction.
In this final state
\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
, each pair
\{T_{i},T_{j}\}\in\mathcal{A}
will be authorized: explicitly, the maps
\mathcal{N}_{T_{i}}
are given as follows: We consider the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{A}}
and find which (unique) connected component
T_{i}
belongs to.
Then
\mathcal{N}_{T_{i}}
is defined as the recovery channel for the corresponding QSS.

4.4
General access structures for stabilizer states

In this section we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for an access structure to be realized in stabilizer constructions.
We work with qubit systems for simplicity.
The generalization to other prime-dimensional qudits is straightforward.

Consider an ESS scheme with unknown partners,
(\rho,\{\mathcal{N}_{T}\})

We may realize each distillation channel
\mathcal{N}_{T}

Define operators

In

As a general comment, we note that the extra conditions—the second condition in

5.1
Known partner

In the beginning of

Namely, it is impossible for a tripartite stabilizer state to contain only one authorized set

Lower bounds on share size

We briefly give some lower bounds on share size in entanglement sharing schemes.
The first lower bound only applies in the known partner case (since the setup is forbidden in the unknown partner case), but the second lower bound strategy applies to both.

Multiple pairings:
Suppose that a set
T_{1}
is one end of an authorized pair.
Then it is clear
d_{T_{1}}\geq d_{E}
for
d_{E}
the dimension of the shared entangled state
\Psi^{+}
.
More is also true: if
T_{1}
is one end of
t
authorized pairs, it must have dimension
d_{T_{1}}\geq d^{t}_{E}
.
We prove this in the next theorem.

The following lemma generalizes the lower bound on entropy/dimension of the party involved in many authorized sets.

Lemma 31
(Degree lower bound)

Consider an ESS storing EPR pairs of dimension
d_{E}
.
Let a subset
T
be an endpoint of
t
authorized pairs, i.e.

\{T,T_{1}\}
,
\dots
,
\{T,T_{t}\}\in\mathcal{A}
, where the partners
T_{1},\dots,T_{t}
are pairwise disjoint and disjoint from
T
.
Then

S(T)\;\geq\;t\,\log d_{E}.

Proof.

Fix
j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}
.
Since
\{T,T_{j}\}
is authorized, there is a pair of local operations recovering a perfect
d_{E}
-dimensional maximally entangled state.
Thus we have that

\displaystyle\log d_{E}\leq E_{sq}(T:T_{j}).

(81)

Monogamy of
E_{\mathrm{sq}}
gives

\sum_{j=1}^{t}E_{\mathrm{sq}}(T{:}T_{j})\;\leq\;E_{\mathrm{sq}}\!\bigl(T{:}T_{1}\cdots T_{t}\bigr).

(82)

It is also true however that

E_{\mathrm{sq}}(A{:}B)\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\,I(A{:}B)\;\leq\;S(A).

(83)

Combining the last three statements, we have

t\,\log d_{E}\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{t}E_{\mathrm{sq}}(T{:}T_{j})\;\leq\;E_{\mathrm{sq}}\!\bigl(T{:}T_{1}\cdots T_{t}\bigr)\;\leq\;S(T),

as claimed.

Note that the bound is tight: if
T
and
T_{1},\cdots,T_{t}
are
d_{E}
-dimensional qudits, and we distribute
t
independent
d_{E}
-dimensional EPR pairs with the
t

\{T,T_{1}\},\dots,\{T,T_{t}\}
, then
S(T)=t\log d_{E}
.

[
5

8
]

Definition 32

A=\{S_{i}\cup T_{1},T_{2}\}
is authorized.

We can show a lower bound on the size of significant shares in schemes that satisfy a stronger condition on unauthorized pairs, which is that their reduced density matrices be separable.
Recall that this is the case in the explicit constructions we’ve given, so this lower bound applies in those cases.
We claim that in this setting any significant share
S_{i}
must have
\log d_{S_{i}}\geq\log d_{E}
, where
d_{E}
is the (one-sided) dimension of maximally entangled state distilled from the pair
\{S_{i}\cup T_{1},T_{2}\}
.

To see this, first note that since we can distill a

-dimensional maximally entangled state, we have

\displaystyle\log d_{E}

\displaystyle\leq E_{R}(S_{i}T_{1}:T_{2}).

(84)

Then from inequality (
11
) we have

\displaystyle E_{R}(S_{i}T_{1}:T_{2})\leq E_{R}(T_{1}:T_{2})+2\log d_{S_{i}}

(85)

Since by assumption the state on

is separable,
E_{R}(T_{1}:T_{2})=0
, so we obtain
\log d_{E}\leq\log d_{S_{i}}
as needed.

We note that the lower bound on significant shares does not hold under the weaker requirement for unauthorized pair, namely, demanding the inability to distill EPR states only.
A counterexample is provide by a Haar random state.
Consider an
n

such that
|T_{1}|=|T_{2}|=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}n
and
|S|=\epsilon n
, where

is an arbitrarily small positive constant.
Under this partition, with high probability, almost
\frac{n}{2}
EPR pairs can be distilled from

and
T_{2}
, making
\log d_{E}\approx\frac{n}{2}
, but no EPR states can be distilled from
T_{1}
and
T_{2}

[
12
]
.

8
Discussion

In this work we defined and studied the notions of an entanglement sharing scheme.
We partially characterized for which access structures entanglement sharing schemes can be implemented, in both the cases of a known and unknown partner.
Concretely, we gave a list of necessary conditions in both cases that we conjecture are also sufficient, and showed they are sufficient under a weakened notion of security.
We also gave if and only if conditions for the natural, strong, notion of security in the case where we restrict to stabilizer encodings.

We believe the entanglement sharing setting gives a natural framework within which to explore the patterns of entanglement that can be realized in quantum states.
As well, they already have found one application (to entanglement summoning) and seem likely to find further applications.
For instance, a distributed entanglement sharing state allows authorized pairs of parties to send and receive quantum messages (by preparing shared entanglement and then using teleportation), but does not allow unauthorized pairs to participate in the communication.
A number of open questions present themselves in further developing the theory and applications of entanglement sharing.

Secret key vs. entanglement:
It would be interesting to understand a modified setting from the one we consider, where the goal is to have authorized pairs of parties able to distill secret, shared classical key, and unauthorized pairs to be unable to recover shared secret keys.
For instance, in the
((2,2,5))
construction pairs of type
(2,2)
can distill key (for instance by first distilling shared EPR pairs) but pairings of type
(1,2)
cannot.
States that allow this flexible distillation of key may find interesting applications in network security.

Efficient schemes for efficient representations:
In the context of quantum secret sharing, some access structures beyond threshold schemes have efficient schemes.
In particular, when whether a subset is authorized or unauthorized can be computed with a small span program, the scheme also has an efficient construction
[
9
]
.
It would be interesting to explore if a similar statement is true for the bipartite settings discussed here.

Multipartite generalizations:
A generalization of our setting would be to consider the distribution of
k\geq 3
party states within a scheme.
For instance, we could have triplet access structures that consist of a set of authorized subsets and a set of unauthorized triplets. We could then ask for states that allow recovery of a fixed, entangled state from each authorized triple (e.g. recovery of a GHZ state) but the same state to not be well approximated by anything recovered from an unauthorized triple.

Entanglement sharing and entanglement summoning:
Inspired by the application of entanglement sharing schemes to the entanglement summoning problem given in figure
1
, we can ask if ESS schemes can be applied more generally to entanglement summoning as defined in
[
2
,
3
]
.

Appendix B
Quantum secret sharing: an alternative construction

In this section, we reprove the following well-known result about quantum secret sharing:

Theorem

An access structure is valid if and only if it satisfies monotonicity and no-cloning.

We will prove this theorem by providing an alternative construction of a stabilizer state satisfying the QSS property.
Compared to the constructions in
[
5
,
9
]
, our construction is arguably more straightforward.

Given an access structure
\mathcal{A}
, we pick the minimal elements that are not a superset of any others, and call the collection
A_{\min}
.
We will first construct logical operators
X_{A}
and
Z_{A}
for each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
such that:

[X_{A},X_{A^{\prime}}]=[Z_{A},Z_{A^{\prime}}]=0,~~\{X_{A},Z_{A^{\prime}}\}=0,~~\forall A,A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}.

(105)

We will specify a rule to assign qubits to parties and a rule to define the logical operators.
Via our construction, each party may contain multiple qubits, and the number of qubits each party has may differ.
We use the following procedure:

•

Initialization: for each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
, assign one qubit for each party in
A
. Define
X_{A}=\otimes X
where the tensor product is over the qubits just added. Define
Z_{A}
similarly.

•

Anticommutation-1: For all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
and
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
such that
A^{\prime}\neq A
:

–

Pick any party
s\in A\cap A^{\prime}
and add a qubit to party
s
.

–

Extend
X_{A}
by (tensor multiplying it with) the Pauli
X
of the newly added qubit.

–

Extend
Z_{A^{\prime}}
by the Pauli
Z
of the newly added qubit.

•

Anticommutation-2: For each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
such that
|A|
is even, we randomly pick a party
s\in A
and extend
X_{A}
and
Z_{A}
by the Pauli
X
and Pauli
Z
respectively.

After this procedure, each
X_{A}
will be a tensor product of Pauli
X
operators, and each
Z_{A}
will be a tensor product of Pauli
Z
operators.
This ensures the first equation in
eq.

105
is satisfied.
Moreover, the following two properties hold:

1.

For each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
and
A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
such that
A^{\prime}\neq A
,
\supp(X_{A})\cap\supp(Z_{A^{\prime}})
contains exactly one qubit.

2.

For each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}
,
\supp(X_{A})=\supp(Z_{A})
has
|A|
or
|A|+1

These properties ensure the second equation in
eq.

105

We then define a (in general mixed) state
\rho
via the following stabilizers:

X_{A}X_{\ell},~~Z_{A}Z_{\ell}~~(\forall A\in\mathcal{A}_{\min}).

(106)

Here,
\ell
is a reference qubit carrying the logical information.
The above operators mutually commute due to
eq.

105
.
Moreover, any other stabilizer that is nontrivial on

must be a product of (odd number of) the above stabilizers.
As a consequence of our initialization, where we assign separate qubits to parties for each authorized set they are a part of, its support always contains at least one

.

The QSS is obtained via the Choi–Jamiołkowski isomorphism by viewing the state

as a map from
\ell
to the support of all subsets
A
.
Under this isomorphism, the QSS has logical
X
and
Z
operators supported on subsystem
S
exactly when the state
\rho
has stabilizers of the form operators
X_{\ell}\mathcal{O}_{S}
and
Z_{\ell}\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{S}
for some
\mathcal{O}_{S},\mathcal{O}^{\prime}_{S}
.
Since we have that

and

are stabilizers of

, this ensures that the encoded qubit can be recovered from every subset

, and hence every
A\in\mathcal{A}
, so that the QSS scheme is correct.
For security, recall that we noted above that every stabilizer with non-trivial support on
\ell
must contain at least one
A\in\mathcal{A}_{\text{min}}
in its support, so no further subsystems can contain
X
and
Z
logical operators.
