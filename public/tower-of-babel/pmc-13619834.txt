Rational discovery of peptide inhibitors targeting Keap1: a focus on neurodegenerative diseases
Authors: Nandhini Senthilkumar, Shanthi Veerappapillai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1)/Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway is a promising therapeutic target for neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs). Nrf2 activation mitigates key pathogenic mechanisms in NDDs by enhancing antioxidant defenses, suppressing neuroinflammation, improving mitochondrial function, inhibiting ferroptosis, and maintaining proteostasis.AimTo design and identify peptide inhibitors capable of disrupting the Keap1/Nrf2 interaction through a rational computational approach.MethodsA combinatorial peptide library comprising 13,167 peptides was generated from an Nrf2-derived template peptide and subjected to rigorous multi-tier screening based on predicted blood-brain barrier permeability, toxicity, allergenicity, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential, and physicochemical properties. Following comprehensive safety assessments, 11 promising peptides were shortlisted and evaluated using molecular docking, MM-GBSA binding free-energy calculations, and per-residue energy decomposition analysis. The top-ranked peptide candidates were subsequently subjected to 200 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to assess complex stability and binding behavior through analyses of structural stability, flexibility, principal component analysis (PCA), hydrogen-bond occupancy, and free-energy landscape analyses.ResultsAmong the shortlisted peptides, LMEETYEFL (pep4369) and LDYETYEFL (pep6649) exhibited stronger binding affinities toward Keap1 than the template peptide in docking and MM-GBSA analyses. MD simulations confirmed the structural stability and favorable conformational behavior of the peptide-Keap1 complexes throughout the simulation period.ConclusionThese findings suggest that LMEETYEFL and LDYETYEFL are promising candidates for disrupting the Keap1/Nrf2 interaction. However, experimental validation is necessary to confirm their therapeutic potential and facilitate further biological evaluation.

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) are a heterogeneous set of neurological conditions involving progressive neuronal loss in distinct regions of the nervous system, leading to cognitive, behavioral, and motor dysfunction (Gadhave et al., 2024). Numerous pathological hallmarks leading to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), Multiple Sclerosis (MS), and Huntington’s disease (HD) are associated with NDDs, including oxidative stress, protein misfolding, neuroinflammation, ferroptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction (Qu et al., 2020; Wilson et al., 2023). These diseases represent a substantial component of the global disease burden, affecting millions worldwide and generating significant medical, social, and economic challenges (Wang et al., 2024).

The nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a transcription factor that regulates cellular defence against oxidative and toxic stress. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 is constitutively degraded by Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1). In response to oxidative stress, Nrf2 escapes Keap1-mediated degradation, translocates to the nucleus, and activates antioxidant response element (ARE)-dependent cytoprotective and detoxifying genes (Suzuki et al., 2023). Nrf2 activation enhances antioxidant defence, suppresses inflammation, maintains proteostasis, supports mitochondrial function, and inhibits ferroptosis (Cuadrado et al., 2019; Qu et al., 2020). Impaired Nrf2 activity has been associated with increased inflammation, oxidative imbalance, and proteotoxic stress in NDDs, highlighting its protective role in disease progression (W.-T. Chen and Dodson, 2023; Mayer et al., 2024). Accordingly, targeting Nrf2 activation has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy for NDDs (Mayer et al., 2024). However, the therapeutic effects of Nrf2 activation are context-dependent. In neurodegenerative diseases, enhancement of Nrf2 activity may provide sustained cytoprotective effects by maintaining antioxidant defence, proteostasis, and anti-inflammatory responses. In contrast, persistent constitutive Nrf2 activation in established cancers can promote metabolic reprogramming, tumor progression, and therapeutic resistance (Cuadrado et al., 2019; Kitamura and Motohashi, 2018). Therefore, therapeutic modulation of the Keap1–Nrf2 pathway should consider the disease context and duration of Nrf2 activation.

Currently, covalent Nrf2 activators such as dimethyl fumarate (Tecfidera) and diroximel fumarate (Vumerity) are used clinically for the treatment of MS. Despite these advances, such agents may present challenges related to off-target effects, toxicity, and pharmacokinetic properties (Barreca et al., 2023; Bresciani et al., 2017). Moreover, no non-covalent Keap1 inhibitors have entered clinical trials to date (Barreca et al., 2023). In this context, Nrf2-derived peptide inhibitors targeting the DLG and ETGE motifs have been investigated as modulators of the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction. However, their limited cell permeability remains a major challenge (Crisman et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2024). Therefore, rational optimization of ETGE-based peptides may provide a potential strategy for developing improved Keap1-targeting inhibitors.

Targeting protein–protein interactions (PPIs) remain challenging because of their large and shallow binding interfaces. Peptides offer a promising approach because of their high target specificity and amenability to sequence and chemical optimization. Nrf2-derived DLG- and ETGE-based peptides have been reported to disrupt the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction, but their limited cell permeability restricts their therapeutic potential (Crisman et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2024). Therefore, rational optimization of ETGE-based peptides, supported by computational approaches such as molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations, may facilitate the identification of improved Keap1-targeting peptides (Arabi-Jeshvaghani et al., 2023; Bhagat et al., 2025; Kaur and Goyal, 2023; Oduro-Kwateng et al., 2024). Building on these approaches, we employed an integrated computational strategy combining peptide-property screening, molecular docking, MM-GBSA analysis, and molecular dynamics simulations to identify peptide candidates targeting the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction.

Methodology

Designing of peptide library

The peptide library was generated using the Peptide Combination Generator (PepCoGen) server (https://www.bicfri.in/pepcogen/) with the Nrf2-derived peptide as the input template sequence (Ali et al., 2020). Amino acids were classified into six groups based on their properties: acidic (D, E), basic (R, H, K), non-polar/aliphatic (A, I, L, M, V), aromatic (F, W, Y), polar uncharged (N, C, Q, S, T), and unique (G, P). Using the basic module of PepCoGen, systematic position-wise amino acid substitutions were performed on the template peptide. The native residues at individual positions were replaced with different amino acids, generating peptide variants through single- and double-residue substitutions. This approach enabled the exploration of sequence diversity while maintaining the original peptide length and template framework. The generated peptide sequences were subsequently extracted and converted into FASTA format using an in-house Python script for further computational analysis.

In silico evaluation of peptide inhibitors

Screening of peptide blood-brain barrier, toxicity, and allergenicity

The BBPpredict tool (https://i.uestc.edu.cn/BBPpredict/cgi-bin/BBPpredict.pl) was employed to evaluate the ability of peptides to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), with a threshold ≥ 0.5 as BBB-penetrating candidates. This value was derived from the probability score generated by the Random Forest-based model (Chen et al., 2022). Following BBB-based filtering, peptides were advanced to safety profiling, where toxicity and allergenicity were assessed using ToxinPred 3.0 (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred3/) and AllerCatPro 2.0 (https://allercatpro.bii.a-star.edu.sg/), respectively. ToxinPred 3.0 was utilized to predict peptide toxicity using machine learning and deep learning-based models. The AllerCatPro 2.0 platform assesses peptide allergenicity by analyzing amino acid sequences and three-dimensional structures with a comprehensive allergen reference set comprising 4,979 allergenic proteins, 165 autoimmune-related allergens, and 162 low-allergenic proteins obtained from WHO/IUIS, COMPARE, FARRP, UniProtKB, and Allergome databases (Nguyen et al., 2022).

Prediction of anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and hemolytic activity

As part of the screening pipeline, the anti-inflammatory potential of the designed peptides was estimated using PreTP-Stack (http://bliulab.net/PreTP-Stack/), which is based on a stacked ensemble learning approach (Yan et al., 2023). Further, the antioxidative properties of peptides were evaluated using AnOxPePred 1.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/AnOxPePred-1.0/), a convolutional neural network-driven prediction platform based on free radical scavenging (FRS) and metal ion-chelating (CHEL) scores. An FRS threshold value of 0.5 was applied, and peptides with prediction scores above this cutoff were considered to possess antioxidant activity (Olsen et al., 2020). HemoPI2 (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/hemopi2/) was employed to evaluate peptide hemolytic activity through classification and regression models that identify and quantify HC50 (Hemolytic concentration) values. These models were trained on a dataset comprising 1,926 peptides with experimentally validated hemolytic activity against mammalian red blood cells (Rathore et al., 2025).

Evaluation of half-life, aggregation propensity and physical-chemical parameters

PLifePred (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/plifepred/) was used to predict peptide stability in the bloodstream, and peptides exhibiting a half-life exceeding 800 s were retained for further analysis. The peptide stability under intestinal conditions was assessed using the HLP webserver (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/hlp/), which classifies peptides as highly stable when their predicted half-life is above 1.0 s. Moreover, the tendency of peptide self-aggregation was assessed using the PASTA 2.0 server (http://protein.bio.unipd.it/pasta2/), which identifies sequence-specific aggregation-prone regions, secondary structure prediction and intrinsic disorder. This server reports aggregation tendency in PASTA Energy Units (PEU), with each unit equivalent to 1.192 kcal/mol (Walsh et al., 2014). ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) and Innovagen (https://pepcalc.com/) tools were used to calculate theoretical properties of the designed peptides, including molecular weight, isoelectric point (pI), GRAVY, instability index, aliphatic index, and water solubility.

Peptide structure modeling and protein preparation

The PEP-FOLD4 web server (https://bioserv.rpbs.univ-paris-diderot.fr/services/PEP-FOLD4/) was used to generate the three-dimensional structures of the peptides using a fragment-based assembly strategy. The server incorporates the updated structure-based Optimized Potential for Efficient structure Prediction (sOPEP2) force field, where nonbonded interactions are described using a Mie representation rather than the conventional van der Waals approach (Rey et al., 2023). The generated peptide models were subsequently evaluated based on their sOPEP energy scores. The Keap1 Kelch domain crystal structure (PDB ID: 2FLU), with a resolution of 1.50 Å was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank database (Lo et al., 2006). The co-crystallized Nrf2-derived peptide located at the Keap1 binding interface was extracted and used to validate the docking protocol. Protein preparation was performed using UCSF Chimera v1.18. Heteroatoms and crystallographic water molecules were removed, followed by the addition of hydrogen atoms. The prepared Keap1 structure was then subjected to energy minimization using the AMBER ff14SB force field implemented in Chimera. Minimization was performed using 100 steps of the steepest-descent algorithm followed by 10 steps of the conjugate-gradient method. The modeled peptide structures were subjected to the same energy-minimization protocol before docking.

Peptide-protein docking

The HPEPDOCK 2.0 server (http://huanglab.phys.hust.edu.cn/hpepdock/) (Zhou et al., 2018) was used to perform peptide-protein docking between Keap1 and the template peptide, as well as the designed peptides. The prepared Keap1 and peptide structures were submitted to the server, and the top 10 predicted docking models were obtained for each peptide. The representative docking pose was selected based on the lowest docking energy among the predicted conformations. To validate the docking protocol, the experimentally co-crystallized Nrf2-derived peptide in the Keap1 crystal structure (PDB ID: 2FLU) was removed from the complex and subsequently redocked using the same HPEPDOCK 2.0 protocol. The predicted redocked peptide conformation was then compared with the experimentally resolved peptide structure by calculating the backbone RMSD.

Binding free energy analysis

Binding free energy estimations of protein-peptide complexes were performed using Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) calculations through the HawkDock server V2 (https://cadd.zju.edu.cn/hawkdock/). The tleap module was used to prepare the complexes by adding missing hydrogens and heavy atoms, followed by the assignment of the ff02 force field. Energy minimization and structural optimization of the complex structures were performed using pmemd. The modified Generalized Born (GBOBC1) model is used to determine the polar desolvation energy (Zhang et al., 2025). In addition, per-residue energy contribution analyses were performed to identify key residues contributing to peptide binding.

Molecular dynamics simulation of Keap1-peptide complexes

MD simulations of the template-Keap1 and hit peptides-Keap1 complexes were performed using the GROMACS 2023.3 package (Abraham et al., 2015). The protein topology files were generated using the OPLS (Optimized Potential for Liquid Simulations) all-atom force field (Jorgensen et al., 1996) and the systems were solvated using SPC/E (Extended Simple Point Charge) explicit water model. Each protein-peptide complex was placed at the center of a dodecahedral simulation box with a 1.0 nm distance from the box edge and sodium (Na+) ions were added to neutralize the systems. Subsequently, energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 50,000 steps. This was followed by two equilibration steps: an NVT (constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) ensemble at 300 K for 100 ps using the Berendsen thermostat and an NPT (constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature) ensemble at 1 bar for 100 ps using the Parrinello-Rahman coupling method. The production MD simulations were subsequently carried out for 200 ns using a 2fs integration time step, and trajectories coordinates were saved every 10 ps. Finally, trajectory analyses were performed using GROMACS utilities, and XMGRACE software. Structural stability and conformational flexibility were evaluated by calculating backbone root mean square deviation (RMSD) and Cα root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) values, respectively. Radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), free energy landscape (FEL), and principal component analysis (PCA) based two-dimensional projection plots were generated to investigate global conformational changes during the simulations. Hydrogen bond occupancy was calculated as the percentage of simulation frames in which a specific hydrogen bond was present, providing a quantitative measure of interaction persistence during the simulation. Protein-peptide interaction stability was assessed by monitoring the minimum heavy-atom distance and the number of intermolecular atomic contacts throughout the 200 ns simulation. These descriptors were used to evaluate the persistence of peptide binding within the Keap1 binding pocket.

Results and discussion

Computational design of novel Keap1-targeting peptides

The interaction between Keap1 and Nrf2 is primarily mediated through the Kelch domain of Keap1, which comprises five polar subpockets (P1-P5). Glu79 of Nrf2 binds to the P1 subpocket and forms key hydrogen bonds with Arg (415, 483), and Ser508, whereas Glu82 interacts within P2 with Ser363, Arg380, and Asn382. Additional hydrogen bonds involving Tyr334, Asn382, Gln530, Ser (555, 602), along with van der Waals and water-mediated interactions, further stabilize the binding of the ETGE motif to Keap1 (Crisman et al., 2023). Therefore, targeting the ETGE-binding region of the Keap1 Kelch domain represents a rational approach for disrupting Keap1/Nrf2 interaction. Several peptide-based Keap1 inhibitors have been reported based on the native Nrf2 Neh2 sequence LDEETGEFL (L76-L84), in which the conserved ETGE motif mediates binding to the Kelch domain of Keap1 (Crisman et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2024). Notably, Colarusso et al. (2020) identified optimized ETGE-based peptide inhibitors from linear and cyclic peptide libraries and demonstrated that proline substitution at the Glu78 position increased peptide affinity by stabilizing the β-turn conformation within the binding pocket (Colarusso et al., 2020).

Accordingly, the Nrf2-derived peptide LDEETGEFL was selected as the template sequence for designing Keap1-targeting peptides in this study. A systematic sequence optimization strategy involving position-wise single and double amino acid substitutions was employed to generate peptide diversity. Using this approach, a virtual library of 13167 peptides was generated and designated pep1 to pep13167 for systematic screening. These peptides were further evaluated using physicochemical and drug-likeness properties. To develop the rational design of peptide inhibitors, a systematic computational screening strategy was implemented (Figure 1). The rational peptide design strategy incorporated three key criteria: i) evaluating pharmacokinetic and safety-related properties, including BBB permeability, toxicity, and allergenicity; ii) assessing predicted antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential and favorable physicochemical properties; and iii) achieving stronger Keap1 binding affinity with the template peptide. Of note, these computational predictions serve as a framework for identifying promising peptide candidates that require subsequent experimental validation to confirm their biological efficacy and therapeutic potential.

Exploring the potential therapeutic peptide inhibitors against Keap1

Blood brain barrier permeability and safety profiling

A crucial step in peptide design and development for therapeutic applications in NDDs is the evaluation of BBB permeability, toxicity, and allergenicity to ensure safety and effective CNS delivery (Gadhave et al., 2024). The BBB permeability of the 13167 designed peptides was assessed using the BBPpredict server. Among these, 756 were predicted to have BBB-penetrating potential based on probability scores exceeding the classification threshold of 0.5. Subsequently, toxicity and allergenicity were computationally evaluated using ToxinPred 3.0 and AllerCatPro 2.0, respectively. Based on ToxinPred 3.0 analysis, 459 of the 756 peptides were classified as non-toxic, whereas 297 were predicted to be toxic. The AllerCatPro 2.0 assessment further indicated that these 459 peptides showed no similarity to known allergens, highlighting a favorable computationally assessed safety profile.

The therapeutic efficacy of neuroprotective drugs is attributed to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, modulation of protein homeostasis, and ability to stimulate neuronal regeneration (Giri and Chandra, 2025). Therefore, the anti-inflammatory potential of the peptides was evaluated using the PreTP-Stack server, which identified 150 peptides as possess anti-inflammatory peptides (AIP), while 309 were classified as non-AIPs. Antioxidant peptides represent promising therapeutic candidates for slowing or preventing the progression of NDDs (Zhang et al., 2024). Accordingly, the top 150 peptides were further assessed for their predicted antioxidant potential using the AnOxPePred 1.0 tool, incorporating both FRS and CHEL scores. Peptides with FRS values above the model threshold of 0.5 were considered to exhibit computationally estimated antioxidant potential. This analysis identified 29 peptides with antioxidant potential, with FRS values ranging from 0.501 to 0.596 and CHEL scores between 0.201 and 0.290. As summarized in Table 1, these 29 peptides were computationally prioritized based on their integrated predicted safety, and pharmacokinetic characteristics, including BBB permeability, toxicity, allergenicity, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory. Although the selected peptides satisfied the predefined computational screening criteria, several prediction scores were close to the respective classification thresholds. Therefore, these results were used for comparative prioritization of candidates and require experimental validation.

Evaluation of hemolytic potency, stability, and aggregation prediction of screened peptides

In recent years, the discovery of therapeutic peptides has received increasing attention. However, many promising peptide candidates encounter challenges, including hemolytic toxicity, which may limit their progression toward clinical development (Baig et al., 2018). The HemoPI2 server was used to assess the predicted hemolytic potential of the peptides. According to the classification criteria, peptides with HC50 ≤ 100 μM were classified as hemolytic, whereas those with HC50 > 100 μM were considered non-hemolytic (Rathore et al., 2025). Based on this computational assessment, all 29 peptides were predicted to exhibit non-hemolytic potential. Despite favourable safety profiles, rapid clearance and short half-life remain major pharmacokinetic limitations of peptide therapeutics (Giri and Chandra, 2025). Therefore, HLP analysis indicated that 28 peptides showed a predicted intestinal half-life greater than 1.0 s. Additionally, PLifePred predicted that 26 peptides exhibited a plasma half-life exceeding 800 s, whereas pep3324, pep3780, and pep8815 showed plasma half-life values below 800 s.

In addition, aggregation propensity, intrinsic disorder, and secondary structural features were analysed using the PASTA 2.0 server. All 29 peptides were predicted to possess a high degree of intrinsic disorder and no self-aggregation-prone regions. Secondary structure prediction indicated that the majority of peptides favoured adopting random-coil conformations, while a small subset demonstrated coil with a contribution to either the α-helix or the β-strand. Based on the integrated assessment, 26 peptides satisfied all screening criteria and proceeded to physicochemical profiling (Table 2).

Assessment of physicochemical properties of peptides

Physicochemical profiling plays a vital role in therapeutic peptide design and development. Table 3 summarizes the physicochemical parameters of the selected 26 peptides, including instability index, aliphatic index, molecular weight, theoretical pI, GRAVY score, and water solubility, as calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool and PepCalc (InnovaGen). The instability index (II) provides an estimate of peptide stability, where values below 40 indicate a predicted stable profile and values above 40 suggest instability (Mirzaei et al., 2019). Among the analysed peptides, pep3438 exhibited the lowest II value (−18.36), followed by pep6288 (II = −17.3), indicating a favourable stability profile. In contrast, pep4815, pep4825, pep10021, pep11152, pep11313, pep11332, pep11342 showed predicted instability based on II values above 40. The overall hydrophobic or hydrophilic character of a protein or peptide is represented by the Grand Average of Hydropathy (GRAVY) value. Hydrophobicity is denoted by positive GRAVY values, while hydrophilicity is indicated by negative values (Mirzaei et al., 2019). The majority of peptides displayed negative GRAVY values ranging from −0.8 to −0.022, confirming a predominantly hydrophilic nature. However, pep4825, pep4597, pep4720, pep8094, pep3438, pep4730, pep4749, and pep3780 showed positive GRAVY values (0.011–0.578), suggesting relatively higher hydrophobicity. Interestingly, pep3799 exhibited amphipathic characteristics. The theoretical pI values were acidic across all 26 peptides, ranging from 3.57 to 4.63. Molecular weights within the preferred range (∼500–5000 Da) are vital for the development of therapeutically effective peptides (Oduro-Kwateng et al., 2024). The molecular weights of all peptides ranged from 1016.08 to 1215.32 Da. Water solubility analysis indicated that 16 peptides demonstrated good solubility profiles. The overall analysis of physicochemical parameters highlighted pep3799, pep4369, pep4720, pep6288, pep6649, pep8094, pep8264, pep8454, pep10259, pep11142, and pep11323 as the most potent candidates among the screened peptides.

The rigorous computational screening pipeline refined an initial set of 13167 peptides to 11 highly prioritized candidates. These peptides were prioritized based on integrated computational predictions of safety profiles, pharmacokinetic, and physicochemical properties. The selected candidates were further evaluated through molecular docking, MM-GBSA calculations, and MD simulations to assess their predicted binding interactions and the stability of peptide-Keap1 complexes, with the aim of identifying the most promising candidates for further investigation.

Stability and binding affinity assessment of designed peptides

The PEP-FOLD4 web server was used to predict the 3D structures of the template and top 11 selected peptides (Figure 2). Peptide conformational stability and structural quality were assessed using sOPEP energy scores. Peptide conformations with lower sOPEP values are more stable (Rey et al., 2023). For each peptide, the model with the lowest sOPEP score was selected for further analysis. The predicted 3D models exhibited sOPEP energy values ranging from −15.38 to −11.25 kcal/mol. Based on peptide modelling analyses, both the template and top 11 peptides were found to adopt energetically favourable conformations, denoted proper peptide folding and supporting their thermal stability (Oduro-Kwateng et al., 2024).

The HPEPDOCK 2.0 was employed to evaluate the binding affinities of the designed peptides toward the Keap1 protein, and the docking results are represented in Figure 3. The template peptide exhibited a docking score of −195.149 kcal/mol, serving as the reference for comparison. All screened peptides demonstrated stronger binding affinities relative to the template, with docking scores ranging from −215.415 to −239.443 kcal/mol. Notably, pep6649 achieved the lowest docking score (−239.443 kcal/mol), followed by pep8264 (−238.767 kcal/mol), pep11323 (−237.63 kcal/mol), and pep6288 (−237.503 kcal/mol), indicating their strong binding with Keap1. The docking protocol was validated by redocking the Nrf2-derived peptide from the Keap1 crystal structure (PDB ID: 2FLU). The predicted peptide pose showed a backbone RMSD of 1.581 Å compared with the experimental resolved structure (Supplementary Figure S1). As this value was below 2 Å, the predicted and experimental poses showed good structural agreement, supporting the reliability of the docking protocol (Tao et al., 2020). Following molecular docking, The MM-GBSA analysis provides an estimation of the relative binding stability of the designed peptides toward the Keap1. Although MM-GBSA scores are represented in kcal/mol, these values represent approximate end-point estimates of binding free energy rather than true binding free energies. Moreover, MM-GBSA-derived binding free energies are generally more negative than the experimental binding free energies due to approximations in solvation and entropy calculations. In addition, water molecules and binding-site cavities are represented using a continuum solvent model, which may not fully capture explicit water-mediated interactions and can sometimes introduce artificial solvent arrangements (El Khoury et al., 2019).

Assessment of peptide-Keap1 complex stability in a dynamic biological environment

The conformational stability and dynamic behaviour of the docked peptide-protein complexes were systematically investigated through 200 ns MD simulations. The MD simulation trajectories are consistently represented by the black for the template, red for pep4369 and turquoise for pep6649.

RMSD analysis was performed to assess the structural stability and conformational behaviour of the peptide-protein complexes during the 200 ns MD simulation (Paranthaman and Veerappapillai, 2025). The RMSD profiles are depicted in Figure 5A. The RMSD values of all complexes remained below 0.28 nm. During the initial phase of the simulation, the Keap1-template complex underwent notable atomic rearrangements, attaining a maximum RMSD of ∼0.24 nm within the 25 ns. The Keap1-template complex displayed high divergence throughout the simulation, maintaining an average RMSD of ∼0.211 nm. On the other hand, Keap1-pep4369 and Keap1-pep6649 complexes were well equilibrated and maintained stable RMSD values below ∼0.15 nm and minimal deviation throughout the simulation. The average RMSD values revealed that the Keap1-pep4369 and Keap1-pep6649 complexes maintained the lowest structural deviation (∼0.110 nm and ∼0.105 nm) respectively, whereas the Keap1-template complex showed slightly higher average RMSD values (∼0.211 nm). Compared with the Keap1-template complex, hit peptide complexes exhibited more stable dynamic behaviour by maintaining structural stability and minor conformational changes.

Molecular dynamics trajectory analysis of Keap1 in complex with the template and hit peptides. (A) Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), (B) Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), (C) radius of gyration (Rg), (D) Solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), (E) two-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) projection, and (F) hydrogen-bond analysis.

RMSF analysis was conducted to assess residue-level fluctuations in the protein-peptide complexes during the simulation (Krishnamoorthy and Karuppasamy, 2023). Figure 5B, depicts the fluctuation patterns of the template, pep4369, and pep6649 in complex with Keap1. The RMSF profiles showed that the template complex displayed greater flexibility, with fluctuations reaching up to ∼0.21 nm, whereas the pep4369 and pep6649 complexes demonstrated lower fluctuations of 0.017 nm and 0.012 nm, respectively. Key binding-site residues of Keap1, such as Arg (380, 415, 483), and Ser (363, 508, 555, 602), Asn382, Tyr334, and Gln530 are displayed lower residue-level fluctuations in the pep4369 and pep6649 complexes compared with the template complex. This reduced flexibility at the binding interface suggests enhanced structural stability and stronger binding interactions within the Keap1 pocket.

The Rg determines the compactness of protein-peptide complexes during MD simulations (Figure 5C). A higher Rg reflects an extended protein conformation, whereas lower Rg values indicate a more compact structure (Krishnamoorthy and Karuppasamy, 2023). For the Keap1-template complex, the Rg fluctuated between 1.69 and 1.75 nm, whereas the Keap1-pep4369 complex maintained Rg values between 1.69 and 1.74 nm. The Keap1-pep6649 complex showed Rg values ranging from ∼1.70 to 1.74 nm, respectively. Notably, the lower and narrower Rg distribution observed for the Keap1-pep4369 complex suggests a more compact and tightly packed conformation compared to the template complex. In contrast, the Keap1-pep6649 complex exhibited a slightly expanded structure while maintaining overall stability throughout the simulation.

SASA analysis was performed to evaluate the conformational stability and solvent exposure of the Keap1-template and hit peptide complexes throughout the simulation (Murali and Karuppasamy, 2024). The Keap1-template complex exhibited a slight variation in SASA, ranging from 157.33 to 167.18 nm2, with an average value of 162.05 nm2. Upon binding with pep4369, the SASA ranged from 157.02 to 166.23 nm2, while maintaining a lower average SASA of 161.74 nm2 compared to the template complex. In contrast, the Keap1-pep6649 complex showed a SASA range, varying from 157.82 to 166.46 nm2, and displayed the average SASA value (162.35 nm2) among the three complexes. By comparing the SASA plots, all complexes maintained narrow and similar fluctuations over the 200 ns simulation (Figure 5D). The SASA analysis suggests that the Keap1-peptide complexes maintained stable conformations over the simulation period.

Principal component (PC) analysis provides insight into the large-scale, concerted protein motions during MD simulations, which are essential for understanding protein structure, dynamics, and biological functions (Das et al., 2024). In the 2D projection plot (Figure 5E), complexes occupying a smaller region of phase space represent stable conformational clusters, whereas those spanning a larger phase space indicate reduced stability and increased conformational flexibility (Ja’afaru et al., 2024). The projection of the first two PCs (i.e., eigenvectors 1 and 2) in the Keap1-template complex showed extensive and widely distributed motions. In contrast, the Keap1-pep4369 and Keap1-pep6649 complexes exhibited more compact and well-clustered motion patterns. These findings strongly suggested that binding with pep4369 and pep6649 enhances superior structural stability.

Hydrogen bond analysis was performed to assess the stability and persistence of intermolecular interactions between Keap1 and the template, pep4369, and pep6649 throughout the 200 ns MD simulations (Figure 5F). Among the three complexes, the Keap1-pep4369 complex consistently maintained the highest number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, indicating a persistent interaction network within the Keap1 binding pocket. During the initial ∼85 ns, pep4369 maintained ∼ 9–15 hydrogen bonds, after which the number gradually decreased but remained relatively stable at ∼ 7–11 hydrogen bonds until the end of the simulation, with an average of ∼9 hydrogen bonds. The Keap1–pep6649 complex displayed a relatively stable hydrogen-bond profile, maintaining ∼ 3–8 hydrogen bonds, with an average of ∼6 hydrogen bonds. The template peptide exhibited a lower hydrogen-bonding pattern, generally maintaining ∼ 2–6 hydrogen bonds with an average of ∼5 hydrogen bonds throughout the simulation. These findings demonstrate that the designed peptides, particularly pep4369, sustained a more stable hydrogen-bonding network with Keap1 than the template peptide during the simulation.

Moreover, Hydrogen bond occupancy analysis was conducted to evaluate the dynamic stability of peptide-Keap1 interactions and identify persistent contacts maintained throughout the 200 ns MD simulations. As summarized in Table 6, the Keap1-template complex showed conserved hydrogen-bond interactions with key residues involved in Keap1 recognition, including Arg415, Arg380, and Gln530. Among these, the Gln530-Gly6 interaction exhibited the highest occupancy (82.34%), followed by Arg415 and Arg380-mediated interactions with Glu7 and Leu9 showing occupancies of 62.44% and 51.79%, respectively. The Keap1-pep4369 demonstrated a more persistent hydrogen bond network involving important binding-site residues, including Arg483, Arg380, Arg415, Arg336, Tyr334, and Tyr572. Notably, Arg483-mediated interactions with leu9 showed high occupancies of 88.28% and 80.00%, indicating persistent anchoring of pep4369 within the Keap1 binding pocket. Similarly, the Keap1-pep6649 showed highly persistent hydrogen bond interactions involving Asn382-Glu4 and Tyr6-Asn414, with occupancies of 97.26% and 89.09%, respectively. Collectively, the hydrogen bond occupancy analysis highlighted that both designed peptides maintained stable interactions with critical Keap1 hotspot residues, supporting their enhanced binding stability compared with the template peptide.

MM-GBSA and per-residues decomposition analysis

To further support the MD simulation findings, MM-GBSA calculations were performed to estimate the binding free energies of the template and designed peptide-Keap1 complexes using the last 10 ns MD trajectories. As shown in Figure 7A, The Keap1–pep6649 complex exhibited the most favourable binding free energy (−80.99 kcal/mol), followed by the Keap1–pep4369 complex (−69.12 kcal/mol), whereas the template peptide showed a comparatively weaker binding energy (−52.55 kcal/mol). The MM-GBSA energy decomposition revealed that van der Waals, electrostatic, and SASA energy components were the major contributors governing peptide-Keap1 binding stability. The improved binding affinity of pep6649 was primarily attributed to stronger van der Waals (−71.19 kcal/mol) and SASA (−9.97 kcal/mol) contributions, whereas pep4369 showed a dominant electrostatic contribution (−252.36 kcal/mol). These favourable energetic contributions resulted in improved binding free energies for the designed peptides compared with the template peptide. These findings suggest that pep4369 and pep6649 possess enhanced binding potential toward the Keap1 Kelch domain and may serve as promising peptide candidates for modulating the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction.

To provide more concrete evidence of binding stability, equilibrated conformations were extracted from the MD trajectories and subsequently subjected to docking and binding free-energy calculations (Supplementary Figure S6). For the Keap1-pep4369 complex, the mean docking score was −219.692, with a standard deviation (SD) of 0.314 and a standard error (SE) of 0.222, while the mean binding free energy was −45.510 kcal/mol, with an SD of 0.255 and SE of 0.180. Similarly, the mean docking score for the Keap1–pep6649 complex was −234.113, with an SD of 0.127 and an SE of 0.042, whereas the mean binding free energy was −65.495 kcal/mol, with an SD of 0.332 and SE of 0.235. The low SD and SE values observed for both peptides indicate minimal variability in the predicted docking scores and binding free energies across the analysed MD conformations. This statistical consistency indicates that the favourable binding characteristics of pep4369 and pep6649 were reproducibly maintained across the trajectory-derived conformations, supporting the stability and robustness of their interactions with Keap1.

Overall, the integrated computational workflow employed in this study identified two novel peptide candidates, LMEETYEFL (pep4369) and LDYETYEFL (pep6649), as potential Keap1 inhibitors. These peptides exhibited favourable binding affinity, structural stability during MD simulations, and encouraging pharmacokinetic and safety profiles, supporting their prioritization for further investigation. However, the BBB permeability, toxicity, allergenicity, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory profiles identified in this study are based entirely on computational predictions and require validation through appropriate experimental studies. Furthermore, BBB permeability does not demonstrate neuronal uptake, intracellular delivery, endosomal escape, proteolytic stability, or favourable in vivo pharmacokinetic behaviour. Therefore, comprehensive experimental validation using in vitro and in vivo models is required to confirm the CNS delivery, biological activity, and therapeutic potential of these peptide candidates for the treatment of NDDs.

Conclusion

Our rational computational design and multi-step screening strategy enabled the identification of two lead peptides, LMEETYEFL (pep4369) and LDYETYEFL (pep6649), as promising candidate inhibitors of the Keap1. The selected peptides exhibited favourable predicted pharmacological and physicochemical properties, including BBB permeability, non-toxicity, non-allergenicity, non-hemolytic behaviour, and predicted antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. Molecular docking, binding free energy calculations, and per-residue energy contribution analyses supported their favourable binding within the Keap1 binding pocket. Furthermore, their dynamic behaviour assessed through MD simulations indicated stable protein-peptide complexes, as evidenced by consistent RMSD values, low residue-level fluctuations throughout the simulation period. FEL analysis further supported the presence of energetically favourable conformational states. Moreover, hydrogen-bond occupancy, conformational analysis, and MD-based per-residue decomposition analyses highlighted the key contributions of Arg415, Arg483, and Arg380 in maintaining the Keap1-peptide binding interactions. Collectively, these findings support the potential of LMEETYEFL and LDYETYEFL as candidate Keap1 inhibitors. While experimental validation is required to confirm their biological efficacy and specificity, these findings provide a promising framework for the development of peptide-based therapeutics targeting Keap1 for the treatment of NDDs.
