TraCS : Trajectory Collection in Continuous Space under Local Differential Privacy
Ye Zheng, Yidan Hu

Abstract
. Trajectory collection is fundamental for location-based services but often involves sensitive information, such as a user’s daily routine, raising privacy concerns. Local differential privacy (LDP) provides provable privacy guarantees for users, even when the data collector is untrusted. Existing trajectory collection methods ensure LDP only for discrete location spaces, where the number of locations affects their privacy guarantees and trajectory utility. Moreover, the location space is often naturally continuous, such as in flying and sailing trajectories, making these methods unsuitable. This paper proposes two trajectory collection methods that ensure LDP for continuous spaces: TraCS-D , which perturbs the direction and distance of locations, and TraCS-C , which perturbs the Cartesian coordinates of locations. Both methods are theoretically and experimentally analyzed for trajectory utility. TraCS can also be applied to discrete spaces by rounding perturbed locations to the nearest discrete points. It is independent of the number of locations and has only Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \Theta(1) time complexity in each perturbation generation. Evaluation results on discrete location spaces validate this advantage and show that TraCS outperforms state-of-the-art methods with improved trajectory utility, especially for large privacy parameters.

1.
Introduction

A trajectory is a sequence of locations that describes movement over time.
Users’ trajectory data serves as a fundamental data source for location-based applications,
such as navigation and route planning.
However, the direct collection of trajectory data from users raises significant privacy concerns,
particularly given the growing privacy expectations of users and stringent regulations
(
gdp 2016
;
Krumm 2009
)
.
Anonymization techniques, such as
k
-anonymity
(
Sweeney 2002
;
Li
et al. 2007
)
, have been shown to be insufficient
for protecting trajectory privacy
(
Chen
et al. 2011
)
.
Therefore, stronger privacy guarantees are needed for such data.

Local differential privacy (LDP) provides a provable worst-case privacy guarantee
by requiring the hardness of distinguishing the presence of a user’s data from any released data.
The privacy level of an LDP mechanism is quantified by its privacy parameter
\varepsilon
,
which also implies the extent of data perturbation.
Through LDP, users can release their perturbed trajectory data to an untrusted data collector
while still benefiting from the location-based services provided by the collector.

Existing LDP methods for trajectory collection are designed for discrete spaces.
They either rely on properly splitting the entire location space into grids
(
Du
et al. 2023
)

or assume the location space consists of a set of labeled locations (a.k.a. points of interest)
(
Cunningham et al. 2021b
;
Zhang
et al. 2023
)
.
These methods’ privacy guarantees are w.r.t. their corresponding discretized sets,
and their efficacy depends on the size of these sets.
Improper discretization leads to a loss of privacy guarantees and reduced trajectory utility.
Moreover, labeled locations are not always available,
especially in continuous spaces such as those encountered in flying and sailing trajectories.
Therefore, it is necessary to design effective trajectory collection methods for continuous spaces.

However, designing such methods is challenging due to the following reasons:
(i) they must guarantee LDP w.r.t. each location in the continuous space;
(ii) despite the rigorous privacy guarantee, they must still provide sufficient trajectory utility.

This paper proposes two LDP methods for trajectory collection in continuous spaces.
The main insight is to decompose the
2
D continuous space into
1
D subspaces and design mechanisms for each subspace.
Our mechanisms leverage the concept of piecewise-based mechanisms
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
)
for
1
D bounded numerical domains.
Based on two different decompositions of the continuous space, we propose two methods,
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C
.

TraCS-D
treats the continuous space as the composition of a direction space and a distance space.
We design a novel direction perturbation mechanism to ensure LDP for the direction space.
Combined with a redesigned piecewise-based mechanism on the distance space,

TraCS-D
ensures LDP for the continuous
2
D space.

TraCS-C
treats the continuous space as the Cartesian product of two distance spaces.
Each location has a
2
D Cartesian coordinate representation, and
TraCS-C
perturbs these coordinates.
Our methods can also be applied to discrete spaces by rounding the perturbed location to the nearest discrete points.
Compared with existing methods based on the Exponential mechanism
(
Cunningham et al. 2021b
;
Zhang
et al. 2023
)
,
our methods are not affected by the number of locations in the location space and cost only
\Theta(1)
time in each perturbation generation.
Specifically, our contributions are as follows:

•

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to provide trajectory collection methods
for continuous space under LDP.
We propose
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C
for this purpose.
These methods introduce novel location perturbation mechanisms that exploit
the direction information and coordinate information of trajectories in continuous spaces.
We theoretically and experimentally analyze their trajectory utility.

•

Our methods
TraCS
can also be applied to discrete spaces.
Compared with existing methods for discrete spaces,
TraCS
have significantly lower computation complexity in generating perturbed locations.
Experimental results on the discrete location spaces
validate the efficiency of
TraCS
, and also
show that it outperforms the state-of-the-art methods with enhanced trajectory utility,
particularly for large privacy parameters.

2.
Preliminaries

This section formulates the problem, introduces LDP, and reviews existing trajectory collection methods.

2.1.
Problem Formulation

A typical trajectory collection schema consists of
a set of
trajectories from users and
one
collector.
Each trajectory
\mathcal{T}
is a sequence of locations
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{n}\}
,
where
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a continuous and bounded domain.
The collector needs to collect these trajectories to provide location-based services.

However, the collector is untrusted and may act as an adversary attempting to infer users’ private data.
Therefore, releasing the private trajectories to the collector pose a privacy risk.
To protect privacy, users perturb their trajectories using a privacy mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{S}\to Range(\mathcal{M})
,
and then send the perturbed trajectories
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\{\tau_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\tau_{n}^{\prime}\}

to the collector.

We aim to design a mechanism
\mathcal{M}
that satisfies
\varepsilon
-LDP, providing a provable privacy guarantee for the private trajectories.
Additionally,
\mathcal{M}
should ensure the utility of the perturbed trajectories and incur limited computational cost for real-time applications.

2.2.
Local Differential Privacy

Definition 2.1 (
\varepsilon
-LDP
(
Duchi
et al. 2013
)
).

A perturbation mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\to Range(\mathcal{M})
satisfies
\varepsilon
-LDP,
iff for two arbitrary input
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, the probability ratio of outputting the
same
y
is bounded:

\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\forall y\in Range(\mathcal{M}):\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x_{1})=y]}{\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x_{2})=y]}\leq\exp(\varepsilon).

If
\mathcal{M}(x)
is continuous, the probability
\Pr[\cdot]
is replaced by probability density function (
pdf
).
Intuitively, Definition
2.1
represents the difficulty of distinguishing

x_{1}
and
x_{2}
given
y
.
Lower values of the privacy parameter
\varepsilon\in[0,+\infty)
mean higher privacy.
For example,
\varepsilon=0
requires
\mathcal{M}
to map two arbitrary
inputs to any output
y
with the same probability,
thus the perturbed output contains no distribution information of the private input,
making any hypothesis-testing method to infer the private
x
powerless.

Theorem 2.2 (Sequential Composition
(
Dwork and Roth 2014
;
Zhang
et al. 2023
)
).

Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
be two mechanisms that satisfy
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
-LDP, respectively.
Their composition, defined as
\mathcal{M}_{1,2}:(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2})\to(Range(\mathcal{M}_{1}),Range(\mathcal{M}_{2}))
,
satisfies
(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})
-LDP.

Sequential composition allows us to design LDP mechanisms for
2
D data by composing two mechanisms for
1
D.

2.2.1.
Piecewise-based Mechanism

If
\mathcal{X}
is continuous and bounded, the state-of-the-art LDP mechanisms are piecewise-based
(
Wang et al. 2019
;
Li et al. 2020
)
,
i.e. they sample the output from piecewise probability distributions defined on
\mathcal{X}
.
We summarize them in the following form:

Definition 2.3 (Piecewise-based mechanism).

A piecewise-based mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\to Range(\mathcal{M})
is a family of probability distributions that,
given input
x\in\mathcal{X}
, outputs
y\in Range(\mathcal{M})
according to

pdf[\mathcal{M}(x)=y]=\begin{cases}p_{\varepsilon}&\text{if}\ y\in[l_{x,\varepsilon},r_{x,\varepsilon}),\\
p_{\varepsilon}/\exp{(\varepsilon)}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
p_{\varepsilon}
is the sampling probability w.r.t.
\varepsilon
and
[l_{x,\varepsilon},r_{x,\varepsilon})
is the sampling interval w.r.t.
x
and
\varepsilon
.

Range(\mathcal{M})\supseteq\mathcal{X}
is also a continuous and bounded domain.

Piecewise-based mechanisms sample the output
y
with a high probability

p_{\varepsilon}
within
[l_{x,\varepsilon},r_{x,\varepsilon})

and a lower probability in the remaining two pieces, while satisfying the
\varepsilon
-LDP constraint.
Typical instantiations include PM
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
for mean estimation
and SW
(
Li et al. 2020
)
for distribution estimation.
Our perturbation mechanisms also leverage the piecewise-based design but with different instantiations
tailed for different data domains.

2.2.2.
k
-RR

If
\mathcal{X}
is discrete, especially when
|\mathcal{X}|
is not large
(
Wang
et al. 2017
)
, a classical LDP mechanism is
k
-RR
(
Kairouz
et al. 2014
)
.

Definition 2.4.

k
-RR is a sampling mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
that,
given input
x\in\mathcal{X}
, outputs
y\in\mathcal{X}
according to

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(x)=y]=\begin{cases}\frac{\exp(\varepsilon)}{|\mathcal{X}|-1+\exp(\varepsilon)}&\text{if}\ y=x,\\
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|-1+\exp(\varepsilon)}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

k
-RR outputs the truth
x
with a higher probability or outputs other values with a lower probability,
while satisfying
\varepsilon
-LDP.

NGram
(
Cunningham et al. 2021b
)
hierarchically decomposes the physical space
\mathcal{S}
into fine-grained discrete regions
with semantics.
Specifically,
\mathcal{S}
is first decomposed into small regions
R_{s}
,
which are further decomposed by categories into
R_{c}
,
and then attached with time information
R_{t}
.
A decomposed “gram” might look like
r_{sct}=\{\text{mountain, church, 3am}\}
.
This decomposition allows trajectory
\mathcal{T}
to be represented at a knowledge level,
enabling the usage of public knowledge to define reachability, i.e. to constrain possible perturbed trajectories.
For example, the above gram
r_{sct}
is unlikely to be visited at
3
am
as a church is closed at that time, reducing the possible
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.

LDPTrace
(
Du
et al. 2023
)

synthesizes trajectories based on a generative model.
Specifically, the physical space
\mathcal{S}
is discretized into cells,
and each private trajectory is represented as a sequence of cells
\mathcal{T}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}\}
.
Neighboring cells, i.e.
c_{i}
and
c_{i+1}
, form transition patterns,
which are perturbed by LDP mechanisms.
These perturbed transition patterns are then used to train a Markov chain model that captures the transition patterns between cells.
Finally, new trajectories
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are synthesized by the trained model.

ATP
(
Zhang
et al. 2023
)
uses direction perturbation to limit the set of possible locations.
It assumes the location space is a finite set of labeled locations
and represents a trajectory
\mathcal{T}=\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\dots,\tau_{n}\}
as a sequence of these locations.
When perturbing a location
\tau_{i+1}
, ATP perturbs the direction from
\tau_{i}
to
\tau_{i+1}
.
It divides the direction space into
k
sectors and uses
k
-RR to sample a direction sector.
The perturbed location
\tau_{i+1}^{\prime}
should be within the sampled sector, thereby limiting the possible
\tau_{i+1}^{\prime}
,
which is then sampled using the Exponential mechanism.

We summarize their limitations in the context of continuous spaces as follows:

•

These methods are limited to handling discrete location spaces.
The number of locations or cells is a crucial parameter that determines the perturbation mechanism and affects the privacy guarantee.
Although it is possible to discretize continuous spaces,
proper discretization is challenging to balance the trajectory utility and privacy.

•

Besides the discretization, methods based on the Exponential mechanism, such as NGram and ATP,
suffer from high complexity in perturbation generation
and trajectory utility loss when the discrete space is large.
Additionally, designing public knowledge in NGram is challenging, especially for flying or sailing trajectories;
synthesis methods in LDPTrace rely on the regularity of the training trajectories, which is not always guaranteed.

Appendix
B.1
details the weaknesses of the Exponential mechanism.
In summary, it requires calculating the score functions of each possible pair of locations.
This costs
\Theta(m)
time, where
m
is the number of locations in the discrete space.
Additionally, each sampling (perturbation generation) also costs
\Theta(m)
time.
This time complexity is not affordable for large-scale location spaces.

To address these limitations, we propose
TraCS
, a novel trajectory collection method that provides LDP guarantees for trajectories in continuous spaces.

3.
Our Method:
TraCS

This section presents our methods,
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C
, along with their theoretical analyses and extensions.

3.1.
Location Space

We consider the location space constrained by a pair of longitude and latitude lines,
i.e.
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a rectangular area
[a_{\text{sta}},a_{\text{end}}]\times[b_{\text{sta}},b_{\text{end}}]
,
where
a_{\text{sta}},a_{\text{end}}
denote the longitudes and
b_{\text{sta}},b_{\text{sta}}
denote the latitudes.
Consequently, each location
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
has a natural representation as a pair of coordinates
(a_{i},b_{i})
.
This representation aligns with real-world location data (e.g. GPS data) and
existing trajectory collection methods for discrete spaces
(
Cunningham et al. 2021b
;
Du
et al. 2023
;
Zhang
et al. 2023
;
Jiang et al. 2022
)
.

The core idea behind having various LDP methods for the location space
\mathcal{S}
stems from its multiple decompositions.
One decomposition means
\mathcal{S}
can be represented by several subspaces.
For example, besides the aforementioned longitude-latitude coordinate representation, each location
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{S}

can also be represented by a direction component and a distance component.
Based on these decompositions, we propose two LDP methods:

TraCS-D
, which perturbs the direction and distance subspaces,
and
TraCS-C
, which perturbs subspaces of the Cartesian coordinates.

3.2.1.
Direction Perturbation

The technical challenge in direction perturbation is designing an LDP mechanism for the circular domain
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=[0,2\pi)
.
This is non-trivial due to the following properties of the circular domain:

•

The circular domain
[0,2\pi)
is bounded but not finite, making the unbounded mechanisms
and discrete mechanisms inapplicable.

•

The circular domain has a unique distance metric, e.g. the distance between
0
and
2\pi
is
0
, not
2\pi
,
making the Euclidean distance-based mechanisms inapplicable.

Specifically, classical mechanisms like Laplace and Gaussian
(
Dwork and Roth 2014
)

add unbounded noise, resulting in an output domain of
(-\infty,+\infty)
.
This output domain makes them inapplicable to the circular domain
[0,2\pi)
.
Additionally, these mechanisms are designed for the distance metric
d(y,x)=|y-x|
, which is inconsistent with the distance in circular domain.
Discrete mechanisms, such as the
k
-RR mechanism
(
Kairouz
et al. 2014
)
,
require a finite domain size
|k|
, making them unsuitable for direct application to
[0,2\pi)
.

To illustrate the necessity and superiority of
TraCS-D
’s piecewise-based design,
we start with a strawman approach that extends the
k
-RR mechanism to the circular domain
[0,2\pi)
.

Strawman approach:
k
-RR + uniform sampling.

ATP
(
Zhang
et al. 2023
)
divides the direction space
[0,2\pi)
into
k
sectors
and applies the
k
-RR mechanism.
This approach guarantees LDP for the
k
sectors but not for the entire
[0,2\pi)
.
Even so, we can extend this approach to
[0,2\pi)
by
further uniformly sampling a direction
\varphi^{\prime}
from
the output sector of
k
-RR.

Specifically, the circular domain
[0,2\pi)
is divided into
k
sectors

[i-1,i)\cdot 2\pi/k
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
.
Assume the private direction
\varphi
falls into the
j
-th sector.
Applying the
k
-RR mechanism outputs sector
i=j

with probability
p=\exp(\varepsilon)/(k-1+\varepsilon)
,
or outputs sector
i\neq j
with probability
p/\exp(\varepsilon)
.
Then, we uniformly sample a direction
\varphi^{\prime}
from the
i
-th sector,
i.e.
\varphi^{\prime}\sim U(i-1,i)\cdot 2\pi/k
.

Limitation.

This strawman approach introduces inherent inner-sector errors due to uniform sampling within each pre-defined sector.
If
k
is small, uniform sampling within each sector leads to significant inner-sector errors,
even for large privacy parameter
\varepsilon
.
Conversely, too large
k
values impair the utility of the
k
-RR mechanism
(
Wang
et al. 2017
)
.

Design rationale.

To address this limitation,
we design a direction perturbation mechanism in which the perturbed direction
is independent of hyperparameters like
k
,
thereby avoiding inherent errors beyond those introduced by the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
.

We leverage the idea of piecewise-based mechanisms’ design.
Specifically, (i) we instantiate a piecewise-based mechanism for the circular domain
[0,2\pi)
,
which guarantees LDP for the entire direction space;
(ii) the perturbed direction is sampled from a piecewise probability distribution,
centered around the private direction with high probability and only dependent on the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
.

Definition 3.1 (Direction Perturbation Mechanism).

Given a private direction
\varphi
and a privacy parameter
\varepsilon
,

TraCS-D
’s direction perturbation mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}:[0,2\pi)\to[0,2\pi)

is defined by:

pdf[\mathcal{M}_{\circ}(\varphi)=\varphi^{\prime}]=\begin{cases}p_{\varepsilon}&\text{if}\ \varphi^{\prime}\in[l_{\varphi,\varepsilon},r_{\varphi,\varepsilon}),\\
p_{\varepsilon}/\exp{(\varepsilon)}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
p_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\exp(\varepsilon/2)
is the sampling probability,
and
[l_{\varphi,\varepsilon},r_{\varphi,\varepsilon})
is the sampling interval that

\begin{split}l_{\varphi,\varepsilon}&=\left(\varphi-\pi\frac{\exp(\varepsilon/2)-1}{\exp(\varepsilon)-1}\right)\mod 2\pi,\\
r_{\varphi,\varepsilon}&=\left(\varphi+\pi\frac{\exp(\varepsilon/2)-1}{\exp(\varepsilon)-1}\right)\mod 2\pi.\end{split}

The above mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
is defined by a piecewise probability distribution with three pieces,
where the central piece
[l_{\varphi,\varepsilon},r_{\varphi,\varepsilon})
has a higher probability density
p_{\varepsilon}
.
Obviously, this mechanism satisfies LDP for the circular domain
[0,2\pi)
.

A key advantage of
TraCS-D
’s direction perturbation mechanism is the “dominant” sector.
We refer to the central piece
[l_{\varphi,\varepsilon},r_{\varphi,\varepsilon})
as the dominant sector because it
centers around the private direction
\varphi
with high probability.
The dominant sector varies with the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
;
a larger
\varepsilon
results in a narrower dominant sector,
closer to the private direction
\varphi
, eliminating the inner-sector errors of the strawman approach.

3.2.2.
Distance Perturbation

The other subspace of
TraCS-D
is the distance space
\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}
.
Besides the boundedness, the size of this space also varies with the reference location
\tau_{i}
and the direction
\varphi
.
The technical challenge in designing an LDP mechanism for this space lies in calculating
the size
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|
for any
\varphi
at any
\tau_{i}
.

Given location
\tau_{i}
, any other location
\tau_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
can be represented by a pair of coordinates
(\varphi,r(\varphi))
relative to
\tau_{i}
.
Since we focus on

3.4.
Rounding to Discrete Space

Although
TraCS
is designed for continuous location spaces, it can be applied to any fine-grained discrete location space
by rounding the perturbed locations to the nearest discrete locations.
The rounding is a post-processing step that does not affect the privacy guarantee for the continuous space.

Specifically, if the discrete location space is a set of cells,
we can round the perturbed location to the cell that contains it.
Formally, assume
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
discretize
\mathcal{S}
into
m
cells.
If
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
is the perturbed location of
\tau_{i}
in
TraCS
, then the discretized
TraCS
outputs

c_{j}
such that
\tau_{i}^{\prime}\in c_{j}
.
Commonly, the cells are evenly divided, so the rounding costs
\Theta(1)
time,
as
c_{j}
can be determined by the coordinates of
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
.

If the location space is a set of location points, we can round the perturbed location to the nearest location point.
Formally, assume
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is a set of location points,
and
\tau_{i}^{\prime}
is the perturbed location of
\tau_{i}
in
TraCS
.
Then, the discretized
TraCS
outputs
\argmin_{p_{j}\in\mathcal{P}}||\tau_{i}^{\prime}-p_{j}||_{2}
.
The time complexity of rounding to the nearest location point is
\mathcal{O}(m)
in the worst case.
In practice, this time complexity can be significantly reduced by using heuristic or approximation algorithms.

3.5.1.
Privacy Comparison Between
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C

Although
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C
provide the same level of privacy guarantee,
they have different privacy interpretations.

In
TraCS-D
, the LDP guarantee is provided for the direction information and the subsequent distance information.
This means that when the usage of
TraCS-D
and the perturbed trajectory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is publicly known,
any observer can hardly infer the private direction
\varphi:\tau^{\prime}_{i}\to\tau_{i+1}

and the private distance
r(\varphi):|\tau^{\prime}_{i}\to\tau_{i+1}|
from the known
\tau^{\prime}_{i}
and
\tau^{\prime}_{i+1}
.
Although the distance space
\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}
relies on a specific direction,
it does not leak the direction information, as the direction space
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=[0,2\pi)
is independent of any location
and
TraCS-D
uses the perturbed direction, which preserves LDP.

In
TraCS-C
, the LDP guarantee is provided for the two independent distance spaces
\mathcal{D}_{a}
and
\mathcal{D}_{b}
.
This means that when the usage of
TraCS-C
and the perturbed trajectory
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is publicly known,
any observer can hardly infer the private distances
d_{a}
and
d_{b}
.

3.5.2.
Impact of the Size of
\mathcal{S}

Intuitively, a larger location space
\mathcal{S}
requires larger privacy parameters to achieve the same level of utility.
For
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C
, this impact is reflected in the size of the direction space and distance spaces.

In
TraCS-D
, the direction space
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=[0,2\pi)
is independent of the size of
\mathcal{S}
,
meaning that the MSE of the perturbed direction,
\mathrm{MSE}[\varphi^{\prime}]
, remains constant regardless of the size of
\mathcal{S}
.
The distance space
\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}
depends on the specific direction
\varphi
and the size of
\mathcal{S}
.
In the worst case,
\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}
is the diagonal of
\mathcal{S}
.
We have shown that the MSE of
\mathcal{M}_{-}
is quadratic to the size of the distance space
after linear mapping in Section
3.2.4
.
Therefore, when
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|
increases linearly, the expected error of the perturbed distance,
i.e.
\mathrm{E}[|r^{\prime}(\varphi)-r(\varphi)|]
, also increases linearly.
However, the error in the direction space
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}
affects the error in
\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}

in a non-linear way.
Specifically, under the Euclidean distance metric,
this effect is
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|\cdot 2\sin(|\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}|/2)
,
which is the chord length of the arc between
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
.

In
TraCS-C
, both
\mathcal{D}_{a}
and
\mathcal{D}_{b}
are determined by the size of
\mathcal{S}
.
Since they use mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{-}
independently,
their expected error increases linearly with the increase of
|\mathcal{D}_{a}|
and
|\mathcal{D}_{b}|
.
Meanwhile, the error in
\mathcal{D}_{a}
affects the error in
\mathcal{D}_{b}
linearly.

3.5.4.
Other Shapes of Location Space

The design of LDP mechanisms is space-specific,
implying that extensions to other shapes of location space are non-trivial.

For
TraCS
, the challenge lies in determining the size of each subspace,
i.e.
|\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}|
,
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|

|\mathcal{D}_{a}|
, and
|\mathcal{D}_{b}|

Among them,
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|
is not apparent but can be calculated for rectangular location spaces.
Although calculating

is generally non-trivial for irregular shapes,

TraCS
can be extended to parallelogram location spaces by a linear transformation to a rectangular space.
Meanwhile,
TraCS-D
can be used for circular areas,
which are widely used in relative location representation, e.g. radar and sonar systems.

TraCS-D
for circular area.

When the location space is circular with radius
R

TraCS-D
has a more straightforward design.
The main insight is that such a location space
\mathcal{S}_{R}
has a natural representation in polar coordinates,

\mathcal{S}_{R}=\{(\varphi,r):\varphi\in[0,2\pi),r\in[0,R)\}

Any location
\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{R}
corresponds to a pair of coordinates

In this case, the direction space
\mathcal{D}_{\varphi}=[0,2\pi)
and the distance space
\mathcal{D}_{r}=[0,R)

\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
to
\varphi_{i}
and
\mathcal{M}_{-}

5.
Related Work

Trajectory Collection under LDP

Based on the privacy notion, trajectory collection methods under LDP can be categorized into two groups: pure LDP and relaxed LDP.
Compared with relaxed LDP, pure LDP imposes a stronger constraint for indistinguishability.

Existing trajectory collection methods under pure LDP include NGram
(
Cunningham et al. 2021b
)
, LDPTrace
(
Du
et al. 2023
)
, and ATP
(
Zhang
et al. 2023
)
.
Among them, LDPTrace focuses on trajectory synthesis within a given location space, while NGram, ATP, and this paper focus on trajectory perturbation.
NGram attempts to ensure pure LDP on the “trajectory space”, which is stronger than the location space.
However, this is proved extremely challenging as the trajectory space increases exponentially with the number of possible locations.
ATP and this paper focus on ensuring pure LDP in the location space.
Unlike ATP and other existing methods, this paper focuses on continuous location spaces, which are stronger than any fine-grained discrete location space.

As a special case of trajectory collection, L-SRR
(
Wang
et al. 2022
)
focuses on single-location publishing.
It discretizes the location space into different-sized cells, with smaller cells near the private location and larger cells farther away.
Then, SRR (a variant of
k
-RR) is applied to the cells.
Similar to other discretization methods, the number of cells affects its privacy and utility.

TraCS
provides solutions to this problem in continuous spaces, achieving better privacy and utility, particularly when the number of cells is large.

A widely used relaxed LDP notion for trajectory collection is Geo-indistinguishability
(
Andrés et al. 2013
;
Mendes
et al. 2020
)
,
where the indistinguishability between two locations is a function of their distance, i.e. privacy decreases with distance.
Input-discriminative LDP
(
Gu
et al. 2020
)
goes one step further, assigning each location its privacy level.
Threshold-integrated LDP
(
Yang
et al. 2024
)
defines a threshold to constrain the private region near a location.
This is similar to NGram’s reachability constraint but with a formal privacy definition.
More relaxed LDP notions for trajectory collection can be found in
(
Miranda-Pascual
et al. 2023
)
.
Although these relaxed LDP notions generally provide better trajectory utility than pure LDP,
they sacrifice the strong privacy guarantee of pure LDP, and often need hyperparameters to define the privacy level.

Trajectory Collection under DP

As a weaker privacy notion than LDP, DP
(
Dwork 2006
;
Dwork and Roth 2014
)

requires only that any two neighboring locations in the location space be indistinguishable,
rather than any two arbitrary locations.

The usage of direction information in trajectory collection dates back to an early work SDD
(
Jiang
et al. 2013
)
,
which attempts to add unbounded Laplace noise to the direction information.
It provides a DP guarantee for unbounded continuous location spaces, but results in poor utility.
The notion of “n-gram” dates back to
(
Chen
et al. 2012
)
, where it abstracts a trajectory pattern as a gram.

External knowledge is also widely used in DP-based trajectory collection methods.
NGram’s prior work
(
Cunningham et al. 2021a
)
provides a DP-based solution for trajectory synthesis
by exploiting the public knowledge of the road network.
Focusing on particular privacy-concerned locations, or sensitive zones
(
Wang and
Kankanhalli 2020
)
, can also
improve the trajectory utility while maintaining partial privacy guarantees.

6.
Conclusions

This paper presents
TraCS-D
and
TraCS-C
, providing local differential privacy guarantees for trajectory collection in continuous location spaces.

TraCS-D
uses a novel direction-distance perturbation procedure, while
TraCS-C
perturbs the Cartesian coordinates of each location.
These two methods can also be applied to discrete location spaces by rounding the perturbed location to the nearest discrete point.
Their trajectory utility is theoretically analyzed and empirically evaluated.
Evaluation results on discrete location spaces show that
TraCS
outperforms
state-of-the-art methods in trajectory utility, particularly when the privacy parameter is large.

Beyond trajectory collection, the proposed perturbation mechanisms can also be applied to other scenarios with continuous and bounded domains, providing a direction for future work.

A.2.
Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

We prove the
\Theta(e^{-\varepsilon/2})
convergence rate by calculating the MSE of the perturbation mechanism.
Specifically, the MSE of
\mathcal{M}_{-}
is calculated as follows:

\mathrm{MSE}[\mathcal{M}_{-}(\overline{r})]=\int_{0}^{1}(\overline{r}^{*}-\overline{r})^{2}pdf[\mathcal{M}_{-}(\overline{r})=\overline{r}^{*}]d\overline{r}^{*}.

The worst-case MSE is achieved when
\overline{r}=0
or
\overline{r}=1
, i.e. at the endpoints (similar to the variance calculation in
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
).
In this case,

\begin{split}\mathrm{MSE}[\mathcal{M}_{-}(0)]&=\int_{0}^{2C}\left(\overline{r}^{*}-0\right)^{2}p_{\varepsilon}d\overline{r}^{*}+\int_{2C}^{1}\left(\overline{r}^{*}-0\right)^{2}\frac{p_{\varepsilon}}{e^{\varepsilon}}d\overline{r}^{*}\\
&=\frac{8C^{3}p_{\varepsilon}}{3}+\frac{(1-8C^{3})p_{\varepsilon}}{3e^{\varepsilon}}.\end{split}

with
p_{\varepsilon}
and
C
defined in Definition
3.3
.
We omit the calculation for
\overline{r}=1
as it is symmetric to
\overline{r}=0
.
Plugging in the values of
p_{\varepsilon}
and
C
, we can simplify the MSE using big
\mathcal{O}
notation:

\begin{split}\mathrm{MSE}[\mathcal{M}_{-}(0)]&=\frac{1}{3}e^{-\varepsilon/2}+\frac{(e^{\varepsilon/2}-1)^{3}}{3e^{\varepsilon/2}(e^{\varepsilon}-1)^{2}}\\
&=\frac{1}{3}e^{-\varepsilon/2}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-\varepsilon}).\end{split}

Therefore, as
\varepsilon\to\infty
, the worst-case MSE of
\mathcal{M}_{-}
converges to zero at a rate of
\Theta(e^{-\varepsilon/2})
.
The MSE of
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}
can be calculated in the same way, resulting in a convergence rate of
\Theta(e^{-\varepsilon/2})
as well.
∎

B.1.
Weakness of the Exponential Mechanism

This section discusses the Exponential mechanism and its weaknesses in the context of trajectory collection.

Definition B.1 (Exponential Mechanism
(
Nissim
et al. 2012
)
).

Given a scoring function
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, a privacy parameter
\varepsilon>0
,
and a set of possible outputs
\mathcal{Y}
, the Exponential mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\exp}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}

is defined by:

\Pr[\mathcal{M}_{\exp}(x)=y]=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon d(x,y)}{2\Delta d}\right)}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp\left(\frac{\varepsilon d(x,y^{\prime})}{2\Delta d}\right)},

where
\Delta d=\max_{y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}|d(x,y)-d(x,y^{\prime})|
is the sensitivity of the scoring function
d
.

In the context of trajectory collection, the most used scoring function is the negative Euclidean distance
d(x,y)\coloneq-||x-y||_{2}
.
Therefore, locations with a smaller Euclidean distance to the private location
x
are more likely to be sampled.

The biggest weakness of the Exponential mechanism is the complexity in calculating the score function and sampling from the probability distribution.
It requires computing the Euclidean distance between every pair of locations in the location space,
which is computationally expensive for large-scale spaces.
In ATP and NGram, the location space for perturbation changes at each time step,
meaning that the score functions need to be recomputed at each perturbation,
resulting in
\mathcal{O}(m)
time complexity for each perturbation, where
m
is the size of the location space.

The complexity of sampling from the Exponential mechanism is also
\mathcal{O}(m)
,
as it needs to sample from an
m
-piece cumulative distribution function (CDF) at each perturbation.

In contrast, we will see that piecewise-based perturbation mechanisms in
TraCS
do not require score functions,
and they sample from a
3
-piece CDF at each perturbation,
resulting in a negligible constant time complexity, i.e.
\Theta(1)
, for each perturbation.

B.3.
Detailed Expression of
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|

For each location
\tau_{i}
, denote its directions to the four endpoints of the rectangular
location space as
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3},\varphi_{4}
. We have:

\begin{split}\varphi_{1}&=\mathrm{atan2}\left(b_{\text{end}}-b_{i},a_{\text{end}}-a_{i}\right),\\
\varphi_{2}&=\mathrm{atan2}\left(b_{\text{end}}-b_{i},a_{\text{sta}}-a_{i}\right),\\
\varphi_{3}&=\mathrm{atan2}\left(b_{\text{sta}}-b_{i},a_{\text{sta}}-a_{i}\right)+2\pi,\\
\varphi_{4}&=\mathrm{atan2}\left(b_{\text{sta}}-b_{i},a_{\text{end}}-a_{i}\right)+2\pi.\end{split}

Then,
|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|
has the following four cases:

\begin{split}|\mathcal{D}_{r(\varphi)}|=\begin{cases}\frac{a_{\text{end}}-a_{i}}{\cos\varphi}&\text{if}\ \varphi\in[0,\varphi_{1})\cup[\varphi_{4},2\pi),\\
\frac{b_{\text{end}}-b_{i}}{\sin\varphi}&\text{if}\ \varphi\in[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}),\\
\frac{a_{i}-a_{\text{sta}}}{-\cos\varphi}&\text{if}\ \varphi\in[\varphi_{2},\varphi_{3}),\\
\frac{b_{i}-b_{\text{sta}}}{-\sin\varphi}&\text{if}\ \varphi\in[\varphi_{3},\varphi_{4}),\end{cases}\end{split}

depending on the direction
\varphi
from reference location
\tau_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i})
.

B.4.
Redesigned SW

We redesign the SW mechanism to achieve
\varepsilon
-LDP for the circular space
[0,2\pi)

and the linear space
[0,1)
.
The key idea is to transform the original sampling distribution to these spaces while preserving the LDP constraint.
Similar to Definition
3.1
, the redesigned SW mechanism for direction perturbation
is defined as follows.

Definition B.2 (Redesigned SW for direction perturbation).

Given a private direction
\varphi
and a privacy parameter
\varepsilon
,
redesigned SW for direction perturbation is a mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{\circ}:[0,2\pi)\to[0,2\pi)

defined by:

pdf[\mathcal{M}_{\circ}(\varphi)=\varphi^{\prime}]=\begin{cases}p_{\varepsilon}&\text{if}\ \varphi^{\prime}\in[l_{\varphi,\varepsilon},r_{\varphi,\varepsilon}),\\
p_{\varepsilon}/\exp{(\varepsilon)}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
p_{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon}(\exp(\varepsilon)-1)
is the sampling probability,
and
[l_{\varphi,\varepsilon},r_{\varphi,\varepsilon})
is the sampling interval that

\begin{split}l_{\varphi,\varepsilon}&=\left(\varphi-\pi\frac{\exp(\varepsilon)(\varepsilon-1)+1}{(\exp(\varepsilon)-1)^{2}}\right)\mod 2\pi,\\
r_{\varphi,\varepsilon}&=\left(\varphi+\pi\frac{\exp(\varepsilon)(\varepsilon-1)+1}{(\exp(\varepsilon)-1)^{2}}\right)\mod 2\pi.\end{split}

Similar to Definition
3.3
, the redesigned SW mechanism for distance perturbation
is defined as follows.

Definition B.3 (Redesigned SW for distance perturbation).

Given a private distance
\overline{r}(\varphi)
and a privacy parameter
\varepsilon
,
redesigned SW for distance perturbation is a mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{-}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
that

pdf[\mathcal{M}_{-}(\overline{r}(\varphi))=\overline{r}^{\prime}(\varphi)]=\begin{cases}p_{\varepsilon}&\text{if}\ \overline{r}^{\prime}(\varphi)\in[u,v),\\
p_{\varepsilon}/\exp{(\varepsilon)}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
p_{\varepsilon}=(\exp(\varepsilon)-1)/\varepsilon
is the sampling probability,
and
[u,v)
is the sampling interval that

\begin{split}[u,v)&=\begin{cases}\overline{r}(\varphi)+[-C,C)&\text{if}\ \overline{r}^{\prime}(\varphi)\in[C,1-C),\cr[0,2C)&\text{if}\ \overline{r}^{\prime}(\varphi)\in[0,C),\cr[1-2C,1)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\end{split}

with
C=(\exp(\varepsilon)(\varepsilon-1)+1)/(2(\exp(\varepsilon)-1)^{2})
.

Compared with Definition
3.1
and Definition
3.3
,
the redesigned SW mechanisms have a larger
p_{\varepsilon}
and a narrower
[l,r)
or
[u,v]
interval for sampling.
We can treat the redesigned SW mechanisms as a more aggressive perturbation mechanism:
it tries to sample from a narrow dominant sector with a higher probability (
p_{\varepsilon}
),
which also leads to a higher probability (
p_{\varepsilon}/\exp(\varepsilon)
) of non-dominant sectors due to the LDP constraint.
