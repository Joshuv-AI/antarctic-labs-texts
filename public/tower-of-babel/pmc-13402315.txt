Ultrastructural changes and defense strategy of yellow lupine during silver nanoparticle and Fusarium oxysporum interaction
Authors: Anielkis Batista, Elżbieta Gabała, Sławomir Samardakiewicz, Katarzyna Sadowska, Zbigniew Karolewski, Jan Bocianowski, Iwona Morkunas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study attempted to elucidate the alterations in ultrastructure of root cap cells and some metabolic reactions in yellow lupine (Lupinus luteus L.) root cells induced by silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) during the seed germination stage and seedling development. Also, the role of AgNPs in defense mechanisms against hemibiotrofic patogen Fusarium oxysporum f.sp. lupini was studied. Therefore, ultrastructural analysis of root cap cells of L. luteus L. cvs. Diament and Mister were performed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Additionally, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was detected in root cells using confocal microscopy. The obtained research results showed distinct alterations in ultrastructure of root cap cells after pretreated with AgNPs or during cross-talk between AgNPs and F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini. It was surprising that the application of AgNPs caused local and clearly visible thickenings of the cell wall (CW) with a wide electron-dense apoplast space (AS) in the root cap cells. Also, an interesting observation was an increased presence of dictyosomes (D), characterized by a highly developed cisternae system in cells of the AgNPs and AgNPs + F. oxysporum variants in comparison to the control. An increase in the processes of endocytosis and exocytosis was noticed, which is associated with the transport of material enclosed or inserted into membrane vesicles. Besides, F. oxysporum infection induced strong autophagic activity and the occurrence of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) and multilamellar bodies (MLBs). Furthermore, in response to AgNPs or F. oxysporum alone, or in combination with AgNPs and F. oxysporum, increase in green fluorescence emission associated with ROS, especially H2O2 in relation to the control.

Introduction

Nowadays, climate change, pests, and crop diseases are the most common environmental problems in agriculture. As reported1, climate change is a global threat to the food and nutritional security of the world. Plants are continually exposed to various environmental extremities during their growing period. The stage of plant development determines the risk and the degree of plant sensitivity to unfavorable environmental factors. Climate change also affects agricultural pests and the microbial population, including fungal pathogens occurring in soil. Moreover2, showed that environmental changes may directly affect not only on several aspects of the biology of host plants, but also on plant pathogens, i.e. pathogen development, pathogenicity and survival rates. Also, according to reports of the World Health Organization (WHO), the use of pesticides against pests, including pathogenic fungi should be limited3. Thereby, there is increasing consideration of engineered nanomaterials (ENMs) as promising alternatives for antimicrobial applications. The contribution of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) in limiting plant diseases caused by fungal pathogen, within nano-phytopathology were highlighted4–6. AgNPs and their nanocomposites are increasingly acknowledged as promising metal nanoparticles against plant pathogens, owing to their unique physicochemical characteristics and antimicrobial properties7. Besides8, provided evidence that nanomaterials may have substantial application in plant disease management as a direct suppressant of pathogens or as a carrier of pathogen suppressive chemicals. It has been shown that NPs may act upon plant pathogens at a much lower dose with much greater efficiency compared to conventional pesticides. The nanomaterials can also be used as carrier of active ingredients of pesticides causing suppression to plant pathogenic fungi9. The application of NPs in agriculture has received considerable attention due to their potential to enhance plant growth, resistance to stress factors, and improvement in overall plant productivity10. Therefore, nanotechnology offers the potential to provide innovative solutions for sustainable plant crop production11. However, as reported by12, some studies showed that uncontrolled application of NMs can negatively influence the morpho-physiological, anatomical, molecular and genetic plant indicators. It must further be taken into account that the sublethal effect of NPs on plants depends on the plant species, cultivation conditions, method of application, exposure time, concentration and size6,11. Recent reports have shown promising biostimulatory effect application of AgNPs at hormesis dose13. The hormetic effect caused by AgNPs was also observed in plants such as poplars (Populus deltoides × nigra) and Arabidopsis thaliana growing hydroponically and exposed to PEG-coated 5 and 10 nm Ag-NPs, and carbon-coated 25 nm Ag-NPs14,15.

Moreover16, showed that AgNPs have potential as alternative antifungal agents because there may be highly effective against strains resistant to multiple fungicides. Additionally, AgNP application can impair the development of pathogenic fungus, the membrane integrity and cell structure, energy utilization, and metabolism. Therefore, nanotechnology provides a competitive alternative to traditional chemical pesticides for the management of plant diseases17.

Regardless of the above, there are reports in the published literature showing bioaccumulation and transport of AgNPs by plants18. For example, cellular accumulation and subcellular transport of AgNPs was showed in Arabidopsis root tips using different microscopy methods, including transmission electron microscopy (TEM). AgNPs were detected in lateral root cap followed by subsequent occurrence in internal root tissue. It was shown that AgNPs accumulated progressively in the following sequence, i.e. border cells, root cap, columella and columella initials. In turn19, showing green synthesis of AgNPs by an environmental isolate named AgNPs1, also observed size and shape nanoparticles using TEM. It was reported that NPs showed more stability in water than in the growth medium. The studies have also shown that NPs may have impact not only on the growth of plants, but also destroy the integrity of cells and subcellular organelles, resulting in the loss of cell membrane integrity and mitochondrial function20–22.

In the present study, we show changes in cell ultrastructure of the yellow lupine root cap, pretreated with AgNPs or non-pretreated with AgNPs, or infected with F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini alone or non-infected, and during cross-talk between AgNPs and F. oxysporum, during the seed germination stage and seedling development. Moreover, the results of this study for the first time, next to changes in cell ultrastructure induced by AgNPs and simultaneously AgNPs and F. oxysporum, showed also the relative generation and the subcellular localization of reactive oxygen species (ROS), especially hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)—an important signaling molecule in the roots of two varieties of yellow lupine, both Lupinus luteus L. cv. Diament as well as Lupinus luteus L. cv. Mister.

Therefore, the first aim of this study was observation of the changes in the ultrastructure of the whole cells of root cap. Then attention was paid to the first structural layer, i.e. the cell wall (CW), then on apoplast (AS) (the space outside the plasma membrane including the cell walls and the intercellular spaces), the plasma membrane (PM), the dictyosomes (Ds), endoplasmic reticulum (ER), mitochondria (M), cell nucleus (N) and the amyloplasts (As). In turn, the second aim was to investigate whether and to what extent oxidative stress is induced in the roots of yellow lupine seedlings, in response to AgNPs or infection caused by F. oxysporum alone, and during cross-talk of AgNPs and F. oxysporum. The same experimental model, including the application of AgNPs at hormetic dose and the combination of AgNPs and F. oxysporum infection, was applied in our previous research13. The results of this study are extremely important because hemibiotrophic pathogen F. oxysporum causes a significant loss of many legume crops in the world23,24. In order to limit these negative consequences by F. oxysporum infection, next to integrated disease management strategies combining various agronomic practices with the protection of plants, should be used the resistant varieties25. Because F. oxysporum is soilborne plant pathogen, it mainly affects plant roots, leading to colonizing and blocking vascular bundle cells, specifically xylem vessels, hindering water transport26.

It should be stressed here that there are no data available on changes in ultrastructure of yellow lupine and generation of H2O2 and its subcellular localization during the influence of AgNPs and cross-talk between AgNPs and F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini. It should be mentioned that this pathogen is a facultative parasite, i.e. possessing both biotrophic and necrotropic feeding mechanisms27. During the seed germination stage, hemibiotrophic pathogen F. oxysporum is responsible for Fusarium wilt as well as pre-emergent sprout root and post-emergent seedling rot28. As reported29, this pathogen causes damage to host plants by penetration of hyphae into host vascular tissues, secretion of hydrolytic enzymes, mycotoxin production, causing cellular apoptosis of host plant cells during the progression of the infection. Besides, F. oxysporum is a ubiquitous fungus widely distributed in the environment30. Additionally, soilborne diseases caused by F. oxysporum as a soilborne pathogen of plants are very difficult to control. The cell wall of mycelial cells and/or some macroconidial cells of F. oxysporum becomes thickened to form chlamydospores, which are durable and survive for several decades in soil. Also, it should be taken into account that climate change further increases outbreak risks by altering pathogen evolution and host–pathogen interactions and by facilitating the emergence of new pathogenic strains as reported by31. Moreover, the occurrence and severity of fusarioses depended on the climatic conditions, applied agrotechnics and plant protection used32.

The yellow lupine seeds (vars. Diament and Mister) used in this study were of good quality. The Mister variety was characterized by a low content of alkaloids and saccharides from the raffinose family. In turn, Diament variety was the sweetest variety on the market and contained lowest alkaloid content. Yellow lupine is an important source of protein as well as macro- and microelements, which makes it a valuable food source for animals and humans33,34. Also35, emphasized that lupins are important ecological plants because they can colonize and thrive in low-nutrient soils due to their ability to fix atmospheric nitrogen in symbiosis with bacteria and efficiently take up phosphorous. This legume is relatively easy to cultivate considering its versatility to be cultivated in different type of soils where other legumes fail to grow. Recent study showed a comparative production of yellow lupine among 27 countries in the period between 2014 to 2022, where in this comparison Australia revealed to be the highest producer of yellow lupine. Also, Poland is one of the largest producers of yellow lupine in Europe36,37. Moreover, the available data showed that yellow lupine is the model plant for basic research on nodulation or abscission of organs33. Our long-term research revealed that yellow lupine is also good model plant for basic research on plant-pathogen interaction.

The effect of AgNPs and F. oxysporum f. sp. lupini on ultrastructure of root cap cells of germinating yellow lupine (L. luteus L.cv. Diament) seeds

Electron-microscopic investigations showed that ultrastructure of root cap cells in control variant of Diament variety was typical for parenchyma cells of the root cap (Fig. 2A,a,e,i,m, 1st column). These cells, usually oval in shape, were surrounded by relatively thin cell walls (CW). A large, centrally located vacuole (V) was observed inside them (Fig. 2A, a). The cell nucleus (N) and cytoplasm with well-preserved cellular organelles were also visible, i.e. the amyloplasts (A), mitochondria (M), endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and dictyosome (D) (1st row, Fig. 2A, a). These organelles were characterized by the typical characteristics of this type of intracellular structures. The amyloplasts were characterized by a variety of the shapes, from spherical to irregular. They usually contained several starch grains (SG) of different sizes. There were also numerous small plastoglobules (PG) visible in amyloplast stroma. Mitochondria had an oval shape, less often spherical, and their crest cisterns were usually tubular, less often circular. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) was usually formed by short and single cisterns. The dictyosomes consisted of typical cisternae stacked several times with accompanying secretory vesicles (SV). The shapes of nuclei were from oval to lobed. Nucleus localized in the spherical part of the cells with domination of electron-sparse chromatin and several regions of electron-dense chromatin were observed.

In turn, the root cap cells after application of AgNPs (AgNPs variant) also contain a centrally located vacuole, cytoplasm with numerous cellular organelles, i.e. the dictyosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, the amyloplasts, mitochondria, and the cell nucleus (the 2nd column, Figs. 2A, b,f,j,n and 2B, a,b,c,d. The root cap cells from AgNPs variant differed from the cells of control roots primarily in the increased number of the dictyosomes, characterized by a highly developed cisternae system, and an increased number of the vesicles of dictyosomal origin (Fig. 2B, d).Pretreatment of the roots with AgNPs resulted in the formation of local thickenings of the cell wall (some cell walls were thicker than other the walls in a given cell) (Fig. 2B, c and d). On the border of the protoplast and the walls often there were clearly visible invaginations of the cell membrane (MI), in which electron-dense granules were located (Fig. 2A, f) Similar granules sometimes also occurred in secretory vesicles (SV). Moreover, the accumulation of electron-dense material (i.e. AgNPs) was also observed in the apoplast (Figs. 2A,.f and 2B, a). Differences between root cap cells from AgNPs variant and control variant were also observed in the case of mitochondria (Figs. 2A, i, j and 2B, c). Mitochondria of root cap cells exposed to AgNPs had more spherical (swollen) crests compared to the elongated crests of control cells.There were no changes observed in cell nucleus.

In turn, in cap cells of the roots inoculated with F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini (Fusarium variant), changes in cell vacuolization were observed. Instead of one large vacuole, there were several medium-size vacuoles (Fig. 2A,c, 3rd column). These vacuoles contained autophagic bodies. The autophagic bodies were surrounded by a single membrane, which suggested microautophagy. In the vicinity of the vacuoles, multilamellar bodies (MLB) of various sizes were often found, which in turn indicated the process of macroautophagy. In the cytoplasm, in the vicinity of dictyosomes, numerous secretory vesicles; the so-called the Trans Golgi—TGN network was observed (Fig. 2A, k). These secretory vesicles were directed toward both the vacuoles as well as the cell walls. Mulivesicular bodies (MVB) were also sometimes visible in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2A, g). In the case of amyloplasts, their number was lower compared to the control. In turn, mitochondria had irregular shapes and an expanded intermembrane space (swollen cristae) and more loosened matrix than in control. Mitochondria were sometimes surrounded by endoplasmic reticulum, which also indicated the process of macroautophagy (mitophagy) (Fig. 2A, k). The strong corrugation of the edges of the cell wall also attracted attention (Fig. 2A, g).

In the cap cells of yellow lupine root of Diament variety, exposed to AgNPs and inoculated with F. oxysporum (AgNPs + Fusarium variant), a change in relation to the control was observed, mainly in cell vacuolization (Figs. 2A, d-p, 4th column and 2B, e,f). Instead of one large vacuole, there were numerous, medium-sized vacuoles in these cells (Fig. 2A, d). Moreover, in root cap cells from AgNPs + Fusarium variant, large and irregular thickenings were observed along the cell walls (Figs. 2A, h,l and 2B, e). There were also strong protrusion or invagination of plasma membrane (Fig. 2A, l). Within the thickenings and in the apoplast space between the wall and the cell membrane, electron-gray dense substances in the form of graininess were observed.

Moreover, there were numerous dictyosomes with large dictyosomal vesicles, which may indicate enhanced transport processes related to endocytosis and exocytosis that facilitate the transport of materials into and out of cells (e.g. the transport of cell wall components) (Fig. 2B, e). Small, electron-dense granules were also located in some of these vesicles. Attention was also drawn to the more numerous and longer cisternae of the ribosomal endoplasmic reticulum (RER) than in the control (Figs. 2A, l and 2B, e). A more loosened matrix was observed in mitochondria than in other variants, which was accompanied by a reduced presence of cristae. In the cells from the AgNPs + Fusarium variant, as in the control, there were also numerous amyloplasts with starch grains (Figs. 2A,.p and 2B, f). No changes were found in nucleus.

It should be added that in the cells of all analyzed the experimental variants, the amyloplasts were characterized by a typical structure, large size, various shapes containing numerous starch grains. Besides, plastoglobuli (PG) in the amyloplasts from all analyzed the experimental variants were clearly visible in the stroma (Fig. 2A, m-p).

The effect of AgNPs and F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini on ultrastructure of root cap cells of germinating seeds of yellow lupine (L. luteus L.cv. Mister)

In the root cap cells of the Mister variety from the control variant, cells similar in appearance to the control cells of the Diament variety were observed (Fig. 3A, a, 1st column). These cells also had a relatively large vacuole, a peripherally located cell nucleus and cytoplasm with well-preserved cell organelles, i.e. mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, the dictyosomes and the vesicles (Fig. 3A, a). The amyloplasts were less frequently observed in these cells of Mister variety in comparison to the Diament variety. Besides, cell walls were of medium thickness. There were often intercellular spaces at the junction of several cells, but this is not a difference in comparison to Diament variety.

In cells from the AgNPs variant of Mister variety, similarly to the control, the dominant structure was the vacuole (Fig. 3A,b, 2nd column). However, the vacuole did not always cover such a large cross-sectional area as in control cells. In some cells, dilated areas were observed between the cell membrane and the edge of the cell wall, i.e. dilated areas of apoplast space (AS), which were filled with a gray, reticular structure containing electron-dense granules (Figs. 3A, b,f,n,r and 3B, a,b,e,h) Additionally, local thickening and corrugation of the edges of the cell walls were observed. In the cytoplasmic area, an increased presence of the dictyosomes with dictyosomal vesicles and rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) was observed (Fig. 3A, h).

In cells from the Fusarium variant, as in the control, most of the cell cross-section was covered by a large, centrally located vacuole (Fig. 3A, c, 3rd column). Inside the vacuole, relatively large electron-dense areas with a lenticular shape were observed, which were usually adjacent to the tonoplast. Autophagy bodies in the form of various types of membrane structures were also observed inside the vacuoles (Fig. 3A, c,k). Moreover, in this variant there was an increased number of the dictyosomes and rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), which in this variant was composed of clearly longer cisternae than in the control (Fig. 3A, c,g,k,o). Dictyosomes were accompanied by numerous and large secretory vesicles (SV) that often merged with the cell membrane. Multivesicular bodies (MVB) were also observed in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3A, o,s, A). In cells of the Fusarium variant, a distinguishing feature was the significant thinning of the mitochondrial matrix in the central part of the mitochondria. Sometimes, electron-dense areas with projections, which were additionally surrounded by membranes, were located in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3A, s). Moreover, only single local thickenings appeared within the cell walls.

In turn, in cells from the AgNPs + Fusarium variant, most of the cell cross-section was occupied by a centrally located large vacuole (Fig. 3A, d, 4th column). Inside the vacuoles there were often dark gray electron-dense areas and lenticular or spherical shapes, as well as membrane structures (Fig. 3A, d,h,l). As in the Fusarium variant, the mitochondria were characterized by a thin matrix (Figs. 3A,h and 3B, k,m,n). Extracellular vesicles were sometimes located at the junction of the cell wall. Additionally, electron-dense granules of small size were also located in some of vesicles occurring in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3B, j). Besides, there were dictyosomes with large dictyosomal vesicles, which may indicate enhanced transport processes. (Fig. 3B, n). Also, numerous sections of endoplasmic reticulum were observed (Fig. 3B, o). Moreover, some intercellular spaces were filled with a gray, dense substance containing spherical granules with a higher electron density (Fig. 3A, p).

The effect of AgNPs and F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini on generation of hydrogen peroxide in the roots of germinating seeds and seedlings of lupine (Lupinus luteus L.cv. Diament and Lupinus luteus L.cv. Mister) yellow lupine

At 48 h after inoculation, considerable differences in H2O2 level were detected in epidermal cells of the roots of yellow lupine (L. luteus L.cv. Diament and L. luteus L.cv. Mister), non-exposed or exposed to AgNPs, and non-infected or infected with hemibiotrophic pathogen F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini (Fig. 4). In all experimental variants of yellow lupine cells of the Diament variety, green fluorescence emission indicating H2O2 generation was observed, while in the control this signal was the weakest. It was shown that at 48 h of culture, AgNPs induced increased fluorescence emission in comparison to the control. Fluorescence in AgNPs variant occurred mainly in the cell walls or cell nuclei of root cells of the Diament variety. In turn, in variants such as Fusarium and AgNPs + Fusarium this signal was the strongest and covered not only the cell wall region but also the entire cytoplasm. Similarly to the Diament cells, the Mister variety showed increased in fluorescence emission in the experimental variants tested. However, in the AgNPs and Fusarium variants of Mister variety, fluorescence emission was lower than in the above variants of the Diament variety.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Yellow lupine (Lupinus luteus L.cv. Diament and Lupinus luteus L.cv. Mister) seeds of the S-elite class were used in the experiments, these were obtained from the Plant Breeding Company in Tulce near Poznań in Poland. The Diament and Mister varieties are resistant to fusariosis according to the data provided by the Plant Breeding Company in Tulce, Poland. The Diament variety is characterized by very high yield potential, sweetest variety because lowest alkaloid content and the highest resistance to Fusarium wilt of lupines. The seeds of this variety of lupine are characterized by a very high nutritional value, high protein content and low alkaloid content. Moreover, it should be mentioned that Diament is the sweetest variety on the market. In turn, special features of yellow lupine Mister are the highest seed crude fat content on the market, the most uniformly maturing variety, good lodging resistance after flowering, a drought-tolerant variety, which provides secure cultivation and good nutritional parameters, i.e. protein content and low proportion of alkaloids. Surface-sterilization of seeds was performed by38. Yellow lupine seeds were surface-sterilized by 2 min in 96% (v/v) ethanol, and were finally washed six times with sterile water, and next immersed in sterile water and left in an incubator (23 °C) for 6 h (Fig. 5). After 6 h of imbibition, the seeds were transferred onto Petri dishes and immersed in a small amount of water to support further absorption. After a subsequent 72 h of seed germination in darkness, the germinating seeds were non-treated or pre-treated with an aqueous solution of AgNPs (Sigma Aldrich, product no 798738, concentration 30wt% dispersion in ethylene glycol, 1 ml AgNPs 30wt% dispersion in ethylene glycol·L−1water) during 7 h13. Besides, the properties of AgNPs were as follows: particle size 115 nm (d90 (by Brookhaven)), 70 nm (d50 (by Brookhaven)), average particle size 180 nm, surface tension 45 dyn·cm−1, viscosity 28 cP(25 °C, Brookfield) (Con Spindle 42), resistivity ≤ 20 μΩ-cm (after sintering at 200 °C, 2 h), ≤ 45 μΩ-cm (after sintering at 120 °C, 2 h), ≤ 7 μΩ-cm (after sintering at 240 °C, 4 h) and quality level was 100. Next at the beginning of the study (0 h), the germinating seeds pretreated with AgNPs or non-treated, were inoculated with a F. oxysporum f. sp. lupini spore suspension or non-inoculated and were transferred to hydroponic grow boxes containing Hoagland medium. Hydroponic boxes were covered with dark foil to mimic soil conditions. Four culture variants were applied: embryo axes non-pretreated with AgNPs, not inoculated and cultured on Hoagland medium (control); embryo axes of germinating seeds pretreated with AgNPs, not inoculated and cultured on Hoagland medium (AgNPs); embryo axes pretreated with AgNPs, inoculated and cultured on Hoagland medium (AgNPs + Fusarium); embryo axes non-pretreated with AgNPs, inoculated with Fusarium and cultured on Hoagland medium (Fusarium). Yelow lupine seedlings were kept in a growth chamber at 23 °C, 65% relative humidity, and light intensity of 130–150 μmol photons·m−2·s−1 with the 12/12 h (light/dark) photoperiod. Hydroponic cultures were aerated with an aeration system. Hoagland medium composition: macro elements, i.e. 1 mM KH2PO4, 2 mM MgSO4, 5 mM KNO3, 5 mM Ca(NO3)2, micro elements, i.e. 0.02 mM (NH4)6Mo7(O2)4, 0.03 mM CuSO4, 0.08 mM ZnSO4, 0.08 mM MnSO4, 0.08 mM FeSO4- Na2-EDTA (Na2C10H16N2O8) (1.34 g in 500 ml H2O). Samples were collected for ultrastructural analysis in transmission electron microscopy and confocal microscopy at 48 h of culture, i.e. 48 h after inoculation.Fig. 5Experimental design.

Experimental design.

Preparation of spore suspension and inoculation

Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lupini strain K-1018 (subsequently referred to as F. oxysporum) was obtained from the Collection of Plant Pathogenic Fungi, Laboratory of the Plant Diseases Clinic and Pathogen Bank, Institute of Plant Protection—National Research Institute in Poznań. The pathogen was incubated in the dark at 23 °C in Petri dishes (diameter 9 cm) on a potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium (pH 5.5, Sigma-Aldrich, Poznań, Poland). After three weeks of growth an F. oxysporum spore suspension was prepared. The spore suspension was obtained by washing the mycelium with sterile water and shaking with glass pearls. Then the number of spores was determined using a hemocytometer chamber (Bürker, Labart, Gdańsk, Poland). Embryo axes of germinating seeds were inoculated with the spore suspension at a concentration of 5 × 106 spores per 1 mL. Inoculation was performed by injecting 10 µL of spore suspension into the upper part of the embryo axis of germinating seeds and next additionally also by spraying the embryo axes of germinating with the inoculum.

Detection of hydrogen peroxide generation

Generation of H2O2 in yellow lupine embryo axes of germinating seeds and roots of seedlings was detected by staining with a specific fluorescence agent, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Sigma-Aldrich, Poznań, Poland), following the methods of39 modified by40 with minor modification. As reported41, in the presence of peroxidase enzymes, such as horseradish peroxidase, cytochrome c, or myeloperox- idase, or transition metals like Fe(II) or Cu(II), H2O2 serves as a substrate to generate highly reactive intermediates or hydroxyl radicals through Fenton-type chemistry. These intermediates are capable of oxidizing DCFH2. DCFH-DA is non-fluorescent and can passively cross the membrane of live cells and it may be oxidized by H2O2 to a fluorescent dye detected by a laser confocal microscope. The fluorescence is proportional to the intracellular H2O2 level. The fresh roots were submerged in 4 µM DCFH-DA dissolved for 12 h in 3 mM dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4. Incubation was carried out in dark conditions. The roots were washed twice with the loading buffer at least 30 min each time and then were observed under a confocal microscope (Zeissa LSM 880, Jena, Germany), excitation 488 nm, emission from 500 to 550 nm. Images were analyzed using the ZEN software, version 3.2.

Preparation of tissues for observations in transmission electron microscopy

At 48 h, sections of approx. 5 mm were cut off in the half-length of the embryo axis of pre-treated with AgNPs or non-treated and inoculated with a F. oxysporum f. sp. lupini spore suspension or non-inoculated. For ultrastructural analysis in transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sections of roots were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.2 at room temperature for 8 h. The root samples in the fixative were initially subjected to a vacuum for 1 h to remove air from the roots. The samples were rinsed several times in 0.05 M phosphate buffer and post-fixed 1% osmium tetraoxide in the same buffer for 1 h at room temperature, then they were washed in water42. Following dehydration in a graded acetone solution series, the materials were embedded in Spurr’s resin. Duration of dehydration steps was 10 min and resin polymerization conditions at 60 °C by 3 day. Ultrathin sections (thickness 70 nm) were examined with the Hitachi HT7700 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at 100 kV.

Statistical analysis

All electronograms from electron transmission microscopy and images from confocal microscopy were conducted within three independent experiments. Moreover, sample size for quantitative comparisons was 20–50 photos were taken for the analysis of each experimental variant. In order to recognize and compare images presenting ultrastructural changes for each experimental variant, Euclidean-distance-based measures43 using classification functions were used44.

Discussion

The results of this study for the first time show that the application of AgNPs at hormetic dose during the seed germination phase, caused significant changes in the ultrastructure of root cap cells, both in the case of L. luteus L. cv. Diament as well as L. luteus L. cv. Mister. It is also noteworthy that AgNPs caused local and clearly visible thickenings of the cell wall (CW) with a wide electron-dense apoplast space (AS) in the root cap cells (Figs. 2 A,f and 2B, a-d, Figs. 3A,b,f,n,r and 3B, a,b). Additionally, dark electron-dense granules in the above apoplast space.

paid attention, which may indicate transport and the accumulation of AgNPs. We also selectively checked the occurrence of dark electron-dense granules in the cells of the root apical meristemas shown in the electronogram Fig. 3C. It should be added that local and clearly visible thickenings of CW with a wide electron-dense apoplast space were not only visible in root cap cells pretreated with AgNPs (AgNPs variant), but also in root cap cells exposed to AgNPs and next infected with fungal pathogen F. oxysporum (AgNPs + Fusarium variant), which may indicate defense-related CW remodeling (Figs. 2A,l, 2B,e, 3A,t,x and 3B,l). This result is a premise to conclude that the application of AgNPs stimulated the changes in CW, probably inducing its remodeling and an increase in the apoplast space in root cap cells of yellow lupine, both in AgNPs and AgNPs + Fusarium variants. As reported by45, for a plant cell to grow or change its shape, the cell wall has to stretch or deform. Besides, our results of the research from TEM showed also the occurrence of AgNPs, both in the apoplastic space as well as near the cell wall in root cap cells in AgNPs and AgNPs + Fusarium variants, both in Diament and Mister varieties. Moreover, in cytoplasm of root cap cells of the Diament variety, were also clearly visible electron-dense aggregated clumps with a spherical shape, indicating AgNPs (Fig. 2 B, a,b). The above electron-dense aggregate clumps were similar in appearance to those exogenous AgNPs observed in Fig. 1.

On the other hand, localisation of AgNPs in root cap cells of Arabidopsis thaliana was demonstrated by18. It was shown that AgNPs occurred all over cell wall or in middle lamella, the authors reported. It should be emphasized that as with our research in TEM, both individual AgNPs and aggregated clumps were also observed by18. Besides, AgNPs occurred also in systematic clumps at the plasmodesmata. An interesting conclusion of the above authors was that the aggregates of AgNPs at plasmodesmata seem to pose physical blockage of symplastic transport between cells and signaling molecules such as phytohormones. It is likely that the aggregates of AgNPs may cause delays or block intercellular transport and signal transduction to the cell nucleus. Additionally20, proved that plants have the ability to absorb, translocate, and accumulate AgNPs from their surroundings. It has been shown that AgNPs are absorbed by plant roots more quickly than dissolve in the soil, suggesting a quick absorption mechanism46,47. As reported by48, plants can internalize silver through two different processes, i.e. either the roots dissolve AgNPs on the root surface, which allows the roots to internalize ionic species, or the roots directly absorb AgNPs and then release oxidized silver species within the root tissues. In addition, the absorption and transport of AgNPs in plants are significantly influenced by their size. As reported49, material’s ultrastructure can be examined at the nanoscale using both TEM as well as scanning electron microscopy (SEM), but TEM allow for precise information on granularity and crystal structure at the atomic level50. Also51, showed representative TEM images of AgNPs, which confirmed the spherical shape of those NPs with an average size of 15–25 nm. It should be mentioned that the stability of AgNPs in different biological media represents a key parameter to maintain their activity. Previous evidences have shown that AgNPs could aggregate and/or agglomerate once they come in contact with biological media52–54. The mechanism of NPs uptake is generally considered as an active-transport mechanism that may include cellular processes such as signaling, recycling, and the regulation of plasma membrane55. Once nanoparticles penetrate the plant roots, it moves in plant tissues by the apoplast and symplastic ways and translocates via cytoplasm to the photosynthetic system56.

However, it should be emphasized that the primary aim of the present study was to show changes in the ultrastructure of root cap cells of yellow lupine resulting from the influence of AgNPs at hormetic dose and the interaction of AgNPs and pathogenic fungus F. oxysporum. Considering the above, we conclude that cell wall remodeling and the increase in apoplast space of root cap cells induced by AgNPs is one of the defensive mechanisms. Recent reports indicate that the apoplast is also a key barrier conferring plant immunity against microbes57. Besides, plant cell wall may function as a barrier, which limits the amount of AgNPs entering the protoplast of the cell. Numerous studies have shown that cell wall remodeling is a widespread plant defense strategy to cope with heavy metals, e.g. lead58,59. It is believed that CW remodeling under heavy metal stress, must be associated with vesicular transport of the CW compounds and plant cell growth60.

Therefore, we can assume in our the research that CW remodeling under AgNPs, may be associated with vesicular transport of the CW compounds and plant cell growth. Our previous research performed on the same experimental model demonstrated that AgNPs at hormetic dose induced the phenomenon of hormesis in the case of yellow lupine embryo axes and seedling roots13.The application of AgNPs significantly enhanced the growth of yellow lupine seedlings, causing increase in root length and fresh biomass in relation to the control. As reported by45, for a plant cell to grow or change its shape, the cell wall has to stretch or deform. It is also worth mentioning that we have previously shown that the application of AgNPs caused significantly increase in phytohormones promoting growth such as indole acetic acid (IAA) and gibberellic acid (GA), in AgNP-pretreated embryo axes of Diament and Mister varieties13. The application of NPs may protect the membranes, enhance water uptake, and maintain water relationship61, but it depends on their dose.

To our knowledge this is the first report which shows not only the formation of local thickenings of the cell wall in AgNP-pretreated the root cap cells, but also often clearly visible invaginations of the cell membrane (MI) occurring on the border of the protoplast and the walls, in which electron-dense granules were located (Fig. 2A, f). As reported by62, plant cells rely on their cell walls for directed growth and environmental adaptation. Besides, synthesis and remodelling of the CWs are membrane-related processes. During cell growth and exposure to external stimuli, there is a constant exchange of lipids, proteins, and other cell wall components between the cytosol and the plasma membrane/apoplast. There is evidence that sensors at the plasma membrane detect changes in the CW architecture, and activate cytoplasmic signalling and ultimately CW remodelling63,64.

An interesting result of our research was the increase also in the presence of dictyosomes that characterized by a highly developed cisternae system, and an increase in number of the vesicles of dictyosomal origin (Figs. 2A,j, 2B,d, 3A,f,j and 3B,b,h). This result may indicate that AgNPs induced transport processes in the root cap cells. Therefore, an increase in the processes of endocytosis and exocytosis was noticed, which is associated with the transport of material enclosed or inserted into membrane vesicles. Moreover, in AgNP-pretreated root cap cells of both varieties, in the cytoplasmic area, next to an increased presence of the dictyosomes with dictyosomal vesicles were noticeable rough endoplasmic reticulum (RERs) (Fig. 3A, h). It is known that numerous RERs are associated with many roles in protein synthesis, which also include post-translational modifications, folding, and sorting. Besides, ER is responsible also for often storage of a large group of proteins and lipids that enter the secretory pathway and one of the calcium storage compartments in plant cell.

Communication between the components of plant endomembrane system, including the ER membrane forming a continuum with the outer nuclear membrane, the membrane of dictyosomal cisternae, or the membrane of any endosome and the tonoplast, is ensured by the exchange of signals and vesicular and non-vesicular transport65,66. The morphologies, functions, and compositions of these organelles are versatile in different cell types, development stages, and environmental stimuli67. Besides, the above organelles are dynamic and exchangeable to facilitate an acute response for plant adaptation and survival.

Moreover, the studies performed by68 indicated several changes in plant cell organelles. The applications of NPs caused a disruption in the cell wall, cell membrane, chloroplast structure, thylakoids, abnormal size of plastoglobules and starch granules, destructive changes in peroxisomes, swollen mitochondrial cristae, abnormal nucleus, loose, rough and thin mesophyll cells, epidermal, cortical, and stellar cells. Besides, deposition of electronically dense materials near cell walls was also observed.

Our research proves that enhanced transport processes related to endocytosis and exocytosis supporting the transport of materials into and out of cells (Fig. 2B, e), were also noted in root cap cells of AgNPs + Fusarium variant. Similarly to the AgNPs, also in the AgNPs + Fusarium variant were noticeable numerous dictyosomes with large dictyosomal vesicles and numerous longer cisternae of RERs (Figs. 2A, l, 2B, e and Fig. 3B, j,n,o). In turn, the observed possibly strong protrusion or invagination of plasma membrane in root cap cells of AgNPs + Fusarium variant, may lead to an uptake of the cytoplasmic components, while invagination of the endosomal membrane producing multivesicular bodies (MVBs) is also known to transport cytoplasmic components into the organelle lumen. This is confirmed by the report presented by69, regarding monitoring of autophagy in human and mammalian organisms. As in animal and yeast cells, endosomes in plants traffic both biosynthetic and endocytic cargo70. It should be emphasized that endocytosis and vesicle recycling via secretory endosomes are essential for many processes in multicellular organisms71. The increased transport processes observed in AgNPs + Fusarium variant may also be related to the defense reactions induced by the host plant cells against F. oxysporum. A surprising observation was the occurrence of border structures (BS) in the cells of AgNPs + Fusarium variant (Fig. 3B, n). These structures, primarily located outside the cytoplasm, may provide mechanical strength and act as a first line of defense against fungal pathogen.

Regardless of the above, analyses performed in SEM and TEM revealed severe damage to the cell wall and membrane, as well as degradation of intracellular organelles, indicating that AgNPs disrupt both the surface as well as internal structure of F. graminearum (the wild-type PH-1 strain, NRRL 31,084)16. Observations of the above authors in TEM showed that AgNPs penetrate the fungal cell wall and membrane, leading to disruption of internal organelles such as mitochondria. Moreover, the cytoplasm appeared less dense or fragmented, and vacuoles can form within the cells. It has been proven that AgNPs have excellent antifungal activities against fungal pathogens such as Aspergillus, Candida, Fusarium, Phoma, Magnaporthe oryzae, and Trichoderma72–74.

In our study, the application of AgNPs enhanced also plant metabolism in embryo axes of germinating seeds, which showed observation of the relative generation of ROS, especially H2O2 in confocal microscopy. Postinfection generation of oxidative stress in yellow lupine embryo axes both varieties may limit the development of infection caused by the hemibiotrophic fungus F. oxysporum. Fluorescence signal increased in the nuclei of cells of the roots of AgNP variant, which may suggest that application of AgNPs enhanced ROS, including H2O2 (Fig. 4). The presence of ROS, including H2O2 in cell nuclei, is a premise to conclude that H2O2 may influence changes in the expression of defense genes and increase the metabolism of root cells against the biotic factor F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini. It was also found that the strong postinfection generation of oxidative stress in yellow lupine embryo axes both varieties may be related to the induction of the autophagy process in embryo axes inoculated with F. oxysporum, both in Fusarium and AgNPs + Fusarium variants. Autophagy is a pathway for sequestering and transporting bulk cytoplasmic constituents, including proteins and damaged organelle material, to the vacuole for degradation75. It should be emphasized that the observed autophagy bodies (AB) in cap cells of Fusarium variant (Fig. 3A, k) may indicate the involvement of autophagy in plant defense against fungal pathogen. Also, the MLBs formation is linked to the autophagy pathway and the response to biotic stress factor. A critical role of autophagy in plant resistance to necrotrophic fungal pathogens was reported by76. Moreover, H2O2 is a key molecule not only because it has a direct antimicrobial activity but also it is a key factor mediating cell wall strengthening. This result suggests that H2O2 generation in infected embryo axes may be one of the strategies of their defence against invasion by a necrotrophic pathogen77. However, it cannot be excluded that the fungus F. oxysporum generates some H2O2 too78. Our research results showed that in cells of the Fusarium variant, AB in the form of various types of membrane structures were observed inside the vacuoles (Fig. 3A, c,k). Also, as a result of the infection itself, an increased presence of the dictyosomes, rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), multivesicular bodies (MVBs) was observed (Fig. 3A, c,g,k,o,s,A). The MVBs are essential endosomal organelles mediating protein trafficking to the vacuole, endocytosis and pathogen defense. It is believed that MVBs are crucial in plant defense against pathogens. Additionally, our observation showed sometimes occurrence of electron-dense areas with projections, which were additionally surrounded by membranes in the cytoplasm (Fig. 3A, s). However, it should be emphasized that strongest pathogen impact was noticeable in Fusarium variant both varieties, especially in Mister variety. In contrast to the Diament variety of yellow lupine, in the Mister variety, the tannins (i.e. dark gray electron-dense areas and lenticular or spherical shapes) were observed in the vacuoles.. Moreover, in the root cap cells of the Mister variety, spores of F. oxysporum f.sp. lupini were also visible, which were not observed in the cells of the Diament variety (Fig. 3A, s). Additionally, the significant thinning of the mitochondrial matrix found in the central part of the mitochondria of the above variety may indicate mitochondrial damage and dysfunction. Mitochondria were sometimes also surrounded by the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 2A, k), which also indicated the process of macroautophagy (mitophagy). This surprising observation was recorded in the root cap cells of the Diament variety (Fig. 2A, k), which was not noted in Mister variety.

It should be emphasized that the results of our ultrastructural studies are important, especially for future application studies on the use of AgNPs in agriculture. We demonstrated that AgNPs, at a hormetic dose, can penetrate root cap cells and also the root merstem. NPs were applied as potential tools to improve the growth of plants, plant tolerance to abiotic stresses and food production, but research on the environmental safety of their use in agriculture is still necessary6,13. However, despite the beneficial effects of AgNPs79, their use requires careful evaluation and responsible management in the environment80,81.

Conclusion

The application of AgNPs at hormetic dose during the seed germination stage caused local and clearly visible thickenings of CW with a wide electron-dense AS, invaginations of the cell membrane (MI), enhance various types of membrane structures, dictyosomes (Ds) and endoplasmic reticulum cisternae in the root cap cells. In turn, hemibiotrofic patogen Fusarium oxysporum f.sp. lupini induced autophagy, caused mitochondrial deformation and CW corrugation. Both the occurrence of AB in vacuole, MVBs and MLBs in the cells showed an association with autophagy. In case of cross-talk of AgNPs and Fusarium oxysporum f.sp. lupini interaction were observed the strongest CW modification, strong protrusion or invagination of plasma membrane, enhance dictyosomes with large dictyosomal vesicles, long cisternae of RERs, and the occurrence of tannins inside vacuoles. Moreover, the application of AgNPs modulated root defense by enhancing oxidative signaling, which may consequently influence pathogen interaction.
