Authors’ response
Authors: Nandini Vallath, Parvathy V., Pratyasa Padhi, Angelin Johny T., Mathangi Krishnakumar, Varghese P.S.
Published: 2026

We thank Dr Havaldar1 for the feedback on our article on implementing legal directives on withholding and withdrawal of life-support (WH-WD-LST) at the institutional level.

Several aspects contribute to the delay in implementing the due processes. Lack of legal clarity amongst administrators and clinicians lead to avoidance of decision making for fear of litigation. A deeper constraint is the conventional organ-centred approach which makes it difficult to discern ‘non-beneficence’ of the ‘effect’ of an intervention, on the overall outcome of a terminally ill patient. The moral and ethical distress caused by lack of training in person-centric prognostication skills, can overwhelm clinicians and administrators when faced with WH-WD-LST situations. Failure to recognise the non-verbal cues, break bad news, and resolve conflicts compound this concern. The short-term solution may be workshops clarifying the legal mandates with case-based discussion integrating the skills to prognosticate and communicate.

The financial burden during the waiting period of executing the processes is especially relevant for our country, where majority of population are uninsured. The ‘preferably within 48 h’ legal clause reduces the out-of-pocket expenses for futile ICU-interventions, preventing catastrophic financial toxicity, impacting food, shelter, and education for the whole family, including children.2,3 By streamlining the WH-WD-LST processes, our institution has been able to complete the due processes within few hours.4,5 Would the modified requirement of approaching the district medical officer for institutional SMB on a ‘case-to-case basis6, impact the State-level procedures? Legal explanation of this is the interpretation of 2023 judgement, by the two-judge bench of 2026 (Harish Rana). The intent is restricting the choice and discretion of hospitals when selecting the Secondary Medical Board (SMB)-expert. The Karnataka state-order which restricts the choice of SMB-expert to the State-nominated panel of experts and spirit, can be considered to be fulfilling the intent of these judgements.5 Alternate legal interpretations are possible.

The legal guideline does not mandate that the clinicians for the medical boards must be from the same institution.4 Hence, rural hospitals or private clinics can avail eligible clinicians through collaborations.

Discharge against medical advice (DAMA) underscores the urgency of implementing well-defined procedures for timely transitions to proportionate, person-centred care-continuum, based on the principles and scope of palliative care to prevent non-beneficial medical care, and to alleviate needless serious health-related suffering including financial wipe-out.6 The 2026 judgement condemns DAMA and mandates access to palliative care services for those needing WH-WD-LST.6

The needless suffering of the terminally ill person admitted into the ICU need to be resolved. We as healthcare professionals are currently dependant on judgements on specific cases, and their interpretations, to provide ethical, lawful, and appropriate care. A clear legislation for the terminally ill, and unambiguous state-level processes clarifying institutional implementation are urgent needs to activate timely, appropriate, and proportionate care, in the ‘best interest’ of the person under consideration. Hopefully this will take place soon.
