Do divorce and widowhood increase mortality risk? A 35-year longitudinal cohort study in a Norwegian population: the HUNT study
Authors: Eivind Meland, Karl Ove Hufthammer, Thomas Mildestvedt, Vegar Rangul, Tina Løkke Vie, Øystein Hetlevik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveSocial relationships are known to influence health outcomes. Getting divorced or widowed may lead to social disconnection, which has been associated with increased mortality.Whether these events independently influence mortality risk—beyond health behaviours, self-rated health and loneliness—remains insufficiently understood. We examined whether divorce or widowhood is associated with increased mortality after accounting for these factors.MethodsWe used data from the Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT), a population-based cohort spanning four waves (1984–2019). Three sub-populations were created by identifying participants who reported being married or cohabiting at baseline (T1) in HUNT1, HUNT2 and HUNT3 and who also participated in the subsequent wave (T2: HUNT2, HUNT3, HUNT4), yielding N=28 912, N=22 780 and N=19 864 participants, respectively. At T2, cohabitation status was reclassified as married/cohabiting, divorced/cohabitation breakup or widowed. Mortality data were obtained from the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry through January 2020. Mortality risks were estimated using proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, health behaviours, self-rated health and loneliness.ResultsDivorce/cohabitation breakup was consistently associated with higher mortality in the sub-populations from all three periods, with fully adjusted HRs ranging from 1.21 to 1.91. Widowhood was associated with higher mortality only in the first observation period (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.21). Interaction analyses showed that for the second period, the mortality effect of breakup was higher and statistically significant only in women, while no other sex interactions were statistically significant.ConclusionsDivorce/breakup and widowhood are associated with increased mortality risk, most consistent for the divorced and independent of other health-related factors. The findings underscore the importance of addressing social disconnection in public health strategies. Interventions aimed at strengthening social bonds may mitigate the adverse health effects of relational loss.Keywords: Epidemiologic Factors, Risk Assessment, Public Health

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Previous research has shown that social disconnection—including getting divorced and widowed—is associated with increased mortality, and that men may be at greater risk than women. Whether or not changes in relationship status independently affect mortality risk is not clear; nor do we fully understand the part played by factors such as loneliness and health behaviours.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that both divorce/breakup and widowhood are associated with increased mortality risk, even after adjusting for confounding factors such as health behaviours, self-rated health and loneliness. Divorce/breakups consistently showed a stronger association with mortality than widowhood. The male vulnerability hypothesis was not confirmed.

Divorce/breakups consistently showed a stronger association with mortality than widowhood. The male vulnerability hypothesis was not confirmed.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings highlight the need for public health strategies that address social disconnection as a determinant of health. Interventions aimed at strengthening social bonds and supporting individuals through relational loss may reduce mortality risk and improve population health outcomes.

Introduction

It is important that we understand the health-promoting effects of social connections. They influence behavioural, biological and psychological factors that prevent later morbidity and mortality. Social baseline theory—the notion that we are interdependent with others, especially with those closest to us—represents a normative lens that may explain how and why our health depends on the human community.1

A causal relationship between social disconnection and ill health, using Austin Bradford Hill’s list,2 was established in a review paper by Holt-Lunstad.3 Another meta-analytic review paper on divorce and death, covering 6.5 million participants, confirmed a consistent increased mortality, especially among young men.4 Research indicates that the increased mortality risk is mitigated by functional coping, with maintaining social relationships playing a prominent role.5

In line with this, the incremental effect of widowhood and bereavement on all-cause mortality among older adults is mitigated by close relationships.6 Other research shows that factors such as social selection, social network outside family, resource disruptions, changes in health behaviours, satisfaction with life and chronic psychological distress have important mediating and moderating effects on the prognosis after divorce.5 7 8

The subjective experience of loneliness may represent a common denominator linking chronic distress and dissatisfaction with poorer general, physical and mental health,9 10 increased risks of all-cause, cardiovascular and cancer mortality,11 12 hypertension,13 cardiovascular disease,14 cognitive impairment,15 depression and anxiety,16 sleep problems and many other concerns.9 Life dissatisfaction linked to loneliness may be an important intermediate factor explaining excessive mortality in the wake of marital disruption, especially in older age.17

In contrast, fulfilling the need for belonging mitigates these adverse health effects.12 18 The need for belonging is supported by three distinct qualities of social support: structural aspects (eg, marriage and cohabitation); functional aspects (eg, perceived availability of social support); and perceived quality of relationships.3 19 These factors impact health behaviour, for example, smoking, physical inactivity and excessive drinking.17 20 21 Health behaviour and the experience of loneliness seem, therefore, important confounding factors in the study of how social disconnection impacts health and mortality. Self-rated health impacts later health already from adolescence,22 and seems a relevant confounder in this study as it also impacts later mortality.23

During the last decades, we have gained deeper insight into how social disconnection impacts biological processes linked to the cardiovascular system.24 25 Biomarkers linked to the neuro-endocrine, immune, epigenetic and inflammatory systems are impacted,26,28 as are psychological factors.5 7 Depression after divorce seems first and foremost to be limited to those who experienced major depressive illness before their marital breakup.29 Therefore, social selection (social disconnection due to personal and behavioural characteristics in individuals that also impact mortality risk) may play an equally important role as social causation (impaired health and increased mortality risk due to social disconnection in itself).

The diathesis–stress model illustrates how individual vulnerability combines with acute and chronic distress in the causation of impaired health and increased vulnerability.29 Combined with attachment theory, we may be able to understand the individual trajectories that lead to divorce and to impaired prognosis following divorce for some of those affected. Attachment anxiety and social avoidance seem to be prominent factors explaining impaired prognosis in the wake of divorce.8 30

Gender differences with male disadvantage have been reported concerning adaptation and worse health outcomes, especially after divorce,31 but less so after the death of a spouse.32 We refer to this as the ‘male vulnerability hypothesis’. More recent studies indicate, however, that male vulnerability is short-term and transient, while the female strain of divorce is chronic.33 Norwegian epidemiological studies do not confirm obvious gender mortality differences in the widowed population in one study,32 whereas another study demonstrated increasing mortality risk in women with time since widowhood.34 Men suffer a higher mortality risk following divorce than women, and experience, unlike women, an increasing mortality with time since divorce.34

The yearly number of divorces in Norway and in Western countries has been close to 50% of the number of marriages in the last 10 years.35 Even modestly increased health risks associated with divorce may therefore translate into substantial population-attributable risks, adding to its public health significance.

We are still struggling to understand how much of the impaired prognosis following divorce is explained by social selection on one hand and social causation on the other. We should also bear in mind that divorce and widowhood for some may entail improvement of social life. Former Norwegian studies have adjusted for re-established marriage or censored for divorce when the widowed were compared with a stable married population. These studies still lack adjustment for important confounders.32 34 In the present study, we were unable to adjust for re-establishment of intimate relationships. However, we adjusted for important health, lifestyle and psychosocial confounders.

Our research objectives were:

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational, longitudinal cohort study is based on survey data from the Trøndelag Health Study (HUNT) linked to national registries. HUNT is a large, population-based cohort study conducted in the former Nord-Trøndelag County, Norway. It comprises four major survey waves: HUNT1 (1984–1986), HUNT2 (1995–1997), HUNT3 (2006–2008) and HUNT4 (2017–2019).36 37 In each wave, all residents aged 20 years and older were invited to participate using their unique 11-digit Norwegian personal identification number, which enables linkage to national registries, including Statistics Norway and the Cause of Death Registry. For this study, linkage included the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry and selected data from Statistics Norway, such as marital status (eg, widowhood).

Participation rates were 89.4% (n=77 212) in HUNT1; 69.5% (n=65 237) in HUNT2; 54.5% (n=50 807) in HUNT3 and 54.0% (n=56 042) in HUNT4, yielding more than 250 000 sets of data from participants across all waves.

The population of Nord-Trøndelag remained relatively stable during the study period, with low migration rates and a population size ranging from ∼ 125 800 in 1981 to ∼ 137 200 in 2017. Participants were broadly representative of the regional population, with slightly higher participation among women and the highest response rates observed in the 40–59 year age group. The age range in the present study was 19–104 years.

We have been able to follow a population in the former county of Nord-Trøndelag across four survey waves (HUNT1–HUNT4) and over a time span of 35 years. For the current analysis, we defined three sub-populations, each originating from one of the first three survey waves (T1: HUNT1, HUNT2 or HUNT3), and we followed them longitudinally to the subsequent wave (T2: HUNT2, HUNT3 or HUNT4, respectively). Participants who reported cohabiting with a spouse or partner at T1 but stated that they lived alone at T2 (approximately 10 years later) were compared in terms of mortality with participants who remained cohabiting. In the Norwegian context, some couples are married while others are cohabiting, but cohabitation is not systematically recorded in official registers. In this article, we therefore defined ‘breakup’ as either a legal divorce or the termination of a cohabiting relationship, the latter based on self-reported information rather than registry data.

Outcome variable

Mortality was our outcome variable, based on a linkage to the Norwegian Cause of Death Registry up to January 2020. These mortality data are based on death certificates reported by doctors in accordance with the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). Both the degree of coverage and completeness were high, with medical information available for >98% of all deaths.38

Main explanatory variable: cohabitant status

We identified all individuals who self-reported cohabiting with a spouse or partner in HUNT1, 2 and 3, constituting three distinct study populations (individuals could be part of multiple populations). For each of these populations, we registered the cohabitant status in the following waves, HUNT 2, 3 and 4, respectively. For the individuals who did not report that they were cohabiting at follow-up, we used marital status data from Statistics Norway to categorise them as ‘divorce/breakup’ or ‘widowed’. This was done to elucidate differences in mortality between divorced and widowed participants.

Adjustment and confounding variables

We adjusted for available factors registered at T1 that we believed (a priori) would be related to increased mortality and possibly also to divorce/breakup: health behaviour (alcohol consumption, physical activity and smoking), subjective health (self-rated health) and self-perceived loneliness. We also analysed whether these factors were associated with divorce/breakup between T1 and T2, fulfilling the criteria for being confounders. This allowed us to examine the effect of divorce/breakup on mortality across three different time periods (HUNT1–HUNT2, HUNT2–HUNT3 and HUNT3–HUNT4), and also to adjust for factors associated (presumed causally) with divorce/breakup.

We did not undertake corresponding analyses of predictors of becoming widowed, as this was not central to the study aim and was considered of limited interpretive value, given that widowhood largely depends on partner mortality rather than processes analogous to separation or breakup. In the final regression models with mortality as the outcome, both widowhood and divorce/breakup were used as explanatory variables; we applied the same covariate set to ensure consistent confounder control and comparability of effect estimates across different forms of partnership dissolution.

The following variables were all based on data from the HUNT surveys. The wording of the questions and response options differed somewhat between the various waves. In some analyses, the variables have been harmonised by collapsing some of the response options.

Age: age in years at T1.

Sex: male or female.

Physical activity measures: leisure-time physical activity (PA) for HUNT1 and HUNT3 was measured using a validated questionnaire,39 where participants reported average weekly frequency, intensity and duration of exercise. In HUNT2, the PA questions were different and asked about light and hard PA during an ordinary week (differentiating between light and hard PA). Based on the PA questions, it was possible to construct a combined index and categorise each individual as either ‘active’ (using a low threshold, corresponding to ≥60 min of PA per week) or ‘inactive’ (<60 min PA per week). Details on the calculations are given in online supplemental material.

Chronic disease: long-term physical or mental illness or injury, coded as ‘yes’ versus ‘no’ (self-reported).

Feeling lonely: ordinal variable (self-reported) with different response options in the different HUNT waves and used accordingly, as shown in the tables. We report loneliness summarised as ‘lonely’ versus ‘not lonely’ by collapsing the levels ‘often’, ‘very often’, ‘a good amount’ and ‘very much’ to ‘lonely’.

Self-reported health: a single item asking participants to rate their overall health, with four response options: ‘poor’, ‘not so good’, ‘good’ and ‘very good’.

Smoking: participants were asked about their current and past smoking habits. We portrayed this as an ordinal variable with different response options in the different HUNT waves (up to five response options). We report smoking summarised as ‘daily smoker’ versus ‘not daily smoker’.

Alcohol consumption: participants were asked to report how many times they had consumed alcohol during the past fourteen days (HUNT1, ordinal variable) or how many units of alcohol they had consumed in the previous week (HUNT 2–4, continuous variable). In some analyses, the original five-level ordinal variable (HUNT1) and the continuous variable categorised into 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6–10 and 10+ weekly units is used. We report alcohol use summarised as ‘high’ (5+ times in the past 14 days or 7+ units in the previous week) versus ‘low/moderate’.

Statistical analyses

We first examined whether the adjustment and confounding variables at T1 predicted divorce/breakup at T2. We fitted logistic regression models with divorce/breakup (vs ‘cohabitant’) at T2 as the outcome. A separate model was fitted for each adjustment variable adjusted for age and sex. To capture any non-linear effect of age, a natural cubic spline was used. The spline had four equally spaced knots, placed at the 0.05, 0.35, 0.65 and 0.95 quantiles.

Second, we fitted a proportional hazards model for each of the adjustment and confounding variables, adjusted for sex (age was also indirectly included, as the time scale is based on the subject’s age), to estimate the association with mortality and to assess if they were confounders for the association between breakup and mortality.

Finally, to examine the association between divorce/breakup/widowed and later mortality, we fitted several survival models. We report all-cause absolute risk (cumulative incidence) of death, stratified by cohabitant breakup (‘no breakup’, ‘breakup’, ‘widowed’). There were few deaths before the age of 50 years, and few subjects were widowed before this age, so we restricted the analyses to subjects who survived to at least 50 years of age. In all survival analyses, we took delayed entry (left truncation) for the subjects into account. Our main analyses were proportional hazards survival models for the association between breakup (before/at T2) and later mortality adjusted for potential confounding variables. The estimates are reported as HRs with CIs. The adjustment variables were taken from T1.

We performed adjusted interaction analyses to test whether vulnerability after widowhood or divorce/breakups differed between males and females.

All CIs reported are 95% CIs and p values ≤0.05 are characterised as statistically significant.

Missing data

The mortality data were based on official records of death and are presumed to include all deaths up to and including January 2020. There were some missing data in the explanatory variables, and we used complete-case analysis throughout. We report the number of missing observations for each predictor. We also report the number of observations and events (deaths) that the three main mortality analyses were based on.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of the research.

However, fewer deaths entail wide CIs, and the CIs overlapped the CIs for HUNT2 and HUNT1. The widowed in these later sub-populations no longer had a mortality statistically significantly different from the stable cohabiting group.

A test for interaction between sex and divorce/breakup or widowhood was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted analysis for either the HUNT1–HUNT2 cohort (p=0.27) or the HUNT3–HUNT4 cohort (p=0.72). However, for the HUNT2–HUNT3 cohort, men had statistically significantly lower HR than women for the effect of breakup on mortality (HR for interaction 0.57, CI 0.36 to 0.91, p=0.02). For men, the estimated HR was 0.97 (CI 0.70 to 1.36, p=0.88), and for women, it was 1.69 (CI 1.24 to 2.31, p<0.001). For the effect of widowhood, there was no statistically significant difference in the effect between men and women (HR for interaction 1.07, CI 0.82 to 1.38, p=0.62).

Most risk factors and behavioural factors were relatively stable in the different groups of cohabitating, divorce/breakup or widowed at T2 when compared with T1 (online supplemental table 5), but consistently reveal the conspicuous increase of reported loneliness after divorce/breakup or becoming widowed. Widowed people experienced increased mortality only after HUNT2, but the proportion reporting feeling lonely was higher than for people experiencing breakups. Those who experienced breakups, however, had a higher frequency of feeling lonely at T1 compared with the widowed, and this group also had consistently higher mortality rates.

Discussion

The present study revealed that relational breakups imply increased mortality risk, even when we controlled for many other factors linked both with breakup and with mortality. The ‘male vulnerability hypothesis’ was not confirmed. As expected, men had consistently higher mortality than women, but both sexes suffered comparable relative mortality risks attributed to divorce/breakups and widowhood, except in the HUNT3 cohort where men were advantaged. The 91% increased mortality risk after HUNT4, as compared with 21% and 27% increased risks after HUNT2 and HUNT3 could indicate that relational breakups had a more deleterious short-term than long-term risk, but CIs were overlapping.

Differences in mortality estimates across the three HUNT periods may reflect variation in observation time until death, which was January 2020 in all sub-populations. Earlier cohorts had more participants reporting cohabitation, reflecting a well-known trend of more people living as singles in the last few decades. Also, changes in the proportion of participants responding to two consecutive surveys may play a role, as shown by a slight decline in participation across waves. Together, these factors may partly explain the variation in findings across study periods.

Reduction of risk factors to prevent major causes of death has a dominating focus on objective biological measures for health. Recent guidelines in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease prevention advise lower thresholds for interventions and imply that an increasing portion of the population are advised to consider follow-up and interventions.40 The eagerness that the medical community invests in drug-based prevention is disproportionate compared with the efforts devoted to mitigating social disadvantages, including social disconnection and loneliness.

Despite four decades of reporting the relationship between social disconnection and health deterioration, we are still faced with indifference and neglect.41 42 From this point of departure, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine42 launched a policy paper in 2020 where they advised the medical community on how to strengthen social bonds both on the public health level and in clinical settings.42 Public health should include a focus on promoting intergenerational relations and family-friendly legislation, and on the clinical level, clinicians must be enabled to recognise socially isolated and lonely patients. Simple and effective methods for counselling can be easily employed, also in primary care settings.43 Self-compassion mitigates the emotional distress observed after divorce and breakups.44

Our findings in context

Our findings are consistent with findings from other epidemiological studies as revealed in reviews and meta-analytic studies.8 45 The increased mortality revealed in this study is moderate, although it entails a significant population-attributable risk and public health concern. Our method of identifying social disconnection was crude. According to some researchers, more comprehensive methods of identifying and rating the social disconnection continuum prove stronger associations with mortality and morbidity.41 An epidemiological study from Norway involving the entire population from 1970 to 2008 revealed higher odds of mortality for men than women, and an increased risk with years since divorce only among men.34

Although a substantial number of studies have proved the association between social disruption and ill health, the mechanisms that could explain this link are still unsatisfactorily understood.8 We revealed common factors that explain both divorce/breakup and mortality risk. In this research field, it seems difficult to discern social selection from social causation.4 In the present study we adjusted for factors that were both predictive of divorce/breakup and simultaneously impacting mortality risk; that is, we adjusted for relevant confounders. We maintain that by doing this, we were able to discern the causal relationship between social disconnection and mortality. However, we are fully aware that residual confounding plays a role, as is the case in any observational study.

A study from the UK revealed that divorce and separation in an ageing population predicted higher mortality rates like the present study. The higher mortality in the UK study, however, was explained by increased smoking prevalence 2 years post-divorce and reduced lung function 4 years post-divorce.17

We only indirectly included factors related to social avoidance and attachment anxiety like subjective loneliness and self-rated health. Both factors strongly predicted divorce/breakup and represented important confounding factors as they also significantly impacted mortality. Social baseline theory represents a normative perspective maintaining that we are human beings by our communitarian attachments with others.1 The present study was, however, insufficient in identifying the individual trajectories that could explain why some people cope with divorce/breakups while others do not.

The present study could not confirm the ‘male vulnerability hypothesis’, maintaining that men are more vulnerable to disconnection than women are, as demonstrated in a previous study from Norway.34 It seems we are faced with time-dependent influence,33 and with influence that works in different directions. Men are more inclined to lose their connections with children, family and social network after divorce,46 whereas women are more prone to socioeconomic deprivation.47

Concerning the widowed population, our results conflicted with another comprehensive epidemiological study from Norway, revealing that increased mortality was most prominent shortly after bereavement and declined over the following 10 years.32 The comparison population was the permanent married population, and people who were divorced during follow-up were excluded from the comparison group.

Strengths and limitations

Cohabitation breakups between T1 and T2 are crude estimates of social disconnection, as they are based on one-time reporting at T2. We were not able to track the individual trajectories of intimate relations and/or other manifestations of preserved or re-established social connections after an eventual breakup between T1 and T2 and re-established social connections after T2. Study participants classified as divorced/broken up may have entered new relationships during the follow-up period after T2. This misclassification is likely to attenuate the observed association and lead to an underestimation of the true effect of divorce and cohabitation breakup on mortality. Our adjustment variables included relevant measures associated both with social disconnection and premature mortality, although we had no variables pertaining specifically to social avoidance and attachment anxiety.

Socioeconomic status (SES) is an obvious and important measure in research on social determinants of mortality. Educational attainment was only reported in some of the HUNT studies and was therefore omitted in our analyses. Smoking and self-rated health may, however, serve as relevant proxies for SES.

Other variables used in HUNT questionnaire items, including those used in our analyses, are drawn from instruments with established validity or longstanding use in large-scale epidemiological research. However, loneliness was measured using a single-item indicator, which, although it shows expected associations in previous HUNT studies, has not undergone dedicated psychometric validation and may introduce some measurement imprecision.

The study had an acceptable response rate and was representative of the general population in the former county of Nord-Trøndelag,36 37 an obvious strength of the study. We also maintain that it is a strength of the study that we included multiple factors which could be predictive of divorce/breakup to single out the causal role of social disconnection from social selection.

This study was based on data from a large population-based cohort including the entire population of Trøndelag across four survey waves. The design ensures broad representativeness across age, sex and common health conditions. Linkage to national health registries provides validated clinical endpoints that reduce bias, while well-documented covariates support comparability with international studies.36 Given the social and health similarities across Nordic countries and other Western countries, the findings are likely generalisable beyond the Norwegian context.

Conclusion

Divorce and widowhood are associated with increased mortality risk, even after adjusting for health behaviours, self-rated health and loneliness. Divorce/breakup showed a more consistent and sustained impact than widowhood. These findings highlight the importance of addressing social disconnection in public health and in clinical practice to reduce preventable mortality.
