Abstract
We study belief polarization among Bayesian agents observing public information about a multidimensional state. Baliga et al. 2013a show that divergence in the sense of first-order stochastic dominance is impossible for one dimensional beliefs, but we find that in multidimensional settings it can occur for all marginal beliefs, even with infinitely many signals. At the same time, we extend their impossibility result: divergence in the sense of multidimensional stochastic dominance is impossible. For an intermediate stochastic order, polarization may arise only in the short-run. We provide necessary and sufficient conditions on signal structures for persistent polarization and discuss implications for polarization in actions.

Introduction

Polarization is a buzzword in current political discourse. Merriam-Webster named it its 2024 Word of the Year, noting that it is “one idea that both sides of the political spectrum agree on.”

DiMaggio et al. 1996a
clarify that polarization can be defined both as a
state
—one in which society’s views are concentrated at opposite extremes, or as a
process
—i.e., the movement towards such a state. Furthermore, polarization can pertain to attitudes, beliefs, preferences etc. This paper focuses on beliefs and on polarization as a process. Specifically, we will use the following notion, based on the definition in
Dixit & Weibull 2007a
(henceforth DW).

Polarization occurs if the beliefs of two agents move further apart and in opposite directions after they observe the same information.

At first glance, this type of polarization appears at odds with rational Bayesian models. A common feature of such models is the prediction that when agents observe the same information their beliefs move closer together. Therefore, widespread polarization seemingly offers evidence of irrationality on at least one side. This intuition is formalized by several theoretical results, notably the impossibility theorem of
Baliga et al. 2013a
(henceforth BHK). Considering two Bayesian agents with different priors, they show that observing a common public signal cannot cause beliefs to diverge in the sense of first-order stochastic dominance.

Interpreting polarization as irrational has deep implications. It fits the broader view that we are living in a post-truth era, marked by erosion of shared facts and a narrowing scope for constructive public discourse

In BHK, agents’ beliefs are defined over a one-dimensional state space. In practice, political beliefs are multidimensional, spanning foreign policy, the economy, cultural issues, and more. The first contribution of this paper is to show that once beliefs are multidimensional, a strong and practically relevant form of Bayesian polarization becomes possible: agents’ marginal beliefs can diverge simultaneously in the sense of stochastic dominance on each dimension. In other words, the type of divergence that
Baliga et al. 2013a
rule out in one dimension may arise coordinatewise across all dimensions.

The analysis also clarifies the limits of Bayesian polarization. Two distinctions matter: whether polarization is measured after a single signal realization (
one-shot
) or after an infinite sequence of i.i.d. realizations (
limit
); and which stochastic order is used to compare beliefs. The coordinatewise order described above requires stochastic dominance on each marginal dimension separately, and here both one-shot and limit polarization are possible. The paper’s second contribution is to establish the restrictions that Bayesian updating places on polarization, by showing impossibility results for stronger notions of divergence. The multidimensional stochastic dominance order is the standard generalization of one-dimensional stochastic dominance to higher dimensions. Under this order, both one-shot and limit polarization are impossible---an extension of BHK’s impossibility result to any number of dimensions. The upper-orthant order requires divergence on events involving multiple dimensions being simultaneously high (or low). It is stronger than the coordinatewise order but weaker than multidimensional stochastic dominance.

Taken together, the results show that while there are forces pushing Bayesian agents towards agreement, these still allow for significant polarization in the face of common information. We demonstrate this idea from a different angle by considering polarization in beliefs on individual states. In any finite state space, there are always two states—the minimum- and maximum-likelihood states—on which both agents’ beliefs must move in the same direction. On all other states, however, their beliefs may move in opposite directions.

Mathematically, the BHK impossibility result generalizes, but substantively it is the coordinatewise order—not multidimensional stochastic dominance—that best reflects how polarization is measured in practice. Survey evidence often tracks divergence across separate issue dimensions such as climate change, trade, or cultural issues (see, e.g.,
Newport 2023a
). The possibility result shows that this form of polarization is fully consistent with Bayesian updating, even in the long run. By contrast, the stronger orders capture divergence on
combinations
of issues, e.g., whether the state is simultaneously high on both economic and cultural dimensions. The impossibility results imply that agreement may persist on such joint events, even when marginals diverge. Evidence of polarization on such issue combinations would be harder to reconcile with rational updating.

The third contribution is a complete characterization of the signals that can lead to limit coordinatewise polarization in two-dimensional state spaces. An immediate necessary condition is
partial identification
: there must be at least one additional state that is observationally equivalent to the true state of the world. Otherwise, beliefs converge to the truth, precluding polarization.
4
4

4

Formally, if the signal is
X

\Theta

\hat{\theta}

\theta\in\Theta\setminus\{\hat{\theta}\}
such that for every realization

\Pr[X=x|\theta]=\Pr[X=x|\hat{\theta}]
. Otherwise, Doob’s Consistency Theorem (
Doob 1949a

Fixing the true state, any polarizing signal must therefore induce a set of states observationally equivalent to it, which we call the
identified set
. The problem of identifying polarizing signals thus reduces to characterizing the identified sets that permit polarization.

Under a mild technical condition, Theorem
5

—each must include states that attain the minimal and maximal values on every dimension. The set must be

—it cannot include all states that lie strictly below some point while excluding all of the states that lie weakly above it, or vice versa. And it must be

—it cannot be the case that whenever states in the set are high on one dimension they are low on the other.

The result connects to a central stylized fact: the steady rise in polarization among US political elites since the 1970s has coincided with rising ideological consistency, with positions on one issue increasingly predicting positions on others
(
McCarty 2019a
)
. The theorem’s conditions permit arbitrarily high correlation between the dimensions but the non-compensatory condition rules out strong negative correlation.
5
5

5

In the
2\times 2
case limit polarization can arise
only
when agents learn that the two dimensions are perfectly correlated. Generally, learning that the two dimensions are perfectly correlated can always lead to polarization while learning that they are perfectly negatively correlated cannot.

This points to a complementary Bayesian mechanism to existing accounts of polarization: if elites encountered evidence of strong correlations among issues, their posteriors would have polarized while becoming more internally consistent, in line with the observed pattern. A suggestive illustration is the fall of ‘Third Way’ politics. This movement, championed in the 1990s by Bill Clinton and Tony Blair, combined conservative economic policies with progressive social stances. Its decline by the early 2000s
(
Leigh 2003a
)
may have signaled the instability of such mixed positions, strengthening ideological consistency and thereby contributing to polarization.

The spanning and balance conditions imply that polarizing signals must (i) preserve the possibility that the state takes very low or very high values on each dimension, and (ii) avoid decisively indicating that the state is simultaneously high (or low) on both dimensions. This echoes journalistic norms of ‘balanced coverage’, which may seem counterintuitive, since media bias is commonly viewed as a key driver of polarization. Yet the biased-media account typically works through selective exposure, with each side consuming different evidence that supports its prior (see, e.g.,
Martin & Yurukoglu 2017a
). By contrast, we study polarization in the presence of
public information
. In this setting, evidence that decisively supports one side cannot lead to polarization, but balanced evidence can. Historical accounts suggest that balanced coverage has, at times, prolonged disagreement. For instance,
Oreskes & Conway 2011a
document how tobacco industry leaders exploited the media’s adherence to the Fairness Doctrine to sustain controversy about the health risks of smoking, despite overwhelming scientific evidence.

While the analysis centers on beliefs, polarization is often discussed in terms of actions, such as voting, campaign donations, or social media posts. In the concluding section, we develop a simple political-economy model that connects the two. The key insight is that whether action polarization arises depends on the separability of preferences: robust action polarization arises when utility functions are additively separable across dimensions, but not when they display strong complementarities.

Related Literature

Our notion of polarization builds on DW, who study a one-dimensional state space and define polarization as divergence in the stochastic dominance order after a single signal realization. DW show that polarization is impossible under Bayesian updating if signals satisfy the monotone likelihood ratio property (MLRP), and in particular in the standard linear-normal model. They also present examples suggesting that polarization may arise when MLRP fails, though, as later noted by BHK these examples display only divergence of expectations, not polarization in the stochastic dominance order. BHK adopt the DW definition and prove that polarization is impossible when agents are Bayesian and the state space is finite and one dimensional.
6
6

6

The examples that DW present in detail do not display polarization according to their definition. They also sketch additional examples with continuum state spaces for which they do not specify the priors. Technically, these do not violate BHK, which assumes a discrete state space. Whether polarization is possible in continuum state spaces is an open question.

BHK’s impossibility result cemented the intuitive notion that belief polarization is inconsistent with Bayesian learning. This notion is in line with classic results in
Doob 1949a
and
Blackwell & Dubins 1962a
, which predict different forms of belief convergence among Bayesians learning from the same information. To reconcile the perceived theoretical impossibility with the apparent prevalence of polarization, several papers have introduced learning biases that generate polarization. These include ambiguity aversion (BHK), confirmation bias
(
Rabin & Schrag 1999a
)
, misperceptions about selective sharing on a network
(
Bowen et al. 2023a
)
, and correlation neglect
(
Loh & Phelan 2019a
)
.

Other work has challenged this view by showing that some forms of polarization are consistent with Bayesian updating. The closest to ours are
Andreoni & Mylovanov 2012a
and
Benoit & Dubra 2019a
, who demonstrate polarization in expectations on one dimension when agents hold beliefs over a two-dimensional state.
7
7

7

Both focus on
2\times 2
state spaces where the choice of stochastic order is irrelevant, but
Andreoni & Mylovanov 2012a
note that their observation can be generalized to polarization in expectations.

Jern et al. 2014a
provide examples of Bayesian networks that may lead to polarization regarding the probability of a hypothesis. Put in our terms, this is another example of one-dimensional polarization in a two-dimensional state space.
Seidenfeld & Wasserman 1993a
show the possibility of belief dilation: for a family of prior beliefs, observing evidence may lead the upper and lower bounds for the probability of a given event to diverge.
Acemoglu et al. 2016a
study asymptotic disagreement rather than polarization. They focus on the
magnitude
of disagreement rather than its
direction
, showing that vanishing uncertainty about likelihood functions can lead to substantial asymptotic disagreement.
Nimark & Sundaresan 2019a
develop a model of persistent polarization due to rational inattention.
Dorst 2023a
presents a philosophical account of predictable polarization, where rational agents’ beliefs diverge in directions they can anticipate ex-ante.

This paper generalizes earlier results on the possibility of Bayesian polarization by demonstrating the possibility of polarization in first order stochastic dominance on all dimensions simultaneously, for any number of dimensions, and following an infinite sequence of i.i.d. signals. It contributes to the debate on the rationality of belief polarization by showing both the possibility of Bayesian polarization and its limits. While most previous papers focused on short-term polarization, we distinguish between one-shot and asymptotic polarization and show that this distinction matters: under the upper orthant order, one-shot polarization is possible but asymptotic polarization is not. Finally, we characterize the signals that can lead to limit polarization and connect polarization in beliefs to polarization in actions.
8
8

8

Benoit & Dubra 2019a
characterize the signals that lead to one-shot polarization in their setting, motivated by experimental evidence and focused on polarization in populations. Since their setting is more specific and the notions of polarization differ, the conditions are not directly comparable.

4.1
Stochastic Orders

For two vectors

We now define three classes of sets, which we will use to define three stochastic orders. A set
U\subset\Theta
is an
upper set
if
\theta\in U
whenever
\theta\in\Theta
,
\theta\geq\delta

is an

if it is of the form
\{\,\theta\in\Theta\mid\theta_{1}>a_{1},\theta_{2}>a_{2},\ldots,\theta_{d}>a_{d}\,\}

. A set
U\subset\Theta
is an

if it is of the form

\{\,\theta\in\Theta\mid\theta_{i}>a\,\}
for some

and

.

Lemma
3
implies that the limit belief following an infinite sequence of signals is almost surely equal to the posterior belief that would arise upon observing a single signal that revealed that the state is in the identified set and nothing else. Formally, say that
X
is a
partitional signal
if there exists a partition

\ell(x(G);\theta)=1\text{ and }\ell(x^{\prime};\theta)=0\text{ for all }x^{\prime}\neq x(G)

The signal

Henceforth, we identify partitional signal realizations with their associated partition cells, i.e., we set
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{G}
and

for all
\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}
. Lemma

implies that limit polarization is equivalent to one-shot polarization upon observing a partitional signal. That is, it shows that the following definition is equivalent to Definition
2

Definition 2’
.

Fix a partitional signal
X
with partition
\mathcal{G}

be the agents’ priors and let

be their respective posteriors upon observing a realization

Q^{L}\prec\ P^{L}\prec P^{H}\prec Q^{H}

This formulation will be our working definition. One advantage is that it clarifies that limit polarization is a special case of one-shot polarization. An immediate implication is that if limit polarization is possible then so is one-shot polarization. Conversely, if one shot-polarization is impossible then limit polarization is impossible as well.

Lemma
4
highlights two restrictions on belief divergence in the face of public information. Yet agents can still polarize through two channels. The first is
state-level polarization
: beliefs can diverge on all but two states. The second is
event-level polarization
: even when beliefs move in the same directions on every state, they may diverge on events because the
magnitude
of belief updates depends on the priors. For one agent, small decreases on some states within an event may be outweighed by large increases on others. For the other, the opposite may hold, leading to polarization on the event.

In the long run, the first channel disappears: for any two full-support priors, limit posteriors move in the same direction on every state, placing positive mass on the identified set and zero elsewhere. Hence, limit coordinatewise polarization operates through the second channel. By contrast, upper orthant and stochastic polarization require divergence on at least one individual state. This is impossible for limit beliefs, but in the short run the combination of the first and second channels allows for upper orthant polarization.

The conditions of Theorem

can be expressed geometrically. A set is spanning if it intersects every extreme row and column. A set is biased downward if it is contained in a “lower L,” while its complement is contained in the corresponding “upper L.” That is, there exists a state such that, for the Euclidean coordinate system originating at that state,
\Gamma
covers the lower left quadrant, while
\Gamma^{C}
covers the upper right quadrant. Similarly, a set is biased upward if it covers the upper right quadrant while its complement covers the lower left quadrant. As illustrated in Figure

7.2
Action Polarization

So far we have focused on belief polarization, but in practice polarization is most often observed in actions. We now demonstrate how the results for belief polarization translate into results for action polarization, using a simple political-economy model.

Consider a finite population of citizens in the run-up to an election between two candidates, an incumbent (I) and a challenger (C). Each citizen
i
has a utility function
u_{i}:\{I,C\}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where
u_{i}(n,\theta)
denotes citizen
i
’s future payoff in the event that candidate
n
is elected in state
\theta
. Assume there is no uncertainty about the incumbent’s future performance, so
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
only describes the challenger’s future performance across
d
different policy dimensions. That is, for each citizen,
u_{i}(I,\theta)
is constant in
\theta
, while
u_{i}(C,\theta)
is not.

The population is partitioned into two groups
L
and
H
, with beliefs
P^{L},P^{H}\in\Delta(\Theta)
. Think of these groups as representing partisans for two opposing parties. Each citizen evaluates their expected utility under the challenger,
U_{i}(C)
, according to their belief and utility function, and takes an action
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
accordingly. For instance, if
i\in L
then
U_{i}(C)=E^{L}[u_{i}(C,\theta)]
. Assume that actions are ordered from against the challenger to in favor of, and that agents with higher
U_{i}(C)
take higher actions. For example, a low action in
\mathcal{A}
might correspond to donating to the incumbent’s campaign, a low-moderate action to tweeting in their favor, and a high action to actively campaigning for the challenger.

Assume group
H
is initially taking higher actions, i.e., has a more favorable view of the challenger. Say
action polarization
occurs if a piece of public information moves the actions of all
L
citizens down and the actions of all
H
citizens up.

To see the relationship between belief and action polarization, consider first the coordinatewise order, which is generated by sums of univariate increasing functions. If agents
i\in L
and
j\in H
both have utility functions that are additively separable and increasing in each dimension, then a signal that induces coordinatewise belief polarization will also drive their expectations apart, resulting in action polarization. By Theorem
1
, coordinatewise belief polarization is possible both in the short-run and in the long-run limit. Hence, if all agents’ utility functions are additively separable and increasing, then action polarization is possible as well.

The implications of impossibility results on belief polarization are a bit more subtle. Although one-shot belief polarization is impossible according to the stochastic order, which is generated by increasing functions, this does not imply that one-shot action polarization is impossible when agents’ utility functions are increasing. Rather, it implies that for any priors and signal, there exists at least one increasing utility function
u
such that if an
L
agent and an
H
agent share this utility function then their actions will not polarize. Thus, impossibility of belief polarization rules out the following stronger notion of action polarization.

For a set
\mathcal{U}
of utility functions

\mathcal{U}
-action polarization
occurs if there exist priors and a signal realization such that action polarization occurs
whenever
all agents’ utility functions are in
\mathcal{U}

Lemma 10
.

Let
P^{L}
and
P^{H}
be two univariate distributions with a common finite support

. Then

implies that

that is weakly increasing and not constant.

Proof.
Let

and
F^{H}
be the corresponding cdfs. Using summation by parts,

\begin{split}E^{H}[u]-E^{L}[u]&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u(x_{i})\left(P^{H}(x_{i})-P^{L}(x_{i})\right)\\
&=u(x_{n})\left(F^{H}(x_{n})-F^{L}(x_{n})\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(F^{H}(x_{i})-F^{L}(x_{i})\right)\left(u(x_{i+1})-u(x_{i})\right)\\

, which implies

.
