A 48-Month, Prospective, Multicenter, Natural History Study of Choroideremia
Authors: David G. Birch, Paul S. Bernstein, Ian M. MacDonald, Tim Stout, David S. Liao, Kirsten G. Locke, Wolfgang Pita-Thomas, Somayeh Honarmand, Jenny Holt, Peter J. Francis, David H. Kirn

ABSTRACT
PurposeState-of-the-art testing modalities evaluated choroideremia (CHM) disease progression and examined the feasibility of measuring specific endpoints over time.DesignProspective multicenter natural history study.ParticipantsMale participants (N = 57) were ≥14 years (range, 14–70 years) with a clinical diagnosis of CHM and a disease-causing variant in the CHM gene. Participants had best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ≥20/200 (measured using the ETDRS letter score), a preserved paracentral foveal (within the central 10°) ellipsoid zone (EZ) and outer nuclear layer on spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT).MethodsParticipants at 5 centers in the United States and Canada consented to testing at 6-month intervals for 24 months. Importantly, several functional measures of visual field correlated highly with PFAF area, supporting its validity as an outcome measure. Choroideremia (CHM) is an X-linked inherited retinal degeneration caused by pathogenic variants in the CHM gene on chromosome Xq 21.2.1 The CHM gene encodes Rab escort protein 1, which is involved in lipid prenylation and intracellular protein trafficking. Although the primary site of physiological disruption is not known, the accumulation of unprenylated Rabs is associated with abnormal photoreceptor outer segment phagocytosis and decreased melanosome transport, leading to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cell death.2, 3, 4 During progression, the photoreceptors, RPE, and choroid degenerate, leading to a pathognomonic appearance in patients with advanced CHM.4

It is clear from cross-sectional studies that night blindness and visual field loss begin in the first decade of life and progress until legal blindness develops around the fifth to seventh decade.5,6 However, with treatment trials for CHM ongoing, and additional trials planned, it is imperative to develop a better understanding of the changes occurring in clinical outcome measures over 1 to 2 years. The most used primary endpoint in CHM gene therapy trials to date has been best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA).7 Best-corrected visual acuity has limitations in CHM since it is typically normal in patients aged less than 30 years, and the best estimates of annual visual decline are only 1 or 2 letters per year.5 The NIGHT prospective observational study of CHM showed that BCVA was stable over 20 months, with most participants (81.4% in the worse eye and 77.8% in the better eye) showing a change of less than 5 letters.8 Potential functional endpoints for CHM in addition to BCVA include contrast sensitivity, kinetic and static perimetry, and microperimetry.

Other potential endpoints for treatment intervention trials include imaging endpoints derived from fundus autofluorescence (FAF) and spectral-domain OCT measurements. Fundus autofluorescence is used to measure preserved FAF (PFAF), which reflects residual RPE area. A recent meta-analysis of available longitudinal studies suggests an exponential decline of 0.05 log mm2/year (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.046–0.055 log mm2/year), corresponding to a loss of residual RPE by 10.88% per year.9 Another potential endpoint is the area of the inner segment ellipsoid zone (EZ), which can be derived from OCT volume scans. The NIGHT study found a mean decrease in the central EZ area of –0.276 mm2 in the worse eye and –0.290 mm2 in the better eye over 12 months.8

The CHM natural history study reported here provides the longest follow-up period to date (48 months) to gather prospective data on CHM progression in participants at 5 sites in the United States and Canada. Both eyes of participants were followed at 6-month intervals for up to 4 years. The goals were to evaluate the rate of progression of CHM based on a diverse array of endpoints and to determine the feasibility of measuring specific endpoints serially over time in a population of participants with CHM.

Methods

Study Design

This was a multicenter, longitudinal natural history study (NCT02994368). The enrollment period was from Dec 2016 to June 2018. Participants (N = 57) were enrolled at 5 sites in the United States and Canada. Potential participants provided written informed consent prior to any study assessments. Participants who were minors gave an assent approved by the relevant institutional review board for execution, and the parent or legal guardian executed an appropriate informed consent. Participants who completed the trial at the month 24 visit were asked to reconsent and re-enroll to participate in visits at months 30, 36, 42, and 48 of the clinical trial. An expansion cohort with a duration of participation of up to 1 year was also included. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review boards or ethics boards associated with each participating site. All analyses were performed on all enrolled participants (all participants who signed the consent form and attended at least one study visit).

Male participants were 14 years and older (range, 14–70 years) with a clinical diagnosis of CHM associated with a disease-causing variant in the CHM gene. Participants had 20/200 or better BCVA, a preserved paracentral foveal (within the central 10°) EZ and outer nuclear layer on spectral-domain OCT. Most participants had a visual field diameter (Goldmann III4e) of >30° in the worse-seeing eye (type 2: intermediate CHM). A second group had a visual field in the worse eye of <30° (type 1: advanced CHM). Both eyes were enrolled in the study. The exclusion criteria for both the primary and expansion cohorts included participation in a clinical study with an investigational drug, agent, or therapy within the past 6 months, adeno-associated virus vector-based treatment, preexisting eye conditions that could affect the interpretation of study endpoints, cataract surgery within the past 6 months, and any condition that would not allow the participant to complete follow-up examinations.

Outcome Measures

Participants who consented to the study after meeting all inclusion and no exclusion criteria were seen at baseline and every 6 months for up to 4 years. Expansion cohort participants were seen every 3 months for up to 1 year. Following subjective refraction, BCVA was measured on each visit as the ETDRS letter score on the electronic visual acuity tester or ETDRS charts.

Kinetic perimetry (KP) was performed on an Octopus 900 (Haag-Streit AG) at each visit, with EyeSuite software to calculate seeing and nonseeing areas in degrees.2 The field was mapped using spot sizes V-4e, III-4e, and I-4e at a speed of 4°/sec for the first 2 years of the study. Beyond 24 months, only the III-4e isopter was followed. Scotomas were mapped at 2°/sec angular velocity originating from the non-seeing area and using at least 12 vectors. Full-field automated static perimetry was also performed at each visit on the Octopus 900 using the German Adaptive Thresholding Estimation and a custom 186-point centrally condensed radial grid with size V stimulus. Visual field measurement and analysis was used by the Casey Reading Center, Oregon Health Sciences University to determine the full volume within the visual field (VTOT) and the volume within the central 30° visual field (V30).10

Spectral-domain OCT was measured with a Spectralis device (Heidelberg Engineering, Inc.). Automatic real-time registration was used with a setting of nine for high-resolution images. Follow-up scans were captured with automatic registration to ensure the same scan placement on each visit. As documented in recent publications, marking the width of the EZ band has unique challenges in CHM.11,12 The following additional considerations helped to mark the edges of the preserved EZ band in challenging cases: (1) interlaminar bridges that mark the abrupt transition from central relatively intact retinal lamination to outer retinal atrophy; (2) a hyperreflective band from the external limiting membrane and the ellipsoid band to the outer nuclear layer plus the Henle fiber layer at the edge of the preserved EZ band; and (3) abrupt change in sub-RPE illumination at the border between preserved and atrophic RPE. Moving from the fovea to the periphery, the RPE will frequently end before the EZ, and there can be edges where the EZ is “floating.” Breaks in the EZ are common. It was often necessary to review several scans/visits before determining the endpoint of the EZ. Measurements of horizontal and vertical midline EZ length were made by graders at the Casey Reading Center, Oregon Health Sciences University.

Blue-light PFAF (30° field of view) was acquired using Heidelberg Spectralis systems, and the area of preserved RPE was measured by the Casey Reading Center using Spectralis RegionFinder software at each visit. Occasional images were not evaluable using the baseline as a reference point, typically due to poor image quality or differences in scan angle between visits. The areas derived from these images are included in the results but were not used in the calculations of change from baseline.

Statistical Methods

The distributions of outcome measures at each visit were summarized with means, standard deviations (SDs), medians, and ranges. Mixed-effect models with random intercepts were used to estimate the annual rates of change and percentile rates of change. Natural log-transformed data were used to estimate the rate of change in visual perimetry values and anatomical values (PFAF and EZ). Logarithmic transformation of area measurements was used to reduce the dependence of shrinkage rates on baseline measurements.9,13 The percentage of change per year was calculated by the exponential function of the annual rate of change obtained in the mixed model, minus 1, and multiplied by 100. Associations between static perimetry and KP measures with PFAF area measures were assessed with Spearman correlation coefficients.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Demographics

Of 57 participants enrolled, 55 were in the primary cohort, and 2 were enrolled in an expansion cohort, which was administratively discontinued due to pending treatment trials. While the exclusion criteria specified participation in a therapeutic trial within 6 months prior to enrollment, to our knowledge, no participants had been enrolled in a treatment trial prior to the start of the study. Eight participants in the primary cohort had advanced CHM (type 1), and 47 had intermediate disease (type 2). In the primary cohort, 47/55 (85.5%) completed the 24-month visit and 26/55 (47.3%) completed the 48-month visit (Fig 1). The most common reason for study discontinuation was enrollment in a gene replacement clinical trial. Baseline demographics are presented in Table S1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). All participants were male, with a mean age (±SD) of 32.4 ± 11.2 years;. PFAF and EZ Width Progression over Time

Preserved FAF scans were graded as evaluable or nonevaluable at each study visit. Twenty participants had nonevaluable image scans at baseline. For the 37 participants with evaluable PFAF images at baseline, OD and OS scans were taken at each follow-up visit. For the OD, evaluable PFAF images were obtained in 25/28 participants at month 12, 23/25 at month 24, 13/13 at month 36, and 8/9 at month 48, for an aggregate evaluability rate of 69/75 (92.0%). For the OS, evaluable PFAF images were obtained in 23/25 participants at month 12, 21/24 at month 24, 13/13 at month 36, and 9/10 at month 48, for an aggregate evaluability rate of 66/72 (91.7%).

The mean PFAF at baseline was 13.0 mm2 (±12.0) and 12.4 mm2 (±12.2) in the OD and OS, respectively (Table 1). Spaghetti plots show the decline of PFAF area per visit during the 48-month study and as a function of age (Fig 5A). The decline was rapid in younger participants, but there is a clear baseline effect with increasing age. Safety Summary

As this was an observational, noninterventional study, only serious adverse events were monitored. No ocular or nonocular serious adverse events were reported. Two participants developed cataracts and had cataract surgery in both eyes, and two participants reported dry eye.

Discussion

Participants in this CHM natural history study tended to be younger than participants in the NIGHT study, with an entry age lower cutoff of 14 years.8 This study, therefore, extends our knowledge into the teenage years and has the longest follow-up (48 months) to date in CHM participants. The data presented here corroborate other studies, showing an extremely slow decline in BCVA, with a predicted loss of approximately 1 letter every 6 years.5,6,8,14 At 24 months, 91% of ODs and 91% of OSs had a change in BCVA of <5 letters. Over the course of the 48-month study, 92% of ODs and 77% of OSs had a change in BCVA of <5 letters. This is a slower rate of BCVA decline than reported in the NIGHT study, where 81.4% in the worse eye and 77.8% in the better eye had a change in baseline <5 letters at month 20.8 This may be due to the older average age of participants in the NIGHT study (47.1 years versus 32.4 years in the current study) and the fact that BCVA rate of change is higher after the age of 39 years.6,8

Kinetic perimetry showed an exponential decline with a rapid decline in younger participants, especially for III4e and I4e isopters, as shown in a previous study.15 However, drawing on prior experience in retinitis pigmentosa, this measure is hampered by large variability, leading to larger CIs than for other functional measures.16 The data presented here are consistent with prior reports that static perimetry was less variable and more acceptable to the participants than KP.17 Measures of the volume of the full remaining field (VTOT), the central 30° field (V30), and mean sensitivity were derived using hill of vision modeling.10 All three indices had a similar rate of decline, but V30 had a higher correlation to PFAF area than VTOT, presumably because it better corresponds to the PFAF 30° field of view. This correlation was observed not only at baseline but also during the rest of the study, suggesting that this correlation was preserved over time (data not shown). Preserved FAF area has an advantage over field measures of lower variability despite a rapid decline, which is also supported by the data reported here. Thus, PFAF is a more desirable primary outcome measure. Importantly, several functional measures of visual field correlate highly with PFAF area, which adds to its validity as a clinical trial outcome measure. The average annual change in PFAF area of about 10% reported here is comparable to estimates from other studies.9,14,18,19 Compared to PFAF, measures of EZ area are extremely difficult to obtain from spectral-domain OCTs in CHM because of the highly irregular transition zones.11,20,21 Measured areas can differ from visit to visit due to numerous factors, and the number of scans sampled (automatic real-time registration) is lower for volume scans than for single line scans. Thus, horizontal and vertical midline scans were utilized for following participants rather than volume scans.

The residual EZ tends to be vertically elongated in CHM, and it appeared that vertical EZ dimensions were greater than horizontal. Also, the rate of change was greater for vertical measures than for horizontal. Most prior studies evaluating vertical versus horizontal EZ measurements have been conducted primarily in retinitis pigmentosa, and to date, none have directly compared vertical and horizontal rates of EZ loss in CHM.22,23 Possible explanations for this discrepancy include disease-specific patterns of degeneration, such as the irregular, scalloped borders of atrophy characteristic of CHM, as opposed to the more uniform patterns typically described in retinitis pigmentosa.22,24, 25, 26, 27 This irregular pattern of degeneration may, in turn, relate to asymmetry in photoreceptor distribution or regional differences in metabolic stress related to light exposure. Further study is warranted to clarify the anatomical and pathophysiological basis for this finding.

Visual Function Questionnaire 25 composite scores decreased by an average of 4.2 points over the first 24 months of the study. Previous work in patients with diabetic macular edema suggests that a change of 3 to 5 points in the composite score is clinically meaningful to patients.28

A weakness of this study is that several participants were lost to follow-up after month 24, partially due to the initiation of two treatment trials during this period. The STAR trial of subretinal gene replacement therapy (Nightstar Therapeutics, Biogen) and the 4D-110 trial of intravitreal gene replacement therapy (4D Molecular Therapeutics, Inc.) attracted participants away from this natural history trial. Of note, the discontinuation of the expansion cohort is acknowledged as a limitation of the study, as it would have provided a valuable opportunity to compare the granularity of observed changes at 3-month intervals versus 6-month intervals; however, given the 4-year duration of the primary cohort, it is believed that no important information was lost. A further limitation of this study is that 20 of the 57 baseline PFAF images (35%) acquired at the screening visit were deemed ungradable by the central reading center, precluding assessment of disease progression in these eyes. Among the remaining 37 eyes with gradable baseline PFAF images, up to 92% of follow-up images were successfully graded. This finding suggests that the high proportion of ungradable images at screening was primarily attributable to disease- or anatomy-related factors intrinsic to the eyes (e.g., the preserved autofluorescent area extending beyond the captured field of view), rather than to poor image quality or inconsistencies in image acquisition. Additionally, the absence of microperimetry in the protocol represents a further limitation of the study. Some sites did not have the necessary equipment at start-up. Despite some limitations, microperimetry has gained support recently as a sensitive and reliable outcome measure in CHM.29,30

Despite recent and ongoing clinical trials in CHM, there is not yet an approved therapy. Collectively, these data emphasize the necessity of natural history studies to inform future treatment intervention trials, enable the selection of sensitive and reproducible outcome measures to follow disease progression, and provide an additional untreated arm for comparison in future trials.
