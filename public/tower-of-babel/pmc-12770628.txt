Human health risk assessment of metals and metalloid in food crops harvested from crude oil impacted community in Niger Delta, Nigeria
Authors: Victoria Koshoffa Akinkpelumi, Chika Maurine Ossai, Prosper Manu Abdulai, Harrison Anezi Ozoani, Eudora Nwanaforo, Cecilia Nwadiuto Obasi, Orish Ebere Orisakwe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the human health risks associated with consuming food crops contaminated with metals and metalloids in a crude oil–impacted area of the Niger Delta, Nigeria. A total of 48 composite samples (12 each of yam, cassava, cocoyam, and waterleaf) were collected from the Ibaa community and its Right-of-Way (ROW) zone. Edible portions were acid-digested (HNO₃/HClO₄, 3:1) and analyzed for As, Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn using Graphite Furnace Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (GF-AAS; Hitachi Z-2000, Tokyo, Japan). Quality assurance included reagent blanks, certified reference materials, and spike recoveries, with recoveries of 92–107%, RSD < 5%, and detection limits of 1.73 µg/L (Pb), 0.02 µg/L (Cd, Cu, Zn), and 2.0 µg/L (As). Mean concentrations (mg/kg) in crops from the ROW were Pb: 0.0028–0.0118, As: 0.0002–0.0030, Cd: 0.0008–0.0027, Cu: 7.99–9.37, and Zn: 16.17–77.51, while control samples showed significantly lower levels. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) indicated two dominant contamination sources: anthropogenic Pb–Cd inputs from petroleum activities and Cu from agrochemical use. Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) revealed higher exposure in children, with maximum values for Zn (7.9 × 10-1 mg/kg/day), Cu (1.0 × 10-1), and As (9.5 × 10-5) in cocoyam. Target Hazard Quotients (THQ) for Zn and Cu in children exceeded unity, and the Hazard Index (HI) peaked at 5.5 for cocoyam, surpassing the USEPA safety threshold (HI > 1). Total Lifetime Cancer Risk (TLCR) for As reached 1.5 × 10-4, exceeding the acceptable limit (1 × 10-4). These findings demonstrate significant non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks, particularly for children consuming food crops from petroleum-exposed soils. Enhanced regulatory oversight, phytoremediation, and continuous biomonitoring are strongly recommended to mitigate dietary exposure in oil-impacted communities.

Introduction

The Niger delta region of Nigeria is characterized by the abundance of crude oil deposits. These mineral resources are central to Nigeria’s economy, as about 90% of the country’s revenue comes from crude oil1. Despite the economic importance of crude oil, its exploration and exploitation have contributed to human health issues. The region has been subjected to severe environmental pollution arising from deliberate effluents discharge from exploration and exploitation activities of the oil and gas sector with significant impact on the flora and fauna of the Niger delta2. These discharges have resulted in cases of mortality and chronic implication in hospitals misery, sickness, pain, and hunger3. The activities of local illegal artisanal refiners have in no small measure too affected the ecological and human health quality of life4. They do so by increasing the environmental burden of contaminants such as heavy metals. These contaminants have been reported to contaminate water resources, soil, air quality and most importantly food sources with attendant public health implications5.

Heavy metals are constituent of petroleum hydrocarbon, and its heavy metal content is associated with porphyrins which is the pyrrolic structure6. Heavy metals unlike organic pollutants, heavy metals do not decay or biodegrade. Whenever an excessive supply of heavy metals finds its way into the pedosphere, the quality of the soil decreases due to the reduction in soil productivity7. Consequently, heavy metals and metalloids exposure to human may pose health risk. Heavy metal contamination in the Niger Delta region, occurs because of oil spillage. Crude oil is a complex mixture of hydrocarbon and non-hydrocarbon compounds, including heavy metals found in sub-surface deposits worldwide8,9.

Hazards associated with the persistence of heavy metals are their ability to bioaccumulate and bio magnify in some organisms than its initial state in the environment. Heavy metals are considered systemic toxicants capable of causing multiple organ damage along with teratogenic and carcinogenic effects even at low levels of exposure (i.e. chronic exposure). Arsenic can cause skin and lung cancer while chronic cadmium (Cd) exposure is associated with breast and ovarian cancer10. Furthermore, exposure to multiple heavy metals can induce severe human health consequences than might be expected from low individual metal concentrations alone. Exposure to heavy metals from food crops occur through bioaccumulation of metals in human food sources11. Hence, even if humans do not ingest heavy-metal contaminated water directly, they are often exposed to high levels of heavy metals from plant and aquatic food sources grown from the polluted soil9,10,12.

In human health risk assessment related to dietary exposure, several key indices are employed, including the Estimated Weekly Intake (EWI), Provisional Tolerable Weekly Intake (PTWI), Target Hazard Quotient (THQ), Maximum Safe Consuming Quantity (MSCQ), Carcinogenic Risk (CR), and Hazard Index (HI). These indices provide critical insights into the potential health risks associated with food consumption13. Several studies have evaluated the application of these indices in the estimation of contamination by metals and metalloid in diverse food matrices3,13–15. The present study has determined the concentrations, trends and source identification of arsenic, lead, cadmium, copper and zinc in cassava, cocoyam, yam and waterleaf which are major staple foods in Niger delta, Nigeria. The study has also evaluated the health risk associated with presence of these metals and metalloid in food crops harvested from crude-oil polluted area in Ibaa community, Rivers State Nigeria.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was carried out in the Ibaa community (crude oil exploration, Latitude: 4o 56’ 45” N; Longitutude:6° 52’ 41” E) with a population around 200,000 inhabitants according to the most recent census. The Ibaa community is predominantly made up of three groups, namely Mgbuisinu, Mgbuosimini, and Omusunu, which live in twenty villages16 (Fig. 1). Samples were also collected from the Right of Way, a designated strip of land reserved for oil and gas pipelines and related infrastructure. The ROW passes through the Ibaa community and represents an area of high environmental concern due to its exposure to petroleum activities, land clearing, and possible contamination.

Sampling and Preparation of food crops sample

Collection of food crops samples

A total of 48 food crop samples commonly cultivated and consumed by the locals were procured from the indigenous farmers in August 2023. The samples comprised 12 yam tubers, 12 cocoyam tubers, 12 cassava tubers, and 12 bunches of waterleaf. All samples were properly washed and transported to the laboratory for laboratory analysis.

Processing and analysis of arsenic and heavy metals (Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn) concentration in food (tubers and vegetables) from Ibaa community and ROW

The edible portions of yam, cassava, cocoyam, and waterleaf were washed thoroughly with distilled water, oven-dried at 65 °C to constant weight, ground, and homogenized using an agate mortar17. For digestion, 2.0 g of each homogenized sample was transferred into acid-washed Teflon beakers and digested following USEPA Methods 3050B18 and 7000B19, and validated against AOAC Official Methods 999.1020 and 2015.0121 for biological matrices.

A mixture of 65% nitric acid (HNO₃) and perchloric acid (HClO₄) in a 3:1 ratio (v/v) was added, and the samples were heated at 110 °C for 5 h on a temperature-controlled hot plate22,23. The digests were then transferred to a muffle furnace, gradually heated to 450 °C for 18 h until white ash was obtained. The residue was dissolved in 5 mL of 1.5% HNO₃, filtered through Whatman No. 42 filter paper, and diluted to 25 mL with ultrapure (18 MΩ·cm) water. All reagents used were ultra-trace analytical grade (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and all containers were pre-soaked in 10% HNO₃ for 24 h before use.

Instrumental determination

Concentrations of As, Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn were determined using Graphite Furnace Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry (GF-AAS; Hitachi Z-2000, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with deuterium background correction and an autosampler. Element-specific wavelengths were employed: As (193.7 nm), Pb (228.8 nm), Cd (283.3 nm), Cu (324.7 nm), and Zn (213.9 nm). For arsenic determination, Hydride Generation AAS (HG-AAS) was used to enhance sensitivity and minimize spectral interferences. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Calibration and sensitivity

Calibration standards (1–50 µg/L) were prepared by serial dilution from 1000 mg/L single-element certified stock standards (Fluka Analytical, Switzerland). Calibration curves exhibited excellent linearity (R² > 0.999 for all analytes). Mid-level calibration verification standards were analyzed after every ten samples, and instrument recalibration was conducted if drift exceeded ± 5%. Analytical precision was monitored using repeated injections of quality control standards.

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) measures

To ensure the accuracy and reproducibility of arsenic (As) and heavy metal (Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn) concentrations, rigorous quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) procedures were applied throughout sample digestion and analysis. All glassware and containers were pre-cleaned by soaking in 10% HNO₃ and rinsing thoroughly with deionized water to prevent cross-contamination. Reagent blanks (n = 3) were processed alongside each digestion batch to monitor background contamination, while uncontaminated food samples from a reference site were analyzed to establish baseline levels.

Instrumental performance was verified through systematic calibration and validation. Calibration curves for Pb and As (40 µg/L) and for Cd, Cu, and Zn (4 µg/L) were prepared using certified reference standards, all showing excellent linearity (R² > 0.999). Calibration verification standards were analyzed after every ten samples, with deviations exceeding ± 5% triggering recalibration. Element-specific wavelengths were employed for quantification—193.7 nm (As), 228.8 nm (Pb), 283.3 nm (Cd), 324.7 nm (Cu), and 213.9 nm (Zn). A 0.5% ammonium dihydrogen phosphate solution served as a matrix modifier to minimize interferences, while hydride generation atomic absorption spectrometry (HG-AAS) was used for As to enhance sensitivity.

Certified Reference Materials (NIST SRM 1570a, spinach leaves) were analyzed concurrently for method validation, and measured concentrations were within ± 8% of certified values. Spike recovery experiments, conducted by fortifying known analyte concentrations into selected digests, produced recoveries between 92 and 107%, with mean recoveries of 95% (Pb), 97% (Cd), 93% (Cu), 96% (Zn), and 94% (As). All digestions and measurements were performed in triplicate, and results were accepted only when the relative standard deviation (RSD) was < 10% (typically < 5%), confirming precision.

Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ), determined as three and ten times the standard deviation of ten reagent blanks, respectively, were: Pb (LOD = 1.73 µg/L; LOQ = 5.77 µg/L), Cd (LOD = 0.02 µg/L; LOQ = 0.07 µg/L), Cu (LOD = 0.02 µg/L; LOQ = 0.07 µg/L), Zn (LOD = 0.02 µg/L; LOQ = 0.07 µg/L), and As (LOD = 2.0 µg/L; LOQ = 6.7 µg/L). No significant differences (p > 0.05) were observed between analytical blanks and replicates, demonstrating procedural consistency. The adopted QA/QC framework ensured the sensitivity, accuracy, and reproducibility necessary for trace-level metal and metalloid determination in food crop matrices.

Results

Arsenic and heavy metal distribution in food crops in right of way

As and heavy metal distribution in food at ROW is presented in Fig. 2. The highest concentration of Pb was recorded in yam with a mean concentration of 0.0118 ± 0.0015 mg/kg and the lowest concentration of lead was observed in waterleaf with a concentration of 0.0028 ± 0.0015 mg/kg. Intermediate lPb concentrations were recorded in cocoyam and cassava with mean concentrations of 0.0103 ± 0.0026 mg/kg and 0.0053 ± 0.0010 mg/kg respectively. There was significant difference in the Pb accumulation in the food crops (K-Observed = 19.298, df = 3, p-value = 0.000). Yam and cocoyam had significantly higher Pb concentration accumulation than waterleaf and cassava. No significant difference was observed in the Pb accumulation between yam and cocoyam, likewise between waterleaf and cassava.

For As concentration, the highest mean value was observed in cocoyam with a mean concentration of 0.0030 ± 0.0009 mg/kg followed by cassava with a mean concentration of 0.0027 ± 0.0008 mg/kg and yam with a mean concentration of 0.0020 ± 0.0013 mg/kg, while waterleaf had the lowest concentration with a mean value of 0.0002 ± 0.0004 mg/kg. As showed significant differences among the food types (K-Observed = 14.459, df = 3, p-value = 0.002). Waterleaf had significantly lower As concentrations than yam, cassava, and cocoyam but there were no significant differences in As concentration among yam, cassava, and cocoyam.

With respect to Cd concentration, both cocoyam and yam showed the highest mean values with concentrations of 0.0027 ± 0.0012 mg/kg and 0.0027 ± 0.0008 mg/kg respectively. Cassava had lower concentration with a mean value of 0.0008 ± 0.0004 mg/kg, while cadmium was not detected in waterleaf with a mean concentration of 0.0000 ± 0.0000 mg/kg. There were significant differences among the food samples (K-Observed = 18.847, df = 3, p-value = 0.000). Cocoyam and yam had significantly higher accumulation compared to waterleaf and cassava, but no significant differences were observed between cocoyam and yam, or between waterleaf and cassava.

As for Cu, yam showed the highest mean value with a concentration of 9.3712 ± 1.2852 mg/kg, followed by waterleaf with a mean concentration of 9.0853 ± 0.2951 mg/kg, cocoyam with a mean concentration of 8.2695 ± 1.5173 mg/kg, and cassava with a mean concentration of 7.9865 ± 1.1971 mg/kg. There were no significant differences among the food types (K-Observed = 4.707, df = 3, p-value = 0.195), indicating that Cu was relatively evenly distributed across all food samples in the ROW.

As regards Zn, yam contained the highest mean level with a concentration of 77.5107 ± 2.3589 mg/kg, followed by cocoyam with a mean concentration of 73.5117 ± 2.3096 mg/kg, cassava with a mean concentration of 57.3593 ± 0.9617 mg/kg, and waterleaf had the lowest concentration with a mean value of 16.1718 ± 3.4075 mg/kg. There were significant differences among the food types (K-Observed = 20.747, df = 3, p-value = 0.000). There was a gradual increase in Zn accumulation from waterleaf to yam, with three overlapping groups: waterleaf and cassava having the lowest concentrations, cassava and cocoyam having intermediate concentrations, and cocoyam and yam having the highest concentrations.

The tubers (yam and cocoyam) accumulated higher concentrations of lead, cadmium, and zinc compared to cassava and waterleaf in the Right-of-Way location. Waterleaf consistently showed the lowest concentrations of all heavy metals except Cu, where the differences were not statistically significant.

Arsenic and heavy metal distribution in food in Ibaa community

Figure 3 is the distribution of As and heavy metals in food crops in Ibaa community. The highest concentration of Pb was seen in cassava with a mean concentration of 0.0055 ± 0.0014 mg/kg, while the lowest concentration was found in waterleaf with a mean concentration of 0.0002 ± 0.0004 mg/kg. Yam had an intermediate Pb concentration with a mean value of 0.0005 ± 0.0008 mg/kg. There was significant difference in the Pb concentration in the food crops (K-Observed = 13.217, df = 2, p-value = 0.001). Cassava had significantly higher Pb concentration in comparison to waterleaf and yam, but no significant difference was observed in the Pb levels in waterleaf and yam.

Regarding As concentration in the community samples, cassava showed the highest mean value with a concentration of 0.0009 ± 0.0005 mg/kg, followed by yam with a mean concentration of 0.0005 ± 0.0001 mg/kg, while waterleaf had the lowest concentration with a mean value of 0.0002 ± 0.00001 mg/kg. There were significant differences among the food types (K-Observed = 13.216, df = 2, p-value = 0.001). Waterleaf had significantly lower As concentration than those found in yam and cassava, but no significant difference was found in As concentrations in yam and cassava.

Cassava showed the highest mean value of Cd with a concentration of 0.0012 ± 0.0002 mg/kg, followed by yam with a mean concentration of 0.0002 ± 0.0000 mg/kg, but Cd was not detected in waterleaf (mean concentration of 0.0000 ± 0.0000 mg/kg). There were significant differences among the food samples (K-Observed = 15.828, df = 2, p-value = 0.000). Cassava had significantly higher accumulation than waterleaf and yam. Similarly, yam had significantly higher Cd concentration than waterleaf.

For Cu concentration, yam showed the highest mean value with a concentration of 9.0097 ± 0.8327 mg/kg, followed by cassava with a mean concentration of 7.6128 ± 1.3581 mg/kg, and waterleaf with a mean concentration of 5.6760 ± 0.7670 mg/kg. Also, there were significant differences among the food types (K-Observed = 11.556, df = 2, p-value = 0.003). Waterleaf had significantly lower Cu concentration compared to cassava and yam. No significant difference was observed between cassava and yam.

With respect to Zn concentration, yam contained the highest mean level with a concentration of 81.4973 ± 1.3529 mg/kg, followed by cassava with a mean concentration of 70.4795 ± 7.2171 mg/kg, and waterleaf had the lowest concentration with a mean value of 10.4293 ± 0.5604 mg/kg. Zn showed significant differences among the food types (K-Observed = 15.158, df = 2, p-value = 0.001). Water leaves and cassava had lower Zn concentrations than yam, though Zn level in cassava overlapped with both waterleaf and yam groups.

All in all, cassava accumulated significantly higher concentrations of Pb and Cd in the community samples, while yam showed higher concentrations of copper and zinc. Waterleaf consistently had the lowest concentrations of all heavy metals examined.

Comparative analysis of arsenic and heavy metal in the Ibaa community and right of way

The comparative analysis of As and heavy metal concentrations in food samples from Ibaa community and ROW locations is presented in Fig. 4a and e. Pb concentration (Fig. 4a) compared values from Ibaa community and Right of Way locations showed higher concentrations in the Right of Way for all food types. In Cassava, Pb concentration increased from 0.0055 ± 0.0014 mg/kg in the Ibaa community to 0.0053 ± 0.0010 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 3.6% decrease. Waterleaf showed a substantial increase from 0.0002 ± 0.0004 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 0.0028 ± 0.0015 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 1300% increase. Similarly, Pb in yam increased from 0.0005 ± 0.0008 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 0.0118 ± 0.0015 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 2260% increase. There was no significant difference in Pb concentration in cassava harvested from the two sites namely Ibaa community and Right of Way (U-Value = 17.500, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 1.000). However, significant differences were observed for water leave (U-Value = 35.500, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.005) and yam (U-Value = 36.000, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.004), confirming the substantial increases in Pb concentrations for these food types in the Right of Way.

There was a higher concentration of As Fig. 4b in the Right of Way compared to Ibaa community for all food types. As levels in Cassava increased from 0.0008 ± 0.0008 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 0.0027 ± 0.0008 mg/kg in the Right of Way representing a 237.5% increase. Waterleaf showed no substantial change with 0.0002 ± 0.0004 mg/kg in both locations. Yam showed an increase from 0.0005 ± 0.0008 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 0.0020 ± 0.0013 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 300% increase.

In the case of Cd concentration Fig. 4c, the comparison showed mixed trends. Cassava Cd levels decreased from 0.0012 ± 0.0010 mg/kg in the community to 0.0008 ± 0.0004 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 33.3% decrease. Waterleaf showed undetectable levels in both locations. Yam showed a substantial increase from 0.0002 ± 0.0004 mg/kg in the community to 0.0027 ± 0.0008 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 1250% increase. There were no significant difference Cd levels in cassava harvested from the two locations (U-Value = 15.500, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.673), but a significant difference for yam (U-Value = 36.000, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.003) was observed.

In Fig. 4d, the comparison showed higher Cu concentrations in the Right of Way in waterleaf, but mixed results for cassava and yam. Cu levels in cassava increased slightly from 7.6128 ± 1.3581 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 7.9865 ± 1.1971 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 4.9% increase. Waterleaf showed an increase from 5.6760 ± 0.7670 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 9.0853 ± 0.2951 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 60.1% increase. Yam from Ibaa community showed a slight increase from 9.0097 ± 0.8327 mg/kg to 9.3712 ± 1.2852 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 4.0% increase. For Zn concentration as shown in Fig. 4.e, the comparison showed lower concentrations in the Right of Way for cassava but higher concentrations for waterleaf and similar levels for yam. Zn levels in cassava decreased from 70.4795 ± 7.2171 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 57.3593 ± 0.9617 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing an 18.6% decrease. Whereas Zn levels in waterleaf showed an increase from 10.4293 ± 0.5604 mg/kg in Ibaa community to 16.1718 ± 3.4075 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 55.1% increase. Yam harvested from Ibaa community showed a slight decrease from 81.4973 ± 1.3529 mg/kg to 77.5107 ± 2.3589 mg/kg in the Right of Way, representing a 4.9% decrease. There were significant differences between the two locations for all food types: cassava (U-Value = 0.000, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.002), water leave (U-Value = 36.000, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.002), and yam (U-Value = 2.000, Expected Value = 18.000, p-value = 0.013).

The comparative analysis revealed that food samples from the Right of Way generally contained higher concentrations of As and heavy metals compared to those from Ibaa community, particularly for Pb and Cd in waterleaf and yam. The most significant increases were observed in Pb concentration in waterleaf (1300% increase) and yam (2260% increase), and Cd in yam (1250% increase).

Source identification

Principal Component Analysis PCA was conducted to identify potential sources of As and heavy metal contamination in food samples collected from the study areas. The analysis revealed that two principal components explained 71.67% of the total variance in the dataset after Varimax rotation (Table not shown). The first principal component accounted for 44.73% of the total variance, while the second component explained 26.94%. Table 1 displays the factor loadings of As and heavy metals on the two principal components. The first principal component showed strong loadings for Pb and Cd with values of 0.918 and 0.935, respectively. Zn demonstrated a moderate loading of 0.593 on this component. The second principal component exhibited a high loading for Cu at 0.934, while Zn also showed a moderate loading of 0.530. As demonstrated moderate loadings on both components with values of 0.405 and 0.415 on the first and second components, respectively, suggesting multiple sources for this metal.

Figure 5 illustrates the biplot showing relationships between As and heavy metals, food types, and sampling locations. The biplot reveals distinct clustering patterns among variables. Pb and Cd vectors are positioned closely together, indicating a strong correlation between these metals. These vectors align with the cocoyam food type and the right-of-way location, suggesting higher accumulation of these toxic metals in cocoyam grown and Right-of-Way sampling location. The Cu vector appears to be relatively strong on the second component, indicating a separate source of contamination. Waterleaf vector point in the opposite direction from most metal vectors, suggesting lower metal accumulation in waterleaf.

The supplementary variables in the biplot provide additional insights into contamination patterns. The Right-of-Way location shows a positive association with most heavy metals, particularly Pb and Cd, indicating a high accumulation of most heavy metals in the food samples in the sampling location. In contrast, the Ibaa community location demonstrates a negative association with these metals, indicating a lower concentration of most of the heavy metals in this sampling location. Among food types, cocoyam shows the strongest association with Pb and Cd, while waterleaf exhibits negative associations with all metals.

The PCA results suggest two primary contamination sources in the study area. The first source, represented by the first principal component, predominantly contributes Pb and Cd to the environment. This source likely relates to anthropogenic activities such as vehicular emissions or industrial processes along the Right of Way. The second source, represented by the second principal component, primarily contributes Cu to the environment, possibly originating from agricultural practices or natural geochemical processes in the study area. The mixed loading patterns observed for Zn and As on both components indicate these elements likely originate from multiple environmental sources. The strong association between toxic metals and specific food types, particularly cocoyam grown in the Right-of-Way, raises potential public health concerns regarding the consumption of these food crops.

Estimated daily intake (EDI) (mg/kg/day) of arsenic and heavy metals in adult and children via consumption of food crops from right of way and Ibaa community (mg/kg/day)

The Estimated Daily Intake (EDI) values (mg/kg/day) of heavy metals – Pb, As, Cd, Cu, and Zn – in adult and children through the consumption of food crops from the Right of Way and control site are presented in Table 2. Across all samples, the trend of exposure levels for both adults and children generally followed the order: Zn > Cu > Pb > As > Cd, with higher exposure levels consistently seen in children compared to adults.

For Pb, the EDI values ranged from 2.0 × 10−6 (adult) in waterleaf from Ibaa Community (control) to 7.4 × 10−5 (adult) in yam from Right of Way (ROW), while children’s intake ranged from 3.5 × 10−6 to 1.3 × 10−4 for the same respective samples. The highest intake for both populations was recorded in yam from ROW, while the lowest was in waterleaf from Ibaa community. Regarding As, adult EDI values varied from 2.0 × 10−6 in waterleaf from the control site to 5.6 × 10−5 in cocoyam from ROW. In children, As intake ranged from 3.5 × 10−6 to 9.5 × 10−5, with the highest also in cocoyam from ROW and the lowest in waterleaf from Ibaa Community. Notably, waterleaf from ROW and Ibaa Community recorded 0, indicating undetectable levels of As.

For Cd, the values ranged between 0 and 1.2 × 10−5 in adults, and 0 to 2.1 × 10−5 in children. The highest intake was seen in yam and cocoyam from ROW, while several samples such as waterleaf from both sites showed undetectable Cd levels Table 2.

The EDI values for Cu in adults ranged from 3.7 × 10−2 in waterleaf (control site) to 6.0 × 10−2 in yam from Ibaa Community. In children, it ranged from 6.3 × 10−2 to 1.0 × 10−1, with yam again showing the highest exposure. Cu was the second most prevalent metal in terms of daily intake for both demographics. Zn exhibited the highest intake levels among the metals evaluated. For adults, the values spanned from 6.5 × 10−2 in waterleaf (control) to 5.1 × 10−1 in yam (control), while children’s intake was between 1.1 × 10−1 and 8.7 × 10−1. The highest Zn intake was found in yam from the control site for both adults and children.

Target hazard quotient (THQ) and hazard index (HI) of heavy metals in adult and children via consumption of food crops from right of way and Ibaa community

Table 3 presents the Target Hazard Quotient (THQ) and Hazard Index (HI) values of heavy metals (Pb, As, Cd, Cu, Zn) for both adult and children’s populations via consumption of edible plants from the dumpsite area (Right of Way) and control site (Ibaa Community). The THQ provides an estimate of the potential health risks associated with long-term exposure to individual heavy metals, while the Hazard Index (HI), the summation of all THQs, indicates the overall non-carcinogenic risk. A THQ or HI value greater than 1 suggests potential health concern, particularly for sensitive populations like children.

At the Right of Way, cocoyam recorded the highest HI in both adults (3.2) and children (5.5), indicating a significant non-carcinogenic risk, particularly for the latter. This was primarily due to elevated THQ values for As (1.9 × 10−1 for adults; 3.2 × 10−1 for children), copper (1.5 for adults; 2.5 for children), and zinc (1.5 for adults; 2.6 for children). Similarly, yam also showed high HI values of 3.0 (adults) and 5.1 (children), again attributed to elevated THQ values for Cu and Zn. Notably, Pb and Cd in yam showed moderate THQ values, while As and Zn contributed significantly to the overall risk.

For cassava in the dumpsite area, the HI values were 2.4 (adults) and 4.1 (children), exceeding the safety threshold, with Cu and Zn contributing the most to the hazard load. The lowest THQ values for this sample were seen in Cd and Pb.

Waterleaf from the dumpsite had the lowest THQ for As and Cd (0), indicating undetectable levels of those metals, yet still showed HI values of 1.8 (adults) and 3.1 (children), primarily due to THQs for Cu and Zn, highlighting the cumulative risk from even non-toxic individual levels.

In the control site (Ibaa Community), while THQ and HI values were generally lower than the dumpsite samples, certain vegetables still exceeded the safety threshold. Yam from Ibaa recorded HI values of 3.2 (adults) and 5.5 (children), closely matching the values in the dumpsite yam, suggesting environmental persistence of metal contamination or bioaccumulation from other sources. Again, Cu and Zn were the major contributors. Cassava from the control site recorded HI values of 2.4 (adults) and 4.1 (children), with significant THQs for Cu and Zn. Meanwhile, waterleaf from the same location showed the lowest overall HI of 1.1 (adults) and 2.0 (children), with THQ values for Pb and Cd near negligible levels Table 3.

Across all samples and locations, children consistently showed higher THQ and HI values compared to adults, due to their lower body weight and increased susceptibility to contaminants. Among the metals, Cu and Zn zinc had the most substantial influence on total hazard indices, while Pb and Cd exhibited relatively lower THQ values. However, As, though present in smaller amounts, still contributed significantly to the risk in several samples.

Lifetime cancer risk (LCR) and total lifetime cancer risk (TLCR) of heavy metals in adult and children via consumption of food crops from right of way and Ibaa community

Table 4 presents the Lifetime Cancer Risk (LCR) values for individual heavy metals (Pb, As, Cd) and the Total Lifetime Cancer Risk (TLCR) from the consumption of edible plants by adult and children populations residing in the dumpsite area (Right of Way) and control site (Ibaa Community). LCR estimates the probability of an individual developing cancer over a lifetime due to exposure to carcinogenic substances, while TLCR is the cumulative risk from all assessed carcinogenic metals. According to USEPA guidelines, an acceptable risk range falls between 1.0 × 10−6 and 1.0 × 10−4, while values greater than 1.0 × 10−4 indicate a potential health concern. Non-assessed cancer metals (NACM), including Cu and Zn, were not considered in TLCR computation.

At the Right of Way, cocoyam showed the highest TLCR for both adults (8.8 × 10−5) and children (1.5 × 10−4), with As being the dominant contributor to cancer risk (8.3 × 10−5 for adults; 1.4 × 10−4 for children), followed by Pb and Cd. The children’s TLCR exceeded the USEPA upper limit, suggesting a significant cancer risk for this population. Similarly, yam and cassava from the same location recorded TLCRs above the acceptable range for children (4.1 × 10−5 and 3.6 × 10−5, respectively), though within borderline limits for adults (2.4 × 10−5 and 2.1 × 10−5, respectively). The primary carcinogenic contributors in these samples were As and Cd, with Pb having a lesser yet notable contribution Table 4. Waterleaf, despite having non-detectable levels of As and Cd, still recorded a TLCR of 1.0 × 10−7 (adult) and 1.8 × 10−7 (child) due to the presence of lead, although these values were well below the threshold of concern.

In the control site (Ibaa Community), TLCR values were comparatively lower, but not negligible. Cassava recorded TLCRs of 1.1 × 10−5 (adult) and 1.9 × 10−5 (child), while yam showed similar risk levels with 1.0 × 10−5 (adult) and 1.7 × 10−5 (child). In both samples, As remained the primary source of lifetime cancer risk, followed by Pb and Cd. Waterleaf from the control site had the lowest TLCRs: 3.1 × 10−6 (adult) and 5.3 × 10−6 (child), again within the acceptable range but still reflecting the bio accumulative nature of As in leafy vegetables. Across all samples and locations, children were consistently at higher cancer risk than adults due to higher ingestion-to-body-weight ratios. As was the leading carcinogenic metal in all samples, followed by Pb and Cd.

Discussion

Concentration of arsenic and heavy metals in food crops

The presence of heavy metals in food crops is a significant public health concern, particularly in developing countries where environmental regulations are less stringent. The evaluation of As, Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn concentrations in food crops collected from Right of Way and a control site (Ibaa Community) in Nigeria provides insight into potential health risks through dietary exposure. The results, when compared with international Permissible limits set by bodies such as FAO/WHO, reveal varying levels of concern and highlight the influence of anthropogenic activities on environmental and food safety.

Pb levels in the studied food crops ranged from 0.00033 ± 0.000 mg/kg in waterleaf from the control site to 0.012 ± 0.002 mg/kg in yam from the dumpsite area. All values were below the FAO/WHO permissible limit of 0.1–0.3 mg/kg for lead in food crops40. The relatively higher levels in yam and cocoyam from the ROW suggest contamination. Pb is a toxic metal that affects neurological development in children and causes hypertension and kidney damage in adults. Hart et al.41 reported significantly higher lead concentrations in food crops such as cocoyam and okra from Rivers State, with values reaching 1.1 mg/kg and 9.1 mg/kg respectively, indicating a more severe contamination scenario compared to the current study. Despite the lower values in this study, the presence of Pb, especially in ROW-food crops, warrants further attention in view of bio cumulative effects.

As levels varied from non-detectable in waterleaf from the ROW area to 0.009 ± 0.01 mg/kg in cocoyam from the same site. These concentrations are well below the FAO/WHO permissible limit of 0.1 mg/kg for arsenic in food33. Nonetheless, As is a well-known human carcinogen, and even low levels of chronic exposure can increase the risk of skin, lung, and bladder cancers. As, a highly toxic heavy metal, has been detected in very low concentrations in the food crops. As is not only a carcinogen but human exposure to it is fraught with multiorgan damage including cardiovascular disorders42. Onianwa et al.43 documented higher As concentrations in food crops from regions with high pesticide usage, suggesting that localized agricultural practices can significantly influence contamination levels. The present study’s lower As values may reflect limited arsenic-based agrochemical application in the study area, although continuous monitoring remains essential.

Cd was detected in cocoyam and yam from the ROW at concentrations of 0.002 ± 0.001 mg/kg, while it was non-detectable in waterleaf and recorded at lower levels in other samples. These values were below the permissible limit of 0.05–0.1 mg/kg (FAO/WHO 2001). Cd poses severe risks to renal and skeletal health due to its accumulation in the kidneys and bones. In contrast to the findings of Adefemi et al.44, that reported elevated Cd levels exceeding permissible limits in vegetables from dumpsites in Ekiti State, this study reveals a less alarming situation. However, the potential for Cd bioaccumulation through long-term exposure necessitates proactive mitigation strategies.

Cu concentrations ranged from 5.984 ± 0.67 mg/kg in waterleaf from the control site to 9.658 ± 1.03 mg/kg in yam from the same site. These values were within the FAO/WHO permissible limit of 10 mg/kg for Cu in food crops36. As an essential trace element, Cu is involved in enzymatic activities and immune function. However, excessive Cu intake can lead to gastrointestinal disturbances and liver toxicity. Agboola et al.45 reported Cu levels exceeding the safe threshold in leafy vegetables sold in Lagos markets, suggesting that urban agriculture might pose greater risks due to exposure to contaminated water or soil. The current study’s findings suggest a relatively safe Cu profile in the sampled crops, although yam exhibited values close to the upper limit, particularly in the control site.

Zn was the most prevalent heavy metal in all samples, with concentrations ranging from 10.534 ± 1.09 mg/kg in waterleaf (control) to 82.066 ± 1.45 mg/kg in yam from the same site. While Zn is essential for immune function and cellular metabolism, excess intake can interfere with Cu absorption and lead to gastrointestinal symptoms. According to FAO/WHO36, the permissible limit for zinc in food is 50 mg/kg. The observed values in yam and cocoyam from both sites exceeded this threshold, indicating potential dietary risk. Agboola et al.45 also reported elevated zinc levels in vegetables from Lagos markets, attributing such findings to the use of zinc-rich fertilizers and proximity to industrial discharge. Although Zn is less acutely toxic compared to other metals, its long-term overconsumption, particularly when compared with other metal exposures, may pose significant health risks.

Ogunbanwo et al.46 have reported similar findings, showing that Pb concentrations in food crops near contaminated areas often exceed the recommended limits, posing a health risk to populations consuming these crops over long periods. The relative accumulation of Pb in food crops in crude oil impacted communities could be attributed to exposure to crude oil. The concentration of As in the Right of Way food crops ranges from 0 mg/kg in waterleaf (Right of way) to 0.009 ± 0.01 mg/kg in cocoyam (Right of way) which are below the permissible limit of 0.1 mg/kg33. In contrast, substantially elevated concentrations of As have been observed in food crops cultivated at industrial areas in Nigeria47. Cd another highly toxic heavy metal was detected at concentrations ranging from 0.000 ± 0.000 mg/kg in waterleaf (Right of Way and Ibaa community) to 0.002 ± 0.001 mg/kg in cocoyam and yam (Right of Way). The highest levels of Cd were observed in Right of Way food crops, but all values albeit within the permissible limit of 0.05–0.1 mg/kg36. Previous research by Okonkwo et al.47 showed that Cd levels in food crops grown in contaminated areas can be influenced by soil characteristics and the types of industrial waste present. In this study, the observed levels of Cd were relatively low compared to those in other regions, where concentrations can exceed the limits, particularly in areas with high industrial waste or mining activities.

Copper (Cu) concentrations in the food crops vary from 5.984 ± 0.67 mg/kg in waterleaf (Ibaa community) to 9.608 ± 1.03 mg/kg in yam (Ibaa community). These values are well below the permissible limit of 10 mg/kg40,48. Copper is an essential trace element for plant growth, but excessive amounts can lead to toxicity, which could affect both plant health and human consumers. Although, Wairich et al.49 reported that while Cu is necessary for plant metabolism, its accumulation may be deleterious severely impacted soils, in the current study, the copper concentrations appear to be within safe levels, and its presence in higher amounts in yam and cassava may be attributed to the nutrient needs of these plants in the soils50,51.

Zinc (Zn), an essential micronutrient for plants and animals, is found in varying concentrations in the analyzed food crop samples, with levels ranging from 10.534 ± 1.09 mg/kg in waterleaf (Ibaa Community) to 82.066 ± 1.45 mg/kg in yam (Ibaa community). These values were within the permissible limit of 50 mg/kg,40,48. Zinc levels in this study were significantly higher than the levels observed in other heavy metals, suggesting that food crops in the Right of Way area may be absorbing zinc from contaminated soils. This result aligns with findings from other studies that have highlighted the role of zinc in plant growth but also warned against its accumulation in contaminated areas52.

The comparison of these results with those from other regions reveals several key trends. First, the Pb, As, and Cd concentrations in the food crops from the Right of Way location are mostly below the permissible limits, indicating that while there may be some environmental contamination, it does not exceed critical thresholds. Odukoya et al.53, reported higher concentrations of these metals, particularly Pb and Cd, in crops grown near polluted soils. These findings highlight the need for regular periodic monitoring and environmental management in crude oil impacted soils to ensure that heavy metal concentrations remain within safe limits.

The heavy metal burden (Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn) of food crops from Right of Way and control site samples (Ibaa Community) were not statistically different suggesting minimal impact of crude oil exposure on heavy metal contamination of these food crops This observation is consistent with findings from other studies that suggest the accumulation of heavy metals in food crops is influenced not only by proximity to pollution sources but also by the specific environmental conditions such as soil composition, plant type, and climatic factors54.

Furthermore, the outcome of our study is in tandem with an Indian based study that reported innocuous levels of heavy metals in some food crops (Brassica sp., Chenopodium sp., leafy and root vegetables, grains) planted in crude-oil polluted soil55. Khan et al.56 also evaluated public health impact of heavy metals (Cu, Pb, Cr and Zn) on some food crops namely Grain, maize (Zea mays), green cabbage, Brassica juncea L, radish (Raphanus sativus L), turnip, Brassica napus, spinach, cauliflower, and lettuce and found similar results. Also, the present study is also in agreement with the other studies by Alam et al.57, Islam et al.58; Rahman et al.59; Tripathi60 and Yang et al.61 from Bangladesh, India, Pakistan, Thailand and Italy, respectively which examined heavy metal contents in food crops such as Triticum aestivum (wheat), Lycopersicum esculentum L. (tomato), Raphanus sativus (radish), Spinacia oleracea (spinach), Solanum melongena (brinjal), Daucus carota subsp. Sativus (carrot), Capsicum annum (chili pepper), Allium sativum (garlic), Coriandrum sativum (coriander), and Abelmoschus esculentus (okra), Oryza sativa (rice) and other paddy crops and vegetables, which reported presence of Cr, Pb, Cu, Zn, Cd, Co, Mn and Ni within acceptable limit. The results of the current study also aligned with work of Tripathi60, Radwan and Salama62; El-Kady and Abdel-Wahhab63 who carried out separate study in Egypt to examine heavy metal content in food crops such as potatoes, yam and Beans. They identified heavy metals Cu, Pb, Cd and Zn which are like the ones evaluated in the current study were all within acceptable limit and therefore may not pose any health risk when consumed.

Despite most values falling within permissible limits, cumulative exposure to multiple metals and the potential synergistic effects must be considered. Chronic intake of low-level heavy metals has been linked to a range of health issues, including developmental delays in children, organ dysfunction, and increased cancer risk64. Children, due to their smaller body mass and developing organs, are particularly vulnerable to heavy metal toxicity, even at concentrations deemed safe for adults. Overall, the findings indicate that while the sampled food crops are largely safe with respect to Pb, As, Cd and Cu, the high levels of Zn in some samples exceeded safety recommendations.

All in all, this study reveals a relatively low to moderate risk of heavy metal contamination in food crops from crude oil impacted farmland, with zinc representing the most significant concern. The results align with findings from other Nigerian regions while also indicating specific local variations.

Human health risk of arsenic and heavy metals in food crops

The human health risk assessment conducted on food crops obtained from the Right of Way and a control site (Ibaa Community) indicate significant concerns, with respect to the levels of toxic metals such as As, Pb, Cd, Cu, and Zn ingested through daily consumption by adults and children. The estimated daily intake (EDI) values were assessed against the US EPA reference dose (RfD) values of 3.5 × 10−3 mg/kg/day for Pb, 3.0 × 10−4 mg/kg/day for As, 1.0 × 10−3 mg/kg/day for Cd, 4.0 × 10−2 mg/kg/day for Cu, and 3.0 × 10−1 mg/kg/day for Zn to understand the level of exposure and possible health implications.

From the Right of Way, all food crops samples analyzed had EDI values for Pb below the RfD of 3.5 × 10−3 mg/kg/day. For instance, cocoyam presented EDI values of 4.9 × 10−5 mg/kg/day for adults and 8.4 × 10−5 mg/kg/day for children, which are within safe limits. However, while Pb levels were within acceptable limits for EDI, the accumulation through multiple exposure pathways and overtime could still present chronic health issues. As values from cocoyam (5.6 × 10−5 for adults and 9.5 × 10−5 for children) were significantly below the RfD of 3.0 × 10−4, indicating lower daily intake but still a concern due to its carcinogenic potential, especially in long-term exposure scenarios. Cadmium also exhibited EDI values (e.g., 1.2 × 10−5 and 2.1 × 10−5 in cocoyam) much lower than the RfD of 1.0 × 10−3 mg/kg/day, suggesting minimal daily intake but its bio accumulative nature raises concerns over time. Cu and Zn levels across all samples from the Right of Way were generally within safe consumption limits when compared to their respective RfDs (4.0 × 10−2 and 3.0 × 10−1 mg/kg/day), although slightly elevated in cocoyam and yam for both adults and children. For example, Zn in cocoyam was 4.6 × 10−1 mg/kg/day for adults and 7.9 × 10−1 mg/kg/day for children, which notably exceeded the RfD of 3.0 × 10−1 mg/kg/day, especially in children, thereby posing potential risk of zinc toxicity, which could manifest as gastrointestinal issues, immune dysfunction, or disruptions in Cu metabolism.

Comparatively, food crops from the control site showed generally lower EDI values across all metals, reinforcing the impact of proximity to Right of Way on metal uptake by food crops.

The metal-induced health risks in this study are largely consistent with findings from other polluted regions. For example, Eze et al.65 reported elevated THQ and HI values in vegetables from roadside and dumpsite areas in southeastern Nigeria, with Cu and Zn often exceeding safe limits. Similarly, Ogunkunle et al.66 found that leafy vegetables from peri-urban areas of Ibadan, Nigeria had high concentrations of Pb and Cd, resulting in HI values greater than 1, especially in children.

Comparison of the daily intake level that is considered safe and unlikely to pose a risk to human health over a lifetime of exposure RfD values of 3.5 × 10−3 mg/kg/day for Pb, 3.0 × 10−4 mg/kg/day for As, 1.0 × 10−3 mg/kg/day for Cd, 4.0 × 10−2 mg/kg/day for Cu, and 3.0 × 10−1 mg/kg/day for Zn, with the EDI values in this study, with the RfD values, the EDI values for Pb, As, and Cd tended to exceed the safe limits, particularly for children. This is of public health concern. Some studies have reported higher levels of heavy metals in food grown in contaminated areas are linked to higher health risks67,68. For instance, a study by Akoto et al.69 found elevated levels of Pb, Cd, As in crops grown near contaminated sites in Ghana, and similar concerns were raised regarding the risks posed to local populations. Another study by Widyastuti et al.70 in Indonesia found that food crops grown in contaminated soils had higher THQ and HI values, indicating potential non-cancer health risks.

The LCR for lead in cocoyam from the Right of Way was 4.4 × 10−7 for adults and 5.0 × 10−7 for children, which is within the safe limit. However, the LCR for As in the same samples is significantly higher, with values of 8.0 × 10−5 for adults and 9.2 × 10−5 for children, both of which exceed the safe limit for cancer risk. Similarly, other heavy metals like cadmium showing cancer risk of concern. In contrast, the Ibaa samples show lower LCR and TLCR values, indicating that these foods pose lower cancer risk which is consistent with other studies that demonstrated that food grown in uncontaminated areas are less likely to accumulate harmful levels of carcinogenic metals28,71.

Interestingly, while metals like Cu and Zn are essential micronutrients, their bioavailability at elevated levels poses toxic threat. This dual nature is evident in the significantly high THQ and HI values recorded for these metals, especially from food crops in the Right of Way. Chronic ingestion of Cu beyond tolerable limits may lead to liver and kidney dysfunction, while excessive Zn can impair immune responses and disrupt normal Cu metabolism. Moreover, the relatively high TLCR values due to As and Cd ingestion through these food crops raise red flags for long-term exposure-related malignancies, especially gastrointestinal and renal cancers.

Furthermore, the human health risk assessment of food crops sourced from a Right of Way environment highlights significant public health concerns. The elevated levels of Cu and Zn, and associated THQ and HI values, suggest a likelihood of non-carcinogenic health effects, especially for children. Moreover, the TLCR values, particularly for As and Cd, indicate a moderate to high potential for cancer development over a lifetime. Comparisons with control site data further emphasize the health burden imposed by environmental contamination and the critical need for effective waste management policies and phytoremediation strategies.

Quantitative human health assessment in food crops

The EWI values, which represent the amount of heavy metals ingested per week per kilogram of body weight, were consistently higher in children than adults across all samples. This trend is attributed to children’s lower body weight and relatively higher food consumption per kilogram of body mass, making them more susceptible to toxic exposures72. For instance, cocoyam from the Right of Way ROW showed an EWI of 6.23 mg/kg/day in children compared to 3.64 mg/kg/day in adults, while yam from the same site had an EWI of 6.50 mg/kg/day for children and 3.80 mg/kg/day for adults. These values suggest that children are significantly more exposed to heavy metals than adults when consuming these plants from contaminated environments.

The health risk attributed to heavy metal exposure, denoted as Riskheavymetals, follows a similar trend, with higher values in children than in adults. For example, the risk value for cassava consumption by children from the dumpsite area was 119087.0 mg/kg/day, compared to 69662.7 mg/kg/day for adults. This disparity further corroborates the increased vulnerability of children to environmental contaminants, as has been well documented in environmental toxicology literature73. Moreover, the extremely high values of Riskheavymetals suggest that the concentration of heavy metals in these food crops is significantly elevated, especially in the samples from the Right of Way compared to those from the Ibaa community. This may be linked to the proximity of the Right of Way to the sample collection area, implying bioaccumulation of heavy metals in the soil and their translocation into the food crops74.

The THQ values calculated using reference doses from USEPA and WHO offer a quantitative indication of non-carcinogenic health risks due to long-term exposure to contaminants through ingestion. THQ values above 1 indicate potential health hazards. While all THQ values from both USEPA and WHO sources in the dataset are below 1, which may imply no immediate non-carcinogenic risk, their closeness to the threshold is alarming, particularly for children. For instance, the USEPA-based THQ for cocoyam children from the Right of Way was 0.045, nearly triple that of adults, which stood at 0.021. This trend of elevated THQ values in children is consistent across all plant types and locations, highlighting a need for caution and further toxicological investigation. Although these values do not exceed the critical threshold of 1, their consistency and magnitude suggest cumulative exposure could still present serious long-term health implications, particularly when dietary diversity is limited75.

When comparing samples from the Right of Way (ROW) with those from the control site, the farming environment affects contamination levels. For example, yam from the ROW had a child THQ of 0.042 (USEPA) and 0.018 (WHO). Surprisingly, yam from the control site had slightly higher THQ values of 0.045 (USEPA) and 0.018 (WHO), even though it came from a supposedly cleaner area. This unexpected result may be due to differences in soil type, how plants absorb substances, or hidden contamination in the control site. The control site may not be completely free from human activities. However, in most cases, samples from the ROW showed higher Estimated Weekly Intakes (EWIs) and Target Hazard Quotients (THQs) than those from the Ibaa community. This supports the idea that the dumpsite near the ROW is causing contamination.

The MSCQ estimates, representing the maximum amount of each food crop that can be safely consumed without health risks, offer further insights into the safety thresholds. Here, a lower MSCQ indicates higher pollution and a reduced safety margin. For example, the MSCQ for cocoyam based on WHO recommendations was 31790.9 mg/day for children from the Right of Way, which is significantly lower than the 73331.4 mg/day estimated for children consuming cassava from the control site. Such findings reinforce the argument that food crops sourced from environmentally compromised areas may impose dietary restrictions due to health safety concerns, especially in children76.

Another notable pattern is the variability in MSCQ across different crops, even within the same location. Waterleaf, for instance, demonstrated the lowest MSCQ values in several instances, reflecting its potential for higher accumulation of heavy metals compared to root tubers such as yam and cassava. The MSCQ for waterleaf in children based on WHO data was 60331.7 mg/day from the Right of Way and 359261.7 mg/day from the control site. These figures suggest that waterleaf grown near contaminated sites should be consumed with extreme caution, especially by vulnerable groups like children and pregnant women, as previously noted in dietary exposure studies77.

The cumulative implications of these findings suggest a complex interplay between environmental pollution, plant uptake behavior, and population vulnerability. Despite the THQ values being below 1, the elevated EWIs and Riskheavymetals metrics, particularly for children, underscore a significant public health concern. Long-term consumption of these contaminated food crops, even at non-immediate toxic levels, could lead to bioaccumulation and chronic health disorders including renal dysfunction, neurotoxicity, and carcinogenesis78. The results advocate for routine environmental monitoring of agricultural zones, particularly those in proximity to waste disposal sites or industrial effluents.

The findings also emphasize the importance of dietary diversification and food source traceability. Regular consumption of crops from heavily contaminated sites without adequate risk communication can lead to food insecurity and health inequality. Public health policies should, therefore, prioritize education on the risks associated with consuming produce from such areas and enforce regulations that prevent the use of polluted land for food crop cultivation. Furthermore, there is an urgent need for remediation programs that can detoxify soils in affected areas, possibly through phytoremediation or soil amendment strategies79.

Furthermore, the estimation of heavy metal exposure through food crop consumption in the studied populations illustrates significant risk, particularly for children. The elevated levels of EWI, Riskheavymetals, and the lower MSCQ values in samples from the Right of Way indicate unsafe levels of exposure, even when THQ values remain marginally below critical thresholds. These findings reinforce the necessity for immediate public health interventions, environmental rehabilitation, and further research into alternative safe agricultural practices in pollution-prone environments. This study was limited by its focus on a single sampling period, which many not fully capture seasonal variations in heavy metals concentration.

Conclusions

Taken together, in the present study, children were more susceptible to heavy metal exposure than adults across all food crop samples due to their lower body weight and higher relative food intake. Among all the metals assessed, Zn and Cu posed the highest dietary exposure, while Cd and As generally presented the lowest values, with some samples showing non-detectable levels. The HI of As and heavy metals from consumption of these food crops from both the Right of Way and Ibaa community exceeded the acceptable HI threshold of 1, indicating potential non-carcinogenic health risks, particularly among children. Some food crops, particularly from the Right of Way, showed TLCR values that exceeded the acceptable threshold, especially in children. This raises public health concerns regarding chronic dietary exposure to carcinogenic metals which will provoke the need for monitoring and risk mitigation strategies in affected communities.

Although the concentrations of Pb, As, Cd, Cu, and Zn detected in food crops in the present study were relatively low and within safe limits, there remains a need for continuous monitoring of heavy metal contamination to safeguard public health. Furthermore, additional research is required to elucidate the cumulative effects of chronic exposure to low levels of these metals through dietary intake, particularly among vulnerable populations such as children and the elderly.
