Identification of circadian-regulated genes for enhancing acidic soil tolerance in rice through integrative transcriptomic and systems analysis
Authors: Alokita Roy, Dev Mani Pandey, Anjana Dwivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The plant circadian clock orchestrates key physiological processes and stress responses by regulating time-dependent gene expression. However, its role in mediating responses to soil acidity, one of the major abiotic constraints affecting rice productivity, remains poorly understood. In this study, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic and systems analysis using publicly available RNA-Seq data to investigate the role of circadian-regulated genes in adapting to acidic stress in Oryza sativa. A total of 2,285 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified under various pH conditions (4.5 and 6.5), of which 37 were associated with the circadian clock. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) classified the DEGs into five modules, three of which were strongly correlated with acidic stress. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses revealed key roles in redox homeostasis, hormone signaling, and photosynthetic regulation. Hub genes within stress-associated modules were further analyzed for protein–protein interactions, hydrophobicity profiles, and 3D structures using AlphaFold. Notably, several circadian-related genes were downregulated under acidic stress, suggesting clock suppression as an adaptive mechanism. These findings reveal the connections between circadian regulation and pH stress tolerance, providing promising genetic targets for developing acidity-resilient rice cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48253-4.

Introduction

Circadian rhythm is an intrinsic timing mechanism that synchronizes various physiological processes, like stomatal opening, hormone signaling, and carbon metabolism, with daily environmental changes and has been associated with plant stress responses1–3. While its role in adapting to abiotic stresses, such as drought and salinity4,5, is well established, its involvement in responses to acidic stress remains largely unexplored.

Acidic stress is a significant abiotic factor that hampers global crop productivity, particularly in staples like rice6,7. As a dietary staple for nearly half the world’s population, rice plays a crucial role in food security and rural economies, especially in regions where its cultivation is highly sensitive to soil conditions7,8. Acidic soils, characterized by a pH level below 5.0, represent a significant abiotic constraint affecting crop productivity worldwide. Approximately 30% of the world’s non-ice-covered land is acidic, and nearly 12% of that is used for agricultural production9. In areas such as Jharkhand, India, where soil acidity is prevalent, rice yields are critically threatened, particularly for tribal communities that depend on traditional landraces and indigenous varieties. Soil acidification arises from several factors, including acid rain, intensive agriculture, excessive nitrogen fertilization, and improper soil management practices10. Under low pH conditions, essential nutrients like phosphorus, calcium, and magnesium become less available, while toxic elements such as aluminum become more soluble and damaging to plant roots. This leads to impaired ion homeostasis, reduced root growth, photosynthetic inhibition, and overall metabolic stress. These physiological disruptions collectively contribute to substantial yield losses, with some studies reporting declines of up to 68% in moderately acidic soils11–16.

Emerging studies suggest that acidic stress not only disrupts metabolic and signaling pathways but also interferes with circadian regulation, becoming mistimed under low pH conditions, compounding stress-related damage17. The conceptual relationship between acidic stress and circadian regulation is illustrated in Fig. 1. Despite this, no study to date has systematically investigated how the circadian clock is involved in rice responses to acidic stress, nor have transcriptome-wide regulatory mechanisms linking clock genes to pH adaptation been elucidated.

Transcriptomic analysis is a powerful tool for exploring stress-responsive gene expression at a genome-wide level. By integrating large-scale RNA-Seq datasets, it enhances statistical power and enables the identification of consistent transcriptional patterns under specific environmental conditions18–20. This study uses an integrative transcriptomic and systems approach to identify circadian-related genes in rice that respond to acidic stress, aiming to uncover novel circadian-regulated genes involved in acidic stress adaptation and provide insights into their biological roles and regulatory mechanisms. The overall workflow of the study is summarized in Fig. 2, which outlines the transcriptomic pipeline including RNA-seq preprocessing, DEG identification, WGCNA network construction, circadian gene mapping, and functional characterization. The findings of this research may facilitate the incorporation of low-pH-tolerant genes into rice and enhance its productivity in acidic soils, thereby ensuring food security and livelihoods in affected regions.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The RNA-seq dataset for acidic stress was obtained from the public database, the National Centre for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive (NCBI SRA), under accession number PRJNA746766, consisting of 8 FASTQ files. Two rice varieties, Jhilli Dhan (JD) and Khijur Jhopa Dhan (KJD), were tested under two pH levels: pH 4.5 and 6.5. Each rice variety was tested under both pH conditions, with two biological replicates included for each. The dataset has four groups: JD at pH 4.5, JD at pH 6.5, KJD at pH 4.5, and KJD at pH 6.5 (Ekta et al., 2024).

Data preprocessing

Preprocessing is the first step in any RNA-seq analysis and serves to remove technical artefacts while ensuring that the data quality is good enough for further analysis. This process begins with removing low-quality reads, adaptors, and rRNA sequences. The preprocessing pipeline conducted quality control of the raw reads through FASTQC, which shows an overview of the sequencing data quality, such as per-base sequence quality, GC content, and duplicate level. Low-quality bases and reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic and kept only with high-quality data. After cleaning, the reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2, which generates BAM files containing the mapped reads. These BAM files were used for quantification by calculating the expression levels of genes based on the aligned reads using featureCounts. The final output was a featureCount matrix used for further analysis21.

Differential gene expression analysis

The differential expression analysis was conducted on RNA-seq data using the DESeq2 package in R22,23. All eight samples, representing two rice varieties (JD and KJD) under two pH conditions (4.5 and 6.5), were analyzed simultaneously to identify genes responsive to acidic stress across the dataset rather than calculating the intersection of separate DEG lists for each variety. A DESeqDataSet object was constructed using the raw count matrix and corresponding sample metadata. The statistical design formula used was ~ pH, with pH treatment (acidic vs. neutral) as the primary factor to identify differentially expressed genes associated with acidic stress. The neutral condition was set as the reference level for fold-change estimation. The analysis was designed to evaluate the overall transcriptional response to pH treatment across the samples. DESeq2 was used to adjust read counts, correcting for differences in data collection across samples and other sources of variation, so that gene expression levels could be fairly compared across all samples. DESeq2 uses a Wald test to calculate the statistical significance of expression changes for differential expression testing. Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) correction was applied to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR) for multiple tests. An adjusted p-value (FDR) threshold of < 0.05 was set to identify significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), filtering out genes unlikely to be biologically relevant due to random variation. Genes with |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Among these, genes with log₂ fold change ≥ 1 were classified as upregulated, whereas genes with log₂ fold change ≤ − 1 were classified as downregulated.

Identification of differentially expressed circadian-related genes (DECRGs)

Oryza sativa subsp. Indica- related circadian gene list was retrieved from the Circadian Gene DataBase (CGDB) (cgdb.biocuckoo.org/browse.php). The CGDB entries contained UniProt/Ensemble Accession and BGI genome identifiers (BGIOSGA IDs), which were harmonized with the gene annotation used in the RNA-seq dataset. Gene identifier harmonization was performed by matching UniProt accessions and gene names with the corresponding rice gene identifiers in the RNA-seq annotation to ensure consistent gene ID formats. The acidic stress-responsive DEGs dataset was compared with the circadian clock gene list from CGDB. The mapping was performed using R, with the dplyr package for organizing data, explicitly utilizing the ‘merge’ function. Only genes with matching identifiers between the DEG dataset and the CGDB circadian gene list were considered as differentially expressed circadian-related genes (DECRGs), while genes with inconsistent annotations or unmatched identifiers were excluded.

Enrichment analysis and identification of transcription factor

Functional annotation of genes was performed using the DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualisation, and Integrated Discovery) (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/) to better understand their biological functions. DEGs were subjected to enrichment analysis based on the Gene Ontology (GO) database to identify significantly enriched biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). An enrichment analysis of Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted, with a cut-off of p < 0.05 for pathways and p < 0.01 for biological processes, respectively. Because GO analysis tests a larger number of functional categories, a more stringent significance threshold (p < 0.01) was applied to reduce false-positive enrichment results, whereas KEGG pathway enrichment used a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The DEG sequences were matched to the rice transcription factor database to identify transcription factors (TFs), and their descriptions were retrieved from the Plant TFDB database (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/).

WGCNA network construction and hub gene identification

Using the WGCNA package in R version 4.3.324, a gene co-expression network was constructed from Oryza sativa expression data. Prior to network construction, rigorous quality control was performed to ensure data reliability. Genes and samples with excessive missing values were initially filtered out with the ‘goodSamplesGenes’ function, and hierarchical clustering together with principal component analysis was used to detect potential outliers. In addition, a variance stabilising transformation was applied, and low-expression genes were filtered to reduce noise in the dataset.

To focus on genes showing significant transcriptional responses to acidic stress and reduce background variation, the network analysis was performed using the set of differentially expressed genes identified from the RNA-seq analysis. The soft-thresholding power (β) was determined using the scale-free topology criterion, and a power of 6 was selected to ensure that the network approximated a scale-free topology. A signed co-expression network was constructed, and genes were grouped into modules using the blockwiseModules function with a minimum module size of 30 genes and a merging threshold (mergeCutHeight) of 0.25. Hierarchical clustering based on the topological overlap matrix (TOM)25 was used to generate a dendrogram for module detection.

Module eigengenes were calculated to summarize the expression profiles of each module, and module–trait relationships were evaluated to identify modules significantly associated with experimental traits, including pH and rice variety. Modules showing strong correlations and significant p-values were selected for further analysis. Within these modules, highly connected genes were identified as hub genes based on their connectivity scores, representing key regulatory candidates potentially involved in acidic stress response pathways.

Protein-protein interaction, hydrophobicity analysis, and AlphaFold-based structure prediction

Hub-gene-encoded proteins were further studied for their interaction and structural analysis using various computational methods. STRING database (version 12.0) (https://string-db.org/) is a comprehensive database of known and predicted protein interactions and was utilized to build a weighted protein-protein interaction (PPI) network in rice. In this network, nodes represent proteins, and edges denote interactions between pairs of proteins. Additionally, each edge is assigned a score to measure the interaction confidence or the possibility that an interaction will occur. The analysis was restricted to Oryza sativa to obtain species-specific interaction networks, and a minimum interaction confidence score of 0.4 (medium confidence) was applied to filter the interactions included in the network. ProtScale (https://web.expasy.org/protscale/) is another tool used for hydrophobicity analysis. Similarly, Protein structural models were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/), which provides structures predicted using the AlphaFold2 deep learning model26. Model confidence was assessed using predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) scores, where > 90 indicates very high confidence, 70–90 indicates high confidence, 50–70 indicates low confidence, and < 50 indicates very low confidence.

Results

Acid-responsive RNA-Seq data assessment

The study analysed gene expression profiles of Oryza sativa under acidic stress conditions using RNA-Seq datasets from the NCBI SRA database (PRJNA746766, Ekta et al. 2024), and all analyses were performed in R using the DESeq2 package. Of 42,031 genes, 2,285 were identified as differentially expressed under acidic stress (Fig. 3). These DEGs were determined through pairwise comparisons, applying a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and a log₂ fold change ≥ 1. Among the 2,285 DEGs, 837 were upregulated, while 1,448 were downregulated across the tested acidic conditions of 4.5 and 6.5.(Fig. 4)

In this study, we identified 37 circadian-related genes that exhibit differential expression among the 2285 DEGs under acidic stress in Oryza sativa (Fig. 5). These genes play a significant role in maintaining the rhythmic regulation of physiological processes. Interestingly, only a small subset of 4 circadian genes responded with upregulation, while the majority, 33 genes, showed a significant downregulation (Table 1). The higher prevalence of downregulated circadian genes under acidic stress suggests that the circadian regulatory network might be repressed under these conditions, potentially as a stress-adaptive mechanism in rice.

GO enrichment-based functional analysis of DEGs and DECRGs

The GO analysis of both 2285 DEGs and 37 DECRGs was performed to identify the specific functions (Fig. 6). The analysis revealed that the “metabolic processes,” “mitochondrial DNA replication,” and “protein complex oligomerization” have a strong involvement in metabolic pathways and mitochondrial function, likely indicative of energy-intensive cellular processes. Also, “response to salt stress” and “response to water deprivation” actively manage abiotic stress conditions. Regarding cellular components, the “chloroplast,” “mitochondrion,” and “plasma membrane” were the most crucial cellular components identified in response to acidity stress, emphasizing their roles in energy metabolism, signaling, and cellular transport. Lastly, the top molecular functions identified were “hydroxylase activity,” “ammonium channel activity,” “heme binding,” and “electron transfer activity,” all of which are vital for managing oxidative stress and maintaining redox homeostasis.

Identification of acidic stress-responsive TF for DEGs and DECRGs

Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression during plants’ responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. These proteins bind to DNA and modulate the transcription of stress-responsive genes, thereby altering the activity of the proteins they encode by either promoting or suppressing their expression. DEGs and DECRGs were mapped with the PlnTFDB database to identify TFs involved in acidic stress responses and revealed 128 TF genes categorized into 29 diverse TF families (Supplementary file) for DEGs and two TF families for DECRGs. The most prominent classes were the MYB-related, NAC, HSF, bZIP, bHLH, and ERF families.

Co-expression network construction of acidic-responsive DEGs

WGCNA, a systems biology technique, was used to find genes among the total DEGs that were highly associated. The analysis classified the DEGs into five modules: turquoise, brown, blue, green, and yellow. The most extensive ‘turquoise’ module contained 908 DEGs, the blue module contained 202 DEGs, the brown module contained 180 DEGs, the yellow module contained 175 DEGs, and the green module contained 78 DEGs. To determine the relationships between the five modules and the two traits, pH and rice varieties, a module-trait relationship graph (Fig. 4a) was plotted. The variety trait represents the two cultivars used in the dataset, JD and KJD. Apart from the turquoise module, all modules are positively correlated with pH. This suggests that the genes within these modules positively regulate the acidic pH tolerance in rice. The corresponding correlation coefficients (r) for the blue, turquoise, brown, yellow, and green modules, and for the pH trait, were 0.57, −0.87, 0.53, 0.78, and 0.4, respectively. The blue, green, brown, yellow, and turquoise modules had respective correlation coefficients (r) of 0.78, −0.76, 0.03, −0.61, and − 0.09 with the rice variety characteristic. These modules were negatively or positively correlated with different pH levels and varieties, and the genes in the corresponding modules were downregulated or upregulated, indicating that the genes respond to acidic stress differently at different pH levels and rice varieties (Fig. 4b). In the module–trait relationship analysis, pH was selected as the key trait. Among them, the turquoise, blue, and brown modules showed the strongest correlations with the pH trait; therefore, turquoise, brown, and blue were selected for further analysis.

Acidic stress-responsive hub genes

Hub genes were identified using WGCNA, with high connectivity within each module, to identify genes that play a key role in the network. A total of fifteen hub genes were identified from the significant co-expression modules. Among these, five representative hub genes, namely thiamine thiazole synthase, lon protease homolog, cysteine proteinase inhibitor, CASP-like protein, and ammonium transporter, were selected for further in-depth analyses, including protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction. These genes were prioritized based on their relatively higher intramodular connectivity and higher module membership (kME) values within the significant modules, functional annotations associated with stress response and metabolic processes, and their presence within modules that showed strong correlations with the pH trait.

The selected hub genes were further analyzed using the STRING database to explore potential protein–protein interactions. However, not all hub genes formed connections within the PPI network. This may be due to the limited availability of experimentally validated interaction data for rice in STRING. Additionally, gene co-expression does not necessarily imply direct protein–protein interaction. However, some of these hub genes had uncharacterized functions and could serve as promising targets for future studies (Table 3).

Functional characterization of acidic stress-responsive hub genes encoded proteins

Hydrophobicity and transmembrane domain-based functional characterization

ProtScale is an essential computational tool that provides over 50 predefined scales for protein analysis, including the Kyte and Doolittle hydrophobicity scale. These amino acid scales assign numerical values to amino acids based on their structure and function. It aids in computing and visualizing protein profiles using these scales to predict putative transmembrane (TM) domains. The Kyte & Doolittle hydrophobicity scale, developed in 198227, is widely used to identify hydrophobic protein regions. In this method, the hydrophobicity profile of a protein is plotted, where the y-axis represents the hydrophobicity values, and the x-axis denotes the amino acid position. A hydrophobicity score above 1.5 indicates a potential transmembrane domain. Identifying transmembrane domains is crucial for understanding a protein’s role in cellular and biological functions. Post-translational modifications (PTMs) often involve membrane-spanning protein domains, typically composed of nonpolar amino acid residues. These domains also contain a single alpha-helix structure, characteristic of transmembrane proteins. The concentration of proteins in the membrane is significantly high in two-dimensional space, particularly in regions where transmembrane domains exist. This concentration can strongly influence protein functions and clustering within the membrane.

Here, the hydrophobicity of five hub genes, namely thiamine thiazole synthase, lon protease homolog, cysteine proteinase inhibitor, CASP-like protein, and ammonium transporter, using the Kyte & Doolittle hydrophobicity scale to predict potential transmembrane domains was analysed (Fig. 7a). The results revealed distinct hydrophobic profiles for each protein. Thiamine thiazole synthase exhibited a predominantly hydrophilic nature, with no significant peaks exceeding the hydrophobicity threshold of 1.5, suggesting its localization in the cytoplasm. Similarly, cysteine proteinase inhibitor displayed a fluctuating hydrophobicity profile, indicating the absence of a transmembrane domain, which aligns with its role as a cytosolic regulatory protein. In contrast, the lon protease homolog showed moderate hydrophobicity, with certain regions approaching the threshold but lacking strong evidence of a TM domain, consistent with its mitochondrial localization. CASP-like protein presented several hydrophobic peaks, suggesting possible transient membrane associations rather than a stable TM structure. Notably, the ammonium transporter exhibited a peak surpassing 1.5, strongly indicating the presence of a transmembrane domain, which aligns with its known function as a membrane-bound transporter. These findings suggest that these proteins’ subcellular localization and functional characteristics, particularly in response to acidic stress, are important. The presence or absence of transmembrane domains influences their structural stability, interactions, and role in cellular processes.

AlphaFold-based protein structure predictions and functional characterization

The structures of five hub genes were predicted using AlphaFold (Fig. 7b), namely thiamine thiazole synthase, lon protease homolog, cysteine proteinase inhibitor, CASP-like protein and ammonium transporter. Thiamine thiazole synthase (THI1) exhibited a predominantly helical structure with a well-defined hydrophobic pocket in the catalytic domain. The low Predicted Aligned Error (PAE) values in this region indicate high confidence in the structural prediction, supporting its role in thiamine biosynthesis, which is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox balance under acidic stress conditions. Similarly, the lon protease homolog displayed a helical ATPase domain, essential for ATP-dependent protein degradation. It has a hydrophobic core in the substrate-binding region and low PAE values, enhancing its role in mitochondrial protein quality control and oxidative stress mitigation. Cysteine proteinase inhibitor (OCI) was predicted to adopt a compact, α-helical fold with a hydrophobic groove that facilitates protease binding and inhibition, effectively regulating proteolytic activity and preventing excessive protein degradation under acidic stress. CASP-like protein featured a well-structured transmembrane domain with defined hydrophobic pockets, suggesting a role in cell membrane stability and cytoskeletal organization, while its low PAE values further validate its function in cell wall remodeling and stress perception. Ammonium transporter (AMT3-1) exhibited a transmembrane helical structure with hydrophobic cavities facilitating ammonium ion transport, and its low PAE values indicate a high-confidence structural prediction, supporting its function in nitrogen uptake and ion homeostasis under acidic stress conditions. These structural insights confirm the functional roles of the hub genes in maintaining metabolic balance, protein stability, cellular integrity, and stress adaptation for enhancing rice tolerance to acidic soils.

Discussion

Soil acidity is a significant abiotic stress that hampers crop growth and productivity worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Due to their acidic nature, this environmental challenge affects approximately 76% of potentially arable soils worldwide9. Aluminium toxicity, a major consequence of soil acidity, disrupts nutrient uptake and photosynthesis, and damages plant roots. To address acidic soil stress, several studies have identified key genes associated with aluminium tolerance in rice, including OsSTAR1, OsSTAR2, OsART1, and transporters such as ALMT, OsNRAT1, OsFRDL4, and OsALS110,13. In addition to aluminium tolerance mechanisms, other genes including AOX1a, GDH1, protein kinase family proteins with leucine-rich repeat domains, and the transcription factor STOP1 (Cys2His2 zinc-finger protein) have been reported to play roles in plant responses to low pH stress (Ekta et al., 2021). These genes are pivotal in mitigating aluminium-induced stress and low pH stress through mechanisms like nutrient transport regulation and transcriptional activation. Moreover, acidic soils impact proton concentrations in plant roots, impairing plasma membrane H+-ATPase activity and ultimately stalling plant growth33.

Plant responses to various abiotic stresses are primarily regulated by distinct pathways directly regulated by the circadian clock. These can interact by activating transcription factors and the circadian core clock genes as key signaling mediators. Thus, it is important to understand the mechanisms involved in plant adaptation to acidic stress. A study by Lager et al.34, investigated the acidic stress response in Arabidopsis thaliana, focusing primarily on genes associated with low-pH stress. These findings were later extended by Ekta et al., (2021, 2024), who identified homologous genes in Oryza sativa and validated their expression under low pH conditions. However, the present study specifically analyzes circadian-related genes and their associated pathways involved in acidic stress response in rice. Using publicly available RNA-Seq data (Ekta et al., 2024), we identified 37 circadian-related genes (CRGs) that were differentially expressed (DECRGs), with 33 downregulated and 4 upregulated under acidic conditions. We then mapped these genes to modules and integrated functional, structural, and regulatory pathways. The downregulation pattern suggests that the circadian regulatory network is suppressed as an adaptive response, likely reallocating resources away from rhythmic processes toward stress mitigation3,6. In this way, plants may temporarily reduce circadian-controlled processes and redirect cellular resources toward stress-responsive pathways. Similar observations have been reported in other studies showing that environmental stresses can alter circadian clock activity to optimize energy use and improve survival under adverse conditions35,36. In this context, the transcription factors and co-expression modules identified in this study may function as key regulatory nodes connecting circadian signaling with stress-response pathways such as metabolic regulation, protein stability, and transport processes. Such coordinated regulation may help plants maintain physiological balance and enhance tolerance to acidic stress conditions37.

Enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed significant associations with abiotic stress responses. The terms ‘response to oxidative stress’, ‘response to salt stress’, and ‘response to water deprivation’ indicate that the gene set is actively involved in managing abiotic stress conditions3,5,38. Additionally, terms like ‘protein folding’ and ‘cellular response to heat’ suggest molecular chaperone activity and protein stabilization mechanisms under stress39,40. Biosynthetic processes such as the ‘brassinosteroid biosynthetic process’ and ‘carotene metabolic process’ further highlight the role of hormone regulation and metabolic adjustments crucial for plant development and stress tolerance41. ‘Chloroplast’, ‘mitochondrion’, ‘plasma membrane’, and ‘extracellular region’ were the cellular components identified in response to acidity stress, emphasizing their roles in energy metabolism, signaling, and cellular transport. Enrichment of components like ‘chloroplast thylakoid membrane’ and ‘photosystem II’ highlights the significance of photosynthesis-related structures, which are tightly regulated by the circadian clock to optimize energy production during stress. The top identified molecular functions were ‘heme binding’, ‘peroxidase activity’, ‘electron transfer activity’, and ‘oxidoreductase activity’, all of which are vital for managing oxidative stress and maintaining redox homeostasis42,43. Other molecular functions like ‘RNA polymerase II cis-regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding’ and ‘DNA-binding transcription factor activity’ suggest that these genes are involved in transcriptional regulation. ‘Chitinase activity’ and ‘beta-amylase activity’ further indicate metabolic adjustments that may assist in energy mobilization during stress. The circadian clock regulates the timing of stress-response pathways, metabolic processes, and photosynthetic activities to synchronize with daily environmental fluctuations3,44. Circadian regulation of photosystem II activity ensures optimal energy production45, while redox homeostasis mechanisms are modulated to protect against oxidative damage during stress43.

Additionally, transcriptional regulation and hormonal biosynthetic processes activate stress-responsive genes at appropriate times, improving plant resilience. Finally, the top three enriched pathways identified through KEGG analysis were ‘biosynthesis of secondary metabolites’, ‘metabolic pathways’, and ‘phenylpropanoid biosynthesis’. These findings suggest that the DEGs participate in various metabolic processes, with significant enrichment observed in pathways responsible for converting primary metabolites into final functional products (Table 4)46.

The WGCNA analyses further elucidated the genetic networks underlying acidic stress responses. The study classified the DEGs into five modules: turquoise, brown, blue, green, and yellow. Among them, turquoise, brown, and blue modules emerged as significant, demonstrating strong associations with pH as a key trait. These modules contained genes involved in stress tolerance, with their expression patterns correlating positively with pH conditions. This indicates that genes within these modules actively contribute to acidic stress adaptation. The study identifies hub genes with high connectivity within the modules, highlighting potential genetic manipulation targets to improve rice stress resilience. Five hub genes were chosen for each module. Among the three modules associated with response to acidic stress, the result indicates that ammonium transporter, cysteine proteinase inhibitor, and CASP-like protein are the top hub genes47.

To understand how genes regulate rice under acidic stress, we identified transcription factors as key regulatory molecules involved in gene transcription, contributing to the plant’s adaptation to diverse environmental conditions48. TFs regulate gene expression during plants’ biotic and abiotic stress responses. These proteins bind to DNA and modulate the transcription of stress-responsive genes, either promoting or suppressing their expression. This analysis revealed 128 TF genes categorized into 29 diverse TF families with prominent classes of MYB-related, HSF, ERF, NAC, bZIP, and bHLH. Also, among 37 DECRGs, we found two transcription factors, namely, bZIP transcription factors and MYB-related transcription factors, suggesting that most circadian genes function through post-translational regulations and signalling pathways rather than direct DNA binding activity. Alternatively, additional TFs may regulate these genes, but were not differentially expressed in our dataset. MYB-related TFs regulate secondary metabolism, cell cycle progression, and stress responses, particularly pathways like flavonoid biosynthesis that provide antioxidant protection49,50. HSFs are essential for maintaining protein homeostasis during stress by activating heat shock proteins (HSPs), which function as molecular chaperones to prevent protein aggregation51. ERFs mediate responses to biotic and abiotic stresses, including drought and salinity, often interacting with other phytohormones, such as jasmonic and abscisic acid52. NAC transcription factors are pivotal for managing abiotic stress and regulating genes involved in water-use efficiency, ion homeostasis, and senescence53. The bZIP family, involved in abscisic acid-mediated signaling, plays a critical role in drought and osmotic stress responses and energy homeostasis54. Meanwhile, bHLH transcription factors contribute to light signaling, stomatal development, and detoxification of reactive oxygen species, often intersecting with other hormonal and stress pathways55,56.

The hydrophobicity analysis of five hub genes revealed diverse localization patterns and functional implications. Thiamine thiazole synthase was predominantly hydrophilic, indicating cytoplasmic localization, while cysteine proteinase inhibitor exhibited fluctuating hydrophobicity, suggesting a lack of transmembrane domains. Lon protease homolog showed moderate hydrophobicity, supporting its mitochondrial association, whereas CASP-like protein displayed multiple hydrophobic peaks, implying transient membrane interactions. In contrast, the ammonium transporter possessed a transmembrane domain, confirming its role as a membrane-bound transporter. Also, AlphaFold was used to predict the structure of these five hub genes. The predicted structures support their roles in maintaining protein stability and cellular integrity. The low PAE values in these proteins indicate high confidence in their structural predictions and their importance in maintaining mitochondrial energy metabolism and redox balance. The STRING database was also used to predict protein-protein interactions among the identified hub genes, highlighting the connections between proteins encoded by distinct genes.

Although this study provides important insights into circadian rhythm–associated regulatory networks under acidic stress in rice, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analyses were primarily based on publicly available transcriptomic datasets and bioinformatic predictions, which may not fully capture the complexity of biological regulation under acidic conditions. Therefore, the identified hub genes and regulatory pathways should be considered as potential candidates rather than definitive regulators. Future experimental validation, including qRT-PCR expression analysis and functional characterization, will be necessary to confirm the roles of these candidate genes in mediating circadian responses to acidic stress.

In addition, the co-expression network analysis was conducted using a relatively small RNA-seq dataset comprising 8 samples. Although rigorous quality control, normalization, and scale-free topology criteria were applied during network construction, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) generally performs more robustly with larger sample sizes. Therefore, the identified modules and hub genes should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using larger transcriptomic datasets will be important for further validating the stability and biological relevance of the co-expression networks.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the molecular interaction between the plant circadian clock and acidic stress responses in Oryza sativa using RNA-Seq-based approaches to investigate differentially expressed genes in rice landraces under acidic stress. RNA-Seq dataset of rice consisting of 42,031 genes to 2285 differentially expressed genes under acidic stress, including 37 circadian-related genes, predominantly downregulated, suggesting suppression of circadian regulatory networks as an adaptive strategy to stress, was identified. GO and KEGG analyses of DEGs emphasized metabolic pathways, oxidative stress management, and hormonal regulation as key biological processes involved in acidic response in rice. Subsequently, the WGCNA, a systems approach, was conducted on DEGs to determine significant gene clusters. The network features of the essential modules were used to identify hub genes. Based on the PPI network construction, the key genes, namely thiamine thiazole synthase (LOC_Os07g34570.1), lon protease homolog, cysteine proteinase inhibitor (LOC_Os01g68670.1), CASP-like protein (LOC_Os12g38100.1), and ammonium transporter (LOC_Os03g62200.1), were identified. The network analysis also highlighted several TFs that could be essential regulators for tolerance to acidic pH in rice. Additionally, ProtScale identified surface-exposed regions and transmembrane regions for the proteins mentioned above. AlphaFold generated a predictive 3D structure and PAE and pLDDT values (shown in different colours in figure legends) that indicated model confidence. The findings of this study may be utilized in developing acidity-tolerant rice cultivars in regions that are affected by soil acidity to ensure food security.
