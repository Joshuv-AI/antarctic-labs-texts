Experimental and numerical study of shear strength on the slide zone soil by the coupling of seepage and shear test
Authors: Qianyun Wang, Huiming Tang, Pengju An, Kun Fang, Sixuan Sun, Jianlin Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe stability of reservoir landslides is determined by the strength of the slide zone. Using both experimental and numerical techniques to analyze the strength of the slide zone has been widely used in the research of reservoir landslides. However, few models consider the strength of the coupling between seepage and shear. This paper aims to analyze the shear strength of slide zone soil combined with different stresses by using the directed shear test and particle flow code PFC2D. The results indicated that slide zone soil clearly experienced the strain-hardening without seepage. While a specific strain-softening phenomenon was observed because the introduction of seepage reduced the shear strength significantly, the internal friction angle demonstrated a higher sensitivity to seepage stress compared to cohesion as the seepage stress increased. The direction and amount of flow velocity dynamically alter during the shear process, as revealed by numerical simulation results, suggesting that the shear strength was nonlinear with increasing seepage stress and changed structure. It can be assumed that seepage deterioration in the Huangtupo landslide is a dynamic process that influences the structure, strength and permeability characteristics.Keywords: Landslide, Slide zone soil, PFC2D, Seepage stress, Nonlinear seepageSubject terms: Natural hazards, Civil engineering

Introduction

The dynamic seepage field of the reservoir water level is a main factor that influences reservoir landslides1–3. Seepage actions are major triggering factors for the deformation and initiation of reservoir landslides, serving as both components of the geological bodies and the external geological environment4,5. For this reason, understanding the strength of the slide zone under seepage impact has a significant effect on the stability of reservoir landslides6,7. Agor8 established a seepage model to reveal that tension or ‘pop-out’ failures occurred due to the slide force caused by seepage exceeding the soil shear strength. Miao9 simulated the reservoir water level by using dry and wet cycles (D-W cycles) methods and revealed the weakening law of the strength of slide zone soils. Further, a weakening model of slip zone soils based on fractal theory during D-W cycles was proposed, which provided an experimental method for the stimulation of reservoir landslides under seepage10. Ma11 conducted a ring shear test with different water contents to stimulate the weak layer soil and found the water contents have notable effects on the shear strengths of the slide zone. Xu12examined the relationship between microstructure characteristics by images processing and structural strength attenuation of soil by the direct shear test under alternate D-W cycles. However, the D-W cycles test or water content cannot clearly simulate the realistic seepage behavior in sliding zone soils due to the lack of sustaining seepage action. Therefore, considering both seepage action and shear behavior when evaluating the strength meets the real hydrogeological conditions.

Numerous studies have been conducted using experimental13,14, numerical simulation15, and analytical methods16–18 to examine the impact of seepage on the shear behavior of sliding zones as a result of the rapid growth of computer technology. For example, Particle flow code (PFC) is a discrete element code that has been used by several researchers to study the shear behavior of sliding zone soil19. Tu20 used the base friction experiment and the PFC simulation to analyze the toppling deformation mechanism of descending masses and evolution process into reservoir landslides. Moreover, Tizpa21 assessed the behavior of shear surface footing by PFC2D by considering the undrained shear strength of the underlying natural clayey deposit and the stiffness of the geogrid. The damage during seepage of no cohesive soils with different particle friction coefficients was numerically simulated by Yua22, demonstrating that the critical hydraulic gradient of no cohesive soil during seepage increases with the raising of particle friction coefficients, and PFC3D results agree well with the experimental results. In conclusion, experimental combined with computational methods to explore the mechanisms of weak sliding surfaces; this approach has been widely used in the field of geotechnical engineering23,24. Nevertheless, the majority of models that are associated with the weak surface are unable to accurately represent the actual geometry of soil under seepage conditions, and most of the intact material consists of angular blocks that cannot break under loading during the test. Hence, monitoring the asperity degradation and propagation of cracks inside the intact sliding zone soil has been inadequately implemented, causing challenges in quantitative representation of the shear strength in the seepage and shear process.

Therefore, this work aims to systematically investigate the seepage and shear combined effect in slide zone soils and reveal the shear behavior under seepage conditions. To examine how seepage affected the shear strength, a direct-shear test with different seepage stresses was carried out in conjunction with a PFC2D numerical simulation of the shear surface change.

Materials and methods

Experimental soils

The soil samples were collected from the Huangtupo landslide, which is one of the largest accumulative reservoir landslides in Badong, Three Georges Reservoir Area (TGRA), China, as shown in Fig. 1(a) and (b). The majority of the silty clay and gravel-filled undisturbed soil comes from the slide zone. Test Apparatus and procedures

The large direct shear system WDJ-300 was used for the shear test, as shown in Fig. 3(a). Water was passed up and down the shear box, and the water inlet was controlled by a constant pressure pump to ensure that a steady seepage stress could be applied through the soil sample. This device works with the water throughout the soil sample and the shear force acting on the shear surfaces. The shear action and seepage action were simultaneously applied to the sample, and the directions are in Fig. 3(b). To keep the seepage stress stable, the rubber film was placed over the two permeable stones and tightly pressed against the specimen, as well as the water inlet and water outflow. To ensure the stability of the samples, it is necessary to provide a significant vertical force, taking into account the weakening of clay strength caused by water softening and the buildup of damage, as well as the sample dimensions16. The soil samples were resized to 125 mm × 125 mm × 45 mm (length × width × height), which necessitates an initial specimen thickness of at least six times the maximum particle diameter and a minimum width-to-thickness ratio of 2. As a result, in the direct shear tests, the seepage stress (SS) of 0 kPa, 100 kPa, and 200 kPa, as well as the vertical stress (VS) of 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa were carried out at a constant shear speed of 0.2 mm/min28 until the shear displacement reached 20 mm. For every shear test, the shear process lasted 100 min. Initially, VS was applied to the specimen to stabilize the fixed value (VS is 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa). Subsequently, the specimen was then subjected to simultaneous application of horizontal shear stress and vertical seepage stress until the desired shear displacement (20 mm) was attained.

PFC2D modeling of slide zone soil sample

The shear behavior of slide soil profiles in this research was simulated with the PFC2D, which is based on DEM, implements the bonded particle model (BPM), and simulates the shear surface using the interaction between an assembly of circular discs29. The numerical direct shear configuration was composed of four walls, as illustrated in Fig. 4(a). During shearing, the servo-control function increased the vertical load on the top wall to the recorded value at a constant velocity of 0.2 m/s. Next, at a speed of 0.02 mm/min, the shear displacements were applied to the left walls. Meanwhile, seepage stress was loaded onto the bottom wall, which was fixed in the Z direction. The simulation ended when the post-peak shear displacement reached a strain rate of 16%. The left and right boundaries are impermeable; the upper boundary has a fixed head of 0 m, corresponding to a water stress of 0 kPa; and the lower boundary has fixed seepage stress working conditions of 0 kPa, 100 kPa, and 200 kPa, as shown in Fig. 3(c).

(a) The boundary condition of numerical modelling. (b) Initial state of the seepage condition.

According to the physical-mechanical characteristics obtained by the laboratory tests, a group of ideal PFC2D mesoscopic parameters was obtained and listed in Table 1. In this study, a linear contact bond model (linear bond), which can imitate ball-to-ball and ball-to-face interactions, was employed to simulate the contact properties of the sliding soil particles. The bonding properties of the material can be simulated using a linear parallel bonding model. The two components of the contact force that the contact model simulates are the tensile force and its torque, which can mimic the properties of the bonding, and the force generated when the particles or clump slip or overlap30.The initial state of the seepage condition is in Fig. 4(b).

Calibration of permeability coefficients

The PFC2D software considers the change in permeability, and the permeability coefficient is related to many factors such as porosity and particle size31. However, when the cracks are generated, the calculation of the permeability coefficient cannot objectively reflect the characteristics of the grid with fissures. To reflect the influence of cracks on the change in seepage field, this paper initially set the permeability coefficient of the whole specimen grid as a fixed value, K. When n cracks appeared in the grid during the shearing process, the permeability coefficient of the grid was assumed to be (n + 1) K. The cycle traversed the cracks, calculated the distance between the centroid of each grid and the cracks, and when a new crack appeared, the minimum value of the distance between the location of this crack and the center of the grid was the basis of judgement. To verify the reliability of the idea, the grid cell test with a square body initially fixed each grid permeability coefficient at 1e− 5 m. After generating a fixed number of cracks and the corresponding position of the grid permeability coefficient of 4e− 5, the simulation results were reasonable.

Under the no-seepage condition in Fig. 6(a), the shear stress gradually increased as the specimen’s shear displacement developed, approaching stabilization. According to the experimental results, the model was in the elastic stage initially as the shear strain increased under the consistent vertical load. A shear surface developed as the shear strain grew and the interparticle connection of soil failed. As the shear strain increased further, a shear surface formed due to the inter-particle bonding failure of the soil. For instance, the larger the vertical stress, the larger the shear displacement required to reach strain hardening, from 7.5 mm to 12 mm to 15 mm, corresponding to vertical forces of 100 kPa, 200 kPa, and 300 kPa, respectively. Generally, the stress-strain curves of the soil sample exhibited stress-hardening characteristics with free seepage, both in experimental and numerical results.

However, there is evidence of the soil softening characteristics exhibited in the stress-strain curves in Fig. 6(b) and (c) under the seepage circumstances, showing three phases with the displacement. Shear stress rose quickly as displacement increased during the initial shear phase. After that, it peaked and started to significantly drop. In the last phase, the stress-strain curve tended to stabilize gradually. Furthermore, compared to a seepage stress of 100 kPa, both the peak strength and residual strength were less needed for additional displacement with a seepage stress of 200 kPa. For instance, with a VP of 300 kPa, the peak strength dropped from 262.58 kPa to 205.41 kPa as the SP rose from 100 kPa to 200 kPa. These variations occurred at 5.78 mm and 5.11 mm, respectively, demonstrating the major effect of seepage stress on the strength change. When numerical simulation was used to verify the results of the shear test, the time to reach the peak strength was greater than the actual shear time, probably due to the fact that the numerical simulation was based on certain simplifying assumptions, which resulted in a different stress-strain relationship between the simulation and the actual situation, but the overall fitting trend was consistent with the results of the shear test.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, integrating the residual and peak strengths with the action of vertical stresses demonstrates that the numerical models’ result was in good agreement with the experimental models. Among them, when the seepage stress increased, the slope of the curve changed, which was no longer parallel under the action of seepage in Fig. 7(a). It indicates that the sepage action on the strength of the slide zone was also nonlinear. Combined with the dynamic change of the shear surface in the shear process, the change of permeability coefficient and the change of structure were nonlinear, which also corresponded to the modelling process of facilitating all dynamic permeability coefficients.

With the increase in seepage stress, the overall displacement and deformation of the specimen were more obvious, and the damage range of the shear surface under the action of seepage was larger, which was reflected in the increase in depth and the obvious edge deformation, such as in Fig. 9(a) and (c), and Fig. 9(b) and (d). When the vertical stress is increased, the overall structure of the specimen is more dense, and the broken depth in the process of shear is increased, especially in the trailing edge of the shear surface, which shows the obvious damage, as shown in Fig. 9 (a) and (b).

In Fig. 10, the seepage field distributions were represented by the magnitude and direction of the water flow velocity. The flow velocity at the shear edges was higher due to the more obvious edge softening phenomenon, resulting in further crack extension. Comparing with Fig. 10(a), the shear damage at the shear surface in Fig. 10(b) was more significant with the increase of VSs, resulting in the fragmentation of particles at the shear surface and the change of pore connectivity. The surge of flow velocity at the broken, larger pores further deepened the effect of water transport on the particles, leading to the more pronounced destruction of the specimen with a higher flow velocity, which was also consistent with the results of smaples ⑤ and ⑥ in Fig. 5.

Variation trend of seepage field under different seepage stresses.

As shown in Fig. 10(a) and (c), the higher the seepage stress under the same vertical stress, the further the specimens’ shear strength was reduced, which was reflected in the creation of more tension cracks. As the seepage stress increased, the connections between the soil particles progressively shifted from a compressed to a looser condition from a mesoscopic perspective. Seepage played an important role in the change of structure. At the same time, the higher the vertical stress, the seepage velocity chain gradually became dense and developed towards the middle of the sample in Fig. 10(d). Additionally, as the seepage stress increased, the more pronounced was the anisotropy of the flow velocity at the shear surface in terms of its direction, and the more significant was the effect of the seepage effect at the shear surface, compared with Fig. 10(b) and (d).

Comparison of shear strength under the seepage effect

In the absence of seepage, the specimens from numerical and experimental results had just peak strength; however, with seepage, both peak strength and residual strength existed in the soil samples. The changes in cohesion (c) in Fig. 11(a) and internal friction angle (φ) in Fig. 11(b) are organized. Compared to the effect of no-seepage, the effect of seepage stress significantly reduced the cohesion, which assumed that the introduction of pore water pressure reduced the effective stress between soil particles, making cohesion lower. Both cohesion and angle of internal friction decreased gradually with increasing seepage stress, which was because the increased seepage stress would also lead to the migration of fine-grained clay particles, leading to structural deterioration and reducing the contact force between clay particles, resulting in lower strength. Further, the internal friction angle demonstrated a higher sensitivity to changes in seepage stress compared to cohesion.

Discussions

The presence of seepage significantly reduces the shear strength, as simplified from point A to point C in Fig. 12. Combined with the changes in the seepage field and particle structure in the numerical simulation, the seepage action destroys the soil structure and reduces the strength of the slide zone soil during the seepage-shear process, simplified as from point B to point C.

The corresponding changes in shear strength.

On the one hand, due to the reduction of effective stress with seepage, seepage action destroys the occlusal force between soil particles and reduces cohesion. On the other hand, the introduction of water reduces the frictional force and interlocking effect between soil particles, which results in the strain softening and reducing the internal friction angle.

In the shear process, the shear strength of the various seepage stresses varies in a non-linear manner, which further reveals the existence of seepage deterioration. Based on the dynamic expression of permeability coefficients in numerical modelling, it is hypothesised that because of the existence of seepage, which makes the change of effective stress within the samples not linear, the seepage field is dynamically changed with the shear behavior, and the seepage effect affects the shear structure and strength to a certain extent.

The Huangtupo landslide is an extremely complex and comprehensive disaster body, and the material composition of the geological slide zone shows anisotropy in spatial distribution32. The seepage channels through which groundwater in various pore structures can pass are different, and the effect on clay particles is also different. The shear effect generated during the landslide movement leads to dynamic changes in the pore structure over time. Additionally, the structure of the pore space influences the change of permeability characteristics and strength, i.e., it can be inferred that the effect of seepage deterioration should be a dynamic process in which the structure-strength-permeability characteristics are influenced by each other.

Conclusions

To further simulate the changes in the seepage field and analyze the effects of seepage on the slide zone structure and strength, this paper investigated the changes in the shear strength of slide zone soils under different seepage stresses.

(1) The slide zone soil demonstrated a clear strain-hardening behavior in the absence of seepage stress; however, a noticeable strain-softening phenomenon was seen in the presence of seepage.

(2) The presence of seepage greatly reduced the shear strength of the slide zone soil. The internal friction angle demonstrated a higher sensitivity to changes in seepage stress compared to cohesion as the seepage stress increased.

(3) The change in strength with increasing seepage stress was nonlinear, and the numerical simulation results demonstrated that the direction and magnitude of flow velocity, along with the soil structure and permeability coefficient, changed dynamically during the shear process.

(4) In fact, the slide zone of the Huangtupo landslide shows anisotropy in spatial distribution. Seepage deterioration should operate as a dynamic process, influencing the characteristics of structure, strength, and permeability.

The rise and fall of reservoir water cause the seepage direction of groundwater in landslides to change periodically. While the test model and numerical model in this paper were simplified to unidirectional seepage conditions, future research could improve the assessment of reservoir landslide stability by examining the strength deterioration pattern of the slide zone under forward and reverse seepage directions.
