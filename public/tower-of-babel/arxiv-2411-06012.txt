Symplectic Hodge Theory on Lie Algebroids
Rankin, Shane

Abstract
. We explore the natural analogues of the Brylinski condition, strong Lefschetz condition, and d ​ δ d\delta -lemma in symplectic geometry originally explored by Brylinski, Mathieu, Yan, and Guillemin to the symplectic Lie algebroid case. The equivalence of the three conditions is re-established as a purely algebraic statement along with a primitive notion of the d ​ δ d\delta -lemma shown establsihed by Tseng, Yau, and Ho. We then show that the natural analogues of these in the Lie algebroid setting holds as well with examples given.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

Hodge theory for symplectic manifolds was introduced by Ehresmann and Liberman and rediscovered by Brylinski
[
3
]
. Brylinski conjectured that on a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
of dimension
2m
, every deRham cohomology class admits a symplectic harmonic representative. Mathieu
[
21
]
and Yan
[
35
]
independently showed that the Brylinski conjecture holds true if and only if
(M^{2m},\omega)
satisfies the strong Lefschetz property, that is for all
0\leq k\leq m
, the map

\displaystyle[L]^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{m-k}(M)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{m+k}(M)

\displaystyle[\alpha]

\displaystyle\mapsto[\omega^{k}\wedge\alpha]

is surjective. This result was then improved by Merkulov in
[
26
]
, and Guillemin in
[
12
]
by showing that these two conditions are equivalent to the symplectic
d\delta
-lemma in the case that
M
was a compact manifold using Poincaré duality. In a key departure from traditional Riemannian Hodge theory, the symplectic Laplacian is no longer an elliptic operator as it vanishes identically, and as such the results that heavily makeup the theory no longer work. Instead, we primarily work with the observation that in the presence of a symplectic form, the space of differential forms on a smooth manifold admits the structure of an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-module. This “symplectic Hodge theory” has been extended to new settings in work by Lin
[
18
]
and others, which require reestablishing many conditions in specific geometric settings. In this paper we work towards streamlining this by proving analogous statements about modules in a relevant module category, and applying them to symplectic Lie algebroids, a minimal smooth setting for the notions involved to make sense. We also generalize the operators
\partial_{+},\partial_{-}
of Tseng and Yau
[
34
]
to this strictly algebraic setting (which we rename
\dee
and
\deebar
respectively), though we do not explore the related Laplacians, or Appelli and Bott-Chern cohomologies corresponding to certain combinations of these operators. We then relate the original trio of equivalences back to
\partial_{+}\partial_{-}
-lemma of
[
13
]
by reestablishing the equivalence of this to the original triple in our algebraic framework rather than the original topological framework considered. Through this generalization we are also able to lift the compactness condition, though it was free to assume in the classical case of the tangent bundle by Poincaré Duality, and standard algebraic topological facts relating to the relevant conditions.

2.1.
The Lie Superalgebra

If
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})

V=\bigoplus_{i=-n}^{n}V_{i},

where

as scaling by

First, we list a number of identities derived from the bracket relations that will be used later.

Proposition 3
.

For any
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, we have the relations:

\displaystyle[f^{k},d]=kf^{k-1}\delta,\quad[e^{k},h]

\displaystyle=-2ke^{k},\quad[e^{k},f]=ke^{k-1}(h+k-1),

\displaystyle[e^{k},\delta]=ke^{k-1}d,\quad

\displaystyle\quad[h,e^{k}]=2ke^{k}.

Proof.

All of these follow by induction, with the base cases coming from the defining relations of
\mathfrak{g}
.
∎

Proposition 4
.

[d\delta,e]=0
.

Proof.

We have that

[d\delta,e]=d(e\delta-d)-ed\delta=de\delta-d^{2}-de\delta=0,

where we use
[d,e]=[d,d]=0
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Every cyclic
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
is finite-dimensional.

Proof.

Let
v
generate
V
as a
U\mathfrak{g}
module, and decompose
v=\sum v_{i}
for
v_{i}\in V_{i}
. It suffices to show that the module generated by such a
v_{i}
is finite-dimensional. By the PBW theorem for Lie Superalgebras, see
[
28
, Theorem 6.1.1]
, it suffices to show that there are finitely many nonzero expressions of the form

e^{\ell}f^{k}h^{r}d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i}.

First, we have that
0\leq s,t<2
by the bracket relations. Next, note that
h(d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i})=(s-t+i)d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i}
, and since
h
has finitely many nonzero eigenvalues, there are finitely many nonzero such expressions. Moving on, we have that

h(f^{k}d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i})=(s-t-2k+i)f^{k}d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i},

and since
0\leq k\leq n
and
h
has finitely many non-zero eigenvalues, again there are only finitely many non-zero expressions of the above form. Finally, we have that

h(e^{\ell}f^{k}d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i})=(2\ell-2k+s-t+i)e^{\ell}f^{k}d^{s}\delta^{t}v_{i},

of which there are finitely many expressions that are non-zero by the same argument as before along with the fact that
0\leq k\leq n
.
∎

Proposition 9

Proposition 10

For primitive

f^{k}e^{k}v=\lambda_{k,i}v,

\lambda_{k,i}=\frac{k!i!}{(i-k)!}.

Proof.

We have that

Continuing, we have that

which is well defined, as if

.

Proposition 11

Let

\displaystyle f^{k}e^{k}(e^{r}v)=\frac{(r+k)!(i-r)!}{r!(i-r-k)!}e^{r}v.

This follows by induction on

We’d like to introduce the notion of the symplectic Hodge star, though the typical method of construction is unavailable. Instead, note that since we have unique primitive decompositions, it suffices to define a star on elements of the form
e^{p}v_{-r}
for
0\leq p\leq r
and
v_{-r}\in V_{-r}\cap\ker(f)

Definition 12
.

Fix
e^{p}v_{-r}
for

and

\star(e^{p}v_{-r})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}e^{r-2p}(e^{p}v_{-r})&\text{if }2p\leq r\\
(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{(r-p)!}{p!}f^{2p-r}(e^{p}v_{-r})&\text{if }2p>r.\end{cases}

Proposition 13
.

\star^{2}=\text{id}
.

Proof.

Suppose
e^{p}v_{-r}
is such that
2p\leq r
. Then

\star(e^{p}v_{-r})=(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}e^{r-p}v_{-r},

from which two cases arise. First, if
r-p=r/2
, then
r=p
and we have that

\star^{2}(e^{p}v_{-r})=(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{0!}{r!}e^{p}v_{-r}=e^{p}v_{-r}.

The other case is when
r-p>r/2
, where using Proposition
11
we have

\displaystyle\star^{2}(e^{p}v_{-r})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}\star(e^{r-p}v_{-r})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}\left((-1)^{\frac{r(r-1)}{2}}\frac{(r-(r-p))!}{(r-p)!}f^{2(r-p)-r}e^{r-p}v_{-r}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}\right)^{2}f^{r-2p}e^{r-2p}e^{p}v_{-r}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p!}{(r-p)!}\right)^{2}\frac{(p+(r-2p))!(r-p)!}{p!(r-p-(r-2p))!}e^{p}v_{-r}

\displaystyle=e^{p}v_{-r},

thus if

Lemma 15

Suppose that

This means for all

which means that
[v]
was the zero class. The only possible point of confusion is why
\pm\star w
is in the kernel of
\delta
, but this again follows from the preceding lemmas.
∎

2.2.
Equivalence of Surjectivity

The following theorem was established independently by Yan and Mathieu:

Theorem 26

(Yan
[
35
]
, Mathieu
[
21
]
)
.

Let
(M^{2n},\omega)
be a symplectic manifold. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The strong Lefschetz map
[\omega^{k}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{n-k}_{dR}(M)\rightarrow H^{n+k}_{dR}(M)
is surjective.

(2)

Every deRham cohomology class has has a (symplectic) harmonic representative.

We can rephrase this purely as an algebraic equivalence: Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, then the following are equivalent:

(1)

For all
0\leq i\leq n
, the map
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
is surjection.

(2)

The inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
induces a surjection in cohomology
H^{i}(\ker(\delta),d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
.

Lemma 27
.

[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
carries harmonic representatives to harmonic representatives.

Proof.

Suppose
[v_{-i}]\in H^{-i}(V,d)
has a harmonic representative
[v_{-i}^{0}]
. We claim that
[e^{i}v_{-i}^{0}]
is a harmonic representative of
[e^{i}v_{-i}]
. First note that they’re cohomologous, as if
v_{-i}=v_{-i}^{0}+d\tau_{-i-1}
, then we have that

e^{i}v_{-i}=e^{i}v_{-i}^{0}+e^{i}d\tau_{-i-1}=e^{i}v_{-i}^{0}+de^{i}\tau_{-i-1}.

Moreover, we have that
e^{i}v_{-i}^{0}
is harmonic since

\delta e^{i}v_{-i}=e^{i}\delta v_{-i}-ie^{i-1}dv_{-i}=0.

∎

The following is essentially a rephrasing of Yan’s original proof
[
35
]
:

Theorem 28

(D. Yan)
.

Suppose
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
induces a surjection in cohomology
H^{i}(\ker(\delta),d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
.

(2)

For all
0\leq i\leq n
, the map
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
is a surjection.

Proof.

First we show that the first condition implies the second. Suppose condition
1
holds, that is the maps
H^{i}(\ker(\delta),d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
are surjections, or equivalently we can say we have surjections
\widehat{V}_{i}\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
. We also know by classical
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-representation theory that
e^{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V_{-i}\rightarrow V_{i}
is a bijection, and this restricts to a bijection on
\widehat{V}_{-i}\rightarrow\widehat{V}_{i}
since the harmonic elements make up a
\mathfrak{g}
-submodule of
V
. Together these give us the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widehat{V}_{-i}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\widehat{V}_{i}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{-i}(V,d)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H^{i}(V,d)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces e^{i}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[e^{i}]}

where the top and vertical arrows are surjections, implying the bottom map is surjective. We now show that the second condition implies the first; towards this suppose that
[e^{i}]
is surjective for all
i
. We’ll show that given any cohomology class
[v]\in H^{\bullet}(V,d)
, we can replace it by a class
[w]
where
w\in\ker(\delta)
.

Step 1:

First, we claim that it suffices to check this on cohomologies in nonpositive degree. To see this, fix
[v_{i}]\in H^{i}(V,d)
for
i>0
. Then as
[e^{i}]
as surjective, we have that
[v_{i}]=[e^{i}][w_{-i}]
for some
[w_{-i}]\in H^{-i}(V,d)
, and we know by Propostion
27
that
[e^{i}]
will carry a harmonic representative of
[w_{-i}]
to a harmonic representative of
[v_{i}]
.

Step 2:

Now, we claim that for
i>0
,
H^{-i}(V,d)
decomposes as

H^{-i}(V,d)=\im([e])+\ker([e^{i+1}]).

Fix a class
[v_{-i}]\in H^{-i}(V,d)
and consider
[e^{i+1}v_{-i}]\in H^{i+2}(V,d)
. Since the
[e^{i}]
are surjective, we have that there is class
[\beta_{-i-2}]\in H^{-i-2}(V,d)
such that
[e^{i+2}\beta_{-i-2}]=[e^{i+1}v_{-i}]
. Now, we can (trivially) write

[v_{-i}]=([v_{-i}-e\beta_{-i-2}])+[e]([\beta_{-i-2}]).

It’s clear that the second summand is in the image of
[e]
, we need only to check that the first is in the kernel of
[e^{i+1}]
. This follows from the definition as

[e^{i+1}]([v_{-i}-e\beta_{-i-2}])=[e^{i+1}v_{-i}-e^{i+2}\beta_{-i-2}]=[e^{i+1}v_{-i}-e^{i+1}v_{-i}]=[0].

Step 3:

We now proceed to prove the main claim by induction. First, note that every class in
H^{-n}(V,d)
and
H^{-n+1}(V,d)
is harmonic. If
[v_{-n}]\in H^{-n}(V,d)
, then
\delta v_{-n}\equiv 0
trivially, and so
v_{-n}
is harmonic. If
[v_{-n+1}]\in H^{-n+1}(V,d)
, then we have that

\delta v_{-n+1}=[d,f]v_{-n+1}=dfv_{-n+1}-fdv_{-n+1}=0

since
fv_{-n+1}\in V_{-n-1}\equiv 0
, and
v_{-n+1}
is closed. So we only need to check on
-n+2\leq i\leq 0
, and the
-n+1
degree serves as the base case. Now, suppose the statement holds for
H^{-i-1}(V,d)
and fix
[\beta_{-i}]\in H^{-i}(V,d)
. By the above decomposition we have that

[\beta_{-i}]=[\alpha_{-i}]+[e]([\beta_{-i-2}]),

for
[\beta_{-i-2}]\in H^{-i-2}(V,d)
and
[\alpha_{-i}]\in H^{-i}(V,d)\cap\ker([e^{i+1}])
. By inductive hypothesis,
[\beta_{-i-2}]
possesses a harmonic representative, and
[e]
carries harmonic representatives into harmonic representatives, so we can assume
[e]([\beta_{-i-2}])
is harmonic. We only need to show that
[\alpha_{-i}]
admits a harmonic representative. By definition of
[\alpha_{-i}]
, we have that
d\alpha_{-i}=0
and
e^{i+1}\alpha_{-i}=d\beta_{i+1}
for some
\beta_{i+1}\in V_{i+1}
. Now, by the bijectivity of
e^{i+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}V_{-i-1}\rightarrow V_{i+1}
, there exists
\gamma_{-i-1}\in V_{-i-1}
such that
\beta_{i+1}=e^{i+1}\gamma_{-i-1}
. We claim that
\alpha_{-i}-d\gamma_{-i-1}
is a harmonic representative of
[\alpha_{-i}]
. It’s clear that
[\alpha_{-i}]=[\alpha_{-i}-d\gamma_{-i-1}]
, and that
\alpha_{-i}-d\gamma_{-i-1}\in\ker(d)
, we only need to show that it’s in
\ker(\delta)
. Since
\delta=[f,d]
, this reduces to showing that
\alpha_{-i}-d\gamma_{-i-1}\in\ker(f)
, i.e. is primitive. Primitivity here is equivalent to showing that
\alpha_{-i}-d\gamma_{-i-1}\in\ker(e^{i+1})
. Computing we have that

\displaystyle e^{i+1}(\alpha_{-i}-d\gamma_{-i-1})=e^{i+1}\alpha_{-i}-d(e^{i+1}\gamma_{-i-1})=e^{i+1}\alpha_{-i}-d\beta_{i+1}=e^{i+1}\alpha_{-i}-e^{i+1}\alpha_{-i}=0.

∎

2.3.
Equivalence of Triples

In the previous subsection we’ve shown that the following two statements are equivalent for
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
:

(1)

The maps
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
are surjections.

(2)

The inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
induces a surjection in cohomology.

which in the original paper(s), Mathieu claims is equivalent to a third condition: The
d\delta
-lemma.

Definition 29
.

The
d\delta
-lemma holds for
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
if the following equalities hold:

\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)=\im(\delta)\cap\ker(d)=\im(d\delta).

This is equivalent to the above two if we replace surjection with isomorphism in both claims; in the original setting of the tangent bundle of a compact manifold, these are equivalent by Poincaré duality. The first of the equivalences - that
d\delta
-lemma is equivalent to the inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
follows from the following:

Theorem 30
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
. Then
the
d\delta
-lemma is equivalent to the inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\rightarrow(V,d)
inducing an isomorphism in cohomology.

Proof.

See
[
19
, Lemma 5.4.1]
.
∎

Proposition 31
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, and suppose that the
d\delta
-lemma is satisfied. Then
[e^{k}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-k}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{k}(V,d)
is an isomorphism for all
k
.

Proof.

Since
d\delta
-lemma implies that there are harmonic representatives, we have that that
[e^{k}]
is surjective and we may reduce the problem to examining when the map
[e^{k}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-k}(\ker(\delta),d)\rightarrow H^{k}(\ker(\delta),d)
is an isomorphism. Suppose that
[\alpha_{-k}]\in\ker([e^{k}])
, so that
e^{k}\alpha_{-k}=d\tau_{k-1}
for some
\tau_{k-1}\in V_{k-1}\cap\ker(\delta)
. Then, note that

\delta(e^{k}(\alpha_{-k}))=\delta d\tau_{k-1}=-d\delta\tau_{k-1}=0,

thus
e^{k}\alpha_{-k}\in\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)
. By the
d\delta
-lemma, we then have that there is a
\rho_{k}
such that

e^{k}\alpha_{-k}=d\delta\rho_{k}.

Since
e^{k}
is a bijection for all
k
, note that there exists
\rho_{-k}
such that
e^{k}\rho_{-k}=\rho_{k}
. Using this along with Proposition
4
, we then have that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=e^{k}\alpha_{-k}-d\delta\rho_{k}

\displaystyle=e^{k}\alpha_{-k}-d\delta e^{k}\rho_{-k}

\displaystyle=e^{k}(\alpha_{-k}-d\delta\rho_{-k}),

thus by injectivity of
e^{k}
we have that
\alpha_{-k}=d\delta\rho_{-k}
.
∎

Proposition 32
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
. If
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
is an isomorphism for all
i
, then
\ker(d)\cap\im(\delta)=\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)
.

Proof.

Unfolding the definitions reveals one must check that
d\delta v=0
implies that
\delta v=d\phi
for some
\phi
. First, we claim that it suffices to check this on primitive elements. Towards this, suppose that the statement holds on primitive elements, and fix arbitrary
v\in V
. We can decompose
v
into primitive elements, i.e.
v=\sum e^{i}v_{i}
where the
v_{i}
are primitive. Now, since
d\delta v=0
, we have that

0=d\delta v=\sum d\delta e^{i}v_{i}=\sum e^{i}d\delta v_{i},

where we repeatedly use Proposition
4
. Since this sum is direct, this forces
e^{i}d\delta v_{i}=0
for all
i
. Since
e_{i}
is acting on the negative weights in this sum, it acts injectively, so
d\delta v_{i}=0
for all
i
. By hypothesis, we have then that there exist
\phi_{i}
such that
\delta v_{i}=d\phi_{i}
, and thus

\displaystyle\delta v

\displaystyle=\sum\delta e^{i}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum e^{i}\delta v_{i}-ie^{i-1}dv_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum e^{i}d\phi_{i}-ie^{i-1}dv_{i}

\displaystyle=d\left(\sum e^{i}\phi_{i}-ie^{i-1}v_{i}\right).

Now, to see that this holds on primitive form we proceed as follows. Suppose
v_{-i}\in V_{-i}
is primitive and
d\delta v_{-i}=0
. Then we have that

e^{i+1}\delta v_{-i}=\delta e^{i+1}v_{-i}-(i+1)e^{i}dv_{-i}.

However,
e^{i+1}v_{-i}=0
since
v_{-i}
is primitive, and we’re left with

e^{i+1}\delta v_{-i}=d(-(i+1)e^{i}v_{-i}).

Rephrased this says that
[\delta v_{-i}]\in\ker[e^{i+1}]
, which by the strong Lefschetz property is an isomorphism, meaning that
[\delta v_{-i}]
is the zero class in cohomology, so
\delta v_{-i}=d\phi
for some
\phi\in V_{-i-2}
. The only degree where this argument does not hold is
V_{-n}
, but for any
v\in V_{-n}
,
\delta v\equiv 0
, so it’s trivially in the image of
d
, i.e.
\delta v=0=d(0)
.
∎

Proposition 33
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, and suppose that
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
is an isomorphism for all
i
. Then

\ker(d)\cap\im(\delta)=\ker(\delta)\cap\im(d)
.

Proof.

From the preceding proposition, we have that
\ker(d)\cap\im(\delta)=\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)\subset\ker(\delta)\cap\im(d)
, all that remains to be shown is that
\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)\subset\im(\delta)\cap\ker(d)
. Fix
\alpha\in\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)
, that is
\alpha=d\beta
and
\delta\alpha=0
. Then we have

0=\delta\alpha=\pm\star d\star\alpha.

Since
\star
is an isomorphism, we have that
d\star\alpha=0
,i.e.
\star\alpha\in\ker(d)
. Now using exactness we have

\star\alpha=\star d\beta=\pm\star d\star\star\beta=\pm\delta\star\beta,

thus
\star\alpha\in\ker(d)\cap\im(\delta)
. By the preceding proposition yet again we have then that
\star\alpha\in\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)
, and so
\star\alpha=d\rho
. Using this we then have that

\alpha=\star\star\alpha=\star d\rho=\star d\star\star\rho=\delta(\pm\star\rho)

giving us
\alpha\in\im(\delta)
.
∎

Proposition 34
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, and suppose that
[e]^{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
is surjective for all
i
. Then
\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)=\im(d\delta)
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. First, note that the statement trivially holds elements in
V_{n}
or
V_{-n}
since if
v\in\im(\delta)\cap V_{n}
, then
v=\delta 0=0
, and likewise for
V_{-n}
. Our base case is on
V_{n-1}
, so choose
v_{n-1}\in V_{n-1}
, and let

v_{n-1}=d\gamma_{n-2}=\delta w_{n}.

Since
dw_{n}
is trivially zero it represents a cohomology class, and so we can choose a harmonic representative of said class giving us
w_{n}=w_{n}^{0}-d\tau_{n-1}
where
w_{n}^{0}
is harmonic. We then have that

\displaystyle v_{n-1}=\delta w_{n}=\delta(w_{n}^{0}-d\tau_{n-1})=-\delta d\tau_{n-1}=d\delta\tau_{n-1}.

Now, suppose this holds for
V_{k}
for some
-n<k<n
, and fix
v_{k-1}\in V_{k-1}\cap\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)
. By definition we have that

v_{k-1}=d\gamma_{k-2}=\delta\beta_{k}.

From this, define
\xi_{k+1}=d\beta_{k}
, and observe that

\delta\xi_{k+1}=\delta d\beta_{k}=-d(\delta\beta_{k})=-d^{2}\gamma_{k-2}=0,

so
\xi_{k+1}\in\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)
. We have that
\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)=\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)
, which by inductive hypothesis extends to
\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)=\im(d)\cap\im(\delta)=\im(d\delta)
, so
\xi_{k+1}=d\delta\phi_{k+1}
. Note that
d(\beta_{k}-\delta\phi_{k+1})=0
, so it admits a harmonic representative, i.e.
\beta_{k}-\delta\phi_{k+1}=\beta_{k}^{0}-d\tau_{k}
for some
\tau_{k}\in V_{k}
. Applying the definition of
v_{k-1}
, we have

\displaystyle v_{k-1}=\delta\beta_{k}=\delta(\beta_{k}^{0}-d\tau_{k}+\delta\phi_{k+1})=-\delta d\tau_{k}=d\delta\tau_{k}.

∎

At this point, we have enough to establish the equivalence of the triple:
d\delta
-lemma, strong Lefschetz property, and Brylinski property. We’ve already proven all the of the relevant pieces, but for clarity state them here in one place.

Theorem 35
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
. The the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

The
d\delta
-lemma holds for
V
.

(2)

The map
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
is an isomorphism for all
i
.

(3)

The inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
is a quasi-isomorphism.

Proof.

We have that the first and third conditions are equivalent by Theorem
30
. Then we have that the first implies the second by Proposition
31
, and the reverse direction follows from the Proposition
32
,
33
, and
34
.
∎

In light of this theorem, we make the following definition:

Definition 36
.

If
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
satisfies any of the above equivalent conditions in Theorem
35
we call
V
a “Lefschetz Module”.

Corollary 37
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
be a Lefschetz module. Then
H^{*}(V,d)
is an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
-module.

Proof.

From the
\mathfrak{g}
relations we have that
e
and
h
always pass to cohomology, i.e.
H^{*}(V,d)
is always a
B
-module, independent of whether or not
V
is Lefschetz. To define the action of
f
, fix
[v]\in H^{*}(V,d)
, and let
\hat{v}
be a harmonic representative of this class, which always exists as
V
is Lefschetz. We can then define

f[v]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[f(\hat{v})].

To see this is well-defined, suppose that
\hat{w}
is another harmonic representative of the class, that is
\hat{w}=\hat{v}+d\phi
. Then we have that

f(\hat{w})=f(\hat{v})+fd\phi=f(\hat{v})+df\phi+\delta\phi,

where we use that
[f,d]=\delta
. Note that

d\delta\phi=-\delta d\phi=-\delta(\hat{w}-\hat{v})=0

by harmonicity, and so
\delta\phi\in\im(\delta)\cap\ker(d)
. Again using that
V
is Lefschetz, the
d\delta
-lemma implies that
\delta\phi=d\delta\varphi
and so we have that

f(\hat{w})=f(\hat{v})+d(f\phi+\delta\varphi).

∎

2.4.
Weakly Lefschetz Modules

There are examples on manifolds where the Lefschetz condition is only partially met, i.e. the maps
[L]^{k}
are isomorphisms for some
k
, but not all
0\leq k\leq n
.

Definition 38
.

Fix
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
,
0\leq s\leq n
, and consider the ordered set

\{[e^{n}],[e^{n-1}],\dots[e^{1}]\},

where
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
. We say that
V
is “
s
-Lefschetz” if the first
s
maps in this set are isomorphisms. In particular,
n
-Lefschetz is Lefschetz.

The same theorems from before hold in this setting as well, but only on the portions on the modules for which the maps
e^{k}
are isomorphisms.

Theorem 39
.

Suppose
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
and fix
0\leq s\leq n
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
induces a surjection in cohomology
H^{i}(\ker(\delta),d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
in degrees
i\in\{-n,\dots-s\}\cup\{s,\dots,n\}
.

(2)

The maps
[e^{i}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{-i}(V,d)\rightarrow H^{i}(V,d)
for
i\in\{n,\dots,s\}
are surjections.

Proof.

The forward direction follows in the same exact way it does in the proof of Theorem
28
. The reverse direction followed by induction that crucially used the fact that we could decompose
H^{-i}(V,d)
for
i>0
. This decomposition was the only part of the proof that relied on the reverse direction, and the rest of the proof holds if we only assume that the
[e^{i}]
are surjective from
V_{-n}
up to
V_{-s}
.
∎

The upgraded equivalence also holds under the added assumption that
e^{i}
is in fact an isomorphism.

Definition 40
.

Given a module
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
and
1\leq s\leq n
, we say that
V
has the “
d\delta
-lemma up to degree
s
” if

\displaystyle\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)

\displaystyle=\im(d\delta)=\im(\delta)\cap\ker(\delta)

on

\displaystyle V_{i}\text{ for }-n\leq i\leq-s

\displaystyle\im(d)\cap\ker(\delta)

\displaystyle=\im(d\delta)

on

\displaystyle V_{-s+1}.

Remark 41
.

Note that by
\star
duality if this equality holds on
V_{i}
for
-n\leq i\leq-s
it then also holds in
V_{i}
for
s\leq i\leq n
. From this observation we’ll only prove statements about it in non-positive degree, as the duality will extend the results to positive degrees.

Theorem 42
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, and fix
1\leq s\leq n
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The
d\delta
-lemma holds for
V
up to degree
s
.

(2)

V
is
s
-Lefschetz

(3)

The inclusion
(\ker(\delta),d)\hookrightarrow(V,d)
is a quasi-isomorphism in degrees
i\in\{-n,\dots,-s\}\cup\{s,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

The proof follows similarly to the original. The first and third condition are equivalent by
[
19
]
, whose argument works in each degree. The first implies the second using Proposition
31
and acknowledging the proof is symmetric in it’s degree arguments about the zero weight. Finally, to see that the second condition implies the first, note that proposition
32
and proposition
33
are degree agnostic, and proposition
34
is done by induction, which we can once again stop early when the hypothesis fail in degree
-s
and achieve the result.
∎

2.5.
Primitive Equivalence

Much of this section is rephrasing of work in Chung-I Ho’s thesis
[
13
]
and Tseng’s paper
[
34
]
.

Definition 43
.

Suppose
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
with decomposition
\bigoplus_{k}V_{k}
. Then the space of primitive elements of degree
k
will be denoted
\mathcal{P}_{k}=\ker(f)\cap V_{k}
.

Throughout the rest of this subsection, fix
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
, so that we may speak of elements
v_{i}\in\Prim_{i}
freely. Recall that for any
v_{i}\in\Prim_{i}
, we have that

dv_{i}=\dee v_{i}+e\deebar v_{i}.

These extend to the entire module by taking primitive decomposition of any element
v\in V
, i.e. if
v=\sum v_{i}
for
v_{i}\in V_{i}
, and
v_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}v_{i-2k}
then we have that

\displaystyle\dee v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}\dee v_{i-2k},

\displaystyle\deebar v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}\deebar v_{i-2k}.

Lemma 44
.

We have that
[\dee,e]=[\deebar,e]=[\dee,\dee]=[\deebar,\deebar]=0
where all are graded commutators, as well as

e(\dee\deebar)=-e(\deebar\dee)

on
V_{n-1},V_{n}
and
[\dee,\deebar]=0
on
V_{k}
for
k<n-1
.

Proof.

This follows the same as it did in
[
34
, Lemma 2.5]
.
∎

Proposition 45
.

For
v_{i}\in\Prim_{i}
, we have that
f\dee v_{i}=0
.

Proof.

Since
v_{i}
is primitive, we have that
[f,\dee]v_{i}=f\dee v_{i}
. By the Jacobi identity we have that

[f,\dee]=\frac{1}{2}[h,[\dee,f]]+[f,\dee],

thus we have to have that
h
and
[\dee,f]
commute. Checking this on
v_{i}
gives us that

(i-1)[\dee,f]v_{i}=i[\dee,f]v_{i},

forcing
[f,\dee]v_{i}=0
.
∎

Proposition 46
.

For
v_{i}\in\Prim_{i}

\delta v_{i}=(1-i)\deebar v_{i}
.

Proof.

Using the definition of
\delta
we have

\delta v_{i}=fdv_{i}-dfv_{i}=fdv_{i}=f\dee v_{i}+fe\deebar v_{i}.

We know the first summand vanishes by the preceding lemma, and on the second we have that

fe\deebar v_{i}=(ef-h)\deebar v_{i}=-h\deebar v_{i}=-(i-1)\deebar v_{i}.

∎

Lemma 47
.

On
\Prim_{i}
, we have that
d\delta=(1-i)\dee\deebar
.

Proof.

Using the preceding lemmas we have

d\delta v_{i}=(i-1)d\deebar v_{i}=(i-1)(\dee+e\deebar)\deebar v_{i}=(1-i)\dee\deebar v_{i}.

∎

Definition 48
.

We say that
v\in V
is “symphonic” if
\dee v=\deebar v=0
.

Remark 49
.

This was previously called “primitive harmonic” in the literature, see
[
13
]
, however to avoid the phrase “harmonic primitive harmonic” we introduce this terminology.

Lemma 50
.

v\in V
is harmonic if and only if each term of it’s primitive decomposition is symphonic.

Proof.

Let
v=v_{i}
with
v_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}w_{i-2k}
for
w_{i-2k}\in\Prim_{i-2k}
. Then if each
w_{i-2k}
is symphonic, since
d=\dee+e\deebar
it’s clear that
v
is closed. To see
v
is coclosed, observe

\displaystyle\delta v

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{k\geq 0}\delta e^{k}w_{i-2k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{k\geq 0}(e^{k}\delta-ke^{k-1}d)w_{i-2k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}(1-i)\deebar w_{i-2k}

\displaystyle=0.

Now, suppose that
v=\sum_{i}v_{i}
for
v_{i}\in V_{i}
is harmonic. This occurs if and only if each
v_{i}
is harmonic, which occurs if and only if the term in the primitive decomposition of each
v_{i}
is harmonic, so it suffices to show this for a primitive harmonic element. Towards this, suppose
v\in\widehat{\Prim_{i}}
. Then

0=\delta v=(i-1)\deebar v,

so
v\in\ker(\deebar)
. Moreover we have

0=dv=\dee v+e\deebar v=\dee v,

thus
v\in\ker(\dee)
.
∎

Definition 51
.

Let
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
. We say that
V
satisfies the
\dee\deebar
-lemma if the following equalities hold

\displaystyle\im(\dee)\cap\ker(\deebar)\cap\Prim_{k}

\displaystyle=\im(\dee\deebar)\cap\Prim_{k}\qquad-n+1\leq k\leq-1,

\displaystyle\im(\dee)\cap\ker(\deebar)\cap\Prim_{-n}

\displaystyle=\im(\dee\deebar)\cap\Prim_{-n},

\displaystyle\im(\deebar)\cap\ker(\dee)\cap\Prim_{0}

\displaystyle=\im(\dee\deebar)\cap\Prim_{0}.

Lemma 52
.

The
d\delta
-lemma holds for
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
if the
\dee\deebar
-lemma does.

Proof.

It suffices to check this on each weight, so fix
v_{i}\in\widehat{V_{i}}
and first suppose that
v_{i}\in\im d
so that
v_{i}=dw_{i-1}
. Then taking primitive decompositions of both we have

v_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}v_{i-2k},\qquad w_{i-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}w_{i-1-2k}.

Combining this with the fact that
v_{i}=dw_{i-1}
, we have

\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}(v_{i-2k}-dw_{i-1-2k})=0,

forcing
v_{i-2k}=dw_{i-1-2k}=\dee w_{i-1-2k}+e\deebar w_{i-1-2k}
for all
k
. Since
v_{i}
is harmonic, each
v_{i-2k}
is symphonic, so we must have that
\dee w_{i-1-2k}\in\im\dee\cap\ker\deebar
and
\deebar w_{i-1-2k}\in\im\deebar\cap\ker\dee
. By the
\dee\deebar
-lemma then we must have that there are
\gamma_{i-2k},\rho_{i-2-2k}\in\im(\dee\deebar)
(possibly zero) such that

\dee w_{i-1-2k}=\dee\deebar\gamma_{i-2k},\qquad\deebar w_{i-1-2k}=\dee\deebar\rho_{i-2-2k}.

From here, we have that

\displaystyle v_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}(\dee w_{i-1-2k}+e\deebar w_{i-1-2k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}(\dee\deebar\gamma_{i-2k}+e\dee\deebar\rho_{i-2(1+k)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}\left(d\delta\frac{\gamma_{i-2k}}{1-(i-2k)}+ed\delta\frac{\rho_{i-2(1+k)}}{1-i+2(1+k)}\right)

\displaystyle=d\delta\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}\left(\frac{\gamma_{i-2k}}{1-(i-2k)}+e\frac{\rho_{i-2(1+k)}}{1-i+2(1+k)}\right)

where we repeatedly use the
\mathfrak{g}
relations, and make note that the constants that appear in the denominator are non-vanishing. The only possible point of confusion is why the
\gamma_{i-2k}
and
\rho_{i-2(1+k)}
are primitive; if they’re not taking a primitive decomposition of them once again allows the proof to go through. A similar argument holds for the case when
v\in\im(\delta)
.
∎

Lemma 53
.

The
\dee\deebar
-lemma holds for
V\in\mathrm{Ob}(\mathscr{C}_{n})
if the
d\delta
-lemma does.

Proof.

Suppose that
v_{i}\in\ker(\dee)\cap\ker(\deebar)\cap\Prim_{i}
and recall that this forces
v_{i}
to be harmonic and each of it’s primitive decomposition terms to be symphonic. First, suppose that
v_{i}\in\im(\deebar)
, i.e.
v_{i}=\deebar\beta_{i+1}
. Taking primitive decompositions, we have

v_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}v_{i-2k},\qquad\beta_{i+1}=\sum e^{k}\beta_{i+1-2k}.

Using coexactness, we have must have that

v_{i-2k}=\deebar\beta_{i+1-2k}=\delta\left(\frac{\beta_{i+1-2k}}{-i+2k}\right)

so
v_{i-2k}\in\im(\delta)\cap\ker(d)=\im(d\delta)
. Thus there exists
\rho_{i-2k}
such that

v_{i-2k}=d\delta\rho_{i-2k}=\dee\deebar(1-i+2k)\rho_{i-2k},

giving us that

v_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}v_{i-2k}=\dee\deebar\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}((1-i+2k)\rho_{i-2k}).

Again it may be unclear as to why the
\rho_{i-2k}
are primitive, but this can be chosen to happen as before. Now, suppose that
v_{i}\in\im(\dee)
so
v_{i}=\dee\beta_{i-1}
for some
\beta_{i-1}
. Equating primitive decompositions we have that
v_{i-2k}=\dee\beta_{i-1-2k}
, where

v_{i}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}v_{i-2k},\qquad\beta_{i-1}=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}\beta_{i-1-2k}.

Since
v_{i}
is symphonic, we have that
\deebar\dee\beta_{i-1-2k}=0
for all
k
, and so

\displaystyle d\delta\beta_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\sum d\delta e^{k}\beta_{i-1-2k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}d\delta\beta_{i-1-2k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2(1-k)-i}e^{k}\dee\deebar\beta_{i-1-2k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{-1}{2(1-k)-i}e^{k}\deebar\dee\beta_{i-1-2k}

\displaystyle=0.

Since
d\beta_{i-1}\in\ker(\delta)\cap\im(d)
, the
d\delta
-lemma then we have that there exist
\gamma_{i}
such that
d\beta_{i-1}=d\delta\gamma_{i}
. Taking a primitive decomposition of
\gamma_{i}
we have that

\displaystyle d\delta\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}e^{k}d\delta\gamma_{i-2k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}(1-i+2k)e^{k}\dee\deebar\gamma_{i-2k}

\displaystyle=\deebar\dee\sum_{k\geq 0}(i-1-2k)e^{k}\gamma_{i-2k},

showing that

v_{i}=\dee\beta_{i-1}-e\deebar\beta_{i-1}=\deebar\left(\dee\sum_{k\geq 0}(i-1-2k)e^{k}\gamma_{i-2k}-e\beta_{i-1}\right)\in\im(\deebar),

from which the result follows from the previous case.
∎

(2)

3.
Lefschetz Algebroids

3.1.
Symplectic Lie Algebroids

Lie algebroids were originally introduced by Pradines
[
32
]
as a generalization of the tangent bundle of a manifold to the Lie Groupoid setting.

Definition 55
.

A “Lie algebroid”
(\mathcal{A},\rho,M)
over a
C^{\infty}
-manifold
M
is a vector bundle
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
, equipped with an “anchor”
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow TM
, and a bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathcal{A}}
on sections of
\mathcal{A}
that is bilinear, alternating, and satisfies the Jacobi identity, such that

(1)

[X,fY]_{\mathcal{A}}=f[X,Y]_{\mathcal{A}}+(\rho(X)\cdot f)Y
for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
and
f\in C^{\infty}(M)
.

(2)

\rho([X,Y]_{\mathcal{A}})=[\rho(X),\rho(Y)]_{TM}
for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
.

Definition 56
.

Given a Lie algebroid
(\mathcal{A},\rho,M)
, we can define the “deRham-Chevalley-Eilenberg complex” of
\mathcal{A}
, where our cochain complex is made of

\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)=\Gamma(\Lambda^{k}\mathcal{A}^{*}),

and we define

\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)=\bigoplus_{k}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M).

This comes with an
\mathcal{A}
-differential,
d_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{k+1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
, defined as

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{A}}\eta(X_{1},...,X_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}(-1)^{i+1}\rho(X_{i})\cdot\eta(X_{1},...,\widehat{X_{i}},...,X_{k+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\eta([X_{i},X_{j}],X_{1},...,\widehat{X_{i}},...,\widehat{X_{j}},...,X_{k+1})

for
X_{i}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
and
\eta\in\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
.

A standard computation shows that
d_{\mathcal{A}}^{2}=0
, and as such we can consider the cohomology of this complex:

Definition 57
.

Let
(A,\rho,M)
be a Lie algebroid. The “Lie algebroid cohomology” of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted
H^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
is the cohomology of the complex

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega^{k-1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Omega^{k+1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d_{\mathcal{A}}}

that is,

H_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M)=\frac{\ker\left(\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\xrightarrow{d_{\mathcal{A}}}\Omega^{k+1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\right)}{\im\left(\Omega^{k-1}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\xrightarrow{d_{\mathcal{A}}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\right)}

which we can rephrase by using
\mathcal{A}
-closed and exact terminology

H_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M)=\frac{d_{\mathcal{A}}\text{-closed forms}}{d_{\mathcal{A}}\text{-exact forms}}.

The standard Cartan Calculus from the Tangent Bundle also extends to Lie algebroids, however we’ll only use the notion of the exterior and interior products.

Definition 58
.

Given
X\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
, we define “
\mathcal{A}
-interior multiplication” with
X
, or “contraction by
X
”, denoted
\iota_{X}
, as the operator
\iota_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{k-1}_{\mathcal{A}}(X)
such that

\iota_{X}\eta(X_{1},...,X_{k-1})=\eta(X,X_{1},...,X_{k-1}),

where
X_{i}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
and
\eta\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M)
.

We now arrive at the central object of the result, a symplectic Lie algebroid.

Definition 59
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\rho,M)
be a Lie algebroid. An “
\mathcal{A}
-symplectic form”
\omega
is a
d_{\mathcal{A}}
-closed, nondegenerate element of
\Omega^{2}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
.

Definition 60
.

A “symplectic Lie algebroid” is a Lie algebroid
\mathcal{A}
equipped with a choice of
\mathcal{A}
-symplectic form.

The presence of an
\mathcal{A}
-symplectic form immediately places restrictions on the underlying vector bundle:

Corollary 61
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M,\rho,\omega)
be a symplectic Lie algebroid. Then the rank of
\mathcal{A}
is even and
\mathcal{A}
is orientable.

There are other obstructions as well, see
[
15
]
. Symplectic Lie algebroids were originally introduced by Nest and Tsygan
[
29
]
while investigating extensions of Fedesov Deformation Quantization, and much work has been done on them in recent years by may authors. As in classical symplectic geometry, symplectic Lie algebroids arise naturally as the “phase space” of any Lie algebroid as originally uncovered by Martínez
[
20
]
.

Proposition 62

(Martinez)
.

Let
B\rightarrow N
be an arbitrary Lie algebroid, and
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B^{*}\rightarrow N
be the projection. Define the bundle

\mathcal{A}=\pi^{!}B=TB^{*}\times_{TN}B

which is a Lie algebroid over
B^{*}
. Then
\omega=-d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha

is a canonical symplectic structure on
B^{*}
, where
\alpha\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}(B^{*})
is defined as follows after identifying elements of
\mathcal{A}
as tuples in the product bundle

\alpha(x,p,v,b)=p(b)\qquad x\in N,p\in B^{*}_{x},v\in T_{p}B^{*},b\in B_{x}

satisfying
T_{p}\pi(v)=\rho_{B}(b)
.

Proof.

See originally Martinez
[
20
, Section 7]
or Lin
[
17
, Proposition 2.2.1]
.
∎

More recently, Lin et al.
[
17
]
proved an analogue of the Darboux-Marsden-Weinstein Theorem and described the processes of symplectic reduction in this context. In general, examples exist plentifully:

•

In the case that the base manifold is a point, we recover the notion of a quasi-Frobenius Lie algebra. In this setting, a Frobienus Lie algebra corresponds to an
\mathcal{A}
-symplectic form that is exact, see
[
4
, Chapter 3.1]
.

•

In the case that
\mathcal{A}=TM
, a
\mathcal{A}
-symplectic form is a symplectic form in the classical sense.

•

b
-symplectic manifolds, also known as log-symplectic manifolds give examples of symplectic Lie algebroids, as the
b
-tangent bundle, typically denoted
{}^{b}TM
is a Lie algebroid with the inclusion map as an anchor, and the Lie bracket of vector fields as it’s bracket. In a neighborhood of the hypersurface of interest, we have that any
b
-symplectic form looks like

\omega=\frac{1}{x_{1}}dx_{1}\wedge dx_{2}+dx_{3}\wedge dx_{4}+\dots+dx_{2n-1}\wedge dx_{2n},

where
x_{1}
is the direction along the hypersurface. For more on
b
-symplectic (and Poisson) geometry, see
[
9
]
.

•

In celestial mechanics, the McGhee coordinate change was introduced
[
24
]
to help solve the planar reduced 3-body problem. In this setting, then natural symplectic structure becomes

\omega=-\frac{4}{x^{3}}dx\wedge dy+d\alpha\wedge dG.

This is an example of
b^{3}
-symplectic manifold. In general,
b^{k}
-symplectic manifolds give examples of symplectic Lie algebroids. For more on these, see
[
33
]
,
[
10
]
.

•

One often employs and encounters symplectic Lie algebroids in Poisson geometry. On some Poisson manifolds, one can construct a symplectic Lie algebroid which induces the underlying Poisson structure. In this case, the Poisson structure can be “lifted” to the algebroid, where it behaves much more like a symplectic object, and some computations are more amenable. Which Poisson manifolds admit these symplectic Lie algebroids is still an open question, though many families of examples are known. For more, see
[
2
]
.

If we have two symplectic Lie algebroids in hand, their product is also a symplectic Lie algebroid.

Proposition 63
.

Given symplectic Lie algebroids
(\mathcal{A}_{1}\rightarrow M_{1},\omega_{1})
and
(\mathcal{A}_{2}\rightarrow M_{2},\omega_{2})
, the product
(A_{1}\times A_{2}\rightarrow M_{1}\times M_{2},\omega)
where
\omega=\pi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1}+\pi_{2}^{*}\omega_{2}
is a symplectic Lie algebroid where
\pi_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{i}
are the projection maps.

Proof.

The product of two Lie algebroids has a unique Lie algebroid structure, see
[
25
]
, so it remains to be seen that the above
\omega
is indeed a symplectic form.
\omega
is nondegenerate as it’s made from the pullback of two nondegenerate forms. Note that
\pi_{i}
are Lie algebroid morphisms
[
25
, Example 7.4]
, and as such they induce chain maps
\pi_{i}^{*}
. Using this we have that

d_{\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}}\omega=d_{\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}}\pi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1}+d_{\mathcal{A}_{1}\times\mathcal{A}_{2}}\pi_{2}^{*}\omega_{2}=\pi_{1}^{*}(d_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\omega_{1})+\pi_{2}^{*}(d_{\mathcal{A}_{2}}\omega_{2})=0.

∎

3.2.
Kahler-Weil Identities

Throughout this section, let
(\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M,\rho,\omega)
be a symplectic Lie algebroid of rank
2m
. The “Lefschetz” map is defined on the space of
\mathcal{A}
forms, and is explicitly defined as

\displaystyle L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Omega^{k+2}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega\wedge\alpha.

If we let
\pi
denote the dual bivector to
\omega
, we can define another operator on the space of
\mathcal{A}
-forms:

\displaystyle\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Omega^{k-2}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\mapsto\iota_{\pi}\alpha.

These two operators classically generate an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
representation on the space of forms of a symplectic manifold, and the same is true in this setting as well. To prove this, we’ll need to work locally however and we have the following weaker analogue of the Darboux Theorem from classical symplectic Geometry. This Lemma is essentially a rephrasing of
[
7
, Lemma 3.23]
, though we state it again here for containment sake.

Lemma 64
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M,\rho,\omega)
be a symplectic Lie algebroid of rank
2m
. Then for all
p\in M
, there exists a smooth symplectic local frame of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e. we can locally express
\omega
as

\omega=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\mu_{i}\wedge\xi_{i}

where
\mu_{i},\xi_{i}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{*}|_{U})
for a sufficiently small open neighborhood
U
of
p
.

Proof.

This is true for any symplectic vector bundle, see
[
16
, Lemma 12.6]
.
∎

Remark 65
.

Note here that we’re not insisting that the algebroid
1
-forms that make up our local coframe of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
are differentials of coordinate functions, or that they’re even closed. The local frame
\{\mu_{i},\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is dual to
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, the local frame of
\mathcal{A}
on the same open set, but a geometrically inclined choice of normal coordinates that correspond to these frames are harder to pin down. In fact, there are multiple obstructions to this. One of these is cohomological as shown by Miranda and Scott
[
27
, Theorem 29]
. Another is the failure for a general Lie algebroid to carry a commutative frame. This can be most easily in the case that our Lie algebroid is a Lie Algebra that is not abelian. For these reasons we will not make an appeal to a full “Darboux Theorem” though work has been done on this see
[
17
]
. In many settings such as
b
-manifolds there are results on local normal forms, and in these settings the following sections results can be more easily understood geometrically, see
[
23
]
,
[
27
]
.

Using the preceding lemma, at any point
p\in M
, we have local symplectic frames of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}^{*}
on an open set
U\ni p
given by

\mathcal{A}|_{U}=\mathrm{span}_{C^{\infty}(M)}\{u_{i},v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m},\qquad\mathcal{A}^{*}|_{U}=\mathrm{span}_{C^{\infty}(M)}\{\mu_{i},\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}.

In such a frame we can write local expressions for
L
and
\Lambda

L=\sum_{i}e_{\mu_{i}}e_{\xi_{i}}\qquad\Lambda=\sum_{i}\iota_{v_{i}}\iota_{u_{i}},

where
e_{v}
denotes the map
v\wedge-
.Using these expressions, we can prove the following:

Lemma 66
.

If
\alpha\in\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
, then
[L,\Lambda]\alpha=(k-m)\alpha
.

Proof.

These are local,
C^{\infty}(M)
-linear operators, so fix a point
p\in M
and an open neighborhood
U\ni p
such that we have the symplectic frames
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\{\mu_{i},\xi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
as provided by Lemma
64
. From here we can proceed as follows in
[
11
, Page 8]
.
∎

Definition 67
.

We define the “counting operator”
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
as the map

H=\sum_{k}(k-m)\pi_{k},

where
\pi_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
is the canonical projection.

Proposition 68
.

Given the operators
L,\Lambda
, and
H
, we have the following
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-commutation relations

[L,\Lambda]=H,\quad[H,L]=2L,\quad[H,\Lambda]=-2\Lambda.

Proof.

The first identity is the result of the lemma
66
, and the remaining two follows from the following computations. Fix
\alpha\in\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
, then we we have

\displaystyle[H,L]\alpha

\displaystyle=H(L\alpha)-L((k-m)\alpha)

\displaystyle=(k+2-m)L\alpha-(k-m)L\alpha

\displaystyle=2L\alpha,

and

\displaystyle[H,\Lambda]\alpha

\displaystyle=H(\Lambda\alpha)-\Lambda(H\alpha)

\displaystyle=(k-2-m)\Lambda\alpha-(k-m)\Lambda\alpha

\displaystyle=-2\Lambda\alpha.

∎

These operators give the even part of the Lie Superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and the Lie algebroid differential gives us the analogue of
d
, leaving us with one operator to define.

Definition 69
.

The “Koszul-Brylinski” differential, denoted
d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k-1}(M)
, is defined as follows for any
\alpha\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
:

d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[\Lambda,d_{\mathcal{A}}]\alpha.

This operator is of degree
-1
, and in the classical setting serves as the boundary map in
Poisson Homology
. The fact
(d^{\star}_{\mathcal{A}})^{2}=0
is still unclear in our setting, but that will soon be remedied.

Definition 70
.

We say that a form
\alpha\in\Omega^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(M)
is “
\mathcal{A}
-symplectic harmonic” or just “harmonic” if
d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha=d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha=0
, and denote the set of harmonic
k
-forms as
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M)
and the set of all harmonic forms as
\widehat{\Omega}_{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(M)
.

Proposition 71
.

Given
X,Y\in\Gamma(\wedge^{2}\mathcal{A})
, we have that
[\iota_{X},[\iota_{Y},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]=\iota_{[X,Y]}
where
[\cdot,\cdot]
is the Nijenhius-Schouten extension of the bracket on
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

It suffices to check this locally on decomposable bivectors, so let
X=X_{1}\wedge X_{2}
and
Y=Y_{1}\wedge Y_{2}
. We have that

[\iota_{Y},d_{\mathcal{A}}]=\iota_{Y_{1}}[\iota_{Y_{2}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]-[\iota_{Y_{1}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]\iota_{Y_{2}}

Applying
\iota_{X}
and expanding, we have

\displaystyle[\iota_{X},[\iota_{Y},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\iota_{X_{1}}[\iota_{X_{2}},\iota_{Y_{1}}[\iota_{Y_{2}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]+[\iota_{X_{1}},\iota_{Y_{1}}[\iota_{Y_{2}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]\iota_{X_{2}}}_{A}

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\left(\iota_{X_{1}}[\iota_{X_{2}},[\iota_{Y_{1}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]\iota_{Y_{2}}]+[\iota_{X_{1}},[\iota_{Y_{1}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]\iota_{Y_{2}}]\iota_{X_{2}}\right)}_{B}.

Note that using the cartan calculus identities
[\iota_{a},\iota_{b}]=0
and
[\iota_{a},[\iota_{b},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]=\iota_{[a,b]}
for sections
a,b\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A})
, we have that

[\iota_{a},\iota_{b}[\iota_{c},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]=\iota_{b}[\iota_{a},[\iota_{c},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]+[\iota_{a},\iota_{b}][\iota_{c},d_{\mathcal{A}}]=\iota_{b}\iota_{[a,c]}

Using this, we can simplify the above to

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\iota_{X_{1}}\iota_{Y_{1}}\iota_{[X_{2},Y_{2}]}+\iota_{Y_{1}}\iota_{[X_{1},Y_{2}]}\iota_{X_{2}},

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\iota_{X_{1}}\iota_{[X_{2},Y_{1}]}\iota_{Y_{2}}+\iota_{[X_{1},Y_{1}]}\iota_{Y_{2}}\iota_{X_{2}}.

Combining these and using that
\iota_{a}\iota_{b}=\iota_{a\wedge b}
, we get

\displaystyle A-B

\displaystyle=\iota_{X_{1}\wedge Y_{1}\wedge[X_{2},Y_{2}]+Y_{1}\wedge[X_{1},Y_{2}]\wedge X_{2}-X_{1}\wedge[X_{2},Y_{1}]\wedge Y_{2}-[X_{1},Y_{1}]\wedge Y_{2}\wedge X_{2}}=\iota_{[X,Y]}

∎

Proposition 72
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M,\rho,\omega)
be a symplectic Lie algebroid, and let
\pi\in\Gamma(\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{A})
be the dual bivector to
\omega
as above. Then
[\pi,\pi]=0
.

Proof.

See
[
29
, Proof of Theorem 2.14]
in the paragraph immediately following the list of 3 equivalences; there the bivector
\pi
is written as
\varpi
.
∎

Lemma 73
.

Let
(\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M,\rho,\omega)
be a symplectic Lie algebroid. Then we have the following commutation relations:

(1)

[L,d_{\mathcal{A}}]=0
.

(2)

[H,d_{\mathcal{A}}]=d_{A}
.

(3)

[H,d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=-d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}
.

(4)

[\Lambda,d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=0
.

(5)

[L,d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=d_{\mathcal{A}}
.

(6)

[d_{\mathcal{A}},d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=0
.

(7)

[d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star},d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=0
.

Proof.

We prove these in order for
\alpha\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M)
:

(1)

Using that
\omega
is closed we have

\displaystyle[L,d_{\mathcal{A}}]\alpha

\displaystyle=\omega\wedge d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha-d_{\mathcal{A}}(\omega\wedge\alpha)

\displaystyle=\omega\wedge d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha-(d_{\mathcal{A}}\omega\wedge\alpha+\omega\wedge d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha)

\displaystyle=0.

(2)

Using only degree considerations we have

\displaystyle[H,d_{\mathcal{A}}]\alpha=Hd_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha-d_{\mathcal{A}}H\alpha=[(k+1)-n]d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha-(k-n)d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha=d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha.

(3)

Again using only degree considerations we have that

[H,d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=Hd_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha-d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}H\alpha=[(k-1)-n]d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha-(k-n)d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha=-d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha.

(4)

Using Proposition
71
, we have that

[\Lambda,d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=[\iota_{\pi},[\iota_{\pi},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]=\iota_{[\pi,\pi]}.

To show this vanishes, we must have that
[\pi,\pi]=0
, but this follows from Proposition
72
.

(5)

Using the fact that
\omega_{\mathcal{A}}
is
d_{\mathcal{A}}
-closed, and that
[L,d_{\mathcal{A}}]=0
, we have

\displaystyle[L,d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]\alpha

\displaystyle=Ld_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha-d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}L\alpha

\displaystyle=L\Lambda d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha-Ld_{\mathcal{A}}\Lambda\alpha-\Lambda(\omega_{\mathcal{A}}\wedge d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha)+d\Lambda(\omega_{\mathcal{A}}\wedge\alpha)

\displaystyle=[L,\Lambda]d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha-d[L,\Lambda]\alpha

\displaystyle=[H,d_{\mathcal{A}}]\alpha

\displaystyle=d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha.

(6)

The graded Jacobi identity tells us that

-[d_{\mathcal{A}},[\Lambda,d_{\mathcal{A}}]]+[\Lambda,[d_{\mathcal{A}},d_{\mathcal{A}}]]+[d_{\mathcal{A}},[d_{\mathcal{A}},\Lambda]]=0.

From this we must have that

-2[d_{\mathcal{A}},d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]=0.

(7)

Using items 4 and 6, along with the graded Jacobi identity we have that

\displaystyle[d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star},d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]

\displaystyle=[d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star},[\Lambda,d_{\mathcal{A}}]]

\displaystyle=[\Lambda,[d_{\mathcal{A}},d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}]]+[d_{\mathcal{A}},[d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star},\Lambda]]

\displaystyle=0.

∎

Remark 80
.

An alternative method of constructing

is to define the symplectic star

3.3.
Cohomology with Coefficients

This whole story works as well for Lie algebroid cohomology with coefficients in a representation of the Lie algebroid. This extension was originally pointed out in
[
35

for traditional vector bundles with connections.

Definition 81
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid over a smooth manifold
M
. An “
\mathcal{A}
-connection” on a vector bundle
E\rightarrow M
is an
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear map

\displaystyle{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Gamma\mathcal{A}\times\Gamma E

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Gamma E

\displaystyle(X,s)

\displaystyle\mapsto\nabla_{X}s

which is
C^{\infty}(M)
linear in the first entry and satisfies

{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{X}(fs)=f{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{X}s+\mathcal{L}_{\rho(X)}(f)s.

The “curvature” of the connection
{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla
is the
\End(E)
-valued algebroid
2

R_{\nabla}(X,Y)={}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{X}{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{Y}-{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{Y}{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{X}-{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{[X,Y]}.

We say that
{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla
is “flat” if
R_{\nabla}\equiv 0
.

Definition 82
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid over a smooth manifold. A “representation” of

E

\mathcal{A}
-connection.

Definition 83
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M
be a Lie algebroid and

\mathcal{A}

\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{*}(M,E)=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\Omega^{k}_{\mathcal{A}}(M,E).

This caries a differential
{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k}(M,E)\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{k+1}(M,E)
given by the induced covariant derivative, i.e. we extend
{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{0}(M,E)\rightarrow\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}(M,E)
via enforcing

{}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla(\alpha\otimes s)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(d_{\mathcal{A}}\alpha)\otimes s+(-1)^{|\alpha|}\alpha\otimes({}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla s),

for
\alpha\otimes s\in\Omega^{k}_{A}(M,E)

Since the connection is flat, this is a cochain complex.

4.1.
Kodaria-Thurston Manifold

One of the first examples of a symplectic manifold that does not admit a Kähler structure is given by the “Kodaira-Thurston” manifold

which is constructed as follows:
Take
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
and choose

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\sim(x_{1}+a,x_{2}+b,x_{3}+c,x_{4}+d-bx_{3}).

The resulting quotient is the connected smooth manifold

. The chosen basis of one forms

is given by

We have a symplectic structure on

\displaystyle H_{dR}^{0}(KT^{4})

\displaystyle H_{dR}^{2}(KT^{4})

\displaystyle=\langle\omega,[e_{12}-e_{34}],[e_{13}],[e_{24}]\rangle,

\displaystyle H_{dR}^{3}(KT^{4})

\displaystyle=\langle[\omega\wedge e_{1}],[\omega\wedge e_{2}],[\omega\wedge e_{4}]\rangle,

\displaystyle H_{dR}^{4}(KT^{4})

\displaystyle=\langle[\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}]\rangle.

The two Lefschetz maps of interest are:

\displaystyle[L]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H_{dR}^{1}(KT^{4})\rightarrow H_{dR}^{3}(KT^{4})\qquad[L]^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H_{dR}^{0}(KT^{4})\rightarrow H_{dR}^{4}(KT^{4}).

where we take

4.3.

\displaystyle de^{1}=de^{2}

Theorem

(Nomizu
[

)
.

The deRham complex
\Omega^{\bullet}(M)

\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathfrak{n}^{*}
of left-invariant forms on

H_{dR}^{\bullet}(M)\cong H_{CE}^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{n})
.

Examining the maps
[L]^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H^{3-k}(\mathfrak{n})\rightarrow H^{3+k}(\mathfrak{n})
where

allows to check when Theorem
79

[L]^{3}

4.4.
E
-manifolds

E
-manifolds are a generalization of a collection of different geometric settings:
b
-manifolds,
b^{m}
-manifolds,
c
-manifolds, and Regular Foliations. Miranda and Scott have thoroughly explored the cohomology and symplectic geometry of
E
-manifolds in many settings, e.g.
[
27
]
. The definition given in the above paper of Miranda and Scott of an
E
-manifold is restated here for convenience.

Definition 88
.

Let
E
be a locally free submodule of the
C^{\infty}
module
\mathfrak{X}(M)
of vector fields on
M
. By the Serre-Swan theorem, there is an
E
-tangent bundle
{}^{E}TM
whose sections (locally) are sections of
E
, and an
E
-cotangent bundle
{}^{E}T^{*}M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left({}^{E}TM\right)^{*}
. We will call the global sections of
\Lambda^{p}\left({}^{E}T^{*}M\right)

E
-forms of degree
p
, and denote the space of all such sections by
{}^{E}\Omega^{p}(M)
. If
E
satisfies the involutivity condition
[E,E]\subseteq E
, there is a differential
d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{}^{E}\Omega^{p}(M)\rightarrow{}^{E}\Omega^{p+1}(M)
given by

\displaystyle{}^{E}d\eta\left(V_{0},\ldots,V_{p}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}V_{i}\left(\eta\left(V_{0},\ldots,\hat{V}_{i},\ldots,V_{p}\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\eta\left(\left[V_{i},V_{j}\right],V_{0},\ldots,\hat{V}_{i},\ldots,\hat{V}_{j},\ldots,V_{p}\right).

The cohomology of this complex is known as
E
-cohomology and is the same as Lie algebroid cohomology if we view this as a Lie Algebroid with anchor given by inclusion. If we have an
E
-closed nondegenerate
E
-
2
-form
\omega
, we call this an
E
-symplectic form and the triple
(E,M,\omega)
an
E
-symplectic manifold. These are symplectic Lie Algebroids. The cohomology of such an object in general is rather difficult to compute, however Miranda and Scott showed the following:

Theorem 89

(Miranda and Scott
[
27
]
)
.

Let
M=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
E
be the involutive subbundle of
\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
generated by

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y},\quad v_{2}=-y\partial_{x}+x\partial y.

with dual bundle generated by

\displaystyle v_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{xdx+ydy}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\quad v_{2}^{*}=\frac{-ydx+xdy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}.

Then the
E
-cohomology or Lie algebroid cohomology is given by

{}^{E}H^{k}(M)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}\quad&i=0,2\\
\mathbb{R}^{2}&i=1\\
0&i\geq 3\end{cases}

Theorem 90
.

The symplectic Lie algebroid defined by Miranda and Scott
(E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2},\rho=\text{id},\omega=v_{1}^{*}\wedge v_{2}^{*})
satisfies Theorem
75
.

Proof.

We only need to check that the map
[L]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{}^{E}H^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\rightarrow{}^{E}H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is an isomorphism, which amounts to showing that
\omega=v_{1}^{*}\wedge v_{2}^{*}
is not an exact form. Suppose towards a contradiction that it is, i.e. there are
f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\omega={}^{E}d(fv_{1}^{*}+gv_{2}^{*})
. Unfolding the definition this implies that

\omega=(v_{1}(g)-v_{2}(f))\omega

which means for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have that

v_{1}(g)-v_{2}(f)=1.

Equivalently

\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)x+\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)y=1,

however at
(0,0)
this fails, so
\omega
is not exact.
∎

Remark 91
.

The above work does not restrict to just
E
-manifolds though. Theorem
75
applies to the setting of symplectic
b^{m}
-manifolds for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
as well, since the
b^{m}
-tangent bundle in these cases is a symplectic Lie algebroid. For more on these see
[
9
]
.

4.5.
Kähler Lie Algebroids

There has been recent attention towards Kähler Lie Algebroids, see
[
14
]
. By the Hard Lefschetz Theorem, Kälher manifolds are always Lefschetz manifolds. This relationship still holds true for a suitable class of Kähler Lie algebroids:

Definition 92
.

A “Kähler Lie algebroid”
(\mathcal{A}\rightarrow M,\rho,g)
is a Lie algebroid with a Hermitian metric
g
whose associated canonical
(1,1)
-
\mathcal{A}
-form
\omega\in\Omega_{\mathcal{A}}^{1,1}(M)
is closed.

Corollary 93
.

Kähler Lie algebroids are symplectic Lie algebroids.

Throughout this section, let
J
be the associated complex structure for
\mathcal{A}
. Reconsidering the construction of
d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}
with the presence of a complex structure reveals that

Jd_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}J^{-1}=d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}

where
d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}
is the metric adjoint to
d_{\mathcal{A}}
, for details see
[
11
]
.

Theorem 94
.

Kähler Lie algebroids which admit harmonic representatives for every
\mathcal{A}
-cohomology class in the Riemannian sense are Lefschetz algebroids.

Proof.

It suffices to show that every cohomology class admits a symplectic harmonic representative. Fix any class
[\alpha]\in H_{\mathcal{A}}^{p,q}(M,\mathbb{C})
, where we’ve chosen
\alpha
to be a harmonic representative. Then
d_{A}\alpha=d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\alpha=0
, and so

0=d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\dagger}\alpha=Jd_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}J^{-1}\alpha.

This forces
d_{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}\alpha=0
up to a nonzero constant, so
\alpha
is symplectic harmonic.
∎

Remark 95
.

When a Kähler Lie algebroid admits a harmonic representative in the Riemannian sense is not immediately obvious, as the complex of
\mathcal{A}
-forms is not always elliptic.
