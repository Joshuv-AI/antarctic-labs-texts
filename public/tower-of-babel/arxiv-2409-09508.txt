stable pairs on local curves and Bethe roots
Maximilian Schimpf

Abstract
. We give an explicit formula for the descendent stable pair invariants of all (absolute) local curves in terms of certain power series called Bethe roots, which also appear in the physics/representation theory literature. We derive new explicit descriptions for the Bethe roots which are of independent interest. From this we derive rationality, functional equation and a characterization of poles for the full descendent stable pair theory of local curves as conjectured by Pandharipande and Pixton. We also sketch how our methods give a new approach to the spectrum of quantum multiplication on 𝖧𝗂𝗅𝖻 n ​ ( ℂ 2 ) \mathsf{Hilb}^{n}({\mathbb{C}}^{2}) .

1.2.
Bethe roots

A commonly used tool in the theory of integrable systems is the

, which goes back to
[

]

[

]

[

,
NS2
]
. The Bethe equations most relevant to us come from the quantum intermediate long wave system (

in the notation of
[

]
), which are as follows:
Let

\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1},t_{2})}

{\mathbb{Q}}(t_{1},t_{2})

[

, Corollary 13.15]

for any

for all

. We are then interested in certain admissible tuples satisfying

(1.4)

\displaystyle p=F_{i}(\mathbf{Y})

For the rest of this section we fix a partition

Definition 2.2

For any tuple of natural numbers

Remark 2.3
.

More precisely,

\left[C^{[\mathbf{n}]}\right]=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\vdash\mathbf{n}}\phi_{*}\left[C^{(\mathbf{m})}\right]

Proof.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

Since this is a local question we may assume that
{\mathbb{E}}=[E^{-1}\to E^{0}]
for

locally free and that the morphism

results from a commutative diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E^{-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces E^{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces I/I^{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{M}|_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\delta}

4.1.
Intersection theory of symmetric products of curves

We now want to compute the integrals appearing in Proposition
3.2
. In order to do that we first need to recall the intersection theory of the symmetric product of a connected smooth projective curve
C
of genus
g
as outlined for example in
[
curvebook
]
.

For any
m>0
and fixed
c\in C
we have an embedding

\displaystyle\iota\colon C^{(m-1)}

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow C^{(m)}

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\longmapsto D+c

of a divisor with cohomology class
u\in H^{2}(C^{(m)})
. Note that
u
does not depend on the choice of
c
as
C
is connected. More generally, for any
n\geq 0
the subvariety

\displaystyle\iota_{n}\colon C^{(m-n)}

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow C^{(m)}

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\longmapsto D+m\cdot c

represents
u^{n}\in H^{2n}(C^{(m)})
, which in particular implies
\int_{C^{(m)}}u^{n}=\delta_{m,n}
. Furthermore, we have the Abel-Jacobi maps

\displaystyle\text{AJ}_{m}\colon C^{(m)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\text{Pic}^{0}(C)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\longmapsto{\mathcal{O}}_{C}(D-m\cdot c)

to the Jacobian of
C
, which are compatible with the
\iota_{n}
. It can be shown that this induces an isomorphism on
H^{1}
and so
H^{1}(C^{(m)})=H^{1}(\text{Pic}^{0}(C))=H^{1}(C)
. Recall that
H^{1}(C)
has a symplectic base
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{g}\in H^{1}(C)
i.e. so that
\int_{C}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}=\delta_{i,j}
and
\int_{C}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}=\int_{C}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}=0
for all
1\leq i,j\leq g
. By abuse of notation we will also denote the pullback of this basis by
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
in
H^{1}(\text{Pic}^{0}(C))
and in
H^{1}(C^{(m)})
. Furthermore, we will denote by
\theta\in H^{2}(\text{Pic}^{0}(C))
the theta divisor
[
curvebook
, Section I.4]
as well as its pullback along
\text{AJ}_{m}
in
H^{2}(C^{(m)})
. More explicitly, we have

\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{g}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}.

We can now state Poincaré’s formula
[
curvebook
, Section I.5]
which says

\left(\text{AJ}_{m}\right)_{*}(1)=\begin{cases}\frac{\theta^{g-m}}{(g-m)!},\text{ if }m\leq g\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

in particular, one can rewrite this without case distinction as

\left(\text{AJ}_{m}\right)_{*}(1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset\{1,\ldots,g\}\\
\lvert I\rvert=g-m\end{subarray}}\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}.

The following Lemma will be very useful for computations later on:

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
m,n\geq 0
and
I\subset\{1,\ldots,g\}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{C^{(m)}}u^{n}\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}=\delta_{\lvert I\rvert+n,m}

and for any two distinct subsets
I,J\subset\{1,\ldots,g\}

\displaystyle\int_{C^{(m)}}u^{n}\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\prod_{j\in J}\beta_{j}=0.

Proof.

For the first integral note that the second factor is pulled back under the Abel-Jacobi map, which commutes with
\iota_{n}\colon C^{(m-n)}\subset C^{(m)}
. Using the projection formula we can rewrite the integral as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{C^{(m)}}u^{n}\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}&=\int_{\text{Pic}^{0}(C)}\left(\text{AJ}_{m-n}\right)_{*}(1)\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset\{1,\ldots,g\}\\
\lvert J\rvert=g-m+n\end{subarray}}\int_{\text{Pic}^{0}(C)}\prod_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}\prod_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\end{split}

where we used Poincaré’s formula in the last equality. Now the only summand that can contribute is the one corresponding to
J=I^{c}
, which occurs in the sum only if
m-n=\lvert I\rvert
, proving the first claim. The second claim follows by a similar argument.
∎

are holomorphic near the origin and can be locally analytically continued to any point in the complement of

X=\Set{{\mathbf{p}}=(p_{i})_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}{\text{there exists }\emptyset\neq S\subset\Set{1,\ldots,d}\text{ so that }\prod_{i\in S}(-p_{i})=1}

Proof.

We consider the subset
Z\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
consisting of
(\mathbf{Y},{\mathbf{p}})
so that
\mathbf{Y}
is admissible, the multivariate Bethe equations

p_{i}=F_{i}(\mathbf{Y})

are satisfied and so that the Jacobian
\left(\frac{\partial F_{i}(\mathbf{Y})}{\partial Y_{j}}\right)_{i,j}
is invertible. As a result,
Z
is smooth of dimension
d
. Since the multivariate Bethe roots come as inversions of convergent power series they themselves converge in a small enough neighborhood. Furthermore, they are admissible and have a nonvanishing Jacobian determinant on the level of power series which implies that there is an open subset of
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
on which they all give sections of
\pi_{2}\colon Z\to{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
. Therefore
\pi_{2}
is dominant and on the complement of some big enough proper algebraic subset
Y\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
it is also finite
[
Hartshorne
, Exercise II.3.7]
, flat
[
stacksproject
, Tag 052A]
, unramified (characteristic 0) and hence a holomorphic covering. All Bethe roots therefore admit local analytic continuations to any point in this complement.

By Riemann’s extension theorem
[
GriffithsHarris
, Chapter 1]
one now only needs to show that these stay bounded when approaching any point in the complement of
X
. For this let
{\mathbf{p}}^{(n)}
be a sequence of points in
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\setminus Y
converging to a point
{\mathbf{p}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\setminus X
and assume that each

either stays bounded or diverges to
\infty
. Let

be the set of indices where the latter happens. We then have

\prod_{i\in S}p^{(n)}_{i}=\prod_{i\in S}f_{i}(\mathbf{Y}^{\lambda}({\mathbf{p}}^{(n)}))\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in S\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}g_{j,i}(\mathbf{Y}^{\lambda}({\mathbf{p}}^{(n)}))=\prod_{i\in S}f_{i}(\mathbf{Y}^{\lambda}({\mathbf{p}}^{(n)}))\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in S\\
j\not\in S\end{subarray}}g_{j,i}(\mathbf{Y}^{\lambda}({\mathbf{p}}^{(n)}))

where we used

in the second equality. Since the right hand side converges to

we must have

∎

This lets us control the poles of most of the factors appearing in (

For the rest we will only examine
