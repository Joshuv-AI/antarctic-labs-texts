Coherent phonon control beyond amplitude saturation
Jan Gerrit Horstmann, Christoph Emeis, Andrin Caviezel, Quintin Meier, Thomas Lottermoser, Fabio Caruso, Manfred Fiebig

Abstract
Nonlinearity is often associated with amplification, instability, or emergent behavior — where small inputs produce disproportionately large outputs. Yet in many physical systems, nonlinearities act as limiting mechanisms, suppressing the response below linear expectations, as exemplified by the breakdown of Hooke’s law at large forces. These limiting effects can become significant in the optical control of quantum materials, where large-amplitude lattice motion is essential for reaching functional states far from equilibrium. Displacive excitation of coherent phonons, perhaps the most widely used control strategy, typically assumes linearity between the driving force on the lattice and the optically excited carrier density. However, the breakdown of this assumption remains largely unexplored, and strategies are needed to overcome the resulting nonlinear bottlenecks. Here, we show that sequential optical excitation overcomes the nonlinear amplitude saturation of a key phonon mode in the van der Waals ferroelectric Td-WTe 2 . Using time-resolved second harmonic generation, we track the response of the interlayer sliding mode governing ferroelectricity to both single- and double-pulse excitation. With increasing optical excitation, the phonon amplitude saturates and declines — a limitation we trace to band-specific electron–phonon coupling that weakens the driving force on the nuclei. Sequential in-phase excitation of coherent sliding motion, which outlasts the electronic excitation, avoids population of counteracting electronic states. This enables higher phonon amplitudes for the same optical energy input. We exploit the enhanced lattice response to perform high-amplitude vibrational spectroscopy in the excited state, revealing a novel form of anharmonic phonon coupling that emerges exclusively under nonequilibrium conditions, and previously elusive coherent vibrational modulations of electronic states. Our results establish a more general picture of electron-phonon and phonon-phonon coupling far from equilibrium, opening new possibilities for ultrafast ferroic switching and lattice control in quantum materials.

Large-amplitude coherent vibrational spectroscopy

In a next step, we exploit sequential excitation to enable large-amplitude coherent vibrational spectroscopy in the excited state. In particular, double-pulse excitation not only facilitates enhanced vibrational amplitudes but also allows precise control over the amplitude while keeping other key degrees of freedom, such as electronic excitation and temperature, constant

Using this approach, we find a strong correlation between the sliding mode amplitude

Crucially, this decay channel is absent in equilibrium crystals, where nuclear wave packets remain centered at their equilibrium positions regardless of temperature. The observed amplitude-dependent damping therefore reflects higher-order anharmonic phonon–phonon interactions

We note that these insights into the processes underlying coherent phonon decay cannot be straightforwardly obtained from conventional (fluence-dependent) optical pump–probe experiments alone, but require precise control over the phonon amplitude in a targeted excited state, as enabled by double-pulse excitation (compare pump-probe and pump-pump-probe data in Fig.

The resulting amplitude modulation of the optical SHG response leads to the emergence of frequency sidebands (see derivation in the Methods section).

While phonon-induced energy shifts near the Fermi level are well established, our results show that repeated optical excitation can modulate optical transitions even in band structure regions that are typically only weakly coupled to structural modes. This capability is particularly relevant for the generation of optical frequency combs via coherent phonons

Our work extends this approach to a broader range of photon energies, beyond linear transmission or reflectivity, and to the regime of optical second-harmonic generation, offering an alternative pathway to the modulation of optical nonlinearity that is complementary to recent advances in Floquet engineering

Discussion and summary

In summary, we have shown that redistributing the energy of a single ultrafast excitation into two or more pulses can overcome the amplitude limitation of key vibrational modes. Extending this concept to multi-pulse sequences of sub-threshold excitations may enable ’cold’ driving of Raman-active modes deep into the nonlinear regime
weiner_femtosecond_1990
;
weiner_femtosecond_1991
;
nelson_prospects_1991
. By analogy with nonlinear phononics
forst_mode-selective_2015
;
nova_metastable_2019
;
disa_photo-induced_2023
, the resulting large-amplitude motion along Raman-active coordinates could access novel dynamical phases of matter and facilitate ultrafast, energy-efficient switching of order parameters. Our approach may be particularly relevant for the class of sliding ferroelectrics, where—due to broken inversion symmetry—decisive modes are typically both Raman and IR-active but carry only small dipole moments, limiting the efficiency of direct resonant excitation.

While a linear relationship between electronic temperature and vibrational amplitude is a central assumption of the DECP model
zeiger_theory_1992
, there is no fundamental reason for it to hold universally. The principles demonstrated here for WTe
2
should therefore be broadly applicable to other materials and their structural modes. In this context, high-amplitude vibrational spectroscopy enabled by sequential excitation

;
cheng_coherent_2017
offers a powerful tool to probe phonon–electron and phonon–phonon interactions in nonequilibrium states beyond conventional pump–probe schemes. Understanding how decisive coherent phonons decay under such conditions is essential for advancing vibrational control strategies. For WTe
2
, our work identifies a nonequilibrium decay channel directly governed by coherent nuclear motion, highlighting amplitude-dependent damping as a key factor in determining phonon lifetimes as well as energy and heat transfer. We envision that the enhancement of vibrational coherences near topologically trivial and paraelectric structural configurations opens new avenues for coherent control of both topology and ferroelectricity in functional materials.

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\text{PT}}(\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}})

\displaystyle=\Delta I_{\text{PT}}^{0}\cdot\left(1-\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}}{\tau_{\text{PT}}}\right)\right),

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\text{SP}}(\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}})

\displaystyle=-\Delta I_{\text{SP}}^{0}\cdot\text{cos}\left(\frac{2\pi\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}}{T_{\text{sliding}}}+\phi_{\text{sliding}}\right)\cdot\text{exp}\left(-\frac{-\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}}{\tau_{\text{sliding}}}\right),

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\text{fast}}^{\text{exc}}(\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}})

\displaystyle=\Delta I_{\text{fast}}^{\text{exc},0}\cdot\left(1-\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}}{\tau_{\text{fast}}^{\text{exc}}}\right)\right),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\Delta I_{\text{fast}}^{\text{decay}}(\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}})

\displaystyle=\Delta I_{\text{fast}}^{\text{decay},0}\cdot\left(\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}}{\tau_{\text{fast}}^{\text{decay}}}\right)\right)

is fitted to the delay-dependent traces in Fig.
1
d. Here,
\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}
is the pump-probe delay,
\Delta I_{\text{PT}}
corresponds to the change in the SHG signal due to the shift of the equilibrium position after photo-excitation (see also Ref.
sie_ultrafast_2019
for details),
\Delta I_{\text{SP}}
is the modulation of the SHG intensity due to coherent sliding motion, and
\Delta I_{\text{fast}}^{\text{exc}}
or
\Delta I_{\text{fast}}^{\text{decay}}
, respectively, model the fast initial suppression of the SHG signal. Parameters
\tau_{\text{PT}}
and
\tau_{\text{sliding}}
correspond to the characteristic time scale of the structural shift or the sliding mode coherence time, respectively. Furthermore,
\tau_{\text{fast}}^{\text{exc}}
and
\tau_{\text{fast}}^{\text{decay}}
are the time constants for the excitation and subsequent decay of the fast initial signal suppression observed in the
\chi_{zyy}
component.
T_{\text{sliding}}
is the sliding mode period.

Analysis of the pump-pump-probe data.
To determine the sliding mode amplitude
A_{\text{s}}
, frequency
f_{\text{s}}
, phase
\phi_{\text{s}}
, and damping
\gamma_{\text{s}}=\tau_{\text{s}}^{-1}
, as well as the quasi-dc signal suppression
\Delta I_{\text{const}}
in double-pulse experiments, we fit the model

\displaystyle I_{\text{SHG}}(\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}})=A_{\text{s}}\cdot\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}}{\tau_{\text{s}}}\right)\cdot\text{cos}(2\pi f_{\text{s}}\Delta t_{\text{p-pr}}+\phi_{\text{s}})+\Delta I_{\text{const}}

(3)

to the trSHG trace after the second excitation for each double-pulse delay
\Delta t_{\text{p-p}}
. The results are shown in Fig.
4
a. The
\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}}
-dependent sliding mode amplitude
A_{\text{s}}
in Fig.
3
f, on the other hand, is fitted by the model

\displaystyle A_{\text{s}}(\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}})=A_{\text{s}}^{\text{osc}}(\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}})\cdot\left(1-A_{\text{e}}^{\text{damp}}\cdot\text{exp}\left(-\frac{\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}}}{\tau_{\text{e}}^{\text{damp}}}\right)\right),

(4)

where

\displaystyle A_{\text{s}}^{\text{osc}}(\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}})=A_{\text{s}}\sqrt{1+2\zeta\cdot(2\pi f_{\text{s}}\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}})+\zeta^{2}}.

(5)

Here,
A_{\text{e}}^{\text{damp}}
is the leverage of electronic excitation for the damping of the sliding mode amplitude and
\tau_{\text{e}}^{\text{damp}}
the electronic relaxation time. To extract the Fourier amplitudes at frequencies
f_{\text{s}}
and
2f_{\text{s}}
, we multiply the delay-dependent SHG trace after the second excitation pulse with a super-Gaussian window function

\displaystyle F_{\text{filt},t}=\text{exp}\left(-\left(\frac{(\Delta t_{\text{p1-p2}}-t^{\prime})^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}}\right)^{3}\right),

(6)

before applying a standard fast Fourier transform algorithm. The quotient
(\Delta\gamma_{\text{s}}/\Delta A_{\text{s}})(T)
is determined by fitting the linear model

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{s}}(A_{\text{s}})=A_{\text{s}}^{0}+(\Delta\gamma_{\text{s}}/\Delta A_{\text{s}})\cdot A_{\text{s}}

(7)

to the two datasets presented in Fig.

Fitting of the polarization-dependent trSHG data.
To fit the SHG polarimetry data shown in Fig.
1
d, we consider the symmetry of the Td polytype. In the orthorhombic Td phase, WTe
2
belongs to the
mm2
point group. We consider only electric-dipole–allowed second-harmonic generation (SHG), described by the odd-parity, polar (i-type) second-order nonlinear susceptibility tensor
\chi^{(2)}
. According to Ref.
birss_macroscopic_1963
, the non-vanishing components of
\chi^{(2)}
for this symmetry are
\chi^{(2)}_{zzz}
,
\chi^{(2)}_{xxz}
,
\chi^{(2)}_{xzx}
,
\chi^{(2)}_{zxx}
,
\chi^{(2)}_{yyz}
,
\chi^{(2)}_{yzy}
, and
\chi^{(2)}_{zyy}
. The incident electric field is given by

\displaystyle\bm{E}=E_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\varphi)/\sqrt{2}\\
\sin(\varphi)\\
\cos(\varphi)/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix},

(8)

where
\varphi=0^{\circ}
corresponds to p-polarized and
\varphi=90^{\circ}
to s-polarized light relative to the plane of incidence. While SHG is collected in reflection at a
45^{\circ}
angle with respect to the surface normal, for simplicity we perform the symmetry analysis in a transmission geometry, which yields equivalent selection rules. The induced second order nonlinear polarization
\bm{P}(2\omega)
is thus given by

\displaystyle\bm{P}(2\omega)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}2\chi_{xxz}E_{x}E_{z}\\
2\chi_{yyz}E_{y}E_{z}\\
\chi_{zxx}E_{x}^{2}+\chi_{zyy}E_{y}^{2}+\chi_{zzz}E_{z}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(9)

\displaystyle=E_{0}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{xxz}\cos^{2}(\varphi)\\
\sqrt{2}\chi_{yyz}\sin(\varphi)\cos(\varphi)\\
\frac{1}{2}(\chi_{zzz}+\chi_{zxx})\cos^{2}(\varphi)+\chi_{zyy}\sin^{2}(\varphi)\end{pmatrix}.

(10)

Using trigonometric identities we yield

\displaystyle\bm{P}(2\omega)=E_{0}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{xxz}(1+\cos(2\varphi))/2\\
\chi_{yyz}\sin(2\varphi)/\sqrt{2}\\
\frac{1}{4}(\chi_{zzz}+\chi_{zxx})(1+\cos(2\varphi))+\frac{1}{2}\chi_{zyy}(1-\cos(2\varphi))\end{pmatrix}.

(11)

To calculate the SHG signal after the analyzer, we project the nonlinear polarization
\mathbf{P}(2\omega)
onto the s- and p-polarized components using the unit vectors
\hat{\mathbf{v}}=(0,1,0)
and
\hat{\mathbf{h}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,0,1)
, respectively:

\displaystyle I_{h}\sim|\hat{\bm{h}}\cdot\bm{P}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(P_{x}+P_{z})|^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{0}^{4}}{2}|\chi_{\text{eff}}\cos^{2}(\varphi)+\chi_{zyy}\sin^{2}(\varphi)|^{2};

(13)

\displaystyle I_{v}\sim|P_{y}|^{2}

\displaystyle=2E_{0}^{4}|\chi_{yyz}\sin(\varphi)\cos(\varphi)|^{2},

(14)

where
\chi_{\text{eff}}=\left(\chi_{xxz}+1/2(\chi_{zzz}+\chi_{zxx})\right)
. For
I_{h}
, we additionally assume a phase difference
\theta
between
\chi_{\text{eff}}
and
\chi_{zyy}
, leading to

\displaystyle I_{h}\sim|\chi_{\text{eff}}\cos^{2}(\varphi)+e^{i\theta}\chi_{zyy}\sin^{2}(\varphi)|^{2}.

(15)

Estimation of the transient lattice temperature increase.
The transient temperature increase
\Delta T
within the probed sample volume induced by the pump pulses in the pump–pump–probe experiments is estimated as

\displaystyle\Delta T\approx\frac{F(1-R)}{\rho\,c_{\text{p}}\,d},

(16)

where
F
is the incident laser fluence,
R=0.49
the reflectivity coefficient of WTe
2
at the pump wavelength and normal incidence,
\rho=9.43\cdot 10^{3}\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-3}
is the material density,
c_{\text{p}}=1.77\cdot 10^{2}\,\text{J}\,\text{kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}
is the specific heat capacity at
300\,\text{K}
,
d=-\ln(1/\text{e})/\alpha=48\,\text{nm}
is the approximate optical penetration depth with
\alpha=2.07\cdot 10^{5}\,\text{cm}^{-1}
denoting the absorption coefficient at the pump wavelength. The penetration depth of the probe pulses (
\lambda_{\text{pr}}=800\,\text{nm}
,
d_{\text{pr}}=35\,\text{nm}
) is smaller than that of the pump pulse (
\lambda_{\text{p}}=1500\,\text{nm}
,
d_{\text{p}}=48\,\text{nm}
). We therefore neglect any effects arising from inhomogeneous excitation within the probed sample volume.

Possible origin of the
2\omega_{\text{s}}
component.
The appearance of sidebands in the Fourier transform of the time-dependent SHG signal can be understood within the framework of the deformation potential formalism. Following the arguments of Fukuda
et al.

fukuda_coherent_2024
, who observed similar features in linear optical pump–probe experiments on Td-MoTe
2
, we consider an optical transition near resonance between valence and conduction band states. In the presence of a coherent phonon, the corresponding optical susceptibility is given by

\displaystyle\chi_{0}(\omega)=\frac{|P_{\text{cv}}|^{2}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{\hbar(\omega-(\omega_{\text{cv}}-\Delta\omega_{\text{DP}})+\text{i}\Gamma}.

(17)

Here,
\hbar\omega
is the photon energy,
\hbar\omega_{\text{cv}}
the energy difference between valence and conduction band states,
\hbar\Delta\omega_{\text{DP}}
the phonon-induced energy shift,
\varepsilon_{0}
the vacuum permittivity,
P_{\text{cv}}
the optical transition matrix element, and
\Gamma
accounts for damping. The deformation potential term can be expressed as
\hbar\Delta\omega_{\text{DP}}=h_{0}Q(t)
, where
h_{0}
reflects the electron–phonon coupling strength and
Q(t)
is the time-dependent phonon amplitude. Assuming
h_{0}Q(t)\ll\hbar(\omega-\omega_{\text{cv}})+\mathrm{i}\Gamma
, we expand
\chi(\omega)
in powers of
Q(t)
:

\displaystyle\chi(\omega,Q(t))

\displaystyle=\chi_{0}\left(1+\frac{h_{0}Q(t)}{\hbar(\omega-\omega_{\text{cv}})+\text{i}\Gamma}\right)^{-1}

(18)

\displaystyle\approx\chi_{0}\left(1+\left(\frac{h_{0}}{\hbar(\omega-\omega_{\text{cv}})+\text{i}\Gamma}\right)Q(t)+\left(\frac{h_{0}}{\hbar(\omega-\omega_{\text{cv}})+\text{i}\Gamma}\right)^{2}Q(t)^{2}\right).

(19)

For
Q(t)\sim Q_{0}\cos(\omega_{\text{s}}t)
,
Q(t)^{2}\sim Q_{0}^{2}\cos^{2}(\omega_{\text{s}}t)=(Q_{0}^{2}/2)(1+\cos(2\omega_{\text{s}}t))
. With this, the second-order nonlinear polarization is given by

\displaystyle P^{2\omega}_{i}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{ijk}(\omega,Q(t))E_{j}E_{k}

(20)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\chi_{ijk}^{0}(\omega)\left(1+\frac{Q_{0}^{2}\beta^{2}}{2}+Q_{0}\beta\cos(\omega_{\text{s}}t)+Q_{0}^{2}\beta^{2}\cos(2\omega_{\text{s}}t)\right)E_{j}E_{k},

(21)

with
\beta=h_{0}/\hbar(\omega-\omega_{\text{cv}})+\text{i}\Gamma
, resulting in oscillations at frequency
2\omega_{\text{s}}
of the SHG intensity in the time-domain.

Computational details.

Density functional theory calculations are conducted within the plane-wave pseudopotential code
Quantum ESPRESSO

giannozzi_quantum_2009
;
giannozzi_advanced_2017
,
using fully-relativistic norm-conserving Pseudopotentials
hamann_optimized_2013
and the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof generalized-gradient approximation (PBE) for the exchange-correlation functional
perdew_generalized_1996
. The plane-wave kinetic-energy cutoff is set to 80 Ry, the Brillouin zone is sampled with a
10\times 6\times 4
Monkhorst-Pack grid and the phonon dispersion is obtained from density functional perturbation theory on a
5\times 3\times 2
q-point mesh
baroni_phonons_2001
. The crystal lattice vectors are chosen to reproduce the experimental one from Ref.
mar_metal-metal_1992
, and the crystal structure is relaxed within that unit cell. The electron and phonon eigenvalues, as well as the electron-phonon coupling matrix
g_{nm}^{\rm\nu}({\bf k},{\bf q})
, are interpolated on a dense
40\times 24\times 16
grid via maximally-localized Wannier functions
marzari_maximally_2012
within the
EPW
code
lee_electronphonon_2023
, which uses
Wannier90
as a library
pizzi_wannier90_2020
.

Ab initio
description of coherent phonon excitation.

The theoretical framework for the simulation of the coherent lattice dynamics is based on the solution of the coherent phonon equation of motion
emeis_coherent_2025
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}Q_{\nu}+\gamma_{\nu}\partial_{t}Q_{\nu}+\omega^{2}_{\nu}Q_{\nu}=D_{\nu}(t)\quad.

(22)

Here,
\partial_{t}=\partial/\partial_{t}
and
Q_{\nu}
is the adimensional displacement amplitude of the coherent phonon with frequency
\omega_{\nu}
.
\gamma_{\nu}
is the coherent phonon damping rate mediated by phonon-phonon interactions
pan_origin_2025
. The room-temperature coherent phonon damping rate is estimated from the third-order force constant, which is obtained from finite differences using the
third-order.py
utility of the
shengBTE
code
li_shengbte_2014
.
The term on the right-hand side of Eq.
22
is the coherent phonon driving force due to electron-phonon interaction:

\displaystyle D_{\nu}(t)=-\frac{2\omega_{\nu}}{\hbar}\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}g_{nn}^{\nu}(\mathbf{k},0)\Delta f_{n\mathbf{k}}(t)\quad,

(23)

where
\Delta f_{n{\bf k}}=f_{n{\bf k}}(t)-f_{n{\bf k}}^{(0)}
is the change of the occupation function with
f_{n{\bf k}}^{(0)}
the (equilibrium) Fermi-Dirac distribution before excitation. The time-dependent electron distribution function is obtained within the time-dependent Boltzmann equations (TDBE)
caruso_ultrafast_2022
;
pan_strain-induced_2024
as implemented in the
EPW
code. The TDBE is solved using an excited-state Ansatz for the electrons described by a high-temperature Fermi-Dirac distribution
f_{n{\bf k}}(t=0)=[{\rm exp}((\varepsilon_{n{\bf k}}-\mu)/k_{\rm B}T_{\rm exc})+1]^{-1}
, where the excitation temperature
T_{\rm exc}
is set to match the energy transferred to the system by an incident pump with fluence
F
, as discussed in Ref.
emeis_coherent_2025
. The TDBE are solved through second-order Runge-Kutta time-stepping with a time interval of 1 fs, within an energy window of 4 eV around the Fermi energy.
The coherent phonon amplitude obtained from Eqs. (
22
) and (
23
) is illustrated in Fig. S3 (a) (single-pulse excitation).

Based on this framework, the maximum coherent-phonon amplitude
Q_{\nu}^{\rm max}(F)
is estimated by considering the static limit of Eq.
22
(
\partial_{t}Q^{\nu}=0
) leading to
Q_{\nu}^{\rm max}(F)=D_{\nu}/\omega^{2}_{\nu}
.
This value constitutes an upper theoretical limit to the maximum lattice displacement that the crystal can undergo for a given electronic excitation
\Delta f_{n\mathbf{k}}
.

To simulate the coherent lattice dynamics induced by two distinct laser pulses, the coherent phonon equation of motion is solved by considering the action of two driving forces:

\displaystyle D^{\nu}_{\rm dp}(t;\Delta t_{p_{1}-p_{2}})=D_{\nu}(t)+D_{\nu}(t-\Delta t_{p_{1}-p_{2}})\theta(\Delta t_{p_{1}-p_{2}})\quad,

(24)

where
\Delta t_{p_{1}-p_{2}}
is the time delay between the two pump pulses.
The coherent phonon amplitudes obtained from the time propagation of Eqs. ((
22
)-(
24
)) is illustrated in Figs. S3 (b-d) for different values of the time delay
\Delta t_{p_{1}-p_{2}}
between the two pulses.
The frequency renormalization proportional to the coherent phonon amplitude observed in experiments can be phenomenologically accounted for by substituting the bare phonon frequency
\omega_{\nu}
with the dressed frequency
\Omega_{\nu}=\omega_{\nu}-\beta|Q_{\nu}|
in Eq. (
22
), where
\beta
is a positive fitting parameter.
