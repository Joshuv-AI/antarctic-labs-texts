SpeakerSleuth: Evaluating Large Audio-Language Models as Judges for Multi-turn Speaker Consistency
Jonggeun Lee, Junseong Pyo, Gyuhyeon Seo, Yohan Jo

Abstract
Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs) as judges have emerged as a prominent approach for evaluating speech generation quality, yet their ability to assess speaker consistency across multi-turn conversations remains unexplored. We present SpeakerSleuth , a benchmark evaluating whether LALMs can reliably judge speaker consistency in multi-turn dialogues through three tasks reflecting real-world requirements. We construct 1,818 human-verified evaluation instances across four diverse datasets spanning synthetic and real speech, with controlled acoustic difficulty. Evaluating nine widely-used LALMs, we find that models struggle to reliably detect acoustic inconsistencies. For instance, given audio samples of the same speaker’s turns, some models overpredict inconsistency, whereas others are overly lenient. Models further struggle to identify the exact turns that are problematic. When other interlocutors’ turns are provided together, performance degrades dramatically as models prioritize textual coherence over acoustic cues, failing to detect even obvious gender switches for a speaker. On the other hand, models perform substantially better in choosing the audio that best matches the speaker among several acoustic variants, demonstrating inherent acoustic discrimination capabilities. These findings expose a significant bias in LALMs: they tend to prioritize text over acoustics, revealing fundamental modality imbalances that need to be addressed to build reliable audio-language judges. 1 1 1 We will release our code and data upon publication.

Introduction

Recent advances in speech synthesis have enabled systems that produce natural, human-like speech
9
;
51
;
7
. These technologies enable diverse applications including voice assistants
1
, voice-overs in podcast generation
12
and movies
37
, and conversational agents
27
. A fundamental requirement for these systems is maintaining consistent speaker identity
26
, that is, preserving acoustic characteristics such as timbre, pitch, and voice quality across all utterances in a multi-turn conversation.
This is particularly important in the speech synthesis of multiple conversation participants, such as voice-overs in movies.
However, achieving this consistency across long-form, multi-turn dialogues remains challenging
44
. Even recent models suffer from speaker confusion
3
;
51
, timbre drift
14
, and voice quality variations
29
. These inconsistencies necessitate reliable verification methods to assess speaker consistency in generated speech.

Most approaches
51
;
19
evaluate speaker consistency by computing acoustic similarity between utterances using embedding models
8
;
6
. However, these methods face fundamental limitations when applied to dialogue evaluation: they operate on pairwise comparisons between two utterances, require manually-set thresholds for binary decisions, and cannot assess consistency holistically across entire conversations.

Recently, Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs) have emerged as potential alternatives for evaluating speech generation quality
39
;
41
. Unlike embedding-based methods that compute pairwise similarities, LALMs can process entire conversations at once, receiving multi-turn dialogues (both text and audio) and directly outputting judgments about speaker consistency.

However, two critical gaps remain: First, no unified benchmark exists to systematically evaluate and compare embedding methods and LALMs for multi-turn speaker consistency assessment. Second, whether LALMs possess the acoustic discrimination capabilities necessary for reliable speaker consistency judgment remains unexplored.

To address these questions, we present
SpeakerSleuth
, a benchmark for evaluating both LALMs and embedding-based methods on speaker consistency in multi-turn dialogues. We design our benchmark around three tasks that mirror real-world application requirements (Figure
1
). These tasks are
Detection
(identifying whether dialogues contain inconsistencies),
Localization
(pinpointing which specific turns are problematic), and
Discrimination
(choosing the correct audio of a speaker from multiple acoustic variants). These capabilities are essential for practical speech generation systems. When dialogue speech is generated, systems must first detect any inconsistencies for each speaker, then localize problematic turns for targeted correction, and finally select optimal outputs from regenerated alternatives. We construct our benchmark from four diverse datasets spanning synthetic and real speech across various conversational settings, comprising 1,818 evaluation instances from 152 speakers, all verified through human annotation to ensure quality.

Our evaluation of nine state-of-the-art LALMs and embedding methods reveals critical insights into their capabilities. We find that models struggle to reliably detect acoustic inconsistencies due to unstable internal thresholds. This leads to inconsistent decisions where some models are too strict while others are too lenient. Moreover, their detection decisions rely on holistic impressions rather than fine-grained acoustic analysis, as evidenced by their inability to localize specific problematic turns even when detecting overall inconsistency. When other interlocutors’ turns are available as dialogue context, LALMs overwhelmingly tend to prioritize textual coherence over acoustic features. They fail to detect even obvious inconsistencies like gender switches for a speaker within coherent dialogue. In contrast, embedding methods achieve stronger detection performance, although they exhibit consistent model-specific biases.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We present SpeakerSleuth, the first benchmark for multi-turn speaker consistency evaluation with 1,818 human-verified instances.

•

We comprehensively evaluate nine LALMs and embedding methods, revealing that models struggle with detection and localization.

•

We identify modality imbalances where LALMs prioritize textual context over their acoustic discrimination capabilities, providing insights for building reliable audio-language judges.

3
Task Formulation

To thoroughly evaluate whether LALMs can reliably distinguish speakers based on acoustic features, we propose an evaluation framework. Rather than relying on a single metric, we decompose speaker consistency into three capabilities:
Detection
(identifying whether all turns are consistent),
Localization
(pinpointing which turn is inconsistent), and
Discrimination
(identifying the original speaker among acoustically modified alternatives).

Formally, we define a multi-turn dialogue as
\mathcal{D}=\{(t_{1},a_{1}),(t_{2},a_{2}),\ldots,(t_{N},a_{N})\}
, where
t_{i}
represents the transcript and
a_{i}
denotes the audio waveform of the
i
-th turn.
Let
I\subset\{1,\ldots,N\}
denote the indices of turns belonging to a specific target speaker
S
. We denote the audio turns of the target speaker as
\mathcal{A}_{S}=\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
.

3.1
Task 1: Detection

In TTS and voice cloning applications, ensuring speaker consistency across generated outputs is critical for quality control. The most fundamental requirement is to detect whether all turns belong to the same speaker. This requires
absolute judgment capability
, where the model must rely on its internal threshold to determine consistency.

We formulate this as a binary classification problem. Given
\mathcal{A}_{S}
, the model predicts:

\hat{y}\in\{\text{Consistent},\text{Inconsistent}\}

(1)

where the ground truth
y
is
Consistent
only if all audio in
\mathcal{A}_{S}
maintains the identity of speaker
S
.

Success on this task demonstrates that the model possesses an appropriate internal threshold to reliably judge speaker identity based on acoustic features. The detailed prompt used for this task is provided in Appendix
E.1
.

3.2
Task 2: Localization

When speaker inconsistencies are detected in multi-turn dialogues, identifying the exact problematic segment is essential for efficient correction and regeneration. Merely detecting an anomaly is insufficient; a robust judge must pinpoint where the inconsistency occurs to enable targeted fixes.

Given
\mathcal{A}_{S}
, the model must identify which turn, if any, disrupts speaker identity. The model predicts:

\hat{k}\in I\cup\{\text{None}\}

(2)

where
\hat{k}=\text{None}
indicates that all audio turns in
\mathcal{A}_{S}
are consistent.

Success on this task demonstrates that the model can distinguish acoustic speaker characteristics at a fine-grained level, rather than relying on dialogue-level patterns. The detailed prompt used for this task is provided in Appendix
E.2
.

3.3
Task 3: Discrimination

When an inconsistent turn is identified (Task 2), TTS systems typically regenerate multiple candidate outputs and must select the one that best matches the target speaker. This requires
relative judgment capability
, the ability to compare and rank audio samples by their acoustic similarity to a reference speaker, rather than making absolute binary decisions.

Given
\mathcal{A}_{S}
where a target turn is masked, the model is presented with a set of three audio candidates
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}
to fill the masked position. These candidates represent varying levels of acoustic similarity to the original speaker. The order of candidates is randomized to avoid positional bias
30
. The model predicts:

\hat{c}\in\mathcal{C}

(3)

where
\hat{c}
is the selected audio that the model believes best matches the target speaker.

Success on this task indicates that the model can discriminate speakers acoustically. The detailed prompt used for this task is provided in Appendix
E.3
.

3.4
Distinction from Traditional Speaker Recognition

It is important to note that our tasks differ fundamentally from traditional speaker recognition
10
;
11
, which consists of two main tasks: Speaker Identification determines
who is speaking
from known speakers, while Speaker Diarization segments continuous audio streams to determine
who spoke when
. In contrast, we evaluate
speaker consistency
: given turns that are assumed or claimed to belong to the same speaker, we assess whether the model can verify that they actually maintain acoustic coherence.

4
SpeakerSleuth

To systematically evaluate the capabilities defined in Section
3
, we introduce SpeakerSleuth, a benchmark comprised of multi-turn dialogues with rigorous acoustic and contextual controls. Figure
2
illustrates our benchmark construction pipeline.

4.1
Step 1. Data Collection

We collect dialogues with audio and transcripts from four datasets to ensure diversity in conversational domains and styles (Figure
2
-1):

Bazinga

20
: Contains multi-party dialogues from TV shows and movies (e.g.,
Friends, Harry Potter
), testing the model’s ability to track speakers in dynamic, scripted interactions.

AMI

4
: Consists of spontaneous business meetings, assessing performance in formal, overlapping, and noisy environments.

Behavior-SD

19
: Provides synthesized dialogues with controlled speech behaviors (e.g., fillers, backchannels), enabling evaluation on clean, generated speech.

DailyTalk

18
: Captures high-quality everyday conversations, serving as a baseline for casual social interaction.

From these sources, we construct an initial pool of 3,682 dialogues spanning 1,358 unique speakers for sampling.

4.2
Step 2. Scenario Generation

Dialogue Extraction.

From the collected pool, we select dialogues and extract segments where a target speaker appears multiple times across the conversation, with constraints on both the number of target speaker turns and total segment length. We also sample a reference audio of the target speaker from outside these segments.

Scenario Construction.

As illustrated in Figure
2
-2, we create three scenarios per dialogue to systematically test acoustic discrimination capabilities: Original conversations serve as positive samples (
S1: Fully Consistent
), establishing baseline performance when acoustic and textual cues are naturally aligned. To create inconsistent scenarios, we randomly select one turn and apply voice conversion using FreeVC
21
, which transforms acoustic timbre while preserving linguistic and prosodic content. For
S2 (Gender Switch)
, we convert the turn to an opposite-gender voice sampled from the pool, creating relatively clear acoustic deviations. For
S3 (Similar Speaker)
, we convert it to an acoustically similar speaker, selected by computing ECAPA-TDNN
8
embeddings and choosing the highest cosine similarity (excluding the target speaker). This requires fine-grained discrimination of subtle timbre differences. By using identical dialogue content across all scenarios, we isolate acoustic discrimination from confounding factors. Implementation details are in Appendix
A.2
.

Experimental Setup

5.1
Evaluation Protocol

Our primary evaluation uses audio-only input with reference audio to directly assess acoustic discrimination capabilities. For each task, LALMs receive at least a 3-second reference sample
38
of the target speaker to judge speaker consistency across the dialogue. This setting reflects realistic text-to-speech (TTS) scenarios, where TTS systems commonly provide target speaker samples to guide generation
22
;
38
. It also enables fair comparison between LALMs and embedding-based methods, as the latter operate on audio-only input and require reference samples.

5.2
Models

5.2.1
LALM Judges

We assess nine state-of-the-art LALMs: GPT-4o-audio

2

45

46

5.2.2
Speaker Embedding Methods

We evaluate three speaker embedding methods using ECAPA-TDNN
8
and WavLM
6
. All methods classify turns as inconsistent when their similarity metric falls below a threshold
\tau
, flag all such turns for localization, and select the highest-similarity candidate for discrimination.

Pairwise Similarity
computes average pairwise cosine similarity between all embedding pairs (
\tau_{\text{pair}}=0.4
).
Centroid Distance
measures distance from each embedding to the centroid of context embeddings (
\tau_{\text{cent}}=0.3
).
Reference Comparison
computes similarity between each embedding and a separate reference speaker audio (
\tau_{\text{ref}}=0.4
).
Thresholds are set based on preliminary validation. Detailed algorithms and threshold sensitivity analysis are in Appendix
B.1
.

5.2.3
Evaluation Metrics

We report accuracy scores for Detection and Discrimination, and F1-scores for Localization as primary metrics. Detailed metric computation methods are provided in Appendix
B.3
, along with additional metrics (Precision, Recall, Exact-Match).

Main Results

6.1
Detection and Localization Performance

Table
2
presents detection and localization performance. We evaluate whether models possess stable internal thresholds (Detection) and fine-grained acoustic perception (Localization).

LALMs.

LALMs exhibit two critical limitations. First, they lack balanced internal thresholds. Models such as MiniCPM-o impartially accept consistent speakers at high rates (S1 at 85.3%) while catastrophically failing to detect speaker changes (S2/S3 at 0.7%/0.0%). In contrast, models such as Gemini-2.5-Flash overly reject consistent speakers (S1 at 40.8%) despite reasonable change detection (S2/S3 around 70%). This instability results in poor overall detection accuracy, with the best model (Gemini-2.5-Flash) achieving only 60.1% and most models scoring below 50%.

Second, localization results reveal that models cannot identify which specific turn contains speaker inconsistency, even when they detect that inconsistency exists in the given audio turns. F1-scores on S2/S3 fall below 25%, only slightly above random selection among the five candidate turns. This indicates that models form impressions based on the overall dialogue rather than performing turn-by-turn acoustic analysis. In other words, they can sense that something is inconsistent across the conversation but cannot pinpoint the exact moment where speaker identity shifts. This directly contradicts the localization task requirement to ground judgments in specific acoustic evidence at the turn level. The modest performance gap between S2 (gender switch) and S3 (similar speaker) further confirms that models cannot fully exploit even coarse-grained acoustic cues.

Speaker Embedding Methods.

For the Detection task, speaker embedding methods achieve stronger performance with ECAPA-TDNN Pairwise at 70.2%, but exhibit systematic biases. ECAPA-TDNN-based methods achieve high accuracy in detecting changes (S2/S3 at 73-95%) but incorrectly reject consistent speakers at high rates (S1 at 8-47%), indicating high sensitivity and conservative thresholds. Conversely, WavLM-based methods show high acceptance of consistent speakers (S1 at 79-92%) but poor change detection (S2/S3 at 32-38%), reflecting lower sensitivity to speaker-specific variations.

Despite their substantially higher performance than LALMs on the Detection task, their performance on the Localization task is surprisingly similar. This indicates that their high detection accuracy may be due to chance and based on incorrect evidence; specifically, inconsistency is being determined based on consistent audio turns.

6.2
Discrimination Performance

LALMs.

Table
2
presents discrimination results, where models perform relative judgment by comparing three audio candidates. Compared to detection performance, discrimination performance improves substantially, with GPT-4o achieving 82.6% accuracy and several models exceeding 60%. This dissociation validates our task design. GPT-4o achieves only 45.1% in detection but 82.6% in discrimination, indicating the model can perceive acoustic differences when directly comparing candidates but lacks stable internal thresholds for making reliable binary judgments without such direct comparisons. Performance remains consistent across domains, with dataset averages ranging from 50-56%, confirming our benchmark does not favor specific conversational settings.

Speaker Embedding Methods.

Speaker embedding methods achieve near-perfect discrimination performance (93-99% average), with ECAPA-TDNN reaching 99-100% on most datasets. This exceptional performance is expected, as discrimination requires acoustic similarity computation, which is the core function for which embeddings are designed. However, performance varies across datasets, particularly for WavLM-based methods (ranging from 73-100%). The stark contrast between embeddings (93-99%) and LALMs (52%) highlights that specialized acoustic representations remain superior for relative comparison tasks.

7
Further Analyses of LALMs

7.1
Impact of Textual Context

Our main results evaluate models using only the target speaker’s audio turns. However, unlike embedding-based methods, LALMs have the potential to leverage additional signals from the dialogue context to identify speaker inconsistencies more accurately. To verify this, we conduct an additional analysis where the full dialogue is provided to LALM judges, with non-target interlocutor turns provided in text form while the target speaker’s turns are provided as audio.

Table
3
shows that adding textual context creates a striking asymmetry. S1 performance improves dramatically (most models >90%) while S2/S3 collapse (most <10%). Models prioritize textual coherence over acoustic features. When dialogue text flows naturally, they judge speakers as consistent regardless of acoustic evidence, failing to detect even obvious inconsistencies like gender switches.
This behavior aligns with recent findings on attention imbalance in LALMs
40
, which demonstrate that LALMs allocate more attention to text tokens than audio tokens. Our results provide empirical evidence of this phenomenon’s practical impact. When textual context is present, models essentially default to text-based reasoning. Localization results exhibit the same pattern (see Appendix
D.1
).

These findings reveal that improving LALMs as speaker consistency judges requires not just better acoustic representations, but mechanisms to balance attention allocation during multi-modal fusion. Developing methods to better leverage dialogue context while maintaining acoustic sensitivity would be a promising future direction.

7.2
Impact of Reference Audio

In our main evaluation, we provide models with a single reference audio sample from the target speaker to establish a comparison baseline. We investigate what happens when this reference is absent, requiring models to judge consistency solely from the dialogue turns themselves.

Table
4
shows that removing reference audio causes models to default to
Consistent
judgments: S1 accuracy rises to up to 99% while S2 and S3 drops below 15%. Without an explicit comparison anchor, models adopt extremely lenient thresholds that accept nearly all inputs as consistent, failing to detect even gender switches. Localization results exhibit the same pattern (see Appendix
D.2
).

This reveals that current LALMs cannot establish appropriate decision boundaries without explicit references. Rather than developing robust internal speaker representations from dialogue context alone, models rely heavily on explicit reference audio to anchor their judgments. This pattern connects to our earlier finding from the discrimination task (Table
2
), where models achieved substantially better performance through relative judgment.

These findings have important practical implications: in real-world applications such as TTS validation, providing reference audio is essential for reliable speaker consistency judgment.

8
Conclusion

We present SpeakerSleuth, a benchmark for evaluating whether LALMs can reliably judge speaker consistency in multi-turn dialogues. We design our benchmark around three tasks that mirror real-world application requirements: Detection, Localization, and Discrimination. Our benchmark comprises 1,818 human-verified instances across four diverse datasets. Our evaluation reveals fundamental limitations: models lack stable internal thresholds, rely on holistic rather than fine-grained analysis, and prioritize textual coherence over acoustic features. However, they demonstrate inherent acoustic discrimination capabilities, revealing critical modality imbalances for building reliable audio-language judges.

Limitations

SpeakerSleuth has several limitations.
First, our study focuses on English dialogues where target speakers appear a fixed number of times per conversation. Consequently, it may not capture the complexities of multilingual settings. Our synthetic data generation methodology can be readily extended to varying turn counts and longer dialogues.

Second, while our benchmark includes 4 datasets spanning distinct domains with naturally varying acoustic conditions, we do not separately analyze performance by specific acoustic factors such as background noise, reverberation, or recording quality. Our benchmark design allows for straightforward incorporation of such controlled acoustic variations in future work, enabling systematic evaluation of model robustness across specific acoustic challenges.

Third, even with state-of-the-art voice conversion, fully reproducing the original speaker’s unique timbre and emotional expressiveness remains challenging. We addressed this through rigorous human verification of all synthetic utterances, ensuring that the generated speech meets perceptual quality standards and maintains speaker consistency.

Finally, we did not explicitly analyze model biases regarding speaker demographics (e.g., accents, age groups), which warrants further investigation to ensure equitable performance across diverse populations.

Despite these limitations, we believe SpeakerSleuth is useful for systematically characterizing current LALMs’ speaker consistency judgment behaviors and motivating more robust evaluation of acoustic features in audio-language models.

Non-native speaker accents and speech patterns

A.2
Scenario Generation Details

From the initial pool of 1,358 speakers and 3,682 dialogues, we apply a structured sampling procedure to extract dialogue segments.

A.2.1
Dialogue Segment Extraction

Due to varying dialogue lengths across datasets, we employ different extraction strategies:

Long-form Datasets (Bazinga, AMI).

These datasets contain extended conversations. To maximize sample diversity, we extract multiple dialogue segments from a single conversation, with each segment containing exactly 5 consecutive audio turns from the target speaker. We limit extraction to a maximum of 5 segments per dialogue to prevent over-representation of individual conversations.

Short-form Datasets (Behavior-SD, DailyTalk).

These datasets contain shorter, more focused dialogues. We extract one dialogue segment per conversation, ensuring each segment includes 5 consecutive target speaker turns.

Context Window Constraints.

To balance conversational context with model limitations, we constrain dialogue segments to a maximum of 20 total turns (across all speakers). This ensures sufficient context while remaining within model context limits.

A.2.2
Scenario Construction

For each extracted dialogue segment, we randomly select one turn from the target speaker’s 5 audio samples and apply voice conversion using FreeVC
21
to create three evaluation scenarios:

•

S1 (Fully Consistent):
Original audio without modification, serving as the baseline condition

•

S2 (Gender Switch):
The selected turn converted to an opposite-gender speaker, testing coarse-grained acoustic discrimination

•

S3 (Similar Speaker):
The selected turn converted to an acoustically similar same-gender speaker (selected via ECAPA-TDNN
8
embeddings), testing fine-grained discrimination

B.1
Speaker Embedding Methods Details

In this section, we explain how we use speaker embeddings to find inconsistent turns and choose the correct answers. We use three different ways to measure consistency: Pairwise Similarity, Centroid Distance and Reference Comparison.

Pairwise Similarity.

The Pairwise Similarity evaluates the internal consistency of context embeddings
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
. By measuring the similarity between all pairs, it identifies turns that have low similarity to the rest of the group.

•

Detection:
We classify a dialogue as inconsistent if any turn
i
shows a mean similarity to all other turns that falls below the threshold
\tau_{\text{pair}}
:

\exists i:\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\text{sim}(e_{i},e_{j})<\tau_{\text{pair}}

•

Localization:
Turn
i
is flagged as the source of inconsistency if its embedding deviates significantly from the mutual agreement of the context group:

\text{Flag turn }i\text{ if }\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\text{sim}(e_{i},e_{j})<\tau_{\text{pair}}

•

Discrimination:
Given options
\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{k}\}
, the model selects the candidate that maximizes the aggregate similarity with all original context embeddings:

\arg\max_{j}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{sim}(o_{j},e_{i})

Centroid Distance.

Centroid Distance defines the reference point
c
as the arithmetic mean of context embeddings,
c=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}
. Distance is defined as
\text{dist}(x,y)=1-\text{sim}(x,y)
.

•

Detection:
Classify as inconsistent if

\exists i:\text{dist}(e_{i},c)>\tau_{\text{cent}}

•

Localization:
Flag turn
i
if

\text{dist}(e_{i},c)>\tau_{\text{cent}}

•

Discrimination:
Given options
\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{k}\}
, select

\arg\min_{j}\text{dist}(o_{j},c)

Reference Comparison.

The Reference Comparison method evaluates each context embedding and candidate option by comparing them directly against a fixed reference embedding
r
.

•

Detection:
Classify as inconsistent if

\exists i:\text{sim}(e_{i},r)<\tau_{\text{ref}}

•

Localization:
Flag turn
i
if

\text{sim}(e_{i},r)<\tau_{\text{ref}}

•

Discrimination:
Given options
\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{k}\}
, select

\arg\max_{j}\text{sim}(o_{j},r)

Threshold Selection

We set
\tau_{\text{pair}}=\tau_{\text{ref}}=0.4
and
\tau_{\text{cent}}=0.3
based on preliminary validation. Sensitivity analysis is in Appendix
7
.

B.3
Derivation of Evaluation Metrics

To provide a comprehensive assessment of Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs) as speaker consistency judges, we employ metrics that capture both global detection and fine-grained localization accuracy.

B.3.1
Task 1: Detection

The Detection task is treated as a binary classification problem where the model identifies whether a dialogue is consistent (
y=1
) or inconsistent (
y=0
).

We use
Accuracy
as the primary metric:

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}

(4)

where:

•

TP
(True Positive): Consistent dialogues correctly predicted as consistent

•

TN
(True Negative): Inconsistent dialogues correctly predicted as inconsistent

•

FP
(False Positive): Inconsistent dialogues incorrectly predicted as consistent

•

FN
(False Negative): Consistent dialogues incorrectly predicted as inconsistent

We additionally report Precision, Recall, and F1-score for comprehensive analysis:

\text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad\text{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}

(5)

\text{F1}=2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}

(6)

Note that in our benchmark, Scenario 1 (S1) contains only consistent dialogues (
y=1
), while Scenarios 2 and 3 (S2, S3) contain only inconsistent dialogues (
y=0
). Therefore, we report accuracy separately for each scenario to reveal threshold calibration patterns across models.

B.3.2
Task 2: Localization

The Localization task requires identifying which specific turn(s) among the target speaker’s utterances are inconsistent. This is formulated as a multi-label classification problem where each turn
i\in I
receives a binary prediction indicating whether it is inconsistent.

We use
F1-score
as the primary metric, computed at the turn level for each sample and then averaged across all samples:

\text{Precision}_{j}=\frac{|P_{j}\cap G_{j}|}{|P_{j}|},\quad\text{Recall}_{j}=\frac{|P_{j}\cap G_{j}|}{|G_{j}|}

(7)

where
P_{j}
denotes the set of turns predicted as inconsistent for sample
j
, and
G_{j}
denotes the ground-truth set of inconsistent turns. The F1-score for each sample is:

\text{F1}_{j}=\begin{cases}2\cdot\frac{\text{Precision}_{j}\cdot\text{Recall}_{j}}{\text{Precision}_{j}+\text{Recall}_{j}}&\text{if }|P_{j}|+|G_{j}|>0\\
1&\text{if }|P_{j}|=|G_{j}|=0\end{cases}

(8)

The overall F1-score is the macro-average across all
N
samples:

\text{F1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\text{F1}_{j}

(9)

We also report
Exact Match (EM)
, which measures the proportion of samples where the predicted set of inconsistent turns exactly matches the ground truth:

\text{EM}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[P_{j}=G_{j}]

(10)

For Scenario 1, the ground truth is
G_{j}=\emptyset
, requiring models to correctly predict no inconsistent turns. For Scenarios 2 and 3, exactly one turn is inconsistent per dialogue, i.e.,
|G_{j}|=1
.

B.3.3
Task 3: Discrimination

The Discrimination task presents models with three audio candidates and requires selecting the one that matches the target speaker.

We use
Accuracy
as the evaluation metric:

\text{Accuracy}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}[\hat{a}_{j}=a_{j}^{*}]

(11)

where
\hat{a}_{j}\in\{A,B,C\}
is the model’s predicted selection and
a_{j}^{*}
is the ground-truth original audio for sample
j
.

This task isolates the model’s acoustic discrimination capability from threshold calibration challenges, as it requires only relative comparison rather than absolute judgment.

Appendix G
Potential Risks

Despite positive applications, we acknowledge several risks. Advanced speaker consistency evaluation could be exploited to create more convincing synthetic voices for impersonation, fraud, or misinformation campaigns. As synthetic speech becomes increasingly indistinguishable from human speech, public trust in audio evidence and voice-based authentication may diminish. Additionally, technologies that analyze speaker characteristics could be misappropriated for unauthorized surveillance or profiling.
