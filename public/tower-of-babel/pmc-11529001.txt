Impact of red and blue monochromatic light on the visual system and dopamine pathways in juvenile zebrafish
Authors: Yan Lu, Meiling Tong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe development of the zebrafish visual system is significantly influenced by exposure to monochromatic light, yet investigations into its effects during juvenile stages are lacking. This study evaluated the impacts of varying intensities and durations of red and blue monochromatic light on the visual system and dopamine pathways in juvenile zebrafish.MethodsJuvenile zebrafish were exposed to red (650 nm) and blue (440 nm, 460 nm) monochromatic lights over four days at intensities ranging from 500 to 10,000 lx, for durations of 6, 10, and 14 h daily. A control group was maintained under standard laboratory conditions. Post-exposure assessments included the optokinetic response (OKR), retinal structural analysis, ocular dopamine levels, and the expression of genes related to dopamine pathways (Th, Dat, and Mao).Results(1) OKR enhancement was observed with increased 440 nm light intensity, while 460 nm and 650 nm light exposures showed initial improvements followed by declines at higher intensities. (2) Retinal thinning in the outer nuclear layer was observed under the most intense (10,000 lx for 14 h) light conditions in the 440 nm and 650 nm groups, while the 460 nm group remained unaffected. (3) Dopamine levels increased with higher intensities in the 440 nm group, whereas the 460 nm group exhibited initial increases followed by decreases. The 650 nm group displayed similar trends but were statistically insignificant compared to the control group. (4) Th expression increased with light intensity in the 440 nm group. Dat showed a rising and then declining pattern, and Mao expression significantly decreased. The 460 nm group exhibited similar patterns for Th and Dat to the behavioral observations, but an inverse pattern for Mao. The 650 nm group presented significant fluctuations in Th and Dat expressions, with pronounced variations in Mao.ConclusionsSpecific red and blue monochromatic light conditions promote visual system development in juvenile zebrafish. However, exceeding these optimal conditions may impair visual function, highlighting the critical role of dopamine pathway in modulating light-induced effects on the visual system.

Background

Light exposure is a pivotal environmental factor that influences vision. The pervasive use of artificial lighting and electronic devices has escalated concerns about childhood vision problems, particularly myopia, posing a considerable public health issue [1, 2]. Research worldwide continues to explore the role of light in vision development, with a particular focus on outdoor lighting [3–5]. There is substantial empirical evidence that outdoor activities may help slow the progression of myopia, a benefit closely linked to the duration of outdoor exposure [6–9]. Dopamine, an essential neurotransmitter, plays a crucial role in the development of the visual system, impacting ocular growth and visual functionality [10–13]. The synthesis and release of dopamine, stimulated by light exposure, are key to the protective effects of outdoor lighting on vision [14, 15]. Unlike natural outdoor light, which offers a spectrum of wavelengths including ultraviolet, blue, green, and red light, the lighting conditions experienced by children and adolescents today have significantly altered in duration, intensity, and spectrum, complicating our understanding of how light affects vision.

This study aims to delve deeper into the effects of artificial illumination, specifically focusing on blue and red monochromatic light, each with its unique wavelength. The choice of zebrafish as the subject of this study is based on several beneficial traits [16–19], including their dependence on the ocular lens for primary refraction, tetrachromatic color vision with four types of color receptors, significant evolutionary similarities with humans, cost-effectiveness, optical transparency, high reproductive output, rapid maturation, and the ease of conducting ocular examinations [20, 21]. By evaluating both light intensity and exposure duration, we explored the response of the juvenile zebrafish visual system to various monochromatic lighting conditions, focusing on visual function and retinal structure. Moreover, we conducted a detailed analysis of dopamine levels in the zebrafish eyes and the expression patterns of genes related to the dopamine pathway (i.e. tyrosine hydroxylase (Th), dopamine transporter (Dat) and monoamine oxidase (Mao)) under different lighting scenarios. By conducting a thorough quantitative analysis of these parameters and investigating the underlying mechanisms, we provided scientific evidence to establish optimal lighting conditions that promote children’s visual health in this study. This could offer new insights and recommendations for the advancement of visual function.

Methods

Fish husbandry and embryo production

Zebrafish larvae were sourced from the Tuebingen (TU) strain progenitors for this study. To streamline embryo collection, three pairs of adult zebrafish were isolated overnight in individual tanks with transparent dividers separating males from females. Following morning illumination, the dividers were removed to allow mating for one hour. Embryos were collected immediately post-fertilization, designated as 0 day post-fertilization (dpf), gently rinsed, and placed in Petri dishes filled with embryo culture medium. These dishes were then placed in a constant temperature incubator set at 28℃. The culture medium was refreshed daily, with meticulous removal of unfertilized or deceased embryos. After hatching, larvae were housed in a temperature-controlled environment at 28℃ with a 14/10-hour light/dark cycle (light from 8:30 to 22:30, dark from 22:30 to 8:30), ensuring an illuminance of 200 to 400 lx. A constant temperature water exchange system and water temperature monitors were used to mitigate the effects of temperature fluctuations on the study. Fish were maintained in water baths equipped with built-in temperature sensors to ensure a consistent temperature of 28℃.

Monochromatic lighting protocols

The study utilized LED lights that emitted specific wavelengths: 440 nm and 460 nm for blue light, and 650 nm for red light. The lighting regimen was structured around three durations: 6, 10, and 14 h. These durations refer to the typical daily average duration of artificial light exposure at different levels. Illumination intensities were set at 500, 1000, 1500, 5000, and 10,000 lx. Zebrafish larvae, hatched and monitored from 3 dpf to 6 dpf, were subjected to these monochromatic lighting conditions. A control group was kept under standard room lighting to provide a baseline for comparison.

Optokinetic response analysis

The optokinetic response (OKR) is a robust method for assessing eye-tracking capabilities in zebrafish, characterized by slow tracking movements (slow phase) followed by rapid-resetting saccades (fast phase) [22–25]. This reflex, which stabilizes the visual field on the retina and facilitates high-resolution vision, is common among vertebrates and serves as an indicator of visual acuity in zebrafish. Notably, the gain value (the ratio of mean angular velocity of the OKR’s slow phase to stimulus velocity) correlates directly with visual acuity in zebrafish larvae, detectable as early as 3 days post-fertilization (dpf) and fully maturing by 5 dpf. At 7 dpf, larvae were dorsally immobilized in a 3-cm diameter petri dish using a 3% methyl cellulose solution to restrict body movement while allowing free eye movement. This immobilization step was essential for ensuring accurate eye-tracking measurements. The dish containing the larvae was placed on a stable platform at the center of a rotating drum, lined with high-contrast black and white vertical stripes serving as the visual stimulus. The drum rotated at a constant angular velocity of 18° per second, and with a spatial frequency of 0.06 cycles per degree, ensuring that the stimulus matched the larvae’s visual acuity. A high-resolution camera positioned above the dish recorded eye movements, capturing the larvae’s tracking of the stripes. These recordings were then used to calculate the mean angular velocity of eye movements, from which the OKR gain value was derived. This analysis was conducted for 2 min per larva, and the results were averaged across all larvae within the experimental groups. The experiment took place in a temperature-controlled room maintained at 28 °C, with ambient lighting set between 200 and 400 lx to ensure consistency and prevent external light interference.

Ocular dopamine content measurement using ELISA

Eye samples were collected from each experimental group between 9:00 and 11:00 on the second day post light exposure, with 100 whole eyes gathered per group. The samples were stored individually in Eppendorf (EP) tubes and homogenized in 500 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using an ultrasonic cell crusher. Throughout the homogenization process, all samples were kept on ice. The ultrasonic probe was thoroughly cleaned and dried after each use to avoid cross-contamination between samples. The homogenates were then stored at -20 °C. After undergoing two freeze-thaw cycles, the samples were re-homogenized at 4 °C and centrifuged at 5000 g for 5 min to separate the supernatant for dopamine analysis. The procedure strictly followed the protocols outlined in the Fish Dopamine Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Kit. All steps were performed in triplicate to ensure the accuracy, reproducibility, and reliability of the results.

Paraffin sectioning and hematoxylin-eosin staining for zebrafish tissue analysis

Following light exposure, juvenile zebrafish were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50% ethanol, 70% ethanol, 80% ethanol, 95% ethanol for 30 min each, followed by anhydrous ethanol I, anhydrous ethanol II, and anhydrous ethanol III for 30 min each), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The tissue blocks were sectioned at 5 μm thickness using a paraffin slicer (Finesse ME+, Thermo, USA), mounted on glass slides, and dried. The sections were then dewaxed, rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70%, and 50% ethanol for 1 min each), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using a standard protocol. The stained sections were dehydrated in 95% ethanol for 5 min (repeated three times), cleared in xylene for 5 min (repeated three times), and mounted with neutral gum. Finally, the sections were examined under a microscope (M205A, Leica, Germany), and images were captured for further analysis.

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of our measurements, we employed a standardized methodology for quantifying the thicknesses of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) and inner nuclear layer (INL). For each retinal section, thickness measurements were performed using ImageJ software. Specifically, five different locations within each section were measured for both the ONL and INL thickness, ensuring that the measurements were taken perpendicular to the retinal layer boundaries. These five measurements for each layer were then averaged to obtain the final ONL and INL thickness values for each sample. The ONL to INL ratio was calculated by dividing the average ONL thickness by the average INL thickness for each sample in the experimental groups. This process was repeated for each light exposure condition to ensure consistency and statistical robustness.

Gene expression analysis via quantitative real-time PCR

Following light exposure, larvae were collected at 9:00 the subsequent day to conduct gene expression analysis. Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme Biotech, USA), adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s protocols. Residual genomic DNA was eliminated using RNase-free DNase (Takara, Japan). Subsequently, 1 µg of the isolated RNA was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan) and stored at -20℃.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was conducted using the ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR system (Life Technologies, USA). The amplification protocol included an initial denaturation step of 30 s at 95℃, followed by 40 cycles of 10 s at 95℃ and 30 s at 60℃, and a final extension step of 15 s at 95℃, 60 s at 60℃, and 15 s at 95℃. The reaction mixture for each 10 µL PCR consisted of 1 µL of cDNA, 5 µL of qPCR Master Mix, 3 µL of ddH2O, and 0.5 µL of both forward and reverse primers. Primer sequences used for the RT-qPCR are detailed in Table 1. β-actin was utilized as the internal control. Expression levels of each target gene were quantified in triplicate to ensure accuracy. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. To guarantee reliability and reproducibility, the experiments were biologically replicated three times.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and graph plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. The normality of the data was evaluated, with normally distributed datasets being represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For multiple group comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized. In instances where data were not normally distributed, median and interquartile ranges (IQR: P25, P75) were reported, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized for group comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, indicating meaningful differences among the datasets.

Results

Analysis of zebrafish optokinetic response

The OKR gain values for all experimental groups were confirmed to follow a normal distribution, which facilitated the use of ANOVA to assess the differences in OKR responses among groups under varying light intensities and consistent illumination durations. The OKR gain values under different monochromatic light conditions are shown in Fig. 1.

In the 440 nm blue light group, an increase in light intensity was associated with elevated OKR gain values, demonstrating statistically significant differences (p < 0.05). For the 460 nm blue light exposure, OKR gain values initially rose with increasing light intensity in the 6-hour group but displayed a pattern of initial increase followed by a decrease in the 10-hour and 14-hour groups. Peak values were observed at 5000 lx for 10 h and at 1500 lx for 14 h.

Under 650 nm red light, the 6-hour group exhibited an initial rise in OKR gain values with increasing light intensity, peaking at 5000 lx, followed by a decline. The 10-hour group reached its peak at 500 lx and then manifested a gradual decline. Conversely, the 14-hour group showed no significant differences in OKR gain values when compared to the control group or across varying illumination durations.

The influence of different spectral lights on zebrafish visual acuity varied; 440 nm blue light notably enhanced visual sensitivity, an effect that intensified with increased light exposure. Under specific conditions, both 460 nm and 650 nm light were found to augment eye movement response gains, though these benefits were reduced with more intense or prolonged illumination.

Retinal morphology in juvenile zebrafish following exposure to monochromatic light

The effects of various monochromatic light exposures on the retinal development of juvenile zebrafish were evaluated through histological analysis of retinal sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Figure 2 indicated that the retinal structures in all light exposure groups were well-preserved, displaying distinct layer stratification without any evidence of tissue disorganization, density reduction, or vacuole formation.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, exposure to a high light intensity of 10,000 lx resulted in thinning of the outer nuclear layer for certain groups. To quantify this effect, the ratio of the ONL thickness to the INL thickness was analyzed after 14 h of exposure to each type of monochromatic light; the analysis results are displayed in Fig. 3. This analysis showed that the outer nuclear layer was significantly thinner in the groups exposed to 440 nm blue light and 650 nm red light at 10,000 lx compared to the group exposed to 460 nm blue light, which showed no significant change. These findings underscore the differential impacts of various spectral light exposures on the retinal structure of juvenile zebrafish, particularly at higher intensities where 440 nm blue light and 650 nm red light may induce thinning of the outer nuclear layer.

Dopamine level detection in zebrafish eyes across light exposure groups

As depicted in Fig. 4, the dopamine content in the eyes of juvenile zebrafish exposed to various light intensities was quantified. ANOVA was employed to perform the statistical analysis. The results revealed a progressive increase in dopamine levels in the 440 nm blue light group as light intensity increased. Notably, during a 6-hour exposure period, significant variations in dopamine levels were observed across different light intensities. While an ascending trend in dopamine levels was apparent during 10-hour exposures, this trend did not reach statistical significance. However, during 14-hour exposures, an increase in light intensity was associated with significant elevations in dopamine levels, distinctly higher than those in the control group.

In the 460 nm blue light group, significant differences in dopamine levels were noted across intensity levels during 6-hour exposures, with notably higher levels observed at 10,000 lx compared to 500 lx. For 10-hour and 14-hour exposures, dopamine levels exhibited an inverted U-shaped distribution, peaking at 1500 lx and 1000 lx, respectively. Conversely, the 650 nm red light group displayed a rising trend in dopamine levels with increasing light intensity, yet without significant differences from the control group. In summary, these results demonstrate that 440 nm blue light promotes dopamine synthesis in zebrafish eyes more effectively than 460 nm blue light and 650 nm red light.

Figure 5 illustrates the gene expression for the 440 nm blue light group. Expression of Th significantly increased with higher light intensities, indicating a direct correlation between Th gene response and the intensity and duration of 440 nm blue light exposure (p < 0.05). Discussion

This study aimed to deepen the understanding of the biological impacts of monochromatic light exposure on the visual system, specifically in juvenile zebrafish, by assessing the effects on visual function, retinal structure, and dopamine pathways. By exposing the zebrafish to varying intensities and durations of monochromatic light, we aimed to elucidate the optimal conditions for promoting visual development and to uncover the underlying mechanisms involving dopamine pathways.

Our findings indicate that exposure to 440 nm blue light significantly enhances the optokinetic response (OKR) in zebrafish, suggesting that this wavelength is particularly effective in stimulating the visual system. This enhancement correlates positively with increased light intensity, highlighting the potential of shorter blue wavelengths to improve visual function. The observed improvement in OKR is likely due to the higher efficacy of 440 nm light in promoting dopamine synthesis, as evidenced by the elevated dopamine levels and upregulated expression of Th and Dat under these conditions. In contrast, 460 nm blue light and 650 nm red light exhibited more nuanced effects on visual enhancement, with significant improvements only under moderate lighting conditions. These wavelengths showed a biphasic response, where initial improvements in OKR were followed by a decline as light intensity increased. This suggests that there may be a threshold beyond which the beneficial effects of these wavelengths diminish, and excessive exposure could potentially impair visual function. This pattern aligns with the observed dopamine levels, where both 460 nm and 650 nm light led to an initial increase in dopamine, followed by a decrease at higher intensities. These findings underscore the crucial role of dopamine in regulating visual functions and provide a mechanistic explanation for the observed changes in OKR.

The synthesis and release of dopamine, a critical neuromodulator in the retina, are controlled by a complex network of biochemical pathways [26–29]. The enzyme Th catalyzes the transformation of tyrosine into L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA), which is subsequently converted into dopamine by the enzyme aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase within dopaminergic neurons. The vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) is responsible for the transport of synthesized dopamine into synaptic vesicles, ensuring its availability for neuronal communication. Once released into synaptic clefts, dopamine is reabsorbed by dopaminergic neurons through the Dat, where it can be either repackaged into vesicles or metabolized into DOPAC by Mao, highlighting a sophisticated control mechanism of dopaminergic activity in the retina. Our analysis of gene expression within the dopamine pathway (Th, Dat, and Mao) further supports the link between monochromatic light exposure and dopamine pathways. The expression of Th and Dat, which are essential for dopamine synthesis and reuptake, respectively, showed consistent patterns under different lighting conditions. These patterns were closely correlated with the changes in ocular dopamine levels, emphasizing the pivotal role of these genes in modulating the dopaminergic response to light exposure. Additionally, the significant downregulation of Mao, particularly in the blue light groups, suggests a reduction in dopamine degradation, leading to an accumulation of dopamine in the retina. These findings provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the light-induced changes in dopamine pathways and emphasize the importance of carefully considering the intensity and duration of monochromatic light exposure when designing lighting environments for zebrafish.

Our results also indicate potential risks associated with high light intensity and prolonged exposure. Notably, retinal thinning in the outer nuclear layer was observed under the most intense 440 nm blue and 650 nm red light conditions, suggesting that excessive light exposure may lead to retinal damage. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the potential for high-intensity light, particularly in the blue spectrum, to cause photoreceptor damage and impair visual function [30, 31]. While the literature often distinguishes between harmful and beneficial categories of blue light [32–35], our study illustrates that the effects of light on vision are highly complex and dependent on specific wavelengths, intensities, and exposure durations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that exposure to 440 nm blue light significantly enhances visual processing, as evidenced by improvements in the OKR, particularly as light intensity increases. However, our histological analysis also underscores the risk of potential retinal damage from prolonged exposure to high-intensity light, as evidenced by thinning of the ONL. Although OKR provides an indirect measure of visual acuity, these findings suggest a complex relationship between light exposure, visual function, and retinal health. Specifically, the distinct responses observed with 460 nm blue light and 650 nm red light suggest that moderate lighting conditions are more conducive to maintaining visual health, as they enhance visual function without the associated risks of retinal damage. These findings underscore the importance of carefully modulating light wavelength, intensity, and duration to optimize visual function while minimizing potential harm.

These results highlight the complex interplay between light’s wavelength, intensity, and duration, and their effects on vision. Our study underscores the need for further research to better understand these interactions and to develop guidelines for safe light exposure, particularly in environments where prolonged exposure to artificial light is prevalent. Moreover, this investigation offers valuable insights into the effects of monochromatic light on the visual system, laying the foundation for future research in vision health and its potential clinical applications.
