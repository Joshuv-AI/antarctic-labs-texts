Hashimoto’s thyroiditis influences tumor cell states and intratumoral microbiota in papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Yue Che, Wei Kou, Lianghui Xu, Zhuoran Liu, Jie Kuang, Xi Cheng, Qiwu Zhao, Haoran Feng, Weihua Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHashimoto’s thyroiditis is associated with reduced lymph node metastasis in papillary thyroid carcinoma, suggesting that the autoimmune microenvironment may influence tumor progression. We utilized single-cell RNA sequencing and intratumoral 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing to investigate malignant epithelial cell states and microbial features in PTC with and without Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. Tumors with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis exhibited malignant epithelial populations enriched for innate immune signaling programs and showed increased intratumoral microbial diversity with reduced abundance of several Gram-negative bacteria, particularly Pseudomonas. Within the Hashimoto’s thyroiditis subgroup, lymph node metastasis was associated with increased Gram-negative bacterial abundance. Functional experiments further showed that Pseudomonas-derived lipopolysaccharide enhanced thyroid cancer cell migration in vitro and in vivo. These findings suggest that Hashimoto’s thyroiditis reshapes both epithelial transcriptional states and the intratumoral microbial environment, potentially contributing to a less aggressive tumor phenotype.Keywords: papillary thyroid carcinoma, intratumoral microbiome, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Thyroid cancer, originating from follicular or parafollicular epithelial cells, represents the most common malignancy of the head and neck. Its incidence has increased markedly in recent decades.1 Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the predominant histological subtype, is generally associated with a favorable prognosis.2 Nevertheless, a subset of patients develops aggressive disease characterized by lymph node metastasis, recurrence, or disease-specific mortality, underscoring the incomplete understanding of the molecular and microenvironmental drivers of PTC progression.

Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (HT) is an autoimmune disorder marked by chronic lymphocytic infiltration and follicular destruction. Epidemiological evidence indicates that patients with pre-existing HT have an increased risk of developing PTC.3 Paradoxically, patients with PTC with coexisting HT often exhibit less aggressive features, including smaller tumor size, lower disease stage, and fewer lymph node metastases.4,5,6 These findings suggest that HT modulates the biological behavior of PTC. Therefore, elucidating the molecular characteristics and tumor microenvironment (TME) of PTC coexisting with HT (HT-PTC) is of critical importance.

The TME plays a critical role in cancer initiation and progression. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables high-resolution characterization of cellular heterogeneity within PTC.7 In parallel, intratumoral microbiota have emerged as important modulators of tumor biology.8,9,10 In thyroid cancer, preliminary studies indicated the presence of distinct microbial signatures.11,12 A previous study identified distinct intratumoral microbial signatures and demonstrated that microbial dysbiosis is associated with thyroid carcinogenesis and HT.13 However, whether intratumoral microbes interact with specific malignant epithelial cell or immune contexts to influence metastatic behavior, particularly in HT-PTC, remains unclear.

In this study, we provide the first integrated analysis of HT-PTC from both single-cell sequencing and intratumoral microbiome perspectives. We identified an epithelial transcriptional program characterized by innate immune activation under the HT background and revealed that the reduced relative abundance of specific Gram-negative bacteria may contribute to a less aggressive tumor phenotype. These findings offer new insights into the role of the autoimmune microenvironment in modulating tumor progression and provide a theoretical basis for metastasis risk stratification and for identifying potential therapeutic targets in PTC.

Results

Single-cell landscape reveals distinct cellular remodeling in the PTC microenvironment

To characterize cellular heterogeneity in the PTC TME with or without HT, scFAST-seq analysis was performed on tumor samples from four treatment-naive patients with PTC, including two with HT and two without HT (nonHT). After quality control filtering, 44,280 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analyses (Figures S1, S2A–S2C; Table S1).

Graph-based clustering identified 27 cell clusters, which were annotated using canonical marker genes into 11 major cell populations, including malignant epithelial cells (Cancer cells), normal thyroid follicular epithelial cells (Thyroid cells), B cells (naive and plasma B cells), regulatory CD4+ cells, neutrophils, IL1B+ M1-like macrophage, C1QB+ M2-like macrophage, dendritic cells, mast cells, endothelial cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Figures 1A–1C and S2D–S2E).

All major cell populations were present in both HT and nonHT tumors, but their relative proportions differed between groups. HT tumors exhibited an increased proportion of B cells, including both plasma cells and naive B cells. In contrast, nonHT tumors showed higher proportions of effector CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells, neutrophils, and IL1B+ M1-like myeloid cells (Figures 1D and 1E). Differences were also observed within malignant and normal thyroid follicular epithelial cell proportions, prompting further subclustering of epithelial cells to assess transcriptional heterogeneity between HT and nonHT tumors.

Distinct transcriptional heterogeneity of malignant epithelial clusters in HT and nonHT tumors

All epithelial subclusters expressed the canonical epithelial marker gene EPCAM, confirming their shared epithelial identity. Thyroid differentiation-related genes (SLC26A4 and TPO) were predominantly expressed in normal thyroid epithelial cells. KRT19, a common marker of epithelial-derived tumors, was mainly expressed in malignant epithelial cells (Figure 2C). Expression of the stemness-associated gene CD44 was detected across clusters, whereas the metabolic reprogramming and therapy resistance-associated gene ALDH1A1 was more highly expressed in normal epithelial clusters (Figure 2D). This pattern suggests functional heterogeneity among malignant epithelial cell clusters.

The distribution of malignant epithelial clusters differed between HT and nonHT tumors. Cluster_0 and cluster_4 were predominantly derived from HT tumors, while cluster_1 was enriched in nonHT tumors (Figure 2E).

To further characterize the biological features of individual clusters, GSVA was performed to assess pathway activity. Cluster_0, cluster_2, and cluster_3 displayed higher enrichment of gene sets related to cell cycle progression, DNA replication, and DNA repair (Figure 2F). InferCNV analysis further revealed heterogeneous CNV burden across malignant clusters. Recurrent chromosomal alterations were detected across malignant cells, including losses of chromosomes 6 and 8 and gains of chromosomes 3 and 12 (Figure 2G). Quantitative analysis showed higher CNV burden in cluster_1 and cluster_4. In contrast, cluster_0, cluster_2, and cluster_3 exhibited lower CNV scores (Figure 2H). HT-PTC-enriched malignant epithelial cell clusters did not display a markedly increased CNV burden, suggesting that the heterogeneity in these cells may be predominantly reflected at the level of transcriptional functional states rather than overt genomic structural changes.

To explore potential developmental relationships among epithelial states, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed. The inferred trajectory revealed a continuous transition from normal thyroid epithelial cells to malignant epithelial populations. The HT-enriched Cluster_0 was located at the distal end of the inferred trajectory, whereas the nonHT-enriched Cluster_1 occupied an intermediate position (Figures S4A–S4B). Thyroid differentiation markers (TPO and SLC26A7) were progressively downregulated along pseudotime, whereas DPP4 and FN1 expression gradually increased (Figure S4C). These findings suggest that Cluster_0 represents a late-stage malignant transcriptional state within the epithelial developmental continuum.

HT-PTC enriched malignant epithelial clusters exhibit an innate immune transcriptional program

To further delineate the transcriptional features of malignant epithelial subpopulations, differential gene expression analysis was performed across all identified clusters. Cluster_0 exhibited significant upregulation of genes including CHI3L1, PLAU, ECM1, CTSC, and APOE (Figure 3A). This pattern indicates elevated expression of genes associated with extracellular matrix remodeling and stress responses within this cluster, rather than canonical inflammatory effector molecules.

Next, we examined the distribution of immune-related signatures. Interferon-responsive genes, including STAT1, IRF1, and MHC class II genes (HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, CD74), were heterogeneously expressed across malignant epithelial clusters. Classical IFN-γ-inducible chemokines (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11) were minimally expressed and primarily sequestered within cluster_3 (Figure 3B). This cluster also showed expression of innate immune and inflammatory genes, including IRAK2, PELI1, TMEM173, CXCL8, IL1B, and TNF.

Moreover, cluster_0, cluster_2, and cluster_3 showed cluster-specific expression of genes involved in innate immune and microbial sensing pathways. These pathways included components of Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, STING-mediated cytosolic DNA sensing, and NF-κB signaling (Figure 3C). Expression of TLR2 and TLR4, as well as classical pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL1B, CXCL8), remained low across the majority of epithelial clusters.

To validate these findings, we analyzed an independent scRNA-seq dataset (GEO: GSE163203). Consistent with our cohort, HT-PTC samples displayed a higher proportion of B cells than nonHT-PTC samples (Figures S5A–S5B). Re-clustering of thyroid epithelial cells identified six malignant epithelial subclusters (Figure S5C), among which Epi-C3 and Epi-C4 were preferentially enriched in HT-PTC. Notably, Epi-C4 exhibited elevated expression of CXCL2, GDF15, HBEGF, CYR61, JUN, DUSP6, and KLF6 (Figure S5D), indicative of an epithelial stress-response program accompanied by activation of inflammatory and innate immune-related signaling pathways. This transcriptional profile showed substantial similarity to the innate immune-associated epithelial state identified in our own cohort.

Collectively, HT-PTC enriched malignant epithelial subpopulations harbor a selectively enriched innate immune-related transcriptome. This state is defined by the priming of innate recognition and signaling pathways, counterbalanced by a relatively restricted expression of classical inflammatory effector molecules (Figure 3D).

Functional enrichment reveals cluster-specific features in epithelial cells

GO enrichment analysis identified distinct functional features across epithelial subpopulations. Cluster_0, cluster_2, and cluster_3 were enriched for processes related to tissue remodeling. These included wound healing, cell adhesion, cytoskeletal organization, angiogenesis, and responses to exogenous biological entities (Figure 3E).

In contrast, cluster_1 and cluster_4 showed different functional patterns. Cluster_1 was enriched for protein synthesis pathways, including ribosomal activity and RNA processing. Cluster_4 was associated with protein homeostasis and responses to oxidative or chemical stress. Cluster_5 showed enrichment in pathways related to cytoskeletal dynamics, cell-matrix adhesion, and morphological plasticity. This cluster also showed activation of multiple signaling and developmental regulatory programs (Figure 3F).

Collectively, these enrichment patterns demonstrate distinct functional programs across epithelial clusters, involving tissue remodeling, biosynthetic activity, and stress response within the TME.

Intratumoral microbiota profiling identifies potential drivers of HT associated epithelial states

Based on the prominent enrichment of innate immune transcriptional programs in HT-PTC epithelial cells and the well-recognized role of innate immunity in mediating host-microbe interactions, we next hypothesized whether intratumoral microbial alterations were associated with epithelial immune states and transcriptional remodeling.

A total of 125 thyroid tissue samples (63 tumor tissues and 62 matched peritumoral tissues) were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Patients were predominantly female with a median age of 45.7 years. Despite early-stage disease, 54.84% (34/62) had lymph node metastasis, and 69.35% (43/62) were classified as intermediate to high risk according to the American Thyroid Association (ATA) risk stratification. Among them, 43.55% (27/62) patients had concomitant HT (Table 1; Figure S1; Table S2).

Sterile ddH2O was included as extraction and PCR negative controls, and no detectable amplicons were observed, indicating minimal background contamination. Negative controls were therefore excluded from downstream sequencing. After quality filtering, 12,544,822 high-quality sequences were retained and clustered into 16,694 ASVs, which were taxonomically assigned to 933 genera. To validate the presence of intratumoral bacteria, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed. Positive bacterial signals were detected in both tumor and adjacent peritumoral thyroid tissues (Figure S6). Together, these results demonstrate the existence of a detectable intratumoral microbiota in PTC tissues.

Microbial community characteristics of intratumoral microbiota in PTC

To establish the overall ecological features of the intratumoral microbiota in PTC, we first compared microbial diversity and community composition between tumor and matched peritumoral tissues. Alpha diversity was assessed using Pielou’s evenness, Shannon, and Simpson indices. Tumor tissues showed increased alpha diversity, but the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05, Figure 4A). Beta diversity assessed by Bray-Curtis distance and NMDS showed a weak separation between tumor and peritumoral tissues, which was not statistically significant by PERMANOVA (R2 = 0.009, p = 0.345; Figure 4B).

Taxonomic profiling revealed the overall composition of intratumoral microbiota in PTC. At the phylum level, the dominant taxa included Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Cyanobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidota (Figure 4C). At the genus level, the most abundant taxa were Delftia, Staphylococcus, and Pseudomonas (Figure 4D).

Differential abundance analysis using ANCOM-BC2 identified several taxa that differed significantly between tumor and peritumoral tissues. Terrimonas, Prevotella_7, Neisseria, and Rubellimicrobium were significantly enriched in tumor tissues, whereas Enterobacter, Parabacteroides, Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group were significantly depleted (p < 0.05; Figure 4E). LEfSe analysis showed consistent results, further supporting distinct microbial features between tumor and peritumoral tissues (Figures 4F and 4G).

HT significantly reshapes the diversity and structural composition of the PTC microbiome

We next examined microbial community differences between tumor tissues from patients with PTC with HT (HT-T) and their matched adjacent tissues (HT-adj), as well as between tumor tissues from patients with PTC without HT (nonHT-T) and their matched adjacent tissues (nonHT-adj). HT-T group showed increased species richness (Chao1, p = 0.031) and higher evenness (Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou’s evenness indices, all p < 0.05). HT-tumor tissues also exhibited higher Shannon and Simpson diversity than matched adjacent tissues (Shannon, p = 0.026; Simpson, p = 0.049), indicating enhanced microbial diversity within the HT TME (Figure 5A). Beta diversity assessed by Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and NMDS showed a weak separation of HT-T samples, which was not statistically significant by PERMANOVA (R2 = 0.026, p = 0.337; Figure 5B). These results suggest alterations in microbial richness and evenness rather than a global community shift.

To identify taxa contributing to community differences, SIMPER analysis was performed. Similarity percentage (SIMPER) analysis identified genera contributing most strongly to Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, including Delftia, Stenotrophomonas, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus, Cutibacterium, Lactobacillus, and Defluviicoccus (Figure 5C). Unweighted pair group method with arithmetic (UPGMA)-based hierarchical clustering demonstrated distinct patterns of dominant microbial taxa at both phylum and genus levels (Figures 5D and 5E).

Differential taxonomic analysis further revealed microbial features associated with HT status. LEfSe identified that Bradyrhizobium, Pseudonocardia, and Solirubrobacter were enriched in HT-T, whereas Variovorax and Sumerlaea were more abundant in the nonHT-T group (Figures 5F and 5G).

These analyses indicate that HT status is associated with distinct intratumoral microbial diversity patterns and taxonomic composition in PTC.

Selective depletion of specific microbial genera characterizes HT-PTC

To determine whether HT associated epithelial states were accompanied by changes in intratumoral microbial composition, we performed differential abundance analyses between patients with PTC with and without HT. In tumor tissues, 134 ASVs were unique to HT-T and 80 to nonHT-T. In paired peritumoral tissues, 38 ASVs were exclusive to HT-adj and 94 to nonHT-adj, with 203 ASVs shared between groups (Figure 6A).

Ternary plot analysis demonstrated group-specific distribution patterns of the eight most abundant genera across HT-T, HT-adj, and nonHT-T samples. Several dominant genera, including Lactobacillus, Staphylococcus, and Pseudomonas, were skewed toward the nonHT-T group, whereas Prevotella_7 showed relative enrichment in HT-T and Porphyromonas in HT-adj (Figure 6B). Correlation analysis between the specific PTC intratumoral microbiome and the clinical factors

CCA analysis revealed that TSH was the clinical parameter most strongly associated with microbial community variation (R2 = 0.92, p < 0.001), followed by thyroid autoantibodies (TgAb, R2 = 0.34, p = 0.008; TPOAb, R2 = 0.17, p = 0.020). Age and nodule size showed weaker but significant associations (Age, R2 = 0.15, p = 0.006; tumor size, R2 = 0.13, p = 0.023; Figure 7A). Spearman correlation analysis showed negative associations between specific genera and clinical parameters. Pseudomonas was negatively correlated with TgAb (p = 0.029) and TPOAb (p = 0.028). Lactobacillus was negatively correlated with age (p = 0.037). Prevotella_7 was negatively correlated with serum FT3 levels (p = 0.047; Figure 7B).

Conserved depletion of selected genera in LNM-positive HT-PTC

Patients with HT exhibited a significantly lower frequency of lymph node metastasis compared with patients with nonHT (χ2(1) = 6.120, p = 0.0134, Figure 7C), consistent with observations reported in previous retrospective studies.

To characterize microbial features by lymph node metastasis status within patients with HT, age- and tumor stage-matched subgroup analyses were performed. In peritumoral tissues, patients with HT with lymph node metastasis showed reduced microbial diversity and evenness compared with patients without metastasis (Figure 7D). Venn analysis and genus-level comparisons showed partial separation between lymph node metastasis-positive and -negative patients. Prevotella_7 was enriched in tumor tissues of metastatic patients. Pseudomonas, Escherichia-Shigella, and Bacteroides were enriched in peritumoral tissues (Figures 7E and 7F). BugBase analysis showed a higher proportion of Gram-negative bacteria in peritumoral tissues of metastatic patients (Figure 7G).

Collectively, these results indicate that lymph node metastasis in patients with HT is associated with localized alterations in peritumoral microbial diversity and composition, rather than uniform changes across tissue compartments.

Pseudomonas-derived lipopolysaccharide (LPS) enhances PTC cell migration in vitro and in vivo

In summary, patients with HT, particularly those without lymph node metastasis, exhibited a significantly reduced abundance of Gram-negative bacteria compared with those with metastasis, with Pseudomonas being the most abundant genus. Lipopolysaccharide is a major pathogen-associated molecule derived from Gram-negative bacteria. Therefore, Pseudomonas-derived LPS was selected for subsequent in vitro functional assays to evaluate its potential effects on PTC cell migration. Human PTC cell lines B-CPAP, TPC-1, and K1 were used to assess the effects of LPS on cell migration in vitro. In wound healing assays, LPS-treated cells showed significantly faster scratch closure than controls (p < 0.05; Figures 8A and 8B). Transwell assays consistently showed increased cell migration following LPS treatment, with higher migration at 400 ng/mL than at 200 ng/mL (p < 0.05; Figures 8C and 8D). Among the three cell lines, B-CPAP showed the strongest migration response to LPS.

For in vivo assessment, B-CPAP cells pretreated with 0, 200, or 400 ng/mL LPS were injected via the tail vein into 6-week-old NCG mice (Figure 8E). Mice receiving LPS-treated cells developed more lung metastatic nodules than controls, with higher counts in the 400 ng/mL group than in the 200 ng/mL group (p < 0.05; Figure 8F). Together, these results indicate that transient LPS pre-exposure is associated with increased migration in vitro and enhanced lung colonization in vivo.

Discussion

This study integrated scRNA-seq with 16S rRNA sequencing to characterize malignant epithelial states and the intratumoral microbiota in HT-PTC. Our findings provide insight into the biological basis underlying the different clinical behavior of HT-PTC and identify potential microbial features with relevance for risk stratification.

HT is characterized by chronic immune activation and extensive remodeling of the thyroid microenvironment.14,15 Consistently, we observed a marked enrichment of plasma cells and naive B cells in HT-PTC, whereas neutrophils and IL1B+ M1-like macrophages were more prevalent in nonHT tumors. The distinct immune ecosystem states may partially explain the favorable clinical characteristics of HT-PTC.7

At the epithelial level, we identified a malignant subpopulation enriched in HT-PTC with increased expression of IRAK2, PELI1, TMEM173, CXCL8, IL1B, and TNF. These genes are involved in innate immune and inflammatory signaling pathways. Notably, although tumor heterogeneity is often attributed to CNV,16 these innate immune transcription patterns exhibited relatively low inferred CNV burdens. This finding suggests that their functional state may be less associated with genomic alterations and more closely related to microenvironmental immune stimuli. Pseudotime analysis further indicated that this population occupies a terminal position along the epithelial trajectory, accompanied by progressive loss of thyroid differentiation markers and increased expression of mesenchymal-associated genes, supporting the existence of a differentiated immune-activated epithelial state in HT-PTC.

Considering that interferon and complement signaling pathways are key regulators of host-microbe interactions and are enriched in HT-associated epithelial states, the observed association between these immune programs and microbial alterations may reflect coordinated remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment and intratumoral microbial ecosystem in HT-PTC. Microbiota have been implicated in tumor progression through immune modulation and metabolic reprogramming.8,9,10,17 Previous studies have reported that increased microbial diversity is more frequently observed in thyroid cancers with advanced clinical stage.11,18 Here, we found that HT-PTC exhibits distinct microbial features, including higher Shannon and Simpson indices and a lower dominance index in both tumor and peritumor tissues. These results indicate a more diverse and evenly distributed microbial community.

Several genera, including Staphylococcus, Lactobacillus, Pseudomonas, Escherichia-Shigella, and Ruminococcus, were significantly depleted in HT-PTC. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing depletion of Staphylococcus and remodeling of microbial interaction networks in HT-PTC.13 Staphylococcus has been linked to immune modulation,19 whereas Lactobacillus and Pseudomonas have been associated with reduced levels of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes20 and worse prognosis21,22 Although their functions in thyroid cancer remain unclear, depletion of these taxa may reduce exposure to pro-tumorigenic microbial signals. This microbial landscape may contribute to the lower frequency of lymph node metastasis and more favorable outcomes observed in HT-PTC., suggesting that intratumoral microbial signatures may have value for risk stratification.

Mechanistically, microbial signals may influence tumor behavior. LPS is a major component of Gram-negative bacterial membranes. It primarily interacts with TLR4 and then activates downstream pathways of inflammatory cytokine production and sustains chronic inflammation.23 LPS has been recognized as a marker of Gram-negative bacterial presence. It was reported that LPS could promote tumor invasion, metastasis, and immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance across multiple malignancies.24,25,26 In vitro and in vivo experiments in this study demonstrate that Pseudomonas-derived LPS enhances PTC cell migration and metastatic colonization, supporting a functional link between microbial signals and epithelial cell phenotypes.

We also observed an association between serum TSH levels and intratumoral microbial composition. While elevated TSH has been associated with thyroid cancer progression,27,28 its relationship with the tumor microbiome remains poorly understood. The gut microbiota has been proposed to regulate thyroid function by modulating the absorption of thyroid-related micronutrients.29,30,31 Recent study reported that microbial diversity patterns in thyroid tissues resemble those observed in oral and fecal samples, suggesting microbial translocation or systemic host-microbiota interactions.32 Our findings further suggest a potential association between endocrine status and intratumoral microbial ecology.

Based on these observations, we propose that intratumoral microbial stimuli may contribute to transcriptional reprogramming of PTC epithelial cells toward innate immune activation. These factors may impose selective pressure on the intratumoral microbiota, preferentially suppressing taxa with higher invasive potential and promoting the establishment of a more balanced microbial ecosystem. Consequently, the depletion of pro-tumorigenic microbes may be associated with microbe-tumor interactions, thereby influencing its metastatic potential. Future studies should determine whether microbial signatures can improve risk stratification and whether microbe-derived signaling pathways can be therapeutically targeted to limit metastatic progression.

In conclusion, HT-PTC exhibits distinct epithelial transcriptional states and intratumoral microbial features, characterized by innate immune activation and altered microbial composition independent of genomic instability. These changes are associated with reduced abundance of specific Gram-negative genera and may contribute to a less invasive phenotype.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be noted. First, this study was based on a single-center cohort. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts are required to validate and extend these findings. Second, LPS stimulation represents a simplified model of microbial signaling and does not fully recapitulate the complexity of interactions between live bacteria and tumor cells. Further studies using live Pseudomonas co-culture systems and in vivo colonization models are needed to validate these observations and to elucidate the mechanistic role of intratumoral microbes in shaping the TME and influencing tumor progression.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design and participants

Ethical approval for this study (No. 2023242) was provided by the Research Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai, China, on 12 September 2023, and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent prior to enrollment.

A total of 66 patients were enrolled between February 2024 and October 2024 at Ruijin Hospital. Participants were recruited from patients newly diagnosed with PTC who underwent thyroidectomy at Ruijin Hospital. The cohort included a single cell sequencing group (n = 4) and a 16S rRNA gene sequencing group (n = 62).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) newly diagnosed papillary thyroid carcinoma, confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination; (b) availability of paired tumor and peritumoral tissue samples; (c) complete clinicopathological data, including sex, age, TNM stage, recurrence risk stratification and the presence of concomitant Hashimoto’s thyroiditis.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) recent use of antibiotics, probiotics, prebiotics, or synbiotics; (b) clinical or laboratory evidence of active infection prior to surgery; (c) a history of malignancy or prior radiotherapy/chemotherapy; or (d) insufficient paired tissue or clinical data.

TNM staging was performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system. Recurrence risk stratification followed the 2024 guidelines of the American Thyroid Association (ATA). The diagnosis of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis was based on the presence of anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPO-Ab) and/or anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (TG-Ab) and/or diffuse lymphocytic infiltration observed on histopathological examination.

The flow diagram of patient enrollment is summarized in Figure S1. Clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Tables 1, S1, and S2. Subgroup analyses were predefined according to the presence or absence of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and lymph node metastasis. Propensity score matching was performed based on age and tumor stage to reduce baseline imbalance between groups. Sex-specific analyses were not conducted, as the study was not powered to detect sex-based differences.

Cell lines

Human papillary thyroid carcinoma cell lines B-CPAP, TPC-1, and K1 were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (NCACC, China), the Rio de Janeiro Cell Bank (BCRJ, Brazil), and the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC, UK), respectively. B-CPAP and TPC-1 cells were derived from female donors, whereas K1 cells were derived from a male donor.33 B-CPAP and K1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; MeilunBio, China), whereas TPC-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI-1640; MeilunBio, China). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Mice

Ethical approval for all animal procedures (No. RJ2023070) was provided by the Animal Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai, China, on 8 December 2023. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Chinese Zoological Society. Six-week-old male NCG mice were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. All animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a temperature- and humidity-controlled room (23°C, 65% humidity) with a 12-h light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water.

Creation of experimental lung metastasis mouse model

Briefly, the human thyroid cancer cell line B-CPAP was seeded in 6 well plates and cultured to approximately 50% confluence before being treated with LPS at 0 ng/mL (control), 200 ng/mL, or 400 ng/mL for 48 h to evaluate the effect of pre-stimulation on metastatic potential. Following treatment, cells were harvested and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per 100 μL.

Twelve mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 4 per group), with each mouse serving as an independent experimental unit. Each mouse received 100 μL of the prepared cell suspension via lateral tail vein injection within 3–5 s. All injections were performed by the same investigator to minimize variability. Body weight was measured twice weekly. At the predefined experimental endpoint, all mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation, and lungs were harvested for assessment of metastatic tumor burden. Gross metastatic nodules were recorded, and lung tissues were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histological evaluation of pulmonary metastases.

Method details

Sample collection

Tumor tissues and paired peritumor tissues were collected under sterile conditions in the operating room and immediately placed in sterile tubes, frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. To monitor potential contamination, three open Eppendorf tubes containing 1 mL sterile ddH2O were placed in the operating room during tissue collection.

Single cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq)

The collected samples were immediately transferred into tissue preservation medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS)), and single cell suspensions were prepared within 6 h. Libraries were prepared using the SeekOne DD scFAST-seq kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.34 Raw sequencing data were processed using SeekSoul software and aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 to generate gene expression matrices. Batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v0.1).35 Quality control was performed using Seurat (v4.1.1),36 excluding genes expressed in fewer than three cells and filtering out cells with <200 or >100,000 UMIs, <100 or >30,000 detected genes, or mitochondrial gene content >20%.

Cell type annotation

Initial cell type annotation was performed using SingleR (v1.8.1) to obtain a coarse classification. The annotations were subsequently confirmed through manual inspection based on canonical marker genes for each cell type. Clusters simultaneously expressing markers of two or more known cell types were considered doublet cells and excluded from further analyses. Seven major cell populations based on previously reported marker genes were annotated,37 including tumor epithelial cells (EPCAM+ KRT18+ KRT19+ KRT7+ KRT8+ TG+), thyroid follicular epithelial cells (TPO+ SLC26A4+), endothelial cells (CD34+ CDH5+ CLDN5+ PECAM1+ PLVAP+ RAMP2+ VWF+), fibroblasts (ACTA2+ COL3A1+ COL1A2+ COL1A1+ DCN+ LUM+ TAGLN+), T cells (CD3D+ CD3E+ CD3G+ GNLY+ KLRD1+ KLRF1+ NKG7+ TRAC+), B cells (CD79A+ CD79B+ IGHD+ IGHM+ MS4A1+), and myeloid cells (C1QB+ CD14+ CD163+ CD68+ LYZ+ S100A8+ S100A9+).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs), functional pathway and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Genes with a log2 fold change ≥0.25 and expressed in at least 25% of cells in either group were considered differentially expressed. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.2.0).38 Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed using GSVA (v2.2.1),39 with gene sets obtained from MSigDB (v7.5.1).

Copy number variation inference (CNV)

Copy number variation (CNV) inference was conducted using inferCNV (v1.17.0),40 with thyroid follicular epithelial cells as reference. Noise reduction and signal smoothing were applied during CNV inference using a sliding window approach with a window size of 101 genes. Hierarchical clustering of inferred CNV profiles was conducted using the Ward.D2 algorithm.

Trajectory analysis

Monocle 3 algorithm was performed to investigate the differentiation trajectories of HT-PTC malignant cell subtypes. A CellDataSet object was constructed from quality-controlled epithelial cells, followed by normalization, preprocessing, and dimensionality reduction using the preprocess_cds() and reduce_dimension() functions with UMAP embedding. Cells were clustered using cluster_cells(), and a principal graph was constructed using learn_graph(). Pseudotime was calculated by ordering cells along the inferred trajectory using order_cells(), with normal thyroid follicular epithelial cells defined as the root. Genes associated with pseudotime variation were identified using graph_test() (q-value <1 × 10-5) and used to characterize dynamic transcriptional changes along epithelial trajectories.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

16S rRNA gene sequencing data were processed using standard pipelines to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Low-quality reads and chimeras were removed, and taxonomic annotation was performed using the SILVA database (https://www.arb-silva.de). Alpha diversity indices were calculated from the ASV table, and beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis distances and visualized by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS). Differentially abundant taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe)41 and Analysis of Compositions of Microbiomes With Bias Correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2).42 Associations between microbial communities and clinical variables were evaluated using canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) and Spearman’s correlation with false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded in 6 well plates and grown to full confluence. A linear scratch was made using a sterile pipette tip held perpendicular to the plate. Detached cells were removed by washing with sterile PBS. Serum free medium containing 0, 200, or 400 ng/mL LPS was added according to the experimental groups. Baseline images were taken immediately (0 h), after which cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2. Wound closure was monitored and photographed under a microscope at the same positions at the indicated time points to assess closure.

Transwell migration assay

Cells were seeded into 6 well plates and grown to approximately 50% confluence, then stimulated with serum containing medium supplemented with 0, 200 or 400 ng/mL LPS for 48 h. After stimulation, cells were collected and resuspended in serum free medium. A total of 5 × 104 cells were added to the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size; Corning, USA), with medium containing 10% FBS placed in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed by sterile PBS, and migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 10% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and quantified under the microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and figures were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.5.0 and R v4.4.1. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t test or the Mann-Whitney U test, depending on data normality. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc tests were applied for normally distributed data, while non-parametric data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Holm correction. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (interquartile range, IQR), or number (percentage). p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance is indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001, ns, not significant.
