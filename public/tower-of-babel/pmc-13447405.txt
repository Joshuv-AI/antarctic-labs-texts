A mortality model for relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma: emulation of subjective life expectancy criteria from a clinical trial in real-world data
Authors: Alexander Breskin, Mostafa Shokoohi, Beatrice Suero, Di Wang, Paul Spin, Qiufei Ma, Glenn S. Kroog, Christian Hampp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with limited life expectancy (LE) are often excluded from oncology trials based on subjective judgment, posing a challenge when contextualizing trial findings with real-world data (RWD). This study evaluated the feasibility of modeling mortality in RWD for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) to replicate patient exclusion (LE < 6 months) for application in an external control arm study.A targeted literature review (TLR, January 2016–March 2022) was performed to identify mortality prediction models in RRMM. Because no suitable model was found, a de novo mortality model was developed using the COTA database.Twenty-one prognostic factors identified via literature review and clinical experts were included in the models. Cox proportional hazards models with bootstrap validation to correct for optimism were used to identify the best-performing model. A threshold of ≥50% predicted mortality probability within 6 months of the start of the line of therapy (LOT) was used for sensitivity and specificity estimates.Patients (N = 1,112) contributed 2,626 LOTs. A model with the top 20 prognostic factors maximized concordance and accuracy while achieving pre-specified performance thresholds, except for the F1 score. The predicted and observed 6-month mortality probability were both 26%.Mortality prediction for RRMM remains challenging with RWD. The de novo model exhibited strong discrimination performance but low F1 score, suggesting it may be useful to identify patients at highest risk who may be least likely to meet trial LE criteria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-07200-z.

Introduction

Clinical trial eligibility criteria are essential to identify a relevant patient population. In oncology trials, these criteria are used to select the study population based on characteristics such as age, disease stage, performance status, prior treatments, and comorbidities. These criteria define the target population of interest and allow stakeholders to determine the applicability of trial findings and facilitate comparisons across studies.

One commonly applied trial eligibility criterion is sufficient life expectancy (LE) at enrollment, which ensures patients are fit enough to receive investigational therapies but may exclude patients with poor prognoses and high risk of early drop-out. Consequently, methodological concerns, such as incomplete data shortly after the start of the trial and underestimated treatment effects due to short follow-up, can be reduced [1, 2]. Among 286 commercial investigational new drug applications for adult oncology indications, 54% included a minimum LE requirement, with 46% requiring an LE of ≥3 months [3]. LE assessments are typically based on subjective evaluations by local investigators. However, LE assessment is not part of routine care and is absent from real-world data (RWD) [4].

To contextualize outcomes for single-arm clinical trials without comparators, RWD is increasingly used to create external control arms (ECAs) that mimic clinical trial patients. Applying trial eligibility criteria to RWD is essential to identify valid ECAs, although adaptation of LE criteria is not well studied. Recently, an ECA was created from RWD to contextualize efficacy findings from a single-arm trial of an investigational therapy for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM) [5]. Patients in this trial had received at least three prior lines of therapy (3 L+ LOTs), including a proteasome inhibitor, immunomodulatory drug, and anti-CD38 antibody (triple-class exposed [TCE]), or were triple‑class refractory (TCR) to these treatments [6]. The LE requirement for this trial was ≥6 months. To emulate a trial, it is ideal to apply the LE criterion to patients from RWD. However, as LE data are often unavailable in RWD, alternative approaches, such as predictive modeling, need to be considered. The current study followed a two-step approach; first, a targeted literature review (TLR) was conducted to assess the feasibility of LE prediction in RWD from patients with RRMM using published models. Second, as no suitable model was identified, a de novo model for 6-month mortality in patients with 3 L+/TCE or TCR RRMM was pre-specified and applied to RWD.

Methods

Literature review and suitability assessment

A targeted literature review, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (https://www.prisma-statement.org/), was conducted in PubMed to identify mortality models for patients with multiple myeloma (MM), published between January 1, 2016 and March 15, 2022. To ensure any potentially relevant model was captured, models in MM, including both newly diagnosed MM and RRMM, were included in initial search criteria. Eligibility criteria for the TLR were pre-defined (Supplementary Table 1) [7], with one reviewer assessing eligibility and consulting a second to resolve uncertainties. Studies meeting the TLR criteria were assessed for feasibility of applying the model in real-world patients, considering population, model design, outcome definitions, model applicability, and study quality. During feasibility assessment, only models with a study population of patients with RRMM were considered. Additional criteria used to assess suitability for predicting mortality included use of variables generally captured in RWD and mortality measured from initiation of the second or later LOT. Models were evaluated on their ability to predict 6-month mortality, making them suitable for application in the RRMM ECA. Study quality was assessed using the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) checklist [8]. If the TLR identified a suitable mortality model, it would be used, but, if not, pre-specified de novo model development would be implemented.

Model development

Study population

The model development cohort was built using data from COTA, a US electronic health record-derived database of oncology patients. COTA includes clinical, demographic, testing, treatment, and outcome data across a diverse range of healthcare settings, including academic institutions, community centers, and hospital systems, representing 500,000 patients treated at over 200 care sites. The data-abstraction process combines clinical and statistical expertise with technological methods to convert structured and unstructured data into a standardized data model, and non-electronic health record sources, including commercially available obituary databases such as legacy.com, are used to complement the dataset [9]. Data from COTA have been used in previous studies of real-world patients with RRMM [10, 11].

In this analysis, eligibility was assessed by LOT. All observed LOTs post-determination of 3 L+/TCE or TCR RRMM status were included (i.e., no other inclusion/exclusion criteria from the trial were applied), capturing a diverse population of patients similar to those who would be screened for trial inclusion. Each patient could contribute multiple LOTs during the analysis. The index date was defined as the start date of each eligible LOT. The follow-up period was defined as the duration from Day 1 after the index date until death or the last visit, whichever was first.

Predictors of mortality

The process of identifying key predictors of mortality has been published previously [12]. A systematic literature review was used to identify prognostic factors for potential inclusion in the model. A panel of clinical experts grouped factors by level of importance, and rankings were determined by consensus. Of 25 originally identified prognostic factors, 21 were available in COTA and used in the current analysis (Supplementary Table 2).

Model specification and evaluation

As baseline demographic and clinical factors were missing for some patients, multiple imputation using multivariate imputation by chained equations [13] was performed to generate five complete datasets. Data were split into training and testing sets before model development and evaluation. In each imputed dataset, 11 candidate Cox proportional hazards models of mortality were fit along with the Breslow estimator of the baseline hazard function. One model was selected using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression [14] to identify variables, and 10 models were iteratively fitted, beginning with a model adjusting for all covariates, followed by a sequence of models in which one covariate was removed at each step, in reverse order of importance based on clinical factor rankings. Bootstrap validation was employed to mitigate the risk of overly optimistic accuracy estimates from model development data. First, a series of bootstrap samples were drawn from the development data. In each sample, the model was fit and the apparent performance metrics in the bootstrap sample were estimated. Next, the out-of-sample performance was estimated by comparing the bootstrap-model predictions in the original dataset to actual survival in the original dataset. Optimism was then calculated as the difference between apparent accuracy and out-of-sample accuracy. Finally, both apparent and optimism-adjusted estimates of the following predictive accuracy measures, averaged across imputations, were reported: integrated calibration index (ICI), F1 score, concordance index, calibration slope, Nagelkerke’s R², Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value, and area under the curve (Supplementary Table 3). These measures for each model were recorded and pooled across the five imputed datasets. The final model aimed for optimism-corrected measures as follows: calibration slope between 0.9 and 1.1, indicating agreement between predicted and observed outcomes [15]; F1 score >0.80, indicating sufficient PPV and recall (sensitivity) [16]; concordance index >0.70, indicating acceptable discrimination [17]; and Nagelkerke’s R² >0.20, indicating sufficient variation explained by the model [18]. In other words, an acceptable calibration slope, F1 score, and concordance index will indicate, respectively, that the predicted event rates from the model match the observed event rates, the model properly balances precision and sensitivity (focusing on correctly identifying individuals with the outcomes), and the model correctly ranks individuals by relative risk.

Patients with a predicted probability of mortality ≥50% (default threshold) at 6 months were classified as having low LE. This threshold was selected to align with the clinical trial inclusion criterion requiring an LE of ≥6 months [6]. A ≥50% predicted probability of death within 6 months (i.e., ≤50% probability of 6-month survival) is consistent with a median overall survival of 6 months, providing a clinically interpretable basis for this definition.

In a sensitivity analysis, a range of thresholds (from 1% to 99%) for predicted probabilities of mortality were examined to determine their impact on the models’ performance metrics.

Results

Literature review

The database search for mortality models in patients with MM identified 1,391 studies. Of these, 1,172 were excluded at the title and abstract screening phase for not meeting pre-specified inclusion criteria. Among the 219 studies remaining, 211 were excluded at the full-text screening phase, with eight studies reporting on six unique models included in the review (Fig. 1). Most excluded studies fitted exploratory multivariate survival models to trial data as part of a survival analysis or prognostic factors assessment rather than mortality prediction model development. Overall, studies were of moderate quality according to the TRIPOD checklist (Supplementary Table 4). Three publications used the same risk stratification algorithm (RSA) in real-world patients with RRMM and were considered related to one study, described in Bouwmeester et al. 2019 [19–21].

Suitability assessment

No prior study has developed a model to predict mortality specifically in RRMM. A summary of the suitability assessment is presented in Fig. 2. All six studies used multivariable Cox regression models to either establish mortality risk categories in patients with MM or RRMM or predict mortality in the broader MM population. Two studies (Bouwmeester et al. 2019 and Engelhardt et al. 2017) defined risk groups with distinct survival expectations based on prognostic factors [19–22]. Two studies developed nomograms to estimate an individual’s survival probability at certain time points [23, 24]. Lastly, two studies only reported a Cox regression model that would calculate the relative risk of mortality [25, 26]. Notably, two studies included gene expression-based risk scores in their predictive models that may not be available in RWD [24, 26]. The RSA by Bouwmeester et al. 2019 was the only study conducted explicitly in patients with RRMM, with mortality defined from the initiation of the second LOT; other studies were in mixed newly diagnosed MM and RRMM populations or unclear regarding patient relapse/refractory status [19].

None of the models were suitable to reliably identify patients with a short LE (<6 months). The RSA by Bouwmeester et al. 2019 defined four risk groups with distinct median overall survival (OS), which may emulate investigator assessment of LE in a clinical trial setting. Notably, one of the risk groups had a median OS of 5.9 months, thus being potentially suitable for the present purpose [19]. However, in model validation, the median OS for this risk group increased to 16.18 months [21]. Englehardt et al. 2017 organized patients into three risk groups. However, the shortest median OS among the groups was 1.2 years [22]. Therefore, the model would not identify patients with an LE <6 months. Similarly, the nomograms were developed to predict the probability of 1- to 5-year survival [23, 24]. Lastly, the Cox regression models predict the relative risk of mortality rather than absolute mortality. Thus, these models are fundamentally different versus investigator-assessed LE and would not yield explicit risk predictions. Therefore, no suitable mortality prediction models were identified in the literature, and de novo model development was required.

Model development and performance

Baseline characteristics of patients in COTA before and after multiple imputation are presented in Table 1. A total of 2,626 LOTs (from 1,112 patients) from COTA were included in the analysis. Before imputation, the mean (standard deviation) age of patients receiving any LOT was 67.1 (9.9) years, 45.5% of patients were female, and 43.8% were at least TCR. Most commonly, patients receiving any LOT had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 1 (40.2%), standard cytogenetic risk (58.0%), and immunoglobulin G subtype (45.9%), and had received more than five prior LOTs (47.0%). In 57.1% of LOTs, the duration from initial diagnosis to start of treatment was ≥5 years. The time from initiation of first-line therapy to RRMM status was ≥12 months in 44.4% of patients. More than 80% of LOTs were missing an International Staging System (ISS) score, best response to prior therapy, or bone marrow plasma cell percentage.

Table 2 shows the results of bootstrap validation for the LASSO model (adjustment for 21 variables) and models adjusted for the top 20, 19, 18, and 17 variables. Most models exceeded the majority of pre-specified minimum performance metrics after adjusting for optimism. All five models had a concordance index > 0.70, Nagelkerke’s R2 >0.20, and calibration slopes within pre-specified limits (0.9 to 1.1). Results were consistent with low ICI. Adjustment for the top 20 variables maximized concordance index (0.772) and accuracy as measured by Nagelkerke’s R² (0.231). This model additionally resulted in a bootstrap-adjusted ICI of 0.012, calibration slope of 1.001, and F1 score of 0.468, and sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and negative predictive value of 0.368, 0.930, 0.645, and 0.808, respectively. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for this model was 0.649. The average 6-month mortality observed in the RWD and mortality probability predicted by the model were both 26%. The 6-month mortality calibration curves with administrative censoring at 6 months are presented in Fig. 3. The regression coefficients of these top five models are presented in Table 3.

Although all candidate models met calibration and discrimination benchmarks, none achieved the pre-specified F1 score threshold (> 0.80). Sensitivity analyses evaluating alternative mortality thresholds showed that F1 scores were maximized at thresholds from 0.24 to 0.26 (Supplementary Table 5), improving the F1 score to 0.59.

Discussion

The TLR showed that no published mortality prediction models were suitable for application to a RRMM RWD cohort to exclude patients with a short LE (<6 months) from the ECA and mimic the LE criterion in the single-arm trial. Therefore, a de novo mortality prediction model was developed. Using RWD and expert-identified and -ranked prognostic factors, it was possible to fit a model for mortality in patients with RRMM that met pre-specified thresholds across performance metrics, with the exception of the F1 score. The lower F1 scores were driven by modest model sensitivity (0.34–0.37) and PPV (~0.65) (Table 2), reflecting the inherent difficulty of accurately classifying individual patients who will die within 6 months. The challenge in achieving high PPV and sensitivity simultaneously suggests a fundamental trade-off between false positives and false negatives when predicting short-term mortality in a clinically heterogeneous, heavily pre-treated RRMM population.

Despite these limitations in individual-level classification, the model demonstrated adequate calibration and discrimination, indicating that it can reliably rank patients by mortality risk and generate reasonable predicted probabilities. While precise identification of patients with survival <6 months remains challenging, the overall performance supports use of the model to identify patients at highest risk who may be least likely to meet trial LE criteria. Accordingly, the model is best suited for population-level research applications, such as cohort selection and characterization, and should not be used for individual-level clinical decision-making. In this context, the model presented here was applied in an ECA study to two standard-of-care cohorts to approximate the clinical trial inclusion criterion of an LE of ≥6 months, with the aim of generating ECA populations that were comparable to the clinical trial cohort [27, 28]. This application illustrates how mortality prediction models can be used to operationalize otherwise subjective eligibility criteria for cohort studies despite limitations in individual classification accuracy.

Several clinically relevant baseline variables, including ISS stage, β2 microglobulin, and bone marrow plasma cell percentage, were missing for a substantial proportion of LOTs prior to imputation, reflecting data capture patterns in routine clinical practice. Restricting analyses to complete cases would have excluded a large fraction of the eligible RRMM population and limited the applicability of the model to a select group of patients. Multiple imputation was therefore used to enhance the clinical relevance and applicability of the model.

The use of predictive models of mortality to replicate trial LE criteria requires careful consideration, regardless of model performance. Notably, the models developed in the current study used actual mortality as the outcome, which is not the same as subjectively assessed LE in trials. Hypothetically, achieving perfect prediction of mortality, which would exclude all patients from the ECA who died within 6 months of their index date and thus ensure perfect 6-month OS, could bias the results against the treatment arm, in which subjectively assessed LE is imperfect. Indeed, among > 900 patients across five advanced pancreatic cancer clinical trials that included a criterion of a minimum LE of 3 months, 21% of patients died within 3 months [2]. Although not feasible with the current data, training a model to predict subjectively determined LE would better replicate the trial eligibility criterion, acknowledging that the interpretation and application of an LE criterion may vary among trial investigators in the absence of specific guidance.

More broadly, the utility of LE eligibility criteria in oncology trials has been debated [1, 2]. One key issue is the subjectivity of the LE assessment, as it relies on the clinical judgment of local investigators. This can result in variability in determining patient eligibility, and may exclude patients who could benefit from investigational treatments, consequently limiting the generalizability of clinical trial findings. By focusing on patients expected to have longer LE (i.e., patients with few complications and a favorable prognosis), trials may not fully represent the broader patient population seen in real-world settings, where a broader range of health conditions and comorbidities are observed. Moreover, use of the LE criterion poses challenges when researchers attempt to mimic eligibility criteria in RWD to construct ECAs. Since LE assessments are not customarily recorded in RWD sources, the application of the criterion outside the controlled trial setting is challenging, as demonstrated by the results of the present study. Conversely, the LE criterion is useful to ensure that patients can be adherent to multiple doses of the treatment and patients can be followed for long enough to observe responses to and adverse effects of treatment. The decision to use an LE criterion in a single-arm trial should be made with these considerations in mind and weighed against its limitations, particularly if it is expected that an ECA will be used to contextualize the findings. In future studies, similar LE modeling approaches could be applied across study cohorts with parallel implementation in both clinical trial populations and ECAs to improve comparability and strengthen the robustness of the analyses.

Conclusion

The models fit in this study achieved three out of four pre-specified performance thresholds, demonstrating sufficient calibration and discrimination but inadequate classification performance. These results highlight the challenges in accurately predicting mortality in the RRMM population. However, the model performance metrics suggest the model has utility for identifying the patients with the highest risk of early mortality and thus the lowest likelihood of meeting trial LE criteria.

Due to the subjectivity of clinician-assessed LE, the challenge of predicting mortality in this population generally, and the lack of data on LE in RWD sources, the choice to use LE-based eligibility criteria in single-arm trials should be made carefully. Such a decision must weigh the potential benefits to adherence, homogeneity, and patient follow-up against the challenges that may arise should an ECA be needed to contextualize the trial results.
