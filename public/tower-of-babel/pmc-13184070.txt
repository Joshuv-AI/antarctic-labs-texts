Phytosomes against melanoma: disrupting molecular pathways with targeted phytochemical delivery
Authors: Prerna Uniyal, Nivedita Bhardwaj, Deepika Raina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelanoma is still the most lethal form of skin cancer causing approximately 56,000 fatalities annually. This cancer is primarily caused by molecular variations in the MAPK, PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, p53, and immune signalling pathways. Even though immunotherapies and targeted agents have extended survival, resistance to therapy, toxicity, and limited accessibility still hinder progress worldwide. Various phytoconstituents obtained from natural sources, incorporating flavonoids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and polyphenols, have shown potent antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-metastatic effects in melanoma. However, their clinical use is limited by rapid metabolism, minimal systemic stability, poor absorption, and lack of tumor targeting despite attractive preclinical data. A significant improvement in phytochemical delivery in the form of nanoliposomes has emerged, introducing phytosome technology. This technology overcomes the drawbacks by producing stable phospholipid complexes with phytochemicals, thereby enhancing their solubility, bioavailability, membrane-permeability, and pharmacokinetic parameters. This comprehensive study covers the cellular mechanisms of melanoma, the drawbacks of existing therapies, the benefits of nanotechnology-based strategies and the increasing importance of phytosomes as a delivery vehicle for anticancer phytochemicals. It explains the progress in preparation methods, therapeutic effects, preclinical investigations, and the challenges in translating phytosome-based drug delivery for cutaneous melanoma.Keywords: Melanoma, Phytosome, Phytochemicals, Nanocarriers, Melanocytes, MAPK

Introduction

Cutaneous melanoma is still a fatal skin cancer, being the main cause of approximately 58,667 deaths worldwide in 2022. As per the American Cancer Society, the United States alone will account for 122,680 new non-invasive cases and 112,000 invasive cases in 2026. However, the incidence and mortality of this disease vary drastically due to the difference in the access to early diagnosis and primary healthcare facilities globally. The cancerous transformation of melanocytes in the stratum basale of the skin results in metastatic melanoma. The conversion of these cells involves a complex interaction of exogenous and endogenous factors, as well as the tumour intrinsic and immune-associated responses [1, 2]. Normal melanocytes divide rarely, while their growth rate gradually rises as they become a neoplasm. The malignancy is accompanied by a continuous increase in genetic errors, particularly point mutations and copy-number modifications, in cancer cells. After metastasizing, melanoma quickly creates life threatening conditions. For more than 40 years, there have been very few therapeutic options, and the results of clinical trials have always been negative. Nevertheless, in the last ten years, the combination of molecular understanding and novel therapeutic agents has changed the management of advanced melanoma completely and positioned it as a model for the treatment of solid tumors. Historically, the most significant limitation in its therapy, which has now been overcome, was triggered by its biological research and new drug development, leading to a drastic transformation of its treatment over the past decade [3, 4]. The advancement of phytoconstituent formulations incorporated into lipid-compatible molecular complexes, most commonly defined as phytosomes, emerges as a potential druggable candidate for cancer. This phytosome acts as a novel phospholipid-based delivery system, can significantly improve the bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy of bioactive metabolites in cancer treatments. In principle, bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids have strong anticancer potential, but their therapeutic use is limited due to low solubility and poor bioavailability [5, 6]. Phytosome techniques have become a breakthrough solution, thereby fundamentally changing the delivery of plant-derived antineoplastic agents into clinical applications [7]. As a phospholipid-based delivery system, it overcomes the problem of poor solubility, which has been the main obstacle in the development of plant-based anticancer agents [8]. The chemical interactions between a phospholipid molecule and a polyphenolic compound, stabilised by hydrogen bonding or van der Waals forces, result in the formation of phytosomes. Such a strong interaction thus prevents the leakage of drugs, allowing for the development of phytosomal formulations that are more stable than conventional liposomes. The inherent properties of the drug-lipid complex incorporating phase transition temperature, solubility, melting point, and lipid constituents evaluate the physicochemical stability of phytosomes and collectively induce improved physical-stability [9, 10]. Phytosomes exhibit pronounced improvements in bioavailability, with absorption rates up to 20 times higher than those of conventional phytochemical extracts. Such an enhanced delivery system enables phytochemicals to reach therapeutic concentrations in the tissues of interest, thereby transforming previously ineffective compounds into new druggable candidates for cancer. The method is particularly useful for water-soluble phytoconstituents, which previously had poor lipid solubility and low membrane permeability. When it comes to cancer signaling pathways, phytosome, encapsulated secondary metabolites have a significantly enhanced potential to inhibit p53, Wnt/β-catenin, MAPK, NF-κB, and PI3K/AKT signaling cascades associated with melanoma [11]. These pathways are essential for survival, proliferation, and metastasis of carcinogenic cells. Here, the phytosome-facilitated bioactive administration can effectively target the receptor and inhibit the signaling pathway leading to anti-proliferatory action. This enhanced cellular uptake facilitated by phytosomes ensures that bioactive compounds can effectively interact with these key molecular targets of melanoma. Moreover, the lipid-compatible nature of phytosomes makes the penetration of cancer cell membranes more efficient, thus resulting in higher intracellular concentrations of active compounds. Such improved cellular delivery is directly responsible for enhanced anticancer efficacy, as evidenced by preclinical models in which phytosome formulations demonstrate superior tumor inhibition over the use of conventional extracts [12, 13].

Recently, preclinical and in-vitro studies have shown that phytosome-based formulations drastically improve the cellular uptake and activity of different anticancer compounds, thus making these compounds more efficient in modulating cancer pathways and causing apoptotic cell death of cancer cells. Consequently, phytosomes represent the possibility for new, potent, and safer therapeutics, thus shortening the separation between conventional phytochemical therapy and contemporary nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems. The clinical potential of phytosome technology is best evidenced by curcumin-based formulations. A Phase II clinical trial assessed Meriva, a curcumin-phospholipid complex, as a complementary therapy with gemcitabine in patients with advanced-stage pancreatic cancer. This complex decreases the expression of NF-κB-controlled genes involved in inflammation, proliferation, survival and metastasis of cancer cells with no treatment associated toxic effects [14–16]. Several clinical investigations have consistently demonstrated that phytosomes exhibit selective toxicity against cancer cells without harming normal cells. This review provides a critical perspective on melanoma progression and the selectivity of phytosome-encapsulated secondary metabolites, which target the abnormal signalling cascades in melanocytes, thus making them a novel and promising delivery strategy.

Molecular pathogenesis of melanoma

Melanoma progression is largely the consequence of genetic changes that lead to the overactivation of mitogenic and signaling pathways. These altered pathways not only regulate tumorigenesis but also provide the tumor with the ability to adapt and make it resistant to therapy. Basically, melanoma progresses through different stages, where initially the melanocytes remain confined to the epidermis layer. Afterwards, melanocytes become thicker in the epidermal layer and spread to the surrounding lymph nodes. Furthermore, due to the growth and proliferation of tumor cells, they invade the dermis of the skin and later metastasise into subcutaneous layer spreading to other organs as described in Fig. 1. Regulatory cascades affected in melanoma incorporate mainly the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathway, the MAPK pathway, the PI3K/AKT pathway, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and the p53 pathway (Fig. 2) [17]. These changes in signalling pathways lead to melanoma development, facilitating tumorigenesis, allowing tumor adaptibility to its microenvironment, and enhancing resistance to targeted therapies making disease management more difficult and worse clinical outcomes [18]. The loss of regulation of key melanoma signaling pathways exponentially increases carcinogenesis through enhanced cell proliferation, survival and metastasis.

Crosstalk between melanoma signaling pathways

In particular, dysfunction of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway pushes tumor cells to acquire a more aggressive, invasive phenotype. These perturbations also play a role in immune evasion and the emergence of resistance to targeted therapies and complicating treatment approaches. The MAPK pathway is activated by mutations in genes BRAF and NRAS, which cause the cells to divide and survive rapidly and in an unregulated manner. Alterations in the PI3K/AKT pathway exacerbate the situation by enabling cell growth, survival, and resistance to apoptotic cell death [19]. Homeostatic imbalance of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway assists the onset of melanoma and triggers metastasis via cellular differentiation. Contrarily, mutation of p53 results in damage DNA repair and apoptosis, thus promoting malignant transformation. Additionally, mutations in CTNNB1 (β-catenin) may lead to aberrant activation of Wnt signalling, which initiates melanoma. The MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) pathway, especially the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK cascade, is the main driver of melanoma pathogenesis. More than 50% of melanomas carry activating mutations in BRAF gene, with the V600E mutant being the major change that gives rise to constitutive kinase activity and thus, sustained cell growth. Since this pathway governs vital cellular processes such as growth, differentiation, migration, and apoptosis, it is the mainstay of melanoma development. Non-BRAF mutant melanomas often carry mutations in upstream MAPK components like NRAS, KIT, GNAQ, and GNA11, thus confirming the central role of the pathway in disease pathogenesis [20–24]. The Wnt signaling pathway constitutes a major regulatory network in melanoma progression, capable of controlling cellular fate development, polarization, division, and migration [25]. The canonical, β-catenin-dependent pathway largely supports melanoma formation, whereas non-canonical pathways are generally linked with metastasis. This signaling is indispensable for melanocyte derivation from neural crest precursors and harmonizes with the MAPK cascade to evoke melanoma formation [26, 27]. The complex nature of the system is exemplified by the WNT5A gene, which can activate both canonical and non-canonical signalling pathways, not only to escalate melanoma cell motility but also to facilitate metastasis through protein kinase C and calcium-dependent pathways. Also, the PI3K/AKT pathway performs as the main mediator of cell survival, proliferation, and metabolism in melanoma. These pathways are frequently activated through oncogenic RAS, is mostly accompanied by loss of the tumor suppressor PTEN, a situation that is found in 30 to 60% of non-inherited melanomas. Active AKT phosphorylates a multitude of target proteins, among them GSK3, resulting in β-catenin accumulation and oncogene expression together with enhanced anti-apoptosis. The linkage between the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways constitutes a master signaling network that is responsible for mitogenesis and survival. Furthermore, the PI3K pathway is responsible for anabolism, whereas MAPK signaling is directed towards proliferation and invasion [28–30]. Metabolic reprogramming is a signature feature of melanoma progression, characterised by a clear transition from mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation to cytoplasmic aerobic glycolysis, namely the Warburg effect. This metabolic switch is controlled by hypoxia inducible factor 1α (HIF1α) and tumor suppressor MITF gene, which regulates mitochondrial metabolism via PGC1α gene modulation. BRAFV600E activation impairs MITF and concurrently elevates HIF1 expression, thus promoting a metabolic milieu that favors rapid proliferation and invasion [31].

The epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is also a core mechanism underlying melanoma development and metastasis. EMT in melanoma is characterized by loss of cell-cell adhesion, cytoskeleton remodeling, and acquisition of mesenchymal traits, most notably by cadherin switching from E-cadherin to N-cadherin. Transcription factors like SNAIL, SLUG, TWIST1, and ZEB1, are involved in the regulation of this process and are activated by different signaling pathways including the PI3K/AKT and MAPK cascades [32, 33]. The EMT machinery enables the tumor cells to migrate and invade, thus vastly increasing melanoma metastatic potential. Among the various molecular mechanisms that contribute to melanoma, p53 malfunction constitutes a major factor, with the majority of melanomas around 90% exhibiting p53 inactivation [34, 35].

The tumor suppressor c-KIT is pathogenic, directly linked to the development of melanocytes and their survival. This gene was reported to carry mutations in 39% of mucosal melanomas and in 36% of acral melanomas coming from sun-damaged skin. Melanotransferrin (p97/MFI2) has been recognized as a major melanoma tumor antigen causing cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and tumorigenesis. MFI2, by regulating the Wnt signaling pathway, interacts with the molecules that are involved in proliferation, thus, qualifying as a probable drug target [36, 37]. Melanoma-communication driven by exosome is an entirely new and different mechanism where WNT5A causes the secretion of exosomes that have pro-angiogenic and immunosuppressive factors. These vesicles serve as communication links between cells, regulate the function of the immune system, and therapy resistance thus, leading to the formation of metastatic microenvironments. Moreover, G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) incorporating endothelin receptors, melanocortin type 1 receptor, and chemokine receptors which are significantly involve in angiogenesis and tumor invasion have the most profound influence on melanoma tumorigenesis and metastatic progression [38]. The molecular complexity of melanoma has been the main driver behind the development of targeted therapeutic strategies like BRAF inhibitors such as vemurafenib, immune checkpoint inhibitors, for instance, nivolumab and pembrolizumab targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway and mTOR inhibitors that resolve the PI3K/AKT cascade. Nevertheless, the complexicity of interrelated pathways and the development of resistitive mechanisms highlights the necessity of combination therapies as well as polypharmacological strategies that are capable of targeting several oncogenic drivers at the same time in order to inhibit melanoma cells from adapting and evading treatment [39, 40].

Nanocarrier for melanoma treatment

Nanocarrier-based therapies have become a revolutionary method in the treatment of melanoma and skin cancer, providing advanced solutions to the drug delivery challenges in cancer treatment [41]. The use of nanotechnology in melanoma treatment is a major shift away from the conventional therapeutic methods, as it addresses the issues that have been the main barriers to treatment effectiveness, like poor drug solubility, instability, multidrug resistance, and systemic toxicity [42, 43].

Enhanced drug properties and delivery systems

Nanocarrier, based systems enable extremely precise targeting of cells through both active and passive means. Passive targeting relies on the enhanced permeability and retention effect, whereby nanoparticles tend to accumulate more in tumor tissues due to the leaky blood vessels and impaired lymphatic drainage that characterize these tissues. In contrast, active targeting is obtained by surface modification with specific ligands, antibodies, or aptamers that recognize biomarkers unique to melanoma, thus allowing drug delivery to selectively target the malignant cells and not the normal ones [44]. The therapeutic benefits are not limited to the mere delivery of drugs, but rather include synergistic effects that lead to higher treatment efficacy. These delivery vehicles can resensitize cancer cells to chemotherapy by circumventing efflux pumps and releasing drugs directly to intracellular targets. Furthermore, they allow for combination therapy strategies in which several therapeutic agents with different mechanisms can be co, delivered in one nanocarrier system [13, 45]. Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems have the potential to radically change the physicochemical properties of the drugs used. These systems enhance the aqueous solubility of poorly soluble compounds, facilitating the administration of potent anticancer drugs [46]. Nanocarriers also offer improved drug stability through isolating therapeutic molecules from the enzymatic degradation occurring in the biological environment, they effectively extend their half-lives and optimise their bioavailability. Additionally, the systems provide for controlled and sustained drug release profiles, thus therapeutic concentrations can be maintained for longer periods at the target sites and systemic exposure together with toxicity can be reduced to the minimum [47].

Diverse nanotechnology-based carriers

A vast range of sophisticated delivery vehicles is utilised in nanotechnology-based melanoma treatments, offering various advantages in different therapeutic applications [48]. Among these, exosomes are perhaps the most attractive natural nanosystems, functioning as biomimetic carriers that do not stimulate immune response and, at the same time, offer targeted drug delivery. These naturally derived vesicles are highly biocompatible and can be loaded with therapeutic agents, allowing them to be directed specifically to melanoma cells. Polymersomes and polymeric nanoparticles, especially those made from biocompatible polymers like PLGA (polylactic-co-glycolic acid), have the potential for controlled release, and their surfaces can be modified to be more target-specific. Also, dendrimers have a number of advantages that derive from their highly branched, 3-D structure, which makes them capable of binding several drug molecules at the same time and also increases cellular uptake [49]. These synthetic macromolecules are remarkably versatile when it comes to loading drugs and can be very specifically engineered for particular therapeutic uses. Polymeric drug conjugates are another advanced method by which drugs are covalently attached to polymer backbones, thus allowing controlled release via certain biological triggers. Polymeric micelles derived from amphiphilic block copolymers have outstanding solubilization abilities for hydrophobic drugs and at the same time, they are given stealth properties that help to evade immune clearance [12, 50, 51].

Nanocarrier-based systems offer targeting capabilities through both active and passive mechanisms. Passive targeting relies on the enhanced permeability and retention effect causing higher accumulation of nanoparticles in tumor tissues due to their leaky blood vessels and lack of lymphatic drainage. While active targeting is attained by surface functionalization with specific ligands, antibodies, or aptamers that recognize biomarkers unique to melanoma, thus allowing selective drug delivery to malignant cells, avoiding normal cells [44]. The therapeutic benefits are even more extensive than just drug delivery, as they include synergistic effects that improve the overall efficacy of the treatment. These delivery systems can mitigate the multidrug resistance mechanisms by evading efflux pumps and drug delivery directly to intracellular targets [13, 45] (Fig. 3).

Overcoming multidrug resistance

The nanotechnology-based therapeutics development significantly contributed to tackling multidrug resistance being a major clinical issue leading to treatment failure in melanoma. These nanocarriers can overcome resistance mechanisms by omitting P-glycoprotein efflux pumps, delivering drugs to organelles where resistance proteins are less exposed, and allowing the use of combination therapy that can simultaneously target different resistance pathways [52]. The clinical outcome of these treatments is supported by the validation of various nanoformulations for cancer therapy and a large number of clinical trials, specifically in melanoma. Compared to conventional formulations, the nanocarrier-based formulation exhibits a good safety profile with lower systemic toxicity, fewer side effects, and enhanced therapeutic indices. The biocompatibility of many nanocarriers, especially natural products or FDA-approved polymers, further supports their clinical use.

The combination of nanotechnology with new therapeutic modalities incorporating immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and photodynamic approaches is the next step in melanoma treatment [53]. These multimodal strategies use the targeting potential of nanotechnology to boost the effectiveness of the Next-Gen therapeutics while maintaining optimal safety profiles. The evolution of nanocarriers with stimuli-responsive characteristics like pH-sensitive release and temperature-induced drug delivery is likely to lead to even more accurate melanoma treatment. A wide range of nanocarriers incorporates polymeric micelles, polymersomes, exosomes, nanoparticles, dendrimers, and polymeric drug conjugates, which offer different benefits as advanced drug delivery systems [54]. These artificially engineered non-bioactive nonviral vectors serve as means for the transfer of therapeutic materials to target cells with the least possible side effects, reduced toxicity, low immunogenicity, and remarkable possibility for chemical transformations. Also, the half-life and bioavailability of the drug can be significantly altered by the surface properties of the nanoparticles, allowing them to efficiently pass through epithelial micropores [55]. Dendrimers are advanced, spherical polymeric macromolecules exhibiting, hyperbranched structure. They provide the adjustable surface properties with multiple valencies, water solubility, uniform size distribution, and internal space for drug loading, making them potential candidates for targeted drug delivery. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are another device that is capable of providing highly selective cancer drug providing multiple benefits for chemotherapy due to their optical property. But CNT-based treatment requires more investigational study as long-term safety evaluation, scale-up manufacturing, and reproducibility related to it are serious concerns that need to be resolved before clinical application. Self-assembled, closed colloidal entities with lipid bilayers enclosing the central aqueous regions, named as liposomes, have found application in many cancer treatments, where immunoliposomes give an advantage of selectivity in drug delivery. These liposomal formulations serve as excellent delivery systems for doxorubicin, paclitaxel, and nucleic acids, simultaneously exhibit better absorption and superior anti-tumor activity. The existence of novel vesicular systems like niosomes, ethosomes, and transferosomes, has led to the enhancement of therapeutic abilities. Niosomes are more stable, have a longer shelf-life, are biodegradable, biocompatible and facilitate controlled drug delivery. While ethosomes are a vehicle of choice for the deep skin delivery of drugs that have poor permeation capacity. Transferosomes, being highly deformable vesicles, are able to transport a large number of molecules through the epidermis, thus significantly beneficial in melanoma and basal cell carcinoma treatment. Exosomes can be considered the most naturally derived nanoparticles, surrounded by bilayered membranes, and are non-immunogenic due to their similarity to the parent cells. Extracellular vesicles are a diverse group of lipid bilayer, enclosed cellular messengers, which, along with a variety of biomolecules, traverse the intercellular space containing lipids, proteins, metabolites, or different RNA species. Since these vesicles are naturally derived and biologically compatible, they are very attractive for therapeutic use in various disease. Aquasomes are another advanced delivery system based on self-assembling characteristics with nanocrystalline cores and polyhydroxy oligomeric films, exhibiting significant preservation of bioactivity for conformation-sensitive compounds [56, 57]. Preparation of phytosomes

The preparation of bioactive phytosomes involves techniques that ensure optimal complexation between phytoconstituents and phospholipids, thereby enhancing bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Currently, three primary methodologies dominate the synthesis of phytosomes, i.e., solvent evaporation/thin film hydration, antisolvent precipitation, and lyophilization, each offering distinct advantages for different bioactive compounds and applications. The ratio of phytoconstituents and phospholipid required for the preparation of phytosome varies according to the properties of both the materials. A table has been summarised considering the common solvent, the general method and the particle size used for the preparation of phytosome [75, 76].

The solvent evaporation technique represents the most conventional and widely adopted approach for synthesizing phytosomes. This method involves dissolving both phytoconstituents and phosphatidylcholine in suitable organic solvents at carefully controlled stoichiometric ratios, typically ranging from 1:1 to 1:3 molar ratios, depending on the specific bioactive compound. The process begins by combining both phytochemicals and lipids in the presence of aprotic solvents, such as tetrahydrofuran, methylene chloride, or dichloromethane, followed by refluxing at optimized temperatures ranging from 40 to 60 °C for predetermined durations of 1–4 h. The critical parameters influencing complex formation include maintaining a temperature of 40–50 °C, continuous stirring to ensure homogeneous mixing, and controlled evaporation under reduced pressure to remove organic solvents. The resulting thin film formation at the flask bottom represents the phyto-phospholipid complex, which is subsequently hydrated with aqueous media to form vesicular structures. This method has demonstrated exceptional success in creating mitomycin C phytosomes with enhanced solubility and berberine-phospholipid complexes, which show improved bioavailability profiles [77–79].

Another technique, antisolvent precipitation, offers an efficient alternative for phytosome synthesis, particularly suitable for temperature-sensitive compounds that may degrade during prolonged heating. This method involves refluxing phytoconstituents with phospholipids in dichloromethane at temperatures below 60 °C, typically for 2–3 h until complete dissolution occurs. The crucial step involves adding n-hexane as an antisolvent to induce immediate precipitation of the phytosome complex while maintaining continuous stirring. The process optimization studies have revealed that the phospholipid-to-extract ratio significantly impacts particle size and entrapment efficiency, with a ratio of 1:3 showing superior results compared to ratios of 1:1 or 1:2. The temperature control remains critical, as reflux temperatures of 50 °C produce optimal particle sizes in the nanometer range (230–470 nm) while maintaining zeta potentials between −46 and −59.2 mV, indicating excellent colloidal stability. Following precipitation, the complex is stored overnight in vacuum desiccators to remove residual solvents, followed by filtration and collection [80–82].

Moreover, lyophilization represents the most sophisticated preparation technique, offering advantages for moisture-sensitive compounds and enabling one-step synthesis without requiring separate evaporation steps. This method involves dissolving phytoconstituents in dimethyl sulfoxide and phospholipids in tert-butyl alcohol, followed by stirring for 3 h at room temperature to facilitate the formation of a complex. The freeze-drying process consists of three critical phases: initial freezing at −80 °C for 4 h, primary drying under vacuum at 40 mbar and 40 °C for 24 h, and secondary drying at 25 °C for another 24 h. This technique has proven particularly effective for diosmin phytosomes, achieving entrapment efficiencies exceeding 99% and particle sizes of 64.44 nm with release rates up to 93% within 2 h [83–85].

There are several critical parameters that influence phytosome formation, including solvent selection, stoichiometric ratios, reaction temperature, and reaction duration. The drug-to-lipid ratio optimization typically ranges from 0.5:1 to 3:1, with 1:1 ratios being most commonly employed for standardized preparations. Temperature control remains paramount, as elevated temperatures may degrade sensitive phytochemicals while insufficient heating prevents complete complexation. The characterization of the synthesized phytosome complex involves dynamic light scattering, transmission electron microscopy, differential scanning calorimetry, particle size distribution analysis, morphological evaluation, and thermal properties assessment. The resulting phytosomes typically exhibit spherical morphology with smooth surfaces, nanoscale dimensions, and improved stability profiles compared to free phytoconstituents. Furthermore, storage conditions play a crucial role in maintaining phytosome integrity, with refrigeration at 4 °C in amber-colored containers under a nitrogen atmosphere being optimal for long-term stability. These preparation methodologies have collectively enabled the successful formulation of numerous bioactive phytosomes, which have significantly enhanced therapeutic potential and improved patient compliance through oral administration routes [86, 87] (Table 3) .

Phytosomes targeting melanoma signaling pathways

Phytosomes are specialized lipid-based vesicular delivery systems where plant-derived bioactive compounds are complexed with phospholipids. This unique structure significantly enhances the bioavailability and therapeutic performance of herbal extracts, especially in skin-care applications. The phospholipid component acts as an emulsifier, facilitating effective dermal penetration and targeted delivery of the phytochemicals, thus improving absorption and efficacy. The modulation of signalling pathways is fundamentally driven by the phytochemicals (drug molecules). The carrier (phospholipid complex of phytosome) does not inherently kill the cancer cells, rather it strictly serve to overcome the kinetic and solubility bottleneck that normally prevent phytochemicals from reaching therapeutic concentration inside tumor cells. The therapeutic superiority of phytosome against melanoma relies on a targeted delivery uptake-release-modulation sequence. This mechanism occurs in three distinct phase: Cellular uptake, Intracellular Release and Targeted pathways modulation. The phospholipid matrix of phytosome closely mimic amphiphilic structure of natural biological cell membranes. When delivered, phytosome interact with the lipid-rich membrane of melanoma cells primarily through two highly efficient mechanism; endocytosis and contact-facilitated drug delivery. Unlike traditional phytochemicals in their free forms which are restricted by lipophilic cell barrier, the internalized phytosome protects its phytochemicals payload from premature enzyme degradation. These vesicles mediated entry helps the drug bypass P-glycoprotein efflux pumps, mitigating multidrug resistance. The phytosome complex undergoes dissociation releasing the free active phytoconstituents directly into intercellular space. As this delivery system successfully transport a concentrated intact payload into the cell the drug easily achieves the critical threshold required to exert pharmacological activity and interacts with specific kinases and transcription factors. The carrier ensures access while phytochemical executes the anticancer modulation.

A glucosinolate, sinigrin, obtained from the Brassicaceae family, was evaluated for its wound healing properties on A-375 and HaCaT cells. A phytosome complex of sinigrin was formulated, exhibiting significant wound healing properties. The phytosome complex completely healed the wound in 42 h at higher concentration of 0.14 mg/ml, while sinigrin independently promoted wound closure up to 71%, suggesting the potential need for synergistic formulations to enhance therapeutic outcomes. The complex demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity towards HaCaT keratinocytes, while exhibiting pronounced cytotoxic effects against A-375 melanoma cells at increased concentrations. These findings emphasize the enhanced therapeutic efficacy of sinigrin upon complexation with phytosomes, particularly in promoting healing. This strategy holds promise for treating cancer-associated wounds and warrants further investigation [88]. The study further investigated the potential of using a sinigrin–phytosome complex as an enhanced delivery system against melanoma. The sinigrin–phytosome complex was prepared by chemically linking sinigrin with phosphatidylcholine, forming vesicular structures that enhance skin compatibility and permeation. In vitro drug release studies demonstrated that free sinigrin was rapidly released within 2 h, while the sinigrin–phytosome complex exhibited a controlled and sustained release over 8 h, releasing only about 45.5% after 2 h. This sustained release suggests that the phytosome complex can provide a more gradual and prolonged delivery of the active compound. This slow-release formulation is beneficial for maintaining therapeutic levels of sinigrin in the skin over a prolonged period. Skin permeation studies using human abdominal skin and the tape stripping technique revealed that the sinigrin–phytosome complex significantly enhanced the delivery of sinigrin into the stratum corneum epidermis layer compared to free sinigrin. The median concentration of sinigrin delivered into this skin layer by the phytosome complex was approximately 0.5155 µg/ml, much higher than the 0.0730 µg/ml observed with free sinigrin. Notably, neither formulation delivered sinigrin into the deeper epidermis–dermis layers, indicating localized retention in the upper skin layers, which is desirable for treatments targeting the skin surface and superficial conditions like wound healing. The increased skin retention is attributed to the formation of a molecular complex between sinigrin and phospholipids, which enhances lipid compatibility and improves skin absorption without damaging the epidermis. The ability of the phytosome complex to deliver sinigrin effectively to the target site with sustained release and enhanced retention highlights its promise as a therapeutic system. This suggests a potential application not only for wound healing but also for treating various dermatological conditions, including melanoma, where targeted topical delivery and prolonged drug availability are crucial for achieving effective treatment outcomes. Further research into the mechanisms of action and permeation through compromised skin is suggested to fully exploit the capabilities of this delivery system [89].

Centella asiatica phytosome (CA phytosome) exhibits a significant anti-inflammatory effect in a phthalic anhydride‐induced mouse model of atopic dermatitis by targeting the NF-κB signaling pathway. Topical application of 0.2% and 0.4% CA phytosome over four weeks significantly attenuated hallmark features of dermatitis, such as ear thickness, hyperkeratosis, epidermal hyperplasia, mast cell proliferation, and inflammatory cell infiltration, compared to untreated controls. These histological improvements were accompanied by decreased auricular lymph node enlargement and reduced serum IgE levels, indicating suppression of the allergic immune response. At the molecular level, CA phytosome decreased NF-κB DNA-binding activity by blocking nuclear translocation of NF-κB subunits p65 and p50 and inhibiting IκBα degradation. This led to lower expression of pro‐inflammatory enzymes COX-2 and iNOS, decreased release of cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α, both in vivo and in LPS‐stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. The phytosome formulation enhances the bioavailability and membrane permeability of hydrophilic Centella asiatica constituents asiaticoside, madecassic acid, and asiatic acid allowing half the dose to achieve similar therapeutic effects as unformulated extracts. This improved delivery system not only strengthens anti‐inflammatory efficacy but also offers a favorable safety profile compared to systemic immunosuppressants. Given the central role of chronic inflammation and NF-κB activation in melanoma progression, the ability of CA phytosome to inhibit NF-κB signaling and downstream inflammatory mediators suggests potential utility in melanoma therapy. NF-κB promotes melanoma cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis; its inhibition by CA phytosome components could enhance apoptosis and reduce tumor invasiveness. Moreover, asiatic acid has been shown to induce apoptosis in human melanoma cells via ROS generation and caspase activation, while asiaticoside suppresses melanogenesis‐associated pathways. Combined with the enhanced tumor cell uptake afforded by phytosome technology, CA phytosome represents a promising candidate for further investigation as a multifaceted therapeutic in melanoma, leveraging its anti-inflammatory, pro-apoptotic, and bioavailability-enhancing properties [90].

The formulation of a phytosomal nanocomplex that includes olive oil for delivering quercetin to the skin offers a hopeful approach for improving melanoma cancer therapy by leveraging several therapeutic mechanisms. The optimized formulation exhibited superior physicochemical properties, having a zeta potential of − 26.3 mV, 206.7 nm of particle diameter, and an encapsulation efficiency of 85.3%. Most significantly, this nanocomplex achieved a 1.3-fold higher skin permeation compared to olive-oil/surfactant-free formulations and 1.9-fold higher permeation than control formulations, enabling substantially improved quercetin bioavailability at the target melanoma site. This enhanced delivery system directly supports melanoma treatment through quercetin’s proven anti-cancer mechanisms. Quercetin induces mitochondrial apoptosis in melanoma cells by depolarizing the mitochondrial membrane potential, promoting the release of cytochrome c, and activating the caspase-3 and PARP cleavage pathways. The compound significantly inhibits melanoma cell proliferation by arresting cells in the G0/G1 phase and downregulating proliferation markers like PCNA and survivin. Additionally, quercetin suppresses melanoma cell migration by reducing wound closure capacity to only 2.6–31.2% in treated cells compared to controls, while also targeting key metastatic pathways, including Raf-Akt signaling. The biphasic activity of quercetin is particularly advantageous, as concentrations above 12.5 µM demonstrate potent anti-cancer effects by inducing oxidative stress and cell death, while lower concentrations may provide protective antioxidant benefits. The olive oil/phytosomal delivery system overcomes quercetin’s inherent limitations, including poor water solubility and rapid clearance, thereby ensuring sustained release and higher local concentrations at melanoma lesions while minimizing systemic toxicity. The formulation showed skin barrier modification capabilities without significant toxicity, supporting its clinical safety profile. By exploiting melanoma cells’ high tyrosinase activity, which readily metabolizes quercetin into anti-cancer metabolites affecting malignant cells while sparing normal cells. The enhanced skin penetration achieved through this nanocomplex formulation makes quercetin a viable topical adjuvant therapy for melanoma, potentially offering a cost-effective, non-invasive treatment option that complements existing therapies through its multi-target anti-cancer mechanisms [91].

Excessive exposure to ultraviolet radiation from sunlight constitutes one of the most significant environmental risk factors for developing skin cancer, prompting widespread research into effective photoprotective strategies. While synthetic sunscreens have traditionally dominated the market, they present notable limitations, including potential skin irritation, toxicity concerns, and environmental impact, that have driven scientists to explore natural alternatives. Herbal-based sunscreens derived from plant phytochemicals offer a promising solution, as these compounds naturally possess both antioxidant activity and Sun Protection Factor (SPF) capabilities that can effectively shield skin from harmful UV damage. However, the predominant challenge with plant-derived compounds lies in their hydrophilic nature, which significantly restricts their ability to penetrate through the lipophilic barriers of human skin, particularly the stratum corneum, thereby limiting their bioavailability and reducing their therapeutic efficacy in deeper skin layers where UV protection is most crucial. To address this bioavailability challenge, researchers have turned to phytosome technology as an innovative delivery system that can enhance the skin penetration and effectiveness of hydrophilic phytochemicals. The primary objective of this comprehensive study was to develop and evaluate phytosomal formulations containing ethanolic extracts from two varieties of Agave americana, specifically A. americana americana and A. americana marginata, with enhanced antioxidant and SPF activities for topical sun protection applications. The study employed soxhlet extraction methodology using ethanol as the solvent to efficiently isolate phytochemicals from dried Agave leaves, ensuring maximum recovery of bioactive compounds while maintaining their structural integrity and biological activity. Following extraction, comprehensive phytochemical screening was conducted using standardized qualitative tests to confirm the presence of key bioactive compound classes, particularly phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and tannins, which are known to contribute significantly to both antioxidant capacity and natural UV protection properties. The antioxidant potential of both Agave extracts was systematically evaluated using the well-established 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay, which provides a reliable, rapid, and cost-effective method for quantifying free radical neutralization capacity. Simultaneously, the Sun Protection Factor activity was determined through UV spectroscopic analysis, a standardized in vitro method that measures the ability of substances to absorb harmful UV radiation across the critical wavelength ranges responsible for skin damage. Following initial characterization, phytosomal formulations were prepared by complexing the plant extracts with soy lecithin using optimized ratios and processing conditions, creating phospholipid-based nanocarriers designed to overcome the skin penetration limitations of the hydrophilic phytochemicals. The resulting phytosomal systems were comprehensively characterized using dynamic light scattering techniques to determine critical physical parameters including particle size distribution, Polydispersity Index (PDI) as a measure of size uniformity, and zeta potential to assess colloidal stability and surface charge characteristics. The comparative analysis revealed that A. americana americana demonstrated significantly superior phytochemical content compared to A. americana marginata, containing higher concentrations of flavonoids, phenolic compounds, and tannins that directly translated into enhanced biological activities. This superior phytochemical profile resulted in considerably better antioxidant performance, with A. americana americana achieving an IC50 value of 4.77 µg/mL compared to 5.66 µg/mL for A. americana marginata, indicating a stronger free radical scavenging capacity at lower concentrations. Similarly, the SPF activity measurements demonstrated a clear advantage for A. americana americana, which achieved an SPF value of 9.95, compared to only 5.84 for A. americana marginata, confirming a direct relationship between phytochemical content and photoprotective efficacy. The phytosomal encapsulation process resulted in remarkable enhancement of SPF activity across all formulations tested, with A. americana americana phytosomes achieving SPF values ranging from 9.96 to 13.3 depending on the specific formulation parameters. The optimal formulation, which contained 40 mg of A. americana extract complexed with 60 mg of soy lecithin, demonstrated the highest SPF activity of 13.3, representing a significant improvement over the performance of the free extract. This superior formulation also exhibited the most favorable physical characteristics for topical application, including the smallest particle size of 91 nm, which is ideal for skin penetration, a Polydispersity Index of 0.235 indicating excellent size uniformity, and a zeta potential of −4.95 mV suggesting adequate colloidal stability for practical formulation purposes. These comprehensive results provide compelling evidence that the encapsulation of Agave americana extracts within phytosomal delivery systems significantly enhances their Sun Protection Factor activity while maintaining the natural antioxidant benefits of the plant compounds. The enhanced performance can be attributed to the improved bioavailability achieved through the phospholipid-based delivery system, which facilitates better skin penetration of the otherwise poorly permeable hydrophilic phytochemicals [92].

Another study presents the development and evaluation of curcumin nanoethosomes as a promising topical formulation for melanoma treatment, addressing the challenges of curcumin’s hydrophobicity and poor skin permeation. The study shows that curcumin, encapsulated in nanoethosomes with an average size of 67 nm and high encapsulation efficiency (87%), exhibits excellent compatibility with formulation additives as confirmed by spectroscopic and thermal analyses. Ex vivo skin permeation studies using full-thickness human skin demonstrated significantly superior skin flux and total permeated curcumin from nanoethosomal formulations compared to control solutions, supporting greater transdermal delivery. Furthermore, cytotoxicity assays on human melanoma cells (SK-MEL-28) revealed concentration- and time-dependent antiproliferative effects, with the nanoethosome formulation showing comparable or even enhanced efficacy to pure curcumin in reducing melanoma cell viability. Overall, the findings suggest that curcumin-loaded nanoethosomes could enable effective, safe, and improved topical melanoma therapy by overcoming permeation barriers and exerting potent anticancer activity [93].

One study investigated the formulation and evaluation of a face serum containing phytosomes made from gingerol oil, Carica papaya pulp extract, and aloe vera gel, exploring their potential applications in skin care and skin cancer prevention. The phytosomes were developed to enhance the bioavailability of plant-derived antioxidants and bioactives, which otherwise have limited skin penetration due to poor lipid solubility. Gingerol, the active compound from ginger, is noted specifically for its anti-inflammatory properties and its ability to inhibit melanin synthesis and tyrosinase activity in melanoma cells, processes directly relevant to skin cancer prevention and pigmentation disorders. The phytosomal serum demonstrated desirable physicochemical characteristics, including nanoparticle sizes (~ 198–245 nm), stable negative surface charge, skin-compatible pH (5.6), and rapid absorption, ensuring suitability for topical application. Antioxidant and antimicrobial assays showed that the formulation possesses strong free radical scavenging and defensive activity against microbial contaminants, while stability tests confirmed its safety and effectiveness over several months. By improving the delivery and efficacy of gingerol and papaya phytoconstituents, the developed phytosomal serum holds promise not only for general skin health but also as a preventive adjuvant in skin cancer care, especially for conditions like melanoma, where phytochemicals with anti-melanogenic and antiproliferative activity are beneficial [94, 95].

Significance of phytosome in cancer treatment

The incorporation of phytosomes into cancer treatment represents a promising breakthrough in the pursuit of highly effective, targeted, and tolerable treatment modalities [96]. These are sophisticated lipid-based delivery systems that encapsulate bioactive compounds derived from plants by binding them to phospholipids, thereby enhancing their solubility, stability, bioavailability and minimizing systemic toxicity and drug resistance. One of the most compelling benefits of phytosomes is their ability to facilitate targeted delivery of therapeutic agents straight into tumor cells, thereby minimizing collateral injury to normal cells, a major drawback of chemotherapy and radiation therapy [97, 98]. The first commercial application of this innovation centered on silybin, a potent flavonolignan and the principal active constituent of silymarin, a flavonoid compound extracted from the fruits of Silybum marianum (milk thistle), a member of the family Asteraceae. This formulation, originally designated as IDB 1016 or Silipide, was later rebranded as Siliphos® Phytosome™ [70, 99]. Phytosomes extracted from fingerroot (Boesenbergia rotunda) demonstrated significant apoptotic activity on colorectal cancer cells at concentrations of 50 µg/mL, while exhibiting no harmful effects on normal colon epithelial cells. This selective targeting is achieved through the upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes, including caspase 9, caspase 3, Bax, and Bcl-2, in conjunction with the release of reactive oxygen species [100, 101]. By complexing silybin with phosphatidylcholine, the phytosome system significantly improved its pharmacokinetic profile, facilitating enhanced absorption and cellular uptake [102]. This silybin-phosphatidylcholine complex has since been clinically validated for their robust anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and hepatoprotective properties. As reviewed in earlier literature, particularly in 2005, its efficacy in liver detoxification and cellular defense mechanisms has been well established [103, 104]. The current discourse seeks to update its therapeutic relevance in light of research developments that have emerged in the intervening years. By improving the pharmacokinetic profile of plant-derived bioactives, phytosomes ensure that these agents reach their intended site of action in therapeutically relevant concentrations, thereby amplifying their anticancer efficacy while reducing adverse effects [105]. Numerous research findings indicated that curcumin phytosome adjunct therapy is both safe and effectively leads to favorable response rates in initial treatment of advanced pancreatic cancer [106]. Quercetin phytosomes have demonstrated exceptional promise across multiple cancer types, with bioavailability improvements of up to 20 times compared to standard quercetin formulations [107, 108]. Clinical applications have shown efficacy in Colorectal cancer and Lung cancer. Combination therapy with curcumin led to a significant reduction of adenomas in familial adenomatous polyposis patients. Dietary quercetin intake influenced tumor suppressor miRNA expression in lung cancer patients Medicinal plants have long been revered in traditional medicine for their multifaceted therapeutic properties, and many of their bioactive constituents, such as resveratrol from grapes, curcumin from turmeric, and epigallocatechin gallate from green tea, exhibit potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer activities [109, 110]. Traditional herbal compounds, despite their proven anticancer properties, suffer from poor water solubility and rapid metabolism, resulting in suboptimal therapeutic concentrations at tumor sites. The primary significance of phytosomes lies in their attribute to significantly enhance the pharmacokinetic profiles of phytochemicals through improved solubility and cellular uptake. Phytosome technology addresses this critical limitation by encapsulating bioactive compounds within phospholipid complexes, resulting in 126-fold increases in solubility and substantially improved absorption across cellular membranes. This enhancement translates to significantly higher plasma concentrations and prolonged therapeutic effects, with studies demonstrating up to 1.58-fold increases in bioavailability compared to conventional formulations [111–113].

Targeted delivery capability represents another crucial advantage of phytosomes in oncological treatments. These nanocarriers utilise the improved permeability and retention effect, allowing preferential accumulation in tumor tissues without damaging the normal cells from unnecessary exposure to therapeutic agents. The phospholipid composition of phytosomes facilitates the efficient penetration of cancer cell membranes, resulting in increased intracellular concentrations of active compounds and a more effective interaction with crucial cancer signaling pathways, including the NF-κB and PI3K/AKT signaling cascades that are essential for cancer cell survival and proliferation (Fig. 2). The synergistic potential of phytosomes with existing cancer treatments represents a paradigm shift in chemotherapeutic approaches [114]. Studies have demonstrated that phytosome-delivered compounds can significantly improve the efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents while diminishing their associated toxicities. It was observed that curcumin-loaded phytosomes have shown the ability to reverse multidrug resistance in cancer cells by inhibiting P-glycoprotein expression, thereby restoring sensitivity to chemotherapeutics that cancer cells had previously become resistant to. This mechanism is particularly valuable in addressing one of the most significant obstacles in cancer treatment, the development of drug resistance that compromises therapeutic outcomes [115]. Safety profiles of phytosome-based therapies offer substantial advantages over synthetic drugs. Studies consistently demonstrate that phytosomes exhibit selective cytotoxicity against cancer cells while showing minimal adverse effects on normal tissues. These phytosome formulations, administered at therapeutic concentrations, induced significant toxicity in various cancer cell lines, while having no visible effects on normal epithelial cells, highlighting their tumor-selective action. This selectivity stems from their natural origin and biocompatible phospholipid composition, which reduces the risk of severe adverse reactions commonly associated with conventional chemotherapy. The versatility of phytosome applications extends across multiple types of cancer and various treatment modalities. Clinical investigations have demonstrated efficacy in breast, colorectal, liver, prostate, brain cancers, and melanoma, with some formulations showing tumor inhibition rates six times higher than conventional drug delivery methods. Phytosomes have successfully delivered a wide range of therapeutic compounds, from traditional phytochemicals like curcumin, resveratrol, and quercetin to modern chemotherapeutic agents such as paclitaxel, docetaxel, and cisplatin, consistently improving their therapeutic indices [116–118].

Mechanistic advantages of phytosomes include their characteristic to safeguard bioactive compounds from metabolic degradation, extend circulation times, and provide controlled drug release. The phospholipid encapsulation shields therapeutic agents from harsh enzymatic conditions in the digestive system while maintaining their structural integrity and biological activity. This protection mechanism ensures that drugs retain efficacy throughout their journey to target tissues, providing sustained therapeutic effects that translate to improved patient outcomes. Furthermore, phytosomes offer both economic and practical benefits for the implementation of cancer treatment. Their cost-effective manufacturing processes and oral bioavailability make them accessible alternatives to expensive intravenous therapies, potentially democratizing access to advanced cancer treatments [119–121] (Fig. 4).

Even subtle pH fluctuations in tumor microenvironments can trigger premature degradation of phospholipids or leakage of payloads. Achieving physicochemical robustness under physiological and storage conditions remains a major hurdle. Additionally, translating laboratory protocols into industrial-scale production without compromising product uniformity or quality requires optimized unit operations, reliable raw material supply chains, and closed-loop control of critical process parameters, which can lead to difficulties in scale-up manufacturing [126, 127]. Despite promising in vitro cytotoxicity against melanoma lines, phytosome formulations lack comprehensive animal and human trials to validate safety and therapeutic superiority. Bridging preclinical pharmacokinetics with clinical efficacy endpoints is essential. Moreover, the absence of universally accepted guidelines for botanical–phospholipid complexes complicates the regulatory approval process. Melanoma’s heterogeneous antigen expression and dense extracellular matrix impede uniform nanocarrier penetration. Engineering ligand-decorated phytosomes that navigate these biological barriers remains an active challenge. Addressing these obstacles through interdisciplinary collaboration, involving formulation scientists, dermatologists, oncologists, and regulatory experts, will pave the way for phytosome-based therapies to become a viable, plant-derived option against melanoma [128, 129].

Future perspectives and challenges

Accelerating the clinical translation of phytosome nanocarriers for melanoma relies on demonstrating clear improvements in bioavailability that translate into enhanced antitumor efficacy in vivo. Emerging evidence suggests that phytophospholipid complexes can significantly improve the oral bioavailability, cellular uptake, and tumor-targeting potential of hydrophobic phytochemicals. By precisely controlling particle size and dispersion, particularly via supercritical fluid drying, future formulations may achieve the optimal enhanced permeability and retention effect crucial for melanoma therapy. Moreover, combining phytosomes with well-characterized controlled-release matrices or co-encapsulating synergistic phytochemicals (for example, curcumin with quercetin) could potentiate anticancer activity by orchestrating multi-targeted mechanisms within melanoma cells. However, substantial obstacles must be addressed before phytosome-based melanoma treatments can progress beyond laboratory proof-of-concept, such as stability optimization, Scale-Up, and Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic Correlation [75, 130].

Traditional solvent-evaporation methods involve multiple unit operations, whose drying steps critically influence particle size, morphology, and hygroscopicity factors that have not yet been optimized for consistency or scale. Implementing supercritical fluid techniques offers tighter control of these properties at low temperatures, but requires systematic study to establish process parameters that maintain phospholipid integrity and payload potency. Additionally, transitioning from bench to industrial scale requires robust, reproducible processes that maintain vesicle stability, particularly against pH variations encountered during storage and administration. Current extrusion and microfluidics platforms can be adapted for large-scale phytosome production; however, each must be validated for batch-to-batch consistency, residual solvent levels, and lipid oxidation. A critical knowledge gap remains in linking enhanced plasma exposure of phytosome-loaded phytochemicals to measurable improvements in tumor suppression and survival in melanoma models. Comprehensive in vitro–in vivo correlation (IVIVC) studies, including real-time imaging of tumor uptake and biodistribution in melanoma xenografts, are crucial for demonstrating clinical superiority over free compounds. Prior to clinical trials, rigorous assessment of bioaccumulation, metabolism, and excretion profiles must confirm that phospholipid carriers pose no long-term toxicity or off-target effects. Harmonizing these data with existing regulatory guidelines for lipid-based nanomedicines will streamline the approval process for melanoma indications [129, 131].

Conclusion

Melanoma is recognized as one of the most aggressive and difficult-to-treat human cancers, with its incidence increasing worldwide at a rate surpassing any other major cancer type. The progressive development of melanoma from normal melanocytes to metastatic disease involves a complex orchestration of molecular events, driven by multiple deregulated signaling pathways that collectively facilitate uncontrolled proliferation, invasion, and therapeutic resistance. Phytosome technology has significantly transformed the utilization of plant-derived compounds in cancer therapy by overcoming major limitations, including low solubility, inferior bioavailability, and unavailability of proper targeted delivery. By forming stable complexes between phytochemicals and phospholipids, phytosomes significantly improve the absorption, therapeutic efficacy, and stability of natural anticancer agents, such as flavonoids and alkaloids. This advancement enables more precise targeting of tumor cells, higher effectiveness at lower doses, and a significant decrease in side effects compared with conventional cancer treatments. The study emphasizes the molecular pathogenesis of melanoma and details about phytosome-encapsulated secondary metabolites can selectively and effectively target the aberrant signaling cascades within melanocytes. The technology’s scalability and regulatory compatibility position phytosomes as viable candidates for widespread clinical adoption, promising to bridge the gap between traditional medicine and modern oncological practice while maintaining the highest standards of safety and efficacy. The advent of phytosome technology marked a significant milestone in enhancing the bioavailability of plant-derived compounds against melanoma treatment.
