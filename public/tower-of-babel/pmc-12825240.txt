Phylogenetic influence on gut microbiome diversity within an African herbivore community
Authors: Rylee Jensen, Erin A McKenney, James C Beasley, Claudine C Cloete, Madeline Melton, Diana J R Lafferty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe microbial community within the gastrointestinal tract, known as the gut microbiome (GMB), is a complex micro-ecosystem that is modulated by the life history and physiological traits of the host as well as environmental conditions experienced by the host. In addition, phylogeny can be an important driver of GMB variability across mammalian species, with closely-related species sharing more similar microbial communities than distantly-related species, an eco-evolutionary pattern known as phylosymbiosis. In this study, we examined GMB diversity across 11 species of large herbivores in Etosha National Park (ENP), Namibia, to determine whether host species exhibit phylosymbiosis and whether different herbivore families host distinct microbial communities. Background

The suite of microorganisms on and within the bodies of mammals confer numerous benefits for associated host species. Many microbial taxa have specialized functions such as degrading plant material (e.g., families Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae) [1], regulating the central nervous system (e.g., Lactobacillus spp.) [2], triggering immune system responses (e.g., Bacteroides fragilis) [3], and resisting pathogen invasion (e.g., genera Dorea and Coprococcus) [4]. The ‘symmorphosis’ hypothesis posits that because an organism’s physiology is regulated to meet their immediate functional demands [5], beneficial microbial gene expression patterns are likely conserved through mammalian lineages over time [6–8]. Furthermore, commensal microbes are passed from mother to offspring immediately after birth [9, 10] and rapidly diversify as the host ages [11]. Thus, as hosts speciate over the course of millions of years, so too do their associated microbes.

One key microbial community within hosts lies within the gastrointestinal tract, known as the gut microbiome (GMB). This complex micro-ecosystem can be substantially modulated by host life history and physiological traits such as sex [12, 13], diet [14, 15], and gut morphology [16, 17], as well as environmental conditions experienced by the host [18–20]. Phylogeny also has been associated with GMB variability across mammalian host species [8, 21–23], with closely-related species typically sharing more similar microbial communities than distantly-related species [24, 25]. This eco-evolutionary pattern has been described as ‘phylosymbiosis’ [24, 26, 27]. While this pattern is observed in some mammalian clades (e.g., great apes) [8], phylosymbiosis is not ubiquitous and the disparity in this pattern may be explained by multiple life history and environmental factors [27]. Thus, the extent to which phylosymbiosis manifests in mammalian GMB communities is ambiguous because the host, environment, and evolutionary history may co-vary [21, 23, 28].

The assemblage of herbivore species in Etosha National Park (ENP), Namibia is an excellent model system for testing the impact of phylogeny on GMB profiles across host species. The large herbivores of ENP not only represent distinct evolutionary lineages, but have evolved a variety of gut morphologies, dietary niches, and habitat requirements, all of which shape gut microbial diversity. For example, while GMB diversity increases from carnivory to omnivory to herbivory [7, 29], GMB diversity has also been shown to increase in tandem with increasing gut complexity and transit time [30]. Our objective was to examine GMB diversity across a large herbivore community in ENP to determine whether host species exhibit phylosymbiosis and whether different herbivore families host distinct microbial communities. While analyzing GMB variability by herbivore species can give insight into more fine-scale drivers of GMB diversity and community structure, analyzing GMBs at host family level provides a broader examination of phylogenetically conserved patterns of microbial diversity.

We predicted that herbivore evolutionary relationships would correlate with microbial community dissimilarity indices as evidence of phylosymbiosis; for example, herbivore species within the same family (e.g., Bovidae) would exhibit less microbial community variation compared to species from different families (e.g., Elephantidae). We also expected significant differences in alpha diversity among herbivore families and within only bovid species due to herbivores’ vast physiological variety in their gut morphology (i.e., ruminants and nonruminants) and feeding strategies (i.e., browsers, grazers, and mixed feeders).

Materials

Study area

ENP is a semiarid ecosystem spanning approximately 22,270 square kilometers in northern Namibia (Fig. 1). ENP is named after the Etosha salt pan, which encompasses the bulk of the eastern half and northeastern portion of the park and is the largest salt pan on the African continent. Precipitation is highly variable throughout the park in different years, with the western half receiving disproportionately less precipitation than the eastern half [31, 32]. ENP is inhabited by a diverse community of terrestrial wildlife including 114 mammal species, 340 bird species, 110 reptile species, and 16 amphibian species [33]. The landscape is comprised of shrubland, woodland, and grassland ecosystems and dotted with a variety of natural and manmade watering holes that attract large congregations of wildlife.

Fecal collection

Fecal samples were opportunistically collected from 11 different herbivore species across ENP during the dry season from July-September 2022. Potential sampling bias could have arisen from collecting samples from individual herbivores that frequent waterhole sites or travel along roadways rather than individuals inhabiting areas further from these sites. However, because wildlife in the park must congregate near waterhole sites during the dry season to survive, we were not concerned about these sample locations skewing our data or influencing results. During the sample collection time period, there was no precipitation and daily temperature varied from ~ 20–24 °C (68–75 °F) in July to ~ 28–32 °C (82–89 °F) in September [35]. Animals were observed from vehicles along roads and at waterhole sites, and samples were collected immediately after defecation from the following species representing four different taxonomic families: Elephantidae, which includes African elephants (Loxodonta africana); Giraffidae, which includes Angolan giraffes (Giraffa camelopardalis angolensis); Equidae, which includes Burchell’s/plains zebra (Equus quagga burchellii) and Hartmann’s/mountain zebra (Equus zebra hartmannae); and Bovidae, which includes kudu (Tragelaphus strepsiceros), common eland (Taurotragus oryx), springbok (Antidorcas marsupialis), black-faced impala (Aepyceros melampus petersi), gemsbok/oryx (Oryx gazella), red hartebeest (Alcelaphus buselaphus), and blue wildebeest (Connochaetes taurinus; Table 1). For each species, samples were collected primarily from adult animals and additional effort was made to ensure equal sampling of males and females. Sex was determined based on primary and secondary sexual morphological traits such as presence of testes or penile sheath, and the presence and morphology of horns. Samples were collected with gloves or sticks to avoid contamination and preserved in PERFORMAbiome (PB-200) animal DNA collection tubes, which contain a stabilizing reagent that lyses bacterial cells to prevent growth and are effective at removing PCR inhibitors (DNA Genotek Inc. Kanata, Canada). Each tube was labeled with essential metadata information (e.g., species, sex, GPS location, date/time). For the purposes of this study, herbivore family and host species ID were the only covariates analyzed. Other physiological and environmental metadata were analyzed in a separate study using these same fecal samples [36].

Laboratory methods

Samples were shipped from Windhoek, Namibia to the Wildlife Ecology and Conservation Science (WECOS) Lab at Northern Michigan University (NMU). Due to permit approval timeframes, samples arrived at NMU in two batches based on whether they were listed with the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Flora and Fauna (CITES): one group of samples from eight non-CITES listed herbivore species (Burchell’s zebra, eland, gemsbok, kudu, hartebeest, wildebeest, impala, and springbok), and another from three CITES-listed species (elephant, giraffe, and Hartmann’s zebra). PB-200 tubes have been proven to preserve microbial community composition and diversity in temperatures ranging from − 20 °C (-4 °F) to 50 °C (122 °F) for up to 230 days [37, 38], which is greater than the delay between our sample batches. Upon arrival at NMU, samples were immediately stored at -62.22 °C (-80 °F). Feces naturally disintegrated in PB-200 tube buffers due to initially being stored at ambient temperatures; to accommodate for this, we vortexed each tube for 10 s before extraction to ensure fecal material was mixed evenly throughout the sample.

We extracted DNA using DNEasy PowerSoil Pro kits (QIAGEN) adapted from QIAGEN’s established protocols; the only change we made to the protocol (with guidance from QIAGEN personnel) was pipetting 200 µL of liquid fecal material to a sterile 2 mL Eppendorf tube for extraction (instead of 250 mg of sample as called for) to ensure sufficient DNA concentration with liquid samples. Using a Nanodrop-2000c (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), we spectrophotometrically quantified nucleic acid concentrations for each sample (using a factor of 50 ng/µL) and stored DNA extracts at -62.2 °C (-80 °F). We then shipped all 312 DNA aliquots (which were aliquoted into 20 µL equimolar ratios) on dry ice to Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) in Chicago, IL; ANL’s protocol includes negative PCR controls in every plate, which are amplified and proceed with pooling and sequencing only if negative controls are clean. Sequencing at ANL targeted the V4 gene region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene using the forward primer 515 F (sequence GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA) and reverse primer 806R (sequence GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT). 2 × 150 paired-end reads were sequenced on Illumina’s MiSeq platform.

Bioinformatic analyses

We imported sequences from ANL into Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME2; version 2023.7) [39] where we joined, quality-filtered, and demultiplexed them. We denoised sequences using the QIIME2 plugin Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm (DADA2; version 1.26.0) via bioconductor [40] and truncated lengths to 150 bp based on quality scores [e.g., 41]. To assign microbial taxonomic classification to the genus level, we used a pre-trained Naïve Bayes classifier [42, 43] with the SILVA 99 reference database (version 138.1) [44, 45] which has a default confidence threshold of 0.70 for taxonomic assignment. We then filtered sequences to remove Eukaryota, mitochondria, chloroplasts, and unassigned bacterial taxa. Removing unassigned reads is typical with microbiome studies as these reads may reflect bias in existing databases and potentially novel bacterial taxa associated with understudied wildlife. We then used scaling with ranked subsampling (SRS) [46] normalization in RStudio (version 4.3.2) [47] to scale all 312 samples to a Cmin threshold of 5,244. SRS normalization is preferred over rarefaction or other compositional methods due to high reproducibility, minimizing subsampling errors, and preserving community structure (i.e., relative abundance) and alpha diversity better than rarefaction [46]. Additionally, Cmin sensitivity was tested with the Shiny app (version 1.11.11) [48] with the package SRS [49] in RStudio [47] to compare the impact of different values on overall richness retention in the dataset.

Phylogenetic tree construction

Using the package ape (version 5.7-1) [50], we generated host phylogenetic trees using mammalian phylogeny subsets (1000 trees) from VertLife [51] from the “Mammals birth-death tip-dated DNA-only trees” source. A consensus tree was generated using probabilistic inference of molecular and fossil data [51]. We updated Taurotragus oryx to Tragelaphus oryx to match the species’ names provided in this dataset. We then calculated host divergence times (i.e., branch lengths) from the VertLife host phylogenetic tree in RStudio using the packages future (version 1.33.2) [52] and furr (version 0.3.1.90) [53].

Statistical analyses

We imported sequence data into RStudio using the package qiime2R (version 0.99.6) [54], then converted the data into a phyloseq object (version 1.46.0) [55]. We calculated three alpha diversity indices to compare across herbivore families: Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (hereafter, Faith’s PD), Shannon diversity, and inverse Simpson’s diversity. Faith’s PD incorporates bacterial richness and phylogenetic relationships [56], which we calculated using the package picante (version 1.8.2) [57]. We used the microbiome package (version 1.24.0) [58] to calculate Shannon diversity, which measures richness and evenness while placing greater weight on rarer species [59]; and inverse Simpson’s diversity, which also measures richness and evenness but gives more weight to common or dominant species [60]. We tested for normality using Shapiro-Wilk tests with the package RVAideMemoire (version 0.9-83-7) [61] and by checking residual plots, then for homogeneity of variance with Levene’s test with the package car (version 3.1-2) [62]. We then used one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and bootstrapped ANOVA to test for significant differences in each alpha diversity metric across herbivore families with the stats (version 4.3.2) [63] and WRS2 (version 1.1-5) [64] packages. Any significant ANOVA results were followed up with post-hoc tests to identify significant pairwise differences between herbivore families.

Next, we calculated two beta diversity indices – which quantify the phylogenetic branch length unique to each microbial community in pairwise comparisons [65] – using the vegan package (version 2.6-4) [66] to compare across herbivore families. Weighted UniFrac distance incorporates the relative abundance of microbial taxa shared among samples, whereas unweighted UniFrac distance measures taxonomic membership based on presence/absence [65]. Both indices were compared across host families with permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) using the vegan package. Significant results were visualized with non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) plots using ggplot2 (version 3.5.0) [67], and followed up with an Analysis of Similarity (ANOSIM) test, a non-parametric analysis that uses ranked dissimilarities to determine microbial community differences [68]. To determine which microbial taxa (down to the genus level) were driving any clustering patterns observed in NMDS plots, we performed a similarity percentage (Simper) analysis (100 permutations) [68] with the package vegan. We also used the package pheatmap (version 1.0.12) [69] to generate a heatmap of “core” microbial genera relative abundances, defined as genera that occur at ≥ 1% relative abundance and are found in ≥ 50% samples within each herbivore species [36].

We then tested for significant relationships between weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances and host evolutionary divergence times with Mantel tests using the non-parametric Spearman rank sum method (2000 permutations) [70]. While Mantel tests have known limitations with potentially spatially autocorrelated data [e.g., 71], Quilodrán et al. [72] demonstrate a range of conditions where Mantel tests are useful for qualitative tests in ecology and evolution. Mantel tests were performed on absolute distances across all herbivore species (n = 11) and then subset for only bovid species (n = 7). A p-value threshold of 0.05 was considered statistically significant for each statistical test performed.

Finally, we generated two dendrograms with the package ape to compare host divergence times against microbial community dissimilarity (i.e., weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances) to determine whether the ENP herbivore community exhibits phylosymbiosis.

Results

Fecal samples (n = 312) from 11 herbivore species were collected from ENP (Table 1). None of our three alpha diversity metrics (i.e., Faith’s PD, Shannon, or inverse Simpson’s diversity) revealed any significant differences by herbivore family.

Unweighted UniFrac distances differed significantly across herbivore families (PERMANOVA R2 = 0.01, F = 1.35, df = 1,311, p = 0.01), specifically between Bovidae and Elephantidae (PERMANOVA post-hoc R2 = 0.007, p = 0.02) and Bovidae and Giraffidae (PERMANOVA post-hoc R2 = 0.007, p = 0.01). An NMDS plot based on unweighted UniFrac distance did not reveal significant microbial groupings by herbivore family (Additional Fig. 1), nor did an ANOSIM follow-up analysis (R2 = -0.08, p = 0.99). However, a Simper analysis identified 10 microbial genera (Oscillospiraceae_UCG-005, Lachnospiraceae_Unclassified, Bacteroidaceae_Bacteroides, Spirochaetaceae_Treponema, Rikenellaceae_RC9, Oscillospiraceae_UCG-010, Family_WCHB1-41, Family_P-251-O5, Rikenellaceae_Alistipes, and Prevotellaceae_UCG-004) that contribute > 0.01 dissimilarity to all pairwise herbivore family differences (Additional Table 1). We acknowledge limitations with using a Simper analysis to identify taxa driving these differences, such as overemphasizing abundant taxa [73], which may overlook ecologically relevant rare taxa. However, the taxa identified in this analysis were assessed in Jensen et al. [36] for important functions they play in herbivores such as butyrate production, lipid metabolism, and nutrient extraction. Discussion

We discovered significant differences in GMB beta diversity among herbivore families in ENP, which included distinct microbial membership that distinguished bovid species from elephants and giraffes. While we found no evidence for phylosymbiosis in ENP’s greater herbivore community, we detected phylosymbiosis among bovids based on a positive correlation between microbial relative abundance and divergence times among bovid host species. This is surprising given the diverse physiological differences in Bovidae (e.g., gut morphology and feeding guilds; Fig. 2), past studies showing weak or inconsistent phylosymbiosis patterns within animal clades [74–77], and a similar study conducted by Rojas et al. [23] which found no evidence for phylosymbiosis among sympatric bovid species in the Maasai Mara. Our finding lends nuance to the mechanisms that shape phylosymbiosis, which may covary by both geographic region and host species. In addition to phylogeny, differences in microbial relative abundance among closely-related host species can be strongly tied to species-specific diets [23, 78, 79]. Because we did not include diet as a variable in this study, future research could more closely examine dietary composition and resource partitioning of ENP’s herbivore community to elucidate a possible driver explaining the phylosymbiosis pattern among bovids in ENP but not other regions of Africa.

In contrast to our study, some of the same non-bovid species (i.e., giraffes, zebras, and elephants) showed clear patterns of phylosymbiosis in the Maasai Mara and Laikipia, Kenya [23]. Although we did not directly compare our results to these studies, it is possible that the environmental stressors unique to ENP’s semiarid environment are decoupling the impact of phylogeny on host GMBs of ENP’s entire herbivore community. While the Maasai Mara and Laikipia encompass regions of higher annual rainfall and more diverse vegetation communities than ENP [80, 81], limited food and water availability, dry climate, and low annual precipitation in ENP [31, 32] could be shaping GMB diversity and community structure [82] independently of host phylogeny. Indeed, environmental factors are stronger drivers than phylogeny in shaping GMB communities in allopatric great apes [83] and lemurs [84]. Additionally, the abundance of specific environmental indicator taxa (e.g., Alistipes, Christensenellaceae_R-7 group, NK4A214, and RF39) in ENP’s herbivore community may contribute to the observed mismatch between host evolutionary history and microbial community dissimilarity [36]. Future research could investigate how the abundance of these environmental indicator taxa shifts in the wet season as resource availability changes across ENP’s landscape.

Unweighted UniFrac distance significantly differed between Bovidae and Elephantidae (PERMANOVA post-hoc p = 0.02) and Bovidae and Giraffidae (PERMANOVA post-hoc p = 0.01). The differences in microbial presence/absence observed between these herbivore families (Fig. 2) likely result from dietary niche partitioning. According to the Jarman-Bell Principle [85, 86], larger-bodied herbivores (e.g., elephants and giraffes) can subsist on abundant, lower-quality forage due to their lower metabolic requirements compared to smaller herbivores [78, 87]. Elephants in ENP, for example, harbor certain microbial genera (e.g., WCHB1-41 and Bacteroidales_RF16) that are not present in the majority of samples from other bovid species in ENP [36]. Elephants also have simpler stomachs and complex hindguts [88] with shorter mean food retention time than bovids [30]. Thus, microbial membership differences between elephants, giraffes, and bovids may reflect unique microbial functions and high inter-individual variation [79] that complement each host species’ respective dietary preferences and digestive strategies.

For example, in an NMDS plot comparing unweighted UniFrac distances across herbivore families (Additional Fig. 1), we observed three distinct clusters of samples that do not correspond to herbivore families. NMDS coordinates for both weighted and weighted UniFrac ordinations are shown in Additional Table 2 and Additional Table 3, respectively. We also tested clustering patterns against other metadata variables including host feeding strategy, host gut morphology, geographic zone within ENP, and waterhole site within ENP (descriptions detailed in Jensen et al. [36]), with no significant patterns observed. We corrected for possible batch effects (with CITES and non-CITES herbivore samples arriving at NMU on different dates) by testing NMDS clusters against host species, but found no correlation. Further, sampling day variation was not considered as a potential confounding factor due to all samples being collected during the dry season. The unexplained clusters may be attributed to an unmeasured physiological variable (e.g., age, sex, or social behavior) driving these groupings; however, this dataset highlights the inter-individual variation across herbivores, thus lending more nuance to GMB diversity and community structure that could be explored in future studies.

We identified a clear outlier in pairwise weighted UniFrac distance comparisons: despite eland and kudus’ more recent divergence time (only 3.93 million years; Fig. 3), these two herbivore species host highly dissimilar GMBs relative to other pairwise bovid comparisons. With such recent divergence, it’s possible that kudu as browsers and eland as mixed foragers overlap in dietary preferences and could be exhibiting strict resource partitioning to minimize competition, particularly as forage becomes more constrained in the dry season. In an analysis of 33 African herbivore species, Kartzinel et al. [28] found that kudu were the only species that exhibited a significant correlation between microbial richness and dietary richness, which may explain the high microbial dissimilarity compared to eland. Further investigation is needed to explore other possible drivers such as social transmission (e.g., gregarious behavior) and feeding ecology.

This study provides insights into the impact of phylogeny on GMB diversity in a closely-related African herbivore community. In particular, the lack of phylosymbiosis observed between the collective herbivore community and their associated GMB communities demonstrates that microbial communities are dynamic and respond to a mixture of host evolutionary strategies and corresponding adaptations. Different host species may show disparate patterns of GMB diversity and community structure in response to different environmental conditions, highlighting the need for ecologists to address targeted conservation needs. If microbial community shifts were to occur in hosts, GMB monitoring could provide early indication of environmental changes impacting host health and fitness [89, 90], enabling wildlife managers to implement conservation strategies sooner. For example, manual intervention to promote beneficial microbial diversity and abundance host species may increase pathogen resistance and promote long-term host health [91, 92]. The findings outlined in this study, combined with advanced microbial analysis techniques, have significant implications for wildlife conservation efforts, especially as environmental disturbances continue to affect sensitive and threatened host species around the world.

Conclusions

GMB beta diversity among ENP’s herbivore families is likely shaped by a variety of physiological factors and dietary niches among host species. Observed differences in GMB diversity and structure between bovids, elephants, and giraffes suggest that certain microbial taxa are specialized for associated host diet and digestive morphology. We also found evidence of phylosymbiosis among bovid species but not the entire herbivore community, suggesting that host physiology, evolutionary history, and environmental factors (e.g., resource availability, dry climate) experienced by hosts may covary to shape GMB diversity in ENP. Therefore, it is imperative for wildlife managers to consider each of these factors when conducting research on microbial communities. In particular, more research is needed to elucidate dietary preferences among ENP’s herbivores across the landscape and throughout different seasons, as well as corresponding microbial functions within hosts as a result of species-specific diets. This study underscores the dynamic nature of GMBs within herbivores and highlights the possible implications of GMB monitoring in hosts as an indicator of environmental stress and host health.

Unweighted UniFrac distances differed significantly across herbivore families (PERMANOVA F=1.35, df=1,311, p=0.01), specifically between Bovidae and Elephantidae (PERMANOVA post-hoc p=0.02) and Bovidae and Giraffidae (PERMANOVA post-hoc p=0.01), but were not distinguishably separated in this plot. Data is based on 312 fecal samples collected from Etosha National Park, Namibia.

Supplementary Material 2: Additional Table 1. Simper results of core microbial genera dissimilarity of African herbivore families. Results of a similarity percentage (Simper) analysis identifying core microbial genera that contributed >0.01 dissimilarity to all pairwise differences observed in four African herbivore taxonomic families. The four herbivore families were Elephantidae, which includes African elephants (Loxodonta africana); Equidae, which includes Burchell’s/plains zebra (Equus quagga burchellii) and Hartmann’s/mountain zebra (Equus zebra hartmannae); Giraffidae, which includes Angolan giraffes (Giraffa camelopardalis angolensis); and Bovidae, which includes kudu (Tragelaphus strepsiceros), common eland (Taurotragus oryx), springbok (Antidorcas marsupialis), black-faced impala (Aepyceros melampus petersi), gemsbok/oryx (Oryx gazella), red hartebeest (Alcelaphus buselaphus), and blue wildebeest (Connochaetes taurinus). The 10 microbial genera that contributed >0.01 dissimilarity between herbivore families included Rikenellaceae_Alistipes, Bacteroidaceae_Bacteroides, Lachnospiraceae_Unclassified, Family_P-251-O5, Prevotellaceae_UCG-004, Rikenellaceae_RC9, Spirochaetaceae_Treponema, Oscillospiraceae_UCG-005, Oscillospiraceae_UCG-010, and Family_WCHB1-41.
