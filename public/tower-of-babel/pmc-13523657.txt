Mineral Profiling of Preterm, Term Breast Milk, and Commercial Infant Formula: Evaluating Nutritional Adequacy for Premature Neonates
Authors: Samreen Latif, Munib Ahmed Shafique, Nauman Khalid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study assessed the mineral adequacy of mothers’ own milk, term milk, and preterm formula for premature neonates, evaluated toxic element contents using Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectrometry, and audited formula labels. Preterm and term milk were inadequate in meeting European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition-modeled mineral requirements for preterm infants at an assumed enteral intake of 165 mL/kg/d, particularly for calcium, chromium, phosphorus, magnesium, zinc, sodium, and selenium. Preterm formula met or even exceeded the requirements for calcium, magnesium, phosphorus, and zinc more frequently than human milk (P<0.05), though these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of formula samples (n=5). Mineral composition was similar between preterm and term milk, except for higher phosphorus in term milk (P<0.05). Aluminum concentrations were greater in human milk－preterm and term: 16.2 and 5.9 µg/mL, respectively－than in preterm formula at 1.16 µg/mL (P<0.05). Labeled and measured formula values for calcium, sodium, sulfur, strontium, titanium, and zinc varied significantly (P<0.05). Despite lower mineral content, mothers’ milk with individualized fortification is recommended for its immune benefits. These findings underline the need for improved formula labeling and monitoring of toxic elements.

INTRODUCTION

Provision of optimal nutrition is one of the most critical factors to improve health outcomes and the survival rate of preterm infants (Liu et al., 2026), who have higher nutritional needs due to greater metabolic demand and physiological immaturity. Therefore, for sufficient nutrient delivery, a precise support plan should be established (Silveira et al., 2023). Although all nutrients contribute to optimal growth and development, minerals play a critical role in several physiological processes (Lugonja et al., 2022; Barbieri and Cober, 2023). For example, calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), and magnesium (Mg) contribute to bone mineralization in infants along with other physiological roles (Kovacs and Ward, 2020). For thyroid functioning, iodine, selenium (Se), and zinc (Zn) are important (Ares et al., 2022; Calcaterra et al., 2025). Similarly, potassium, sodium (Na), and chloride are electrolytes crucial for water fluxes and enzymatic processes (Segar, 2020). Due to their essentiality, minerals must be provided in sufficient amounts to meet the in utero accretion rates to favor catch-up growth and to prevent compromised long-term neural and cognitive development (Hossain et al., 2021).

In neonatal intensive care units, common enteral feeding options include mother’s milk, human donor milk, preterm commercial formulas, and fortified human milk (Zheng et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). These alternatives differ considerably in their nutritional composition. For example, mothers’ own milk is the preferred option for feeding preterm infants; however, its mineral content may not adequately meet the enhanced nutritional requirements of preterm neonates (Meier, 2026). Human donor milk is an alternative in many countries; however, it is typically obtained from term deliveries, in which the mineral content is slightly lower than that of preterm breast milk (Gates et al., 2023). To overcome this shortfall, hospitals often add human milk with commercial fortifiers; however, in many resource-limited settings, preterm formulas are also employed for fortification (Aryadevi et al., 2026); these are undergoing innovations to meet the elevated needs of infants. However, a recently published study has raised a concern by reporting a prominent discrepancy between mineral information provided on product labels and actual measurements, making this choice questionable (Haji et al., 2025).

Despite extensive nutrient profiling of milk options, a significant gap remains in the comparative evaluation of their mineral adequacy for premature neonates, as most research has focused on evaluating macronutrient contents (Lin et al., 2020; Sahin et al., 2020; Dave and Cunningham, 2025). Relatively few studies have explored mineral levels in relation to maternal factors, lactation stage, or regional variations; however, adequacy relative to the requirements of infants remains unaddressed (Sabatier et al., 2019; Jonathan et al., 2025; Rocha-Pinto et al., 2025). Limited studies comparing term and preterm milk reported preterm milk to often have higher protein contents and those of some minerals early postpartum (Gross et al., 1980; Lemons et al., 1982) but lower levels of Zn, copper (Cu), and Se at equivalent postmenstrual ages (Sabatier et al., 2019). In contrast, some have reported insignificant differences between term and preterm milk in mineral content, indicating inconsistencies in the evidence available (Gupta et al., 1984; Jonathan et al., 2025). Commercial formulas have elevated mineral and trace element levels, including certain toxic elements, compared to human milk (Krachler et al., 2000; Mandiá et al., 2021). However, studies that assessed mineral content adequacy of preterm milk reported deficiencies, specifically in Zn, Cu, and iron (Fe) (Gates et al., 2021; Haji et al., 2025).

Although mineral composition of human milk and infant feeding options have been examined, direct comparisons of a broad range of minerals across preterm and full-term breast milk and commercial preterm formulas, along with adequacy assessments, remain limited. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to simultaneously compare these three feeding options and assess mineral adequacy against recommendations applicable for preterm infants. Although a recent study closely aligns with our goal, it reports only four minerals (Haji et al., 2025). Therefore, this study aims to quantify and compare a wide range of minerals across these sources, assess their adequacy against the most recent international recommendations for preterm infants, and audit the product labels of commercial infant formulas. It may provide precise evidence regarding the need for mineral fortification for each feeding option to optimize neonatal health outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and sample collection

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted at three tertiary hospitals in Lahore, Pakistan. In total, 25 milk samples were collected, including 10 each from mothers with term and premature delivery, and from five commercial preterm infant formulas. Only mothers without co-morbidities were included, and samples were collected in the third week postpartum to ensure uniformity. A total of 30 mL of milk was expressed through a sterile breast pump and stored in Falcon tubes at −20°C until analysis. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Ethics approval letters were obtained from Fatima Memorial Hospital (IRB-1608; dated: 24/02/2025), Shareef City Hospital (SMDC/SMRC 361-25), and Children’s Hospital, Lahore (1133/CH-UCHS). The approval letter was also secured from the Bioresearch Ethical Committee of the University of Management and Technology, Lahore, Pakistan (RE-015-2025).

Sample preparation and analysis

Milk samples were homogenized before analysis and digested using a microwave-assisted acid digestion system (Milestone Ethos Up), with 6 mL of 0.1 mol/L HNO3 and 2 mL of 30% H2O2 at 200°C for 25 min following a validated protocol for milk matrices. Postdigestion, samples were diluted with deionized water to a final concentration of 2% HNO3 before Inductively Coupled Plasma-Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis. All reagents were of analytical grade (TraceSELECTⓇ, Sigma-Aldrich). Procedural blanks containing digestion reagents without a sample were prepared and analyzed alongside each digestion batch to monitor background contamination and blank correction. An ICP-OES spectrometer (iCAP 6500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed to measure aluminum (Al), barium (Ba), Ca, chromium (Cr), Cu, Fe, Mg, manganese (Mn), Na, P, sulfur (S), Se, strontium (Sr), titanium (Ti), and Zn levels. Multielement plasma standard solutions (Specpure Alfa Aesar, Sigma-Aldrich) were utilized for calibration; these were prepared in 2% HNO3 to match the final acid concentration of each digested sample. Microwave-assisted digestion using diluted HNO3 (0.1 mol/L) and H2O2 produces compounds with very low residual carbon content (typically <300 mg/L), minimizing matrix impacts; therefore, external calibration in an acidic solution was considered appropriate without further matrix matching.

Calibration curves for all analytes consisted of seven concentration points with linearity (R2) >0.999 except for sulfur (R2=0.9988). The ranges were 0.2－500 mg/L for Ca, Mg, Na, and P; 0.05－60 mg/L for Fe, Mn, and Zn; 0.05－10 mg/L for Al, Ba, Cr, Cu, Sr, and Ti; 0.1－20 mg/L for Se; and 1－500 mg/L for S. Recovery of the digestion product was verified using certified reference material NIST 2710a (Montana I Soil, National Institute of Standards and Technology). In addition, a multielement quality-control standard (Bernd Kraft) was analyzed after every 10 samples; recoveries ranged from 89% to 103%.

Precision was assessed employing three replicate measurements per sample. Intra- and inter-day precisions, expressed as relative standard deviation, were ≤5% and ≤7%, respectively. Limits of detection (LODs) and limits of quantification (LOQs) were calculated as 3s and 10s, respectively, of ten method-blank measurements. Values below the LOD were considered nondetectable and excluded from statistical analysis. Investigative performance characteristics, including LOD, LOQ, recovery, precision, and calibration linearity, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

For quantification, optimal wavelengths were selected to minimize spectral interferences: Al, 396.152 nm; Ba, 233.527 nm; Ca, 317.933 nm; Cr, 267.716 nm; Cu, 324.754 nm; Fe, 238.204 nm; Mg, 279.553 nm; Mn, 257.610 nm; Na, 589.592 nm; P, 177.434 nm; S, 180.669 nm; Se, 196.026 nm; Sr, 407.771 nm; Ti, 334.941 nm; and Zn, 213.857 nm. Instrument operating conditions were radio frequency power of 1,150 W, nebulizer gas flow at 0.7 L min−1, auxiliary gas flow at 0.5 L min−1, outer gas flow at 12 L min−1, and plasma viewing height of 14 mm. Samples were measured in triplicate following a 60 s preflush stabilization period.

For comparisons with human milk, mineral concentrations of preterm infant formulas were calculated as reconstituted values (µg/mL) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For comparison with the manufacturer-declared mineral values, powdered infant formula was analyzed directly, and the data were expressed as µg/g to match the measurement data of the powdered product.

Assessment of mineral adequacy

An individualized intake‑based approach was applied to evaluate the adequacy of feed in meeting the mineral requirements of preterm infants. For this purpose, daily mineral intake of each preterm infant, the composition whose mother’s milk was ascertained, was calculated by multiplying the measured mineral concentration (µg/mL) in the milk sample by the infant’s daily enteral feeding volume (165 mL/kg/d) and by the infant’s body weight, which was recorded at the time of milk collection with a calibrated electronic weighing scale. The results obtained were then compared with the nutrient recommendations of the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) (Embleton et al., 2023). Units were converted to conform to the recommendations (e.g., mg/d to mmol/d for Ca, P, Mg, and Na) whenever needed. To estimate full-term mothers’ milk and formula adequacy, a representative preterm infant weight of 1.86 kg－the mean weight of the preterm cohort－was used. Based on the results of these calculations, samples were classified as below (<90%), meeting (90%－110%), or above (>110%) requirements. The ±10% range was used as an acceptable deviation from the ESPGHAN reference intakes to account for biological variability and analytical uncertainty.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (IBM Corp.) was used for data analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages, and continuous variables as mean±standard deviation. Mineral concentrations in full-term and preterm human milk and preterm infant formula were compared using a one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test. A Chi-square test was used to assess the adequacy of each feed type. To compare the labeled and laboratory-measured mineral values of infant formula, a paired t-test was applied. Statistical significance was defined as a P<0.05.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Demographic characteristics of the study groups are presented in Table 1, and calculated daily mineral requirements for a typical preterm infant are shown in Table 2. The study comprised ten preterm mother-infant dyads and ten term mothers. In the preterm milk group, mean maternal age was 23.1±4.5 years, with a prepregnancy body mass index (BMI) of 21.4±2.9 kg/m2 and a mean postpartum BMI of 26.5±4.1 kg/m2 at the time of milk collection. Preterm infants had a mean birth weight of 1.86±0.27 kg, a mean gestational age of 31.1±0.9 weeks, and 60% were female (n=6). In the term milk group, mean maternal age was 22.1±3.9 years, with a prepregnancy BMI of 21.8±2.5 kg/m2 and a postpartum BMI of 25.9±3.7 kg/m2; all term deliveries occurred at ≥39 weeks of gestation. Infant-specific data were not recorded for the term group, as these mothers were recruited solely to provide milk samples for comparative mineral analysis against preterm infant requirements, not to characterize their own infants.

Mineral adequacy in preterm milk, term milk, and preterm formula

The mineral adequacy of preterm and full‑term mothers’ milk, and preterm infant formula was evaluated against ESPGHAN‑recommended intakes based on a modeled preterm infant: assumed enteral intake 165 mL/kg/d and weight 1.86 kg; the results are summarized in Table 3. While it is well established that unfortified human milk may not fully meet the magnified mineral needs of preterm infants, this study extends this knowledge by providing a quantitative, side-by-side adequacy assessment of three feeding options. For example, the adequacy of Ca, Mg, P, and Zn varied significantly across groups (P<0.05). While preterm infant formula showed higher adequacy rates, as most samples met or exceeded requirements for Ca (60%), P (80%), and Zn (40%), these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of formula samples tested (n=5); both preterm and full-term mothers’ milk repeatedly fell short of these requirements. Correspondingly, the estimated daily Mg intake from 90%－100% of samples of both milk types fell below ESPGHAN recommended levels; however, all samples of preterm formulas met requirements: 40% meeting and 60% exceeding. While Cr and Se were mostly below the limits for all feeding types, Mn in all three groups consistently exceeded requirements (100%). Across groups, Fe, Cu, and Na demonstrated varying adequacy patterns, but none reached statistical significance. These results align with a study showing that preterm mothers’ milk and infant formulas fall below ASPEN’s recommended ranges for Zn, Fe, and Cu. Moreover, the greater Mn content of infant formula aligns with these findings. However, the same study found that the Mn levels of 34% of human milk samples were below the recommended values, which contrasts with our study’s results, in which all samples exceeded the requirements (Haji et al., 2025). Although the preterm formula exhibited better adequacy for most minerals, it is not a preferred option over mother’s milk because it lacks several immunoactive compounds; it is preferable to fortify mother’s milk to better support neurodevelopment and lower infection rates (Parker et al., 2026).

Comparison of essential mineral contents across milk feeds

A comparison of the mineral concentrations between preterm and full-term mother’s milk, and preterm formula showed several significant variations (Table 4). E.g., the concentrations of Ca (869.4±373.4 µg/mL), Mg (72.1±13.1 µg/mL), P (514.1±208.6 µg/mL), and Zn (8.84±2.43 µg/mL) were elevated in preterm formula as compared to human milk, including both term and preterm (P<0.05). When we compared preterm and term milk, mineral levels varied by element; preterm milk had numerically enhanced contents of Ca, Mg, Zn, and Mn, but the difference was statistically insignificant (P>0.05), corroborating meta-analyses that found an insignificant variation in Ca across these feeds (Gidrewicz and Fenton, 2014). Similarly, our results also validate the findings of Jonathan et al. (2025), where differences between term and preterm milk were statistically insignificant; however, the levels of Zn and potassium were relatively greater compared to term milk. In contrast, Na, chloride, Mg, and Fe were markedly higher in preterm milk compared to term milk (Lemons et al., 1982). Similarly, Sabatier et al. (2019) reported elevated Mg in preterm milk at weeks 6 and 7, but lower Na and Cu levels at week 2. Mn concentrations varied significantly across feed types (P=0.042). However, no individual pairwise comparison survived Tukey’s HSD post-hoc correction, likely reflecting limited statistical power given the small formula sample size (n=5). Numerically, preterm mothers’ milk showed the maximum Mn levels (1.05±1.24 µg/mL), compared to full-term milk (0.18±0.11 µg/mL), and preterm formula (0.14±0.02 µg/mL). However, P concentration was significantly lower in preterm mothers’ milk as compared to full-term milk (P<0.001) and formula milk (P=0.015), contradicting the findings of Sabatier et al. (2019), who reported enhanced levels in preterm milk than in term milk at week 1. However, Gidrewicz and Fenton (2014) found an insignificant variation in phosphate levels between these feeds. A possible explanation for these contradictions is differences in the mode of delivery, maternal age, race, and newborn sex, which remarkably affect milk mineral concentrations (Gates et al., 2021; Fernández-Tuñas et al., 2025). Se was detected only in preterm formula samples (0.044±0.050 µg/mL). However, Cr, Cu, Fe, Na, and S did not vary markedly across the three feeding types.

Comparison of nonessential/toxic element contents across milk feeds

The quantification of nonessential and trace elements across the evaluated groups revealed distinct distribution patterns (Table 5). Al was most highly concentrated in preterm human milk (16.2±15.7 µg/mL), followed by full-term human milk (5.9±4.3 µg/mL), and preterm formula (1.16±1.70 µg/mL) (P=0.028). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that Al levels were significantly greater in both preterm and full-term human milk than formula, with mean differences of 15.01 µg/mL (P=0.035) and 4.79 µg/mL (P=0.023), respectively. The mean Al level in preterm milk (16.2 µg/mL) here was higher than the median of 57.63 µg/L reported by Freire et al. (2023), however closer to the mean of 2.1 mg/L found in another study (Shawahna et al., 2024). Possible factors for variations, suggested by these studies, include urban residence, diet, and personal care product use, which could also account for the elevated levels in our cohort. Furthermore, the possibility of contamination during sample processing or analysis cannot be ruled out. Moreover, low Al in our preterm formula contradicts studies that reported prominent formula contamination, underscoring the importance of manufacturing processes as a variable and a crucial source of exposure (Elaridi et al., 2020; Ibrahim et al., 2020). The concentration of Sr was greater in commercial formulas (0.35±0.14 µg/mL) than in preterm milk (0.16±0.21 µg/mL); however, it was not detected in full-term mother’s milk. Recent findings indicating a higher Sr content in formula than in human milk are consistent with other studies (Krachler et al., 2000; Tahboub et al., 2021).

Regarding Ba, our study found insignificant variations (P=0.331) across feeding types; however, our human milk values (0.89±1.41 µg/mL) are slightly higher than the range reported by Tahboub et al. (2021), who found a mean of 145 µg/L (0.145 µg/mL) in term milk. Similarly, the contents detected in our preterm infant formulas, 0.26±0.31 µg/mL (1.70±2.16 µg/g), were greater than those in the literature. For example, Elaridi et al. (2020) found a mean Ba concentration of 0.256 µg/g and Tahboub et al. (2021) of 434 µg/kg or 0.434 µg/g in powdered formula. Apart from dietary sources, Ba may leach from container materials into parenterally administered solutions in preterm infants (Bohrer et al., 2014). Epidemiological evidence linking gestational Ba exposure to increased cardiometabolic risk supports the need for continued monitoring of this element in infant nutrition products (Tong et al., 2025).

The concentration of Ti was maximum in preterm human milk (1.90 µg/mL), followed by full-term milk (1.33 µg/mL), and lowest in preterm formula (0.21 µg/mL), though these differences were statistically insignificant (P=0.105). The values obtained here are greater than those reported for mature human milk (0.0063 µg/mL) and formula milk (<0.0013 µg/mL) (Krachler et al., 2000). In contrast, Melo et al. (2024) found high Ti contents ranging from 8.62 to 25.7 mg/kg (1.44 to 4.17 µg/mL) in formula for young children. Such a contrast across magnitudes highlights differences in geographic and product usage, likely due to the use of Ti dioxide (E171) as a whitening additive, a practice now restricted in the European Union.

Discrepancies between labeled vs measured mineral contents in infant formulas

Significant discrepancies (P<0.05) were identified between labeled and actual measured values for several minerals (Table 6). These results demonstrated that the Ca content (3,662±1,032 µg/g) was significantly lower than the labeled value (5,310±1,743 µg/g; P=0.007), as were Na (P=0.027) and Zn (P=0.003) levels. These findings are supported by a study from Qatar, which found similar inaccuracies for Zn and Cu (Haji et al., 2025). The labeled and measured values for Al, Ba, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mg, Mn, P, and Se did not differ significantly (P>0.05). Another notable finding was the detection of significant contents of S (1,142±344 µg/g; P=0.002), Sr (2.15±0.63 µg/g; P=0.002), and Ti (1.37±0.72 µg/g; P=0.013), which were not declared on the product labels. These findings, along with batch-to-batch variability observed by Remer et al. (1990), further underscore systemic quality control issues specifically for vulnerable preterm infants, as they are exposed to toxic elements as well as inadequate nutrition.

The present study provides vital insights into the mineral adequacy of mothers’ own milk and preterm formula in meeting the nutritional needs of premature infants. Assessing the adequacy of term milk is especially crucial, as many preterm mothers initially cannot express enough milk, and families often depend on relatives at a similar stage of lactation if formula intolerance occurs, since donor milk banks are infrequent in many Islamic countries. Moreover, a quantitative comparison of minerals across feeds is particularly critical, as it provides precise evidence regarding the need for mineral supplementation, given that most fortifiers focus on optimizing macronutrients and often overlook micronutrients. Limitations of this study include a small sample size and limited statistical power. As this was a cross-sectional study, changes in mineral contents during the lactation period could not be assessed. In addition, the limited number of commercial formula samples (n=5) may limit generalizability across all available products. Maternal environment-exposure data were not obtained, creating uncertainty about the source of the toxic trace-element concentrations observed in milk. Furthermore, contamination during sample collection, processing, or digestion cannot be completely ruled out, given the inherent challenges of trace-element analysis, especially for those such as Al. Given these drawbacks, future studies with larger sample sizes, longitudinal designs, and a comprehensive assessment of maternal environmental exposures are recommended to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, both unfortified mothers’ own milk and term milk are inadequate in meeting the estimated mineral requirements of preterm infants under the modeled enteral intake conditions. Although preterm formula demonstrated sufficient mineral adequacy rates for most nutrients, its bioavailability remains an important consideration in clinical practice. Considering immune-protective factors, it is better to choose the mother’s own milk, along with an individualized fortification strategy. The higher levels of potentially toxic elements in human milk need further investigation to evaluate the contributing factors and thereby alleviate exposure. Additionally, discrepancies among measured and labeled mineral values underscore the need for robust quality assurance and regulatory oversight of infant nutrition products, with implications for the safety and nutritional adequacy of preterm infant feeding in clinical practice.
