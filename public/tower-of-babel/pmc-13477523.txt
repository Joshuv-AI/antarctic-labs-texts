Single-Cell Transcriptome Analysis Reveals IRF1-Driven Epithelial States and Glycosaminoglycan–Glycolysis Coupling in Cisplatin-Resistant HGSOC
Authors: Zhaoyang Jia, Wenjing Pan, Xibo Zhao, Lianxiu Li, Wenhua Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCisplatin resistance is the principal cause of relapse in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), but bulk-expression signatures cannot localize resistant malignant states or the tumor–microenvironment (TME) interactions that sustain them. This study aimed to define cisplatin-resistant epithelial cell states and their regulatory and metabolic circuits by integrating multi-cohort single-cell transcriptomes with pharmacogenomic and clinical data.MethodsWe assembled 159,419 cells from 32 HGSOC tumors across six public single-cell RNA-sequencing cohorts and performed harmonized integration, clustering, and lineage annotation. Cisplatin response was mapped to single cells by coupling scRNA-seq data to Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer predicted cisplatin response score values using Scissor, with AUCell-based validation. We then applied receptor–ligand–based cell–cell communication analysis (CellChat), transcription-factor (TF) activity inference (SCENIC and NetAct with TRRUST), pseudotime trajectory reconstruction (Monocle3), and pathway-level metabolic scoring. Associations with drug sensitivity and patient outcome were evaluated in ovarian cancer cell lines and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) HGSOC cohort.ResultsAmong 34 epithelial subclusters, 14 were significantly enriched for a cisplatin-resistant phenotype and collectively accounted for most predicted resistant cells. These states were transcriptionally characterized by stress, interferon, and apoptotic programs and formed dense communication hubs with endothelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and T/NK cells via extracellular-matrix and adhesion pathways (for example, LAMA3–CD44, COL6A1/2–CD44, NECTIN3–NECTIN2, and CD99–CD99 interactions). TF-activity modeling converged on an IRF1-centered regulatory program that increased along an epithelial trajectory and coordinated inflammatory and apoptotic gene expression. Metabolically, resistance-enriched epithelial states showed selective up-regulation of glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, particularly keratan sulfate, coupled to heightened glycolysis; glycolytic activity correlated with predicted cisplatin response score in cell lines and an IRF1/STAT1 readout (GBP3) stratified survival in TCGA HGSOC.ConclusionCisplatin resistance in HGSOC is encoded in discrete IRF1-driven epithelial states that are supported by specific TME communication networks and a glycosaminoglycan–glycolysis metabolic axis. This integrative single-cell informatics framework yields testable biomarkers and therapeutic targets for overcoming platinum resistance in ovarian cancer.Keywords: high-grade serous ovarian cancer, cisplatin resistance, single-cell RNA sequencing, tumor microenvironment, metabolic reprogramming, IRF1

Background

High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) is the most lethal epithelial ovarian cancer subtype and frequently acquires cisplatin resistance, driving relapse and poor survival.1,2 Although resistance has been linked to drug efflux, defective DNA-damage responses, epigenetic remodeling, and metabolic rewiring, the specific cell states and regulatory programs that sustain resistance in patients remain incompletely defined.3-5

Bulk transcriptomic analyses have proposed resistance signatures,6-8 but bulk averaging across cell types obscures rare or transitional states and tumor–microenvironment crosstalk. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) resolves HGSOC heterogeneity and TME architecture, revealing epithelial diversity and immune–stromal complexity.9-11 Notwithstanding these advances, prior scRNA-seq studies—while delineating epithelial stress/progression programs—have not aligned single-cell states with drug-response phenotypes across cohorts.9-11 Because malignant epithelium is the direct platinum target and a principal source of regrowth, defining cisplatin-resistant epithelial subpopulations at single-cell resolution represents a critical prerequisite for mechanistic interpretation. The ligand–receptor circuits through which resistant epithelium engages endothelium, fibroblasts, and myeloid/lymphoid cells remain incompletely charted.12-14 At the regulatory layer, epigenetic mechanisms—including histone post-translational modifications—modulate chemoresistance, and IRF1 shows mixed associations with platinum response.4,15,16 Metabolically, glycolytic reprogramming and extracellular-matrix–linked pathways have been implicated in chemosensitivity, yet their coupling to defined resistant epithelial states is insufficiently characterized.5,17 Accordingly, outstanding questions include which epithelial subpopulations are cisplatin-resistant, how they communicate with stromal/immune lineages, and which regulatory and metabolic circuits maintain these states at single-cell resolution.

To address these gaps, we integrated scRNA-seq from 32 tumors across six HGSOC cohorts and coupled single-cell profiles to GDSC2 drug-response information using Scissor, with AUCell validation, to localize cisplatin-resistant epithelial subpopulations. We then quantified cell–cell interactions to define TME circuits, inferred TF activity with emphasis on IRF1, and reconstructed lineage dynamics to examine acquisition of resistance along differentiation. Finally, we scored glycosaminoglycan (GAG) biosynthesis and glycolysis, related metabolic activity to predicted cisplatin response score in cell lines, and evaluated the clinical association of IRF1 in TCGA.

This study yields a coherent evidence chain—from identifying resistant epithelial subpopulations, to defining stromal/immune crosstalk, to nominating IRF1-centered regulatory programs, to tracing pseudotemporal acquisition of resistance, and finally linking GAG–glycolysis coupling to drug response and outcome—thereby outlining testable therapeutic entry points in HGSOC.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and Processing

Public single-cell RNA-seq datasets from six HGSOC cohorts were obtained from GEO under accessions GSE154600 (n = 28,029 cells), GSE158937 (n = 7,041 cells), 18 GSE173682 (n = 27,463 cells), 19 GSE184880 (n = 27,983 cells), 10 GSE147082 (n = 9,157 cells), 20 and GSE217517 (n = 59,746 cells),21,22 totaling 159,419 cells.

Preprocessing and Quality Control

Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger (v6.1.2) for sample demultiplexing, barcode processing, and gene counting against the GRCh38 reference genome.23,24 Low-quality cells were filtered out based on the following criteria: mitochondrial gene content < 25%, ribosomal gene expression > 3%, hemoglobin gene expression < 1%, number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA) between 300 and 10,000, total UMI counts (nCount_RNA) below 100,000. Doublets were predicted and removed using Scrublet (v0.2.3). 25

Data Integration and Batch Correction

Following initial preprocessing and quality control, the datasets were integrated and batch-corrected using Harmony (v1.2.0) to mitigate technical variations arising from different sequencing batches and experimental conditions. 26 The PCA embeddings calculated from the top 3,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were used as input. Harmony was run with the following parameters: theta = 2, lambda = 0.1, and max.iter.harmony = 20. The algorithm successfully aligned cells across cohorts without overcorrecting biologically meaningful variations, as verified by uniform distribution of batch sources in UMAP space post-integration.

Clustering and Cell Type Annotation

Normalization and feature selection were performed using SCTransform implemented in Seurat (v4.1.0).27,28 Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on highly variable genes. The Louvain algorithm was applied for graph-based clustering at a resolution of 0.8. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes: EPCAM, CD24, KRT8, KRT18, KRT19 for epithelial cells; CD3D, CD3E, TRAC, NCAM1, KLRD1 for T/NK cells; MS4A1, SDC1, CD38, CD19, CD79A, IGHA1, IGHG1 for B cells; LYZ, CD163, CD68, FCGR3A for myeloid cells; DCN, BGN, THY1, COL1A1, COL1A2 for fibroblasts; PECAM1, CLDN5, FLT1, RAMP2 for endothelial cells.

Identification of Cisplatin-Resistant Epithelial Subpopulations

To link cisplatin-response phenotypes to single epithelial cells, we applied Scissor v1.0.2 to the epithelial expression matrix derived from the GEO HGSOC scRNA-seq cohorts. GDSC2 was used as an external pharmacogenomic reference, providing matched bulk transcriptomic profiles of tumor cell lines and experimentally measured cisplatin-response data; it was not used as a source of patient single-cell expression data or as a stand-alone clinical definition of resistance. Scissor projected the GDSC2 cisplatin-response phenotype onto the epithelial single-cell expression space and classified epithelial cells as resistance-associated (Scissor+) or non-resistant (Scissor−).29,30 Cluster-level enrichment of Scissor+ cells was assessed using one-sided hypergeometric tests and permutation tests, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Epithelial clusters with significant enrichment of Scissor+ cells were designated as cisplatin-resistance-enriched epi_clusters.

To further quantify resistance-associated transcriptional activity at the single-cell level, we derived a resistance-related gene set from genes upregulated in Scissor+ versus Scissor− epithelial cells (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; FDR < 0.05; |log2FC| > 1), and scored each cell with AUCell v1.14.0. 31

For downstream analyses, epithelial cells were dichotomized into cisplatin-resistant (cis_R) and cisplatin-sensitive (cis_S) groups according to the median AUCell score, with higher AUCell scores indicating stronger resistance-associated activity. At the cluster level, an epithelial cluster was defined as a cisplatin-resistant candidate if it simultaneously met the following criteria: (1) presence of resistance-related cells within the cluster; (2) a median AUCell score higher than the overall median across epithelial clusters; and (3) a median predicted cisplatin response score higher than the overall median across epithelial clusters. Based on these criteria, five epithelial clusters (2, 6, 16, 21, and 28) were ultimately retained as cisplatin-resistant clusters, while the remaining epithelial clusters were classified as non-resistant for downstream analyses.

Differential Expression and Functional Enrichment

Differential gene expression analysis between cis_R and cis_S groups was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test in Seurat with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment were carried out using clusterProfiler (v4.2.2) with Hallmark and KEGG gene sets. 32

Cell-Cell Communication Analysis

CellChat (v1.5.0) was used to infer ligand–receptor interactions between cisplatin-resistant epithelial cells and stromal/immune cell populations. Significant interactions were determined by CellChat permutation testing (P < 0.05). 33 For selected dot-plot visualizations, ligand–receptor pairs were displayed only if they met two criteria: (1) inclusion in the predefined pairLR.use set, representing group-specific/specific ligand–receptor pairs, and (2) statistical significance in the corresponding source–target comparison. Therefore, these panels show selected significant interactions rather than all interactions present in the overall communication network.

Transcription Factor Regulatory Network Analysis

SCENIC (v1.2.4) was used with GRNBoost2 for regulon inference.31,34 NetAct (v1.0) was applied to estimate transcription factor activity using DoRothEA regulatory networks. 35 Key TFs were selected based on regulon specificity score (RSS) and NetAct activity score. Protein–protein interaction networks were visualized in Cytoscape (v3.9.1).

Pseudotime Trajectory Analysis

Monocle3 (v1.0.0) was used to reconstruct developmental trajectories of epithelial cells. 36 Cells were ordered along a pseudotime axis based on transcriptional similarity. Branch expression analysis modeling (BEAM) was applied to identify genes associated with cell fate decisions.

Metabolic Analysis

Glycolysis and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis activities were estimated using AUCell and scMetabolism based on gene sets from KEGG pathways.

Statistical Analysis and Visualization

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.2.1). Data were visualized using ggplot2 (v3.3.5), ComplexHeatmap (v2.10.0), and ggpubr (v0.4.0).37,38 For differential expression analysis was assessed using the limma package with linear modeling and empirical Bayes moderation. Differentially expressed genes were defined using P < 0.05 and |log2 FC| > 1. For differential analysis of single-cell metabolism pathway scores, limma was also applied, with significance defined as P < 0.05 and |log2 FC | > 0.5; Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were additionally used in selected two-group comparisons of pathway activity. GO and KEGG analyses were performed using clusterProfiler, with P values adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Survival analyses were conducted using the survival and survminer packages; optimal cutoffs were determined using surv_cutpoint, Kaplan–Meier curves were generated with survfit, and group differences were assessed by two-sided log-rank tests. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis where indicated. Unless otherwise stated, all tests were two-sided, and FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-Cell Profiling Identifies Cisplatin-Resistant Epithelial Subpopulations

We profiled 32 HGSOC samples from six independent cohorts (159,419 cells). After quality control, graph-based clustering (resolution 0.8) resolved 55 cell clusters, which were annotated by differentially expressed genes as T/NK, B/plasma, myeloid, fibroblast, endothelial, and epithelial lineages (Figure 1A and B).

Focusing on the tumor epithelial compartment, re-embedding and reclustering yielded 34 epithelial subclusters (hereafter epi_clusters; Figure 1C). To map drug response at single-cell resolution, we applied Scissor to couple our scRNA-seq data with bulk drug-response profiles from GDSC2. Scissor identified 14 of 34 epithelial subclusters enriched for a cisplatin-resistant phenotype—epi_clusters 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 10, 12, 13, 16, 18, 22, 28, 30, and 32 (Figure 1C). Scissor-positive and Scissor-negative cells were observed across multiple samples, indicating that these states are not restricted to a specific sample (Supplementary Fig. S1A).

After defining resistant and sensitive cell populations using Scissor, we further applied AUCell to evaluate gene-set activity within these Scissor-defined populations. This downstream analysis showed a similar pattern: clusters highlighted by Scissor showed the highest cisplatin-resistance signature activity (Figure 1D and E). Using the median AUCell score, epithelial cells were dichotomized into cisplatin-resistant (cis_R) and cisplatin-sensitive (cis_S) groups; as expected, cis_R cells displayed significantly higher resistance scores (Figure 1F). In the GSE154600 cohort with available chemotherapy-response annotations, clinically resistant samples showed significantly higher activity of the AUCell-derived resistance signature than sensitive samples (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

Differential expression between cis_R and cis_S cells revealed up-regulated programs in cell-cycle progression, cellular senescence, ferroptosis, and FOXO signaling (Figure 1G and H), indicating that specific epithelial subpopulations harbor transcriptional features associated with cisplatin resistance.

Multicellular Crosstalk Features of Cisplatin-Resistant Epithelial Cells

We next asked whether the cis_R cells identified in the first Results section engage the tumor microenvironment (TME) differently from cis_S epithelial cells. Using a receptor–ligand–based CCI framework (CellChat), we quantified both the number and strength of interactions (“information flow”) with major TME lineages while controlling for cell number by down-sampling (Figure 2A-G). Overall, cis_R cells displayed stronger and denser communication networks with endothelial cells, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and T/NK cells, but not with B/plasma cells (permutation test with FDR correction; Figure 2A-G).

At the pathway level, cis_R clusters were preferentially enriched for several secreted/ECM signaling axes (FDR < 0.05), including: 1) CD99–CD99 and NECTIN3–NECTIN2, consistent with enhanced cell–cell adhesion/immune transmigration; 2) LAMA3–CD44 and COL6A1/2–CD44, indicating active ECM–receptor engagement and matrix remodeling in the resistant epithelium (Figure 2H). Consistently, CD44 expression was higher in cis_R epithelial cells than in cis_S epithelial cells. (Supplementary Fig. S1B)

Collectively, these results indicate that cis_R epithelial cells are communication hubs that reshape the TME through vascular interaction, stromal remodeling, and immune modulation, providing a mechanistic context for their drug-resistant phenotype.

Identification of Key Transcription Factors Associated With Cisplatin Resistance

To pinpoint regulators of the cis_R state, we inferred TF activity with two complementary frameworks—SCENIC (regulon activity) and NetAct (network-constrained activity)—within the epithelial compartment and contrasted cis_R vs cis_S cells (Figure 3A and B). Both approaches identified a restricted set of activated TFs with overlap at both the individual-TF and TF-family/regulatory-program levels: SCENIC highlighted SOX15, MBD2, ZNF580, FOSL2, FOSB, IRF1, and UBE2K, whereas NetAct identified IRF1, CEBPB, JUN, NFKB1, and STAT1, including related signals involving IRF1, AP-1-related factors, the CEBP family, and NF-κB signaling. Consistent with these regulatory-inference results, IRF1 was expressed at higher levels in cis_R cells (Figure 3C). In the TF–TF interaction map, NFKB1 and JUN showed the highest network connectivity, indicating central positions in the regulatory landscape (Figure 3D).

To prioritize robust candidate drivers, we integrated cross-method consensus with prior published evidence linking IRF1 to platinum response and prognosis in ovarian cancer, which nominated IRF1 as the top candidate transcription factor. 15 We then evaluated its downstream network by intersecting the predicted targets with the stringent differentially expressed genes between cis_R and cis_S cells, identifying 46 overlapping candidate targets (Figure 3E). To characterize the functional profile of this specific network, pathway enrichment analysis was performed on these 46 genes, revealing an overrepresentation of immune/inflammatory and stress-response pathways (Figure 3F); these results serve as a descriptive functional characterization of these high-confidence targets in the resistant state. To cross-reference our computational predictions with experimentally validated literature, we utilized the TRRUST TF–target database, which confirmed that these specific downstream effectors are established, literature-documented targets of the IRF1 pathway (Figure 3G).

Together, these results consistently nominate IRF1 as a key transcriptional regulator of the cis_R epithelial state, linking inflammatory/immune signaling to drug-resistant phenotypes in HGSOC epithelium.

Acquisition of Cisplatin Resistance Through an IRF1-Driven Differentiation Program

We reconstructed epithelial lineage dynamics with Monocle3 (learn_graph; Methods), using an early cis_S subset as the root (Figure 4A). The trajectory resolved five transcriptional states along a continuous pseudotime. cis_R cells concentrated at the terminal branch, indicating a late/advanced differentiation state.

Topology of the lineage revealed two commitment phases (branch points) that progressively funneled cells toward the terminal cis_R fate (Figure 4B). Along pseudotime, IRF1 activity (regulon/NetAct score) increased monotonically, peaking at the terminal resistant state (Figure 4C). Co-varying TFs—JUN, MCL1, STAT3—displayed similar rising trajectories, suggesting co-activation within the same branch.

Generalized additive modeling along pseudotime showed stepwise induction of IRF1-linked transcriptional programs during the transition from cis_S to cis_R (Figure 4D). These programs included an interferon/IRF1-response module marked by IRF1, STAT1, XAF1, APOL6, SAMHD1, TRIM21, RNF213, and a JAK–STAT/STAT3-associated transcriptional module marked by STAT3, SOCS3, STAT6, and BCL6, an NF-κB/TNF-response transcriptional module marked by NFKBIZ, JUNB, and MCL1, and an antigen-presentation transcriptional module marked by HLA-A, B2M, and CD74.

Cumulatively, these analyses support a model in which IRF1-centered transcriptional activation accompanies and likely drives the differentiation trajectory toward the cis-R epithelial state.

Enhanced Glycosaminoglycan Biosynthesis and Glycolysis Are Linked to Cisplatin Resistance

To characterize metabolic reprogramming in the epithelial compartment, we analyzed curated metabolism-related pathways, including KEGG glycosaminoglycan (GAG) biosynthesis modules. Within this metabolic framework, cis_R clusters showed selective up-regulation of GAG biosynthesis, with a particularly prominent enrichment of keratan sulfate-related modules (Figure 5A).

To examine how GAG biosynthesis relates to glycolytic and cisplatin-response phenotypes, epithelial cells were stratified into GAG biosynthesis–high and –low groups using pathway activity scores. GAG-high cells exhibited elevated glycolysis activity relative to GAG-low cells, linking the two metabolic programs at the single-cell level. In addition, a high KEGG glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis–keratan sulfate pathway score was associated with high oncoPredict-derived cisplatin response scores (trained on the GDSC2 cell-line panel) (Figure 5B and C). Consistently, correlation analysis show that GAG biosynthesis (keratan sulfate) was also positively correlated with glycolytic activity at both the single-cell level (Supplementary Fig. S1D) and in the bulk TCGA-HGSOC cohort (Supplementary Fig. S1E). These results indicate that increased GAG biosynthesis activity is coupled to glycolysis and associated with predicted cisplatin resistance. Notably, GAG biosynthesis is metabolically coupled to glycolysis: assembly of GAG chains—particularly the GlcNAc-based disaccharide of keratan sulfate—depends on UDP-GlcNAc supplied by the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP), a glycolytic branch in which fructose-6-phosphate is diverted by GFAT1/2.39,40 Consistent with this shared carbon flux, enhanced glycolysis was likewise associated with the cis_R state and predicted cisplatin resistance, indicating that the resistant epithelial compartment is characterized by a coupled glycolysis–GAG biosynthetic program.

Because metabolic reprogramming of this magnitude is generally orchestrated by transcriptional regulators, we next sought a transcription factor that coordinates with the glycolytic resistant state. Notably, previous studies have suggested an association between glycolytic remodeling and interferon/STAT1-related signaling in HNSCC, 41 and cisplatin-induced DNA damage can directly activate IRF1 in ovarian cancer cells. 42 We therefore examined IRF1-associated transcriptional features in the resistant compartment. Differential expression between cis_R and cis_S epithelial cells highlighted a set of up-regulated genes in the resistant state, including IRF1 and interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) (Figure 5D). Among these ISGs, GBP3 showed strong up-regulation, consistent with activation of an IRF1/STAT1-associated interferon-responsive program, as STAT1 and IRF1 have been shown to cooperatively regulate IFN-responsive gene expression and GBP family members are canonical IFN-inducible effectors. 43 TCGA HGSOC cases stratified by the expression of GBP3 exhibited significantly worse overall survival in the GBP3-high group (Figure 5E). Notably, this is concordant with previous study showing that GBP family members, including GBP1, are linked to chemoresistance in ovarian cancer. 44

To further link the transcriptional and metabolic features observed in single-cell analysis, we performed validation in TCGA HGSOC bulk tumors. Stratification by IRF1 expression revealed that the IRF1-high group exhibited significantly higher glycolysis scores than the IRF1-low group (p = 0.00048) (Figure 5F). Consistently, IRF1 expression was strongly positively correlated with GBP3 expression (R = 0.51, p < 2.2 × 10-16) (Figure 5G), in line with GBP3 being a representative interferon-stimulated gene associated with IRF1/STAT1 signaling. Moreover, IRF1 expression was positively correlated with glycolytic activity, as measured by HALLMARK_GLYCOLYSIS GSVA scores (R = 0.18, p = 0.0071) (Figure 5H and I), and was associated with poorer prognosis in the TCGA-HGSOC cohort (Supplementary Fig. S1F,G). These data position IRF1 as a transcriptional node coupling glycolytic remodeling to the resistant epithelial transcriptional program and to adverse clinical outcome.

Together, these findings define the resistant epithelial state by a glycolysis-coupled GAG biosynthetic program and an IRF1-coordinated transcriptional program, in which shared glycolytic carbon flux mechanistically links the two metabolic arms and IRF1 connects this metabolic state to cisplatin resistance and patient outcome.

Discussion

The present study aligns multi-cohort single-cell profiles with drug-response phenotypes and thereby resolves cisplatin resistance as a property of discrete epithelial states, not a diffuse feature averaged in bulk tumors.6,7,45 This state-resolved view extends prior scRNA-seq maps of HGSOC heterogeneity9-11 by pinpointing which epithelial subpopulations are enriched for resistance and embedding them within microenvironmental, regulatory, and metabolic contexts.

Across 34 epithelial subclusters, 14 were enriched for a cisplatin-resistant phenotype and were reproduced by signature scoring, indicating a multi-cluster architecture of resistance. Differential programs in the resistant states—spanning cell-cycle progression, senescence, ferroptosis, and FOXO signaling—are concordant with stress-adapted survival phenotypes previously described in ovarian cancer.9,12,46,47 Conceptually, these data support a model in which resistance behaves as a stable cell state maintained by coupled transcriptional and metabolic circuits rather than a single on/off pathway; a corollary is that cells prospectively isolated from resistance-enriched clusters should show higher predicted cisplatin response score and greater persistence in organoids/xenografts than cells from non-enriched clusters.

At the ecosystem level, resistance-enriched epithelial states communicated more densely and strongly with endothelium, fibroblasts, myeloid cells, and T/NK cells—but not B/plasma cells—and favored ECM/adhesion and junctional axes (e.g., LAMA3–CD44, COL6A1/2–CD44, NECTIN3–NECTIN2, CD99–CD99). These routes fit the broader paradigm that stromal and immune cues shape therapeutic response in ovarian cancer12,13,48 while specifying which epithelial states engage which partners through which channels. Notably, CD44 has been previously implicated in platinum resistance,49,50 and its enrichment in resistance-associated epithelial states in our analysis further supports the biological relevance of these clusters. In addition, several other resistance-related genes, including ABCC1, GSTP1, and ERCC1, also showed higher expression in cis_R cells, consistent with the resistant phenotype identified in our single-cell analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Mechanistically, CD44-centered adhesion can engage FAK/PI3K/AKT or YAP/TAZ signaling, elevate survival thresholds, and modulate ROS/ferroptosis sensitivity, whereas NECTIN/CD99-type junctions may alter immune transmigration and local clearance. These observations nominate ECM–receptor and endothelial/CAF signaling as tractable co-targets with platinum and motivate spatial validation of proximity and pathway activity.51-53

Two complementary TF-activity frameworks converged on IRF1, whose activity rose monotonically along pseudotime toward the resistant terminus and tracked JUN/STAT/NF-κB trends. Prior work reported context-dependent relationships between IRF1 and platinum response.15,42 Positioned within resistant epithelial states and along lineage progression, IRF1 emerges as a candidate driver node, plausibly stabilized by epigenetic remodeling that fixes drug-tolerant circuitry.54-57 These results argue for perturbational tests of IRF1 and its regulon—and for evaluating synergy between IRF1-axis modulation and platinum or immune-modulatory agents—to distinguish driver from bystander roles.

Metabolically, resistance-enriched states showed selective up-regulation of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) biosynthesis—notably keratan sulfate—together with heightened glycolysis; glycolytic activity correlated positively with predicted cisplatin response score in reference cell-line data. This suggests a GAG–glycolysis coupling in which ECM remodeling clusters CD44 and transduces mechanical/biochemical signals that enhance survival and influence drug access, while glycolytic reprogramming supplies ATP/NADPH to buffer DNA-damage stress 5 ; consistent with prior links between mitochondrial–metabolic state and chemosensitivity. 17 These relationships are directly testable with isotope-tracing and pharmacologic interference with glycolysis and GAG pathways in combination with platinum.

The findings have several clinical implications. First, state-specific markers and IRF1-activity signatures merit evaluation for risk stratification and MRD monitoring; existing associations of IRF1 with platinum response and survival support feasibility.15,42 Second, they nominate combination strategies that simultaneously (i) interrupt TME support (ECM–receptor/adhesion such as CD44; endothelial/CAF signaling49,58,59), (ii) re-wire TF circuitry (IRF1 with JUN/STAT/NF-κB co-activation), and (iii) constrain metabolic plasticity (glycolysis and/or GAG-pathway inhibition5,17). Trials incorporating state-enriched enrollment and spatial/ctDNA monitoring of state transitions could provide pharmacodynamic readouts aligned with the biology.

Although we applied harmonized quality control, preprocessing, batch correction, and cross-cohort integration to minimize technical bias, differences in patient background, treatment exposure, and sample origin may still affect the strength and stability of some observed signals. Therefore, our conclusions should be understood in the context of cross-cohort variation rather than as entirely cohort-independent biological findings. Future validation in prospectively collected uniformly treated cohorts with more complete clinical annotation will be important.

In aggregate, the data support a coherent model in which defined epithelial states, sustained by stromal–immune circuits, anchored by IRF1-centered transcriptional control, and buffered by GAG-coupled glycolysis, underpin cisplatin resistance in HGSOC—providing a mechanistic rationale for multi-pronged interventions that weaken paracrine/matrix support, modulate TF networks, and limit metabolic flexibility.

Conclusion

Cisplatin resistance in HGSOC is best understood as a state-encoded property of malignant epithelium reinforced by its microenvironment, IRF1-centered regulation, and a GAG–glycolysis metabolic axis. By tying single-cell states to drug response, lineage dynamics, and outcome, this work outlines a clinically actionable blueprint: develop state-informed biomarkers, disrupt ECM/adhesion and stromal signaling, modulate the IRF1 regulatory network, and target metabolic coupling to restore platinum sensitivity and curb relapse.

Limitation of the Study

Limitations include reliance on transcriptomic coupling to external drug-response resources, particularly GDSC2-derived cisplatin sensitivity data, for resistance inference, as well as inference-based estimates of TF activity and cell–cell communication. In addition, the public scRNA-seq cohorts included in this study do not uniformly provide complete clinical annotations, such as platinum-response status or consistent information on primary versus recurrent tumors, which limits direct evaluation of the association of IRF1 or GBP3 expression with reported clinical response. Such data may not fully reflect clinically relevant platinum exposure and may also have certain limitations in characterizing resistance phenotypes across different ovarian cancer models.

Although resistance-associated epithelial states identified by Scissor/AUCell showed concordance with biologically relevant features, including CD44 enrichment, a systematic evaluation of established platinum-resistance genes—such as multidrug transporters—was not performed and remains a limitation of the current study. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution in light of the study design, cross-cohort heterogeneity, and the inherent limitations of transcriptome-based resistance inference.

Furthermore, the current study is predominantly based on computational and bioinformatics analyses, lacking direct experimental validation. In our follow-up research, we plan to systematically supplement in vivo and in vitro experiments to confirm these findings. In addition, clinical associations should be prospectively confirmed in uniformly treated cohorts with comprehensive annotation.
