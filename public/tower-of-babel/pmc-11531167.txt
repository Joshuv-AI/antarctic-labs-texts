Trans-Achilles percutaneous fixation technique for posterior malleolus fracture: a cadaveric study
Authors: Hilal Yağar, Fatih Çiçek, Faruk Gazi Ceranoğlu, Hüseyin Karadağ, Selim Çinaroğlu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt has been reported that 43.6% of ankle fractures are accompanied by posterior malleolus fractures. The aim of this study is to define a safe zone for posterior malleolus fractures by determining the locations of the important anatomical structures in this region. Additionally, it aims to identify the trans-Achilles passage line for Kirschner wire insertion through a posteroanterior approach for posterior malleolus fragments.MethodsSix below-knee amputee fresh-frozen leg cadavers were used in this study. A trans-Achilles Kirschner wire was applied to the cadavers in the posteroanterior direction under the guidance of fluoroscopy. The areas where the Kirschner wire passed were dissected, and their proximity to vital anatomical structures was measured.ResultsIn all cadavers, the transverse thickness of the Achilles tendon at the level of the trans-Achilles Kirschner wire was 15.5 mm and the trans-Achilles Kirschner wire application was 18.6 mm from the sural nerve, 16 mm from the posterior tibial tendon, and 12.16 mm from the flexor digitorum longus muscle. It was performed 15.16 and 14.6 mm from the posterior tibial artery and vein, 12.3 mm from the tibial nerve, 13.6 mm from the tibiofibular joint, and 55.5 mm from the insertion site of the Achilles tendon to the calcaneus and at a sufficient distance from vital anatomical structures.ConclusionsThe proposed trans-Achilles percutaneous surgical technique is safe from neurovascular structures for fixing posterior malleolar fractures. However, the long-term clinical outcomes of this technique need to be explored.Level of evidenceLevel III, A cadaveric study.

Introduction

Ankle fractures are among the most common fractures in adults, accounting for 9% of all adult fractures. Ankle fractures accompany 43.6% of malleolus fractures [1]. The accompaniment of a posterior malleolus fracture alongside other malleolus fractures indicates that the trauma may result from high-energy trauma and can negatively impact the prognosis [1, 2]. Numerous studies define posterior malleolus fracture fragments using various terms such as posterior Volkmann fragment, posterior rim, posterior margin of the tibia, and posterior fragment of the distal tibia [1, 3, 4]. The posterior–inferior tibiofibular ligament starts from the posterior tibial tubercle (Volkmann fragment), adheres to the posterior aspect of the lateral malleolus, and forms the strongest part of the syndesmosis [5]. The posterior malleolus is closely adjacent to the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) tendon, posterior tibial tendon, flexor digitorum longus (FDL) tendon, posterior tibial artery and vein, and tibial nerve posteromedially. Posteriorly, it is nearby to the Achilles tendon and sural nerve [6–9]. This proximity should be considered in surgical interventions.

Surgical fixation of posterior malleolus fractures with avulsion of posteroinferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL) increases syndesmosis stabilization [10]. After the fixation of posterior malleolus fractures, strong syndesmosis stability can be achieved without using transsyndesmotic screws [11]. Many open and percutaneous fixation techniques have been described for posterior malleolus fixation [12–14]. Posteroanterior or anteroposterior percutaneous screw applications can be used for fixing the posterior malleolus. A risk of injury to the structures, especially in the case of posteroanterior percutaneous screw fixation, occurs, causing serious complications [12–14].

The rate of local infection, skin necrosis, and local complications is found to be 15% in the the patients undergoing surgery for posterior malleolus fracture using the posterolateral approach [15]. Patients undergoing surgery for supination external rotation injury using the posterolateral approach, the complication rate was found to be 9.8% for minor wound complication, 2.7% for major wound complication and 4.4% for deep infection [16]. Percutaneous fixation of malleolus fractures has been shown to result in fewer skin complications, faster rehabilitation, shorter hospital stays, and fewer neurovascular injuries [17, 18]. Wound site problems develop more frequently due to the presence of only subcutaneous tissue on both malleoli so percutaneous surgeries gain importance in these patients [19].

The aim of this study is to define a safe zone for posterior malleolus fractures by determining the locations of the important anatomical structures in this region. Additionally, it aims to identify the trans-Achilles passage line for Kirschner wire insertion through a posteroanterior approach for posterior malleolus fragments.

Materials and methods

Six fresh-frozen below-knee amputated foot cadavers with a mean age of 65 (62–88) years were used in this study. This study was performed in the dissection laboratory of the anatomy department.

Surgical technique

The surgical procedure was performed in the prone position with a radiolucent table visible to the fluoroscopy. The ankle was maintained in full dorsiflexion, and the toes in were kept in full extension throughout the surgical procedure. These movements helped the surgical procedure in several ways. Ankle dorsiflexion created a larger surface area for the Achilles tendon, reducing the risk of Achilles tendon rupture. After the extension of the toes, the tendinous region of the FHL on the posterior malleolus moved away from the surgical field. Then, the medial and lateral borders of the Achilles tendon at the ankle level on the posterior side of the leg were marked on the skin with a pencil (Fig. 1A). Two 1.6-mm trans-Achilles Kirschner wires were transversely inserted over the posterior malleolus without crossing the contralateral cortex under the guidance of a fluoroscopy (Fig. 1B). Applying at an anteromedial angle of 15-20o will direct the Kirschner wire just medial to the tibialis anterior tendon [20]. The logic of the two wires is established as follows; under fluoroscopy, the most medial and lateral points for the posterior malleolus are indicated, and Kirschner wires are inserted accordingly. Dimitroulias et al. stated that if necessary, adjustments can be made for the points where the screws will be placed. If started too laterally, the screw might not capture the posterior malleolus fragment [21].

Surgical dissection

Cadaveric feet that underwent screw application were dissected to evaluate the reliability of the method. To clearly examine the tissues such as fat and connective tissue anterior to the Achilles tendon and the anatomical structures such as vessels and nerves in the tissue, the Achilles tendon was incised from the line where the wire passed through. Also, the areas where the Kirschner wire passed through were dissected for further analysis. At the end of the dissection, the anatomical structures of the posterior tibial muscle, FDL muscle, FHL muscle, peroneus longus muscle, peroneus brevis muscle, tibial nerve, sural nerve, posterior tibial artery and vein, Achilles tendon, medial malleolus (the most medial part), and lateral malleolus (the most lateral part) were exposed (Fig. 2). Then, the distance between each exposed anatomical structure and the Kirschner wires (mean of the two wires) was measured and recorded using calipers (Fig. 3). Measurements were made with the ankle in dorsiflexion at the level of the apex of the medial malleolus and between the points where the distance between the anatomical structure and the wire was the closest. Figures 4 and 5 show the measurement of the distances between the Kirshner wires and the surrounding structures (Figs. 4 and 5). Table 1 shows the mean values obtained from six fresh-frozen cadaveric feet.

The mean transverse thickness of the Achilles tendon at the level where the Kirschner wire was applied was 15.5 mm (Table 1). The anatomical structure at the farthest distance of the Kirschner wire was the point where the Achilles tendon adhered to the calcaneus bone with an average of 55.5 mm in all samples. The structures at the closest distance were the Achilles tendon and the muscular belly of the FHL muscle. In addition, an average distance of 12.3 mm between the Kirschner wire and the tibial nerve provided both sensation and motor innervation of the foot muscles of the plantar aspect of the foot and ankle. The average distance was approximately 18.6 mm between the wire and the sural nerve, which received the sensation of the lateral side of the dorsum of the foot. The distance between the Kirschner wire and the tibiofibular joint adjacent to the point of application was 13.6 mm on average. These values indicated that percutaneous trans-Achilles screw application might be a safe method.

Discussion

This study is novel in proposing the trans-Achilles percutaneous surgical technique for fixing posterior malleolus fractures. A biomechanical study found that stiffness caused by posterior malleolus fixation was higher than that with syndesmosis stabilization [10]. A study evaluating short-term radiologic results showed that posterior malleolus fixation could provide adequate stability without the need for trans-syndesmosis screw fixation [11]. In the present cadaveric study, we aimed to provide safe fixation with our percutaneous screw application, but biomechanical studies are needed to validate the findings.

In addition, fixation of posterior malleolus fractures provided a more effective reduction than anteroposterior screw fixation [22]. Another study reported that the fixation of posterior malleolus fractures with a plate via the posterolateral approach was superior to anteroposterior percutaneous screw application. Dimitroulias et al. described the Achilles through posteroanterior percutaneous fixation technique and presented a case series with follow-ups longer than 6 months. They noted that this technique is simple and avoids neurovascular structures. Similar to our study, implantation was performed with the ankle in dorsiflexion in their surgical technique [21]. In addition, adequate compression force could not be provided because the threads of the screws inserted in the anteroposterior direction remained in the fracture line [23]. In the surgical technique applied in the present study, all threads provide strong interfragmentary compression because the threads will secure entirely beyond the fracture line allowing for compression across the fracture.

In a cadaveric study evaluating safe zones in the percutaneous fixation of posterior malleolus fractures, a 1.35-mm Kirschner wire was moved just medial to the anterior tibialis tendon and directed to exit just lateral to the Achilles tendon. It was reported to be an average distance of 18.1 mm (min 9 to max 24) from the posteromedial neurovascular structures. However, the Kirschner wire was 7.3 (0–23) mm from the sural nerve, 8 (3–12) mm from the tendinous part of the FHL, 3.6 (0–10) mm from the tibialis anterior tendon, 5.1 (0–12) mm from the Achilles tendon, and 7.4 (0–20) mm from the short saphenous vein, and these structures could be injured [24]. In the present study, the wire was positioned at an average distance of 18.6 mm from the sural nerve, 16 mm from the posterior tendon, 12.16 mm from the FDL, 15.16 and 14.6 mm from the posterior tibial artery and vein, and 12.3 mm from the posterior tibial nerve, ensuring a safe distance from anatomical structures. In addition, in all cadavers, the wire could pass distal to the muscle belly of the FHL and stay some distance away from its tendinous portion. Williams et al. defined a safe zone for screw placement in posterior malleolus fractures. According to their results, the screw placement corridor is described as being between the PVSL (posteromedial vertical syndesmotic line) and the lateral side of the FHL tendon. It has been shown that staying within 12 mm medial to the defined radiographic line (PVSL) ensures a safe distance from the syndesmosis and neurovascular structures [25]. In a cadaveric study on the posteroanterior percutaneous screw application of posterior malleolus fractures, Clarke et al. considered the entry point as just lateral to the Achilles tendon and 1 cm above the level of the medial malleolus. They showed that the anatomical structures were not damaged in all cadavers [26]. The surgical technique employed in the present study did not cause damage to any neurovascular structures, especially neurovascular structures. In addition, it aimed to provide stronger interfragmentary compression through fixation perpendicular to the fracture line, especially in Haraguchi type 1 fractures.

Limitations

The most significant limitation of this study was the small number of cadavers used because of insufficient below knee cadaveric materials. Different parameters of the technique used in the study could have been thoroughly evaluated with a larger sample size. As the study was performed on cadavers, no clinical information on wound complications after Kirschner wire application could be obtained. Complications related to paratendineal tissues, bursae around the Achilles tendon, and other soft tissues could not be evaluated. The use of this percutaneous technique isn’t suitable if there are intercalated fragments that blocks reduction.

Conclusions

The trans-Achilles percutaneous surgical technique is safe from neurovascular structures for posterior malleolar fracture fixation. However, clinical studies are needed to evaluate the results of using this technique for treating posterior malleolus fractures.
