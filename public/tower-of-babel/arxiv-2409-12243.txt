Convergence of Markov Chains for Constant Step-size Stochastic Gradient Descent with Separable Functions Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 7, 2026.
David Shirokoff, Philip Zaleski

Abstract
Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is a popular algorithm for minimizing objective functions that arise in machine learning. For constant step-sized SGD, the iterates form a Markov chain on a general state space. Focusing on a class of separable (non-convex) objective functions, we establish a “Doeblin-type decomposition,” in that the state space decomposes into a uniformly transient set and a disjoint union of absorbing sets. Each of the absorbing sets contains a unique invariant measure, with the set of all invariant measures being the convex hull. Moreover the set of invariant measures are shown to be global attractors to the Markov chain with a geometric convergence rate. The theory is highlighted with examples that show: (1) the failure of the diffusion approximation to characterize the long-time dynamics of SGD; (2) the global minimum of an objective function may lie outside the support of the invariant measures (i.e., even if initialized at the global minimum, SGD iterates will leave); and (3) bifurcations may enable the SGD iterates to transition between two local minima. Key ingredients in the theory involve viewing the SGD dynamics as a monotone iterated function system and establishing a “splitting condition” of Dubins and Freedman 1966 and Bhattacharya and Lee 1988.

Introduction

In recent years, stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
39
]
has become an immensely popular algorithm for minimizing objective functions
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}
of the form

F(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{i}=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)\,,\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}\,\qquad(f_{i}\neq 0)\,.

(1)

Define the maps
\varphi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\varphi_{i}(x):=x-\eta\nabla f_{{i}}(x)\qquad(1\leq{i}\leq n)\,,

(2)

where the parameter
\eta>0
is the
step-size
(or
learning rate
).

In its simplest form, the constant step-size SGD generates a random sequence of iterates

\{X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\}
via the following dynamics:

Given
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, sampled from an initial distribution
\mu_{0}
, update

\displaystyle X_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{{i}_{k}}(X_{k})\,\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad{i}_{k}\in\{1,2,3,\ldots,n\}=:[n]

(3)

is drawn independently and identically from a uniform distribution (i.i.d.); each map
\varphi_{i}
has probability
1/n
of being applied in (
3
).

Since the values of
{i}_{k}
are i.i.d., the random variable
X_{k+1}
satisfies the Markov property and the sequence
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
forms a Markov chain taking on values in the
general state-space

\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
23
,
36
]
for background on Markov chains in general state spaces).

Associated to each
X_{k}
is the corresponding law of probability
\mu_{k}\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
characterizing the probability distribution of
X_{k}

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(A):=\textrm{Prob}(X_{k}\in A)\qquad(A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\,.

Here
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of probability measures over the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The probability laws for a Markov chain then evolve via deterministic linear dynamics according to a Markov operator

\displaystyle\mu_{k+1}=\mathcal{P}\mu_{k}\,.

(4)

For general state spaces, the Markov operator is defined as

(\mathcal{P}\mu)(A):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x,A)\,d\mu(x)\qquad(A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))

(5)

where
p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\xrightarrow{}[0,1]
is the
transitional kernel

\displaystyle p(x,A):=\textrm{Prob}\left(X_{k+1}\in A\;|\;X_{k}=x\right)\,.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
p(x,\cdot)
is a probability measure, while for each Borel set
A
,
p(\cdot,A)
is a measureable function so that
\mathcal{P}
is well defined to act on probability measures (or more generally finite measures).

Intuitively, the value of
p(x,A)
measures the probability that the Markov chain transitions from a point
x
into the set
A
(which is the infinite dimensional analogue to matrix elements of a Markov matrix). Hence, for the SGD Markov chain (
3
),
p
is the fraction of maps
\varphi_{i}
that map
x
into
A

p(x,A)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{i}=1}^{n}\chi_{A}\big(\varphi_{i}(x)\big)\,,

(6)

where
\chi_{A}
is the characteristic function of the set
A
.

For SGD (
3
),
\mathcal{P}
then takes the form

\displaystyle(\mathcal{P}\mu)(A)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{i}=1}^{n}\mu\left(\varphi_{i}^{-1}(A)\right)\,,

(7)

where
\varphi_{i}(x)
are defined in (
2
) and
\varphi_{i}^{-1}(A)
is the preimage of
A
.

A probability measure
\mu^{\star}
is invariant (or stationary) with respect to the operator
\mathcal{P}
if

\mathcal{P}\mu^{\star}=\mu^{\star}\,.

(8)

The purpose of this work is to establish the convergence of the probability measures
\mu_{k}
for constant step-size SGD. We restrict our attention to separable (but non-convex) objective functions
F
and
f_{i}
. This has the advantage of enabling a simple, yet relatively complete, characterization of the how the probability measures
\mu_{k}
converge to a convex combination of the extreme points of the invariant measures. Our results make use of techniques from the theory of
iterated function systems
for monotone maps. A technical part of the proofs involves verifying a splitting condition
[
14
,
6
]
associated to Markov operators for these maps.

Analyzing the exact dynamics defined by the operator
\mathcal{P}
then enables a rigorous study of bifurcations. In particular, we provide an exact bifurcation study of whether iterates of SGD escape a local minima of
F
— contradicting predictions inferred by the diffusion approximation.

1.1
Background on SGD with Adaptive
\eta_{k}\rightarrow 0

An intuitive motivation for the dynamics (
3
) is that the expectation of
X_{k+1}
is a gradient step of
F
evaluated at
X_{k}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X_{k+1})=X_{k}-\eta\nabla F(X_{k})\,.

(9)

Equation (
9
) implies that on average, one expects the iterates
X_{k}
to move towards local minima of
F(x)
. This observation can be made precise and has lead to a significant body of work on SGD in the small
\eta\ll 1
step-size limit. When the step-size
\eta
in (
3
) is adaptive, that is,
\eta
is replaced with
\eta_{k}
with
\eta_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, it is well-established that the iterates
X_{k}
converge the dynamics of the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{x}=-\nabla F(x)\,.

(10)

For instance, see
[
15
]
,
[
4
, Proposition 4.1–4.2]
and
[
9
, Chapter 2]
(and references within). Closely related to the asymptotic trajectory (
10
) are a range of results establishing that
X_{k}
converges (almost surely) to minimizers of
F
, e.g.
[
43
,
12
]
for functions
F
satisfying a Łojasiewicz inequality in lieu of convexity.

1.2
Background for SGD with Constant
\eta

While much is known about SGD with adaptive step-sizes, far less is known in the constant step-size setting when
F
is non-convex. In particular, regarding (
3
): Does the Markov chain
X_{k}
remain trapped in an energy well of
F
? Or explore all minima of
F
? And if so, over what time-scales? Answers to these questions may help to provide insight into the initial phase of adaptive step-size SGD.

The difficulty in establishing a general theory for either the Markov chain
X_{k}
, or the associated probability laws
\mu_{k}
, is highlighted by the generality of SGD. For instance, the dynamics (
3
) include, as special cases:

•

All continuous
1
-dimensional deterministic iterative maps. This includes both the Logistic map and Tent map. For instance, take
n=1
and
F(x)=-\frac{3}{2}x^{2}+\frac{4}{3}x^{3}
so that
X_{k+1}=4X_{k}(1-X_{k})
when
\eta=1
. Varying
\eta\in(0,1]
, the iterates
X_{k}
exhibit the classic behavior of period doubling and the emergence of chaos;

•

Random walks, e.g., set
F(x)=0
with
f_{1}(x)=x
and
f_{2}(x)=-x
;

•

Infinite Bernoulli convolutions, which up to a linear change of variables, have the form
F(x)=x^{2}
, with
f_{1}(x)=(x-1)^{2}
,
f_{2}(x)=(x+1)^{2}
. Erdős
[
16
]
showed that in this quadratic setting the corresponding invariant measures may be singular. Quadratic models have also found recent applications in biological settings
[
11
]
, and remain an area of research in dynamical systems
[
17
,
5
,
30
,
31
,
1
,
2
]
.

In light of the (extremely) broad class of dynamics given by (
3
), we focus on separable
f_{i}
.

Continuous time partial differential equations (PDEs) provide one approach for approximating the evolution (
4
). Treating
\eta\ll 1
as a small parameter, (
10
) can be viewed as a leading order approximation to the dynamics for
X_{k}
; this also yields a corresponding advection PDE approximation for (
4
). Truncating formal expansions of the operator
\mathcal{P}
at progressively higher orders in the asymptotic parameter
\eta

[
32
]
then yield successive improvements. For instance, a second order expansions in
\eta
to
\mathcal{P}
yields a
diffusion approximation
to (
4
) (see §
3.1
). The diffusion approximation is known to accurately describes the evolution of
\mu_{k}
for finite time
[
32
,
27
,
19
]
, and infinite time when
F
is convex
[
18
]
. The diffusion approximation has also been used to gain insight into SGD dynamics and (in the regime for which it is valid) can estimate the probability distribution of
X_{k}
near the minimum of
F
(cf.
[
38
]
for estimates and convergence rates when
f_{i}
are convex). As we discuss below, the diffusion approximation can fail significantly to capture the correct long-time dynamics such as the number of, and regularity of, invariant measures (cf.
[
34
]
).

Variants of the diffusion equation have also been used to model SGD dynamics
[
33
,
10
]
. Rigorous PDE theory, such as the existence and geometric convergence to the invariant measure, have also been established for diffusion equation models
[
44
]
. Partial differential equation models can also arise from SGD as limiting dynamics where the asymptotic parameter is the dimension (not
\eta
!)
[
3
]
.

One approach to establish convergence of
\mathcal{P}^{k}\mu
to a unique invariant measure (on a state space
X
), is through a Doeblin condition of the form

\displaystyle p(x,A)\geq\epsilon\nu(A)\qquad\qquad\textrm{for all}\;A\in\mathscr{B}(X),\;x\in X

(11)

holds for some
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
,
\epsilon>0
. Some algorithmic variants of SGD, such as those that add random noise at each step (e.g.,
stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics
), or make strong assumptions on
f_{i}
which mimic random noise (e.g.,
[
47
]
), closely resemble a stochastic ODE. In these cases, conditions such as (
11
) may be verified to prove convergence to a unique invariant measure. The version of SGD (
3
), in general, does not satisfy (
11
) (even when
X
is restricted to the absorbing sets).

Our main result establishes the convergence of the exact Markov chain dynamics (
4
). We emphasize that we do not make use of concepts that often arise in Markov chains with random noise, e.g.,
\varphi
-irreducibility (which is a notion of irreducibility for general state spaces), detailed balance/reversibility, or (
11
). We also make no continuous time approximation.
Rather, the starting point is to view (
3
) as a random
iterated function system
(IFS). We then show that the SGD dynamics (
3
) satisfy the
splitting
conditions
[
14
,
6
]
that guarantees convergence to an invariant measure for IFS with monotone maps.

1.3
Background on Iterated Function Systems

There is a long history of iterated function systems theory in dynamical systems — notably as means to construct fractals. Two broad approaches to establish existence and convergence to invariant measures in IFS are: (i) to show that the maps
\varphi_{i}
satisfy a contraction condition, or (ii), which is the approach we adopt, to show that the maps are monotone and satisfy a splitting condition. For example, when
\varphi_{i}
are contractions (which can occur when
f_{i}
is convex and
\eta
is constant but small enough), a result by Hutchinson
[
28
, Section 4.4, Theorem 1]
shows that
\mathcal{P}
is also a contraction on the space of probabilities and
\mu_{k}
converges geometrically to a unique invariant measure.

Convergence results for IFS have also been generalized to allow for weaker notions of contractivity
[
13
,
20
,
24
,
37
,
40
,
41
,
45
]
. Contraction conditions have recently been used to establish convergence in specific instances (e.g., when
f_{i}
are convex) of SGD
[
22
,
21
]
. Unfortunately, contractivity conditions can be difficult to verify (or possibly fail) in practical settings. For instance, when
f_{i}
is non-convex, the map
\varphi_{i}
(for small
\eta
) is never contractive in the standard Euclidean norm. Since we are particularly interested in settings where
f_{i}
and
F
are non-convex we avoid the use of contractions and instead make use of monotone maps
[
14
,
6
,
46
,
8
]
.

1.4
Contributions and Organization of the Paper

For constant step-size SGD with separable (non-convex) functions, the main contributions of the paper are:

•

A decomposition of the Markov chain state space for (
4
) into a uniformly transient set and disjoint union of absorbing sets — each supporting a unique invariant measure (these are the extreme points in the set of invariant measures);

•

The set of invariant measures are attractors with
\mathcal{P}^{k}\mu
converging geometrically to a convex combination of the extreme points;

•

Bounds on the number and support of the invariant measures;

•

Examples demonstrating the failure of the diffusion approximation; that the invariant measures may be supported outside a neighborhood of the global minima of
F
; and a rigorous bifurcation study of the dynamics (
4
).

The manuscript starts in §
2
by introducing the assumptions on
f_{i}
and presenting the main result. Examples are then provided in §
3
. The remainder of the paper is devoted to establishing the main result: §
4
introduces the required mathematical notation while §
5
reviews convergence theory for Markov operators with monotone maps
\varphi_{i}
. Several theoretical results for SGD in one dimension are established in §
6
, which are then expanded on in a non-trivial way to prove the main result in §
7
.

2.1
Assumptions and Problem Setting

Throughout, we assume that each of the functions
f_{i}
(and consequently
F
) are

f_{i}^{({j})}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle f_{i}(x)=\sum_{{j}=1}^{d}f_{{i}}^{({j})}(x_{j})\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad x=(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\ldots,\,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(12)

With this convention,
f_{i}^{({j})}
is allowed to be
0
, however to avoid trivialities
f_{i}\neq 0
.

For functions of the form (
12
), the maps
\varphi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
become

\varphi_{i}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{i}^{(1)}(x_{1}),&\varphi_{i}^{(2)}(x_{2}),&\ldots\;,&\varphi_{i}^{(d)}(x_{d})\end{pmatrix}

(13)

where each of the components of
\varphi_{i}
is a single variable function of
x_{j}

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}^{({j})}(s)=s-\eta\frac{d}{ds}f_{i}^{({j})}(s)\qquad(1\leq{j}\leq d)\,.

For all non-zero
f_{i}^{({j})}

({i}=1,\ldots,n
;
{j}=1,\ldots,d)
we assume that

(A1)

f_{i}^{({j})}
is continuously differentiable, i.e.,
f_{i}^{({j})}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

(A2)

f_{i}^{({j})}
has a finite number of critical points.

(A3)

f_{i}^{({j})}(x)\rightarrow\infty
as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
.

For each family of functions
\{f_{i}^{({j})}\}_{{i}=1}^{n}
the set of critical points

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{({j})}=\bigcup_{{i}=1}^{n}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;\frac{d}{dx}f_{i}^{({j})}(x)=0,\;f_{i}^{({j})}\neq 0\right\}

is then finite and contained in an interval of the form

\displaystyle I^{({j})}:=[a,b]\,,

(14)

where we define
a
and
b
to be the smallest and largest elements of
\mathcal{C}^{({j})}
. With this notation, the general
state space
will be

\displaystyle I:=I^{(1)}\times I^{(2)}\times\ldots\times I^{(d)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

(15)

We further assume that

(A4)

The derivatives of
f_{i}^{({j})}
are
K
-Lipschitz on
I^{({j})}
, i.e., for some
K>0

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{d}{dx}f_{i}^{({j})}(x)-\dfrac{d}{dy}f_{i}^{({j})}(y)\right|\leq K\,|x-y|\qquad\textrm{for}\quad x,y\in I^{({j})}\;\textrm{and}\;1\leq{i}\leq n\,.

(A5)

(
Inconsistent optimization
) For each
1\leq{j}\leq d
the functions
f_{i}^{({j})}
share no common critical point, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bigcap_{{i}=1}^{n}\left\{x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\>:\;\frac{d}{dx}f_{i}^{({j})}(x_{j})=0\right\}=\phi\,.

The assumptions
(A1)
–
(A4)
(or minor variations of) are standard in the gradient descent optimization literature. Together they ensure that when
\eta<K^{-1}
each
\varphi_{i}^{({j})}
is an increasing function — a property we use in the proofs. Condition
(A2)
is made for simplicity to rule out complications when
f_{i}
admits an infinite number of critical points, however the condition can be relaxed, for instance some of the theory we present generalizes to allow for some
f_{i}
to be constant on an interval.

Assumption
(A5)
is sometimes referred to as
inconsistent optimization
since it implies the
f_{i}
do not share a common minimizer. Condition
(A5)
also implies that for each
{j}
at least two functions in the set
\{f_{i}^{({j})}\}_{{i}=1}^{n}
are non-zero (otherwise
(A5)
fails trivially).

While it may appear that
(A5)
is overly simplifying, it is necessary to establish both convergence in a (strong) metric, and uniform geometric convergence rates in
theorem

2
. When Assumption
(A5)
is removed, the convergence theory we build on from
[
6
,
14
]
no longer applies as stated.

The necessity of Assumption
(A5)
in the main result is highlighted with the simple example: set
n=1,d=1
and
F(x)=x^{2}
. Taking
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, the SGD dynamics revert to deterministic gradient descent

. If
X_{0}=1
, so that
\mu_{0}=\delta_{1}
is a Dirac mass, then
\mu_{k}\rightarrow\delta_{0}
converges weakly as

, but does not converge in the metric used in the main result.

When Assumption
(A5)
is removed, additional techniques are required to establish convergence. For instance, suppose
x^{*}
is a simultaneous critical point of each

so that
\delta_{x^{*}}
is an invariant measure. If
x^{*}
is a local minimizer for each
f_{i}
, with

being convex (not necessarily separable), then an application of Hutchinson
[
28
]
can directly be used to show an initial measure
\mu_{0}
converges geometrically to
\delta_{x^{*}}
in the Wasserstein metric (as opposed to the metric used in the main result). If
x^{*}
is a local minimum for some

and a saddle or local maximum for others, then general sufficient conditions for convergence of
\mu_{k}
to

2.2
Main Result

In this section we outline our main result; some technical definitions are deferred to §
4
.

Our starting point is to define formulas for disjoint closed rectangles
T_{{\mathbf{m}}}
that, as we will show, are
absorbing sets
. With positive probability the SGD dynamics
X_{k}
will reach one of these sets within a finite number of steps. Each
T_{\mathbf{m}}
will contain exactly one invariant measure for the SGD Markov chain.

The first step is to define disjoint closed intervals
T_{m}
in dimension
d=1
in terms of sets

and
R
characterizing left and right moving dynamics. For notational brevity we drop the superscripts in

and
\varphi_{i}^{(1)}
in this

When

have the property that, for all
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(x)>x\quad\textrm{if}\quad f_{i}^{\prime}(x)<0\,\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\varphi_{i}(x)<x\quad\textrm{if}\quad f_{i}^{\prime}(x)>0\,.

Hence,
\varphi_{i}
maps the point

to the left when
f_{i}^{\prime}(x)>0
and to the right when
f_{i}^{\prime}(x)<0

\varphi_{i}(x)=x
is a fixed point when

L:=\bigcup_{{i}=1}^{n}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;f^{\prime}_{i}(x)>0\right\}\,,

(16)

(17)

Note that if

with positive probability (an analogous result holds for

and

also characterize when the SGD dynamics move to the left or right with probability one: A point

if and only if

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(x)\geq x\,,\qquad\textrm{for all }1\leq{i}\leq n\,,

(18)

and

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}(x)\leq x\,,\qquad\textrm{for all }1\leq{i}\leq n\,.

(19)

Several properties of

and
R
are established in §

x_{j}

T^{({j})}:=\bigcup_{{m}=1}^{M_{j}}T_{m}^{({j})}\subset\mathbb{R}\,,

With this notation, the set
T\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
admits an alternative form

T=T^{(1)}\times T^{(2)}\ldots\times T^{(d)}.

(22)

We can now state the main result.

Theorem 2
(Main Result).

Given the Markov chain (
4
) and (
7
) corresponding to the SGD dynamics (
1
)–(
3
), assume
(A1)
–
(A5)
hold. Let
I
and
T_{{\mathbf{m}}}
be defined as in (
15
) and (
20
), and let
\eta
be any value
0<\eta<1/K
. Then,

(a)

The statespace
I
is positive invariant and decomposes into the disjoint union

\displaystyle I=B\cup\left(\bigcup_{{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}}T_{{\mathbf{m}}}\right)\,\qquad(B:=I\setminus T)\,,

where

(i)

T_{\mathbf{m}}
(
{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}
) is positive invariant/absorbing, and contains at least one local minimizer of
F
. The number of
T_{{\mathbf{m}}}
is at most the number of local minima of
F
.

(ii)

T
is non-empty and is an attractor in the sense that there exists
\ell_{0}=\ell_{0}(\eta)
such that for every
x\in I
there is a path
\vec{p}
of length
\ell_{0}
satisfying
\varphi_{\vec{p}}(x)\in T
.

(iii)

B
is uniformly transient with

\displaystyle({\mathcal{P}}^{\ell_{0}}\mu)(B)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\ell_{0}}}\right)\,\mu(B)\,,

(23)

for any probability measure
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(I)
.

(b)

For each
{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}^{\star}
with support contained in
T_{\mathbf{m}}
.
Moreover, there exist an
\ell_{{\mathbf{m}}}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{{\mathbf{m}}}\in\{+1,-1\}^{d}
such that for any
\mu
with support contained in
T_{\mathbf{m}}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{\alpha_{{\mathbf{m}}}}\left({\mathcal{P}}^{k}\mu,\mu_{{\mathbf{m}}}^{\star}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\ell_{\mathbf{m}}}}\right)^{\lfloor k/\ell_{\mathbf{m}}\rfloor}\qquad\qquad k>0\,.\end{split}

(24)

In the case of
d=1
, the convergence (
24
) is a (stronger) contraction

\displaystyle\begin{split}d_{F}\left({\mathcal{P}}^{\ell_{{m}}}\mu,\mu_{m}^{\star}\right)\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\ell_{m}}}\right)\,d_{F}(\mu,\mu_{m}^{\star})\,.\end{split}

(25)

Here the metrics
d_{\alpha_{{\mathbf{m}}}}
and
d_{F}
are defined in
section

4
and
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the greatest integer (floor) function.
The measures
\mu_{{\mathbf{m}}}^{\star}
are the only invariant measures supported in
I
, and the number
M_{T}
is bounded by the number of local minima of
F
.

(c)

For any probably measure
\mu_{0}\in\mathscr{P}(I)
,
\mu_{k}:={\mathcal{P}}^{k}\mu_{0}
converges geometrically to an invariant measure of the form

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}=\sum_{{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}}c_{{\mathbf{m}}}\,\mu_{{\mathbf{m}}}^{\star}\qquad\left(0\leq c_{{\mathbf{m}}}\leq 1,\quad\sum_{{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}}c_{{\mathbf{m}}}=1\right)\,,

in the sense that

\tilde{d}(\mu_{k},\mu^{\star})\leq 3\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\right)^{\lfloor k/\ell\rfloor}\qquad\qquad k>0\,.

(26)

Here
\ell:=2\,(\ell_{0}\vee\max_{{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}}\ell_{\mathbf{m}})
, and the metric
\tilde{d}
is defined on probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(I)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{d}(\mu,\nu):=d_{\rm TV}\big(\mu|_{B},\nu|_{B}\big)+\sum_{{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}}d_{\alpha_{{\mathbf{m}}}}\big(\mu|_{T_{{\mathbf{m}}}},\,\nu|_{T_{{\mathbf{m}}}}\big)\,\,,

(27)

where
\nu\big|_{A}
is the restriction of the finite measure
\nu
to a Borel set
A
.
Lastly, in the case where
d=1
, (
26
) becomes

d_{F}(\mu_{k},\mu^{\star})\leq 3\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\right)^{\lfloor k/\ell\rfloor}\qquad\qquad k>0\,.

A few remarks are in order:

•

The decomposition of
I
in
theorem

2
(a) is in the spirit of a “Doeblin decomposition” (cf.
[
35
]
);

•

The theorem bounds the support of
\mu_{{\mathbf{m}}}^{\star}
to lie in
T_{\mathbf{m}}
and does not imply the support is equal to
T_{\mathbf{m}}
. For instance, when
F
is quadratic the invariant measure may be supported on a set of Lebesgue measure zero (cf. infinite Bernoulli convolutions §
1.2
);

•

If we let
W
be the set of invariant measures of
\mathcal{P}
with support in
I
, then the extreme points of
W
are exactly
\mu_{\mathbf{m}}^{\star}
(
{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{M}
);

•

Since the
T_{{\mathbf{m}}}
are disjoint closed rectangles,
theorem

2
implies the supports of every pair of distinct invariant measures may be separated by a hyperplane, i.e., the supports of any pair of invariant measures are not “interlaced;”

•

While the number of sets
T_{{\mathbf{m}}}
(and invariant measures) is bounded in terms of
F
, the definition of
T_{\mathbf{m}}
depends on how
F
is split into the functions
f_{i}
;

•

The number of invariant measures does not depend on
\eta
provided
\eta<1/K
, and the associated Markov chain for SGD exhibits no bifurcations as a function of
\eta
. The rate of convergence to equilibrium does however depend on
\eta
;

•

The SGD iterates
X_{k}
may traverse between two local minima of
F
only if both minima are contained in the same
T_{{\mathbf{m}}}
(somewhat akin to a “mountain pass” theorem);

•

Somewhat surprisingly, the converse to
theorem

2
(a)(i) need not hold: the local (and even global) minima of
F
do not need to be in
T
(see §
3
). In other words, there are instances of SGD where even if the iterates
X_{k}
are initialized to lie in a neighborhood of the global minimum of
F
, they will eventually leave (with probability
1
);

•

The contraction estimate (
25
) is sometimes referred to as a spectral gap estimate (in the metric
d_{F}
);

•

When
d>1
, the invariant measures
\mu_{{\mathbf{m}}}^{\star}
are not necessarily product measures. In addition, the main result in dimension
d>1
does not follow as a corollary of the one dimensional case;

•

An explicit bound on
\ell
in
theorem

2
determining the convergence rate can be obtained in terms of
f_{i}^{(j)}
, e.g., see §
3
.

3
Examples in 1D

This section provides examples highlighting
theorem

2
in
d=1
. In each example, we analyze an objective function
F
with the specific splitting

F(x)=\frac{1}{2}\big(f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)\big)\,,

(28)

where

f_{1}(x):=F(x)+\lambda x\,,\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad f_{2}(x):=F(x)-\lambda x\,.

(29)

Here
\lambda>0
is a free parameter that modifies the functions
f_{1},f_{2}
in the decomposition. In this case, the maps
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
become

\varphi_{1}(x)=x-\eta F^{\prime}(x)-\lambda\eta\,,\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\varphi_{2}(x):=x-\eta F^{\prime}(x)+\lambda\eta\,.

Notice that for this splitting, the SGD update becomes gradient descent plus an additional random walk with step-size
\lambda\eta
, and is also the one dimensional analog of the algorithm proposed in
[
29
]
.

3.1
Diffusion Approximation Background

We collect here several basic facts regarding the diffusion approximation in dimension
d=1
as we reference it in subsequent examples.

The diffusion approximation is a variable coefficient advection-diffusion equation of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(u(x)\rho\big)+\frac{\eta}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\Big(D(x)\rho\Big)\,\qquad\textrm{in}\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,T]\,,

(30)

with initial data
\rho(x,0)=\mu_{0}
, where
\mu_{0}
is the SGD initialization distribution.

The velocity
u(x)
and diffusion coefficient
D(x)
in (
30
) are given in terms of the SGD functions
f_{i}(x)
and
F(x)
as

\displaystyle u(x):=\frac{d}{dx}\Phi(x)\qquad\textrm{where}\qquad\Phi(x):=F(x)+\frac{\eta}{4}(F^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,,

and

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{i}=1}^{n}\Big(f_{i}^{\prime}(x)-F^{\prime}(x)\Big)^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{{i}=1}^{n}\big(f_{i}^{\prime}(x)\big)^{2}-\big(F^{\prime}(x)\big)^{2}\geq 0\,.

Intuitively, the advective term in (
30
) evolves the probability
\rho
towards minizers of
F
, while the diffusion term arises from the stochastic terms
f_{i}
in SGD. For instance, formally setting
\eta=0
in (
30
) results in an advection equation for
\rho
, with
characteristics
defined by the gradient flow
\dot{x}=-F^{\prime}(x)
.

The equation (
30
) arises as a formal asymptotic approximation to the (exact) discrete-in-time Markov evolution
\mu_{j+1}=\mathcal{P}\mu_{j}
in the small parameter
\eta\ll 1
by matching terms up order
\mathcal{O}(\eta)
. When
\eta\ll 1
,
\rho(x,t)

(t=\eta j)
approximates the SGD probability evolution
\mu_{j}
for finite times (cf.
[
32
,
19
,
18
,
27
]
).

The stationary solutions of (
30
) satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\big(u(x)\rho\big)+\frac{\eta}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\Big(D(x)\rho\Big)=0\,.

(31)

Note that (
31
) is a singular ordinary differential equation whenever the diffusion coefficient
D(x)
vanishes at a point
x^{*}
, namely

\displaystyle D(x^{*})=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad f_{1}^{\prime}(x^{*})=f_{2}^{\prime}(x^{*})=\ldots=f_{n}^{\prime}(x^{*})=F^{\prime}(x^{*})\,.

(32)

Under Assumption
(A5)
,
D(x)\neq 0
since the expression in the right of (
32
) holds nowhere.

Equation (
31
) admits the following unique solution in the space of probabilities densities

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\,\textrm{exp}\Big(-\frac{2}{\eta}V(x)\Big)\,,

(33)

where

\displaystyle V(x)

\displaystyle:=\int^{x}D^{-1}(x)\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\Phi(x)+\frac{2}{\eta}D(x)\Big)\,dx\,,

provided

\displaystyle Z:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textrm{exp}\Big(-\frac{2}{\eta}V(x)\Big)\,dx<\infty\,.

For the splitting given in (
28
) and (
29
), the stationary density of the diffusion approximation given in (
33
) is simplifies to

\rho^{\star}(x)\propto\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{2}{\eta\lambda^{2}}\Phi(x)\right)=\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{2}{\eta\lambda^{2}}\left(F(x)+\frac{\eta}{4}(F^{\prime}(x))^{2}\right)\right)\,.

(34)

In the following examples, we will compare the approximation (
34
) to the true invariant measures of the SGD Markov operator
\mathcal{P}
. We will see that for larger values of
\lambda
the Markov operator
\mathcal{P}
has a unique invariant measure
\mu^{\star}
which is approximated by the density
\rho^{\star}(x)
. However, as
\lambda
decreases, the Markov operator
\mathcal{P}
may have multiple invariant measures that differ from the diffusion approximation.

Mathematical Background

This section collects the mathematical background used throughout the paper.

We say that a non-empty set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
positive invariant
for the SGD dynamics, if

\varphi_{i}(T)\subset T\,,\qquad\textrm{for all }1\leq i\leq n\,.

(43)

For a general state space, a Borel set
T
is
absorbing

[
36
, Section 4.2.2]
if the Markov transition kernel
p(x,T)=1
for all
x\in T
. Identity (
43
) ensures that if the initial measure
\mu_{0}
is supported on
T
, then all successive iterations
\mu_{k}
defined by (
4
) will be supported on
T
. Hence, for the SGD dynamics, a Borel set
T
is absorbing if and only if it is positive invariant.

A Borel set
B
is
uniformly transient

[
36
, Chapter 8]
if the function

\displaystyle U(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{P}^{n}\delta_{x})(B)\,,

is bounded on
B
, i.e.,
\sup_{x\in B}U(x)<\infty
. The function
U(x)
measures the expected number of times the Markov chain initialized to
X_{0}=x\in B
visits
B
; uniformly transient sets are expected to spend only a finite time in
B
. The inequality (
23
) implies
U(x)
is bounded by a geometric series and hence
B
is uniformly transient.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2},\,\ldots,\,\alpha_{d})\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
denote the
d
-tuple of signed unit coefficients, and associate to each of the
2^{d}
vectors
\alpha
the closed orthont of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{d}:=\left\{c_{1}\,(\alpha_{1}e_{1})+c_{2}\,(\alpha_{2}e_{2})\ldots+c_{d}\,(\alpha_{d}e_{d})\,:\,c_{j}\geq 0\textrm{ for }1\leq j\leq d\right\}\,.

Here
e_{j}
denotes the usual basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Each orthant
\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{d}
then defines a partial ordering
\preceq_{{\alpha}}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

x\preceq_{{\alpha}}y\qquad\textrm{if and only if}\qquad y-x\in\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{d}\,.

For two (non-empty) sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
x\in A\setminus B
if
x\in A
and
x\notin B
, and also
A\preceq_{\alpha}B
if
a\preceq_{\alpha}b
for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
.

A map
\gamma:S\rightarrow S
(
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
) is said to be monotone with respect to the cone
\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{d}
on a set
S
if for any
x,y\in S

x\preceq_{\alpha}y\qquad\textrm{implies}\qquad\gamma(x)\preceq_{\alpha}\gamma(y)\,.

(44)

Note that if
x\preceq_{\alpha}y
then
y\preceq_{-\alpha}x
. To avoid this redundancy, we take
\alpha_{1}=+1
.

The metric used in the convergence result of Bhattacharya and Lee makes use of the following restricted class of sets. Define
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
to be the family of sublevel sets of continuous monotone maps

A\in\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\qquad\textrm{if}\qquad A=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;:\;\gamma(y)\preceq_{\alpha}c\right\}\,,

for some constant vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and continuous function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is monotone with respect to
\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{d}
. When
\alpha=(+1,+1,\ldots,+1)
, the set
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
includes all (semi-infinite) rectangles of the form
(-\infty,c_{1}]\times(-\infty,c_{2}]\times\ldots\times(-\infty,c_{d}]
, for
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but also includes other sets as well.

For a closed and bounded Borel set
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(which, in practice, we will take as a rectangle), and two (Borel) probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(I)
, the metric of Bhattacharya and Lee is

d_{\alpha}(\mu,\nu):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}}|\mu(A\cap I)-\nu(A\cap I)|\,.

(45)

For closed and bounded
I
, the metric space
(\mathscr{P}(I),d_{\alpha})
is complete
[
6
,
7
]
.

In dimension
d=1
,
d_{\alpha}(\mu,\nu)
reduces to the Komolgorov distance (which we write as
d_{F}
):

d_{F}(\mu,\nu)=\|F_{\mu}(x)-F_{\nu}(x)\|_{\infty}\,\qquad\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{P}(I)\quad(I=[a,b])\,,

where
F_{\mu}(x):=\mu\left([a,x]\right)
is the cumulative distributions function (CDF) and

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)|\,.

The metric
d_{\alpha}
is stronger than the Wasserstein metric and weak convergence. The converse is not true, e.g., in
d=1
,
\mu_{k}=\delta_{\frac{1}{k}}
converges weakly to
\delta_{0}
but not with respect to
d_{F}
. On the other hand,
d_{\alpha}
is weaker than the total variation metric
d_{\rm TV}(\mu,\nu)
which is defined as (
45
) where the supremum is taken over all sets in the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

The metric
d_{\alpha}
generalizes naturally to all finite non-negative measures
\mathscr{M}(I)
with mass
|\mu|
. The following properties also hold:

d_{\alpha}(\mu,\nu)\leq\max\{|\mu|,|\nu|\}\,,\qquad(\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{M}(I))\,;

(46)

and, for all
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathscr{M}(I)
and
c>0
,

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2},\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})

\displaystyle\leq|\mu_{1}|+|\nu_{1}|+d_{\alpha}(\mu_{2},\nu_{2})\,,

(47)

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}(\mu_{1}+\mu_{2},\nu_{1}+\nu_{2})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\alpha}(\mu_{1},\nu_{1})+d_{\alpha}(\mu_{2},\nu_{2})\,,

(48)

\displaystyle d_{\alpha}(c\,\mu_{1},c\,\nu_{2})

\displaystyle=c\,d_{\alpha}(\mu_{1},\nu_{1})\,.

(49)

The identities (
46
)–(
49
) follow directly from the definition of
d_{\alpha}
and the triangle inequality.

To represent trajectories taken by iterates of SGD, let

\displaystyle\vec{{i}}=\begin{pmatrix}{i}_{1},&{i}_{2},&\ldots\,,&{i}_{m-1},&{i}_{m}\end{pmatrix}\in[n]^{m}\,,

where each
{i}_{k}\in[n]

(1\leq k\leq m)
and
|\vec{i}|=m
is the
path length
. The
path
defined by
\vec{i}
is the composition

\displaystyle\varphi_{\,\vec{{i}}\,}(x):=\varphi_{{i}_{m}}\big(\cdots\varphi_{2}\big(\varphi_{1}(x)\big)\cdots\big)=\varphi_{{i}_{m}}\circ\varphi_{{i}_{m-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{{i}_{1}}(x)\,.

Two paths are distinct,
\vec{i}\neq\vec{j}
, if they differ in at least one entry. For two paths
\vec{j}
and
\vec{i}
with lengths
|\vec{j}|=m_{1}
and
|\vec{i}|=m_{2}
we write

\displaystyle\vec{i}\circ\vec{j}=\begin{pmatrix}j_{1},&\ldots\;,&j_{m_{1}},&i_{1},&\ldots\;,&i_{m_{2}}\end{pmatrix}

to be the concatenation of the paths, so that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\,\vec{i}\circ\vec{j}\,}(x):=\varphi_{\,\vec{i}\,}\big(\varphi_{\,\vec{j}\,}(x)\big)=\varphi_{\,\vec{i}\,}\circ\varphi_{\,\vec{j}\,}(x)\,.

Markov Operators for Iterated Function Systems with Monotone Maps

We state here a result of Dubins and Freedman
[
14
]
and the higher dimensional extension by Bhattacharya and Lee
[
6
,
7
]
; note that a similar result was independently proven by Hopenhayn and Prescott
[
25
]
(cf.
[
26
]
). The theorems yield sufficient conditions for convergence of Markov operators arising from iterated function systems with monotone maps. The original theorems are stated in greater generality than we need, and so we state them as they would be applied to the notation and dynamics in (
3
).

Theorem 3
(Dubins and Freedman
[
14
, Theorem 5.10]
).

Assume
\varphi_{i}:[a,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are continuous and strictly monotone (increasing or decreasing) on
[a,b]
and that
[a,b]
is positive invariant. If there exists distinct pairs
i\neq j
,
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and a point
x\in[a,b]
for which

\varphi_{i}\big([a,b]\big)\subset[a,x]\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\varphi_{j}\big([a,b]\big)\subset[x,b]\,,

(50)

then there exists a unique invariant measure
\mu^{\star}
to the operator
\mathcal{P}
given in (
7
).

Furthermore, for any measure
\mu
supported on
[a,b]
we have,

d_{F}(\mathcal{P}\mu,\mu^{\star})\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)d_{F}(\mu,\mu^{\star})\,,

(51)

where
(1-\frac{1}{n})
is the geometric rate of convergence.

Dubins and Freedman referred to (
50
) as a
splitting
condition since
\varphi_{i}
and
\varphi_{j}
map
[a,b]
into disjoint subintervals — split by
x
. If the maps
\varphi_{i}
are monotone increasing, we can re-state (
50
) as

\varphi_{i}(b)\leq\varphi_{j}(a)\qquad(i\neq j)\,,

(52)

for some distinct pairs
1\leq i,j\leq n
(see
fig.

13
).

a
b
\varphi_{j}(a)
\varphi_{i}(b)

Visualization of condition (

) and (

).

It may be that no pair of
i,j
satisfy (
52
). In this case,
theorem

3
can be applied to powers of the operator
(\mathcal{P})^{\ell}
where (
52
) (equivalently (
50
)) can be replaced with a pair of distinct paths of the same length satisfying

\varphi_{\,\vec{i}\,}(b)\leq\varphi_{\,\vec{j}\,}(a)\qquad(\vec{i}\neq\vec{j})\qquad|\vec{i}|=|\vec{j}|=\ell\,.

(53)

Inequality (
53
) is exactly (
52
) with
\{\varphi_{i}\;:\;i\in[n]\}
replaced by
\{\varphi_{\vec{i}}\;:\;\vec{i}\in[n]^{\ell}\}
. Applying
theorem

3
with condition (

) in lieu of (
50
) modifies the geometric factor appearing in the convergence rate (
51
) to
(1-n^{-\ell})
.

The result of Bhattacharya and Lee extends
theorem

3
to dimensions

where the splitting and monotonicity conditions are with respect to a cone.

Theorem 4
(Bhattacharya and Lee
[
6
, Theorem 2.1 & Corollary 2.4]
).

Set
I=[0,1]^{d}
and let
\alpha
be fixed. Suppose
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}:I\rightarrow I
are each continuous and monotone with respect to
\mathbb{R}_{\alpha}^{d}
.
If there are two paths
\vec{p}_{1}
and
\vec{p}_{2}
of length
\ell
satisfying

\varphi_{\vec{p}_{1}}(I)\preceq_{\alpha}x_{0}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad x_{0}\preceq_{\alpha}\varphi_{\vec{p}_{1}}(I)

(54)

for some
x_{0}\in I
, then the Markov operator
\mathcal{P}
has exactly one invariant measure
\mu^{\star}
with support contained in
I
. In addition, for any
\mu
supported on
I

d_{\alpha}({\mathcal{P}}^{k}\mu,\mu^{\star})\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{\ell}}\right)^{\lfloor k/\ell\rfloor}\,\qquad\quad k>0\,.

(55)

There are a few minor differences from the exact theorem statement
[
6
, Theorem 2.1 & Corollary 2.4]
and the version we state in
theorem

4
. In
[
6
]
, (
55
) is stated with
\mu
replaced by
\delta_{x}
(the upper bound in (
55
) being uniform in
x\in I
). This is equivalent to (
55
) as written above.

The formulation in
[
6
]
also allows for the maps
\varphi_{i}
to be drawn from an infinite index set. In this case, the convergence rate (
55
) (or (
51
)) is given in terms of the probability that the event (
54
) holds. If
\kappa
pairs of distinct paths satisfy (
55
), then
[
6
]
yields the stronger geometric rate of
1-\kappa/n^{\ell}
in (
55
).

Lastly,
[
6
, Theorem 2.1 & Corollary 2.4]
is stated only for
\alpha
being the positive orthont. However, the theorem extends trivially to the version stated in
theorem

4
defined over any cone
\alpha
by the change of variables:
\varphi(x)\rightarrow\Lambda\varphi(\Lambda x)
where
\Lambda=\textrm{diag}(\alpha)
.

The assumptions

Since for every

.

Proposition 6
(Properties of

Let

(d)

The sets

Proof.

(a) Through direct construction, we show

(b) Since

(c) We show that
