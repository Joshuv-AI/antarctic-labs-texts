The heart of the hair fiber: How the inner structure of the coat reflects adaptive variation across color, sex, age, and body region in dromedary camels (Camelus dromedarius)
Authors: C. Iglesias Pastrana, E.C. Quispe Peña, E. Ciani, N. Cisneros Rodríguez, J.V. Delgado Bermejo, M.E. Camacho Vallejo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Quantitative analysis of camel hair medullation using Smart Fiber Medullometer.•Coat color, sex, age, and body region jointly influence hair microstructure.•Dark and white camels show higher medullation, suggesting adaptive coat strategies.•Males and dorsal regions display coarser, more medullated fibers than females and ventral ones.•Combined biological and structural traits help explain camel adaptation to hot deserts.

Introduction

The prevalence of dark-coated camels in hot desert environments presents an intriguing paradox in animal adaptation. While lighter coats are generally expected to reflect solar radiation and provide thermal advantages (Diya & Gerken, 2024; Mota-Rojas et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2022), dark-coated dromedary camels thrive in some of the world's hottest regions (Alhaddad & Alhajeri, 2019). This observation suggests a complex interaction of genetic, physiological, and ecological mechanisms supporting survival under extreme heat. Conversely, white camels, with reduced pigmentation, may face distinct thermoregulatory challenges due to altered heat absorption and radiation penetration (Gebremedhin et al., 2023).

Coat color in dromedary camels is largely determined by polymorphisms in the agouti signaling protein (ASIP) and melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) genes, which are associated with white, black and dark-brown phenotypes (Almathen et al., 2018). The light-brown coat is hypothesized to represent the species' ancestral coloration. Dark coloration, however, could confer adaptive advantages through physiological mechanisms that minimize radiant heat load at the skin level and mitigate heat stress (Singh et al., 1997; Ward et al., 2002). Thermophysiological studies indicate that although dark- and light-coated dromedary camels show similar rectal temperature (Tr), surface temperature (Ts), and respiratory rate (RR) under moderate conditions, exposure to high heat produces coat color-dependent responses. Dark-coated dromedaries exhibit higher Ts but maintain stable Tr and RR, implying effective head dissipation and thermal homeostasis (Samara et al., 2013). This highlights that factors beyond pigmentation —such as morphological hair traits—play a key role in thermoregulation in this animal species.

Comparable findings in other species inhabiting arid and semiarid areas underscore the importance of coat structure. In black goats, darker coats increased heat gain but enhanced evaporative cooling (Finch et al., 1980), challenging the assumption that dark coats are disadvantageous. Similarly, in red kangaroos, coat color effects on solar heat load are balanced by structural adaptations in fibers density (Dawson & Maloney, 2024). Notably, studies have shown that white fibers (from cashmere goats and South American camelids)often display higher medullation (McGregor, 2016; Mueller et al., 2013), influencing insulation and heat dissipation. For sheep managed in hot, semiarid environments, homeothermy is maintained regardless of the coat color (Leite et al., 2020,; 2018). More recently, research in Angus cattle reported that thermotolerance was more strongly associated with hair coat characteristics—such as fiber length, fineness, and smoothness—than with coat color itself (Farias et al., 2024).These findings emphasize the need to examine coat structure alongside pigmentation to fully understand thermal adaptation.

Assuming that the ancestral dromedary coat was light brown, both natural and artificial selection may have favored dark and white variants through distinct adaptive routes, maintaining physiological efficiency while promoting structural differentiation. Consequently, coat color diversity in dromedary camels may reflect adaptive plasticity to variable desert environments. It is plausible that white camels, like their dark-coated counterparts, exhibit specialized hair fiber structures that facilitate thermoregulation (Almathen et al., 2018).

Recent thermographic research conducted on a comparable population of dromedary camels—encompassing most of the individuals included in the present study—showed that coat color did not significantly affect body surface temperature during physical exercise (Iglesias Pastrana et al., 2023). However, that study also reported that sex, castration status, age, and iris pigmentation significantly influenced the thermo-physiological response to exercise. These findings indicate that while thermal stability is maintained across color morphs, multiple biological and morphological factors interact to shape the camel’s thermoregulatory capacity. In this context, intrinsic coat properties—particularly the structure of the hair medulla—are likely to play a central role, interacting with coat color, body region, and demographic traits to modulate heat exchange.

Based on these premises, the present study primarily aims to unravel the adaptive paradox underlying the coexistence of dark- and light-colored (white) dromedary camels in hot desert environments, by examining whether structural differences in their hair fibers —particularly in the degree and type of medullation— could explain how both pigmentation extremes contribute to thermoregulatory efficiency. Furthermore, we also explore the potential adaptive influence of other animal-dependent factors such as sex, age, and body region, to determine whether they may likewise be reflected in the inner architecture of the hair shaft. Through this integrative perspective, we seek to advance understanding of how multiple traits interact to shape the adaptive flexibility of the dromedary coat under hot desert conditions..

Material and methods

Sample acquisition and animal population characteristics

Hair fiber samples were collected from a total of 138 dromedary camels (Camelus dromedarius) pertaining to the ‘Canarian Camel’ breed and reared in four different farms in Spain. The ‘Canarian Camel’ breed is known to exhibit a wide variety of coat color phenotypes, with a predominant abundance of light-brown and reddish colors (Fernández de Sierra & Fabelo Marrero, 2017). Of the 138 camels analyzed for fiber medullation, 130 were adult individuals previously evaluated for thermographic responses during physical exercise in a related study (Iglesias Pastrana et al., 2023). The remaining eight animals were younger than one year of age and were intentionally included to provide a meaningful age contrast and explore whether fiber medullation patterns might differ in younger individuals. Thermographic data were not available for these juvenile animals.

The study population consisted of 77 males and 61 females, with ages ranging from 3 months to 35 years old. Animals were categorized into eight groups based on coat color: 26 bay camels, 4 black camels, 2 ashed camels, 15 roan camels, 5 white camels, 16 blonde camels, 33 cinnamon camels, and 37 chestnut camels. Bay camels are characterized by a cinnamon brown or brown-reddish coat, with darker intensities at the withers, hump, and limbs. Ashed camels show a combination of dark and light hairs distributed throughout the body. Roan camels present a cinnamon brown or brown-reddish coat with scattered white hairs. Blonde camels possess a light cinnamon coat, with a more subdued tone at the limbs. Cinnamon camels exhibit a homogeneous cinnamon brown coat. Chestnut camels are distinguished by a uniform brown-reddish coat (Iglesias Pastrana et al., 2024).

For descriptive purposes, the eight color phenotypes were also grouped into two general categories: dark-colored camels (bay, black, ashed, roan, chestnut, and cinnamon) and light-colored camels (white and blonde). This grouping was not used in statistical analyses but serves to facilitate comparison and interpretation in the Discussion.

Hair samples were procured through shearing during the camel's annual molting period, a process lasting six to eight weeks from late spring (Babu, 2015). Five specific body regions were targeted for sampling: shoulder, hump, belly, rump, and tail. These locations were prioritized due to their increased hair density, ensuring sufficient sample volume (Bhakat, 2019). However, in some animals, hair collection from certain body regions was not possible due to the absence of hair in those areas. The sampled camels were raised in a semi-extensive environment and fed a standardized diet.

For each animal, a minimum of 1000 hair fibers were collected, considering the total sum from the five selected body regions. The results from the analysis of the five body regions were treated as a pooled dataset for each individual, as the objective of the study was to assess fiber medullation at the whole-animal level rather than by specific body region. No invasive procedures that could harm the animals were performed, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines for the care and use of farm animals.

Hair fiber medullation analysis

Camel hair samples were analyzed using the S-Fiber Med (after ‘Smart Fiber Medullometer’). Sample preparation was conducted according to the procedure described by E. Quispe et al. (2023). Initially, fiber fragments measuring 0.2 to 0.4 mm in length were obtained using a Hardy microtome and a thin blade. Subsequently, the fragments were placed in the center of a microscope slide, and a drop of immersion oil (refractive index = 1.50) was added. The fiber fragments were then dispersed within a defined area using a slide retainer accessory. A coverslip was carefully placed over the sample, avoiding the formation of air bubbles, thus completing the sample preparation. For sample evaluation, the slide was placed in the slide holder of the S-Fiber Med system, and the device was activated (M. Quispe et al., 2023).

This method facilitated the determination of mean fiber diameter (µm), its standard deviation, percentage of non-medullated fibers (fibers lacking a central medulla) and percentage of medullated fibers (fibers with central medulla). Medullated fibers are further categorized into five subtypes: percentage of fibers with fragmented medulla (medulla exhibiting discontinuous segments), percentage of fibers with uncontinuous medulla (medulla with irregular interruptions), percentage of fibers with continuous medulla (uniform, uninterrupted medulla), and percentage of strongly medullated fibers (dense medulla occupying a significant portion of the fiber diameter) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Example of automated recognition and classification of camel hair fiber medullation using the Smart Fiber Medullometer. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the following variables by coat color, sex, age group and body region: percentage of non-medullated fibers (p_NoMed), percentage of medullated fibers (p_Med), percentage of fibers with fragmented medulla (p_Frag_Med), percentage of fibers with uncontinuous medulla (p_Uncont_Med), percentage of fibers with continuous medulla (p_Cont_Med), and percentage of strongly medullated fibers (p_Strong_Med). These variables will be referred to by their abbreviations henceforth. The normality of these variables was assessed within each coat color group using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The data did not meet normality assumptions (p < 0.05),

For the first specific objective, which aimed to describe and compare medullation patterns among coat colors, non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests (α=0.05) were initially performed to assess differences among coat colors. Additionally, violin plots with overlaid boxplots were generated for the variables p_Med and p_NoMed, while stacked bar plots were used to represent the relative composition of fiber types (p_NoMed, p_Frag_Med, p_Uncont_Med, p_Cont_Med and p_Strong_Med) across coat color groups. Each bar scaled to 100 % to represent the percentage composition of fiber types. p_NoMed was displayed in black, while shades of brown were used for other fiber types.

To further evaluate the influence of additional factors (sex, age, and body region) and to account for potential data imbalance due to missing regional samples, generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were subsequently fitted for the second specific objective. The general structure of the models was:g(μijkl)=β0+β1(CoatColor)i+β2(Sex)j+β3(AgeGroup)k+β4(BodyRegion)1+(1AnimalID),where g(μijkl) is the logit link function, β0 is the intercept, β1–β4 are the fixed effects for coat color, sex, age group, and body region, and (1∣AnimalID) represents the random intercept accounting for repeated measures within individuals.

For the response variables p_Med, p_NoMed, p_Frag_Med, and p_Uncont_Med, models were fitted using the beta distribution with a logit link. These datasets contained no boundary proportions (0 or 1), convergence was achieved without instability, and residual diagnostics confirmed an adequate model fit. Therefore, comparison with the beta-binomial family was not required, since no overdispersion or zero/one inflation was detected and the beta distribution appropriately described the data.

For the remaining response variables —p_Cont_Med and p_Strong_Med— both beta and beta-binomial families were compared, as the data occasionally included boundary proportions (0 or 1). The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was used to select the best-fitting model. The beta models, which exhibited markedly lower (more negative) AIC values than the beta-binomial alternatives, were retained as the most parsimonious and best-fitting approach.

Coat color, sex, age group, and body region were included as fixed effects, and Animal ID was incorporated as a random intercept to model within-animal clustering across body regions (one observation per region, with some regions missing for certain individuals). The variable age was categorized into three groups —younger than 12 months, between 12 and 36 months, and older than 36 months— to facilitate interpretation and assess potential developmental effects.

All GLMMs were fitted using the glmmTMB package (Brooks et al., 2017). Model adequacy was assessed with the DHARMa package (Hartig, 2016), evaluating residual uniformity and dispersion. Type III Wald χ² tests were applied to assess the significance of fixed effects (car package), and estimated marginal means (EMMs) for coat color were computed with the emmeans package (Lenth, 2023). Diagnostic residual plots (DHARMa) and EMM plots were used to evaluate model assumptions and visualize predicted means by coat color.

All analyses were conducted in RStudio (v.4.3.0). Data wrangling and visualization were performed using dplyr (Wickham, 2015) and ggplot2 (Gómez-Rubio, 2017).

Marked sex differences were observed in fiber structure. Males showed substantially higher median percentages of medullated (p_Med) and strongly medullated fibers (p_Strong_Med), together with lower proportions of non-medullated fibers (p_NoMed) compared to females. In contrast, females exhibited a greater share of non-medullated and fragmented medulla fibers, suggesting finer and less structurally robust coats.

Fiber structure also varied markedly with age. Young calves (<12 months) displayed the highest proportions of fragmented and continuous medulla fibers, while adults (>36 months) showed increased strongly medullated and overall medullated fibers. Intermediate values were found in juveniles (12–36 months), which had the most homogeneous medullation profiles (lowest IQRs).

Clear regional trends were also evident. The tail region showed the highest medullation percentages (median p_Med ≈ 94 %) and a predominance of strongly medullated fibers, while the shoulder and hump exhibited intermediate values. In contrast, the belly and rump had lower medullation and higher proportions of non-medullated fibers, consistent with thinner and softer hair in ventral areas.

Both p_Med and p_NoMed showed highly significant effects of coat color (p < 0.001), sex (p < 10⁻¹⁹), and body region (p < 10⁻¹¹). This indicates that the overall degree of medullation, as well as the presence of non-medullated fibers, varies consistently across pigmentation types, between sexes, and among anatomical regions. In contrast, age did not significantly affect these variables, suggesting that medullation patterns become established early in life and remain relatively stable thereafter.

For the more specific fiber categories, fragmented (p_Frag_Med) and uncontinuous medulla (p_Uncont_Med) fibers were significantly influenced by sex, age, and body region but not by coat color, implying that these intermediate medullation forms are more sensitive to intrinsic biological variation than to pigmentation.

In the case of continuous (p_Cont_Med) and strongly medullated fibers (p_Strong_Med), all four fixed effects were significant, though with different magnitudes. Coat color and body region consistently showed the strongest statistical associations (p < 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻²⁰), reinforcing the idea that both pigmentation and local micro-environmental exposure contribute to structural differentiation within the hair shaft. Sex also exerted a robust effect, with males showing greater medullation intensity, whereas age displayed moderate but significant influences (p ≈ 0.001–0.01), indicating a gradual structural reinforcement of the medulla with maturity.

Residual diagnostics using the DHARMa package confirmed adequate model performance, with generally uniform residual distributions and no evidence of major overdispersion or influential outliers. Minor deviations detected in some models were within acceptable limits for beta-distributed data (see Supplementary Figure S3a-f). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) derived from the GLMMs corroborated the patterns described above: darker and white-coated camels exhibited higher predicted medullation values, while intermediate and light phenotypes showed lower proportions. Similarly, males and dorsal body regions (hump and tail) displayed higher predicted medullation percentages than females and ventral areas. In contrast, age effects were weak or variable, influencing only specific medullation categories. Overall, these model-based predictions confirm that coat color, sex, age, and body region jointly modulate the inner structure of camel hair fibers, though with differing magnitudes of effect across traits. The EMM plots summarizing these patterns are presented in Supplementary Figure S4a-f, highlighting the multifactorial nature of hair structural adaptation in dromedaries.

Discussion

Building upon the established knowledge that camel fibers are inherently medullated (Liu et al., 2018), this study provides new evidence that variation in the inner structure of the coat relates to pigmentation and may underlie thermoregulatory adaptation in dromedary camels. The coexistence of dark and white phenotypes in the same hot-desert environments represents a long-standing adaptive paradox, as darker coats absorb more radiation while lighter coats reflect it. Our results suggest that structural variation at the fiber level compensates for these opposite pigmentation effects, allowing both color morphs to maintain efficient thermal homeostasis.

The analyses confirmed marked differences in fiber morphology among coat color groups. Darker coats—and to some extent white coats—were associated with thicker fibers and higher medullation percentages, while lighter colors such as ashed and blonde exhibited lower values. These findings indicate that fiber structure contributes to regulating heat transfer through the coat, functioning as an adaptive adjustment rather than a neutral phenotypic trait. Thicker, more medullated fibers may enhance convective and radiative heat dissipation, while thinner, less medullated ones might improve insulation. The interplay between pigmentation and inner fiber architecture thus appears to form a compensatory system optimizing heat management under intense solar exposure. Beyond the observed color-related variation, previous research on camel fiber traits has described substantial variability in medullation, but studies have yet explored its relationship with coat pigmentation. Studies on Sudanese, North African and Arabian dromedaries have reported wide variation in medullation percentages across breeds and sexes (Agamy et al., 2024; Akbar et al., 2024; Helal et al., 2015; Singh & Patni, 2004), while Sharma and Pant (2013) found markedly lower medullation levels in Indian Bikaneri dromedaries. Such inter-breed and regional variation indicates a strong genetic basis underlying differences in fiber structure. At the same time, interspecific comparisons highlight the role of environmental pressures in shaping medullation patterns. Tunisian Sahara dromedaries display a predominance of continuously medullated fibers (Kuźnicka & Grondkowska, 2014), whereas dromedaries and Bactrian camels raised under colder Polish conditions exhibit a much higher proportion of non-medullated fibers (Zabek et al., 2022). These contrasting patterns likely reflect opposing thermal regimes—where high medullation facilitates air circulation and evaporative cooling in hot deserts, while low medullation enhances insulation in cold climates. This variability demonstrates that both genetic background and environmental exposure interact to shape fiber morphology, emphasizing that medullation is a plastic trait primarily driven by thermoregulatory demands rather than phylogeny alone.

In our study, the predominance of medullated fibers in dark phenotypes supports the hypothesis that a robust medullary structure facilitates air movement within the coat, enhancing convective cooling. Interestingly, white camels also showed high proportions of medullated fibers, which may help maintain insulation despite their reflective pigmentation. Conversely, the lower frequency of medullated fibers in ashed individuals suggests a more intermediate form of adaptation, possibly associated with partial pigmentation and mixed structural demands. Although this structural pattern could indicate greater thermal heterogeneity within the coat, previous thermographic assessments conducted on the same camel population reported no significant differences in surface temperature among coat colors (Iglesias Pastrana et al., 2023). However, the absence of significant differences in uncontinuous and fragmented medullation across colors suggests that this fiber subtype plays a limited adaptive role in dromedaries. Discontinuous medulla has been associated with reduced outward heat flow through the hair coat (Guirgis et al., 2005), but such an effect would be disadvantageous for camels, which rely on adaptive heterothermy to modulate body temperature in response to extreme thermal conditions (Tibary & El Allali, 2020). Thus, fibers showing irregular interruption in the medulla may have limited evolutionary relevance in camels, as efficient thermoregulation in this species depends on maintaining dynamic heat exchange rather than insulation alone. These findings collectively reinforce the idea that pigmentation and fiber microstructure operate as complementary adaptive strategies rather than independent traits in dromedary camels. Comparable structural compensations between color and hair architecture have been reported in goats, cattle, and sheep, where medullation levels modulate heat flux and surface temperature independently of coat color (Acharya et al., 1995; Finch et al., 1980; Leite et al., 2020; Maia et al., 2003; McManus et al., 2011).GLMM analyses confirmed that coat color, sex, age, and body region significantly affected fiber structure, though the strength and direction of these effects varied among response variables. Dark and white phenotypes showed the highest predicted medullation percentages, while intermediate colors had lower values. Sex also emerged as a strong determinant, with males exhibiting coarser fibers and higher degrees of medullation than females. These findings agree with previous studies suggesting that male camels have thicker, rougher coats, potentially related to sexual dimorphism in hormone levels and energy output, and consequently in coat function and thermoregulatory efficiency (Salehi et al., 2010; Zabek et al., 2022). The predominance of medullated fibers in males may also reflect the fact that male camels are preferentially selected for physically demanding activities such as transport and draught work (Khan et al., 2003), since a thicker medulla could provide both thermal and mechanical protection under such demanding conditions.

Age had a more variable influence, but the observed increase in medullation with maturity suggests structural reinforcement of the hair shaft through development. Young calves (<12 months) displayed finer fibers and higher proportions of fragmented and continuous medulla, while adults showed greater strongly medullated fibers. This progressive pattern likely corresponds to functional maturation of the coat, improving its ability to buffer extreme temperatures and mechanical abrasion over time. Such ontogenic trends are consistent with reports in other mammals showing that medullation increases with age—for example, in Huacaya alpacas the proportion of medullated fibers rises across age classes, while non-medullated fibers decline (Valdez et al., 2024); similar age-related shifts in the distribution of medullated fibers have been documented across the lifetime of Angora goats (McGregor et al., 2013).

Anatomical region exerted one of the strongest and most consistent effects on fiber traits. The tail region exhibited the highest mean fiber diameter and medullation, followed by the shoulder and hump, whereas the belly and rump showed thinner, less medullated fibers. These spatial differences indicate that local exposure to sunlight, wind, and friction influences coat architecture. Dorsal and caudal regions, which experience intense solar radiation and mechanical wear, develop thicker and more medullated fibers that enhance both heat dissipation and physical protection, while ventral regions maintain finer coats to avoid overheating from reflected ground radiation. These findings are in line with the notion that the camel coat functions as a spatially heterogeneous thermoregulation system.

This interpretation is supported by recent work by Taha and Nasr (2025), who reported significant regional differences in fiber structure in Egyptian dromedaries. They found that medullated fibers and medulla index were highest in the neck, breech, and nape, and lowest at the withers. These authors, citing Alibayev et al. (2020), proposed that these patterns represent functional adaptations to environmental and mechanical demands, including local heat load and friction. While their sample size was relatively limited and their medulla quantification method differed from ours, their results converge with our findings, reinforcing that regional variation in medullation reflects site-specific adaptations rather than random structural variability. The spatial heterogeneity observed here thus represents local optimization of coat function for protection, ventilation, and efficient thermal exchange across the camel’s body surface.

By combining descriptive and inferential approaches, our analyses provide quantitative evidence that hair structure in camels results from the integrated effects of multiple factors rather than isolated influences. The GLMM framework confirmed that color, sex, age, and body region interact to modulate the inner structure of the hair fiber, while residual diagnostics (DHARMa) validated the robustness of the models. The EMM-based post-hoc comparisons supported the directionality of these effects, visually confirming that darker and white coats, males, and dorsal regions exhibited higher predicted medullation values. Age effects were comparatively weaker, limited to specific medullation categories such as fragmented and uncontinuous medulla. These outcomes collectively highlight that the structural architecture of the camel’s coat is governed by the combined action of pigmentation, biology, and anatomy, each contributing to maintain thermal homeostasis in hot deserts.

Taken together, the results demonstrate that camel hair microstructure is a multifactorial adaptive system, integrating pigmentation and inner architecture to balance the competing needs of heat absorption and dissipation. The interplay among coat color, sex, age, and body region provides the camel with an exceptional degree of morphological plasticity, allowing individuals of contrasting colors and conditions to achieve comparable thermal outcomes under extreme heat. The present study therefore contributes to resolving the adaptive paradox of the coexistence of dark and white camels in hot deserts, demonstrating that adaptation in this species is not determined by pigmentation alone, but by the fine-tuning of the inner structure of the hair fiber.

Beyond these multifactorial effects, additional research directions emerge from our findings. Although regional and circadian variations in sweating rate and body surface temperature have already been documented in dromedary camels (Abdoun et al., 2012), it would be valuable to complement these observations by exploring potential regional and circadian variations in sweat gland functionality in relation to coat color. Such analyses could clarify whether local differences in fiber structure are coordinated with differential evaporative capacity across the body surface. Furthermore, medullation has been shown to be both phenotypically and genetically associated with body size in other fiber-producing species, such as Angora goats (McGregor et al., 2013); investigating whether similar relationships occur in dromedaries could shed light on the interplay between somatic growth, hair morphology, and thermal adaptation.

Despite the robustness of the dataset and the integrative analytical approach applied, some limitations should be acknowledged. The number of individuals within certain coat color categories was relatively small; however, this uneven group size did not compromise model validity or the detection of significant effects. Additionally, fiber sampling was restricted to a limited number of body regions, and environmental variables such as microclimatic exposure, nutrition, and seasonal effects were not directly measured. Extending the sampling framework to include additional populations and environmental contexts would help verify the consistency of these patterns and to test for possible eco-geographical variation. Future studies integrating controlled environmental data, longitudinal monitoring, and larger sample sizes would help confirm the consistency of these patterns across populations and seasons. At the mechanistic level, combining histological and transcriptomic analyses could clarify the molecular pathways linking pigmentation genes (e.g., ASIP, MC1R) to follicular differentiation and medullation processes.

Conclusions

This study provides new evidence that hair fiber microstructure in dromedary camels varies systematically with coat color and other biological factors. Both dark and white phenotypes exhibited higher degrees of medullation compared with lighter color groups, indicating that variation in fiber inner structure contributes to functional differences in the coat. Males and dorsal regions showed coarser and more medullated fibers than females and ventral areas, whereas age effects were less pronounced. These results suggest that pigmentation, sex, and body region jointly influence the internal organization of the hair shaft, reflecting multiple biological and morphological factors acting together. Although the exact functional implications of these structural differences remain to be determined, our findings provide important evidence to help explain the long-recognized adaptive paradox of the coexistence of dark and white camels in hot deserts.
