Recursive Aperture Decoded Ultrasound Imaging (READI) With Estimated Motion-Compensated Compounding ( E ​ M ​ C 2 EMC^{2} )
Tyler Keith Henry, Darren Dahunsi, Randy Palamar, Negar Majidi, Mohammad Rahim Sobhani, Afshin Kashani Ilkhechi, and Roger Zemp

Abstract
Fast Orthogonal Row-Column Electronic Scanning (FORCES) is a Hadamard-encoded Synthetic Transmit Aperture (STA) imaging sequence using bias-sensitive Top-Orthogonal to Bottom Electrode (TOBE) arrays. It produces images with a higher Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and improved penetration depth compared to traditional STA techniques but suffers from motion sensitivity due to ensemble size and aperture encoding. This work presents Recursive Aperture Decoded Imaging (READI) a novel decoding and beamforming technique for FORCES that produces multiple low-resolution images out of subsets of the FORCES sequence that are less susceptible to motion, but sum to form the complete FORCES image. Estimated Motion-Compensated Compounding (EMC 2 ) describes the process of comparing these low-resolution images to estimate the underlying motion, then warping them to align before coherent compounding. READI with EMC 2 is shown to fully recover images corrupted by probe motion, and restore tissue speckle and sharpness to an image of a beating heart. READI low-resolution images by themselves are demonstrated to be a marked improvement over sparse STA schemes with the same transmit count, and are shown to recover blood speckle at a flow rate of 42 cm/s.



I
Introduction

Synthetic Transmit Aperture (STA) ultrasound is a well-established technique that offers multiple improvements over traditional scan line imaging.
STA
imaging uses small, unfocused transmit apertures that change position between events. Combining these events forms a large effective aperture that can be dynamically focused for all points in the image. Alongside global focusing,
STA
also provides a wider
field-of-view (FOV)
that is independent from transmit count, and decouples frame rate from
Pulse Repetition Frequency (PRF)

[
1
]
. STA imaging is not without its drawbacks, however, as it suffers from lower
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)
, worse penetration, and a high sensitivity to motion artifacts
[
2
]
.

Fast Orthogonal Row-Column Electronic Scanning (FORCES)
is a recently developed
STA
imaging technique that utilizes bias-sensitive
Top-Orthogonal to Bottom Electrode (TOBE)
arrays and Hadamard aperture encoding to achieve the same synthetic aperture as
STA
while transmitting with the complete aperture on each event
[
3
]
. Additionally,
TOBE
arrays enable transmit elevational focusing and steering. Full aperture transmissions and elevational focusing maximize the energy transmitted into the image plane, improving
SNR
and penetration depth.
FORCES
has been shown to provide significant improvements in image quality compared to other high frame rate imaging techniques such as
Virtual Line Source (VLS)
and
Tilted Plane Wave (TPW)
imaging
[
4
]
. Figure
2
provides a visual comparison between classic
STA
imaging and
FORCES
.

As
FORCES
uses aperture encoding, the entire sequence must be completed and the dataset decoded together to form an image. This is undesirable in the presence of motion, as any changes in the imaged region during the transmit sequence will create artifacts in the decoded dataset. This degradation is compounded with the inherent motion sensitivity of
STA
imaging.

This work introduces
Recursive Aperture Decoded Imaging (READI)
, a new decoding and beamforming technique for
FORCES
that produces a lower resolution image from a subset of the
FORCES
sequence. An image formed from a sequential subset of the
FORCES
events will be less affected by the same motion as the full sequence. By separating the
FORCES
dataset into sequential groups of transmit events and performing
READI
beamforming on each, a series of low-resolution images are produced that are less affected by motion artifacts. Coherently summing these low-resolution images forms the full
FORCES
image. Before combining, these
READI
images can be compared with each other to estimate the underlying motion. Using these motion estimates, the images can be warped to align. Combining these aligned images yields a high-resolution
FORCES
image that is also resilient to motion. This method is referred to as
Estimated Motion-Compensated Compounding (EMC
2
)
.

Five experiments were conducted to evaluate the proposed methods. First, a
FORCES

Point Spread Function (PSF)
was simulated using Field II
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
to validate that compounded
READI
images perfectly reproduce the
FORCES
image. Second, a static anechotic cyst phantom was imaged with
READI
and
Ultrafast FORCES (uFORCES)
, another method for reducing
FORCES
transmit counts
[
7
]
. Then, the same phantom was imaged while the probe was in motion to determine if
EMC
2
can recover the static image. Next, a beating heart phantom was imaged in motion and compensated with
EMC
2
. Finally, a flow phantom was imaged to determine if
READI
images can resolve blood flow and speckle.

II
Background

This paper presents an enhancement to the
FORCES
imaging sequence
[
3
]
, which is an extension of classic
STA
imaging using bias-sensitive
TOBE
arrays. Reviewing
TOBE
arrays and
STA
imaging is necessary to understand
FORCES
and
READI
.

II-A

TOBE
Arrays

Top-Orthogonal to Bottom Electrode (TOBE)
arrays are a refinement of row-column arrays that allow for individual element addressing while retaining the same channel count geometry. As with standard
Row-Column Arrays (RCAs)
, they are rectangular, consisting of N rows and M columns of elements, with one channel on each row and column. Unlike traditional
RCAs
, in its base unenergized state, the elements of the TOBE array are not piezoelectric. They are instead either electrostrictive relaxors or
Capacitive Micromachined Ultrasonic Transducers (CMUTs)
. The results presented in this work were collected with electrostrictive relaxor-based
TOBE
arrays.

Electrostrictive materials only behave piezoelectrically when an external electric field is applied across them. The strength of this bias field determines the piezoelectric strength. Critically, reversing the direction of this field also inverts the polarity of any AC signals transmitted or received by the element.

Fig. 1:
Construction and biasing of a TOBE array.

In the
TOBE
array geometry, the rows and columns act as both the AC contacts and DC biases for the elements. Placing a DC bias on one column and transmitting across rows will only trigger the elements under the biased column, while all other elements in the row will remain inert. If multiple columns are biased at once, then transmitting on a row will trigger all active elements, scaled by each element’s bias. On receive, the electric signal will be a linear combination of the responses of all biased elements along the row. Figure
1
highlights the construction and biasing of a
TOBE
array.

TOBE
arrays are capable of all sequences performable on traditional
RCAs
. Additionally, they open a new field of imaging techniques that leverage these bias modulation capabilities. One class of these techniques are Hadamard-encoded sequences, which have been shown to drastically improve
SNR
versus comparable row-column sequences but suffer from motion degradation, which this paper seeks to address.

II-B

Synthetic Transmit Aperture (STA)
Imaging

Modern ultrasound systems can beamform images with 2D receive focusing at all points within the field of view, eliminating the need for the analog dynamic receive delays typically required in traditional scan line imaging. The memory available on these systems allows them to store the complete signal received from every channel, for every transmission in the sequence. Then, for each pixel in the image, a unique set of receive delays is calculated to bring the point into focus. The signals are sampled at these delays and summed to form the pixel. This is known as delay-and-sum beamforming
[
8
]
.

Achieving the same global focus in transmission is more difficult. Delay-and-sum relies on each receive element producing a unique signal that can be delayed independently from other elements; however, the contributions of all transmit elements are combined in any one received signal and are not easily separable. The simplest solution to this dilemma is to fire each transmit element one at a time and record the response on all receive channels. This is the basis of
STA
imaging. Figure
2
A illustrates this transmission sequence on an eight-element linear array.

The
STA
sequence produces
N\times M
unique signals representing all possible combinations of N transmit and M receive elements. This is known as the "multistatic" dataset
[
9
]
and may be defined as a matrix of signals:.

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times M}\\
\\
\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1,1}(t)&\cdots&s_{1,M}(t)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{N,1}(t)&\cdots&s_{N,M}(t)\end{bmatrix}\end{array}

(1)

With
s_{ij}(t)
representing the signal received on element
j
, transmitted by element
i
. This dataset can be beamformed with the
Delay-and-Sum (DAS)
reconstruction algorithm
[
8
]

The delay-and-sum reconstruction on the multistatic dataset
S
(t) is defined as:

\mathbb{D}\{\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t),r\}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}(r)s_{ij}(\tau_{ij}(r))

Where
r\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the location of a point in the imaged region
\Omega
,
N
is the total number of elements with
i
indexing the transmitting element and
j
indexing the receiving element.

The delay function
\tau_{ij}(r)
calculates the total path length from transmitting element
i
, through point
r
, back to receiving element
j
and divides it by the speed of sound in the imaged region
c
. This provides the time that
s_{ij}(t)
should be sampled at for pixel
r
. The definition of
\tau_{ij}(r)
is reliant on the transmission focusing scheme, and is defined for unfocused transmissions as:

\tau_{ij}(r)=\frac{|d_{i}(r)|+|d_{j}(r)|}{c}

d_{n}(r)
calculates the distance between element
n
and point
r
while
a_{j}(r)
applies dynamic receive apodization for element
j
. Applying the reconstruction operator across a region of interest
\Omega
yields a fully focused RF B-mode image.

The methods discussed in the rest of this paper are applied in parallel to all receive elements; for simplicity, this work will only analyze the case of a single receive element (
M=1
) and disregard the receive apodization
a_{j}(r)
. The multistatic
DAS
reconstruction is then expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}\{\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t),r\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}s_{i}(\tau_{i}(r))

(2)

\displaystyle\tau_{i}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{|d_{i}(r)|}{c}

In summary,
DAS
:

1.

Associates each signal
s_{i,j}(t)
with a unique transmit element
i
and receive element
j
.

2.

Calculates the round trip delay

\tau_{i,j}(r)

for the signal from transmit, to the pixel location, back to the receive element.

3.

Samples the signal at the calculated delay time and adds it to the pixel value.

4.

Repeats for all signals in the multistatic dataset.

Figure
2
A demonstrates this method on an 8-element linear array. In each transmission, only a single element is excited, transmitting an unfocused, diverging wave into the volume. Then all elements are listened to in receive and their
N
signals form the
i
’th row of the multistatic dataset
s
.

A significant drawback to this method is that for any given transmission, only a small part of the total array is utilized. This drastically reduces the amount of energy transmitted into the volume and subsequently lowers
SNR
.

Fig. 2:
Standard walking transmit aperture sequence (A) and the
FORCES
sequence (B). After Hadamard decoding, the
FORCES
dataset has the same transmit geometry as the
STA
sequence.

II-C

Fast Orthogonal Row-Column Electronic Scanning (FORCES)
Imaging

FORCES
imaging utilizes bias-sensitive
TOBE
arrays and Hadamard encoding to achieve the same
STA
transmit geometry after decoding, while physically transmitting using the entire array on each event. If an array has
N
transmitting elements, the
FORCES
sequence consists of
N
transmission events, the same as
STA
. Unlike
STA
,
FORCES
utilizes the full aperture on every transmit event. This increases the total energy transmitted per pulse by a factor of
N
, improving the signal-to-noise ratio by
\sqrt{N}

[
3
]
.

II-C
1
Hadamard Matrices

A Hadamard matrix is defined as a square matrix consisting of only +1 and -1 elements, with the property that all rows and columns are mutually orthogonal. Additionally, their transpose is a scaled version of their inverse:

\displaystyle\bm{H}\bm{H}^{T}

\displaystyle=n\bm{I}

(3)

\displaystyle\bm{H}^{T}=

\displaystyle n\bm{H}^{-1}

Where
n
is the rank of the matrix. The first and simplest method of constructing Hadamard matrices is a recursive method given by Sylvester
[
10
]
, valid for all powers of two:

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{2}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}+1,+1\\
+1,-1\end{pmatrix}

(4)

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{2^{a+b}}=\bm{H}_{2^{a}}

\displaystyle\otimes\bm{H}_{2^{b}},\ a,b\in\mathbb{N}

Note this construction always produces a symmetric matrix where
\bm{H}=\bm{H}^{T}
. It is an open conjecture that a Hadamard matrix exists for all multiples of four, while the construction in equation
4
limits us to powers of two.

II-C
2
FORCES
Sequence

An example of the
FORCES
sequence is provided in figure
2
B. By biasing the rows of a
TOBE
array with a row of the Hadamard matrix and then transmitting across a column, the generated pressure wave will be a linear combination of all transmitting
elements

i
. As the transmission
events
are now decoupled from the transmitting
elements
,
e
is defined as the transmit
event
index. Thus, the received data
g_{e}(t)
from event
e
is a linear combination of the multistatic dataset (eq
1
).

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t)=\bm{H}_{N}\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t)\\
\vskip 4.30554pt\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t)=\bm{H}_{N}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t)\\
\vskip 4.30554pt\mathrm{g}_{e}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h^{(N)}_{ei}s_{i}(t)\end{array}

(5)

In this equation, the Hadamard matrix
H_{N}
can be thought of as a mapping between the transmission events
e
and the individual transmitting elements
i
. Thus, after decoding the
FORCES
dataset
G
(t) into the multistatic dataset
S
(t), the B-mode image can be rendered via delay-and-sum identically to a
STA
sequence. Combining the decode step with the multistatic reconstruction operator
\mathbb{D}_{N}
(equation
2
) forms the
FORCES
reconstruction equation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}=\mathbb{D}\{\bm{H}_{N}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}\\
\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{\sum_{e=1}^{N}\hat{h}^{(N)}_{ie}g_{e}\}(\tau_{i}(r))\end{split}

(6)

FORCES
has been shown to provide significant improvements in image quality compared to
STA
,
VLS
, and
TPW
transmission sequences.
[
4
]
. This is because, unlike the
VLS
and
TPW
sequences,
FORCES
achieves transmit and receive focusing everywhere in the scan plane, while
VLS
and
TPW
provide only one-way transmit or receive focusing.

Another enhancement
FORCES
provides is elevational transmit focusing. As the transmission orientation is orthogonal to the receive direction, a delay profile can be applied across the transmitting rows. This delay results in an elevationally focused, but azimuthally unfocused, transmit. This transmission focusing is visible in figure
2
B. The additional focusing minimizes the amount of energy lost out of plane, boosting
SNR
and penetration depth. As
FORCES
produces 2D images with no elevational component, this focusing does not affect the beamforming algorithm.

II-C
3
FORCES Motion Challenges

A significant challenge faced by
FORCES
and other Hadamard-encoded aperture sequences is motion. The
FORCES
reconstruction equation (
6
) assumes that after decoding, the exact multistatic dataset is recovered. However, as the state of the volume changes, the "true" multistatic dataset changes as well. This means that the
S
(t) obtained from equation
5
is actually a time-averaged version of
S
(t) across the N transmit events. Any motion during the transmit events will create artifacts in this dataset, degrading image quality.

III
Methods

III-A

READI
Grouping

The goal of
READI
is to reduce the effect of motion blurring in
FORCES
images by beamforming subsets of the
FORCES
ensemble taken over smaller periods.

Due to the recursive Sylvester definition, the rank
N
Hadamard matrix can be expressed as a Kronecker product of rank
S
and
Q
matrices, provided
S
and
Q
are both powers of two
[
10
]
. From the Kronecker product definition:

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{N}=\bm{B}_{S}\otimes\bm{C}_{Q},\quad\bm{A}_{N}^{-1}=\bm{B}_{S}^{-1}\otimes\bm{C}_{Q}^{-1}

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{(v+Q(l-1)),(s+Q(q-1))}=\bm{B}_{l,s}\bm{C}_{v,q}

\displaystyle N=QS

The
N

FORCES
transmit events are divided sequentially into
S
groups of
Q
. These groupings are applied to the
FORCES
reconstruction equation (
6
) by re-indexing the transmitting
element

i
and transmit
event

e
:

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=v+Q(l-1)

(7)

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=q+Q(s-1)

Where
l
and
s
are the transmit element and event group numbers, while
v
and
q
are the element’s and event’s indices within their group.

Applying this reshape to the Hadamard definition (equation
4
) allows each entry of
\bm{H}_{N}
to be expressed as the product of an entry in
\bm{H}_{S}
and an entry in
\bm{H}_{Q}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{H}_{N}=\bm{H}_{S}\otimes\bm{H}_{Q}\\
h^{(N)}_{ie}=h^{(N)}_{(v+Q(l-1)),(q+Q(s-1))}=h^{(S)}_{s,l}h^{(Q)}_{q,v}\end{array}

(8)

Thus
FORCES
decoding (equation
5
) can be thought of as a linear two-step process. Partially decode each group with itself using
\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}
, then decode the groups together with
\bm{H}_{S}^{-1}
.

The encoded data matrix is also reshaped to reflect this grouping:

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}\to\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times S}\\
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t):=\mathrm{col}_{s}\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t)\ (s=1,\ldots,S)\end{array}

(9)

Now each column
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
represents the data collected from the
s
’th group of
Q
transmit
events
.

Implementation

All beamforming and motion compensation is performed in a custom C++ Nvidia

application
[
11

. All data was collected on a Verasonics Vantage 256 system with custom DC biasing electronics
[
4
]
and transferred to the CUDA application for processing.

The
CUDA

CUDA

CUDA Basic Linear Algebra Subroutine (cuBLAS)

[
12
]
,
CUDA Fast Fourier Transform (cuFFT)

[
13

, and

[

Fast Fourier Transforms (FFTs)
, and image
Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC)

III-B
1
READI
Beamforming

The
READI
beamforming process performs the following steps:

1.

Select
S
and
Q
such that
N=SQ
, break the data into
S
sequential groups of
Q
transmissions

2.

Partially decode each group (
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
) with
\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}
using
cublasSgemm
matrix multiplication

3.

Hilbert transform the partially decoded data to obtain the RF analytic signals using
cuFFT

[
15
]

(a)

Take the real to complex forward
FFT
of the signal with
cufftExecR2C

(b)

Zero all negative frequency bins and double all positive frequency bins (custom kernel)

(c)

Take the complex to complex inverse
FFT
of the modified spectrum (
cufftExecC2C
)

4.

Apply the
READI
reconstruction operator (eq.
11b
) to each partially decoded group:

(a)

Beamform the group
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
into
S
sub-images, together containing all in-focus terms plus extra cross terms.

(b)

Create a sum of these sub-images weighted by the
s
’th row of
\bm{H}_{S}^{-1}
to form the low-resolution
READI
image

5.

Sum the low-resolution
READI
images from all groups to form the final
FORCES
image

Both the low-resolution
READI
images and the full
FORCES
image are stored in their complex RF form for further processing.

Optionally, each pixel is weighted by its coherence factor to improve
SNR

[
16
]
. This is a method for estimating the coherence between each sample
S_{k}
forming the pixel. It is defined as:

\mathrm{CF}=\frac{|\sum S_{k}|^{2}}{\sum|S_{k}|^{2}}

Coherence Factor Weighting (CFW)
is a non-linear operation, thus applying it to the
READI
low-resolution images before compounding will produce a slightly different result than applying it while beamforming the
FORCES
image directly. As such, it is disabled when appropriate in comparisons.

III-B
2
Motion Compensation

Motion compensation on the
READI
images is performed using a CUDA implementation of
Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC)
block matching
[
17
]
utilizing the CUDA
NPP
image processing library. One of the
S
images is selected as the reference, and all other images (targets) are compared with it. Motion is estimated on a down-sampled grid with 4-16 pixel spacing to reduce computations; the motion grid is interpolated back to image resolution before warping.

At each location in the motion grid, a template patch is extracted from the reference image. The reference patch is slid over a larger region in the target image, and the
NCC
is calculated for each position.
NCC
produces a similarity map of values in the range [-1,1], with 1 indicating perfect correlation. The peak of this map is the location in the target image that best matches the reference patch. The motion value for this patch is the vector between the peak location and the center of the reference patch. This process is illustrated in figure
4
.

Fig. 4:
Example
EMC^{2}
motion compensation process. The first
READI
image is taken as the reference and not warped.

The correlation map for each reference patch is calculated with the
NPP
library’s
nppiCrossCorrValid_NormLevel
method. The peak of each similarity map is found using a custom CUDA kernel; the sub-pixel peak location is obtained by fitting a 2D paraboloid to a 5x5 region around the peak value
[
18
]
.

While
NCC
is robust to changes in local brightness and contrast
[
19
]
, it is still susceptible to noise corrupting the comparisons. To reject spurious motion vectors, three criteria are applied to the
NCC
surface around the detected peak:

•

The peak value must exceed a predefined absolute threshold (reject guessing in noisy regions with weak correlation).

•

The peak must also be a set percentage above the non-motion value (reject motion if the match is only slightly stronger than the non-motion).

•

The curvature of the fitted paraboloid must exceed a minimum (reject flat surfaces where exact motion is hard to guess).

–

The smallest eigenvalue of the paraboloid’s Hessian matrix is considered its curvature for this test
[
18
]
.

Table
I
lists the parameters used for motion detection and their ranges. These parameters were manually tuned for each experiment to produce the optimal quality image.

After this process, each down-sampled motion field is interpolated up to the image’s resolution and used to warp its source image to match the reference. Summing these warped images produces a full
FORCES
image with drastically reduced motion artifacts.

IV-E

Flow Phantom

As a final proof of concept, a 6 mm diameter blood vessel phantom was imaged with an 8 MHz 128x128 element
TOBE
array at a
PRF
of 4 kHz. Blood-mimicking fluid was pumped through the phantom at a rate of 42 cm/s. At these flow rates, a coherent blood speckle cannot be formed, making the vessel appear empty.
READI
was performed with sixteen groups of eight transmits (
Q=8
,
S=16
) to improve blood speckle coherence.

6 mm diameter blood vessel phantom imaged with READI using 16 groups of 8 transmits. Top: Standard FORCES Image, Middle: Unfiltered READI low-resolution image, Bottom: The same READI image post-clutter filtering.

The higher transmission frequency,
PRF
, and
READI
group counts combine to create noisier low-resolution images than the previous example, with significant
READI
artifacts present. To remove these artifacts, an ensemble of sixteen
FORCES
images was taken in sequence, then each beamformed with
READI
to form 256 total sequential low-resolution images. These images were cluttered filtered using basic
Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

[
21
]
, but the largest tissue singular value was kept. This produced an ensemble of images where both the static vessel structure and blood speckle are clearly visible. Figure
13
compares the
FORCES
image with a single

image pre and post clutter filter.

Fig. 14:
Four sequential READI low-resolution images where blood speckle can be tracked. An ensemble of three speckle grains are tracked across the four frames.

After filtering, the blood’s speckle is clearly visible flowing through the vessel, and a parabolic flow profile can be observed directly. Figure
14
tracks three speckle grains across four images. The grains’ motion is easily followed between frames, and their speed differences due to parabolic flow are evident. Successfully recovering this speckle opens the door for
READI
to be applied to high-speed flow estimation techniques.

V
Discussion

V-A

Result Analysis

These results demonstrate the effectiveness of
READI
with
EMC
2
in forming high-resolution images from motion-corrupted datasets. The
gCNR
statistics from the probe motion experiment (figure
10
) show that
EMC
2
can recover image quality comparable to a static
FORCES
image.

Comparing the
EMC
2
compensated cyst image (figure
10
) with the static
FORCES
image (figure
8
), the leftmost column of large cysts is outside the compensated image’s
FOV
. While
EMC
2
successfully resolves structures in this
FOV
, it does not return the
FOV
to the width of the static case. Decreasing the number of transmissions per group to
Q=8
and adjusting the receive apodization both failed to recover this lost information. This indicates that
READI
can easily recover information that remains under the
FOV
for the entire ensemble, but significant bulk motion can result in lost information at the edges of the image.

The cyst phantom low-resolution images (figure
11
) illustrate that while
READI
introduces brightness artifacts, each image still contains the same structural information as the full
FORCES
image. In general, earlier groups in the ensemble tend to have weaker artifacts, and smaller
READI
group sizes
Q
produce worse artifacts. This is in line with equation
B.1
, as group count
S
increases, so do the number of terms mixed in each partially decoded signal. Also, later groups are encoded with later rows in
\bm{H}_{S}
, which tend to have more complex patterns than earlier rows.

The beating heart experiment posed a more substantial challenge to the proposed methods, as it involved more complex motion at different speeds and locations. This challenge required finer tuning of the motion compensation parameters. On the top wall of the heart (figure
12
), where
SNR
is higher, the estimator easily detects the coherent motion and recovers the speckle pattern. On the bottom wall, the output of the
NCC
estimator becomes noisier, making it increasingly difficult to determine the correct peak in the motion map. This leads to regions that experience motion being rejected by the
NCC
estimators’ threshold and sharpness conditions. In these regions the speckle retains some motion blur. These issues may be mitigated by implementing more robust motion detection algorithms, such as biased block matching using priors
[
22
]
or optical flow-based methods that require local continuity of motion
[
23
]
.

EMC
2
’s success in recovering tissue speckle and margins with this simple motion detection algorithm proves it to be a viable candidate for heart flow characterization tasks, where a precise knowledge of tissue boundaries is required for an accurate flow estimation.

The flow phantom results confirm that
READI
beamforming can handle more than simple tissue motion, and reveal a potential method for removing
READI
artifacts. Applying basic
SVD
clutter filtering to the ensemble of 256 low-resolution images successfully removed the
READI
artifacts and reduced general noise, leaving only the blood and tissue speckle. Testing revealed that the majority of
READI
artifacts are mapped to singular values lying between the static tissue and flow bands
[
24
]
. This creates the possibility of filtering out these artifacts while preserving both flow and static tissue. The filtered image in figure
13
was produced by keeping the first singular value out of 256, along with the values 30-80 corresponding to the flow, resulting in an image with both blood and tissue speckle clearly visible. 42 cm/s flow meets or exceeds peak flow velocities in almost all veins, and is comfortably in the range of average large arterial flow rates
[
25
]
.

Future Work

There are several possible directions for future work on
READI
based on these results. This paper applies
READI
to 2D
FORCES

Hadamard Encoded Row Column Ultrasonic Expansive Scanning (HERCULES)
is a recently presented 3D Hadamard encoded sequence on
TOBE
arrays
[
26
]
, that encodes the receive elements orthogonal to the transmission. Rather than generating a 2D image with global transmit and receive focusing, it produces a 3D volume with global receive focusing in all directions. This technique suffers from the same motion sensitivity as
FORCES
, and could benefit from
READI
and
EMC
2
to reduce motion artifacts.

The success of the flow phantom experiment suggests that flow imaging techniques could be applied to
READI
images. The example is already close to a power Doppler image after clutter filtering, and the clear speckle flow visible in figure
14
suggests that more robust colour and vector flow techniques should be applicable. This is significant in that a single transmit sequence could produce both a high-quality B-mode image from the unprocessed
FORCES
data, and a detailed flow map using
READI
. Both these images would be perfectly co-registered in time without needing to interleave separate B-mode and Doppler sequences.

Another field of imaging involving motion is shear-wave elastography. This technique induces a shear wave in tissue and tracks its propagation via high-framerate B-mode imaging
[
27
]
. Shear waves travel and attenuate rapidly in soft tissue, requiring the imaging method to have a high framerate while still producing detailed enough images to track the subtle deformations as the shear wave propagates. The comparison of
READI
images with
uFORCES
images of the same transmit count suggests that
READI
may be ideal for this application, as they maintain high resolution over a large field of view at every transmit count. Shear-wave elastography has previously been implemented in 3D with row-column arrays
[
28
]
, so it is a good next step for
READI
on
TOBE
arrays.

VI
Conclusion

This work presents
Recursive Aperture Decoded Imaging (READI)
with
Estimated Motion-Compensated Compounding (EMC
2
)
, a new method for beamforming the
FORCES
Hadamard encoded sequence that reduces motion artifacts while maintaining high spatial resolution. The intermediate
READI
low-resolution images are shown to be a marked improvement over sparse transmit
uFORCES
images with the same transmission count.
READI
with
EMC
2
successfully removed blur due to probe motion, reproducing an image of comparable quality to standard
FORCES
with a static probe. With a beating heart phantom,
READI

EMC
2
removed speckle blur and sharpened the edges of moving tissue regions, showing its potential for tissue motion canceling and region segmentation.
READI
also successfully recovered blood speckle in a vessel phantom with a flow rate of 42 cm/s, demonstrating its potential for high-resolution flow imaging in large arteries and veins.
Multiple avenues for future work on
READI
have been identified, including 3D volumetric imaging, vector flow characterization, and shear-wave elastography. These applications will be explored further in future work.

Appendix A
READI Derivation

This section provides a detailed derivation of the
READI
reconstruction operator (eq.
11
) from the base
FORCES
reconstruction operator (eq.
6
):

Starting point:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}=\mathbb{D}\{\bm{H}_{N}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}

Goal:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}=\sum_{l}^{S}\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{l}\{\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t),r\}=\sum_{s}^{S}\mathbb{R}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}

In this work,
\bm{H}_{N}
denotes a Hadamard matrix of rank
N
, and the following notation is used to reference individual elements of the matrix:

\bm{H}_{N}=[h^{(N)}_{ij}],\ \bm{H}_{N}^{-1}=[\hat{h}^{(N)}_{ij}]

To review, the
FORCES
dataset of
N
encoded transmits is split into
S
groups of
Q
transmissions. The transmit element
i
and transmit event
e
are reindexed to reflect this grouping:

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=v+Q(l-1)

(A.1)

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=q+Q(s-1)

Where
l
and
s
are the transmit element and event group numbers, while
v
and
q
are the element’s and event’s indices within their group. In this analysis,
(vl)
is written in place of
(v+Q(l-1))
and
(qs)
is written in place of
(q+Q(s-1))
for space.

To align with this grouping, the dataset
\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t)
is reordered:

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1}\to\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Q\times S}\\
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t):=\mathrm{col}_{s}\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t)\ (s=1,\ldots,S)\\
g^{\prime}_{q,s}(t)=\sum_{l=1}^{S}\sum_{v=1}^{Q}h^{(N)}_{(qs),(vl)}s_{(vl)}(t)\end{array}

Now
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
represents the
s
-th group of
Q
transmit events.

Thanks to its recursive Kronecker product definition, the Hadamard matrix
\bm{H}_{N}
can be linearly separated into two smaller Hadamard matrices
\bm{H}_{S}
and
\bm{H}_{Q}
provided N, S, and Q are all powers of 2:

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{H}_{N}=\bm{H}_{S}\otimes\bm{H}_{Q}\\[6.45831pt]
h^{(N)}_{ie}=h^{(N)}_{(vl),(qs)}=h^{(S)}_{s,l}h^{(Q)}_{q,v}\\[6.45831pt]
\bm{H}_{N}=[h^{(N)}_{ij}]\end{array}

(A.2)

Similarly, for its inverse:

\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{H}^{-1}_{N}=\bm{H}^{-1}_{S}\otimes\bm{H}^{-1}_{Q}\\[6.45831pt]
\hat{h}^{(N)}_{ei}=\hat{h}^{(N)}_{(qs),(vl)}=\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}\end{array}

(A.3)

Next, the reindexing and Kronecker definitions are applied to the
FORCES
reconstruction equation; the decode step is kept within curly braces:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}\{\bm{H}_{N}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t),r\}

Expand

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{i}^{N}\{\sum_{e}^{N}\hat{h}^{(N)}_{ie}g_{e}\}(\tau_{i}(r))

Reindex

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{l}^{S}\sum_{v}^{Q}\{\sum_{s}^{S}\sum_{q}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(N)}_{(vl),(qs)}g^{\prime}_{q,s}\}(\tau_{(vl)}(r))

\displaystyle\text{Split }\bm{H}^{-1}_{N}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{l}^{S}\sum_{v}^{Q}\{\sum_{s}^{S}\sum_{q}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}g^{\prime}_{q,s}\}(\tau_{(vl)}(r))

Reorder

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{s}^{S}[\sum_{l}^{S}\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\sum_{v}^{Q}\{\sum_{q}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}g^{\prime}_{q,s}\}(\tau_{(vl)}(r))]

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t),r\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s}^{S}[\sum_{l}^{S}\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\sum_{v}^{Q}\{\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)\}(\tau_{(vl)}(r))]

(A.4)

The matrix multiplication is now a partial decoding of a single transmit group, and is defined as a
partially decoded dataset

\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)=\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{v,s}(t)=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}g^{\prime}_{q,s}(t)

(A.5)

As discussed in section
II-B
, the
DAS
operator associates each signal with a unique transmit element for beamforming, but in equation
A.4

DAS
must be applied to
Q
partially decoded signals from an array with
N
transmit elements. Like the events, the
N
transmit elements are also divided into
S
groups of
Q
. Thus, a modified
DAS
operator is defined that associates an array of
Q
signals with the
l
’th group of elements for delay calculations:

\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{l}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{(}t),r\}=\sum_{v=1}^{Q}d^{\prime}_{v}(\tau_{v+Q(l-1)}(r))

(A.6)

Applying this definition to equation
A.4
produces the
READI
reconstruction operator (eq.
11
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t),r\}=\sum_{s}^{S}[\sum_{l}^{S}\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{l}\{\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}=\sum_{l}^{S}\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{l}\{\bm{H}_{Q}^{-1}\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t),r\}=\sum_{s}^{S}\mathbb{R}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t),r\}

To form a single
READI
low-resolution image from
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
, the modified
DAS
operator is called
S
times, once for each element group
l
. Each DAS iteration produces a sub-image, and the
READI

s
’th row of
\bm{H}_{S}^{-1}
.

Appendix B
READI Reconstruction Operator Analysis

The partially decoded dataset group
\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
is defined in equation
A.5
from the encoded dataset group
\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
. To understand how to beamform
\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
, it must be defined in relation to the multistatic dataset
\bm{\mathrm{S}}(t)
instead.

Substituting the definition of
g^{\prime}_{q,s}(t)
(eq.
9
) into equation
A.5
:

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{v,s}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}g^{\prime}_{q,s}(t)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{q}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}\sum_{l=1}^{S}\sum_{v^{\prime}=1}^{Q}h^{(N)}_{(qs),(v^{\prime}l)}s_{(v^{\prime}l)}(t)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{q}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}\sum_{l}^{S}\sum_{v^{\prime}}^{Q}h^{(S)}_{s,l}h^{(Q)}_{q,v^{\prime}}s_{(v^{\prime}l)}(t)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{l}^{S}\sum_{v^{\prime}}^{Q}h^{(S)}_{s,l}s_{(v^{\prime}l)}(t)\sum_{q}^{Q}\hat{h}^{(Q)}_{v,q}h^{(Q)}_{q,v^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\sum_{l}^{S}\sum_{v^{\prime}}^{Q}h^{(S)}_{s,l}s_{(v^{\prime}l)}(t)\delta(v,v^{\prime})

\displaystyle d^{\prime}_{v,s}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l}^{S}h^{(S)}_{s,l}s_{v+Q(l-1)}(t)

(B.1)

Thus
v
’th signal in the partially decoded dataset
\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{s}(t)
is a linear combination of the contributions from the
v
’th transmit element of every group. This is very visible in figure
2
, where with four groups of two events (
N=4\ S=2\ Q=2
) the partially decoded apertures contain four signals apiece.

For a smaller example, take two groups of two transmit events each (
N=4\ S=2\ Q=2
), then
\bm{\mathrm{D^{\prime}}}(t)
can be expressed as:

\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}(t)+s_{3}(t)\\
s_{2}(t)+s_{4}(t)\end{bmatrix}\ \bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{2}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}(t)-s_{3}(t)\\
s_{2}(t)-s_{4}(t)\end{bmatrix}

Both partially decoded groups contain all four multistatic signals, mixed according to the group’s row in the
\bm{H}_{S}
(
\bm{H}_{2}
) matrix. Attempting to directly beamform these groups using the
DAS
operator (
2
) would produce images with significant artifacts.

Per equation
6
, the correct reconstruction of
\bm{\mathrm{G}}(t)
for an
N=4

FORCES
sequence is:

\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t),r\}=s_{1}(\tau_{1})+s_{2}(\tau_{2})+s_{3}(\tau_{3})+s_{4}(\tau_{4})

While attempting standard
DAS
beamforming on
\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t)
yields:

\mathbb{D}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}=s_{1}(\tau_{1})+s_{2}(\tau_{2})+s_{3}(\tau_{1})+s_{4}(\tau_{2})

Compared with the
FORCES
reconstruction, the first group of elements (1 and 2) are beamformed with correct delays, while the second group (3 and 4) are incorrect and out of focus. To obtain all four in-focus signals
\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t)
must be beamformed twice, once for each element group using the modified
DAS
operator (
A.6
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{1}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}=\mathbb{D}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{2}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}=s_{1}(\tau_{3})+s_{2}(\tau_{4})+s_{3}(\tau_{3})+s_{4}(\tau_{4})

\mathbb{R}_{1}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}=\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{1}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}+\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{2}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}=\\
s_{1}(\tau_{1})+s_{2}(\tau_{2})+s_{3}(\tau_{3})+s_{4}(\tau_{4})\\
+s_{1}(\tau_{3})+s_{2}(\tau_{4})+s_{3}(\tau_{1})+s_{4}(\tau_{2})

(B.2)

Now the low-resolution image contains all in-focus data present in the full
FORCES
image, but with significant artifacts from the cross-terms.

To achieve the same result with
\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{2}(t)
, the second sub-image must have its sign flipped to ensure all in-focus signals are in phase:

\mathbb{R}_{2}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{2}(t),r\}=\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{1}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{2}(t),r\}-\mathbb{D^{\prime}}_{2}\{\bm{\mathrm{d^{\prime}}}_{2}(t),r\}=\\
s_{1}(\tau_{1})+s_{2}(\tau_{2})+s_{3}(\tau_{3})+s_{4}(\tau_{4})\\
-s_{1}(\tau_{3})-s_{2}(\tau_{4})-s_{3}(\tau_{1})-s_{4}(\tau_{2})

(B.3)

This is the origin of the
\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}
term in the
READI
reconstruction operator, to correct for the effect of
h^{(S)}_{s,l}
on the partially decoded datasets.

Both
READI
low-resolution images now contain the fully focused dataset, plus leftover decoding artifacts that will degrade the images. When combined, all cross terms cancel out and the full
FORCES
image is recovered:

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}\{\bm{\mathrm{G^{\prime}}}(t),r\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}_{1}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{1}(t),r\}+\mathbb{R}_{2}\{\bm{\mathrm{g^{\prime}}}_{2}(t),r\}

\displaystyle=s_{1}(\tau_{1})+s_{2}(\tau_{2})+s_{3}(\tau_{3})+s_{4}(\tau_{4})

In summary, a sum of sub-images is required to form a
READI
low-resolution image that contains all in-focus elements. Weighting this sum according to
\hat{h}^{(S)}_{l,s}
ensures that all out-of-focus cross-terms cancel out when the
READI
images are compounded.
