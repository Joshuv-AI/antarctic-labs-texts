Evaluation of optical coherence tomography in patients with coronary artery disease and systemic hypertension
Authors: Shikha Bhardwaj, Farhat Abrar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Systemic hypertension and coronary artery disease (CAD) cause widespread vascular and neurodegenerative changes, including in the retina. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) offers a noninvasive means to visualise these changes, serving as a potential marker of systemic vascular health. Retinal layers like the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (p-RNFL) and ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL) are especially vulnerable to vascular damage, making them useful indicators of cardiovascular risk. This study aimed to evaluate and compare the p-RNFL and GC-IPL thickness among hypertensive individuals with and without CAD and healthy controls.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This prospective observational study included 140 participants divided into three groups: healthy controls (Group A, n = 40), hypertensives without CAD (Group B, n = 50), and hypertensives with CAD (Group C, n = 50). All subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic evaluation, including spectral-domain-OCT, to measure p-RNFL and GC-IPL thickness in both eyes. Data were analyzed using analysis of variance with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.RESULTS:Both p-RNFL and GC-IPL were significantly thinned in hypertensive subjects, especially those with CAD. Group A had the highest mean RNFL (114.11 ± 10.09 μm) and GC-IPL (85.07 ± 2.30 μm), whereas Group C showed the lowest (RNFL: 84.90 ± 11.81 μm; GC-IPL: 105.97 ± 11.72 μm). Differences were significant (P < 0.001).CONCLUSION:OCT can find early signs of retinal nerve damage related to high blood pressure and CAD, indicating it can be a helpful noninvasive method for checking and tracking heart health risks.

Introduction

Hypertension remains a leading global health issue, contributing to over 9.4 million deaths annually.[1] It is defined as systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mmHg or a diastolic pressure of ≥90 mmHg, thus contributing as a major modifiable risk factor for both cardiovascular and renal diseases.[2] Hypertension can also seriously affect the eyes and is known to cause hypertensive retinopathy (HTNR), which includes changes in blood vessels such as narrowing of the retinal arteries, AV nicking, small bulges, flame-shaped bleeding, cotton wool spots, and hard patches.

Hypertension and coronary artery disease (CAD) frequently coexist due to shared risk factors such as smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and sedentary lifestyle.[3] In India, the burden of CAD has risen substantially over the past three decades.[4] CAD is usually identified through symptoms such as chest pain (angina), changes in electrocardiography, positive cardiac biomarkers, or coronary angiography that shows a blockage of 50% or more in the main coronary arteries. Despite its high prevalence, early detection of CAD, especially in hypertensive patients, remains suboptimal. Therefore, there is a growing interest in noninvasive tools that could aid in early risk stratification and intervention.

The retina offers a special, noninvasive way to look at overall blood vessel health because of its tiny blood vessel structure and its development similarities with the brain. Traditional fundoscopy has long been used to identify HTNR, and the European Society of Hypertension recommends its use in patients with moderate to severe hypertension.[5] However, subtle and early retinal changes may go undetected on fundoscopy alone.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT), particularly spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT), provides detailed images of the layers of the retina and has become an effective tool for spotting changes in the eyes of people with high blood pressure. Specifically, reductions in the thickness of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (p-RNFL) and ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GC-IPL) have been consistently observed in hypertensive patients.[67] These changes are believed to reflect microvascular compromise and neurodegeneration due to chronic hypertension.

More advanced OCT-based techniques, such as OCT angiography (OCTA), provide microvascular imaging and have been used to detect vascular rarefaction in patients with hypertension, diabetes, and CAD.[89] However, although the association between hypertension and retinal thinning is well established, few studies have investigated whether retinal structural changes differ in hypertensive patients with CAD compared to those without. Some studies indicate that CAD might lead to more noticeable thinning of the RNFL, which could mean a higher overall strain on the blood vessels.

von Hanno et al. and Chen et al. emphasized the potential of retinal imaging as a surrogate for cardiovascular health, citing the retina’s ability to mirror cerebral and coronary microvascular changes.[1011] Nonetheless, the exact relationship between CAD, hypertension, and retinal thinning remains unclear.

Our study aims to measure and compare the thickness of p-RNFL and GC-IPL using SD-OCT in patients with high blood pressure, both those with and without CAD, as well as in healthy individuals of the same age. Finding a dependable eye test could change how we assess heart disease risk by providing an easy, affordable, and noninvasive way to identify high-risk individuals early on.

Materials and Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted over 19 months, from June 2023 to January 2025. The study aimed to evaluate the role of OCT in assessing the p-RNFL and GC-IPL thickness in three distinct groups: individuals with systemic hypertension without CAD, those with systemic hypertension with CAD, and healthy individuals without systemic illness. Participants were recruited from the outpatient departments of ophthalmology and cardiology. We enrolled and categorised a total of 140 participants as follows: Group A (control group) comprised 40 age-matched healthy individuals, Group B included 50 hypertensive patients without CAD, and Group C consisted of 50 hypertensive patients with confirmed CAD. Eligibility criteria required participants to be adults aged 35–75 years who met the specific definitions for their respective groups. Individuals were excluded if they had comorbid health conditions; ocular diseases such as glaucoma; hypertensive ocular damage; a history of ocular surgery; severe ocular pathology; high myopia; or neuropsychiatric disorders, including dementia.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, and written informed consent was secured from all participants. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. Each subject underwent a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including detailed systemic and ocular history with special emphasis on hypertension, CAD, and associated risk factors. Ophthalmic examination involved uncorrected and best-corrected visual acuity assessment using Snellen’s chart, intraocular pressure measurement using a noncontact tonometer or Goldmann applanation tonometer, and slit-lamp examination of the anterior segment. Posterior segment evaluation was performed using indirect ophthalmoscopy and a 78D lens after pharmacological mydriasis with tropicamide 0.8% and phenylephrine 5%. OCT imaging was done with the MOPTIM MOCEAN 4000 device to get clear cross-sectional pictures of the retina, including specific measurements of p-RNFL and GC-IPL thickness. All scans were performed by a trained ophthalmic technician under standardized lighting and positioning conditions to ensure consistency and minimise artefacts.

Statistical analysis

Data for all participants were recorded in a detailed patient form that included personal information, medical history, eye exam results, and OCT measurements. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM (International Business Machines Corporation,) Armonk, New York, The United States of America). Continuous variables, such as p-RNFL and GC-IPL thickness, were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical data were represented as frequencies and percentages. The Chi-square test was used for intergroup comparison of categorical variables, and one-way analysis of variance was applied to compare means of continuous variables across the three groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests, indicating a meaningful association or difference. This structured methodology enabled a reliable and reproducible assessment of retinal changes in hypertensive patients, with and without concomitant CAD, in comparison to healthy controls. It also facilitated the investigation of the potential utility of OCT as a noninvasive modality for evaluating cardiovascular risk.

Discussion

This study investigated the role of OCT in identifying retinal structural changes in patients with systemic hypertension and CAD. By measuring the thickness of the p-RNFL and the GC-IPL in healthy people, hypertensive patients without CAD, and hypertensive patients with CAD, the research aimed to determine the extent to which retinal neurodegeneration and microvascular abnormalities are associated with these medical conditions.

The age distribution among study participants was statistically balanced, ensuring that differences in retinal parameters could be attributed to disease status rather than age-related variations. Previous studies highlighted age as a critical factor influencing retinal thickness, particularly GC-IPL.[110] The well-matched age groups in our study provide robust comparative analysis free from age-related confounding.

The gender distribution showed a statistically significant difference, with a higher proportion of females in the hypertensive with CAD group. Even though gender can affect the thickness of retinal layers, with females usually having a bit thicker RNFL and GC-IPL, research shows that changes caused by disease are more important than differences between genders. Previous studies reinforce the notion that systemic vascular health exerts a more substantial influence on retinal neurodegeneration than gender alone.[212]

A clear trend of progressive RNFL thinning was observed from healthy individuals to hypertensive patients and further in those with concurrent CAD. This pattern of structural degeneration was consistent across all retinal quadrants. The average RNFL thickness was significantly reduced in hypertensive subjects compared to controls, with the lowest values seen in the CAD group. These results support earlier studies, which showed that people with high blood pressure often have thinner RNFL, probably because of poor blood flow in the retina and long-term blood vessel problems.[113]

The superior and inferior quadrants, typically the thickest regions of the RNFL, showed significant thinning in hypertensive and CAD patients. The inferior quadrant exhibited pronounced degeneration, suggesting increased vulnerability to vascular compromise. These findings are supported by Shin et al., who emphasised the inferior quadrant’s susceptibility to hypoxic stress.[14] The nasal and temporal quadrants also showed significant thinning, with temporal RNFL thickness being notably reduced in CAD patients. Zhong et al. found similar results, linking the thinning of the temporal RNFL to reduced blood flow caused by coronary microvascular disease.[12]

GC-IPL thickness showed important differences between groups, acting as a sensitive sign of early neurodegenerative changes. Hypertensive patients exhibited higher GC-IPL values than controls, likely due to edema or compensatory changes in early disease stages. However, patients with both hypertension and CAD displayed substantial thinning across multiple quadrants, indicating chronic ischemic damage. This two-part pattern matches a study which found, showing that GC-IPL thinning in people with hypertension happens over time.[1]

Among the GC-IPL quadrants, the inferior and temporal regions showed the greatest thinning, particularly in the CAD group. These results are similar to the previous studies, which show that the temporal quadrant is thinner and more affected by problems with blood vessels throughout the body.[28] The inferonasal and superonasal areas also showed ongoing thinning, showing how the retina is affected by small blood vessel changes that aren’t easily noticed. These areas, which have a lot of ganglion cells and are supplied by small blood vessels, might be especially vulnerable to reduced blood flow in systemic vascular diseases.

The thinning in the superotemporal quadrant of CAD patients was very noticeable, indicating a clear link between how serious the coronary artery issues are and the loss of retinal neurons. A study found similar decreases in GC-IPL in the side part of the macula, supporting the idea that changes seen in OCT can indicate the overall impact of systemic vascular issues.[15] Peng et al. further validated this association by correlating retinal thinning with HTNR severity and blood pressure indices.[8]

Overall, these changes in structure show that OCT is a useful, noninvasive imaging tool that can identify early damage to the retina in patients with systemic vascular diseases. The retinal thinning seen in CAD patients, in addition to what is seen in just hypertension, highlights the extra impact of damage to the small blood vessels in the heart. Emerging literature, including studies by von Hanno et al. and Chen et al., has demonstrated inverse correlations between GC-IPL thickness and the number of occluded coronary vessels, suggesting that retinal imaging could offer clues about systemic disease burden.[1011]

Advanced imaging techniques such as OCT and OCTA are increasingly being explored for systemic applications. Recent studies show the feasibility of using artificial intelligence and retinal biomarkers to predict cardiovascular events.[1617] Our findings support the integration of retinal evaluations into cardiovascular risk stratification models. By identifying retinal neurodegeneration in asymptomatic patients, clinicians may be able to implement early interventions and mitigate long-term vascular complications.

However, this study has several limitations. Being cross-sectional, it cannot establish temporal relationships or causality. Although the sample size is sufficient for preliminary analysis, it may not represent the full spectrum of retinal changes across various populations. Potential confounders, such as the duration of hypertension or CAD, medication effects, and coexisting systemic diseases, were not fully controlled. Furthermore, the structural nature of OCT findings did not correlate with functional visual outcomes, potentially providing additional clinical relevance.

Future studies should focus on longitudinal follow-up to assess progression and therapeutic response. Including functional tests such as visual field analysis or electrophysiology with OCT, could help us better understand how blood vessel issues affect the retina. In addition, the use of OCTA may provide more granular insights into microvascular alterations preceding structural loss.

Conclusion

Measuring the thickness of p-RNFL and GC-IPL using OCT shows clear changes in the retina linked to systemic hypertension and CAD. These structural changes, particularly in the inferior, nasal, and temporal quadrants, reflect the cumulative impact of systemic vascular disease on the retina. The integration of retinal imaging into cardiovascular assessment protocols could enhance early detection and management of high-risk individuals. As retinal imaging technologies evolve, they may redefine preventive strategies in systemic vascular health monitoring.
