Association between comorbidity burden and glycaemic profiles in adults with type 1 diabetes using hybrid closed‐loop systems
Authors: Aatif Syed, Akash Konantambigi, Vasant Shenoy, Gaurav Puri, Harshal Deshmukh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA secondary analysis was conducted of a multicentre Australian cohort of 158 adults with type 1 diabetes using hybrid closed‐loop systems to examine the relationship between comorbidity burden and glycaemic outcomes. Comorbidities such as obesity, dyslipidaemia and anxiety/depression were highly prevalent, particularly in regional centres. Higher comorbidity burden was significantly associated with increased time below range (<3 mmol/L; r = 0.17, P = 0.013), but not with mean glucose or time in range. These findings highlight the influence of multimorbidity on glycaemic stability and underscore the need for individualised optimisation of automated insulin delivery therapy in real‐world settings.Keywords: type 1 diabetes, hybrid closed‐loop systems, continuous glucose monitoring

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is a complex, lifelong condition requiring careful management to reduce the risk of both acute complications and long‐term sequelae such as cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, retinopathy and neuropathy. 1 , 2 Beyond classical diabetes‐related complications, people living with T1DM are also at risk of developing additional comorbidities, including autoimmune thyroid disease, cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal disorders and psychiatric conditions. 3 The burden of multimorbidity is well established in type 2 diabetes but remains less well defined in T1DM, particularly in real‐world, non‐trial populations and in regional healthcare settings. 4 Understanding how comorbidities intersect with glycaemic management in T1DM is clinically relevant, as they may alter both physiological glucose responses and the practical effectiveness of advanced diabetes technologies.

In recent years, hybrid closed‐loop and automated insulin delivery (AID) systems have revolutionised the management of T1DM by integrating continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) with algorithm‐driven insulin delivery. 3 These technologies ease the daily burden of glucose monitoring and insulin titration, and clinical trials have shown improvements in time in range and reductions in hypoglycaemia. 5 , 6 Increasingly, their effectiveness and safety are also being assessed in real‐world settings.

We recently conducted a multicentre study, representing the first large‐scale real‐world evaluation of AID use among Australian adults with T1DM across both metropolitan and regional centres. 7 The study demonstrated significant improvements in glycaemic control, with mean HbA1c reduced from 8.62% to 7.34% (P < 0.0001). Continuous glucose‐monitoring metrics also improved, with time in range (3.9–10.0 mmol/L) increasing from 53.4% to 70.0% and time above 13.9 mmol/L decreasing from 18.7% to 8.4%. These findings highlight the substantial clinical benefits of AID in routine practice, translating into improved glucose stability and reduced risk of long‐term complications.

However, while this analysis established the efficacy of AID therapy, it did not explore the influence of comorbidity burden on glycaemic profiles. This is a critical gap, as the presence of comorbidities may increase the risk of hypoglycaemia, contribute to variability in CGM outcomes and complicate the optimisation of closed‐loop systems. To address this, we conducted a secondary analysis of the Konantambigi et al. cohort, with two specific objectives: (a) characterise the comorbidity burden among adults with T1DM commencing AID therapy across regional and metropolitan sites in Queensland and (b) evaluate associations between comorbidity burden and baseline CGM metrics, including time in range, time above range and time below range (TBR).

A retrospective observational cohort study across three Australian public hospitals in the state of Queensland: Logan (metropolitan), Mackay and Townsville (regional). These areas serve geographically and socioeconomically diverse populations, with each site having access to specialist endocrinologist services. Adults with confirmed T1DM who commenced AID therapy between January 2020 and December 2024 were eligible. The study included patients who were commenced on any type of AID system (including Medtronic 670G/770G/780G systems, Tandem t:slim X2 with Control‐IQ and YpsoPump with CamAPS FX). Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, cessation of AID therapy within 1 month or unavailable CGM/pump data. CGM and pump metrics were extracted from manufacturer‐specific online platforms (CareLink Personal/Professional, t:connect, Diasend/Glooko), with comorbidities recorded by extracting de‐identified patient data from hospital electronic medical records system (iEMR).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the baseline characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comorbidity burden was assessed using a modified Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) derived from comorbidities available within the dataset. Standard Charlson weights were applied to the following conditions when present: myocardial infarction/ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease and chronic kidney disease (weighted as 2 points for moderate/severe renal disease). Given all patients have T1DM, diabetes contributed 1 point for eligible cases by definition. The resulting CCI score was used as a standardised summary measure of comorbidity burden. Conditions such as anxiety, depression, obesity and dyslipidaemia were not included in the Charlson scoring framework and reported separately as baseline characteristics, given their clinical relevance in diabetes technology cohorts. CGM metrics used in this analysis were collected from baseline pre‐AID data prior to the initiation of automated insulin delivery systems; therefore, AID system type did not influence baseline glycaemic metrics.

The comorbidity burden was compared across the three sites using chi‐squared testing. Comorbidities were identified from medical records and grouped to generate a comorbidity burden score. Statistical analyses for continuous variables were analysed using ANOVA testing and Pearson correlation to examine associations between comorbidity burden and CGM metrics. A P‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all statistical analysis was conducted using R software (4.0.3).

Discussion

A total of 158 adults with T1DM who commenced AID therapy were included in this multicentre audit across three sites in regional and metropolitan Australia. The mean age varied across sites: Logan 27.4 years (SD 17.6), Mackay 29.2 years (SD 15.7) and Townsville 37.7 years (SD 15.4). The majority were female (65.0%–67.8%) and of white ethnicity (90%–94.7%). Pump system type varied substantially by site (P < 0.001), with the Medtronic system more common in Mackay, while T:Slim was the most frequently used in Townsville. Demographic characteristics of participants by study site are provided in Table S1 of appendices in this letter.

Comorbidities were highly prevalent across the cohort. A significant proportion of the cohort had at least one comorbidity and a sizeable subset had two or more (Fig. 1). Analysis of comorbidity burden by site revealed certain comorbidities were more prevalent in certain areas compared to others. Anxiety/depression, obesity and dyslipidaemia were also common but, consistent with Charlson methodology, were reported descriptively and did not contribute to the CCI‐derived comorbidity burden. Obesity prevalence was significantly higher in Mackay (33.9%, P‐0.001), whereas anxiety and depression were more prevalent in Townsville (28.3%, P = 0.023). However, dyslipidaemia was common across sites, and rates of ischaemic heart disease did not differ significantly. Comorbidity burden was similar across sites. The mean CCI score was 1.23 (SD 0.67) in Logan (n = 39), 1.22 (SD 0.67) in Mackay (n = 59) and 1.13 (SD 0.54) in Townsville (n = 60).

Distribution of comorbidity burden in the cohort.

Median CCI scores were identical across all three sites (median 1, IQR 0–1). There was no statistically significant difference in comorbidity burden between sites (Kruskal–Wallis) χ 2 = 1.12, P = 0.57). Further breakdown of comorbidity burden across different sites in the cohort is presented in Table S2. The high prevalence of comorbidity burden underscores the need for holistic diabetes care that extends beyond glucose numbers.

Correlation analysis between comorbidity burden and CGM metrics demonstrated that a higher comorbidity burden was significantly associated with increased TBR (<3 mmol/L; r = 0.17, P = 0.013). All CGM metrics were derived from baseline pre‐AID data, collected prior to the initiation of automated insulin delivery therapy; therefore, variability in AID system type did not influence these associations. No significant associations were observed between comorbidity burden and mean glucose, time in range, time above range, or other CGM‐derived metrics (Fig. 2). This suggests that patients with multiple comorbidities may be more vulnerable to hypoglycaemia and a need for careful individualised adjustment when introducing closed‐loop systems in complex patients. It highlights the need for clinicians to consider comorbidity profiles when initiating automated insulin delivery, and rationalising glycaemic targets in patients with high comorbidity burden would prevent the rate of hypoglycaemic episodes.

Associations between comorbidity burden and CGM metrics. TAR, time above range; TBR, time below range; TIR, time in range.

This is the first multicentre audit to evaluate comorbidity burden among adults with T1DM initiating AID therapy in regional Australia. Comorbidities were common and influenced glycaemic outcomes, particularly increasing exposure to hypoglycaemia. These findings emphasise the importance of individualised comorbidity assessment when optimising AID therapy in real‐world settings. Recognition and management of comorbidities should be prioritised to ensure safe and effective use of closed‐loop insulin delivery systems.
