Application of Natural Zeolite as Catalyst in the Degradation of the Azo Dye DO39 by Plasma
Authors: Josefina Vergara Sanchez, Cesar Torres, Esteban Montiel Palacios, David Olivos, Angel Tlatelpa, José C. Palomares, Aaron Gómez, Pedro G. Reyes, Horacio Martínez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The discharge of industrial wastewater with dyes into natural tributaries has serious environmental consequences on aquatic ecosystems and the health of living beings. Atmospheric plasmas have proven to be very useful for the degradation of organic contaminants in water; in this context, corona type discharge, owing to its physical characteristics, is capable of degrading and even mineralizing dyes dissolved in water. In this work, zeolite was used as a catalyst in the degradation of the textile azo dye Direct Orange 39 (DO39) through interaction with plasma generated at atmospheric pressure. The zeolite was characterized by microscopic analysis, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Microscopic analysis was carried out to determine the roughness and grain size of the zeolite, and scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive spectroscopy were carried out to understand the morphology and chemical composition of this material, respectively. The initial concentrations were 1.0 mM for the dye and 4.0 g/L for zeolite, in an initial volume of 250 mL. The results indicate that the coloration of the samples was reduced depending on the treatment time, which was verified by the absorption spectrum in the UV/vis range. The mineralization of the dye after 120 min, using zeolite, was 95%. In addition, the removal percentages in relation to chemical oxygen demand were greater than 96%. Notably the zeolite did not undergo chemical changes during its use as a catalyst in the plasma treatment.

Introduction

A dye is a compound that absorbs a part of the visible spectrum, which occurs when white light interacts with matter, and is selectively absorbed by the excitation of certain molecular bonds known as chromophores.1

In industrial terms, approximately 7 million metric tons of dye are produced per year, of which there are more than ten thousand types,2 and are mainly used in the plastics, printing, pharmaceutical, food, cosmetics, photography, textile, and leather industries.3 More than 70% of the synthetic dyes produced each year are composed of molecules with one or more azo groups (N=N), which are responsible for the color of the dye.4,5 Azo dyes can be classified as cationic (basic dyes), anionic (direct, acidic, and reactive dyes) or nonionic (disperse dyes), depending on their charge.6 Direct dyes are characterized by their solubility in water, the presence of one or more azo groups and the ability to dye cellulose fibers through van der Waals forces,2 a direct example of its use is in dye cotton fabrics due to their low cost.4

The textile industry generates large volumes of wastewater containing different types of contaminants, of which dyes stand out. The discharge of wastewater with dyes into bodies of water causes aesthetic problems, altering the pH, reducing oxygen levels, and decreasing light penetration, thus affecting photosynthesis.7 It has been reported that 60 and 70% of azo dyes are toxic and carcinogenic,1 and their byproducts have also been reported to be toxic and may cause mutagenic/carcinogenic2,4,6,8 and genotoxic effects on aquatic life forms.1 They may also cause cancer because of the splitting of soluble azo dyes into aromatic amines by liver enzymes and intestinal flora.8

Several types of dyes are resistant to biodegradation owing to their chemical structure;1 in particular, Direct Orange 39 (DO39) is an azo dye that is difficult to treat owing to its recalcitrant nature and solubility in water. Furthermore, metabolites that are more toxic than the original molecules can be generated during its aerobic biological treatment. This dye inhibits seed germination in Triticum aestivum and Phaseolus mungo and causes reproductive and developmental effects in rats.4 Therefore, it is of utmost importance to find effective treatments to eliminate DO39 from water without increasing risks to the environment and all living organisms present.4

These dyes have high solubility in water, which makes them difficult to remove using traditional methods.2 Conventional treatments such as adsorption on activated carbon, flocculation, coagulation, ultrafiltration, and reverse osmosis have been used, but these are inadequate to eliminate this type of contaminant because they generate a large amount of sludge, which requires post-treatment and makes the process more expensive.6 Previous reports have established the discoloration of dyes using various bacteria of the genera Bacillus, Aeromonas, and Pseudomonas, isolated from different effluents, and the isolation of these cultures is laborious and time-consuming.7 The biodegradation of azo dyes is difficult because of the presence of electron-withdrawing groups in their molecules, and their long hydraulic retention time2 and high specificity9 limit their use. In addition, these dyes are resistant to bacterial attack in the presence of oxygen, and the formation of aromatic amines under anaerobic conditions has been reported.2 Adsorption is one of the most effective treatment methods for dye removal, and many different types of adsorbents, such as activated carbon, zeolites, biomass, clays, metal oxide nanoparticles,10 porous carbon,11 transition-metal oxide nanoparticles doped onto porous carbon,12 metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), hierarchically porous zeolite-carbon composites13 and hierarchical porous carbons14 are effective in removing dyes from aqueous effluents. The most widely used is activated carbon, but this is very expensive. Therefore, many researchers have focused on natural substances for dye removal.12,15 Zeolites are commonly used as adsorbents because of their porosity, adsorption, and ion exchange. This absorbent mineral has been reported in several investigations on dye removal.16 Zeolites are natural or synthetic microporous minerals composed of aluminosilicates17 and present a crystalline structure with a three-dimensional network of pores.18 There is a great diversity of zeolites, the most common are mordenite, clinoptilolite, heulandite, phyllisite, eroinite and chabazite, being the Clinoptilolite one of the most abundant and used minerals. It contains two-dimensional channels that incorporate exchangeable cations of ions such as Na+, K+, Ca2+ and Mg2+.15 The high adsorption efficiency of zeolites is related to their large internal surface area. The chemical composition is Si (69.3%), Al (10.7%), Na (0.6%), K (3.8%), Ca (4.2%), Mg (2. 6%), Fe (0.3%) and Ti (∼0.1%). Its physical properties include surface area (21.1 m2/g), total pore volume (0.068 cm3/g) and average pore diameter (3.9 nm).15

Their properties such as large surface area, molecular shape selectivity, and high thermal and chemical stability make them suitable for use as catalysts.17 However, the size of the micropores can limit the diffusion of reagents and products, slowing down the reaction and blocking the pores to become blocked.17 Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are alternatives to the previously mentioned processes.8 AOPs, including ozonation, photocatalysis and Fenton-like processes, based on the generation of reactive species with high oxidation potential (+1.8–2.8 V) for example •OH, •OOH and •O2–, these processes mainly involve •OH, which are highly reactive and nonselective for the degradation of primary pollutants to secondary pollutants like CO2, and H2O.19,20 The degradation of azo dyes has been carried out using different advanced oxidation processes. Gnanaprakasam et al. carried out photocatalytic degradation of coralene red F3BS dye using TiO2 and ZnO as catalysts.8 Unal et al. used magnetite (Fe3O4) as a catalyst to evaluate the catalytic performance in Fenton-type reactions in the decolorization of azodyes Basic Red 18 and Acid Red 88.20 Muniyasamy et al. used ozone for the degradation of the textile dyes Reactive Orange 16 (Mono azo), Reactive Red 120 (Dia-azo) and Direct Red 80 (Poly azo).6 These technologies require radiation sources or the addition of chemical reagents, which consume a large amount of energy and are dangerous to the environment.9

Recently, a method that has shown great efficiency for the treatment of wastewater with dyes, without the need to add chemicals, is the use of plasma on the surface of the solution. Jiang et al. used nonthermal plasma to degrade the azo dye Methyl Orange.9 This type of treatment leads to various physical and chemical effects, such as pyrolysis, UV photolysis, electrohydraulic cavitation, and the formation of reactive oxygen species (•OH, •H, •O, HO2•, O2–), reactive nitrogen species (N•, NO•, N2*)21 and molecules (H2O2, O3) which are capable of eliminating organic matter.9 Plasma wastewater treatment has attracted enormous interest because of its excellent performance in the degradation of organic contaminants without causing secondary pollution.9 A combination of physical, chemical, and biological methods has been used to complete the treatment of the effluent, not only to reduce the dye concentration but also to reduce the chemical and biological oxygen demand and toxicity.2 Tarkwa et al. used laterite (soil containing Al2O3, Fe2O3 and TiO2) as a catalyst and combined it with a sliding arc plasma for the degradation of Orange G. Their results showed that the use of laterite soil (as a catalyst) in the solution exposed to nonthermal plasma significantly improved the degradation of Orange G azo dye.21 The objective of this work was to evaluate the use of mineral zeolite (clinoptilolite 70%- muscorita 30%) as a catalyst for the degradation of the textile dye Direct Orange 39 (DO39) through interaction with an electric discharge, corona type.

The DO39 compound displays persistent characteristics, and its derivative compounds have the potential to pose significant toxicity risks to aquatic organisms.4 Its primary applications include printing, dyeing textiles, and providing shading for leather and paper, with a molecular weight of 299.28 g/mol, CAS number of 1325–54–8, color index of 40215, and chemical formula (Table 1).

Methodology

Materials

The Direct Orange 39 dye was provided by Fermont, while the zeolite was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich. Some characteristics of the dyes and zeolites are listed in Table 1.

The deionized water was provided by a Milli-Q ultrapure water system (Academic A-10, resistance 18.2 MΩ cm).

Characterization Methods

Total organic carbon (TOC) and chemical oxygen demand (COD) were determined using the HACH method every 15 min for 120 min.

Microscopic analysis of the zeolite was carried out before and after being used in water treatment using the microscope Keyence model VHX-970F at 100X and 200× magnification. The morphological characterization of the zeolite through SEM/EDS was performed using a Scanning Electron Microscope (Zeiss model EVO MA25) to evaluate the spectrum of both states of zeolite before and after being used in wastewater.

Degradation Experiments

The experimental system was similar to that used by Vergara et al. for the degradation of dyes in water by plasma.23 a Batch reactor was used to generate plasma on the surface of the liquid samples (Figure 1) using a direct current voltage source (HP Mod. 6525A, 4.0 kV-50 mA) at a constant power of 80 W. The treatment time was increased every 15 min until a final time of 120 min was reached.

Experimental system of wastewater treatment by plasma and zeolite. (A) Batch reactor, (B) side view of batch reactor.

Preparation of the DO39 Solution

The DO39 dye was weighed on an analytical balance and dissolved in distilled water in a beaker. The solution was placed in a volumetric flask. 1.0 g of zeolite were added, and the volume was adjusted to 250 mL. The initial solution contained 1.0 mM of DO39 dye and 1.0 g of zeolite. A Hach HQ40d multiparameter meter was used to measure the pH, electrical conductivity, and temperature. The initial temperature was 25 °C, the initial pH was 6.38, and initial electrical conductivity was 336 μS/cm. A Hach DR3900 spectrophotometer was used to measure the initial absorbance of the DO39 dye solution. Specifically, 1.0 mL of the dye solution was obtained, diluted with 2.0 mL of distilled water, and placed in a quartz cell. The sample was measured using a spectrophotometer. In this case, the characteristic absorbance peak was at 445 nm. The analysis was performed every 15 min for 120 min. All solutions were made from analytical-grade chemicals, and the experiment was performed five times under the same initial conditions.

The initial parameters obtained from the experiment carried out with zeolite were pH = 6.38, electrical conductivity of 336 μS/cm, and the final parameter pH = 3.26 and electrical conductivity of 514 μS/cm. The behavior of pH and EC when zeolite was used as a catalyst, shows a minor change compared to the solution treated without zeolite. The above is beneficial because the final pH value is higher, which would allow fewer reagents to be used for neutralization (Figure 7). Likewise, lower conductivity values would suggest a lower presence of dissolved ions (including dissolved salts).

Behavior of pH and CE in function of time treatment.

The COD (Figure 8A) and TOC (Figure 8B) of the samples were determined every 15 min for a period of 120 min. The COD values decreased approximately 66.6% when zeolite was not used and 96.6% with zeolite. On the other hand, TOC decreased 95.9% in zeolite presence and 49.8% without zeolite.

Behavior of COD and TOC vs Time obtained during the treatment of DO39 dye, (A) COD and (B) TOC.

TOC provides an overview of the degradation process of the dye and its byproducts. This quantity can be interpreted as an approximation of the amount of contaminant present in each sample. Figure 9 shows the behavior of TOC/TOC0 (which normalizes the TOC value) for samples treated with and without zeolite. By fitting the values shown in Figure 9 for TOC/TOC0 with an exponential decay function (y = e–kt), time (t1/2) can be obtained, which is the time at which half of the dye has been mineralized through plasma treatment. The half-life times were 48.4 and 82.4 min with and without zeolite, respectively, which indicates that the use of zeolite improves the efficiency of the treatment.

Comparison of TOC/TOC0 during plasma treatment of aqueous solutions of the dye DO39.

Conclusions

The zeolite was characterized by microscopic analysis, SEM, EDS, and FAAS, indicating that it is a porous material containing mostly metals that favor plasma treatment. It was possible to reduce the concentration of the DO39 dye in the solution when the treatment was carried out with plasma and zeolite as catalysts. Furthermore, the changes in pH and electrical conductivity were more moderate than those in the case of the treatment without zeolite. The percentage of discoloration after 120 min of treatment was 78% when zeolite was used, whereas this value was 63.6% in the other case. In the treatment with plasma and zeolite, higher COD and TOC removal and energy yield were achieved.

There was no chemical difference between the materials before and after being used in wastewater plasma treatment. The element with the highest percentage of participation is O, followed by Si in both cases of the zeolite state before and after processing in wastewater. Morphologically, only it is possible to identify differences due to zeolite before being used, with a major number of grains from 0.40 to 1.60 mm in size. After the use of zeolite, the material looks more sand and has a very small grain size of a 70%. The zeolite accelerated the reaction rate, and no chemical difference was observed between the zeolite before and after use in wastewater, which allowed us to infer that the zeolite functioned only as a catalyst. In addition, the resulting water after treatment did not have such low pH values, and the process was efficient. In agriculture, zeolite is used as a fertilizer retainer; therefore, under conditions that meet official standards, wastewater treated by plasma and zeolite could be used for irrigation.
