Dinitrogen Activation and Functionalization Affording Chromium Diazenido and Hydrazido Complexes
Authors: Gao-Xiang Wang, Zhu-Bao Yin, Junnian Wei, Zhenfeng Xi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ConspectusThe activation and functionalization of N2 to form nitrogen-element bonds have long posed challenges to industrial, biological, and synthetic chemists. The first transition-metal dinitrogen complex prepared by Allen and Senoff in 1965 provoked researchers to explore homogeneous N2 fixation. Despite intensive research in the last six decades, efficient and quantitative conversion of N2 to diazenido and hydrazido species remains problematic. Relative to a plethora of reactions to generate N2 complexes, their functionalization reactions are rather rare, and the yields are often unsatisfactory, emphasizing the need for systematic investigations of the reaction mechanisms.In this Account, we summarize our recent work on the synthesis, spectroscopic features, electronic structures, and reactivities of several Cr–N2 complexes. Initially, a series of dinuclear and trinuclear Cr(I)–N2 complexes bearing cyclopentadienyl-phosphine ligands were accessed. However, they cannot achieve N2 functionalization but undergo oxidative addition reactions with phenylsilane, azobenzene, and other unsaturated organic compounds at the low-valent Cr(I) centers rather than at the N2 unit. Further reduction of these Cr(I) complexes leads to the formation of more activated mononuclear Cr(0) bis-dinitrogen complexes. Remarkably, silylation of the cyclopentadienyl-phosphine Cr(0)–N2 complex with Me3SiCl afforded the first Cr hydrazido complex. This process follows the distal pathway to functionalize the Nβ atom twice, yielding an end-on η1-hydrazido complex, Cr(III)=N–N(SiMe3)2. In contrast, upon substitution of the phosphine ligand in the Cr(0)–N2 complex with a N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) ligand, the corresponding reaction with Me3SiCl proceeds via the alternating pathway; the silylation occurs at both Nα and Nβ atoms and generates a side-on η2-hydrazido complex, Cr(III)(η2-Me3SiN–NSiMe3). Both silylation reactions are inevitably accompanied by the formation of Cr(III) hydrazido complexes and Cr(II) chlorides with a 2:1 ratio. These processes exhibit a peculiar ′3-4-2-1′ stoichiometry (i.e., treating 3 equiv of Cr(0)–N2 complexes with 4 equiv of Me3SiCl yields 2 equiv of Cr(III) disilyl-hydrazido complexes and 1 equiv of Cr(II) chloride). Upon replacing the monodentate phosphine and/or NHC ligand with a bisphosphine ligand, a monodinitrogen Cr(0) complex, instead of the bis-dinitrogen Cr(0) complexes, is obtained; consequently, the silylation reactions progress via the normal two-electron route, which passes through Cr(II)–N=N–R diazenido species as an intermediate and furnishes [Cr(IV)=N–NR2]+ hydrazido as the final products. More importantly, this type of Cr(0)–N2 complex can be not only silylated but also protonated and alkylated proficiently. All of the second-order reaction rates of the first and second transformations are determined along with the lifetimes of the intervening diazenido species. Based on these findings, we have successfully carried out nearly quantitative preparations of the Cr(IV) hydrazido species with unmixed or hybrid substituents.The studies of Cr–N2 systems provide effective approaches for the activation and functionalization of N2, deepening the understanding of N2 electrophilic attack. We hope that this Account will inspire more discoveries related to the transformation of gaseous N2 to high-value-added nitrogen-containing organic compounds.

Introduction

Nitrogen is an essential element for sustaining life on earth and constitutes a crucial foundation for human civilization.5,6 As a main component of air, N2 accounts for about 78% of the atmospheric volume, rendering it the most abundant and cost-effective source of nitrogen, which can be described as an essentially inexhaustible reservoir.7−10 However, due to being a nonpolar molecule and having a high bond dissociation energy (942 kJ/mol) and a large HOMO–LUMO gap (10.82 eV), N2 is often an inert gas. Therefore, the activation and conversion of N2 are extremely challenging.

The Haber-Bosch process, used for industrial ammonia synthesis, directly hydrogenates N2 to NH3, which represents a significant breakthrough in human science and technology in the 20th century, lays the foundation for modern agriculture, and substantially advances the progress of modern civilization.11,12 However, the large energy consumption, which accounts for 1 to 2% of the total global energy consumption annually, makes it difficult to meet the needs of sustainable development.13−15 Therefore, the quest for new technologies that are capable of efficiently activating and functionalizing N2 under mild conditions has long been a hot topic for a large body of research work all over the world.16−19 Inspired by the fact that the nitrogenases in nature are able to fix N2 at ambient temperature and pressure, scientists have been committed to using chemical methods to mimic the biological nitrogen fixation processes.20−23 This endeavor is aimed at developing efficient N2 conversion methods involving transition metals and synthesizing high-value-added nitrogen-containing organic compounds.24−28

Since the 1960s, scientists have found that coordination and backdonation interactions between transition metals and N2 (Dewar–Chatt–Duncanson model) could stabilize the coordinated N2 ligand.29−31 Almost all transition-metal dinitrogen complexes characteristically feature strong π-back-donation from electron-rich metal centers to N2 π* orbitals. Therefore, the following N2 functionalization is typically achieved through electrophilic attacks on metal–N2 complexes with potent electrophiles (H+, C+, and Si+), leading to diazenido and/or hydrazido complexes (Scheme 1). Over the past 60 years, more than 600 terminal dinitrogen complexes characterized by X-ray crystallography have been reported. However, of them fewer than 50 terminal dinitrogen complexes (<8%) have been documented to be converted to diazenido and/or hydrazido complexes, and those reactions typically exhibit unsatisfactory yields.4 Because these transformations serve as the initial phase of the N2 catalytic cycle, the observed low efficiency impedes the turnover of the catalytic N2 reduction reaction.32−35

Research Status of Electrophilic Functionalization of Terminal Dinitrogen Complexes

Earlier investigations have been largely devoted to the formation of N–H,36−38 N–C,39−41 and N–Si bonds42−44 using transition-metal N2 complexes. Particular focus has been placed on 4d and 5d transition metals, especially Mo and W, which culminate in the Chatt45,46 and Schrock cycles.47,48 However, 3d transition-metal dinitrogen complexes are markedly inferior to their heavier counterparts in terms of N2 functionalization reactivities. At the intersection of the reactive group 6 metals and the first-row transition metals, Cr holds great potential to realize N2 activation and functionalization. However, the study of Cr dinitrogen complexes is relatively limited in the existing literature.49,50

This Account provides an overview of several novel Cr–N2 complexes bearing cyclopentadienyl, phosphine, and NHC ligands and their further transformations to diazenido and hydrazido intermediates. Under different ligand fields, electrophilic functionalization reactions followed either a distal or an alternating pathway and involved either even-electron or rare odd-electron processes. We hope that our work will further the understanding and development of dinitrogen activation and functionalization, enabling the creation of high-value-added nitrogen-containing organic compounds.

Chromium Dinitrogen Complexes Bearing Cyclopentadienyl-Phosphine Ligands

Synthesis of Cr(I)–N2

We designed and synthesized two types of cyclopentadienyl-phosphine ligands with two different linkers, L and L′.1,2,51,52 The latter one contains an additional methylene group between the cyclopentadienyl and phosphine moieties. The reaction of potassium salts LK and L′K with CrCl2 in THF generated chromium chloride complexes 1 and 2, respectively, in good isolated yields (Scheme 2). The solution magnetic moments of complexes 1a–c and 2a–c demonstrate their S = 1 spin ground states.53

Synthesis of Cr(II) Chlorides

The reduction of 1a, 1b, 2a, and 2b with one equivalent reductant (KHBEt3 or KC8) resulted in Cr(I)–N2 complexes 3a, 3b, 4a, and 4b in high isolated yields (Schemes 3 and 4). The nuclearity of these complexes depended on the substituent on the phosphine group. When the substituent is Ph, 3a and 4a show trinuclear structures; when the substituent is Cy or iPr, 3b and 4b show dinuclear structures. When a methylene linker is added between the phenyl ring and the cyclopentadienyl ring in the ligand framework, 4a and 4b display novel asymmetric structures, and the Cr centers are selectively coordinated by one or two N2 ligands. Complexes 3a, 3b, 4a, and 4b have solution magnetic moments of 4.1 ± 0.2, 2.9 ± 0.1, 5.2 ± 0.1, and 4.2 ± 0.1 μB at room temperature, respectively. The N–N bond lengths of the terminal N2 ligands in the Cr(I)–N2 complexes (1.052(5)–1.127(4) Å) are slightly shorter than those of the bridging N2 units (1.162(3)–1.1841(17) Å). The N2 moieties in the above two coordination modes can be readily distinguished by their characteristic N–N stretching vibrations of 1933–1981 and 1739–1752 cm–1, respectively, in their IR spectra.54,55 Both N–N bond lengths and N–N stretching vibrations of those Cr(I)–N2 complexes suggest a weak to moderate degree of N2 reduction (dN–N = 1.10–1.20 Å, νN–N = 1700–2331 cm–1).9

Reactions of Cr(I)–N2

We explored the reaction chemistry of Cr(I)–N2 complex 4a toward organic small molecules. The treatment of phenylsilane with 4a at room temperature resulted in the formation of mixed-valence dinuclear complex 5. This process caused the oxidative addition of the Si–H bond to one of the Cr centers, while the bridging N2 ligand was preserved (Scheme 5). Complex 5 has a solution magnetic moment of 3.3 ± 0.1 μB at 296 K. The presence of hydrogen on the Cr center was verified by 1H- and 2H -labeled NMR experiments. The N–N bond length of the bridging N2 ligand is 1.175(2) Å, and the IR spectrum shows one strong vibrational band at 1761 cm–1.

Reaction of Cr(I)–N2 and PhSiH3

Cr(I)–N2 complex 4a also exhibits reactivity with organic molecules containing double and triple bonds, such as azobenzene, benzylideneaniline, and 2-butyne. Oxidative addition reactions occurred on the Cr(I) centers, resulting in the formation of mononuclear Cr(III) complexes 6–8, accompanied by the release of N2 (Scheme 6). The solution magnetic moments of complexes 6–8 are consistent with the high-spin Cr(III) centers (S = 3/2). The above-mentioned reactivities of Cr(I)–N2 predominantly occur at the metal center rather than on the coordinated N2 unit. These reactions can be regarded as oxidative addition reactions of unsaturated substrates to the Cr(I) center, leading to a change in the oxidation state of Cr from Cr(I) to Cr(III).

Reaction of Cr(I)–N2 with Unsaturated Organic Compounds

Synthesis and Reactions of Cr(0)–N2

Due to the insufficient degree of reduction extent of the coordinated N2 in the Cr(I) complex, the N2 ligand always behaves as a leaving group, and the subsequent reactions typically occur at the metal centers. Thus, we used alkali metals (K, Rb, and Cs) as the reductants to get lower oxidation state Cr(0)–N2 complexes 9 (Scheme 7). Anionic mononuclear Cr(0)–N2 complexes 9a–c are diamagnetic, and the longer N–N bond lengths (1.132(2)–1.151(2) Å) and lower IR vibrational frequencies (1906–1907 and 1822–1823 cm–1) reveal more activated terminal end-on N2 units.

Synthesis of Mononuclear Cr(0)–N2

The reactions of complexes 3 and 9a with acids (H(OEt2)2)BArF4, [LutH]Cl, [LutH]OTf, etc.) produced trace amounts of ammonia or hydrazine (∼5% based on N atoms), while the methylation of 9a with MeOTf gave an unidentified mixture. When 9a was reacted with 1 equiv of Me3SiCl, a Cr(III) disilyl-hydrazido complex 10 was isolated in 45% yield, along with the regeneration of Cr(II) chloride 1b in 24% yield (Scheme 8). The crystal structure of end-on η1-hydrazido 10 features a Cr–N bond distance of 1.679(5) Å, indicating double-bond character, and the N–N bond distance in 10 is 1.372(8) Å (Figure 1). X-band EPR studies (Figure 2) established that complex 10 is a rare example of an S = 1/2 low-spin Cr(III) complex instead of the S = 3/2 spin state reported in ref (1). Complex 10 can effectively catalyze the silylation process of N2 to form N(SiMe3)3.

It is worth noting that the product distribution of Cr(III) hydrazido 10 and Cr(II) chloride 1b was found to be a ratio of 2:1. The total equation of the reaction can be balanced according to the coefficient of ′3-4-2-1′, wherein three Cr(0) centers were oxidized to two Cr(III) and one Cr(II). This observation indicates that unusual odd-electron reactions occurred during this silylation process. A similar distal pathway silylation was also reported by the Holland group.56 Their β-diketimate Fe system goes through the same process from Fe(0)–N2 to Fe(III) disilyl-hydrazido and Fe(II) iodide in a ratio of 2:1.

Chromium Dinitrogen Complexes Bearing Cyclopentadienyl and NHC Ligands

Synthesis of Cr(I)–N2 and Cr(0)–N2

Cr(II) chloride 11 (S = 2) could be easily obtained by mixing [Cp*CrCl]2 with NHC (IiPr2Me2). Different from analogues 1a–c and 2a–c (S = 1) bearing cyclopentadienyl-phosphine ligands, complex 11 possesses a high-spin ground state presumably due to the fact that NHC is a weaker π acid than the phosphine ligand. Cr(I)–N2 complex 12 and Cr(0)–N2 complex 13 could be generated by controlling the equivalents of reducing agent KC8 (Scheme 9).3 Complex 12 is paramagnetic and has a solution magnetic moment of 4.6 ± 0.1 μB, while complex 13 is diamagnetic. The crystal structure parameters and IR vibrational frequencies of complexes 12 and 13 are similar to those of their phosphine analogues. However, the differences between NHC and the phosphine ligand cannot be simply judged by the degree of N2 reduction. This motivated us to delve into the reaction chemistry of Cr(0)–N2 bearing an NHC ligand.

Synthesis of Cr(I)–N2 and Cr(0)–N2

Reactions of Cr(0)–N2

The silylation of the Cr(0)–N213 complex was carried out. As shown in Scheme 10, the reaction with 1 equiv of bulky iPr3SiCl gave Cr(II)–N=N–SiiPr3 diazenido complex 14 in 55% yield. When a less sterically hindered silyl reagent Me3SiCl was used, Cr(III) side-on η2-hydrazido complex 15 and Cr(II) chloride 11 were obtained in 43 and 24% isolated yields, respectively. Complexes 14 and 15 are paramagnetic and have solution magnetic moments of 2.9 ± 0.1 and 3.5 ± 0.1 μB at 296 K, thereby indicative of an intermediate-spin (S = 1) Cr(II) and a high-spin (S = 3/2) Cr(III) center, respectively. X-ray analysis of 14 shows that the Cr–N distance is 1.6975(17) Å and the N–N bond distance is 1.243(2) Å (Figure 3). The strong N–N bond vibration at 1534 cm–1 in the IR spectrum of 14 confirms the existence of an η1-diazenide fragment. Unlike η1-hydrazido complex 10, the crystal structure of 15 shows that N2 is converted to a side-on η2-hydrazido unit with a long single N–N bond distance of 1.4671(15) Å. The Cr–N bond distances of 1.9247(11) and 1.9287(11) Å are in the typical range found for Cr–N single bonds. Compared with the phosphine ligand, the NHC ligand leads to differences in both the structures and spin states of the Cr(III) hydrazido complexes. Complex 10 features a low-spin Cr(III) (S = 1/2) coordinated with an end-on [η1-NN(SiMe3)2]2– unit, whereas complex 15 exhibits a high-spin Cr(III) (S = 3/2) connected with a side-on [η2-Me3SiNNSiMe3]2– unit. Further investigations are needed to pinpoint the underlying factors that direct the reactions to proceed via distal or alternating pathways.

The total silylation reaction of 13 with Me3SiCl is quite similar to that observed with the phosphine analogue. The final products, Cr(III) η2-hydrazido 15 and Cr(II) chloride 11, also have a ratio of 2:1. A similar alternating pathway for silylation has been reported by Deng’s group in their Co(−1)–N2 system.57

Further Transformation of Cr(III) η2-Hydrazido

Protonation of 15 with H2SO4 led to the quantitative formation of hydrazine N2H4. Further transformations of the [η2-Me3SiNNSiMe3]2– unit to construct N–C bonds were also investigated (Scheme 11). The reaction of complex 15 with unsaturated substrates CO2 and tBuNCO was carried out. The reaction was initiated by nucleophilic attack of the N atoms in the [η2-Me3SiNNSiMe3]2– motif. N,O-chelating hydrazido Cr complex 16 was formed via 1,2-insertion of two CO2 ions into the N–Si and Cr–N bonds. Another N,O-chelating hydrazido Cr complex 17 was achieved via 1,2-insertion of one tBuNCO into the Cr–N bond with the removal of SiMe3. This type of insertion reaction with unsaturated bonds is similar to that with Chirik’s side-on coordinated Zr, Hf dinitrogen complexes, highlighting the nucleophilic nature of formal N24– moieties.58−60

Chromium Dinitrogen Complexes Bearing Cyclopentadienyl and Bisphosphine Ligands

Synthesis of Cr(I)–N2 and Cr(0)–N2

During the subsequent N2 functionalization reaction, one coordinated N2 ligand dissociated from Cr(0) bis-dinitrogen complexes 9a and 13 to start odd-electron reactions. This type of reaction is accompanied by the formation of Cr(II) chloride, limiting the theoretical yield of the target hydrazido product to 66.7%. In order to achieve more efficient functionalization reactions, we replaced the monophosphine and monocarbene ligands with a simple bisphosphine ligand and synthesized the corresponding dinuclear Cr(I)–N2 complex 19 and mononuclear Cr(0)–N2 complex 20 (Scheme 12).4 The magnetometric study of precursor Cr(II) chloride 18 revealed its S = 1 ground state. Compared with the monophosphine and monocarbene analogues, the reduction extent of N2 units is stronger in both 19 and 20, as evidenced by the longer N–N bond lengths and lower N–N bond-stretching vibrational frequencies. These results indicate that substituting one N2 ligand with a phosphine ligand allows the π-backdonation from the Cr center to be more effectively focused on a single N2 rather than being shared by two molecules of N2.

Synthesis of Cr(I)–N2 and Cr(0)–N2

Electrophilic Functionalization of Cr(0)–N2 with HOTf, MeOTf, and Me3SiOTf

We first tried the functionalization reactions of diamagnetic 20 with different electrophiles at low temperatures (e.g., 193 K). It was found that the single electron transfer (SET) side reaction was always unavoidable. Complex 19 was always observed as the major byproduct. This prompted us to use low-temperature fast UV–vis spectroscopy to systematically investigate the electrophilic functionalization reactions of Cr(0)–N2 complexes (Scheme 13). The existence of isosbestic points on the UV–visible spectra could clearly show that the stepwise protonation, alkylation, and silylation reactions with HOTf, MeOTf, and Me3SiOTf in each step were quantitative conversions at low temperatures.4 As shown in Figure 4, upon treatment with one equivalent MeOTf with 20b in a 2-MeTHF solution at 153 K, an isosbestic point at 620 nm was observed, indicative of the quantitative formation of Cr(II)–N=N–Me (21-Me). Similarly, the quantitative protonation of 21-Me to furnish [Cr(IV)=N–N(H)Me]+ (22-Me/H) is evidenced by the appearance of another isosbestic point at 680 nm.

Kinetics of Electrophilic Functionalization of Cr–N2 Complexes

At 153 K, the corresponding diazenido intermediates of the first step were in situ identified as [CrII–N=NH] (21-H), [CrII–N=NMe] (21-Me), and [CrII–N=NSiMe3] (21-SiMe3). The second step could convert the diazenido to more stable hydrazido derivatives. A series of hydrazido complexes with the same or different substituents were characterized by in situ spectroscopy (UV–vis, NMR, and IR): [CrIV=N–NH2]+ (22-H/H), [CrIV=N–NMe2]+ (22-Me/Me), [CrIV=N–N(Me)H]+ (22-Me/H), [CrIV=N–N(SiMe3)H]+ (22-SiMe3/H), and [CrIV=N–N(SiMe3)Me]+ (22-SiMe3/Me). Detailed kinetic investigations demonstrated that the second-order reaction rate of the first electrophilic attack is comparable for H+, Me+, and Me3Si+, whereas that of the second step follows the order H+ > Me3Si+ ≫ Me+. Because of the limited lifetime of the intermediate [CrII–N=NH] (21-H) (21-H completely decays within 5 min at 153 K), 21-H could continue to be protonated to generate 22-H/H. Subsequent silylation and alkylation reactions cannot occur because they cannot compete with the self-decay of 21-H. In addition, due to the comparable reaction rates of the first and second protonation steps, the formation of 21-H and 22-H/H was observed simultaneously in the UV–vis spectrum when only 1 equiv of HOTf was added. The relative lifetimes of the diazenido species were measured to be [CrII–N=NSiMe3] (21-SiMe3) > [CrII–N=NMe] (21-Me) ≫ [CrII–N=NH] (21-H). We reasoned that increasing the steric hindrance of the substituent can effectively increase the lifetime of the diazenido intermediate. When the substituent was replaced by bulky SiiPr3, a stable diazenido complex [CrII–N=NSiiPr3] (21-SiiPr3) was isolated in 88% yield (Scheme 14). Crystal structure analysis of 21-SiiPr3 shows that the Cr–N bond distance is 1.7061(15) Å and the N–N bond is 1.224(2) Å (Figure 5). The strong N–N bond vibration at 1611 cm–1 in the IR spectrum of 21-SiiPr3 closely resembles those of 21-Me (1537 cm–1) and 21-SiMe3 (1573 cm–1), which supports our assignments of 21 as the diazenido species.

Molecular structures of diazenido complex 21-SiiPr3 (upper left) and the cation part of hydrazido complexes 22: 22-H/H (upper right), 22-Me/Me (middle left), 22-Me/H (middle right), 22-SiMe3/H (bottom left) and 22-SiMe3/Me (bottom right). Data are from ref (4).

Consequently, the life span of the diazenido intermediate and the rate of the second reaction dictate the final reaction outcome. On the basis of the kinetic findings, we optimized the experimental conditions and successfully realized a nearly quantitative preparation of unmixed and hybrid hydrazido complexes. Five Cr(IV) hydrazido complexes were isolated in high yield (82–96%), while the SET side reactions were effectively suppressed (Scheme 15). These hydrazido complexes were all characterized by single-crystal X-ray diffraction experiments. The crystal structures showed more pronounced structural distortions with even longer N–N (1.293(2)–1.327(3) Å) and shorter Cr–N (1.653(2)–1.6745(15) Å) bonds relative to those of diazenido species (Figure 5).

Synthesis of Cr(IV)=N–NR2

In our cyclopentadienyl and bisphosphine ligand Cr–N2 system, electrophilic functionalization of N2 could be achieved through two sequential two-electron processes in satisfactory yields. This mirrors the pathways observed in Mo and W analogues (Chatt cycle: M0 → MII → MIV; Schrock cycle: MII → MIV → MVI, M = Mo, W). It should be noted that it is easier for 3d metal chromium to undergo a SET reaction than its heavier 4d/5d counterparts. Consequently, a much lower reaction temperature (for Cr: below 193 K; for Mo, W: near room temperature) is required for Cr systems to suppress side reactions. In studies of 3d metal Fe–N2 complexes reported by the Peters and Holland groups, N2 functionalization reactions are often carried out near the melting point of the solvent.38,41 Addressing the challenges of these stringent reaction conditions is a pressing concern in the realm of 3d metal dinitrogen fixation chemistry.

Summary and Future Prospects

We have explored Cr-promoted N2 activation and functionalization reactions, especially the isolation and characterization of key diazenido and hydrazido key intermediates. The combination of cyclopentadienyl with phosphine and NHC ligands proves effective in stabilizing low-valent Cr centers and enables the isolation of a series of novel Cr(I)–N2 and Cr(0)–N2 complexes.

As for the Cr(I)–N2 complexes bearing cyclopentadienyl-phosphine ligands, the weaker π-backdonation of Cr(I) centers to N2 led to subsequent reactions mainly occurring on the metal centers. This was exemplified in oxidative addition reactions between Cr(I) centers and phenylsilane as well as unsaturated organic compounds. A further reduction of Cr(I) centers yielded more active mononuclear Cr(0)–N2 complexes. Bis-dinitrogen Cr(0) complexes have gratifying performances in silylation reactions. The reactions proceed via either distal or alternating pathways, regulated by the monodentate phosphine or NHC ligands. The total silylation processes follow the coefficient of ′3-4-2-1′ and do not require the participation of additional reducing agents. When three equiv of Cr(0)–N2 complexes react with 4 equiv of Me3SiCl, 2 equiv of Cr(III) disilyl-hydrazido complexes and 1 equiv of Cr(II) chloride are found to be the final products. Similar odd-electron silylation reactions have been reported sporadically in the literature, and the underlying reaction mechanisms need further in-depth investigations (Scheme 16).1,3,56,57 This type of disilylation reaction is not found in the Fe, Mo systems reported by the Peters,61,62 Ashley,63 Lu,64 and Mézailles65,66 groups.

Different Functionalization Reaction Types of Transition-Metal Dinitrogen Complexes

The dissociation of another molecule of N2 from the metal center of bis-dinitrogen complexes appears to be critical for such odd-electron processes. Upon occupying the coordination site with a bisphophine ligand, the electrophilic functionalization reactions follow the normal two-electron processes, and not only silylation but also protonation and alkylation could occur smoothly. Detailed kinetic investigations revealed the fact that even-electron electrophilic processes proceed stepwise and quantitatively, yielding transient Cr(II) diazenido intermediates and stable Cr(IV) hydrazido final products.

In our efforts with functionalization reactions of Cr–N2 complexes, we disclosed that the coordination numbers and ligand types dictate the final reaction outcome. The dissociation of an additional leaving group (e.g., another molecule of N2) at the metal center likely initiates odd-electron reaction pathways. Adjusting the π-acidity of the supporting ligand can affect the spin state of the metal center, thereby enabling distal or alternating pathways.

Overall, the unique N2 binding modes and diverse reactivities exhibited by Cr undeniably echo the characteristics of its heavier congeners, Mo and W. Our research on Cr not only deepens our understanding of the early-stage N2 functionalization via the distal and/or alternating pathways but also lays a robust foundation for the rational design of efficient catalytic cycles involving N2, leading to the generation of high-value-added nitrogen-containing organic compounds.
