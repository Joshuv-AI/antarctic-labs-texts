Impact and evolution of minimal invasive surgery in a centralized colorectal service: trends, safety and oncological outcomes across 15 years
Authors: Ali Toffaha, Mahmood Al-Dhaheri, Sherif Ahmed, Ayman Ahmed, Ibrahim Amer, Mahwish khawar, Ammar Aleter, Ejaz Latif, Hamza A Abdul-Hafez, Salwa Ahmed, Mohammed Yousif, Noof Al Naimi, Mohamed A Kurer, Amjad Parvaiz, Mohamed Abunada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimSpecialization with subsequent evolving minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has been increasingly adopted in colorectal cancer care. This study aims to evaluate the impact of centralization and team specialization on oncologic quality and patient safety in colorectal cancer over 15 years by comparing an early phase with a later (post-specialization) phases; and to describe trends in MIS adoption over unit’s progression period with the framework of MIS adoption.MethodsA retrospective cohort study that included all elective oncologic colorectal resections at a tertiary referral center for the past 15 years. Data was analyzed in five cohorts with timeline intervals consisting of three years duration from 2010 to 2024. Outcomes included MIS utilization, conversions, R0 resection, lymph node adequacy (≥ 12), major complications (Clavien–Dindo ≥ III), reoperation for leak, and length of stay (LOS).ResultsAmong 1,142 colorectal cancer resections, MIS adoption rose from 51.2% in 2012 to 93.6% in 2024. Robotic surgery, introduced in 2017, accounted for 31% of cases by 2022–2024. Oncologic quality improved significantly (R0 resection 87.6% to 96.0%, ≥ 12 nodes 88.8% to 96.9%; both p < 0.001). Major complications decreased from 12.3% to 8.8% and reoperation for leak from 4.3% to 2.1%. Mean LOS was 6.4 days for MIS vs. 8.4 days for open (p < 0.001), reducing to 5.5 days for MIS in 2024.ConclusionA phased centralization and specialization in colorectal unit enabled safe and effective adoption of laparoscopic and robotic surgery. In addition to increased proportion of colorectal resection being done using MIS technique, improved clinical and oncological outcomes were seen during the study period and unite development. MIS consistently shortened hospital stay without compromising safety, affirming its role as the standard in specialized care.Keywords: Minimal invasive surgery, Colorectal cancer, Colorectal surgery, Robotic surgery, Gastrointestinal malignancy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide, contributing significantly to cancer-related mortality and morbidity [1, 2]. Advances in surgical techniques over the past three decades have transformed the therapeutic landscape, particularly with the rise of minimally invasive surgery (MIS), including laparoscopic, robotic, and endoscopic approaches.

MIS has become the standard of care in colorectal cancer management [3–5]. However, adoption of MIS for rectal cancer has been more gradual, due to anatomical complexity and functional concerns [5]. To overcome these challenges, the robotic surgical platform has been increasingly utilized, offering three-dimensional visualization, enhanced instrument dexterity, stable camera control, and elimination of tremors, particularly in the narrow pelvic cavity [5].

Beyond surgical approach, patient outcomes are strongly influenced by surgeon subspecialization and institutional volume. Multiple population-based studies and meta-analyses demonstrate that colorectal cancer patients treated by colorectal specialists and in high-volume centers experience improved short and long-term outcomes, including higher rates of optimal resection, lower perioperative mortality, fewer complications/reoperations, greater use of MIS, and better survival- when compared with care delivered by general surgeons or low-volume providers [6]. Contemporary commentaries and national data further argue for service centralization and specialist-led rectal cancer programs, given the technical demands of high-quality TME and pelvic nerve preservation [7]. This context supports our institution’s structured subspecialty pathway for colorectal surgery unit, MIS adoption and quality assurance.

We conducted a retrospective cohort study to evaluate the impact of centralization and team specialization on oncologic quality and patient safety in colorectal cancer over 15 years by comparing an early phase with a later (post-specialization) phases; and to describe trends in MIS adoption over unit progression period with the framework of specialization and MIS adoption.

Methodology

Study design and setting

We performed a retrospective observational cohort study of adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent elective oncologic colorectal resections for primary colorectal malignancy at tertiary center between January 2010 and December 2024.

The study took place at Hamad medical corporation (HMC), Qatar. Key service milestones included the establishment of a centralized specialist colorectal surgery unit in 2010, to which all colorectal cancer cases over the country were referred; the introduction of a multidisciplinary tumor board in 2011; and the introduction of robotic colorectal resection in 2017 whist the structured robotic colorectal program got established in 2020.

Data collection and patient selection

Using electronic medical record, operative notes, histopathology reports, and multidisciplinary team records, we include consecutive adults (≥ 18 years) who underwent elective oncologic colorectal resections with curative intent for primary colorectal cancer between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2024. Patients who have non-primary colorectal neoplasms, underwent emergency or palliative resections, or underwent local excisions without formal oncologic resection (endoscopic or transanal local excision) were excluded from the study. Also, we exclude any records lacking essential data for the study.

Surgical procedures

Operations adhered to oncologic principles with standardized mesocolic/mesorectal dissection, multivisceral or enblock resection of any involved nearby structure was undertaken when needed with the aim to achieve R0 resection. Surgical approaches included open, laparoscopic, and robotic platforms; conversions is defined by any open incision to facilitate tumor mobilization or achieving vascular control during the MIS procedure. Extraction site incision was only utilized for extraction of specimens.

Variables and outcomes

Baseline variables included patient demographics (age, sex, body mass index [BMI]) and physiological status (American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] classification). Tumor-related variables comprised the primary tumor site and distance from the anal verge as documented in preoperative assessment and operative notes. Operative variables intended surgical approach (open, laparoscopic, robotic) and conversion status (defined as an intended minimally invasive procedure completed via laparotomy).

Primary oncologic outcomes were R0 resection (negative longitudinal and radial margins on final pathology) and lymph node adequacy (≥ 12 nodes retrieved). Safety outcomes were major postoperative complications defined as Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III and reoperation for anastomotic leak. We also reported the postoperative length of stay (LOS), calculated in days from day of surgery to hospital discharge for the index admission.

We implemented a four-phase framework aligned with the launch of a specialized colorectal pathway. Each phase included predefined training, supervision, credentialing checkpoints, governance, and audit/feedback (Table 1).

Phase 1

Supervised Introduction (0–18 months): Introduced MIS for low-complexity right/left colectomy under proctoring by an experienced colorectal MIS surgeon. Credentialing phase1: minimum 10 supervised MIS colectomies (≥ 80% checklist adherence; conversion < 20% without safety compromise; no unaddressed critical incidents). Phase 2

Consolidation (18–36 months): Expanded indications (including select rectal resections with TME in carefully staged cases). Training: Animal and cadaveric lab for pelvic dissection; video-based peer review at monthly M&M. Credentialing phase 2: additional 20 MIS cases with complexity ladder (simple → intermediate), leak ≤ published benchmarks for case-mix; maintain conversion ≤ 15% for intermediate cases. Introduced standardized ICG perfusion assessment and anastomosis bundles where available.

Optimization (≥ 36 months): full indication spectrum by credentialed surgeons and introduction of MIS for multivisceral resection and locally advanced tumors; cross-coverage within the specialized team to maintain volume thresholds. Annual maintenance: ≥25 MIS colorectal resections/surgeon; targeted simulation for rare complications; quarterly KPI dashboard review with corrective action plans when any metric drifts beyond control limits. Each one of the trainees were expected to fulfill the above pre-requisites before embarking on hand on training under direct supervision. On average 30 h of simulated training (in designed center belong to Hamad Medical Corporation called ITQAN [8] was achieved by each of the trainees completing competence score in each module of simulation, in parallel to bedside assistance.

Once the trainees complete the above pre-requisites, they proceed to hands-on training under direct supervision of the trainer. The training commences as per modules described in EARCS and assessed using the Global Assessments Score form (GAS) [8].

The EARCS contains four modules, each containing several components. These include robotic docking, colonic dissection, TME, and resection and anastomosis. Each component is scored from 1 to 6 (or not applicable if the step is not performed) with the scores given corresponding to the competence levels, the higher the score, the higher the competence level for each component. Each supervised operation was scored in real time by the proctor.

On an average the trainees performed a median of 18 supervised procedures the duration of that range between 30 and 40 weeks before they were assessed for competence and were allowed to perform independent procedures. However, the challenge remained in difficult low rectal cancer where they occasionally needed help from senior robotic surgeon.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board of Hamad Medical Corporation approved the study (MRC-01–25−1012). Data were collected in routine clinical practice and patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the analysis.

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

A total of 1,142 patients underwent elective oncologic colorectal surgery over the 15-year period. The patients distributed into five consecutive time intervals: 2010–2012 (n = 162), 2013–2015 (n = 180), 2016–2018 (n = 204), 2019–2021 (n = 268), and 2022–2024 (n = 328). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar across the five periods. Median age ranged from 56 years in 2010–2012 to 51 years in 2022–2024. Median BMI showed a small increased trend from 25.0 kg/m² in 2010–2012 to 27.0 kg/m² in 2019–2024 but did not differ significantly across intervals (p = 0.33). American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status was also stable, with 71.6–77.2% classified as ASA I–II and 22.8–28.4% as ASA III–IV. Tumor location was predominantly colonic in all periods (67.9% → 72.6%), with rectal cancers comprising the remainder. In the rectal cancer subgroup, the median distance from the anal verge remained essentially unchanged across cohorts (approximately 8.0–8.5 cm Overall, there were no statistically significant differences in the measured baseline variables between time intervals (Table 2).

Discussion

This paper evaluated our 15-year experience with centralization of specialized colorectal cancer service with progressive adoption of MIS at a tertiary care hospital. The phased introduction of laparoscopic and subsequently robotic approaches was undertaken alongside the structured development of the unit, including dedicated multidisciplinary pathways, standardized operative training, and continuous outcome monitoring.

Beyond reaffirming the value of specialized colorectal pathways, our contribution is a pragmatic framework for stepwise MIS adoption. By sequencing simulation and proctored cases, setting explicit credentialing thresholds, codifying escalation/conversion policies, and auditing KPIs in a live registry, we observed increased MIS use alongside improvements in oncologic and perioperative outcomes. We provide phase definitions, credentialing criteria, and KPIs so that developing units can adapt the model to their resources and case-mix.

This dedicated process of unit development enabled a safe and significant transition from open to minimally invasive techniques. Our findings showed that the unit transformation was associated with progressive improvements in oncologic quality, enhanced operative efficiency, and a steady decrease in postoperative complications, confirming the critical role of specialization and institutional growth in achieving high-quality outcomes.

Over the past few decades, colorectal surgery has undergone a significant shift from open procedures toward less invasive alternatives, including laparoscopic and robotic surgery [12]. This evolution has been driven by the well-established advantages of laparoscopic surgery, such as reduced postoperative pain, decreased opioid requirements, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery of bowel function. Robotic surgery, emerging more recently, offers enhanced three-dimensional visualization, improved ergonomics, and the ability to overcome some of the technical limitations of traditional laparoscopy [13–15].

In our colorectal unit, we observed a significant increase in the adoption of MIS over the open surgery throughout the study period, reaching 93% in the most recent 3-year interval. This trend aligns global patterns and is consistent with the findings from the recent study by Azevedo et al., who reported a 90% adoption rate of MIS among 661 patients undergoing colorectal cancer resection at the Champalimaud Foundation in Portugal between 2012 and 2023 [13].

In our study, there was a significant reduction in LOS days. The mean LOS was 6.4 days for MIS compared with 8.4 days for open surgery, decreasing to 5.5 days in the most recent period. Our findings align with published literature, reflecting the impact of unit specialization and structured MIS adoption. The COLOR II trial reported median stays of 8 days for laparoscopic versus 9 days for open surgery, the ACOSOG Z6051 trial found means of 7.3 versus 7.0 days, and the ROLARR trial reported 8 days for both approaches. Specialized centers often achieve even shorter stays. Such as, a large Italian colorectal unit reported median LOS 6 days for laparoscopic resections (vs. 9 days open) [16]. A recent U.S. center found mean LOS 4.35 days after minimally-invasive rectal surgery (vs. 8.48 days for open) [17]. Moreover, these findings align closely with the Azevedo et al. study, which reported a median length of stay of 6.7 days. Krieg et al., documented a mean hospital stay of 11 days among 4,525 colorectal cancer patients undergoing MIS across 36 hospitals in Germany [18].

The maturation and specialization of our colorectal service were central to the outcomes reported. Beginning with the formal establishment of a centralized colorectal unit in 2010, the team introduced an institutional MDT in 2011 (restructured to a dedicated colorectal MDT in 2018) and phased a structured MIS and robotic adoption pathway (first robotic case was 2017). These programmatic milestones, combined with standardized operative modules, stepwise proctoring, and continuous outcome audit, coincided with a progressive shift from open to minimally invasive surgery (51.2% → 93.6%) and a marked reduction in conversions (7.4% → 1.8%). Moreover, oncologic quality improved in parallel, as R0 increase from 87.6% to 96.0%; and ≥ 12 nodes from 88.8% to 96.9%.

A multivariable models showed that calendar time/unit experience (per 3-year step) was the dominant predictor of R0 and node adequacy, supporting a systems-level rather than technique-specific explanation for these findings. At the same time, MIS adoption provides consistent efficiency benefits, up to 2-day reduction in length of stay, without compromising safety. Together, these findings highlight that safe MIS implementation depends less on isolated technology and more on institutional design, centralization, sustained subspecialization, and structured training were key enablers of high-quality, efficient colorectal cancer care in our unit.

We observed a rising rate of R0 resections over the study period, with an overall R0 rate of 92%, indicating a high level of oncologic adequacy across our cohort. While this finding reflects the successful transition from open to minimally invasive techniques without loss of surgical quality, our multivariable analyses indicate that these findings were more strongly associated with the unit maturation and specialization than with surgical approach, after adjustment there was no independent difference in R0 rates between open and MIS procedures. These results are consistent with those reported by Azevedo et al., who observed a 95% R0 resection rate in their cohort, and are supported by major randomized controlled trials in both colon and rectal cancer, such as the COLOR II and ACOSOG trials [13, 19–23].

A major concern during the transition to MIS is the potential for increased perioperative complications during the learning curve and training phases. Interestingly, our study demonstrated a notable decline in complication rate, presented as CD ≥ 3, throughout the study period with an overall rate of 9.9%. This is lower than previously published articles, including the ACOSOG trial that reported severe complications (CD ≥ 3) in up to 22% of patients undergoing laparoscopic resection [22], and also lower than ALaCaRT trial [23]. Our results are comparable to those of Azevedo et al., who reported a 11% rate of CD ≥ 3 complications [13].

Regarding the anastomotic leak, we observed a progressive decline in leakage rates over time, reaching 2.1% in the final study period. However, the trend didn’t reach statistical significance (P = 0.14). The overall rate is 2.7% which is appropriate in comparison to previously published data, including 4.2% in Azevedo et al., 8.5% in the CLASICC trial, 11% in the ROLARR trial, and 12% in the COLOR II trial [13, 19–21]. The incidence of anastomotic leak is multifactorial and influenced by perioperative chemotherapy, tumor characteristics, the type and location of the anastomosis, use of diverting stomas, surgeon experience, patient comorbidities, and quality of postoperative care.

Strength and limitations

The strengths of our study are its Large, contemporary cohort (N = 1,142) spanning 15 years, enabling robust temporal analyses, additionally the prospectively maintained database with predefined outcomes improved data completeness. The clear implementation timeline (centralized unit, MDT, robotics) allowed phase-wise evaluation of adoption. Lastly multivariable adjustment for case-mix (age, sex, BMI, ASA, site, procedure).

The limitations included the retrospective, single-center design that may limit generalizability, the period (“time/experience”) may also capture secular improvements (Anesthesia, peri-operative oncology). Moreover, potential selection bias and unmeasured confounding (e.g., tumor stage, neoadjuvant therapy, anastomotic level/diversion, comorbidities). We studied only the short-term outcomes, and for best understanding long-term oncologic and functional outcomes need to be assessed.

Future directions

Incorporate tumor stage and neoadjuvant variables; perform rectal-only stratifications; consider mixed-effects models with surgeon clustering; add sensitivity analyses by intended approach; and report long-term oncologic and functional outcomes as the program matures.

Conclusion

Over 15 years, centralization and subspecialization within our colorectal unit enabled the safe and effective adoption of laparoscopic and robotic surgery, yielding a marked shift away from open procedures. This transition occurred without compromising safety or oncologic outcomes and was associated with shorter hospital stays. In adjusted analyses, improvements were independently associated with the unit’s development. Key enablers included service centralization, subspecialist surgeons, a structured unit-progression framework, and a strengthened multidisciplinary team (MDT) process, which together supported the safe, progressive uptake of MIS.
