Tunable arbitrarily located quasi-corner states in multiphase weak topological insulator
Authors: Minhan Lou, Xinkuo Li, Jianrong Qiu, Dezhi Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the established symmetry-based higher-order topological insulator (HOTI) phase classification, zero-dimensional (0D) topological states are treated as a pure bulk topology phenomenon determined only by symmetry indicators. Here we report unconventional compactly supported 0D topological states termed as quasi-corner states located protected by two-dimensional (2D) weak topology at desirable vacancy-defects in 2D anisotropic honeycomb lattices (AHLs). Two weak topological indexes are derived to classify four topological phases and the corresponding positions of Wannier charge center. The emergence of quasi-corner state at the boundary site requires that the Wannier center is located at the site’s broken bond. Quasi-corner states do not exist under strong topological phase. At single vacancy defect, the three weak topological phases correspond to three quasi-corner states localized at different sites, which enables localization position tuning by uniaxial strain. Topology-protected long-range coupling with dynamic oscillation of coupled quasi-corner states is also observed and allows flexibly tunable light localization.

Introduction

0D topological states in 2D systems facilitate topological protection against disorder to subwavelength nano cavity1, laser generation2,3, photonic crystal fibers4, orbital angular momentum (OAM) mode transport5,6, optical coupling and switching7, nonlinear control8,9, and quantum computing10–12. Corner states13,14 in 2D conventional periodic second-order HOTI15,16 are typical 0D topological states induced at lattice corners of symmetry-enforced boundary17–19. HOTIs are further generalized to include aperiodic structure hosting topological defects20 such as disclination centers21–23, and Dirac-vortex centers24 with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}$$\end{document}Cn rotational symmetry generated by non-uniform deformation of periodic HOTI. Inner corner states can also be realized in rotationally symmetric fractal HOTI generated by the same unit cell for periodic HOTIs25,26. The experimental 2D HOTIs are typically rotational symmetric \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbb{Z}}}_{2}$$\end{document}Z2 TIs with two topological phases: non-trivial and trivial phases corresponding to the simultaneous creations and annihilation of all 0D states, respectively27–30. Creation and tuning of multiphase 0D topological states at more available locations would not only enable various applications like topological multimode lasing31–33 and topology-protected high-throughput communication and quantum computing34,35, but also be essential for topology switching in programmable topological devices36,37. Creating 0D topological states at arbitrarily shaped boundaries beyond the rotation-symmetry-protected defects challenges the conventional bulk-boundary correspondence (BBC)38,39, and has never been demonstrated in HOTIs.

The universal topological indicator is lacking for all types of 0D states in HOTIs, and the BBC varies from lattice to lattice40. Bulk polarizations and fractional corner charge are calculated differently for various \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{n}$$\end{document}Cn rotational symmetries41. To simplify higher-order phase classification and topological invariant calculation, reported theoretical and experimental works tend to enforce certain space-symmetry in the boundary and lattice geometry17,21,27. For example, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{4}$$\end{document}C4 rotational symmetry and mirror symmetry are enforced in the study of 2D square lattice so that the multipole moments can be quantized and easily calculated, but the corner states continue to exist under aforementioned symmetry-breaking13. Fractional disclination charge derived from rotational symmetry indicator manifests the bulk-disclination correspondence at the disclination center, and preservation of the original unit cell at the center is required22. Besides that, vortex-type topological HOTIs24,42, in principle, can only allow one defect center in the whole lattice. The inner corner mode locations in the inner boundary of the fractal HOTI constructed by the fractal generator are also not arbitrary26. Such intrinsic topology classification43 using unnecessary symmetries hinders the study of underlying symmetry-protected robustness to disorder and significantly restricts possible locations for 0D states. The extrinsic second-order topological classification accounts for the corner geometry and defines a topological phase with respect to the preservation of bulk and boundary gaps43,44, which allows for more generic corner states but raises a challenge for a new HOTI theoretical framework.

Here, we propose and realize the 0D weak topological quasi-corner states at vacancy-defects or desirable edges in 2D AHLs of 3D photonic structures inscribed in glass by ultrafast laser direct writing. In the single vacancy case, three non-equivalent quasi-corner states can be excited at different positions under exclusive strain conditions, which indicates the existence of multiple topological phases beyond traditional \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbb{Z}}}_{2}$$\end{document}Z2 classification. This proves that formation of topological defects does not necessarily require rotational symmetry protection, and a simple vacancy in a highly uniformly strained lattice can also generate topological 0D states. Furthermore, the conventional topological classification approach fails by using common topological indices to characterize all 0D states in an asymmetric lattice with arbitrarily located and arbitrarily shaped defects. Instead, we introduce \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathbb{Z}}}_{2}\times {{\mathbb{Z}}}_{2}$$\end{document}Z2×Z2 weak topological indexes45,46 analog to 3D weak topological indexes to characterize four topological phases and corresponding four available positions (four Wyckoff positions of the AHL unit cell) for the Wannier charge center of AHL. When the Wannier center is at the AHL hexagonal center, the topological phase is conventionally named as a strong topological phase due to the existence of a gapless Dirac cone46. The remaining three topological phases are all called weak topological phases due to the common bandgap opening feature, and they are distinguished by the Wannier center location at different unit bond centers. The emergence of quasi-corner states is determined by whether the broken bond of the defect site contains a Wannier charge. Compared with other vortex-type topological defects, more defects can be easily generated by simply removing more atoms. AHLs with only single 0D states, multiple uncoupled 0D states, with multiple coupled states, or a mixture of coupled and uncoupled 0D states can all be easily realized by choosing different combinations and geometries of vacancy defects. We further study the coupling between multiple 0D states when multiple defects exist. Coupling strength can be controlled by strain strength or the separation distance between coupled 0D states. The high degree of freedom in choosing location and number of defects greatly enriches the physics and functionality of the photonic devices. The proposed real-space analytical expression provides an alternate perspective and rigorous proof of the robustness against random disorder breaking any rotational symmetry, mirror symmetry, or translational symmetry, which lays the foundation for the future study of amorphous topological insulators47. Our theoretical and experimental findings expand our understanding of the bulk-corner/defect correspondence in HOTI, weak topological insulators, and the functionality of photonic topological devices.

Even though each weak topological phase corresponds to a unique Wannier charge position, it is not possible to predict the emergence of 0D state without knowing the defect shape (Supplementary Fig. 2c–n). The one-to-one correspondence between weak topological phases and three different 0D states only applies to vacancy with one missing site (Supplementary Fig. 2c–e). Instead, whether a quasi-corner hosts a 0D state can be unambiguously determined by whether the broken bonds of the quasi-corner have a Wannier charge (the Wannier charge center is located at the broken bond). For example, when broken bond \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({{{{\rm{A}}}}}_{1},{B}_{1})$$\end{document}(A1,B1) of quasi-corner A1 has Wannier charge due to strong strain \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${h}_{i} > 0.5$$\end{document}hi>0.5, Q1 quasi-corner state localizes at A1 (Supplementary Fig. 2c). In contrast, the broken bonds of quasi-corner A2 and A3 do not have the Wannier charge, and thus A2 and A3 do not host 0D state. This extrinsic topological classification applies to defect sites of arbitrarily shaped vacancy such as vacancy defect containing two nearby missing sites in Supplementary Fig. 2f–n. For some vacancies, no quasi-corner state emerges in one of the weak topological phases because the broken bonds of all defect sites do not contain Wannier charge in those phases (Supplementary Fig. 2g, k). In some vacancy geometries, defect sites have two broken bonds and one remaining bond connected to the bulk. Such defect boundary site also follows the same localization criteria as sites with only one broken bond (Supplementary Fig. 2l–n). Since Wannier charges are located at all equivalent Wyckoff positions in the whole lattice, the positions of the 0D state can be at any position, not limited to the lattice corners or disclinations. Furthermore, because the Wannier center is not at any bond center of AHL in a strong topological phase, quasi-corner states cannot emerge for any geometries.

When zigzag-bearded ribbon is weakly strained, and under a strong topology phase, a non-compact 0D state can still be obtained by partial integration of edge states. For example, the gapless zero-energy flat band (Fig. 4g) in an unstrained zigzag-bearded ribbon consists of both zigzag edge states and bearded edge states. Since zigzag edges only occupy small portion of the flat band around \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${k}_{x}=\pm \pi /{L}_{x}$$\end{document}kx=±π/Lx, SWI at the zigzag edge site produces a strongly diffused wave pattern (Fig. 4h). Bearded edge states occupy a large portion of the full flat band, thus the residual light intensity is higher at the probed waveguide at the bearded edge site (Fig. 4i). However, the gapless 0D state is not protected by weak topology and can be easily destroyed by lattice disorder54,55. We can also observe weak topological quasi-corner states in the obliquely strained armchair ribbon (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Note 9) and twig edge ribbon (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Note 10).

Robustness of the quasi-corner state against random disorder

The analytical expression provides a clear fundamental clue to the robustness against general hopping disorder for the quasi-corner states. Random position disorder does not break sub-symmetries (sublattice chiral symmetry), the state energy is fixed at zero56. However, in the experiments on-site energy detuning, on-site energy disorder, and next-nearest-neighbor hopping can all break the chiral symmetry and shift the 0D state energy from zero energy. Large chiral symmetry breaking can strongly delocalize the quasi-corner state as the energy shift brings the 0D state closer to the bulk spectrum. We use a large enough lattice constant so that next-nearest-neighbor hopping effect is negligible (Supplementary Note 11). The observation of strong 0D localization indicates that all chiral symmetry breaking effects are very weak. Other experimental issues, such as lattice size effect and boundary reflections effect, can potentially affect the observation of 0D localization (Supplementary Note 12).

Discussion

In this work, we provide a theoretical framework and experimental realization of quasi-corner states protected by multiphase weak topology at desirable, arbitrarily shaped boundaries such as vacancy-defects, and line-defects in optical coupled waveguide arrays, which is not possible for conventional corner states in previously reported HOTIs with enforced spatial symmetry. Quasi-corner states can be efficiently excited by direct injection of light at the localized waveguide and are robust against general disorder and certain defects. The 0D state can continue to exist with chiral symmetry breaking from various effects such as onsite-energy disorder and next-nearest-neighbor hopping. The established analytical expression offers a new real-space analysis approach for the localization and robustness of the topological state. More importantly, multiphase topology in AHL is not only switched between trivial and non-trivial cases but also locks to certain localization positions, which provides unique tunability when AHL is implemented with deformable material57 or programable setup58. For example, when light with different wavelengths is injected into different phase-locked localized waveguides, the transmitted light wavelength can be selected by applying strain. Although only the single-mode waveguides are demonstrated in this manuscript, more interesting physics can be discovered for AHLs composed of multimode waveguides59,60. The allowance of multiple degenerate 0D states permits high-capacity topological state encoding and communications61. Coupled quasi-corner states networks demonstrate an alternate controllable strain-dependent long-range coupling mechanism in a 2D system62. Our idea paves the way for general phase classification in HOTIs with only time-reversal symmetry, and provides new opportunities for non-linear and non-Hermitian physics31,32 in weak TIs. In summary, the highly strained photonic honeycomb lattice not only presents novel topology physics, but also has great potential to empower more advanced and compact topological photonic devices.

Methods

Fabrication

3D waveguides in glass were written by a femtosecond laser with a wavelength of 1030 nm, a pulse duration of 169 femtoseconds, and a repetition frequency of 1 MHz. The laser beam is focused into Eagle XG glass at a desirable depth using a 50x, 0.8-NA Nikon microscope objective. The platform that controls the movement of the sample is a set of 3-axis high-precision Aerotech airbearing linear stages with a scanning speed of 5 mm/s.

Optical characterization

To probe a 0D state, we use the single waveguide injection method by focusing a Gaussian laser beam (wavelength of 850 nm) at the input end of the selected waveguide, and the image of light emerging at the output facet of the waveguide arrays is captured by a CMOS camera (Supplementary Fig. 4a).
