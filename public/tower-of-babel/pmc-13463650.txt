Adherence to the MIND (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay) diet and depression, stress, and anxiety: a systematic review
Authors: Mona Golmohammadi, Seyedeh Sarina Kabiri, Fatemeh Sadeghi, Sayyid Ali Mousavi Jam, Seyed Mostafa Nachvak, Vahideh Ebrahimzadeh Attari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression and anxiety are prevalent mental disorders that contribute significantly to global disability. While the MIND diet has demonstrated benefits in preventing Alzheimer’s disease, its potential impact on mental health outcomes remains unclear. This study systematically reviews evidence on the association between adherence to the MIND diet and symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress.MethodsWe conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases up to August 2025 to identify observational and interventional studies examining the relationship between the MIND diet and depression, anxiety, or stress. Twenty one eligible studies—including eleven cross-sectional designs—were included.ResultsOf the 21 studies, twelve reported a significant inverse association between adherence to the MIND diet and depression symptoms, seven found similar associations with anxiety, and three with stress. However, findings were inconsistent, likely due to heterogeneity in study design, assessment tools, and populations.ConclusionCurrent evidence suggests that greater adherence to the MIND diet may be associated with lower levels of depression and anxiety, but the overall quality and consistency of the evidence are limited. Well-designed longitudinal and clinical trials are needed to confirm these potential benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-026-01305-4.

Introduction

Mental disorders are among the leading causes of disability worldwide [1]. According to recent estimates, 4.4% of the world’s population suffers from depression, wherease 3.6% are affected by anxiety disorders [2]. Mental disorders are associated with higher morbidity, including resistant hypertension (HTN) and cardiovascular and gastrointestinal diseases [3, 4]. This underlines the importance of prioritizing mental health in public health policymaking. Current popular approaches to treating mental disorders include psychopharmacological and psychotherapeutic methods. Despite their established effectiveness in treating depression, many individuals fail to achieve complete remission [5].

Previous research has demonstrated the association between diet and mental health [6]. The relationship between dietary habits and mental health is bidirectional; dietary choices can significantly influence mental well-being, while mental state can also affect an individual’s food preferences and eating behaviors [7]. Nutrients such as omega-3 fatty acids, tryptophan, and B vitamins are essential for improving mental health, as they contribute to the production of neurotransmitters that regulate mood and cognition [8–10].

Numerous studies investigated how dietary patterns influence anxiety and depression. A meta-analysis of 16 intervention studies demonstrated the effectiveness of dietary interventions in reducing depressive symptoms [11]. Accordingly, pro-inflammatory dietary patterns are associated with a significant increase in the prevalence of depression [12–14]. The Mediterranean diet is considered healthy, with a high intake of plant-based foods such as vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains. It emphasizes the use of extra virgin olive oil as the primary source of fat, contains moderate amounts of fish and restricts the consumption of sweets, red meat and processed foods [15]. This dietary pattern has been associated with lower psychological distress, which is likely due to its favorable nutrient composition [16]. In a randomized controlled trial, participants with self-reported depression who participated in a Mediterranean dietary intervention —including food hampers, cooking classes, and fish oil supplementation— showed a significant reduction in depressive symptoms and an improvement in psychological quality of life after three months compared to a control group that participated in social support sessions [17]. Another healthy eating plan is the DASH (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) diet, which emphasizes consuming fruits, vegetables, nuts and low-fat dairy products [18]. It was associated with a significant reduction in both depression and anger levels after 14 weeks of dietary intervention [19].

Moreover, the recent MIND (Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay) diet represents a novel nutritional concept that integrates components of the Mediterranean and DASH diets. This innovative approach has been shown to have positive effects on the risk of Alzheimer’s disease and the improvement of cognitive function [20, 21]. The MIND diet is characterized by several key components that promote brain health, including green leafy vegetables, berries [20, 22] and omega-3 fatty acids [23]. Furthermore, the MIND diet restricts the consumption of foods detrimental to brain health, including red meat, cheese, and margarine [20].

Given the novelty of the subject, research on the relationship between MIND dietary patterns and mental health, especially anxiety and depression, is growing. Therefore, the present study aimed to systematically review the existing literature on the association of the MIND diet with anxiety and depression, along with its mechanisms of action.

Methods

The search strategy

The present systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [24]. Although a formal study protocol was not prospectively registered, a predefined approach was followed throughout the review process. A comprehensive literature search was executed across several databases, including PubMed/Medline, Web of Science, Scopus, and the Google Scholar search engine, covering publications up to August 2025. This search strategy considered relevant keywords related to the MIND diet and mental health and specifically focused on terms such as “MIND diet”, “Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay”, “depression”, “anxiety”, “stress”, and “mental health”, which appeared in the titles or abstracts of the articles.

The screening of studies

Two independent reviewers, MG and SSK, assessed the articles against the predefined inclusion criteria and eliminated those that were not relevant by examining the titles and abstracts of the articles. Subsequently, both researchers thoroughly reviewed the full texts of the remaining articles to confirm their eligibility for the study and to extract the required data. Further relevant studies were found by reviewing the reference lists of the selected articles. Any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved with the help of a third author, SMN. All studies found during this process were organized in an EndNote library, with duplicate references removed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included all relevant observational studies and clinical trials published in English, regardless of the publication date. Animal studies, in vitro models, conference abstracts, review articles, protocols and any research that did not provide sufficient data on the relationship between the MIND diet and mental health were excluded.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data collection was conducted using a standardized extraction form that recorded various details, including author name, country of origin, type of research, population or sample size, average age of participants, type and duration of intervention, mental health assessment tools, and dietary intake. Additionally, the form recorded potential confounding variables and the primary outcomes of each study.

The quality of the studies was evaluated using the NOS (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) and Jadad checklists. The NOS assesses studies based on three key domains: selection, comparability, and outcomes, with scores ranging from 0 to 9. According to the NOS results, a score of seven or higher indicates a minimal risk of bias, while scores between four and six reflect a high risk of bias. Scores below four are considered to indicate a very high risk of bias [25].

In contrast, the Jadad checklist consists of five criteria, each of which must be answered with “yes” or “no”, resulting in a total score between 0 and 5. Scores of three or more on the Jadad checklist are interpreted as indicators of the high quality of the studies assessed [26].

The results of the quality assessment of the cross-sectional and case-control studies and clinical trials are presented in Supplementary Tables S1-3.

Results

Selection of studies & characteristics of the included studies

As shown in Fig. 1 and 204 potentially relevant articles were found in our initial search. A total of 81 articles were removed due to duplication. Of the 123 remaining articles, 99 were excluded after screening the title and abstract, and the full text of two additional studies could not be accessed [27, 28]. Thus, 21 studies were eligible for review.

The participants in all the studies were over 18 years old, except in Pu’s study (Mean age 10.9 years) [48]. Both sexes were included in all studies, except for six studies in which participants were female in five studies [40, 44–47] and male in the other one [33]. The designs of most studies were cross-sectional studies (n = 11) [29, 33, 34, 37–40, 42, 45, 46, 49], the remaining studies were 6 cohort studies [30–32, 35, 36, 48], 1 case-control study [41], and 3 randomized controlled trial (RCT) [43, 44, 47]. The studies were performed in Iran (n = 11) [29, 33, 34, 37, 39–41, 44–47], the USA (n = 3) [30, 31, 48], China (n = 3) [35, 43, 49], Spain (n = 1) [32], Greece (n = 1) [42], and the United Kingdom (n = 2) [36, 38] (see Table 1).

The MIND diet score is calculated on the basis of adherence to 15 dietary components and ranges from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to the diet. The scoring system includes 10 healthy and five unhealthy food groups for the brain, with each component scored from 0 to 1 depending on the frequency of consumption. A food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was used to assess the MIND diet score in most studies. However, in Vassilopoulou et al.’s study [42], participants were asked about their dietary habits using an instrument based on the MIND scoring matrix. Two studies (Golmohammadi et al. and Kabiri et al.) employed 3-day food records [44, 47], while in Pu et al.’s study, Child Nutrition Assessment (CNA) and Block Kids Food Screener (BKFS) were used to evaluate the dietary intake [48].

The evaluation of the MIND diet score differed among studies. Seven studies examined all MIND diet components and used the full score of 15 [30–32, 38, 40, 42, 43]. Ten studies used a total score of 14 because wine consumption was not included [29, 33, 36, 37, 39, 41, 44, 45, 47, 48]. In two studies, wine and olive oil were not considered, resulting in a total score of 13 [34, 46]. In two studies, a score of 12 were used based on the Chinese version of the MIND diet (cMIND) according to Chinese food culture [35, 49]. The assessment of mental health was also very heterogeneous, and the studies examined different areas of mental health, such as depression, anxiety, and stress. Various questionnaires were used to assess mental health in the studies, including the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale 21 (DASS-21) [29, 33, 37, 39, 40, 42, 44, 47], the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CESD) [30, 31, 36, 49], the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) [38], the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [34, 38], the General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) [34], the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 questionnaire (GAD-7) [41, 49], the Phenotypes and eXposures (PhenX) Toolkit [35], the State and Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) [43], the Profile of Mood States Short Form (POMS-SF) [43], the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) [45, 46], the Child Behavior Checklists (CBCL) [48], and the Spielberger Anxiety Questionnaire (SAQ) [46].

The most significant potential confounders, such as age, gender, marital status, energy intake, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, education level, tobacco use and pre-existing conditions, were adjusted in the analysis of most articles.

Quality of the articles

Based on the NOS checklist, all eleven cross-sectional studies, six cohort studies and the single case-control study were found to have a low risk of bias. In addition, three clinical trials were of high quality based on the Jadad checklist. The quality results of the studies are described in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

Association between the MIND diet and mental health

Cross-sectional studies

This review investigated the relationship between the MIND diet and mental health in 11 cross-sectional studies. In one study, a significant negative association was found between the MIND diet score and the likelihood of stress in obese people, but no significant association was found between the MIND diet score, depression and anxiety [39]. Another study indicated that 7156 individuals with higher MIND diet scores were significantly less likely to have depression and anxiety, but there was no significant association with psychological stress [29]. Lawrie et al. showed no significant association between the MIND diet score, depression and anxiety in their study on Parkinson’s disease [38]. In addition, one study revealed no associations between adherence to the MIND diet and the occurrence of depression, anxiety, and stress in Iranian male adults [33]. On the other hand, Salari-Moghaddam et al. found a negative association between adherence to the MIND diet and the incidence of depression and mental health problems but not with the incidence of anxiety in 3176 Iranian adults [34]. Vassilopoulou et al. examined 203 older people in a cross-sectional study (people with dementia: n = 115, healthy control subjects: n = 52, who described themselves as healthy: n = 36). They found that people who had fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety adhered to the MIND diet significantly better [42]. In another study of overweight and obese women, no significant difference was found between adherence to the MIND diet and the severity of depression, anxiety, and stress [40]. Javdan and colleagues, in their study of 214 women 2–8 weeks after childbirth, revealed that individuals with the highest adherence to the MIND diet had lower odds of experiencing postpartum depressive symptoms [45]. Another study found that greater adherence to the MIND diet was significantly associated with a lower risk of depression, anxiety, and stress among Iranian adults [37]. In one study of 350 breastfeeding women in Iran, after adjusting for confounders, no statistically significant association was observed between adherence to the MIND diet and anxiety and postpartum depression [46]. Niu et al. in a study of Chinese older adults, showed that individuals in the higher tertiles of the MIND diet had a significantly lower likelihood of developing depressive and anxiety symptoms compared with those in the lower tertiles [49]. Overall, a significant negative association was observed between adherence to the MIND diet and depression in 6 of the 11 cross-sectional studies [29, 34, 37, 42, 45, 49], anxiety in 4 studies [29, 37, 42, 49], and stress in 3 studies [34, 37, 39].

Cohort and case-control studies

The relationship between the MIND diet and the occurrence of depression was investigated in a total of 6 cohort studies conducted among adults, except for one study conducted among children aged 9–11 years [48]. Four studies showed a significant inverse association between the results of the MIND diet and the incidence of depression [31, 35, 36, 48]. However, the results of two other studies showed no significant differences between the MIND diet and the incidence of depression [30, 32]. In a case-control study (case: patients with anxiety disorders, control: healthy people), a significant inverse association was found between the MIND diet score and the incidence and severity of anxiety disorders [41].

Interventional studies

In the present review, three RCTs were systematically evaluated. In participants with stage 1 or 2 HTN, a four-week intervention with either the MIND diet al.one or the MIND diet combined with forest bathing significantly reduced anxiety compared with the control group [43]. Kabiri et al. conducted an eight-week RCT on overweight or obese women with PCOS, showing that adherence to the MIND diet significantly improved symptoms of depression and anxiety compared with the control group [47]. In the RCT by Golmohammadi et al. adherence to the MIND diet for twelve weeks resulted in a significant reduction in depression scores among women with type 2 diabetes and insomnia compared with the control group [44].

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the present study is the first systematic review assessing the association between the MIND diet and mental health adherence. The results of our study showed that among the 21 reviewed articles, twelve showed an association between adherence to the MIND diet and depression, seven with anxiety and three with stress. One key factor contributing to inconsistencies across studies is inadequate control for confounding variables. Unmeasured physical activity [30, 38, 46], education [32, 38, 40], and socioeconomic factors [30, 32, 33, 38, 40] may differentially influence both adherence to the diet and mental health outcomes across diverse populations. Another notable issue is the omission of wine consumption data in some studies, particularly those conducted in Iran, where alcohol use is restricted [33, 39]. Methodological heterogeneity further complicates interpretation. Variations in dietary assessment tools—especially the use of different versions of FFQs and scoring methods for the MIND diet—reduce comparability and may lead to under- or overestimation of true adherence. Mental health outcomes were also measured using diverse instruments with distinct psychometric properties and scoring algorithms, affecting the sensitivity and specificity of assessments and contributing to conflicting findings. Small sample sizes in several studies, including those by Cherian [30] and Lawrie [38], reduced statistical power and limited generalizability. Population heterogeneity also plays a significant role. Some studies have focused on specific groups, such as individuals with obesity [39, 40] or patients with Parkinson’s disease [38], whose unique characteristics may account for the differing results reported. Finally, the relatively short duration of interventions in some randomized controlled trials—for example, those lasting only four weeks—may be insufficient to detect meaningful changes in mental health outcomes [43].

Several studies have investigated the effects of a healthy diet on anxiety and depression, and their findings are consistent with those of this study. Gibson-Smith et al. found that longer and more severe anxiety and depressive disorders were associated with poorer diet quality [50]. This relationship is also supported by research on specific diets. A systematic review revealed that following a Mediterranean diet was associated with reduced anxiety and depression in children and adolescents [51]. Another study showed that adherence to a Western diet was associated with an increase in depression, while adherence to a Mediterranean diet was associated with a decrease in depression [52]. Furthermore, in a study by Tan et al. other healthy diets such as the DASH diet were shown to reduce anxiety and depression [53]. Additionally, adherence to the Mediterranean diet for three months was associated with improved mental health in individuals with stress, anxiety, and depression [54]. Some studies have reported conflicting results, likely due to differences in assessment tools, population diversity, or inadequate control of confounding variables. For example, Winpenny et al. found no association between dietary quality and depression in adolescents [55], and a 12-year cohort study reported no link between diet quality and depressive symptoms in middle-aged Australian women [56]. Given these inconsistencies and the fact that the MIND diet integrates elements of both the DASH and Mediterranean dietary patterns, this systematic review synthesized and evaluated studies assessing the association between adherence to the MIND diet and mental health outcomes, particularly depression and anxiety.

According to recent findings, the MIND diet has neuroprotective properties associated with improved cognition and age-related neurological deficits. The MIND diet is rich in bioactive substances such as carotenoids, polyphenols, and vitamins E, C and B, which are essential for the proper functioning of neurons [57–59]. B vitamins are essential as coenzymes for the synthesis of neurotransmitters related to depression [60]. Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) is an important cofactor in the production of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline and GABA (ϒ-aminobutyric acid). In addition to its essential role in melatonin production and the sleep-wake rhythm, this vitamin is also involved in regulating hormone secretion in the hypothalamic-pituitary axis. Pyridoxine also has an anti-inflammatory effect and reduces pro-inflammatory factors that play an important role in the development of mental disorders [61]. Folate and B12 are involved in the synthesis of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, noradrenaline and serotonin through their involvement in the metabolism of one-carbon units [60]. Moreover, by regulating homocysteine levels, these vitamins contribute to the prevention of excitotoxicity and cerebrovascular disease, thus protecting against depression [60, 62, 63]. The MIND diet is also rich in magnesium. There is evidence that magnesium is a key nutrient for brain function and mental health, involved in various enzymatic reactions, nerve transmission, and the formation of membrane structures and myelin. Through its role in neuromodulation and neurotransmission, this mineral protects neurons from overstimulation, which leads to the death of neurons in the cerebral cortex [64]. Recent evidence suggests that hyperactivation of glutamate receptors, including NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) receptors, is involved in the pathogenesis of mental disorders. Conversely, a reduction in the activity of these receptors has mood-improving and antidepressant effects. Magnesium acts as an antagonist of NMDA receptors and is, therefore, effective in improving mood disorders such as depression and anxiety [65]. Moreover, magnesium reduces ACTH (Adrenocorticotropic hormone) secretion, regulates adrenocorticotropic sensitivity to this hormone and prevents excessive HPA (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal) activation. In other words, magnesium deficiency is associated with increased stress and depression by dysregulating the HPA axis [66]. Recent findings emphasize the role of magnesium in the gut microbiota. Magnesium can cause changes in the microbiota that correlate with altered inflammatory and oxidative responses [67–69]. Furthermore, a negative correlation has been found between magnesium and inflammatory biomarkers such as IL-6 (interleukin-6), TNF-α (tumor necrosis factor-alpha) and CRP (C-reactive protein), which are associated with the pathogenesis of depression [70, 71]. Magnesium has antidepressant properties as it plays a crucial role in neurotransmitter activity [72, 73] and induces the expression of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor) [74]. As part of the MIND diet, berries are rich in various flavonoids, including flavanols and anthocyanidins. The positive effects of these flavonoids on oxidative stress, vascular health, and the gut microbiota may help alleviate depressive symptoms by supporting a more balanced neurobiological state [75].

The MIND diet al.so influences mental health through changes in the gastrointestinal microbiota [41]. Eating pro-inflammatory foods such as sweets, processed carbohydrates and fats instead of fiber, vegetables and whole grains can lead to dysbiosis and inflammatory responses in the body [76, 77]. The MIND diet al.so emphasizes the consumption of fish and nuts, which are important sources of omega-3 fatty acids. Omega-3 fatty acids have a positive effect on mental disorders such as depression by regulating neurotransmitters and signaling. These fatty acids are involved in the regulation of signal transduction by increasing the levels of membrane enzymes and protein kinase C [78, 79]. In mental disorders such as depression, the concentration of 5-HIAA (5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid), a metabolite of serotonin, is reduced in the cerebrospinal fluid [80]. In this context, omega-3 fatty acids can increase the 5-HIAA concentration in the cerebrospinal fluid [81]. Omega-3 fatty acids can modulate dysfunction of the HPA axis by decreasing corticosterone secretion [82]. Also, omega-3 fatty acids have anti-inflammatory properties and reduce the production of inflammatory markers such as CRP, TNF-α, and IL-6 [83]. Moreover, omega-3 fatty acids have antioxidant properties, increase the expression of heme-oxygenase-1, decrease the expression of cyclooxygenase-2 in microglia and modulate oxidative reactions in neuronal cells, which may contribute to their potential therapeutic effects on depression by reducing inflammation and oxidative stress [84, 85].

Compared with other typical diets, the MIND diet tends to contain more vitamin D. Recent studies have demonstrated the positive effects of vitamin D on depression and anxiety [86–88]. Vitamin D plays a role in the activation of brain cell receptors and has a direct effect on the nervous system. According to some evidence, the presence of vitamin D receptors in some brain regions such as the limbic system, the cerebellum and the cerebral cortex indicates the important role of this vitamin in the regulation of mood and behavior [89]. Vitamin D also has neuroprotective effects in the brain, partly due to its ability to modulate BDNF and neurotrophic signaling [90]. Vitamin D also has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, contributes to redox homeostasis and reduces neuroinflammation [91]. Vitamin D also exerts neurormodulatory effects by influencing the gut microbiota [92]. This vitamin also plays an important role in the expression of genes involved in serotonin synthesis [93]. The expression of tryptophan hydroxylase 2 and tryptophan hydroxylase 1 is induced and suppressed by this vitamin, respectively [94]. The mechanisms discussed are related to reducing anxiety and depression.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This is the first systematic review to examine the relationship between adherence to the MIND diet and mental health outcomes, such as depression and anxiety. Previous systematic reviews have investigated this dietary pattern in relation to aging or cognitive performance. Although quality assessments using the NOS and Jadad scales indicated a generally low risk of bias among the included studies, several important limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Most studies were cross-sectional or observational in design, making it difficult to establish a causal relationship between the MIND diet and mental health outcomes. The included studies also showed substantial methodological diversity, including heterogeneity in patient populations, variations in mental health assessment tools, and the use of different FFQ questionnaires to measure dietary intake. Reliance on self-reported data, small sample sizes, and omission of potentially relevant demographic confounders such as menopausal status further reduced the certainty and generalizability of the results. These factors introduce considerable variability and may compromise the consistency and interpretation of the findings.

In addition, information regarding depression severity and its potential influence on sample selection was not consistently reported or controlled across the included studies. This may have led to the underrepresentation of individuals with more severe depressive symptoms, introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability. The exclusion of non-English language studies may also have introduced language bias, potentially omitting relevant literature. Furthermore, the intervention periods in the included randomized controlled trials may have been too short to fully assess the effects of the MIND diet on mental health outcomes. This short duration limits both the validity and generalizability of the RCT findings and underscores the need for longer-term clinical trials.

Therefore, despite the generally low formal risk-of-bias ratings, these limitations call for cautious interpretation of our results and highlight the urgent need for more standardized, longitudinal, and well-controlled studies to clarify the direction and strength of the observed associations. Due to substantial heterogeneity in study designs (cross-sectional, cohort, case-control, and RCTs), populations, and mental health assessment instruments, conducting a meta-analysis was not considered appropriate or feasible for this review.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of the studies included in this review that investigated the relationship between the MIND diet and depression, anxiety, and stress are promising, but more studies are needed. Therefore, further well-designed clinical studies are needed to understand the relationship between the MIND diet, depression, anxiety, and stress to draw better conclusions.

Clinical and research implications

In this context, consideration of the key role of diet and eating habits in depression and anxiety is essential. Adherence to healthy dietary patterns, including the MIND diet, which includes brain-healthy foods and limits unhealthy food components, along with other lifestyle changes, has marked effects on the mental health of individuals and society in general.

Future clinical and research directions

Further clinical studies of sufficient duration and sample size should investigate the cause-effect relationship between adherence to the MIND diet and mental health, such as depression and anxiety. Further observational studies, particularly cohort studies with large samples, should also be conducted to confirm these findings.
