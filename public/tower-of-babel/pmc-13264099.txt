Targeting the ELF1/EGFR/ERK positive feedback loop overcomes resistance to androgen receptor inhibition in AR-Vs positive prostate cancer
Authors: Menghan Zhou, Longxin Deng, Xianglan Chen, Guanglin Yang, Yurun Huang, Chunlin Zhang, Yiping Yang, Yingxi Mo, Zhao Li, Shanshan Luo, Qingyun Zhang, Shan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Long-term enzalutamide exposure of AR-Vs positive PC cells triggers an AR-independent bypass through the transcriptional upregulation of EGFR.•Identification of an ELF1/EGFR/ERK positive feedback loop that sustains oncogenic signaling and drives enzalutamide resistance.•Pharmacological inhibition of EGFR using gefitinib restores enzalutamide (or mithramycin) sensitivity in resistant CRPC cells and patient-derived organoids.•Elevated EGFR protein expression correlates with high Gleason scores and serves as a robust predictor of shorter PFI and DFI in prostate cancer patients.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most common male urological malignancy and has the second-highest incidence and fifth-highest mortality worldwide among cancer-related malignancies [1]. Although prostatectomy, androgen deprivation therapy, and radiotherapy could be successful in most patients with localized PCa, some patients will eventually develop castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), which remains incurable and deadly [2,3]. CRPC treatment still poses significant challenges, primarily due to the complexity of drug resistance mechanisms and the lack of specific targets [4].

The androgen signaling axis is vital for PCa progression, and androgen synthesis is tightly regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [5,6]. Androgen receptor (AR) inhibition, by blocking androgen binding to AR or rapidly degradation of AR, reduces AR transcriptional activity and alleviating PCa symptoms [[7], [8], [9]]. Despite the initial therapeutic efficacy of enzalutamide, patients with PCa will eventually develop resistance to it; therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new strategies to address enzalutamide resistance in CRPC [10]. The AR-related signaling pathway is a key driver of enzalutamide resistance in CRPC, including abnormal AR amplification or overexpression [11,12], AR mutations [[13], [14], [15]], and the generation of AR-Vs [10,16]. Approximately 60% of metastatic CRPC cases exhibit AR gene amplification and mutations, and AR amplification is detected in approximately 50% of patients receiving enzalutamide treatment [17,18]. Additionally, AR-Vs, including AR-V7 and AR-V567es, lack a complete or functional ligand-binding domain (LBD) but retain the ability to bind DNA in an androgen-independent manner, thereby conferring resistance to AR inhibitors in patients [[19], [20], [21]]. Therefore, AR and AR-related pathways remain major targets for combating CRPC.

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) promotes cell proliferation and differentiation by activating intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity and downstream pathways (RAS/RAF/MEK, ERK, JAK/STAT, or PI3K/AKT/mTOR), positioning EGFR as a pivotal therapeutic target in oncology [22,23], whereas mutations and overexpression are the primary triggers for dysregulation of EGFR [24,25]. EGFR overexpression in various tumors, including breast cancer, lung cancer, PCa, and pancreatic cancer, leads to patient resistance to hormone therapy, cytotoxic agents, and radiotherapy. In PCa, EGFR overexpression is associated with high Gleason scores and advanced disease progression [26,27], and EGFR activation promotes tumor metastasis and recurrence [28]. However, the specific mechanisms of EGFR and enzalutamide resistance in PCa remain unclear. Here, we performed transcriptomic and proteomic sequencing in wild-type and enzalutamide-resistant androgen-insensitive cells, explored the EGFR role in enzalutamide-resistant PCa, and observed that pharmaceutical EGFR inhibition could enhance CRPC sensitivity to anti-AR treatment. This combination of existing clinical drugs facilitates rapid translation of our findings to the clinic.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and enzalutamide-resistant cell screening

22Rv1 cells were cultivated in DMEM media with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). LNCaP cells were cultured in RPMI media containing 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL). For enzalutamide-resistant cell screening, 22Rv1 cells were cultured in complete medium with 2 μM enzalutamide for 1month and continued with 5 μM enzalutamide for 2-3 months until single colonies formed. Single colonies were expanded and maintained in complete medium with 5 μM enzalutamide. For generating LNCaP-EnzaR cells, LNCaP cells were initially cultured in a complete medium with 1 μM enzalutamide for one month, and then with 2 μM enzalutamide for 2-3 months until single colonies were established. Single colonies were cultivated and maintained in a medium complete with 2 μM enzalutamide.

Cell proliferation assay

Briefly, 2000 22Rv1 and 22Rv1-EnzaR cells were plated per well in 96-well culture plates and allowed to adhere overnight before treatment. Relative cell proliferation was measured over time using the Cell Counting Kit-8, with a SpectraMax® iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader. Three experiments were independently performed, with four replicates for each condition.

Dose-response curves

To analyze the relationship between drug dose and physiological effects, 3,000 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of incubation, the drug-containing medium was added to the wells. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 on a microplate reader (SpectraMax® iD5, Molecular Devices). Curve fitting was performed using the GraphPad Prism software.

Clonogenic assay

The clonogenic assay was performed as previously described [29]. Briefly, 1000 22Rv1 cells and 22Rv1-EnzaR cells/well were plated in a 6-well plate, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 14days or until colonies were visible. For drug treatment, 22Rv1-EnzaR cells were plated in a 6-well plate for 24 h before adding drugs and then incubated in a cell incubator. Colonies were fixed and stained with a solution of 20% ethanol and 0.5% crystal violet, and their numbers were quantified using ImageJ software. Each treatment had three biological replicates.

Soft agar assay

The anchorage-independent growth of cells was evaluated using a soft agar colony formation assay. Briefly, 1.5 mL of 0.5% agar in complete medium was plated in a 6-well plate and solidified at RT for 30min. Then, 5000 cells per well in 1.5 mL complete medium with 0.35% low-melt agarose, with or without treatments, were added on top of the base agar and incubated for 14-21 days. Media with treatments were replaced every three days, and colonies were counted under a phase-contrast microscope after staining with 0.5% crystal violet.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were harvested and fixed in 70% ethanol. After washing twice with PBS, the cells were stained with RNase A and PI in PBS and loaded into a BD flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. We conducted three independent experiments for every condition.

Xenograft experiments

BALB/c nude mice (4–6weeks) were acquired from the Laboratory Animal Center of Guangxi Medical University. Animal care was conducted in compliance with the Science and Technology Ethical Review Committee's guidelines at Guangxi Medical University Cancer Hospital. The 22Rv1 and 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (50 µL cells and 50µL Matrigel/site, 5 × 106 cells/site) were injected subcutaneously into the flank region of the mice. Tumor volume was quantified using a digital caliper and calculated employing the formula: Volume = (length × width2)/2. For drug treatment, nude mice were injected subcutaneously in the flank with 5 × 106 of 22Rv1-EnzaR cells mixed with Matrigel (BD Biosciences). Drug treatment was initiated when tumors reached an approximately volume of 100mm3. Enzalutamide (10mg/kg) (dissolved in 2% DMSO, 40% PEG-300, 5% Tween-80, and 53% saline) was administered via oral gavage every other day. Mithramycin (0.1 mg/kg) (dissolved in 2% DMSO, 40% PEG-300, 5% Tween-80, and 53% saline) was delivered via oral gavage twice a week. Gefitinib (10 mg/kg) dissolved in 2% DMSO, 40% PEG-300, 5% Tween-80, and 53% saline was intraperitoneally injected every day.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using a Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quality was determined in an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). After total RNA extraction, mRNA was enriched by Oligo(dT) beads. The enriched mRNA was then fragmented and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. The purified double-stranded cDNA fragments were ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. The ligation reaction was purified with the AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter) and amplified by PCR. The cDNA library was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Proteomics analysis

The samples were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen, and the protein concentration was determined using a Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of each protein sample were digested with enzymes and adjusted with lysis buffer. Pre-cooled acetone was added and mixed to a total volume of four times the original volume. The samples were stored at -20°C for 2h to precipitate the proteins. After washing 2-3 times with pre-cooled acetone, the precipitate was dispersed in 200 mM TEAB and ultrasonically dispersed. Trypsin was added at a 1:50 ratio to protein for overnight digestion. The sample was then reduced with 5 mM DTT at 56°C for 30 min and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The peptides were desalted using a Strata X SPE column. The tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A (0.1% formic acid and 2% acetonitrile in water) and were segregated on an EASY-nLC 1200 UPLC system (ThermoFisher Scientific). Precursors and fragments were examined using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 with a nano-electrospray ion source. The full MS scan had a resolution of 60,000 over a 350-1400 m/z range, whereas the MS/MS scan started at 120.0 m/z with a resolution of 15,000. Data were acquired using the data-independent acquisition (DIA) method. HCD fragmentation used a normalized collision energy of 27%. The automatic gain control was configured to 1E6 with a peak injection time of 22 ms. The DIA-NN search engine (version 1.8) was employed to process the DIA data using Homo_sapiens_9606_SP_20230103.fasta (20,389 entries), combined with a reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P was set as the cleavage enzyme, allowing one missed cleavage. The fixed modifications included carbamidomethyl on Cys and N-terminal Met excision, with a false discovery rate (FDR) of < 1%.

Analysis of omics data

The data analysis and visualization for RNA-seq were performed using OmicShare tools. Proteomics data were analyzed using the R software (https://www.r-project.org). Protein-protein interaction networks were generated using STRING (https://cn.string-db.org/).

ChIP-qPCR

ChIP assays using an ELF1 antibody were performed in 22Rv1 cells as previously described [29]. Purified DNA was then as a template for qPCR using specific primers from the EGFR promoter.

Cell migration assay

The 22Rv1 cells and 22Rv1-EnzaR cells were transfected with EGFR siRNA or non-targeting siRNA. Cells were reseeded into the upper chamber of a 24-well plate Transwell® insert (8.0μm, Corning Costar) containing serum-free medium at 48 h post-transfection, while the lower chamber was filled with complete medium. For pharmacological treatment, gefitinib and enzalutamide were added to the lower chambers. After incubation for either 24 or 48 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2, the inserts were stained with a 0.05% crystal violet solution. Any residual cells in the upper chamber were carefully removed using cotton swabs. Cells that had migrated to the basolateral side of the insert were subsequently photographed using a microscope. To accurately quantify the cell numbers, images were captured from 10 fields per well using a 10 × objective lens, and cell counts were determined using ImageJ software.

Tumor microarray and immunohistochemical staining

A PCa tumor microarray (TMA) was obtained from Shanghai Outdo Biotech (HProA120Su02), including 60 paired localized adenocarcinoma samples without treatment (Table 2). Organoid culture

The organoid culture method was adapted from the protocols described in previous studies [31,32]. Briefly, prostate tumor tissue samples were collected and washed in PBS buffer containing penicillin-streptomycin, amphotericin B, and gentamicin. Tissues were cut into approximately 1 mm3 pieces in Advanced DMEM/F12 medium with Y27632 and digested in DMEM/F12 medium containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 5 mg/mL type II collagenase, and 10 µM Y27632 at 37°C for 1h on a gentle shaker. After washing the cells with DMEM/F12 medium, we performed a secondary digestion in TrypLE Express containing Y27632 at 37°C for 10 min. Following the washing of cells with PBS, the cell pellets were resuspended in Matrigel (5 × 104 cells/40 µL) and dropped onto the center of the bottom of a 24-well plate. The plate was inverted and incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 15 min to solidify the matrix. Then, 500 µL of pre-warmed (37°C) PCa organoid medium was added to each well, and the medium was refreshed every 2–3 days.

Drug treatment on organoids

Organoids were seeded at 2000 cells per well in a 96-well culture plate and incubated in a cell incubator until they reached a size of greater than 50μm. Mono- or combination treatment of gefitinib (10 μM) and enzalutamide (10 μM) on organoids at 37°C for 3 days was conducted. Cell viability was evaluated using a Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, C1371), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

A two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was employed to assess statistical significance, with P-values of 0.05 or lower regarded as significant.

Results

Enzalutamide treatment promoted cell proliferation in 22Rv1 cells

Given the AR expression in the PCa cell lines, AR-FL (full length) and AR-Vs were concurrently observed in androgen-independent 22Rv1 cells (Fig. S1A) [33]. However, the cells still responded to enzalutamide treatment, probably due to the presence of AR-FL (Fig. 1A-B). To investigate the long-term effects of enzalutamide on androgen-insensitive cells, we screened 22Rv1 cells treated with enzalutamide and the screened colony, 22Rv1-EnzaR, demonstrated a higher IC50 than the parental cells (Fig. 1A-B). Cell proliferation and colony formation assays revealed that the resistant cells had a higher proliferation rate (Fig. 1C-E). Furthermore, consistent with the colony formation assay, the soft agar experiments indicated that 22Rv1-EnzaR cells had enhanced malignant transformation activity (Fig. 1F-G). Cell cycle analysis showed that the short S phase contributed to accelerated cell growth in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. 1H, Fig. S1B). Immunoblotting results revealed that the protein expression levels of AR and AR-Vs did not differ significantly between the two cell lines. However, the phosphorylation of ERK1/2, rather than AKT, was notably increased in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells, indicating the vital role of the ERK-related pathway in facilitating cell growth under enzalutamide treatment (Fig. 1I-J, Fig.S1C). Interestingly, the expression of several epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers—E-cadherin and β-catenin—was downregulated, while vimentin expression was upregulated (Fig. 1J). No expression of N-cadherin was detected; however, Transwell chamber assays indicated that the migratory capacity of the resistant cells did not change significantly (Fig. S1D-E). These results suggest that significant changes have occurred at the molecular level of EMT in resistant cells, but these changes are insufficient to drive phenotypic alterations. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) data of AR downstream genes confirmed the inhibited transcriptional activity of AR and AR-Vs in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. 1K), which was further cleared by dihydrotestosterone (DHT) treatment (Fig. S1F). Besides, qPCR analysis of several genes was performed in DU145 cells with overexpression of AR-V7, identifying the regulatory effects of AR-V7 on AR downstream genes (Fig. S1G). In vivo experiments demonstrated that xenograft tumors of 22Rv1-EnzaR cells grew at a faster rate than those of control cells (Fig. 1L-O). Enzalutamide reprogrammed transcriptomic and proteomic profiling in 22Rv1 cells

Next, we analyzed the transcriptome of 22Rv1-EnzaR cells identified 2,369 upregulated genes and 1,015 downregulated genes (Figs. 2A-B, Fig. S2A-B). The ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, cAMP signaling pathway, and Hedgehog signaling pathway were the top-enriched pathways in KEGG pathways (Fig. 2C). Heatmap and GSEA demonstrated the downregulation of the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway (KO00140), and the upregulation of the homologous recombination pathway (KO03440), suggesting reduced androgen biosynthesis and enhanced DNA repair capacities in CRPC (Fig. S2C-F). Notably, the Rap1 signaling pathway was enriched in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Figs. 2D-E), including FGFR4, LPAR2, EGFR, and F2R, which are associated with drug resistance in cancer [34]. Considering the differences between the expression trends of intracellular mRNA and protein, we conducted a proteomic analysis in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells identified 180 upregulated proteins and 336 downregulated proteins (Fig. 2F-G, Fig. S2G-H). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis results indicated that the drug resistance pathway was enriched in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, through a comprehensive analysis of protein and mRNA expression changes and their correlation with cellular drug resistance, we inferred that EGFR is a key molecule in 22Rv1-EnzaR cell resistance to enzalutamide (Fig. 2I, Figs. EGFR knockdown or pharmacological inhibition enhanced cell sensitivity to enzalutamide and suppressed cell proliferation

To scrutinize the EGFR role in the resistance mechanism of 22Rv1-EnzaR cells, we examined protein expression in these cells, demonstrating elevated levels of EGFR and phosphorylated EGFR, which validate the transcriptomic and proteomic data (Fig. 3A). Given the important role of the EGFR/ERK signaling pathway in promoting cell proliferation, this pathway likely contributed to the increased cell growth in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. 1J, Fig. 3A). Importantly, EGFR knockdown with specific siRNA could significantly increase the sensitivity of enzalutamide in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells, and decrease cell proliferation and colony formation in these cells (Fig. 3B-F, Fig. S3A). Additionally, cell migration slowed down in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells following siRNA transfection (Fig. 3G-H). EGFR knockdown could enhance the inhibitory effects of enzalutamide on 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. 3I), with no significant impact of expression levels of AR downstream genes and AR localization (Fig. S3B-C). Cell cycle analysis indicated that EGFR knockdown led to a prolonged G0/G1 phase (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, the estimated IC50 for enzalutamide increased in 22Rv1 cells with EGFR overexpression (Fig. 3K-L) and LNCaP-EnzaR cells (Fig. S3D). Immunoblotting data demonstrated increased EGFR and p-ERK1/2 expression in LNCaP-EnzaR cells (Fig. S3E-F), consistent with the data in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells. Upregulation of transcription factor ELF1 promoted the EGFR expression and ELF1/EGFR/ERK1/2 formed a positive feedback loop in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying EGFR upregulation in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation using JASPAR Enrichment Analysis, the GEPIA server (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), and transcriptome data. The results displayed that the expression of E74-like ETS transcription factor 1 (ELF1) and EGFR was highly correlated in PCa (Fig. 6A-B). We also examined the gene expression of the ETS family in the transcriptomic data and observed that ELF1 exhibited the most significant change in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. S6A). ChIP-seq data from the ENCODE database demonstrated specific binding peaks of ELF1 in the EGFR promoter region in several cell lines (Fig. 6C). Besides, ELF1 knockdown with specific siRNA significantly attenuated EGFR expression (Fig. 6D). ChIP-qPCR of ELF1 on the EGFR promoter validated the ChIP-seq data in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. S6B, Fig. 6E-F). Interestingly, ERK1/2 interacts with and regulates several E26 transcriptional factors (ETS), including ELK1, ETS1, and ERF [35]. Analysis of the STRING database identified the protein-protein interactions between ERK1/2 and multiple ETS family members (Fig. 6G, Fig. S6C). To further investigate the interaction between ERK1/2 and ELF1, we performed co-IP and immunofluorescence assays, which demonstrated that ERK1/2 interacts with ELF1 and compared to ERK2, more ERK1 interacts with ELF1 (Fig. 6H-K). The finding suggests that the ELF1/EGFR/ERK1/2 signaling axis forms a positive feedback loop. Additionally, the expressions of ELF1 and ERGR were both upregulated in R1AD1 cells from GSE225481 and KD of ELF1 inhibited cell proliferation in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells but not 22Rv1 cells (Fig. S6D-6E), supporting our findings. Taken together, these data indicate that ELF1 is a major transcription factor regulating EGFR expression in PCa. EGFR protein was highly expressed and was associated with poor progression-free interval (PFI) in PCa

To assess EGFR protein expression in PCa, we employed a tissue microarray (TMA) containing 60 tumors and paired adjacent samples (Fig. S7A). Immunohistochemical staining and quantification identified that EGFR protein expression was higher in tumors than in adjacent tissues (Fig. 7A-B). Data mining from the literature (PDX009868) revealed that the EGFR protein was upregulated in metastatic CRPC (Fig. 7C) [36]. Interestingly, the mRNA expression level of EGFR in TCGA tumors was lower than that in normal tissues, which contrasts with the EGFR protein expression level (Fig. 7D). According to the single-cell data from GSE176031, EGFR was expressed at the highest level in the malignant group (Fig. 7E-G). Survival analysis of EGFR protein expression was performed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test based on TCGA reverse-phase protein array (RPPA) data (cSurvival, cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics). The data indicated that patients with high EGFR protein expression levels had significantly shorter PFI and disease-free intervals (DFI) (Fig. 7H-I), while EGFR mRNA expression levels did not significantly associate with PFI and DFI (Fig. S7B-C). Moreover, the area under the curve (AUC) analysis indicated that EGFR protein was significantly associated with a high Gleason Group (GG), as reported in the literature (WRNMMC, Ref#808925, Fig. S7D) [37]. Discussion

Resistance to hormone therapy in PCa is among the primary limitations to improving patients' quality of life. Even in patients with CRPC, the AR-related signaling axis remains a critical therapeutic target, although it requires higher concentrations of anti-androgen drugs. However, these elevated drug concentrations carry greater side effects and increase systemic toxicity. Consequently, identifying suitable combination therapies to enhance CRPC sensitivity to anti-AR treatment is of significant clinical value.

In this study, we demonstrated that long-term treatment of androgen-insensitive PCa cells with enzalutamide can reprogram their transcriptomic and proteomic profiles. EGFR was upregulated at the mRNA and protein levels, without mutations, in enzalutamide-resistant PCa cells. A high level of EGFR protein was associated with poor prognosis, consistent with other reports [38,39]. Additionally, EGFR knockdown and pharmacological inhibition can significantly enhance the sensitivity of enzalutamide-resistant PCa cells to enzalutamide.

AR mutations, amplifications, and splice variants have been identified as essential mechanisms driving PCa resistance to anti-androgen treatment [40]. We did not observe significant differences in AR and AR-Vs in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells (Fig. 1I). However, the downregulation of AR transcriptional activity and increased proliferation in resistant cells suggested an additional resistance mechanism (Fig. 1K). EGFR overexpression was reported in circulating tumor cells (CTC) and advanced PCa, associated with high stages, metastasis, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in PCa [27,38,[41], [42], [43]]. Similarly, we identified that EGFR protein was highly expressed in tumor tissues (Fig. 7A-C), and high EGFR protein expression indicated poor PFI and DFI in patients with PCa (Fig. 7H-I). Conversely, based on TCGA data, the mRNA expression level of EGFR in tumors was lower than that in normal tissues (Fig. 7D), and high EGFR expression indicated a better DFI trend in PCa (Fig. S7C), which is contrary to the data for EGFR protein levels. Considering its biological function, data on the EGFR protein should be considered in PCa treatment. Additionally, EGFR overexpression was closely associated with PCa metastasis [27,41,42,44]. Our findings demonstrated that EGFR was upregulated in androgen-insensitive cells during continued enzalutamide treatment, and that ERK1/2 was activated to promote cell growth (Fig. 3A, Fig. 1J). ELF1, a member of the ETS transcriptional factor family, has been implicated in cancer progression and drug resistance [45,46].

The PI3K-AKT pathway (p-AKT T308) has been shown to be activated in enzalutamide-resistant PCa cells [47], or to show no change (p-AKT T308 and S473) in PCa cells with enzalutamide treatment [48]. However, we found that p-AKT S473 was downregulated in resistant cells. The possible reasons for this difference could include the drug concentration during the establishment of resistant cells, different phosphorylation sites, or the presence of AR-Vs in 22Rv1 cells, which indicates the complexity of enzalutamide resistance in PCa. In this study, we demonstrated that ELF1 transcriptionally regulated EGFR expression in 22Rv1-EnzaR cells. Furthermore, we identified an interaction between ERK1/2 and ELF1, suggesting that under chronic enzalutamide pressure, cells establish an ELF1/EGFR/ERK1/2 positive feedback loop to tolerate drug-induced stress. Consequently, simultaneously targeting EGFR and AR signaling axes may achieve a synergistic therapeutic effect by enhancing the efficacy of anti-androgen therapy in advanced PCa and inhibiting its proliferation and metastasis.

In summary, we propose a combined treatment strategy targeting both EGFR and AR signaling axes in high-grade AR-positive PCa or CRPC, which is likely to provide significant clinical benefits.
