Preclinical development and selection of nanobody-based CAR-T cells targeting HER2-positive solid tumors
Authors: Fien Meeus, Arne Van der Vreken, Dorien Autaers, Timo W.M. De Groof, Arthur Esprit, Lorenzo Franceschini, Philippe Parone, Cleo Goyvaerts, Yannick De Vlaeminck, Nick Devoogdt, Karine Breckpot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy revolutionized treatment of hematological malignancies, but translation to solid tumors remains difficult. Therefore, different avenues are explored to develop novel CAR-T cell products. Nanobodies have advantageous features for CAR design, such as ease of engineering and low immunogenicity, but predictive values on nanobody characteristics for optimal CAR-T cell function remain unclear. We performed a side-by-side evaluation of 12 nanobody-based (nano)CARs against human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). All nanoCARs showed the ability to activate a reporter T cell line upon recognition of HER2pos cells. When evaluated in primary T cells, 6/12 nanoCARs showed the strongest reactivity and cytotoxicity against HER2pos glioblastoma and breast cancer cells. Moreover, only 1/12 was able to confer potent cytotoxicity against HER2pos melanoma cells. These data add to the current view that nanobody selection for CAR design remains subject to extensive side-by-side screening. Still, superior cytotoxicity across multiple solid tumor cell lines—determined using in vitro assays evaluating activation, cytokine secretion, and target cell-specific killing—led to selection and further characterization of a lead nanoCAR 1R59b, showing tumor control in an in vivo xenograft model, providing a promising HER2 nanoCAR for further (pre)clinical investigation.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy revolutionized the treatment of hematological malignancies with currently seven products approved for expanding indications.1,2,3 Long-term follow-up data from the first patients treated a decade ago show prolonged remissions and suggest curative outcomes in a subset of patients.1 CARs combine the antigen-recognition capacity of antibodies with T cell activating domains, redirecting T cell-mediated killing toward surface-expressed antigens.4 While optimization of the CAR design was first mainly focused on the intracellular portion driving T cell activation, proliferation and persistence, the importance of the antigen-binding domain becomes increasingly recognized as an important factor influencing CAR-T cell behavior.5,6,7

Most clinically applied CARs contain a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) derived from a conventional antibody as the antigen-binding domain. From the seven approved CAR-T products, six incorporate a scFv targeting CD19 or B cell maturation antigen (BCMA).1,2 Notwithstanding their proven usefulness, some drawbacks have been associated with the incorporation of scFvs in CARs. Linker optimization is required to preserve VH-VL region pairing and hydrophobic interaction regions can cause self-aggregation on the cell surface leading to tonic signaling and premature exhaustion.5 In addition, scFvs can be immunogenic as they are often derived from murine antibodies and possess an artificial linker to connect the VH to the VL which can limit persistence and repeated dosing.8

Nanobodies are recombinant proteins derived from the variable domain of camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies. As these lack light-chains, the antigen-binding component only consists of a single variable domain, the VHH. Nanobodies are characterized by a small size, high affinity, good solubility, and high stability, making them useful in a number of imaging and therapy applications.9,10,11 The single-domain nature of nanobodies makes them highly soluble and easy to engineer, while avoiding the need for a potentially immunogenic linker, rendering them ideally suited for use in CAR design.5,12,13,14 The first nanobody-based (nano)CAR-T cell product that became approved was cilta-cel in 2022 and targets BCMA in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma patients.15

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is an attractive target for CAR-T cell therapy of solid tumors because of its high expression on a variety of solid tumor types. Expression on healthy tissue is considered low but present on cardiomyocytes, pulmonary artery epithelial, and smooth muscle cells, keratinocytes, osteoblasts, and neural progenitor cells.16,17,18,19 Therefore, assessment of safety remains an important consideration during development of HER2-targeted therapies. Experience from HER2-targeted antibody and cell-based therapies mainly highlight cardiac and lung epithelial cell-mediated toxicity as points of attention.19,20 Importantly, HER2 is a well-validated target owing to long-term experience with HER2-targeting monoclonal antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates as standard-of-care in breast cancer with HER2 amplification.21 Beyond breast cancer, HER2 is expressed in varying other solid tumors including central nervous system tumors, melanoma, lung, gastric, ovarian, (osteo)sarcoma, and colorectal cancer.22,23,24,25,26,27,28,29,30 Here, HER2 expression is generally lower, limiting efficacy of antibody-based therapies.25 These patients could benefit from cellular therapies that show increased sensitivity toward lower antigen levels, providing a rationale for development of HER2 CAR-T cell therapies.21,31,32,33,34 While different clinical trials have investigated HER2 CAR-T cells, most of them have failed to provide lasting anti-tumor responses. Relapse post HER2 CAR infusion has been reported to be associated with antigen loss or downregulation as well as poor CAR-T expansion and persistence, highlighting the need for optimized HER2 CAR-T cell products.31,35,36,37,38,39,40,41

It is established that specific antigen-binding domain characteristics, such as affinity or epitope binding location, can impact CAR-T cell function.5 Still, studies on this subject have generally shown the inability to predict optimal CAR-T cell function based on the soluble binding characteristics of targeting domains, requiring case-by-case evaluation in a CAR context.8,42,43,44 We previously generated and characterized 13 HER2 nanobodies for use as tracers in nuclear imaging.45 Of these, nanobody 2Rs15d was developed into a PET tracer to stratify patients on HER2 status, currently in phase II clinical trial.46 Lacking predictive values on nanobody characteristics for optimal CAR-T cell function, we evaluated the remaining 12 nanobodies for performance in a CAR context.

Results

HER2-targeting nanoCAR-T cells are differentially activated by HER2pos target cells

The generation and characterization of soluble HER2 nanobodies following HER2 protein immunization of dromedaries were previously reported.45 Thirteen binders were characterized as purified proteins. Of these, nanobody 2Rs15d was selected for further development as a PET tracer.46 We selected the remaining 12 nanobodies to evaluate their functionality in a nanoCAR context. This would allow combination strategies where nanobody 2Rs15d is used as a diagnostic imaging tool to stratify HER2 status in patients, whereas another HER2 nanobody is incorporated in the CAR molecule for therapeutic targeting. These 12 nanobodies cover a wide range of affinities for HER2, ranging from 1.4 to 96.9 nanomolar (nM, Table 1), as reported previously.45 We used AlphaFold modeling to estimate binding epitopes of the different nanobodies on HER2. While confidence scores were too low for most of the nanobodies tested (Table S1), we could reliably predict binding of nanobodies 1R59b, 2R5a, 2Rb3b, and 2Rb17c on HER2. Nanobodies 1R59b and 2Rb17c were shown to bind the membrane proximally located domain IV of HER2 (Figure 1A). This confirms previously performed competition studies showing that both nanobodies compete with binding of trastuzumab to this domain.45 By contrast, nanobodies 2R5a and 2Rb3b bind to a more membrane distally located position on HER2 (Figure 1A). Nanobody sequences were cloned into an in-house designed lentiviral CAR vector, wherein the nanobody is downstream of a short EF1α promoter and upstream of a CD8α hinge and transmembrane domain, and intracellular 4-1BB and CD3ζ T cell co-stimulatory and activation domains, respectively. NanoCARs generated using nanobody R3B23 targeting the 5T2 idiotype of murine multiple myeloma cells were used as a negative control throughout this study.47 NanoCARs were co-expressed with the Thy1.1 reporter protein, using a P2A sequence, previously shown to enable flow cytometric quantification of CARpos cells.48 A graphical representation of the nanoCAR construct and the nanoCAR expressed on T cells is shown in Figure 1B. To evaluate functionality of the nanoCAR constructs, we transduced a Jurkat T cell line with lentiviral vectors encoding the different nanoCAR constructs. NanoCARpos Jurkat T cells were stained with recombinant HER2 protein to assess whether the nanobodies retain their HER2 binding capacity upon expression in a CAR molecule. Co-staining with Thy1.1 antibody and HER2-protein showed that HER2 binding capacity was >80% for all nanoCARs (Figure 1C). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) values are shown in Table S2. The Jurkat T cell line is a reporter cell line carrying the green fluorescent protein (GFP) encoding gene under control of a nuclear factor of activated T (NFAT)-cell dependent promoter. The ability of the nanoCAR constructs to induce Jurkat T cell activation was evaluated by co-culturing the nanoCARpos Jurkat T cells with LN229 glioblastoma target cells that endogenously express HER2 (Figure 1D). The increase in GFP signal was quantified using live cell imaging (Figure 1E) and flow cytometric analysis at the end of the 24-h co-incubation. Upon confirming HER2-binding ability and signaling functionality of all nanoCARs using a reporter T cell line, we proceeded to evaluate their therapeutic potential in primary donor T cells (Figure 2A). T cells from a healthy donor were transduced with equal amounts of lentiviral vectors (LV) to generate HER2-targeting nanoCAR-T cells for all different nanoCARs. Transduction efficiencies were determined seven days later (Figure S1). NanoCARpos cells were co-cultured at a 1:1 ratio for 12 h with LN229 glioblastoma, BT474 breast cancer and 624MEL melanoma cells that all endogenously express HER2 (Figure 2B). Then, upregulation of 4-1BB was evaluated as a measure of activation using flow cytometry (Figure 2C). Results showed variable expression of 4-1BB across different nanoCAR-T cells. In addition, we observed varying activation depending on which target cell line was used. Overall, the strongest upregulation of 4-1BB was observed when nanoCAR-T cells were co-cultured with LN229 glioblastoma cells. To verify the trends seen in this first screening experiment and to confirm robustness of the data, we repeated transductions in three donors with a new batch of lentiviral vectors. We transduced cells with equal LV doses and evaluated transduction efficiency seven days later. These ranged from 55 to 92% across all three donors and all 12 constructs (Figure S2A). While no statistical differences between CAR expression levels could be observed based on MFI, nanoCARs 1R133a, 1R119b, and 2Rb17c were associated with the lowest expression levels (Figure S2B). We assessed upregulation of different T cell activation markers CD25, CD69, and 4-1BB, and secretion of IFNγ and TNFα upon co-culturing these CAR-T cells with HER2pos LN229 glioblastoma cells (Figures 2D and 2E). We observed similar trends of activation for the different nanoCARs in this experiment, which were consistent for the different activation markers and cytokines assessed. Overall, six nanoCARs, generated with nanobodies 1R136d, 2R5a, 1R59b, 1R143c, 2Rs23c, and 2Rb3b, enabled the most potent nanoCAR-T cell activation upon co-culture with target cells. Selected nanoCAR-T cells kill glioblastoma, breast, and melanoma cell lines with varying potency

We next evaluated the cytotoxic potential of nanoCAR-T cells generated with the six selected nanoCARs in three donors (these were the same donors as used in Figures 2D and 2E) (Figure 3A). To this end, we co-cultured all nanoCAR-T cell conditions with endogenously HER2pos glioblastoma, breast, and melanoma cell lines (Figure 2B) that were rendered GFPpos through lentiviral transduction. Tumor cell killing was evaluated based on the decrease in GFPpos cells over time, which was evaluated and quantified using IncuCyte imaging analysis. In addition, cultures were subjected to flow cytometric analysis to determine the absolute target cell counts at 72 h post co-culture and supernatants were collected to evaluate IFNγ secretion. IncuCyte imaging and flow cytometry analysis showed the ability of all six HER2 nanoCAR-T cells to kill HER2pos breast and glioblastoma target cells with only nanoCAR 1R136d unable to completely kill BT474 breast cancer cells (Figure 3B–3D). By contrast, 624MEL melanoma cells appeared to be less sensitive to killing by most of the nanoCAR-T cells. Here, only nanoCAR-T cells containing a nanoCAR generated with nanobody 1R59b were able to completely eliminate tumor cells (Figure 3B–3D). Individual graphs for each nanoCAR condition obtained using IncuCyte imaging are shown in Figure S3. To ensure the robustness of these findings, we repeated the transduction in one corresponding donor using different lentiviral vector batches encoding five well-performing nanoCARs. IncuCyte killing analysis confirmed the same trends for all nanoCARs across LN229 and 624MEL tumor cells (Figure S4A), assigning nanobody 1R59b to perform best in a nanoCAR-T cell context across the different tumor types tested. We additionally evaluated nanoCAR-T cell expansion and IFNγ secretion upon three consecutive challenges with LN229 cells (Figure S4B). These results were consistent with previous observations and indicated that nanoCAR-T cells containing the nanoCAR generated with nanobody 1R59b reached the highest levels of expansion and IFNγ secretion. Altogether, these findings led to the selection of nanobody 1R59b-based CARs as lead for further characterization, based on its ability to endow nanoCAR-T cells with superior killing across different tumor types.

1R59b nanoCAR-T cells kill HER2pos tumor cells in an antigen-specific manner

We used an antigen-specific killing assay to ensure HER2-specificity of tumor cell killing by the lead 1R59b nanoCAR-T cells, (Figure 4A). To this end, U87 glioblastoma cells lacking HER2 expression were transduced using lentiviral vectors to stably express HER2 (Figure 4B). Next, this HER2pos variant was additionally transduced to express GFP, while wild-type HER2neg U87 cells were transduced to express the red fluorescent protein Katushka2S (Figure 4C). We co-cultured 1R59b nanoCAR-T cells with equal amounts of GFPpos target and Katushka2Spos non-target cells and compared specific killing to the runner-up 1R143c nanoCAR-T cells. IncuCyte live cell imaging revealed the disappearance of target but not non-target cells, confirming the antigen-specificity of killing for both HER2 nanoCAR-T cell products (Figure 4D). Evaluating the cytotoxic potential of both nanoCAR-T cell products at decreasing effector-to-target ratios (2:1, 1:1, 1:2) using quantified IncuCyte image analysis and endpoint flow cytometric analyses, demonstrated the resistance of the lead 1R59b nanoCAR-T cells to these lower ratios. Since nanobody 1R59b is not cross-reactive to the mouse variant of HER2 (Figure S5), experiments in an in vivo model will not inform us on potential off-tumor effects. To estimate the nanoCAR’s sensitivity to low levels of HER2 expression on healthy tissue, we electroporated HER2neg U87 cells with decreasing amounts of HER2-encoding mRNA (10–0.001 μg) (Figure 4G). Flow cytometric analysis of HER2 expression after 24 h confirmed increasing levels of HER2 expression with increasing HER2 mRNA doses (Figure 4H). At this time, lead 1R59b nanoCAR-T cells were added at a 1:1 ratio. Another 24 h later, supernatants were collected to evaluate IFNγ secretion. This showed that IFNγ secretion decreased when 1R59b nanoCAR-T cells were co-cultured with cells expressing HER2 at lower levels (0.1 μg and lower). Still, at least some IFNγ secretion could be observed when HER2 was detectable by flow cytometry (0.02 μg) (Figure 4I). To better situate the risk for on-target toxicity of nanoCAR 1R59b in a clinically relevant context, we performed a follow-up experiment in which we included the 4D5 scFvCAR which is based on the trastuzumab antibody. This scFvCAR has been well-characterized in the context of HER2-mediated toxicity as it was associated with a lethal event following HER2 CAR-T cell therapy and was shown to be very sensitive toward low levels of HER2 antigen.16,20 Previous competition studies showed that nanobody 1R59b binds the same HER2 epitope as trastuzumab,45 which we also confirmed by AlphaFold modeling (Figures 1A and 4J). We characterized sensitivity of nanoCAR 1R59b compared to the 4D5 scFvCAR using the same set-up but evaluating IFNγ secretion after 96 instead of 24 h in two additional donors. Overall, the scFvCAR secreted higher levels of IFNγ and appeared to be more sensitive than the nanoCAR at lower HER2 levels (0.02 μg) (Figure 4K). To further estimate the nanoCAR's activity toward HER2 on healthy tissue, we acquired primary human cardiac myocytes. These were shown to express HER2 at low levels based on evaluation by flow cytometry (Figure 4L). Using these cells, we set up a co-culture experiment using the 4D5 scFvCAR as a positive control (Figure 4M). Assessment of total cardiomyocyte counts, 4-1BB upregulation and IFNγ secretion confirmed the scFvCAR's activity toward these healthy tissue cells (Figure 4N). In contrast, nanoCAR 1R59b showed no decrease in target cell counts or secretion of IFNγ but some upregulation of 4-1BB expression was observed (Figure 4N), especially in one of three donors tested (Figure S6).

1R59b nanoCAR-T cells control tumor growth in vivo

The capacity of 1R59b nanoCAR-T cells to exert anti-tumor function in a xenograft in vivo model was first evaluated in a setting of low tumor burden (Figure 5A). To this end, we subcutaneously injected NOD/SCID/IL2Rγ−/− (NSG) female mice with 1×106 firefly luciferase-positive LN229 glioblastoma cells before intravenous infusion with 5×106 1R59b or 1R143c nanoCAR-T cells at four days post tumor inoculation. We included runner-up nanoCAR 1R143c to assess whether the trend observed in our in vitro screening experiments was mirrored in the in vivo setting. As a negative control, we used mice treated with non-targeting 5T2Id nanoCAR-T cells. Thy1.1 expression of CAR-T cell products before infusion was evaluated using flow cytometry (Figure 5B) and tumor engraftment before infusion was confirmed in all mice using in vivo bioluminescence imaging (BLI) (Figure 5C). Pooled data from two separate experiments are shown. BLI was used to evaluate tumor development following CAR-T cell therapy. We observed progressive tumor growth in 5T2Id nanoCAR-T cell-treated control mice, while a complete regression of tumors in 1R59b nanoCAR-T cell-treated mice could be observed at week 4 (Figures 5C and 5D). Half of 1R143c nanoCAR-T cell-treated mice showed complete tumor regression then as well (Figure 5C). Caliper measurements of tumors developing in 5T2Id nanoCAR-T cell-treated control mice are provided in Figure S7. Mice in which complete tumor eradication was observed at four weeks post-treatment, were re-challenged with an additional 1×106 firefly luciferase-positive LN229 tumor cells in the contra-lateral flank, without an additional nanoCAR-T cell infusion. As a negative control, mice treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were inoculated with 1×106 firefly luciferase-positive LN229 tumor cells during the re-challenge. BLI images at four days post-inoculation confirmed tumor take in all mice (Figure 5C). Discussion

In this study, we characterized 12 HER2 nanobodies side-by-side for performance in a CAR. In vitro characterization across different solid tumor cell types led to the selection of a lead HER2 nanoCAR that showed potent anti-tumor activity in an in vivo xenograft model. From the 12 nanobodies tested on HER2pos cells, six showed substantial reactivity and cytotoxicity against glioblastoma and breast cancer cells and only one showed potent cytotoxicity against melanoma cells. Overall, our data indicate that nanobodies with strong binding affinities do not necessarily give rise to the most potent nanoCAR-T cells. This confirms the need for context-specific nanobody selection, as no clear correlation between binding characteristics of soluble nanobodies and their performance in a CAR context could be shown. Nanobodies 1R94a and 2Rb18a exhibit relatively poor affinities to HER2 (KD of 95 and 96.9 nM, respectively), which correlated with relatively weak CAR-T cell activation. By contrast, the remaining 10 nanobodies all bind HER2 with strong affinities—in the low nanomolar range—but exhibit marked differences in CAR-T cell function. This is illustrated by nanobodies 1R135a and 1R119b that show the strongest affinities for HER2 (KD of 1.4 and 2.1 nM, respectively) but fail to confer the most potent CAR signaling. While we observed lower CAR expression levels for nanoCAR 1R119b which may have contributed to weaker CAR signaling, nanoCAR 1R135a consistently showed high CAR expression levels. Moreover, while nanobodies 1R59b and 2R5a bind HER2 with similar affinities (KD of 4.9 and 4.5 nM, respectively), nanoCAR 1R59b consistently showed more potent antitumor activity compared to nanoCAR 2R5a.

Beyond affinity, another characteristic of the antigen-binding domain frequently reported to influence CAR-T function, is the location of its bound epitope. While the exact location (membrane proximal or distal) associated with optimal CAR-T cell function may depend on structural dimensions of the target antigen and spacer length, its potential importance is widely recognized.5 While affinities of nanobodies 1R59b and 2R5a are highly similar, AlphaFold modeling revealed different epitope binding sites for each nanobody. Where nanobody 2R5a binds the more membrane distally located domains I and III of HER2, nanobody 1R59b binds to a more membrane proximally located position which may explain the differences in their performance. Indeed, previous competition studies with trastuzumab revealed that the lead-performing nanobody 1R59b competes with trastuzumab for binding on HER2 (membrane-proximal domain IV).45 Since trastuzumab-based CARs were also shown to be highly efficacious,16,49,50 a role for epitope-binding site could be suggested. However, besides nanobody 1R59b, nanobody 2Rb17c equally binds this HER2 epitope, with comparable affinity (4.9 and 6 nM KD for nanobodies 1R59b and 2Rb17c, respectively).45 Since these nanobodies differ strongly in terms of CAR activation, we cannot identify epitope location as a determining factor for optimal CAR-T activation. However, because nanoCAR 2Rb17c was associated with a lower CAR expression level, we cannot exclude that this may have in part influenced the lower activity of this CAR.

Overall, these data support the view that nanobody binding affinity or epitope location are relatively weak predictive factors for activity in a CAR format. More specifically, our observations are in line with other studies evaluating different CAR constructs (either nanobody or scFv-based) side-by-side, that have reported on the difficulty in predicting optimal CAR function based on binding properties of the soluble proteins.8,42,43,44 A study evaluating five BCMA-specific CARs side-by-side from a human scFv library reported on substantial differences in CAR-T cell expansion and in vivo anti-tumor activity, despite a narrow range of low nanomolar affinities of the scFvs. Since the small 54 amino acid extracellular domain of BCMA is unlikely to impose differential epitope proximity to the membrane, the authors postulate that epitope location could not explain the differences seen in CAR efficacy.8 In addition to this study, we have previously evaluated a panel of seven nanobodies targeting the 5T33 mouse multiple myeloma idiotype51 side-by-side in a CAR context43 While all nanobodies bound the antigen in the low nanomolar range, only two exhibited potent CAR-T cell activation in a reporter T cell line. Competition studies revealed that all nanobodies competed for binding to the same epitope on the target antigen indicating that affinity and epitope binding of nanobodies had no predictive value in terms of CAR activation.43 Similar results were obtained evaluating a panel of 10 CS1-specific nanoCARs. Intermediate affinity binders proved surprisingly potent compared to higher affinity nanobodies that failed to induce potent T cell activation. Here, CAR competition studies with soluble nanobodies suggested a role for epitope binding, but this could not be confirmed by in silico structure modeling of nanobody-antigen interactions.42 A final study evaluating more than 20 CD22 nanoCARs similarly showed that only relatively rare nanobodies were able to drive cytotoxicity and in vivo therapeutic efficacy. While no correlation with monovalent nanobody affinity (ranging from 0.03 to 250 nM) could be shown, the authors found epitope location to be a key determinant for CAR activity, showing superior function of CARs targeting membrane proximal epitopes on CD22.44 Overall, the combined results of our studies indicate that although affinity and epitope location can certainly affect CAR-T function, no standard predictive values exist, leaving the selection of new antigen-binding domains subject to extensive screening in the CAR context.

We selected nanoCAR 1R59b as lead based on its superior antitumor activity across multiple HER2pos solid tumor types. While HER2-targeted therapies have proven most useful in breast cancer with HER2 amplification, the applicability of HER2 antibody-based therapies has been limited in patients with HER2low tumors.21 The optimized antibody-drug-conjugate trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd, Enhertu) was the first HER2-targeted product that became approved for metastatic breast,52 advanced gastric cancer26 and non-small-cell lung cancer25 with low HER2 expression, paving the way for HER2 CAR-T cell therapies for these indications as well. So far, clinical trial data from HER2 CAR-T trials show mixed results. A first study resulted in a lethal toxic event attributed to the highly active 4D5 scFv CAR showing very high affinity (0.3 nM KD) for HER2,16 resulting in on-target off-tumor toxicity of healthy lung tissue and a cytokine storm.49 Subsequent clinical studies have invested in evaluating different scFv-based CAR formats, routes of administration and lymphodepleting regimens.31,33,36 The most promising case of HER2 CAR-T cell therapy so far, reports on a child with metastatic rhabdomyosarcoma remaining in remission 4.3 years after initial CAR-T cell infusion, without signs of toxicity.53 Together, these reports show the potential promise of HER2 CAR-T cells, but at the same time highlight the safety risk when targeting HER2.

Multiple studies have invested in enhancing safety of HER2 CAR-T cells by affinity tuning. More specifically, by evaluating HER2 scFvs with varying affinities, researchers have shown the potential benefit of using weaker affinity CARs to exhibit robust anti-tumor efficacy while sparing normal cells expressing physiologic levels of HER2, increasing tumor selectivity.16,17,54 However, despite the potential benefit in terms of safety, weaker affinity HER2 CARs have been associated with increased susceptibility to PD-L1-mediated inhibition compared to their strong affinity counterparts.50 Moreover, while weakening sensitivity can be attractive in terms of safety, it entails a risk of tumor escape through antigen downregulation and survival of low antigen-expressing clones.5 This illustrates the difficulty in balancing selectivity and efficacy for CARs targeting antigens shared with vital healthy tissues. We have characterized the activity of the lead HER2 nanoCAR 1R59b to be lower than the trastuzumab-based 4D5 scFvCAR in in vitro models mimicking off-tumor toxicity toward healthy tissue.16 Still, we observed some activity toward cardiomyocytes upon 1R59b nanoCAR-signaling (based on 4-1BB expression), warranting further attention. Overall, the artificial context of in vitro assays does not suffice to determine safety of complex living drugs such as CAR-T cells, and more detailed safety studies should follow to evaluate potential toxicities.55

With this study, we show the potential to develop a potent HER2 nanoCAR for therapy of HER2pos solid tumors. At the same time, we show the relatively low rate of nanobodies functioning well in a CAR context. We could not identify predictive values for antigen-binding domains to achieve optimal CAR-T cell function, leaving the design of optimal CARs subject to extensive side-by-side CAR screening methods. Currently, nanobodies are selected using methods optimized to select strong affinity binders for use as tracers for molecular imaging purposes, not retaining weak-affinity binders during the process.42,56 While in our study nanobodies with the weakest binding characteristics correlated with low CAR-T activity, other studies have shown the ability of those to elicit potent and safe CAR-T function.16,42,57,58,59 Methods to screen entire nanobody immune libraries (∼1×106) directly in a CAR format, instead of using pre-selected nanobodies would avoid early exclusion of weak-affinity binders. While currently no reports exist on direct CAR-contextual screening of nanobody libraries, screening strategies for scFv libraries have been developed in this context. These include a CAR-T cell display platform to screen a library of HER2-targeting scFvs based on antigen-binding and IL-2 signaling.60 Other researchers generated a CD38 scFv library using light-chain exchange technology, and identified a CAR of ∼1,000-fold reduced affinity to show optimal efficacy and safety.61 In comparison to the larger and often artificial scFv libraries, nanobody libraries have the advantage of being small and natural immune libraries.42,60,61,62,63 Thus, direct CAR-contextual screening of nanobody libraries, combined with in-depth safety and efficacy testing may further enhance current nanoCAR design for solid tumor antigens as HER2.

Materials and methods

In silico epitope binding modeling of HER2 nanobodies

Binding of nanobodies to the extracellular domain of HER2 was modeled using the online AlphaFold3 tool.64 Predicted structures were visualized using PyMOL (version 3.1.6.1).

Mice, cell lines, and primary T cells

Six-week-old NOD/SCID/IL2Rγ−/− (NSG) mice (Charles River) were used for therapy experiments. All animal experiments were approved by the ethical committee for use of laboratory animals of the Vrije Universiteit Brussel (protocol 22-214-6). Human embryonic kidney (HEK)293T (CRL-3216) and human glioblastoma LN229 (CRL-2611) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Human melanoma 624MEL cells were provided by S.L. Topalian (National Cancer Institute, Baltimore, MD, USA). Human breast cancer BT474 cells were obtained from Duke University Cell Culture Facility. Human glioblastoma U87ΔEGFRvIII (U87) cells were a gift from Prof. Webster K. Cavenee (Ludwig Institute for Cancer Research, University of California, San Diego, CA, USA). Jurkat-76 cells were a gift of Prof. Hans Stauss (Institute of Immunity and Transplantation, University College London, UK). All human cells were registered at the biobank of the Vrije Universiteit Brussel under the study code B084. The HEK293T, U87, LN229, BT474, and 624MEL cells and their variants were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, TICO Europe), 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin and 2 mM L-glutamine (all Sigma-Aldrich). Jurkat-76 cells and variants were cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin and 2 mM L-glutamine (all Sigma-Aldrich). All cells were cultured at 37°C, 5% CO2, 21% O2, and 95% humidity (Heracell 240i incubator). Human cardiac myocytes were obtained from PromoCell and cultured as recommended. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donors after written informed consent at the hematology unit of the university hospital of Brussels (UZ Brussel, Belgium) using the Spectra Optia apheresis system (TerumoBCT). After cell count and viability assessment, the leukapheresis product was cryopreserved in 5% dimethyl sulfoxide cryopreservation medium (CryoStor CS5, Biolife Solutions). Cryovials were immediately transferred into a freezing container and placed at −80°C. After overnight incubation at −80°C, vials were stored in the vapor phase of a liquid nitrogen container. These procedures and subsequent use for nanoCAR-T cell development were approved by the medical ethical committee of the UZ Brussel (BUN. 1432023000220).

Generation and characterization of lentiviral vectors

The VSV.G, REV and GAG-encoding helper plasmids, and the pCCL-eGFP lentiviral transfer plasmid were obtained from Brian Brown (Mount Sinai Icahn School of Medicine, New York). Cloning of the sequence encoding the red fluorescent protein Katushka2S into the pCCL vector was previously described.65 Generation of the reporter construct for T cell activation was previously described.48 The pMD.G envelope, the pCMVΔR8.9 packaging and pHR’ lentiviral transfer plasmids were obtained from Didier Trono (University of Geneva, Switzerland) and modified in-house as previously described.66 Generation of the firefly luciferase (fLuc)-encoding pHR’ vector has been described earlier.67 To generate HER2-targeting nanoCARs, sequences coding for the anti-HER2 nanobodies were obtained in-house.45 A nanoCAR specific for the mouse 5T2 multiple myeloma idiotype (5T2Id) was generated as negative control and is based on the R3B23 nanobody sequence.47 The 4D5 scFv based on the trastuzumab sequence was used as a positive control.68 Coding sequences were ordered as gBlocks (IDT) containing 20 bp overhangs to the target pHR’ lentiviral transfer plasmid. The Gibson Assembly Method was used to link the gBlocks in frame to the hinge and transmembrane domains of human CD8α and the cytoplasmic domains of 4-1BB and CD3ζ. In this expression cassette, nanoCAR constructs are under control of the short EF1α core promotor and downstream of the Igκ leader sequence. The nanoCAR-encoding region is followed by a P2A and Thy1.1 reporter sequence to serve as a proxy for nanoCAR expression.

All plasmid sequences were confirmed by sequence analysis (Eurofins). Lentiviral vectors were produced, and functional titers were determined as described.65,69 Briefly, lentiviral vectors were harvested at 48 and 72 h post transfection. Harvests were filtered through 0.45 μm pore-sized filters (Greiner). Purified lentiviral vectors were obtained by concentrating filtered harvest a 1000-fold by ultracentrifugation (Beckman Optima LE-80K Ultracentrifuge), before storage in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10 μg/mL protamine sulfate (LeoPharma), at −80°C until further use.

Lentiviral transduction of human cells and cell lines

Endogenously HERpos LN229, BT474, and 624MEL cells were transduced in-house with GFP-encoding lentiviral vectors, generating HER2pos GFPpos LN229, BT474, and 624MEL target cells. This was achieved by transduction of 1×106 cells in 5 mL filtered viral harvest in 6-well plates. LN229 cells were transduced with fLuc-encoding lentiviral vectors, resulting in HER2pos fLucpos LN229 target cells. To this end, 1×105 cells were transduced with 2 μL purified lentiviral vectors in 1 mL supplemented DMEM in 24-well plates. HER2neg U87 cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding Katushka2S, resulting in HER2neg Katushka2Spos U87 cells. The HER2neg U87 cell line was also transduced with HER2-encoding lentiviral vectors to generate HER2-overexpressing U87 cells. These were additionally transduced with GFP-encoding lentiviral vectors, generating HER2pos GFPpos U87 cells. All U87 derivatives were generated by transduction of 1×105 cells with 2 μL of purified lentiviral vectors in 1 mL supplemented DMEM in 24-well plates. Jurkat-76 reporter cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding the 12 HER2-targeting nanoCARs or 5T2Id nanoCAR. To this end 1×106 cells were transduced directly in 5 mL filtered viral harvest in 6-well plates.

For generation of primary CAR-T cells, PBMCs were thawed and cultivated in TexMACS medium (Miltenyi Biotec). The next day, cells were resuspended at a density of 1×106 cells and activated with CD3/CD28 MACS T cell TransAct reagent in the presence of IL-7/IL-15 at 40U/mL (all Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were transduced 24 h after activation with nanoCAR-encoding lentiviral vectors at a multiplicity of infection of 2.5 and activation medium was changed 48 h later. Cultures were expanded every 2 to 3 days using TexMACS medium containing IL-7/IL-15 at 40 U/mL, until harvest at day 7–11, for analysis and use in assays.

Generation and electroporation of HER2-encoding mRNA

To generate HER2-encoding mRNA, the coding sequence for the HER2 extracellular and transmembrane domain was ordered as gBlock (IDT) and linked into the pLMCT plasmid using the Gibson Assembly Method. Plasmid sequence was confirmed by sequence analysis (Eurofins). Further details on plasmid DNA template synthesis and in vitro mRNA transcription were previously reported.70

HER2neg U87 cells were electroporated with HER2 mRNA as follows. Cells were washed twice in OptiMEM Reduced Serum Medium. Electroporation was performed in OptiMEM in a 4-mm electroporation cuvette (Cell Projects) using the following parameters: exponential decay, 300V, capacitance 100 microfarad, resistance ∝ Ω, using the Gene Pulser XCell device. More specifically, 5x105 U87 cells in 200 μL OptiMEM were electroporated with 10, 2, 0.5, 0.1, 0.02, 0.05 and 0.01 μg mRNA, respectively.

Flow cytometry

All reagents were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. FcR Blocking reagent (Miltenyi Biotec) and fixable viability dye Zombie Aqua (Biolegend) were used when appropriate. Expression of the HER2-targeting nanoCARs was detected with human Fc-linked recombinant HER2 protein (ACRO Biosystems). Human Fc-linked antigen was detected in a secondary step with phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled anti-human IgG Fc antibody (eBioscience). Thy1.1-expression on T cells was evaluated using an Alexa Fluor (AF) 647 or PE-labeled Thy1.1 antibody (Biolegend). Expression of T cell activation markers was evaluated using a PE-labeled anti-4-1BB antibody, a BV605-labeled anti-CD25 antibody and a PerCP/Cy5.5-labeled anti-CD69 antibody (all antibodies from Biolegend). Absolute target cell counts in cultures were determined using the 123count eBeads Counting Beads (Thermo). HER2 expression on cells was evaluated using a PE-Cy7-labeled anti-HER2 antibody (Biolegend). Irrelevant isotype-matched antibodies (Biolegend) served as negative controls. Expression of GFP and Katushka2S was evaluated without additional staining steps. Presence of human CD45pos cells in mouse spleens was determined using an AF700-labeled anti-human CD45 antibody (Biolegend). All samples were acquired on the LSR Fortessa (Beckton Dickinson), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software, version 10.0 (Tristar, Inc).

Reporter nanoCAR-T cell activation assay

NanoCARpos Jurkat-76 reporter cells were co-cultured at an effector-to-target ratio of 1:1 with HER2pos LN229 cells. Co-cultures were carried out in Jurkat-76 medium in a flat-bottom 96-well plate (Sarstedt). Culture plates were placed in the IncuCyte ZOOM device for 24 h to measure the occurrence of a GFP signal. After 24 h, cells were harvested and analyzed using flow cytometry. To determine the percentage of activated (GFPpos) cells within the CAR-T cell fraction, cells were stained with a Thy1.1-targeting antibody.

Primary nanoCAR-T cell activation assay

NanoCAR-T cells were co-cultured with human HER2pos LN229 cells at an effector:target ratio of 1:1 in 48-well plates in TexMACS medium in the absence of exogenous cytokines. 12, 19, or 72 h later, supernatants and cells were collected. Cytokines (IFNγ and TNFα) were measured by ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo). Expression of activation markers 4-1BB, CD25 and CD69 on nanoCAR-T cells at this time was evaluated using flow cytometry through staining of Thy1.1, 4-1BB, CD25, and CD69. In another experiment, nanoCAR or scFvCAR-T cells were co-cultured with human HER2 RNA-electroporated U87 cells at an effector:target ratio of 1:1 in 96-well plates in TexMACS medium in the absence of exogenous cytokines. 24 or 96 h later, supernatants were collected and IFNγ secretion was measured by ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo). In another experiment, nanoCAR or scFvCAR-T cells were co-cultured with primary human cardiac myocytes at a 1:1 ratio. After 48 h, cells and supernatants were harvested.

IFNγ secretion was measured by ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo) and expression of activation marker 4-1BB on CAR-T cells at this time was evaluated using flow cytometry through staining of Thy1.1 and 4-1BB.

In vitro nanoCAR-T cell killing assays

NanoCAR-T cells were co-cultured for 72 h with HER2pos GFPpos LN229, BT474, or 624-MEL cells at effector:target ratios of 2:1 in 96-well flat-bottom plates (Sarstedt) in TexMACS medium in the absence of exogenous cytokines. The reduction in GFPpos tumor cells was evaluated using the IncuCyteZOOM device (EssenBio). After 72 h, cells were harvested and target cell killing was determined by counting target cell numbers using 123count eBeads (Thermo) and flow cytometry. In another experiment, nanoCAR or scFvCAR-T cells were co-cultured with primary human cardiac myocytes at a 1:1 ratio. After 48 h, cells were harvested and target cell killing was determined using 123count eBeads (Thermo) and flow cytometry. To verify antigen-specific tumor cell killing, nanoCAR-T cells were co-cultured for 24 h with a 1:1 mix of GFPpos HER2pos U87 target cells and Kat2Spos HER2neg U87 non-target cells at an effector-to-target ratio of 2:1, 1:1, or 1:2 in 96-well flat-bottom plates (Sarstedt) in TexMACS medium in the absence of exogenous cytokines. Green and red fluorescence were imaged using the IncuCyteZOOM device (EssenBio). After 24 h, cell cultures were harvested, and the percentage of target cell-specific killing was quantified by flow cytometry using the following formula: % specific killing = 1 – ((%GFP/Kat2S with HER2 CAR-T cells)/(%GFP/Kat2S with 5T2Id CAR-T cells)).

Xenograft tumor model to study nanoCAR-T cell efficacy

To evaluate nanoCAR-T cell efficacy in a model with low tumor burden, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane gas anesthetics (Abbot Laboratories) and injected s.c. in the right flank with 1×106 HER2pos fLucpos LN229 cells in a volume of 50 μL PBS. Three days later, mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin (Promega) and 10 min later subjected to BLI in an IVIS device for 2 min to ensure tumor take in each mouse. One day later, an adoptive cell transfer of 5×106 nanoCAR-T cells in a total of 200 μL PBS was performed through i.v. injection. Tumor growth was followed up by BLI at weeks 1 and 4 post therapy. Mice that showed tumor clearance at week four post treatment were re-challenged with 1×106 HER2pos fLucpos LN229 cells. Four days later, BLI was performed to confirm tumor engraftment in all mice. From thereafter, BLI was performed weekly until week 8. Caliper measurements were additionally performed when tumors developed. The experiment was repeated once. Four mice of the 5T2Id nanoCAR control group were shared with a study evaluating B7-H3 nanoCAR-T cells that followed the same experimental protocol and was performed in parallel with this study.48

To evaluate nanoCAR-T cell efficacy in a model with higher tumor burden, mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane gas anesthetics (Abbot Laboratories) and injected s.c. in the right flank with 1×106 HER2pos fLucpos LN229 cells in a volume of 50 μL PBS. 14 days later, an adoptive cell transfer of 5×106 nanoCAR-T cells in a total of 200 μL PBS was performed through i.v. injection. Tumor dimensions were measured using a digital caliper until day 36 post nanoCAR-T cell therapy at which time mice were euthanized to collect spleens for flow cytometric analysis. To this end, spleens were minced over 40 μm filters (Greiner) in PBS. Red blood cells were eliminated using ACK lysis buffer and isolated cells were immediately processed for flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 10 software. Data are represented as mean ± SD. Biological replicates represent individual donors or mice. The replicate number is provided in the figure legends. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed unpaired t tests, one or two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s or Sidak’s post hoc test for multiple comparison correction. p values were reported as follows: ∗p ≤ 0.05; ∗∗p ≤ 0.01; ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p ≤ 0.0001.
