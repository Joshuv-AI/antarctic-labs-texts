Preliminary Bias Analysis of Experiments for Multi-Item Multi-Period Inventory Control Policies
Xinqi Chen, Xingyu Bai, Zeyu Zheng, Nian Si

Abstract
Randomized experiments, or A/B testing, are the gold standard for evaluating interventions but are underutilized in the area of inventory management. This study addresses this gap by analyzing A/B testing strategies in multi-item, multi-period inventory systems with lost sales and capacity constraints. We examine switchback experiments, item-level randomization, pairwise randomization, and staggered rollouts, analyzing their biases theoretically and comparing them through numerical experiments. Our findings provide actionable guidance for selecting experimental designs across various contexts in inventory management.



Keywords:
Experimental designs, A/B testing, inventory management, interference.

1
Introduction

Randomized experiments, or A/B testing, are the gold standard for assessing the effectiveness of different strategies or interventions.
The fundamental principle of traditional randomized experiments involves randomly assigning experimental units to treatment and control groups, then inferring the effect of intervention by measuring the average performance difference between groups on metrics of interest. In practical applications, decision makers employ A/B testing to evaluate whether implementing a new intervention (
treatment
) across the entire platform would yield better performance compared to the existing approach (
control
), referred to as Global Treatment Effect (GTE).

On large online platforms, thousands of experiments are conducted daily to assess the impact of changes, ranging from minor adjustments to major overhauls. Extensive literature exists on bias analysis across different experimental designs and platforms, including examples from room-sharing platforms
(
Johari et al., 2022
)
, advertising platforms
(
Basse et al., 2016
;
Liao et al., 2024
)
, recommendation systems
(
Si, 2023
;
Farias et al., 2023
;
Zhan et al., 2024
)
, ride-sharing platforms
(
Bright et al., 2024
)
, and gaming platforms
(
Weng et al., 2024
)
.

While the technology industry has widely embraced A/B testing, such data-driven methods remain underexplored and underutilized in other industries, such as inventory management. However, as Prof. Thomke stated in his book

Experimentation Works: The Surprising Power of Business Experiments

(
Thomke, 2020
)
:

“Large-scale, controlled experimentation would revolutionize the way all companies operate their businesses and how managers make decisions.”

We also anticipate that A/B testing will increasingly be adopted in those industries as well. In particular, industries such as inventory management have witnessed a prominent trend to become more and more data-oriented, providing an advantageous position to effectively implement A/B tests.

This paper considers A/B tests in inventory management. In recent years, many automatic, data-driven inventory control methods have been proposed
(
Shi et al., 2016
;
Lyu et al., 2024b
;
Xie et al., 2024
)
. While these methods demonstrate strong theoretical properties under specific assumptions, how to reliably quantify and examine their practical performance in real-world environments, where assumptions are easily violated, remains underexplored. This study aims to address this gap by systematically evaluating different A/B testing strategies and providing guidance on the design of experiments in different scenarios.

Specifically, we focus on A/B testing different inventory policies in multi-item, multi-period systems with lost sales and a warehouse capacity constraint
(
Snyder and Shen, 2019
)
. We analyze two canonical experimental designs: switchback experiments
(
Cochran et al., 1941
;
Bojinov et al., 2023
;
Xiong et al., 2023
)
and item-level randomization.
In switchback experiments, the treatment assignment for all experimental items randomly alternates between the control group and the treatment group over time. In contrast, item-level randomization involves randomly assigning items to treatment and control groups, with their treatment assignments remaining fixed throughout the experiment.

However, both the aforementioned experimental designs have limitations due to bias caused by interference. In switchback experiments, temporal interference or carryover effects are present, meaning that a treatment not only impacts the outcome of the current period but also influences the system’s state and subsequent outcomes in future periods. For item-level randomization, cannibalization effects may occur due to capacity constraints, where the treatment assigned to one item affects the outcomes of other items.
These scenarios violate the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) in causal inference
(
Imbens and Rubin, 2015
)
. And previous studies showed that the bias caused by interference can be as large as the GTE itself
(
Blake and Coey, 2014
;
Fradkin, 2019
;
Holtz et al., 2020
)
.

In this work, we examine the bias of the naive inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator under different experimental designs, including switchback experiments and item-level randomized experiments. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

We demonstrate that, under mild assumptions, switchback experiments consistently underestimate the true global treatment effect (GTE), while item-level randomization consistently overestimates the GTE.

2.

Motivated by
Johari et al., 2022
and
Bajari et al., 2023
, we propose a pairwise randomized experiment by viewing product and time as two-sided factors and analyze its bias. Furthermore, we examine the experiment of the staggered rollouts introduced by
Xiong et al., 2024
in the stationary environment.

3.

We perform numerical experiments across various scenarios and provide specific recommendations for selecting appropriate experimental designs in different contexts.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
1.1
we review related work on A/B testing and multi-item inventory control. Section
2
specifies the model of multi-item multi-period inventory control. In Section
3
, we introduce the experimental designs and conduct bias analysis. In Section
4
, we show simulated A/B testing results and explore the effect of certain parameters.
Finally, we conclude with several practical implications and future work in Section
5
.

Problem Formulation

In this section, we formally describe our model. We introduce the multi-item multi-period inventory model with capacity constraint in Section
2.1
, describe the inventory policy in Section
2.2
, and define the global treatment effect in Section
2.3
.

Throughout this paper, we use
[N]
to denote
\{1,\dots,N\}
for any
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. We specify that
\sum_{i=a}^{b}x_{i}=0
when
a>b
.

2.1
Multi-Item Multi-Period Inventory Model

We consider a periodic-review inventory system of
N
different items with a warehouse capacity constraint

B
over a planning horizon of
H
periods. The replenishment lead time is zero and any unsatisfied demand is lost. The random demand vector in period
t\in[H]
is denoted as
\mathbf{D}_{t}=(D_{1,t},...,D_{N,t})
.
We use
\underline{D}_{n,t}
to denote the essential infimum of the random demand
D_{n,t}
for any
n\in[N]
,
t\in[H-1]
, i.e.,
\underline{D}_{n,t}=\operatorname{ess}\inf D_{n,t}
. We assume
\underline{D}_{n,t}>0
, i.e., there is always a positive demand realization in each period.
The inventory manager is implementing base-stock policies, where the base-stock levels in period
t\in[H]
are given by
\mathbf{S}_{t}=(S_{1,t},\dots,S_{N,t})
and satisfy the capacity constraint:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}{S}_{n,t}\leqslant B,\ \forall t\in[H].

(1)

For each period
t
, the event sequence is given as follows:

1. At the beginning of each period
t
, inventory manager observes the initial inventory level
\mathbf{X}_{t}=(X_{1,t},\dots,X_{N,t})
.

2. For any item
n\in[N]
, a new order
O_{n,t}=\max(S_{n,t}-X_{n,t},0)
is placed and immediately delivered, thereby updating the inventory level to
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}_{t}=(\widetilde{S}_{1,t},\dots,\widetilde{S}_{N,t})
with
\widetilde{S}_{n,t}=X_{n,t}+O_{n,t}
.

3. For any item
n\in[N]
, the demand
D_{n,t}
is realized and then satisfied by the on-hand inventory
\widetilde{S}_{n,t}
.

4.
The profit in this period is
R_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}R_{n,t}
with
R_{n,t}=r_{n}\min(\widetilde{S}_{n,t},D_{n,t})-c_{n}O_{n,t}
, where
r_{n}
and
c_{n}
represent the unit selling price and unit ordering cost for item
n\in[N]
, respectively. Define
R_{n}^{+}(s,d)=(r_{n}-c_{n})(s-(s-d)^{+})
, then
R_{n,t}
can also be expressed as

\displaystyle R_{n,t}=R_{n}^{+}(\tilde{S}_{n,t},D_{n,t})-c_{n}(\tilde{S}_{n,t}-D_{n,t})^{+}+c_{n}X_{n,t},\forall n\in[N],\forall t\in[H].

(2)

5.
The initial inventory for item
n\in[N]
in the next period is given by
X_{n,t+1}=(X_{n,t}+O_{n,t}-D_{n,t})^{+}
.

At the end of period
H
, for each item
n\in[N]
, a salvage value
c_{n}X_{n,H+1}=c_{n}(\widetilde{S}_{n,H}-D_{n,H})^{+}
is generated and we include this term in
R_{n,H}
. Moreover, we assume the initial inventory level
\mathbf{X}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
without loss of generality.

2.2
Base-Stock Inventory Policy

In a single-item newsvendor problem without salvage value, it is well known that the optimal order-up-to level is given by

\displaystyle S^{*}=F^{-1}\left(\frac{r-c}{r}\right),

(3)

where
r
,
c
denote the unit selling price, unit ordering cost respectively and
F
denotes the cumulative distribution function (c.d.f.) of demand variable.
However, in the multi-item inventory model, the existence of capacity constraint (
1
) may limit the base-stock levels from reaching their optimal values, leading to interference among different items.

Given the above concern, we adopt a simple heuristic proposed by
Nahmias and Schmidt, 1984
to meet the capacity constraint. To be specific,
suppose that we are given with a series of base-stock levels
\{\mathbf{s}_{t}\}_{t\in[H]}
with
\mathbf{s}_{t}=(s_{1,t},...,s_{N,t})
, which we refer to as
predetermined
base-stock levels. In practice, for each item

n\in[N]
, the predetermined base-stock levels
\{s_{n,t}\}_{t\in[H]}
can be determined at the very beginning or obtained by the newsvendor formula (
3
) with demand distributions estimated based on historical data through a rolling horizon approach. For each
t\in[H]
, if
\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n,t}>B
, we scale down the order-up-to levels to meet the capacity constraint, i.e., for each item
n
,

\displaystyle{S}_{n,t}=k_{t}s_{n,t},\ \text{where }k_{t}=\min\left(1,\frac{B}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}s_{m,t}}\right).

(4)

This heuristic is easy to implement and computationally efficient in practice compared with other methods using Lagrangian multipliers (see
Nahmias and Schmidt, 1984
and
Erlebacher, 2000
).

2.3
Global Treatment Effect

Our objective is to estimate the Global Treatment Effect (GTE) of two series of base-stock levels. To be specific, suppose that in the control group, the predetermined base-stock level for item
n
in period
t
is
s^{C}_{n,t}
, and in the treatment group it is switched to another value
s^{T}_{n,t}
. We want to identify how much such a change will increase the profit. Global Treatment(GT) means that all items in every period are assigned to the treatment group, and Global Control (GC) means that all items in every period are assigned to the control group.

We define GTE as the difference in the expected average profit between global treatment and global control:

\displaystyle GTE:=\overline{R}_{H}^{GT}-\overline{R}_{H}^{GC}=\frac{1}{NH}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}R_{n,t}^{GT}-\frac{1}{NH}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}R_{n,t}^{GC}.

3
Experimental Designs and Estimators

Fix two series of predetermined base-stock levels

\{\mathbf{s}^{w}_{t}\}_{t\in[H]}
, where
\mathbf{s}^{w}_{t}=(s^{w}_{1,t},...,s^{w}_{N,t})
with
w=T
denoting the treatment group and
w=C
denoting the control group.
Denote the treatment assignment matrix as

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}=[\mathbf{W}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{W}_{H}]=(W_{n,t})_{n\in[N],t\in[H]},\ \text{where }W_{n,t}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if item $n$ is in treatment in period $t$,}\\
0,&\text{if item $n$ is in control in period $t$.}\end{cases}

Therefore, according to the heuristic (
4
), under the treatment assignment
\mathbf{W}_{t}
in period
t
, the base-stock level for item
n
is given as

\displaystyle S_{n,t}(\mathbf{W}_{t})=\min\left(1,\frac{B}{\sum_{m=1}^{N}(W_{m,t}s^{T}_{m,t}+(1-W_{m,t})s^{C}_{m,t})}\right)(W_{n,t}s^{T}_{n,t}+\left(1-W_{n,t})s^{C}_{n,t}\right).

Naive estimator.

For switchback, item-level randomized and pairwise randomized experiments introduced in Sections
3.1
,
3.2
and
3.3
, the Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) estimator in A/B tests is given by:

\displaystyle\widehat{GTE}=\frac{1}{NH}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{H}\left(\frac{W_{n,t}R_{n,t}}{p}-\frac{(1-W_{n,t})R_{n,t}}{1-p}\right),

(5)

where
p
represents the probability of treatment assignment, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(W_{n,t}=1)=p
.
However,
\{W_{n,t}\}
may not be independent and its joint distribution will be specified later.

To facilitate the bias analysis, We assume that

s_{n,t}^{C}\leqslant s_{n,t}^{T},\forall t\in[H],\forall n\in[N].

(6)

This assumption implies that the treatment is somewhat uniform.

In the remainder of this section, we introduce four experimental designs and analyze the bias of their naive estimators. We use superscripts to distinguish IPW estimators in different experiments. All proofs are provided in the appendix.

3.1
Switchback Experiment

Experimental Design.
The switchback (SW) experimental design is as follows: in each period
t\in[H]
, with probability
p
, all items are assigned to the treatment group (i.e.
\mathbf{W}_{t}=\boldsymbol{1}
); with probability
1-p
, all items are assigned to the control group (i.e.
\mathbf{W}_{t}=\boldsymbol{0}
). Formally,
\{\mathbf{W}_{t}\}_{t\in[H]}
are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d), and
W_{1,t}=\dots=W_{N,t}\sim Bernoulli(p),\forall t\in[H]
. Figure

provides an example of switchback experiments.

The following assumption states that the base-stock levels after scaling in the treatment group are always greater than those in the control group.

Assumption 1

(Monotonicity)
.

For any
n\in[N]
,
t\in[H]
,
0\leqslant S_{n,t}(\boldsymbol{0})\leqslant S_{n,t}(\boldsymbol{1})
.

In practice, the predetermined base-stock levels in the treatment group may be larger than those in the control group because they are obtained from the newsvendor formula (
3
) where the demands are overestimated. When
\sum_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{T}\leq B
and
s_{n,t}^{C}\leq s_{n,t}^{T}
for any
n\in[N]
, Assumption
1
holds immediately. However, when
\sum_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{T}>B
, Assumption
1
does not always hold true.
The following lemma provides another two cases where Assumption
1
is satisfied.

Lemma 1
.

When one of the following conditions holds, Assumption
1
is satisfied:

1.

(Homogeneous) For any
t\in[H]
, all items have the same predetermined base-stock levels in control (treatment), i.e.,

s_{n,t}^{T}=s_{t}^{T}\geqslant s_{t}^{C}=s_{n,t}^{C},\forall n\in[N].

2.

(Affine) For any
t\in[H]
, there is an affine transformation between predetermined base-stock levels in control and treatment:

\displaystyle s_{n,t}^{T}=\alpha s_{n,t}^{C}+\beta,

where
\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{C}<B
,
\alpha\geqslant 1
,
0\leqslant\beta\leqslant\overline{\beta}
, and

\displaystyle\overline{\beta}=\begin{cases}\infty,&\text{ if }\max\limits_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{C}\leqslant\frac{B}{N},\\
\frac{\alpha(B-\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{C})}{N-\frac{B}{\max\limits_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{C}}},&\text{ if }\max\limits_{n\in[N]}s_{n,t}^{C}>\frac{B}{N}.\end{cases}

We further assume that the base-stock levels after scaling in switchback experiments cannot change too much in two consecutive periods.

Assumption 2
.

For any
n\in[N],\ t\in[H-1],

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1}\}}S_{n,t}(\boldsymbol{w})-\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{w^{\prime}}\in\{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{1}\}}S_{n,t+1}(\boldsymbol{w^{\prime}})\leqslant\underline{D}_{n,t}.

Assumption
2
rules out the cases where the inventory level at the beginning of one period is greater than the base-stock level in the inventory policy and thus no order is placed in that period. This is a mild assumption because in practice the base-stock levels in the treatment group are usually close to those in the control group.

The next theorem states that in switchback experiments, the naive estimator is conservative and always underestimates the GTE.

Theorem 1

(bias of IPW estimator, switchback experiment)
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
GTE>0
and the bias of the IPW estimator in switchback experiment is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{GTE}^{SW}\right]-GTE=-\frac{1}{NH}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{t=1}^{H-1}c_{n}\left[\mathbb{E}(S_{n,t}(\boldsymbol{1})-D_{n,t})^{+}-\mathbb{E}(S_{n,t}(\boldsymbol{0})-D_{n,t})^{+}\right],

which means that it will underestimate
GTE
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{GTE}^{SW}\right]\leqslant GTE.

Intuitively, in the SW experiment, the bias of IPW estimator stems from the temporal dynamics of inventory levels. Due to the alternating treatment assignments, inventory levels in the SW experiment fluctuate between those in GT and GC.
Specifically, when transitioning from the control group to the treatment group, the base-stock policy from the previous period results in a lower initial inventory level in the current period compared to that in GT. Consequently, the inventory manager must place a larger order to achieve the desired base-stock level, leading to higher ordering costs than in GT. Conversely, when shifting from the treatment group back to the control group, the higher initial inventory level in the current period requires smaller orders than in GC.
These discrepancies in ordering costs cause the naive estimator to overestimate the cost difference between treatment and control, ultimately resulting in an underestimation of GTE.

Under stationary conditions, the SR design demonstrates behavior analogous to the IR design, primarily because both derive their estimation bias from item-level randomization. In SR experiments, each item undergoes a single transition from control to treatment, with subsequent periods assigned to the treatment group. This design feature reduces the impact of temporal inventory variations, as treatment adjustments occur only once per item at the transition period
H_{n}
, rather than repeatedly as seen in switchback designs. As a result, the dominant source of bias stems from the differential treatment of items over time, similar to IR design, leading to GTE overestimation.

However, the SR design becomes problematic under non-stationary conditions, where demand distributions or order quantities exhibit temporal dependencies. For example, consider a scenario with an increasing demand trend: if demand grows over time while order quantities remain constant, items transitioning to treatment in later periods naturally experience higher profits due to increased demand, regardless of the treatment effect. This could result in spurious GTE estimates - either positive when treatment periods coincide with higher demands, or negative in the opposite case - even when the true treatment effect is null. Such systematic bias, combined with the difficulty in disentangling temporal trends from treatment effects, renders the SR design unsuitable for non-stationary environments.

In contrast, under relaxed capacity constraints where capacity is sufficient to meet desired base-stock levels, the system can be decomposed into independent parallel experiments for each item. The independence of item-level treatment effects in this regime explains the superior performance of the IR estimator.

In intermediate capacity constraint scenarios, where both capacity sharing effects and temporal dynamics are present, the Pairwise Randomization (PR) estimator effectively balances these competing factors. By integrating both time-varying and item-varying randomization, PR leverages the benefits of both SW and IR designs, resulting in relatively low bias across a reasonable range of constraint conditions.

It is also worth noting that the SW estimator exhibits significantly larger variance compared to other estimators across all scenarios. This increased variance is primarily due to the relatively short experimental time horizon (

1400

When the system operates under tight capacity constraints or with low base-stock levels (understocking scenarios), the SW estimator is recommended due to its ability to maintain consistent treatment effects through proportional scaling and minimal changes in inventory levels.

5
Conclusion and Future Works

In this paper, we examine various experimental designs in multi-item, multi-period inventory systems and analyze the biases associated with each approach. Our findings summarized in Table

4
provide clear managerial insights. Specifically, we recommend switchback experiments when capacity constraints are tight or when the supply-demand relationship leads to understocking. Conversely, item-level randomization is more suitable when these constraints are looser. For scenarios where the capacity constraints or supply-demand relationships are moderate, we suggest pairwise randomization as a balanced approach.

For future work, we plan to explore alternative forms of two-sided randomization and evaluate their performance under weaker assumptions.
