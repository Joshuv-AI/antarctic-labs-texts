Recurrent somatic copy number alterations in resected cerebral cavernous malformations
Authors: Andrew K. Ressler, Evon Debose-Scarlett, Amanda Fuenzalida, Rhonda Lightle, Shantel Weinsheimer, Marie E. Faughnan, Edda Spiekerkoetter, Katharina Schimmel, Michael Lawton, Helen Kim, Issam Awad, Douglas A. Marchuk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cerebral Cavernous Malformations (CCMs) are brain vascular lesions that occur in sporadic or inherited (autosomal dominant) forms. The malformations are driven by mutations in KRIT1, CCM2, PDCD10 or MAP3K3. Known oncogenic variants in PIK3CA accompany CCM-specific variants in lesions. While the primary genetic etiology of CCM lesions is relatively well understood, a subset of lesions does not yet have an identified molecular genetic etiology. Moreover, whether large genomic alterations occur somatically in CCM lesion tissue has been largely unexplored. In PIK3CA + cancers, large somatic copy number alterations (‘CNAs’) are frequent, with whole genome doubling and aneuploidy identified in most tumors. Such CNA events are known to be associated with course of disease and therapeutic response. In this study, using whole genome SNP-genotyping and Mosaic Chromosome Alteration (MoChA) analysis, we identify the presence of large (> 1 MB) somatic CNAs in CCMs, with specific enrichment of events in chromosome arms 16p,19p,17q, 20q. We also identify additional chromosome arm level events encompassing known CCM genes in a subset of lesions. Thus, we characterize a pattern of large genomic events that had remained hidden by the insensitivity of the molecular and analytical methods previously used. Finally, we propose that similar events may be found in other vascular malformations or PIK3CA overgrowth syndromes that have yet to be analyzed in this manner.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00886-8.

Introduction

Cerebral Cavernous Malformations are brain vascular lesions driven by abnormal collections of capillaries. The majority of lesions are sporadic (~ 80%), with patients typically exhibiting a solitary lesion. A minority of lesions are inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion (~ 20%), with familial patients exhibiting tens or hundreds of lesions [1]. Germline mutations have been identified in one of three genes that cause the inherited forms of CCM [2–4]. Currently, the genetic etiology of CCM formation can broadly be summarized in one of two established mechanisms:Biallelic loss-of-function events in one of the three genes that make up the CCM signaling complex (KRIT1, CCM2, PDCD10).A somatic variant in MAP3K3 (I441M).

Notably, our lab and others have regularly identified additional somatic activating mutations in PIK3CA, in familial and sporadic lesions, alongside both CCM gene and MAP3K3 variants [5–9]. This multigenic mechanism, inclusive of driver genes and growth suppressor genes, is reminiscent of what is observed in cancer. In cancer, activating PIK3CA variants are regularly found alongside other modulatory genomic events that contribute to the aggressivity of tumors and/or their response to therapeutic agents [10–13]. One prominent mechanism seen in PIK3CA + cancers is somatic large genomic alterations, inclusive of telomere-spanning loss or gain of chromosomal arms and copy-neutral loss-of-heterozygosity. In fact, approximately 1/3 of all chromosome arms in PIK3CA + cancers have a large somatic copy number alteration ‘CNA’ [14].

CNAs in cancer have distinct signatures depending on tissue type and mutational burden [11, 15, 16]. CNAs have been well characterized in a variety of cancers, with a subset of large events directly implicated in the course of disease [17] and response to therapy [18, 19]. Given the large number of CNAs in PIK3CA + cancers, along with PIK3CA’s known roles in centrosome amplification and whole genome doubling [10], we hypothesized CCMs were also likely to exhibit a number of previously unidentified CNAs.

To efficiently identify CNAs across all chromosomes in a large set of CCM samples requires a scalable approach. Recently, we showed that using MoChA [20, 21], a Hidden Markov Model (‘HMM’) based algorithm that utilizes estimated phasing and B-allele frequencies (the allelic intensity ratio of a heterozygous SNP) to identify mosaic CNAs, we can identify a full chromosome event in a previously studied sample with estimated cell fraction < 2% using whole genome sequencing data [22, 23]. SNP arrays, although a less rich dataset than WGS, provide a scalable source of genomic information from which to identify CNAs. However, algorithmic adjustments to MoChA’s base pipeline may be required to optimize for sensitive identification of somatic CNAs in CCMs due to the prevalence of low frequency events and the enrichment of telomere and centromere bounded events common in cancer, including PIK3CA + cancers [14, 24]. Thus, despite the obstacles, we hypothesized that utilizing SNP arrays and fine-tuning of MoChA, we could identify a pattern of large somatic CNAs in CCMs that have heretofore remained hidden from view.

Materials and methods

Sample details

Samples were acquired and DNA extracted as previously described [9, 22, 23]. Briefly, surgically resected vascular malformation tissue was flash frozen, frozen in OCT or formalin-fixed and stored at Duke. A section of resected lesion was then cut and processed using Qiagen DNeasy blood and tissue kit. Formalin fixed tissues were pretreated with Qiagen FFPE Tissue kit. All DNA samples used for this analysis had previously undergone targeted sequencing and all CCM samples had undergone ddPCR for PIK3CA and MAP3K3 variants, as described previously [8, 9].

SNP array data acquisition

Extracted DNA was sent to CD Genomics (cd-genomics.com) for SNP array processing using Illumina’s Infinium Global Diversity Array v1.0, providing genome-wide coverage with over 1.8 million SNPs. SNP array data consisted of 106 samples resected from patients with either CCMs or arteriovenous malformations (AVMs). AVMs included sporadic brain AVMs and telangiectases and visceral organ AVMs from Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia (HHT) patients. 105/106 samples generated SNP array data, while one sample failed to amplify. Illumina’s Array Analysis CLI v2.1 was used to generate genotype call (GTC) files from IDAT files returned by CD Genomics. This allowed for a B allele frequency (BAF; proportion of B allele), Log-R ratio (LRR; total genotyping intensity of A and B alleles) and genotype call for each locus.

Sample level quality control

We excluded any samples with a genotype call rate < 95% and further filtration was performed by assessing for likelihood of contamination using BAFRegress [25], which utilizes a linear regression model and population frequencies to estimate contamination. We excluded any sample with BAFRegress values >2%. Our final data set of samples passing quality controls included 93 lesions and associated non-lesional controls from patients with CCMs or AVMs (arteriovenous malformations) as an outgroup for comparison.

Data processing of SNP arrays

BCF files were generated from GTC files using gtc2vcf (https://github.com/freeseek/gtc2vcf). Similarly, loci in BCF files were lifted over from GRCh37 to GRCh38 as described by gtc2vcf. BCFs were phased using the SHAPEIT4 software [26]. Regions known to be difficult to sequence, including segmental duplications, repetitive sequences and regions challenging to map, were taken from Genome In a Bottle v3.0 [27] utilizing the union of all potentially problematic regions. All difficult regions were then excluded from the phased BCF files prior to identification of Copy Number Variants.

Next, the BCFtools plug-in MoChA (2021-01-20 release) was used to call mosaic CNVs, taking advantage of haplotype phasing of heterozygous SNP sites and BAF estimates. When using MoChA, an LRR-weight of 0.1 was used for all conditions to minimize the calling of events predominantly identified using estimated depth. Then, six different conditions were used for the underlying Hidden Markov Model. Base (xy-major = 65,xy-minor = 35,auto-tel = 20), minor* (65,20,20), minor tel (65,20,10), minor tel* (65,20,5), major (50,35,20), major* (35,35,20). These conditions were selected to vary penalties for events spanning centromeres and telomeres with all adjusted conditions more sensitive to events spanning the centromeres and telomeres than ‘Base’ (default) conditions. All calls for each condition are shown in extended data Table 1.

For analysis of enrichment of CNVs by disease and by domain, the most sensitive condition (minor tel*) was utilized with events requiring logarithm of the odds (LOD) scores > 3, estimated cell fraction < 20% and event size > 1 MB. For lesion enriched events, events with a shared start or end point were reported if either the lesional or non-lesional event was > 1 MB.

MoChA estimates the cell fraction of events and estimates the type (direction) of event (Gain, Loss or Copy Neutral-Loss of Heterozygosity, CN-LOH) using BAF deviation and an expectation maximization algorithm respectively [28]. We report the determinations of cell fraction and event type but note it remains challenging to efficiently call the direction of an event with the low cell frequencies of somatic events that are typical in CCMs and AVMs. Thus, we do not emphasize event type.

Statistical tests of enrichment

We used a two-sided proportional test to determine whether events were enriched in CCMs or in samples with certain PIK3CA protein domain variants. Events were categorized by presence or absence in a chromosome arm by patient. For example, patients with events identified in chr5p in multiple lesions would count as 1. Such an approach makes our analysis more conservative as our AVMs samples consist of 52 lesions across 31 patients, while CCMs samples consist of 36 lesions across 34 patients. However, quantifying by lesion would be problematic as somatic events are not necessarily independent and may have shared clonal origination.

For each structural domain of the PIK3CA protein, we tested that event compared to the events across the other two domain types, e.g. we test C2 domain events versus helical and kinase domain event.

HMM parameter validation

HMM parameters were assessed using LOD score of called events, positive control samples and ddPCR. Specifically, we used the LOD score based on BAF and phase across all loci in CNV called by the HMM. Briefly, the LOD score estimates the ratio of likelihood that an event has deviated from heterozygosity compared to the likelihood the event is heterozygous. The test statistic used is calculated using , which is the absolute value of B-allele frequency deviation from 0.5 multiplied by the haplotype, which is either − 1 or 1 depending on imputed phase. The null distribution is simulated empirically by randomizing phase across a sample and assuming a majority of the sample is diploid. Finally, the LOD score is calculated for a given event by quantifying 10 (likelihood not heterozygous / likelihood heterozygous). Thus, a LOD score of 3 states that the likelihood the region is not heterozygous is 1000x more likely than the likelihood the region is heterozygous.

For the positive controls, we used events we previously identified in < 2% of cells in Chr7p (CCM5004) and Chr7p + q (CCM5078) [22]. Notably, the base algorithm did not identify an event in CCM5004. The only conditions to call an event in Chr7p were minor tel and minor tel*, yet both extended the event across both arms, underlying the continued challenge in identifying breakpoints in samples with low cell fraction events. For CCM5078, all conditions called this event, but minor tel incorrectly split the event, excluding the p arm. Based on these results, we selected minor tel* as the most applicable algorithm to identify large, low frequency, somatic events in our data set.

We then selected the events with highest estimated cell fraction called in minor tel* that were absent in the base algorithm and used ddPCR CNV assays from Bio-Rad to quantify evidence of CNVs orthogonally. Two samples with no known events in any of the three regions were used as controls. For chromosomes 17q and 19q, two independent CNV assays in distinct chromosomes were used as internal controls, while only one internal control was used to test 16p events. Two technical replicates were used for each internal control. Events were considered ‘present’ if the 95% CI determined using a Poisson distribution for one (16p) or both (17q and 19q) does not overlap with that of either control sample. 3/5 events tested were confirmed present, while the other two had nominal increases, but overlapping confidence intervals.

Cancer comparisons

CNA events for cancer were taken directly from the supplementary data from Taylor and colleagues analysis of aneuploidy in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [14]. Aneuploidy events were categorized as -1 (loss), 1 (gain), 0 (no event) by chromosome arm. To assess CNA patterns, data was subset to only include PIK3CA + cancers. We then generated proportions of events by chromosome arm either by disease or by domain.

We first assessed cancer independently, benefitting from the additional direction data that is not quantified in our CCM results due to the high frequency of undetermined events and limited total number of called gains / losses, representing a limitation for interpreting functional impact of somatic CNAs in CCMs. In addition to proportion of events by arm, we quantified the strength of direction by calculating the average of all called events, generating a number between − 1 and 1. Using both proportion and direction by chromosome arm, we generated principal component analysis (PCA) plots and similarity scores across types and domains (extended data Table 2). Similarity scores were determined by the Euclidean distance across the top 5 principal components (PCs).

We then compared cancer CNA patterns to CCMs determined by proportion of patients with events for each chromosome arm. Short p-arms of the acrocentric chromosomes 13,14,15 and 21 are not considered. Distance metrics were calculated similarly. For all comparisons, only types or type/domain combinations with at least 10 samples were considered.

To determine the correlation between perturbed domain and cancer CNA patterns, we performed PERMANOVA [29] tests on the raw CNA data from PIK3CA + cancers using both interaction and additive linear models. Analysis was performed using the adonis2 function in R with 999 permutations and Euclidean distance. PERMANOVA outputs are shown in extended data Table 3. We further assessed the contribution of domain to each of the top five principal components using an ANOVA additive linear model (extended data Table 3).

DdPCR

ddPCR was performed with CNV assays described below in duplicate with ~ 3-6ng of DNA used in each reaction and PCR conditions based on Bio-Rad’s recommendations for each CNV assay. Droplet digital PCR was performed using Bio-Rad’s QX200 and analyzed using QX Manager software.

DdPCR assays

To target chromosomal events in chromosomes 16p, 17q and 19p, Bio-Rad assays targeting genes SOX8 (dHsaCP2507184 hg19|chr16:1036556–1036678), ACTG1 (dHsaCNS350421021 hg19|chr17:79478075–79478197) and STK11 (dHsaCP2506366 hg19|chr19:1221153–1221275) were used respectively. For both HEX-labeled probes, SOX8 and STK11, internal control probe targeted FAM-labeled PIK3CA (dHsaCP2506260 hg19|chr3:178951888–178952010). For SOX8, RPP30 (dHsaCP2500313.

hg19|chr10:92660373–92660495) was used as a secondary control. STK11 and RPP30 probes interfered, preventing the use of a secondary internal control for 16p. For FAM-labeled ACTG1, HEX-labeled probes RPP30 (dHsaCP2500313.

hg19|chr10:92660373–92660495) and AGO1 (dHsaCP2500349 hg19|chr1:36359312–36359434) were used as internal controls. All internal control regions did not show evidence of CNAs.

Results

36 CCM lesions from 34 patients passed SNP-array QC

We performed SNP-array analyses on DNA extracted from lesions from 34 CCM patients and 31 AVM patients as an outgroup for comparison, creating a set of 106 CCMs and AVMs (Fig. 1a) to identify somatic CNAs. We expected large events leading to loss-of-heterozygosity to be prevalent in CCM lesions, given frequency of somatic mutations and large chromosomal events, including aneuploidy and whole genome doubling, in PIK3CA + cancers. To filter out potentially contaminated or low quality samples, we excluded samples based on baf_regress > 0.02 and effective call rate < 95%. 93 of 106 samples passed QC, including 36 CCM lesions, 5 non-lesions control samples and 52 AVMs (Fig. 1b).

The CCM tissue samples represent a genetic spectrum, with familial, sporadic, PIK3CA+, PIK3CA- and MAP3K3 + lesions all present (extended data Table 4). A subset of SNP-array data for the AVMs have been previously published, with identified somatic CNAs leading to biallelic loss of known HHT genes, ACVRL1 or ENG [23]. The remainder of AVM lesions, alongside 36 CCM lesions and 5 non-lesion controls have previously undergone ddPCR and targeted-sequencing as previously described [8, 9]. We sought a source of tissue to serve as a control for the analysis of the CCM lesions. It is not possible to obtain adjacent normal vascular or brain tissue from the patients during surgical resection of the CCMs. However, we surmised CNA events that were identified in the CCM samples but underrepresented or absent in the AVM samples would be more likely be involved in CCM pathobiology. And importantly, unlike most of the CCMs, none of the AVMs were associated with a PIK3CA somatic mutation. Thus, these AVMs could serve as a vascular lesion “control” in the analyses. Critically, we hypothesized the existing base MoChA algorithm would lack sensitivity to low frequency somatic CNAs, thus we included two samples with previously reported [22], low frequency, somatic loss-of-heterozygosity events as algorithmic controls.

Optimized identification of somatic LOH events in CCMs

We aimed to identify large, somatic events and utilized thresholds of < 20% of cells and >1 MB in size for confirmed CNAs. Notably, MoChA was trained for broad utility on UK Biobank data and the vast majority of events identified had minor allele frequencies well in excess of those expected in vascular malformations (< 5%). Further, cancer is enriched for telomere and centromere bounded [24] events, and the PIK3CA signaling pathway modulates telomere stability [30]. Thus, we hypothesized the base MoChA algorithm may miss low frequency events in CCMs extending to the telomere and centromere. However, the model is easily adjusted, with the ability to reduce the penalty in MoChA’s Hidden Markov Model (‘HMM’) on events that extend towards the telomere (‘tel’), towards the centromere (‘major’) or away from the centromere (‘minor’). We further explored a combination of reducing both the minor and telomere penalty (‘min tel’), and a larger reduction of penalties (min tel*,major*).

Using the base MoChA HMM parameters, we identified 74 CNAs, with 71 exhibiting a LOD score greater than 3. Critically, while the HMM identifies likely events, a LOD score is separately determined based on the B-allele frequency and estimated phasing across the called event. The predominance of LOD > 3 events suggest high specificity. Adjusting the major penalty had minimal impact on event identification, while the most permissive condition was identified by loosening the minor penalty, alongside large reduction on telomeric penalty (Fig. 2a). Notably, even with the most permissive events, there was a much larger increase in LOD > 3 (+ 140) events than LOD < 3 (+ 55) events. However, the increase in lower likelihood results suggest a likely increase in spurious events, even when controlling for LOD score.

To orthogonally assess the performance of the different conditions chosen for analysis, we assessed the capacity for the conditions to identify known, low frequency events previously identified in chromosomes 7p (Fig. 2b) and 7p + q (Fig. 2c). Notably, only the most permissive condition, minor + tel*, correctly identified the presence of chr7 events in both samples, while correctly identifying CCM5078’s event as encompassing both chromosome arms. While the best performing, this condition extended the event in CCM5004 to encompass the whole chromosome, likely driven by MoChA’s approach to arm-level events, where arm-level events utilize a direct comparison between chromosomal arms [20, 21], which is susceptible to errors in ultra-low frequency events.

Given the performance on known low frequency events, we then attempted to validate that the more permissive condition was not predominantly identifying spurious events. While no existing method exists to confirm such low frequency events without availability of primary tissue, we hypothesized ddPCR may be able to validate events estimated in higher cell fractions. We selected five events across three samples with > 10% minor allele frequency to validate utilizing commercially available ddPCR CNV assays targeting genes mapping within the regions of the identified CNAs, alongside genes in regions without identified events to be used as internal controls. Further, we took two samples without events in any of the known regions to use as negative controls. Notably, three of five events tested showed copy number differences using ddPCR and the remaining two events showed nominal increases compared to the two controls (Fig. 2d). Given the prevalence of events with LOD > 3, performance on two lesions with known large somatic events and validation of a subset of CNAs with ddPCR, we selected min + tel* for downstream analyses of CCM CNAs.

LOH events in CCMs encompass known CCM-related genes

Somatic CNAs have yet to be robustly characterized in any vascular malformations, and thus all VM events are of interest. However, the potential for technical artifacts when loosening parameters of HMM, along with propensity of telomere spanning events, led us to focus on events enriched in CCMs, while utilizing AVMs, a set of different vascular malformations, as control samples. We emphasize that leveraging statistically enriched events in CCMs compared to AVMs, while removing the capacity to identify shared signatures across both types of vascular malformations, is critical to minimize the risk of artifactual findings.

We were first interested in evidence of events encompassing any of the three CCM signaling complex genes (KRIT1, CCM2, PDCD10), as a single variant is not sufficient to cause disease, thus CNAs may present a genetic ‘second-hit [22, 23, 31].’ All six lesional events encompassing loss-of-function CCM genes were in MAP3K3- somatic mutation containing CCMs from sporadic (non-familial) patients, including five lesions (two positive controls, three newly reported) and one sample from neighboring non-lesional brain. Notably, no events encompassing any of these three genes were found in any AVMs or lesions from MAP3K3 + CCM patients.

We then considered all other chromosomal arms and note significant enrichment in chromosome 16p, 19p and 17q (Fig. 3a). Notably, there is strong overlap with a recent analysis of copy number alterations in cancer, which re-confirmed aneuploidy in chromosomes 1q,7,8q,16p,17q and 20, with chromosomes 16p,17q and 20q representing the three of the four largest number of single arm CNAs, while chromosome 7 represents the largest nominal enrichment across entirety of a chromosome [11].

Notably, events were predominantly telomere spanning events, which remains true using base MoChA conditions (extended data Table 1). Critically, CNAs in chromosomes 7, 16p,17q and 19p are enriched compared to AVM controls. Further, in cancer, telomere-bounded somatic CNAs were larger than other CNAs and were numerically enriched compared to internal events. Thus, enrichment of telomere-bounded events in CCMs is to be expected, but the predominance of such events may suggest limited sensitivity to internal events using current approach.

Further, MoChA was unable to determine an estimated direction (loss, gain, copy neutral LOH) for ~ 28% of identified events. Such estimations are determined by deviation from the expected log R ratio, which is not robust for samples low variant cell fractions such as commonly found in vascular malformations. Thus, we cannot confidently identify the direction of events and focus instead on the presence or absence of CNAs.

While the signature of copy number events in CCMs shows some consistency and overlap with PIK3CA + cancers, vascular malformations and malignant tumors are distinct disorders. Thus, when we consider regions of CNAs in CCMs below, we explore events with oncogenic and angiogenic implications (extended data Table 5).

16p

Encompassed within somatic 16p CNAs are genes TSC2 and PKD1. TSC2 is a well-established tumor suppressor gene and variants lead to the development of hamartomas, including in the brain. Concurrent TSC2 injury in CCMs may affect the capacity of growth for cavernomas.

More notably, two sisters with Autosomal Dominant Kidney Disease, driven by biallelic loss of PKD1, each had co-occurring solitary CCMs without any identified CCM variant [32]. 16p events, or PKD1 variants may lead to predisposition for CCMs, but further work would be required given the limited reports.

19p

19p CNAs are the most common event in CCM lesions (n = 9, median estimated size ~ 2.5MBs). Notably, a rare condition of terminal region 19p has been reported, but lacks any evidence of vascular malformations, suggesting these events are unlikely to be directly related to lesion genesis [33]. Further, a ~ 200KB duplication encompassed within this region has been seen in thousands of healthy patients, suggesting a portion of this CNA is well tolerated (extended data Table 5). However, majority of events include genes CFD and STK11. CFD participates in the innate immune system and has been found to be upregulated across cell types in resected CCMs [34]. Similarly, STK11 is a known tumor suppressor gene, which has been implicated in modulating immune response in lung cancer [35]. Together, we suggest 19p events are unlikely to specifically contribute to lesion genesis but may modulate the distinct immune response within CCM lesions.

17q

Terminal CNAs in chromosome 17q have been reported to cause a genetic syndrome inclusive of developmental delay and intellectual disability, but no clear evidence of vascular malformations [36, 37]. Chromosome 17q events have been associated with shorter progression free survival in neuroblastoma and thus may contribute to aggressivity of CCM lesions [38, 39].

20q

We also explored the relationship between 20q microdeletions and vascular malformations, given their aforementioned prevalence in cancer. Microdeletions in chromosome 20q13.3 lead to epileptic encephalopathy [40], and thinness or extreme obesity [41]. Further this region includes SRMS, which has previously been identified as dysregulated in hemorrhagic CCMs [42]. It also includes SOX18 which is proposed to be a central regulator of infantile hemangioma [43] and is a direct target of the drug propranolol [44]. Notably, propranolol is currently in clinical trials for CCM, and events encompassing SOX18 in a subset of lesions may impact the therapeutic response.

Characterizing CNAs in C2, helical and kinase domain variant lesions

Notably, the relationship between the PIK3CA variant protein domain and copy number signature has not been previously established. Further, genetic perturbation of distinct domains of PIK3CA operate through distinct mechanisms [45] and generate specific conformational changes [46]. Thus, using previously quantified CNAs [14], we assessed the relevance of domain to copy number signature.

Cancer type (‘type’) has previously been established as a contributor to copy number signature [47]; thus any domain effects were assessed alongside cancer type. We first performed PCA on copy number events of all PIK3CA + cancers and quantified the relative contribution of type and domain. We identified a significant contribution of type to all five top principal components, but additionally found the variant domain contributes to PCs3-5 (extended data Table 3), suggesting a critical role of domain to CNA signature. To further quantify the relationship between domain and patterns of aneuploidy, we used permutational multivariate analysis of variance across the raw CNA data and found a significant contribution of domain to CNAs using both additive and interaction models. These results suggest the mutated PIK3CA domain impacts the CNA signature in cancer, with affects pronounced in certain cancer types (extended data Table 2).

Given the pre-eminent correlation of tissue type on CNAs in the larger cancer data set, we first investigated the similarity between the CNA signature of CCMs and tumors. We find the CCM signature is more like endometrial cancer and low-grade glioma than other cancer types (Fig. 4a, extended data Table 2). We then assessed all domain / type combinations with at least 10 events and show strong similarity between PIK3CA Helical and Kinase domain subsets (Fig. 4b). chr17q was the only arm representing a significant increase in Helical domain variants (Fig. 4c).

Notably, C2 domain variants are more prevalent in PIK3CA-overgrowth syndromes compared to cancer [48]. Further, in uterine corpus endometrial cancer (UCEC), helical and kinase domain CNA signatures cluster together, while C2 domain tumors are more distant in PC space (Fig. 4b, extended data Table 2). Unfortunately, C2-domain lesions remain infrequent in this CCM dataset, limiting the power to assess the specificity of CNAs to the C2 domain. Nevertheless, 2/3 and 3/3 C2-domain lesions have large events in chr18q and chr20q (Fig. 4c), which are both significant increases. Notably, both chr18 events were identified as copy neutral events, representing the only copy neutral events in chr18 in the dataset. The only known imprinted gene included in both events is PARD6G, which is known to regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway [49].

Given the significant association of PIK3CA C2 domain variants with a distinct pattern of CNAs in a published large cancer dataset and the intriguing findings in our underpowered dataset of three C2 domain CCMs, this work motivates further exploration of the interplay between C2 domain variants and genetic CNAs in CCMs.

Finally, we examined the presence or absence of lesion specific events in four CC < samples with associated DVAs (Developmental Venous Anomaly) and/or non-lesional brain tissue. We identified several lesion-specific (Fig. 5a) or lesion enriched (Fig. 5b) events. Events were considered lesion specific if no overlapping event was identified by MoChA in the associated control, regardless of LOD score. Similarly, events were considered lesion enriched if an overlapping event was identified by MoChA in the associated control and estimated cell fraction of the lesion was at least double the neighboring tissue. Four lesion specific events occurred in chromosome arms specifically implicated in CCMs. Lesion enriched events in Hu151 are suggestive of clonal expansion of the neighboring DVA, which has been previously reported [9].

Discussion

The development of CCM lesions is driven by a combination of either loss-of-function variants in a CCM gene or an activating variant in MAP3K3, alongside an activating variant in PIK3CA. Such a mechanism is reminiscent of PIK3CA + cancers, where activating variants in PIK3CA fuel tumor growth [8]. In such cancers, large copy number events encompassing genes and loci associated with the aggressivity or therapeutic response of cancers are regularly identified. Notably, after optimizing existing algorithms, we identify similar large CNAs in CCM lesions, with a subset of chromosomal arms specifically implicated.

We find that a robust analysis of copy number alterations in CCMs may partially answer both critical outstanding questions. Events encompassing the three CCM genes are exclusively identified in CCM lesions, corroborating our previous work confirming such events may underlie a substantial portion of lesions without a known variant in a CCM gene or MAP3K3. Further, we identify recurrent events in chromosomes 16p, 19p, 17q and 20q, encompassing both known oncogenes and vascular malformation genes. In cancer, such events are known to impact aggressivity and therapeutic response and may reasonably have a similar impact in CCMs, given the shared perturbation of the PI3 kinase signaling pathway.

The frequency of whole genome doubling, aneuploidy and large structural events in PIK3CA + cancers is consistent with the large proportion of CNAs in CCMs extending to the telomere, and this pattern remained true both before and after algorithmic adjustment to optimize for the identification of such events. Notably, regions near the telomere have high levels of genomic instability. To minimize the risk of spurious identification of CNAs, particularly with more lenient MoChA HMM parameters, we utilized AVMs as a control cohort and highlight events enriched in CCMs. A statistics-based approach was necessary given the inability to broadly experimentally validate low frequency somatic CNAs through alternative mechanisms. Nevertheless, we were able to utilize ddPCR to orthogonally validate evidence of CNAs in three distinct sub-telomeric regions that only were identified with loosened HMM conditions and had estimated cell frequencies > 10%. Nevertheless, the work above would benefit from a larger sample size of CCMs and/or additional types of control tissue to more robustly rule out technical artifacts as a contributor to smaller events extending to the telomere.

When comparing the pattern of CNAs in CCMs to PIK3CA + cancers, the signature of CNAs in CCMs overlaps most with endometrial cancer and low-grade glioma, which may suggest some overlap in etiology between slow growing cancers and vascular malformations in the brain. Intriguingly, in a mouse model of CCM, testicular cavernomas emerged in mature mice, the only such organ outside the CNS to generate lesions [8]. Further, recent work has identified a critical association between progesterone signaling and the CCM signaling complex [50, 51] and Pik3ca-driven vascular malformations in mice consistently appeared in reproductive organs [52]. In combination, these results suggest the relationship between CCMs and the development of reproductive vascular anomalies should be further investigated.

PIK3CA-overgrowth disorders are more likely to be driven by C2 domain variants than PIK3CA + cancers. Although such variants represent a minority of CCMs, we suggest that a modulating event may be necessary to cause the development of a CCM lesion. Unfortunately, CCM tissue that has acquired a PIK3CA C2 domain variant is rare, limiting the power to detect this domain-specific enrichment of CNAs. Nevertheless, we find repetitive events in the three C2 domain samples (2/3 18q and 3/3 20q) and believe further investigation of domain specific effects may elucidate genetic mechanisms of CCMs, and PIK3CA overgrowths more broadly, driven by C2 domain variants. One potential hypothesis involves CN-LOH events affecting the imprinted gene PARD6G, which is frequently inactivated in certain cancers leading to increased proliferation [49, 53]. Further, PARD6G has been found to control protein composition of the centrosome [54], which may lead to a synergistic affect given the established relationship between oncogenic PIK3CA variants and centrosome amplification.

Critically, accurately identifying low frequency CNAs remains challenging. In addition to technical challenges described in this manuscript, we have previously described heterogeneity within lesions leading subsamples of same malformation with distinct mutational burden [22]. Here, we focused on increased sensitivity by adjusting the HMM within the MoChA algorithm but note an increase in results with less statistical significance, suggesting concurrent introduction of additional noise. Further, validating precise breakpoints is inaccessible without an abundance of tissue. This is true even in tumors where much higher variant allele frequencies are often observed. Precise breakpoints would inform functional interpretation as they may suggest truncated, alternatively spliced or fused proteins, which have been frequently identified as pathogenic in cancer. Given the limitations on identifying breakpoints and the direction (gain / loss) of these low frequency events, we focused our analysis on presence or absence of somatic CNAs in chromosomal arms and only considered genes encompassed in the majority of nearby events.

We also note that identification of CNAs using MoChA is dependent on accurate identification and phasing of germline SNPs. A much higher number of variants would be phased with access to trio data, leading to both increased sensitivity and specificity. However, a requirement of parental DNA samples for individuals with CCM, which are often resected in late stages of life, would severely restrict sample acquisition. Enriching for somatically mutant cells within the tissue sample might also lead to the identification of additional CNAs, but this would be at the cost of the scalability when employing bulk tissue.

Given the limited availability of resected CCM lesions, associating CNAs to specific phenotypes, such as hemorrhage, epilepsy or lesion volume, and rapid growth, would require collaboration across multiple surgical and research establishments. Resected lesions necessarily were determined aggressive and/or accessible enough to operate on, suggesting such lesions may have a higher burden of genetic injury than asymptomatic lesions. Additionally, our dataset predominantly included sporadic lesions, and the majority of lesions did not include a known somatic variant in MAP3K3 or a CCM gene. Such lesions may be more or less prone to CNAs than CCMs with different genetic background. Further, phenotypic characterization of lesions is often sparse and has historically not been consistent across surgical centers. Our analyses involve SNP-arrays on bulk tissue, which is a relatively economical and scalable approach. Larger scale studies on CNAs, alongside stereotyped phenotypic patient data, may allow us to elucidate which of the events identified above lead to, or can be used as biomarkers of, the heterogeneity of CCM lesions. Of particular interest is whether the aneuploid events identified have a similarly deleterious effect as in cancer.

In summary, we characterize a pattern of large genomic events in CCM tissue that had heretofore remained hidden by the insensitivity of commonly used molecular and analytic methods. Using CCM tissue harboring previously described CNAs identified by single nucleus sequencing, we benchmarked and fine-tuned our molecular and analytical approaches. By orthogonally validating a subset of the newly described CNAs using sensitive ddPCR, we have shown that these genomic events are authentic. We propose that these events should be considered in a larger set of CCMs with detailed clinical characterization to determine whether these genomic events contribute to the phenotypic heterogeneity seen in CCMs. Finally, we propose that this scalable identification of CNAs should be employed to a wider spectrum of vascular malformations, as well as in PIK3CA-overgrowth disorders. Identification of previously hidden genome-scale molecular events may provide for a more robust understanding of the full spectrum of genetic injury in disorders with both distinct and overlapping pathogenesis.
