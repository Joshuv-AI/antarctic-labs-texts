Left Main Coronary Artery Stenting in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension–Related External Compression
Authors: Surenthiran Ramanathan, Chee Kent Er, Asri Ranga Abdullah Ramaiah, Kamaraj Selvaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) can cause significant dilatation of the pulmonary arteries, which may externally compress the left main coronary artery (LMCA). We present 2 such cases successfully treated with percutaneous coronary intervention using drug-eluting stents. Case A was a 29-year-old woman with idiopathic group 1 PAH with severe LMCA stenosis due to main pulmonary artery compression, diagnosed during routine cardiac catheterization. Case B was a 23-year-old woman with PAH secondary to secundum atrial septal defect who presented with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction by LMCA compression from a dilated right pulmonary artery. Both patients underwent successful intravascular ultrasound–guided percutaneous coronary implantation with drug-eluting stent. Follow-up computed tomography coronary angiography at 6 months showed patent stents. Case A is awaiting heart-lung transplantation, and case B is planned for surgical atrial septal defect closure, yet unsuitable given high pulmonary vascular resistance. As this condition is serious but rare, we provide our cases and treatment strategy as recommendations to consider. Coronary treatment aside, addressing the underlying PAH is crucial for sustained outcomes.Key words: coronary stenting, external compression, IVUS, left main coronary artery, MSCT coronary angiography, pulmonary arterial hypertension

Left main coronary artery (LMCA) compression is increasingly recognized from dilatation of the pulmonary arteries (PAs), known to happen in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH). This phenomenon was first described by Corday et al1 in 1957, and subsequent imaging-based studies have confirmed that pulmonary artery dilatation is the dominant driver of LMCA narrowing. Patients can present in a variety of ways, including chronic angina symptoms,1, 2, 3 depressed left ventricular function,3,4 and acute ischemia with elevated troponin levels.3, 4, 5, 6 Currently, LMCA compression from external pulmonary artery enlargement is treated predominantly with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), although selected cases have undergone surgical PA reduction and, more rarely, concomitant coronary artery bypass or pulmonary artery-plasty.3,7, 8, 9 Here we present 2 cases of LMCA compression with completely different presentations that were treated the same way.

Case A

Case A was a 29-year-old woman diagnosed in 2017 with idiopathic group 1 PAH and managed by the pediatric cardiology unit before transfer to our center. Investigations such as right heart study (RHS), multislice computed tomography (MSCT) of the thorax/pulmonary angiography, and transesophageal echocardiography were all done previously, but no coronary angiography was considered. Since 2022, she has been admitted for intermittent hemoptysis, cleared off as PAH-related. During the current work-up, computed tomography (CT) angiography was performed to rule out any focus of pulmonary hemorrhage and pulmonary embolism (Table 1, Figure 1). She had been listed for heart-lung transplantation. As part of the work-up, repeat RHS and routine invasive coronary angiography (ICA) were performed on April 2024, and findings showed compression to the LMCA, the rest of the coronary arteries being normal. Reviewing her previous MSCT in 2023, it was noted that the LMCA was already externally compressed by the main PA, which was not reported. Hence, no repeat CT scan was needed. She was at NYHA functional class I status, with no other symptoms to note. She was planned for staged PCI to the LMCA. The procedure was performed successfully (Table 2, Figure 2, Video 1). She was kept as an inpatient for optimization of medications first before discharge. Follow up MSCT-coronary angiography (MSCT-CA) showed patent LMCA stents. (Figure 3). She was treated with dual antiplatelet therapy for 1 year then single antiplatelet. She is awaiting heart-lung transplant surgery. Optimized with medications: bosentan, tadalafil and pending for approval for selexipag.

Case B

Case B was a 23-year-old woman who had been diagnosed on transthoracic echocardiography as having secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) with pulmonary hypertension since 2019, but defaulted multiple referrals to the cardiac center for evaluation. She presented in June 2024 with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction with a significant rise in troponin I of 1,860 ng/L (normal: <40 ng/L). She was referred back to us for coronary and shunt evaluation. Echocardiography showed large ASD, significant left-to-right shunt, and pulmonary hypertension. In our center, we first performed transesophageal echocardiography, which showed a large ASD with deficient rims, which may need surgical closure. Subsequently, ICA and RHS (same setting) showed LMCA stenoses, with no other involvement. Urgent CT angiography showed LMCA stenosis secondary to external compression from a grossly dilated right PA (Table 1, Figure 4). As with past experience, we planned a staged PCI performed by the same team of operators. This procedure (Table 2, Figure 5, Video 2) was also successful, with the patient hemodynamically stable throughout. She was discharged in good condition. Follow-up MSCT-CA showed a patent LMCA stent (Figure 6). She is planned for fenestrated patch ASD secundum closure and possible tricuspid valve annuloplasty. Reassessment by our team for percutaneous ASD percutaneous closure during a repeat catheter study found the patient was not suited. PAH therapy is currently optimized before ASD closure, as her pulmonary vascular resistance remains high (>5 Wood Units). Her RHS is scheduled again in April 2026 for reassessment before surgery.

Discussion

These cases reflect anatomical LMCA compression from enlarged pulmonary arteries (Table 3, Figure 7). Key predictors include a main PA diameter of ≥40 mm and a PA-to-aorta ratio of 1.2 to 1.5,3,8,9 while CT studies identify a take-off angle <60° and downward LMCA displacement as additional markers.9,10 Transthoracic echocardiography can estimate PA size, but definitive assessment requires CT or invasive angiography.3,8,9 LMCA compression is reported in ≈40% of PAH patients presenting with angina8 and ≈24% of precapillary pulmonary hypertension patients with ≥50% LMCA stenosis. Most cases are detected only after symptoms develop, as data supporting the screening of asymptomatic patients are limited.3,7 PCI is an effective treatment, with favorable long-term outcomes in the absence of intrinsic atherosclerosis.3,7,10 Large series report low restenosis and reintervention rates and no late recoil or fracture.2,5,7 PCI is technically challenging because of frequent ostial involvement, requiring precise aorto-ostial positioning.7,8 Predilatation practices vary and are typically individualized based on intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) findings.7,8 IVUS is essential for accurate diagnosis and optimal stent expansion.4,7 Long-term results are generally favorable, with sustained patency and symptom relief.3,7,8,10 Rare cases of underexpansion or early mechanical failure have been described, including immediate recoil after LMCA PCI due to severe PA compression.4,9 Both of the cases presented here align with published anatomical and clinical patterns, including enlarged pulmonary arteries, elevated PA-to-aorta ratios, and ostial LMCA involvement.9,10 Their presentations reflect the recognized spectrum of LMCA compression, from exertional angina to acute ischemia.3, 4, 5 Procedural and short-term outcomes were favorable and consistent with published PCI experience.2,5,7 Six-month MSCT in both cases confirmed patent stents. No standardized surveillance strategy exists; follow-up practices vary from clinical review7,8 to invasive assessment with selective IVUS or CT reassessment.3,7,8,10

Conclusions

Evidence on coronary intervention in this field remains limited, particularly regarding screening of asymptomatic individuals with enlarged PA, lesion preparation, and postprocedural surveillance intervals. However, available data support the effectiveness and long-term safety of stenting for LMCA compression.

For PAH patients who have symptoms or features of ischemia, ICA or MSCT-CA should be performed. In cases of acute coronary syndrome (raised cardiac biomarkers), ICA should be the first-line imaging modality.

Screening for LMCA compression in PAH is best approached in a targeted manner. In patients without previous MSCT, a baseline MSCT (even pulmonary angiography or thorax as part of PAH work-up) is reasonable to define PA size and assess the LMCA-PA relationship. For patients under long-term follow up, when interval echocardiography shows signs the main PA appears large (main PA ≥40 mm or PA-to-aorta ratio exceeding 1.2), an MSCT should be ordered next.

When LMCA compression is suspected, confirmation must be done with both modalities, ICA and MSCT-CA, then subsequent management planned. In patients with LMCA compression and complex or concomitant coronary lesions, a heart team discussion is appropriate to weigh PCI against surgery. In cases of isolated LMCA compression or when accompanying lesions are straightforward, LMCA ostial stenting is the preferred strategy, with IVUS-guided PCI essential for accurate assessment and optimization. Predilatation is used to improve coronary flow and facilitate device delivery.

For follow-up, MSCT-CA at 6 to 12 months is reasonable to assess stent patency and re-evaluate the LMCA-PA relationship; this reflects local practice rather than an evidence-based standard.
