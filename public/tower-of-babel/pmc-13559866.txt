Comparative Phytochemical and Biological Evaluation of Four Thymus Species From Türkiye for Dermocosmetic Applications
Authors: Ebru Özdemir Nath, Pierre‐Eric Campos, Audrey Le Cabec, Gül İpek Gündoğan, Diren Ergin, Ahsen Çetin, Sefa Sönmez, Emilie Destandau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpecies of Thymus have long been used in Anatolia for wound healing and skin disorders. This study characterized metabolites and biological activities of four taxa—Thymus leucostomus (TL), Thymus sipyleus (TS), Thymus brachychilus (TB), and Thymus migricus (TM)—using UHPLC‐HRMS/MS‐based molecular networking, anti‐aging enzyme inhibition assays, and cellular models (L929 fibroblasts, HaCaT keratinocytes, HUVEC endothelial cells). All taxa showed triterpene‐rich profiles, though TL was enriched in polymethoxylated flavonoids and TM in diterpenes. Extracts strongly inhibited hyaluronidase, collagenase and elastase, with more modest tyrosinase inhibition; the highest values were recorded for TB (hyaluronidase, 89.64%), TM (elastase, 78.31%), and TL (collagenase, 82.92%; tyrosinase, 39.74%). Biologically, TL demonstrated the broadest cytocompatibility and significantly enhanced wound closure in fibroblast (≈84.7%) and keratinocyte (≈71.3%) models. TL and TS achieved nearly complete endothelial wound closure, suggesting potential pro‐regenerative effects. TL showed lower cytotoxicity in HaCaT and HUVEC cells (IC50 ≈ 0.5 mg/mL), whereas TM exhibited stronger cytotoxicity in keratinocytes (IC50 ≈ 0.1025 mg/mL). TM moderately increased intracellular ROS levels in L929 fibroblasts, while TL and TS maintained ROS levels closer to control conditions. Overall, TL and TS displayed more balanced in vitro profiles regarding wound closure, oxidative balance, and enzyme inhibition, whereas TM required more cautious concentration‐dependent interpretation.

Introduction

Thymus L. belongs to the Lamiaceae family, which includes 341 species worldwide [1], 46 species in Türkiye, and among them, 19 species are endemic [2, 3]. The genus Thymus often contains perennial herbs, cushion plants, and tiny shrubs. Thymus species are known to have high concentrations of phenolic compounds such as phenolic acids (rosmarinic acid, ferulic acid, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, p‐coumaric acids) and different classes of flavonoids such as flavanones (hesperidin), flavanones (naringenin, eriodictyol, dihydroquercetin), flavones (luteolin, apigenin, apigenin‐7‐glucoside), flavonols (quercetin, isoquercitrin, and rutin) [4].

Thymus species have been used by people in Anatolia for years, for a wide variety of purposes. They are used for abdominal pain, anxiety, anorexia, asthma, backache, breathing problems, bronchitis, cancer, colds, diabetes, edema, enteralgia, flu, gastritis, halitosis, hemorrhoid, high cholesterol, hypertension, kidney diseases, renal inflammations, prostrate disorders, stomachache, tonsillitis, skin allergies, wounds, psoriasis, eczema, acne, and dandruff according to ethnobotanical studies conducted in different regions of Türkiye [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Since various Thymus species are widely used in wound healing and other skin diseases among local people in Türkiye, four widely used Thymus species collected from various regions were selected in this study, Thymus brachychilus Jalas (TB), Thymus leucostomus Hausskn. & Velen. (TL), Thymus migricus Klokov & Des.‐Shost. (TM), Thymus sipyleus Boiss. (TS) based on their traditional uses as well as previous biological studies on these plants.

The phytochemical composition of these four species has been partially documented in the literature, though systematic and comparative characterization across species remains scarce. T. brachychilus has been identified as particularly rich in pentacyclic triterpenes—notably ursolic and oleanolic acids—alongside phenolic acids and flavonoids [13, 14], the ethanol extract of this species has also shown cytotoxic effects against various cancer cell lines, including adenocarcinoma (MCF‐7, HTB‐22), human lung adenocarcinoma (A549, CRM‐CCL‐185), and glioblastoma (U‐118 MG, HTB‐15), while its triterpenoid fraction is recognized for antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, and wound‐healing‐related properties. T. leucostomus has been characterized by the presence of rosmarinic acid, luteolin, and apigenin derivatives, as well as volatile terpenoids; it exhibits antimicrobial, antioxidant, and enzyme inhibitory properties, including acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, tyrosinase, and amylase inhibition, although comprehensive LC‐MS/MS profiling of this species remains limited [15, 16]. T. migricus has been shown to contain methanol‐ and water‐soluble phenolic compounds with demonstrated inhibitory activity against acetylcholinesterase, butyrylcholinesterase, glutathione S‐transferase (GST), and α‐glycosidase, though its full metabolomic profile has yet to be systematically established [17]. T. sipyleus, despite its documented antimicrobial activity, remains largely understudied at the phytochemical level [18]. Compared to the extensively characterized T. vulgaris—whose high thymol and carvacrol content, rosmarinic acid concentration, and flavonoid profile are well established [4]—these four species represent a largely unexplored chemical diversity within the genus.

While all four species have thus demonstrated antioxidant and enzyme inhibitory activities in independent studies, direct comparisons across species are lacking, and the experimental conditions—including extraction solvents, enzyme sources, and reference compounds—vary considerably between studies, limiting cross‐study interpretation. T. leucostomus and T. migricus share a documented capacity to inhibit both acetylcholinesterase and butyrylcholinesterase [15, 17], suggesting a potentially common neurochemical mode of action, yet the underlying phytochemical drivers of this shared activity have not been compared. In contrast, T. brachychilus has been studied primarily for its cytotoxic potential and triterpenoid content, while T. sipyleus has been evaluated mainly for antimicrobial and antibiofilm properties. Notably, none of these studies have addressed cosmetic‐relevant targets such as collagenase, elastase, or hyaluronidase inhibition, nor have they evaluated biological activity in skin cell models—a gap that directly motivates the present work.

In this context, a clinical and in vitro study of a phytocosmetic formulation containing Thymus vulgaris L. and lecithin reported that topical application reduced facial wrinkles and expression lines and reshaped the facial oval. Furthermore, the formulation was shown to increase PPAR‐γ expression in 3T3‐L1 fibroblast cells, thereby increasing adiponectin production and stimulating adipogenesis. These findings support the formulation's potential use of Thymus spp. extracts as a safe and innovative anti‐aging agent [19] but, no equivalent studies have been conducted yet on the four lesser‐known species selected here, despite their documented biological activities and longstanding ethnobotanical use in skin‐related conditions.

Skin aging is a complex biological phenomenon that occurs under the influence of both chronological processes and environmental factors. During this process, the structural integrity of the extracellular matrix (ECM) is disrupted, largely mediated by enzymes such as collagenase, elastase, hyaluronidase, and tyrosinase. Collagenase degrades collagen—the principal structural protein of the dermal matrix—leading to wrinkle formation and loss of skin firmness. Elastase breaks down elastin fibers, leading to loss of elasticity. Hyaluronidase, which plays a critical role in maintaining moisture balance, causes dryness and volume loss in the skin by breaking down hyaluronic acid. Tyrosinase, on the other hand, is a key enzyme that determines the rate of melanin biosynthesis, and its excessive activity is associated with hyperpigmentation and the formation of age spots [20]. This copper‐containing enzyme catalyzes the oxidation of phenolic substrates, mainly L‐tyrosine and L‐DOPA, leading to the formation of dopaquinone, an intermediate that subsequently contributes to the biosynthesis of eumelanin and pheomelanin pigments [21]. Therefore, inhibiting the excessive activity of these enzymes represents a central strategy in anti‐aging approaches to reduce wrinkles and loss of elasticity by preserving collagen, elastin, and hyaluronic acid. It may also help regulate melanin production and reduce pigmentation‐related changes. However, currently available inhibitors may present limitations such as side effects, instability, or limited efficacy, highlighting the need for novel, safe, and effective natural alternatives, particularly for cosmetic applications. Given Türkiye's remarkable plant biodiversity and the longstanding traditional use of Thymus species in skin‐related conditions, these plants emerge as promising candidates for the development of natural anti‐aging agents. The high polyphenolic content of Thymus species may confer protective effects in skin care through enzyme inhibition and antioxidant activity. Indeed, polyphenols have been reported to inhibit key skin‐degrading enzymes, including elastase, collagenase, and hyaluronidase [22, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Moreover, they may play a crucial role in skin regeneration and wound healing by promoting the proliferation of keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells [27]. Polyphenols, well‐known for their potent antioxidant properties, can protect cells from oxidative stress by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Recent studies have further demonstrated that polyphenols may regulate intracellular redox balance through multiple mechanisms, including free radical scavenging, modulation of antioxidant enzymes, and regulation of oxidative stress‐related signaling pathways. In addition to their direct antioxidant effects, several polyphenolic 1compounds have been reported to interact with inflammation‐ and apoptosis‐related molecular targets, thereby contributing to cellular protection under oxidative stress conditions. These multifunctional properties support the potential relevance of polyphenol‐rich plant extracts in skin‐related and oxidative stress‐associated biological applications [28, 29, 30].

They may also exert anti‐inflammatory effects by downregulating proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α and IL‐6 [31]. Recent studies have further demonstrated that polyphenolic compounds may modulate inflammatory signaling pathways associated with oxidative stress and tissue damage. In particular, several plant‐derived polyphenols were shown to suppress NF‐κB‐mediated inflammatory responses and regulate cytokine‐related cellular pathways involved in inflammation, apoptosis, and cellular homeostasis. These effects may contribute to the protective and regenerative biological responses observed in skin‐related cellular models [32, 33].

Some compounds commonly found in Thymus species, such as rosmarinic acid, luteolin, and apigenin, have already been shown to promote keratinocyte renewal and enhance fibroblast proliferation, thereby enhancing collagen synthesis, which is essential for maintaining ECM structure and supporting effective wound healing [34, 35]. Additionally, certain polyphenolic compounds have been reported to promote endothelial cell proliferation, which plays a key role in vascular formation and tissue repair, suggesting that polyphenol‐rich extracts may contribute to wound‐healing‐related responses and wound healing for both skin and internal organs [23, 27, 36, 37].

The underlying rationale of this work is to explore the dermocosmetic potential of four species of the genus Thymus (T. brachychilus, T. leucostomus, T. migricus, and T. sipyleus) with the aim of diversifying the biological resources currently exploited in cosmetic formulations. Indeed, despite the rich phytochemical diversity of the genus, its use in cosmetic applications remains largely confined to Thymus vulgaris L. and Thymus serpyllum L. In this context, the present study investigates the phytochemical profiles and biological activities of these four lesser‐studied Thymus species to assess their suitability for cosmetic and dermatological applications, with particular emphasis on anti‐aging enzyme inhibition, skin regeneration, wound healing, and the promotion of overall skin health.

We hypothesized that interspecies differences in the metabolomic composition of Thymus extracts would be associated with distinct dermocosmetic‐related biological responses, particularly regarding cytocompatibility, wound closure behavior, oxidative balance, and anti‐aging enzyme inhibition. This comparative approach is essential given that existing studies on these species have been conducted independently, under heterogeneous experimental conditions, and with no common biological framework, making it currently impossible to draw conclusions about their relative cosmetic potential. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comparative study integrating metabolomic profiling with dermocosmetic‐related cellular and enzyme‐based evaluations of these four Thymus species. The study further expands the current understanding of the dermocosmetic potential of lesser‐investigated members of the Lamiaceae family beyond the commonly studied Thymus vulgaris and Thymus serpyllum species.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material

Thymus brachychilus Jalas (TB), Thymus leucostomus Hausskn. & Velen. (TL), Thymus migricus Klokov & Des.‐Shost. (TM), Thymus sipyleus Boiss. (TS) were obtained from different localities in Türkiye in August, 2024. The voucher specimens were prepared after Ebru Özdemir Nath and Sefa Sönmez identified the plant species. Voucher specimens are preserved in the Altınbaş University Faculty of Pharmacy's Herbarium, which is an indexed herbarium.

TB samples (HERA 1029) were collected from the Maden and Cimbar Valleys in the Aladağlar mountain range, also known as Anti‐Taurus Mountains, Niğde, on August 26, 2024. TL samples (HERA 1059) were collected from the town of Bayat, Çorum, on August 1, 2024. TM samples (HERA 1166) were collected from Van on August 15, 2024. TS samples (HERA 1073) were collected from Tüllüce Mountain, Divriği, Sivas on August 10, 2024.

Extraction of Plant Materials

The aerial parts of the Thymus species were air‐dried in the shade at room temperature and then ground into a powder. The powder of the Thymus species was macerated with a ratio of 1 part of the plant soaked in 10 parts of solvent. By using 96% ethanol solvent, in a tightly closed container for 3 days, and protected from light, while stirring frequently. The solvent was evaporated to dryness under a rotary evaporator (Heidolph Hei‐VAP Advantage Rotary Evaporator) at 40°C and 120 rpm. After complete removal of solvents, the resulting dry extracts were stored at −20°C. Extraction yields were 2.0 %, 9.0 %, 9.9% and 3.3 % for T. leucostomus, T. sipyleus, T. migricus and T. brachychilus respectively (UHPLC‐DAD‐ESLD profiles available in Figure S1−S4).

UHPLC‐HRMS/MS Analyses

Chromatographic analyses were performed with a Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 RSLC system using a binary pump, an automatic sampler, a thermostated column compartment, and a DAD detector (200–800 nm) (Dionex, Germering, Germany). The column was a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm (Phenomenex, Le Pecq, France). Gradient mode was used to separate the compounds. The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both acidified with 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was fixed at 1.0 mL/min. Elution started with 5% B, and the amount of B increased up to 100% in 15 min. From 15 to 25 min, the amount of B was maintained and then decreased over 0.2 min to the initial conditions until 30 min.

The MS/MS experiments were carried out on a maXis UHR‐Q‐TOF mass spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany) using the data‐dependent acquisition mode (DDA) with an electrospray ion source (ESI) working in positive ionization mode. Nitrogen was used as the drying gas at a flow rate of 9 L/min, heated at 200°C, and as the nebulizing gas at a pressure of 2 bar. The mass spectra were recorded in the m/z range 100–1600 at 1.6 Hz for MS and MS/MS. The capillary voltage was set at 4.5 kV. Two precursor ions were selected per cycle, fragmented at two collision energies (25–45 eV), and averaged into one MS/MS spectrum.

HRMS/MS Treatment Analyses were converted to mzXML format using CompassXport (Bruker, Bremen, Germany). The mass spectrometry data were first processed with MZmine 3.8. Mass detections were fixed at 5 × 103 for the MS1 and 8.0 × 102 for the MS2 level [38]. An ADAP chromatogram builder and resolver were employed, with a minimum of 4 consecutive scans at a threshold of 5 × 103. Isotope 13C grouper, filtering, and Ion Identity networking, followed by a correlation grouping, were then performed. Alignment was performed based on a m/z tolerance of 0.005 or 8 ppm and a retention time tolerance of 0.8 min. A feature list comprising 1281 features, spanning between 2 and 16 min, was finally obtained. A molecular network was then generated using GNPS with the Feature‐Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) workflow [39, 40]. A molecular network was created in which the edges were filtered to have a cosine score above 0.7 and more than 3 matched fragment ions. The molecular network was then visualized using Cytoscape 3.10.2 software [41].

To achieve dereplication of the extracts, three different annotation tools were employed. First, Feature‐Based Molecular Networking (FBMN) was annotated using GNPS. Library spectra were filtered in the same manner as the FBMN processing, with a cosine score above 0.7 and at least 3 matched fragment ions. GNPS provides a cosine score ranging from 0.7 to 1, reflecting the similarity between the experimental and reference spectra in the library. Next, TIMA‐R v2.9, an R‐based tool, was used with MGF files and a metadata table containing taxonomic information of the samples. This tool compares experimental spectra to in silico spectra generated from the LOTUS database and integrates taxonomic context (e.g., species, genus, family) to calculate a similarity score. This score, ranging from 0 to 1, reflects the combined spectral and taxonomic proximity, with a score of 1 indicating a well‐annotated metabolite from a closely related taxonomic group. Finally, the MGF files were also analyzed using SIRIUS 6.1, an in silico annotation platform that performs molecular formula prediction and infers molecular fingerprints based on fragmentation trees and MS/MS spectra. The resulting score ranges from 0 (high‐confidence candidate) to negative infinity, with higher scores representing more plausible structural assignments. According to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative, GNPS annotations correspond to confidence levels 2 or 3, while TIMA‐R and SIRIUS provide annotations at level 3.

Chemicals

Ultrapure water was produced with a Milli‐Q EQ7000 system equipped with an LC‐Pak filter from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). UHPLC‐HRMS analyses were performed with water and LC‐MS grade acetonitrile from Honeywell (Seelze, Germany).

Cell Culture

The mammalian cell lines used in this study were NCTC clone 929 (L929), a mouse fibroblast cell line (ECACC 85011425; RRID:CVCL_0462; European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures, Salisbury, UK); HUVEC, a human umbilical vein endothelial cell line (ATCC CRL‐1730; RRID:CVCL_2959; American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA); and HaCaT, a spontaneously immortalized human epidermal keratinocyte cell line (RRID:CVCL_0038), kindly provided by Dr. Merve Erginer (Department of Biotechnology, Institute of Nanotechnology and Biotechnology, Istanbul University‐Cerrahpaşa, Istanbul, Türkiye).

The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; D5546, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biowest, Nuaillé, France; S1810‐500), 50 U/mL penicillin and 50 µg/mL streptomycin (Biological Industries, Beit–Haemek, Israel; 03‐031‐1B), and 2 mM L‐glutamine (Biological Industries; BI03‐020‐1B). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

The cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; D5546, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biowest, Nuaillé, France; S1810‐500), 50 U/mL penicillin and 50 µg/mL streptomycin (Biological Industries, Beit–Haemek, Israel; 03‐031‐1B), and 2 mM L‐glutamine (Biological Industries; BI03‐020‐1B). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

The Antiproliferative Effects of Thymus Extracts on Various Cell Lines: The MTT Assay

Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates and allowed to attach overnight. The cells were then treated with various concentrations of the extracts’ solvents (1% DMSO containing media) of Thymus sp. (TS, TL, TB, and TM) for 24, 48, and 72 h. After treatment, 12 mM stock solution of MTT reagent (3‐(4.5‐dimethylthiazol‐ 2‐yl)‐2.5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide) (Neofrox 3580 MTT) was added to each well as described in [42], and the cells were incubated for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide Double Staining

Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide (AO/EtBr) Dual staining technique was performed as described by Liu et al. [43]. Briefly, cells were seeded in a 96‐well plate at a density of approximately 104 cells/well. Following incubation with TS, TL, TB, and TM IC50 values for 48 h, initially, cells were cultured in a 96‐well plate with a volume of 100–150 µL of appropriate culture medium per well. To ensure cell sedimentation, the 96‐well plate was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 16 µL of EB/AO dye mix working solution was added to each well containing 150 µL of medium, allowing the dye to diffuse and penetrate the cells for 1‐2 min. Cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope. Live cells exhibited normal nuclei with green‐stained chromatin and organized structures. Apoptotic cells displayed condensed or fragmented chromatin stained green or orange, while necrotic cells showed nuclei similar to those of live cells but with orange‐stained chromatin. A minimum of 200 cells per well was counted to determine the percentages of live, apoptotic, and necrotic cells. For statistical analysis, at least 200 cells were counted, and the results were expressed as mean values obtained from at least three independent experiments [44, 45].

PI/Annexin V Staining

The PI/Annexin V staining method was performed to detect apoptosis and necrosis after treatment with of TS, TL, TB, and TM at their IC50 concentrations. Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of approximately 104 cells per well and incubated with the test compounds for 48 h. Throughout the procedure, cells were not detached from the plates to prevent artifacts caused by cell handling. After the 48‐h incubation, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS to eliminate debris. Freshly prepared Annexin‐V working solution (with 2.5 mM CaCl2 in PBS) was added at a volume of 25 µL per well. Subsequently, 2 µL of Annexin‐V stock solution (50 µg/mL) was added to each well to achieve a final concentration of 2 µg/mL. The plates were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min.

Following the first staining step, 2 µL of PI working solution (2 µg/mL) was directly added to each well. The plates were further incubated in the dark at room temperature for an additional 20 min. Cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Live cells (Annexin‐V‐PI‐) showed no fluorescence, apoptotic cells (Annexin‐V+PI‐) exhibited green fluorescence due to Annexin‐V binding to phosphatidylserine translocated to the cell surface, and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (Annexin‐V+PI+) showed orange or yellow fluorescence due to both Annexin‐V binding and PI uptake.

For each experimental condition, at least 200 cells per well were counted, and the percentages of live, apoptotic, and necrotic cells were calculated. The experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability. The results were expressed as mean values obtained from three independent experiments.

In Vitro Scratch Assay

The in vitro scratch assay was conducted to evaluate wound closure responses according to the protocol described by [46]. Murine fibroblast cells (L929), Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVEC), and Human keratinocyte cells (HaCaT) were used in the experiment. These cells were cultivated in 6‐well plates and treated with extract solvents (TS, TL, TB, and TM) at their respective IC50 concentrations for 48 h. Untreated cells served as negative controls, and H2O2‐treated cells were used as positive controls to evaluate cytotoxicity and migration inhibition.

To initiate the assay, a sterile 10 µL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch on the monolayer of cells. After scratching, the wells were gently washed with culture medium to remove detached cells and debris. Images of the scratches were captured immediately (0 h) and at 48 h post‐scratch under a Carl–Zeiss Axio Observer 3 microscope at 10x magnification. Gap sizes were measured using ImageJ software.

The rate of wound closure was calculated as follows: WoundClosurePercentage%=0−hgapsize−48−hgapsize×1000−hgapsize

This formula quantifies the percentage of the initial scratch area covered by cells after 48 h. All results were expressed as mean values.

Detection of ROS by DCF‐DA Assay

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured in the specified cell lines using the fluorescence microscopy method with the DCF‐DA probe (Sigma–Aldrich, D6883), a widely recognized technique for detecting ROS in living cells [47]. Intracellular ROS generation was detected using the DCF‐DA fluorescence probe.

For this experiment, Murine/ mouse fibroblast (L929), Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial cells’ (HUVEC), Human keratinocyte cells’ (HaCaT) were seeded in a 96‐well plate at a density of 20,000 cells per well and allowed to reach 70%–90% confluence before cultivating with extract solvents (TS, TL, TB, and TM) at their respective IC50 doses for 48 h, with untreated cells serving as negative controls. The staining procedure was initiated at 48 h of incubation. No trypsinization was performed, and the cells were maintained in their culture medium throughout the process. A 20 mM stock solution of DCFDA (2’,7’‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate) was prepared by dissolving the appropriate amount of DCFDA in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The working concentration was 20 µM, achieved by diluting the stock solution 1000‐fold with culture medium. The culture medium was then removed from the wells, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. DCFDA working solution (20 mM) was added to each well (100 µL per well), and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove any excess dye, and fluorescence was observed using a fluorescence microscope. DCFDA is converted into a fluorescent product in the presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Fluorescence intensity, indicative of ROS levels, was qualitatively assessed. For quantitative analysis, fluorescence intensity was measured using a Carl–Zeiss/Axio Observer 3 microscope with Zen 2.3 Blue Edition software. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a 60 × 60 pixel box from at least 100 cells (n = 6) and averaged for analysis [45, 48].

In Vitro Anti‐Aging Enzyme Activities

Plant extracts were evaluated at a selected concentration of 1 mg/mL, and enzyme inhibitory activities were calculated as percentage inhibition. Reference substances used as positive controls were prepared according to the assay requirements. The selected extract concentration was used as an initial comparative screening concentration because the tested samples were crude plant extracts containing complex mixtures of constituents rather than isolated compounds, and it allowed measurable inhibitory responses and comparison of the extracts under identical experimental conditions. Absorbance measurements were performed using a BIOTEK Synergy H1 microplate reader.

The % inhibition for enzyme activities was calculated using the following equation: %inhibition=Acontrol−AsampleAcontrol×100 A: Absorbance.

Anti‐Collagenase Activity

50 mM tricine buffer solution, pH 7.5 (400 mM NaCl and 10 mM CaCl2) was prepared to determine anti‐collagenase activity. Collagenase obtained from Clostridium histolyticum (Chc‐EC. 3.4.24.3) was diluted in 50 mM tricine buffer solution to attain the preliminary concentration of 0.8 U/mL. It was used 2 mM of N‐[3 (2‐furyl) acryloyl]‐Leu‐Gly‐Pro‐Ala (FALGPA) dissolved in tricine buffer as substrate. Before initiating the process, the extracts were incubated with the collagenase enzyme in a buffer solution for 15 min. Final reaction mixture with a total volume of 150 µL; tricine buffer, 0.8 mM FALGPA, 0.1unit ChC, and 50 µg test extract. The absorbance was measured after the substrate was added [49].

Anti‐Elastase Activity

Anti‐elastase activity was determined in 0.2 M Tris‐HCl buffer, pH 8.0. Stock solution of porcine pancreatic elastase was prepared at a concentration of 3.33 mg/mL, while N‐Succinyl‐Ala‐Ala‐p‐nitroanilide (AAAPVN) was dissolved in 1.6 mM buffer. Before adding the substrate, test extracts were incubated with the enzyme at 37°C for 15 min. Then, the enzyme mixture with 1 mg/mL of plant extract was incubated with 0.8 mM AAAPVN for an additional 15 min at 37°C after preincubation. The absorbance was read at 365 nm. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) was used as a positive control [49, 50].

Anti‐Hyaluronidase Activity

Hyaluronidase from bovine testes was prepared in 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 7.0), and hyaluronic acid sodium salt was dissolved to 0.4 mg/mL in the same buffer. Plant extracts were mixed with the enzyme solution and incubated at 37°C for one hour. Then, 10% cetylpyridinium chloride was added and incubated for another hour at 37°C. The final mixture contained 110 µL Tris‐HCl, 10 µL hyaluronidase, 10 µL hyaluronic acid, 10 µL cetylpyridinium chloride, and 10 µL plant extract. The positive control was tannic acid at a 1.1 mg/mL concentration. Inhibition of hyaluronidase activity was recorded at 415 nm [51].

Anti‐Tyrosinase Activity

In vitro anti‐tyrosinase activity was measured using a method modified by Ersoy et al. [50]. A 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.8) was prepared as the reaction medium, and L‐DOPA was prepared as the substrate. The reaction mixture contained 100 µL phosphate buffer, 20 µL 1 mg/mL plant extract, and 20 µL tyrosinase enzyme and was incubated at 37°C for 10 min. Then, 20 µL 3 mM L‐DOPA was added and incubated at 37°C for another 30 min. Tyrosinase inhibition was measured at 492 nm with kojic acid as the reference standard [50].

Statistical Analysis

For enzyme inhibitory assays, all measurements were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Differences among groups were evaluated using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post‐hoc multiple comparison test. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

IC50 values were calculated from the MTT cell viability data. First, the absorbance values measured at 540 nm were corrected by subtracting the blank absorbance. The corrected absorbance value of the untreated negative control group was accepted as 100% cell viability. Cell viability for each extract concentration was calculated using the following formula: cell viability (%) = (Absorbance of treated cells / Absorbance of negative control cells) × 100. The percentage inhibition of cell viability was then calculated as 100 − cell viability (%). The normalized concentration–response data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 by nonlinear regression with the “log(inhibitor) versus normalized response—variable slope” model. IC50 was defined as the concentration of extract required to reduce cell viability by 50% compared with the negative control. When 50% inhibition was not reached within the tested concentration range, the IC50 value was reported as greater than the highest tested concentration rather than extrapolated beyond the experimental range.

All experiments were performed using three independent biological replicates conducted on separate experimental days. Within each biological replicate, all treatment conditions were analyzed in technical triplicate. The 48‐hour exposure period was selected as the optimal time point because preliminary time‐course observations indicated that this interval provided the most reproducible and biologically consistent cellular responses while minimizing nonspecific long‐term cytotoxic effects that became more pronounced at extended exposure durations.

Results

Phytochemical Analysis of the Four Thymus L. Species

A feature‐based molecular networking (FBMN) approach was performed to explore the chemodiversity of the four species. Examining species‐specific differences in metabolite composition may help in understanding the biological activities of each extract. The four ethanolic extracts were subjected to UHPLC‐HRMS/MS analysis, and a molecular network (MN) was generated using the FBMN tool on the GNPS platform. To improve confidence in metabolite dereplication, several complementary tools were combined: an Ion Identity Molecular Network (IIMN) was first built to correlate the different adducts and losses associated with the same compound, then collapsed to display only the “best ion” per node, limiting redundancy. The resulting IIMN comprised 1006 nodes grouped into 37 clusters (>2 nodes each) and 37 single nodes (Figure 1).

It should be noted that all compound annotations discussed below result from spectral dereplication (accurate mass matching combined with GNPS, TIMA‐R and/or SIRIUS/CSI:FingerID predictions) and therefore correspond to putative identifications rather than definitive structural assignments (Level 2 or 3 on the Schymanski scale); confirmation by authentic standards or NMR would be required to validate these annotations at the level [52]. Annotation confidence is indicated throughout using the matching scores reported in Supplementary Tables S1–S2, and discrepancies between tools—particularly frequent for non‐canonical adducts ([M+Na]+, [M‐H2O+H]+) and for structurally related isomers—are discussed below and detailed in the Supplementary Discussion.

Dereplication focused on clusters corresponding to bioactive specialized metabolite families: terpenic compounds (cluster 1) and phenolic compounds (clusters 3, 5, 7, 15, and 21). Cluster 9 also appeared to group diterpenic‐type compounds, although no annotation could be proposed with reasonable confidence. Clusters 2, 4, 8, 11 and 12 grouped various lipid classes (fatty acids, fatty amides, fatty acyls, glycerophospholipids), cluster 6 grouped chlorophyll derivatives, and cluster 10 grouped sugars.

Cluster 1 (106 nodes) was putatively annotated as triterpenic compounds, predominantly of the ursane/taraxastane‐ or friedelane‐type (including oleanolic, ursolic, maslinic, and glycyrrhetic acid‐type annotations), with a smaller subgroup of lupane‐type triterpenes (betulinic acid, betulin) (Figure 2, Table S1). Annotations were first proposed from GNPS spectral library matches and subsequently cross‐checked against TIMA‐R and SIRIUS predictions.

Several annotation discrepancies between tools were noted, particularly for sodium adducts and dehydrated [M‐H2O+H]+ species, which were not always reliably handled by TIMA‐R, and for closely related triterpene skeletons (e.g., uvaol vs. erythrodiol) that mass spectrometry alone cannot unambiguously discriminate. These cases, together with the oleanolic acid and glycyrrhetic acid examples illustrating the sensitivity of automated annotation to adduct type and structural similarity, are detailed in the Supplementary Discussion. Two additional nodes (613, 614), eluting earlier than the major triterpenic acids and showing molecular formulas consistent with more oxygenated triterpenoids (C30H48O4 and C30H46O4), suggest the presence of more polar triterpenoids not well described to date in Thymus species; given the lack of prior reports of glycyrrhetic‐acid‐type compounds in Lamiaceae, this annotation should be considered with caution.

Within cluster 1, two sub‐groups could be distinguished: a major part (ursane/taraxastane‐ or friedelane‐type) and a smaller part (lupane‐type) (Figure 2). TL, TS and TB appeared to share a comparatively rich and diverse triterpenoid profile, dominated by oleanolic‐acid‐type derivatives, with a minor contribution of lupane triterpenes. TM displayed the lowest overall triterpenoid abundance despite notable structural diversity (∼60 nodes), but showed a comparatively higher proportion of diterpenoid‐type features (cluster 9).

Triterpenes are widely reported in medicinal plants for their wound‐healing and skin‐regenerating potential. Ursane‐ and lupane‐type skeletons in particular have been associated with inhibition of skin‐ageing enzymes such as collagenase and elastase [23, 53, 54], as well as with cellular processes relevant to tissue repair [55], supporting the relevance of triterpene‐rich extracts for skin‐regeneration applications.

Clusters 3 and 5 (47 and 18 nodes, respectively) were putatively annotated as polyphenolic compounds, following the same dereplication strategy (GNPS, then cross‐checked with TIMA‐R and/or SIRIUS; Figure 3, Table S2). Given the high number of annotated nodes, annotation propagation was applied when TIMA‐R and SIRIUS results were concordant even in the absence of a GNPS match (nodes 327, 371, 406). The IIMN proved particularly useful for resolving glycosylated forms, for instance distinguishing a luteolin‐glucoside‐type annotation (node 82) from the free aglycone (node 207) based on co‐occurring adduct nodes in the network (detailed in the Supplementary Discussion S1).

The polyphenolic profile of TM, TS, and TB appeared broadly similar, dominated by glycosylated derivatives putatively assigned to luteolin, apigenin and naringenin (cluster 3), whereas TL showed a markedly different composition, with comparatively minor amounts of luteolin/apigenin/sakuranetin‐type aglycones and an apparent absence of glycosylated flavonoids. Conversely, for cluster 5, TL and TM appeared comparatively rich in polymethoxylated flavonoid‐type annotations, while TS did not show an equivalent enrichment.

These compositional differences may translate into distinct bioactivities, as glycosylated and polymethoxylated flavonoids differ substantially in physicochemical behavior. Glycosylation increases polarity and molecular size, limiting passive membrane diffusion and generally requiring microbiota‐mediated hydrolysis to release the more bioavailable aglycone [56], while also improving aqueous solubility and stability. Polymethoxylation, conversely, increases lipophilicity and membrane permeability, potentially enhancing bioavailability [57, 58], at the cost of reduced aqueous solubility.

Clusters 7, 15, and 21 (13, 6, and 4 nodes, respectively) were putatively annotated as phenolic acid derivatives, predominantly of the caffeic acid type, using the same annotation strategy (Figure 4, Table S2). TS appeared as the species richest in caffeic‐acid‐type derivatives. These compounds are widely reported for roles relevant to wound healing, including antibacterial activity, ROS scavenging, modulation of inflammatory mediators (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, COX‐2, NF‐κB), and stimulation of fibroblast proliferation, keratinocyte migration and angiogenesis [59, 60].

Overall, the FBMN‐based dereplication suggests interspecies differences in the relative distribution of major specialized metabolite families among the four Thymus extracts. TL, TS, and TB appeared characterized by abundant and diverse triterpenoid annotations (mainly ursane/taraxastane‐ and friedelane‐type, with oleanolic‐acid‐type derivatives predominating), while TM showed comparatively lower triterpenoid content but a higher proportion of diterpenoid‐type features. Regarding phenolics, TM, TS and TB shared a profile apparently dominated by glycosylated flavonoid‐type annotations (luteolin, apigenin, naringenin derivatives), whereas TL showed a distinct profile with fewer flavonoid glycosides and more polymethoxylated flavonoid‐type annotations; TS additionally stood out for its caffeic‐acid‐derivative content. While these results indicate that the four species share common metabolite classes (triterpenes, flavonoids, diterpenoids, phenolic acids), their relative distributions appeared to differ substantially, which may contribute to species‐specific biological activities. These findings should be interpreted as a semi‐quantitative chemical screening rather than as definitive structural or quantitative characterization, given the inherent limitations of MS‐based dereplication discussed above and in the Supplementary Material.

The effect of TS, TL, TB, and TM extracts on cell viability was then investigated based on their IC50 values (Table 2 and Figure 6).

For this purpose, six different concentrations (0.0125–0.5 mg/ml) of each extract were tested on various cell lines for 24, 48, and 72 h. The 48‐h exposure period was selected as the optimal time point because it yielded the most robust and reproducible cellular responses. While 24 h may be sufficient for early responses, and 72 h could potentially induce toxic effects in specific cell types, 48 h represents a balanced timeframe that maximizes measurable biological responses while minimizing nonspecific toxicity. Consequently, this time point was considered the most appropriate for ensuring data consistency and reliability.

In this study, the effects of plant extracts obtained from Thymus species on L929 fibroblast, HaCaT keratinocyte, and HUVEC endothelial cell lines were examined in terms of cell viability. The data indicated that the effects of the plant extracts on the cell lines varied with cell type and concentration. IC50 values were used as an indicator of the cytotoxic effects of the plant extracts, and varying viability rates were observed for each cell line.

The L929 fibroblast cell line exhibited high viability with the TL extract, demonstrating its potential to support the role of fibroblasts in skin tissue repair. Moreover, this plant extract is thought to be may warrant further investigation for use in anti‐aging and skin rejuvenating cosmetic products. Similarly, the TM extract exhibited comparable activity to TL at lower concentrations; however, a marked cytotoxic effect was observed at the highest tested concentration (0.5 mg/mL). This similarity in activity may be attributed to their comparable phytochemical profiles, particularly their richness in polymethoxylated flavonoids, in contrast to the other extracts.

In HaCaT keratinocytes, extracts of TM, TS, and TB reduced cell proliferation, with TM exhibiting pronounced cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. Given the essential role of keratinocytes in maintaining the epidermal barrier, these findings highlight the need for careful dose optimization in topical applications. The observed antiproliferative effect may be associated with the presence of diterpenoids, as TM—which displayed the strongest inhibitory effect—contained the highest diterpenoid content. In contrast, TL, the only extract that did not inhibit keratinocyte proliferation, lacked detectable diterpenoids in its phytochemical profile. Certain diterpenes are already known to exert cytotoxic effects on HaCaT cells, such as fruticuline A and isofruticuline A (IC50 = 4.6 and 6.5 µM, respectively) isolated from Salvia lachnostachys [61].

Tests on the HUVEC endothelial cell line revealed that the four extracts exhibited beneficial effects on endothelial cells, improving blood circulation and showing anti‐inflammatory properties; however, the TM extract again displayed significant cytotoxicity at the highest concentration tested. Overall, these findings indicate that Thymus extracts may hold promise for cosmetic applications targeting vascular support and inflammation modulation, provided that concentration‐dependent cytotoxic effects are carefully controlled.

Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide Double Staining

The effects of Thymus extracts on cell viability were assessed by calculating the percentage of dead cells in each experimental condition, assuming an average total cell count of 150. Cells were treated with the IC50 concentrations of each extract. The analysis revealed distinct cytotoxic responses across the three cell lines.

In L929 cells, the control group exhibited a low percentage of dead cells (≈16%), whereas H2O2 treatment markedly increased cell death (≈71%). Among the extracts, TB and TL exhibited relatively low toxicity (≈30% and 25%), whereas TS and TM showed more pronounced cytotoxicity (≈57% and 68%). Statistical analysis (Tukey's multiple comparison test) confirmed significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05). The greatest reduction in viability and the highest proportion of dead cells were observed in the TM group (p < 0.001), followed by TS (p < 0.001); TL and TB exhibited milder effects (p < 0.05–0.001). Compared with H2O2, TS, TL, and TB maintained significantly higher viability (p < 0.001), whereas TM showed no significant difference (p > 0.05), indicating comparable cytotoxicity to the positive control.

In HUVEC cells, the baseline level of cell death in controls was approximately 30%, while H2O2 exposure resulted in complete cell death (100%). Among the extracts, TL, TB, and TM significantly reduced cell viability and increased cell death (p < 0.001), whereas TS had no significant effect (p > 0.05). All extracts were less cytotoxic than H2O2 (p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons indicated that TS maintained the highest cell viability and the fewest dead cells compared to the other extracts (p < 0.001), while TB and TM showed similar effects (p > 0.05).

In HaCaT cells, the control group showed ≈31% cell death, which increased to ≈64% following H2O2 exposure. All extracts significantly reduced viability and elevated cell death (p < 0.001). The TM and TS groups exhibited the strongest cytotoxic effects, whereas TL and TB were less toxic (p < 0.001). Compared with the positive control, TS, TL, and TB showed significantly lower levels of cell death (p < 0.01–0.001*), while TM did not differ significantly (p > 0.05). Among the extracts, TS induced the fewest dead cells, whereas TM caused the highest number (p < 0.001).

Overall, the results demonstrate that Thymus extracts exert varying cytotoxic effects depending on the cell type and extract composition. TB and TL displayed the lowest toxicity across all cell lines, particularly in L929 cells. At the same time, TS and TM were consistently associated with stronger cytotoxic responses, especially in HUVEC and HaCaT cells (Figure 7).

PI/Annexin V Staining

The effects of Thymus extracts (TL, TS, TM, and TB) at their IC50 concentrations on apoptosis and necrosis were evaluated in L929, HUVEC, and HaCaT cell lines using the Annexin V/PI double staining method. The proportions of necrotic, early apoptotic, and late apoptotic cells were quantified by fluorescence microscopy. Representative data are presented in Figure 8, while statistical analysis results are summarized below.

Annexin V/PI double staining results for L929, HUVEC, and HaCaT cell lines treated with Thymus extracts (TL, TS, TM, and TB) at their IC50 concentrations for 48 h. The percentages of necrotic, early apoptotic, and late apoptotic cells were determined by fluorescence microscopy. Data are presented as mean ± SD of three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using repeated measures ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05 considered significant). All groups showed statistically significant differences compared with the control, confirming that the Thymus extracts induced distinct patterns of apoptosis and necrosis depending on the cell type and extract composition.

In L929 fibroblasts, the control group exhibited a high level of necrosis (61.8%) with moderate early (17.6%) and late apoptosis (20.6%). Following H2O2‐induced oxidative stress, necrosis further increased (70.3%), whereas early apoptosis declined sharply (5.4%), indicating membrane damage rather than regulated apoptotic activation. Among the tested extracts, TL treatment resulted in a striking elevation of late apoptosis (77.2%) while completely suppressing necrosis (0%), suggesting a potent apoptotic but non‐necrotic mechanism of action. TM, on the other hand, markedly increased necrosis (46.2%) and late apoptosis (44.2%), consistent with a mixed cytotoxic response combining apoptosis and membrane disruption. TS and TB caused milder changes, primarily inducing early apoptotic activity with minimal necrosis. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant overall difference among treatments (Repeated Measures ANOVA, F(17,34) = 19.29, p < 0.0001), indicating that the extracts distinctly modulated apoptotic pathways in fibroblasts.

In HUVEC endothelial cells, the control group displayed a balanced distribution of early (40.9%) and late apoptosis (38.2%) with low necrosis (20.9%). H2O2 exposure did not notably increase necrosis but significantly decreased both early and late apoptosis, confirming oxidative inhibition of programmed cell death—treatment with Thymus extracts restored and enhanced apoptosis to varying degrees. TB induced the most pronounced increase in late apoptosis (61.8%), whereas TM elevated necrosis to 20% and maintained moderate early apoptosis (18.0%). TL displayed a balanced profile with controlled early and late apoptosis and limited necrosis, consistent with mild cytotoxicity. Statistical testing revealed strong treatment‐related effects (Repeated Measures ANOVA, F(17,34) = 83.22, p < 0.0001), and post‐hoc analysis confirmed that all extracts significantly increased early apoptotic cell populations (p < 0.001), particularly TS, TB, and TM. Overall, these data indicate that Thymus extracts promote apoptosis in endothelial cells predominantly through regulated pathways rather than necrosis.

In HaCaT keratinocytes, the control group exhibited 14.0% necrosis, 23.0% early apoptosis, and 61.0% late apoptosis, indicating a naturally high turnover via apoptosis. Exposure to H2O2 led to a marked decline in both necrosis (1%) and late apoptosis (6%), consistent with rapid oxidative cell death outside the classical apoptotic pathway. Among the extracts, TS markedly increased early apoptosis (68.0%) and necrosis (28.0%) while reducing late apoptosis (18.0%), suggesting accelerated apoptotic initiation with partial transition to secondary necrosis. TM treatment also elevated early apoptosis (52.0%) and necrosis (22.0%), maintaining late apoptosis at approximately 20%. TL and TB treatments maintained cell populations closer to control levels, with moderate late apoptotic activity and limited necrosis. Statistical evaluation (Repeated Measures ANOVA, F(17,34) = 56.22, p < 0.0001) demonstrated significant treatment effects, and Tukey's test revealed that TS and TM caused the most prominent apoptotic shifts (p < 0.001), whereas TL and TB exerted milder influences.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Thymus extracts differentially affect apoptosis and necrosis in a cell–type–dependent manner. TL consistently induced controlled apoptotic activity while minimizing necrosis, indicating favorable biocompatibility. TM and TS triggered higher levels of both apoptosis and necrosis, suggesting greater cytotoxic potential. TB produced moderate apoptotic responses without excessive loss of necrotic cells. Altogether, the results highlight TL as the extract with the most balanced and biocompatible profile, capable of promoting regulated apoptosis without disrupting membrane integrity (Figure 9).

Collectively, these results indicate that Thymus extracts promote wound healing and cell migration in a cell type– and extract‐dependent manner. TL consistently exhibited high closure percentages across all cell lines, suggesting potential pro‐regenerative effects. TS and TM also enhanced migration, particularly in HaCaT and HUVEC cells, whereas H2O2 inhibited migration due to oxidative stress‐induced cytotoxicity.

TL and TM moderately increase ROS in fibroblasts yet reduce oxidative stress in endothelial and keratinocyte cells, whereas TB and TS maintain levels close to the control, reflecting overall biocompatibility and possible antioxidant behavior.

Discussion

Previous metabolomic investigations on Thymus species have primarily focused on essential oil composition, antioxidant capacity, or selected bioactivities of individual species such as Thymus vulgaris, Thymus serpyllum, and Thymus pectinatus. In contrast, comparative studies integrating advanced metabolomic dereplication approaches with dermocosmetic‐related cellular models remain limited. The present study, therefore, contributes to the expanding literature on Lamiaceae‐derived bioactive compounds by combining FBMN‐based metabolomic profiling with wound closure, oxidative balance, apoptosis, and anti‐aging enzyme inhibition assays across multiple Thymus species.

The combination of metabolomic profiling and in vitro biological evaluation provides a comprehensive understanding of the similarities and differences among the four Thymus species investigated (TL, TS, TB, and TM). Although FBMN analysis revealed clear variations in the relative distribution of specialized metabolite families, the extracts shared the same principal chemical classes, namely triterpenoids, flavonoids, diterpenoids, and phenolic acid derivatives. These shared chemical features likely explain the comparable enzymatic inhibition profiles observed across species.

Indeed, all extracts exhibited notable inhibitory activity against hyaluronidase, elastase, and collagenase, while showing limited efficacy toward tyrosinase. TB demonstrated the highest hyaluronidase inhibition (89.64 ± 4.45%), even exceeding the activity of tannic acid, which may be attributed to its richness in pentacyclic triterpenes. Previous studies have shown that oleanolic and ursolic acids are effective hyaluronidase inhibitors, and that hydroxyl groups at C‐3 and C‐28 of ursane and oleanane skeletons play a key role in enzyme inhibition [62, 63]. The predominance of ursan and friedelan derivatives in our extracts, therefore, likely underlies the strong anti‐hyaluronidase activity observed. When collagenase inhibition was examined, TL exhibited the highest activity, although all extracts remained below the standard EGCG. Nevertheless, their inhibition levels were higher than those reported for some ethanolic extracts of other Thymus species, suggesting that these species constitute promising sources of anti‐collagenase agents for cosmetic use. In contrast, tyrosinase inhibition was moderate, with TL showing the strongest effect. This activity may be linked to the presence of sakuranetin, previously reported for its antimelanogenic properties [64]. However, all extracts were less active than kojic acid, indicating that depigmenting applications would require optimization or combination strategies. In another study, the inhibitory effects of the essential oil and ethanolic extract obtained from Thymus pectinatus Fisch. & C.A.Mey. on collagenase and tyrosinase enzymes were investigated, and it was determined that the essential oil exhibited higher anti‐collagenase (52.3 ± 0.12% inhibition) and anti‐tyrosinase (73.5 ± 0.23% inhibition) activities than the ethanol extract. The inhibition of collagenase and tyrosinase of the ethanol extract was determined to be 25.2 ± 0.19% and 18.9 ± 0.28%, respectively [65]. Despite quantitative differences in triterpene and flavonoid content, no marked interspecies differences were detected in most enzyme assays. Triterpenoids—particularly ursane‐, taraxastane‐, and friedelane‐type skeletons—are well documented for their capacity to inhibit matrix‐degrading enzymes and support extracellular matrix preservation. Since TL, TS, and TB were particularly rich in these compounds, and TM still contained them in lower amounts, it is plausible that the shared presence of these bioactive scaffolds underlies the similar enzymatic responses.

More pronounced differences emerged from the cell‐based assays. Among the four extracts, TL consistently demonstrated the most balanced biological profile. It maintained high viability in L929 fibroblasts, supported cell migration in all three cell lines, and induced predominantly regulated apoptosis with minimal necrosis. Moreover, TL did not significantly increase intracellular ROS levels, suggesting good redox compatibility. This favorable profile may be related to its richness in triterpenoids and polymethoxylated flavonoids, compounds known to combine membrane permeability with antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties.

In contrast, TM displayed a more complex behavior. Although it enhanced migration in keratinocytes and endothelial cells and showed comparable enzyme inhibition to the other extracts, it induced higher cytotoxicity, particularly at elevated concentrations. TM increased both apoptosis and necrosis in several cell lines and moderately elevated ROS production in fibroblasts. This stronger cytotoxic profile may be associated with its higher diterpenoid content. Diterpenes are frequently reported to exert antiproliferative and pro‐apoptotic effects, which could account for the marked reduction in viability and the altered apoptotic balance observed with TM treatment. In the HaCaT keratinocyte line, TM's pronounced cytotoxicity raises concerns regarding potential impairment of the epidermal barrier. Keratinocytes are essential for maintaining skin integrity, and extracts that induce high toxicity must therefore be carefully dosed. TL, in contrast, preserved high keratinocyte viability, indicating relatively favorable in vitro compatibility. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that triterpenes enhance keratinocyte and fibroblast migration and promote re‐epithelialization by modulating antioxidant and pro‐regenerative pathways [66, 67].

TB and TS exhibited intermediate profiles. TB combined moderate cytotoxicity with good wound‐healing response and controlled apoptotic responses. TS, distinguished by its higher content of caffeic acid derivatives, exhibited strong pro‐migratory effects and stable ROS levels close to those of controls. Phenolic acid derivatives, particularly caffeic acid conjugates, are known for their antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties, as well as their ability to promote fibroblast proliferation and keratinocyte migration. Their presence in TS may therefore contribute to its regenerative potential.

The scratch assay further emphasized extract‐specific effects. TL enhanced consistent wound closure across fibroblast, keratinocyte, and endothelial models, suggesting potential regenerative effects. TM strongly stimulated keratinocyte migration but impaired fibroblast migration, highlighting cell–type–dependent responses. These findings illustrate the complexity of plant extract activity, in which the same chemical profile can exert beneficial or deleterious effects depending on the cellular context. In HUVEC endothelial cells, TL and TS achieved complete wound closure, suggesting enhanced endothelial wound‐closure responses. Endothelial cells play a central role in angiogenesis and tissue repair, and terpenoids have been shown to stimulate endothelial proliferation through HIF‐1α/VEGF signaling pathways [68]. The strong migratory response observed in TL and TS may therefore reflect both antioxidant protection and angiogenic stimulation.

ROS measurements supported the overall biocompatibility of the extracts. None of them induced oxidative stress comparable to H2O2, and in endothelial and keratinocyte cells, ROS levels remained similar to or below those of the control. Even when moderate increases were observed (notably with TM in fibroblasts), the levels were substantially lower than those induced by the oxidative control. This suggests that the extracts do not trigger severe oxidative damage at IC50 concentrations and may even exert mild redox‐modulating effects, likely attributable to their flavonoid and phenolic constituents. Controlled ROS generation is known to play a pivotal role in initiating fibroblast proliferation and migration during wound healing [69, 70, 71] while excessive ROS leads to apoptosis or necrosis [72]. The extracts, particularly TL, appear to induce a balanced oxidative environment that favors tissue regeneration. However, in HaCaT cells, TM exhibited significant cytotoxicity despite reduced ROS generation, suggesting that its toxicity may involve mechanisms independent of oxidative damage—potentially linked to diterpenoid constituents with known cytotoxic activity [73].

An important methodological aspect of this work is the selection of a 48‐h exposure period, which allowed the detection of both early and sustained biological responses while minimizing nonspecific long‐term toxicity. This timeframe provided a reliable balance for evaluating proliferative, apoptotic, migratory, and oxidative parameters consistently across cell lines.

Taken together, these results indicate that although the four Thymus species share common classes of bioactive metabolites leading to broadly similar enzyme inhibition profiles, differences in the relative abundance of triterpenoids, flavonoid subclasses, diterpenoids, and phenolic acids significantly influence cellular responses. TL emerges as the extract with the most favorable balance between efficacy and safety, particularly in the context of dermo‐cosmetic applications, probably due to its richness in polymethoxylated flavonoids, which differ in bioavailability and redox potential from non‐methoxylated ones [74, 75]. TM, while biologically active, requires careful concentration control given its greater cytotoxic potential. TB and TS represent intermediate profiles that combine moderate safety with regenerative and antioxidant properties.

A limitation of the present study is that the scratch assay was performed without the use of a mitosis inhibitor such as mitomycin C. Therefore, the observed wound‐closure responses may reflect the combined contributions of cell migration and proliferation. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as evidence of wound closure enhancement rather than as direct evidence of isolated migratory activity or definitive pro‐angiogenic effects.

Overall, this study highlights the importance of integrating detailed phytochemical characterization with functional cellular assays. While compositional differences may not drastically affect enzyme inhibition in cell‐free systems, they play a crucial role in modulating cell viability, apoptosis, migration, and oxidative balance, which are essential parameters for the development of safe and effective plant‐based formulations.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of four traditionally used Thymus species from Türkiye—T. brachychilus, T. leucostomus, T. migricus, and T. sipyleus—by integrating metabolomic characterization with enzyme‐based and cell‐based biological assays relevant to cosmetic applications. Although the four species shared major classes of specialized metabolites, including triterpenoids, flavonoids, diterpenoids, and phenolic acid derivatives, their relative distribution differed substantially.

Despite these phytochemical differences, all extracts exhibited comparable inhibitory activity against collagenase, elastase, and hyaluronidase, while tyrosinase inhibition remained limited. This suggests that the shared presence of bioactive scaffolds, particularly triterpenes and polyphenols, may account for their similar anti‐aging enzyme profiles. However, clearer distinctions emerged in cellular models. Among the tested extracts, T. leucostomus demonstrated the most balanced and biocompatible profile, combining low cytotoxicity, controlled apoptosis, strong wound‐healing response, and stable redox behavior. T. migricus showed pronounced biological activity but also higher cytotoxic and pro‐necrotic effects at elevated concentrations, indicating the need for careful dose optimization. T. brachychilus and T. sipyleus displayed intermediate characteristics, with moderate safety and promising regenerative and antioxidant properties.

Importantly, this study suggests that the dermocosmetic potential of the genus Thymus may extend beyond the traditionally used species such as Thymus vulgaris and Thymus serpyllum. In particular, T. leucostomus demonstrated a comparatively balanced biological profile in the present in vitro models. However, additional studies addressing formulation stability, skin penetration, and in vivo safety and efficacy are necessary before considering potential cosmetic applications.

Among the investigated species, T. leucostomus demonstrated the most balanced in vitro profile, combining strong wound‐closure responses, controlled oxidative balance, and comparatively low cytotoxicity across multiple cell models.

Overall, the results highlight that while enzyme inhibition assays provide valuable preliminary insights, cell‐based evaluations are essential to discriminate between extracts in terms of safety and regenerative potential. The present findings provide preliminary in vitro evidence supporting the biological relevance of lesser‐investigated Thymus species in dermocosmetic‐related research and warrant further mechanistic, formulation‐based, and in vivo investigations.
