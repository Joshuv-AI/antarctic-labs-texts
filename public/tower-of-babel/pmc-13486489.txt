A distribution-level statistical framework for reliable pipeline leak detection using multi-domain signal analysis
Authors: Muhammad Umar, Muhammad Farooq Siddique, Jaeyoung Kim, Jong-Myon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early and reliable detection of pipeline leaks is essential for ensuring operational safety and minimizing environmental and economic losses. In practice, however, pipeline monitoring signals are inherently non-stationary and strongly influenced by operating conditions, making robust leak detection challenging, particularly when labeled fault data are scarce or unavailable. This paper presents a statistically grounded, signal-processing-based framework for pipeline leak detection that operates without reliance on machine learning or deep learning models. Pipeline signals are represented using a compact multi-domain feature set integrating time-domain statistics, frequency-domain spectral descriptors, and time-frequency features derived from wavelet packet decomposition. Instead of monitoring pointwise feature deviations, pipeline condition is assessed through distribution-level comparison between feature sets extracted from sliding monitoring windows and a reference distribution constructed under normal operation. Multiple complementary two-sample statistics energy distance, maximum mean discrepancy, and Hotelling’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}^{2}$$\end{document} statistic are employed to capture distinct aspects of distributional divergence and fused into a unified health indicator (HI). Leak detection is achieved by comparing this indicator against statistically derived thresholds obtained exclusively from normal-condition data, enabling automated detection with controlled false-alarm behavior. The proposed framework is validated using experimental acoustic emission data collected from pipelines conveying gas and water at pressure levels of 13 bar and 18 bar. Results demonstrate stable normal-state behavior with false-alarm rates below 1%, rapid leak detection within 1–2 samples after leak onset, and detection accuracy exceeding 99% across all operating scenarios. Permutation-based statistical significance analysis confirms strong detection confidence with p-values in the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{-6}$$\end{document}, while robustness evaluation across more than 60 parameter configurations demonstrates consistent performance without operating-condition-specific tuning. The proposed approach provides an interpretable, data-efficient, and statistically rigorous solution for practical pipeline leak monitoring.

Introduction

Pipeline systems play a critical role in the transportation of fluids such as gas and water in industrial, municipal, and energy infrastructures1. Due to their extensive geographical coverage, complex network topology, and long-term exposure to harsh operating environments, pipelines are inherently susceptible to various degradation mechanisms, including corrosion, material fatigue, mechanical damage, and manufacturing defects2. If left undetected, pipeline leaks may lead to severe economic losses, environmental contamination, and significant safety hazards to both infrastructure and surrounding communities3. Consequently, the development of reliable, timely, and robust pipeline leak detection techniques remains an important and active research topic in condition monitoring, structural health assessment, and industrial safety4.

A wide range of sensing modalities has been investigated for pipeline leak detection, including pressure and flow measurements, acoustic and ultrasonic sensing, vibration-based monitoring, and optical fiber-based methods5. Among these techniques, signal-based monitoring approaches have attracted considerable attention due to their non-intrusive nature, ease of deployment, scalability to long-distance pipelines, and suitability for continuous online monitoring6. However, pipeline signals acquired in real operating environments are often non-stationary, highly variable, and strongly influenced by operating conditions such as fluid type, flow rate, internal pressure, and environmental disturbances7. These characteristics significantly complicate the reliable discrimination between normal operational variability and leakage-induced anomalies, particularly in complex industrial systems8.

Traditional pipeline leak detection techniques commonly rely on monitoring pointwise changes in selected signal features, such as root mean square, variance, skewness, kurtosis, or spectral energy9. While such indicators are straightforward to compute and easy to interpret, they are often highly sensitive to background noise, environmental disturbances, and operational fluctuations10. As a result, these methods frequently exhibit unstable behavior and elevated false-alarm rates in practice11. Moreover, threshold values for individual indicators are typically selected heuristically and are strongly dependent on specific operating conditions. This reliance on manual tuning limits their generalizability and robustness when pipelines operate under varying fluids, pressures, or flow regimes12.

To overcome these limitations, recent research has increasingly explored data-driven approaches, particularly machine learning and deep learning techniques, for pipeline leak detection13. These methods aim to automatically learn discriminative representations from measured data and have demonstrated promising performance in controlled experimental studies14. However, their practical deployment in real-world pipeline systems remains constrained by several important factors. First, supervised learning approaches require large volumes of labeled leakage data for training, which are often difficult, costly, or unsafe to obtain due to operational constraints15. Second, learned models frequently exhibit sensitivity to changes in operating conditions, necessitating retraining or complex domain adaptation strategies when system conditions vary16. Third, the lack of interpretability associated with many learning-based approaches poses challenges in safety-critical applications, where transparent decision-making, traceability, and confidence assessment are essential requirements17.

An alternative and increasingly relevant perspective is to formulate pipeline leak detection as a problem of statistical distribution monitoring, rather than pointwise feature deviation18. From this viewpoint, the occurrence of a leak is not necessarily characterized by abrupt or isolated changes in individual feature values, but rather by persistent and systematic changes in the underlying statistical distributions of signal features over time. Such distributional changes may manifest as shifts in mean, variance, correlation structure, or higher-order statistical characteristics, particularly in non-stationary signals collected from complex mechanical systems operating under variable conditions19.

Distribution-level analysis offers several advantages for pipeline monitoring applications. By analyzing aggregated statistical behavior over time windows, it provides inherent robustness against random fluctuations, measurement noise, and transient disturbances. It also naturally accommodates multi-domain feature representations, enabling complementary signal characteristics from different domains to be jointly analyzed. Furthermore, distribution-based metrics facilitate principled statistical hypothesis testing, allowing detection confidence and alarm thresholds to be derived from well-established statistical theory rather than heuristic rules. Despite these advantages, distribution-level monitoring techniques remain relatively underexplored in the context of pipeline leak detection, especially in frameworks that remain interpretable, data-efficient, and suitable for online deployment in real industrial environments20.

Related work

Research on pipeline leak detection has evolved along three primary directions: signal-processing-based feature analysis, statistical and distribution-based monitoring methods, and data-driven approaches based on machine learning and deep learning21,22. Early pipeline leak detection studies predominantly relied on classical signal processing techniques, where characteristic features were manually extracted from measured signals to indicate abnormal conditions23. Time-domain features such as mean value, RMS, variance, skewness, and kurtosis have been widely used due to their simplicity, low computational cost, and ease of interpretation24. Frequency-domain features derived from Fourier analysis or power spectral density estimation have also been explored to capture changes in spectral energy caused by leakage-induced turbulence or acoustic emissions25.

To better address the non-stationary nature of pipeline signals, time–frequency analysis techniques, including short-time Fourier transform, continuous wavelet transform, and wavelet packet decomposition, have been widely adopted26,27. These methods enable localized analysis of transient events and have demonstrated improved sensitivity compared to purely time- or frequency-domain approaches. In particular, wavelet packet-based energy features have shown promise in capturing leakage-induced transient behavior across multiple frequency bands28. Nevertheless, signal-processing-based methods that rely on individual features or fixed thresholds remain strongly affected by operating conditions29. Variations in pressure, flow rate, and fluid properties can induce feature changes comparable in magnitude to those caused by leaks, leading to unstable detection performance and frequent false alarms30.

To improve robustness, statistical approaches have been introduced to analyze aggregated signal behavior rather than instantaneous feature values31. Parametric hypothesis tests targeting changes in mean or variance have been applied in pipeline monitoring; however, their effectiveness is often limited by assumptions of data normality and stationarity, which are rarely satisfied in real pipeline systems. As a result, non-parametric statistical tests have attracted increasing attention. Distribution comparison techniques such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Mann-Whitney test, and related rank-based methods frame leak detection as a two-sample statistical problem and offer greater flexibility under unknown or non-Gaussian data distributions32.

Recent studies have demonstrated that comparing empirical distributions of signal features can significantly improve leak detection reliability by capturing global changes in statistical behavior33. However, existing distribution-based approaches typically rely on a single statistical test or a limited feature representation, which restricts their ability to characterize complex distributional changes34. In addition, commonly used metrics such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic focus primarily on maximum cumulative distribution differences and may fail to capture changes in covariance structure, multimodality, or higher-order statistics. Furthermore, many statistical approaches report raw test values without normalization or confidence calibration, making consistent threshold selection and cross-condition comparison challenging.

In parallel, machine learning and deep learning approaches have been increasingly explored for pipeline leak detection. Supervised classifiers and deep neural networks have demonstrated strong discrimination capability in experimental studies35. However, their reliance on large labeled datasets, sensitivity to operating condition changes, and limited interpretability hinder their adoption in safety-critical pipeline monitoring applications36. Moreover, most learning-based methods treat leak detection as a classification problem, focusing on decision boundaries rather than the underlying statistical behavior of the signals, which may obscure important distributional information relevant for online monitoring and early detection37.

Overall, existing pipeline leak detection methods face a trade-off between simplicity, robustness, interpretability, and data efficiency. Feature-based signal processing approaches are interpretable but sensitive to operating variability, statistical distribution-based methods improve robustness but are often limited in expressiveness, and learning-based approaches offer strong discrimination capability at the expense of data dependency and transparency. These limitations motivate the development of a distribution-level, statistically principled leak detection framework that remains interpretable, data-efficient, and suitable for online monitoring under varying operating conditions.

Preliminaries

This section presents the theoretical foundations required for distribution-level pipeline leak detection. Emphasis is placed on the statistical characterization of non-stationary pipeline signals, windowed multi-domain feature representation, distributional divergence measures, statistical hypothesis testing, and online change detection. These concepts provide the mathematical and statistical background supporting the proposed methodology introduced in Sect. 4.

Experimental setup

The proposed distribution-level pipeline leak detection framework is validated using AE signals acquired from a laboratory-scale pipeline testbed operating under controlled and repeatable conditions. The experimental design closely reflects practical pipeline monitoring scenarios in which normal operating data are available for reference modeling, while leak conditions occur unexpectedly and must be detected online without prior fault labeling.

Experimental testbed and data acquisition

The experimental pipeline testbed used for data acquisition is shown in Fig. 3. The setup consists of a metallic pipeline instrumented with multiple AE sensors mounted externally along the pipe wall. A controllable leak orifice is introduced at a predefined location to generate repeatable leakage conditions. AE signals are captured using piezoelectric sensors connected to a high-speed data acquisition system (NI 9223), with synchronized multi-channel recording. A schematic of the pipeline configuration is provided in Fig. 4, detailing sensor placement, inter-sensor spacing, leak position, fluid flow direction, and pressure monitoring instrumentation. Two AE sensors (CH1 and CH2) are mounted with a fixed spacing of 2500 mm, while the leak point is located 800 mm downstream from the pipeline start. Pressure levels are monitored continuously to ensure stable operating conditions throughout data collection.

The dataset comprises four operating scenarios formed by combining two fluid types (gas and water) with two pressure levels (13 bar and 18 bar). For each fluid-pressure combination, AE signals are recorded under both normal (no-leak) and leak conditions. A total of 360 signal samples are available for each scenario, consisting of 120 normal samples and 240 leak samples. This intentional class imbalance reflects real-world pipeline operation, where leak events are rare compared to normal operating periods.

Prior to feature extraction, all signals are standardized in length and amplitude scale to ensure consistency across operating conditions. No leakage-specific filtering, denoising, or heuristic preprocessing is applied, allowing the proposed framework to operate directly on raw signal characteristics. This choice is intentional, as it reflects realistic deployment scenarios where prior knowledge of fault signatures is unavailable. Each signal is segmented into overlapping time windows of fixed length to balance temporal resolution and statistical reliability. Overlapping windows enable smooth tracking of distributional changes while providing sufficient samples within each window for robust statistical estimation.

From each windowed signal segment, a multi-domain feature vector is extracted. The feature set includes time-domain statistical descriptors, frequency-domain spectral features, and time–frequency energy features derived from wavelet packet decomposition. The final feature dimensionality is fixed across all operating conditions to ensure comparability. For each fluid-pressure scenario, the reference state is constructed exclusively from the first 120 normal samples. These samples are assumed to represent healthy pipeline operation and are used to estimate all normalization and transformation parameters, including robust scaling and feature whitening. Importantly, no leak-condition data are used during reference construction, which ensures that the baseline distribution remains uncontaminated and reflects realistic deployment conditions.

After reference construction, the remaining samples, including both normal and leak conditions, are processed sequentially to simulate online monitoring. At each time step, a sliding window of recent feature vectors is formed to represent the current pipeline state. Distributional divergence between the reference state and the current state is then computed, fused into a unified health indicator, and evaluated for leak detection using the methodology described in Sect. 4.

All experiments are implemented using Python-based scientific computing libraries. Feature extraction parameters, windowing configurations, normalization procedures, and thresholding strategies are held constant across all experiments to ensure fair comparison and reproducibility. Random seeds are fixed where applicable. Notably, no machine learning or deep learning models are trained, tuned, or optimized using leak-condition data at any stage of the analysis. Overall, this experimental setup provides a rigorous and realistic validation environment for the proposed distribution-level pipeline leak detection framework. By incorporating multiple fluids, pressure levels, imbalanced operating conditions, and statistically principled evaluation, the experimental design enables comprehensive assessment of robustness, interpretability, and practical applicability for real-world pipeline monitoring systems.

Proposed distribution-level leak detection method

This section presents the proposed distribution-level statistical framework for pipeline leak detection. Building upon the theoretical foundations introduced in Sect. 2, the method detects leakage by monitoring deviations between feature distributions observed during operation and a reference distribution constructed under normal conditions. Unlike sample-level or feature-threshold-based approaches, the proposed framework focuses on persistent distributional changes, thereby enhancing robustness under non-stationary operating conditions. An overview of the complete processing pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 6.

Step 1: The measured pipeline signal is first segmented into overlapping time windows of fixed length. Window overlap is employed to balance temporal resolution with statistical reliability, enabling gradual changes in signal behavior to be tracked over time. From each windowed segment, a multi-domain feature vector is extracted, comprising time-domain statistics, frequency-domain descriptors, and time-frequency features obtained using a Daubechies-4 wavelet at level four. As described in Sect. 2.3, this feature representation captures complementary aspects of pipeline signal behavior across multiple domains. The resulting feature vectors form a time-ordered sequence that reflects the evolving statistical characteristics of the pipeline under varying operating conditions.

Step 2: A key advantage of the proposed method is that it requires only normal-condition data for model construction. Feature vectors extracted from an initial reference period (first 120 samples) are assumed to represent healthy pipeline operation and are used to construct the reference feature distribution. To ensure robustness against outliers and scale differences across features, all features are normalized using robust scaling based on median and interquartile range (IQR) statistics derived exclusively from reference data evaluated using Eq. This formulation enables detection of small but persistent distributional changes that may not be observable at the sample level, particularly under non-stationary operating conditions.

Step 4: To quantify the discrepancy between the reference and monitoring distributions, three complementary divergence measures are employed, each capturing a distinct aspect of distributional change. Energy distance provides a global measure of divergence sensitive to differences in both location and dispersion. MMD evaluates divergence in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space and is capable of capturing higher-order and nonlinear distributional differences. By integrating these complementary measures, the proposed framework avoids reliance on a single statistical assumption and remains robust across diverse leakage scenarios, including changes in signal energy, spectral content, and correlation structure.

By focusing on persistent distributional deviations rather than instantaneous feature changes, the method achieves improved robustness, interpretability, and data efficiency under non-stationary operating conditions. Results and performance evaluation

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed distribution-level statistical framework for pipeline leak detection. The analysis is designed to assess the suitability of the proposed HI for practical online monitoring, focusing on (i) temporal behavior under normal and leakage conditions, (ii) robustness with respect to parameter configurations, (iii) classification performance and error characteristics, (iv) detection delay and indicator stability, and (v) statistical confidence based on multi-scale permutation testing. All results are reported for gas and water pipelines operating at pressure levels of 13 bar and 18 bar.

Indicator behavior and temporal evolution

This subsection examines the temporal evolution of the proposed fused HI under normal operation and leakage conditions. The objective is to verify whether the indicator exhibits the fundamental properties required for reliable online monitoring, namely stability during normal operation, sensitivity to leakage-induced changes, and sustained separation between normal and abnormal states after leak onset.

Across all operating conditions and parameter configurations, the HI remains tightly bounded during normal operation, exhibiting low variance and no observable drift. This behavior confirms that the reference distribution construction, robust normalization, and whitening procedures effectively suppress sensitivity to stochastic fluctuations and operating variability. Following leak onset, the indicator increases sharply and persistently, crossing the detection threshold within a small number of samples and remaining elevated thereafter. Importantly, once the leakage condition is detected, no oscillatory behavior or reversion below the threshold is observed.

Representative indicator trajectories are shown in Fig. 7(a)-(h) for different fluids, pressures, and configuration settings. In all cases, the separation between normal and leakage states is visually and quantitatively distinct, with no overlap observed. These results demonstrate that the proposed indicator captures persistent distributional changes induced by leakage rather than transient feature fluctuations, forming a reliable basis for online leak monitoring.

Multi-scale statistical significance and confidence analysis

While threshold exceedance indicates abnormal behavior, it does not directly quantify statistical confidence. To address this limitation, the proposed framework incorporates permutation-based hypothesis testing to assess the statistical significance of detected distributional changes across multiple temporal scales. Permutation tests are conducted by comparing the feature distribution observed in each monitoring window with the reference distribution obtained under normal operation. Statistical significance is expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:-{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}}_{10}\left(p\right)$$\end{document}, where larger values correspond to stronger evidence against the null hypothesis of normal operation. Representative multi-scale significance evolution plots for gas and water pipelines at 13 bar and 18 bar are shown in Fig. 11(a)-(h). During normal operation, significance values remain near zero and well below the weakest rejection threshold, indicating the absence of spurious statistical confidence. Following leak onset, significance values increase rapidly and exceed even the most stringent thresholds, providing strong and persistent rejection of the null hypothesis. Intermediate window sizes consistently offer a favorable balance between detection speed and statistical stability. Importantly, once sufficient abnormal data are accumulated, high statistical significance is maintained across all window sizes, demonstrating that leakage manifests as a persistent distributional change rather than a scale-specific artifact. The proposed framework was designed to maintain computational efficiency suitable for real-time monitoring applications. Unlike data-intensive machine learning approaches requiring iterative model training and high computational resources, the proposed methodology operates on compact statistical feature representations combined with lightweight divergence computations and threshold-based decision rules. The primary computational cost arises from feature extraction and distributional divergence estimation within sliding monitoring windows. However, because the framework processes low-dimensional feature vectors rather than raw signal streams, the resulting computational burden remains moderate and scalable for online implementation. Furthermore, the use of fixed reference statistics and percentile-based thresholding eliminates the need for continuous retraining or operating-condition-specific optimization during deployment, making the proposed framework well suited for practical real-time pipeline monitoring and embedded implementation scenarios.

To further quantify the confidence of leak detection decisions, this subsection evaluates the statistical significance of the observed distributional changes using permutation-based hypothesis testing across multiple temporal scales. While previous subsections demonstrated that the proposed HI reliably separates normal and leakage states, the present analysis explicitly addresses the question of how statistically confident the detected changes are, beyond threshold exceedance alone.

The consistency of statistically significant detection across different fluids, pressure levels, and window sizes provides strong evidence that the proposed framework captures fundamental leak-induced changes rather than configuration-specific artifacts. Complete significance results for all tested scenarios and parameter combinations are provided in the supplementary material, further confirming the robustness of the proposed method. Overall, this analysis establishes that the proposed distribution-level framework not only detects leaks reliably but also provides rigorous statistical confidence for detection decisions. The integration of permutation-based hypothesis testing and multi-scale analysis enhances interpretability, supports principled threshold selection, and strengthens the suitability of the proposed approach for safety-critical pipeline monitoring applications.

Collectively, the experimental results demonstrate that the proposed distribution-level statistical framework provides reliable, robust, and interpretable pipeline leak detection across a wide range of operating conditions and parameter settings. By focusing on persistent distributional changes rather than instantaneous feature deviations, the framework effectively addresses challenges posed by non-stationary signals and operating variability. The combination of high detection accuracy, low false-alarm rates, rapid detection, and rigorous statistical confidence makes the proposed approach well-suited for practical and safety-critical pipeline monitoring applications.

Despite the strong performance demonstrated under the evaluated operating conditions, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the experimental validation was conducted under controlled laboratory-scale conditions using specific fluid types, pressure levels, and leak configurations. Although multiple operating scenarios were considered, additional variability commonly encountered in real industrial environments, including changing flow regimes, environmental disturbances, sensor aging, structural heterogeneity, and long-term operational drift, was not explicitly investigated. Furthermore, the current framework utilizes fixed sliding-window configurations and offline reference construction based on initially available normal-condition data. In continuously evolving industrial systems, adaptive reference updating and dynamic windowing strategies may further improve long-term robustness and responsiveness. Consequently, the reported detection performance should be interpreted within the context of the experimental conditions considered in this study rather than as definitive proof of universal industrial performance. While the proposed framework demonstrated strong robustness and generalization across the evaluated operating conditions, additional validation using large-scale field data, broader operational scenarios, and previously unseen leakage conditions remains an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

This paper presented a statistically grounded, distribution-level framework for pipeline leak detection that operates without reliance on machine learning or deep learning models. By formulating leak detection as a problem of distributional change rather than instantaneous feature deviation, the proposed approach effectively addresses the challenges posed by non-stationary pipeline signals and variability in operating conditions. Pipeline behavior was characterized using a compact multi-domain feature representation integrating time-domain statistics, frequency-domain spectral descriptors, and time-frequency information derived from wavelet packet energy analysis. Robust feature normalization and whitening enabled stable reference modeling based exclusively on normal operating data, ensuring data-efficient and interpretable monitoring. Leak detection was performed by comparing feature distributions extracted from sliding monitoring windows with a reference distribution using multiple complementary two-sample statistical measures, including energy distance, maximum mean discrepancy, and Hotelling’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}^{2}$$\end{document} statistic. These measures were fused into a unified health indicator that provides a statistically meaningful and interpretable quantification of pipeline condition. Detection thresholds were derived directly from normal operating data, enabling automated leak identification with controlled false-alarm behavior and minimal parameter tuning. Experimental validation using acoustic emission data collected from pipeline systems conveying gas and water under different pressure levels demonstrated the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework. The results showed stable behavior under normal operation, rapid detection of leak events, high detection accuracy, and consistent performance across all tested operating conditions. These findings highlight the practical applicability of the proposed method for real-world pipeline monitoring scenarios where labeled fault data are limited or unavailable.

Future work will focus on extending the proposed framework to address additional operational complexities, including varying flow rates, temperature fluctuations, and background environmental disturbances. The investigation of adaptive windowing strategies and incremental reference updating mechanisms is expected to further enhance long-term robustness under evolving operating conditions. In addition, extending the distribution-level framework to multi-sensor configurations and leak localization tasks represents a promising direction for future research. The incorporation of physically informed feature representations and uncertainty quantification techniques may further improve interpretability and reliability in large-scale industrial pipeline monitoring applications.
