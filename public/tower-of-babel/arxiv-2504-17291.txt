Top on a smooth plane
Maria Przybylska, Andrzej J. Maciejewski

Abstract
We investigate the dynamics of a sliding top that is a rigid body with an ideal sharp tip moving in a perfectly smooth horizontal plane, so no friction forces act on the body. We prove that this system is integrable only in two cases analogous to the Euler and Lagrange cases of the classical top problem. The cases with the constant gravity field with acceleration g ≠ 0 g\neq 0 and without external field g = 0 g=0 are considered. The non-integrability proof for g ≠ 0 g\neq 0 based on the fact that the equations of motion for the sliding top are a perturbation of the classical top equations of motion. We show that the integrability of the classical top is a necessary condition for the integrability of the sliding top. Among four integrable classical top cases the corresponding two cases for the sliding top are also integrable, and for the two remaining cases, we prove their non-integrability by analyzing the differential Galois group of variational equations along a certain particular solution. In the absence of constant gravitational field g = 0 g=0 the integrability is much more difficult. At first, we proved that if the sliding top problem is integrable, then the body is symmetric. In the proof, we applied one of the Ziglin theorem concerning the splitting of separatrices phenomenon. Then we prove the non-integrability of the symmetric sliding top using differential Galois group of variational equations except two the same as for g ≠ 0 g\neq 0 cases. The integrability of these cases is also preserved when we add to equations of motion a gyrostatic term. A rigid body, apart from the material point, is the most important model in classical mechanics that represents various physical systems. The analysis of the dynamics of a rigid body with one fixed point in the gravitational field, called the heavy top, has been a research problem for hundreds of years and a testing ground for which various methods have been used and gave impetus to the creation of new methods. This paper provides a complete integrability analysis of an almost forgotten top model with an ideal sharp tip that slides on a perfectly smooth horizontal plane in a constant gravity field.

We consider the constrained motion of a rigid body in a constant gravity field.
For the description of the problem, we use two orthonormal reference frames. The

(3)

see,

&\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=m[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{L})](\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{L})-mg\boldsymbol{L},\end{split}

(11)

the explicit form of equations (

\begin{split}&\left[\boldsymbol{I}+m(\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\otimes(\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mspace{1mu}t}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega}\\

&\frac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mspace{1mu}t}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{\Omega},\end{split}

(12)

where

denotes the outer product of vectors, see e.g.
[

.

Equations (

\begin{split}&H=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\frac{1}{2}m\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\times\boldsymbol{L})\right]^{2}+mg\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{L},\\
&F_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{2},\quad F_{2}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{\Omega}.\end{split}

(13)

Let us introduce the angular momenta defined as

\boldsymbol{M}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{\Omega},\quad\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{I}+m(\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\otimes(\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma}),

(14)

compare with formula (1.100) in

Multiplying both sides of the first equation (

Using these identities we find the final form of equations of motion for the

System (

(17)

Equations of motion (
16
) can be written as

\dot{\boldsymbol{X}}=\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{X})\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}}(\boldsymbol{X}),\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{X}=(\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})

and

The matrix

defines a degenerate Poisson bracket. For two

and

Thus, the Poisson bracket of

and

. The functions

are Casimir functions of this Poisson structure. Thus, on a generic leaf,

,

.

Theorem 1
.

System (
16
) is integrable with complex meromorphic first integrals only in the
following cases:

1.

The Euler case when
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{0}
and the additional first integral is the

. The form of

The Lagrange case where the body is symmetric and the center of mass lies on the axis of symmetry, for example,

. Then the additional

Moreover, we can also identify the Hess-Appelrot case. In this case, there

, and then we set

L_{2}=0,\quad\sqrt{(A-B)C}\,L_{3}\pm\sqrt{(B-C)A}\,L_{1}=0.

(21)

Under these conditions, the zero level of the polynomial

is invariant.

[
4

in the context

II
Integrability analysis. Preliminary considerations.

II.1
Relation of the system with classical heavy top

To compare the considered system with the classical heavy top problem (a body with a fixed point in a constant gravity field), we introduce the weights
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(1,1,1,2,2,2)
for variables
\boldsymbol{X}=(\boldsymbol{M},\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
, so that the weighted degree with respect to these weights is defined by

\deg_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}M_{i}=1,\qquad\deg_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\Gamma_{i}=2,\qquad i=1,2,3.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
be the Euler field associated with these weights

\boldsymbol{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\sum_{i=1}^{6}\alpha_{i}X_{i}\dfrac{\partial\phantom{X}}{\partial X_{i}}.

Then, a function
F(\boldsymbol{X})
is weight-homogeneous of degree
k
if

\boldsymbol{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}[F]=kF,

and a vector field
\boldsymbol{V}(\boldsymbol{X})
is weight-homogeneous of degree
k
if

[\boldsymbol{E}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\boldsymbol{V}]=k\boldsymbol{V},

where here
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the Lie bracket.

The chosen weights are compatible with the Poisson structure defined by

\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{X})
. Thus, if
F(\boldsymbol{X})
is weight-homogeneous of degree
k
, then the
Hamiltonian vector field
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{X})\tfrac{\partial F}{\partial\boldsymbol{X}}
is weight
homogeneous of degree
k-1
. Moreover, if
F(\boldsymbol{X})
and
G(\boldsymbol{X})
are weight
homogeneous, then
\{F,G\}
is weight-homogeneous and if
\{F,G\}\neq 0
then

\deg_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\{F,G\}=\deg_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}F+\deg_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}G-1
.

Let
F(\boldsymbol{X})
be a meromorphic function. Then it admits weight homogeneous
expansion

F(\boldsymbol{X})=F_{m}(\boldsymbol{X})+\cdots,

here
F_{m}(\boldsymbol{X})
is a non-zero weight-homogeneous function of degree
m
, and
dots denote weight-homogeneous terms of degree higher than
m
. Function
F_{m}

is called the lowest order term of
F
and is denoted
F^{\circ}
.
The Hamiltonian
H(\boldsymbol{X})
is a rational function, so it has a unique expansion

H(\boldsymbol{X})=H_{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+H_{6}(\boldsymbol{X})+\cdots,

(23)

starting from the lowest second degree term
H_{2}(\boldsymbol{X})=H^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{X})
and the next is of degree 6, which have explicit forms

\displaystyle H_{2}(\boldsymbol{X})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}+mg\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{L}.

(24)

\displaystyle H_{6}(\boldsymbol{X})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}m\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{I}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}\times\boldsymbol{L})\right]^{2}.

(25)

Let us notice that Hamilton equations (
18
) with
H=H_{2}
and
the Poisson structure given in (
19
) are the Euler-Poisson equations for the classical heavy top

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mspace{1mu}t}\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{M}\times\boldsymbol{I}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}-mg\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma},\\
&\frac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mspace{1mu}t}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{I}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}.\end{split}

(26)

To make this observation useful, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

If the system (
18
) is integrable with meromorphic first
integrals, then system (
26
) is integrable.

Proof.

By the above-shown properties, the Poisson bracket of
F(\boldsymbol{X})=F^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{X})+\cdots
and
G(\boldsymbol{X})=G^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{X})+\cdots
is

\{F,G\}=\{F^{\circ},G^{\circ}\}+\cdots

Hence, if
F(\boldsymbol{X})
is a first integral of (
18
) which is
functionally independent with
H
,
F_{1}
, and
F_{2}
, then

F^{\circ}(\boldsymbol{X})
is a first integral (
24
).
Furthermore, if
F
commute with
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
then
F^{\circ}
also commute with

F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. By the Ziglin Lemma
[
26
]
we can always choose

F
in such a way that
H^{\circ}
,
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
and
F^{\circ}
are functionally
independent.
∎

By the above lemma, the sliding top is integrable only in the cases for which
the heavy top is integrable. But the problem of the integrability of the heavy
top is solved completely. In the following, we recall known facts.

The heavy top equations of motion (
26
) have first integrals

H_{2}
,
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
given by (
17
). For their integrability,
just one additional first integral is necessary. The problem of the
integrability of these equations has been analyzed for centuries and the following integrable cases have been identified.

1.

The Euler case (1758) corresponds to the situation when there is
no gravity (i.e. when
g=0
) or
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{0}
(the fixed point of
the body is the center of mass). The additional first integral in
this case is the total angular momentum
F_{3}=\boldsymbol{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{M}
.

2.

In the Lagrange case
[
14
]
the body is symmetric (i.e. two of its principal moments of inertial are equal)
and the fixed point lies on the symmetry axis. The additional first integral in this case is the projection of the angular momentum onto the symmetry axis. If we assume that
A=B
, then in the Lagrange case
L_{1}=L_{2}=0
, and
F_{3}=M_{3}
.

3.

In the Kovalevskaya case
[
10
,
11
]
the
body is symmetric and the principal moment of inertia along the symmetry axis is
half of the principal moment of inertia with respect to an axis perpendicular to
the symmetry axis. Moreover, the fixed point lies in the principal plane
perpendicular to the symmetry axis. If
A=B=2C
, then (after an appropriate
rotation around the symmetry axis) we have in the Kovalevskaya case
L_{2}=L_{3}=0
.
The additional first integral has the form

F_{3}=\left(\frac{1}{2}(M_{1}^{2}-M_{2}^{2})-mgL_{1}A\Gamma_{1}\right)^{2}\\
+(M_{1}M_{2}-mgL_{1}A\Gamma_{2})^{2}.

4.

In the Goryachev-Chaplygin case
[
7
]
the body is
symmetric and, as in the Kovalevskaya case, the fixed point lies in the
principal plane perpendicular to the symmetry axis. If we assume that the third
principal axis is the symmetry axis, then in this case we have
A=B=4C
and

L_{2}=L_{3}=0
. In the Goryachev-Chaplygin case equations (
26
) are
integrable only at the level
F_{1}=0
and the additional first integral has the
following form:

F_{3}=M_{3}(M_{1}^{2}+M_{2}^{2})-mgL_{1}AM_{1}\Gamma_{3}.

For more details on recent studies and results in rigid body dynamics, mathematical structures, interpretations, and generalizations of these cases, see
[
5
]
.

question was taken by

]
, who proved the following theorem.

(Ziglin, 1980)

If

)
does not admit a real meromorphic first integral which is functionally independent together with

,

, and

More precisely, the proof consists in the application of Theorem

. Using his elegant theory based on properties of the monodromy group of variational equations, he proved the following theorem.

(Ziglin, 1983)

The complexified Euler-Poisson system for a symmetric body is integrable on the

with complex meromorphic first integrals only in the four classical cases.

The non-integrability with real meromorphic first integrals was proved

. Similar results for a symmetric heavy top were given by the authors of the differential Galois group of variational equations in

II.2
Numerical analysis of the system

In the previous section we showed how the sliding top is related to the classical heavy top. But immediately we notice the differences: namely in our Theorem

Simple numerical experiments show that both systems are fundamentally different.

We numerically integrate the equations (

III
Non-integrability theorems

The analysis of the integrability of systems of high dimensions and dependent on
parameters is complicated. One can expect generically non-integrability, but for
certain values of parameters the system can be integrable. To select such
values, one needs theorems formulating necessary conditions for integrability,
or just necessary conditions for the existence of a certain number of additional
functionally independent first integrals.

In this paper, we will use two such theorems. One of them is related to the
phenomenon of splitting of surfaces asymptotic to a hyperbolic periodic
solution of a system which is a perturbation of an integrable system; for more details, see, e.g.
[
2
, Sec. 7.2]
or
[
13
, Ch. V]
.

Here, we use the formulation proposed
in
[
24
,
25
]
.
Let us consider a Hamiltonian system given by Hamiltonian

H(q,p,\varphi,I,\mu)=H_{0}(q,p,I)+\mu H_{1}(q,p,\varphi,I)+\cdots,

(27)

which is a real analytic function
2\pi
-periodic with respect to
\varphi
; variables
\boldsymbol{x}=(q,p)
belong to an open set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
I\in(I_{0}-\epsilon,I_{0}+\epsilon)
,

\varphi\in\mathbb{R}\mod 2\pi
, and
\lvert\mu\rvert<\epsilon
.

Hamilton’s equations take the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},\,\,\,\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q},\,\,\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial I},\,\,\,\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\varphi},

(28)

and for
\mu=0
unperturbed Hamiltonian system is

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial H_{0}(q,p,I)}{\partial p},\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\partial H_{0}(q,p,I)}{\partial q},\\
&\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial H_{0}(q,p,I)}{\partial I},\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t}=0.\end{split}

(29)

We assume that:

1. the system

\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\partial H_{0}(q,p,I_{0})}{\partial p},\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\partial H_{0}(q,p,I_{0})}{\partial q},

(30)

has two hyperbolic equilibria
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
(not necessarily distinct),
and the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
and
-\lambda_{i}
of the linearization of the
system (
30
) at the point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
,
i=1,2
are real and
different from zero;

2. equilibria
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
are joined
by double-asymptotic solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)
such that

\lim_{t\to-\infty}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t)=\boldsymbol{x}_{2};

3. Inequality
\tfrac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial I}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t),I_{0})>c>0
holds for all
-\infty<t<\infty
.

Let us introduce the notation

\begin{split}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t)=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t),I_{0},\widehat{\varphi}(t)),\quad\widehat{\varphi}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial I}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(\tau),I_{0})d\tau,\\
&\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t,\overline{\varphi})=(\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}(t),I_{0},\widehat{\varphi}(t)+\overline{\varphi}),\end{split}

and make the following further assumptions.

4. Solution
\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t)
is analytical, although generally it has an
analytic continuation (in general not single-valued) with at most a finite
number of singular points in the strip
\Pi:0\leq\mathrm{Im}\,t\leq\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda_{1}}
. We denote by
\Pi^{\prime}
the strip
\Pi
with these
singular points removed;

5. Hamiltonian
H(q,p,\varphi,I,\mu)
is analytic, although generally it has
an analytic continuation (in general not single-valued) in a domain of complex

(q,p,\varphi,I,\mu)
-space containing the point

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t,\overline{\varphi}),\mu=0)
for every
t\in\Pi^{\prime}
and

\overline{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}
;

6. The functions
\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial I}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t))
and

\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial\varphi}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t,\overline{\varphi}))
for
fixed
\overline{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}
are single-valued in
\Pi
.

In
[
24
]
, see also
[
25
]
the following result was proved.

Theorem 4
.

If for at least one value of
\overline{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}
the function

\frac{\partial H_{1}}{\partial\varphi}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}(t,\overline{\varphi}))
has
nonzero residues in
\Pi
, then there exists a solution of (
28
)
which is not single-valued in the sense that

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\frac{\Delta I(\mu)}{\mu}\neq 0,

where
\Delta I(\mu)
is the increment of the coordinate
I(t,\mu)
of

(q,p,\varphi,I)(t,\mu)
after one circuit of closed contour

\Gamma\subset\Pi^{\prime}
. If the sum of these residues is not equal to zero, then for
any
|\mu|\neq 0
small enough, the system (
28
) does not have a
first integral analytic in
U
and functionally independent of
H
.

In this paper we will use a more convenient formulation of these integrability
obstructions given in
[
25
]
. We expand
H_{1}
in the Fourier series

\begin{split}&H_{1}(q,p,\varphi,I)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}h_{k}(q,p,\varphi,I),\\
&h_{k}(q,p,\varphi,I)=\overline{h}_{k}(q,p,I)e^{\mathrm{i}k\varphi},\end{split}

and then we have the following theorem.

Theorem 5
.

If for at least one
k\neq 0
the sum of residues of function
h_{k}(q,p,\varphi,I)
is non-zero, then not even one small enough
|\mu|
has in phase space
U

analytic first integral independent of
H
.

This theorem applies to systems that can be considered as Hamiltonian
systems that are perturbations of appropriate integrable systems with two
immovable hyperbolic equilibria.
For the proof and details, see
[
24
,
25
]
. We will use this
theorem in the proof of Theorem
8
which is, in a certain sense, a
version of Ziglin Theorem
2
for the sliding top without gravity.
Amazingly enough, the proof of the Ziglin Theorem
2
is quite simple, but the
proof of our theorem
8
is quite complicated and laborious.

In the case when for a considered non-linear system a non-equilibrium particular solution can be explicitly written, variational equations along this solution
can be calculated. The presence of first integrals and the integrability of a
nonlinear system implies the presence of the same number of first integrals and
the integrability of the variational equations.

Moreover, variational equations are linear equations and for them the monodromy
group and the differential Galois group are well defined. The monodromy group
acts linearly in a solution space of a linear system (or scalar linear equation
of a certain degree) by analytic continuations of solutions along closed loops.
More precisely, the monodromy matrices form an anti-representation of the first
homotopy group of a Riemann surface related to the particular solution. First
integrals of variational equations appear invariant of the monodromy group. The
integrability obstructions can be translated to the properties of the monodromy
group of variational equations. They were formulated by
[
26
]
.
This approach is now called
the Ziglin theory.

Since the monodromy group is just a matrix group, more useful for applications is
the differential Galois group which is an algebraic group. The aim of the
differential Galois theory is to study the question of the solvability of linear
differential equations with coefficients in a certain differential field.
Usually, solutions do not belong to the basic differential field containing
coefficients of the considered equation. The smallest differential field
containing all solutions is called the Picard-Vessiot extension of the basic
field. The differential Galois group is the group of automorphisms of the
Picard-Vessiot extension (i.e. invertible transformations of preserving field
operations) that commute with differentiation and do not change elements of the
basic differential field. For a detailed exposition of the differential Galois
theory, see, e.g.
[
8
,
18
]
or a short introduction
focused on applications in
[
16
]
.

Differential Galois group is an algebraic group that has a few components, and
this one containing the identity is called the identity component. The first
integrals of variational equations are also invariant of the differential Galois
group. The conditions of the integrability in the Liouville sense for Hamilton
equations translate in the properties of the differential Galois group
formulated in the following theorem due to J.J Morales-Ramis and J.-P. Ramis,
for details see
[
18
]
.

Theorem 6
.

If a Hamiltonian system is meromorphically integrable in the Liouville sense in
a neighborhood of a phase curve
\Gamma
corresponding to a particular
solution, then the identity component of the differential Galois group of
variational equations along
\Gamma
is Abelian.

Despite its abstract definition, the differential Galois group is known for
various linear equations, e.g. the hypergeometric equation or the Lamé
equation, and in the case of second-order linear equations with rational
coefficients, there is an algorithm, called the Kovacic algorithm, see
Appendix
A
, that always allows its determination. We will
use this theorem for the analysis of cases where the considered sliding top is
symmetric.

VI
Final remarks

We perform a complete integrability analysis of the sliding top problem. The final
result was achieved by applying several methods. We show that
the considered system is, in a certain sense, a perturbation of either the heavy top or the Euler top. Using this fact, we show that the integrability of the classical top is the necessary condition of the sliding top. In effect, under the assumption that the gravity field does not vanish, our analysis was reduced to studying two cases of Kovlaevskaya and Goryachev-Chaplygin.

The case of vanishing gravity appeared considerably harder. The basic problem in this case is the lack of a suitable particular solution that can be used to study the integrability. One way to overcome this difficulty was to add an appropriate
assumption concerning the problem parameters. However, this automatically limits
the generality of the results. This is why we decided to follow S.L. Ziglin Integrability Investigations of the classical top. The crucial point was to prove that if the sliding top is integrable, then the body is symmetric. We obtain this result using one, not so well-known, theorem of S.L. Ziglin describing the splitting of separatrices in a system with two degrees of freedom close to an integrable one.

The similarities between the classical and sliding tops are misleading. Nevertheless, both tops have two common integrable cases of Euler and Lagrange. Moreover, we mentioned that for the sliding top we also have the Hess-Appelrot case. It is remarkable that in this case, for the sliding top, similarly as for the classical
top, only one pair of surfaces asymptotic to the hyperbolic periodic solutions
intersects. One can prove it using the method of
[
25
]
and our calculation presented in Section
V.1
.

It is known that the addition of gyrostat terms to the Euler equations of classical heavy top does not destroy the integrability in famous cases giving the Zhukovskii case (an extension of the Euler case), the Lagrange case for gyrostat and Yehia case (an extension of the Kovalevskaya case), see e.g.
[
19
]
and references therein. It is also integrable on the
level
F_{2}=0
t the Sretenskii case (an extension of the Goryachev-Chaplygin). One can ask whether the two integrable cases of the sliding top after the addition of the gyrostatic term remain integrable.

Equations of motion for the sliding top with gyrostat take the form

\begin{split}&\frac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mspace{1mu}t}\boldsymbol{M}=(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{B})\times\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}-mg\boldsymbol{L}\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\\
&+m\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}\times\boldsymbol{L})\right]\left((\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}\times\boldsymbol{L})\times\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\right),\\
&\frac{\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\mathrm{d}\mspace{1mu}t}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\times\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M},\end{split}

(83)

where
\boldsymbol{B}
is the constant gyrostatic moment.

System (
83
) has three first integrals

\begin{split}&H=\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{I}\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}+\frac{1}{2}m\left[\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{M}\times\boldsymbol{L})\right]^{2}\\
&+mg\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{L},\qquad F_{1}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}^{2},\quad F_{2}=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\cdot(\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{B}).\end{split}

Equations of motion (
83
) can be written as Hamiltonian system (
18
) with degenerated
Poisson structure defined by slightly modified matrix
\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{X})

\mathbb{J}(\boldsymbol{X})=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{M}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{B}}&\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}&\boldsymbol{0}\end{bmatrix}.

The functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are Casimir functions of this Poisson structure.

We have additional first integral in cases that are generalizations of the Euler and the Lagrange rigid body cases:

1.

in the Zhukovskii–Volterra
case when
L_{1}=L_{2}=L_{3}=0
that is

F_{3}=\boldsymbol{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{M}-2\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{M}

and

2.

in the Lagrange case when
B=A
and
L_{1}=L_{2}=B_{1}=B_{2}=0
equals to
F_{3}=M_{3}
. In the completely symmetric case when
A=B=C
the additional first integral is
F_{3}=\boldsymbol{M}\cdot\boldsymbol{L}
provided
\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

In fact change of variables
\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{B}\to\boldsymbol{M}
transforms the first case into the Zhukovskii–Volterra system with the standard bracket defined by the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{e}(3)
, for details see
[
5
, Sec. 2.7, Ch. 2]
.

Appendix B
Differential Galois group of the Lamé equation

The Weierstrass
form of the Lamé equation is the following

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}y}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=(\alpha\wp(t;g_{2},g_{3})+\beta)y,

(100)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are, in general, complex parameters and

\wp(t;g_{2},g_{3})
is the elliptic Weierstrass function with invariants

g_{2}
,
g_{3}
. In other words,
\wp(t;g_{2},g_{3})
is a solution of the
differential equation

\dot{v}^{2}=f(v),\qquad f(v)=4v^{3}-g_{2}v-g_{3}.

(101)

The polynomial
f(v)
is assumed to have three different roots, so

\Delta=g_{2}^{3}-27g_{3}^{2}\neq 0.

The modular function
j(g_{2},g_{3})
associated with the
elliptic curve (
101
) is defined as follows

j(g_{2},g_{3})=\frac{g_{2}^{2}}{g_{2}^{3}-27g_{3}^{2}}.

The algebraic form of the Lamé equation is

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}y}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{2f(x)}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}-\frac{\alpha x+\beta}{f(x)}m=0,

(102)

and is related to the Weierstrass form (
100
) by the transformation
x=\wp(t)
.

Classically, the Lam’e equation is written with parameter
n

instead of
\alpha
which are related by the formula
\alpha=n(n+1)
.

We see that the Lamé equation depends on four parameters
(n,\beta,g_{2},g_{3})
.
The following lemma lists all the cases in which the identity
component of the differential Galois group of Lamé
equation (
100
) is Abelian, see, e.g.,

[
18
, Sec. 2.8.4]
and references therein.

Lemma 6
.

The identity component of the differential Galois group of Lamé
equation (
100
) is Abelian only in the following cases:

1.

the Lamé-Hermite case when
n\in\mathbb{Z}
i.e.
\delta:=n+\frac{1}{2}\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
and three other parameters are arbitrary.

2.

the Brioschi-Halphen-Crowford case for which

\delta:=n+\frac{1}{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, and the remaining parameters
(g_{2},g_{3},\beta)
satisfy an algebraic equation obtained from the condition of vanishing of the so-called Brioschi determinant.

3.

the Baldassarri case for which

\delta:=n+\frac{1}{2}\in\frac{1}{3}\mathbb{Z}\cup\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{Z}\cup\frac{1}{5}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
,
with additional algebraic restrictions on
(g_{2},g_{3},\beta)
.
