Nonmodal amplitude equations
Yves-Marie Ducimetière, François Gallaire

Abstract
We consider fluid flows for which the linearized Navier-Stokes operator is strongly non-normal. The responses of such flows to external perturbations are spanned by a generically very large number of non-orthogonal eigenmodes. They are therefore qualified as “nonmodal” responses, to insist on the inefficiency of the eigenbasis to describe them. In the aim of the article to reduce the system to a lower-dimensional one free of spatial degrees of freedom, (eigen)modal reduction techniques, such as the center manifold, are thus inappropriate precisely because the leading-order dynamics cannot be restricted to a low-dimensional eigensubspace. On the other hand, it is often true that only a small number (we assume only one) of singular modes is sufficient to reconstruct the nonmodal responses at the leading order. By adopting the latter paradigm, we propose a general method to analytically derive a weakly nonlinear amplitude equation for the nonmodal response of a fluid flow to a small harmonic forcing, stochastic forcing, and initial perturbation, respectively. In these last two problems, we assumed a parallel base flow with a spatially monochromatic external excitation. The present approach for deriving nonmodal amplitude equations is simpler than the one we previously proposed, for neither the operator perturbation nor the ensuing compatibility condition is formally necessary. Additionally, it provides an explicit, rigorous treatment of the sub-optimal responses. When applied to the stochastic response, the present method makes it possible to derive an amplitude equation that is substantially easier to solve and interpret than the one we proposed previously. Eventually, the three derived amplitude equations are tested in three distinct, two and three-dimensional flows. For sufficiently small excitation amplitudes, yet up to values large enough for the flow to depart from the linear regime, they can systematically predict the weakly nonlinear modification of the gains as the amplitude of the external excitation is increased. This, at an extremely low numerical cost as compared to fully nonlinear techniques. However, the proposed weakly nonlinear approach, precisely because it condemns the flow spatial structure to be mostly along the leading singular mode, is often too simplistic to predict the occurrence of subcritical transitions as the excitation amplitude is increased to too large values.

I
Introduction: responses to external disturbances

Incompressible fluid flows, governed by the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations, are of considerable phenomenological richness. This includes forming complex spatio-temporal patterns, chaos, turbulence, and many others. Some of these observed phenomena could find elements of explanations by characterizing the linear response, i.e., the response to infinitesimally small disturbances, of the Navier-Stokes equations, which we briefly recall in the following lines. In doing so, we introduce some specific notations that will be used throughout this article. Some fully nonlinear or semi-linear extensions proposed in the literature are also succinctly presented.

I.1
Response to an initial perturbation

Let
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},t)=(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t),p(\mathbf{x},t))^{T}
, with
\mathbf{u}
the velocity perturbation field and
p
the pressure field, designates an infinitesimal perturbation of the flow state around one of its (possibly many) fixed points, whose velocity field we name
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}(\mathbf{x})
in what follows (the knowledge of the corresponding pressure field
P_{\mathrm{e}}(\mathbf{x})
is not found necessary in the sequel). The velocity
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
is sometimes also called “base flow”. The dynamics of
\mathbf{q}
is linear and governed by

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q},

(1)

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{q}_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\\
p_{0}(\mathbf{x})\end{pmatrix},

(2)

with
\mathbf{q}_{0}
the initial condition of the perturbation and where no sustained external forcing is considered for the moment. The linear operator
\mathbf{L}
results from linearizing the Navier-Stokes equations around
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
. The explicit expression for
\mathbf{L}
shall be given later in this article. The operator
\mathbf{B}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&0\end{pmatrix},

(3)

with
\mathbf{I}
the identity operator of the dimension of the velocity field. The presence of the singular operator
\mathbf{B}
in Eq. (
1
) ensues from the fact that the pressure field is not differentiated with respect to time. Indeed, for incompressible flows considered here, the role of the pressure is solely to ensure the instantaneous satisfaction of the divergence-free (incompressibility) condition on the velocity.
The system in Eq. (
1
) is also equipped with appropriate boundary conditions of
\mathbf{q}
on
\partial\Omega
, the latter symbol designating the boundary of the considered spatial domain
\Omega
.

Assuming the system in Eq. (
1
) to be diagonalizable and omitting the possible presence of continuous spectra, it is associated with a family of generically complex eigenmodes. Some of them belong to the non-trivial kernel of
\mathbf{B}
, corresponding to eigenmodes with null velocity but non-null pressure, and thus are associated with infinite eigenvalues. The latter eigenmodes are unimportant for our purposes and will not be considered in what follows. Instead, we denote by
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}=\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{2},...\right\}
the eigenmodes which do not belong to the kernel of
\mathbf{B}
, and thus are associated with finite eigenvalues. By hypothesis, they provide a complete basis for the velocity field, but not for the full state variable consisting of both velocity and pressure, precisely because the ignored eigenmodes have a null velocity field.
Each of these eigenmodes solves by definition

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\mathbf{B}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}=\mathbf{L}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...,

(4)

where the scalar
\sigma_{j}
, finite and generically complex-valued, is the eigenvalue associated with the eigenmode
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}
.

It is instructive to expand the velocity field solution of Eq. (
1
) in the basis formed by the family
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, which by abuse of language we call the eigenbasis. For this purpose, it is necessary to construct another basis, bi-orthogonal to the eigenbasis under some inner product, such as to determine the component of the solution on each of the eigenmodes. The
L^{2}(\Omega)
inner product for generally complex functions on
\Omega
, is commonly considered and is expressed as

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{q}_{a}\middle|\mathbf{q}_{b}\right\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{q}_{a}^{H}\mathbf{q}_{b}\mathrm{d}\Omega,

(5)

with the superscript
H
designating the Hermitian transpose. Following this choice of inner product, it is possible to construct an operator that is said to be “adjoint” to
\mathbf{L}
, denoted by
\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}
, and ensuing from the definition

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}\middle|\mathbf{q}^{{\dagger}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\mathbf{q}\middle|\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{q}^{{\dagger}}\right\rangle,\quad\forall\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}),\mathbf{q}^{{\dagger}}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}).

(6)

The function space
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L})
(resp.
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}})
) is the domain of the operator
\mathbf{L}
(resp.
\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}
) and contains appropriate
boundary and regularity conditions on the direct field
\mathbf{q}
(resp. adjoint field
\mathbf{q}^{{\dagger}}
). Furthermore, it is easy to show that
\mathbf{B}^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{B}
. The eigenmodes of the system adjoint to that in Eq. (
1
), denoted
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
solve

\displaystyle\sigma^{*}_{j}\mathbf{B}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}=\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{{\dagger}}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...

(7)

with the superscript “
*
” standing for the complex conjugation. Again, we have ignored the eigenmodes belonging to the kernel of
\mathbf{B}
. The eigenmodes of the adjoint system,
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, will be referred to as the “adjoint eigenmodes”, as opposed to
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, referred to as the “direct eigenmodes”. It is easy to demonstrate that the direct and adjoint eigenmodes are such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{B}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{k}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle=0,\quad\mbox{if}\quad j\neq k.

(8)

In other terms, as desired, the adjoint and direct eigenmodes form bi-orthogonal bases under the inner product in Eq. (
5
) weighted by
\mathbf{B}
. Note that, as a consequence of this inclusion of
\mathbf{B}
, the bi-orthogonality property of the adjoint and direct eigenmodes applies directly to their respective velocity fields.

From here, by using the bi-orthogonality property, the velocity field solution of Eq. (
1
) can be expanded on the eigenbasis as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{j}t}\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{B}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\mathbf{q}_{0}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\mathbf{B}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right\rangle}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\sigma_{j}t}\frac{\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle},

(9)

where only the temporal dependencies are highlighted. Note that, as expected, the initial condition on the pressure perturbation field,
p_{0}
, does not influence
\mathbf{u}(t)
, while the initial velocity
\mathbf{u}_{0}
does. By measuring the amplitude of
\mathbf{u}(t)
according to the norm induced by the
L^{2}
inner product, which is directly proportional to the kinetic energy of the flow, we can derive from Eq. (
9
) the following expression

\displaystyle||\mathbf{u}(t)||^{2}=\left\langle\mathbf{u}(t)\middle|\mathbf{u}(t)\right\rangle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\mathrm{e}^{2\sigma_{j,r}t}\frac{\left|\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}{\left|\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle\right|^{2}}||\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}||^{2}+\underbrace{\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{k\neq j}\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma_{j}^{*}+\sigma_{k})t}\frac{\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle^{*}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{k}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle^{*}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{k}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle}\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle}_{\parbox{142.26378pt}{$=0$ if $\mathbf{L}$ is normal or if $\mathbf{u}_{0}$ is along an eigenmode.}}

(10)

where the subscript “
r
” in
\sigma_{j,r}
designates the real part. Equation (
10
), which can also be found for instance in Ref.
[
1
]
, is enlightening in many ways, some of which we develop now.

I.1.1
Linear time-asymptotic response

Let us first consider the time asymptotic response for
t\rightarrow\infty
, and distinguish three cases.

•

In this limit
t\rightarrow\infty
, the energy expressed in Eq. (
10
) is dominated by the term associated with the eigenvalue (or the pair of eigenvalues if complex conjugates) with the largest real part, as a consequence of the exponential dependencies. Thereby, if the largest real part is strictly negative, then the perturbation eventually vanishes, i.e.
||\mathbf{u}(t)||^{2}\rightarrow 0
for
t\rightarrow\infty
. Accordingly, the fixed point
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
around which the equations were linearized is said to be “linearly stable”.

•

If, on the contrary, the largest real part is strictly positive, then the perturbation eventually grows exponentially as
t\rightarrow\infty
and
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
is then said to be “linearly unstable”.

•

Eventually, if the largest real part is null, then the present linear theory does not conclude as to the stability of
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
, and the latter is said to be “linearly neutral” (or also “linearly marginal”).

In the rest of this article, whenever the adjectives “stable”, “unstable” or “neutral” are used, they will systematically refer to linear stability analysis.

I.1.2
Linear finite-time response

We emphasize that the discussion above, where conclusions can be drawn from the sole knowledge of the eigenvalues, concerned only the time asymptotic limit
t\rightarrow\infty
. However, we argue now that the finite time behavior might be just as relevant if
\mathbf{L}
is non-normal. The operator
\mathbf{L}
is said to be non-normal if it does not commute with its adjoint, i.e., if

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}\neq\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{L},

(11)

(which can also be caused by the fact that the domains
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}})
differ from each other).
Accordingly, it is said to be normal if
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}=\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{L}
(where the equality also implies that the domains
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}})
are the same), and to be self-adjoint if
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}
. Note that a self-adjoint operator is necessarily normal, but the reciprocal is not true. Important properties concerning the eigenmodes of
\mathbf{L}
arise from this classification. In particular:

•

if
\mathbf{L}
is normal, then each eigenmode is proportional to its adjoint, i.e.
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\propto\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}
(equal up to the phase if normalized the same way) for
j=1,2,...
. Consequently, the relation in Eq. (
8
) implies that the eigenmodes
\left\{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
constitute an orthogonal family under the inner product in Eq. (
5
), with the inclusion of the operator
\mathbf{B}
. An important consequence is that in Eq. (
10
), the double-sum term is necessarily null if
\mathbf{L}
is normal, even when
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is not along the velocity field of one of the eigenmodes. That is because
\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle=0
if
k\neq j
. Therefore, the energy of the perturbation,
||\mathbf{u}(t)||^{2}
, decays monotonously if the largest real part of the eigenvalue is negative, for it reduces to the sum of decaying exponentials pondered by positive coefficients. Moreover,
||\mathbf{u}(t)||^{2}
converges exponentially fast to a single exponential decay (resp. growth) with the rate given by the least stable (resp. most unstable) eigenvalue.

•

If
\mathbf{L}
is non-normal, however, then the eigenmodes do not form an orthogonal set. Accordingly, the double sum term in Eq. (
10
), which involves eigenmode-eigenmode interactions through the inner product
\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle
, has no reason to vanish if
\mathbf{u}_{0}
projects over more than one eigenmode. Even if all the eigenvalues have a negative real part and thus all the exponential terms
\propto\mathrm{e}^{(\sigma_{j}^{*}+\sigma_{k})t}
decrease, their multiplying coefficients can be negative. This implies that the energy
||\mathbf{u}(t)||^{2}
can grow at finite times, as these terms, which contribute negatively to the sum, can decay faster than those that contribute positively. Furthermore, such growth can be shown to be algebraic (see §
4.1.1
in Ref.
[
2
]
). This phenomenon, of a stable system showing an energy increase at finite times, is referred to as “transient growth”. For larger times, the exponential behavior ultimately takes over if the linearization is still valid. Generically, a very large number of eigenmodes are needed to characterize the transient growth, which suggests that these latter form an inefficient basis for the flow dynamics. In other terms, at finite time, the dynamics cannot be reduced to the least stable or most unstable eigenmode. As an example, Ref.
[
3
]
considered the transient growth in the energy of a fluid flow over a separated boundary-layer flow. Using eigenmodes as a projection basis for analyzing the flow dynamics, they have shown that about one hundred modes are required for converged results of optimal growth (please see their Fig. 4a).

Indeed, at least under the inner product in Eq. (
5
), the linearized Navier-Stokes operator generically is non-normal whenever the base flow
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
is non-zero
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
2
,
7
]
. That is because the sign of the term representing the advection of the perturbation by the base flow has been reversed in
\mathbf{L}^{{\dagger}}
with respect to that in
\mathbf{L}
. The direct and adjoint eigenmodes thus present different spatial supports and cannot be equal, for they have been advected in opposite directions. Nonetheless, it is important to realize that, by definition, the degree of non-normality of an operator depends on the choice of the inner product under which the adjoint operator is constructed. For instance, in the context of plane parallel shear flows, it was shown in Ref.
[
8
]
that a certain measure of the flow non-normality differs if the adjoint is constructed under the inner product in Eq. (
5
), or if it is under another inner product inducing the enstrophy norm, i.e., the
L^{2}
norm of the vorticity field.

I.1.3
Linear optimal transient growth

While eigenmodes are sufficient to characterize the time asymptotic dynamics, they typically form an inefficient basis for describing the finite-time dynamics for non-normal systems. Thereby, fluid flow studies are often extended to computing the initial condition that maximizes the gain in energy at a certain time
t_{o}
, called a “temporal horizon”. One of the many benefits of this approach is to directly reveal the full potential of the system for transient growth. This amounts to solving the maximization problem

\displaystyle G(t_{o})

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}\frac{||\mathbf{u}(t_{o})||}{||\mathbf{u}_{0}||}=\max_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}\frac{||\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\mathbf{u}_{0}||}{||\mathbf{u}_{0}||}=||\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)||

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbf{u}_{0}}\sqrt{\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\mathbf{u}_{0}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\mathbf{u}_{0}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle}}.

(12)

In Eq. (
12
), we have introduced the “propagator” operator
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{a},t_{b})
, whose application on the velocity field at time
t=t_{b}
, i.e.
\mathbf{u}(t_{b})
, maps it onto its evolution at time
t=t_{a}
, i.e.
\mathbf{u}(t_{a})=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{a},t_{b})\mathbf{u}(t_{b})
. Specifically, the propagator
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{a},t_{b})
is a synthetic manner to represent the temporal integration of Eq. (
1
) from
t=t_{b}
, knowing
\mathbf{u}(t_{b})
, to
t=t_{a}
, leading to
\mathbf{u}(t_{a})
. Note that writing
\mathbf{u}(t_{a})=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{a},t_{b})\mathbf{u}(t_{b})
implies that the pressure field at time
t_{b}
has no influence on the velocity field at time
t_{a}
. Indeed, as we mentioned previously and as it appears from the dyadic expansion in Eq. (
9
), this is a consequence of the specific expression of the singular operator
\mathbf{B}
.

The solution to the maximization problem in Eq. (
12
) can be found by computing the operator norm of
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
, or, equivalently, the largest eigenvalue of the operator
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
. We denote by
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
the eigenmodes of
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
, each solving by definition

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}=G^{2}_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j},\quad j=1,2,...

(13)

The eigenvalue problem in Eq. (
13
) is self-adjoint, even if
\mathbf{L}
is not, which implies that the eigenvalues
G_{1}>G_{2}>...
, sorted by decreasing magnitude, are real and positive. Each eigenmode
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
is normalized such that
||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}||=1
, for all
j\geq 1
. The maximum transient gain, at time
t=t_{o}
, is directly given by
G(t_{o})=G_{1}
. The associated optimal initial condition corresponds to
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}
. The family of structures
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
correspond to sub-optimal initial conditions. The eigenmode
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
also coincides with the
j
th right “singular” mode of
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
, associated with the singular value
G_{j}
. To be specific, it is possible to write

\displaystyle G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j},\quad G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)^{{\dagger}}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o}),

(14)

where
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})
, also normalized such that
||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})||=1
for
j\geq 1
, is the
j
th left singular mode of
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
. Thereby,
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})
is the normalized response at
t=t_{o}
of the system seeded by
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
. Of central importance, and inherited from the fact that the operator in Eq. (
13
) is self-adjoint, both the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
and the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})\right\}_{j\geq 1}
families are respectively orthonormal. Thereby, some arbitrary initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j},

(15)

leading to a response at
t=t_{o}
described by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t_{o})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\mathbf{u}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o}).

(16)

Equation (
16
) helps understand why amending the paradigm from computing the eigenmodes of
\mathbf{L}
to solving Eq. (
13
), is fruitful. In doing so, we replaced an inefficient, non-orthogonal eigenbasis, with two orthonormal ones into which both the initial condition and the response at
t=t_{o}
can be projected, respectively. Above all, the component of
\mathbf{u}(t_{o})
onto
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})
is directly given by the component of
\mathbf{u}_{0}
onto
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
, multiplied by the associated gain
G_{j}
. This makes it possible to prioritize the respective contribution of each structure
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})
in the response
\mathbf{u}(t_{o})
. For instance, in a configuration where
G(t_{o})=G_{1}\gg G_{2}>G_{3}>...
, i.e. where
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
is close to being of unitary rank, and where an arbitrary initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}
supposedly does not project particularly well on one of the sub-optimal
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
, the response in Eq. (
16
) is well approximated by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(t_{o})\approx\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}\middle|\mathbf{u}_{0}\right\rangle G_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t_{o}).

(17)

This simple result has profound consequences: the structure (not the amplitude) of the response does not depend on the structure of the initial condition but becomes inherent to the propagator itself. Indeed,
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t_{o})
is computed solely from the knowledge of
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
. As soon as a few singular modes
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})
are associated with gains much larger than all the others, these leading structures are expected to dominate that of the response for some times around
t_{o}
, regardless of how the latter was initiated (and ignoring the unlikely initial conditions that are orthogonal to the set of corresponding leading
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
). This implies that it is possible to predict the flow structure even in an uncontrolled environment, where
\mathbf{u}_{0}
can’t be known or prepared. In this perspective, note that the configuration where solely
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t_{o})
has a much larger gain than all the others is particularly favorable.

The maximum transient gain and associated optimal structures are parameterized by the temporal horizon
t_{o}
, and thus need to be computed for each value of the latter. They can be very different depending on
t_{o}
, and the desired property
G_{1}\gg G_{2}>G_{3}>...
generically only holds over a restricted range of intermediate
t_{o}
values.

Solving Eq. (
12
) and computing the associated singular modes is referred to in the literature as a “nonmodal” analysis, where “non-eigenmodal” should be understood. Indeed, the adjective nonmodal emphasizes the difference with a stability analysis, concerned with the computation of eigenmodes.

Due to the non-normality of the linearized Navier-Stokes operator, fluid flows furnish numerous examples of transient growth phenomena (please see Ref.
[
2
]
for a review). To the authors’ knowledge, nonmodal studies have been first carried out in the context of parallel shear flows by Refs.
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
and revealed two non-normal growth mechanisms, Orr and “lift-up”. The adjective “parallel” means that the base flow is invariant in the direction of the main advection.

Non-parallelism of the base flow was shown in Ref.
[
12
]
and later in Refs.
[

,

,

]
to be an additional source of non-normality. This was also formalized in the more general framework of variable-coefficients operators in Ref.
[

]

.
The authors of Ref.
[

]
argued that this additional source of non-normality is related to the presence of a region of space within which the base flow profile is (locally) convectively unstable.
Accordingly, a considerable body of work has been devoted to computing transient growth properties of non-parallel flows. Among them, Refs.
[

,

I.1.4
Nonlinear effects and possible bypass transition

Until now, the developments and conclusions were within the framework of linear dynamics, exact only in the limit of infinitesimal perturbations. However, even if a perturbation is small enough for the linearization to be valid at initial times, precisely because its response can be substantially amplified through linear non-normal mechanisms, the nonlinear interactions of the latter may not remain negligible. This calls for the study of nonlinear effects on transient growth, raising the question of the relevance of linear optimal structures in characterizing nonlinear flow regimes. As we argue below, although nonlinear behaviors might be categorized, there is no generic answer to this question.

The authors of Ref.
[
6
]
argue that nonlinear terms, the amplitude of which is made significant by linear transient growth, can make the flow escape from its original attractor. This can, for instance, happen as the perturbation nonlinearly feeds back onto the base flow, thus modifying it to render it unstable. Such phenomenology, where linear transient growth and nonlinearities act collaboratively and successively to bring about a flow transition, was called the “bypass scenario” in Ref.
[
6
]
. It is nowadays a well-accepted scenario for the nonlinear transition of some linearly stable flows to another state, including a turbulent attractor.

The bypass transition scenario is exemplified in Ref.
[
22
]
and Ref.
[
23
]
, where the linearly stable pipe and plane Poiseuille flows, respectively, were shown to transition to turbulence following a low-amplitude initial perturbation (provided the Reynolds number is sufficiently large). The bypass scenario was also found to be relevant in Refs.
[
24
,
25
]
for the transition of the Lamb-Oseen vortex flow to another state, whose vorticity field shows three poles instead of one. In boundary layer flow, the non-normal “lift-up” mechanism appears to be an essential ingredient for the transition to turbulence
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
, in addition to the role it plays in the so-called self-sustained cycle of turbulence
[
30
,
31

In the bypass scenario presented above, the perturbation is even more amplified in a nonlinear regime than in a purely linear one, but this need not be the case. Nonlinearities can have a “saturating” effect on the energy of the perturbation, inducing a decrease in the transient gain with the amplitude of the initial perturbation. This is, for instance, occurring in the backward-facing step flow at
\mbox{Re}=500
considered in Ref.
[

]
, as visible in Fig.

To systematically assess the effect of nonlinearities on the optimal transient gain and its associated structure, a comprehensive analysis framework has recently been condensed in Ref.
[

Preceding this review article, this method was applied to compute the nonlinear optimal initial perturbations leading to the flow transition to turbulence, in the Blasius boundary layer flow
[

,

, in the plane Couette flow
[

[

and plane

I.2
Response to a harmonic forcing

Until now, we have focused on the response to an initial perturbation. Importantly, some of the reported observations can be easily adapted to the responses to other types of excitations. Among them, the response of a stable system to a harmonic forcing and a stochastic forcing in the momentum equations, respectively, are also of interest in this article. We briefly introduce these two problems in the following lines.

I.2.1
Linear optimal harmonic response

Let a harmonic forcing in momentum have a frequency
\omega_{o}
and a structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}(\mathbf{x})
. Its linear response over a stable system, implying
\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}
not to be an eigenvalue of
\mathbf{L}
, also oscillates at
\omega_{o}
in the time asymptotic regime
t\rightarrow\infty
and reads
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t)\rightarrow\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}
, with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}=\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}}{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}-\sigma_{j}}\frac{\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle}.

(18)

In Eq. (
18
) we have introduced the resolvent operator
\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})
, mapping the forcing structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
, onto the response structure
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
. The latter shall be referred to as the “harmonic response”. The energy
||\mathbf{u}(t)||^{2}
of the response averaged over one oscillation period is proportional to
||\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}||^{2}
. Using the dyadic represention of the resolvent operator in Eq. (
18
), the full expression for
||\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}||^{2}
is easily shown to also involve a double sum term
\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{j\neq k}...\left\langle\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle
as in Eq. (
10
). Thereby it is also true here that, if
\mathbf{L}
is non-normal and thus the eigenmodes are not orthogonal, then the energy of the harmonic response is determined by a generically very large number of eigenmode-eigenmode interactions. Again, the eigenmodes thus form a very inefficient basis, in the sense that the harmonic response is inefficiently described by a single or even a few of them. This is in stark contrast with vibration analysis in structural mechanics, precisely called modal analysis.

For this reason, nonmodal tools are also typically employed to characterize the harmonic response. They typically consist of computing the singular values and associated left and right singular modes of the resolvent operator
\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})
. Again, these two singular modes families both constitute orthonormal bases, into which some unknown forcing structure and its response can be efficiently projected, respectively. Crucially, the respective contributions of the pair of singular modes to the overall harmonic response can also be prioritized according to their associated singular value, or “harmonic gain”.

For these reasons, the computation of the largest singular value, i.e., maximum harmonic gain, of the resolvent operator and associated optimal structures was widely performed in fluid mechanics literature. This is true for both parallel (please see Ref.
[
39
]
for a review) and non-parallel shear flows
[
3
,
21
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
. Large values are often found for the maximum harmonic gain, which, as argued,
is not necessarily related to a specific eigenmodal mechanism but can result from an interaction between a vast amount of eigenmodes. This is, for instance, the case in the mean turbulent jet flow studied in Ref.
[
42
]
. Indeed, their Fig.
4
shows a large harmonic gain of
O(10^{2})
over a range of frequencies for which, in their Fig.
3
, no eigenvalues stand out.

As for the transient gain, large values of harmonic gain in non-parallel flows can sometimes be linked to the presence of a convectively unstable region
[
43
]
. In such cases, there is typically a strong gap between the leading singular value of the resolvent operator and the others
[
44
]
. This was illustrated in Ref.
[
41
]
for the flow past a backward-facing step, as well as in Ref.
[
42
]
and Refs.
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
for incompressible and compressible turbulent jets, respectively. In consequence, the response of the flow to a structurally arbitrary forcing, at a frequency associated with convective mechanisms, will systematically resemble the most amplified response. This is again an appreciable progress: by changing the paradigm from computing eigenmodes to computing singular modes, it is possible to reduce the dynamics of the flow to a single (singular) mode.

I.2.2
Nonlinear extensions

A large harmonic gain over some frequency interval implies the flow to largely amplify any sustained small external disturbance whose Fourier decomposition contains these frequencies. Therefore, as for the transient gain, it also indicates the propensity for the flow to trigger nonlinearities, which could make it transit to another state or regime, for instance, turbulence. This motivated the development of methods to extend the resolvent analysis in nonlinear regimes.

Among them, the “self-consistent” method outlined in Ref.
[
48
]
considered the effect of nonlinearities on the harmonic response to external harmonic forcing, under some simplifying hypothesis.
The velocity field is first split into a mean (in time) component and a fluctuation. The problem is then closed by assuming the fluctuation to be monochromatic and neglecting the effects of all harmonics. Under this hypothesis, the nonlinear fluctuation-fluctuation interaction term does not contribute to the fluctuation itself, as it produces different frequencies, and thus the fluctuation obeys a linear equation. The closed system was then solved iteratively for the flow past a backward-facing step at
\mbox{Re}=500
. It successfully compared with fully nonlinear simulations, and revealed the harmonic gain to decrease with nonlinearities.

The technique advanced in Ref.
[
48
]
, which is equivalent to the idea of harmonic balance, can be generalized by including an arbitrary number of harmonics, as performed in Ref.
[
49
]
. This comes at the cost of augmenting the dimension of the system and making the equation for the fluctuations nonlinear. The number of harmonics to be included for an accurate description of the nonlinear flow is case-dependent, and the truncation is difficult to justify
a priori
. Indeed, contrary to asymptotic expansion methods, the method does not formalize a hierarchical ordering between the amplitude of the harmonics.
Nevertheless, since the method in Ref.
[
49
]
describes a nonlinear harmonic response, it also provides a systematic framework to generalize the problem of maximizing the harmonic gain in nonlinear regimes, in the same way as Ref.
[
7
]
did for the transient growth problem.

As another approach, Refs.
[
50
,
51
]
considered the response of a spatially slowly-varying flow or system to a harmonic forcing localized in space. In the absence of an absolutely unstable region, they have shown that the nonlinear modulation of the linear response, in terms of both amplitude and wavenumber, can be simply deduced by solving a one-dimensional nonlinear spatial dispersion relation, parameterized by the streamwise coordinate. The theoretical framework proposed in Refs.
[
50
,
51
]
comes at a very cheap computation cost with modern techniques and makes possible considerable conceptual progress.

I.3
Response to a stochastic forcing

The question of the flow response to some stochastic forcing is also appraised in this article. Such stochastic forcing can, for instance, be chosen as a sum of uncorrelated white noise processes, each multiplying a frozen spatial structure, as in Ref.
[
52
]
. Again, if
\mathbf{L}
is non-normal, then the response of the system in Eq. (
1
) to such stochastic forcing is generically inefficiently described by eigenmodes. They may all be stable with large damping rates, and yet a very large number of them can combine to bring about a substantial response-to-forcing variance amplification. Thus, here too nonmodal tools are employed to propose an efficient description of the linear flow dynamics. By “efficient”, we mean a description in which only one or a few basis modes are needed.

I.3.1
Linear optimal stochastic response

The theoretical framework for a nonmodal stochastic forcing analysis of the linearized Navier-Stokes equations was introduced in Refs.
[
52
,
53
,
54
]
. Taking the stochastic forcing as white noise processes, the stochastic gains and the associated optimal and sub-optimal forcing and response structures were computed for the plane Poiseuille and Couette flow
[
52
]
. The analysis was also carried out for the Lamb-Oseen vortex flow
[
55
]
and the non-parallel flow past a backward-facing step
[
41
,
56
]
. In all these studies, the stochastic gains have revealed considerable.

I.3.2
Nonlinear extensions

Once again, linear nonmodal analysis of the receptivity to stochastic forcing can be extended to nonlinear flow regimes.

A possible
a priori
approach, named “SSST” for stochastic structural stability theory, was introduced in Ref.
[
57
]
in the context of two-dimensional turbulent jets in a streamwise-periodic domain. The idea of the SSST is also to decompose the flow into a mean flow, averaged in the streamwise direction, and the sum of fluctuations, each with a different streamwise wavenumber.
The fluctuation equations, one for each wavenumber, are forced by a nonlinear convolution term, accounting for the fluctuations-fluctuations interactions generating the considered wavenumber. To close the problem, this nonlinear fluctuations-fluctuations forcing term is modeled by a series of uncorrelated random noise processes which are white in time, with the intensity and the spatial auto-correlation function as free parameters. The equations for the fluctuations thus become linear.
Among many other examples, the SSST system successfully described the interaction between rolls and streaks structure appearing in the transition to turbulence in the three-dimensional Couette flow
[
58
]
.

The fluctuations-fluctuations nonlinear interaction terms in the equation for the fluctuations, instead of being replaced by white noise, are sometimes simply ignored, leading to a class of models called “semi-linear”: nonlinear for the mean, and linear for the fluctuations. The semi-linear approach has been generalized in Ref.
[
59
]
to include large-scale fluctuations-fluctuations interactions, and tested on externally stochastically driven jets over the spherical surface and
\beta
-plane (see Ref.
[
60
]
for a recent review).

The authors of Ref.
[
61
]
considered the effect of nonlinearities on the response of the backward-facing step to an external white noise forcing. Neglecting the fluctuations-fluctuations interaction terms, they arrived a to a coupled system that they solved iteratively, predicting nonlinear stochastic gain in good agreement with fully nonlinear results. Specifically, the stochastic gain significantly decreased while increasing the forcing amplitude.

I.4
In this article: deriving weakly nonlinear amplitude equations for nonmodal responses

This introductory part was relatively exhaustive, for it was concerned with the flow response to three different types of external excitations: an initial perturbation, a harmonic forcing, and a stochastic forcing. Nevertheless, the statements made can be summarized under the same general comments as follows:

•

An operator is non-normal if its application does not commute with that of its adjoint. A central consequence of non-normality is that the eigenmodes are not orthogonal under the inner product with which the adjoint was constructed.

•

Non-orthogonality of the eigenmodes implies that the corresponding induced norm of the linear flow response can take substantial values, determined by interactions between a generically large number of eigenmodes. Eigenmodes thus form an inefficient basis for describing the flow response, at least when the response is observed under this specific induced norm.

•

Instead, nonmodal tools make it possible to construct an orthonormal basis for the structure of the flow excitation. This is true whether the latter is an initial condition, a harmonic forcing, or a stochastic forcing. Furthermore, the respective contribution, to the induced norm of the response, of each element of this basis, can be prioritized.

•

In fluid mechanics, it is often the case that the responses to only a few of these forcing structures dominate the linear response, in terms of this induced norm, a property referred to as the low-rank approximation. In other words, projecting the linear response in the subspace spanned by the few dominant nonmodal responses extracts the leading-order response. This means that, at least in the linear regime, the Navier-Stokes equations can be rigorously reduced to a low-dimensional system of equations for the coordinates within this subspace.

In the present article, we argue that projecting the flow response in the subspace spanned by the few dominant linear nonmodal responses, also extracts the leading-order response in a weakly nonlinear regime. Thereby, we derive a low-dimensional system of equations for the amplitudes of the dominant nonmodal responses, which incorporate the leading-order nonlinearities of the Navier-Stokes equations. They are valid in a regime where the error resulting from neglecting the higher-order nonlinearities is small according to the chosen induced norm. Owing to their simplicity, such nonmodal amplitude equations bring insight into the weakly nonlinear mechanisms that modify the gains as one increases the amplitude of the initial condition, the harmonic forcing, or a stochastic forcing, respectively.

Nevertheless, precisely because of the perturbative nature of the method, where the leading-order is restricted to the low-dimensional dominant nonmodal subspace and/or to a low number of spatial or temporal harmonics, we shall see that the derived amplitude equations can be too simplistic to predict subcritical transitions of the flow.

As mentioned above, nonmodal responses to these three types of excitations are already provided with techniques capable of predicting fully nonlinear or semi-linear effects. Amplitude equations, concerned with the leading-order expression of nonlinear terms, are necessarily less predictive. However, they are easier to solve and interpret. In addition, fully nonlinear techniques highlighted in this introduction were each developed for their respective purposes. It is perhaps difficult to extract a general nonlinear theory linking these methods, whereas a clear analogy exists at the linear level. Instead, the amplitude equations that we derive in this article may each describe the response to a different nature of excitation, and yet, as we shall expose, they all ensue from the same backbone principle.

I.5
Existing model reduction methods

There are already well-established techniques for reducing the Navier-Stokes equations to a low-dimensional system of weakly nonlinear ones for the amplitudes of (linear) eigenmodes. These modal amplitude equations are
a priori
relevant for flows where only one or a few eigenmodes dominate the long-term linear dynamics (and thus, we expect, also the weakly nonlinear one). This is the case, for instance, if
\mathbf{L}
is neutral with a low-dimensional neutral eigenspace, or if
\mathbf{L}
is stable and its spectrum presents a large “spectral gap”. This means that a few eigenvalues have a damping rate much smaller (in amplitude) than all the others. In other terms, there are a few eigenmodes damped much more slowly than all the rest, such that the long-term linear dynamics can be well approximated by its projection into this low-dimensional, slow linear eigenspace. It is then
a priori
relevant to project the weakly nonlinear dynamics into the slow manifold, i.e., to reduce the Navier-Stokes equations to a system of amplitude equations for the coordinates along the slowly decaying eigenmodes. Whether
\mathbf{L}
is neutral or stable, the overall principle is to slave the coordinates along the many quickly damped eigenmodes, to these along the few comparatively slow eigenmodes. From here, it is possible to reconstruct an asymptotic solution of the total nonlinear flow field.

As reviewed in Ref.
[
62
]
, such reduction can be performed by relying on weakly nonlinear multiple-scale expansions. It has been performed for a variety of parallel
[
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
]
and more recently non-parallel
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
flows. The mathematical formalism of the multiple-scale method requires a nonempty neutral or close-to-neutral eigenspace in which the dynamics is projected. The center manifold theory, outlined in Refs.
[
71
,
72
,
73
]
and recently applied, for instance, in Refs.
[
74
,
75
]
, constitutes another technique. Among others, the center manifold theory was employed in Ref.
[
76
]
, concerned with the convection in a horizontal layer rotating about a vertical axis. The authors also demonstrate that the amplitude equation they derived is identical to that obtained by using instead the multiple-scale technique.
Recent developments from Refs.
[
77
,
78
]
, restricted to stable systems, give the mathematical conditions for the existence and uniqueness of a “spectral submanifold” (sometimes abbreviated in “SSM”). A SSM is an invariant manifold that is the smoothest nonlinear continuation of some
a priori
chosen (linear) eigensubspace. It is not a mathematical requirement that the latter must emanate from a slow eigenspace. However, as mentioned in Ref.
[
77
]
, it is in this last configuration that a SSM yields the most physically relevant reduced-order model. The question of how to optimally select the slow or “master” eigenspace onto which to project the dynamics, i.e. the eigenspace whose coordinates constitute the master coordinates to which all the remaining ones are slaved, is addressed in Refs.
[
79
,
80
]
. In the former article, the authors propose to compute the scalar curvature of the SSM in the direction of, say, the
j
th eigenmode and to include the latter in the master eigenspace if such curvature is large. Indeed, a large curvature implies that the
j
th eigenmode is substantially excited by weakly nonlinear interactions, thus, it must be associated with a master coordinate to capture its nonlinear retroaction on other master coordinates. As examples of application, the SSM theory was at the basis of the numerical tools deployed in Ref.
[
81
]
for transitions among exact coherent states in the plane Couette flow. Eventually, the normal form theory
[
71
,
72
]
is another reduction technique amenable to nonlinear dynamical systems. The works in Refs.
[
82
,
83
]
consider extending the multiple scale technique above the instability threshold, where an eigenmode is unstable with a growth rate that does not enter at a perturbative level. As another approach, the Galerkin method is commonly employed to reduce the dimension of the flow dynamics
[
84
,
85
,
86
]
. The idea is to directly project the original equation into a subspace spanned by a certain number of eigenmodes, without prioritizing
a priori
their contributions, e.g., in an asymptotic expansion. Consequently, the number of eigenmodes that should be included for describing accurately the flow dynamics is determined in an
ad-hoc
manner, for the truncation error cannot be quantified according to some small parameter. As a famous example, the Lorentz system results from such a truncated Fourier-Galerkin expansion.

None of these modal reduction techniques is directly relevant to the current problem.
That is because, as we already mentioned, by using the eigenmodes as a projection basis for the nonmodal response of a stable system, a generically considerable number of them is necessary for an accurate description. While for weakly non-parallel flows it is sometimes possible to link the linear nonmodal response to a single convectively unstable eigenmode per streamwise location
[
4
,
41
]
(i.e., to a single ’local’ eigenmode, in opposition with ’global’ eigenmodes, which are these considered throughout this article), the weakly nonlinear extension of such local analysis was shown in Ref.
[
87
]
and Ref.
[
88
]
(Section 2.2.3) to be mostly ill-posed.

We refer again to Ref.
[
3
]
: no spectral gap is visible in their Fig.
2
and, indeed, about a hundred eigenmodes were necessary to reproduce the transient response in their Fig.
4
. Consequently, even the SSM approach is inappropriate here, for the master eigenspace in which to project the dynamics cannot be low-dimensional. Indeed, a system of (at least) one hundred nonlinearly coupled amplitude equations could not be reasonably called a reduced model.
Another practical difficulty that may arise in using reduction methods based on modal quantities is that, for non-normal operators, eigenvalues and associated eigenmodes are excessively sensitive to small perturbations of the latter. Specifically, a
O(\epsilon)
perturbation of a non-normal operator can modify the locations of its eigenvalues over distances substantially larger than
O(\epsilon)
(please see Fig.
15
in Ref.
[
89
]
or Fig.
3
in Ref.
[
90
]
for illustrations). As mentioned in Ref.
[
4
]
, this implies in particular that a small perturbation of the operator can stabilize or destabilize the flow even far from the threshold. That is why a non-normal operator is typically characterized by its pseudospectrum (i.e., a set bounding the displacement of the eigenvalues under an
\epsilon
-perturbation of the operator) rather than by its spectrum, because the latter is generically, largely non-robust. We refer to Ref.
[
16
]
Chapter
2
for an exhaustive survey on the topic.

Instead, we reorient the paradigm from assuming a spectral gap and projecting the weakly nonlinear flow dynamics onto the dominant eigenmodes, to assuming a large gap in the singular values of the response and projecting the weakly nonlinear flow dynamics onto the dominant nonmodal responses. It may be important to mention that a large spectral gap and a large gap in singular values are not mutually exclusive properties. It may be that the former is the only reason for the latter, even for non-normal operators (please see, for instance, Figs.
4
and
7
in Ref.
[
91
]
). In this situation, where the flow exhibits weakly damped eigenvalues responsible for the large gains, our method still applies. Simply, a classical modal amplitude equation is recovered in the limit of vanishing damping rate, as the optimal excitation structure and its response converge to the weakly damped adjoint and direct eigenmodes, respectively, and thus our method does not bring any originality compared to the modal methods introduced above. For that reason, in this article, the discussion and application cases will put the focus on nonmodal responses.

In increasing order of technicality, we will dedicate Sec.
II
to the response to a harmonic forcing, Sec.
III
to the response to a stochastic forcing, and, eventually, Sec.
IV
to the response to an initial perturbation. In these last two sections, to keep the calculations as light as possible, the analysis will be restricted to parallel base flows with spatially monochromatic external excitations (pure harmonic disturbance at a single wavelength).

II
Weakly nonlinear response to a harmonic forcing

II.1
Linear regime

In this section, we propose a method to reduce the Navier-Stokes equations to an amplitude equation for the linearly optimal harmonic response. The operator
\mathbf{L}
is not assumed to possess any close-to-neutral eigenvalue(s), or any spectral gap, but to be stable and non-normal.

The first step is to characterize the linear response by solving

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}+\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{P}^{T}\left(\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}\right),

(19)

where we recall that
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},t)=(\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},t),p(\mathbf{x},t))^{T}
contains both the velocity and the pressure field. Equation (
19
) is subject to a null initial condition, i.e.
\mathbf{q}(\mathbf{x},0)=(\mathbf{0},0)^{T}
, although a precise expression of the latter is unimportant. We have also introduced
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}(\mathbf{x})
as a complex-valued arbitrary forcing structure, and
\omega_{o}
is the associated frequency. In Eq. (
19
), we have defined the projection operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

(20)

such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{u},\quad\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

Specifically, applying
\mathbf{P}
on the state variable removes the pressure field, and the operator
\mathbf{B}
can be written as
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{P}
. Including
\mathbf{P}
in Eq. (
19
) guarantees that the forcing acts as a source of momentum only, and not as a source of mass in the continuity equation. Note that it is also possible to force the pressure field. However, a forcing in pressure can be translated into a forcing in momentum via the incompressibility condition.

Since
\mathbf{L}
is assumed stable
\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}
cannot be one of its eigenvalues and the resolvent operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})=\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}

(22)

exists. The solution of Eq. (
19
) for the velocity fields reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}-\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}+\mbox{c.c.}.

(23)

The second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
23
) must be present to enforce the velocity field to be initially null, but vanishes exponentially with time if
\mathbf{L}
is stable, which we assume in the rest of this section. Thereby, this term represents the transient part of the response. After it fades away, the response relaxes towards the harmonic response
\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}
such that, as we introduced already, in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\mathbf{u}\rightarrow\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}
with

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}=\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}.

(24)

For the reasons advanced in the introductory part, if
\mathbf{L}
is non-normal, then it is particularly relevant to seek the forcing structure leading to the largest possible ratio between the norm induced by the
L^{2}
inner product of the response, divided by that of the forcing. This
L^{2}
inner product will be the only one considered in the present section, and all orthogonality properties are defined relatively to the latter. We aim to solve the maximization problem

\displaystyle G(\omega_{o})

\displaystyle=\max_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}}\frac{||\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}||}{||\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}||}=\max_{\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}}\sqrt{\frac{\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\middle|\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}},

(25)

where
G(\omega_{o})
is the maximum harmonic gain. The eigenmode family of the self-adjoint operator
\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})
is denoted by
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, where each eigenmode
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}
is normalized according to
||\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}||=1
, for all
j\geq 1
. It is associated with the real and strictly positive eigenvalue
G^{2}_{j}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}=G_{j}^{2}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...

(26)

The eigenvalues
\left\{G^{2}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are sorted by decreasing values, and the largest eigenvalue,
G^{2}_{1}
, is equal to the optimal harmonic gain squared. Furthermore, as a consequence of the self-adjoint property of the operator
\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})
, the family
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
forms an orthonormal family of forcing structure. Among them,
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
is the most amplified, i.e., the optimal one, such that the following modes
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
constitute sub-optimal forcing structures. The mode
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}
is also the
j
th right singular mode of
\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})
, associated with the singular value
G_{j}
and with the left singular mode
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
, also normalized as
||\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}||=1
for all
j\geq 1
. Specifically, it is possible to write

\displaystyle G_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}=\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j},\quad G_{j}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}=\mathbf{R}(\omega_{o})^{{\dagger}}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j},\quad j\geq 1.

(27)

This makes
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
the optimal harmonic response and
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
the sub-optimal ones.

Decomposing the harmonic forcing structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
in the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
orthonormal family, then applying the resolvent operator, leads to the following decomposition of the harmonic response

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}=\sum_{j\geq 1}G_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle.

(28)

Importantly, in Eq. (
28
), the contribution of each structure
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
, to
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
, is weighted by the corresponding
G_{j}
.

Through the nonmodal mechanisms mentioned in the introduction, it is consistent to assume the optimal gain
G_{1}
to be large. Furthermore, it is plausible to place ourselves in a configuration where
G_{1}
is much larger than the sub-optimal gains, i.e.,
G_{1}\gg G_{2}>G_{3}>...
(as supported at least by Refs.
[
41
,
44
,
42
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
for nonmodal mechanisms of a convective nature). Note that this supposedly large gap between the first and the subsequent singular values amounts to assuming that the resolvent operator is close to being of unit rank. We show in this section that, under these assumptions, it is possible to reduce the weakly nonlinear dynamics to solely a scalar equation for the amplitude of the optimal response
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
. Due to the assumed large gap between the leading singular value and all others, the amplitude of the weakly nonlinear response along the sub-optimal structures
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
, can be slaved to that for
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
. Quantitatively, the sub-optimal gains are assumed to be either of the order of the square root of the optimal gain, or of order one. The reasons behind these specific scalings will become clear in a moment. Mathematically, we assume that
G_{j}=\gamma_{j}G_{1}^{1/2}
for
N\geq j\geq 2
and
G_{j}=\gamma_{j}
for
j\geq N+1
, with,
\gamma_{j}=O(1)
for
j\geq 2
. The integer
N
is the index of the smallest sub-optimal gain which scales like the square root of the optimal one. It is introduced for formal reasons only, and its precise value has no practical importance. The optimal gain
G_{1}
must be sufficiently large for its square root to be much smaller. Note that this square root scaling of the largest sub-optimals is, for instance, appropriate in the (laminar) flow past the backward-facing step at
\mbox{Re}=500
in Ref.
[
41
]
, as visible in the inset of their Fig.
4
(b). There, the optimal gain was found to be
G_{1}=7453
whereas the first-sub-optimal is
G_{2}=40.36\approx 0.5\sqrt{G_{1}}
. As further examples, this specific scaling also applies to the incompressible jet turbulent mean flow at
\mbox{Re}=10^{3}
studied in Ref.
[
42
]
(please see their Fig.
4
(a) for the gain curves). It is also relevant, at least in a certain region of the parameter space, for the compressible jet turbulent mean flows considered in Ref.
[
47
]
(Figs.
8
and
9
therein), in Ref.
[
45
]
(Fig.
2
therein) and in Ref.
[
46
]
(Fig.
4
therein).

In Eq. (
25
), the notation
\epsilon_{o}
was introduced to designate the inverse of the optimal harmonic gain, i.e.
\epsilon_{o}=1/G_{1}
, and thus
\epsilon_{o}
is small by assumption. In terms of this
\epsilon_{o}
, we have assumed

\displaystyle\left\{G_{1},G_{2},...,G_{N},G_{N+1},...\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}},\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}},...,\frac{\gamma_{N}}{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}},\gamma_{N+1},...\right\},\quad\gamma_{j}=O(1),\quad j\geq 2,

(29)

(and
\gamma_{1}=1
). Using Eq. (
28
), the scalings in Eq. (
29
) imply the linear harmonic response to some forcing structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
to read

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\Biggl(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle+\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\gamma_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j>N}\gamma_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle\Biggl).

(30)

In Eq. (
30
), there is, by assumption, an order separation between the contribution along the optimal response, and that along all of the sub-optimal ones which are at least
\epsilon_{o}^{-1/2}\gg 1
smaller. Thereby, the linear harmonic response
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
is along the optimal one
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
at leading-order even though the arbitrary forcing structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
generically excites all those in the family
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
.

We shall assume the latter assertion to remain true also in a weakly nonlinear regime and then exploit this assumption to reduce there the Navier-Stokes equations to a scalar one for the amplitude of
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
.

II.2
Weakly nonlinear continuation

We now describe the harmonic response in a weakly nonlinear regime. For this, let us first come back to the (nonlinear) Navier-Stokes equations, subjected to a harmonic forcing according to

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}\\
P\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-(\bm{U}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla})\bm{U}+\mbox{Re}^{-1}\Delta\bm{U}-\mathbf{\nabla}P\\
\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\bm{U}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\begin{pmatrix}\phi\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

From now on, the arbitrary forcing structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
is normalized such that
||\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}||^{2}=1
. We have introduced in Eq. (
31
) the free parameter
\phi=O(1)
, such that overall the harmonic forcing amplitude scales like
\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}
(this specific scaling explained in a moment). In a weakly nonlinear regime, we approach the solution with an asymptotic expansion in terms of integer powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\ll 1
, according to

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}\\
P\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}\\
P_{\mathrm{e}}\end{pmatrix}+\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\left[A_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\\
\check{p}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}\right]+\epsilon_{o}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\
\check{p}_{j}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}+\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2}\\
\overline{p}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\left[\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\
\check{p}_{j}\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}+\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{3}\\
\overline{p}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\right]+\underbrace{\sum_{m\geq 4}\epsilon_{o}^{m/2}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}\\
\overline{p}_{m}\end{pmatrix}}_{=O(\epsilon_{o}^{2})},

(32)

where the complex-valued, scalar amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, each
A_{j}=O(1)
, are for the moment unknown. We recall that
(\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}},P_{\mathrm{e}})^{T}
denotes a steady solution of the unforced Navier-Stokes equation.

Coming back to Eq. (
31
), the fact that the harmonic forcing was introduced at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
ensues from the following reasoning. Say that the asymptotic expansion in Eq. (
32
) is made in terms of integer powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{n}
, with
n
some rational number (chosen as
n=1/2
in Eq. (
32
)). The first order of the expansion, containing the optimal response oscillating at the forcing frequency
\omega_{o}
, is thus of order
\epsilon_{o}^{n}
. Consequently, because of the quadratic nature of the nonlinearity in Eq. (
31
), we anticipate that the first nonlinear interaction term oscillating at
\omega_{o}
will arise at order
\epsilon_{o}^{3n}
. Indeed, at order
\epsilon_{o}^{2n}
, the quadratic nonlinear interaction of the first order with itself will only produce harmonics terms oscillating at
\omega_{o}-\omega_{o}=0
and
\pm(\omega_{o}+\omega_{o})=\pm 2\omega_{o}
; consequently, at order
\epsilon_{o}^{3n}
, the nonlinear interaction of the first order with the second will produce terms oscillating at
\pm(2\omega_{o}-\omega_{o})=\pm\omega_{o}
and
\pm(2\omega_{o}+\omega_{o})=\pm 3\omega_{o}
, which this time contains the fundamental frequency
\omega_{o}
. Furthermore, if the optimal response appears at order
\epsilon_{o}^{n}
, then it must result from an external forcing appearing at order
\epsilon_{o}^{n+1}
since the gain in amplitude is of order
\epsilon_{o}^{-1}
. We impose that the external forcing and the leading-order nonlinear interaction term appear in the same order, which leads to solving
3n=n+1
and thus
n=1/2
. This explains both why the expansion in Eq. (
32
) is made in terms of powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
and why the external forcing appears at order
\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}
. Choosing to scale the sub-optimal gains also in terms of powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
in Eq. (
29
) is the consequence of this fact, not the cause. More precisely, it was made so that the contributions of the sub-optimal responses could be ordered rigorously in the expansion in Eq. (
32
), which, as just explained, had to be done in terms of powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
.

We have also introduced in Eq. (
32
) the nonlinearly-induced corrections terms
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}
for
m\geq 2
, the overbar denoting harmonics fields which oscillate at multiples of
\omega_{o}
, but not at
\omega_{o}
. These are generated by nonlinear interactions only and can be described by the Fourier series

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}=\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,0}+\left(\sum_{n\geq 2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n\omega_{o}t}+\mbox{c.c.}\right),\quad m\geq 2,

(33)

which, we insist, does not include the fundamental frequency
\omega_{o}
. In other terms, the equality
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,1}=\mathbf{0}
holds for all
m\geq 2
. This is made on purpose so that the weakly nonlinear harmonic response, say
\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})
for the velocity field, by definition oscillating at
\omega_{o}
only, directly reads

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}.

(34)

without the need to incorporate contributions from the
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}
. Such contributions would necessarily be redundant, since the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family constitutes a complete basis for the velocity field. Thereby,
\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})
can be fully reconstituted solely from the knowledge of the amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
(there are no
O(\epsilon_{o}^{2})
terms in Eq. (
34
)). Owing to the orthonormality property of the family
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, each
A_{j}
immediately corresponds to the amplitude of the (orthogonal) projection of
\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})
onto
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
, rescaled by the appropriate power of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
.

The weakly nonlinear response in Eq. (
34
) is constructed to be very similar to its linear counterpart in Eq. (
30
), rescaled by
\phi\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}
, but where at each order the prefactor multiplying
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
is replaced by the unknown amplitude
A_{j}
, for all
j\geq 1
. This similarity is meant to ensure the (assumed) continuity between the weakly nonlinear and linear regimes. It implies in particular that each amplitude
A_{j}
must thus reduce to
\phi\gamma_{j}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle
in the linear regime, and for all
j\geq 1
(with
\gamma_{1}=1
). In addition, as the forcing amplitude is increased, the amplitudes typically are modified by weakly nonlinear effects and thus depart from their linear values.

We note that it would be more asymptotically rigorous to expand each amplitude according to the power series

\displaystyle A_{j}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\epsilon_{o}^{k/2}A_{j}^{(k)}=A_{j}^{(0)}+\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{j}^{(1)}+\epsilon_{o}A_{j}^{(2)}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}),

(35)

for
j\geq 1
. This way, the contributions to each
A_{j}
can also be ordered asymptotically in Eq. (
32
). However, this would make the calculations heavier while we expect not to change the final equation for
A_{j}
. For the interested reader, evidence of the latter assertion is proposed in Appendix
A
for an
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
truncation (and we expect the results to generalize up to an arbitrarily large one).
Therefore, we shall solve for the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
directly, with each
A_{j}
absorbing its contributions of all orders.

Since the amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are assumed to remain of order one, Eq. (
34
) inherently restricts the weakly nonlinear harmonic response to remain along the (linearly) optimal one,
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
, at leading-order, and with an amplitude
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}
. In addition, Eq. (
34
) includes higher-order correction terms, smaller by a factor of at least
\epsilon_{o}^{-1/2}\gg 1
, along the sub-optimal structures. These correction terms can be already present at a linear level, as they appear in Eq. (
30
), but also, as we shall see in Secs.
V
and
V
, nonlinearly excited.

The velocity field corresponding to the expansion in Eq. (
34
) is sketched in Fig.
1
, in the phase space represented in the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
basis.

Figure 1
:
Schematic representation of the weakly nonlinear harmonic response
\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})
(blue arrows, one for each forcing amplitude) in the phase space, as described by the expansion in Eq. (
34
). The phase space is shown here in the orthonormal basis formed by the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family. The subspace
V_{h}=\mbox{Span}\left\{\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}\right\}
, where the linear response
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
to some arbitrary harmonic forcing
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
is expressed in Eq. (
30
), is represented in red. By hypothesis, the contribution to
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{h}
which is spanned by the sub-optimal responses, i.e., in
V_{1}^{\perp}=\mbox{Span}\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
, is at least
\epsilon_{o}^{-1/2}=\sqrt{G_{1}}\gg 1
times smaller than that along the optimal one
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
. Thereby
V_{h}
makes only a small angle with the subspace
V_{1}=\mbox{Span}\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\right\}
. For vanishing harmonic forcing amplitude, the weakly nonlinear harmonic response must be tangent to
V_{h}
. However, as the forcing amplitude is increased, they become increasingly misaligned (please see the evolution of the blue arrows for increasing forcing amplitude). Consequently, as the forcing amplitude is increased, the locus of the weakly nonlinear responses (blue dashed line) is curved in the phase space. Yet, by continuity, the weakly nonlinear response still makes a small angle with the subspace
V_{1}
, such that its projection on
V_{1}
still gives the leading-order response.

Let us insist on the fact that, for Eq. (
34
) to be a proper description of the harmonic response, the largest sub-optimal gain must be no larger than
O(\epsilon_{o}^{-1/2})
, as expressed in Eq. (
29
). This hypothesis will be systematically verified in all application cases. If there exist one or more sub-optimal gains of the same order as the optimal one, then Eq. (
32
) must be amended so that the amplitude(s) of the corresponding sub-optimal response(s) also appear at leading order, alongside
A_{1}
. This yields no fundamental difficulties, but would make the subsequent calculations heavier and is beyond the scope of this article.

Furthermore, the central assumption that the weakly nonlinear response is along the linear optimal one at leading order, i.e., that the angle between the responses locus (blue dashed line) and the subspace
V_{1}
(black horizontal line) remains small in Fig.
1
, is expected to be valid for sufficiently small forcing amplitude
\phi
(yet up to large enough values for the flow to depart from is purely linear regime). That is in the continuity of the linear regime, where both curves must be tangent. Nevertheless, as we shall see in Sec.
IV.3
and Sec.
III.3
, this assumption may be rapidly put into default as
\phi
increases if the flow experiences a subcritical transition to another state (e.g., a turbulent attractor), for the latter typically exhibits a considerable enrichment both in terms of spatial structure and temporal harmonics. More generally, this assumption becomes quickly false as soon as the weakly nonlinear responses locus in Fig.
1
exhibits a severe curvature in the phase space, for instance, because the nonlinear forcing term favors a specific sub-optimal response over the optimal despite the large gap in the gains. In the latter case, a solution in the spirit of Ref.
[
79
]
could be proposed, and the proper set of optimal and sub-optimal responses to be included at leading-order could be chosen
a priori
, still by gains values, but also by including the sub-optimal structure whose directions are associated with the largest local curvature of the weakly nonlinear responses locus.

Eventually, each pressure field
\check{p}_{j}
introduced in Eq. (
32
) is the one associated with the velocity structure
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\
\check{p}_{j}\end{pmatrix}=G^{-1}_{j}\left(\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j},\quad j\geq 1.

(36)

Before proceeding with the calculations, let us briefly emphasize that specifying a base flow,
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
, and a Reynolds number,
Re
, completely determines
\epsilon_{o}
. Expansion in Eq. (
32
) is then not truly asymptotic, since the small parameter
\epsilon_{o}
is fixed and cannot be chosen arbitrarily small. Only in the limit
\mbox{Re}\rightarrow\infty
does it hold that
\epsilon_{o}\rightarrow 0
, such that Eq. (
32
) only is truly asymptotic in the limit of high Reynolds numbers, provided the scalings in Eq. (
29
) hold. Nevertheless, as we shall demonstrate, for a fixed value of
\epsilon_{o}
the difference between the weakly nonlinear description in Eq. (
32
), and the linear solution, can still be made arbitrarily small in the limit of vanishing forcing
\phi\rightarrow 0
.

Injecting both Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
) in the Navier-Stokes equations (
31
), leads to a new expansion for each of the Fourier components. The first, for the Fourier component oscillating at
0\times\omega_{o}=0
, writes

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\epsilon_{o}\left[\mathbf{L}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}\\
\overline{p}_{2,0}\end{pmatrix}-2|A_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*})\right]+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}),

(37)

where the superscript
``*"
denotes the complex conjugation operation. In Eq. (
37
), we have introduced the nonlinear, bilinear operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{u}_{a},\mathbf{u}_{b})=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\mathbf{u}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla})\mathbf{u}_{b}+(\mathbf{u}_{b}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla})\mathbf{u}_{a}\right].

(38)

Additionally, the application of the linear operator
\mathbf{L}
over some
\left(\mathbf{u}_{a},p_{a}\right)^{T}
is computed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{a}\\
p_{a}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-2\mathbf{C}(\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}},\mathbf{u}_{a})+\mbox{Re}^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{u}_{a}&-\mathbf{\nabla}p_{a}\\
\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{a}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(39)

The expansion for the Fourier component oscillating at
2\omega_{o}
reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}\left[-\left(2\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}\\
\overline{p}_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}-A_{1}^{2}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1})\right]

\displaystyle+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}).

(40)

The expansions for the even higher harmonics are not written down explicitly, as they will not be necessary for the following. In Eq. (
37
) (resp., Eq. (
40
)), each
O(\epsilon_{o}^{m/2})
is associated with its own unknown field
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,0}
(resp.,
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,2}
). Thereby, to have as many equations as unknown fields, the terms are collected at each specific order. In particular, collecting terms at
O(\epsilon_{o})
leads to

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}=|A_{1}|^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}},\quad\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}=-2\mathbf{R}(0)\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}),

(41)

together with

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}=A_{1}^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}},\quad\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}}=-\mathbf{R}(2\omega_{o})\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}).

(42)

for the zero and second harmonics velocity fields, respectively. Let us insist that the superscripts
A_{1}^{2}
and
|A_{1}|^{2}
are not exponents but simply notations evoking the prefactors of the fields. We recall that the resolvent operator is such that
\mathbf{R}(z)=\mathbf{P}\left(\mathrm{i}z\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}
. Note that, to guarantee that the asymptotic expansion is well-posed, we implicitly assume both
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}
and
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}
to have an
O(1)
norm. This way, these two fields indeed remain at
O(\epsilon_{o})
and do not need to the rescaled to appear at higher orders.

The expansion for the Fourier component oscillating at
\omega_{o}
is

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\left[-A_{1}\left(\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\\
\check{p}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right]+\epsilon_{o}\left[-\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\left(\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\
\check{p}_{j}\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\left[-\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\left(\mathrm{i}\omega_{o}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\
\check{p}_{j}\end{pmatrix}-2\mathbf{P}^{T}\left(A_{1}\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0})+A_{1}^{*}\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2})\right)+\phi\mathbf{P}^{T}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right]+O(\epsilon_{o}^{2}).

(43)

Eq. (
43
) can be simplified by remembering the definition in Eq. (
36
), and becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\left[-A_{1}G^{-1}_{1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\right]+\epsilon_{o}\left[-\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}G^{-1}_{j}\mathbf{P}^{T}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\Biggl[-\sum_{j>N}A_{j}G^{-1}_{j}\mathbf{P}^{T}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}-2\mathbf{P}^{T}\left(A_{1}\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0})+A_{1}^{*}\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2})\right)+\phi\mathbf{P}^{T}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\Biggl]+O(\epsilon_{o}^{2}).

(44)

By then using scalings of the gains in Eq. (
29
), as well as Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
), and eventually applying the operator
\mathbf{P}
to project in the velocity space, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\Biggl[-A_{1}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}-\sum_{j\geq 2}\gamma^{-1}_{j}A_{j}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}-A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}\left(2\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}})+2\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}})\right)+\phi\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\Biggl]+O(\epsilon_{o}^{2}),

(45)

where the terms pre-multiplied by the gains at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
and
O(\epsilon_{o})
in Eq. (
44
) are eventually appearing at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
in Eq. (
45
).

By construction, the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are the only unknowns in Eq. (
45
) (all orders considered). This implies that the terms at each order should not be collected in Eq. (
45
), for this would lead to as many equations (the term at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
would need to be equal to zero, but also the term at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{2})
, and also that at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{5/2})
, etc…) whose solutions each fix all the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
once for all. In other terms, unlike the expansions for the harmonics fields, Eq. (
45
) must here be solved all at once and not order-by-order, or the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are over-determined. The leading-order equation for each
A_{j}
can be obtained by projecting Eq. (
45
) over
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}
and invoking on the orthonormality property of the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family. In particular, taking the inner product with
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
leads to the following equation for
A_{1}
,

\displaystyle\boxed{0=-A_{1}-A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}(\mu+\nu)+\phi\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}),

(46)

where we have defined the coefficients

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|2\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}})\right\rangle,

(47a)

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|2\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}})\right\rangle.

(47b)

We obtain similarly for each
A_{j}
with
j\geq 2
the equation

\displaystyle-\gamma^{-1}_{j}A_{j}-A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|2\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}})+2\mathbf{C}(\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\phi\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})=0.

(48)

We note that projecting Eq. (
45
) into the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
basis, specifically, has the advantage of, for each
j
, removing the contributions from the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1,k\neq j}
. In Eq. (
46
) and Eq. (
48
), the terms at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
embed higher-order nonlinearities.

Eq. (
46
) for
A_{1}
is similar to that derived in Ref.
[
90
]
, in the steady regime (please see Eq. (2.12) therein, where the notations
A
,
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{o}
,
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{o}
,
\mathbf{u}_{2,0}
and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}
correspond in the present paper to
A_{1}
,
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
,
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
,
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}
and
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}}
, respectively). However, in Ref.
[
90
]
, this equation was derived differently. It required perturbing the inverse resolvent operator to make it singular, then encompassing such operator perturbation in a multiple-scale asymptotic expansion closed by a standard compatibility condition. Although the development in Ref.
[
90
]
has the advantage of linking with classical multiple-scale developments, in the present approach, neither the operator perturbation nor the compatibility condition was formally necessary. Furthermore, while it was stated without proof in Ref.
[
90
]
that Eq. (
46
) is valid on condition that the sub-optimal gains are
O(\epsilon_{o}^{-1/2})
, the present approach gives the mathematical reason behind this, as it proposes a rigorous treatment of the sub-optimal responses.

As expected, Eq. (
46
) reduces to the linear solution
A_{1}=\phi\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle
in the limit of vanishing forcing amplitude
\phi\rightarrow 0
. The first nonlinear term
A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}\mu
takes into account the fact that the nonlinear interaction of the optimal response
A_{1}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
with its complex conjugate yields a non-zero temporal average
|A_{1}|^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}
, which in turn retro-act on
A_{1}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
thus modifying its amplitude as compared to the linear regime. The second nonlinear term
A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}\nu
takes into consideration that the nonlinear interaction of the optimal response
A_{1}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
with itself produces a second harmonic field
A_{1}^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}
, whose retro-action on
A_{1}^{*}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}
also modifies the amplitude of
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
.

It is in principle possible to introduce a dynamical term
\propto\mathrm{d}A_{1}/\mathrm{d}t
in Eq. (
46
), which could characterize some transient regime before reaching the steady equilibrium solution. For this,
A_{1}
must be made dependent on a slow time scale according to
A_{1}=A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}t)
, and thereby the temporal derivative in the Navier-Stokes equations together with the chain rule derivative will naturally make appear a temporal derivative of
A_{1}
in Eq. (
46
). This generalization was not performed in the calculation above because (i) we are interested in the harmonic gain which is defined on the steady amplitude, and (ii) the transient evolution of
A_{1}
would still only give the transient evolution of the component oscillating at
\omega_{o}
whereas we understand from the transient term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
23
), that the latter may involve many different instantaneous frequencies.

The equations for the sub-optimal amplitude in Eq. (
48
) are linear in
A_{j}
at leading-order (i.e., by neglecting the
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
term). They translate the fact that the sub-optimal response
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
is excited by the component on
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}
onto (i) the externally applied forcing structure
\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}
, and (ii) the third-order nonlinear interaction term
\propto A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}
, involving the optimal structure only. The solution
A_{j}=\gamma_{j}\phi\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}\right\rangle
is also consistently recovered in the limit of vanishing forcing amplitude. Interestingly, at leading-order, solving for the sub-optimal amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
requires the knowledge of the optimal one
A_{1}
, whereas the reciprocal is not true and
A_{1}
is determined by solving Eq. (
48
) only. Such a slaving of the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
to
A_{1}
is a direct consequence of the assumed large singular value gap. The latter results in the asymptotic ordering in Eq. (
32
), stipulating that the sub-optimal responses appear one order below the optimal one in terms of the expansion parameter.

The harmonic gain associated with
\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})
, the component of the weakly nonlinear solution oscillating at
\omega_{o}
and expressed in Eq. (
34
), reads

\displaystyle G(\omega_{o})

\displaystyle=\frac{||\hat{\bm{U}}(\omega_{o})||}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\phi||\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}||}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\epsilon_{o}|A_{1}|^{2}+\epsilon_{o}^{2}\sum_{N\geq j\geq 2}|A_{j}|^{2}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3})\right)^{1/2}}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}|A_{1}|(1+O(\epsilon_{o}))}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A_{1}|}{\epsilon_{o}\phi}(1+O(\epsilon_{o})).

(49)

Between the first and the second line in Eq. (
49
), we have used the orthonormality property of the family
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, together with the normalization
||\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{h}||=1
. From Eq. (
49
), the harmonic gain is given at leading order solely by a rescaled value of
A_{1}
, which incorporates leading-order nonlinear effects as
\phi
is increased towards
O(1)
values.

Eventually, we note that the nonlinear corrections fields
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{A_{1}^{2}}_{2,2}
and
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}_{2,0}
have prefactors quadratic in
A_{1}
. Thereby, since
A_{1}
becomes proportional to
\phi
in the linear limit
\phi\rightarrow 0
, these two fields can indeed be made arbitrarily smaller than the linear solution by decreasing the forcing amplitude. It can be shown that the latter fact is also true for nonlinear correction terms of higher orders.

III
Weakly nonlinear response to a stochastic forcing

In this second section, we subject the Navier-Stokes equations to a weak stochastic forcing. These latter equations are then reduced to a scalar one for the amplitude of the dominant component, optimal under some pre-selected gain measure, of the overall stochastic response. Again, this scalar amplitude equation incorporates leading-order nonlinearities, becoming increasingly important as the forcing intensity is increased. The general idea is the same as in the previous section, as it proceeds according to the projection of an asymptotic expansion in the subspace spanned by the dominant response. An implementation difference lies in the fact that the stochastic nature of the forcing and its response presently suggests considering a more general inner product. The latter here induces the variance norm, which includes a temporal and an ensemble average.

Let us stress that the present section, and the next one, are destined for readers specifically interested in the implementation details of the method when applied to stochastic forcing and transient growth problems, respectively. On the contrary, readers only interested in the global principle of the method may satisfy themselves with the developments presented in the previous section, and could directly jump to the conclusion of this long article for a summary.

III.1
Linear regime

Anticipating the application case which we will consider in a moment, the base flow
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
chosen in this section is a three-dimensional plane channel flow, bounded in the
y
(“crosswise”) direction, but invariant in the
x
(“streamwise”) and
z
(“spanwise”) directions. Furthermore, the chosen external stochastic forcing will act on only one wavenumber
\alpha
in the streamwise direction, and on only one wavenumber
\beta
in the spanwise one. Specifically, the stochastic forcing reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{s}(x,y,z,t;\theta)=\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}(y)\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.},

(50)

where the complex-valued field
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}(y)
is an arbitrary spatial structure, generically complex-valued. In the rest of this section, the prime accent emphasizes that the field is the spatial Fourier component associated with the
(\alpha,\beta)
wavenumber pair. We have introduced the random (parametric) argument
\theta\in\Theta
, with
\Theta
the sample space, i.e., the set of all possible elementary outcomes. The sample space
\Theta
is associated with a given probability measure. To each outcome
\theta
, occurring with a certain probability given by the probability measure, is assigned a scalar and complex-valued function of time,
\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)
, according to a given rule. Such a particular
\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)
constitutes a “realization” of what is called in the following a “stochastic process”, thus defined as the collection of all realizations (one for each outcome
\theta
). Alternatively, for a fixed
t
, the scalar
\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)
can be seen as a classical random variable (we refer to Ref.
[
92
]
Chapter
2.1
for a more elaborate definition of a stochastic process). The stochastic process
\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)
is typically chosen as a colored (including white) noise.

Owing to the invariance of the base flow along the
x
and
z
direction, the linear stochastic response
\mathbf{q}
will also oscillate only along the
(\alpha,\beta)
wavenumber pair. This way, it is possible to write

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(x,y,z,t;\theta)=\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(y,t;\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.},

(51)

and the field
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(y,t;\theta)
obeys the linear equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}+\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\xi^{\prime}.

(52)

The action of the linear operator
\mathbf{L}_{n}
over some
\left(\mathbf{u}_{a},p_{a}\right)^{T}
is computed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{n}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{a}\\
p_{a}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{C}_{n}(\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}},\mathbf{u}_{a})-\mathbf{C}_{0}(\mathbf{u}_{a},\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}})+\mbox{Re}^{-1}\Delta_{n}\mathbf{u}_{a}&-\mathbf{\nabla}_{n}p_{a}\\
\mathbf{\nabla}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}_{a}&0\end{pmatrix}

(53)

together with
\Delta_{n}=(n\alpha)^{2}+\partial_{yy}+(n\beta)^{2}
, as well as
\mathbf{\nabla}_{n}=\left(\mathrm{i}n\alpha,\partial_{y},\mathrm{i}n\beta\right)^{T}
and
n
an integer. The nonlinear, bilinear operator
\mathbf{C}_{n}
was also defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{a},\mathbf{u}_{b})=(\mathbf{u}_{a}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla}_{n})\mathbf{u}_{b},

(54)

The operator
\mathbf{C}_{n}(\mathbf{u}_{a},\mathbf{u}_{b})
computes the transport, by the velocity field
\mathbf{u}_{a}
, of the velocity field
\mathbf{u}_{b}
.

Note that the stochastic forcing in Eq. (
50
), to which we subject the flow, is far from being general. That is first because, as said, it only acts over a single wavenumber pair
(\alpha,\beta)
. This constitutes a loss of generality in the nonlinear flow regime where the responses over different pair values cannot just be summed, but interact. Existing literature on parallel shear flows provides much evidence that such nonlinear interactions (or energy transfers) between different wavenumber pairs are an essential feature of both the transition and the self-sustaining some fully nonlinear states
[
22
,
34
,
33
,
49
]
. In addition, the forcing consists of a scalar noise process depending solely on time, multiplying a structure that is frozen in space. We refer to Ref.
[
93
]
(Eq.
(3)
therein) for an example of a more general stochastic forcing to which the flow can be subjected.

Due to the stochastic nature of the present problem, the ensemble averaging operation over all realizations, or “expected value”, denoted
\mathbb{E}\left[\bullet\right]
, is further introduced. It is important to realize that the expected value is a deterministic quantity and in this no longer depends on
\theta
. The temporal averaging operation over a finite-time signal of length
T\rightarrow\infty
, denoted
\overline{\bullet}
with

\displaystyle\overline{\bullet}=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\bullet\ \mathrm{d}t,

(55)

is also introduced, as well as the finite-time Fourier transform and its inverse

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}[\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\int_{0}^{T}\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)e^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}\mathrm{d}t,

(56a)

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)]=\frac{\sqrt{T}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\mathrm{d}\omega,

(56b)

respectively. We verify that
\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}[\xi^{\prime}]]=\xi^{\prime}
as well as
\mathcal{F}[\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\hat{\xi}]]=\hat{\xi}
, if we adopt the following interpretation of the Dirac impulse
\delta

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega(t-b)}\mathrm{d}\omega=2\pi\delta(t-b),

(57a)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}t(b-\omega)}\mathrm{d}t=2\pi\delta(b-\omega),

(57b)

where
\delta(x)
has a diverging amplitude if
x=0
, and is null otherwise.

Let us insist that the Fourier transform of the noise process,
\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)
, is known
a priori
. It is associated with a power spectral density computed as
\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}\right]
. An important quantity associated with the noise is its overall intensity, named
\Gamma
, which is equal to the zero-frequency part of its power spectral density, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Gamma=\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(0;\theta)|^{2}\right].

(58)

For instance, in case of white noise,
\Gamma
corresponds to the constant value of
\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}\right]
over the frequencies.

The base flow is again assumed to be stable, such that the transients ensuing from switching on the forcing eventually fade away in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
. In the statistically steady regime, Eq. (
52
) can be solved in the Fourier domain for the velocity field
\hat{\mathbf{u}}(y,\omega;\theta)=\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(y,t;\theta)]
according to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}(y,\omega;\theta)=\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}(y),

(59)

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{n}(\omega)=\mathbf{P}(\mathrm{i}\omega\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}.

(60)

If
\mathbf{L}_{1}
is non-normal, then in line with the previous approach concerning the harmonic response, for a given stochastic process
\xi^{\prime}
, it is relevant to construct an orthonormal basis of forcing structure onto which an arbitrary structure
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}
can be projected. These structures are then ranked according to their contributions to some response-to-forcing amplification measure. As a consequence of the stochastic nature of the forcing prescribed in Eq. (
52
), it is relevant to measure the response under the mean square norm of the velocity, which we call “variance” in the following by abuse of language. We mathematically define the variance as being the norm
||\bullet||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}
induced by the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
.
The subscript “
v
” stands for “variance” whereas the subscript “
\mathcal{T}
” emphasizes the inner product is applied to time-dependent fields, i.e., applies in the temporal domain (in opposition with that applying in the frequency domain, defined in a moment). Specifically, this inner product measures the resemblance between two stochastic velocity fields, say
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{a}(t;\theta)
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{b}(t;\theta)
, according to

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{a}(t;\theta)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{b}(t;\theta)\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\overline{\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{a}(t;\theta)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{b}(t;\theta)\right\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{a}(t;\theta)^{H}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{b}(t;\theta)\mathrm{d}\Omega\mathrm{d}t\right].

(61)

This inner product takes the expected value of the temporal average of the
L^{2}
inner product between two fields. Even if it is no longer mentioned explicitly from Eq. (
61
), the only spatial coordinate over which the fields depend is the
y
coordinate, thereby the
L^{2}
inner product only integrates over the latter.

We can also define the equivalent inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}
, inducing the same variance norm but applying on the Fourier transforms (hence the subscript “
\mathcal{F}
”) as

\displaystyle\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}(\omega;\theta)\middle|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{b}(\omega;\theta)\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}(\omega;\theta)\middle|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{b}(\omega;\theta)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\omega\right],

(62)

and which is such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{a}(t;\theta)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{b}(t;\theta)\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{T}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}(\omega;\theta)\middle|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{b}(s;\theta)\right\rangle\overline{e^{\mathrm{i}t(s-\omega)}}\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}(\omega;\theta)\middle|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{b}(s;\theta)\right\rangle\delta(s-\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}s\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{a}(\omega;\theta)\middle|\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{b}(\omega;\theta)\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}.

(63)

In other words, computing
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
between two fields in the temporal domain is equivalent to computing
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}
between their Fourier transforms. In Eq. (
63
), the interpretation of the Dirac impulse in Eq. (
57a
) was used, and the transformation from integrating over time to integrating over the frequencies amounts to using Parseval’s theorem.

Optimizing over the forcing structure
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}
, the maximum ratio between the variance of the response, i.e.
||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}=\left\langle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
, divided by the forcing intensity, i.e.
\Gamma||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||^{2}
, attainable by the system is defined as

\displaystyle G^{2}_{(\alpha,\beta)}=\max_{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}}\frac{||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t;\theta)||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}}{\Gamma||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||^{2}}=\max_{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\overline{||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t;\theta)||^{2}}\right]}{\Gamma||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||^{2}}.

(64)

This problem is similar to that in Eq. (
25
), except that the response is here measured under an induced norm
||\bullet||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}
, which is more general and more suited to the nature of the present problem, than simply taking the
L^{2}
of a specific Fourier component. We insist that the variance of the response is divided by the intensity of the forcing, instead of also its variance (the latter being
||\xi^{\prime}||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||^{2}
and not just
\Gamma||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||^{2}
), for
||\xi^{\prime}||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}
is possibly extremely large and diverges in case of a white noise (which would give a zero stochastic gain). The variance of the response, however, remains finite even in case of white noise excitation, as the large frequencies are naturally damped thanks to the presence of the temporal derivative in Eq. (
52
).

By using Eq. (
59
) the variance of the response,
||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t;\theta)||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}=||\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\omega;\theta)||_{v,\mathcal{F}}^{2}
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle||\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\omega;\theta)||_{v,\mathcal{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}\right]\left\langle\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\middle|\mathbf{R}_{1}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\omega

\displaystyle=\Gamma\left\langle\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\middle|\mathbf{B}^{\infty}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle,

(65)

and where the self-adjoint, strictly positive definite operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{2\pi\Gamma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}\right]\mathbf{R}_{1}^{{\dagger}}(\omega)\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega,

(66)

was defined. The superscript “
\infty
” emphasizes the statistically steady nature of the operator. Note that if
\xi^{\prime}
is chosen as a white noise process, then
\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}\right]=\Gamma
for all frequencies, and the operator
\mathbf{B}^{\infty}
in Eq. (
66
) is equal to that of the same name derived in Refs.
[
52
,
54
]
. Eventually, by using Eq. (
65
), the stochastic gain associated with the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
becomes

\displaystyle G^{2}_{(\alpha,\beta)}=\max_{\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}}\frac{\left\langle\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\middle|\mathbf{B}^{\infty}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}^{2}},

(67)

where we have also defined
\epsilon_{o}=1/G_{(\alpha,\beta)}
as its inverse.

The family of optimal and sub-optimal stochastic forcing structures, generically complex-valued, are then found as the eigenmodes
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
of the operator
\mathbf{B}^{\infty}
. Each
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}
is normalized as
||\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}||=1
and associated with a real, strictly positive eigenvalue
G^{2}_{j}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\infty}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}=G^{2}_{j}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j},\quad j=1,2,...

(68)

The eigenvalues
\left\{G^{2}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are again sorted by decreasing value, and the stochastic forcing structure
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
leads to the largest gain
G_{1}=G_{(\alpha,\beta)}
. The remaining modes
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
constitute sub-optimal stochastic forcing structures. Ensuing from the fact that
\mathbf{B}^{\infty}
is a self-adjoint operator, the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family is orthonormal under the
L^{2}
inner product, i.e.
\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\check{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\right\rangle=\delta_{ij}
(the Kronecker delta symbol). In what follows, without loss of generality let us consider a noise process with a unit intensity, i.e.,
\Gamma=1
.

We further define
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)
as the normalized linear response, in the Fourier domain, to the forcing
\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}
, according to

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)=G_{j}^{-1}\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j},\quad j\geq 1,

(69)

and its equivalent
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)
in the temporal domain

\displaystyle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)].

(70)

This for instance makes
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(\omega;\theta)
the normalized response to the optimal stochastic forcing structure (also called “optimal response” in what follows by abuse of language), as well as
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 2}
the normalized responses to the sub-optimal ones. The prefactor
G_{j}^{-1}
in Eq. (
69
) ensures that each
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)
possesses a unit variance. Indeed, computing the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}
between two responses
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)
and
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(\omega;\theta)
leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\check{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{j}^{-1}G_{k}^{-1}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}\right]\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)^{{\dagger}}\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\check{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\omega

\displaystyle=G_{j}^{-1}G_{k}^{-1}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{B}^{\infty}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=G_{j}^{-1}G_{k}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\check{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{jk},

(71)

which is equal to one if
j=k
and zero otherwise (and where we have used the definition in Eq. (
66
) with
\Gamma=1
). Thereby, the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family of responses is orthonormal under the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}
(although it generically is not under the
L^{2}
inner product). By definition, this implies the temporal equivalent
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
to be orthonormal under the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
.

Decomposing the forcing structure
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}
in the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family, then applying
\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)
, leads to the rewriting of the linear stochastic response in Eq. (
59
) according to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\omega;\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle G_{j}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta).

(72)

From now on, we assume the optimal gain
G_{1}=1/\epsilon_{o}
to be large and thereby
\epsilon_{o}
, its inverse, to be very small. Furthermore,
G_{1}
is also assumed to be much larger than the sub-optimal ones, i.e.
G_{1}\gg G_{2}>G_{3}>...
. More quantitatively, the same specific scaling as in Eq. (
29
) is assumed, which we recall to be

\displaystyle\left\{G_{1},G_{2},...,G_{N},G_{N+1},...\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}},\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}},...,\frac{\gamma_{N}}{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}},\gamma_{N+1},...\right\},\quad\gamma_{j}=O(1),\quad j\geq 2.

(73)

The scalings in Eq. (
73
), together with the expression in Eq. (
72
), implies the linear stochastic response to rewrite

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\omega;\theta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\Biggl[\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(\omega;\theta)+\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j>N}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\Biggl].

(74)

Equation (
74
) indicates that the stochastic response to an arbitrary structure
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}
, is dominated by the response
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(\omega;\theta)
to the optimal forcing structure
\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
, complemented by a smaller rest. This rest is contained in the orthogonal subspace spanned by the responses to the sub-optimal forcing structures
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 2}
. It is in this sense in perfect analogy with Eq. (
30
) for the harmonic response, again at the difference that the orthogonality property here refers to the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}
(or
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
in the temporal domain) and not simply the
L^{2}
one.

III.2
Weakly nonlinear continuation

Referring again to Fig.
1
but keeping in mind this change in the inner product, we now describe the stochastic response in a weakly nonlinear regime. The fully nonlinear Navier-Stokes equations, subjected to a small stochastic forcing under the form given in Eq. (
50
), read

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}\\
P\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-(\bm{U}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla})\bm{U}+\mbox{Re}^{-1}\Delta\bm{U}-\mathbf{\nabla}P\\
\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\bm{U}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\begin{pmatrix}\phi\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\xi^{\prime}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

(75)

Let us normalize
\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}
according to
||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||^{2}=1
, such that the forcing intensity in Eq. (
75
) directly is
\phi\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}
(please remember that we have chosen
\Gamma=1
). In other terms, the free scalar parameter
\phi=O(1)
appears as the intensity of the stochastic forcing when rescaled by
\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}
. We insist that the prescribed stochastic forcing can contain infinitely many temporal frequencies but only acts on the
(\alpha,\beta)
-wavenumber pair in space. This assumption is made to lighten the calculations, as it precludes the apparition of terms corresponding to the interaction between different wavenumber pairs. However, the proposed method would also apply to more involved forcing terms.

We again seek a solution
\left(\bm{U},P\right)^{T}(t;\theta)
in the continuity of the linear one in Eq. (
74
), implying, for the fundamental component, order separations in terms of powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
between the leading contribution of
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(\omega;\theta)
, and these of the sub-optimal responses. Practically, in the statistically steady regime,
\left(\bm{U},P\right)^{T}(t;\theta)
is sought under the form of an asymptotic expansion in terms of integer powers of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
according to

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}(t;\theta)\\
P(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}\\
P_{\mathrm{e}}\end{pmatrix}+\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\left[A_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t;\theta)\\
\breve{p}_{1}(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right]+\epsilon_{o}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\\
\breve{p}_{j}(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}+\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2}(t;\theta)\\
\overline{p}_{2}(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\left[\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\\
\breve{p}_{j}(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}+\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{3}(t;\theta)\\
\overline{p}_{3}(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}\right]+\underbrace{\sum_{m\geq 4}\epsilon_{o}^{m/2}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}(t;\theta)\\
\overline{p}_{m}(t;\theta)\end{pmatrix}}_{=O\left(\epsilon_{o}^{2}\right)}.

(76)

The complex-valued, deterministic, constant scalar amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, where
A_{j}=O(1)
for all
j\geq 1
, are the unknowns of interest. In Eq. (
76
) we have introduced the nonlinearly-induced small (they are multiplied by
\epsilon_{o}^{m/2}
) corrections terms
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}(t;\theta)
for
m\geq 2
. Each is described as a Fourier series in space

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}(t;\theta)=

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,0}(t;\theta)

\displaystyle+\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,n}(t;\theta)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right),

(77)

for
m\geq 2
which, unlike in Eq. (
33
), this time must include a contribution from the fundamental wavenumber pair
(\alpha,\beta)
at
n=1
. That is because the family
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
contains as many members as the number of spatial degrees of freedom. Thereby, a linear combination of the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, weighed by the amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
that depends neither on time nor on the stochastic argument, cannot describe any kind of temporal and stochastic variations. By construction, only is such a decomposition complete when describing the response to a specific forcing of the form
\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)
times an arbitrary spatial structure (as expressed in Eq. (
72
)). However, the higher-order nonlinear interactions forcing terms have no reason to be written under this specific form. Thereby, the description of the fundamental field must be completed at a nonlinear level, to account for additional sorts of stochastic and temporal variations that do not belong to the span of
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
(by “span” we mean all linear combinations with constant coefficients). From Eq. (
77
), the component of
\bm{U}(t;\theta)
in Eq. (
76
) which oscillates in space along
(\alpha,\beta)
, say
\bm{U}^{\prime}(t;\theta)
, is then

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\prime}(t;\theta)=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t;\theta)

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,1}(t;\theta)+\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right)

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\left(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{3,1}(t;\theta)+\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right)

\displaystyle+O\left(\epsilon_{o}^{2}\right).

(78)

Nevertheless, in order to avoid redundancy in the description of
\bm{U}^{\prime}(t;\theta)
in Eq. (
78
), for each
m\geq 2
we exclude
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,1}(t;\theta)
from the subspace spanned by the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family. Specifically, for each
m\geq 2
, we impose
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,1}(t;\theta)
to be orthogonal to all the members of the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family as

\displaystyle\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\middle|\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,1}(t;\theta)\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}=0,\quad j\geq 1,m\geq 2

(79)

and rename
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,1}=\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{m,1}
to emphasize this important orthogonality property. This way, the part of the weakly nonlinear response
\bm{U}^{\prime}(t;\theta)
which is spanned by the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family (complete at linear level), can be reconstituted solely from the knowledge of the amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
. The
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{m,1}
contributions, for all
m\geq 2
, only describe a nonlinearly-induced orthogonal part. Consequently, as in the previous section, the weakly nonlinear expansion in Eq. (
78
) is here too constructed in such a way as to be consistent with its linear counterpart in Eq. (
74
). This implies that, in the linear regime, the fields
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{m,1}
must vanish (not only their projections) and that each amplitude
A_{j}
must reduce to
\phi\gamma_{j}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle
(with
\gamma_{1}=1
) for all
j\geq 1
. Additionally, the amplitudes
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
incorporate leading-order nonlinear effects, which progressively come into play as
\phi
is increased towards order one values, making the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
typically depart from their linear values.

Each amplitude
A_{k}
can be directly extracted by projecting Eq. (
78
) onto
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(t;\theta)
with the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\middle|\bm{U}^{\prime}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}},

(80)

(which does not contain
O(\epsilon_{o}^{2})
terms). Relying on the orthonormality property of the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family,
A_{k}
is the only remaining amplitude in Eq. (
80
). Importantly, that is also why the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are chosen as being deterministic and constant: without this, they could not go out of the inner product in Eq. (
80
) and thus could not bear this specific, immediate meaning.

In Eq. (
76
) the pressure field
\breve{p}_{j}
, associated with the response
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}
, is such that, in the Fourier domain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\\
\check{p}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\end{pmatrix}=G^{-1}_{j}\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)\left(\mathrm{i}\omega\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{L}_{1}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{P}^{T}\check{\mathbf{f}}_{j},\quad j\geq 1.

(81)

Inserting Eqs. (
76
) and (
77
) in the Navier-Stokes equations (
75
), leads to an expansion for each of the spatial Fourier component. The one for the component oscillating at
(0,0)
reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}\Biggl[-(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{0})\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}\\
\overline{p}_{2,0}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle-|A_{1}|^{2}\mathbf{P}^{T}\left(\mathbf{C}_{1}(\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1},\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1})+\mbox{c.c.}\right)\Biggl]+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}).

(82)

As for it, the component oscillating at
(2\alpha,2\beta)
is governed by

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}\left[-\left(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{2}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}\\
\overline{p}_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}-A_{1}^{2}\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{C}_{1}(\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1})\right]

\displaystyle+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}).

(83)

Collecting terms at
O(\epsilon_{o})
in Eqs. (
82
) and (
83
) leads to equations whose solutions are expressed in the Fourier domain (in time) as

\displaystyle\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}_{2,0}(\omega;\theta)=|A_{1}|^{2}\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}(\omega;\theta),

\displaystyle\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}(\omega;\theta)=-\mathbf{R}_{0}(\omega)\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{C}_{1}(\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1}(t;\theta),\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t;\theta))+\mbox{c.c.}],

(84)

for the nonlinearly induced (spatial) mean flow distortion, as well as

\displaystyle\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}_{2,2}(\omega;\theta)=A_{1}^{2}\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}}(\omega;\theta),

\displaystyle\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}}(\omega;\theta)=-\mathbf{R}_{2}(\omega)\mathcal{F}[\mathbf{C}_{1}(\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t;\theta),\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t;\theta))].

(85)

for the nonlinearly induced second harmonic field.

The expansion for the fundamental component oscillating at
(\alpha,\beta)
is

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\left[-A_{1}\left(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}\\
\breve{p}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right]+\epsilon_{o}\left[-\left(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{2,1}\\
\overline{p}^{\perp}_{2,1}\end{pmatrix}-\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\left(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\
\breve{p}_{j}\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\Biggr[-\left(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{3,1}\\
\overline{p}^{\perp}_{3,1}\end{pmatrix}-\sum_{j>N}A_{j}\left(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1}\right)\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\\

\displaystyle\check{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)=G_{j}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(\omega)\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta),\quad j\geq 1.

\displaystyle=G_{j}^{-1}G_{k}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\check{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{{\dagger}}(\omega;\theta)

\displaystyle\boxed{0=-A_{1}-(\mu+\nu)A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}+\phi\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}),

with

\displaystyle\mu

(94a)

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(\omega;\theta)\middle|\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{nl,2}(\omega;\theta)\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

(94b)

\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\check{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}=0
if

\displaystyle\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\xi}\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=G_{j}^{-1}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\xi}\mathbf{R}_{1}(\omega)\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

(95)

The terms in
\hat{\overline{\mathbf{f}}}^{\perp}_{m,1}
do not contribute to any of the equations for the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
since

\displaystyle\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\overline{\mathbf{f}}}^{\perp}_{m,1}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle=G_{j}^{-1}\left\langle\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}\middle|\hat{\overline{\mathbf{u}}}^{\perp}_{m,1}\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{F}}=0,

(96)

for every

).

Equation (
93
), for the amplitude of the response to the optimal stochastic forcing structure, is found to possess a similar form as that for the amplitude of the optimal harmonic response in Eq. (
46
). In particular, in both cases, the leading-order nonlinear term is
\propto A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}
. It is a consequence of the fact that the excitation is periodic, in space along the wavenumber pair
(\alpha,\beta)
for the present section, and in time in the previous section. Indeed, the quadratic terms
A_{1}^{2}
and
|A_{1}|^{2}
cannot oscillate along the fundamental wavenumber or frequency and, thereby cannot consistently appear in the amplitude equations. However, the nonlinear coefficients in Eqs. (
94a
)-(
94b
) are more computationally costly to evaluate than that in Eq. (
46
), for they also involve an integration over the frequencies and an ensemble average.

In the linear limit where the forcing intensity vanishes
\phi\rightarrow 0
(while keeping
\epsilon_{o}
fixed), Eq. (
93
) reduces to
A_{1}=\phi\left\langle\check{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\middle|\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}\right\rangle
, which is the expected linear solution. The nonlinear term
(\mu+\nu)A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}
captures leading order nonlinear effects on
A_{1}
, making it depart from its linear solution as
\phi
is increased. The term
\mu A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}
quantifies how the nonlinear spatial mean flow distortion,
|A_{1}|^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,0}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}(t;\theta)
, interacts with the stochastic response
A_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t;\theta)
, to, on average, result in a modification of its amplitude
A_{1}
. The term
\nu A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}
, for its part, embeds how the second harmonic field,
A_{1}^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2,2}^{A_{1}^{2}}(t;\theta)
, interacts with
A^{*}_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1}(t;\theta)
, to, on average, also bring about a modification of the response amplitude.

Similarly, for each
A_{j}
with
j\geq 2
, the corresponding leading-order equation can be obtained by projecting Eq. (
90
) onto
\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{{\dagger}}(\omega;\theta)
. As for the harmonic forcing case, these equations are linear at leading order and slaved to
A_{1}
. This is a result of the assumed large gap between
G_{1}
and the sub-optimal gains, which led to introducing
A_{1}
at dominant order in the asymptotic expansion in Eq. (
76
), and the sub-optimal amplitudes only at the next orders. These equations for the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
are not written down, since only
A_{1}
is necessary for determining the leading-order stochastic gain.

Indeed, the weakly nonlinear stochastic response in Eq. (
78
) is associated with the gain

\displaystyle G_{(\alpha,\beta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{||\bm{U}^{\prime}(t;\theta)||_{v,\mathcal{T}}}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\phi||\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{s}||}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\epsilon_{o}|A_{1}|^{2}+\epsilon_{o}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}|A_{j}|^{2}+\epsilon_{o}^{2}||\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{2,1}||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3})\right)^{1/2}}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A_{1}|}{\epsilon_{o}\phi}\left(1+O(\epsilon_{o})\right),

(97)

where the orthonormality of the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
basis under the inner product
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
was invoked, together with Eq. (
79
).

Eventually, let us insist on the fact that the expansion in Eq. (
76
) is a particular form under which to seek the weakly nonlinear response, but another could have been chosen, perhaps leading to a more accurate description. For instance, as already mentioned, the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
could represent amplitudes according to some other basis. But even in keeping the same basis, the
\left\{A_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
could also have been chosen depending on the frequency and/or the stochastic argument. This would have considerably complicated the calculations. However, these
\left\{A_{j}(\omega;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
would have carried more information than the constant, deterministic one considered in the present section. In particular, the
A_{1}(\omega;\theta)
would not have given only the weakly nonlinear evolution of the scalar measure
G_{(\alpha,\beta)}
, but that of the whole Fourier spectrum of the leading-order response according to
A_{1}(\omega;\theta)\check{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(\omega;\theta)
. Furthermore, a linear combination of the
\left\{\check{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(\omega;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
with amplitudes depending on both the frequency and the stochastic argument would have made it possible to describe any kind of stochastic and spatio-temporal variations, without the need to add the complementary terms
\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{m,1}
. In this sense, the approach would have shared some similarities with that proposed in Ref.
[
94
]
. A difference is that, since we restrict ourselves to weakly nonlinear regimes, the basis elements and the hierarchization of their respective contributions are determined
a priori
from purely linear considerations.

Deriving an equation for such
A_{1}(\omega;\theta)
was performed in Ref.
[
95
]
, and indeed found to give excellent results at the cost of heavier calculations. The method used in Ref.
[
95
]
was different from that presented here, and relied on an operator perturbation technique and the introduction of multiple time scales. However, we believe that making the amplitudes in Eq. (
76
) depend on the frequency (or time) and the stochastic argument, then proceeding with the projection technique proposed here, would lead to the same equation as in Ref.
[
95
]
.

III.3
Application case: the Couette-Poiseuille flow

We now test the performance of the amplitude equation (
93
) on the Couette-Poiseuille flow. The latter was numerically shown in Refs.
[
96
,
97
]
to support substantial nonmodal linear responses, which we understand from Ref.
[
6
]
can interact with nonlinear mechanisms to bring about a transition to a turbulent state despite linear stability. Such subcritical transition to turbulence was found to occur in the laboratory experiment reported in Refs.
[
98
,
99

100

The Couette-Poiseuille flow, varying over the
y\in[-1;1]
coordinate within a wall moving at velocity
1
at
y=1

0
) at

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}(y)=\left(\frac{3}{4}(y^{2}-1)+\frac{1}{2}(y-1)+1,0,0\right)^{T}.

This flow is purely along the streamwise direction and is invariant along the latter, as well as along the spanwise one.

The stochastic process
\xi^{\prime}(t;\theta)
is chosen as being a band-limited “white” noise, Gaussian distributed, and with zero average. It is characterized in the Fourier domain according to

\displaystyle|\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)|^{2}=1\ \mbox{for}\ |\omega|\leq\omega_{c},

and
\hat{\xi}(\omega;\theta)=0
otherwise. The symbol

denotes the band-limiting cut-off frequency (hence the subscript “

\omega_{c}=\pi
, sufficiently large to encompass the dominant dynamics, and yet sufficiently small to make it possible to choose a large time step in the numerical simulations (the latter having to be smaller or equal to
\pi/\omega_{c}
).

for details concerning the numerical method). This makes possible the nonlinear production of wavenumber pairs (
n\alpha,p\beta
) with
n
and
p
two independent integers. Indeed, let us insist on the fact that, while the forcing solely excites the pair
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)
, nonlinearities generically excite different wavenumbers, typically harmonic pairs, but not only. However, by definition, the fully stochastic gain reported in Fig.
7
(b) only refers to the
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)
component of the stochastic response, denoted
\bm{U}^{\prime}
in the calculations. Therefore, the latter must be extracted from the DNS by a spatial Fourier decomposition of the data beforehand.

Due to the norm to which the stochastic gain refers, the temporal and the ensemble average of
||\bm{U}^{\prime}||^{2}
must be performed. The DNS lasted
T=4200
units of time, to which we have discarded the first
300=t_{i}
to remove the transient part of the response and only average in the presumably statistically steady regime. To assess the convergence of the temporal average of
||\bm{U}^{\prime}||^{2}
with the averaging time
t_{av}
, where
t_{i}\leq t_{av}\leq T
, we show in Fig.
7
(a) the evolution of the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(t_{av}-t_{i})}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{av}}\frac{||\bm{U}^{\prime}(t)||^{2}}{F^{2}}\mathrm{d}t,\quad F=\phi\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}.

(101)

It should converge to a fixed value as
t_{av}
is increased towards
T
, where it is equal to the temporal average of
||\bm{U}^{\prime}||^{2}/F^{2}
. For all considered values of
\phi
but the two last ones, i.e., for the continuous line curves in Fig.
7
(a), we observe that the quantity defined in Eq. (
101
) indeed converges towards some fixed value as
t_{av}=T
, highlighted by a diamond marker. The qualitatively similar evolution of all continuous line curves with
t_{av}
is to be noticed. It is first made possible by the fact that they all have been subjected to the same noise process, only the value of
\phi
being different between the two different curves. Another important element is that, for the corresponding
\phi
values, the flow remains laminar. We further assume that these stochastic responses are ergodic processes, such that the last values of the continuous lines in Fig.
7
(a), apparently converged and denoted by a diamond marker, directly give the stochastic gain without the need to additionally perform an ensemble average. This assumption was made for practical reasons only, since each curve in Fig.
7
(a) takes about one week of computational time to be produced. Accordingly, the diamond marker in Fig.
7
(a) are directly reported in Fig.
7
(b).

For the two largest considered forcing intensities, corresponding to the dash-dotted continuous lines in Fig.
7
(a), the flow becomes turbulent at a time smaller the larger
\phi
is. The flow is declared turbulent by visual inspection of snapshots corresponding to different times and their corresponding Fourier spectra in space. Indeed, the turbulent regime is characterized by a substantially richer wavenumber content than the laminar regime. In this latter, the spectrum is dominated by the fundamental excited pair and its harmonics, i.e., by
n(\alpha,\beta)
with
n=0,1,2,3,..
. This has no reason to remain true in a turbulent state and the latter is structurally fundamentally different from the one approached with the weakly nonlinear expansion in Eq. (
76
). Therefore, to only compare flow states of the same (laminar) nature and not mislead the reader, we did not report any corresponding stochastic gain value in Fig.
7
(b) when the flow has transited to turbulence.

Let us now comment on the agreement, in Fig.
7
(b), between the weakly and fully nonlinear approaches. As long as the flow remains laminar for sufficiently small
\phi
, the weakly nonlinear approach performs well in predicting the nonlinear decrease of the stochastic gain as
\phi
becomes larger. It brings a substantial improvement over the linear approach, and at a numerical cost that is considerably reduced as compared to that of the DNS. In addition, by comparing
\mu_{r}=-7.381
with
\nu_{r}=-1.454
, we understand that this initial stochastic gain decrease is mostly due to a (spatial) mean flow distortion effect. The stochastic forcing appears to be less amplified by the spatial mean flow than by the base flow. This initial gain decrease, found in both approaches, may appear surprising given the subcritical nature of the flow and the subsequent transition to turbulence for larger forcing, although there is no contradiction there. It is possible that a more general forcing, exciting several wavenumber pairs, would lead to a gain increase even for small forcing values through nonlinear (triadic) interactions between these latter. Furthermore, the applied forcing structure is only optimal at the linear level, and need not resemble the nonlinear optimal (the latter being parameterized by the forcing intensity). The coefficients
\mu_{r}
and
\nu_{r}
, being relative to the forcing structure considered, they could as well change sign if the nonlinear optimal was applied instead of the linear one.

However, the weakly nonlinear approach fails to predict the flow transition to turbulence, in the sense that the weakly nonlinear branch in Fig.
7
(b) continues to exist and be unique for arbitrarily large
\phi
. This result could appear disappointing in the sense that the flow transition to another state could have corresponded to a loss of solution of Eq. (
93
), or a loss of its stability. Going back to the harmonic forcing problem, this was for instance found to be the case in Ref.
[
90
]
for the Poiseuille flow (please see Fig.
10
therein). Instead, the fact that the amplitude equation here gives no information about the flow transition to turbulence above some value
\phi
, seems to indicate that such transition is due to a leading-order nonlinear interaction between the stochastic response, and some “secondary” mode appearing on top (and as a consequence) of the latter. Indeed, such nonlinear coupling between the stochastic response and some other structure(s) is inherently absent in the weakly nonlinear approach. That is because the weakly nonlinear approach postulates in Eq. (
76
) that the stochastic response is along the linear optimal one at the leading order. Thus, it only consists of a single amplitude equation for linear optimal response, and cannot take into account a leading-order structural enrichment that could arise, for instance, from a secondary instability. Thereby, it cannot take into account the ensuing nonlinear interactions that can lead to another flow state (here, turbulent). By contrast, in the weakly nonmodal harmonically forced Poiseuille flow in Ref.
[
90
]
, Fig.
10
, the amplitude equation in itself could predict the subcritical transition, since the latter was due to the effect of nonlinearities directly on the eigenvalue largely responsible for the harmonic response.

, but by increasing the Reynolds number to
\mbox{Re}=500
, is shown in Fig.

IV
Weakly nonlinear response to an initial perturbation

In this third and last section, we complete our survey by considering the nonmodal response to an initial perturbation, in a weakly nonlinear regime. Proceeding according to the same general idea as in the two previous sections, the Navier-Stokes equations are presently reduced to a scalar one for the amplitude of response to the optimal initial condition. While the nature of the problem suggests using the same
L^{2}
inner product as in the first section, a particularity of the reduction procedure lies here in the fact that the amplitude must here depend on time.

Once again, we invite readers who are not interested in the technical details of the method, when specifically applied to the transient growth problem, to skip directly to Sec.
V
for concluding remarks.

IV.1
Linear regime

As in the previous section, the base flow
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}
is also chosen here as a three-dimensional plane channel flow, bounded in the
y
direction, but invariant in the
x
and
z
directions. In addition, an infinitesimal initial perturbation
\mathbf{u}_{0}
is assumed to be spatially periodic with wavenumbers
\alpha
and
\beta
along the
x
and
z
coordinates, respectively, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{0}(x,y,z)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}(y)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}.

(102)

The field
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}(y)
is an arbitrary spatial velocity structure, generically complex-valued. Owing to the invariance of the base flow along the
x
and
z
directions, the linear response
\mathbf{q}
to the initial perturbation in Eq. (
102
) will also oscillate only along the
(\alpha,\beta)
pair. It is thus possible to write

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}(x,y,z,t)=\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(y,t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.},

(103)

and the field
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(y,t)
obeys the linear equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{1}\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\mathbf{q}^{\prime}(y,0)=\mathbf{P}^{T}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}(y).

(104)

We refer to Eq. (
53
) for the definition of the linear operator
\mathbf{L}_{1}
. Note that in this particular section, no assumption is made regarding the stability of
\mathbf{L}_{1}
, which could as well be unstable. As shown in Sec.
I.1.3
, Eq. (
104
) can be formally solved by using the propagator operator
\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)
. The latter is such that its application onto
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}
, the velocity field at time
t=0
, directly gives the velocity field
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
at some time
t
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(y,t)=\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}(y).

(105)

From now on, the dependence of the field on the spatial coordinate
y
will not be made explicit anymore. As argued in Sec.
I.1.3
, it is relevant to seek the initial perturbation structure
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}
leading to the largest amplification under the norm induced by the
L^{2}
inner product, and evaluated at some
t_{o}
called “temporal horizon”. Specifically, it is relevant to compute the transient gain

\displaystyle G_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t_{o})

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}}\frac{||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t_{o})||}{||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||}=\max_{\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}}\frac{||\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||}{||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}},

(106)

where the subscript
``(\alpha,\beta)"
emphasizes that the gain is parameterized by the wavenumber pair
(\alpha,\beta)
. We have again defined
\epsilon_{o}
as the gain inverse. The maximization problem in Eq. (
106
) is solved by computing the right and left singular modes of the propagator evaluated at some temporal horizon
t_{o}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)
, which yield the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
and
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})\right\}_{j\geq 1}
families, respectively. Each family is orthonormal under the
L^{2}
inner product and the normalization
||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}||=||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})||=1
applies for every
j\geq 1
. Each pair of singular vectors is associated with the singular value
G_{j}
, such that the relations

\displaystyle G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j},\quad G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}=\mathbf{\Phi}(t_{o},0)^{{\dagger}}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o}),

(107)

are true for every
j\geq 1
. The singular values
\left\{G_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
are sorted by decreasing values. This makes
G_{1}
the optimal gain, i.e.,
G_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t_{o})=G_{1}
, and
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}
the associated optimal initial condition. Accordingly,
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t_{o})
is the normalized response to the optimal initial condition, evaluated at
t=t_{o}
. All the
j\geq 2
correspond to sub-optimal gains and pairs.

While
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t_{o})
denote the response to
G_{j}^{-1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
evaluated at
t=t_{o}
, the latter response can also be computed for all times
0\leq t\leq t_{o}
according to

\displaystyle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)=G_{j}^{-1}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j},

(108)

where the first relation in Eq. (
107
) is recovered at
t=t_{o}
. In other terms,
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)
is the linear trajectory seeded by
G_{j}^{-1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
.

From now on, let us normalize
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}
, an arbitrary initial condition, according to
||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||=1
. By decomposing
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}
in the orthonormal basis
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
, we are led to the following decomposition of the transient response in Eq. (
105
),

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)=\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\geq 1}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t).

(109)

This decomposition, however, has to be interpreted with more caution than its equivalent in Eq. (
28
) for the harmonic forcing problem, or in Eq. (
72
) for the stochastic one. Indeed, the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family is generically not orthonormal for
t<t_{o}
. That is both because it is not orthogonal for
0<t<t_{o}
, and because, for all
j\geq 1
,
||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)||
is not equal to one for
0\leq t<t_{o}
. Consequently, to remain consistent with the previous sections, we would rather be interested in describing the transient response
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)
as some component along
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
, plus another term
\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t)
, defined as being orthogonal to the latter for all times, rather than only at
t=t_{o}
and
t=0
(i.e.,
\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t)\right\rangle=0
for all
t
). This gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)+\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t),

(110)

which, by identification with Eq. (
109
), leads to

\displaystyle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t)=\sum_{j\geq 2}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle G_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t),

(111)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)=\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)-\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t),\quad j\geq 2.

(112)

The field
\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)
is the component of
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)
which remains orthogonal to
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
, for all times and
j\geq 2
. We note that
\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t_{o})=\breve{\mathbf{u}}(t_{o})
of unit norm, for all
j\geq 1
.

In what follows, the optimal gain
G_{1}=1/\epsilon_{o}
is again assumed to be large and thus
\epsilon_{o}
to be small. Furthermore, we also assume a large gap between the value of the optimal gain and that of the sub-optimal ones, i.e.,
G_{1}\gg G_{2}>G_{3}>...
and, for the third time throughout this article, we postulate the scalings

\displaystyle\left\{G_{1},G_{2},...,G_{N},G_{N+1},...\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}},\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}},...,\frac{\gamma_{N}}{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}},\gamma_{N+1},...\right\},\quad\gamma_{j}=O(1),\quad j\geq 2.

(113)

This scaling implies that the transient response
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\Biggl(\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\epsilon_{o}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)+\underbrace{\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle\gamma_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j>N}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle\gamma_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)}_{=\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t)}\Biggl),

(114)

where Eqs. (
110
) and (
111
) have been used. Since for
t\simeq t_{o}
we have
||\epsilon_{o}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)||=O(1)
as well as
||\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)||=O(1)
for
j\geq 2
, in Eq. (
114
) the component along
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
has a contribution which is
\epsilon_{o}^{-1/2}\gg 1
larger than the term
\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t)
for
t
around
t_{o}
. In other terms, for
t
around
t_{o}
, it is possible to interpret the transient response as being predominantly along
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
plus a smaller rest contained in the orthogonal subspace, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{o}}\left(\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\epsilon_{o}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)+\underbrace{O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})}_{=\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}(t)}\right),\quad t\simeq t_{o}.

(115)

Let us, however, insist that this order separation, between the component along
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
and the rest, only holds for
t
around
t_{o}
. For times around
t=0
in particular, by definition in Eq. (
108
) the norm
||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)||
is of order
G_{j}^{-1}
for
j\geq 1
. Thereby, we deduce from Eq. (
109
) that the respective components along each of the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
all contribute to the same (order one) amount to
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)
. Since the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family is orthonormal at
t=0
, it is thus not possible to conclude that, at short times, the component along
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
dominates over its orthogonal counterpart.

only, but generically not for smaller ones.

.

IV.2
Weakly nonlinear continuation

Let us consider the fully nonlinear, unforced Navier-Stokes equations

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}\\
P\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-(\bm{U}\cdot\mathbf{\nabla})\bm{U}+\mbox{Re}^{-1}\Delta\bm{U}-\mathbf{\nabla}P\\
\mathbf{\nabla}\cdot\bm{U}\end{pmatrix},

(116)

with the initial condition

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}(0)\\
P(0)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}\\
P_{\mathrm{e}}\end{pmatrix}+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\mathbf{P}^{T}\left(\psi\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right),

(117)

where recall the initial condition structure,
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}
, to be normalized as
||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||=1
. It is scaled to appear at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
with an amplitude
\psi=O(1)
, the latter being a complex-valued free parameter. We emphasize that the initial condition oscillates in space purely along the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
. In the nonlinear regime, this constitutes a loss of generality which is purposely made to lighten the ensuing calculations. The method, however, is not fundamentally limited to spatially harmonic initial conditions.

We aim to determine the scalar, complex-valued amplitude
A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)=O(1)
corresponding to the following postulated form for the weakly nonlinear transient response

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}(t)\\
P(t)\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{U}_{\mathrm{e}}\\
P_{\mathrm{e}}\end{pmatrix}+\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\left[A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\\
\breve{p}_{1}(t)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right]+\epsilon_{o}\left[\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)\\
\breve{p}^{\perp}_{j}(t)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}+\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{2}(t)\\
\overline{p}_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right]

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\left[\sum_{j>N}A_{j}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\begin{pmatrix}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)\\
\breve{p}^{\perp}_{j}(t)\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}+\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{3}(t)\\
\overline{p}_{3}(t)\end{pmatrix}\right]+\underbrace{\sum_{m\geq 4}\epsilon_{o}^{m/2}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}(t)\\
\overline{p}_{m}(t)\end{pmatrix}}_{=O\left(\epsilon_{o}^{2}\right)},

(118)

where all the others
A_{j}=O(1)
for
j\geq 2
are also unknowns.

The symbols
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}(t)
with
m\geq 2
denote spatial harmonics fields without including the fundamental, such that

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m}(t)=\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,0}(t)+\left(\sum_{n\geq 2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,n}(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}n(\alpha x+\beta z)}+\mbox{c.c.}\right),

(119)

for
m\geq 2
. As in the two previous sections, expansion in Eq. (
118
) is such that the component of the weakly nonlinear transient response along the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
, reading

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\sum_{j>N}A_{j}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t),

(120)

is similar to its solution in the linear regime given in Eq. (

). This is at the exception that, at each order, the prefactors in front of the fields
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
and
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}\right\}_{j\geq 2}
in Eq. (

which also incorporate weakly nonlinear effects. Again, this was purposely made to ensure the continuity between the linear and weakly nonlinear regimes.

We insist, however, that the separation of the orders in Eq. (

t\simeq t_{o}
. On the contrary, for small times
t\simeq 0
, the terms in

\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)

due to the scaling of the initial condition. The latter fact will become clear in a moment.

Contrary to the previous sections, the amplitude
A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)
must here depend on time. It is still for reasons of consistency with the linear regime, where the time-dependent solution
A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)=\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\epsilon_{o}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle/\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle
should be recovered in the limit
\psi\rightarrow 0
. Indeed, the latter expression follows from the decomposition in Eq. (
110
), where
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)=\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\psi\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}
and recalling that
A_{1}
is pre-multiplied by
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}
in the expansion of the response. We further assume the amplitude
A_{1}
to vary slowly with time, in the sense that
\mathrm{d}_{t}A_{1}=O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
, as we anticipate that it will simplify the temporal integrals involving powers of
A_{1}
. While this hypothesis presumably degrades the accuracy of the prediction, as the strength of the nonlinearities is increased, it also makes the resulting amplitude equation substantially easier to solve. It would also have been possible to make
A_{1}
depending on
t
directly, which could presumably capture faster nonlinear modification of
A_{1}
at the cost of a more complicated amplitude equation.

The initial condition associated with each field contribution in Eq. (
118
) is obtained by evaluating the latter expansion at
t=0
, applying
\mathbf{P}
, and then identifying with the externally applied initial condition in Eq. (

). This gives

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}(0)\epsilon_{o}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}(0)G_{j}^{-1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\sum_{j>N}A_{j}(0)G_{j}^{-1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}=\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\psi\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0},

(121)

where we have used that
\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(0)=G_{j}^{-1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
. Equivalently,

\displaystyle A_{1}(0)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}+\sum_{j\geq 2}A_{j}(0)\gamma_{j}^{-1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}=\psi\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}.

(122)

The orthonormality of the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\}_{j\geq 1}
family further implies

\displaystyle A_{1}(0)=\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle,\quad A_{j}(0)=\gamma_{j}\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle,\quad j\geq 2.

(123)

In addition, since only the
(\alpha,\beta)
pair is initialized, all harmonics fields must initially be null, i.e.,
\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{m,n}(0)=\mathbf{0}
for all considered

and

\displaystyle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(t)=\frac{\epsilon_{o}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)^{{\dagger}}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle},

\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1})\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,0}\\

as well as

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}\partial_{t}-\mathbf{L}_{1})\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,2}\\

(135)

respectively, with
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,2}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,0}(0)=\mathbf{0}
.
In Eq. (

\left(\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}-\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{{\dagger}}\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}\right\rangle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}\right)
can easily be shown to be orthogonal to
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{{\dagger}}

A_{1}
can be extracted by taking the inner product between
\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(t)
and Eq. (

). This gives

\displaystyle\boxed{0=-A_{1}+\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle-A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}(\mu+\nu)}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}),

(136)

where we insist that the terms at

can be neglected generally only for

, and at the condition that the temporal integration performed in transforming Eq. (

) into Eq. (

) did not alter the asymptotic hierarchy at these times. We have defined the coefficients

\displaystyle\mu(t)=\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(t)\middle|\mathbf{\Phi}(0,t)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,0}(t)\right\rangle=\epsilon_{o}\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,0}(t)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle},

(137a)

\displaystyle\nu(t)=\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(t)\middle|\mathbf{\Phi}(0,t)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,2}(t)\right\rangle=\epsilon_{o}\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,2}(t)\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle}.

(137b)

By evaluating Eq. (
136
) at
t=0
and noticing that
\mu(0)=\nu(0)=0
since
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,2}(0)=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{nl,0}(0)=\mathbf{0}
, we check that
A_{1}(0)=\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle
in accordance with Eq. (
123
). Furthermore, by letting the rescaled amplitude of the initial condition
\psi
tend towards zero, the nonlinear term in Eq. (
136
) becomes negligible such that

\displaystyle A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\rightarrow\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}(t)\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle=\psi\frac{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\epsilon_{o}\mathbf{\Phi}(t,0)\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\middle|\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)\right\rangle},

(138)

as
\psi\rightarrow 0
. We indeed recover the correct expression for
A_{1}
in the linear regime.

In addition, the nonlinear term
(\mu+\nu)A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}
in Eq. (
136
) captures the leading-order nonlinear effects, making
A_{1}
depart from Eq. (
138
) as the amplitude of the initial condition is increased through
\psi
. The coefficient
\mu(t)
takes into account the fact that the transient response to the optimal initial condition,
A_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
, evolves over a base flow which is corrected by a time-dependent nonlinear (spatial) mean flow, equal to
|A_{1}|^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{|A_{1}|^{2}}_{2}
at leading-order. Such a nonlinear correction to the base flow feeds back on the transient response and modifies its amplitude
A_{1}
. We note that this nonlinear coupling between the perturbation, and the mean flow carrying it, was also accounted for in Ref.
[
101
]
, and the basis for finding weakly nonlinear optimal initial conditions. The fact that the mean flow correction is simply proportional to
|A_{1}|^{2}
despite resulting from the temporal integration of the interaction of the time-dependent
A_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
with its complex conjugate. This would not have been true if
A_{1}
had depended directly on
t
instead of
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t
, for such
|A_{1}(t)|^{2}
could not have got out of the temporal integral in computing the mean flow correction. Instead, the assumed time scale separation between
A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)
and
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
here guarantees that
|A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)|^{2}
can get out of the integral and give the leading-order response (please see Eq. (
150
)). In other words, the nonlinear term in Eq. (
136
) can be seen as resulting from a “quasi-static” approximation, where the modulation of the mean flow distortion is sufficiently slow for the latter to be instantaneously proportional to
|A_{1}|^{2}
. The coefficient
\nu
incorporates the fact that (spatial) leading-order, second harmonic field
A_{1}^{2}\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{A_{1}^{2}}_{2}
interacts with
A_{1}^{*}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{*}_{1}
to also bring about a nonlinear modification of
A_{1}
.

We note that differentiating Eq. (
136
) with respect to time
t
, then keeping the linear term
\mathrm{d}_{t}A_{1}=\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}\dot{A}_{1}
at leading-order while absorbing in
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
the nonlinear contribution in
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}(\mu+\nu)(\dot{A}_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}+...)
, which results from the chain rule derivative, gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}A_{1}=\psi\mathrm{d}_{t}\left(\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{{\dagger}}_{1}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle\right)-A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}(\dot{\mu}+\dot{\nu})+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}),

(139)

where the
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
term only contains higher-order nonlinear corrections. By neglecting these latter, Eq. (
139
) is similar to that derived in our previous work in Ref.
[
25
]
, Eq.
(3.27)
therein. In the latter article, we resorted to a perturbation of the inverse propagator to make it singular, embedded in a multiple time scales expansion closed with the Fredholm alternative. The resulting expression of the amplitude equation under the form
\mathrm{d}_{t}A_{1}=...
is inherent to the multiple-time scale method used in the latter article. Because of the term in
\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}(\mu+\nu)(\dot{A}_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}+...)
, absorbed at
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
then neglected in Eq. (
139
), but still present at leading order in Eq. (
136
), solving these two equations should thus lead to a
O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})
difference between the results.

It would have been possible to proceed with a weakly nonlinear expansion that follows directly from Eq. (
109
), and not from Eq. (
114
). Specifically, in the expansion in Eq. (
120
), the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\geq 2}
would have been replaced more simply by the
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\geq 2}
, so as to become

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle+\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\sum_{j>N}A_{j}(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}t)\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t).

(140)

By performing the same calculation sequence, this would have led to the same expansion as in Eq. (
131
), but where the summation term in Eq. (
131
) would have become more simply
\sum_{j\geq 2}\gamma^{-1}_{j}A_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
. From here, an equation for
A_{j}
, linearly decoupled from all the others, could have been extracted upon taking the inner product simply with
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,j}
. This would have led to the equation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-A_{1}+\psi\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}\middle|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-A_{1}|A_{1}|^{2}\left\langle\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{0,1}\middle|\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{\Phi}(0,s)\left(\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{nl,0}(s)+\mathbf{f}^{\prime}_{nl,2}(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s\right\rangle

\displaystyle+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}),

(141)

for
A_{1}
, perhaps more intuitive than Eq. (
136
). However, the coefficient multiplying the nonlinear term in Eq. (
141
) requires a backward integration of the direct problem (which is not the case for the coefficients defined in Eqs. (
137a
) and (
137b
)). This is notoriously known to lead to numerical difficulties, due to the existence of eigenvalues with very negative real parts, whose corresponding eigenmodes contribute to the backward integration in an exponentially diverging manner. In addition, we recall that the family
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
is not orthogonal for
0\leq t<t_{o}
, such that, for these times, the
A_{1}
solving Eq. (
141
) would not have corresponded to the amplitude the orthogonal projection of the weakly nonlinear response onto
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}(t)
, whereas it is in the method developed above and leading to Eq. (
136
). A relative advantage of defining the
A_{1}
as the amplitude of the orthogonal projection, is to relegate the error made in computing the transient gain to the next order for all
t\simeq t_{o}
and not only at
t=t_{o}
. Indeed, the transient gain of the
(\alpha,\beta)
-component corresponding to the expansion in Eq. (
118
) is

\displaystyle G_{(\alpha,\beta)}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{||\bm{U}^{\prime}(t)||}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\psi||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||}

\displaystyle=\frac{||\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}A_{1}\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}+\epsilon_{o}\sum_{j=2}^{N}A_{j}\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})||}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\psi||\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{0}||}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\epsilon_{o}|A_{1}|^{2}||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}||^{2}+\epsilon_{o}^{2}\sum_{j=2}^{N}|A_{j}|^{2}||\breve{\mathbf{u}}^{\perp}_{j}||^{2}+O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})\right)^{1/2}}{\epsilon_{o}^{3/2}\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{|A_{1}|||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}||}{\epsilon_{o}\psi}(1+O(\epsilon_{o})),

(142)

where the terms inside the
O(\epsilon_{o})
are veritably negligible for
t\simeq t_{o}
. Now, if in the second equality in Eq. (
142
) the correction of
O(\epsilon_{o})
had not been orthogonal to
\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}
, then it would have produced, in the third equality, a term of
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
instead of
O(\epsilon_{o}^{2})
. Thereby, the transient gain would have been
(\epsilon_{o}\psi)^{-1}|A_{1}|||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}||(1+O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2}))
and the error made in approaching the gain with
(\epsilon_{o}\psi)^{-1}|A_{1}|||\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{1}||
would have been larger. That is one of the reasons for which the amplitude
A_{1}
was defined as the amplitude of an orthogonal projection in this section: to minimize the error made in approaching the gain. Other reasons are that, as said, this makes easier the computation of nonlinear coefficients and guarantees a conceptual consistency with the two previous sections, where the amplitudes
A_{1}
here too resulted from orthogonal projections.

IV.3
Application case: the Lamb-Oseen vortex flow

Let us now appraise the validity of the amplitude equation (

) on an application case.
To this end, we show below some results for the same two-dimensional, axisymmetric, Lamb-Oseen vortex flow as considered in Ref.
[

. The results below are obtained by solving Eq. (

in Ref.
[

O(\epsilon_{o}^{1/2})

[

The calculations were made in radial coordinates, and the base flow invariance was along the azimuthal direction, instead of being along the streamwise and spanwise directions. and experimentally in Refs.

V
Summary and perspective

V.1
Summary

In summary, we have considered strongly non-normal flow systems, governed by the Navier-Stokes equations, subjected to weak external excitations. From here, we have proposed a general method to reduce the Navier-Stokes equations to a weakly nonlinear one for the amplitude of the dominant nonmodal flow response. We have appraised independently three different types of external excitation: a harmonic forcing, a stochastic forcing, and eventually an initial perturbation. Despite being excitations of different natures, the reduction procedure followed from the same backbone principle, which we state as follows

1.

The nature of the excitation suggests the choice of a certain induced norm under which to measure the flow response. For instance, in the harmonic forcing problem, it is relevant to measure the component of the response oscillating at the forcing frequency under the
L^{2}
norm. In the stochastic forcing problem, however, the response is more appropriately measured under the variance norm, i.e.
||\bullet||_{v,\mathcal{T}}^{2}=\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
.

2.

Once the induced norm for measuring the flow response has been selected, it is relevant to seek to optimize the flow response-to-forcing amplification. Note that this also requires selecting a norm under which to measure the forcing. It is generally chosen to be the same as for the response, but it need not be so (please see Eq. (
64
) for an example). Optimizing the response-to-forcing amplification amounts to constructing a family of excitation structures (e.g., forcing or initial condition) together with a family for their respective responses. The latter family of responses, in particular, is orthonormal under the inner product inducing the selected norm (for the response). For instance, for the stochastic forcing problem, solving Eq. (
64
) led to the family
\left\{\breve{\mathbf{u}}_{j}(t;\theta)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
of responses processes, orthonormal under the
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
inner product.

3.

Crucially, when measuring the response to some arbitrary structure under the selected induced norm, the contribution of the
j
th response constituting the family can be ranked according to the associated gain
G_{j}
. For strongly non-normal systems, it is often true that the contributions of only a few responses in the family dominate the linear response to some arbitrary structure. In other terms, it is often true that a small number of gains are much larger than all the others. It is sometimes called the “low-rank” property.

4.

If the “low-rank” property holds, then projecting the Navier-Stokes equations in the low-dimensional subspace spanned by these few leading responses still gives the leading-order flow response, both in a linear and a weakly nonlinear regime at least. This projection is done under the inner product inducing the selected norm (for the response), e.g.
\left\langle\bullet\middle|\bullet\right\rangle_{v,\mathcal{T}}
for the stochastic response. In the phase space represented in the orthogonal basis formed by the responses set, as done in Fig.
1
, the weakly nonlinear expansion we have proposed captures the curvature of the locus of the responses as the amplitude of the excitation is increased. Such curvature makes this locus depart from the linear response subspace, to which it is tangent in the linear regime.

To simplify the calculations and not obscure the general idea of the article, we have restricted the analysis to situations were, in the responses set, only the optimal response contributed the most to the overall linear response (i.e.,
G_{1}\gg G_{2}>G_{3}>...
, equivalent to a “unit-rank” assumption). In addition, in the stochastic forcing and transient growth problems, we have assumed the base flow to be parallel. Since no temporal symmetries generically exist in these two problems, this assumption was necessary for the base flow still to carry periodic perturbations, only in space instead of time. As an additional layer of simplification, only monochromatic perturbations (i.e., associated with a single wavenumber pair) have been considered at leading-order, which guaranteed that the first nonlinear interaction term to retroact on the fundamental pair was collected at the last order considered in the expansion, i.e.,
O(\epsilon_{o}^{3/2})
, instead of the intermediate one, i.e.,
O(\epsilon_{o})
, thus making the calculations lighter. This spatial symmetry was not assumed in the harmonic forcing problem since the inherent temporal symmetry had the same effect on the calculations.

Made possible by these assumptions, we could formally derive two-degrees of freedom (since complex-valued) amplitude equations for the optimal harmonic response in Eq. (
46
), for the response to the optimal stochastic forcing in Eq. (
93
), and for the response to the optimal initial condition in Eq. (
136
). In doing so, in stark contrast with classical techniques, we did not need to assume the existence of neutral or close-to-neutral eigenvalues of the linearized operator, and/or to assume the low-dimensionality of some master eigenspace in which to project the dynamics.

For each of the three problems, we selected a different application case: a two-dimensional convectively unstable non-parallel flow for the harmonic forcing problem, a three-dimensional parallel one for the stochastic problem, and eventually a two-dimensional axisymmetric one for the transient growth problem. For sufficiently small excitation amplitudes, yet up to values large enough to depart from the linear regime, the amplitude equations systematically performed well in predicting the nonlinear evolution of the gain as the amplitude of the excitation was increased. This was found to be true whether the nonlinearities enhance, decrease, or induce a non-monotonic behavior of the gain. Owing to their simplicity of interpretation, they could also bring physical insights into the physical mechanisms responsible for such nonlinear evolutions of the gain.

These good performances, however, did not systematically maintain themselves as the forcing amplitude was increased to large values, causing the nonlinear flow response to transit to a state structurally completely different from to the linear one. The amplitude equations mostly failed to predict such subcritical transitions of the flow. This was the case for the Couette-Poiseuille and the Lamb-Oseen vortex flows, subjected to a stochastic forcing and initial condition, respectively. Indeed, for these flows, the amplitude equation still predicted the branch stemming from the linear solution to exist and be unique, as the amplitude of the excitation was increased to arbitrarily large values. Meanwhile, the DNS showed that above some threshold value, the flow transited to another state. This defeat we interpreted as the subcritical transition being initiated by the apparition of a “secondary” unstable mode on top of the nonmodal response, whose amplitude was not included in the weakly nonlinear expansion, whereas it must lead to leading-order interaction after a sufficient amount of time for the unstable mode to have grown. By contrast, in Fig.
10
of Ref.
[
90
]
, the nonmodal amplitude equation in itself could predict the subcritical transition of the harmonically forced plane Poiseuille flow, by loss of stability of the solution or loss of the solution. The good performance of the amplitude equation in predicting there the subcritical behaviors was inherent to the fact that the response was mostly modal and linked to the presence of a weakly damped eigenvalue at the same frequency, and that this is precisely this eigenvalue which, by crossing the neutral axis, is responsible for the subcritical transition of the flow.

Since the amplitude equations missed the subcritical transitions in the application cases considered in Secs.
III
and
IV
, they also could not predict the flow structures past the transitions. Again, this is a consequence of seeking the weakly nonlinear response as an asymptotic expansion where only the optimal response is included at leading order. This, by definition, condemns the former response to remain structurally close to the latter, such that our approach, at least in its current implementation, has very little freedom on the spatial structure. Although the latter feature is what makes possible the reduction to a very low-dimensional system, it is problematic in the subcritical transition scenario. There, the flow may go into a nonlinear state that is spatially much richer than that around which the expansion is performed. This enrichment may involve many sub-optimal structures (which were not relevant around the original attractor), but also, in the parallel shear flow cases considered Secs.
III
and
IV
, many additional wavenumber pairs (and not just harmonics of the fundamental pair, already included here). Indeed, in the subcritical transition to turbulence, for instance, existing literature indicates that the latter often involves nonlinear energy transfers between different wavenumber pairs
[
22
,
101
,
49
]
. Overall, for our approach to make predictions regarding subcritical transitions, we believe that it should incorporate more degrees of freedom in space. This could be done either by including more structures at leading-order (please see discussion below) or, possibly, by introducing a slow space scale
X=\epsilon_{o}x
such that the amplitudes eventually depend on it
A_{1}=A_{1}(X,...)

[
107
]
.

V.2
Perspectives

Although we are aware of the limitations of the approach adopted in this article, for the reasons mentioned above, or more fundamentally by the fact that it relies on a weakly nonlinear expansion, we do believe that it offers numerous perspectives. We list some of them below.

•

From a methodological point of view, the approach could be generalized to flows for which some sub-optimal gains scale like the optimal one. This was found to happen in Ref.
[
9
,
13
]
, among many other examples. Thereby, the associated sub-optimal responses would need to be included in the leading order of the expansion, together with the optimal one. When the base flow is taken parallel, including different wavenumber pairs at the leading order could constitute another relevant extension of the method. These two types of enrichment of the leading-order spatial structure, i.e., by including sub-optimal responses and/or additional spatial wavenumbers, would result in a system of nonlinearly coupled amplitude equations which could characterize much richer nonlinear regimes. Such a system of amplitude equations, as they involve more degrees of freedom in space, could yield better performances in trying to predict subcritical transitions and subsequent states.

•

Another possible extension of the method is motivated by the fact that non-parallel base flows are also subject to strong nonmodal stochastic and transient responses, as illustrated in Refs.
[
56
]
and
[
13
]
, respectively. Thereby, it would be useful to extend the calculations proposed in Secs.
III
and
IV
to non-parallel base flows, where a spatial Fourier decomposition of the perturbation is not possible anymore. This would be at the cost of slightly more complex calculations, resulting in the apparition of a quadratic term
\propto A_{1}^{2}
in the final amplitude equation together with the cubic one
\propto A_{1}^{3}
(as shown in Ref.
[
90
]
, Eq. (
3.25
) therein).

•

Another perspective is motivated by the question of the role played by non-normality in noise-induced transition in fluid flows, which we believe to be of central physical importance. Please consider, for instance, a flow past the onset of a supercritical pitchfork bifurcation, where two attractors exist. Subjecting this flow to a stochastic forcing makes noise-induced transitions between the two attractors possible, after unpredictable and possibly extremely long times. This motivated us to derive in Ref.
[
108
]
a stochastically forced amplitude equation for the symmetry-breaking eigenmode, which could reproduce transition statistics at a meager numerical cost.
However, in Ref.
[
108
]
we only considered a narrow-band, slowly varying noise. The latter only triggered the slow dynamics, which was reduced on the symmetry-breaking eigenmode. It would be of great interest now to consider the flow response to a noise that is not narrow-banded. This was recently done in Ref.
[
109
]
for the flow past a cylinder, where the noise was an intrinsic feature of real molecular fluids. In the latter article, the noise was decomposed as the sum of a slow plus a fast component. The latter triggers a fast dynamics, which, in our context, could be greatly amplified by non-normality. Thereby, it would be interesting to couple the modal approach in Ref.
[
108
]
with the nonmodal one proposed in Sec.
III
, to be able to consider the flow response to an arbitrary noise and not only a narrow-band one. The ensuing low-dimensional system would couple an equation for the symmetry-breaking eigenmode, with an equation for the part of the response triggered by non-normality. In this, it would quantify the extent to which the nonlinear coupling between the modal and the nonmodal part of the stochastic response is an essential ingredient to noise-induced transitions.

•

From a more fundamental perspective, we believe that a precise link with the center manifold theory could possibly be drawn, at least for the harmonic forcing problem. Indeed, in Fig.
1
, the locus of the weakly nonlinear response (dashed line) may be seen as analogous to a center manifold for the modal paradigm, whereas
V_{h}
would be analogous to the center eigenspace (the former being tangent to the latter in the linear regime). Thereby, proceeding along the calculation lines proposed in Refs.
[
74
,
75
]
, but rewriting the Navier-Stokes equations in the singular mode basis instead of the eigenbasis, then seeking the part of the nonlinear correction of solution which is contained in the sub-optimals subspace (i.e.
V^{\perp}_{1}
), as a graph over the amplitude along the optimal subspace (i.e.
V_{1}
) and the forcing amplitude, could be another manner to derive Eq. (
46
).

•

Eventually, the method presented in this paper was applied, but not restricted to the Navier-Stokes equations. The scope of the proposed approach also covers other physical systems governed by other stochastic partial or ordinary differential equations, as long as the linearized operator presents a high degree of non-normality. This includes solid mechanics
[
110
]
, network science
[
111
,
112
,
113
,
114
]
, the modeling of music instruments
[
115
,
116
]
, pattern formation
[
117
,
118
]
, astrophysics
[
119
]
, population dynamics
[
120
]
, and many others fields of physics.
