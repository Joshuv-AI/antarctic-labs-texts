Fully nonlinear second-order mean field games with nondifferentiable Hamiltonians
Thomas Sales and Iain Smears

Abstract
We analyse fully nonlinear second-order mean field games (MFG) with nondifferentiable Hamiltonians, which take the form of a coupled system of a fully nonlinear Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation and a Kolmogorov–Fokker–Planck partial differential inclusion (PDI) featuring the set-valued subdifferential of the Hamiltonian. We show the existence of solutions of some stationary MFG systems with quite general coupling operators and nonnegative distributional source terms, on general bounded convex domains, under the primary assumptions of uniform ellipticity and the Cordes condition on the diffusion coefficient. The existence proof is founded on an original, and equivalent, reformulation of the PDI as a nonstandard variational inequality (VI), that offers significant flexibility in passages to limits. Furthermore, the uniqueness of the solution of the PDI/VI system is proved in the case of strictly monotone couplings. We then show how the MFG PDI/VI system in the fully nonlinear setting can be obtained as the limit of a sequence of PDE systems with differentiable Hamiltonians, and we give further results on the continuous dependence of the solution. Mathematics Subject Classification. 35J57, 35Q89, 49N80, 49J53.



Introduction

Fully nonlinear mean field games

Mean field games (MFG), introduced by Lasry & Lions
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
and, independently, by Huang, Malhamé & Caines
[
20
]
, are models of the Nash equilibria of dynamic differential games for large populations of players.
These can be described by a system of coupled nonlinear partial differential equations (PDE), including a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation (HJB), originating from the underlying optimal control problem of a typical player, and a Kolmogorov–Fokker–Planck (KFP) equation, describing the evolution of the density of players under the players’ controlled stochastic dynamics.
In models where the players’ controls only enter the deterministic part of the stochastic dynamics, with nondegenerate noise, the resulting MFG system consists of second-order quasilinear PDE.
We refer the reader to
[
1
]
for an introduction to the quasilinear setting.
However, in the more general case where the controls can also enter the diffusion terms, then the resulting PDE becomes fully nonlinear.

Consider, as model problems, fully nonlinear second-order elliptic MFG systems of the form

\displaystyle H(x,D^{2}u)

\displaystyle=F[m](x)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.1a)

\displaystyle D^{2}:\left(m\frac{\partial H}{\partial M}(x,D^{2}u)\right)

\displaystyle=G(x)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle u=0,\quad m

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(1.1c)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, denotes the state space of the game, where the function
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the value function, and where
m\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the density of players.
In the analysis below, we will assume that the domain
\Omega
is open, bounded and convex.
This will imply that the boundary
\partial\Omega
will be at least Lipschitz regular
[
19
, Corollary 1.2.2.3]
, but we will not make any further smoothness assumptions on
\partial\Omega
.
The Hamiltonian
H\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
, which appears above, is defined in terms of the underlying stochastic optimal control problem by

\displaystyle H(x,M)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{-a(x,\alpha):M-f(x,\alpha)\right\}

\displaystyle\forall(x,M)\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}},

(1.2)

where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the set of player controls, where
a\colon\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
is the controlled diffusion tensor, and where the function
f\colon\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the control-dependent component of the players cost.
Here,
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
denotes the space of real
d\times d
symmetric matrices and
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
is the subset of matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
that are additionally positive semidefinite.
To explain the notation in (
1.1b
), the partial derivative
\frac{\partial H}{\partial M}
refers to the matrix-valued partial derivative of
H
with respect to its second argument, with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, and
D^{2}:
denotes the double divergence operator, i.e.
D^{2}:W\coloneqq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}W_{ij}
for all sufficiently regular matrix-valued functions
W\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
.
Note that in order to write (
1.1b
), it is very often assumed that
H
is differentiable in
M
.
However, we shall avoid differentiability assumptions and instead allow
H
to be nondifferentiable in
M
, with the only assumptions on the data in (
1.2
) being that
\mathcal{A}
is a given compact metric space, and that the functions
a
and
f
are uniformly continuous over
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}
.
We will explain in much more detail below how the system (
1.1
) should be understood when
H
is not required to be differentiable in
M
.
The coupling term
F
is the part of the players’ cost functional that depends on the overall density of players; in the analysis we will allow it to be a possibly nonlinear, nonlocal operator mapping
L^{2}(\Omega)
into itself.
Regarding the boundary condition, we consider for simplicity homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions for
u
and
m
in the system (
1.1
) above. Dirichlet boundary conditions arise when the underlying stochastic optimal control problem of the players involves stopping at the first-exit time from
\Omega
, i.e. players exit the game upon reaching the boundary
\partial\Omega
.
The source term
G
appearing on the right-hand side of (
1.1b
) represents the source of new players entering the game.
Naturally, the problem (
1.1
) can be seen as a simplified steady-state system motivated by a corresponding time-dependent problem.

At present, there are only a handful of papers on fully nonlinear MFG.
Andrade and Pimentel
[
3
]
have studied some particular fully nonlinear MFG systems of potential type, where the system can be derived from the minimization of a global functional for the Hessian of the value function.
In
[
7
,
8
]
, Chowdhury, Jakobsen & Krupski have analysed time-dependent problems where the Hamiltonian takes the form
H(\mathcal{L}u)
, where
H\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{L}
is a possibly nonlocal diffusion operator tied to a Lévy process appearing in the stochastic dynamics; in the case of Brownian motion without deterministic drift, this reduces to a Hamiltonian of the form
H(\Delta u)
, cf.
[
7
, Footnote 3, p. 6306]
.
They have shown the existence of a classical–very-weak solution for the time-dependent system, for problems without boundary conditions, under suitable assumptions on the data.
In another recent work, Ignazio & Ricciardi
[
21
]
also consider problems on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, also in the setting of classical–very-weak solutions where the fully nonlinear part of the Hamiltonian is also depending only on the Laplacian
\Delta u
, rather than the full Hessian
D^{2}u
.
Ferreira and Gomes have also outlined in
[
14
, Section 7.1]
some possible approaches to showing existence of Minty weak solutions of fully nonlinear MFG if some global monotonicity structure is available, e.g. if the coupling operator
F
above is assumed to be monotone.

The analysis of the system (
1.1
) presents several challenges that motivate a different approach to prior works.
First, the system is not generally of potential type.
The coupling operator is also not required to be monotone for the existence theory below.
Second, the Hamiltonian
H
, defined in (
1.2
), is allowed to depend on the full Hessian
D^{2}u
, rather than only on its Laplacian
\Delta u
.
Third, the lack of smoothness of the boundary
\partial\Omega
generally limits the possible regularity (up to the boundary) of the solution.
In addition to the above, the most significant challenge that we address here is that, in many applications, the Hamiltonian
H
is generally not differentiable with respect to the Hessian variable
M
.
As a very simple example, consider
\mathcal{A}=[0,1]
and
a(x,\alpha)=(1+\alpha)I_{d}
, with
I_{d}
the
d\times d
identity matrix, and
f\equiv 0
, in which case
H(x,M)=\max\{-\Trace M,-2\Trace M\}
, which is clearly not differentiable for all
M
.
If
H
is nondifferentiable, then it is clear that the term involving
\frac{\partial H}{\partial M}
in the KFP equation (
1.1b
) might not exist as a well-defined function for general
u
, since the Hessian
D^{2}u
might take values at points of nondifferentiability of
H
on a set of positive measure in
\Omega
.
We stress that Lipschitz continuity of
H
is not sufficient to avoid this difficulty.
Therefore, when
H
is nondifferentiable, the system (
1.1
) must be understood in a suitably generalized sense, as we detail further below.

MFG with nondifferentiable Hamiltonians and main results

The analysis of MFG systems with nondifferentiable Hamiltonians has recently been developed in the sequence of papers
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
in the context of second-order quasilinear MFG systems.
It is well-known in optimal control that nondifferentiability of the Hamiltonian is related to the nonuniqueness of the optimal feedback controls for the underlying stochastic optimal control problem of the players.
The insight is that, in general, players occupying the same state may have to use different optimal feedback controls in order to sustain a Nash equilibrium.
The question of determining the players’ choices of controls then becomes implicitly part of the problem to be solved.
Mathematically, this is expressed by the generalization of the usual KFP equation as a
partial differential inclusion
(PDI) where the partial derivative of the Hamiltonian is replaced by its set-valued subdifferential.
We refer the reader in particular to
[
36
]
for both a heuristic derivation in terms of the underlying stochastic optimal control problems, and also a more rigorous analysis in the quasilinear setting showing that the resulting MFG PDI system is, in some sense, the limit of classical MFG PDE systems under regularization of the Hamiltonian.

In the context of the present problem (
1.1
), the appropriate generalization of the problem for nondifferentiable
H
is in terms of the PDI system: find a pair of functions
(u,m)
, with appropriate regularity, such that

\displaystyle H(x,D^{2}u)

\displaystyle=F[m](x)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.3a)

\displaystyle D^{2}:\left(-\overline{a}m\right)

\displaystyle=G(x)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(1.3b)

\displaystyle u=0,\quad m

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

(1.3c)

for some measurable function
\overline{a}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
satisfying

\displaystyle-\overline{a}(x)\in\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}u(x))

\displaystyle\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega,

(1.3d)

where
\partial_{M}H
denotes the subdifferential of
H
.
We will make the functional setting and problem statement more precise in Section
2
below, in particular see (
2.10
) for the precise formulation of (
1.3
).
The equation (
1.3b
) and inclusion (
1.3d
) above can be jointly expressed, at least formally, as

\displaystyle G\in D^{2}:\left(m\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}u)\right)

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega.

Observe that the matter of determining a suitable
\overline{a}
satisfying the inclusion condition (
1.3d
) is implicitly part of the problem to be solved, and such an
\overline{a}
is not necessarily unique; cf.
[
36
]
for some examples in the quasilinear setting.
It is also known from examples in the quasilinear setting that discontinuities in the coefficients appearing in the inclusion can lead to loss of interior regularity of the solution, see e.g.
[
31
, Example 2]
.
Note also that the PDI problem (
1.3
) simplifies to the usual PDE problem (
1.1
) if
H
is differentiable everywhere in
M
.

Our first main result, in Theorem
3.3
below, shows the existence of solutions of the PDI system (
1.3
), under appropriate assumptions on the problem data.
More precisely, we will work with strong–very-weak solutions with value function
u\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and density
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
The precise assumptions on the problem data are given in Section
2
below.
In Theorem
3.5
below, we also show the uniqueness of the solution if the coupling
F
is additionally strictly monotone, thus extending the well-known uniqueness result of Lasry & Lions.
We also show in Theorem
7.2
that the MFG PDI problem can be obtained as the limit of MFG PDE with regularized Hamiltonians, thus extending results of
[
36
]
to the fully nonlinear setting.

Approach to the analysis and further contributions

Let us now explain the motivation for several key features of our approach, and how these address the main challenges appearing in the analysis.
First, it is clear that the nondifferentiability of
H
, and the resulting lack of good continuity properties of the set-valued subdifferential maps, are major obstacles in the analysis.
For example, in a typical situation encountered below, we may have a sequence of functions
u_{j}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
converging to some limit
u
in the norm of
H^{2}(\Omega)
, and also a sequence of functions
m_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
that converges only weakly to some limit
m
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and we would like to pass to limits in the various terms appearing in (
1.3
).
A critical difficulty appears when considering some sequence of selections
\overline{a}_{j}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
such that
-\overline{a}_{j}(x)\in\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}u_{j}(x))
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, since it is easy to find examples where the lack of differentiability of
H
causes the sequence
\{\overline{a}_{j}\}_{j}
to be weakly-
*
converging along some subsequences, but have no subsequence that is converging strongly.
Given only weak convergence, it is difficult to establish any convergence properties of the sequence of products
\overline{a}_{j}m_{j}
that appear in the principal term of the KFP equation.
Note that this problem is more critical in the fully nonlinear case than in the quasilinear one treated in
[
31
,
33
,
35
,
36
]
, as in the latter case there is some additional regularity for the densities allowing for some strong compactness by the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem.

In order to address this difficulty, the first main ingredient of our approach is a reformulation of the PDI system, in particular the KFP inclusion, as a nonstandard variational inequality (VI) problem, see Section
2
below, in particular (
2.11
).
The VI problem takes a rather original form, as the subdifferential
\partial_{M}H
no longer makes an explicit appearance.
We emphasize that the VI formulation of the problem considered here differs substantially from the variational inequality problem in
[
14
]
, which requires differentiability of the Hamiltonian; see Remark
2.2
below for a more detailed explanation of the essential differences.
One of the central contributions of this work, stated in Theorem
3.1
, and its Corollary
3.2
, is that the PDI problem (
2.10
) and the VI problem (
2.11
) are equivalent under quite weak conditions on the problem data.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
is essentially founded on the verification of a minimax principle for some functional defined on the test functions and the measurable selections of the subdifferential, see Section
4
below.
The principal benefit of working with the VI formulation of the problem is that it greatly simplifies passing to limits in the various terms of the problem, thereby avoiding the difficulties described in the previous paragraph.
Note, however, that we do not work exclusively with the VI formulation, as certain key steps will make use also of the PDI form, so both points of view are complementary.
This forms the cornerstone of the proof of Theorem
3.3
on the existence of solutions, as it enables the verification of the hypotheses of Kakutani’s fixed point theorem for some suitably chosen set-valued map.
The VI formulation of the problem is also useful for handling various limiting arguments in Section
7
where we consider the effects of regularization and perturbation of the data.
More generally, it will become clear in the analysis below that the equivalence of PDI and VI formulations can be generalized to many other MFG systems, and not just the one considered here, cf. Remark
4.2
below, so we expect that it may be a useful tool for a much wider range of problems.

The second key ingredient of our approach is motivated by another difficulty that appears for fully nonlinear MFG systems with nondifferentiable Hamiltonians.
The problem is that, in the KFP equation (
1.3b
), the coefficient
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
will generally be discontinuous, regardless of the regularity of the value function
u
.
Thus the KFP equation involves the formal adjoint of a second-order elliptic operator in nondivergence form with a discontinuous coefficient.
It is well-known that the well-posedness of such equations is quite delicate: see
[
28
, p. 18]
for an example of nonuniqueness of strong solutions, and see also
[
29
,
38
]
for nonuniqueness of extended notions of solution, including viscosity solutions (with measurable ingredients).
We emphasize that these issues arise from the combination of discontinuity of the coefficient and the nondivergence structure of the second-order terms, in marked contrast to second-order elliptic equations in divergence form.
However, well-posedness of linear nondivergence form elliptic PDE with discontinuous coefficients (and their adjoints) can be recovered if the leading coefficients satisfy a Cordes condition
[
10
,
28
]
.
This motivates our assumption that the diffusion coefficients appearing in the Hamiltonian satisfy the Cordes condition, see (
2.13
) below for a precise statement.
Note that in the case of problems in two space dimensions, i.e.
d=2
, uniform ellipticity implies the Cordes condition (
2.13
), so the condition is only more restrictive than uniform ellipticity for
d\geq 3
.
As further motivation, it was shown by Süli and the second author in
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
that the Cordes condition also allows for an effective analysis of strong solutions of fully nonlinear HJB equations on nonsmooth convex domains, with good quantitative stability bounds in
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.
Problems with Cordes coefficients have since been studied in other contexts, e.g. fully nonlinear Isaacs equations
[
23
,
24
]
, Monge–Ampère equations
[
16
,
17
]
, and homogenization theory
[
6
,
15
,
44
]
.
We briefly detail several key well-posedness results for the HJB and KFP equations with Cordes coefficients, when considered separately, in Section
5
below.
We will also show below, in Theorem
5.3
and its Corollary
5.4
, that a comparison principle is available for operators with Cordes coefficients, which requires less integrability on the second-order derivatives of functions than the usual Aleksandrov–Bakelman–Pucci maximum principle when
d\geq 3
; this original comparison principle extends some ideas in
[
44
]
, which treated periodic boundary conditions on a torus, to Dirichlet boundary-value problems on nonsmooth convex domains.
The comparison principle shown here is, to the best of our knowledge, also another original contribution.
These results provide fundamental tools for the analysis of the MFG system.

The outline for this paper is as follows. Section
2
below sets out the notation and assumptions used for the analysis, and states the PDI and VI formulations of the problem.
The main results are gathered in Section
3
, including the theorems on the equivalence of the PDI and VI problems, as well as the existence and uniqueness of solutions.
Section
4
contains the proof of the equivalence of the PDI and VI formulations.
Then, in Section
5
, we summarize some key results on the HJB and KFP equations with Cordes coefficients on convex domains, alongside the original comparison principles for problems with Cordes coefficients.
The proofs of the existence and uniqueness results are given in Section
6
.
In Section
7
, we consider the properties of the MFG system under regularization of the Hamiltonian and perturbations of the data.
Appendix
A
contains the proof of the comparison principle.

Notation and formulation of the problem

Recall that
\Omega
is assumed to be an open, bounded, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.
Let
L^{2}(\Omega)
denote the usual space of Lebesgue measurable square-integrable extended real-valued functions on
\Omega
, and let
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\Omega}
denote the inner-product on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Let
L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
denote the subset of functions in
L^{2}(\Omega)
that are nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
.
For each integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
H^{k}(\Omega)\coloneqq W^{k,2}(\Omega)
and
H^{k}_{0}(\Omega)\coloneqq W^{k,2}_{0}(\Omega)
denote the usual Sobolev spaces, cf.
[
2
]
, equipped with their usual norms and inner products.
As we will be interested in strong solutions of the HJB equation (
1.1a
) satisfying a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
, we will work with the space
V
defined by

V\coloneqq H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(2.1)

The space
V
is a Hilbert space equipped with the same norm and inner-product as
H^{2}(\Omega)
.
The source term
G
appearing in (
1.3b
) will allowed to be distributional.
More precisely, we suppose that
G\in V^{*}
, where
V^{*}
denotes the dual space of
V
.
We will also frequently assume that
G
is nonnegative in the sense of distributions, by which we mean that
\left\langle G,v\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\geq 0
for all nonnegative a.e. functions
v\in V
, where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
denotes the duality pairing between
V
and
V^{*}
.
We assume that
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is a given operator.
To highlight the fact that
F
is possibly nonlinear and nonlocal, we write
F\colon m\mapsto F[m]
for each
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
In the analysis of the existence of solutions developed below, we will also assume that
F
is
completely continuous
, i.e. if
\{m_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a weakly converging sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega)
with weak limit
m
, then
F[m_{j}]\rightarrow F[m]
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
.
Note that completely continuous operators are bounded, i.e. they map bounded sets to bounded sets.
We do not require any growth condition on
F
.
As a remark, it is well-known that if the operator
F
is linear, then
F
is completely continuous if, and only if, it is compact, cf.
[
9
, Chapter VI]
.

Example 2.1
.

Simple examples of operators
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
that are completely continuous are provided by Hilbert–Schmidt operators of the form
F[m](x)=\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)m(y)\,\mathrm{d}y
for all
x\in\Omega
, where
K\in L^{2}(\Omega\times\Omega)
.

The Hamiltonian and its subdifferential.

Recall that the Hamiltonian
H
is defined in (
1.2
) above, and recall that the control set
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to be a compact metric space, and the functions
a\colon\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
and
f\colon\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
are assumed to be continuous.
It follows that
H
is real-valued, and also convex with respect to its second argument.
Furthermore, the hypotheses above also imply that
H
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
M
, in particular

\displaystyle\lvert H(x,M_{1})-H(x,M_{2})\rvert\leq L_{H}|M_{1}-M_{2}|\quad\forall M_{1},\,M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}},

(2.2)

where, in a slight abuse of notation,
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denotes the Frobenius norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and where the constant
L_{H}
is defined by

L_{H}\coloneqq\sup_{(x,\alpha)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}}|a(x,\alpha)|<\infty.

(2.3)

The continuity of the functions
a
and
f
on
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}
and the Lipschitz continuity of
H
, cf. (
2.2
) above, imply that the operator
H[\cdot]\colon V\to L^{2}(\Omega)
defined by

\displaystyle H[v](x)\coloneqq H(x,D^{2}v(x))

\displaystyle\text{a.e. }x\in\Omega,\quad\forall v\in V,

(2.4)

is well-defined, and is moreover also Lipschitz continuous.

Since
H
is real-valued, convex, and continuous in its second argument, its partial subdifferential
\partial_{M}H(x,M)
, defined by

\partial_{M}H(x,M)\coloneqq\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\colon H(x,M)+A:(\widetilde{M}-M)\leq H(x,\widetilde{M})\quad\forall\widetilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\},

(2.5)

is a nonempty, closed and convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
for each
(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
. Moreover, by Lipschitz continuity of
H
, the subdifferential
\partial_{M}H(x,M)\subset\overline{B_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}}(0,L_{H})}
the closed ball of radius
L_{H}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
w.r.t. the Frobenius norm.
The following characterization of the subdifferential sets is useful in order to deduce their properties in terms of the properties of the diffusion coefficient function
a
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, and let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
and
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
. Let
H
be defined by (
1.2
).
For each
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
and
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, let
\Lambda(x,M)\subset\mathcal{A}
be defined by

\Lambda(x,M)\coloneqq\argmax_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{-a(x,\alpha):M-f(x,\alpha)\right\}.

(2.6)

The set
\Lambda(x,M)
is nonempty and compact in
\mathcal{A}
.
For each
(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, we have

\partial_{M}H(x,M)=\Conv\left\{-a(x,\alpha):\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)\right\}.

(2.7)

Note that the set
\Lambda(x,M)
denotes the set of optimal feedback controls associated to
(x,M)
.
Thus, Proposition
2.1
shows that the subdifferential of the Hamiltonian is the convex hull of the set of opposite diffusion tensors corresponding to the optimal feedback controls.

Proof.

The result is perhaps well-known, so we include an elementary proof only for the sake of completeness.
It can be shown that the compactness of
\mathcal{A}
and the continuity of the functions
a
and
f
imply that the set-valued map
\Lambda\colon(x,M)\rightrightarrows\Lambda(x,M)
is upper semicontinuous, and that
\Lambda(x,M)
is nonempty and closed, hence compact, in
\mathcal{A}
for each
(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, see for instance
[
41
, Lemma 9]
.
We now show (
2.7
). Let
(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
be fixed but arbitrary.
By continuity of the data and compactness of
\Lambda(x,M)
, the set
\left\{-a(x,\alpha):\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)\right\}
is also compact, and hence the convex hull
\Conv\left\{-a(x,\alpha):\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)\right\}
is compact in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
.
It is straightforward to check that
\Conv\left\{-a(x,\alpha):\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)\right\}
is a subset of
\partial_{M}H(x,M)
.
To show the converse and complete the proof, it is enough to show that the complement of
\Conv\left\{-a(x,\alpha):\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)\right\}
is a subset of the complement of
\partial_{M}H(x,M)
.
To this end, let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\setminus\Conv\left\{-a(x,\alpha):\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)\right\}
be arbitrary.
Then, by the hyperplane separation theorem, there exists a
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, with
|W|=1
, and a
\rho>0
such that
-a(x,\alpha):W\leq A:W-\rho
for all
\alpha\in\Lambda(x,M)
.
Since
a
is continuous, there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
\Lambda(x,M)
such that
-a(x,\alpha):W\leq A:W-\rho/2
for all
\alpha\in U
.
Then, by upper semicontinuity of
\Lambda
, there is a
\delta>0
such that
\Lambda(x,\widetilde{M})\subset U
for all
\widetilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
such that
\lvert\widetilde{M}-M\rvert\leq\delta
.
It is then easy to check that
H(x,M+\delta W)\leq H(x,M)+A:(\delta W)-\delta\rho/2
, which shows that
A\not\in\partial_{M}H(x,M)
.
This shows (
2.7
) and completes the proof.
∎

We now define the set-valued map
\mathcal{D}H\colon V\rightrightarrows L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
by

\mathcal{D}H[v]\coloneqq\left\{A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})\colon A(x)\in\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}v(x))\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\Omega\right\},

(2.8)

for each
v\in V
.
In other words,
\mathcal{D}H[v]
is the set of all measurable selections of the set-valued map
x\mapsto\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}v(x))
.
It is known that
\mathcal{D}H[v]
is nonempty for each
v\in V
.
Indeed, for any
v\in V
, by
[
41
, Theorem 10]
, there exists a Lebesgue measurable
\alpha_{*}\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\alpha_{*}(x)\in\Lambda(x,D^{2}v(x))
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, where the set-valued map
\Lambda
was defined in (
2.6
) above.
Then, by Proposition
2.1
, we see that
x\mapsto-a(x,\alpha_{*}(x))\in\mathcal{D}H[v]
.
The fact that
\mathcal{D}H[v]
is necessarily a subset of
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
for all
v\in V
follows from the boundedness of the subdifferential sets as shown above.
In fact, it follows from (
2.2
) that the set-valued map
\mathcal{D}H[\cdot]
satisfies the stronger uniform boundedness property

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}H[v]\subset\{A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}):\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})}\leq L_{H}\}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V.

(2.9)

It is also immediately clear that
\mathcal{D}H[v]
is a convex set for any
v\in V
.
The following result shows that the graph of the set-valued map
\mathcal{D}H[\cdot]
is sequentially closed in a suitable sense.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\{v_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
V
and let
A_{j}\in\mathcal{D}H[v_{j}]
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
v_{j}\to v
in
V
and if
A_{j}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}A
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
as
j\to\infty
for some
v\in V
and
A\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
, then
A\in\mathcal{D}H[v]
.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward extension of
[
31
, Lemma 4.4]
.
∎

Observe that Lemma
2.2
together with the boundedness property (
2.9
) imply that
\mathcal{D}H[v]
is weak-
*
sequentially compact in
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
for any
v\in V
.

Remark 2.1
.

We emphasize the fact that, beyond Lemma
2.2
, the set-valued map
\mathcal{D}H
does not generally have good continuity properties, especially with regards to convergence in norm.
There are known examples of sequences of functions
\{v_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, with corresponding selections
A_{j}\in\mathcal{D}H[v_{j}]
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
A_{j}
has no Cauchy subsequence in any Lebesgue space
L^{p}
for any
p\geq 1
.
This creates significant difficulties when one wishes to pass to limits in various terms of the equations.

Formulations of the problem

We now introduce the two problem formulations of the MFG system.
The first formulation, which we name the PDI problem, is the precise problem formulation for the PDI system (
1.3
) discussed above.
This formulation is the natural extension from
[
31
]
to the fully nonlinear setting.
In the second formulation that we consider, the KFP equation is replaced by a variational inequality (VI).
We therefore refer to it as the VI problem.
As explained above, the motivation for considering the VI problem is flexibility with respect to passage to the limits, as will become clearer in the analysis below.
The theorem on equivalence of the two formulations is given in the next section.

Formulation of the PDI problem.

First, we consider the MFG PDI problem: find
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}(\Omega)
such that there exists an
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
for which

\displaystyle H[u]

\displaystyle=F[m]

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2.10a)

\displaystyle\left(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V.

(2.10b)

We shall refer to such a pair
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}(\Omega)
as a solution of the PDI problem, or, more informally, a
PDI solution
.
The HJB equation in (
2.10a
) is understood in the sense of strong solutions, i.e.
u\in V
solves
H[u](x)=F[m](x)
a.e. in
\Omega
, where we recall that
H[u](x)=H(x,D^{2}u(x))
for
x\in\Omega
.
The KFP equation (
2.10b
), which is given in very weak form, is obtained from (
1.3b
) after multiplication by a test function
v\in V
and integrating-by-parts twice the second-order derivative terms, and using the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions for both the test function
v
and for
m
to cancel the boundary terms.

Formulation of the VI problem.

Second, we define the VI problem: find
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle H[u]

\displaystyle=F[m]

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(2.11a)

\displaystyle\langle G,v-u\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\leq\left(m,H[v]-H[u]\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V.

(2.11b)

We say that a pair
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
that satisfies (
2.11
) is a solution of the VI problem, or, more informally, a
VI solution
.
As before, the HJB equation
H[u]=F[m]
in
\Omega
is understood in the sense of strong solutions.
It is straightforward to derive the VI problem (
2.11
) above from the PDI problem (
2.10
) when assuming the existence of a PDI solution
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
; the interested reader may skip ahead to Lemma
4.1
below.
The VI in (
2.11b
) can also be stated in abstract form as requiring that
G\in V^{*}
must be contained in the subdifferential, evaluated at
u
, of the
m
-dependent convex functional
V\ni v\mapsto(m,H[v])_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}
.

We emphasize that the definition of a VI solution above includes the requirement that
m\in L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
, i.e.
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
must also be nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
.
This condition is natural since the density
m
should be nonnegative to be physically meaningful, and since the nonnegativity of
m
is used in the derivation of the VI problem (
2.11
) from the PDI problem (
2.10
) that will be shown in Lemma
4.1
below.
This is in contrast to the definition of a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
), where the nonnegativity of the density
m
is not a requirement of the definition. It is instead usually deduced as a consequence of a comparison principle for KFP equations, under suitable assumptions on the problem data, e.g. if
G
is assumed to be nonnegative, cf. Corollary
5.4
below.

Remark 2.2

(Relation to other variational inequalities)
.

Variational inequalities for MFG systems have been analysed previously by Ferreira and Gomes in
[
14
]
, and also with Ucer in
[
13
]
, through the lens of monotone operator theory. They have obtained existence results for broad classes of MFG systems that have some global monotonicity structure.
Although their approach provides much insight and inspiration, for the avoidance of any confusion, we wish to clarify that the VI problem (
2.11
) above, along with our approach to the analysis, is substantially different.
First, the operator defining the variational inequality in
[
14
, Eq. (1.7)]
requires significantly more differentiability of the Hamiltonian, in particular that
H
must be differentiable in
M
with
\frac{\partial H}{\partial M}
additionally at least
C^{2}
regular with respect to the Hessian variable
[
14
, Section 7.2, p. 6003]
.
By contrast, the VI problem (
2.11
) is still meaningful for nondifferentiable Hamiltonians.
Second, the HJB equation in (
2.11
) is understood in the sense of strong solutions, rather than in the form of a variational inequality as is the case in
[
14
, Eq. (1.7)]
.
Finally, the analysis of existence of solutions that follows below does not require monotonicity of the coupling operator
F
.
On the other hand, we only focus here on the fully nonlinear second-order setting, whereas the references above also consider first-order problems.

Cordes coefficients

The analysis of existence and uniqueness of solutions of the PDI and VI problems stated above will focus on the class of problems with Cordes coefficients, as we now detail.
Recall that
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
denotes the set of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
For positive constants
\overline{\nu}\geq\underline{\nu}>0
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we define
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
to be the set of all matrices
\overline{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
that satisfy the uniform ellipticity condition

\displaystyle\underline{\nu}|\xi|^{2}\leq\xi^{T}\,\overline{a}\,\xi\leq\overline{\nu}|\xi|^{2}

\displaystyle\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(2.12)

as well as the Cordes condition

\frac{\lvert\overline{a}\rvert}{\Trace\overline{a}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1+\varepsilon}},

(2.13)

where it is recalled that
\lvert\cdot\rvert
denotes the Frobenius norm of matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Note that
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is a closed, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
.
Indeed, it is clear that
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
closed.
To check that
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is convex, note that, for any
a_{1}
,
a_{2}\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
, and any
\lambda\in[0,1]
, it is clear that
\lambda a_{1}+(1-\lambda)a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
also satisfies the uniform ellipticity condition (
2.12
); furthermore, the triangle inequality implies that

\lvert\lambda a_{1}+(1-\lambda)a_{2}\rvert\leq\lambda\lvert a_{1}\rvert+(1-\lambda)\lvert a_{2}\rvert\leq\frac{\Trace\left(\lambda a_{1}+(1-\lambda)a_{2}\right)}{\sqrt{d-1+\varepsilon}}.

(2.14)

The uniform ellipticity condition (
2.12
) implies that
\Trace\left(\lambda a_{1}+(1-\lambda)a_{2}\right)>0
, and thus we conclude from (
2.14
) that
\lambda a_{1}+(1-\lambda)a_{2}
also satisfies (
2.13
), and thus also belongs to
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
.
This shows that
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is convex.

The principal assumption that we will use in the analysis of existence and uniqueness of solutions of the MFG PDI and VI problems is that the problem has Cordes coefficients. More precisely, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 2.3
.

There exists constants
\overline{\nu}\geq\underline{\nu}>0
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
such that
a(x,\alpha)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
and all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 2.3
.

For problems in two space dimensions, i.e.
d=2
, the uniform ellipticity condition (
2.12
) implies the Cordes condition (
2.13
) for some
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu})\in(0,1]
, c.f
[
41
, Example 2]
.
The condition (
2.13
) only becomes more restrictive when
d\geq 3
.
Geometrically, the Cordes condition can be seen as defining a particular cone in the space
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
of symmetric matrices with vertex at the origin and principal axis passing through the identity matrix.

Remark 2.4
.

Following on from the previous remark, notice that uniformly elliptic Hamiltonians that depend only on the Laplacian
\Delta u
, rather than the full Hessian
D^{2}u
, are special cases of the class of Hamiltonians considered here.

Remark 2.5
.

In
[
10
]
, Cordes formulated his condition in terms of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
of the diffusion coefficient of the elliptic operator.
In particular, after adapting slightly the notation, the condition in
[
10
, Definition 1]
is written as
(d-1)\sum_{i<k}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}\leq(1-\widetilde{\epsilon})\left(\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)^{2}
for some positive
\widetilde{\epsilon}
, where
\sum_{i<k}
denotes a double sum over all indices
(i,k)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{2}
with
i<k
. For
\overline{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
with eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
, we have the elementary identities
\Trace\overline{a}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
and
\sum_{i<k}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{k})^{2}=d\lvert\overline{a}\rvert^{2}-\left(\Trace\overline{a}\right)^{2}
, thus the condition there is equivalent to
\frac{\lvert\overline{a}\rvert^{2}}{(\Trace\overline{a})^{2}}\leq\frac{d-\widetilde{\epsilon}}{d(d-1)}
, and since
d-\widetilde{\epsilon}<d
, we see that the above condition is equivalent to (
2.13
) for some
\varepsilon
depending on
\widetilde{\epsilon}
.

An important, although elementary, consequence of the convexity of the set
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is that the uniform ellipticity and the Cordes condition are preserved upon taking measurable selections from the subdifferential sets of the Hamiltonian.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.3
holds.
Then, for any
v\in V
and any
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[v]
, we have
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is a closed convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, and recall also Proposition
2.1
, in particular (
2.7
).
It is then clear that, for any
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, any
\overline{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
such that
-\overline{a}\in\partial_{M}H(x,M)
also satisfies
\overline{a}\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
.
This completes the proof, upon recalling that, by definition, any
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[v]
must satisfy
-\overline{a}(x)\in\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}v(x))
for almost every
x\in\Omega
.
∎

Main results

Equivalence between the PDI and VI problems

The first main result of this work is that the concepts of PDI solution and VI solution coincide for solution pairs where the density is nonnegative.

Theorem 3.1

(Equivalence of PDI and VI solutions)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
If
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
) and if
m\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, then
(u,m)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).
Conversely, if
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
), then
(u,m)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
).

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is given in Section
4
below.
Observe that, in Theorem
3.1
, a PDI solution
(u,m)
is shown to be a VI solution if
m
is additionally assumed to be nonnegative in
\Omega
.
In practice, the nonnegativity of
m
will be obtained by means of a comparison principle, cf. Section
5.3
below.
In particular, under the further conditions that Assumption
2.3
holds and that
G\in V^{*}
is nonnegative in the sense of distributions, the equivalence of the notions of PDI and VI solutions is complete.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be an open, bounded, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Let
G\in V^{*}
be nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
Then a pair
(u,m)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
) if and only if
(u,m)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).

The proof of Corollary
3.2
is given in Section
5.3
after showing a suitable comparison principle for solutions of the KFP equation.

Remark 3.1
.

We emphasize that Theorem
3.1
does not require many of the other assumptions that will be used later in this work.
In particular, Theorem
3.1
does not require the assumptions of uniform ellipticity or the Cordes condition in Assumption
2.3
, which only play a later role in the existence and uniqueness results.
The proof of Theorem
3.1
also does not require any properties of the coupling term
F
, nor does it require convexity of the domain
\Omega
.
As will soon become apparent, the main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
3.1
are merely the nonemptiness, convexity, and uniform boundedness of the sets
\mathcal{D}H[v]
for
v\in V
, along with the closed graph property expressed in Lemma
2.2
.
We therefore expect that the VI formulation of MFG systems may be a useful tool in many other contexts and other functional settings.

Existence of a solution

The second main result of this work is the existence of a PDI solution and, equivalently, a VI solution for the problem, still under the hypotheses that
\Omega
is convex, that
G
is nonnegative, and that Assumption
2.3
holds.
Since Corollary
3.2
holds under these hypotheses, the notions of PDI and VI solution coincide.
The principal additional assumption to those above for existence of a solution is that
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is completely continuous.

Theorem 3.3

(Existence of a solution)
.

Let
\Omega
be an open, bounded, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Let
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
be completely continuous.
Let
G\in V^{*}
be nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
Then there exists a solution
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
of the PDI problem (
2.10
) and, equivalently, of the VI problem (
2.11
).
Furthermore, any solution
(u,m)
satisfies the
a priori
bounds

\displaystyle\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}},

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.1b)

where the above constants depend only on
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon
,
\underline{\nu}
,
\overline{\nu}
, and, in the case of (
3.1b
), additionally on
F
,
\|f\|_{C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})}
, and on
\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
is given in Section
6
.

Remark 3.2
.

We leave the form of the dependence on
F
and
\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}
of the constant of the
a priori
bound (
3.1b
) implicit since we have not specified a growth condition on
F
.
However, the bound can be made explicit if
F
obeys some specific growth condition, since one can then simply use the bound (
5.4
) from Lemma
5.1
below.
For example, if one assumes a growth condition of the form
\left\lVert F[m]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{\kappa}
for some
\kappa\geq 0
, then one can show that
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(1+\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}^{\kappa}\right)
, for a constant that now only depends on
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon
,
\underline{\nu}
,
\overline{\nu}
, and
\|f\|_{C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})}
.

Uniqueness of the solution

Similar to the results on existence of solutions, we place ourselves again in the setting of Corollary
3.2
, whereby the notions of PDI and VI solution coincide.
Under the well-known Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition on the coupling term
F
, cf.
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, we are able to show the uniqueness of a solution for the PDI problem (
2.10
), and equivalently of the VI problem (
2.11
), even when
H
is not required to be differentiable.

Assumption 3.4
.

We assume that the operator
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is
strictly monotone
on
L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
, i.e. for all
m_{1},m_{2}\in L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
, if
\left(F[m_{1}]-F[m_{2}],m_{1}-m_{2}\right)_{\Omega}\leq 0
then
m_{1}=m_{2}
.

We emphasize that
F
is only required to be strictly monotone on the subset of nonnegative functions in
L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 3.5

(Uniqueness of the solution)
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Let the Hamiltonian
H
be defined by (
1.2
).
Let
G\in V^{*}
be nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
If the operator
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies Assumption
3.4
, then any solution
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}(\Omega)
of the PDI problem (
2.10
) and, equivalently, of the VI problem (
2.11
), is unique.

The proof of Theorem
3.5
is given in Section
6.2
below.
The first step towards its proof is to show that any two pairs of solutions necessarily have the same density.
The argument is essentially the well-known one of Lasry and Lions
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
, which takes an especially simple and elegant form when working with VI solutions, as we immediately demonstrate below.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that the operator
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
satisfies Assumption
3.4
.
If
(u_{i},m_{i})\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, are both solutions of the VI problem (
2.11
), then
m_{1}=m_{2}
.

Proof.

Under the hypotheses above, we have

\begin{split}0&\hskip 8.0pt=\hskip 8.0pt\left\langle G,u_{2}-u_{1}\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}+\left\langle G,u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\\
&\underset{\eqref{eq:KFP_VI}}{\leq}(m_{1},H[u_{2}]-H[u_{1}])_{\Omega}+(m_{2},H[u_{1}]-H[u_{2}])_{\Omega}\\
&\underset{\eqref{eq:HJB_strong_form_2}}{=}-(F[m_{1}]-F[m_{2}],m_{1}-m_{2})_{\Omega}.\end{split}

(3.2)

Thus
(F[m_{1}]-F[m_{2}],m_{1}-m_{2})_{\Omega}\leq 0
which implies that
m_{1}=m_{2}
by Assumption
3.4
.
∎

The remainder of the proof of Theorem
3.5
, which is detailed in Section
6.2
below, then consists of showing that any pair of VI solutions must also have the same value function, which will be obtained from the uniqueness of strong solutions in
V
of HJB equations with Cordes coefficients.

Remark 3.3
.

The uniqueness result above does not suppose, or imply, the uniqueness of elements from
\mathcal{D}H[u]
that appear in the KFP inclusion.
Indeed, it is known from the analysis of MFG PDI systems in the quasilinear setting that the uniqueness of these measurable selections is not generally guaranteed.
See
[
31
]
for further discussion and examples.
As a further point, it is also known from examples in the quasilinear setting
[
36
]
that uniqueness of the solution is not expected if the coupling
F
is only assumed to be monotone, rather than strictly monotone.
A brief explanation is that the case
F\equiv 0
, which is monotone but not strictly monotone, decouples the problem, resulting in a game of uncoupled players with possibly nonunique optimal controls, for which we cannot generally expect uniqueness of the density.

Thus, in light of the results above, we deduce the existence and uniqueness of a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
) and, equivalently, of the VI problem (
2.11
), whenever the hypotheses of Theorems
3.3
and
3.5
are satisfied.

Remark 3.4
.

It is possible to drop the hypothesis that
G
should be nonnegative in Theorem
3.5
if one is only interested in the uniqueness of solutions of the VI problem (
2.11
).
This is because the nonnegativity of the density is required as part of the definition of a VI solution.

Equivalence of the PDI and VI problems

In this section, we prove the equivalence of the PDI and VI problems, as stated in Theorem
3.1
.
Throughout this section, the existence of a solution is assumed, given that the existence of solutions is the subject of Theorem
3.3
.
The proof that PDI solutions with nonnegative densities are also VI solutions is quite straightforward, as we now show.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
) and that
m\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Then
(u,m)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).

Proof.

By the hypothesis that
(u,m)
solves (
2.10
), there exists an
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
such that
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
and
\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=\left(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}
for all
v\in V
.
Let
w\in V
be arbitrary and set
v=w-u
.
Since
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
, it follows from the definition of the subdifferential and from
-\overline{a}(x)\in\partial_{M}H(x,D^{2}u(x))
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
that

\displaystyle-\overline{a}:D^{2}v

\displaystyle=-\overline{a}:D^{2}(w-u)\leq H[w]-H[u]

\displaystyle\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega.

The hypotheses that
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
m\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
then imply that

\langle G,w-u\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=\left(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}(w-u)\right)_{\Omega}\leq\left(m,H[w]-H[u]\right)_{\Omega}.

Since
w\in V
is arbitrary, this shows that
(u,m)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).
∎

Our next goal is to show that the converse statement is true, i.e. any VI solution is a PDI solution.
Thus, if we suppose that
(u,m)
is a given VI solution, the task is to show the existence of an element
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
such that (
2.10b
) holds.
The first step in this direction is the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).
Then, for each
v\in V
, there exists an
\overline{a}
such that
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
and

\left(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}.

(4.1)

Proof.

Let
v\in V
be given. For each integer
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

w_{+j}\coloneqq u+\frac{1}{j}v,\quad w_{-j}\coloneqq u-\frac{1}{j}v.

(4.2)

By hypothesis,
(u,m)
satisfies (
2.11b
), so
\langle G,w_{\pm j}-u\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left(m,H[w_{\pm j}]-H[u]\right)_{\Omega}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Choose an arbitrary element
-\overline{a}_{\pm j}\in\mathcal{D}H[w_{\pm j}]
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, we have
H[w_{\pm j}]-H[u]\leq-\overline{a}_{\pm j}:D^{2}(w_{\pm j}-u)
a.e. in
\Omega
, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
m\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j}\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{j}\left(m,-\overline{a}_{+j}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{j}\langle G,-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{j}\left(m,-\overline{a}_{-j}:D^{2}(-v)\right)_{\Omega},

for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, after simplification, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(m,-\overline{a}_{-j}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}\leq\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left(m,-\overline{a}_{+j}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.3)

Since
w_{\pm j}\to u
in
V
as
j\to\infty
, and since
-\overline{a}_{\pm j}\in\mathcal{D}H[w_{\pm j}]
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we deduce from Lemma
2.2
and from the uniform boundedness of the sets
\mathcal{D}H[w_{\pm j}]
, cf. (
2.9
) above, that there exist elements
-\overline{a}_{+}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
and
-\overline{a}_{-}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
, and a subsequence such that
\overline{a}_{+j_{k}}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\overline{a}_{+}
and
\overline{a}_{-j_{k}}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\overline{a}_{-}
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
as
k\to\infty
.
We can then pass to the limit in (
4.3
) to find that

\left(m,-\overline{a}_{-}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}\leq\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left(m,-\overline{a}_{+}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}.

(4.4)

For each
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let
\overline{a}_{\lambda}\coloneqq\lambda\overline{a}_{+}+(1-\lambda)\overline{a}_{-}
.
Convexity of
\mathcal{D}H[u]
implies that
-\overline{a}_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
Combining the inequalities in (
4.4
) with the intermediate value theorem, we conclude that there exists a
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that
\left(m,-\overline{a}_{\lambda}:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

We emphasize that, in Lemma
4.2
, the element
\overline{a}
appearing in (
4.1
) is allowed to depend on the given test function
v
.
Thus, Lemma
4.2
does not immediately verify (
2.10b
).
However, if one is only interested in the case where
H
is assumed to be differentiable, in which case
\mathcal{D}H[u]
is a singleton set, then (
2.10b
) can be deduced immediately from Lemma
4.2
.

We now prove Theorem
3.1
on the equivalence between the notions of PDI and VI solutions.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Suppose that
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
is a given solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).
Let the functional
\mathcal{L}\colon\mathcal{D}H[u]\times V\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

\mathcal{L}(A,v)\coloneqq\left(m,A:D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}-\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\quad\forall(A,v)\in\mathcal{D}H[u]\times V.

(4.5)

The functional
\mathcal{L}
is affine in its first argument and linear in its second argument.
Next, we define the functional
\mathcal{J}\colon\mathcal{D}H[u]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

\mathcal{J}(A)\coloneqq\max_{v\in B_{V}}\mathcal{L}(A,v)\quad\forall A\in\mathcal{D}H[u],

(4.6)

where
B_{V}\coloneqq\{v\in V:\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq 1\}
denotes the closed unit ball in
V
.
It is clear that the maximum is achieved in (
4.6
), and that
\mathcal{J}
is nonnegative since
B_{V}
contains the origin and
\mathcal{L}(A,0)=0
for any
A\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
.
Note also that the boundedness of
\mathcal{D}H[u]
in
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
, cf. (
2.9
), implies that
\mathcal{J}
is also uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{D}H[u]
.
Recall that
\mathcal{D}H[u]
is convex and sequentially weak-
*
compact as a subset of
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}})
by Lemma
2.2
.
It is also straightforward to check that
\mathcal{J}
is sequentially weak-
*
lower-semicontinuous on
\mathcal{D}H[u]
.
Therefore, there exists an
A_{0}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
such that

\mathcal{J}(A_{0})=\min_{A\in\mathcal{D}H[u]}\mathcal{J}(A).

(4.7)

We will show shortly below that
\mathcal{J}(A_{0})=0
, which will be sufficient to conclude that
(u,m)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
). Indeed, once we have shown that
\mathcal{J}(A_{0})=0
, we can set
\overline{a}\coloneqq-A_{0}
and deduce that
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
and
(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v)_{\Omega}\leq\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
for all
v\in V
, which, by linearity in
v
, is equivalent to
(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v)_{\Omega}=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
for all
v\in V
. Thus it is enough to show that
\mathcal{J}(A_{0})=0
to deduce that the pair
(u,m)
solves the PDI problem (
2.10
).

We now show that
\mathcal{J}(A_{0})=0
.
Since
V
is a Hilbert space and since the map
v\mapsto\mathcal{L}(A,v)
is a bounded linear functional on
V
, the Riesz representation theorem shows that there exists a unique
v_{0}\in V
such that
(v_{0},v)_{V}=\mathcal{L}(A_{0},v)
for all
v\in V
, where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
denotes the
H^{2}
-inner product on
V
.
It is easy to check that
\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\mathcal{L}(A_{0},v_{0})=\mathcal{J}(A_{0})^{2}
.
We now apply Lemma
4.2
to find an
A_{1}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
such that

\mathcal{L}(A_{1},v_{0})=\left(m,A_{1}:D^{2}v_{0}\right)_{\Omega}-\langle G,v_{0}\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=0.

(4.8)

Let
A_{\lambda}\coloneqq(1-\lambda)A_{0}+\lambda A_{1}
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
, and note that
A_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
since
\mathcal{D}H[u]
is convex.
For each
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let
v_{\lambda}
denote the unique element in
V
that satisfies

(v_{\lambda},v)_{V}=\mathcal{L}(A_{\lambda},v)\quad\forall v\in V.

(4.9)

By choosing
\lambda=1
and the test function
v=v_{0}
in (
4.9
) above, we deduce from (
4.8
) that
(v_{1},v_{0})_{V}=0
, i.e.
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
are orthogonal in
V
.
Note also that (
4.9
) implies that
\left\lVert v_{\lambda}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\mathcal{J}(A_{\lambda})^{2}
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
It is furthermore clear that
v_{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)v_{0}+\lambda v_{1}
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
since
\mathcal{L}(A_{\lambda},v)=(1-\lambda)\mathcal{L}(A_{0},v)+\lambda\mathcal{L}(A_{1},v)
for all
v\in V
.
Therefore, by expanding the square, we find that, for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}(A_{\lambda})^{2}&=\left\lVert v_{\lambda}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=\left\lVert(1-\lambda)v_{0}+\lambda v_{1}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
&=(1-\lambda)^{2}\left\lVert v_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+2(1-\lambda)\lambda(v_{1},v_{0})_{V}+\lambda^{2}\left\lVert v_{1}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=(1-\lambda)^{2}\mathcal{J}(A_{0})^{2}+\lambda^{2}\mathcal{J}(A_{1})^{2},\end{split}

(4.10)

where, in passing to the last line above, we have used the orthogonality
(v_{1},v_{0})_{V}=0
that was shown above.
We recall in passing that
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
denotes the
H^{2}
-inner-product on
V
.
Thus (
4.10
) shows that the function
\lambda\mapsto\mathcal{J}(A_{\lambda})^{2}
is a quadratic polynomial in
\lambda
.
Since
A_{0}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
is a minimizer of the functional
\mathcal{J}
by (
4.7
), it is then clear that

0\leq\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\right|_{\lambda=0}\mathcal{J}(A_{\lambda})^{2}=-2\mathcal{J}(A_{0})^{2},

(4.11)

and thus we conclude that
\mathcal{J}(A_{0})=0
.
As explained above, this shows that
(u,m)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
).
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We have aimed to keep the techniques of the proof of Theorem
3.1
as elementary as possible for the sake of simplicity and accessibility.
However, at a more fundamental level, the essence of the proof of Theorem
3.1
can be seen as a verification of the minimax principle

\min_{A\in\mathcal{D}H[u]}\max_{v\in B_{V}}\mathcal{L}(A,v)=\max_{v\in B_{V}}\min_{A\in\mathcal{D}H[u]}\mathcal{L}(A,v),

(4.12)

for the Lagrangian functional
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
4.5
) above.
Observe that it is sufficient to establish (
4.12
) to conclude that a VI solution
(u,m)
is a PDI solution, since the left-hand side of (
4.12
) is necessarily nonnegative, and the right-hand side is nonpositive by Lemma
4.2
, and thus both sides must be identically zero.
Thus, Theorem
3.1
can also deduced from well-known minimax theorems, see e.g.
[
11
,
12
,
39
,
45
]
.
Given that minimax theorems are available in many functional settings under very general structural assumptions, it appears likely that the equivalence between PDI and VI formulations of MFG systems can be generalized to a wider range of problems.

Elliptic operators with Cordes coefficients

It is well-known that for a bounded convex domain
\Omega
, solutions of Poisson’s equation on
\Omega
with right-hand side in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, coupled with a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
, belong to the space
V\coloneqq H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, see for instance
[
19
, Theorem 3.2.1.2, p. 147]
.
Furthermore, in
[
22
, Theorem 10, p. 391]
, Kadlec gave an exact constant for the bound on the solution, in particular,

|v|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\forall v\in V,

(5.1)

where the
H^{2}
-seminorm is defined by
\lvert v\rvert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}|D^{2}v|^{2}\mathrm{d}x
, with
|D^{2}v|
denoting the Frobenius norm of the Hessian
D^{2}v
.
Indeed, the bound (
5.1
) is deduced from
[
22
, Theorem 10, p. 391]
where the constant
\alpha
appearing there simplifies to
\alpha=1
in the case of the Laplacian.
Using the Poincaré inequality, it is then straightforward to verify that

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\forall v\in V,

(5.2)

where the constant
C
depends only on
d
and
\diam(\Omega)
.
As a remark, in the earlier papers
[
40
,
41
]
, the inequality (
5.1
) was named after Miranda and Talenti, following the reference
[
28
]
, as the second author was not previously aware of
[
22
]
.

Fully nonlinear HJB equations

In this section, we recall some known results from
[
40
,
41
]
on the well-posedness of strong solutions in
V
of fully nonlinear HJB equations with Cordes coefficients.
Note that well-posedness results of a similar nature are also available for time-dependent HJB equations
[
42
]
, and also Isaacs equations
[
23
,
24
]
.
We assume below that
\Omega
is a bounded, convex open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that Assumption
2.3
holds.
Recall that the Hamiltonian defines an operator
V\ni v\mapsto H[v]\in L^{2}(\Omega)
defined in (
2.4
) above.
In this section, we consider the problem of finding a strong solution
u\in V
of a fully nonlinear HJB equation of the form

H[u]=\widetilde{F}\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(5.3)

where
\widetilde{F}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
is a given function. Observe that the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
is encoded in the choice of space
V
defined in (
2.1
) above.
The following result is a modest extension of
[
41
, Theorem 3]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded convex open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Then, for any
\widetilde{F}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique
u\in V
solving (
5.3
) pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
.
There exists a constant
C
, depending only on
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon
,
\underline{\nu}
,
\overline{\nu}
, and on
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})}
, such that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(1+\|\widetilde{F}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

(5.4)

Furthermore, there exists a constant
C
, depending only on
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon
,
\underline{\nu}
and
\overline{\nu}
, such that

\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\widetilde{F}_{1}-\widetilde{F}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\forall\widetilde{F}_{1},\,\widetilde{F}_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(5.5)

where
u_{i}
denotes the unique solution in
V
of
H[u_{i}]=\widetilde{F}_{i}
in
\Omega
, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

The proof essentially follows
[
41
, Section 3]
, so we outline the main ideas of the proof for completeness.
The principal idea is to apply the Browder–Minty theorem for the well-posedness of a strongly monotone operator equation that is obtained by renormalization of the nonlinear Hamiltonian and testing against the Laplacians of test functions.
We define the function
\gamma\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
by
\gamma(x,\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{\Trace a(x,\alpha)}{\lvert a(x,\alpha)\rvert^{2}}
for all
(x,\alpha)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}
.
Note that continuity of
\gamma
follows from uniform ellipticity (
2.12
), and moreover that there exists a
\gamma_{0}>0
, depending only on
\underline{\nu}
and
\overline{\nu}
, such that
\gamma\geq\gamma_{0}
on
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}
.
It is furthermore easy to verify that
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})}
can be bounded solely in terms of the constants
\underline{\nu}
and
\overline{\nu}
.
Let
\widetilde{F}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be fixed but arbitrary.
We now define the operator
V\ni v\mapsto H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[v]\in L^{2}(\Omega)
by

H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[v](x)\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\big\{\gamma(x,\alpha)\big(-a(x,\alpha):D^{2}v(x)-f(x,\alpha)-\widetilde{F}(x)\big)\big\}\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

(5.6)

Observe that the operator is well-defined, i.e.
H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[v]
is Lebesgue measurable and belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
v\in V
and all
\widetilde{F}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, since the map
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}\ni(x,M,z)\mapsto\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\gamma(x,\alpha)\left(-a(x,\alpha):M-f(x,\alpha)-z\right)\right\}\in\mathbb{R}
is continuous in
x
and Lipschitz continuous in
(M,z)
, and the functions
\gamma
,
a
and
f
are uniformly bounded over
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}
.
Since
\mathcal{A}
is compact, since the functions
\gamma
,
a
and
f
are continuous, and since
\gamma
is bounded from above and also from below away from zero, i.e.
\gamma\geq\gamma_{0}>0
on
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}
for some constant
\gamma_{0}
, it is follows that a function
u\in V
solves
H[u]=\widetilde{F}
pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
if and only if
H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[u]=0
pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
.
The argument to show this is from
[
41
]
, which we briefly recall here: for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, it is clear that
H[u](x)=\widetilde{F}(x)
if, and only if,
-a(x,\alpha):D^{2}u(x)-f(x,\alpha)-\widetilde{F}(x)\leq 0
for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, with equality achieved by some
\alpha
. This is equivalent to
\gamma(x,\alpha)\left(-a(x,\alpha):D^{2}u(x)-f(x,\alpha)-\widetilde{F}(x)\right)\leq 0
for all
\alpha
, again with equality achieved by some
\alpha
, which is equivalent to
H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[u](x)=0
.

Furthermore, since the Laplacian
\Delta\colon V\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is bijective for convex
\Omega
, it is clear that
H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[u]=0
pointwise a.e. in
\Omega
if and only if

\langle\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[u],v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=0\quad\forall v\in V,

(5.7)

where the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}\colon V\to V^{*}
is defined by
\langle\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[u],v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\coloneqq(H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[u],-\Delta v)_{\Omega}
for all
v\in V
.
Thus the problems (
5.3
) and (
5.7
) are equivalent.

We now show show that
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}
is strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous on
V
, so the existence and uniqueness of the solution of (
5.7
) follows from the Browder–Minty theorem
[
37
, Theorem 10.49, p 364]
.
Under the Cordes condition (
2.13
), we follow the arguments in
[
41
, Lemma 1]
to find that

\begin{split}|H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[w](x)-H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[v](x)+\Delta(w-v)(x)|&\leq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\lvert\left(I_{d}-\gamma(x,\alpha)a(x,\alpha)\right):D^{2}(w-v)(x)\rvert\\
&\leq\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\,\lvert D^{2}(w-v)(x)\rvert,\end{split}

(5.8)

for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, and all
w
,
v\in V
, where
I_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denotes the identity matrix.
Therefore, we deduce from (
5.1
) and (
5.8
) that
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}
is strongly monotone on
V
with

\langle\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[w]-\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[v],w-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\\
=\left\lVert\Delta(w-v)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-(H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[w]-H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[v]+\Delta(w-v),\Delta(w-v))_{\Omega}\\
\geq\left(1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\right)\left\lVert\Delta(w-v)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}{C^{2}}\left\lVert w-v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},

(5.9)

where
C
is the constant appearing in (
5.2
).
It is also straightforward to check that
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}
is Lipschitz continuous from
V
to
V^{*}
.
Therefore, we may apply the Browder–Minty theorem to find that there exists a unique
u\in V
that solves (
5.7
), and thus, equivalently, that solves (
5.3
).
The bound (
5.4
) is easily found from

\frac{1-\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}}{C^{2}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq-\langle\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[0],u\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq C\big\lVert H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[0]\big\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)},

(5.10)

which is a consequence of (
5.9
), and from the bound
|H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}}[0](x)|\leq\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})}(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})}+|\widetilde{F}(x)|)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
We now prove the Lipschitz continuity of the inverse map (
5.5
).
For each
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
\widetilde{F}_{i}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be given, and let
u_{i}\in V
denote the corresponding unique solution of
H[u_{i}]=\widetilde{F}_{i}
in
\Omega
, and, equivalently, of
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{i}}[u_{i}]=0
in
V^{*}
.
We use the strong monotonicity (
5.9
) to find that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{C}\left\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}&\leq\langle\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{1}]-\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{2}],u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\\
&=\langle\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{1}]-\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{1}}[u_{1}],u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle_{V^{*}\times V},\end{split}

(5.11)

where the constant
C
depends only on
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
and on
\varepsilon
, and where, in passing to the second equality above, we have used the fact that
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{2}]=0=\mathcal{H}_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{1}}[u_{1}]
. The inequality
|\sup_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}-\sup_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}|\leq\sup_{\alpha}|x^{\alpha}-y^{\alpha}|
, for arbitrary bounded sets of real numbers
\{x^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}},\{y^{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathbb{R}
, implies that
|H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{1}](x)-H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{1}](x)|\leq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}|\gamma(x,\alpha)(\widetilde{F}_{2}(x)-\widetilde{F}_{1}(x))|
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
Therefore
|H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{2}}[u_{1}]-H_{\gamma,\widetilde{F}_{1}}[u_{1}]|\leq C|\widetilde{F}_{1}-\widetilde{F}_{2}|
a.e. in
\Omega
, for a constant
C
depending only on
\underline{\nu}
and
\overline{\nu}
.
Therefore, we obtain (
5.5
) by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to (
5.11
), and using the above bounds to simplify.
∎

Kolmogorov–Fokker–Planck equations

We now turn to KFP equations with discontinuous coefficients of the general form: find
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
such that

\displaystyle(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v)_{\Omega}=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,

(5.12)

where
G\in V^{*}
, and
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
are given as data, satisfying the condition
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, where it is recalled that
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is the subset of matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+}
that satisfy the uniform ellipticity condition (
2.12
) and the Cordes condition (
2.13
).
Recall that (
5.12
) corresponds to the weak form of the KFP equation
-D^{2}{:}(\overline{a}m)=G
in
\Omega
with
m=0
on
\partial\Omega
, with
D^{2}{:}
denoting the double divergence operator, after integration-by-parts.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
Then, for each
G\in V^{*}
, there exists a unique
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
that solves (
5.12
).
Moreover, there exists a constant
C
, depending only
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon
,
\underline{\nu}
and
\overline{\nu}
, such that

\|m\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|G\|_{V^{*}}.

(5.13)

Proof.

We consider the pair of Hilbert spaces
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
V
and the bilinear form
B\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\times V\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

B(m,v)\coloneqq(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v)_{\Omega}\quad\forall(m,v)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\times V.

It is clear that
B
is bounded on
L^{2}(\Omega)\times V
since
\overline{a}
is essentially bounded on
\Omega
.
By
[
40
, Theorem 3]
, cf. also
[
28
, Theorem 1.2.1]
, for each
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exists a unique
v_{g}\in V
such that
-\overline{a}:D^{2}v_{g}=g
a.e. in
\Omega
, and furthermore
\left\lVert v_{g}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for a constant
C>0
depending only on
d
,
\diam(\Omega)
,
\varepsilon
,
\underline{\nu}
and
\overline{\nu}
.
This immediately implies the inf-sup conditions

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v\in V\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B(m,v)}{\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\forall m\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(5.14a)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{m\in L^{2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{B(m,v)}{\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V,

(5.14b)

with the same constant
C
above.
Indeed, (
5.14a
) follows from the choice
g=m
, and
(
5.14b
)
follows from the choice
g=-\overline{a}:D^{2}v
.
We may therefore apply the inf-sup theorem of Nečas
[
30
, Theorem 3.1]
to conclude that, for any
G\in V^{*}
, there exists a unique
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
that solves
B(m,v)=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
for all
v\in V
, i.e.
m
solves (
5.12
). The bound (
5.13
) follows immediately from (
5.14
).
∎

Comparison principles for operators with Cordes coefficients

The following result, which appears to be original, gives a comparison principle for nondivergence form elliptic equations with discontinuous coefficients that satisfy the Cordes condition, on general bounded convex domains with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, bounded, convex set.
Let
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
Let
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and let
v_{g}\in V
be the unique strong solution of
-\overline{a}:D^{2}v_{g}=g
in
\Omega
,
v_{g}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
If
g
is nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
, then
v
is nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
.

Recall that the existence and uniqueness of the solution
v_{g}\in V
in the statement of Theorem
5.3
is well-known, cf.
[
28
,
40
]
.
The original aspect of Theorem
5.3
is the conclusion of nonnegativity of the solution for nonnegative data.
Since the proof of Theorem
5.3
is rather technical, we postpone it to Appendix
A
.

Remark 5.1
.

It is known that the conclusion of Theorem
5.3
generally fails if one drops the hypothesis that the coefficient
\overline{a}
satisfies the Cordes condition (
2.13
), whenever
d\geq 3
.
Indeed, a family of examples is given in
[
28
, p. 18]
of linear uniformly elliptic equations on the unit ball
B_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
arbitrary, with discontinuous coefficients that do not satisfy the Cordes condition, such that, in each case, there exist two different strong solutions in
H^{2}(B_{1})\cap H^{1}_{0}(B_{1})
. By linearity, these give counterexamples for any comparison principle.
Moreover, it is shown in
[
28
, p. 20]
that the Cordes condition is essentially sharp for these examples, since the coefficients are discontinuous at a single point, and come arbitrarily close to satisfying the Cordes condition.
This explains the need for the additional assumption of the Cordes condition in Theorem
5.3
.
Note that this stands in contrast to the Aleksandrov–Bakelman–Pucci maximum principle, which only requires uniform ellipticity, but only concerns functions in
W^{2,d}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
, i.e. functions with higher local integrability of the second-order derivatives when
d\geq 3
.

The principal use of Theorem
5.3
for our purposes is that it entails the following comparison principle for very weak solutions of KFP equations related to the MFG system.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded convex open set.
Let
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for almost every
x\in\Omega
.
Let
G\in V^{*}
and let
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
denote the unique solution of (
5.12
).
If
G
is nonnegative in the sense of distributions, then
m
is nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of the solution
m
is shown in Lemma
5.2
.
Let
\chi_{\{m<0\}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
denote the indicator function of the set
\{x\in\Omega:m(x)<0\}
, and let
v_{*}\in V
denote the unique strong solution of
-\overline{a}:D^{2}v_{*}=\chi_{\{m<0\}}
in
\Omega
with
v_{*}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
Theorem
5.3
implies that
v_{*}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Then, using the test function
v_{*}
in (
5.12
) leads to

0\leq\langle G,v_{*}\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=\left(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v_{*}\right)_{\Omega}=\int_{\{m<0\}}m\,\mathrm{d}x=-\left\lVert m^{-}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

where
m^{-}
denotes the negative part of
m
, i.e.
m^{-}\coloneqq\max\{-m,0\}
.
We conclude that
m^{-}=0
a.e. in
\Omega
and thus
m
is nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
.
∎

We now prove Corollary
3.2
.

Proof of Corollary
3.2
.

By Theorem
3.1
, it is enough to prove that if
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
), then
m\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
By definition, there exists an
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
such that
(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v)_{\Omega}=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
for all
v\in V
.
By Lemma
2.4
, we have
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
. Therefore, using the hypothesis that
G\in V^{*}
is nonnegative, we may apply Corollary
5.4
to conclude that
m\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
∎

Existence and uniqueness of solutions of fully nonlinear MFG PDI with Cordes coefficients

Proof of the existence of a solution

In this section, we place ourselves in the setting of the hypotheses of Theorem
3.3
.
We will show the existence of solutions of the VI problem, and equivalently of the PDI problem, by applying the Kakutani fixed point theorem to a suitably defined set-valued map, which we now construct in several steps.
Recall that Lemma
5.1
shows that, for each
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique strong solution
u\in V
of the HJB equation
H[u]=F[m]
in
\Omega
.
This defines the solution map
T\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to V
, i.e.
T\colon m\mapsto T(m)
for each
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with

\displaystyle T(m)

\displaystyle\coloneqq u,

\displaystyle H[u]

\displaystyle=F[m]\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(6.1)

We also know from Lemma
5.1
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert T(m_{1})-T(m_{2})\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert F[m_{1}]-F[m_{2}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\forall m_{1},\,m_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(6.2)

for a constant
C
independent of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
.

We now define a set-valued map
S\colon V\rightrightarrows L^{2}(\Omega)
as follows.
Recalling the bound (
5.13
), let
\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\{m\in L^{2}(\Omega):\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}\}
denote the closed ball of radius
R=C\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}
centred at the origin in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, where the constant
C
is the same as the one in (
5.13
).
For each
v\in V
, let
S(v)
denote the set of all
\widetilde{m}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
that satisfy

\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{B},\quad\widetilde{m}\geq 0\text{ a.e.\ in }\Omega,\quad\langle G,w-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left(\widetilde{m},H[w]-H[v]\right)_{\Omega}\quad\forall w\in V.

(6.3)

We will show that
S(v)
is nonempty for all
v\in V
as part of Lemma
6.1
below.
Note that, by definition,
S(v)\subset L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{B}
for all
v\in V
, hence the sets
S(v)
,
v\in V
, are uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
by definition.
The following lemma shows several essential properties of the set-valued map
S
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\Omega
be an open, bounded, convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Let
G\in V^{*}
be nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
Then, for each
v\in V
, the set
S(v)
is a nonempty, bounded, closed, convex subset of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
v\in V
be fixed but arbitrary.
The set
S(v)
is bounded since
S(v)\subset\mathcal{B}
by definition.
To show that
S(v)
is nonempty for any
v\in V
, we use the fact that
\mathcal{D}H[v]
is nonempty and choose an
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[v]
.
Lemma
2.4
shows that
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
.
Therefore, we may apply Lemma
5.2
to deduce that there exists a unique
\widetilde{m}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
that solves
\left(\widetilde{m},-\overline{a}:D^{2}w\right)_{\Omega}=\langle G,w\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
for all
w\in V
.
Furthermore, the bound (
5.13
) implies that
\left\lVert\widetilde{m}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}
so
\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{B}
.
It is additionally clear from Corollary
5.4
that
\widetilde{m}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, since
G
is nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
Moreover we find that
\langle G,w-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq(\widetilde{m},H[w]-H[v])_{\Omega}
by following the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
This shows that
\widetilde{m}\in S(v)
and thus
S(v)
is nonempty.
The set
S(v)
is also convex, since, for any
m_{0}
and
m_{1}
in
S(v)
, and any
\lambda\in[0,1]
, then
(1-\lambda)\widetilde{m}_{0}+\lambda\widetilde{m}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}\cap L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
, and also, for any test function
w\in V
,

((1-\lambda)\widetilde{m}_{0}+\lambda\widetilde{m}_{1},H[w]-H[v])_{\Omega}=(1-\lambda)(\widetilde{m}_{0},H[w]-H[v])_{\Omega}+\lambda(\widetilde{m}_{1},H[w]-H[v])_{\Omega}\\
\geq(1-\lambda)\langle G,w-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}+\lambda\langle G,w-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}=\langle G,w-v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}.

(6.4)

Thus
(1-\lambda)\widetilde{m}_{0}+\lambda\widetilde{m}_{1}\in S(v)
for all
\widetilde{m}_{0}
and
\widetilde{m}_{1}
in
S(v)
and all
\lambda\in[0,1]
, so
S(v)
is convex.
It is furthermore clear that
S(v)
is closed in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
∎

We now define the set valued map
\Phi\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\rightrightarrows L^{2}(\Omega)
as

\Phi(m)\coloneqq S(T(m))\quad\forall m\in L^{2}(\Omega),

(6.5)

where it is recalled that
T
, respectively
S
, is defined in (
6.1
), respectively (
6.3
), above.
It is clear that a pair
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
) if and only if
m
is a fixed point of
\Phi
, i.e.
m\in\Phi(m)
, and
u=T(m)
is the solution of the corresponding HJB equation.
We will thus prove Theorem
3.3
on the existence of solutions of the PDI and VI problems by applying Kakutani’s fixed point theorem to the set-valued map
\Phi
defined above.
For completeness, we recall Kakutani’s theorem below, and refer the reader to
[
45
, Theorem 9.B, p. 452]
for its proof.

Theorem 6.2

(Kakutani’s fixed point theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
is a nonempty, compact, convex set in a locally convex space
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{B}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{B}
be a set-valued map such that
\Phi(m)
is nonempty, closed and convex for all
m\in\mathcal{B}
. Suppose that
\Phi
is upper semicontinuous.
Then
\Phi
has a fixed point in
\mathcal{B}
, i.e. there exists an
m\in\mathcal{B}
such that
m\in\Phi(m)
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.3
on the existence of solutions of the PDI and VI problems.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We show that the set-valued map
\Phi
defined in (
6.5
) above satisfies all the hypotheses of Theorem
6.2
.
Consider the space
L^{2}(\Omega)
equipped with the weak topology, which is a locally convex topological vector space.
We take here
\mathcal{B}=\{m\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}\}
, where the constant
C
is from (
5.13
).
Hence
\mathcal{B}
is nonempty, convex and compact in the weak topology of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Note that the separability of
L^{2}(\Omega)
implies that the weak topology on
\mathcal{B}
is metrizable.
Recall that the set-valued map
S
maps elements of
V
to subsets of
\mathcal{B}
by definition, cf. (
6.3
) above.
Hence
\Phi\colon\mathcal{B}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{B}
.
Furthermore, by Lemma
6.1
above, for any
m\in\mathcal{B}
, the set
\Phi(m)=S(T(m))\subset\mathcal{B}
is nonempty, (strongly) closed, and convex.
Thus, by Mazur’s theorem, the set
\Phi(m)
is also closed in the weak topology of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, for any
m\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

It remains only to show that
\Phi
is upper-semicontinuous.
Since
\Phi\colon\mathcal{B}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{B}
with nonempty, closed, convex images, with
\mathcal{B}
compact in the weak topology, the upper-semicontinuity of
\Phi
is equivalent to proving that
\Phi
has closed graph, see
[
4
, Corollary 1, p. 42]
.
In other words, for any given sequence
\{(m_{j},\widetilde{m}_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}
, such that
\widetilde{m}_{j}\in\Phi(m_{j})
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and such that
m_{j}\rightharpoonup m
and
\widetilde{m}_{j}\rightharpoonup\widetilde{m}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
, it is enough to show that
\widetilde{m}\in\Phi(m)
.
Suppose that we are given such a sequence
\{(m_{j},\widetilde{m}_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Note that
\widetilde{m}_{j}\in\Phi(m_{j})
implies that
\widetilde{m}_{j}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus Mazur’s theorem implies that
\widetilde{m}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
It is also clear that
\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Let us denote
u_{j}\coloneqq T(m_{j})
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
u\coloneqq T(m)
.
The complete continuity of
F
implies that
F[m_{j}]\to F[m]
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and hence also that
H[u_{j}]\rightarrow H[u]
, in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
j\to\infty
.
It also follows from (
6.2
) that
u_{j}\to u
strongly in
V
as
j\to\infty
.
Since, by hypothesis, we have
\widetilde{m}_{j}\in\Phi(m_{j})
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we get

\langle G,v-u_{j}\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left(\widetilde{m}_{j},H[v]-H[u_{j}]\right)_{\Omega}\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N},\quad\forall v\in V.

(6.6)

It then follows from the weak convergence
\widetilde{m}_{j}\rightharpoonup\widetilde{m}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, the strong convergence
H[u_{j}]\to H[u]
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and the strong convergence
u_{j}\to u
in
V
as
j\to\infty
, that we can pass to the limit in (
6.6
) to find that

\langle G,v-u\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left(\widetilde{m},H[v]-H[u]\right)_{\Omega}\quad\forall v\in V.

(6.7)

Recalling that
\widetilde{m}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
was already shown above, we conclude from (
6.7
) that
\widetilde{m}\in S(u)=\Phi(m)
, and thus
\Phi
is upper semicontinuous.
Therefore, Theorem
6.2
shows that there exists an
m\in\mathcal{B}
such that
m\in\Phi(m)
.
It is then clear that
(u,m)\coloneqq(T(m),m)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
).
By Theorem
3.1
, the VI solution
(u,m)
is equivalently a solution of the PDI problem (
2.10
).

We now turn towards the proof of the
a priori
bounds (
3.1b
) and (
3.1a
) for generic solutions.
Let
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
be any solution of the VI problem (
2.11
), and, equivalently, of the PDI problem (
2.10
).
By definition, there exists an
\overline{a}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
such that
-\overline{a}\in\mathcal{D}H[u]
and
(m,-\overline{a}:D^{2}v)_{\Omega}=\langle G,v\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}
for all
v\in V
.
Since
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
by Lemma

, we may apply Lemma
5.2
to deduce that
\left\lVert m\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\lVert G\right\rVert_{V^{*}}
, which is (

).
Then, the bound (

) of Lemma

shows that
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\Bigl(1+\left\lVert F[m]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\Bigr)
.
Since the operator

is completely continuous, and thus maps bounded sets to bounded sets, we have
\left\lVert F[m]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\sup_{\widetilde{m}\in\mathcal{B}}\left\lVert F[\widetilde{m}]\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\infty
.
This implies (

).
∎

Remark 6.1
.

Although the analysis above for showing the existence of solutions makes greater use of the VI formulation of the problem, the attentive reader may notice that the PDI point of view plays a key role in showing the nonemptiness of the sets
S(v)
in the proof of Lemma

.
This is, of course, an essential step in the analysis towards applying the Kakutani fixed point theorem.
It is thus seen that both points of view are complementary.

Proof of the uniqueness of the solution

Recall that for the uniqueness of the solution, the coupling term

is assumed to satisfy the strict monotonicity condition of Assumption

.
The proof of Theorem

is a straightforward consequence of the results shown above.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Recall that, by Corollary
3.2
any PDI solution is a VI solution and thus it is enough to consider only the uniqueness of the solution of the VI problem.
Recall that Lemma
3.6
shows that, if
(u_{i},m_{i})\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
are both solutions of the VI problem (
2.11
) for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
m_{1}=m_{2}
.
Then, the uniqueness of the solution of the corresponding HJB equation, cf. Lemma

, implies that

.
∎

Continuous dependence under regularization and perturbation of the data

We now demonstrate the usefulness and flexibility of the equivalence of PDI and VI formulations of the problem in analysing further properties of the MFG system.
In particular, the VI formulation allows for a very effective and simple treatment of limiting arguments in the absence of compactness on the density.
More specifically, we consider continuous dependence results with regards to perturbation of the problem data.

Regularization of the Hamiltonian

We show below how the the PDI/VI problems for MFG with nondifferentiable Hamiltonians arise naturally as the limit of MFG PDE with differentiable Hamiltonians.
Consider a family of regularized problems of the form: find
(u_{\lambda},m_{\lambda})\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
that solve

\displaystyle H_{\lambda}[u_{\lambda}]

\displaystyle=F[m_{\lambda}]

\displaystyle\text{a.e. in }\Omega,

(7.1a)

\displaystyle\left(m_{\lambda},\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial M}(x,D^{2}u_{\lambda})\colon D^{2}v\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\left\langle G,v\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V.

(7.1b)

where
\lambda\in(0,1]
is a regularization parameter, and where
H_{\lambda}\colon\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a regularized approximation of
H
with
H_{\lambda}\to H
as
\lambda\to 0
, see (
7.3
) below.
More precisely, we will make the following assumptions on the family of regularized Hamiltonians
\{H_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in(0,1]}
.

Assumption 7.1
.

The family of functions
\{H_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in(0,1]}
is such that, for each
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the following hold:

•

the function
H_{\lambda}
is continuous on
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
, and such that

|H_{\lambda}(x,M)-H_{\lambda}(x,N)|\leq C|M-N|\quad\forall(x,M,N)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+},

(7.2)

for some constant
C
independent of
\lambda
.

•

for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
, the map
M\mapsto H_{\lambda}(x,M)
is convex and continuously differentiable with partial derivative
\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial M}\colon\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
;

•

there exists a continuous function
\omega\colon[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\omega(0)=0
and

|H_{\lambda}(x,M)-H(x,M)|\leq\omega(\lambda)\quad\forall(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}};

(7.3)

•

and the partial derivative
\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial M}
satisfies

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial M}(x,M)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)\quad\forall(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A}.

(7.4)

Note that (
7.3
) signifies uniform convergence of the Hamiltonians.
Moreover, the condition
\lambda\in(0,1]
is not essential, as it could be replaced more generally by any bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}
with
0
being a limit point.

Example 7.1
.

A suitable example of a family of regular Hamiltonians satisfying Assumption
7.1
is provided by Moreau–Yosida regularization, also known as Moreau envelope, where, for each
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the function
H_{\lambda}
is defined by

H_{\lambda}(x,M)\coloneqq\min_{N\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}}\left\{H(x,N)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}|N-M|^{2}\right\}\quad\forall(x,M)\in\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}.

(7.5)

In this case, Assumption
7.1
holds, cf.
[
5
, Theorem 6.5.7]
, and it is known that one may take
\omega(\lambda)=\frac{L_{H}^{2}\lambda}{2}
, which is proved similarly to
[
36
, Lemma 3.4]
, where it is recalled that
L_{H}
is defined in (
2.3
).
For the final point in Assumption
7.1
, it is well-known that

\frac{\partial H_{\lambda}}{\partial M}(x,M)\in\partial_{M}H(x,M_{\lambda}),

(7.6)

where
M_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym}}
is the unique minimizer of (
7.5
). Furthermore,
M_{\lambda}\rightarrow M
as
\lambda\rightarrow 0
.
Therefore (
7.4
) follows from (
7.6
) and Proposition
2.1
.

From Theorem
3.3
, for each
\lambda\in(0,1]
, there exists a solution pair
(u_{\lambda},m_{\lambda})\in V\times L_{+}^{2}(\Omega)
of (
7.1
), where differentiability of
H_{\lambda}
w.r.t.
M
implies that the KFP equation (
7.1b
) is a differential equation rather than merely a differential inclusion.
Our main result regarding regularization is the following theorem.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, bounded, convex domain.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\mathrm{sym},+})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Let
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
be completely continuous.
Let
G\in V^{*}
be nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
Let
\{H_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in(0,1]}
be a family of regularizations of (
1.2
) satisfying Assumption
7.1
.
Then, there exists a subsequence
\{(u_{\lambda_{j}},m_{\lambda_{j}})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of solutions of (
7.1
) for
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
, and a solution
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
of (
2.10
), equivalently of (
2.11
), such that
u_{\lambda_{j}}\rightarrow u
strongly in
V
, and
m_{\lambda_{j}}\rightharpoonup m
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, as
j\to\infty
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, for each
\lambda\in(0,1]
, there exists a pair
(u_{\lambda},m_{\lambda})\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
that solves

\displaystyle H_{\lambda}[u_{\lambda}]

\displaystyle=F[m_{\lambda}]

\displaystyle\text{a.e. in }\Omega,

(7.7a)

\displaystyle\left\langle G,v-u_{\lambda}\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\leq\left(m_{\lambda},H_{\lambda}[v]-H_{\lambda}[u_{\lambda}]\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V.

(7.7b)

Moreover, the uniform bounds in (
3.1
) imply that
\{m_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in(0,1]}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Hence, there exists a
{m}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and a sequence
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
, with
\lambda_{j}\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, such that
m_{\lambda_{j}}\rightharpoonup{m}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Mazur’s theorem implies that
{m}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.
Let
u\in V
denote the unique solution of
H[u]=F[m]
a.e. in
\Omega
, which exists by Lemma
5.1
.
To show that the
u_{\lambda_{j}}
converge strongly to
u
in
V
, we may rewrite (
7.7a
) for
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
as

H[u_{\lambda_{j}}]=F[m_{\lambda_{j}}]+H[u_{\lambda_{j}}]-H_{\lambda_{j}}[u_{\lambda_{j}}],

and hence we may use Lemma
5.1
to find that

\|{u}-u_{\lambda_{j}}\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|F[{m}]-F[m_{\lambda_{j}}]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\omega({\lambda_{j}})\right),

(7.8)

where we have also used (
7.3
).
Thus, by complete continuity of
F
, we find that
u_{\lambda_{j}}\rightarrow{u}
strongly in
V
as
j\rightarrow\infty
.
We now show how to pass to the limit in (
7.7b
) to deduce that the pair
({u},{m})\in V\times L_{+}^{2}(\Omega)
solves (
2.11
).
Indeed, for any test function
v\in V
, we may write

\left(m_{\lambda_{j}},H_{\lambda_{j}}[v]-H_{\lambda_{j}}[u_{\lambda_{j}}]\right)_{\Omega}=\left(m_{\lambda_{j}},H[v]-H[{u}]\right)_{\Omega}\\
+\left(m_{\lambda_{j}},H_{\lambda_{j}}[v]-H[v]\right)_{\Omega}+\left(m_{\lambda_{j}},H[{u}]-H_{\lambda_{j}}[u_{\lambda_{j}}]\right)_{\Omega},

and we note that the last two terms on the right-hand side in the equation above vanish in the limit as
j\rightarrow\infty
, since the
\|m_{\lambda_{j}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
are uniformly bounded, and since
\|H_{\lambda_{j}}[v]-H[v]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and
\|H[{u}]-H_{\lambda_{j}}[u_{\lambda_{j}}]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
vanish in the limit by (
7.3
) and by
H_{\lambda_{j}}[u_{\lambda_{j}}]\to H[u]
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, one may pass to the limit in (
7.7b
) to find that
\left\langle G,v-{u}\right\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}\leq\left({m},H[v]-H[{u}]\right)_{\Omega}
for all
v\in V
. This shows that the pair
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
is a solution of the VI problem (
2.11
), and, equivalently, of the PDI problem (
2.10
) by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Remark 7.1
.

In Theorem
7.2
, there is no assumption of strict monotonicity on
F
, hence solutions of both the original and regularized problems may be nonunique in general.
This is why convergence is only shown up to subsequences.
However, if one adds the assumption of strict monotonicity of
F
, then, by Theorem
3.5
, uniqueness of solutions holds for both the original and regularized problems, and convergence of the whole sequence is recovered.

Remark 7.2
.

It is interesting to contemplate if one can also prove Theorem
7.2
without using the VI formulation of the problem.
The difficulty is that there are examples showing that, generally, the sequence of functions
\frac{\partial H_{\lambda_{j}}}{\partial M}(\cdot,D^{2}u_{\lambda_{j}})
need not contain any strongly converging subsequence, in any
L^{p}
norm with
p\geq 1
, to some element of
\mathcal{D}H[u]
, even when
u_{\lambda_{j}}\to v
in
V
.
This difficulty is similar to the one mentioned in Remark
2.1
above.
It is then hard to pass to limits directly in the KFP equation (
7.1b
).
This further highlights the benefit of working with the VI formulation of the problem with regards to passages to the limit.

Perturbations of
G

In the previous subsection, we considered the effect of regularization of the Hamiltonian. One can also consider perturbations of the other problem data.
To illustrate this, we end this section with a short proof of continuous dependence of solutions, up to subsequences, on the datum
G
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open, bounded, convex domain.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact metric space, let
a\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
satisfy Assumption
2.3
, and let
f\in C(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathcal{A})
.
Let
F\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
be completely continuous.
Let
G\in V^{*}
be nonnegative in the sense of distributions.
Let
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
V^{*}
that is weak-
*
converging to
G
in
V^{*}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(u_{n},m_{n})\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
be a solutions of the VI problem

\displaystyle H[u_{n}]

\displaystyle=F[m_{n}]

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(7.9a)

\displaystyle\langle G_{n},v-u_{n}\rangle_{V^{*}\times V}

\displaystyle\leq\left(m_{n},H[v]-H[u_{n}]\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall v\in V.

(7.9b)

Then there exists a solution
(u,m)\in V\times L^{2}_{+}(\Omega)
of the VI problem (
2.11
), and a subsequence
\{(u_{n_{j}},m_{n_{j}})\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
u_{n_{j}}\rightarrow u
strongly in
V
, and
m_{n_{j}}\rightharpoonup m
weakly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, as
j\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Firstly, since we have that
G_{n}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}G
in
V^{*}
, one finds that
\{G_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in
V^{*}
.
Hence by using (
5.13
) we find that the sequence
\{m_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly bounded in

, and thus there exists a weakly converging subsequence
\{m_{n_{j}}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
m_{n_{j}}\rightharpoonup{m}
in

as

.
By Mazur’s theorem we again see that

almost everywhere in
\Omega
.
If we now define
u\in V
to be the unique solution to

, then, from (

) and the complete continuity of

, one finds that
u_{n_{j}}\rightarrow u
strongly in

as

.
By using the weak-

in

, and the weak convergence

, we may pass to the limit

).

).

Similar to before, if the operator

is additionally assumed to be strictly monotone, then the conclusion of Proposition

Conclusion

We have shown the existence of solutions of fully nonlinear second-order MFG, on general bounded convex domains, with uniformly elliptic coefficients satisfying the Cordes condition.
Solutions are additionally unique if the coupling is additionally strictly monotone.
The key challenges addressed include the possible nondifferentiability of the Hamiltonian, the lack of smoothness of the domain boundary, the dependence of the Hamiltonian on the whole Hessian of the value function, and also the absence of any potential structure for the system.
We have also obtained results on the continuous dependence of the solution, where we have shown that the MFG system for the problem with nondifferentiable Hamiltonian arise as limits of problems with differentiable Hamiltonians.
In so doing, we have obtained several results of independent interest play a role in the analysis, such as the original comparison principle for nondivergence-form PDE with discontinuous coefficients, and their adjoint KFP equations.

A key original ingredient of our approach is the nonstandard variational inequality formulation of the problem, which was shown to be equivalent to the partial differential inclusion formulation.
We expect that the approach based on the equivalence of PDI and VI formulations will continue to provide useful tools in the analysis of a wide range of problems in mean field games, since it enables passages to the limits under very broad circumstances.
Possible further directions for investigation include problems with lower order terms, time-dependent problems, the case of nonseparable Hamiltonians, first-order problems, as well as the development of numerical methods.

Appendix A
Comparison principle for nondivergence form elliptic operators with Cordes coefficients

We now prove the comparison principle for nondivergence form elliptic equations with Cordes coefficients, as detailed in Theorem
5.3
.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

We prove the result by a regularization argument.
We first extend
\overline{a}
from
\Omega
to the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by setting
\overline{a}|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega}\coloneqq\overline{\nu}I_{d}
where
I_{d}
denotes the
d\times d
identity matrix. It is clear that
\overline{\nu}I_{d}\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
, so
\overline{a}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For each
\rho>0
, let
\phi_{\rho}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the standard mollifier supported in the closed ball of radius
\rho
.
Since the standard mollifier
\phi_{\rho}
is nonnegative with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\rho}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=1
, and since
\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
is closed and convex, it follows that the mollification
\overline{a}_{\rho}
, which is defined by
\overline{a}_{\rho}(x)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\overline{a}(y)\phi_{\rho}(x-y)\,\mathrm{d}y
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, also satisfies

\overline{a}_{\rho}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(A.1)

since mollification preserves convexity of sets.
We will also regularize the domain
\Omega
by using the following result from
[
43
, Theorem A.1]
: for each
\delta>0
, there exists an open convex set
\Omega_{\delta}
with
C^{1,1}
boundary such that
\Omega_{\delta}\subset\Omega
and
\mathrm{dist}(\Omega,\Omega_{\delta})<\delta
, where

\mathrm{dist}(\Omega,\Omega_{\delta})\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\Omega}\inf_{y\in\Omega_{\delta}}|x-y|+\sup_{y\in\Omega_{\delta}}\inf_{x\in\Omega}|x-y|.

(A.2)

Also, for each
\delta>0
, we can find a
\rho_{\delta}>0
so that
\left\lVert\overline{a}-\overline{a}_{\rho_{\delta}}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})}<\delta
.
We also choose
g_{\delta}\in L^{d}(\Omega)
to be a nonnegative approximation of
g
that satisfies
\left\lVert g-g_{\delta}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<\delta
.
Consider now, for each
\delta>0
, the regularized problem

\displaystyle-\overline{a}_{\rho_{\delta}}:D^{2}v_{\delta}

\displaystyle=g_{\delta}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega_{\delta},

(A.3)

\displaystyle v_{\delta}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega_{\delta},

This is a case of a nondivergence form elliptic equation on a bounded domain with
C^{1,1}
boundary with
\overline{a}_{\rho_{\delta}}\in C(\overline{\Omega_{\delta}})
and
g_{\delta}\in L^{d}(\Omega)
.
Therefore, by the Caldéron–Zygmund theory of nondivergence elliptic equations, see
[
18
, Theorem 9.15]
, we deduce that, for each
\delta>0
, there exists a unique
v_{\delta}\in W^{2,d}(\Omega_{\delta})\cap W^{1,d}_{0}(\Omega_{\delta})
that solves (

).
Recall that

is continuously embedded in

.
Since
g_{\delta}
is nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega
, and hence also in
\Omega_{\delta}
, it follows from the Alexandrov–Bakelman–Pucci maximum principle
[

, Theorem 9.1]
that

is nonnegative a.e. in
\Omega_{\delta}
.

Since
\overline{a}_{\rho_{\delta}}(x)\in\mathcal{C}(\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu},\varepsilon)
for all

and since the functions

form a uniformly bounded subset of

, and since

[

shows that there exists a constant

, independent of

\left\lVert v_{\delta}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega_{\delta})}\leq C\quad\forall\delta>0.

(A.4)

Observe that in the statement of
[

, the constant of the a priori bound is allowed to depend on the diameter of the domain, namely

in the current context, yet by tracing its explicit form in the proof, it is found that it can be made independent of

for all

\delta>0

to
\Omega

\delta
.
Then, after possibly passing to a subsequence, we may assume, without loss of generality, that there exists a

\overline{v}_{\delta}\rightharpoonup\overline{v}
in

We claim that
\overline{v}=v
is the unique strong solution in

\displaystyle-\overline{a}:D^{2}v

\displaystyle=g

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(A.5)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega.

To verify this, let
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
be arbitrary, and let
K\coloneqq\supp\psi
denote its support. Since
K

\Omega
and since
\mathrm{dist}(\Omega,\Omega_{\delta})<\delta
, with
\Omega_{\delta}
and
\Omega
convex, it follows that, for all
\delta

K\subset\Omega_{\delta}\subset\Omega
, cf.
[

. This implies that, for any

\int_{\Omega}M_{ij}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\left(M_{\delta}\right)_{ij}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\partial_{x_{i}x_{j}}v_{\delta}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x\\
-\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\partial_{x_{i}}v_{\delta}\partial_{x_{j}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x=-\lim_{\delta\to 0}\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{i}}\overline{v}_{\delta}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{i}}\overline{v}\,\partial_{x_{j}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}x,

(A.6)

where, in the identities above, we have first used the weak convergence
M_{\delta}\rightharpoonup M
, followed by the fact that
M_{\delta}=D^{2}v_{\delta}
on
K=\supp\psi
for all
\delta

\overline{v}_{\delta}\rightharpoonup\overline{v}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
as
\delta\to 0
.
Since
\psi

A.4

\overline{v}\in H^{2}(\Omega)

\Omega

This shows that

Then, it follows from (

), from the strong convergence
\overline{a}_{\rho_{\delta}}\to\overline{a}

Notice that, in the first equality above, we use the weak convergence

\delta\to 0

Since

\Omega
. Therefore
\overline{v}=v

).
To conclude the proof, recall that

\Omega

, so the convergence

\delta\to 0

a.e. in
\Omega
