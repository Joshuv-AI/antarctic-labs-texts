Accelerometer-derived “Weekend Warrior” physical activity and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality across CKM stages 0–3
Authors: Xinru Guo, Shiting Mi, Yijie Lin, Lin Zhao, Tielong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe 2023 American Heart Association Presidential Advisory introduced cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, but the mortality impact of “Weekend-Warrior” (WW) physical activity in this population remains uncertain. Using data from 1,927 adults in the 2003–2006 NHANES with accelerometer-measured activity and CKM stages 0–3, participants were classified as inactive, regularly active (≥150 min/week moderate-to-vigorous activity across ≥3 days), or WW (≥150 min/week concentrated in 1–2 days). Mortality status was tracked through 2019. Over a median 13.2-year follow-up, 306 deaths occurred, including 98 cardiovascular. Compared with inactivity, regular activity was associated with 75% lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.13–0.48) and 82% lower cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.18, 0.04–0.76). WW activity reduced all-cause mortality by 41% (HR 0.59, 0.37–0.94) but showed no significant effect on cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.76, 0.36–1.64). Subgroup analyses suggested that the survival benefits of physical activity were more evident among older adults (≥60 years), non-smokers, and individuals with early CKM stages (0–1), underscoring the public health importance of early promotion of physical activity along the CKM continuum. These findings suggest that while regular activity confers the greatest longevity advantage in early CKM syndrome, a WW pattern still offers meaningful protection and may be a practical alternative for individuals unable to maintain consistent exercise routines.Keywords: Weekend-Warrior, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, accelerometer, NHANES

Background

The American Heart Association (AHA) first systematically defined the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome in a Presidential Advisory issued in October 2023 [1]. CKM syndrome describes the pathophysiological interactions between cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic risk factors. It emphasizes that these three conditions do not occur in isolation but are involved in an interacting, progressive, systemic pathological process. Importantly, the early stages of CKM syndrome represent a potentially modifiable phase, in which timely lifestyle interventions may alter disease trajectories before the development of overt cardiovascular events [2]. Therefore, CKM syndrome is considered a systemic disease, and its prevention and management should begin with early intervention.

Lifestyle interventions, particularly physical activity [3], are essential for preventing and managing chronic diseases. The World Health Organization and the American Heart Association both recommend that adults engage in at least 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) per week [3,4]. However, spreading activity over multiple days is not feasible for many people in our fast-paced modern lives. In recent years, there has been growing interest in the Weekend Warrior (WW) model, which involves focusing the recommended 150 min of MVPA on one or two days per week. Studies have shown that the WW model is as effective as traditional, regularly distributed activities in reducing all-cause mortality, cardiovascular events, and stroke risk [5–7]. From a public health perspective, flexible physical activity patterns may help reduce barriers to participation and improve adherence, especially during earlier, preclinical stages of chronic disease, when lifestyle-based interventions are most feasible.

However, its role in the high-risk but reversible population with CKM syndrome remains to be elucidated. Therefore, the present study utilized the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database in the United States and employed wrist-worn accelerometers to objectively measure physical activity patterns. The study was the first to assess the relationship between the WW pattern and all-cause mortality as well as cardiovascular mortality in patients with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. Its objective was to provide new behavioral intervention evidence for early intervention in this syndrome.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is based on data from the NHANES, which received ethical approval from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion, and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed throughout the investigation.

We selected the 2003–2004 and 2005–2006 NHANES cycles because they contained data on accelerometers (N = 20,470) [8]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: participants aged ≥18 years and accelerometers worn for at least four valid days. The exclusion criteria were as follows: age <18 years (N = 9,287); missing accelerometer data, returned uncalibrated, or with <4 valid days of wear (N = 2,867); missing eGFR or UACR data (N = 457); missing data on covariates such as smoking and alcohol consumption (N = 1,920); missing data on CVD (N = 1,980) or 10-year CVD risk (N = 1,840); exclude patients with CKM stage 4 (N = 264). Finally, 1,927 participants were included in this study (Figure 1).

Flow chart of participant selection.

Physical activity

Participants without mobility limitations were asked to wear a wrist accelerometer (ActiGraph model 7164) for seven consecutive days during waking hours, except during water-based activities or sleep. This device was used to objectively assess their moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) levels. Participants whose monitors were not calibrated upon their return were excluded. Non-wear time was defined as an activity intensity count of zero for at least 60 consecutive minutes, with allowance for counts between 0 and 100 of one to two minutes. Wear time was determined by subtracting non-wear time from 24 h. To have valid accelerometer data, a participant must have data from at least four valid days (the accelerometer must be worn for at least 10 h per day) [9,10].

For adults and older adolescents (18 years of age or older), intensity thresholds were calculated to define MVPA based on treadmill or track walking using 2020 cpm activity points [10,11]. Specific criteria for each age are provided in https://epi.grants.cancer.gov/nhanes-pam/ SAS codes are specified.

This study divided participants into three groups based on the World Health Organization’s recommended 150 min of MVPA per week and whether this activity was concentrated on one to two days. The groups were as follows: the inactive group (MVPA <150 min), the WW group (MVPA ≥150 min concentrated over one to two days, with ≥50% of the total MVPA on one or two days), and the regular group (MVPA ≥150 min, but not WW) [7].

CKM stages

As detailed in previously published literature [12], CKM syndrome is classified into stages 0 through 4, as defined by the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory on Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) health [2]. Stage 0 includes individuals with both general and abdominal obesity but without any additional metabolic risk factors or clinical cardiovascular disease (CVD). Stage 1 includes individuals with excess or dysfunctional adiposity and/or impaired glucose metabolism, in the absence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), or clinical CVD. Specifically, general obesity is defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 for non-Asian populations and ≥ 23 kg/m2 for Asian populations. Abdominal obesity is defined as a waist circumference of ≥ 88 cm for women and ≥ 102 cm for men in non-Asian populations and ≥ 80 cm for women and ≥ 90 cm for men in Asian populations [13]. Impaired glucose metabolism is defined as a hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level between 5.7 and 6.4% or a fasting plasma glucose level between 100 and 125 mg/dL [14]. Stage 2 includes individuals with emerging cardiometabolic risk factors—such as hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, or moderate to high CKD risk—but without very high CKD risk, ASCVD, or clinical CVD. Stage 3 is characterized by the presence of very high CKD risk or established ASCVD, along with at least one other metabolic abnormality, but in the absence of clinical CVD. Stage 4 represents the most advanced stage of CKM syndrome, defined by a history of clinical CVD, in combination with any cardiometabolic or renal abnormality (Supplementary Tables S1–2). The study’s participants were selected from CKM stages 0–3.

Outcomes

Mortality follow-up was conducted via the National Death Index (NDI), with cause-of-death codes based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). All-cause mortality was defined as death from any etiology, whereas cardiovascular mortality was restricted to codes I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, and I60–I69. Observation ended on December 31, 2019; extracted variables comprised vital status, survival time, and underlying cause of death. Additional mortality information is publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm.

Covariates

Covariates included both continuous and categorical variables. Continuous variables were age, body mass index (BMI), weight, height, C-reactive protein (CRP), urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk score (ASCVD). Categorical variables included demographic characteristics (sex, race, and education level); lifestyle behaviors (smoking and alcohol use), and comorbid conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic syndrome (MetS).

Race was categorized as Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races. Educational attainment was classified into less than high school, high school graduate, and above high school. Smoking status was defined as never (<100 cigarettes in lifetime), former (≥100 cigarettes, not currently smoking), and current (≥100 cigarettes and still smoking). Alcohol use was categorized as nondrinker, light/moderate (0–12 drinks/day), and heavy (≥12 drinks/day). Diabetes was defined by self-report, HbA1c ≥6.5%, fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, 2-h OGTT glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or use of glucose-lowering medications. Hypertension was defined by self-report, BP ≥140/90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive drugs.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or the median plus the interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Differences between groups were compared using a t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and a chi-square test for categorical variables. We assessed associations between physical-activity patterns and mortality among adults with CKM stages 0–3 using multivariate Cox proportional-hazards models. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age (18–39, 40–59, 60–85 years), sex, race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other), education (below high school, high school, above high school), and continuous BMI; Model 3 further adjusted for smoking status (never, former, current), CKD risk (low, moderate, high, very high), CKM stage (0–3), use of oral antihypertensive agents, oral statin use, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, overweight/obesity, sex-specific HDL-C ≥ 50/40 mg/dL, fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL, C-reactive protein, eGFR, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and 10-year CVD risk. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and these associations were explored in prespecified subgroup analyses. For subgroup analyses, we collapsed sparse categories to improve stability (age dichotomized as <60 vs ≥60, CKM stage collapsed to Stage 0–1 vs 2–3, and smoking recoded as smoker vs non-smoker). Strata with fewer than five events were excluded from stratified Cox models to avoid unstable estimates and quasi-complete separation. Where a full multivariable model failed to converge, we fitted a reduced model adjusting for core covariates (age, sex, education) and flagged these results. Because the optimal level of MVPA determined from accelerometer data has not been fully defined, sensitivity analyses were performed using multiple thresholds. In our preliminary analysis, we examined guideline-based thresholds (≥150 min/week) and sample quartiles (≥32.5 min/week, ≥81 min/week, and ≥171 min/week). A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 1,927 participants, 266 (13.8%) were classified as weekend warriors, 305 (15.8%) as regularly active, and 1,356 (70.4%) as inactive based on weekly MVPA. The mean age was 49.7 ± 17.29 years, and 58.5% were female (Table 1). Compared with inactive individuals, those in the active groups were generally younger, had higher education, and showed lower prevalence of CKD, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and advanced CKM stages. Significant differences were also observed in CRP, eGFR, UACR, and lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol use across groups (all p < 0.05).

Distribution of physical activity

Compared with the remaining five days, individuals in the active weekend warrior group showed a markedly higher MVPA concentration on their two most active days, whereas those in the regular activity group demonstrated a more consistent distribution of MVPA across the week (Figure 2).

Associations between physical activity patterns and mortality

In the fully adjusted sensitivity analyses across the 25th (≥32.5 min), 50th (≥81 min), 75th (≥171 min), and guideline (≥150 min) MVPA thresholds, both Weekend-Warrior and regular patterns conferred graded protection against mortality in CKM stages 0–3 (Supplementary Tables S3–5). As shown in Table 2, using the guideline-recommended threshold of ≥150 min of MVPA per week, participants who accumulated their activity in a Weekend-Warrior pattern experienced a 41% lower risk of all-cause mortality compared with inactive adults (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.37–0.94, p = 0.026), whereas no significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality was observed (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.36–1.64, p = 0.487). Across all MVPA levels examined, regular activity consistently produced the most robust and significant risk reductions for both endpoints (all p < 0.05).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses revealed that the mortality benefit of physical activity was more evident among older adults, nonsmokers, and those at early CKM stages (Figure 3). In participants aged ≥60 years, the Weekend-Warrior pattern was associated with a 74% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR 0.26, 95% CI 0.11–0.63, p = 0.003). Similarly, nonsmokers and individuals with early CKM stage (Stage 0–1) derived substantial benefit, with HRs of 0.45 (0.23–0.90) and 0.30 (0.12–0.73) for the Weekend-Warrior group, respectively. The CKM Stage 0–1 model was simplified (adjusted for age, sex, and education) due to sparse events [15], and the estimate should be interpreted with caution. Subgroup analyses for CVD mortality were not presented because of insufficient events, but the overall direction of association remained consistent.

Discussion

Using objective wrist-accelerometer data, we provide the first evidence that both the “Weekend-Warrior” (WW) pattern and regularly distributed physical activity lower all-cause mortality among adults with CKM stages 0–3, yet only regular activity significantly reduces cardiovascular mortality. These findings align with prior general-population studies [6,7] and extend them into the newly defined CKM framework proposed by the American Heart Association in 2023 [1]. By integrating renal (eGFR, UACR), metabolic (MetS, diabetes), and cardiovascular endpoints (ASCVD risk, CVD), the CKM staging system offers a holistic view of cardio-renal-metabolic health. The lower CKM stages observed in active participants mirror their favorable inflammatory (lower CRP), renal (higher eGFR, lower UACR), and metabolic profiles. Additionally, the protective effect of PA was more pronounced among former smokers, consistent with previous studies [16].

Regular activity showed the most consistent protective associations against both all-cause and cardiovascular death, while the WW pattern was still linked to a significant 41% reduction in all-cause mortality compared with inactivity. This finding reinforces that meeting the recommended amount of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, regardless of its distribution across the week, can provide substantial survival benefits. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that concentrated, high-intensity bouts may elicit cardiorespiratory and metabolic adaptations similar to those induced by evenly spaced sessions, particularly in previously sedentary individuals [17]. In comparison with the absence of physical activity, the weekend warrior mode has been associated with a reduced risk of all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality [16,18].

Several biological mechanisms may underlie these benefits. Exercise lowers resting blood pressure by reducing the excitability of the sympathetic nervous system and improving vascular endothelial function. This continuously lowers peripheral resistance [19]. At the same time, exercise activates lipoprotein lipase, which accelerates triglyceride clearance and promotes high-density lipoprotein synthesis [20]. Exercise also modulates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, a key driver of hypertension and renal injury in CKM syndrome. Furthermore, physical activity exerts anti-inflammatory effects, reflected in lower circulating CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α levels—cytokines implicated in insulin resistance [21], endothelial dysfunction, and glomerular damage. Our data confirm significantly lower CRP concentrations among active participants, especially those with regular activity. Finally, exercise improves mitochondrial function and oxidative-stress regulation, promoting visceral fat reduction and insulin sensitivity [22,23]—core features of early CKM stages.

From a public health perspective, these findings are particularly relevant. The WW pattern represents a pragmatic and potentially scalable physical activity strategy for individuals constrained by work demands, caregiving responsibilities, or other social barriers. By lowering participation thresholds while still conferring meaningful protection against early metabolic and renal dysfunction, flexible activity patterns may help expand population-level adherence to physical activity recommendations, especially during earlier, preclinical stages of CKM when lifestyle modification is most feasible.

Although the WW pattern was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality, its association with cardiovascular mortality did not reach statistical significance. This overall pattern remains broadly in line with prior large-scale population studies showing survival benefits for both regular and concentrated activity patterns [24,25]. Several factors may help explain this observation. First, although accelerometer-based measurements reduce recall bias, they capture only a single week of behavior and may therefore not fully represent long-term habitual activity. Second, cardiovascular deaths were fewer than all-cause deaths, and although the associations consistently trended in a protective direction, the precision of the estimates was inherently limited [26,27]. Third, different ways of accumulating physical activity may elicit distinct physiological responses. Regularly distributed activity may provide more frequent episodes of hemodynamic stimulation, vascular shear stress, and endothelial activation, processes that contribute to vascular remodeling and cardiometabolic regulation over time [28,29]. Weekend-concentrated activity may still improve overall fitness, yet its pattern of physiological stimulation may differ from that of regular activity, which could partly account for the observed results.

In addition, we further explored cause-specific mortality, including deaths from malignant neoplasms, nephritis/nephrotic syndrome/nephrosis, and diabetes-related causes. Due to the limited number of deaths in the latter two categories (n = 8 and n = 23) and complete model separation, only malignant neoplasm mortality was analyzed (Supplementary Table S6). The Weekend-Warrior group showed a nonsignificant trend toward lower cancer mortality (HR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.27–2.00). The absence of a significant association likely reflects the complex, multifactorial pathways linking physical activity to cancer outcomes [30]. The mortality rate due to cancer is influenced by a multitude of factors, including metabolic, inflammatory, hormonal, and lifestyle elements. These factors may exhibit varied responses to different activity patterns [31]. Additionally, the impact of weekend-concentrated activities may vary according to the specific type of cancer. Furthermore, accelerometer data cannot fully represent long-term activity levels associated with cancer risk.

A key strength of this study is the use of accelerometer-derived physical activity, which provides objective assessment and avoids recall bias inherent to self-reported questionnaires. Although the survey scope could have encompassed a broader range of NHANES cycles, we deliberately used accelerometer-measured physical activity from the 2003–2006 cycles to ensure greater objectivity, accuracy, and reliability of physical activity assessment.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective NHANES design precludes causal inference; prospective studies are needed to confirm these associations. Second, the accelerometer recordings only captured activity for one week, so they may not fully represent long-term behavioral patterns. Misclassification may also arise from estimated non-wear periods or the use of debated wrist-based MVPA cut points. Third, selection bias cannot be excluded, as NHANES oversamples minority and low-socioeconomic groups, who often face barriers to consistent exercise; this may lead to underestimation of PA levels and bias toward healthier participants. Finally, the limited number of cause-specific deaths, particularly CVD deaths, reduced statistical power and caused unstable estimates in several strata. Accordingly, CVD mortality results are presented only at the overall level, and stratum-specific estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In adults with CKM stages 0–3, both regular and weekend warrior physical activity patterns were associated with lower risks of all-cause mortality, while only regular activity was linked to reduced cardiovascular mortality. Notably, these findings emphasize that in the early stages of CKM, achieving the recommended weekly level of physical activity, even if concentrated within one or two days, can provide meaningful survival benefits and underscores the public health importance of promoting physical activity before CKM advances.
