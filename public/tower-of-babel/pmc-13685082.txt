Effectiveness of three remote tele-prehabilitation strategies in patients awaiting lower limb arthroplasty: a three-arm randomized controlled trial
Authors: Pamela Patanè, Vittoria Carnevale Pellino, Federica Manzoni, Emanuele Caldarella, Massimiliano Febbi, Luisella Pedrotti, Matteo Vandoni, Alessandro Gatti, Francesca de Caro, Luca Marin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLower limb arthroplasty (LLA), including total hip and total knee arthroplasty (THA and TKA), ranks among the most frequently performed elective orthopedic procedures worldwide. Patients on surgical waiting lists commonly present with reduced muscle strength, impaired mobility, and diminished cardiorespiratory fitness, all associated with delayed postoperative recovery. Prehabilitation aims to optimize preoperative functional capacity; however, adherence to center-based approaches remains suboptimal. Telerehabilitation offers a viable alternative, yet evidence on tele-prehabilitation for LLA candidates and comparisons between remote modalities remain scarce. This randomized clinical trial evaluated three tele-prehabilitation strategies on preoperative functional outcomes in patients awaiting LLA.MethodsSixty patients scheduled for elective TKA or THA were randomly allocated 1:1:1 to one of three four-week programmes: neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES), physiotherapist-supervised online exercise (SV), or unsupervised brochure-guided home exercise (BR), each comprising three 30-min sessions per week. No usual-care or non-intervention control group was included, as all three study arms received an active tele-prehabilitation intervention. Assessments were conducted at baseline (T0) and post-prehabilitation (T1) remotely via videoconference, and in person on postoperative day 4 (T2) and day 10 (T3). The primary outcomes were the 30-Second Chair Stand Test (30-CST), the Timed Up and Go test (TUG), and the Oxford Hip/Knee Score (OHS/OKS), assessed preoperatively at T0 and post-prehabilitation at T1. The secondary outcomes were the Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) and length of hospital stay (LOS), assessed postoperatively at day 4 (T2) and day 10 (T3). The a priori sample size calculation was based on the 6MWT as a clinically relevant and responsive indicator of early postoperative functional recovery following arthroplasty; the rationale for this choice and its implications for interpreting preoperative functional endpoints are discussed in the Limitations section.ResultsAll three groups demonstrated significant within-group improvements across most functional outcomes (p < 0.001). NMES achieved significantly greater reductions in TUG compared with both SV and BR, and superior gains in 6MWT and Oxford score relative to SV. No significant differences were observed between NMES and BR for 30CST, 6MWT, or Oxford score. Notably, BR demonstrated significantly greater improvement in 6MWT than SV, despite the absence of real-time supervision.ConclusionsTele-prehabilitation is feasible, well tolerated, and associated with meaningful functional improvements across all modalities. However, as all three groups received an active intervention, these findings reflect relative comparisons between remote modalities rather than efficacy versus standard care or no intervention. NMES demonstrated a more favorable comparative profile for selected mobility-related outcomes, particularly TUG, whereas no significant between-group differences were found for the 30-s chair stand test, particularly for mobility-related outcomes, while brochure-guided exercise proved largely equivalent to NMES on most endpoints. These findings support remote NMES and structured self-directed exercise as accessible, scalable alternatives to supervised prehabilitation for patients awaiting LLA.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06363643; Registered 12 April 2024. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06363643Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10281-6.Keywords: Tele-prehabilitation, Lower limb arthroplasty, Neuromuscular electrical stimulation, Functional recovery, Presurgery exercise

Introduction

Lower limb arthroplasty (LLA), including total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA), is one of the most commonly performed elective orthopedic procedures worldwide, primarily indicated for end-stage hip and knee osteoarthritis [1]. Both interventions are associated with substantial improvements in pain, joint function, and health-related quality of life [2], but, postoperative recovery trajectories are highly heterogeneous and are strongly modulated by patients’ preoperative functional reserve [3]. Patients on surgical waiting lists frequently exhibit reduced lower limb muscle strength, impaired mobility, and lower cardiorespiratory fitness [4], deficits that have been independently associated with delayed functional recovery, prolonged hospitalization, and increased risk of postoperative complications [5]. Consequently, optimizing patients’ physical and psychological status before surgery has become a cornerstone of contemporary perioperative care pathways [6].

Within this framework, prehabilitation programmes aim to enhance an individual’s functional capacity before surgery, thereby facilitating postoperative recovery [7, 8]. Multimodal prehabilitation programmes typically encompass structured exercise training, nutritional optimization, and psychological support. Among exercise-based approaches, interventions targeting lower limb muscle strength, aerobic conditioning, and neuromuscular control have demonstrated efficacy in improving functional performance, reducing postoperative pain, and shortening time to functional independence in patients awaiting LLA [9, 10].

Alongside traditional exercise, neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) has been proposed as a complementary preoperative strategy to preserve or enhance skeletal muscle function [11]. NMES elicits involuntary muscle contractions through the percutaneous delivery of electrical impulses, thereby activating motor units independently of the patient’s voluntary effort, a feature that may be particularly advantageous in individuals with severe joint pain, limited weight-bearing capacity, or poor exercise tolerance [12]. Preliminary evidence suggests that preoperative NMES of the quadriceps femoris may attenuate muscle weakness, support early functional recovery, and reduce length of hospitalization following knee arthroplasty [13].

Despite the well-documented benefits of prehabilitation, real-world implementation is hindered by suboptimal adherence to center-based programs [14]. Structural barriers, including pain-related mobility restrictions, transportation difficulties, geographic distance from healthcare facilities, and scheduling constraints, frequently prevent patients on surgical waiting lists from consistently attending in-person sessions [15]. Telerehabilitation, defined as the remote delivery of rehabilitation services through information and communication technologies, has emerged as a viable alternative that may overcome these barriers by enabling patients to complete exercise programs at home while maintaining structured contact with clinicians [16, 17]. Growing evidence supports the benefits of telerehabilitation compared to conventional in-person approaches across a range of musculoskeletal conditions, with comparable outcomes in functional performance, pain, and patient satisfaction [18].

While the feasibility and effectiveness of telerehabilitation have been explored in several musculoskeletal and post-surgical contexts, evidence specifically addressing tele-prehabilitation for patients awaiting LLA remains scarce [19–21]. Moreover, no study to date has directly compared distinct remote intervention modalities, including technology-assisted neuromuscular stimulation, physiotherapist-supervised online exercise, and unsupervised home-based exercise guided by educational materials, within the same randomized framework. Such a head-to-head comparison is necessary to identify the most feasible and clinically effective model for remote preoperative care in this population.

Therefore, the primary aim of the present randomized clinical trial was to evaluate and compare the effects of three distinct tele-prehabilitation modalities, NMES, physiotherapist-supervised online exercise (SV), and unsupervised brochure-guided home exercise (BR), on preoperative functional capacity and postoperative recovery outcomes in patients scheduled for LLA. We hypothesized that NMES would be associated with greater improvements in functional mobility outcomes compared with supervised or brochure-guided exercise, given its potential to elicit direct neuromuscular activation independently of voluntary effort.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical considerations

The report of this study was prepared in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines [22]. This prospective, three-arm, parallel-group randomized clinical trial was conducted at the Minimally Invasive Surgery Unit, Department of Orthopedic Surgery, Istituto di Cura “Città di Pavia” (Pavia, Italy), between 15 April and 15 October 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Lombardia 6 Territorial Ethics Committee (Protocol No. 0003762) and was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration No. NCT06363643; registered 12 April 2024). All procedures were conducted in full accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments [23].

Eligible patients were randomly allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three tele-prehabilitation programmes NMES, SV, or BR. Randomization was performed using a block randomization procedure (block size = 3) generated with Stata statistical software (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The random sequence was generated by a statistician not involved in participant recruitment or intervention delivery. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, opened only after each participant had formally consented and completed baseline assessments, thus preventing recruiters from predicting group assignment prior to enrolment. Outcome assessments at all time points were conducted by a physiotherapist blinded to group allocation and not involved in the delivery of any intervention.

Patient selection

All patients listed for elective LLA during the recruitment period were systematically screened for eligibility against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: I) they were scheduled for elective TKA or THA; II) they were aged between 55 and 80 years at the time of recruitment; III) and they were considered functionally and cognitively able to independently perform the proposed tele-prehabilitation activities without direct on-site supervision. Exclusion criteria were as follows: I) the presence of concurrent orthopedic or neurological conditions affecting walking ability or lower limb function; II) disorders potentially compromising postural balance or vestibular function, including diagnosed neurological or vestibular impairments; III) contraindications to the use of any of the medical devices employed in the study (in particular, the neuromuscular electrical stimulator in the NMES arm); IV) and inability to understand study procedures, independently use video-conferencing technology, or provide written informed consent.

Eligible patients were identified through the orthopedic unit’s surgical waiting lists and subsequently contacted by telephone by a member of the research team. During this initial call, candidates received detailed verbal information regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and anticipated benefits, as well as the time commitments involved. Patients who expressed a willingness to participate and who confirmed fulfilment of all eligibility criteria were invited to attend a brief remote information session, at which written informed consent was formally obtained prior to enrolment. No financial or other incentives were offered for participation. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence.

Tele-prehabilitation programs

Five weeks before each participant’s scheduled surgical date, they were contacted by telephone and invited to attend an individual orientation session delivered via videoconference. During this session, the supervising physiotherapist explained in detail the structure, rationale and expected outcomes of the assigned tele-prehabilitation programme, provided group-specific practical instructions (including device setup in the NMES group) and collectively scheduled both the baseline assessment (T0) and the start date of the four-week training programme. The tele-prehabilitation programme included three sessions per week, each of approximately 30 min duration, over four consecutive weeks, for a total of 12 supervised or self-directed training sessions per participant. Irrespective of group allocation, all participants received a weekly telephone call from the supervising physiotherapist throughout the intervention period. The purpose of these calls was to monitor individual adherence to the prescribed schedule, promptly address any technical difficulties or concerns related to the exercises or device use, and provide motivational and behavioural support to reduce dropout. Session attendance, recorded prospectively by the supervising physiotherapist on a structured log, was used as the primary measure of programme adherence.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation group (NMES)

Participants allocated to the NMES group received a portable neuromuscular electrical stimulator (I-Tech Physio, I-Tech Medical Division, I.A.C.E.R. S.r.l., Scorzè, VE, Italy), delivered directly to their home address by the research team prior to the start of the programme. The device is a CE-marked programmable medical unit featuring two independent stimulation channels and self-adhesive surface electrodes, designed for unsupervised home use following appropriate instruction. The first stimulation session was conducted under direct real-time physiotherapist supervision via videoconference (see Fig. 1 for the participant setup and electrode placement). During this initial supervised session, participants received standardised verbal and visual instructions on device operation, correct electrode placement, how to adjust the intensity and recognize any adverse sensations that should prompt session discontinuation. Electrodes were applied bilaterally over the quadriceps femoris of the operated limb: the distal electrode was positioned approximately 4 cm proximal to the superior border of the patella, and the proximal electrode was placed over the mid-anterior thigh targeting the motor point of the rectus femoris, in accordance with established neuromuscular electrical stimulation protocols for quadriceps strengthening prior to arthroplasty [24]. A pre-programmed stimulation protocol was employed, delivering a symmetrical biphasic waveform at a carrier frequency of 45 Hz. Stimulation intensity was individually set at each session and progressively increased across the four-week programme in line with each participant’s tolerance, with the target being a strong, visible, and comfortable quadriceps contraction without pain or discomfort. Finally, the patients were instructed to contract the quadriceps during the peak of the electrical impulse. Following the initial supervised session, all 11 remaining sessions were completed independently at home by each participant according to the prescribed schedule, without real-time physiotherapist oversight.

Treatment Status

Physiotherapist-supervised exercise group (SV)

Participants in the SV group completed all 12 training sessions remotely under the real-time supervision of a qualified physiotherapist via videoconference. Each session followed a structured, progressive protocol targeting lower limb strength, dynamic balance, and functional mobility in preparation for LLA surgery. The protocol included 30-min sessions with three sets of 10–15 repetitions of each of the following exercises: seated leg extensions, standing lower limb extensions, closed box squats, wall planks, hip abductions, and calf raises. The number of repetitions and the level of exercise difficulty were individually progressed by the supervising physiotherapist based on each participant’s performance, reported effort, and perceived tolerance across sessions. Throughout each session, the physiotherapist monitored exercise execution quality via the videoconference feed, provided real-time corrective feedback on technique, and modified the programme as required to ensure both safety and optimal training stimulus. Participants were instructed to perform exercises barefoot or in flat shoes in a clear, safe space at home, using a stable chair and a wall as required.

Brochure-guided unsupervised exercise group (BR)

Participants allocated to the BR group followed an exercise programme identical in content, frequency, and progression structure to that prescribed for the SV group but completed all sessions independently without any real-time physiotherapist supervision. The programme was delivered to participants via email at the time of the orientation videoconference, in the form of a structured illustrated brochure. The brochure contained written descriptions and step-by-step photographic demonstrations of each of the six exercises, detailed instructions on sets, repetitions, and rest intervals, and clear guidance on how to progress exercise load and difficulty week by week. Participants were instructed to perform exercises at the same frequency (three sessions per week) and approximate session duration (30 min) as those in the other groups, and to adhere to the self-progression criteria outlined in the brochure. No real-time feedback on exercise technique was provided; however, any concerns raised by participants during the weekly monitoring telephone calls were addressed by the physiotherapist.

Intervention fidelity was monitored differently across the three arms. In the NMES group, correct electrode placement and device operation were verified during the initial supervised session, whereas the remaining sessions were self-administered using pre-programmed stimulation parameters without direct oversight. In the SV group, exercise technique and progression were monitored in real time at every session by the supervising physiotherapist. In the BR group, exercise technique was not directly observed, and progression relied on participant self-management according to the brochure instructions, with technical concerns addressed only during the weekly monitoring telephone calls. This resulted in differing levels of fidelity oversight across the three modalities, which should be considered when interpreting between-group comparisons.

Data collection and outcome measures

Outcome measures were collected at four pre-specified time points as illustrated in Fig. 2. The baseline assessment (T0) was conducted the day before commencement of the tele-prehabilitation programme; the post-prehabilitation assessment (T1) was performed the day following programme completion, approximately one week before the scheduled surgery date, thereby capturing changes attributable to the four-week prehabilitation intervention in the immediate preoperative period. Both T0 and T1 assessments were conducted remotely through videoconference by the blinded assessor. Postoperative assessments were conducted in person by the same physiotherapist at the hospital on post-surgery day 4 (T2) and post-surgery day 10 (T3), reflecting the acute early recovery phase and the hospital discharge period, respectively.

Timeline of features

At T0 and T1, participants performed two validated functional tests widely used to assess physical performance in musculoskeletal populations. The 30-Second Chair Stand Test (30CST) evaluates lower limb muscle strength and endurance by recording the total number of complete sit-to-stand cycles performed within 30 s from a standard chair without the use of arm support. Higher scores reflect greater muscular strength and functional reserve. The Timed Up and Go test (TUG) assesses functional mobility, dynamic balance, and gait performance by recording the time (in seconds) required to rise from a chair, walk 3 m at a self-selected pace, turn around a cone, return to the chair, and resume a fully seated position. Shorter completion times reflect better functional mobility. Both tests have demonstrated good reliability and validity in patients awaiting or recovering from LLA [24, 25].

Disease-specific health status and joint-related symptoms were assessed using the Oxford Hip Score (OHS) or the Oxford Knee Score (OKS) at T0 and T1, administered according to the type of scheduled arthroplasty (THA or TKA, respectively). Both instruments are validated, disease-specific patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) comprising 12 items each, assessing pain severity and activity-related functional limitations experienced over the preceding four weeks. Total scores range from 0 to 48, with higher scores indicating better joint function and lower pain levels. In this study, the standard (additive) scoring version of the OKS and OHS was used, as originally described and validated by Murray et al. (1998), in which each item is scored from 0 (severe symptoms) to 4 (no symptoms), yielding a total score of 0–48. Both the OHS and OKS have established measurement properties including responsiveness to prehabilitation-related change [26]. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) questionnaire was administered to NMES group participants following the intervention to evaluate their perception of the technology employed. The TAM assesses both the usability of a given technology and the degree of satisfaction experienced during its use, capturing the extent to which individuals believe that adopting a specific technology will enhance their daily functioning. According to this model, a technology perceived as useful is more likely to be adopted and consistently used. The questionnaire consists of 22 items distributed across four domains, with individual item scores ranging from 1 (“totally dissatisfied”) to 7 (“totally satisfied”), yielding a maximum total score of 28 [27].

Postoperative functional recovery was assessed using the Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), administered by the blinded physiotherapist at T2 (post-surgery day 4) and T3 (post-surgery day 10). The 6MWT quantifies functional exercise capacity by recording the total distance covered in meters during a six-minute continuous walking bout at a self-selected pace along a standardized indoor hospital corridor. Participants were standardly instructed and received no verbal encouragement during the test. The 6MWT is a well-established, clinically meaningful indicator of early postoperative functional recovery in patients undergoing LLA and has demonstrated adequate reliability and sensitivity to change in this population [28].

In addition, total length of hospital stay (LOS) was recorded as a secondary indicator of postoperative recovery and healthcare resource utilization. LOS was defined as the total number of days from the day of surgery (day 0) to the day of discharge from the orthopedic ward. Adverse events were systematically monitored throughout the intervention period. Participants were instructed to report any adverse events during the weekly telephone monitoring calls and during assessment sessions. Potential adverse events specifically monitored included muscle soreness, skin irritation at electrode sites (NMES group only), falls during exercise, and exacerbation of joint pain. No adverse events were reported in any group during the four-week tele-prehabilitation programme, supporting the safety and tolerability of all three remote intervention modalities.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated a priori based on the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT), considered a clinically relevant indicator of postoperative functional recovery. Steven-Lapsley et al. [29] showed that, at 3.5 weeks postoperatively, patients receiving NMES walked a mean distance of 390.1 m (SD 92.4), compared with 295.3 m (SD 103.4) in controls, corresponding to a between-group difference of 94.8 m. As the present study did not include a comparable control group, this effect was considered likely to overestimate the treatment effect in our sample. Therefore, a more conservative assumption of a moderate effect size was adopted for the sample size calculation. Based on this assumption, we performed a sample size calculation was performed using G*Power for a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), within-between interaction. The calculation assumed a moderate effect size (f = 0.25), a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95. The model was specified for three groups and two measurement time points, with an assumed correlation of 0.65 among repeated measures and a nonsphericity correction of 1. Under these assumptions, the required total sample size was 48 participants, corresponding to 16 participants per group. To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the target sample size was increased to 60 participants (20 participants per group). Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD or 95%CI), whereas categorical variables are reported as number (percentage). Repeated continuous outcomes were analysed using mixed-effects models including fixed effects for group, time, and the group × time interaction, with age, sex, number of sessions, and prosthesis type (TKA vs THA) entered as covariates. Participant ID was included as a random intercept to account for within-subject correlation over time. For each outcome, distributional assumptions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the outcome was non-normally distributed, a generalized linear mixed-effects model with Gamma distribution and log link was fitted; otherwise, a linear mixed-effects model was applied. Estimated marginal means were obtained from the fitted models, while for Gamma models, estimates were back-transformed to the response scale. Post hoc contrasts were used to examine within-group post–pre changes and between-group differences in change over time, with adjustment for multiple comparisons. To address the potential influence of baseline between-group differences, we performed an additional constrained baseline-adjusted mixed-model sensitivity analysis. In this model, baseline group means were constrained to be equal by removing the group main effect and modelling each outcome as a function of time, the time × group interaction, age, sex, number of sessions, prosthesis type, and a participant-specific random intercept. When the outcome was non-normally distributed, a Gamma generalized linear mixed model with log link was used and estimated marginal means were back-transformed to the response scale. Estimated marginal means, within-group changes, and between-group difference-in-change estimates from this constrained model are reported in Table S1 and illustrated in Figure S1. Results are presented as estimates with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 86 participants were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 26 were excluded, including 17 who did not meet the inclusion criteria, 5 who declined to participate, and 4 for other reasons. The remaining 60 participants were randomized equally into three groups: BR (n = 20), NMES (n = 20), and SV (n = 20). All participants received the allocated intervention in their respective groups. No participants dropped out of the intervention during the study, as shown in Fig. 3.

Descriptive characteristics of the total sample, divided by intervention group

Within groups analyses showed significant improvements from baseline to end of intervention in most outcomes across the three intervention groups (Table 2). In the BR, significant improvements were observed in the 30-s chair stand test, Timed Up and Go, 6-min walk test and Oxford questionnaire score (all p < 0.001). In the NMES, all functional outcomes and quality-of-life scores also improved significantly over time (all p < 0.001). In the SV, significant improvements were found in the 30-s chair stand test, Timed Up and Go, and 6MWT (all p < 0.001), whereas improvement in Oxford score did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.123). Overall, all groups showed better functional performance at end of intervention, while significant improvement in quality of life was observed only in the brochure and neuromuscular electrical stimulation groups. Exploratory prosthesis-specific constrained analyses were additionally performed to examine whether the pattern of change was consistent among patients awaiting THA and TKA. In the THA subgroup, all three intervention groups showed significant within-group improvements in the 30-s chair stand test, Timed Up and Go, Oxford questionnaire, and 6-min walking test. Improvements were numerically largest in the NMES group for the Oxford questionnaire and 6-min walking test, although no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for any outcome within the THA subgroup. In the TKA subgroup, significant within-group improvements were observed across most outcomes in all intervention groups; however, the Oxford questionnaire did not significantly improve in the supervised group. Between-group comparisons of change scores showed few significant differences across the intervention arms. No significant differences were observed between BR and NMES for the 30-s chair stand test, 6-min walking test, or Oxford questionnaire score. However, a significant difference was found for Timed Up and Go, with a greater reduction in the NMES than in the BR. When comparing NMES and SV, significant differences were observed for Timed Up and Go, with a greater reduction for NMES, while for both 6-min walking test and Oxford questionnaire there was a greater increase for the NMES. No significant difference between these groups was found for the 30-s chair stand test. Between BR and SV, a significant difference was observed only for the 6-min walking test, with a greater increase in the BR group, whereas no significant differences were found for the remaining outcomes. Because baseline differences were observed across groups for some functional outcomes, we conducted a constrained baseline-adjusted mixed-model sensitivity analysis. The constrained model confirmed significant within-group improvements across the analyzed outcomes in all three intervention groups. The pattern of between-group comparisons was broadly consistent with the main analysis, supporting the interpretation that all three tele-prehabilitation modalities were associated with functional improvements, while between-group advantages remained selective rather than global. Overall, this sensitivity analysis indicates that baseline imbalance did not materially alter the interpretation of the study findings. Between-group comparisons in the TKA subgroup showed significantly greater improvement in the Oxford questionnaire for NMES compared with supervised exercise, and significantly greater improvement in the 6-min walking test for brochure-guided exercise compared with supervised exercise. Discussion

The primary aim of this three-arm randomized clinical trial was to evaluate and compare the effects of three different four-week tele-prehabilitation modalities, NMES, SV and BR, on functional performance and health-related quality of life in patients awaiting elective LLA. The main finding was that NMES showed a more favorable comparative profile for selected outcomes: it demonstrated significantly greater improvement in TUG than both SV and BR, and superior gains in 6MWT and Oxford score compared with SV. By contrast, differences between NMES and BR were limited, with a significant advantage observed only for TUG. Importantly, no significant between-group differences were observed for the 30-s chair stand test across any pairwise comparison, and BR performed similarly to NMES on most endpoints, indicating that the superiority of NMES was selective rather than global. While statistically significant, these between-group differences warrant interpretation through the lens of clinical relevance, as explored below by means of minimal clinically important difference (MCID) analysis. The selective, rather than global, nature of NMES's advantage is a thread that runs through the remainder of this discussion.

These findings suggest that while NMES may offer incremental benefits in dynamic balance and gait capacity over both comparators. The higher gains in mobility-related outcomes observed with NMES might be explained by its direct intervention on quadriceps muscle. Indeed, quadriceps muscle strength is a central determinant of functional performance in patients with advanced knee or hip osteoarthritis, and preoperative weakness is independently predictive of worse functional recovery after arthroplasty [30–32]. An early RCT by Stevens-Lapsley et al. demonstrated that home-based NMES applied to the quadriceps from 48 h post-TKA significantly accelerated the recovery of muscle strength and functional performance compared with standard rehabilitation alone [29]. Consistent with this, the pilot RCT by Walls et al. [13] specifically investigated preoperative home-based NMES in TKA candidates reported significant gains in chair-rise, walk, and stair-climb performance, and evidence of attenuated postoperative muscle atrophy in the NMES group compared to controls, a pattern that mirrors the higher mobility-related outcomes observed in the NMES group of the present trial. Furthermore, Şavkin et al. [33] evaluated preoperative NMES in patients undergoing fast-track TKA and found significant improvements in patient-reported functional outcomes during the pre-surgery period, although no significant between-group differences in strength or functional performance were detected post-surgery. This discrepancy with the present findings may reflect differences in protocol duration (six weeks in Şavkin et al. [33] vs. four weeks in the present study), stimulation parameters, and the absence of a comparator exercise arm in the former study [33].

Although the differences between groups were selective rather than global, as noted above, all three interventions were associated with significant preoperative improvements across most functional outcomes, including the 30CST, TUG, and 6MWT. Given the absence of a no-treatment control group, these within-group changes should be interpreted cautiously; however, they are broadly consistent with the prehabilitation literature showing that structured preoperative exercise can improve physical function before TKA or THA.

When interpreting the clinical relevance of these findings, it is important to consider not only statistical significance but also the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for each outcome. For the TUG, an MCID of approximately 0.8–1.0 s has been reported in arthroplasty populations; the between-group differences for TUG observed in this trial approach or exceed this threshold, suggesting potential clinical relevance. For the 6MWT, a change of 25–50 m is commonly considered clinically meaningful, and the between-group differences observed between NMES and SV, and between BR and SV, may meet this criterion. For the Oxford score, an MCID of approximately 4–5 points has been described; the within-group changes observed in NMES and BR exceed this threshold, whereas the between-group differences may be of borderline clinical significance. These considerations should temper overconfident interpretation of the statistical findings. These results are consistent with the literature, which has established that structured exercise-based program delivered prior to TKA or THA improve muscle strength, functional mobility, and physical performance in the weeks preceding surgery [33–35]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by De Klerk et al. [6] confirmed that home-based prehabilitation significantly improved pain and function prior to both TKA and THA, with a mean compliance rate exceeding 90% across included trials. Our high session attendance observed across all three groups is consistent with this and reinforces that remote delivery modalities can sustain participant engagement as effectively as traditional in-person program. It should be noted, however, that adherence in the present study was quantified primarily as session completion; differences in adherence quality, such as exercise technique accuracy or stimulation intensity progression, may have varied across intervention groups and could not be captured by attendance logs alone. Future trials should incorporate objective monitoring strategies, such as wearable sensors or digital activity tracking, to more accurately capture adherence quality across remote intervention modalities. A noteworthy finding was the differential response in health-related quality of life, as measured by the OHS or OKS, across intervention groups. Statistically significant improvements were observed in the BR group and in the NMES group, whereas the SV group did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, the SV group entered the study with a markedly higher baseline Oxford score compared with the BR and NMES groups, leaving less room for improvement over a short four-week window. This baseline imbalance likely introduced a ceiling effect in the SV group, which may partially account for the smaller and non-significant Oxford score improvement observed in this group. This potential ceiling effect should be taken into account when interpreting the comparative effectiveness of the three modalities, and readers should be cautious not to attribute the differential Oxford score response solely to differences in intervention efficacy. [36, 37]. It would therefore be premature to conclude that physiotherapist-supervised exercise is intrinsically less effective than NMES or self-directed exercise in improving joint-specific quality of life in LLA candidates. The high TAM score recorded in the NMES group (mean 26.4 out of 28) indicates that participants perceived the technology as both useful and easy to use, suggesting strong acceptance of the remote neuromuscular stimulation device. This finding is consistent with the broader literature on technology acceptance in healthcare, which identifies perceived usefulness and ease of use as primary determinants of the adoption and sustained engagement with digital health interventions [27, 38]. In the context of tele-prehabilitation, a high level of technology acceptance is particularly relevant, as it may mediate adherence and, consequently, functional outcomes; patients who find the technology easy to operate and beneficial to their recovery are more likely to complete the prescribed sessions without direct clinician oversight [39, 40].

Another finding of the present study is the efficacy of the unsupervised brochure-guided exercise group. Despite the complete absence of real-time supervision, BR participants achieved significant improvements across all four primary outcomes, and no significant between-group differences were observed relative to NMES for the 30CST, 6MWT, or Oxford score. Notably, the BR group showed significantly greater gains in the 6MWT compared with the SV group. These results are consistent with emerging evidence that carefully structured, written self-directed exercise program, specifically when accompanied by regular monitoring contacts, can achieve outcomes comparable to supervised modalities in the preoperative period for LLA [6, 34, 35]. A pilot RCT by Doiron-Cedrin et al. [41] reported comparable functional outcomes between tele-prehabilitation and in-person prehabilitation, with similarly high compliance in both arms. From a health systems perspective, the functional equivalence of brochure-based exercise on most endpoints, combined with its minimal resource requirements, has important implications for scalability and equitable access. Patients facing geographical barriers, limited digital literacy, or insufficient access to synchronous physiotherapy services may be effectively supported through a well-designed written programme, without requiring clinician input for each session [42].

Several limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, the absence of a no-treatment control group precludes definitive attribution of the observed gains to the prehabilitation interventions themselves, as spontaneous variability in function, regression to the mean, or anticipatory effects related to the upcoming surgery cannot be excluded. Critically, the present design permits only comparison between different tele-prehabilitation modalities; it does not allow conclusions about whether tele-prehabilitation as a whole is more effective than standard care or no preoperative intervention. The within-group improvements observed from T0 to T1 should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of absolute treatment efficacy, but rather as change occurring within a randomized comparison of three active remote strategies. Future trials should include a usual-care control arm to address this fundamental question. Second, the intervention window of four weeks may have been insufficient to maximize the functional and quality-of-life benefits of supervised exercise, particularly given the higher baseline scores in the SV group. Another limitation is that, although the type of prosthesis was included as a covariate in the primary analysis, and THA- and TKA-specific sensitivity analyses were performed, the study was not powered to detect prosthesis-specific treatment effects. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution and considered as generating hypotheses. Additionally, the postoperative outcome assessment was limited to very early time points (post-surgery days 4 and 10), without a preoperative 6MWT baseline. This makes it difficult to determine whether observed postoperative walking capacity differences reflect the influence of preoperative between-group differences in function, and precludes conclusions about the longer-term impact of tele-prehabilitation on postoperative recovery. This represents an important limitation, as the principal clinical rationale for prehabilitation lies in its potential to enhance postoperative recovery trajectories rather than preoperative function in isolation; the present findings should therefore be regarded as evidence of preoperative feasibility and functional benefit rather than of improved postoperative recovery. Future studies should include assessments at least 3 months postoperatively, and should establish a preoperative 6MWT baseline to enable valid pre-post surgical comparisons.

Recruitment from a single specialist orthopedic center in Italy may limit the generalizability of these findings to other healthcare contexts and patient populations. Additionally, given the nature of the interventions, blinding of participants to their allocated treatment was not feasible; differential expectations regarding the perceived efficacy of NMES, supervised exercise, or brochure-guided exercise may have influenced patient-reported outcomes such as the Oxford score.

From a clinical standpoint, the findings of this trial support a cautiously optimistic view of NMES-based tele-prehabilitation as a scalable, remotely deliverable strategy that may offer advantages in mobility-related outcomes over physiotherapist-supervised exercise, consistent with the selective pattern described above, while largely matching brochure-guided exercise on most endpoints. The results also demonstrate that effective preoperative functional improvement does not necessarily require real-time clinician supervision, a critical consideration given the increasing pressure on physiotherapy services in many healthcare systems. The convergence of outcomes between the BR and NMES groups across multiple endpoints raises the possibility that patient engagement and adherence, rather than the specific exercise modality, may be a principal driver of functional benefit in the preoperative period. Clinicians should therefore consider both NMES and structured brochure-guided exercise as evidence-supported, accessible alternatives to face-to-face or videoconference-supervised prehabilitation, particularly where full supervision is difficult or impossible to provide [33, 40].

Conclusion

Tele-prehabilitation prior to LLA is feasible, well tolerated, and associated with meaningful preoperative functional improvements irrespective of modality. As all three arms received an active intervention and no usual-care control group was included, these findings should be understood as reflecting the comparative effectiveness of three remote delivery strategies rather than the absolute efficacy of tele-prehabilitation versus standard care or no intervention. Within this framework, NMES represents a promising option, particularly when access to supervised exercise is limited. However, even the structured brochure-guided programme demonstrated meaningful functional improvements across all primary outcomes, highlighting its potential as a low-resource, scalable tool for preoperative preparation in patients with limited access to technology or clinical supervision. Future studies should clarify whether these preoperative gains translate into improved postoperative outcomes, define the optimal NMES protocol, and evaluate cost-effectiveness and patient-level predictors of response to support personalized and scalable implementation.
