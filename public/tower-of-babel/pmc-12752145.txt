From function to omics: endophytic Beauveria bassiana promotes maize growth by activating phytohormone signaling pathways under elevated carbon dioxide
Authors: Yafeng Zhang, Huan Wen, Qiyun Li, Yang Lu, Zhengkun Zhang, Li Sui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral entomopathogenic fungal (EPF) species can colonise and establish symbiotic relationships with plants as endophytes, which affects crop growth under elevated carbon dioxide (eCO₂) concentrations. However, how EPF facilitates eCO2 in plants is poorly understood, especially at the transcriptional and metabolic levels. Here, the authors used transcriptomics and metabolomics to examine the effects of a widely used EPF, Beauveria bassiana, on maize growth under eCO2, and how it regulated enzyme activity and endogenous hormone metabolism.ResultsBeauveria bassiana colonisation significantly enhanced maize growth across CO₂ concentrations. Key effects include: 39.64% greater leaf area than controls at ambient CO₂ during S3. Significant developmental divergence in leaf area between S3-S4 under eCO₂, 11.8% higher 100-grain weight in eCO₂+Bb vs. eCO₂ alone. Concurrent increases in stress-responsive enzymes and hormones aligned with omics-revealed activation of primary metabolic pathways (ZMA01100) and secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways (ZMA01110).ConclusionsThese findings suggested that B. bassiana colonization modulates plant growth under eCO2 by regulating the expression of related genes, and in turn, enzyme activity and hormone metabolism. The findings of the present study offered a theoretical foundation for elucidating the interactions between EPFs and plants under climate change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07763-5.Keywords: Entomopathogenic fungi, Climate change, Phytohormone, Transcriptomics, Metabolomics research

Introduction

Anthropogenic activities are altering nearly all of Earth’s natural systems; the ambient carbon dioxide (CO₂) concentration has increased from preindustrial levels of 280 µmol·mol−1 to over 410 µmol·mol−1 today, in turn affecting plant physiological processes and ecosystem dynamics [1, 2]. Elevated atmospheric CO2 (eCO2) concentrations in the atmosphere are key environmental factors affecting crop growth [3, 4]. It significantly increases the net photosynthetic efficiency of C3 and C4 plants via diverse physiological mechanisms, thereby promoting crop biomass accumulation and yield improvement [5–7]. These changes not only increase crop yield but also improve crop quality and positively impact agricultural production. Elevated CO2, in many cases, affects the physiological and growth characteristics of many species. For example, eCO2 alters the functional diversity and composition of beneficial microbial communities, in addition to increasing the resistance threshold of plants to lead stress by inducing specific defence responses [8].

The interaction between plants and microorganisms has increasingly attracted attention in the context of climate change. Plants coexist with microorganisms, which significantly influence plant growth and functional traits [9]. Beneficial plant-associated microorganisms may stimulate plant growth and increase resistance to disease and abiotic stress [10]. Some studies have confirmed that the combined application of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and plant growth-promoting endophytes can significantly influence plant height, aboveground and underground fresh weights, and abscisic acid and indole acetic acid contents under drought stress [11]. As a key environmental factor, fluctuating CO₂ concentrations have dual regulatory effects on plant systems: direct physiological impacts and indirect effects on growth modulation through modulation of microbial colonisation dynamics [12]. Studies have shown that the effects of CO2 on fungal‒plant symbionts are exerted mainly through fungal colonisation, toxins, and plant physiology. For example, ericoid mycorrhizae and dark septate endophytes exhibited increased colonisation in ericaceous dwarf shrubs under eCO2 [13]. In perennial ryegrass colonised by endophytic fungi, the carbohydrate content was significantly greater than that in uninfected plants [14]. In addition, under eCO2, Beauveria bassiana inoculation can offset the adverse effects of eCO2 on maize plants and maintain the nitrogen (N) content at the baseline level [15]. This mutualistic mechanism acted as an ecological amplifier, where enhanced plant environmental resilience initiates cascading effects: optimised nutrient turnover rates coupled with stabilised ecosystem integrity indices.

As a broad-spectrum entomopathogenic fungus, B. bassiana has an endophytic colonisation capacity that significantly enhances host plant growth parameters [16, 17]. It can directly stimulate cells to promote cell division and elongation, thus having a positive effect on plant growth [18, 19] or enhancing plant resistance to biotic stress and abiotic stress, thereby indirectly promoting plant growth and development [20, 21]. In addition, the symbiotic relationship between B. bassiana and plants enhances nutrient absorption by plants, especially the absorption of essential elements such as phosphorus (P) and N, which are crucial to healthy plant growth [22–24]. Furthermore, symbiotic interactions enhance agricultural productivity through diverse inoculation strategies. Experimental evidence has demonstrated that optimised microbial delivery methods (e.g., seed priming and soil inoculation) coordinately regulate root architecture and shoot development [17, 25]. However, how B. bassiana facilitates plant growth and development under eCO2 is poorly understood, especially the associated genes and metabolites.

Plant hormones are key regulators of plant growth and development, and changes in their levels affect plant physiological responses directly [26]. Gibberellin solution supplementation of corn plants can increase the grain-filling rate by increasing the auxin, gibberellin, zeatin, and abscisic acid contents in the grain [27]. Recent studies have confirmed that entomopathogenic fungi can regulate the activation of the defence response hormone signaling pathway in corn by producing defence-related enzymes, nonenzymatic antioxidants, plant hormones, and their corresponding genes [28]. Furthermore, eCO2 promoted plant secondary metabolite synthesis and enhanced plant resistance [29]. The integration of core omics tools such as transcriptomics and metabolomics has facilitated physiological research in several Solanaceae and horticultural crop species [30]. Multi-omics analysis can reveal intricate biochemical pathways involved in plant responses and activities, providing deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying such beneficial symbioses. By deciphering such pathways, researchers could develop targeted strategies for optimising plant-microbe interactions, ultimately fostering sustainable agricultural practices.

Here, the authors investigated how the effects of CO2 and B. bassiana colonisation regulate maize photosynthetic characteristics and endogenous hormone metabolism, in addition to the molecular mechanisms of the effects of their interaction on plant resistance and growth, under eCO2. Transcriptomics and metabolomics were used to investigate how B. bassiana colonisation regulated associated pathways and functions in maize growth under eCO2. The findings of the present study could provide a theoretical foundation for elucidating the relationship between entomopathogenic fungi and plants under climate change.

Materials and methods

Fungal strains and plants

The EPF B. bassiana strain BbofDH1-5 (D1-5) (GenBank accession number: PRJNA178080) was isolated from a dead Ostrinia furnacalis larva found at a farm in Dehui county, Jilin province, China. The strain was deposited at the Agricultural Culture Collection of China (accession number: ACCC 32726). The fungus was cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA, Hopebio Spectrum Instruments Co., Ltd., China) for 15 days at 26 °C in the dark. The conidia were harvested by scraping with a sterile spatula and then kept at 4 °C in the dark before use.

The authors selected maize B73 as the study subject because it is a foundational germplasm for molecular breeding programs that has 38,620 annotated genes, with 1.8 million SNPs catalogued in the Panzea database [31].

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted from May to September 2023 at the Gongzhuling Experimental Station of the Institute of Plant Protection, Jilin Academy of Agricultural Sciences (43°30’N, 124°47’E; 225 m above sea level), Gongzhuling City, Jilin Province, Northeast China. There were four treatments: (1) maize inoculation with Tween-80 solution and plants grown at ambient carbon dioxide (CO2) concentration (Control); (2) maize inoculation with B. bassiana suspension and plants grown at ambient CO2 concentration (Bb); (3) maize inoculation with Tween-80 solution and plants grown at elevated CO2 concentration (eCO2); and (4) maize inoculation with B. bassiana suspension and plants grown at eCO2 (eCO2 + Bb). The maize was planted in 32 × 36-cm plastic pots, which were placed in open-top chambers (OTCs). Three maize seeds per pot were sown, and the 12 pots were placed into one OTC with ambient or increased CO2 concentrations. One corn plant was planted per pot, with 12 pots per treatment, and 4 replicates, yielding a total of 192 corn plants. A total of 3360 kg of soil was collected from the field (17.5 kg per pot × 192 pots), sterilised at 121 °C for 2 h, and used to fill each pot with 17.5 kg of soil.

Two CO2 levels, the ambient CO2 concentration (c. 400 µmol·mol−1 CO2, Control) and eCO2 (c. 600 µmol·mol−1 CO2, eCO2), were set up within regular octagonal OTCs with rigid frames (4.2 m in diameter, and 3.0 m in height). There were eight OTC replicates for each CO2 level, for a total of 16 chambers in the study. For eCO2 treatment, CO2 (purity 99.8%, Gongzhuling Hongyuan Gas Co. Ltd, Gongzhuling, China) was added to the chambers through the inlet airstreams, achieving the target c. 600 µmol·mol−1. For the Control treatment, the concentrations were monitored in the chambers without CO2 input, once every 60 s to ensure relatively stable CO2 levels. These chambers were set up outdoors, so that the temperature, relative humidity, and light regime in the chambers were similar to natural environmental conditions during the experimental period (May–September).

Maize planting, inoculation, and endophytic EPF colonisation

Three maize seeds were sown per pot, with 16 pots per replicate for each of the four treatments. In each pot, 12.7 g of fertiliser (NPK 28-14−12) was added for seedling survival and growth. The seedlings were then thinned to one after emergence. All the plants were watered as needed. In an attempt to establish B. bassiana as an endophyte in Z. mays, root irrigation treatments were used. The specific operations were as follows: At 7–21 d after maize emergence, maize plants were treated with B. bassiana at a concentration of 1 × 108 spores/mL, 50 mL each time, with an interval of 7 d, for a total of three times. The control group received an equal amount of 0.05%(v/v) Tween-80 solution. After 24 h of the third irrigation, the colonisation rate of B. bassiana was determined via the PDA detection method [32].

After the surface of the corn leaves were disinfected via a five-step disinfection method, the edge of the corn leaves were cut off, nine 1-cm2 sections were cut from the remaining leaves, and the excess water on the surface of the leaves was removed with sterile filter paper. After 7 d of culture, the growth of bacteria on the plant tissues was observed and recorded every day. The calculation method for the colonization rate is as follows:

Colonization rate (%) = 100 × (the number of B. bassiana colonized plants/total number of plants).

Effects of B. bassiana on maize growth

To examine the effects of endophytes on plants, the development process of maize was divided into five key stages: the seedling stage (S1), jointing stage (S2), bell mouth stage (S3), heading stage (S4), and milk-ripe stage (S5) [32]. The maize height, leaf area, and maize biomass in the S1–S5 period were measured. The heights of all maize plants in each pot were measured via a ruler from the soil surface to the stem tip. The length from the base of the corn leaf to the tip of the leaf was the leaf length, and the widest part of the leaf was the leaf width. Leaf area = k × length × width, k = 0.75. In addition, maize shoot and root biomass were determined by drying in a forced-air oven for 48 h at 80 ℃ and subsequent weighing. Five corn plants were selected randomly from each treatment for measurement. Seed production was calculated as the weight of 100 maize kernels per plant at the mature stage of corn [33].

The LI − 6400 photosynthetic measurement system was used to measure the net photosynthetic rate, transpiration rate, stomatal conductance, and intercellular CO₂ concentration of maize leaves after inoculation with Beauveria bassiana [34]. The measurement was carried out from 9:00 to 12:00 a.m. each time. One leaf was measured per plant, and the third fully expanded leaf from the top was selected for measurement. Three maize plants were randomly selected for each treatment, and each leaf was measured three times, and the average value was taken.

Effects of endophytes on endogenous hormones in maize

At the bell mouth of maize, six maize plants were selected randomly from each replicate. From each plant, an area about 25 cm2 was selected for cutting aseptically. The pooled leaf tissue samples were flash-frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen, homogenised via LN2-cooled mills, and stored at −80 ± 2 °C in cryovials. Ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry was used to analyze and detect the endogenous hormones related to maize, and hormone analysis was performed based on Wang et al., with slight modifications [35]. The hormones included salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), gibberellic acid (GA), and abscisic acid (ABA).

Effect of endophytes on maize enzyme activity

To further analyze the effects of endophytes on maize growth and development and stress resistance, associated enzyme activities were determined, including peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL). POD, SOD, and CAT were analyzed via the methods of Wang et al., and PAL was analyzed according to the method of Riaz et al. [36, 37]. The specific sampling method is the same as that in 2.5.

Multivariate data analysis

Twelve maize leaves were selected randomly from each treatment at the bell mouth of the maize, wrapped in tin paper, frozen in LN2, and stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction. Approximately 1 µg RNA per sample was used for library construction. Corn samples were subjected to deep sequencing by Anshengda Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China, https://web.azenta.com), and six biological replicates were set up for each treatment. The sequencing company detected RNA integrity accurately, carried out strict quality control using the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and conducted Illumina (Illumina NovaSeq 6000, Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) sequencing after library inspection. In addition, the company was commissioned to perform metabolomics analysis on the samples using a quadrupole-electrostatic field orbital trap high-resolution mass spectrometer (HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbittrap Mass Spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and to beat the UHPLC system for extensive targeted metabolomics analysis.

Two plant hormones (IAA and GA), related differential metabolites, and related differential genes, were combined with different treatment samples. Through gene clustering and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis in the module, a co-expression regulatory network (weighted association network analysis) was constructed. The gene regulatory network was constructed and visualised using Cytoscape v3.10.0 (https://cytoscape.org/download.html).

The qRT-PCR method was used to obtain the relative quantitative levels of the target genes. Etcpr1 was used as the internal reference gene in the present study. Four comparison groups in the RNA-Seq results were selected to identify genes linked to plant resistance and growth promotion [38] and differentially up-regulated genes Supplementary Tab. S1). The 2-ΔΔCT method was used to detect the expression of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the samples of the four treatment groups. The operation was performed according to the instructions of 2 × Q3 SYBR qPCR Master Mix Universal (Beyotime Biotechnology, Seoul, Korea), and the negative control was ddH2O. The qRT-PCR reaction involved pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles, extension at 95 °C for 15 s, extension at 60 °C for 60 s, and extension at 95 °C for 15 s [39].

Data analyses

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to examine differences in growth data (plant height, chlorophyll, and aboveground, underground, whole plant biomass, and yield) among treatment groups. Two-way ANOVA was used to test differences in endogenous hormones, enzyme activity, and relative expression of DEGs in each treatment group. All data analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3 (R Core Team, 2024) with the built-in stats package.

Results

Growth-promoting effects of B. bassiana colonization on plants

B. bassiana colonization rates in inoculated plants at ambient CO2 and eCO2 were 35.4% and 37.5%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1). B. bassiana colonization, eCO2, and their interaction had significant effects on maize plant height, leaf area, and dry matter (Fig. 1). B. bassiana colonization and interaction between CO2 and B. bassiana had significant effects on maize plant height (Fig. 1A). The plant height of the Bb treatment group increased by 10.44% compared with that of the control group, under ambient CO2 concentration, in the S1 period (P < 0.05, F = 6.440). Maize leaf area after B. bassiana colonization was significantly greater than that of the control group, under ambient CO2 concentration, and it increased by 39.64% in the S3 period (P < 0.0001, F = 35.322). Maize leaf area after B. bassiana colonization was significantly different between the S3 and S4 stages, under eCO2 (Fig. 1C).

In addition, B. bassiana colonization influenced maize total biomass significantly. The difference was more significant under ambient CO2, and the total biomass of the treatment group after colonization was 9.40% greater than that of the control group (Fig. 1B). The total biomass of the Bb treatment group was 9.5% greater than that of the control group, under ambient CO2 (P < 0.001, F = 19.929). In addition, eCO2 promoted an increase in the total biomass of maize. Under ambient CO2, the difference between the treatment group and the control group was mainly reflected in the underground part. Under the high CO2 condition, the aboveground biomass of the treatment group was significantly different from that of the control group (Fig. 1D). Among treatments, the eCO₂ + Bb group exhibited the highest 100-grain weight. Weight per 100 seeds of the Bb treatment group was 14.5% greater than that of the control group (P < 0.001, F = 13.163), and the eCO2 + Bb treatment group was 11.8% greater than that of the eCO2 treatment group (P = 0.754, F = 0.100).

The results showed that both high CO₂ concentration and B. bassiana colonization significantly affected the gas exchange parameters of maize leaves (P < 0.05). Under ambient CO₂ conditions, the transpiration rate of the Bb treatment was significantly reduced by 10.3% compared with the control (P < 0.05). In contrast, under high CO₂ concentration (eCO₂) conditions, there was no significant difference between the eCO₂ treatment and the eCO₂ + Bb treatment. In contrast, the stomatal conductance of the control group (Control) was significantly higher than that of other treatments (P < 0.05). The intercellular CO₂ concentration of the eCO₂ + Bb treatment was significantly higher than that of other treatments (P < 0.05). That is, the colonization of B. bassiana may improve water use efficiency by reducing transpiration loss (Supplementary Figure S2).

Transcriptome analysis of enzyme activity and related genes

B. bassiana colonization, CO2, and their interaction had significant effects on maize enzyme activity. POD, SOD, CAT, and PAL activities in maize leaves were increased significantly, by 54.30%, 12.85%, 15.28%, and 23.49%, respectively, under ambient CO2 concentration, (P < 0.05), following colonization by B. bassiana (Fig. 2B). Conversely, under eCO2, SOD and PAL decreased significantly after colonization, with no significant difference between CAT and POD activity. Furthermore, two-way ANOVA showed that B. bassiana colonization exhibited a significant main effect on POD activity (P < 0.001, partial η² > 0.14); eCO₂ had significant main effects on SOD, CAT, and PAL activities (P < 0.01, partial η² > 0.14); and excluding CAT, significant Bb × CO₂ interaction was observed for all enzymatic parameters (P < 0.001, partial η² > 0.14). In addition, there was a synergistic interaction effect between CO2 concentration and fungal treatment (Supplementary Tab. S2).

Transcriptomic analysis of maize growth-related enzyme activity expression under elevated CO2 concentration and Beauveria bassiana colonization. A Differential gene statistics and cluster analysis. B Total numbers of up and down regulated gene expression compared with the Control. C KEGG bubble diagram of differential genes between different treatment groups

High-throughput sequencing generated 160.89 Gb of high-quality clean reads across all samples; all samples exhibited exceptional base-call accuracy, with Q20 (99.99% base confidence) and Q30 (99.90% base confidence) scores ≥ 97.43% and ≥ 93.30%, respectively (Supplementary Tab. S3). Raw data were processed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina Inc.) with parameters optimized for plant transcriptome studies. The comparison efficiency of sequencing data ranged from 96.31% to 97.63%, and the unique comparison rate ranged from 89.51% to 93.39%. In the four groups of comparisons, 16 co-expressed genes were identified (Fig. 2A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs across treatment comparisons were significantly enriched in three core biological processes (P < 0.05): defense response (GO: 0006952), carbohydrate metabolic processes (GO: 0005975), and photosynthesis (GO: 0015979). At the molecular functional level, sequence-specific DNA-binding transcription factor activity (GO: 0003700) and chlorophyll a/b binding (GO: 0016168) were the dominant categories (Supplementary Fig. S3).

KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed distinct pathway activation patterns among different treatment groups (Fig. 2C). In both the Bb-VS-eCO2 + Bb and Control-VS-Bb comparison groups, the up-regulated DEGs were predominantly enriched in fundamental metabolic pathways, particularly metabolic pathways (ko01100) and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ko01110). The Control-VS-eCO2 comparison group showed unique enrichment of up-regulated genes in the glycosaminoglycan degradation pathway (ko00531). In addition, the eCO2-VS-eCO2 + Bb comparison demonstrated significant enrichment of up-regulated DEGs in specialised biosynthetic pathways, including steroid biosynthesis (ko00100), diterpenoid biosynthesis (ko00904), and biosynthesis of benzoxazinoids (ko00402). In addition, transcriptome analysis revealed that after B. bassiana colonization, the expression of multiple genes related to enzyme activity in maize was up-regulated, such as POD-related gene px24. Under eCO2, the genes still exhibited similar synergistic effects on the whole.

Metabolomics analysis of plant hormones and related metabolites

Phytohormone profiling revealed significant B. bassiana-induced modulation of endogenous hormone homeostasis, particularly enhanced under eCO₂ (Fig. 3C). Comparative analysis demonstrated that fungal colonization triggered marked enhancement of stress-related hormones SA and JA, along with auxin derivatives IAA and Indole-3-butyric Acid (IBA). In addition, eCO₂ exposure amplified the effects substantially, with SA and IAA showing the most pronounced accumulation, based on Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry quantification.

Metabolomics analysis results showed that all samples showed significant separation. Although Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis validation indicated some risk of model overfitting (Q² intercept range: −0.13 to −0.6, P < 0.05), the high R²Y values (0.87–0.93) supported reliability of the results, confirming the robust interpretation of metabolic variation (Fig. 3A). A total of 3,800 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified across treatment groups. Subsequent VIP screening (VIP > 1.5) prioritized 20 key metabolites with significant discriminatory power (Supplementary Fig. S4). KEGG pathway mapping revealed predominant involvement in core biological processes including metabolic pathways, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of cofactors (Fig. 3D). Among them, the plant hormone signal transduction pathway showed significant enrichment after B. bassiana colonization, which was one of the most significantly up-regulated pathways in the metabolome data (Fig. 3B). Under eCO2, IAA was accumulated significantly. In addition, several other metabolites showed significant differential accumulation between different treatment groups. Glutamine, malic acid, and fructose-6-phosphate contents showed significant differences across different treatment groups.

Combined multi-omics analysis

Combined with transcriptomics and metabolomics analysis, six candidate genes related to different metabolic pathways were up-regulated significantly between the treatment groups, and mainly showed synergistic regulation with three key metabolites, including the Super Class: lipids and lipid molecules, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and organic acids and derivatives (Fig. 4A). Among them, lipids, which are the main components of the cell membrane (phospholipids), are involved in energy storage (triglycerides), signal transduction (JA), and grain development (oil accumulation); phenylpropanoids (lignin, flavonoids) are involved in cell wall construction (enhancing stem mechanical strength) and stress response (antioxidant, pest resistance); organic acids (such as citric acid and malic acid) are the core intermediates of energy metabolism (TCA cycle), which are involved in carbon and N balance, ion homeostasis, and stress response.

Multi-omics joint analysis and verification of relative expression of differential genes. A Association network diagram of differential genes and metabolites. B Verification of relative expression of differential genes by qRT-PCR

The DAMs and DEGs were enriched in metabolic pathways (ZMA01100) and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (ZMA01110). Comparative transcriptomic profiling revealed significant upregulation of six candidate genes associated with distinct metabolic pathways across treatment groups (Fig. 4B). For the Bb-VS-eCO2 + Bb group, carbohydrate metabolic pathway-related genes, LOC100279398, dhr2, and LOC103625882, which are associated with cofactors and vitamin metabolic pathways, were observed. In the Control-VS-Bb comparison groups, px24 and TIDP3136, related to the biosynthetic pathways of other secondary metabolites, ga2ox13, related to the metabolic pathways of terpenoids and polyketides, were observed. qRT-PCR validation demonstrated high concordance between RNA-Seq and experimental measurements for all six genes, confirming their roles in pathogen-induced metabolic reprogramming.

Discussion

Interactions between plants and microbes are extremely complex, and a recent proliferation of studies characterising plant-associated microbiomes and their effects on host performance has further highlighted the role that microbes play in plant health [40–42]. Plants may exhibit alterations in plant hormones and enzyme activity following microbial colonization, which could have positive effects on plant growth [43]. In addition, plants and microorganisms form complex interaction networks, and CO2 is a key factor affecting the interaction [32, 44]. Here, our results indicated that the interaction between eCO2 and endophytic B. bassiana could promote plant growth by regulating growth-related genes and metabolite pathways (Fig. 5).

Multi-omics analysis of the mode of action of Beauveria bassiana colonization, elevated CO2 concentration, and their interaction on maize growth

Endophytic fungi could promote the growth and development of host plants by synthesising plant hormones and enhancing nutrient absorption, especially absorption of essential elements such as P and N, which are vital for the healthy growth of plants [45–47]. These promoting effects are still significant when plants are subjected to specific environmental factors. Studies have confirmed that the increase in ambient CO2 concentration will significantly increase plant yield and biomass, and alter flower size and flowering time of plants [48, 49]. Similarly, in the present study, B. bassiana colonization increased plant height, leaf area, biomass, and yield of maize during the entire growth period under either ambient concentration or eCO2. Compared to ambient CO2 concentration, plant biomass increased significantly under eCO2. In our previous study, increased CO2 concentration promoted plant growth and enhanced their capacity to recruit B. bassiana in soil, which may also facilitate growth [16]. Endophytic B. bassiana colonization could promote the secretion of various growth hormones by host plants [19, 50]. Yan et al. (2025) confirmed that high CO2 concentration can effectively alleviate the negative effects of high temperature and salinity on plant physiology, reduce ABA content, and increase the levels of growth-promoting plant hormones [51]. Our results also indicated that compared with maize plants treated with B. bassiana under ambient CO2 concentration, the contents of IAA, IBA and other hormones promoting plant growth increased in maize colonized by B. bassiana under eCO2. This may be due to a synergistic effect between eCO2 and B. bassiana colonization, regulating hormone content and key enzyme activity, thereby promoting plant growth. Indeed, the synergistic effect between elevated CO2 concentration and B. bassiana colonization on hormone content is largely unknown, and further elucidation is required.

The observed upregulation of metabolic pathway genes in the present study further implies that endophytes may act as key regulators of plant metabolic networks, potentially through the secretion of signaling molecules or the modulation of hormonal pathways that coordinate resource allocation toward defence and growth-related metabolic fluxes [52, 53]. Recent studies have begun to identify key molecular players in the process. Genes associated with metabolic and secondary metabolites pathways were reportedly upregulated significantly in maize seedlings colonized by endophytes [54]. In addition, peroxidase-related genes have been shown to regulate hydrogen peroxide expression, and plants can synthesise active endogenous gibberellins through GA20ox [55–57]. The findings suggested that the presence of endophytes significantly activated metabolic activities of maize seedlings, promoted the synthesis of secondary metabolites, and enhanced the stress resistance and growth potential of plants [58, 59]. Furthermore, Dhr2, a β-glucosidase isoenzyme derived from sorghum, was essential for plant defense and response to environmental stress or herbivore invasion. It was closely related to the production and regulation of plant secondary metabolites [60]. Elevated CO2 reportedly increased the activities of glucosidase, β-xylosidase, and phosphatase significantly [61]. In the present study, the ZMA01100 and ZMA01110 pathways were upregulated significantly, indicating improved plant growth reinforcement under the interaction of B. bassiana and eCO2. Additionally, the genes px24, Dhr2, and ga2ox13 within these pathways were significantly upregulated, further indicating enhanced plant growth reinforcement due to the interaction of B. bassiana and eCO2. These pathways may also have an impact on specific plant traits, such as hormone biosynthesis and stress adaptation. RT-qPCR verified the transcriptome data, indicating that the synergistic effect of CO2 and fungi on plant growth had a molecular basis. This may be due to the synergistic effect of CO2 and fungi, which also promotes the synthesis of plant secondary metabolites, further enhancing the stress resistance and growth potential of plants.

The plant hormone auxin controls growth and developmental responses throughout the life of a plant [62]. It influences the formation of roots, stems, leaves, and other organs by regulating cell division, elongation, and differentiation, ensuring that plants adapt to growth under various environmental conditions [63]. The auxin signaling mechanism involves multiple receptors and transcription factors, which regulate gene expression precisely through complex signal cascade reactions, thereby achieving fine-tuned control over plant growth and development [64]. Our findings revealed that the plant hormone signal transduction pathway was enriched significantly following B. bassiana colonization, and it was among the most up-regulated pathways. The finding further substantiates the synergistic effect of eCO2 and fungi on plant hormone regulation. In general, eCO2 promotes SA signaling and represses the JA pathway; however, the responses vary depending on plant species, growth conditions, photoperiod, and fertilizer management [65]. Additionally, our results indicated that B. bassiana colonization led to greater SA and JA contents in maize plants compared to the control group, under eCO2 conditions. This may be because B. bassiana colonization adjusts the dynamic balance between SA and JA, thereby optimizing the balance in plant growth, which may facilitate stable plant growth in changing environments. Microbes can promote plant growth by regulating nutritional and hormonal balance, producing plant growth regulators, solubilizing nutrients, and inducing resistance against plant pathogens [66]. Further studies should focus on the synergistic effect of eCO2 concentration and B. bassiana colonization on specific pathways to identify the molecules involved in maize growth and development.

The comprehensive response of plants to abiotic stresses is unique and cannot be inferred only by studying plants exposed to a single stress factor [67]. Adaptation mechanisms include changes in gene expression, ion regulation, hormone balance, and metabolite synthesis or degradation [68]. Compared with single-omics research, multi-omics analysis can reveal the cascade effects of pathways in joint analysis, and each omics level is affected by other omics levels, which highlights the complexity of the role of metabolism in stress response [69]. Similarly, our study found that the combined action of B. bassiana and eCO2 improved plant growth significantly, and the plant hormone signaling pathways were enriched. This is consistent with the results of a previous study where Azotobacter chroococcum inoculation and high CO2 concentration affected plant hormone signal transduction in maize [70]. In addition, Huang et al. (2019) showed that an increase in CO2 concentration significantly inhibited gene expression in photosynthesis and carbohydrate biosynthesis pathways [71]. Our results indicated that B. bassiana colonization under eCO2 increased GA2ox expression and GA content, thus promoting root growth. This may be due to the synergistic effect of B. bassiana and CO2, which optimizes the plant hormone network and further improves overall plant growth performance [72]. The synergistic effect of climate change and fungi, and the multi-level regulatory mechanism, makes plants exhibit stronger adaptability and more robust growth in the face of complex environments [73]. Through deep mining of multi-omics data, we observed that under the combined action of B. bassiana and eCO2, synthesis of plant metabolites and secondary metabolites were enhanced significantly, which improved the stress resistance of maize plants and facilitated maintenance of a growth advantage under a changing climate [74]. The synergistic effect provides insight on how future crop production could cope with climate change. Therefore, future studies on the relationship between eCO2 and B. bassiana colonization should provide more information on the regulatory requirements of the pathway.

Conclusion

Our findings indicated that the synergistic effect of eCO2 concentration and B. bassiana colonization could enhance maize plant growth and development. B. bassiana, eCO2 concentration, and their interaction had significant effects on plant height, leaf area, and maize dry matter. Furthermore, there was accumulating evidence that B. bassiana significantly induced endogenous hormone homeostasis and enzymatic activity, particularly under eCO2 conditions [75]. The findings suggested that maize plants inoculated with B. bassiana clarify the synergistic regulation between eCO2 concentration and B. bassiana colonization. It may provide a theoretical foundation for elucidating the interaction between entomopathogenic fungi and plants under climate change scenarios.
