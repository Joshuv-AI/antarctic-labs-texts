Understanding Sex and Gender Effects on Autism Spectrum Disorder and Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder: Clinical Presentation and Risk Factors
Authors: Allison Pearlman, Amelia Abell, Pia Jauhar, Erynn Christensen, Elvisha Dhamala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) exhibit significant sex and gender differences in prevalence, clinical presentation, and risk factors. Males are diagnosed with ASD 4 times more frequently than females, and females show slightly higher OCD prevalence, though diagnostic biases may obscure true rates. Current diagnostic criteria predominantly reflect male phenotypes, contributing to underdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis in females who often camouflage symptoms and present with less stereotypical behaviors. Here, we examine sex and gender influences on these disorders across clinical presentation, genetic factors, and developmental risks. Beyond sex, we emphasize the understudied role of gender, including elevated prevalence in gender-diverse populations and the influence of gender norms on symptom expression. We examine how other sociodemographic factors, such as race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, can compound diagnostic disparities. Finally, we provide recommendations for reforming diagnostic criteria and clinical practice to better serve females, gender-diverse individuals, and marginalized populations. Overall, implementing sex- and gender-informed diagnostic practices is essential to reduce diagnostic delays, improve treatment outcomes, and ensure equitable care for all individuals affected by ASD and OCD.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Camouflaging, Developmental psychology, Diagnostic disparities, Obsessive-compulsive disorder, Sex and gender

Sex and gender influence mental disorders, affecting prevalence, clinical presentation, and risk factors (1). These differences emerge early in development and persist across the lifespan (2). Two disorders that exhibit these complex influences are autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD).

ASD is characterized by persistent difficulties in social communication and interaction, along with restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (3). OCD involves a cyclical pattern of obsessions (persistent, intrusive thoughts or urges that cause distress) and compulsions (repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed to alleviate the distress caused by the obsessions or prevent feared consequences) (3). Both disorders feature repetitive behaviors and cognitive inflexibility that can have significant impacts on daily functioning, although they arise from different underlying mechanisms (4). ASD is an ego-syntonic disorder, where thoughts, behaviors, or symptoms are in harmony with an individual’s self-perception or values, while OCD is an ego-dystonic disorder, where thoughts, behaviors, or symptoms feel inconsistent with an individual’s self-image or values (5). As such, repetitive behaviors and cognitive inflexibility are often driven by restricted interests in ASD and compulsions in OCD (4,5).

Both disorders are influenced by sex and gender in terms of their clinical presentations and underlying risk factors. The term sex generally refers to biological influences, while gender refers to sociocultural influences. Effects of sex include the influence of genes, hormones, and chromosomes, while effects of gender include gender roles and stereotypes that shape how individuals express and cope with mental health concerns (6). However, this distinction fails to acknowledge the bidirectional effects of biology and sociocultural influences (2,6,7).

Here, we examine how sex and gender influence ASD and OCD, with 4 specific aims. As sex effects (as opposed to gender effects) have been more extensively studied, we present more detailed findings on sex, while highlighting the influence of gender where possible. First, we delineate disorder-specific patterns in how sex and gender shape each condition, with particular attention to clinical presentation and risk factors. Second, we identify shared sex and gender effects across both disorders to establish transdiagnostic patterns. Third, we explore how social norms and sociodemographic factors, beyond sex and gender, influence the manifestation of these disorders. Fourth, we highlight the research and clinical advancements needed to improve the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of these disorders in all individuals. Although ASD and OCD are etiologically unique disorders, they share behavioral characteristics, neural correlates, and cognitive features (8,9). Most notably, both disorders involve repetitive behaviors and have been shown to present with similar symptom severity across multiple domains, including stereotyped movement, repetitive language, self-injury, sameness behaviors, and attachment to and manipulation of objects (8). These repetitive behaviors can create diagnostic confusion in clinical practice. ASD and OCD also frequently co-occur (10) and are especially prevalent in gender-diverse populations. By examining these disorders together, we can distinguish transdiagnostic patterns from disorder-specific features, informing diagnosis and treatment.

Prevalence

ASD and OCD show distinct sex-based prevalence patterns that may be obscured by diagnostic biases.

A systematic review found prevalence rates ranging from 0.01% to 4.4% for ASD, with a median prevalence of approximately 1%, with males being 4 times more likely than females to experience ASD (11).

Analyses of World Mental Health surveys found prevalence rates ranging from 0.4% to 5.5% for OCD (12), while a separate meta-analysis found an overall lifetime prevalence of 1.3% (13). Although sex differences in prevalence are debated, recent estimates suggest females are 1.6 times more likely than males to experience OCD (13).

ASD and OCD are highly likely to co-occur. An assessment of electronic health records from over 7900 youths found that approximately 5% of those with ASD have a diagnosis of OCD, and 25% of youths with OCD also have a diagnosis of ASD (14), while a separate meta-analysis found that approximately 12% of youths with ASD also exhibit OCD, and 10% of youths with OCD also exhibit ASD (15). These high comorbidity rates may, in part, reflect diagnostic uncertainty rather than true co-occurrence. Both disorders involve repetitive behaviors, but they are driven by restricted interests in ASD versus compulsions in OCD. In clinical practice, distinguishing between these presentations can be challenging as clinicians may misinterpret repetitive behaviors in ASD as compulsions or vice versa, particularly when presentations are atypical. Sex differences in co-occurrence patterns have also been reported, although findings have been mixed, with some suggesting higher rates of co-occurrence in females and others in males (15).

However, these prevalence statistics may not accurately reflect true rates due to sex differences in diagnostic biases and clinical presentations.

Clinical Presentation

Sex influences the clinical presentations of ASD and OCD, with females typically exhibiting more subtle, socially acceptable symptoms that contribute to delayed or missed diagnosis (Figure 1).

Diagnosis of ASD requires persistent deficits in 3 areas of social communication (social-emotional reciprocity; nonverbal communicative behaviors; and developing, maintaining, and understanding relationships) and at least 2 of 4 types of restricted and repetitive behaviors (motor movements, inflexible routines, interests abnormal in intensity or focus, and sensory hyper- or hyporeactivity) with these symptoms present in early development and causing clinically significant impairment (3). Although ASD symptoms emerge in early childhood, recognition and diagnosis are often delayed, if not missed entirely, in females (16). Camouflaging refers to behaviors that hide or mask aspects of oneself to fit in with social norms and avoid negative consequences (17). Throughout development, females with ASD are more likely to camouflage through masking (i.e., strategies to hide one’s autistic characteristics, such as forced sustained eye contact) and assimilation (i.e., strategies to fit in within a social group, such as mimicking tones of voice and mannerisms) (18, 19, 20, 21). Females also exhibit more gender-typical interests (e.g., dolls) and less noticeable repetitive behaviors (e.g., lining up toys) (22,23). In contrast, males exhibit higher levels of more obvious atypical, repetitive behaviors (e.g., hand and finger mannerisms, object preoccupation), and social isolation (23), which are more likely to be recognized and reported as atypical by teachers in males, contributing to earlier diagnosis (22,24). Further, ASD diagnostic criteria are biased toward male-specific presentations (25), emphasizing the importance of considering the female autism phenotype that describes the unique presentations of ASD in females. This bias has also contributed to the perception of autism as a boy’s disorder, an idea that is reinforced by the extreme male brain theory (26). The theory portrays autism as a hyper-masculinized condition centered upon logic and pattern-based thinking as well as reduced capacity for empathy, which are traits commonly associated with male development (26). As a result, many believe that sex is the main influencer of ASD clinical presentation, although this oversimplification reduces the role of gender and other factors.

Diagnosis of OCD requires the presence of obsessions (recurrent, persistent thoughts/urges/images that are intrusive and cause distress) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviors or mental acts in response to obsessions or based on rigid rules to alleviate anxiety or prevent dreaded events) that are time consuming (take up more than 1 hour a day) and cause clinically significant distress or impairment (3). Although some studies have reported earlier onset of OCD symptoms in males (27, 28, 29), others have found no significant sex effects on age of onset (12). Externally oriented obsessions (e.g., contamination, aggressive thoughts) and cleaning compulsions are more common and severe in females, while internally oriented obsessions (e.g., sexual, religious) are more common and severe in males (30). Overall symptom severity is also higher in females (30). This difference in presentation, including more covert compulsions in females, and the higher level of societal acceptance of female-biased manifestations may influence how OCD is recognized and diagnosed across the sexes. Finally, parental and family accommodation [i.e., ways in which parents and family members participate in rituals, avoid certain situations, or change daily routines to support an individual with OCD (31)] may influence recognition and diagnosis. Family/parental accommodation is linked to greater symptom severity (32), with emerging research suggesting this effect may be male specific (33). Therefore, this may result in heightened recognition and earlier diagnosis in males compared with females.

Psychiatric comorbidities are highly common in ASD and OCD. Individuals with ASD or OCD frequently report anxiety, attention, conduct, eating, mood, psychotic, and stress disorders, although exact rates vary across studies and diagnoses (34,35). These comorbidities may lead to core ASD/OCD symptoms being overshadowed by other diagnoses, especially in females (36). As an example, sensory sensitivities in ASD or weight-related obsessions and checking compulsions in OCD may lead to disordered eating behaviors, resulting in individuals being treated for an eating disorder but not the underlying ASD or OCD (36,37).

Collectively, these findings emphasize the need for sex-informed diagnostic practices and increased awareness of how ASD and OCD may manifest differently across the sexes to improve recognition and reduce diagnostic delays, especially in females.

Genetic Risk Factors

Both ASD and OCD show significant genetic contributions with emerging evidence of sex effects.

Several genes have been linked to ASD [see (38) for a detailed overview]. Sex affects heritability for ASD, with heritability estimated to be significantly higher for males at 87%, compared with 76% for females, based on a Swedish cohort study (39). This phenomenon has been termed the female protective effect, which theorizes that a higher threshold of genetic liability (i.e., a greater number or larger magnitude of risk factors) is required for females to develop ASD compared with males (40). The female protective effect has been reported in many genetic studies of ASD and may, in part, explain the higher prevalence in males (41, 42, 43).

Several studies have examined the genetic underpinnings of OCD [see (44) for an in-depth review]. While many genetic associations are shared across males and females, sex-specific associations have also been identified (45), suggesting certain genetic risk factors may confer greater risk in one sex compared with the other. Importantly, sex-specific genetic effects are generally probabilistic and context dependent, reflecting differential effect sizes or biological pathways rather than genetic variants that operate exclusively in males or females.

There is a significant overlap in the genes linked to ASD and OCD (46,47). These shared genes are involved in a wide range of biological functions, including synaptic transmission, dopamine signaling, memory, and behavioral fear response pathways (46). This shared genetic contribution may, in part, explain the overlap in the core symptomatology observed in both ASD and OCD. Future research examining whether sex influences this genetic overlap may provide insights into sex differences in the co-occurrence of ASD and OCD and, more broadly, into how sex-specific genetic mechanisms operate across diagnostically distinct but phenomenologically overlapping disorders.

Developmental Risk Factors

Prenatal and perinatal risk factors for ASD and OCD exhibit sex-specific effects, with many stressors differentially impacting male versus female neurodevelopment (Figure 2).

Developmental risk factors for ASD have been extensively characterized [see (48, 49, 50)]. Importantly, many of these risk factors seem to have greater effects in male children. An Australian birth cohort study found that prenatal exposure to bisphenol A, found in certain plastics and epoxy resins, was linked to higher ASD-related symptoms and diagnosis in males, but not in females (51). Maternal immune conditions, including autoimmune, asthma, and other chronic immune/inflammatory conditions, are also more prevalent in mothers of males with ASD compared with mothers of females with ASD (52). These effects may, in part, be driven by sex-specific responses to early-life stressors (53). Emerging research suggests that prenatal and perinatal stressors (e.g., immune activation, environmental toxins) may have a greater impact on male neurodevelopment through effects that disrupt social behaviors (53,54). Conversely, other risk factors exhibit female-specific effects. Maternal hyperandrogenism, due to hypertensive disorders of pregnancy or polycystic ovary syndrome, is linked to higher ASD-related behaviors only in females (55). Additional research has independently confirmed that maternal polycystic ovary syndrome increases ASD risk, with stronger associations in females than in males (56). These effects may be driven by elevated maternal androgen levels shaping the neurodevelopment of hormone-dependent brain regions (55). Further, although low maternal body mass index increases ASD risk in males and females, high maternal body mass index is only linked to greater risk in females (57). The same study also found that socioeconomic status (SES) has opposite effects by sex, with higher SES linked to risk in females but lower SES linked to risk in males, although the mechanisms driving these differences are not understood (57).

Several studies have examined developmental risk factors for OCD [see (58,59)]. However, few studies have examined sex effects (58). Obstetric complications and birth trauma broadly increase OCD risk, but they may have a greater effect in males compared with females (58). Birth order and advanced maternal age also appear to be male-specific risk factors, while parental education, occupation, and income are more strongly linked to risk in females (60).

Taken together, this suggests that sex differences in ASD and OCD risk may be driven by complex interactions between sex-specific developmental pathways and hormonal factors, among other mechanisms. More broadly, many perinatal risk factors are shared between ASD and OCD, including maternal age, immune activation, and mental illness, as well as low birth weight, preterm birth, breech presentation, cesarean delivery, and perinatal trauma (48, 49, 50,57, 58, 59,61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66). Importantly, several of these factors are not specific to ASD and OCD but rather linked to general psychopathology. However, few studies have examined sex differences in this overlap, emphasizing the need for future research examining these sex-specific patterns.

Influence of Gender

Gender, a fundamental aspect of the human experience, shapes biology, behavior, and life outcomes (2,6). However, the influence of gender, independent of sex, has rarely been considered in studies investigating ASD and OCD. Sex differences observed in symptom presentation, recognition, and diagnosis can likely, in part, be attributed to gender (2). Below, we highlight studies that have examined how gender, especially gender diversity, is related to prevalence, clinical presentation, and risk factors.

Prevalence

The prevalence of ASD is significantly elevated in gender-diverse populations, including transgender and nonbinary individuals, with some estimates indicating rates up to 10 times higher than in the general population (67, 68, 69). Gender-diverse populations also show elevated rates of OCD, with studies finding a 9.8% prevalence among transgender individuals, representing up to a 10-fold increase over general population estimates (70).

Clinical Presentation

Gender-diverse individuals with ASD show elevated rates of comorbid psychopathology, including substance use disorders, psychosis, personality disorders, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts (69). This aligns with the double disadvantage hypothesis, which proposes that multiple marginalized identities interact to worsen health outcomes (71). Similarly, gender-diverse individuals with OCD experience more severe contamination symptoms and elevated rates of comorbid substance use disorders, trauma/stress-related disorders, personality disorders, and other psychiatric conditions relative to cisgender individuals (72). Importantly, accurate differential diagnosis becomes particularly complex when ASD and OCD co-occur with gender diversity, as overlapping symptoms can complicate clinical presentation and assessment (73). This complexity can lead to misdiagnosis or underdiagnosis when clinicians incorrectly attribute symptoms like social withdrawal, sensory sensitivities, and emotional dysregulation entirely to gender dysphoria (73). The expression of symptoms, both in gender-diverse and general populations, can also be influenced by gender. As an example, camouflaging behaviors, more commonly expressed by girls, may be driven by gender norms to meet societal expectations (19).

Risk Factors

While it is not known whether the gender of the individual experiencing ASD or OCD is linked to risk factors, the gender of the birthing parent may play a role. Gender identity of the birthing parent may have an indirect but significant effect on the risk of developing ASD or OCD, although these effects are not specific. Transgender men face unique challenges during pregnancy, in part due to fears and concerns of pregnancy inducing feelings of gender dysphoria (74). Transgender men may also experience structural barriers within healthcare systems, including the lack of cultural competency, transphobia by providers, institutional erasure of transgender and gender nonconforming patients, and a lack of transgender-specific provider training (75). In fact, only 51% of obstetrics and gynecology (OB/GYN) residency programs offered training for transgender patients, and 67% of OB/GYN residency program directors reported not having an established transgender training curriculum (76,77). Further, 55% of OB/GYN residents and 23% of OB/GYN residency program directors reported that they do not feel prepared to care for transgender patients (77). The lack of training and resulting lack of confidence in being able to provide appropriate care to pregnant transgender men may lead to increased risk of complications during pregnancy and birth. However, few studies have examined pregnancy outcomes in transgender men, and they show mixed results. As an example, one study did not find a relationship between the likelihood of preterm birth and transgender identity (78), while another showed that higher levels of structural heteropatriarchy (i.e., a system of regulation that privileges cisgender men and heterosexuality) were associated with higher risks of preterm birth and lower birth rate (79). Therefore, further research is necessary to fully understand how the gender identity of the birthing parent may influence developmental risk factors for ASD and OCD.

Influence of Other Sociodemographic Factors

Beyond sex and gender, other sociodemographic factors such as race, ethnicity, and SES also influence ASD and OCD. Minoritized individuals are more likely to be under- or misdiagnosed (80, 81, 82), underserved, and undertreated (80,82,83). Many barriers contribute to this underdiagnosis and undertreatment, including treatment costs, stigma, therapy fears, doubts about clinician effectiveness, perceived lack of need for treatment, and logistical barriers (2,84). Although this phenomenon is not specific to these disorders, these disparities must be considered during diagnosis and treatment (2). Similarly, pregnancy outcomes differ by race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, with widely documented discrepancies in reproductive healthcare and outcomes among women of color (85). Babies born to Black and South Asian women are at an increased risk of preterm birth and being small for gestational age (85), which in turn are shared risk factors for ASD, OCD, and other psychopathologies. As a result, through systemic failures to serve marginalized groups, developmental risk factors for ASD and OCD may have a disproportionate effect on these individuals and communities.

Future Directions

Here, we highlight that sex and gender fundamentally influence the development and presentation of both ASD and OCD. Despite their distinct clinical features and underlying mechanisms, both disorders show strikingly parallel patterns. Males typically present with more stereotypical, observable symptoms aligned with traditional diagnostic criteria (e.g., overt repetitive motor behaviors and social isolation in ASD and externalized compulsions in OCD), while females demonstrate subtler presentations (e.g., camouflaging behaviors in both, gender-typical restricted interests in ASD and covert obsessions in OCD), leading to systematic underdiagnosis. Developmental risk factors also exhibit consistent sex-specific effects, with males showing greater vulnerability to perinatal stressors while females may be more susceptible to hormonal influences. Beyond sex, gender norms may also shape symptom presentation, with camouflaging behaviors in females potentially driven by societal expectations for social conformity. The elevated prevalence of ASD and OCD in gender-diverse populations also represents a critical area of research, with these individuals experiencing more severe symptoms and elevated psychiatric comorbidities. Diagnostic complexity increases substantially when these disorders co-occur, as overlapping symptoms can lead to misattribution and delayed recognition of underlying conditions. Beyond sex and gender, other sociodemographic factors create additional layers of complexity. Marginalized populations face compounded barriers, including diagnostic bias, treatment access limitations, and structural healthcare inequities. These intersectional effects highlight that addressing sex and gender differences alone is insufficient without considering the broader sociodemographic context that shapes health outcomes.

Many of the risk factors identified in this review are not unique to ASD and OCD but rather confer risk for general psychopathology. Sex differences in psychiatric vulnerability to these risk factors have also been documented across multiple neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions. This raises questions about whether the sex and gender effects on ASD and OCD that we have highlighted here reflect disorder-specific mechanisms or general principles of sex differences across neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders. While certain risk factors may be transdiagnostic, other aspects of this review appear specific to ASD and OCD. The parallel camouflaging behaviors in females across both disorders, the diagnostic bias toward male presentations, and the sex-differentiated symptom patterns likely reflect interactions between transdiagnostic sex differences in neurobiology and disorder-specific features. Moving forward, distinguishing between transdiagnostic and disorder-specific mechanisms of sex and gender effects will be crucial. Transdiagnostic patterns will inform broader frameworks for how sex and gender shape psychiatric risk and resilience throughout the lifespan, while disorder-specific patterns will inform targeted prevention and treatment efforts.

Examining ASD and OCD together reveals insights specific to these disorders that would be obscured in isolation. First, both disorders involve repetitive behaviors, but accurate differential diagnosis requires distinguishing ego-syntonic behaviors (ASD) from ego-dystonic compulsions (OCD), a distinction that becomes particularly complex with sex-specific presentations. As an example, females with gender-typical restricted interests in ASD may be misdiagnosed as having OCD, while females with contamination-focused OCD who avoid social situations may be misidentified as having ASD. Second, the pattern of sex bias differs between disorders in clinically meaningful ways. ASD criteria emphasize male-typical motor stereotypies and overt social deficits, while OCD criteria may undercapture female-predominant contamination-related obsessions and compulsions. This creates opposite diagnostic challenges. In ASD, females who camouflage are missed entirely; in OCD, females may be diagnosed, but with incomplete symptom profiles. Third, the high co-occurrence rates mean that clinicians must simultaneously consider whether repetitive behaviors reflect one disorder, both disorders, or misattribution between them. These disorder-specific insights have direct implications for the diagnostic reforms outlined below.

Current diagnostic approaches systematically fail to identify ASD and OCD in females and marginalized populations, necessitating comprehensive reforms. For ASD, more inclusive diagnostic criteria are needed to identify females who are often undiagnosed, not due to a lack of symptoms, but because current diagnostic criteria are based on stereotypical male presentations (25). Common diagnostic tools, such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, must be revised to better reflect diverse presentations. Clinicians should also actively assess camouflaging behaviors using validated tools such as the Camouflaging Autistic Traits Questionnaire and consider peer-based assessments to capture naturalistic social behaviors (18). Similarly, OCD diagnostic criteria must be improved to better identify females and account for sex-specific presentations. Current symptom scales, such as the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale and Dimensional Obsessive-Compulsive Scale, must be refined to better capture obsessions and compulsions more prevalent in females. For gender-diverse individuals, clinicians must account for symptom overlap between gender dysphoria and both ASD and OCD. Current diagnostic criteria do not adequately address these intersections, leading to potential misdiagnoses. Diagnostic frameworks should be expanded to consider an individual’s sex and gender to better contextualize symptom profiles and ensure accurate diagnosis.

Moving forward, research priorities must include systematic examination of sex and gender as fundamental variables rather than confounders, investigation of the mechanisms underlying potential protective effects in females, and development of evidence-based treatment protocols tailored to diverse presentations and needs. Critically, future studies must examine the intersectional effects of multiple sociodemographic factors to fully understand how these identities interact to influence clinical presentation and outcomes. The field must move beyond one-size-fits-all approaches to embrace personalized frameworks that recognize the interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors. Comprehensive, inclusive approaches are necessary to ensure that all individuals receive accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment, resulting in optimal life outcomes. The ultimate goal is not merely to understand these differences but also to translate this knowledge into meaningful improvements in diagnosis and treatment that benefit all individuals affected by ASD and OCD.
