CRH neurons in the dorsal bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (dBNST) mediate contextual fear generalization
Authors: Mengxue Chen, Hu Meng, Xianjing Li, Kaiyu Guan, Yiqian Shao, Deshan Kong, Weihua Yue, Dai Zhang, Xiaoxun Sun, Lifang Wang, Jun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextual fear generalization is a hallmark of psychiatric disorders including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), generalized anxiety disorder and panic disorder. Among these disorders, PTSD is particularly associated with contextual fear generalization. However, the mechanisms by which fear responses extend to similar contexts remain unclear. Here we tested whether corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) neurons in the dorsal bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (dBNST) contribute to contextual fear generalization. Using a contextual fear generalization paradigm with variable shock timing, we observed that intense foot shock conditioning enhanced freezing responses in both the training context and the generalization context in adult male mice, accompanied by elevated dBNST activity. Fiber photometry showed that dBNSTCRH neurons were strongly engaged by foot shock and exhibited clear activity changes around freezing onset during both training and generalization tests. Functionally, chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons reduced freezing responses in the generalization test, whereas activation increased generalized freezing and promoted contextual fear generalization. Mechanistically, local pharmacological blockade of CRH receptor 1 (CRHR1) within the dBNST reduced freezing responses in the generalization test, indicating that CRHR1-dependent signaling within the dBNST contributes to fear generalization. Together, these findings identify dBNST CRH neurons and local CRHR1 signaling as key components underlying contextual fear generalization.Keywords: Contextual fear generalization, Stress, dBNST, CRH, CRHR1

Introduction

Fear generalization refers to the phenomenon whereby conditioned fear responses extend to stimuli that are similar but not identical to the original fear-inducing event (Shepard, 1987). Adaptive fear generalization allows individuals to respond to novel yet threatening stimuli based on prior experiences. However, excessive or maladaptive fear generalization can be detrimental, as it impairs the ability to distinguish between safe and dangerous cues. Notably, overgeneralized fear is a key feature of multiple psychiatric disorders, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), generalized anxiety disorder, and panic disorder (Lissek et al., 2010, 2014; Ressler et al., 2022). Despite over a century of research on fear generalization (Shepard, 1987), and evidence for its evolutionary conservation across species (Dymond et al., 2015; Orr and Lukowiak, 2008), the neurobiological mechanisms driving this phenomenon remain unclear. Consequently, elucidating the mechanisms of fear generalization is a critical and unresolved question in the field of mental disorders.

Prior studies have suggested that stronger aversive stimulation is often associated with more pronounced generalization of fear responses (Li et al., 2024). Although behavioral protocols used to elicit contextual fear generalization vary across laboratories in several key parameters, including contextual cue design, shock intensity and number, inter-shock timing, and the post-conditioning test interval (Bian et al., 2019; Ge et al., 2025; Jo et al., 2018; Li et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2017), most studies used a fixed inter-shock interval. Convergent evidence from human and translational studies indicates that temporally unpredictable aversive events can bias organisms toward more sustained defensive states than temporally predictable threat, even when overall aversiveness is held constant (Grillon et al., 2004; Herry et al., 2007). However, whether variable inter-shock timing induces much more fear generalization and how stress responsive mechanisms are engaged under this condition remain largely unknown.

The bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) is a key regulator of defensive responding to unpredictable threats (Goode and Maren, 2017; Goode et al., 2019). Functionally, the BNST comprises multiple subregions and cell populations with distinct connectivity and behavioral roles (Giardino et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2013; Lebow and Chen, 2016; Song et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2020). Its dorsal part (dBNST), which includes the anterior dBNST (adBNST) and oval BNST (ovBNST), is a component of the extended amygdala and has been strongly implicated in affective and behavioral responses to aversive or stressful conditions (Garcia-Garcia et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020b; Kim et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Beyond this role, cortical pathways can recruit dBNST circuits to generate aversive states in non-threat domains, underscoring its broad relevance for negative valence control across behavioral contexts (Xia et al., 2021). In contrast, ventral BNST (vBNST) is more often associated with motivational selection and reward behaviors (Girven et al., 2021; Jennings et al., 2013). Although the dBNST has been strongly implicated in stress-related processes, it remains unclear whether it participates in stress-induced contextual fear generalization.

Within the BNST, corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) signaling constitutes a prominent stress-related neuromodulatory system (Tran et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2024). In the dBNST, CRH neurons and their associated circuits can regulate aversive and affective states, as well as stress-related avoidance and anxiety-like behaviors (Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). CRH receptor 1 (CRHR1) signaling within BNST subregions has been implicated by both neural circuits and pharmacological studies in shaping stress-related behavioral outputs, supporting CRH pathways as experimentally tractable targets for intervention (Tran et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2024). Nevertheless, a critical gap remains. Although the BNST is implicated in stress-related processes, and dBNSTCRH systems have been linked to stress- and anxiety-related behaviors, it remains insufficiently resolved whether a defined CRH neuron population in the dBNST contributes specifically to contextual fear generalization. In particular, the manner in which dBNSTCRH activity modulates fear expression during contextual fear and generalization tests has not yet been fully established.

Here, we addressed this knowledge gap by focusing on dBNSTCRH neurons and their receptor in a contextual fear generalization protocol that incorporates graded aversive stimulation and variable inter-shock timing. Using a low-stress and high-stress conditioning design, we quantified fear responses in both the conditioned context and a similar context, thereby enabling behavioral assessment of contextual generalized fear responding. We then correlated these behavioral measures with dBNST activation patterns and directly tested the contribution of dBNSTCRH neuronal activity to fear responses across distinct contexts. Finally, we evaluated pharmacological interventions targeting local CRH receptor signaling within the dBNST. Together, this integrated approach allowed us to test the hypothesis that dBNSTCRH neurons modulate contextual fear generalization.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6J male mice (8-12 weeks old) used in this study were obtained from SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Crh-IRES-Cre (Crh-cre) mice (Stock #012704) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar harbor, ME, USA) and were crossed to C57BL/6J mice to produce heterozygous animals for experiments in this study. Mice aged 6–8 weeks were used for viral injection experiments, and mice aged 8–12 weeks were used for behavioral experiments. Prior to optical fiber implantation, mice were housed in groups of 4–5 per cage. Following implantation, all animals were individually housed under standard conditions. The mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) animal room with free access to food and water, maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle from 08:00 to 20:00, with a temperature range of 20-24°C and consistent humidity of 40-60%. Animal care and experimental procedures followed the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals and received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Peking University Health Science Center.

Mouse genotyping

Genotyping PCR primers used were for Crh-cre mice: forward primer 10574 (5′-CTT ACA CAT TTC GTC CTA GCC-3′), reverse WT primer 10575 (5′-CAC GAC CAG GCT GCG GCT AAC-3′), reverse mutant primer 10576 (5′-CAA TGT ATC TTA TCA TGT CTG GAT CC-3′).

Behavioral experimental procedures

Fear conditioning

The experimental procedure for studying contextual fear generalization was modified from established protocols. The conditioning context served as the conditioned stimulus (CS), associated with an unconditioned stimulus (US), without the introduction of additional stimuli such as light or sound. Prior to fear conditioning, mice were habituated to the behavioral room for 2 h. Each mouse was then placed into a conditioning chamber with two aluminum sidewalls, two plexiglass walls, and a stainless-steel grid floor (Med Associates, USA). After a 2-min acclimation, foot shocks were delivered through the floor grids. In the weak shock condition protocol, each mouse received a single foot shock of 0.75 mA for 2 s (low-stress protocol). In the high-intensity conditioning protocol, each mouse received 5 foot shocks of 0.75 mA for 2 s each, with randomized intervals of 30 to 90 s between shocks (high-stress protocol). The total duration in both protocols was 300 s, followed by an additional 2 min in the chamber to reinforce contextual fear. The chamber was cleaned with 75% ethanol before and after each session.

Conditioned fear testing and generalization assessment

Twenty-four hours after fear conditioning, the conditioned fear response was assessed by re-exposing the mice to the conditioned stimulus (CS+, context A) in the absence of the US. To evaluate fear generalization, the mice were placed in a different environment (CS−, context B). Context B was an oval area defined by three white plastic walls, with a brown wooden floor, while one wall and the ceiling matched those of Context A. On the day of testing, each mouse was exposed to Context B and Context A for 5 min each, with a 2-h interval between the two sessions. The two contexts differed in shape, size, and texture. Freezing behavior, defined as the absence of all movement except for necessary respiratory movements, was recorded and analyzed using VideoFreeze software. The testing chambers were cleaned with 75% ethanol before each test.

Open field test

The assay was conducted in a transparent chamber (27.5 cm L × 27.5 cm W × 20 cm H; MED Associates, USA). At the start of each session, mice were placed in the center area and allowed to freely explore the apparatus for 60 min under standardized conditions. The time spent in the center zone (25% of the total arena area) during the first 10 min was used as an OFT-based anxiety-related measure.

Virus injection and stereotaxic surgery

Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (75 mg/kg) and subsequently placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). After performing a craniotomy to expose the skull surface, a small hole was drilled above the BNST according to the following coordinates from bregma: AP +0.26 mm, ML ±0.8 mm, DV –4 mm. Viral solutions were delivered using a 10 μL syringe (Hamilton, Nevada, USA) connected to an infusion pump (KD Scientific, USA) at a constant rate and volume. Following injection, the syringe was left in place for 5–10 min before slow withdrawal to prevent backflow. Viruses used for chemogenetic manipulation and control included: AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-hM4D (Gi)-GFP; AAV2/9- EF1α-DIO-hM3D (Gq)-GFP; AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO- GFP. For fiber photometry, AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s-WPRE was injected.

In vivo fiber photometry recording

Following the previously described viral injection procedure and BNST coordinates, AAV2/9-EF1a-DIO-GCaMP6s was injected into the unilateral BNST of Crh-cre mice. After a 3-week expression period, a fiber-optic cannula (200 μm core diameter; Inper, China) was implanted to the BNST (AP +0.26 mm, ML ±1.8 mm, DV −3.78 mm) using a 12° mediolateral angled trajectory (relative to the vertical axis) and secured to the skull with dental cement. One week after implantation, the previously described conditioned fear and generalization tests were conducted.

Fiber photometry recordings were conducted using a system equipped with a 488 nm laser (OBIS 488LS; Coherent). The laser light was reflected through a dichroic mirror (MD498; Thorlabs), a 10× objective (NA 0.3; Olympus), and an optical commutator (Doric Lenses) to excite GCaMP6. The resulting fluorescence signal was processed using a bandpass filter (MF525-39; Thorlabs) and detected by a photomultiplier tube (R3896; Hamamatsu), amplified (C7319; Hamamatsu), and then subjected to low-pass filtering (cutoff frequency 35 Hz; Brownlee 440). The analog signals were digitized at a sampling rate of 500 Hz (Thinkerbiotech, Nanjing, China).

During fear conditioning, fiber photometry signals were time-locked to the timing of foot shocks for analysis. During conditioned fear and generalization tests, freezing time was defined as the onset of fear-associated immobility (excluding respiratory movements, duration >0.5 s) immediately following sustained locomotor activity (>3 s), with transient pauses, trivial movements, or interrupted transitions excluded as invalid events, and fiber photometry signals were time locked to this transition for analysis. For each mouse, 2–5 shock- or freezing-aligned onset events were selected for analysis, and fluorescence changes in the pre- and post-event windows were quantified as ΔF/F, calculated as (F − F0)/F0, where F0 represents baseline fluorescence. The ΔF/F signals are presented as averaged peri-event traces and heatmaps, with shaded areas indicating SEM.

Guide cannula implantation

For BNST microinfusion, custom stainless-steel guide cannulas were stereotaxically implanted bilaterally and secured to the skull with dental cement. Coordinates were referenced to bregma. A 12° mediolateral angled trajectory (defined relative to the vertical axis) was used, and guide cannula tips were positioned above the BNST at AP +0.26 mm, ML ±1.80 mm, DV −3.78 mm. During drug delivery, a stainless-steel internal injector extending 0.2 mm beyond the guide cannula was inserted to target the BNST.

Drug preparation and administration

DREADD CNO

For the DREADD experiment, Clozapine N-oxide (CNO; MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-17366, USA) was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Beyotime Biotechnology, Cat# ST038, China) at a concentration of 100 mg/mL. This solution was then diluted with 0.9% sterile saline to achieve a final DMSO concentration of 5%. CNO was administered at a dose of 3 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injection 30 min prior to behavioral testing.

CRHR1 antagonist

The selective CRHR1 antagonist R121919 (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-14127, USA) was dissolved in 5% DMSO and diluted with 0.9% saline to the working solution immediately before use. R121919 was delivered intra-BNST at 2 μg per side in a total volume of 0.2 μL, infused at 0.05 μL/min using a microsyringe pump. After infusion, the injector was left in place for 5 min to minimize backflow. Control mice received the same DMSO/saline solution without drug.

Tissue processing and immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was carried out based on the established laboratory protocol (Sun et al., 2025). Mice were transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The brain was then removed and fixed in 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h, followed by immersion in 30% sucrose solution for at least 72 h. After sinking, the brains were embedded in OCT, rapidly frozen, and sectioned coronally at 30 μm using a freezing microtome (Leica CM, 1950; Germany). Sections were washed three times in PBS (10 min each), blocked in buffer containing 3% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in the same blocking solution. Primary antibodies included: Guinea pig anti-c-Fos (Synaptic Systems, Cat# 226308, Germany), rabbit anti-CRH (BMA Biomedicals, Cat# T-4037, Switzerland), rabbit anti-CRHR1 (Proteintech, Cat# 20967-1-AP, USA). The next day, sections were washed three times with PBS, followed by a 2-h incubation at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibodies: Donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (Invitrogen, Cat# A-31572, USA); donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, Cat# A-21206, USA); goat anti–guinea pig Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen, Cat# A-21450, USA). After three additional PBS washes, sections were mounted on slides, air-dried, and cover slipped with antifade mounting medium. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal microscope (Olympus, Cat# VS200; FV3000, Japan).

Experimental design and statistical analysis

In all experiments, mice were randomly assigned to the respective groups. Behavioral tests and data analysis were performed in a blinded manner. Animals with inaccurate viral targeting or cannula placement were excluded from the analysis. Sample sizes were chosen based on those reported in previous studies with similar designs (Ge et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). The number of animals or replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends. Data conforming to normal distribution are presented as mean ± SEM. Paired two-tailed Student's t-tests were used for within-group comparisons and for fiber photometry analyses of pre- and post-shock or freezing onset activity. Two-way mixed-design ANOVA followed by Sidak's multiple-comparisons test was used for analyses involving group and context (CS + vs. CS−). Other two-group comparisons were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS 16.0, while graph plotting and further analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10. The levels of statistical significance in the figures are indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001. n.s., not significant.

Results

Intense conditioning promotes contextual fear generalization and engages CRH-expressing neurons in dBNST

Previous work has demonstrated that stronger aversive stimulation during conditioning enhances fear response generalization when animals are tested in a contextually similar environment (Li et al., 2024). To elucidate the mechanisms underlying this process, we established a contextual fear generalization paradigm in which adult male wild-type (WT) mice were conditioned in Context A using either a low-stress (LS) protocol (one 0.75 mA foot shock, 2 s duration) or a high-stress (HS) protocol (five 0.75 mA foot shocks, 2 s each, delivered at randomized inter-shock intervals of 30–90 s). This conditioning phase was followed by a generalization test in Context B (CS−) and a context specificity test in Context A (CS+) (Fig. 1A).

During testing, mice in the LS group exhibited significantly higher freezing levels in the trained context (CS+) compared to the similar context (CS−). This finding indicates robust conditioned fear expression in CS + context but no detectable generalized fear responding in the CS− context. In contrast, mice in the HS group exhibited comparable freezing levels between the CS+ and CS− contexts. Between-group comparisons further revealed that HS mice showed significantly greater freezing than LS mice in the CS− context, whereas freezing responses in the CS + context did not differ between the two groups (Fig. 1B)—a pattern consistent with the presence of contextual fear generalization. Consistent with these behavioral observations, the generalization score (calculated as CS−/[CS− + CS+]) was significantly elevated in the HS group relative to the LS group (Fig. 1C), confirming that high-intensity conditioning promotes contextual fear generalization.

To rule out the possibility that high-intensity conditioning altered locomotor activity or OFT-based anxiety-related which could confound our freezing measurements, mice were subsequently tested in the open field test. Neither total distance traveled nor time spent in the center of the open field differed significantly between the LS and HS groups (Fig. 1D and E), suggesting that the enhanced freezing responses observed in HS mice were unlikely to be explained by changes in basal locomotor activity or OFT-based anxiety-related behavior.

We next quantified c-Fos expression in the dBNST following conditioning, as c-Fos serves as a reliable marker of neuronal activation. HS conditioning significantly increased the number of c-Fos positive cells in the dBNST compared to LS conditioning (Fig. 1G and H). Co-immunolabeling experiments further revealed that a subset of c-Fos positive cells in the dBNST of HS-conditioned mice exhibited CRH immunoreactivity (Fig. 1I ). Together, these data demonstrated that intense conditioning is associated with elevated neuronal activation (as reflected by c-Fos expression) in the dBNST and that CRH-positive cells are present among activated dBNST neurons.

Fiber photometry reveals dBNSTCRH neuronal activity during conditioning and contextual fear generalization

To monitor Ca2+ dynamics in dBNSTCRH neurons throughout conditioning and subsequent behavioral testing, we expressed the genetically encoded Ca2+ indicator GCaMP6s in dBNSTCRH neurons via stereotaxic delivery of Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus (AAV)-EF1α-DIO-GCaMP6s into the dBNST of Crh-cre mice (Fig. 2A). Robust GCaMP6s expression was confirmed within the targeted dBNST region, and an optical fiber was implanted above the dBNST to capture bulk Ca2+-dependent fluorescence signals (ΔF/F) in freely moving mice (Fig. 2B). Fiber photometry recordings were acquired during HS conditioning in Context A, followed by the CS− generalization test in Context B and the CS + contextual test in Context A (Fig. 2C).

During conditioning, foot shock presentation was accompanied by a pronounced increase in Ca2+ dependent fluorescence in dBNSTCRH neurons. Event-aligned average ΔF/F traces revealed a rapid elevation immediately following foot shock onset (Fig. 2D), a pattern that was consistent across individual trials in the heatmap (Fig. 2E). Pre–post quantification (pre: −2–0 s; post: 0–2 s; paired t-test; n = 7 mice; 2–5 events per mouse) confirmed a significant increase in ΔF/F relative to baseline (Fig. 2F). During the CS− generalization test, freezing onset was similarly associated with enhanced dBNSTCRH neuronal activity. Event-aligned average ΔF/F traces showed a marked elevation following freezing onset (Fig. 2G), with corresponding heatmaps illustrating consistent responses across freezing bouts included in the analysis (Fig. 2H). Pre–post quantification revealed significantly higher ΔF/F in the post-freezing window compared with the pre-freezing baseline (Fig. 2I). A comparable activation profile was observed during the CS + contextual test: average ΔF/F traces showed increased signals around freezing onset (Fig. 2J), with consistent responses across bouts in the heatmap (Fig. 2K). Pre–post analysis using identical time windows confirmed a significant elevation in ΔF/F following freezing onset (Fig. 2L).

Collectively, these fiber photometry data demonstrate that dBNSTCRH neurons are robustly activated during foot shock delivery and at freezing onset during both the contextual fear test and the generalization test.

Chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons reduces contextual fear generalization

To determine whether dBNSTCRH neuronal activity contributes to contextual fear generalization, we employed a Cre-dependent designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) approach to selectively inhibit dBNSTCRH neurons. Six-week-old male Crh-cre mice received stereotaxic injections of either a Cre-dependent inhibitory DREADD virus (AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-hM4Di-GFP) or a control virus (AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-GFP) into the dBNST. Viral expression within the target dBNST region was confirmed by GFP fluorescence (Fig. 3A). After recovery, mice underwent the HS fear conditioning protocol (Fig. 3B). All mice received intraperitoneal injections of clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) 30 min before conditioning to activate hM4Di and suppress the activity in dBNSTCRH neurons. Behavioral testing was then conducted according to the timeline (Fig. 3B).

Following CNO administration and HS conditioning, mice were subjected to the CS− generalization test and the CS + context test. Chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons significantly reduced freezing behavior in the CS− context compared with control mice, whereas freezing levels in the CS + context did not differ between groups (Fig. 3C). Consistent with this observation, the generalization scores were significantly lower in the hM4Di group relative to the control group (Fig. 3D), indicating that inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons attenuates contextual fear generalization. Open-field assays revealed that locomotor activity and OFT-based anxiety-related behavior were unaffected by chemogenetic inhibition during foot shock conditioning (Fig. 3E and F).

Upon completion of behavioral testing, we examined cellular activity in the dBNST by quantifying c-Fos expression under identical CNO and conditioning conditions. Representative images showing c-Fos and CRH immunoreactivity in the dBNST are presented in Fig. 3H. Quantification of total c-Fos–positive cells in the dBNST revealed a significant reduction in the hM4Di group compared with controls (Fig. 3I).

Together, these results demonstrated that chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons selectively reduces freezing during the CS− generalization test without altering CS + freezing or OFT-based anxiety-related behavior, and is accompanied by decreased c-Fos expression in the dBNST following HS conditioning.

Chemogenetic activation of dBNSTCRH neurons enhances contextual fear generalization after single-shock conditioning

Given that chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons attenuated contextual fear generalization, we next examined whether increasing activity of this neuronal population is sufficient to drive fear generalization under only a mild, single-foot shock conditioning protocol. To this end, 6-week-old male Crh-cre mice received stereotaxic injections of either a Cre-dependent excitatory DREADD virus (AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-hM3Dq-GFP) or a control virus (AAV2/9-EF1α-DIO-GFP) into the dBNST, and viral expression was verified by GFP fluorescence (Fig. 4A). After recovery, mice were subjected to the LS conditioning protocol, with CNO administered 30 min prior to conditioning as illustrated in the timeline (Fig. 4B).

Following CNO administration and LS conditioning, mice were tested in the CS− generalization test and the CS + context test. Chemogenetic activation of dBNSTCRH neurons significantly increased freezing in the CS− context compared with control mice, whereas CS + freezing levels were comparable between groups (Fig. 4C). Consistent with this behavioral profile, the generalization scores were significantly higher in the hM3Dq group relative to controls (Fig. 4D), indicating enhanced contextual fear generalization following activation of dBNSTCRH neurons. Open-field assessments confirmed that locomotor activity and OFT-based anxiety-related behavior unchanged by chemogenetic activation during foot shock conditioning (Fig. 4E and F).

After behavioral testing, we evaluated cellular activity in the dBNST by quantifying c-Fos expression in dBNSTCRH neurons under identical CNO and conditioning conditions (Fig. 4H). Quantification of total c-Fos–positive cells in the dBNST revealed a significant increase in the hM3Dq group compared with controls (Fig. 4I).

In summary, chemogenetic activation of dBNSTCRH neurons selectively increased freezing in the CS− generalization test without affecting CS + freezing or OFT-based anxiety-related behavior, and elevated c-Fos expression in the dBNST following conditioning. These findings demonstrate that enhancing dBNSTCRH neuronal activity is sufficient to augment freezing behavior in the CS− context and enhance contextual fear generalization, even under a weak, single-shock conditioning paradigm.

WT mice received intra-dBNST microinfusions of the CRHR1 antagonist R121919 (2 μg) 30 min before conditioning via implanted cannula, followed by the CS− generalization test and the CS + context test (Fig. 5B). During testing, R121919-treated mice exhibited significantly reduced freezing in the CS− generalization context compared with control mice, whereas CS + freezing levels were similar between groups (Fig. 5C). Consistent with this behavioral profile, generalization scores were significantly lower in the R121919-treated group relative to controls (Fig. 5D), indicating enhanced contextual specificity of fear responses. Open-field measures of locomotion and OFT-based anxiety-related behavior were unaffected by R121919 treatment (Fig. 5E and F). These results suggest that local CRHR1 signaling in the dBNST contributes to contextual fear generalization.

Discussion

Although overgeneralization of contextual fear is a core feature of stress-related psychiatric disorders including PTSD, the critical brain regions and underlying neurobiological mechanisms governing this process remain unclear (Liberzon and Abelson, 2016; Morey et al., 2015). The BNST is strongly implicated in sustaining defensive states under conditions of uncertainty and is highly enriched in stress-related CRH signaling (Giardino et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Here we demonstrate that dBNSTCRH neurons and local CRHR1 signaling selectively promote contextual fear generalization. Specifically, HS conditioning with randomized inter-shock intervals increased CS− freezing and generalization scores without altering CS + freezing or OFT-based anxiety-related behavior, and robustly activated a large subset of dBNSTCRH neurons. Fiber photometry revealed that these neurons exhibit shock-evoked responses and elevated activity at freezing onset during both the context and generalization tests. Mechanistically, chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons reduced CS− freezing and fear generalization, whereas activation of these neurons was sufficient to enhance CS− freezing even under mild single-shock conditioning. Finally, intra-dBNST CRHR1 antagonism attenuated CS− freezing and generalization, highlighting a local CRH receptor-mediated mechanism that contributes to contextual fear generalization.

Fear generalization is commonly defined as defensive responding that scales with the contextual similarity to the original threat environment (Li et al., 2024). This conceptual framework distinguishes two distinct components: context processing (the ability to assign contextual meaning and assess similarity) and fear responding (the propensity to express defensive states, as well as the magnitude of such expression (Bian et al., 2019; Ge et al., 2025; Lesuis et al., 2025). Rodent circuit studies have further demonstrated that anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)→ventral hippocampus (vHPC) signaling promotes contextual fear generalization (Bian et al., 2019). Collectively, these findings suggest that fear generalization emerges when context processing circuits recruit downstream modulatory mechanisms that regulate fear responding under conditions of uncertainty. Prior research has established the BNST as a core node mediating defensive responding under uncertainty and sustained threat, complementing amygdala-centered defensive responses (Daniel and Rainnie, 2016; Lebow and Chen, 2016; Shackman and Fox, 2016; Walker et al., 2009). Consistent with this role, BNST circuits are engaged by unpredictable threat and can regulate defensive behavior in tasks where threat timing is uncertain (Goode et al., 2019). Moreover, acute stress can causally promote generalized threat responding through CRH-related mechanisms (Li et al., 2024). Building on these foundations, our study extends the field in two key ways. Firstly, we link a defined dBNSTCRH population to contextual fear generalization with cell type specificity. The dBNSTCRH neurons may function as a downstream hub for fear responding: they preferentially amplify defensive responses when contextual evidence is unclear, thereby biasing behavior toward elevated CS− responding rather than uniformly enhancing CS + responding. Secondly, pharmacological evidence reveals that CRHR1-dependent signaling within local dBNST is an operational mechanism that improves CS− rather than CS + fear responses.

Although BNST involvement in stress and anxiety related states is well recognized, its contribution to contextual fear generalization has remained less directly tested compared to other important brain regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, or prefrontal cortex (Bian et al., 2019; Lesuis et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2017). This relative gap is notable given evidence that BNST contains functionally heterogeneous cell populations whose balance is shifted by stress and neuromodulators (Daniel and Rainnie, 2016; Gungor and Pare, 2016; Hu et al., 2020a). In particular, CRH signaling within BNST subregions is repeatedly implicated in stress-driven behavioral responses: chronic stress engages ovBNST CRH signaling and CRHR1-dependent cellular mechanisms (Hu et al., 2020a), and early life stress effects can be reversed by local CRHR1 antagonism within ovBNST (Hu et al., 2020b). Our findings complement this literature by demonstrating that, beyond broad stress and anxiety roles, a defined dBNST CRH population and CRHR1 signaling can specifically bias defensive response toward contextual fear generalization, linking BNST stress modulation to context guided behavior.

Our results are also compatible with a broader literature showing that acute stress can causally promote generalized threat responding through defined CRH-related processes (Lesuis et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). Notably, recent studies demonstrate that stress can shift neural systems toward generalized defensive states via discrete neuromodulatory mechanisms, providing a principled link between stress intensity, unpredictability and generalized responding (Lesuis et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024). In parallel, work outside BNST indicates that CRH-related signaling in interconnected extended-amygdala networks can shape defensive response and avoidance behavior in a CRHR1-dependent manner, highlighting receptor level convergence across circuits (Hu et al., 2020b; Zhang et al., 2024). We propose that fear generalization reflects context-processing drive combined with fear responding in extended-amygdala circuits engaged by stress. Overall, dBNSTCRH neurons and local CRHR1 signaling preferentially enhance the CS− fear response in the generalization test while preserving the CS + response.

Because both chemogenetic manipulation and pharmacological blockade with R121919 were performed before the HS protocol in the present study, our findings are more appropriately interpreted as supporting a preventive, rather than strictly therapeutic, strategy. However, an important question is whether such interventions would retain efficacy if administered after the traumatic event. Although this possibility was not directly tested here, several lines of evidence suggest that the time window for modulating fear generalization may not be limited to the moment of the initial aversive experience. Fear generalization appears to evolve over time rather than being fully established immediately after conditioning. Previous studies have shown that acute stress preferentially promotes the generalization of older threat memories, that disruption of mechanisms maintaining contextual precision can induce generalized contextual fear at later post-training stages, and that contextual fear generalization increases as a function of both conditioning strength and retention interval (Dunsmoor et al., 2017; Lynch et al., 2017; Poulos et al., 2016). These findings suggest that generalized fear remains dynamically shaped after trauma, leaving a potential post-trauma window for intervention. In addition, several biologically defined mechanisms that regulate generalized fear continue to operate after the aversive event, including stress-induced changes in cotransmitter identity (Li et al., 2024), hippocampal GABAB(1a) receptor-dependent maintenance of contextual precision (Lynch et al., 2017), and adenosine A2A receptor signaling that influences the later development of contextual fear generalization (Simoes et al., 2023). The results of fiber photometry data showed that dBNSTCRH neurons were activated not only during HS conditioning, but also around freezing onset during both the contextual and generalization test, suggesting that this neuronal population may contribute to later expression of generalized defensive responses. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that CRHR1 antagonism can attenuate fear response even when administered after foot shock or before later context test (Hikichi et al., 2000; Thoeringer et al., 2012), indicating that CRHR1-dependent signaling is not restricted to the initial aversive event but may remain functionally relevant during later phases of fear expression. A related limitation is that the present study does not establish whether targeting this pathway after trauma can attenuate already-formed generalized fear responses. This limits the translational interpretation of our findings, particularly regarding their relevance to established stress-related disorders. Future studies should test whether treatment administered after the traumatic event can still attenuate generalized fear responses and determine whether dBNST CRH–CRHR1 signaling represents a therapeutic target for maladaptive overgeneralization.

One limitation of the present study is that anxiety-related behavior was assessed only with the open field test. Although the OFT allowed us to examine general locomotor activity and an OFT-based anxiety-related measure, it does not capture the full range of anxiety-related behavioral dimensions. Future studies incorporating additional assays, such as the elevated plus maze and light-dark box test, would help determine whether HS conditioning affects other aspects of anxiety-related behavior beyond contextual fear generalization. Another limitation is that we did not systematically test sex as a biological variable. This is particularly important for BNSTCRH systems, because recent work shows that stress induces a female-biased increase in CRH release and hyperexcitability of ovBNST CRH neurons, which contributes to female biased anxiety-like avoidance behaviors and involves CRHR1-dependent mechanisms (Zhang et al., 2024). These findings raise the possibility that the strength, recruitment, or receptor dependence of dBNSTCRH signaling during contextual fear generalization may differ across sexes. Second, although our mechanistic focus is on CRHR1, CRH-family signaling in BNST is receptor- and subregion-specific; notably, posterior BNSTCRHR2 neurons are critical for stress recovery, suggesting that CRHR2-related signaling may shape the termination of defensive states after generalized responding and may complement CRHR1 fear response (Henckens et al., 2017). Beyond these priorities, the next critical step is to expand the analysis to include different BNST compartments and their input-output pathways (Giardino et al., 2018; Jennings et al., 2013). This will help identify how CRH signaling contributes to the observed CS− elevation. This analysis should be based on the well-established evidence of functionally distinct BNST subregions and their downstream pathways involved in regulating fear responses.

Clinically, overgeneralization is a hallmark of PTSD phenotype (Ressler et al., 2022). Our research indicates that dBNSTCRHR1 signaling represents a local mechanism that can amplify fear responding under stress, highlighting a candidate circuit level process that may contribute to maladaptive overgeneralization. Given the limited efficacy of systemic CRHR1 antagonism in PTSD (Dunlop et al., 2017), these findings should not be taken to imply that broad CRHR1 blockade is sufficient to treat established stress-related disorders. Our findings instead suggest that the contribution of CRHR1 signaling to pathological fear may depend on its recruitment within specific neural circuits, rather than on global CRHR1 activation alone.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that intense contextual conditioning with randomized inter-shock intervals increases freezing in a similar, non-conditioned context (CS−) and elevates generalization scores. dBNSTCRH neurons are strongly engaged by this conditioning procedure, exhibiting shock-evoked Ca2+ transients and increased activity at freezing onset during both contextual and generalization tests. Chemogenetic inhibition of dBNSTCRH neurons reduces CS− freezing and generalization, whereas activation enhances CS− freezing even after mild single-shock conditioning. Intra-dBNST CRHR1 antagonism attenuates CS− freezing and generalization. Together, these findings support a local dBNST CRH–CRHR1 mechanism that promotes generalized freezing across contexts.
