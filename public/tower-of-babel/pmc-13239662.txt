Validation of 3nethra specto, a wearable digital subjective refractor versus conventional subjective refraction for refractive error measurement in young adults: a comparative study
Authors: Kaushik Murali, Namratha Hegde, Afia S, Varshini M, Disha Agarwal, Revathy Manthri Rangaraj, Rashmi Hullur, Kousar Sidiq, Venkatakrishnan S
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRefractive errors cause significant preventable visual impairment globally, particularly in underserved areas. Conventional subjective refraction remains the gold standard but proves examiner-dependent, time-consuming and challenging in high-volume settings. The 3nethra Specto, a wearable digital subjective refractometer, offers rapid refractive assessment. This study evaluates its accuracy and repeatability versus conventional methods.MethodsA prospective comparative study was conducted on 135 subjects who underwent both conventional subjective refraction and 3nethra Specto assessment. Spherical (S), cylindrical (C) and axis measurements were analysed using power vector transformation (J₀, J₄₅). Primary predictive validity was assessed using univariate general linear models (GLMs) (IBM SPSS V.25.0) with age/sex as covariates. Model fit was evaluated via F-statistics, R² and residual SD. Instrument repeatability used GLM Repeated Measures, agreement assessed via Bland-Altman plots, keeping significance at p<0.05. Patient and examiner feedback on the instrument was captured using the User Experience questionnaire.ResultsOf 135 adults (mean age 31.5±8.9 years; 60% male), compound myopic astigmatism predominated (45%). 3nethra Specto measurements closely matched subjective refraction (sphere: −1.02 D vs −0.99 D; cylinder: −0.49 D vs −0.83 D; M: −1.26 D vs −1.24 D). GLM analysis showed excellent predictive validity (all p<0.001; R² 0.914 to 0.994; residual SD 0.084 D to 0.228 D). Bland-Altman plots confirmed 95% limits of agreement of −0.39 D to +0.47 D for M and −0.62 D to +0.64 D for J₀ and −0.32 D to +0.35 D for J₄₅. Device repeatability was high (all F>96, p<0.001; R²≥0.956; residual SD≤0.148 D). Patients rated subjective refraction higher for comfort/enjoyability but found 3nethra more inventive/exciting; examiners preferred 3nethra for efficiency, speed, innovation and ease of use.ConclusionsThe 3nethra Specto wearable liquid lens refractor demonstrated excellent agreement and repeatability with traditional subjective refraction across all refractive parameters, confirming clinical equivalence. Positive patient and examiner feedback highlighted its innovation, efficiency and usability. This device offers a reliable alternative for routine refractive assessment in clinical settings.

Introduction

Refractive errors constitute the predominant aetiology underlying visual impairment on a global scale, contributing to an estimated 49% of the total burden of visual dysfunction.1 According to the WHO, at least 2.2 billion people worldwide suffer from visual impairment or blindness, with more than 1 billion of these cases either preventable or left untreated.2 A systematic review (1990–2023) shows myopia prevalence rising steadily from 24.32% (95% CI 15.23% to 33.40%) to 35.81% (95% CI 31.70% to 39.91%), with projections to 36.59% by 2040 and 39.80% by 2050. Higher rates prevail in East Asia (35.22%), urban areas (28.55%), females (33.57%), adolescents (47.00%) and high school students (45.71%).3 Uncorrected refractive errors impose massive global productivity losses, US$244 billion from myopia and US$25.4 billion from presbyopia annually. Addressing these via spectacles requires only US$16 billion, yielding over US$250 billion in net economic gains yearly.2

The correct detection and correction of refractive errors are imperative to avoid long-term visual disability and enhance quality of life.4 5 Visual acuity testing followed by refraction testing is routine in clinical evaluation. Subjective refraction continues to be the clinical gold standard, which includes optimisation of optical correction as per the feedback from the patient to arrive at the best-corrected visual acuity.6 Although precise, conventional subjective refraction is time-consuming, operator-dependent and unsuitable for large-scale screening, particularly in high-throughput clinics or field operations.7

Contemporary methods for refraction include automated subjective refraction using motorised phoropters, which employ bisection root-finding and power vector algorithms for non-cycloplegic testing, achieving repeatability (0.17 D (M) 0.03 D (J0) 0.07 D (J45)) comparable to clinical subjective refraction and superior limits of agreement (LoA) (±0.56 D (M)) versus autorefractors (±0.77 D) in healthy adults. These systems offer high precision, brief duration (~4 min) and clinician independence, advantageous for underserved regions, but are limited by LoA exceeding the ±0.50 D clinical threshold, absent accommodation control and fixed axis orientation restricting astigmatism refinement.8

Emerging wearable devices like the 3nethra Specto, a liquid lens-based digital subjective refractor, address these gaps through portability, repeatability and integration of patient feedback, enhancing accessibility in mobile and rural eye care. This study validates the accuracy, reliability, clinical utility and user satisfaction of 3nethra Specto against conventional subjective refraction in young adults, contributing evidence on innovative devices to mitigate the global burden of uncorrected refractive error.

Methods

A prospective study was conducted to compare the wearable digital subjective refractor 3nethra Specto with conventional subjective refraction using trial frames in adults. Exclusion criteria included spherical refractive errors beyond ±18.00 D, cylindrical errors beyond ±6.00 D, recent ocular surgery, pregnancy, medications affecting vision and active ocular pathology.

A sample size of 135 participants was calculated for 90% power at 95% confidence to detect a 0.5 D mean refractive difference. After clinical history and visual acuity assessment, monocular retinoscopy was performed for each eye, followed by subjective refraction using trial frames and lenses, including standard techniques such as fogging, duochrome, Jackson Cross Cylinder (JCC) refinement, Borish delayed technique and binocular balancing for best-corrected visual acuity determination.

Blinding was maintained by having separate trained optometrists perform subjective refraction and 3nethra Specto assessments independently in different rooms. Repeatability was assessed by a second optometrist repeating the procedure under identical conditions using the same baseline retinoscopy and standardised subjective refraction protocol. Examiners were blinded to each other’s results. Refractive values—including spherical power, cylindrical power, axis and best corrected visual acuity—were recorded independently after each session.

Comprehensive anterior segment evaluation with slit-lamp biomicroscopy, pre- and post-dilatation and posterior segment examination was performed. Participants with ocular abnormalities or exclusion criteria post-dilation were withdrawn. After examination, participants and examiners completed the User Experience Questionnaire, a 6-scale, 26-item structured questionnaire using a 7-point semantic differential Likert scale (−3 ‘fully agree with negative term’ to +3 ‘fully agree with positive term’), evaluating comfort, comprehension, satisfaction, usability, workflow efficiency and interaction from patient and examiner perspectives.9 10

About 3nethra Specto, a wearable digital subjective refractor

3nethra Specto is a wearable digital subjective refractor by Forus Health that measures myopia, hypermetropia, astigmatism and presbyopia, aiding prescriptions for patients aged eight and above, as shown in figure 1. It connects to a smartphone for efficient data management, patient registration, vision screening and prescription printing. The device supports spherical and cylindrical corrections with JCC refinement, as well as near vision testing in both binocular and monocular modes.

Front view of 3nethra Specto, wearable digital subjective refractor from Forus.

As the world’s first wearable, wireless digital subjective refractor using advanced liquid lens technology, it replaces traditional trial frames and manual techniques. Electronically controlled liquid lenses provide precise adjustments with ±0.25 dioptre accuracy. With FDA clearance, the device aligns with the pupil via software, which also allows interpupillary distance adjustment and automatic data saving in the app for easy tracking.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of 135 adults for 90% power (β=0.10), 95% confidence (α=0.05, Zα/2=1.96), detecting a minimum clinically important difference of 0.50 D in refractive power between instruments (μ=0.50 D), with pooled SD σ=0.75 D (from pilot data and literature) and 10% relative precision (r=0.10), using the formula n=[(Zα/2)⋅σ)/r⋅μ]2 yielding n≈135. Right eye data only were analysed to ensure statistical independence and avoid inter-eye correlation bias. All refraction measurements were transformed into vector components via Fourier analysis to assess the refractive components in a standardised manner. This process included computations of M (spherical equivalent), J0 (Jackson cross-cylinder at axis 0° for regular astigmatism) and J45 (Jackson cross-cylinder at axis 45° for oblique astigmatism). The spherical equivalent (M) was calculated as sphere + (cylinder/2). The vector components J0 and J45 were determined via the following equations: J0=(-C/2) cos(2a), J45=(-C/2) sin(2a), where C is the cylinder power, and a is the axis of astigmatism.11

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean±SD (95% CIs). Primary predictive validity was assessed using univariate general linear models (GLMs) on IBM SPSS Statistics, V.25.0, with age and sex as covariates. Dependent variables included subjective refraction spherical power, cylindrical power, spherical equivalent (M) and power vectors (J₀, J₄₅). Power vector analysis employs parametric statistics with normally distributed, uncorrelated and axis-independent parameters (J₀, J₄₅), enabling valid GLM application and direct Bland-Altman LoA. Primary predictors comprised 3nethra Specto measurements. Model performance was evaluated using F-statistics, p values, R², adjusted R² and residual SD. Instrument repeatability was evaluated using GLM Repeated Measures for multiple 3nethra Specto. Agreement analysis employed Bland-Altman plots to visualise bias and 95% LoA between subjective refraction and 3nethra Specto measurements.12 Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 (two-tailed).

Results

A total of 135 adult participants with a mean age of 31.49±8.93 years were included in the study. Among these participants, 60.0% (n=81) were male and 40.0% (n=54) were female. Inter-eye correlation analysis of subjective refraction revealed strong agreement between fellow eyes for the spherical equivalent power component (M). The left eye refraction significantly predicted right eye refraction with F (38,96)=50.32, p<0.001, with an excellent R²=0.952 (adjusted R²=0.933). This demonstrates 95.2% shared variance between eyes, confirming substantial within-subject correlation and justifying the use of only the right eye for further analysis.

Assessment of refractive error status among the study participants revealed that compound myopic astigmatism was the most prevalent, affecting 45.18% (n=61) of individuals. This was followed by simple myopia in 20% (n=27), emmetropia in 17% (n=23), compound hypermetropic astigmatism in 11.85% (n=16), simple hyperopia in 5.9% (n=8) of participants.

The refractive status, measured in terms of sphere, cylinder, M (spherical equivalent), J0 and J45, was assessed using two clinical techniques: subjective refraction and the 3nethra Specto device, as summarised in table 1. Mean spherical power was −0.99 D (subjective) versus −1.02 D (3nethra Specto), confirming consistent mild myopia. Cylindrical power indicated with-the-rule astigmatism dominance (−0.83 D vs −0.49 D), with 3nethra showing less negative cylinder. Spherical equivalent values demonstrated excellent method agreement (−1.24 D subjective vs −1.26 D 3nethra), with overlapping 95% CIs confirming non-significant differences. Power vector analysis revealed minimal astigmatism asymmetry with J₀ values near zero (−0.01 D vs −0.02 D), indicating balanced against-the-rule astigmatism, while J₄₅ values were small but positive (0.08 D vs 0.07 D), suggesting slight oblique components.

Repeatability of 3nethra Specto

3nethra Specto demonstrated exceptional measurement for repeatability across all refractive parameters (all F>96, p<0.001 as shown in table 3). Spherical equivalent showed the highest explanatory power (R²=0.997), while J₀ vector exhibited superior precision (residual SD=0.041 D). All parameters maintained excellent adjusted R² values (≥0.956), confirming minimal overfitting. Age effects were non-significant except for J₄₅ (p=0.036), suggesting minor developmental influences on oblique astigmatism measurement. Residual SD remained within clinical tolerance (≤0.148 D), with power vectors demonstrating superior stability (J₀: 0.041 D; J₄₅: 0.050 D) compared with meridional powers. These results align with strong predictive validity (R2 0.914 to 0.994), confirming its interchangeability with subjective refraction. With residual SD below 0.15 D across parameters, the device achieves spectacle prescription accuracy within ±0.25 D tolerance, making it a clinically reliable alternative to conventional subjective refraction methods for clinical practice.

User Interface Questionnaire (UIQ) ratings from study participants and examiners

The patient’s perspective on the user interface for the 3nethra Specto was collected, seeking their input on comfort, enjoyable, friendly, inventive and exciting. The participants rated the subjective refraction method significantly higher in comfort than the 3nethra Specto (shown in online supplemental figure 1). The subjective refraction was also perceived as more enjoyable compared with the 3nethra Specto. Both methods were rated roughly equally friendly, with a slight preference for 3nethra Specto. The 3nethra Specto scored much higher on being seen as inventive, while subjective refraction was rated negatively in this aspect. The 3nethra Specto was considered more exciting than subjective refraction by participants.

Overall, study participants found subjective refraction more comfortable and enjoyable but appreciated the 3nethra Specto’s inventiveness and excitement. The friendliness rating was similar for both methods. This suggests that while traditional subjective refraction with trial frame and trial lenses may feel more familiar and comfortable, participants recognise the innovative nature of the 3nethra Specto technology.

The examiner’s feedback comparing 3nethra Specto and subjective refraction across five criteria: efficiency, meeting expectations, speed, innovation and ease of use. 3nethra Specto scored approximately 2.8 for efficiency and expectations, 2.7 for speed, 2.5 for innovation and 2.6 for ease of use, consistently outperforming subjective refraction. These results indicate examiners consider 3nethra Specto a superior, faster, more user-friendly and innovative alternative to conventional subjective refraction (represented in online supplemental figure 2).

Discussion

Uncorrected refractive errors affect over 2.3 billion people worldwide, causing significant avoidable vision loss.2 Conditions like myopia, hypermetropia and astigmatism impair daily life, especially in low-resource settings with limited eye care access.13 14 Timely and accurate refractive correction is essential, as it remains one of the most cost-effective public health interventions.15 The portability, ergonomic design and cost-effectiveness of the 3nethra Specto make it ideal for underserved regions with limited infrastructure.

Although autorefractors have shown promise for refractive screening, subjective refraction is the gold standard for spectacle prescriptions because it mitigates accommodation effects.16 17 The 3nethra Specto, a wearable digital subjective refractor controlled via a smartphone app, offers advantages over conventional trial frames and lenses by eliminating cumbersome setups and discomfort. Its advanced technology provides a modern, patient-friendly and space-efficient solution. Remote control via mobile app enhances convenience and adaptability in clinical and field settings.18 These attributes establish the 3nethra Specto as a compelling alternative to traditional refractive assessment in high-volume, resource-constrained settings, a hypothesis we will test in the next study phase.

This study evaluated the diagnostic concordance, repeatability and user acceptability of the 3nethra Specto, a wearable, liquid lens-based technology subjective refractor, in comparison with conventional subjective refraction. 3nethra Specto demonstrated exceptional within-subject repeatability across all refractive parameters, with residual SD ranging from 0.041 D (J₀) to 0.148 D (spherical equivalent), all below the 0.25 D clinical significance threshold. The J₀ vector exhibited the highest precision (0.041 D), surpassing spherical equivalent (0.148 D) and cylinder (0.145 D) components. These values compare favourably with clinical subjective refraction repeatability (0.15 D to 0.38 D) and exceed typical autorefractor performance. Pesudovs and Weisinger’s study has reported spherical equivalent SD of 0.04 D to 0.07 D for Topcon KR-8000 and Nidek AR-800 autorefractors, while Otero et al found 0.17 D for AOVA wavefront sensor sphere measurements.8 19

A similar study evaluating a software application for tele-controlled subjective refraction using a phoropter reported good agreement in spherical and spherical equivalent measurements but found less agreement for cylindrical vector components. Specifically, their 95% LoA were wider for the Jackson cross-cylinder components J₀ (±0.57 D) and J₄₅ (±0.79 D), indicating greater variability in astigmatism measurements with the tele-controlled method.20 In contrast, the current study showed that J₀ and J₄₅ components from the 3nethra Specto exhibited high concordance with subjective refraction (R2 0.984–0.987; residual SDs 0.041 D to 0.050 D), achieving over 96% agreement within clinically acceptable limits (±0.25 D). This suggests that the 3nethra Specto provides reliable cylindrical and astigmatic measurements, making it suitable for clinical use, including among patients with astigmatism.

Bland-Altman analysis confirmed 3Nethra Specto’s clinical interchangeability with subjective refraction, with 95% LoA within ±0.60 D across all parameters narrower than autorefractor benchmarks. Spherical equivalent demonstrated excellent agreement (LoA −0.39 D to +0.47 D, bias of 0.04), surpassing objective autorefraction (LoA ±0.75 D to 0.95 D) and automated subjective methods (LoA ±0.69 D to 0.88 D). Power vector analysis revealed superior astigmatism precision (J₀ LoA −0.62 D to +0.64 D; J₄₅ −0.32 D to +0.35 D) versus cylinder (LoA ±0.50 D).

A key advantage of the 3nethra Specto is its repeatability. It demonstrated exceptional repeatability across all refractive parameters (R² 0.960–0.997, residual SDs 0.041 D to 0.148 D. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating superior repeatability of electronic or wavefront-based devices compared with manual subjective methods.21 Such consistency is essential for both longitudinal patient follow-ups and large-scale screening initiatives.

The usability and examiner experience with the device were notably favourable. Examiner-rated user interaction quality scores were the highest for the 3nethra Specto in the domains of speed (2.72±0.73), workflow efficiency (2.80±0.45) and perceived innovation (2.63±0.92). Subjective refraction—while considered the clinical gold standard—scored lower in speed and innovation, highlighting its limitations in high-throughput or mobile environments. Similar studies of handheld autorefractors, including the 3nethra Aberro, have demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy and operational feasibility in both adult and paediatric populations.2224 These findings reinforce the potential of such technology to extend equitable vision care to remote or resource-limited communities.

Some limitations include the single-centre design, adult-only sample (age ≥8 years), and the exclusion of high refractive errors beyond the ±18 D sphere or ±6 D cylinder, which may limit extrapolation to individuals with extreme refractive errors.

Overall, the 3nethra Specto has strong clinical validity as a portable, reliable and user-friendly alternative to traditional subjective refraction. Its alignment with patient-centred subjective input, combined with automation and digital data capture, positions it well for scalable deployment in community, outreach and high-volume clinical settings.

Conclusion

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical performance of the 3nethra Specto, a wearable, liquid lens technology digital subjective refractor, in comparison with traditional methods of subjective refraction. The device demonstrated excellent agreement in spherical and cylindrical measurements across all refractive parameters, confirming its diagnostic equivalence to conventional techniques. The 3nethra Specto also showed strong test–retest repeatability, with minimal variability between repeated measurements by different examiners, supporting its reliability in routine practice and screening environments. Patient and examiner feedback indicated positive perceptions of the 3nethra Specto’s usability, innovation and ease of operation. In summary, the 3nethra Specto offers a reliable and reproducible alternative for refractive assessment in a clinical setup.
