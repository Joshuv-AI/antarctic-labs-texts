Understanding women’s physical and emotional experiences following medication abortion in china: a qualitative study using the writing express method
Authors: Yanhong Du, Mengying Bai, Ying Chang, Waixiang Yuan, Yun Gao, Xiangfei Zhu, Wei Cheng, Liyan Wang, Shuyi Ling, Wenbo Wu, Yuehui Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMedication abortion (MA) is legally accessible and commonly practiced in China; however, limited qualitative research has explored women’s physical and emotional experiences in this context. Understanding these experiences is crucial for improving clinical care and social support. This study aimed to explore the physical and emotional responses of Chinese women following MA using the Writing Express Method.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative study involving 30 women who underwent MA between January and June 2023 at a tertiary hospital in southern China. Participants completed written narratives in response to six open-ended prompts. Data collection was continued until thematic saturation was achieved. Three independent researchers with clinical and qualitative training conducted reflexive thematic analysis. Data were managed and coded manually using Excel spreadsheets, and themes were developed through iterative discussion and consensus.ResultsParticipants reported diverse experiences organized into three domains of inquiry: (1) physical responses to MA, including pain, bleeding, and fatigue; (2) emotional reactions such as fear, sadness, guilt, and relief; and (3) coping strategies and sources of support. Most participants described moderate to severe pain and prolonged bleeding, while some reported minimal physical discomfort. Many women expressed feelings of guilt and self-blame, whereas a few described relief after the procedure. Sources of support included family, friends, and online resources, though some participants noted stigma and lack of professional counseling.ConclusionsWomen’s physical and emotional experiences following MA in China are multifaceted and shaped by both biomedical and sociocultural factors. These findings highlight the need for integrating psychological support into post-abortion care and for public health initiatives aimed at reducing abortion-related stigma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04089-0.Keywords: Medication abortion, Women's experiences, Emotional impact, Side effects, Decision-making, Qualitative research, Writing express method

Background

MA is a method of terminating pregnancy using pharmacological agents such as mifepristone and misoprostol, has become an important reproductive health service worldwide. Compared with surgical abortion, MA is less invasive, can be more discreet, and is generally associated with fewer complications when administered appropriately [1, 2]. Globally, MA has been increasingly promoted as a safe and effective option for women seeking early pregnancy termination. In China, MA has been legally accessible since the early 1990 s and is widely practiced in clinical settings. It is typically offered up to 49–63 days of gestation, depending on national and institutional protocols [3]. While the procedure is relatively inexpensive, costs are usually borne out-of-pocket by patients and may still pose a barrier for some women. MA is predominantly administered within medical facilities under clinical supervision, although in some cases women may complete the process at home after the initial clinic visit. Despite its widespread use, there is limited public health information about MA, and sociocultural factors—including stigma surrounding abortion and traditional preferences for male children—continue to shape women’s reproductive experiences in China [4–6]. Although numerous quantitative studies have documented the safety, efficacy, and clinical outcomes of MA in China, relatively little is known about women’s subjective physical and emotional experiences during and after the procedure. Prior research has primarily focused on medical complications, with less attention given to psychological impacts such as fear, guilt, sadness, or relief. Moreover, few studies have explored how Chinese cultural and social contexts influence these experiences [7, 8]. Understanding women’s perspectives is essential for developing holistic models of post-abortion care that address both physical recovery and psychological well-being. The Writing Express Method, originally developed as a therapeutic writing technique to alleviate psychological distress among patients with serious illness, offers a promising qualitative approach to capture personal narratives. By encouraging participants to write openly about their feelings and experiences, this method enables researchers to access rich, first-person accounts that may not surface in structured interviews [9, 10]. While the method has been applied in various health research contexts, its use in studying abortion experiences remains limited. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore Chinese women’s physical and emotional experiences following MA using the Writing Express Method. By analyzing participants’ written narratives, we sought to identify key themes related to their bodily symptoms, emotional responses, and coping strategies [7, 11].

This research contributes to filling a critical gap in the literature by providing insights into the lived experiences of women in China, with implications for improving clinical practice, enhancing psychosocial support, and informing public health strategies [12, 13].

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative research design to explore women’s physical and emotional experiences following MA in China. Data were collected using the Writing Express Method, a structured narrative-writing approach, and analyzed through reflexive thematic analysis, which emphasizes researcher reflexivity and interpretation of meaning beyond surface content [14]. This analytic strategy was chosen because it emphasizes researcher reflexivity and allows for in-depth exploration of both explicit and underlying meanings in participants’ narratives.

The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [15, 16].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted between January and June 2023 at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, The University of Hong Kong–Shenzhen Hospital, a tertiary teaching hospital in southern China. Women were recruited purposively if they met the following inclusion criteria:

aged 18 years or older;

aged 18 years or older;

had undergone medication abortion within the past four weeks;

had undergone medication abortion within the past four weeks;

were able to read and write in Chinese;

were able to read and write in Chinese;

provided written informed consent.

provided written informed consent.

Women were excluded if they had severe psychiatric conditions or declined to participate. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was reached, meaning no new themes emerged from the data. A total of 30 women were included, which was considered sufficient to capture a range of experiences while allowing for analytic depth. Participants were recruited by clinicians at the gynecology outpatient clinic, who informed eligible women about the study after their follow-up visits. Interested women were given study information sheets and invited to contact the research team.No financial or material incentives were provided for participation.

Data collection

Participants were invited to complete written narratives in response to six open-ended prompts designed to elicit both physical and emotional experiences. Sample prompts included: “Please describe your physical symptoms during and after the abortion,” and “Please describe your emotional reactions following the procedure.” Narratives were written in a private room at the hospital to ensure confidentiality. Each participant was given 30 min to complete the task, with the option to write more if desired. All responses were collected on the same day to minimize recall bias. The six narrative prompts used in this study were developed specifically for this research to elicit participants’ physical and emotional experiences. The English version of the prompts has been provided as a supplementary file (Additional file 1) and cited accordingly in the manuscript.

The writing express method

The Writing Express Method is a therapeutic writing technique that encourages participants to reflect on and express their personal experiences in written form. Initially developed as a psychological intervention to reduce distress among patients with serious illnesses, it has been adapted for qualitative research as a tool to capture rich, first-person narratives. In this study, the method provided participants with a safe space to document their experiences, while simultaneously generating data suitable for qualitative analysis. This approach has previously been validated as a therapeutic and qualitative research tool to explore sensitive health experiences [7].

Data management and analysis

All narratives were transcribed verbatim into electronic files and anonymized by assigning codes (e.g., Participant 01, Participant 02). Data were stored on password-protected hospital computers accessible only to the research team.

Microsoft Excel spreadsheets were used to organize, code, and retrieve data segments.

Three independent researchers conducted the analysis. Two were obstetrics and gynecology clinicians with qualitative research training, and one was a qualitative researcher with expertise in thematic analysis. Each researcher conducted independent coding of the narratives.

Codes were then compared and refined through iterative discussions, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Codes were grouped into categories and subsequently into broader themes.

Reflexive thematic analysis enabled the team to identify both semantic meanings (directly expressed content) and latent meanings (underlying assumptions or values).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of The University of Hong Kong–Shenzhen Hospital (Approval No. 2023 − 152). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were assured that their participation was voluntary and that all information provided would remain confidential and anonymized. Participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any point without any consequences.

Domains of inquiry and emerging themes

Analysis of the written narratives yielded three main domains of inquiry: (1) physical responses to MA, (2) emotional reactions, and (3) coping strategies and sources of support. Within each domain, several subthemes were identified.

Physical responses to medication abortion.

Physical responses to medication abortion.

Most participants reported moderate to severe abdominal pain and cramping, often described as similar to or more intense than menstrual pain. Many women also experienced prolonged vaginal bleeding, sometimes lasting more than two weeks, which disrupted their daily routines.

Fatigue and weakness were also commonly noted.

Coping strategies and sources of support.

Women employed diverse strategies to cope with their experiences. Some sought emotional or practical support from partners, friends, or family members, while others relied on online forums and peer communities. Summary of findings

Overall, women’s experiences following MA in China encompassed substantial physical symptoms, complex emotional responses, and varied coping strategies. Most participants described pain and bleeding as significant physical burdens, while many also reported guilt and fear.

Support was often sought from informal networks rather than professional sources, underscoring the need for improved counseling and stigma reduction in reproductive healthcare.

Discussion

This qualitative study explored Chinese women’s physical and emotional experiences following MA using the Writing Express Method. By analyzing written narratives from 30 participants, we identified three domains of inquiry: physical responses, emotional reactions, and coping strategies.

The findings provide new insights into how women in China experience MA, highlighting the importance of addressing not only biomedical outcomes but also psychological and sociocultural factors [17–19].

Physical responses

Most participants experienced abdominal pain, bleeding, and fatigue, consistent with previous research on common side effects of MA. While these symptoms are expected, their severity varied, and some women described severe pain during MA, and a few reported insufficient pain managements, suggesting that clinical protocols should integrate more proactive analgesic strategies. Enhanced anticipatory guidance and proactive pain relief strategies should be integrated into MA care [20, 21].

Emotional reactions

Emotional outcomes ranged from guilt and sadness to relief. The prevalence of guilt and self-blame reflects the influence of abortion stigma in China, where abortion is legal yet often morally contested within families and communities. Prior studies in stigmatized contexts have shown that women may experience isolation and heightened distress following abortion [19, 22]. Conversely, a subset of women expressed relief, particularly when the pregnancy conflicted with personal or educational goals. These findings suggest that women’s emotions are not uniform but shaped by individual circumstances and cultural expectations [23, 24].

Sociocultural influences

Some participants highlighted family pressure and gender norms, including references to son preference by in-laws. This reflects enduring cultural values in parts of China and underscores how reproductive decisions remain embedded in family and societal contexts. Stigma from community members and peers further exacerbated negative emotions. Currently, China lacks large-scale public health campaigns addressing abortion stigma, and reliable reproductive health information is limited. Expanding access to accurate information and supportive messaging could improve women’s post-abortion experiences [25, 26].

Coping strategies and support systems

Participants relied heavily on partners, family, and online forums for support, while professional counseling was rarely accessed. This gap points to the need for strengthening psychosocial services within abortion care. Counseling—either in person or via digital health platforms—should be routinely offered as part of post-abortion follow-up.

Clinical and public health implications

Our findings indicate that improving MA services in China requires a holistic model that integrates physical care, emotional support, and stigma reduction. Clinicians should prepare patients for expected symptoms, offer effective pain management, and provide psychological support. Policymakers should consider public health campaigns that normalize abortion, reduce stigma, and disseminate reliable information to empower women in their reproductive choices.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, reliance on the Writing Express Method may have constrained participants’ ability to elaborate compared to interviews. Second, the study was conducted at a single hospital, limiting transferability. Third, narratives were self-reported and may be influenced by recall or social desirability bias. Future studies should include multiple regions and consider mixed qualitative methods to enhance validity.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the physical and emotional experiences of women in China following MA. Women reported diverse physical symptoms and a wide spectrum of emotional reactions, shaped not only by the procedure itself but also by cultural stigma and family expectations. These findings emphasize the need for integrating psychological support into abortion services and for developing public health strategies to reduce stigma and provide reliable reproductive health information [22, 27]. By foregrounding women’s voices, this research contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of abortion experiences in China and underscores the need for patient-centered, culturally sensitive models of care [28, 29].
