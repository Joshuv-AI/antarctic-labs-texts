Cumulative risk and graded associations with high-risk sexual behaviors among Chinese university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lu Jie, Zhou Ping, Hu Mei, Liao Maoxu, Ma Pingchuan, He Jing, Yang Lin, Cao Rongzhe, Li Yujuan, Zhang Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChinese university students experience sexual maturation and psychosexual development, which may be associated with an increased likelihood of high-risk sexual behaviors (HRSB). This study aimed to estimate the prevalence of HRSB among Chinese university students and quantify the cumulative effects of multiple risk factors on HRSB.MethodsThis cross-sectional survey employed stratified cluster sampling and was conducted among 2,722 students from four universities in Luzhou City, Sichuan Province, China. Data on demographics, sexual knowledge, attitudes, and experiences were collected via a self-administered questionnaire. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with HRSB, and the cumulative effect of risk factors was examined by categorizing participants according to the number of accumulated risk factors, with a test for linear trend to evaluate the graded association.ResultsOverall, 325 students (11.94%, 95% CI: 10.72–13.16) reported HRSB. Male sex (aOR = 2.75, 95% CI: 2.05–3.69), grade ≥ 4 (aOR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.35–2.51), non-medical major (aOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.41–2.72), same-sex sexual experience (aOR = 4.00, 95% CI: 2.63–6.10), permissive attitude toward premarital sex (aOR = 2.63, 95% CI: 2.01–3.43), other-relatives caregiver before age 18 (aOR = 2.75, 95% CI: 1.58–4.77), childhood sexual abuse (aOR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.85–3.76), and adequate sexual knowledge (aOR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.36–2.76) were independently associated with HRSB. A total of 33.72% of students had two or more cumulative risk factors. A clear graded association was observed between the number of accumulated risk factors and HRSB. Compared to students with zero risk factors, those with one factor had non-significantly increased odds (aOR = 1.63, 95% CI: 0.98–2.72), whereas those with two factors had 5.01-fold higher odds (aOR = 5.01, 95% CI: 3.01–8.53), and those with three or more factors had 15.66-fold higher odds (aOR = 15.66, 95% CI: 8.69–28.21).ConclusionApproximately one in eight university students reported HRSB, with risk escalating progressively as risk factors accumulated. One-third of students had multiple cumulative risks, and those with three or more factors had nearly 16-fold higher odds of HRSB compared to those with none. These findings underscore the urgent need for comprehensive, evidence-based sexual health programs that simultaneously address cumulative risk profiles in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28671-9.Keywords: University students, High-risk sexual behaviors, Childhood sexual abuse, Cumulative Risks

Introduction

Sexual health issues among college students have become a priority area for public health intervention in the 21st century. According to the Global Burden of Disease study 2019, the global incidence rate of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among adolescents was approximately 18,378 per 100,000 person-years (18.38%) [1], highlighting the substantial disease burden in this demographic. In China, university students have experienced a significant increase in HIV infections, with an annual growth rate of 30–50% in recent years [2]. Alarmingly, only 11.43% of students obtain HIV testing after engaging in HRSB [3]. Despite the Chinese government’s 2015 policy to implement comprehensive sex-education and testing programmes, new infections among students have continued to rise [2], suggesting an urgent need for further evidence to refine current prevention strategies.

HRSB is commonly defined as unprotected intercourse, multiple or casual partners, sexual relations with high-risk individuals, engagement in sex while under the influence of alcohol or drugs, and commercial or coerced sexual encounters [4]. In this study, HRSB was operationally defined as any of the following behaviors in the past 12 months: (a) inconsistent condom use, (b) multiple sexual partners (≥ 2), or (c) commercial sex. These three indicators were selected because they represent the most frequent and reliably measurable high-risk sexual behaviors among university students in the Chinese context and have been commonly used in previous studies of similar populations [5, 6]. These behaviors are particularly prevalent during emerging adulthood, a developmental period characterized by increased autonomy and exploration. In China, a national survey showed that 11.3% of college students had engaged in sexual intercourse, of whom 23.5% reported forceful sexual encounters and 24.2% experiencing unplanned pregnancies [7], underscoring the public health challenge posed by HRSB among Chinese university students. Engaging in HRSB substantially increases the risk of unintended pregnancy, STI acquisition and sexual violence; these adverse outcomes can impair academic progress, exacerbate mental-health problems and diminish long-term quality of life among university students [8, 9].

HRSB is associated with a complex interplay of individual, familial, school-level, and socio-environmental factors. These influences can be understood across multiple interconnected domains: individual characteristics, family dynamics, and broader environmental contexts [10]. This study focuses on the cumulative impact of individual and familial risk factors. At the individual level, developmental characteristics significantly influence sexual behavior. College students experience sexual physiological maturation alongside heightened psychological curiosity, which is associated with exploratory behaviors related to sexuality [11]. Concurrently, this population holds progressively liberal perspectives on sexuality [12]. However, inadequate sexual knowledge and insufficient guidance on sexual values—deficiencies common among this population—may further be associated with risky sexual behaviors [13, 14]. Moreover, the pursuit of independence and autonomy, a hallmark of emerging adulthood, increases students’ susceptibility to external influences from peers and online media, which serve as primary socialization agents for sexual learning during college years [15].

Beyond individual characteristics, the familial environment constitutes a critical layer of influence. Lower parental monitoring and weak parent-child intimacy consistently predict higher odds of HRSB, whereas supportive family relationships exert a significant protective effect [16–18].

At the broader environmental level, school and sociocultural contexts either reinforce or mitigate these risks. Comprehensive, scientifically-based sex education helps students develop appropriate attitudes and self-protection awareness, thereby reducing HRSB [19]. However, the current state of sexuality education in Chinese universities remains problematic, characterized by unsystematic content, limited pedagogical approaches, and inadequate faculty resources, and thus fails to adequately address students’ needs.

While a considerable body of research has identified single risk factors for HRSB, these determinants rarely occur in isolation ; such co-occurrence may create a cumulative burden that exceeds the sum of single-risk effects and, in turn, be associated with the initiation of HRSB [20]. However, the prevalence of risk factor clustering and its cumulative impact on HRSB remain quantitatively unexplored in Chinese university populations. Specifically, it is unknown whether the simple count of cumulative risks follows a graded association with HRSB. To address this gap, we conducted a large-scale cross-sectional survey to: (1) estimate the prevalence of HRSB among Chinese university students, (2) identify independent factors associated with HRSB, and (3) quantify the cumulative associations between risk exposure and HRSB. The findings are expected to inform multi-level, evidence-based interventions tailored to the most vulnerable subgroups.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional survey employed a stratified cluster sampling design. The target population comprised approximately 110,000 undergraduate and junior college students across seven institutions in Luzhou City, Sichuan Province, China. Four of these seven institutions (two undergraduate universities and two junior colleges) were selected to represent the diversity of higher education institutions in the region. The sample allocation ratio across the four participating institutions was predetermined as 5:4:2:2, based on institutional size and disciplinary composition. Within each of the four participating institutions, stratification was performed independently using two factors: grade level (grades 1–5) and academic discipline (specific major categories as defined by each institution). Classes were randomly selected as the primary sampling unit within each stratum, and all eligible students in selected classes were invited to participate.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated with the formula. where p = expected prevalence (0.235 based on Chinese college data [7], α = 0.05 and δ = 0.10p. This yielded a minimum sample size of 1,306. To account for the design effect (deff = 2) of cluster sampling, the target sample size was doubled to approximately 2,600; further adjusting for an anticipated 10% non-response rate, we aimed to recruit approximately 2,880 participants. The final sample (n = 2,722) represented 2.47% of the total target population (approximately 110,000 students across seven institutions), which is below the conventional 5% threshold for applying finite population correction; accordingly, the standard sample size formula without finite population correction is appropriate for this study.

Investigation methods

Data were collected between January and April 2022. Prior to survey administration, trained investigators provided a detailed explanation of the study purpose, significance, and confidentiality procedures during class breaks. Students who agreed to participate then signed a paper-based informed consent form, after which they completed the structured questionnaire online via the Questionnaire Star platform. The questionnaire was pilot-tested among 32 college students prior to the full-scale survey to assess clarity, comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness; based on feedback, items with ambiguous wording were revised. The final questionnaire was then administered to the full sample. The survey was completed anonymously, and participants were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. To ensure data quality, investigators received standardized training and provided uniform instructions to all participants. Quality control measures included systematic review of response patterns to ensure data integrity. All questionnaires retained for analysis were complete, with no missing values for any key variable. The final sample comprised 2,722 valid questionnaires (see Fig. 1 for the participant flow diagram).

Measurement

(1) Sociodemographic characteristics included sex, residence, grade, ethnicity, same-sex sexual experiences (yes/no), educational level (junior college/undergraduate), academic major, only-child family status, Primary caregivers before age 18 were categorized into two groups (parents or grandparents, including biological parents, paternal grandparents, or maternal grandparents; and other relatives, including aunts, uncles, cousins, or other distant relatives.)

(2) Sexual knowledge was assessed using a 10-item scale [21]. The scale covered topics such as emergency contraception efficacy and pregnancy prevention myths (e.g., post-coital urination as a method to avoid pregnancy). Each correct answer scored 1 point (range, 0–10). A score ≥ 6 (≥ 60% of the total score) was classified as adequate sexual knowledge. This cut-off was selected based on conventional practice in educational and health research, where 60% is commonly used as the threshold for adequate knowledge [22]. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.80).

(3) Attitudes toward premarital sex were assessed using a culturally adapted version of Reiss’s Premarital Sexual Permissiveness Scale [23]. The adapted scale comprised six items covering both male and female perspectives across three relationship types (stranger, dating partner, and fiancé/e), with each item rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 5 = “Strongly Agree”). Total scores ranged from 6 to 30, with higher scores indicating more permissive attitudes. A score of ≥ 18 (≥ 60% of the maximum score) was classified as a permissive attitude toward premarital sex. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.87).

(4) Childhood sexual abuse (CSA) was assessed using an 11-item scale [24]. The scale comprised three domains: items 1–4 assessed sexual harassment, items 5–9 assessed molestation, and items 10–11 assessed sexual assault. Respondents answering “yes” to any item were classified as having experienced CSA. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.91).

(5) High-risk sexual behaviors (HRSB) were defined as any of the following in the past 12 months: (a) inconsistent condom use, (b) multiple sexual partners (≥ 2), or (c) commercial sex. Participants reporting any of these behaviors were classified as having HRSB (yes/no).

The full questionnaire (Chinese original and English translation) is available in Supplementary_File_1_Questionnaire_Chinese_English.pdf.

Statistical analysis

Data were exported and analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and group differences were compared using the chi-square test. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered simultaneously into a multivariable binary logistic regression model (enter method). Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. Clustering at the class and institution levels was not explicitly modeled because cluster identifiers were not retained in the final dataset; we assessed the robustness of our findings to clustering effects through a design-effect sensitivity analysis. Standard errors were inflated by √(DEFF), and design-effect-adjusted Wald P-values were recomputed across three predefined DEFF values (1.5, 2.0, and 3.0), with DEFF = 2.0 corresponding to the value assumed in the sample size calculation. To examine cumulative risk effects, participants were categorized by the number of accumulated risk factors. The dose - response relationship was formally evaluated by entering the cumulative risk index as a continuous variable in a logistic regression model to obtain a P for trend. Departure from linearity was assessed using a quadratic term and restricted cubic spline (knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles), with adjusted predicted probabilities of HRSB presented graphically. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of informed consent, voluntariness, and confidentiality. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University approved this study (reference number: KY2022240). All subjects provided informed consent.

Results

Participant flow

Figure 1 presents the participant flow diagram. Of the 2,978 invited students, 78 declined to provide informed consent and did not participate; the remaining 2,900 students provided written informed consent and completed the questionnaire. After excluding 98 questionnaires due to patterned responses and 80 questionnaires due to > 20% missing data, 2,722 valid questionnaires (91.4% of invited students) were retained for final analysis.

Sociodemographic characteristics

The survey participants ranged in age from 18 to 23 years, with females accounting for 58.82% of the total. The majority were non-medical majors, grade 1–3 students, non-only children, and of Han ethnicity. Most participants were primarily raised by parents or grandparents before age 18, with a slightly higher proportion residing in rural areas. Of the 2,722 respondents, 5.29% reported same-sex sexual experiences, and 10.32% reported CSA experience. Regarding sexual knowledge, the majority of participants demonstrated adequate sexual knowledge, while most held non-permissive attitudes toward premarital sex. (Table 1).

Chi-square analysis revealed that males had a higher prevalence of HRSB than females (χ² = 73.035, P < 0.001). Additionally, ≥ grade 4 students (χ² = 27.042, P < 0.001), non-medical majors (χ² = 6.157, P = 0.013), and individuals with adequate sexual knowledge (χ² = 29.477, P < 0.001) exhibited higher rates of HRSB. Multivariable logistic regression analysis of HRSB

We conducted binary logistic regression to identify factors independently associated with HRSB. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analyses were simultaneously included in the multivariable model. In the final model, all variables were mutually adjusted, and adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate the independent associations with HRSB. Additionally, to assess the robustness of our findings against potential design effects arising from the stratified cluster sampling approach, we performed a sensitivity analysis by applying design-effect corrections with variance inflation factors of 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0.

Male students had significantly higher odds of HRSB compared to female students (aOR = 2.75, 95% CI: 2.05–3.69, P < 0.001). Students in non-medical majors had higher odds than those in medical majors (aOR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.41–2.72, P < 0.001). Grade ≥ 4 students exhibited higher odds compared to grade 1–3 students (aOR = 1.84, 95% CI: 1.35–2.51, P < 0.001). Students raised by other relatives before age 18 had higher odds than those raised by parents or grandparents (aOR = 2.75, 95% CI: 1.58–4.77, P < 0.001). Individuals with a history of CSA had 2.64-fold higher odds of engaging in HRSB than those without such a history (aOR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.85–3.76, P < 0.001). Counter-intuitively, those with adequate sexual knowledge had higher odds of engaging in HRSB (aOR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.36–2.76, P < 0.001). Individuals with same-sex experiences (aOR = 4.00, 95% CI: 2.63–6.10, P < 0.001) and those with a permissive attitude toward premarital sex (aOR = 2.63, 95% CI: 2.01–3.43, P < 0.001) were additionally associated with elevated HRSB risk. Sensitivity analyses with design-effect corrections (variance inflation factors = 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0) confirmed that all associations remained statistically significant (all P ≤ 0.038), indicating robust findings against potential clustering effects (Table 3).

Cumulative effects of risk factors

We selected factors that met three criteria: (a) they were independently associated with HRSB in the multivariable model (P < 0.001); (b) they represent modifiable or life-course factors relevant to prevention; and (c) they are conceptually distinct from demographic characteristics (e.g., sex, grade, major). Demographic variables such as sex, grade level, and academic major were excluded from the cumulative score because they are non-modifiable structural factors not amenable to intervention. In contrast, the four selected factors—adequate sexual knowledge, permissive attitude toward premarital sex, CSA, and same-sex experiences—were prioritized as potential targets for behavioral and psychological interventions.

Based on the multivariable analysis, participants were categorized by the number of accumulated risk factors (0, 1, 2, ≥ 3). The results showed that 19.36% (527) had zero factors, 46.91% (1,277) had one factor, 27.99% (762) had two factors, and 5.73% (156) had three or more factors. Overall, 33.72% (918/2,722) had two or more accumulated risk factors. Figure 2 illustrates the distribution.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the cumulative risk index, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an expanded eight-factor definition that included all independent predictors from Table 3 (sex, grade, major, same-sex experience, permissive attitude, caregiver type, CSA, and adequate sexual knowledge). The results remained consistent with the primary four-factor index: the continuous model showed an OR of 2.48 per additional risk factor (95% CI: 2.20–2.80, P < 0.001), and the threshold comparison of ≥ 3 vs. < 3 factors yielded an OR of 5.96 (95% CI: 4.32–8.22, P < 0.001). These sensitivity analyses confirmed that the cumulative risk effect is robust to alternative definitions of the risk index (Table 4).

Analysis of the association between cumulative effects and HRSB

We performed binary logistic regression with HRSB as the dependent variable and the number of accumulated risk factors (0, 1, 2, ≥ 3) as the independent variable, adjusting for sex, ethnic, residence, and educational level. A significant graded association relationship was observed (P for trend < 0.001). Compared to students with zero risk factors, those with one factor showed non-significantly increased odds (aOR = 1.63, 95% CI: 0.98–2.72, P = 0.061), whereas those with two factors had 5.01-fold higher odds (aOR = 5.01, 95% CI: 3.01–8.53, P < 0.001), and those with three or more factors had 15.66-fold higher odds (aOR = 15.66, 95% CI: 8.69–28.21, P < 0.001). To assess the robustness of these findings to potential clustering effects, we performed a design-effect sensitivity analysis by inflating standard errors by √(deff) for design effects of 1.5, 2.0, and 3.0. The associations for two and three or more factors remained highly significant across all design-effect scenarios, whereas the one-factor category remained non-significant throughout, confirming the robustness of the graded association relationship (Table 5).

Non-linearity assessment

To formally test whether the relationship between the cumulative risk score and HRSB was linear, we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the cumulative risk score distribution (knots = 0, 1, and 2). The non-linear spline component was not significant (Wald test: P = 0.167; likelihood ratio test: P = 0.176), indicating no evidence of non-linearity. These findings are consistent with a linear dose-response relationship between cumulative risk exposure and HRSB (Table 6).

Discussion

The transition from adolescence to adulthood involves rapid social role changes that are associated with college students’ sexual cognition, attitudes, and social contexts. Consistent with this developmental perspective, we found that 11.94% of university students reported at least one high-risk sexual behavior (HRSB) in the past year, and that the likelihood of such behavior increased exponentially with the number of accumulated risk factors. Grounded in the multi-system framework [10], which posits that risk behavior arises from multiple interconnected domains, our findings confirm that HRSB is influenced by factors at both the individual level (e.g., sex, sexual knowledge, attitudes, same-sex experience) and the family level (e.g., caregiver type, childhood sexual abuse). The observed cumulative effects further suggest that risk factors from these domains do not operate in isolation but converge to be associated with increased HRSB risk in a graded manner. These results underscore that HRSB is seldom explained by a single factor; rather, it is the cumulative burden of individual, family, and contextual risks that is associated with students’ behavioral patterns. By quantifying this graded association, our study extends previous single-factor research and provides an empirical foundation for more precise multi-level interventions.

In this study, 11.94% of students reported at least one high-risk sexual behaviour during the past year. This figure is markedly lower than the 35% recorded among sexually active Chinese university students in 2019 [25] and also below rates reported in the United Kingdom (25%) [26] and Zambia (34.7%) [27]. Inconsistent condom use was noted in 10.4% of participants, a behaviour previously linked to insufficient usage skills and negative attitudes toward condoms [28, 29], and remained well under the 47.7% documented in a recent Irish cohort [30]. Multiple partnerships (3.7%) and commercial contacts (0.2%), both of which elevate STI risk through partner uncertainty [31], were far below the corresponding Ethiopian (42.7%) [32] and Nepalese (28.0%) figures [33]. These differences may reflect genuine regional variations in sexual norms, supported by the comparatively conservative sociocultural climate and strict legal restrictions on commercial sex in mainland China [34, 35].

At the individual level, we identified several key factors associated with HRSB. In our study, being male was associated with significantly higher odds of HRSB, consistent with more permissive sexual scripts, attenuated impulse-control expectations, and weaker sociocultural constraints on male sexual behavior [36]. Relative to non-medical majors, medical students receive systematic instruction in reproductive health and infection control, which is associated with a clearer perception of the consequences of unprotected intercourse and more cautious behavioural choices [37, 38]; conversely, students in other disciplines are exposed to limited formal sexual-health education, increasing the probability of risk-taking. Additionally, progression to senior years coincides with expanded social networks, greater exposure to sexual cues and heightened autonomy, while institutional supervision declines and targeted sexual-health guidance remains scarce, factors that are collectively associated with unprotected or multiple-partner encounters among senior students [6]. These findings indicate that sex, academic major, and grade are associated with HRSB, highlighting the need for stratified, precision-based prevention strategies.

Beyond demographic characteristics, we found that students reporting same-sex sexual experiences and those holding permissive attitudes toward premarital sex exhibited elevated odds of HRSB. Same-sex attracted university students often face minority stress and social stigma, which are associated with a greater search for emotional intimacy and with unprotected or multiple-partner encounters as a form of coping [39–41]. Meanwhile, the association between permissive attitudes and HRSB aligns with the attitude–behavior hypothesis, which posits that more liberal sexual attitudes lower perceived behavioral constraints, thereby facilitating risk-taking [42]. Collectively, these findings highlight the need for school-based educational programs that move beyond knowledge transmission to actively address attitudinal and socio-emotional dimensions of sexual health [43]. Importantly, the inclusion of same-sex experiences and permissive attitudes as statistical predictors in this study does not imply a moral or behavioral judgment. Rather, these associations should be interpreted within their broader social contexts—they may reflect the influence of minority stress, social stigma, or prevailing peer norms, rather than inherent individual characteristics or deficits.

The positive association between adequate sexual knowledge and HRSB was unexpected but not without theoretical precedent. The reasoned action approach posits that behavior is primarily driven by attitudes and perceived normative beliefs rather than by knowledge alone [44, 45]; consequently, even well-informed individuals may engage in risky behaviors when they hold permissive attitudes or perceive that such behaviors are socially accepted. Indeed, a recent study among Chinese college students found that although condom use knowledge was high, a marked dissociation between knowledge and actual behavior was common [46], highlighting the gap between knowing and doing in sexual health contexts. Moreover, didactic knowledge transfer alone appears insufficient to drive behavioral change, whereas interactive risk-reduction counseling has been shown to promote condom use and reduce sexually transmitted infections [47], suggesting that the manner in which knowledge is acquired may be as important as its mere possession. Several alternative explanations for this finding also warrant consideration. First, reverse causation may partly account for the observed association: students who engage in HRSB might subsequently seek out sexual health information in response to their experiences, resulting in greater knowledge acquired after the initiation of risk behaviors, rather than knowledge preceding and promoting risk. Second, despite adjustment for key sociodemographic variables, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Unmeasured factors—such as sensation-seeking personality traits, peer norms, or family communication patterns—could plausibly influence both the acquisition of sexual knowledge and engagement in HRSB, thereby contributing to a spurious positive association. Future research incorporating longitudinal designs and more comprehensive measures of psychosocial covariates is warranted to further delineate the nature of this relationship.

At the family level, our findings underscore the critical role of early caregiving environments. Students reared by other relatives displayed significantly elevated odds of HRSB. This pattern mirrors US data indicating that even brief contact with the child-welfare system—without formal foster placement—is associated with higher rates of unsafe sex [48]. These findings suggest that disruptions in early caregiving environments, even in the absence of formal foster care, may have lasting implications for adolescent sexual health. A plausible mechanism is that deficient familial affection and truncated sexual socialisation during critical developmental windows erode self-control capacities. Low adolescent self-control, in turn, predicts persistent engagement in sexual risk-taking across the transition to adulthood [49]. Together, these findings underscore that stable, affectionate and educationally rich early environments are distal but powerful influences on later sexual health, and suggest that universities should offer targeted psychosocial support for students with atypical caregiving backgrounds.

CSA emerged as one of the strongest predictors of HRSB in this study. Overall, 10.32% of participants reported CSA, a figure consistent with the 12.9% observed among Chinese middle-school students [50]. A robust body of evidence indicates that maltreatment, whether sexual, physical or emotional, disrupts normative developmental processes and markedly increases the probability of subsequent sexual risk-taking [20]. Mechanistically, early trauma can generate persistent negative affect, attachment insecurity, and maladaptive coping strategies; in adulthood, these sequelae often manifest as efforts to alleviate emotional pain through sexual activity or to seek validation via multiple or unprotected partners [51, 52]. Importantly, CSA survivors also display higher levels of online social network isolation and intentional exposure to online pornography, which in turn are associated with higher offline sexual risk [51]. These findings underscore the need for universities to integrate trauma-informed psychological services into routine sexual-health programs and to prioritize CSA-affected students for intensive counselling and skills-based interventions. However, the pathway from CSA to the specific HRSB outcomes assessed in this study (inconsistent condom use, multiple sexual partners, and commercial sex) is likely complex and indirect. CSA may increase the risk of these behaviors through unmeasured mediators such as depression, post-traumatic stress, or substance use. Consequently, although psychosocial support for CSA survivors remains essential for their well-being, its direct effect on reducing the three HRSB outcomes requires further investigation.

Cumulative risk effects. Nearly one in three students (33.72%) simultaneously carried two or more risk factors. The likelihood of HRSB rose exponentially: two factors conferred 5.01-fold higher odds and three or more conferred 15.66-fold higher odds, supporting a clear dose–response relationship rather than simple additivity. To understand the complex etiology of HRSB, we adopted the multi-system framework [10], which posits that risk factors from individual, family, and broader environmental domains converge within the individual’s developmental context to influence behavioral outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that these risk factors do not operate in isolation but accumulate to be associated with increased HRSB risk in a graded manner, lending strong support to the cumulative risk model [53]. The exponential increase in odds we observed (from 5-fold to nearly 16-fold) suggests the possible existence of a critical threshold (≥ 2 factors) beyond which risk escalates disproportionately, a finding with direct implications for screening and intervention triage. These findings suggest that multiple exposures cluster within the same individuals and that each additional risk amplifies the probability of behaviour through cumulative psychosocial pathways. Therefore, universities should abandon one-size-fits-all lectures and adopt tiered, multi-level strategies: sexual-health education that integrates skills-based condom training, trauma-informed counselling for students with CSA or same-sex experience, and peer-led programs that address permissive norms [10]. Such comprehensive approaches are likely to offset the combined impact of risk factors and, ultimately, curb the rising burden of HRSB among Chinese undergraduates.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and temporality assessment. Second, all measures were self-reported, which may introduce recall and social desirability bias; however, anonymous completion was used to minimize under-reporting. Third, the survey was restricted to one city (Luzhou) and to students aged ≥ 18 years, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, although the proportion of excluded questionnaires was low, the possibility of non-response bias cannot be completely ruled out. Fifth, our definition of HRSB was limited to three specific behaviors, and other potentially important behaviors were not assessed; moreover, despite the use of standardized instruments, measurement error and residual confounding due to unmeasured factors—including socioeconomic status, financial hardship, and social support networks—cannot be entirely excluded. Consequently, we could not examine whether these unmeasured factors, particularly economic pressures, may be stronger drivers of commercial sex than the psychosocial variables assessed in this study. Sixth, class-level and institution-level identifiers were not retained, precluding survey-weighted estimation or multilevel modeling; however, a design-effect–based sensitivity analysis (DEFF = 1.5–3.0) confirmed the robustness of our core findings. Future longitudinal, multicenter cohorts that integrate biological outcomes, broader socioeconomic measures, and cluster identifiers are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusions

This study documents an 11.94% past-year prevalence of high-risk sexual behavior among Chinese undergraduates in Luzhou and demonstrates a steep graded association between the number of accumulated risk factors and HRSB. Male sex, grade ≥ 4, non-medical major, same-sex sexual experience, permissive attitudes toward premarital sex, adequate sexual knowledge, other-relative caregivers, and childhood sexual abuse were identified as independent predictors. Notably, students with three or more risk factors had nearly 16-fold higher odds of HRSB compared to those with none. These findings underscore that HRSB is rarely associated with isolated exposures but rather emerges from the accumulation of individual, familial, and psychosocial risk factors.

Practice implications

Our findings highlight several actionable strategies for reducing HRSB among Chinese university students. First, given that one-third of students carried two or more cumulative risk factors, with risk escalating sharply beyond this threshold (5.01-fold higher odds for two factors and 15.66-fold for three or more), campus health services should integrate a brief “risk-cluster” checklist into routine health assessments to identify students with ≥ 2 risk factors for targeted follow-up. Second, the independent associations of modifiable factors—suggest that interventions should address these specific behavioral and psychological targets. Universities should prioritize skills-based sexual health education that promotes attitude change, condom-use rehearsal, and sexual negotiation skills, while students disclosing CSA or same-sex experiences should be referred to trauma-informed counselling. However, given that the specific drivers of commercial sex may differ (potentially involving economic hardship or lack of social support), campus services should also consider socioeconomic screening and referral pathways for students who may be engaged in or at risk of commercial sex. Multi-centre implementation trials are needed to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of this scalable intervention model in Chinese higher-education settings.
