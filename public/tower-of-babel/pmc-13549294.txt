Epidemic Comparison of Ebola and Zika—the Only Two Neglected Tropical Diseases with Vertical Transmission Potential and Associated with Public Health Emergency of International Concern—among Childbearing Women
Authors: Yaoyi Wu, Xuan Lian, Jinyuan Song, Mengying Fei, Ying Sun, Xiaopeng Yu, Yingfeng Lu, Yimin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComparative epidemiological evidence on Ebola virus disease (EVD) and Zika virus disease (ZVD) in women of childbearing age (WCBA) is limited, hindering understanding of their mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) potential and maternal–neonatal protection. This study aimed to compare EVD and ZVD epidemiology in WCBA, assess sex-specific differences, and examine the association between ZVD incidence and live births to inform maternal–neonatal protection and disease control.MethodsData on incidence, deaths, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) of EVD and ZVD (2011–2023) among WCBA and other populations were extracted from the Global Burden of Disease 2023 study. Cumulative cases, age-standardised incidence rates (ASIRs), and mortality-to-incidence ratios (MIRs) were calculated. Sex-specific differences and correlations between ZVD ASIRs and live births were assessed.ResultsDuring 2012–2022, WCBA generally experienced higher cumulative and annual EVD incidence than males aged 15–49 years in Sub-Saharan Africa; higher ZVD incidences were also observed among WCBA in Latin America and Caribbean, although no differences were significant. In main-endemic super-regions, EVD exhibited low incidence but extremely high fatality (MIR 0.39–0.70) in WCBA, with DALYs primarily driven by YLLs; whereas ZVD showed high incidence but low fatality (MIR 1.92 × 10−6–9.45 × 10−6), with DALYs mainly driven by YLDs. Two EVD outbreaks occurred amid low endemicity in Sub-Saharan Africa, while a ZVD outbreak occurred in 2016 during persistent endemicity in Latin America and Caribbean. ZVD ASIRs in WCBA were strongly correlated with corresponding live births in Latin America and Caribbean, Brazil (2011–2022), and constitute countries/territories and Brazilian states in 2016.ConclusionStrengthened EVD monitoring in Sub-Saharan Africa and a Latin America-Caribbean-centred strategy for ZVD prevention targeting WCBA may improve maternal-neonatal protection and control of neglected tropical diseases with MTCT potential.Keywords: Ebola virus disease, Zika virus disease, women of childbearing age, mother-to-child transmission, comparative analysis, global burden of disease study 2023

Introduction

Ebola virus disease (EVD) and Zika virus disease (ZVD) are two neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) categorized under the Neglected tropical diseases and malaria group within the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework,1 and are the only two diseases within this category to have been associated with public health emergencies of international concern (PHEICs) over the past decade.2 Between 2012 and 2023, four of the nine PHEICs declared by the World Health Organization (WHO) were attributable to EVD or ZVD.2 Both diseases have imposed substantial social and economic burdens, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, while poverty and resource constraints have further hindered effective outbreak control.3–5 Moreover, both diseases have been designated by the WHO Research and Development Blueprint as priority diseases with epidemic potential requiring accelerated research and development in emergency contexts.6 Most recently, in May 2026, the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) declared a new Ebola outbreak caused by Bundibugyo virus. The outbreak was subsequently designated by the WHO as a PHEIC on 17 May 2026,7 highlighting the persistent epidemic potential of Ebola virus and the challenges of outbreak control in resource-limited settings.

Moreover, as two PHEIC-associated diseases with established mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) potential, both EVD and ZVD are linked to severe adverse pregnancy outcomes.8–10 Maternal infection with EVD carries a high risk of maternal mortality, and nearly all pregnant women infected with EVD experience adverse pregnancy outcomes.8 Zika virus infection during pregnancy disproportionately affects the fetus and neonate rather than the mother.9 Vertical transmission of ZVD can result in serious adverse fetal and neonatal outcomes, with 20–30% of fetuses becoming infected, leading to pregnancy loss (4–7%), congenital Zika syndrome (CZS) (5–14%), and Zika virus-associated microcephaly (4–6%).10 Beyond adverse pregnancy outcomes, vertical transmission of EVD and ZVD can impose substantial public health and socioeconomic burdens through increased healthcare needs, long-term care, and impacts on affected families;3,11 notably, the societal cost per child with severe congenital CZS was US$50523 to 10 years of age (born in 2015 and 2016) in Brazil, substantially higher than the costs without CZS.11 Previous comparative epidemiological studies of MTCT diseases have largely focused on human immunodeficiency virus infection, syphilis, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C, helping to refine management strategies against MTCT diseases.12–15 However, comparative studies on EVD and ZVD—emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) with vertical transmission—remain absent.

Existing single-disease epidemiological studies have shown marked differences between EVD and ZVD in terms of transmission patterns, and maternal and child health outcomes.10,16 Since its initial identification, nearly all recorded EVD outbreaks have occurred in Sub-Saharan Africa.17,18 A previous GBD-based study reported that in 2021, most Zika virus infections among women of childbearing age (WCBA) were clustered in Latin America and Caribbean; however, it did not conduct an in-depth analysis of the main-endemic super-regions.19 As the main-endemic regions for EVD and ZVD, respectively, these areas possess relatively robust epidemiological data.10,16 Focusing on these regions in the present analysis allows for a clearer characterization of disease patterns while minimizing bias from marginally affected settings.

The GBD 2023 study provides a platform for multilevel comparative epidemiological analyses of these two EIDs among WCBA, the advantage of focusing on their main-endemic super-region thereby addressing gaps in the epidemiology of MTCT diseases and offering valuable insights for maternal and child health policies and preventive strategies. Furthermore, comparative analyses of the only two NTDs, both with MTCT potential and a history of multiple PHEICs, may provide important lessons for maternal and child protection, ultimately informing strategies to confine future “Disease X” threats to their points of emergence before escalating into global crises.16,20

Material and Methods

Methods related to GBD 2023 are detailed in capstone articles.1,21,22 Briefly, GBD integrates diverse data sources, including surveillance systems, vital registration, and published studies, and applies standardized modelling frameworks to generate internally consistent estimates by age, sex, location, and year. These estimates are derived using Bayesian meta-regression approaches and propagate uncertainty through posterior sampling. Here, we describe methods and assumptions relevant to this secondary data analysis.

Data Sources

We accessed data from the IHME Global Health Data Exchange for EVD as absolute counts and rates per 100000 population for incidence, deaths, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) for female and male aged 15–49, live births from 2011 to 2023 across countries and territories in Sub-Sahara Africa. Corresponding data for ZVD were accessed across countries and territories in Latin America and Caribbean. Particularly, incidence numbers of ZVD for WCBA and live births from 2011 to 2023 across states in Brazil were also accessed. According to GBD 2023 data use policy, this study followed the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement (Table S1), with all data input sources and statistical codes available online.23–25

In GBD 2023 estimates, cause-specific mortality was estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble model, while non-fatal outcomes were estimated using DisMod-MR 2.1, a Bayesian meta-regression tool used to ensure consistency between incidence, prevalence, remission, and mortality estimates across age, sex, location, and year. Spatiotemporal regression approaches and covariate-based modeling were additionally applied in settings with sparse or heterogeneous data. For EVD, estimates were mainly derived from outbreak reports and surveillance summaries, with adjustments for under-reporting and redistribution of age-sex patterns when detailed data were unavailable. For ZVD, surveillance-based statistical models incorporating environmental and health system covariates were used to estimate transmission patterns, and expansion factors were applied to account for under-ascertainment. Additional modeling was used to estimate infections among pregnant women and congenital outcomes associated with Zika virus infection. We analysed incidence, YLLs, YLDs, and DALYs as defined in the GBD framework. YLLs were calculated as the number of deaths multiplied by the standard life expectancy at the age of death, while YLDs were estimated as the prevalence of sequelae multiplied by corresponding disability weights. DALYs were calculated as the sum of YLLs and YLDs.

Analysis and Reporting

Data were reported as absolute numbers, rates (per 100000) and age-standardised rates (ASRs) of metrics. Estimates were reported by GBD super-regions, GBD regions, and across countries and territories (Table S2). EVD is predominantly endemic in Sub-Saharan Africa, whereas ZVD mainly affects the Latin America and Caribbean; therefore, in this study, the main-endemic super-region refers to Sub-Saharan Africa for EVD and to Latin America and Caribbean for ZVD. Since no data were available for WCBA for EVD in 2011 and ZVD in 2023, the comparative analysis of EVD and ZVD was conducted for the period 2012–2022. The cumulative burden was calculated by summing the annual number of new cases and deaths over the study period. We reported the mortality-to- incidence ratio (MIR) for EVD and ZVD as a measure of disease severity and health system response. MIR was calculated by dividing the ASR of cause-specific deaths by the ASR of incidence (ASIR) for a given year.26 This study examined the correlation between ZVD ASIR among WCBA and live births lagged by one year, both for Latin America and Caribbean and Brazil (2011–2022), as well as countries/territories in this super-region and states in Brazil in 2016. Assuming a nine-month gestational period, for each year t, the corresponding live birth ASIR was calculated as a weighted average of 0.25 × the ASIR in year t plus 0.75 × the ASIR in year t+1.

Data were analysed and visualizations prepared in RStudio 4.3.2.

Results

Incidences of EVD and ZVD Among WCBA Compared with Males Aged 15-49 in Main-Endemic Super-Regions and Constituent Regions

The cumulative cases of EVD among WCBA in sub-Saharan Africa were significantly higher than those in men aged 15–49 years over the 2012–2022 period. By 2022, the female-to-male ratios had reached 1.23 (1.03 to 1.47) for cumulative cases (22910 (19972 to 25759) vs 18690 (16262 to 20985)) in EVD (Figure 1A and Table S3). Although cumulative ZVD cases among WCBA were also higher than those among men aged 15–49 years, the difference was not statistically significant (4831711 (1998964 to 12284729) vs 3083337 (1186478 to 7833399); female-to-male ratio: 1.57 (0.34 to 7.13)) (Figure 1B and Table S3). During this observation period, the annual number of EVD cases among WCBA in their main endemic super-regions was higher than that among men aged 15–49 years in 2012–2015 and 2020–2022 (Figure 1C and Table S4). In contrast, no clear sex-specific differences in annual ZVD case numbers were observed throughout the observation period (Figure 1D and Table S4). However, ASIRs of both diseases in their main endemic super-regions showed only limited sex-specific differences between WCBA and men aged 15–49 years throughout the study period (Table S5).

This pattern remained consistent across regions within main-endemic super-regions with reported cases, where cumulative and annual case numbers of EVD and ZVD among WCBA were generally higher than those in men aged 15–49 years, although some differences were not statistically significant (Figures S1–S4, Tables S6 and S7).

Main-Endemic Super-Region and Region-Level Comparison of EVD and ZVD Burden Among WCBA

Between 2012 and 2022, EVD cases among WCBA in Sub-Saharan Africa ranged from 1 (1 to 2) in 2013 to 14312 (12492 to 16093) in 2014, with two peaks observed in 2014 and 2019 (1827 (1587 to 2069) cases) (Figure 1C and Table S4). During the same period, ZVD cases in Latin America and Caribbean among WCBA ranged from 40011 (14705 to 93065) in 2022 to 3228596 (1363395 to 8343108) in 2016 (Figure 1D and Table S4).

In Sub-Saharan Africa, the 2014 EVD outbreak was concentrated in Western Sub-Saharan Africa, whereas the 2019 outbreak mainly occurred in Central Sub-Saharan Africa, with Southern Sub-Saharan Africa being the only constituent region without any reported EVD cases (Figure S3). In contrast, all four constituent regions of Latin America and Caribbean experienced ZVD outbreaks, with only Andean Latin America peaking in 2017, while the other regions peaked synchronously with the super-regional trend (Figure S4).

From 2012 to 2022, the MIR of EVD among WCBA in the Sub-Saharan African fluctuated from 0.39 (0.28 to 0.53) in 2014 to 0.70 (0.51 to 0.96) in 2019, while that of ZVD among WCBA in Latin America and Caribbean peaked in 2015 (9.45×10−6 (5.39×10−7 to 7.33×10−5)), varied in 2016–2017, and then stabilised at 1.94×10−6 (3.75×10−7 to 1.07×10−5)–2.01×10−6 (3.56×10−7 to 1.30×10−5) (Figure 2A, B and Table S8). Analyses of DALY composition across the main-endemic super-regions showed that DALYs for both EVD and ZVD were largely driven by YLLs, except for ZVD among WCBA in the Americas, where DALYs were primarily attributable to YLDs (Figure 2C and D).

Country- and Territory-Level Comparison of EVD and ZVD Burden in WCBA

In main-endemic super-regions, between 2012 and 2022, the highest cumulative numbers of EVD cases among women aged 15–49 years were reported in three countries in Western Sub-Saharan Africa—Sierra Leone (10067 (8732 to 11355) cases), Liberia (7522 (6568 to 8419)), and Guinea (2639 (2294 to 2983))—followed by the DRC (2543 (2211 to 2873)) in Central Sub-Saharan Africa (Table 1). Together, these four countries accounted for 99.398% (99.388% to 99.403%) of the cumulative cases among WCBA in Sub-Saharan Africa (Table 1). During the same period, cumulative ZVD cases among WCBA in Latin America and Caribbean were highest in Brazil (1396432 (582189 to 3280920) cases), followed by Colombia (695443 (70227 to 2707544)), Venezuela (449083 (45545 to 1893710)), and Guatemala (348347 (16381 to 1851675)). These four countries together represented 60.037% (55.184% to 74.311%) of cumulative cases among WCBA in Latin America and Caribbean (Table 1). During epidemic periods over the past decade, the ASIR of EVD among WCBA in Sub-Saharan Africa countries was generally much lower than that of ZVD in Latin America and Caribbean. The highest EVD ASIR was 561.67 (489.87 to 631.38) per 100000 in Liberia in 2014, whereas the highest ZVD ASIR reached 22082.86 (2695.24 to 81320.58) per 100000 in Jamaica in 2016 (Figure 3). In 2014, EVD among WCBA was concentrated in Western Sub-Saharan Africa, with the highest ASIR observed in Liberia, Sierra Leone (425.43 (369.02 to 479.88) per 100000), and Guinea (81.72 (70.82 to 92.45) per 100000), and then shifted to Central Sub-Saharan Africa in 2019 with cases in the DRC (10.85 (9.38 to 12.30) per 100000) and Uganda (3.6×10−2 (2.9×10−2 to 4.2×10−2)) per 100000) (Figures 3 and S5). In 2016, nearly all countries in Latin America and the Caribbean reached their peak incidence, and the outbreak displayed asynchronous epidemic dynamics across the super-region (Figure 4). In 2014, the largest number of EVD incident cases among WCBA were reported in Sierra Leone (6726 (7582–5839) cases) and Liberia (5659 (6339–4938)), followed by Guinea (1868 (2107–1632)), reflecting the concentration of the West African Ebola epidemic (Figure S6 and Table S9). By 2019, the epidemic burden had shifted to Central Sub-Saharan Africa, with the DRC reporting the highest number of incident cases (1825 (2067–1585)) (Figure S6 and Table S9). In outbreak years, the MIRs of EVD among WCBA of countries and territories in Sub-Saharan Africa were markedly higher than those of ZVD in Latin America and Caribbean (0.29 (0.21 to 0.40)–0.99 (0.69 to 1.41) vs 1.61×10−6 (4.13×10−8 to 6.47×10−5)–1.59×10−5 (3.55×10−7 to 5.71×10−4)). Notably, in 2016, ZVD MIRs were particularly elevated in Puerto Rico (1.59×10−5 (3.55×10−7 to 5.71×10−4) and Brazil (4.61×10−6(4.04×10−7 to 3.23×10−5)) (Figure 3).

Correlation Between ZVD ASIR Among WCBA and Live Births from Temporal and Geographical Perspectives

Furthermore, we examined the correlation between ZVD ASIR in WCBA and live births from two perspectives: timeline analysis in Latin America and Caribbean and Brazil (2011–2022), as well as outbreak-year analysis across countries/territories in this super-region and states in Brazil. From 2011 to 2022, ZVD ASIRs among WCBA in Latin America and Caribbean, and Brazil were significantly correlated with live births lagged by one year (adjusted R2 = 0.703 and 0.695, both P<0.001) (Figure S7A and B). During the 2016 epidemic peak, ZVD ASIR among WCBA in countries/territories in this super-region and states in Brazil showed an even stronger correlation with the ASIR of ZVD in live births lagged by one year (adjusted R2 = 0.984 and 0.999, both P<0.001) (Figure S7C and D).

When accounting for a nine-month gestational period, correlations with corresponding live births became stronger, with higher R2 values. From 2011 to 2022, ZVD ASIRs among WCBA in Latin America and Caribbean, and Brazil were significantly correlated with corresponding live births lagged by one year (adjusted R2 = 0.752 and 0.750, both P<0.001) (Figure 5A and B). Additionally, Figure S8 presents the correlations between ZVD ASIRs among WCBA and corresponding live births (lagged by one year) in other countries in Latin America and Caribbean during 2011–2022 (adjusted R2 = 0.676–0.897, all P<0.001). In 2016, ZVD ASIRs among WCBA in countries/territories in this super-region and states in Brazil showed a much stronger correlation with the ASIR of ZVD in corresponding live births lagged by one year (adjusted R2 = 0.988 and 0.999, both P<0.001) (Figure 5C and D).

Discussion

EVD and ZVD were EIDs first identified in Africa, and are also the only two NTDs with confirmed evidence of MTCT within the GBD framework, yet exhibit markedly different characteristics.1,8,9 Furthermore, both diseases have at times been declared PHEICs by the WHO.2,16,20 Increasing attention has been directed toward studies of these two diseases in WCBA, particularly with regard to maternal–neonatal protection.27–30 The ongoing 2026 Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in the DRC underscores the persistent epidemic potential of EVD. By 31 May 2026, a total of 349 suspected cases, 272 confirmed cases, and 43 deaths had been reported, with a high female proportion of suspected cases between 20 and 39 years old, underscoring the continued need for vigilance, particularly regarding transmission risks among women.7,31,32

Previous studies based on global burden data have predominantly characterised the overall epidemiological patterns of EVD and ZVD on a global scale.19,33 In addition, both global burden studies and cohort-based investigations have largely examined EVD and ZVD separately, with limited efforts to conduct comparative analyses between the two diseases.34,35 In contrast, our study focused on main-endemic super-regions and specific outbreak periods, during which the disease burden was highly geographically and temporally concentrated. For example, Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for over 99% of global EVD incident cases during the 2014 outbreak and 100% in 2019, while Latin America and Caribbean contributed more than 98% of global ZVD incident cases during the 2016 outbreak. This approach enables a more refined characterisation of spatiotemporal patterns that may not be captured in global-level analyses. Moreover, by jointly analysing EVD and ZVD, we further delineate their distinct regional distributions and differential impacts among WCBA, thereby providing evidence to inform more targeted and context-specific prevention strategies.

Previous studies on the incidence characteristics of EVD across sexes, particularly among individuals aged 15–49 years, have yielded inconsistent results, likely due to limitations in study populations and geographic scope.36,37 However, this study provides robust evidence that WCBA constitutes a priority population for both diseases, supported consistently at both the main-endemic super-regional level and within their constituent regions. We compared sex differences in incident cases and ASIR among individuals aged 15–49 years. For EVD, WCBA exhibited significantly higher cumulative and annual incident cases than males aged 15–49 years, whereas sex-specific differences in ASIRs were substantially attenuated after age standardisation. Nevertheless, this attenuation does not diminish the epidemiological importance of WCBA as a priority population. From a public health perspective, the persistently higher absolute case counts among WCBA indicate a substantial disease burden, as they directly reflect the population requiring prevention, surveillance, and healthcare resources.

For ZVD, females consistently showed higher point estimates of disease burden than males across cumulative, annual, and age-standardised analyses. However, these differences were accompanied by exceptionally wide uncertainty intervals (UIs), likely reflecting the high proportion of asymptomatic infections, underreporting, and model-based adjustments within the GBD framework.21 As a result, substantial uncertainty may have obscured potentially meaningful sex-specific differences, and the absence of statistical significance should therefore be interpreted cautiously. More precise epidemiological surveillance and sex-disaggregated data are needed to better characterize the true burden of ZVD among WCBA. Supporting evidence indicates that among the sexually active age group (15–65 years), the number of registered ZVD cases per 100000 women was 90% higher than that among men,38 a trend consistent with our findings. This sex disparity may be explained by the greater exposure of women to domestic mosquito vectors and the higher probability of male-to-female transmission.38

Furthermore, this study focused on WCBA in the main-endemic super-regions, and their constituent countries/territories, as well as on peri-epidemic years, to characterise the geographic distribution, outbreak dynamics, DALYs composition of the two diseases, and the correlation between ZVD ASIR in WCBA and corresponding live births. The MIR was further applied to describe the relative relationship between mortality and incidence at the population level across diseases and locations. These innovative analyses, tailored to the non-global epidemic patterns of these two NTDs, clearly delineated their distinct epidemic features among WCBA and provided novel, high-quality evidence to inform disease-specific public health interventions targeting maternal–neonatal protection.

Selecting high-burden regions and key peri-epidemic years for analysis not only clarified disease characteristics but also reduced potential interference from marginal data. Moreover, correlation analyses across multiple dimensions—timeline analyses in Latin America and Caribbean and Brazil, as well as outbreak-year analyses across countries/territories in this super-region and states in Brazil—highlighted the generalisability and robustness of the findings. Brazil was chosen as the representative country because it recorded the highest cumulative cases and deaths from 2012 to 2022; and Brazil served as the epicenter of the 2015–2016 Zika virus epidemic and provided the earliest epidemiological evidence linking Zika virus infection to microcephaly, which contributed substantially to the WHO declaration of a PHEIC.10 Given that deaths from both diseases occurred within a relatively short period after symptom onset (for EVD, 88% within the first two weeks after symptom onset,39 and for the very few documented fatal cases of ZVD, within 48 hours to 12 days),40 MIR may serve as a pragmatic indicator for population-level fatality burden. Compared with the case fatality ratio derived from directly reported cases and deaths, MIR based on GBD modeled estimates may be less affected by variations in case ascertainment and surveillance capacity across settings.16,41

Our study indicated that the outbreak patterns of both diseases in WCBA were generally consistent with trends previously reported in the overall population, yet significant differences existed between the two diseases.10,16 The findings highlight that surveillance mechanisms across countries/territories should be established according to the epidemic characteristics of each disease, with a regional focus and WCBA as the core target population, in order to reduce infection rates among WCBA.

Specifically, for EVD, WCBA infections persisted between 2012 and 2022, with epidemic trends exhibiting pulse-like outbreaks on a background of low endemicity. The two major outbreaks in 2014 and 2019 were concentrated in Western and Central Sub-Saharan Africa, respectively, with multiple epidemics reported in the DRC and Uganda (Tables S9 and S10), consistent with patterns observed in all age groups.42,43 These findings underscore the need for continuous EVD control efforts targeting WCBA in Sub-Saharan Africa, including cross-border surveillance in high-risk countries/territories. In contrast, ZVD infections among WCBA experienced a widespread, large-scale outbreak in the Latin America and Caribbean in 2016 (Tables S11 and S12), occurring against a background of sustained endemic human-to-human transmission.20 This pattern aligns with that observed in the overall population.44 These findings highlight that ZVD prevention should focus on Latin America and Caribbean, establishing surveillance systems that prioritise pregnant women and WCBA in this super-region with widespread Aedes mosquito distribution.45,46

Given the absence of effective measures to block MTCT for both diseases, and based on previous studies as well as the present findings, we propose that efforts to prevent infant infections should focus on reducing infection rates among WCBA. For EVD, prior research has shown that foetuses with congenital infection rarely survive.8 Consistent with this, GBD 2023 data indicate that between 2012 and 2022, EVD cases in Sub-Saharan Africa during the perinatal period (0–6 days) were zero (no results for live births).47,48 For ZVD, we calculated the ASIR of ZVD in WCBA and corresponding live births, adjusting the latter by a nine-month gestational period to more accurately reflect the epidemiological link between maternal infection and live births infection. Correlation analyses conducted across two dimensions demonstrated strong correlations between ZVD ASIR in WCBA and in live births (adjusted R2 ranged from 0.676 to 0.897 in timeline analyses, adjusted R2 = 0.988 and 0.999 in outbreak-year analyses), highlighting the critical role of protecting WCBA in reducing the burden of congenital infections caused by Zika virus.

In the context of these correlation analyses, the relatively weaker correlations observed over the longer period (Figure 5A and B), compared with those during the peak outbreak in 2016 (Figure 5C and D), may be explained by both methodological and biological factors. On the one hand, the long-term analyses incorporate data from multiple countries and time points, and are further complicated by asynchronous outbreak dynamics across regions, which may introduce greater heterogeneity and potential confounding, thereby attenuating the observed correlations.49 In contrast, data from the 2016 outbreak are more temporally and geographically concentrated, potentially providing a more consistent and less heterogeneous epidemiological signal. However, the weaker correlations over time may also partly reflect increasing population immunity following widespread transmission. Accumulated immunity, particularly among women infected before pregnancy, may reduce the likelihood or severity of secondary prenatal infection and subsequent vertical transmission.50,51

Furthermore, our study revealed asynchronous outbreaks within countries and regions of Latin America and Caribbean. For example, ZVD peaks occurred in 2017 in Peru, Paraguay, and Bolivia, located southwest of Brazil, reflecting a lag in epidemic peaks across the region. Previous studies suggest that this may be due to the virus spreading from the northeastern coast of Brazil to the western borders over approximately five to six months.52 Such asynchronous outbreaks underscore the importance of early warning in source countries and coordinated preventive measures, such as The Integrated Arbo viral Disease Prevention Strategies (IMS-arbovirus), in other countries to reduce WCBA infections,53 thereby relieving MTCT-related neonatal infections and adverse outcomes.

The findings of this study suggest tailored public health measures to mitigate maternal and neonatal harm from these two diseases: early intervention and clinical management for EVD, and long-term vector control and antenatal care for ZVD. Given the zoonotic and vector-borne nature of these diseases, respectively, a One Health approach is also essential for integrating human, animal, and environmental health considerations into their prevention and control. Such an approach emphasises cross-sectoral surveillance, early detection, coordinated outbreak response, and multisectoral collaboration.54,55

In detail, for EVD, the primary goal is to reduce infection and mortality among pregnant women. In the main-endemic super-region, EVD incidence in WCBA is relatively low but MIR is high, with DALYs largely driven by YLLs, underscoring the need for prompt emergency response and clinical interventions to prevent premature deaths. Furthermore, analyses focusing on outbreak years in the main-endemic super-region indicate that, although the number of WCBA EVD cases in Sub-Saharan Africa was much lower in 2019 than in 2014 (1827 (1587 to 2069) vs 14312 (12492 to 16093)), the MIR reached its highest value of 0.70 (0.51 to 0.96) in 2019 compared with 0.39 (0.28 to 0.53) in 2014. The most likely explanation is that in 2019, regional security challenges hindered the implementation of international support and national response measures.56 Large-scale international medical assistance, by expanding treatment capacity, standardising care, and providing specific therapies, can substantially reduce EVD case fatality.56,57 The differences in MIR between the two outbreak years underscore the critical role of international aid and supported national response measures.

In contrast, for ZVD, our study shows that DALYs in WCBA are primarily driven by YLDs, with a relatively low MIR. Given that deaths due to ZVD prior to the WHO declaration of PHEIC may not have been widely recognised and could have been underreported,58 the MIR data from 2012 to 2014 were not included in our analysis. In Latin America and Caribbean, the highest MIR among WCBA occurred in 2015, followed by minor fluctuations during 2016–2018, after which MIR remained stably low (1.92×10−6–2.01×10−6), likely reflecting enhanced surveillance, diagnosis, and antenatal management of pregnant women through international and national collaboration.10 Combined with prior evidence that adverse fetal outcomes of maternal ZVD infection far outweigh maternal morbidity,9 these findings indicate that maternal–neonatal protection for ZVD should prioritise the long-term health and socio-economic burden of pregnant women and newborns, through sustained investment in IMS-arbovirus,53 antenatal care, and personalised counselling within maternal and child health systems.

Our study has several limitations. First, the analyses were based on GBD 2023 modelled estimates with annual temporal resolution, lacking finer temporal resolution (eg, monthly) to capture the epidemiological dynamics during the early and later stages of acute infectious disease outbreaks. In addition, some age- and sex-specific estimates were generated through statistical redistribution and modelling of aggregated data, which may introduce additional uncertainty and bias. Second, the study mainly focused on major endemic regions with a high disease burden rather than all countries and territories globally, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Third, the analyses were conducted among WCBA rather than confirmed pregnant women, and therefore may not fully reflect the epidemiological characteristics and risks in pregnant populations.21,59

Building on these comparative findings, future studies should incorporate higher-resolution data among pregnant women to better characterise the temporal dynamics and risks of MTCT. Further research should also evaluate targeted maternal–neonatal prevention and surveillance strategies tailored to the distinct epidemiological patterns of EVD and ZVD.

Conclusions

In brief, our study highlights WCBA as a key population requiring particular attention to both EVD and ZVD due to their MTCT potential. We further revealed the distinct DALY structure, geographic distribution, and outbreak patterns of the two NTDs in this population. The findings underscore the need to prioritise these often-overlooked EIDs and to implement interventions tailored to their unique epidemiology and MTCT features. Specifically, this calls for strengthening EVD monitoring targeting WCBA in Sub-Saharan African with rapid international support response, alongside Latin America and Caribbean-centred IMS-Arbovirus control and ZVD surveillance in WCBA, thereby enhancing maternal and neonatal protection against these NTDs.
