Tunable Molecular Photonic Wires via Geometric Control of Excitonically Coupled Cyanine Dimer Relays
Authors: Adam A. Meares, Sara R. Ansteatt, Paul D. Cunningham, Drew Lysne, Igor L. Medintz, Smriti Thomas, Victoria Segal, Aidan C. Malloy, Thi Hai Yen Pham, Patrick M. Vora, Joseph S. Melinger, Sebastián A. Díaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The assembly of molecular photonic wires (MPWs) on DNA scaffolds offers a powerful platform for controlling nanoscale energy transfer. This work demonstrates how the geometric configuration of an excitonic relay, composed of a cyanine (Cy5) dye dimer, regulates energy flow within an MPW. Exploiting linker chemistry, either H-type or J-type aggregates are selectively formed at room temperature. H-type dimers act as energy transfer inhibitors, while J-type dimers function as effective energy relays. In an optimized architecture, J-dimer MPWs outperform equivalent systems using monomeric relays. This performance is significantly amplified upon transitioning the system from solution to solid-state films, where the energy transfer efficiency of J-dimer wires is enhanced by up to 300% relative to monomeric versions. Experimental results also support approximating the dimers as single-point dipoles for Förster resonance energy transfer considerations. These results establish a robust strategy for engineering the optical properties of molecular materials, where nanoscale energy transport is precisely directed by controlling the geometry of excitonic aggregates.

Introduction

Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) is the phenomenon by which a donor chromophore, in its excited state, can transfer energy to a compatible acceptor chromophore via dipole–dipole interactions over relatively short distances (1–10 nm). , FRET-based systems present myriad applications, ranging from covalently linked multichromophore arrays, − quantum dot to dye FRET relays, − or the widely known molecular beacons , to name a few. For additional examples of FRET centric materials developed over the last several decades, we direct the reader toward a recent review. Within the context of DNA, FRET was first employed as a molecular ruler, useful for determining the configuration of four-armed Holliday Junctions (HJs) or observing the rotation within double-stranded (ds) DNA. Currently, DNA scaffolded arrays are of high interest because DNA duplexes, along with larger structures like HJ or DNA origami, , enable placement with subnanometer precision and high fidelity. Greater precision can in turn facilitate energy transfer over longer distances, allowing the construction of systems intended for long-range, multistep energy transfer, termed molecular photonic wires (MPWs). − Many approaches have been taken toward constructing and elucidating structure–property relationships of MPWs on DNA. Sanborn and Kretschy observed that the local DNA domains could strongly influence system properties. Furthermore, Hirashima et al., using fluorescent nucleosides, found that energy transfer was minimal when donor–acceptor pairs were separated by 4 and 9 basepairs (bp). This was due to misalignment of transition dipole moments, brought about by the natural rotation of dsDNA, which leads to a low orientational factor, κ2, and correspondingly low FRET efficiency consistent with Förster theory. Though we note that this can be overcome if the lifetime of the emitter is long enough to survey all conformations, as in the case of long-lived lanthanides. Examples of attempted chemical control include a broad variety of dyes incorporated either during DNA synthesis via 2-point insertion through phosphodiester precursors, − or postsynthetic labeling, , and have been employed on all manner of DNA structures from dsDNA to DNA origami. , Overall, the performance of MPWs has been limited by nonideal orientation factors between the dyes engaged in FRET as well as suboptimal absorption and emission properties that limit step efficiencies to ≤90%, which ultimately limits the length of efficient transport.

In separate, but related research, significant efforts have gone into the construction and control of discrete dye dimers within DNA scaffolds, inspired by the potential of molecular logic gates. , The properties of dye dimers (or aggregates) are heavily dictated by the coupling strength (J), center-to-center distance, and mutual orientation of the transition dipole moments (TDMs). In all dimers, hybridization leads to a splitting of the excited state that is proportional to J, where one possible transition has higher energy than that of the parent monomer, and the other has lower energy. Mutual orientation is the most important factor in determining which transitions are allowed and thus the photophysical properties. In H-type dimers, the dyes are stacked parallel (cofacially) to one another and the higher-energy transition is permitted, resulting in a characteristic blue (hypsochromic) shift to absorption and a reduced radiative rate and fluorescence quantum yield (ΦF). , The formation of H-dimers is dominated by the van der Waals and π–π stacking interactions, and the hydrophobic character of dye molecules. In J-type dimers, the dyes are aligned head to tail, and the lower-energy transition is allowed, resulting in red-shifted absorption and an increase in the radiative rate and ΦF. , Oblique dimers cover other dimers that cannot be neatly categorized into H- or J-type and manifest Davydov splitting; , they display both higher- and lower-energy transitions (see Figure C). Oblique dimers are by far the most common, as perfect stacking (particularly to afford J-dimers) is challenging to achieve. The strong absorption and emission properties of J-dimers should favor efficient FRET, and κ2 reaches its theoretical maximum for FRET between two aligned J-dimers, making them intriguing candidates for MPWs.

Over the past decade or so, significant strides have been made in understanding how to control the dimer type within DNA structures, focused primarily around indocyanines (Cy3 and Cy5) that are directly inserted into the DNA backbone through 2-point phosphodiester linkages (attachment demonstrated in Figure A). This includes fundamental studies that first demonstrated the ability to prepare zero base pair (0bp) separated dimers on dsDNA using Cy3, probing distance dependence of coupling strength for Cy3 in dsDNA measured by bp separation, as well as the effect neighboring nucleobases have on dimer geometry, and the observation of 0bp heterodimers, when coupling was seen between Cy3 and Cy5. Closer examination of Cy3 and Cy5 homodimers in dsDNA determined that the 0bp dimers are capable of sequential absorption, forming molecular biexcitons (potentially valuable as AND gates), and by increasing salt concentrations, the J-like 0bp dimers can be converted to the so-called “mobile tetramers”, which behave like larger H-type tetramers centered within closely associated DNA duplexes. For more precise control over placement and orientation, Cy5 dimers, trimers, and tetramers were characterized when placed at the core of Holliday Junctions (HJs). Here, it was determined that adjacent dimers (Cy5s that are located on neighboring sequences and are partially hybridized to one another) form oblique dimers with slight favoring of J-like characteristics, while transverse dimers (Cy5s that are located on sequences that do not participate in hybridization with each other) favor H-like configuration (see Figure B for visualization of adjacent vs transverse dimers on HJ). However, in all cases, heterogeneity was observed, with subensembles of multiple dye packing geometries. Out of Cannon’s observations, , a hypothesis was formed that by tuning physicochemical properties of the dyes, further control over the dimer geometry could be afforded. To this end, a series of 5,5′-disubstituted (-tBu, -Cl, -hexyloxy, and -PEG) indodicarbocyanines was prepared with differing hydrophobicities and electronic properties and then incorporated into HJs. Studying the various permutations of homo- and heterodimers provided valuable insight into the relationship between hydrophobicity, π-stacking interactions, and orientation, as well as the ability to disrupt (or increase) that through steric bulk or mismatching substituent sizes. Similar results were afforded by Cy5 and Cy5.5 homo- and heterodimers in HJs. , While relatively homogeneous H-dimers were observed in a few cases, homogeneous J-like packing remained elusive. A key development was achieved when linkers of the parent Cy5 were modified to either 2C (n′ = 2) or 4C (n′ = 4) in length (from commercial standard of 3C) to understand how more or less freedom of movement would dictate dimer properties. , Importantly, it was discovered that for adjacent HJ dimers, 2C-linker restricts conformational variability and favors a nearly 100% J-like configuration, while 4C-linker provides more freedom, and favor H-like configuration (dominated by π-stacking) in all scenarios. The ability to reliably generate J-like aggregates is a crucial component to the present work, where we utilize dimers as a FRET intermediary (i.e., dimers first serving as an energy acceptor and then subsequent donor to a terminal dye).

Previously, we attempted to utilize cyanine dimers with 3C linkers as a component in MPWs to improve the properties of the central relay. , In both cases, room-temperature (RT) energy transfer was less efficient than for the control scaffolds that utilized monomeric dyes. This was attributed to an increased level of nonradiative relaxation, which was suppressed upon cooling. At the time, we posited that even though the dimers were in J-like oblique orientation, there was significant enough H-character to encourage nonradiative relaxation.

Herein, we redress the limitations of previous work by using short dye linkers to improve the efficiency of MPWs that consist mainly of Cy5 J-dimers as the intermediary relay. The short linker effectively eliminates the subpopulation of H-like dimers, which are less effective FRET donors than J-like dimers. We demonstrate this effect by comparing the properties of more optimal MPWs containing J-like dimer relays against analogous MPWs with central H-dimers and controls that utilized monomeric Cy5 in lieu of either dimer type. For the first generation of these MPWs, we examined all permutations in which each of the four strands contained a single dye. The MPW with the greatest energy transfer efficiency was then selected for optimization, where the HJ was modified for tailored energy transfer steps and a more linear arrangement of all components’ TDMs. In this system, the J-dimer containing MPW outperformed control systems with Cy5 monomers used as the central relay. Finally, select MPWs were deployed as films onto glass surfaces by either drop-casting or spin coating, and resultant properties were compared against solution phase MPWs.

Materials and Methods

Chemical Dye Synthesis, DNA Synthesis, and Purification

Commercial DNA (unlabeled, Atto590, Cy5.5, and 3′ linker Cy5-labeled strands) was obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies and used without further purification. 2′ and 4′ linker Cy5-labeled strands were produced, purified, and characterized as detailed in previous work. ,

DNA Assembly

DNA HJs were prepared from equal volumes of the 100 μΜ desired strand stock solutions, combined and diluted to a final concentration of 10 μM of DNA HJ nanostructures in 1 x TAE (40 mM tris­(hydroxymethyl)­aminomethane, 20 mM acetic acid, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 8.0) buffer, and 15 mM MgCl2. Once combined, samples were subjected to thermal annealing in a PCR thermal cycler. Annealing followed a program that maintained the lid temperature at 95 °C constantly, began with a melt at 95 °C for 5 min, cooled to 85 °C at a rate of 5 min/1 °C drop, held at 85 °C for 10 min, then cooled to 4 °C at a cooling rate of 10 min/1 °C drop. , Samples were held at 4 °C or stored in the refrigerator in the dark until they were removed for experiments without further purification. All chemicals utilized for buffers were purchased from either Fisher Scientific (Hanover Park, IL) or Millipore-Sigma (Burlington, MA).

Film Deposition

For absorbance and fluorescence lifetime spectroscopy, samples were spin-coated (10 s at 2500 rpm, then 15 s at 3500 rpm) as is in the original buffer alone or with a 1% w/v poly­(vinyl alcohol) (poly vinyl alcohol (PVA), M w 31 000–50 000, Millipore-Sigma) addition on transparent glass cover slides realizing 3 additions of 15 μL at 12.25 μM of the HJ DNA. For microscopy data, the samples were spin-coated on plasma-cleaned SiO2/Si substrates with a 90 nm oxide thickness (University Wafer Inc., South Boston, MA). For buffer only samples, spin-coating was performed by adding 6 μL of 10 μM of HJ DNA solution to the SiO2/Si substrate and then spinning the sample for 10 s at 2500 rpm (1000 rpm/s ramp) followed by 15 s at 3500 rpm (1000 rpm/s ramp). Samples containing PVA were prepared by adding 8 μL of 360 nM of HJ DNA solution to the SiO2/Si substrate and then spinning the sample for 10 s at 1000 rpm (500 rpm/s ramp) followed by 20 s at 3000 rpm (1000 rpm/s ramp).

Spectroscopy

Absorption spectra were measured at RT (20 ± 1 °C) using a Cary 60 UV–vis (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA), in the range of 200–850 nm, with 1 nm intervals and a 0.1s integration time. In all instances, a quartz spectrophotometer cell with a 1 cm path length was used; the sample concentration was 2 μM.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were measured at RT (20 ± 1 °C) using a J-1500 CD spectrophotometer (Jasco Corp., Tokyo, JPN) in the range of 200–800 nm, with 1 nm intervals at a rate of 100 nm/min and a 4 s integration time. In all instances, a quartz spectrophotometer cell with a 1 cm path length was used; the sample concentration was 2 μM.

Fluorescence spectra for all dye-labeled DNA and HJ assemblies were acquired on a Spark microplate reader (Tecan Group, Ltd., Männedorf, CH) at RT (20 ± 1 °C), with the excitation source above the plate. Excitation wavelengths of 565 and 615 nm were used, and fluorescence was recorded with 1 nm intervals, 50 flashes per nm, and an integration time of 3 ms. For each sample, 100 μL of a 1 μM solution was added to a well in a 96-well black microtiter plate, resulting in a 1.25 mm path length and a maximum OD of 0.05. The obtained fluorescence spectra were corrected for wavelength-dependent instrumental sensitivity.

Fluorescence quantum yields (ΦF) for Atto590 and Cy5.5 were determined at RT (20 ± 1 °C) using a FluoroMax-Plus spectrofluorometer (Horiba Scientific, Irvine, CA). The Cy5.5 value was determined against the 5,10,15,20-tetraphenylporphyrin (TPP) standard (ΦF = 0.07 in toluene). Reported ΦF obtained over a scan window of 610–850 nm, a 0.3 s integration time (at 0.5 nm intervals), and excitation at 600 nm, using a quartz spectrophotometer cell with a 1 cm path length and an OD below 0.1 to prevent inner filter effects. All fluorescence spectra were corrected for wavelength-dependent instrumental sensitivity. The TPP standard was purchased from Frontier Specialty Chemicals (Logan, UT) and then oxidized with 2,3-dichloro-5,6-cyano-p-benzoquinone (DDQ) to convert the stated 1–3% chlorin impurity to TPP. TPP was cross-referenced against oxazine 720 perchlorate (Luxottica-Exciton, Lockbourne, OH, ΦF = 0.63 in MeOH) upon excitation at 580 nm and found to have an agreement of ±5%.

Excited-State Lifetimes

The excited-state lifetimes were measured at RT (20 ± 1 °C) using time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC). The excitation source was a laser diode (Becker-Hickl GmbH, Berlin, DE) operating at 50 MHz, with a 70 ps pulse, at a wavelength of 630 nm for Cy5 and Cy5.5 and a wavelength of 515 nm for Atto590. To accommodate the longer lifetimes in films, a repetition rate of 20 MHz was used. The transparent glass cover slides containing the dye-DNA structure were characterized at multiple sites. Sample fluorescence was collected and sent through a polarizer set to the magic angle and then filtered using a monochromator. A microchannel plate photomultiplier tube was used to detect the fluorescence with an ∼40 ps instrument response function. The TCSPC waveforms were collected with greater than 10,000 counts in the peak channel.

Confocal

A confocal microscope setup integrated with a close-cycle cryostat (Montana Instruments Corporation, Bozeman, MT) allowed for the interrogation of the MPWs within films. The excitation source was a 532 nm laser focused through a 0.42 NA long working distance objective with a 50× magnification. The laser spot diameter was determined to be ∼2.4 μm, and the laser power was fixed at 40 μW for samples with PVA and 10 μW for buffer only samples. The emission signal was directed to a 500 mm focal length spectrometer with a liquid nitrogen-cooled CCD (Teledyne Princeton Instruments, Trenton, NJ). We note that due to the relatively large spot size, we are confident that the measurements are of ensemble quality and that we were not reading out individual MPWs.

Results

Design of Molecular Photonic Wire

The MPWs were formed through the self-assembly of four DNA strands into a Holliday Junction (HJ) DNA nanostructure (See Figure ) in 1× TAE + 15 mM MgCl2 aqueous buffer. The DNA strands were labeled with one of three fluorophores creating an MPW with 3-components: donor, relay, and acceptor. The donor (input) dye is an Atto590 attached through a single-point 3-carbon linker to the 5′ end of the DNA strand. The relay component is the focus of this manuscript and is composed of a double phosphodiester internally labeled Cy5 dye or dye-dimer. By choosing the length of the linker that conjugates the Cy5 (Cy5-n′, n′ = 2–4; Figure A) to the DNA as well as whether the Cy5-labeled strands are adjacent or transverse within the HJ, we can tune the relay to H- or J-dimers, as well as oblique dimers. ,, Greater discussion of the dimers and nomenclature is provided in the next section. The acceptor (output) dye is Cy5.5 attached through a single-point 3-carbon linker to the 5′ end of the DNA strand. The photophysical properties of the dyes are provided in Table . The resultant structures can present a linear- or triangular-like array (see Figure ) depending on HJ conformations. Due to the nature of the attachments, there are 4 possible transverse configurations and 8 possible adjacent configurations leading to a total of 36 permutations surveyed (12 configurations, with 3 possible Cy5-n′).

Cy5 Relay Dimer Configuration

Our work builds off the determination in previous publications that cyanine dimer packing configuration (i.e., H-, J-, or oblique dimers) is a function of the linker length and HJ conformation, specifically if the Cy5 dyes are positioned in adjacent or transverse DNA strands. For specifics on the experimental determinations, we direct the reader to the original publications. ,− , As detailed in the introduction, the use of shorter linkers (2 carbons, nomenclature 2′) in the adjacent HJ conformation (i.e., ab, bc, cd, ad) led to high-fidelity J-like dimers, while longer linkers (4 carbons, nomenclature 4′) and transverse conformation of the HJ (i.e., ac and bd) led to H-like dimers. Dimers have a baseline of 90% packing fidelity, with most dimers being 95–99% the desired configuration. , Oblique-like aggregates, or heterogeneous mixtures of H- and J-dimers can be obtained by choosing 3 carbon linkers (3′) in the adjacent HJ conformation. We confirmed reproducibility of the previous results, and importantly, that the addition of the end-labeled dyes did not disrupt the dimer geometry. This was realized by comparing steady-state absorbance (Figure A) and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy. In the case of the absorbance spectroscopy, the MPW spectra corresponded to the linear combination of the respective components (Figure C). In the case of the CD spectroscopy, where the only signal is due to alignment of the excitonically coupled dimers, we were able to observe that addition of the Atto590 or Cy5.5 did not meaningfully alter the CD spectra (Figure D). Additional fluorescence spectra of the remaining conformations are available in the Supporting Information Figure S2. Transfer through the Molecular Photonic Wires

To determine energy transfer efficiency and compare the relative relay capabilities of the dimers to the monomers, we use the end-to-end transfer efficiency (E ee), which correlates to the efficiency of an exciton created in the Atto590 donor dye transferring to the final acceptor dye, Cy5.5, through the relay. The E ee corrects for downstream direct excitation and provides a value that allows for comparison across different designs, with a value of 1 signifying perfect transfer and 0 signifying no excitons reaching the final output dye. As discussed in Klein et al., there are multiple approaches to realize the downstream excitation correction. When available, we have found that using red-shifted excitation wavelengths that minimally excite the initial donor dye to be the most accurate approach to the downstream excitation correction of E ee since it avoids concentration variations. The I A λ is the photon counts emitted by the acceptor dye (Cy5.5) in an MPW excited at either the bluer wavelengths (λ1 = 540 nm) or the redder wavelength (λ2 = 615 nm). We note that λ2 did still excite some portion of the Atto590 dye; this could cause a slight overcorrection by our part. As such, the reported E ee can be considered as a lower bound for transfer efficiency. While I D λ(D only) is the number of photons emitted by the donor dye (Atto590) in an HJ structure that only contained the Atto590 dye. Φ F (D) and Φ F (A) are the respective fluorescence quantum yields of the donor and acceptor, respectively. The correction factor, f, is obtained by comparing the relative photon count of the final acceptor Cy5.5 dye while exciting at the two different wavelengths using DNA nanostructures composed of control HJ structures that lack the initial Atto590 donor dye.

The solution-based E ee values were obtained by measuring absorbance and fluorescence spectra on a Tecan plate reader. This allowed for high-throughput measurements (15–50 constructs per experiment), where control structures were included in each measurement. Our principal interest was to determine if RT aggregates, in this work limited to dimers, could be used to regulate energy transfer through MPWs. Within this hypothesis, it is the J-dimers that, by virtue of increased ΦF as compared to oblique and H-dimers, should present the best transfer efficiencies. Furthermore, the larger transition dipole moments of the J-dimers should improve transfer over monomeric dyes. To date, cyanine dimers on DNA have only shown equivalent or improved relay capabilities under cryogenic conditions. We characterized the complete suite of possible HJ MPWs, varying the donor, relay, and acceptor dye position over all DNA strands and present the results in Figure C. We note that H-, J-, and oblique-like dimers were based on classifications realized in Meares et al. Moroz et al. have shown that in the presence of closer proximity relay dyes direct donor–acceptor FRET transfer is minimal; as such, even with the triangular MPW configuration, E ee is a valid representation of the exciton transfer in our system. Furthermore, control samples and each construct with the varying linker lengths were tested within each experimental repeat to ensure we compared like-to-like.

In the example spectra of Figure A,B, we can see that the quenching of the Atto590 is similar for both dimers, which is in line with the similar Förster distances R 0 determined for the two dimers (vida infra). Yet, the intensity of the downstream acceptor emission is very different for the two MPWs. In Figure C, each individual data point is presented in gray with box plots providing simpler visual comparisons. The E ee values for the monomer and 2′-adjacent dimer (J-dimers) relays are statistically distinct from the 4′-dimers (H-dimer), or 3′-adjacent (oblique), and no-relay MPWs. Furthermore, the H-dimer is an even less efficient relay than a complete lack of relay dye. We interpret this as arising from the poor FRET donor capability of H-dimers due to their low ΦF and not related to their properties as FRET acceptors. This is consistent with previous reports of the weak fluorescence and aggregation-induced quenching through nonradiative decay pathways in cyanine dye H-dimers. Interestingly, the monomer appears to perform slightly better than the J-dimer, though they are not distinguished by the Student t test at a value of p < 0.05. The J-dimer E ee values have a larger range, E ee, between 0.10 and 0.20, compared to the monomer E ee. This is likely due to greater variations in the dipole alignments of the dimers as well as variations in the ΦF.

Experimental E ee results can be compared with predicted values based on estimated distances and individual FRET steps. We obtain the Förster distance (R 0), the distance corresponding to 50% energy transfer efficiency, for the FRET pairs making the following assumptions: (A) we utilize the dynamic average 0.66 for the κ2. Dynamic dipoles are likely only valid for the end-point labeled dyes, but using molecular dynamics simulations it has been shown that DNA-dye systems with static averaging will often remain in the κ2 = 0.7 ± 0.1 range, making the use of 0.67 value a reasonable assumption. (B) The J- and H-dimers can be treated as a single molecular dipole. With these assumptions, the calculated R 0 for the FRET pairs were Atto590-Relay [3.6 nm Cy5, 4.3 nm J-dimer, and 4.1 nm H-dimer]; Relay-Cy5.5 [3.9 nm Cy5, 3.5 nm J-dimer, and 2.5 nm H-dimer], and Atto590-Cy5.5 at 3.2 nm.

If we estimate donor–acceptor distances, r DA, values as ∼4.0 nm based on the dsDNA distances and dye linker lengths for Atto590 and Cy5.5 dyes, it becomes apparent that the r DA and R 0 are of the same scale, and we should expect relatively good FRET efficiency in the individual steps. Assuming E ee can be estimated as the product of the FRET efficiency of each energy transfer step, the predicted E ee would be 0.16, 0.19, and 0.03 for monomer, J-dimer, and H-dimer, respectively. These estimates are in good agreement with the experimental values, where we find the mean E ee were 0.16 ± 0.02, 0.14 ± 0.03, and 0.04 ± 0.02, for the monomer, J-dimer, and H-dimer, respectively. Experimentally determined r DA values based on the FRET efficiency of the individual transfer steps utilizing control structures (see Supporting Information Figure S4A–C) found the dyes are in closer proximity. Averaging the obtained values results in r DA = 3.0 ± 0.2 nm, which is within the range of physically reasonable distances.

Second Generation of Molecular Photonic Wires

We observed that the 2′bc adjacent dimer provided the best transfer efficiency of the dimer systems, which is consistent with previous reports that showed it to be the highest fidelity J-dimer. To further test the system, we modified the positions of the donor and acceptor dye and created a second-generation MPW. When realizing the design only, the 5′-end dye-labeled or unlabeled DNA oligonucleotides were altered as those could be obtained commercially. Due to the nature of base-pairing in HJs, the only way to realize the target arrangement was by adding a sequence that preceded strand b. Thus, we expanded the distance of the r DA(D-R) by creating a longer a strand oligomer, called a-extended (37 bp), with a complementary DNA strand, e (11 bp), that contained the Atto590 donor dye (see Figure and Table ). While the sequence is short, due to the 15 mM Mg2+ concentration, the melting temperature is estimated to be 51 °C, indicating that hybridization will approach unity. We also used a shorter d oligonucleotide strand, called d-truncated (21 bp), to allow for smaller r DA(R-A) between the relay and the Cy5.5 dye. Linear dsDNA estimates would predict that the Atto590 is 3.7 nm further from the relay positioned at the center of the HJ DNA nanostructure, while the Cy5.5 is 1.7 nm closer to the center. The intent here was to maximize the beneficial properties of the dimers, namely, the large extinction coefficients that make them good acceptors while minimizing the effect of the shorter τ that make them less effective FRET donors. Since we limited the exploration to a single HJ conformation, we included 3′ bc adjacent dimers, which have been previously assigned as mixed H/J structures, as well as comparing the individual monomer relays with 2′, 3′, and 4′ linker lengths.

Molecular Photonic Wires in Films

Though the mechanism is not completely understood, it is often observed that nonradiative decay pathways in cyanine dyes and dimers can be inhibited through increased environmental rigidity. Examples include DNA intercalation, cryogenics, and film deposition. ,, In our previous work, we were able to increase energy transfer through dye-monomer MPWs by transferring it from solution to a surface by creating films through spin-coating. , We hypothesized that similar benefits could be obtained with Cy5 dimers.

We investigated two approaches for creating the films, with and without poly vinyl alcohol (PVA, M w 31 000–50 000) polymer added at 1% w/v. PVA was previously shown to provide rigidity and improve energy transfer within films. Addition of the PVA did not result in noticeable changes in solution-based absorbance, signifying that the dimer coupling was not modified (Figure S7). Solutions were then spin-coated or drop-cast (see Materials and Methods for details) onto microscope slides for absorbance and fluorescence lifetime spectroscopy or onto plasma-cleaned SiO2/Si substrates with a 90 nm oxide thickness for fluorescence emission spectral determinations. We observed mostly homogeneous film formation based on spectral intensity and shape consistency; all presented data in this section are averages of spectra (N ≥ 6) collected over multiple spots on the films to compensate for any spatial variation in optical properties.

Since the approach was not high throughput, we limited ourselves to the HJ conformations with Cy5 in the bc DNA strands, Atto590 in a, and Cy5.5 on the d oligonucleotide of the first-generation MPW. In solution, this selection provided high-fidelity J-dimer formation upon use of the 2′ Cy5 as well as H-dimer upon use of the 4′ Cy5 dimers. We tested second-generation MPWs but observed that the d-truncated strand appeared to separate from the HJ structure during the chosen deposition process, likely due to a decreased number of complementary base pairs (bp).

Absorbance spectra were measured to confirm the stability of the dimers within the films. In Figure B–E, the absorbance spectra of the Cy5 relays can be compared (Atto590 and Cy5.5 are presented in the Supporting Information). It is readily apparent that the Cy5 monomer and the 2′bc dimer (J-dimer) maintain their structures, yet the 4′bc dimer (H-dimer) does not. When films were formed directly from the buffer solution, the blue-shifted maximum peak at 603 nm observed in solution drops in intensity, with the peak shifting to the red (656 nm). With the addition of PVA to the solution, after subsequent film formation, a third peak in the 565 nm range appears, possibly arising from tetramer formation. The H-dimer appears to lose stability upon desolvation and forms heterogeneous oblique-like aggregates. Attempts to stabilize the H-dimer were unsuccessful; as such, we proceeded with experiments to compare the J-dimers (2′ Cy5) with what we presume are heterogeneous oblique dimers (4′ Cy5).

Using a previously reported confocal microscope setup , (see details in Methods Section) with a 532 nm laser excitation source allowed for the interrogation of the MPWs and the MPW components within films on surfaces. It was not possible to determine exact ΦF for the dyes within the films but estimates were obtained through τ determinations as discussed below. It can be seen by comparing Figures F and B that the ΦF of the dimers increased relative to the Cy5 monomer. The increase was estimated at ∼1.5-fold for the 2′-dimer and ∼6-fold for the 4′-dimer. We attributed this to the inhibition of aggregation-induced quenching, i.e., the nonradiative decay pathways that are opened up upon dimerization. We observed 4 nm red-shifts of the dimer peaks in film as compared to solution, possibly due to a decrease in the environmental polarity. One complicating factor was that the Cy5.5 fluorescence appeared weak within all variations of the films. Absorbance spectra of the Cy5.5 in films (see Figure S8) showed a clear red-shift in the maximum absorbance, which could be accounted for by an increase in the refractive index (n) from 1.33 in the aqueous films to ∼1.5 in the films. , We also observed a redistribution to the 0–1 vibrational shoulder in the absorbance spectra, which we cannot currently explain.

TCSPC fluorescence lifetime measurements were also done on the PVA films containing the individual fluorescent MPW components. The donor and acceptor dyes τ were Atto590 = 4.1 ± 0.2 ns and Cy5.5 = 1.39 ± 0.07 ns, which are a slight decrease and increase of the solution values, respectively. We observed a single exponential decay for the Cy5 monomers with a τ of 1.49 ± 0.01 ns, while the 2′-dimer and 4′-dimers had double exponential decays with amplitude weighted τ of 0.66 ± 0.05 and 0.72 ± 0.05 ns, respectively (data and fits available in Supporting Information, Figure S9 and Table S1). While the τ of the Cy5 monomer is shorter in the film than in solution, the average lifetime of the 2′-dimer increased by ∼20% due to suppression of aggregation-induced quenching. The τ of the 4′ Cy5 dimer was similar to the 2′-dimer in film conditions.

The shape of the spectra differs between the film and the solution; particularly in the way that the Cy5.5 peak is harder to distinguish, appearing as a shoulder on the Cy5 emission. Spectral deconstruction is still able to separate out the three peaks as shown in Figure C. We did correct for direct excitation of the final acceptor Cy5.5 dye. Due to this distinction, we refer to the values presented here as an apparent end-to-end (E′ ee), though the interpretational value is similar for E ee and E′ ee.

The film formed with just the buffer showed decreased transfer efficiency for all choices of relay; the monomer showed the greatest decrease and the 2′ dimers had a statistically insignificant decrease. The PVA films again showed an efficiency decrease for the monomer and 4′ dimer, but the 2′ dimer increased in efficiency by 30%. Discussion

Within MPWs, aggregates may need to function as both FRET donors and acceptors, as such it is important to consider both functions individually. We begin by discussing their capability as FRET acceptors in solution (based on the assignation in Meares et al., and for simplicity of discussion, we will use the term J- and H-dimers in this section instead of the 2′, 3′, and 4′ nomenclature). The predicted R 0 values for FRET between the Atto590 donor dye and the dimers are ∼0.5 nm greater than for the monomer, which is due to the larger extinction coefficients. In our specific case, we find that the H-like and J-like dimers have a similar R 0 even with their respective hypsochromic and bathochromic absorbance shifts. These predictions align well with the observed experimental quenching of the Atto590 dye, where the FRET efficiency increased from 0.78 for the monomer acceptor to 0.86 for both dimers in the first-gen MPW and from 0.30 to 0.50 in the second-gen MPW.

Theory predicts that ideal J-dimers, with their capability for superradiance, larger transition dipole, and permitted fluorescent transition, should make better FRET donors than both the monomer and H-dimers. The benefit over monomeric dyes has been observed in pseudoisocyanine, for example, by multiple groups. − In our experiments, we observed (see Supporting Information, Figures S4–S6, for additional data) that the monomer remained the best donor with FRET efficiency to the Cy5.5 of 0.86 and 0.89 in the first- and second-gen MPWs, respectively. The J-dimer (first: 0.71 and second: 0.80) does outperform the H-dimer (first: 0.51 and second: 0.60) in both generations. As with their function as FRET acceptors, their function as FRET donors is consistent with their relative estimated R 0 values, which in this case are dominated by the reduced ΦF of the dimers. As Cannon and colleagues initially demonstrated and was confirmed for all our Cy5 dimers, cyanine aggregates have increased nonradiative decay pathways that lower ΦF and shorten τ. Therefore, the incongruity between the predicted better performance of J-dimers over monomers and the experimental results may be specific to the case of cyanine-based aggregates and other dye systems where aggregation-induced quenching dominates.

Overall, we observed that the individual FRET steps are acting as FRET theory would predict, supporting the hypothesis that the dimers are functioning as a single-point dipole for FRET. Furthermore, the assumption that the FRET dipole alignments can be approximated by the dynamic average (κ2 = 2/3) does not appear to create faulty predictions for individual FRET steps.

If we analyze energy transfer through the systems globally, comparing E ee values, we find that in the first-generation MPW, the monomer and J-dimer performances match predictions. Predictions assume that transfer through the MPW is a function of the multiplication of each individual FRET step efficiency. An ∼10% drop in efficiency from monomer to J-dimer was predicted, and experimentally the decrease in mean performance was 14%, though as seen in Figure C the range of values is such that they are not statistically distinct. The H-dimer underperforms predictions, as FRET theory predicts an ∼40% decrease as compared to the monomer, while experimentally we observed a 75% decrease in efficiency. The second generation of MPWs had an experimentally determined E ee that increased across the board compared to the first design. We note that the higher E ee in the second-generation MPWs is counter to the expected FRET predictions based on increasing the r DA(D-R) by 11 bp, which should have outweighed improvements from the decreased r DA(R-A). While some of this is likely accounted for by the difference in ΦF(A), it has also been shown by multiple laboratories that the rotational effect of the DNA double-stranded helix can modify κ2 and therefore the FRET efficiency, so there is a possibility that this played a role in the increased E ee. Also, the second-generation MPW was limited to a single conformation, which was chosen for being the best performing (most J-like) in the first-gen MPW. In the second-gen MPW. we observed our critical result; the J-dimer MPW outperformed not only the H-dimer but the monomer MPW as well. Experimentally, we observed an almost 50% increase of E ee in the J-dimer MPW over the monomer MPW. We hypothesize that the relative improvement over the monomer going from the first- to second-generation MPW is due to the dimer-Cy5′s FRET donor capabilities being the limiting factor in the MPW efficiency; by decreasing the r DA(R-A), we limited the effect of the shorter τ of the J-dimer.

In this manuscript, we present the data from the film-based experiments, and while we recognize that further investigation is required, e.g., how to stabilize H-dimer packing in films, we believe some important results have been obtained. For the film-based discussion, we will consider 2′ dimers as J-like and the 4′ dimers as heterogeneous, but still strongly coupled, dimers. In solution, J-like dimer MPWs were inherently more efficient than both heterogeneous and H-like dimer MPWs by 2.5-fold, yet in films this difference increased to a nearly 10-fold improvement. The initial thought is to adjudicate this to the increase in τ of Cy5 dimers within the more rigid environment of the films due to suppression of twisting about the polymethine bridge. In contrast, the Cy5 monomer had a slight decrease in τ within the films and we observed a small decrease in the energy relay capability. Therefore, we assign the improvement in FRET and increase in the dimer emission lifetime to suppression of aggregation-induced quenching. Similar to what was observed in the second-gen MPW in solution, by maximizing the Cy5 J-like dimer capability to function as a FRET donor, in this case by extending its τ, we greatly enhanced the overall energy transfer through the MPW. While in solution the monomeric and J-like dimer have similar E′ ee values (Figure D), upon deposition on the surface, the E′ ee of the J-dimers outperform the monomer by ∼1.7 and ∼6-fold with and without PVA, respectively. Due to the lack of shifts in the absorption spectra of both the monomer and J-like dimers in the films (Figure B,C), it would stand to assume that the Cy5′s FRET acceptor capability is unchanged. In conjunction, the energy transfer in film data further supports the hypothesis that the relay’s FRET donor capability is the key bottleneck to MPW efficiency.

Interestingly, when comparing the H-like dimer and the heterogeneous dimers that the 4′ Cy5 dimers form in solution or film, respectively, we observe a slight decrease in the E′ ee values when going from solution to film. This seems counterintuitive as our determinations of τ for 4′ Cy5 dimers, i.e., heterogeneous dimers, was longer than the τ for 2′ Cy5 dimers (J-like dimers) and much longer than the undetermined τ of H-like dimers in solution. These results would appear to be inconsistent with the hypothesis we postulated in the previous paragraph, since the lifetimes of the dimers in film are very similar (Figure G). Our interpretation is that the measured τ of the 4′ Cy5 dimers in films is dominated, not by the weak fluorescence from the H-like or oblique dimers, but from small proportions of weakly coupled “monomer-like” dimers. While this small proportion dominates the detected fluorescence arising from the relay only fluorophore HJs, when proportionally considered within an ensemble measurement, they result in minimal transfer efficiency values through the MPW. The solution samples, we expect, to be more dynamic than the film samples, similarly allowing a small subpopulation of efficient MPWs to increase the E′ ee above the film samples. Future experiments utilizing single particle (sp) fluorescence/FRET could help confirm this hypothesis.

We recognize that oblique and heterogeneous dimers are not discussed as in depth as the J- and H-like dimers. We consider that their varied packing configurations make generalizations harder for those samples. The results must be treated as unique, though in general it was observed that oblique-like dimers (which have J- and H-like properties) usually fit within the bounds of those two conformations. We believe that further investigations using spFRET will provide greater insight into the precise distribution of geometries within these systems.

We chose to explore the dimer relays within MPWs exploiting HJ as the DNA template since this approach prevented the need for labor-intensive synthesis and purification of several sequences containing both central Cy5s (Cy5-n′) and donor/acceptor dyes, dye-free and Atto590/Cy5.5 end-labeled oligonucleotides being commercially available. This approach allowed for the highest throughput, and greatest control of the orientation of the various components. Yet, we postulate that there is no reason that dsDNA or more complex DNA origami systems could not be utilized to achieve similar results.

Conclusion

Control over aggregate structure has been a key goal for photonics researchers, due to the unique photophysical properties of the J- and H-like aggregates, and their correlative power to regulate energy transfer. In this work, we definitively demonstrate such control for a cyanine dye. By regulating the dimer packing structure, we can tune its capability to function as an energy relay in both aqueous and film conditions. The results are in line with theoretical predictions where the more J-like the dimers are, as compared to heterogeneous, oblique, and H-like dimers, the greater the energy transfer relay capabilities. We experimentally validated that dimers can be estimated as point-dipoles by correlating FRET predictions with our experimental energy transfer determinations and found good correlation. By varying the distances between the MPW components, we confirmed that the dimer relay capability is not determined by the dimer’s FRET acceptor function, where all dimers were similar, but to the dimer’s FRET donor competence. This signifies that while J-like aggregates may function as optimal nanoscale energy movers, H-like aggregates could be used as optimal energy collectors akin to the photosynthetic special pair. For example, H-aggregates could serve as end-points in light harvesters for creating charge-separated states in photovoltaics via symmetry breaking charge separation. Dynamic control of dimer packing could also be imagined as a means of creating nanoscale optophotonics where J-like packing provides an ON state, while transitions to oblique or H-like packing creates OFF states.

The experimental confirmation of controlled energy transfer through molecular dimers and aggregates may have implications for RT excitonic gates where traditional fluorophores run into scaling issues. Exploiting the delocalized excitons of J-like aggregates, excitons could be moved through the aggregates faster and more efficiently, while oblique and H-like aggregates could provide exciton localization along with biexciton and charge-separation states. ,

We also demonstrate the use of cyanine-based J-like dimers as improved FRET relays over monomers in solution and in films at RT for the first time. We demonstrated a 45% increase in energy transfer through the MPW when comparing J-dimers to monomers in solution and up to 350% in film. We note that experimentally we saw that the efficiency difference is correlated to the design parameters of the MPW. The dimer can function as an improved energy transfer acceptor, and this effect is more prevalent the greater the r DA(D-R). As the dimer has a reduced ΦF and τ, we observed that minimizing r DA(R-A) was important when comparing J-like dimers to monomers as FRET donors. We note that this result may be specific to our systems, and as such, other fluorophores may have improved FRET donor capabilities upon J-dimer formation. These investigations are being actively pursued by our group.
