Revisiting Reviewer Rewards: A Critical Commentary on “Incentivizing Peer Reviewers”: Exploring Monetary and Nonmonetary Rewards
Authors: Anjum John
Published: 2026

Dear Editors,

Reference to the letter by Yagnik, Bhattacharya et al.[1] published in the esteemed journal on April 17th, 2025.

I thank the authors and the editors for having brought up such a relevant but little spoken-about topic in the academic world—“incentives for reviewers.” The letter references a study by Zaharie and Seeber,[2] which intriguingly suggests that, contrary to conventional assumptions, offering rewards for reviewing may actually reduce a reviewer’s willingness to participate.

Drawing on experience from their dual roles as editors and reviewers, the authors effectively address common perceptions about article publishing charges (APC) such as the belief that reviewers are entitled to a share of the APCs in open-access (OA) publishing. They rightly caution that if reviewers of OA journals were paid per review, the objectivity of their reviews and the credibility of the journal might be jeopardized. The authors have thoughtfully examined the ethical implications of compensating peer reviewers for their services and considered alternative forms of recognition publishers might offer in lieu of monetary compensation.

Peer review is a voluntarily undertaken scholarly task, with altruistic intent. Introducing payments could incentivize reviewers to accept more reviews than they can reasonably manage, allowing monetary gains to edge out the spirit of service.

Incentives, particularly monetary ones, may compromise the integrity of the peer review process if they become the primary motivation for reviewers. To address this, journals must develop ethical and innovative approaches to recognize reviewers’ contributions. As the authors suggest, a more balanced reward system could include nonmonetary incentives such as awarding credits to affiliated institutions—similar to CME or CNE points—which could also be considered for academic promotion. I propose categorizing reviewers based on their domain expertise, years of experience, and academic seniority. Incentive-based payments could then be offered to early-career reviewers or those who opt in, while senior or more established reviewers might continue to view peer review as a voluntary, service-oriented contribution to the scholarly community.

It is commonly accepted that there are considerable expenditures for OA journals, particularly for various editing and publishing tasks. While it is true that costs are involved, it is important to conduct an evidence-based analysis of task-specific expenses associated with OA publishing, as this helps demystify actual cost structures, distinguishes essential operational expenses from profit margins, and supports transparency in pricing—particularly critical for early-career researchers and those from low- and middle-income settings who often bear the financial burden of publishing, without clarity on where their fees are directed. In today’s age of rapid digitalization, where documents are processed quickly and efficiently, it raises the question of why OA publishing still incurs such exorbitant charges. Addressing such issues would add further merit to the discourse.

Manuscripts submitted to OA, hybrid, or traditional journals frequently encounter challenges related to peer review. These include difficulties in identifying suitable reviewers, refusals by potential reviewers due to time constraints or competing priorities, leading to subsequent delays that disrupt the overall timeline of the review process. Often, the critical bottleneck in the path from submission to editorial decision lies at the level of reviewer engagement and availability.[3] An investigation into the reasons for academic procrastination[4]—why reviewers delay their responses, or hesitate to accept review invitations, could provide valuable insights into improving the efficiency of the peer review process.

As we move forward, there is a pressing need for innovative approaches to enhance peer review—ideas that could be actively solicited from reviewers themselves, especially early-career scholars who bring fresh perspectives and are more attuned to emerging technologies. While artificial intelligence will increasingly support various aspects of the peer review process in the days to come, the critical role of human judgment and human minds in reviewing manuscripts remains indispensable.

Incentives for peer reviews are both necessary and not inherently taboo. Financial compensation should be available to those who need or prefer it, while alternative forms of recognition—such as academic credits, institutional rewards, or career advancement opportunities—can be offered to others.[56] A flexible and inclusive approach to rewarding reviewers is essential to sustain and strengthen the peer review ecosystem.
