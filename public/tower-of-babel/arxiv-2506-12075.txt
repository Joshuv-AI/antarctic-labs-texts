T-TExTS ( T eaching T ext Ex pansion for T eacher S caffolding): Enhancing Text Selection in High School Literature through Knowledge Graph-Based Recommendation
Nirmal Gelal, Chloe Snow, Ambyr Rios, Hande Küçük McGinty

Abstract
The implementation of transformational pedagogy in secondary education classrooms requires a broad multiliteracy approach. Due to limited planning time and resources, high school English Literature teachers often struggle to curate diverse, thematically aligned literature text sets. This study addresses the critical need for a tool that provides scaffolds for novice educators in selecting literature texts that are diverse—in terms of genre, theme, subtheme, and author—yet similar in context and pedagogical merits. We have developed a recommendation system, Teaching Text Expansion for Teacher Scaffolding (T-TExTS), that suggests high school English Literature books based on pedagogical merits, genre, and thematic relevance using a knowledge graph. We constructed a domain-specific ontology using the KNowledge Acquisition and Representation Methodology (KNARM), transformed into a knowledge graph, which was then embedded using DeepWalk, biased random walk, and a hybrid of both approaches. The system was evaluated using link prediction and recommendation performance metrics, including Area Under the Curve (AUC), Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), Hits@ K K , and normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG). DeepWalk outperformed in most ranking metrics, with the highest AUC (0.9431), whereas the hybrid model offered balanced performance. These findings demonstrate the importance of semantic, ontology-driven approaches in recommendation systems and suggest that T-TExTS can significantly ease the burden of English Literature text selection for high school educators, promoting more informed and inclusive curricular decisions. The source code for T-TExTS is available at: https://github.com/koncordantlab/TTExTS

Introduction

Scholars argue that in order for “transformative pedagogy” to occur, a multiliteracy approach must be used within the secondary education classroom
Jacobs 2012
. An example of multiliteracy is text sets, which are textual works of any format that are grouped together in order to teach a specific concept or deepen students’ understanding of a topic because they are thematically aligned
Opatz and Nelson 2022
. Text sets are advantageous to instruction because they can build background knowledge, make texts accessible, and aid comprehension of a certain topic through scaffolding
Elish-Piper et al. 2014
;
Lupo et al. 2018
. Beyond academics, text sets also ensure that students are being introduced to Literature and informational texts that are culturally relevant and create engaging reading experiences
Elish-Piper et al. 2014
. Thus, text sets offer secondary teachers the opportunity to integrate texts of various genres, authors, length, complexity, and modalities into their curriculum
Lupo et al. 2020
.

However, despite the educational benefits of text sets, many educators have not realized full benefits of text sets because they do not have the pedagogical knowledge, planning time, or material to compile and implement this in their curriculum
Lupo et al. 2018
;
Lupo et al. 2020
. A sound recommendation system suggesting texts with similar complexity and pedagogical merits could solve the above-mentioned problem. This study focuses on capturing the complexities in the domain, systematically creating a formal ontology, and generating a recommendation system using a knowledge graph (KG) populated with the formal ontology schema and database backend to suggest English language arts instructional texts for novice educators.

Ontologies are domain-specific representations of knowledge employing Description Logic (DL) to capture the complexities inherent in data. Widely utilized in fields such as bioinformatics
Ashburner et al. 2000
;
Lin et al. 2017
;
Haendel et al. 2008
;
Köhler et al. 2021
, ontologies formally express complex domain knowledge and manage extensive domain vocabularies. In this study, we use knowledge graphs as multi-relational graphs that employ ontologies as a schema, utilizing Resource Description Framework (RDF) triples such as <
Romeo&Juliet, written_by, William_Shakespeare
>. Figure
1
illustrates the basic structure of a knowledge graph. In our approach, a knowledge graph can stay aligned with the datasets, while the ontology backend extends beyond graph structures by incorporating logical constructors such as
existential & universal quantifiers, subclasses
, and
class disjointness
. Additionally, it supports
annotations
, which store metadata such as
synonyms, comments
, and
human-focused definitions
of a class. Consequently, ontologies are more semantically rich than schema-light knowledge graphs, which prompted us to develop an ontology on English Literature texts, using the Knowledge Acquisition and Representation Methodology (KNARM)
Mcginty 2018
approach on data provided by our domain experts.

Extensive research has been conducted on the semantic embedding of knowledge graphs and ontologies, aiming to represent KG entities in a vector space
Hogan et al. 2021
;
Yan et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2021
. Although some studies have incorporated logical constructors of ontologies into the semantic embeddings
Chen et al. 2021
, the majority of the embedding generation algorithms are designed for knowledge graphs containing RDF triples.
As our custom-generated graph was not highly complex in terms of axioms and logical constructs, we opted to convert the ontology into a graph composed of RDF triples. We implemented DeepWalk
Perozzi et al. 2014
, which performs random walk sampling on the graph to generate a sequence of entities and a biased random walk, a modified version of DeepWalk with weighted edges. These sequences were then used to generate embeddings through the skip-gram method via Word2Vec
Mikolov 2013
, effectively capturing the context of neighboring entities within the sequence. A hybrid embedding was also generated, concatenating the embeddings from DeepWalk and biased random walks to capture the strength of both models. After generating the embeddings, cosine similarity was employed to measure the proximity between the books in the high-dimensional vector space. This similarity enabled the identification of related books, which were subsequently recommended to users. The detailed embedding generation and similarity computation processes are described in Section
3.3
.

The traditional recommendation approaches, such as collaborative filtering and content-based recommendation, have demonstrated effectiveness
Ricci et al. 2010
; however, they also exhibit notable limitations. For example, collaborative filtering often encounters challenges such as the cold-start problem, where insufficient data regarding new entities hinders recommendation quality
Thorat et al. 2015
;
Yang 2018
. Similarly, content-based recommendation systems struggle to expand beyond a user’s existing interests, limiting their ability to introduce new or related topics. In contrast, semantic approaches such as ontologies and knowledge graphs possess a robust capability for capturing complex contextual information. This research aims to address the limitations of traditional implementations of recommendation systems by incorporating semantic richness and structural knowledge into the recommendation process.

Related Work

English Language Arts teachers’ selection of classroom instructional texts has been described as significant and consequential to students’ skill development and interest toward reading
Robertson and Padesky 2020
;
Tan and Mante-Estacio 2021
. In particular, teachers’ selection of high-quality, diverse texts relevant and representative of the learners in their care has been connected to various social and academic student benefits
Graff et al. 2022
;
Yokota and Teale 2017
. However, despite the potential benefits and the growing number of resources for teachers’ diverse text selection, teachers’ instructional text selection has remained largely stagnant within the last century
Rios 2024
;
Watkins and Ostenson 2015
. Teachers continue to choose texts they know, those available within their schools, and those within the secondary English canon (Rios, 2024), all sources that yield a group of texts that is more traditional, less diverse, and a mismatch to students’ identities and interests
Ervin 2022
;
Glaws 2021
;
Ivey and Broaddus 2001
. Teachers need additional tools and resources to support their creation and integration of text sets in the secondary English language arts curriculum.

A significant amount of research has been conducted on the representation learning of ontologies
Smaili et al. 2018
;
Smaili et al. 2019
and knowledge graphs
Chen et al. 2020
;
Yi et al. 2022
;
Khoshraftar and An 2024
. Through graph representation learning, various downstream graph-related tasks have been addressed, including link-prediction
Kumar et al. 2020
, node-classification
Bhagat et al. 2011
, relation-prediction
Rossi et al. 2021
, and recommendation tasks
Guo et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2022
. Studies such as
Agibetov and Samwald 2018
and
Alshahrani et al. 2017
employed feature learning in biological knowledge graphs and demonstrated improvements in link-prediction performance. These findings indicate that knowledge graphs have shown strong performance across diverse domains, effectively capturing both implicit and explicit domain knowledge and applying it to the aforementioned applications.

Rosati et al. 2016
utilized general knowledge graphs such as DBpedia and Wikidata for content-based recommendation of movies and book shows. The study employed a language modeling approach for unsupervised feature extraction from sequences of words, adapted to RDF graphs. However, its performance on domain-specific knowledge graphs remained untested. Furthermore, while
Rosati et al. 2016
relied solely on a uniform random walk strategy for sequence generation, our approach introduces a biased random walk and hybrid methods.

Cochez et al. 2017
proposed 12 different strategies for biased random walks based on uniform, edge-centric, node-centric object frequency, and node-centric PageRank approaches. The study evaluated these strategies across five datasets from different domains, employing all 12 methods to weight and bias the walk. Although the specific strategies proposed by
Cochez et al. 2017
were not employed in our approach, our method was instead guided by the importance of predicates for our particular task, as identified by domain experts. Nevertheless, their work provided valuable inspiration and highlighted areas for potential improvement.

Node2Vec
Grover and Leskovec 2016
, a widely used random walk-based embedding algorithm, introduced a biased walk procedure controlled by two parameters: the return parameter
p
and the in-out parameter
q
. These parameters allow the walk to balance exploration strategies, effectively interpolating between Breadth First Search (BFS) and Depth First Search (DFS). BFS captures structural equivalence by focusing on immediate local structures, while DFS explores broader neighborhoods, aiding the discovery of homophilous communities at the cost of high variance. Despite its effectiveness, Node2Vec was not employed in this study, as the objective was to compare uniform and biased random walk sampling guided by the weights of relationships based on the preference of domain experts.

Another strategy for learning features from a graph involves the incorporation of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which are based on the concept of
message passing
. In GNNs, message passing refers to the process by which information, represented as vectors, is exchanged between nodes along the edges that connect them.
De Nadai et al. 2024
employed a GNN for personalized audiobook recommendations at Spotify.
Kipf and Welling 2016
;
Veličković et al. 2017
demonstrated improved performance in prediction and classification tasks over traditional graph feature learning algorithms, introducing architectures such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) and Graph Attention Networks (GATs). Although GNNs such as GCNs and GATs have demonstrated superior performance in various graph-based tasks, this study employed DeepWalk and biased random walk approaches due to the small, domain-specific nature of the manually curated knowledge graph. Moreover, our experimental results indicate that DeepWalk outperformed more complex variants, suggesting that simpler structure-based methods were better suited to the characteristics of the domain and dataset.

3.2.5
Structured Interview

The knowledge obtained in the previous steps was utilized to conduct interviews with domain experts involved in data creation. We made an effort to ensure that no important aspects were overlooked during the modeling process. We added a few rule-based axioms in this step, such as: if the year of a text is earlier than 1945, then the triple (Book, hasEra, Traditional) is generated, as it describes texts written before the end of World War II
Rybakova and Roccanti 2016
; else, the triple (Book, hasEra, Contemporary) is generated. Additionally, if any of the qualitative measures contain “slightly complex” as a field and the Lexile level is less than 925, then the triple (Book, hasTextComplexity, Slightly_Complex) is included in the graph. Similarly, for Lexile levels greater than or equal to 925 and less than 1185, with any of the qualitative measures being “moderately complex,” a triple (Book, hasTextComplexity, Moderately_Complex) is added. Texts with Lexile level ranges 1185 to 1335 were designated for “very complex” and 1335 to 1440 for “exceedingly complex” texts. Likewise, through continuous interaction with the domain experts, we collected the knowledge for each book and associated concepts.

3.2.6
Knowledge Acquisition Validation

This phase can be considered as the initial feedback loop in the process. During this stage, outputs from previous steps—such as the modeled knowledge—were presented to domain experts. The primary aim of this step is to identify any gaps in the knowledge that may have been missed or misinterpreted.

Graph databases, such as GraphDB and Neo4j, offer numerous advantages, including handling large-scale data, structural compatibility with the schema, scalability, and their inherent graph-based architecture.

3.2.9
Ontology Validation

Diverse approaches are available to validate ontologies.
Raad and Cruz 2015

Since there exists no gold-standard ontologies in high-school-level English Literature texts containing pedagogical merits for gold standard-based evaluation, nor any document corpus that comprehensively captures all the concepts and relationships defined and incorporated in our ontology and knowledge graphs for corpus-based evaluation, we adopted a task-based ontology evaluation mechanism. Our task-based ontology validation approach involves measuring the performance of an ontology for a particular task, regardless of its structural characteristics. This approach focuses on addressing the domain-expert requests identified during the interview phases of ontology development.

3.3
Recommendation Model

Machine learning applications and statistical analyses utilize numerical data, making the conversion of text- and graph-based data into vector representations a common approach.
Chen et al. 2021
advocates two paradigms of representing entities in a vector space while preserving their semantics. The first approach involves computing embeddings in an end-to-end manner, iteratively adjusting the vectors using an optimization algorithm to minimize the overall loss across all the triples. Losses are typically calculated by scoring the truth or falsity of each triple (positive and negative samples). Algorithms such as DistMult
Yang et al. 2014
and TransE
Bordes et al. 2013
are based on this technique.

Another paradigm involves explicitly exploring neighborhoods of entities and relations in the graph prior to learning embeddings using a word embedding model. DeepWalk
Perozzi et al. 2014
adopts this strategy by employing a random walk technique to explore neighborhoods and generate sequences of nodes (and relations in KGs), treating these sequences as “sentences”. For each concept and relation within the sentence, a latent feature or embedding is learned using the skip-gram method described in Word2Vec
Mikolov 2013
.

For a given ontology
\mathcal{O}
, transformation is performed to obtain a graph
\mathcal{G}
based on OWL to RDF Graph Mapping, as originally defined by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C)
12
12

12

https://www.w3.org/TR/owl2-mapping-to-rdf/
, in order to store and exchange OWL ontologies as RDF triples. Let
\mathcal{E}
= {
e_{1},\ldots,e_{N_{e}}
} represent the set of entities (concepts), and let R ={
r_{1},\ldots,r_{N_{r}}
} in
\mathcal{G}
denote the set of relations connecting two entities. A walk
\mathcal{W}
{}^{m}_{e_{i}}
is defined as {
e_{i},e_{j},e_{k},\ldots
}, and is generated using a uniform random walk algorithm from DeepWalk. “Uniform” here indicates that for an entity
e_{i}
, there exists an equal probability of transitioning to any of its neighboring nodes. We also explored the modification of this approach by assigning weights to the relations (assessing the priority), thereby altering the likelihood of selecting the subsequent node in the walk. Data properties, i.e., relationships between a class and a literal value, such as strings, numbers, and other data types that define attributes or characteristics of entities, were discarded. Given that the graph is domain-expert curated and small-scale, DeepWalk and biased random walk were chosen instead of Node2Vec, as the latter is more suitable for large-scale graphs where it can more effectively control the depth and breadth of walks.

The conversion of a graph into a sentence-like structure enables the application of natural language processing (NLP) tasks, such as Word2Vec, to learn word (entity) embeddings based on the context words (entities). Skip-gram is a neural network-based model commonly employed in NLP to learn vector embeddings for words. It is effective in capturing semantic relationships between the words within a sentence. The objective of the model is to predict the context words of a given target word within a fixed window in a sentence. For a word
w_{t}
, it aims to predict words within the context window size
c
around it:

\displaystyle maximize\prod_{-c\leq j\leq c,j\neq 0}P(w_{t+j}|w_{t})

A model learns more effectively when negative samples are included in the dataset
Madushanka and Ichise 2024
. Embeddings are better learned when the model can distinguish between triples that exist and those that do not. To facilitate this, we generated negative samples using a common negative sampling technique. During negative sample generation, either the subject (i.e., head) or the object (i.e., tail) of a triple is replaced by another entity, ensuring that the resulting triple does not exist in the graph. For example, from the true triple
<Romeo&Juliet, written_by, William_Shakespeare>
, negative triples are generated by substituting either the head
Romeo&Juliet
or the tail
William_Shakespeare
with other entities. The resulting negative triples may include
<Romeo&Juliet, written_by, George_Orwell>
or
<1984, written_by, William_Shakespeare>
, which are false. A selected subset of negative samples was manually reviewed to check the relevance to the scope. This negative sample generation technique has a few limitations. For instance, for a triple
<Romeo&Juliet, isA, Book>
, changing a head to another book that is not in the knowledge graph would classify this triple to be a negative sample. In an open-world assumption, what is not known to be true may still be true. Since knowledge graphs follow the open world assumption, we cannot imply that the resulting negative triple is false. However, we are aiding the model training process to learn the existence of triples in the graph by adding false triples, thus negative samples were generated and added to the dataset. Using this negative sample generation technique, the size of the graph was increased, and the dataset with added negative samples was used as the training dataset.

We tuned the hyperparameters of DeepWalk, including walk length, number of walks, embedding size, and context length using an automatic hyperparameter optimization framework, Optuna
13
13

13

https://github.com/optuna/optuna
. The tuning process aimed to maximize the performance of the link prediction model, which predicts the likelihood of a link (or edge) between two nodes in a graph. In our context, link prediction classifies whether a given triple is likely to exist in the knowledge graph. For instance, if the triple
<Romeo&Juliet, written_by, William_Shakespeare>
receives a score of 0.92, it indicates a 92% likelihood that the triple exists in the graph. To evaluate the performance, the Area Under the Curve (AUC) score was used, representing the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. ROC plots the True Positive Rate
(Sensitivity)
against the False Positive Rate
(1-Sensitivity)
at various threshold settings. The AUC score ranges from 0 to 1, where a score of 1 indicates a perfect model and a score below 0.5 is worse than random. AUC assesses how effectively the model ranks positive cases higher than negative ones. 50 iterations across different ranges of hyperparameters were conducted, and the configuration yielding the maximum AUC score was selected.

The pseudocode of the biased random walk in a weighted graph is presented in Algorithm 1. The next node
u_{next}
is selected randomly based on the normalized probability, which modifies the original uniform random walk and aims to generate more focused walks. One of the major advantages of the biased random walk approach is that the walks can be adjusted based on concepts and relationships of interest. For instance, if the objective is to recommend a book primarily based on genre, the weight of the edges representing genre can be maximized. This will result in sequences with greater involvement of genres and books as entities, thereby facilitating the embedding model to better capture relevant information.

The embeddings of nodes and entities are positioned close to one another in high-dimensional vector space if they are structurally related in the graph. This is because the embedding generation algorithms aim to preserve the graph structure. If two nodes are similar, the vectors (embeddings) corresponding to them should lie in close proximity. This is determined using similarity measurement techniques such as Cosine Similarity, Euclidean Distance, Manhattan Distance, and Jaccard Similarity. In this work, the Cosine Similarity technique was employed, which measures the cosine of the angle between two non-zero vectors and emphasizes the direction of the vectors rather than their magnitude. The similarity range is
[-1,1]
, where 1 indicates that the two vectors are identical, 0 denotes orthogonality (no similarity or correlation), and -1 signifies that the vectors are diametrically opposite. For any two vectors
A
and
B
, cosine similarity is given by:

\displaystyle cos(\theta)=\frac{A\cdot B}{\|A\|\|B\|}

For a given book
e_{k}
and its embedding
\vec{e_{k}}
, cosine similarity was applied to all other books to extract the
top\_n
recommendations.

Recommendation systems generated using knowledge graphs (KGs) are enriched with semantic information and contribute to producing more personalized, accurate, and context-aware recommendations. Knowledge-graph-aided recommendation systems seek to address limitations such as the cold-start problem inherent in collaborative filtering and the lack of diversity commonly associated with content-based recommendation techniques.

Hits@K, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), and normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG) were used to evaluate the ranking performance of the recommendation system. Hits@K measures whether the correct item appears within the top-
K
predicted results. A prediction is counted as a hit (success) if the correct item is included among the top-
K
predictions. The final score represents the percentage of test cases in which the correct answer appears in the top-
K
. Hits@K is computed as:

\text{Hits@K}=\frac{\text{\# of correct items in top-}K}{\text{\# of test cases}}

Similarly, Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) measures the average quality of ranks by considering the inverse of the rank of the first correct answer. The value of MRR ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates the worst case with no correct answers, and 1 indicates the best case where all correct items are ranked at the top. MRR is computed as:

\text{MRR}=\text{Average}\left(\frac{1}{\text{First correct rank}}\right)

The other metric, normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG), measures the extent to which the relevance of the items returned by the system aligns with the relevance of items that an ideal system would return. It is given by:

\displaystyle\text{DCG@K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\text{rel}_{i}}{\log_{2}(i+1)}

\displaystyle\text{IDCG@K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\text{rel}_{i}^{{sorted}}}{\log_{2}(i+1)}\quad\text{(sorted descending)}

\displaystyle\text{nDCG@K}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{DCG@K}}{\text{IDCG@K}}\quad\text{(ranges 0 to 1)}

5
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduced T-TExTS, a recommendation system designed to assist high school teachers in selecting high school English Literature texts that are diverse yet similar in pedagogical merits, using knowledge graphs. The need for an improved text selection tool was addressed by developing a domain-specific ontology that captures not only genres and themes but also instructional and pedagogical elements. This ontology was transformed into a knowledge graph, on which popular and widely used feature learning techniques—including DeepWalk, biased random walk, and a hybrid approach—were applied to generate recommendations for high school English Literature texts.

Through a comparative analysis of the evaluation metrics across the three models, the distinct performance characteristics of each model have been highlighted. The DeepWalk model demonstrated superior performance on most metrics, while the hybrid approach yielded more balanced results. Several factors hinder the performance of these models. Data for this project were manually curated by domain experts, as publicly available datasets do not include the literary elements of books. The labor-intensive nature of manual annotation limited the dataset to fewer than 100 books due to constraints in time and resources. This study aimed to evaluate model performance on a limited dataset, and the results suggest that there is potential for improvement with the availability of additional training data.

Future directions for this research include expanding the training dataset and exploring additional algorithms, particularly in the context of larger datasets, such as those proposed in Node2Vec
Grover and Leskovec 2016
, OWL2Vec*
Chen et al. 2021
, and Heterogeneous Graph Neural Networks (HGNNs)
De Nadai et al. 2024
. Furthermore, GraphRAG
Edge et al. 2024
, a sophisticated technique that integrates knowledge graphs and large language models (LLMs) to retrieve information from a data corpus, presents a promising avenue for extension. The incorporation of GraphRAG could contribute to the development of a more robust question-answering tool, thereby extending the system’s capabilities beyond recommendation.
