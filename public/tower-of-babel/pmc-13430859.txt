Late-onset events in parosteal osteosarcoma: findings from a two-decade retrospective study
Authors: Khodamorad Jamshidi, Karo Khosravi, Seyyed Saeed Khabiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) is generally considered indolent with favorable short-term outcomes, its long-term biological behavior remains poorly understood. This study investigated late-onset events and prognostic factors for POS through an extended follow-up.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 84 patients with histologically confirmed POS treated between 2000 and 2020 at our institution. All patients were followed up for a minimum of 5 years. A subgroup analysis was performed for patients followed for more than 10 years to assess very late (> 10 years) local recurrence and distant metastases. Clinical, radiological, and pathological data were also analyzed. The secondary outcomes were survival rates and patterns of disease progression.ResultsAt a mean follow-up of 10.33 years (range, 5–21 years), local recurrence occurred in 22.6% (19/84) of patients, with 47.37% (9/19) of cases occurring after 10 years. Distant metastases developed in 13.1% (11/84) of patients, with 45.45% (5/11) manifesting after 10 years. Dedifferentiation occurred in 7.1% (6/84) of patients at a median of 8.7 years post-treatment. Multivariate analysis revealed that a tumor size > 8 cm (HR: 3.53, p = 0.018), medullary involvement (HR: 2.52, p = 0.049), and hemicortical resection (HR: 2.94, p = 0.041) were independent predictors of late-stage recurrence. The overall survival rates at 5, 10, and 15 years were 95.2%, 91.7%, and 88.1%, respectively.ConclusionsPOS has significant potential for late recurrence and metastasis beyond the conventional follow-up periods. These findings support the need for extended surveillance, particularly in patients with identified risk factors, and suggest that current follow-up protocols may require revision to account for late-onset events.Keywords: Parosteal osteosarcoma, Late recurrence, Dedifferentiation, Prognostic factors

Introduction

Parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) is a distinct clinicopathological entity among surface-based bone malignancies, characterized by its predilection for the metaphyseal region of long bones and its purportedly indolent biological behavior [1, 2]. While contemporary treatment protocols centered on wide surgical resection have achieved remarkable short-term outcomes, with 5-year survival rates exceeding 90% [3, 4], increasing evidence suggests that this initial therapeutic success may not fully reflect the long-term biological potential of these methods [5].

A critical analysis of the current literature reveals significant limitations in our understanding of POS’s natural history of POS. First, the temporal window of most existing studies, typically confined to 5–10 years post-treatment [3, 4, 6], may inadequately capture the full spectrum of disease progression. Second, the prognostic implications of medullary involvement remain contentious, with reported prevalence ranging from 7% to 65% across institutional series [7, 8]. Third, the absence of validated risk-stratification criteria for late recurrence has impeded the development of evidence-based surveillance protocols.

This 20-year longitudinal investigation aimed to address these fundamental knowledge gaps through a systematic analysis of late recurrence patterns, identification of prognostic factors, and evaluation of dedifferentiation phenomena in long-term survivors. Our findings may contribute to a better understanding of the biological behavior of POS beyond the conventional follow-up period and inform the development of more evidence-based long-term surveillance strategies.

Materials and methods

This institutional review board-approved retrospective cohort study included patients with histologically confirmed parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) treated at our tertiary sarcoma center between January 2000 and December 2020. All included patients had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of low-grade POS with complete medical documentation and a minimum follow-up period of five years. No patients were excluded based on the follow-up duration, as we were able to successfully track and evaluate all cases over the long term.

In this study, late-onset events were defined as local recurrence or distant metastasis occurring > ten years after primary surgical treatment. Emphasizing late-onset events (over ten years post-treatment), a subgroup analysis was conducted for patients with follow-up exceeding ten years to assess very late local recurrence and distant metastases. However, all patients were included in the overall oncological outcomes analysis regardless of the follow-up duration. Patients were excluded if their records were incomplete, if they were lost to follow-up before five years post-treatment, or if they presented with concurrent malignancies initially.

A fellowship-trained orthopedic oncologist reviewed the clinical data via standardized extraction protocols. The comprehensive clinicopathological database included patient demographics, tumor characteristics, and treatment parameters. All pathological specimens were subjected to systematic re-evaluation by an experienced musculoskeletal pathologist who was blinded to the original diagnoses and outcomes. The pathological assessment focused on the histological grade, dedifferentiation status, and extent of medullary involvement, where available. A subspecialty-trained musculoskeletal radiologist independently reviewed all the imaging studies. Standardized MRI protocols evaluate tumor location, size (maximum diameter), patterns of medullary involvement, and soft tissue extension. Serial chest CT scans, performed during routine surveillance, documented the temporal patterns of pulmonary metastases. Particular attention has focused on the radiological characteristics of late recurrences and the progression of medullary involvement over time.

The primary outcome measures included the incidence and timing of very late (> 10 years) local recurrence and distant metastasis. The inclusion of patients with follow-up durations of up to 21 years allowed for the capture of late-onset events, providing a comprehensive understanding of long-term risks. The secondary outcomes included overall survival, disease-free survival, and patterns of disease progression, with a specific focus on dedifferentiation phenomena. Our institutional follow-up protocol mandated clinical examinations with imaging studies at 3-month intervals for the first two years, 6-month intervals until year five, and annual evaluations thereafter.

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (Version 21.6, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Survival analyses were performed via the Kaplan‒Meier method with log-rank tests for group comparisons. We performed landmark analyses at 5, 10, and 15 years posttreatment to specifically address very late outcomes. Competing risk analysis was used to calculate the cumulative incidence of local recurrence and distant metastasis, with death as a competing event. Cox proportional hazard models were used to identify factors associated with late recurrence and metastasis, and the results are expressed as hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals. The Benjamini‒Hochberg procedure controlled for a false discovery rate in multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided).

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [9] and the Declaration of Helsinki principles [10].

Results

Among the 84 patients who met the inclusion criteria, the median age at diagnosis was 27.23 ± 11.06 years (range, 14–56 years), with a female predominance (69%, 58/84). The primary tumor location was predominantly in the distal femur (57 patients, 67.85%), followed by the tibia (9 patients, 10.71%, of whom 6 were proximal), forearm (8 patients, 9.52%), proximal humerus (7 patients, 8.33%), and proximal fibula (3 patients, 3.57%). Patterns of late events and disease progression

During the mean follow-up period of 10.33 years (range, 5–21 years), we observed distinct patterns of disease progression that challenged the conventional understanding of POS behavior. Local recurrence developed in 22.6% (19/84) of patients, with a noteworthy 47.37% (9/19) of these events occurring beyond the 10-year mark. The cumulative incidence of local recurrence demonstrated a continuous upward trajectory, from 18.2% at 10 years to 22.6% at 15 years, suggesting persistent risk even in long-term survivors.

Surgical margins were classified as R0 (wide resection) in 62 patients (73.81% ± 4.80), R1 (marginal) in 18 patients (21.43% ± 4.47), and R2 (intralesional) in 4 patients (4.76% ± 2.32). Among patients with local recurrence, those with R1/R2 margins had a significantly shorter median recurrence time of 6.2 years (range: 3.1–9.6 years) compared to 13.1 years (range: 7.8–19.4 years) in the R0 group (p = 0.008). These findings suggest that non-R0 margins primarily contribute to early recurrence, whereas late recurrence is likely influenced by biological factors rather than surgical technique alone. Furthermore, multivariate analysis confirmed that margin status and tumor size > 8 cm were independent predictors of recurrence risk (HR 2.87, 95% CI 1.24–6.65; p = 0.014). Interestingly, hemicortical resection remained an independent risk factor for recurrence (HR 2.94, p = 0.041) even when controlling for margin status, suggesting that its anatomical limitations may contribute to disease progression beyond the influence of surgical margins alone.

The median time to local recurrence and metastasis was 12.8 years and 11.6 years, respectively. Given that nearly half of these events occurred beyond 10 years, further stratification revealed a bimodal recurrence pattern. Local recurrences had a median onset of 6.7 years for cases occurring within the first 10 years, compared to 14.3 years for those developing later. Similarly, metastases appeared at a median of 5.8 years within the first decade and 13.7 years beyond 10 years. These findings suggest that early recurrences are more influenced by surgical factors, whereas late events are driven by tumor biology, emphasizing the need for extended surveillance strategies.

Distant metastases emerged in 13.1% (11/84) of the patients, exhibiting a bimodal temporal distribution pattern. While 54.55% (6/11) of the metastases developed within the first decade, 45.45% (5/11) manifested beyond 10 years posttreatment. All documented metastases were pulmonary in nature, with no cases of skeletal dissemination observed throughout the follow-up period. Among the 11 patients with metastases, 5 had a history of local recurrence, with a median time of 2.4 years from recurrence to metastatic progression. Pathological analysis confirmed that all metastatic lesions retained the well-differentiated histology of parosteal osteosarcoma, with no evidence of dedifferentiation. Multivariate analysis identified three independent predictors of late recurrence: tumor size > 8 cm (HR: 3.53, 95% CI: 1.24–10.05, p = 0.018), medullary involvement (HR: 2.52, 95% CI: 1.01–6.31, p = 0.049), and hemicortical resection (HR: 2.94, 95% CI: 1.05–8.27, p = 0.041). Notably, medullary involvement, observed in 35.71% (30/84) of patients, gained prognostic significance beyond the 10-year mark (HR: 3.87, 95% CI: 1.46–10.25; p = 0.006), suggesting an evolving risk profile over time.

Dedifferentiation emerged as a critical late event, occurring in 7.1% (6/84) of patients at a median of 8.7 years posttreatment (range, 4.2–16.3 years). Although rare, its exclusive presentation during late follow-up indicates a delayed but high-risk transformation, warranting sustained surveillance. All dedifferentiation cases were associated with subsequent disease progression, significantly impacting survival (HR: 3.87, p < 0.001). These findings underscore the necessity of vigilant histopathological assessment during long-term follow-up.

Regarding adjuvant treatment, the use of chemotherapy and radiotherapy in this cohort was limited, consistent with the established management paradigm for low-grade parosteal osteosarcoma, in which surgery alone remains the primary treatment. Among the 84 patients, systemic chemotherapy was administered to eight patients (9.5%), all of whom presented with either dedifferentiation (n = 6) or pulmonary metastasis (n = 2) during follow-up. Radiotherapy was not employed as a primary treatment modality in any patient in this series.

Survival analysis revealed favorable but gradually declining outcomes. The overall survival rates at 5, 10, and 15 years were 95.2%, 91.7%, and 88.1%, respectively. Disease-free survival demonstrated more pronounced deterioration over time: 89.3% at 5 years, 77.4% at 10 years, and 69.0% at 15 years. Landmark analysis at the 10-year mark revealed that patients with high-risk features, such as a tumor size > 8 cm and medullary involvement, experienced significantly worse survival. Discussion

Our extensive 20-year analysis of 84 patients diagnosed with parosteal osteosarcoma (POS) provides valuable observational data on the long-term oncological behavior of this rare tumor, with a particular focus on events occurring beyond the conventional follow-up period. These findings challenge the adequacy of traditional 5- to 10-year follow-up protocols, as they reveal a persistent risk of disease progression, even among long-term survivors. Extending the follow-up duration could facilitate the earlier detection of these late events, potentially enhancing patient outcomes [3, 4]. Our extended observation period allowed us to capture these late events, which would likely be missed in studies with shorter follow-ups, leading to an underestimation of the true risk of recurrence in the long term. This underscores the importance of our study in providing a more accurate assessment of the long-term risks associated with POS. This extended follow-up aligns with the findings of Laitinen et al. and Ritschl et al., who emphasized the importance of understanding the prognostic and therapeutic factors influencing long-term oncological outcomes in patients with parosteal osteosarcoma [6, 11]. It should be noted that these observations were derived from a single-center retrospective cohort, and their generalizability requires validation in larger, prospective, or multi-institutional studies. A comparison with the existing literature contextualizes these findings. While many studies report recurrence rates within the first 5–10 years [7, 12], our data demonstrate a continued risk of recurrence beyond this timeframe. For instance, Funovics et al. [13] reported a local recurrence rate of 7.1%, which was considerably lower than the 22.6% reported in our study. This difference likely stems from the shorter follow-up duration in their study, highlighting how our extended observation period revealed a more complete picture of POS behavior. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of studies with shorter follow-up periods. Variations in patient selection, surgical techniques, and institutional experience may also contribute to these differences, necessitating cautious interpretation of direct comparisons [14]. Similarly, Ruengwanichayakun et al. [4]reported a higher rate of dedifferentiation (24.6%) than that in our study (7.1%); however, their series included cases with dedifferentiation at the initial diagnosis. Our analysis, however, specifically focused on delayed dedifferentiation occurring during follow-up, a distinct clinical phenomenon that underscores the necessity for ongoing vigilance, even in patients initially diagnosed with low-grade tumors [15]. The case presented by Combalia et al. [16]illustrates the local recurrence of POS 21 years following an initial misdiagnosis and incomplete resection, emphasizing the potential for long-term dormancy and subsequent recurrence, even in low-grade tumors. This phenomenon has also been documented in other studies [17–19].

We found that hemicortical resection, tumor size exceeding 8 cm, and the presence of medullary involvement were significant predictors of local recurrence. These findings emphasize the need for individualized surgical planning and risk-adapted surveillance protocols, particularly for patients with high-risk features such as medullary involvement or large tumors [7, 14, 20]. Our observation that hemicortical resection is linked to a higher risk of local recurrence underscores the critical role of achieving adequate surgical margins, a principle well-supported by prior studies [21, 22]. However, our long-term data also show that even in R0 margin cases, hemicortical resection is still associated with an increased rate of recurrence. This suggests that anatomical limitations contribute to the risk of recurrence beyond the mere condition of the margin. Multivariate findings also suggest that early and late relapse can have different drivers. However, this interpretation is observational and should be considered as hypothesis-based rather than definitive. These findings support the need for expanded surveillance strategies that take into account both surgical and biological risk factors rather than relying on the status of the margin alone as a predictor of long-term recurrence. The importance of large surgical margins is also emphasised in the literature, which points out that the margin without tumors is the main determinant of overall survival [8, 23, 24]. Similarly, the association between larger tumour size and an increased risk of recurrence is a quantifiable risk factor [25].

The occurrence of late recurrence and metastasis in POS raises questions about the underlying biological mechanisms involved. Several hypotheses warrant investigation, including the gradual accumulation of genetic changes [26, 27], shifts in the tumor microenvironment [28], and alterations in the body’s immune surveillance system [29, 30], as suggested in various studies. These possibilities highlight the limitations of current diagnostic tools, emphasizing the need for more sensitive methods to detect residual disease or predict future recurrence [31]. To better understand these late events, further research is crucial. This research could involve analyzing the molecular characteristics of tumor samples to identify biomarkers associated with late recurrence and investigating the role of the environment surrounding the tumor in facilitating delayed tumor growth.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, the retrospective design risks selection and information bias, as data were from existing records, not prospectively defined protocols. Unmeasured confounders affecting recurrence risk cannot be excluded. Secondly, this single-center study at a tertiary sarcoma center may introduce referral bias, as complex cases are likely overrepresented compared to the general POS population, limiting generalizability. Thirdly, although the sample size of 84 patients is among the larger series for this rare tumor, it remains small. This constrains the statistical power of subgroup analyses, especially for events after 15 years, where patient numbers decrease, widening confidence intervals around some hazard ratios. Fourthly, surveillance protocols evolved over the 20-year study period; earlier cohorts were managed under older guidelines, introducing variability in recurrence detection, influencing late event observation. Fifthly, the absence of molecular profiling precludes investigation into biological mechanisms underlying late recurrence and dedifferentiation. Sixthly, the lack of a control group prevents formal causal inference regarding extended surveillance impact on outcomes; thus, observed associations should not be interpreted as causation evidence. Seventhly, quality-of-life data were not systematically collected, preventing assessment of late-onset events’ impact on patient well-being. Despite these limitations, this study provides observational evidence on long-term oncological behavior of POS from one of the longest single-institution follow-up series, and the findings may inform future prospective studies and multi-institutional registries.

Conclusion

This 20-year study highlights that late-onset local recurrence and distant metastasis in parosteal osteosarcoma can occur over 10 years post-surgery, with nearly half of recurrences occurring beyond this timeframe. Factors such as tumor size over 8 cm, medullary involvement, and hemicortical resection correlate with increased recurrence risk. The findings, derived from a single-center cohort, necessitate further validation in larger studies before influencing clinical practices. Future research should focus on molecular profiling and prospective follow-up to enhance surveillance strategies.
