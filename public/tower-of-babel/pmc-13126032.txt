Reimagining resuscitation ethics: a narrative review and emergent ethical pathways model
Authors: Saeed Khayat Kakhki, Majid Daneshfar, Alireza NamaeiQasemnia, Mehrdad Yousefnezhad, Sara Kazemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CPR ethics problems are structurally produced, not isolated bedside dilemmas.•Futile and symbolic CPR recurs under legal, family and hierarchical pressures.•Ambiguous DNR/DNAR pathways drive unwanted CPR and clinician moral distress.•The Ethical Pathways Model links contextual, relational and individual determinants.•Ethics support, clear policies and better communication can reduce conflicted CPR.

Introduction

Resuscitation occupies a uniquely complex ethical space in contemporary healthcare. While cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is widely regarded as an essential life-saving intervention, its application in the context of advanced illness, uncertain prognosis, or conflicting expectations frequently generates profound ethical tensions. International literature consistently demonstrates that decisions surrounding CPR and Do-Not-Resuscitate (DNR/DNAR) orders are influenced not only by clinical indicators but also by legal frameworks, organizational norms, cultural values, and interpersonal dynamics.1, 2 These multi-level influences often result in care that is misaligned with patient preferences, creates moral distress for clinicians, or perpetuates non-beneficial or futile interventions.3, 4

Despite extensive scholarship on moral distress, end-of-life communication, and ethical decision-making in acute care, current evidence remains fragmented. Existing studies tend to examine individual components such as family involvement, legal ambiguity, or clinician distress in isolation rather than as interacting elements of a broader ethical system. Although several frameworks address ethical decision-making in critical care, none provides an integrated explanation of how systemic, relational, and individual factors interact to shape ethical pathways during resuscitation.5, 6 Moreover, little is known about how these pathways contribute to phenomena such as symbolic CPR, defensive resuscitation, or moral residue.

This gap is particularly significant because resuscitation decisions are often made under time pressure, within hierarchical team structures, and in contexts where the patient’s values are insufficiently known or poorly communicated. Within these acute, high-stakes environments, nurses are frequently positioned at the nexus of ethical conflict, tasked with executing, clarifying, and sometimes challenging treatment plans while advocating for the patient’s expressed or presumed wishes. Without a coherent conceptual understanding of these dynamics, clinicians and health systems remain vulnerable to ethically compromised practices, inconsistencies in DNR/DNAR implementation, and escalating moral distress among staff with nursing personnel often bearing a disproportionate burden of this distress.

To address this gap, this narrative review synthesizes evidence across diverse cultural and clinical settings to clarify the multi-level ethical forces shaping resuscitation decisions. During the synthesis, a set of recurring patterns emerged that subsequently informed the development of a preliminary Ethical Pathways Model (EPM). The model is therefore presented as an interpretive output of the review, not as a priori objective.

Methods

Review design

A structured narrative review methodology was used. This design was selected because it allows the integration of heterogeneous empirical studies while retaining the flexibility needed to explore conceptually complex and context-dependent ethical issues. The goal of the review was interpretive rather than aggregative, and therefore unsuitable for quantitative synthesis.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across four electronic databases: Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, and Embase. The search covered publications from January 2020 to November 2025. The final electronic search was performed on 3 December 2025. We limited the search for two reasons. First, the volume of available literature on CPR/DNR decision‑making and moral distress is substantial and focusing on the most recent five‑year period ensured inclusion of the most contemporary and representative evidence. Second, the years following 2020 reflect important shifts in clinical communication, ethical deliberation, and organizational dynamics that have shaped current CPR/DNR practice, particularly in the context of the COVID‑19 pandemic and evolving resuscitation policies.7, 8, 9

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) with free‑text keywords related to nursing practice, resuscitation‑related interventions (such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation, do‑not‑resuscitate decisions, and limited resuscitation approaches), and ethical or moral dimensions of clinical care. Search strings were adapted for each database to account for differences in indexing structures. A consolidated version of all databases‑specific queries is now summarized in the Supplementary Search Strings file for full reproducibility.

Study selection

Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The initial screening was conducted manually by the first author. Full‑text articles of potentially relevant studies were then assessed independently by two reviewers to determine eligibility. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to achieve consensus. Articles with uncertain relevance at the title or abstract stage were provisionally retained and assessed at the full‑text review stage.

Data extraction

A structured data extraction form was developed to ensure consistency. Extracted information included basic publication details, study design, setting, participant characteristics, and the specific resuscitation-related ethical focus addressed. This approach ensured comparability across diverse study types and facilitated systematic synthesis.

Narrative synthesis

Because the included studies varied in design, method, and focus, a narrative synthesis approach was used. The process involved repeated reading of the included studies, identification of recurring concepts, and organization of these concepts into broader categories. The aim of the synthesis was to provide an integrative account of the literature rather than to perform quantitative aggregation. Quality appraisal of the included studies was conducted using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT), version 2018, which allows the assessment of methodological quality across diverse study designs. Two reviewers independently evaluated the methodological rigor of the included studies according to the MMAT criteria appropriate to each study design. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and when consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted.

Analytical approach

A thematic analysis approach was used to synthesize the findings from the included studies. Thematic analysis was selected because it allows for the identification, comparison, and integration of concepts across heterogeneous empirical literature, which is particularly suitable for exploring complex ethical phenomena. This review followed the reflexive thematic analysis framework described by Braun and Clarke, involving familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, constructing candidate themes, reviewing and refining themes, and producing a narrative synthesis of the final thematic structure.10

The progression from initial codes to study-level themes and their alignment with the final seven synthesized themes is detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

The approach was interpretive rather than aggregative and prioritized conceptual patterns related to ethical experiences, decision-making processes, and contextual influences in resuscitation-related nursing practice. The review process was iterative, and themes were developed inductively from the included studies while remaining sensitive to recurring ethical concepts reported across diverse clinical contexts. Reflexivity was incorporated through continuous reflection on how the reviewers’ professional and ethical perspectives could influence coding and interpretation.

Initial coding was conducted independently by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, and when agreement could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted. Category groupings and candidate themes were subsequently refined collaboratively to enhance analytic rigor.

Model development

Although the primary aim of this review was not model development, the reflexive and iterative nature of the thematic analysis allowed patterns across studies to converge into a coherent conceptual structure. The Ethical Pathways Model (EPM) presented in this paper therefore represents an emergent, data‑driven interpretation rather than a pre‑specified analytic objective.

Building on the seven final themes, we undertook an interpretive mapping process to translate thematic findings into the domains and pathways represented in the EPM. Themes describing individual emotional responses, moral distress, and nurses’ ethical competence were located predominantly at the micro‑level of the model, capturing bedside moral experience and agency. Themes related to team hierarchy, power dynamics, and communication within resuscitation teams were mapped to the meso‑level, reflecting unit and organizational‑level structures. Themes concerning legal frameworks, institutional policies, and wider cultural or societal expectations were situated at the macro‑level, representing structural and systemic determinants.

Through iterative team discussions, we identified and traced ethical pathways linking these levels illustrating, for example, how macro‑level legal or policy pressures may shape meso‑level organizational norms, which in turn can constrain micro‑level nursing moral agency and contribute to moral distress. In this way, the EPM integrates the thematic findings into a conceptual, multi‑level depiction of how resuscitation ethics may be experienced and negotiated in practice.

Accordingly, the EPM should be understood as an interpretive and hypothesis‑generating conceptual framework rather than a definitive explanatory model.

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018. Overall, the quality of the studies was variable. While several studies clearly reported their objectives and methodological approaches, some demonstrated limitations such as small sample sizes and incomplete reporting of data collection or analysis procedures. Detailed results of the quality appraisal for each included study are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Across methodologies, the dataset included qualitative studies using phenomenology, grounded theory, thematic analysis and interpretive description,20, 21, 22, 23 cross-sectional surveys,24, 25, 26 and narrative analyses.14, 27 This methodological diversity allowed for a rich synthesis of experiential, attitudinal and behavioral dimensions of resuscitation ethics.

Repeated patterns emerged across contexts, revealing that ethical complexity in resuscitation is driven not by isolated individual factors, but by the interaction of clinical uncertainty, organizational pressures, legal ambiguity, cultural expectations, and relational dynamics. Ethical conflict was identified in decisions regarding whether to initiate, withhold or terminate CPR; interpretation and implementation of DNR/DNAR orders; and management of post-resuscitation care for patients with extremely poor prognoses. Studies consistently described clinicians performing CPR or continuing life-sustaining treatments despite their belief that such interventions were futile or non-beneficial,11, 21, 23, 24, 25, 26 At the same time, slow code or symbolic resuscitation practices partial, minimal or intentionally ineffective CPR were recurrently reported, particularly in settings characterized by legal ambiguity, family pressure or hierarchical constraints.11, 16, 19, 28

A key cross-cutting finding was the pervasive presence of moral distress among nurses, physicians and EMS personnel when they were expected to enact decisions that contradicted their ethical judgment or professional values. This phenomenon was described across high-resource and low-resource settings,15, 27, 20, 21, 22 often compounded by inadequate institutional support, poor communication structures, and conflicting expectations from families or senior clinicians.

Together, these themes depict a multi-layered ethical landscape in which resuscitation decisions are shaped by structural determinants, team-level interactions, and individual moral responses, often culminating in complex ethical tensions at the bedside.

Theme 1. Futility, inappropriate and symbolic CPR

Futile CPR was repeatedly reported across studies: resuscitation was started or continued despite poor prognosis, irreversible decline, or an established end-of-life trajectory, and was consistently linked to ethical unease and emotional burden. Large data reinforced prevalence: in a survey of 5099 emergency clinicians, over two-thirds frequently witnessed CPR they judged inappropriate, associated with higher moral distress and intention to leave.24 Similar acute and prehospital accounts described CPR performed “because it must be done,” or as “meaningless,” sometimes driven by family pressure or legal uncertainty.21, 25, 26 A recurrent pattern involved symbolic/limited/slow-code CPR reduced-intensity efforts used to appear compliant or manage conflict, fear, or unclear policy.11, 16, 19, 28, 29 CPR was also performed for non–patient‑centered reasons (avoiding accusations, meeting institutional expectations, or allowing time for family arrival), including resuscitating patients perceived as “already gone” in order to prevent conflict or confrontation with family members.11, 14, 15 Across studies, these practices produced moral distress/residue and perceived erosion of integrity, shaped by legal ambiguity, organizational culture, hierarchy, and family expectations.18, 20, 22

Theme 2. Moral distress, emotional burden, and moral residue

Moral distress was pervasive across studies of resuscitation and arose when clinicians were compelled to provide CPR, they judged futile, coerced, or inconsistent with professional values often under unclear DNR/DNAR pathways and hierarchical or family pressure.15, 20, 21, 22, 24, 25, 32 Clinicians described intense post‑code burden (helplessness, guilt, self‑doubt), including feeling they were either harming patients by prolonging suffering or having to permit a natural death when further treatment was futile, with some reporting profound shame or spiritual anguish when delivering “meaningless” interventions.21, 22 Quantitative evidence linked perceived inappropriate CPR to higher moral distress and intention to leave.24 Across settings, repeated exposure shifted acute distress into moral residue (intrusive memories, regret, moral contamination) and, over time, moral erosion normalizing slow codes, documentation distortion, or covert withdrawal.11, 12, 19, 20 Distress was amplified by weak support systems (limited debriefing, poor ethics access) and high workload/unsupportive cultures, contributing to burnout, withdrawal, and workforce attrition.15, 21, 22, 24, 25

Theme 3. Ethical ambiguity and unclear decision pathways (DNR/DNAR/ToR/LR)

Ethical ambiguity around initiating, limiting, or terminating resuscitation was pervasive across studies. Unclear and inconsistently interpreted DNR/DNAR, ToR, and LR directives shaped by weak policies and fragmented communication left clinicians making rapid, high-stakes decisions in “gray zones”.28, 29, 30, 31 Uncertainty about scope (e.g., whether DNR covered only compressions or also airway management and medications) produced hesitation, team inconsistency, and, at times, symbolic or partial CPR.21, 26, 31 Late or avoided goals-of-care discussions, including “pacts of silence” that sidelined patients, further undermined autonomy and forced abrupt shifts from escalation to comfort care.12, 17, 18, 21 LR/partial codes were repeatedly described as especially vague and sometimes used as substitutes for difficult end-of-life conversations.26, 29 Similar ambiguity surrounded ToR, particularly where field clinicians lacked authority or standardized stopping criteria, contributing to prolonged or unwanted efforts.16, 28 Overall, ambiguity eroded teamwork and moral clarity and promoted nonbeneficial CPR, slow-code practices, escalation to avoid conflict, or acquiescence to family demands.16, 21, 26, 29

Theme 4. Hierarchy, power, and constrained nursing moral agency

Hierarchical structures and medical dominance were repeatedly reported as key ethical constraints in resuscitation. Across studies, physician-centered authority over DNR/DNAR, limited resuscitation, and end-of-life planning reduced nurses’ moral agency, limited ethical dialogue, and contributed to ethically problematic CPR.

Nurses frequently described exclusion from resuscitation decisions despite close knowledge of patients and responsibility for implementation, creating “doctors decide, nurses obey” dynamics and intensifying moral distress.17, 18, 23 Hierarchy also operated through coercion and fear: nurses in Iran and South Korea reported being unable to question orders during CPR due to risk of reprimand or retaliation, while junior physicians likewise felt constrained or punished for raising futility concerns or initiating end-of-life discussions.20, 21 These pressures shaped practice directly, including imposed and ambiguous limited-resuscitation orders and continuation of CPR in prehospital settings because team leaders insisted, even when clinicians judged it futile.16, 29 Poor communication climates in some settings further discouraged speaking up and enabled ethically compromised practices such as performative care or documentation distortion.15, 19 In contexts where cultural norms and family authority reinforced deference to physicians, nurses carried disproportionate emotional and mediating labor without decision power.13, 14, 18, 31

Overall, constrained moral agencies consistently led to silenced concerns, role conflict, moral distress/residue, disengagement, and reduced patient advocacy, reinforcing patterns of futile, symbolic, or defensive CPR across diverse systems.11, 21, 23, 25, 29

Theme 5. Family influence, cultural and religious norms, and truth-telling practices

Family influence and cultural-religious norms strongly shaped resuscitation decisions across studies, often overriding clinical judgement and patient preferences. Clinicians frequently reported initiating or prolonging CPR to satisfy family demands, demonstrate effort, or avoid conflict even when CPR was judged futile.11, 14, 15, 16 In several contexts, family-centered decision-making and “silence” around prognosis delayed DNR discussions and drove escalation until crisis arrests.12, 17, 21 Families sometimes overruled documented DNR/DNAR orders, particularly where complaints or legal uncertainty were feared, forcing clinicians into ethically contradictory practice.21, 26 Religious meanings of DNR as “abandonment” further intensified requests for full resuscitation despite expected suffering.20, 27, 30, 31 Communication framing also mattered: vague or emotionally loaded questions pushed families toward “doing everything,” while delayed conversations left them unprepared, increasing pressure for CPR at the end of life.12, 14, 15, 17, 18 Overall, these forces repeatedly produced non–value-concordant resuscitation and contributed to symbolic CPR, defensive practice, and clinician moral distress.11, 19, 29 Family influence, cultural expectations, religious beliefs, and communication practices played a decisive role in shaping resuscitation decisions across almost all included studies. These factors operated at relational, cultural, and structural levels, often exerting a stronger influence on CPR/DNR pathways than clinical judgement, patient preferences, or ethical principles. Collectively, these studies revealed a complex ethical landscape in which families not patients or clinicians frequently became the final arbiters of resuscitation decisions.

Theme 6. Systemic, legal, and organizational drivers of ethically problematic practice

Ethical problems in resuscitation were consistently described as systemic rather than individual. Across studies, legal ambiguity, organizational culture, and resource constraints made ethically appropriate CPR decisions difficult to enact and routinely produced futile, symbolic, or defensive resuscitation and weak end-of-life communication. Unclear legal status and limited protection for DNR/DNAR and termination-of-resuscitation fostered fear of complaints or prosecution and encouraged “defensive CPR,” especially where policies were absent and informal orders replaced standardized pathways.11, 19, 21, 23, 26, 28, 30 Organizational climates that discouraged ethical discussion, relied on hierarchy, or tolerated shortcuts (including documentation manipulation) further reinforced ethically compromised practice and moral distress.18, 19, 25 Overcrowding, understaffing, and limited palliative capacity pushed default escalation and crisis-driven arrests, while prehospital systems without ToR authority or protocols prolonged unwanted efforts and compelled transport.14, 15, 16, 21, 31 Performance and documentation pressures also incentivized visible intervention regardless of appropriateness.11, 20, 28 Many settings lacked ethical support (debriefing, consultation, legal guidance), leaving clinicians to cope through avoidance or symbolic CPR.15, 16, 22, 25 Overall, problematic CPR patterns reflect structural ethical failure embedded in legal and organizational systems, not clinician moral deficit.

Theme 7. Ethical competence, moral sensitivity, advocacy, and ethics support

Ethical competence (moral sensitivity, policy knowledge, communication, and advocacy) and the availability of ethics support strongly shaped clinicians’ responses to complex resuscitation situations. Moral sensitivity helped clinicians detect futility, suffering, and value-discordant CPR, but when their concerns were overridden, it often intensified moral distress and exhaustion.32, 20, 21, 22 Training gaps and unclear understanding of DNR/DNAR increased uncertainty and anxiety, whereas stronger ethical and policy competence improved confidence and patient-centered decision-making.17, 26, 27, 30, 31 Advocacy for patient values was repeatedly described as essential yet risky: it was strongest in collaborative teams, but commonly constrained by hierarchy and family opposition, leading some clinicians to withdraw or avoid confrontation and accumulate moral residue.11, 12, 15, 19, 23, 29 Across studies, formal ethics supports (ethics consultation, debriefing, case reviews, training) were often absent or inaccessible, leaving clinicians isolated after traumatic codes; when present, these supports reduced distress and strengthened learning and team cohesion.15, 16, 22, 24, 25 Prolonged lack of support was linked to moral erosion, with normalization of slow codes, performative care, or covert withdrawal.11, 19 Overall, ethics competence and support functioned as “ethical safety” mechanisms: when weak, compromised CPR and clinician harm were more likely; when strong, value-concordant care and advocacy were more achievable.

Discussion

Findings indicate that resuscitation ethics are driven less by isolated bedside dilemmas than by systemic tensions that span autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, and justice. Persistent ambiguity in DNR/DNAR pathways undermined autonomy by leaving preferences unknown, poorly communicated, or overridden by families and institutions, echoing broader evidence that delayed end-of-life discussions promote crisis-driven interventions.23, 26, 28, 30, 33, 34 Recurrent futile or symbolic CPR challenged beneficence and nonmaleficence by prolonging suffering without meaningful benefit; although ethical analyses argue clinicians are not obliged to provide non-beneficial treatment, legal risk, organizational culture, and hierarchy continued to sustain these practices.11, 16, 19, 24, 35, 36 Widespread moral distress reflected the gap between clinicians’ ethical judgement and constrained action, consistent with Jameton’s account of institutional barriers producing enduring moral residue and harm, intensified here by legal, cultural, and relational pressures.20, 21, 22, 25, 37, 38 Justice especially distributive justice was implicated where overcrowding, resource limits, and weak palliative pathways drove CPR overuse and inadequate end-of-life care, consistent with analyses linking structural inequity to default aggressive intervention when alternatives are unavailable.14, 21, 31, 39, 40 Overall, resuscitation decisions emerged as multi-level ethical tensions across all principles, supporting the need for a coherent guiding framework.

These findings largely align with international resuscitation-ethics literature, while extending it by showing how recurring patterns are structurally reproduced across settings.1, 2 The prominence of DNR/DNAR ambiguity echoes long-standing concerns that inconsistent policies undermine patient-centered decisions and contribute to unwanted CPR; similar cross-regional variation has driven repeated calls for clearer, unified guidance.41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46 Our dataset also mirrors global reports of futile CPR and symbolic/slow-code (“ceremonial”) resuscitation performed despite minimal benefit, often shaped by litigation fears, cultural expectations, and institutional norms, suggesting a structural response to unresolved ethical and communicative tensions rather than isolated clinician behavior.47, 48, 49, 50 Moral distress findings are consistent with robust evidence in critical care, oncology, and emergency medicine, but this review adds a clearer trajectory from distress to moral residue/disengagement under repeated ambiguity, hierarchy, and family-driven CPR.6, 51, 52, 53, 54 Family influence similarly aligns with prior scholarship, yet this review more explicitly documents cases where family preferences override patient wishes or documented DNR orders, producing ethically compromised pathways that warrant further study.55, 56 Overall, identified barriers (overcrowding, weak policy infrastructure, poor communication, limited ethics support) reaffirm the need for integrated, system-level ethical frameworks rather than sole focus on individual clinicians.

Together, the seven synthesized themes revealed cross-cutting patterns that informed the development of a conceptual model that seeks to interpret how ethical dynamics may unfold during resuscitation decision-making. The Ethical Pathways Model (EPM), presented in Fig. 2, illustrates how multi-level determinants converge around the proportionality of care decision during resuscitation and shape potential ethical pathways in clinical practice. While existing literature highlights isolated challenges such as moral distress, futile CPR, or communication failures, it lacks an integrated framework that conceptualizes how systemic, relational, and individual factors may interact to shape ethical trajectories in resuscitation. The model proposed here aims to address this gap by conceptualizing resuscitation ethics as a dynamic process influenced by multi-level determinants that may shape decision pathways at the bedside.Fig. 2Ethical Pathways Model (EPM) of resuscitation decision‑making.

Ethical Pathways Model (EPM) of resuscitation decision‑making.

The model is structured around four interrelated domains: structural determinants, relational determinants, individual determinants, and the proportionality‑of‑care decision point during resuscitation. First, structural determinants including legal ambiguity, organizational culture, resource constraints, and cultural or religious norms form the ethical “pressure landscape” within which clinicians operate. These factors were consistently described across included studies as shaping what actions were perceived as permissible or obligatory.21, 24, 28, 30 Second, the model incorporates relational determinants at the meso level, including family expectations, hierarchy within clinical teams, and communication patterns that shape how decisions unfold during resuscitation.15, 17, 20, 23 Third, the model includes individual determinants at the micro level, such as clinicians’ moral sensitivity, ethical competence, and personal value frameworks. These individual capacities influence how clinicians interpret clinical situations, recognize ethical tensions, and respond during acute deterioration or cardiac arrest.

At the center of the model lies the proportionality-of-care decision point during resuscitation, where contextual, relational, and individual factors converge. The model proposes two primary decision pathways: An Ethically Aligned Pathway, characterized by clarity, shared understanding, and value-concordant decisions; and an Ethically Conflicted Pathway, characterized by ambiguity, disagreement, and constrained moral agency. When ambiguity dominates such as unclear DNR orders, conflicting family expectations, or team hierarchy clinicians experience moral distress, which can lead to either adaptive ethical responses (advocacy, consultation, debriefing) or maladaptive behaviors, including symbolic CPR, defensive resuscitation, documentation manipulation, or moral disengagement.11, 19, 29

Finally, the model conceptualizes clinical, professional, and organizational outcomes, acknowledging that ethically compromised pathways lead not only to patient suffering and misaligned care but also to clinician burnout, moral residue, and long-term erosion of professional integrity.12, 22, 25 These outcomes then feed back into the broader system, reinforcing or reshaping normative and organizational expectations.

This EPM advances current understanding by offering a holistic, mechanism-based explanation of how ethically problematic CPR practices emerge and persist. Unlike prior frameworks that focus on isolated components such as moral distress or decision-making autonomy, the proposed model integrates structural, relational, and psychological dimensions into a unified conceptual structure. As such, it provides a foundation for future empirical testing, education, policy development, and ethics support interventions aimed at improving the quality and integrity of resuscitation decision-making.

Accordingly, the EPM should be understood as an interpretive and hypothesis‑generating conceptual framework rather than a definitive explanatory model. As such, it provides a conceptual basis for future empirical inquiry, through which aspects of the model may be further examined and refined.

Implications for practice, policy, and education

The EPM highlights the need for coordinated strategies across clinical, organizational, and policy levels. Clinically, early and structured communication about goals of care rather than crisis-driven decisions can reduce ambiguity around DNR/DNAR status and prevent unwanted or symbolic CPR. Interprofessional discussions and consistent documentation should become routine in acute and critical care. Given their central role at the bedside, nurses require strengthened moral agency and clearer involvement in resuscitation decision-making to prevent ethically compromised practice.

At the organizational level, clear and accessible policies for DNR, DNAR, and termination-of-resuscitation are essential. Health systems should also establish practical support mechanisms such as rapid ethics consultation and structured debriefings, which help clinicians navigate ethically complex resuscitation events and mitigate moral distress.

Education must strengthen clinicians’ ethical competence, communication skills, and understanding of local legal frameworks. Training that incorporates case-based discussion and simulation can empower clinicians to address ambiguity, family pressure, and hierarchical constraints more confidently.

Finally, alignment between institutional policies and national legislation is necessary to ensure that clinicians can deliver ethically sound, patient-centered resuscitation without fear of legal or organizational repercussions.

Strengths and limitations

This review’s main strength is its globally diverse evidence base, synthesizing 22 studies across regions, health systems, and clinical settings. This breadth supports transferability and informed development of a multi-level model of ethical decision-making in resuscitation. Using reflexive thematic analysis enabled an interpretive synthesis that generated a new conceptual model (EPM) explaining how systemic, relational, and individual factors interact to shape ethical outcomes in CPR and DNR decisions.

Key limitations include the narrative (non-systematic) design, which increases risk of publication or selection bias. Heterogeneity in methods, contexts, and publication outlets limits direct comparability across studies. Although all included studies met the eligibility criteria and underwent quality appraisal, variability in reporting standards and methodological rigor may influence the strength of evidence underlying certain themes. In addition, the predominance of qualitative studies may restrict generalizability despite the depth of contextual insights they provide. Finally, the model is literature-derived rather than prospectively tested, so empirical validation is needed in specific clinical settings.

Future research directions

Future research may explore ways to empirically examine aspects of the EPM proposed in this review, particularly through mixed‑methods or longitudinal designs that investigate how contextual, organizational, and relational factors interact during real‑time resuscitation decision‑making. Cross‑cultural studies could further illuminate how legal frameworks, communication norms, and family dynamics shape ethical pathways across diverse healthcare systems. Additionally, future work might examine whether interventions such as ethics education, structured communication training, and institutional ethics support (e.g., rapid ethics consultations or structured team debriefings) influence clinicians’ experiences of moral distress and the delivery of value‑concordant care. Finally, empirical research may help identify strategies to reduce symbolic or non‑beneficial CPR practices within acute and critical care settings.

Conclusion

This narrative review shows that ethical challenges in resuscitation stem from the convergence of legal, organizational, relational, and individual factors rather than isolated clinical dilemmas. Across diverse settings, futile and symbolic CPR, moral distress, and persistent ambiguity surrounding DNR/DNAR decisions reflected the systemic nature of these ethical tensions. Through thematic synthesis, an emergent Ethical Pathways Model (EPM) was developed to explain how ethical clarity or conflict unfolds at the bedside. Strengthening communication practices, enhancing ethical competence, and implementing supportive institutional policies are essential for promoting ethically coherent, value-aligned resuscitation decisions and improving outcomes for patients and clinicians alike.
