CCR1 as a potential regulator of neutrophil-mediated pathogenesis in post-acute ischemic stroke: a multi-omics Mendelian randomization
Authors: Xiaoguang Zhang, Xuerui Xiang, Ruoyu Li, Jiehong Huang, Yi Yang, Hongkai Yao, Xining Ren, Liang Hu, Yunping Song, Lingjing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe genetic and immunological mechanisms underlying post-acute ischemic stroke (IS) remain incompletely understood, particularly those involving neutrophil-mediated inflammation. This study aimed to identify genes associated with IS using a multi-omics Mendelian randomization framework.MethodsSummary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) was used to identify upstream genetic regulators of neutrophil-driven pathogenesis. We systematically screened 949 neutrophil-related genes using SMR analyses across multi-omics quantitative trait loci (QTL) datasets (methylation, expression, and protein). We then validated temporal expression patterns in independent IS cohorts and constructed a diagnostic nomogram to assess clinical discrimination.ResultsMulti-omics SMR analysis identified CCR1, DEFA4, SH2B3 and CDKN1A as potential genes associated with IS risk. In independent clinical validation cohorts, CCR1 and DEFA4 were consistently upregulated in the peripheral blood of IS patients. Notably, CCR1 emerged as a central hub gene; its expression exhibited a potential association with neutrophil abundance (R = 0.31) and functional hyperactivation pathways, including chemotaxis and cytotoxicity. Furthermore, a diagnostic nomogram integrating CCR1 and DEFA4 demonstrated high discriminatory power (AUC = 0.875), underscoring their potential as precision biomarkers.ConclusionsIdentified as a potential neutrophil-related genetic factor, CCR1 is associated with ischemic injury and may contribute to neutrophil chemotaxis and functional hyperactivation. Consequently, targeting CCR1 offers a precision therapeutic strategy to attenuate neuroinflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02132-x.

Background

Ischemic stroke (IS) is one of the leading causes of mortality and long-term disability worldwide, accounting for the vast majority of stroke cases and imposing a substantial clinical and socioeconomic burden [1]. IS typically results from the sudden occlusion of cerebral arteries, leading to abrupt cessation of cerebral blood flow and initiating a cascade of pathological processes, including energy failure, oxidative stress, and sterile inflammation, that culminate in neuronal death and brain infarction [2]. Despite significant advances in acute interventions, such as intravenous thrombolysis, which is highly effective within the first 4.5 h, a large number of patients miss this narrow therapeutic window [3, 4]. Therefore, exploring alternative or complementary therapeutic strategies is a critical area of future ischemic stroke research.

Beyond the 4.5-hour thrombolytic window, a secondary wave of injury driven by sterile inflammation and the recruitment of immune cells, particularly neutrophils, becomes a major contributor to brain damage [5, 6]. This inflammatory cascade, characterized by rapid and substantial neutrophil infiltration into the ischemic penumbra, progressively converts salvageable tissue into irreversible infarction, exacerbates neurological deficits, reduces the effectiveness of late thrombolysis, and may increase the risk of hemorrhagic transformation. Among polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs), neutrophils represent the predominant and earliest infiltrating population following ischemic stroke. Neutrophils contribute to tissue damage via mechanisms such as the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), which disrupt the blood–brain barrier, promote thrombosis, and amplify local inflammation [7, 8]. Conversely, neutrophils may aid in debris clearance and tissue repair [9]. However, the upstream genetic regulation that may influence neutrophil abundance, activation status, and functional phenotype during IS remains poorly understood. Which genetic “switches” govern these processes, and how their variation influences disease risk and progression, remains unclear.

Therefore, a therapeutic strategy that mitigates neutrophil-mediated inflammation could, in principle, protect the ischemic penumbra, stabilize the neurovascular unit, and thereby extend the effective window for reperfusion therapy. To identify key genetic regulators that may serve as tractable targets, we employed a multi-omics Mendelian randomization (SMR) approach, which provides a powerful causal inference framework [10]. By integrating genome-wide association study (GWAS) data with multi-level molecular quantitative trait loci (QTL) datasets, including DNA methylation QTLs (mQTLs), expression QTLs (eQTLs), and protein abundance QTLs (pQTLs), this strategy allows for the systematic identification of genes whose regulation is causally linked to disease risk [11]. This multi-omics SMR strategy not only improves statistical power and robustness but also allows for the pinpointing of key variants.

In this study, we applied an integrated multi-omics SMR approach to systematically identify neutrophil-related genes with potential effects on IS risk, followed by clinical transcriptomic validation and functional network analyses. Specifically, we aimed to (1) comprehensively screen neutrophil-related genes across mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL datasets for associations with IS; (2) elucidate potential genes and their molecular networks; and (3) provide novel candidate biomarkers and therapeutic targets for precision intervention in ischemic stroke.

Materials and methods

Study design

We systematically integrated SMR analysis with functional validation and clinical model construction (Fig. 1). We first identified protein-coding genes related to neutrophils using the GeneCards database, then extracted cis-acting QTL data—including methylation QTLs (mQTLs), expression QTLs (eQTLs), and protein QTLs (pQTLs)—from large public repositories. These datasets were integrated with IS GWAS summary statistics from the International Stroke Genetics Consortium (ISGC, MEGASTROKE project).

SMR with colocalization testing was applied to detect potential associations, with the HEIDI test used to distinguish pleiotropy from linkage. Key findings were validated using independent IS GWAS datasets from the GWAS Catalog and FinnGen, and results were integrated across mQTL–eQTL and eQTL–pQTL levels to prioritize candidates.

Functional relevance was explored by validating gene expression in independent IS cohorts from GEO, estimating immune cell composition (using CIBERSORTx) and immune activation states (using ssGSEA), and performing GO/KEGG enrichment analyses. Finally, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using STRING, and hub genes were identified via the Maximum Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm. All analyses followed STROBE-MR guidelines and utilized ethically approved, publicly available summary-level data [12].

Data sources for SMR analysis

Neutrophil-related genes were systematically identified in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org) by searching the keyword “neutrophil” and filtering for protein-coding genes. To ensure high-confidence functional associations and minimize false positives derived from incidental text-mining co-occurrences, we applied a stringent relevance score threshold of > 5 [13]. At the time of data retrieval, this filtering criteria excluded 10,288 low-scoring genes out of 11,237 total hits, yielding 949 target genes for downstream analysis.

For IS GWAS data, the primary discovery dataset was obtained from the MEGASTROKE consortium (GWAS ID: MEGASTROKE.2.AIS.EUR.OUT), comprising 34,217 cases and 406,111 controls of European ancestry, with 8,340,184 SNPs analyzed [14]. Two independent datasets were used for replication: the UK Biobank (GWAS ID: GCST90104540) with 62,100 cases and 1,234,808 controls (7,482,033SNPs) [15], and FinnGen Release 5 (GWAS ID: finngen_R5_I9_STR_EXH, https://r5.finngen.fi/) with 10,551 cases and 202,223 controls (16,380,444SNPs). These datasets are fully independent, with no sample overlap.

Molecular QTL data were obtained from multiple large-scale resources: blood mQTL summary statistics from a meta-analysis of the Brisbane Systems Genetics Study (n = 614) and the Lothian Birth Cohorts (n = 1366), totaling 1980 European individuals [16]; blood eQTL data from the eQTLGen consortium (n = 31,684, majority European ancestry) [17]; and pQTL data from a GWAS of 54,219 individuals of European descent [18]. For tissue-specific validation, brain cortex cis-eQTL summary data from BrainMeta V2 (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr/#eQTLsummarydata) was used, integrating RNA-seq and genome-wide SNP data from 2865 cortex samples of 2,443 unrelated European individuals [19]. Details of all datasets are summarized in Table S1.

Mendelian randomization and colocalization analyses

SMR analyses were performed to evaluate relationships between neutrophil-related mQTLs, eQTLs, pQTLs, and IS. The SMR approach integrates GWAS summary statistics with cis-QTL data to test whether the association between a genetic variant and phenotype is mediated through molecular traits [20].

Instrumental variable (IV) selection

Cis-QTLs were defined as SNPs within ± 1000 kb of the target gene. For each probe, the top associated cis-SNP was selected under a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸ [19, 21]. SNPs with allele frequency differences > 0.2 between QTL and outcome datasets were excluded, and a maximum mismatch rate of 0.05 was permitted. LD clumping was performed with r² < 0.05 and a window size of 1000 kb (1000 Genomes Project European panel).

SMR analysis

SMR analyses were performed using SMR software v1.3.1. The primary SMR test was complemented by multi-SNP SMR (SMR-multi, --smr-multi), accounting for multiple independent association signals within a locus. For SMR-multi, all SNPs within the probe window (± 1000 kb) with P < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸ and LD r² < 0.9 with the top SNP were included [22]. The HEIDI test was used to distinguish pleiotropy from LD, with P-HEIDI > 0.01 indicating no evidence of linkage. Results were considered significant if P-SMR < 0.05, P-SMR-multi < 0.05, and P-HEIDI > 0.01. This relaxed threshold was well-recognized and necessitated by the complex genomic architecture of the genomic loci, where multiple tightly linked variants may exist [23]. FDR was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg method [24].

Beyond direct QTL–IS analyses, we also examined relationships between mQTLs and eQTLs, as well as between eQTLs and pQTLs, by treating one molecular trait as the exposure and another as the outcome. This multi-omics SMR integration aimed to identify genes with consistent signals across regulatory layers.

Colocalization analysis

Colocalization was performed using the coloc R package [25], a Bayesian method estimating posterior probabilities for five hypotheses (H0–H4). This Bayesian method estimates posterior probabilities for five hypotheses: H0 (no association with either trait), H1 (association with the first trait only), H2 (association with the second trait only), H3 (independent associations with both traits), and H4 (a shared causal variant for both traits). Analyses were restricted to ± 1000 kb around the top cis-QTL for each locus, with PPH4 > 0.5 indicating strong evidence of colocalization [26].

All statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.3.0. Manhattan and forest plots were generated using ggplot2 and forestplot, respectively. SMR Locus and Effect Plots were generated using code adapted from Zhu et al. [20].

Validation and mechanistic exploration in patient cohorts

Gene expression validation

Peripheral blood transcriptomic datasets from two independent IS cohorts were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE58294 (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0, whole blood from 69 IS patients and 23 controls) [27], GSE16561 (Illumina HumanRef-8 v3.0, whole blood from 39 IS patients and 24 controls), and GSE106520 (Illumina HiSeq 2500, 16 IS patients and 16 controls) [28]. Raw data were preprocessed using standard platform-specific procedures, including probe-to-gene mapping, exclusion of unannotated probes, and summarization of multiple probes per gene by median expression. To avoid technical biases and complex cross-platform batch effects, the three datasets were not merged into a single matrix. Instead, differential expression between IS and control groups was evaluated independently within each cohort using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with multiple testing correction by the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The consistency of the expression trends (e.g., upregulation) across these diverse platforms was utilized to confirm the biological robustness of the prioritized genes.

Immune cell deconvolution

Immune cell composition was estimated from normalized transcriptome data using CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu) with the LM22 leukocyte gene signature matrix [29]. The relative abundance of 22 immune cell subsets was inferred using 1,000 permutations. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Visualizations (boxplots, stacked bar plots) were generated in R (ggplot2).

Functional pathway analysis

Neutrophil-related functional enrichment analysis was conducted using single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) (R package GSVA) [30]. Neutrophil-related gene sets were obtained from the GO Biological Process (GO BP) category of the MSigDB (via msigdbr), restricted to 'terms containing "neutrophil”'. Enrichment scores were computed for each sample, with group differences assessed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Correlation analysis

Associations between the expression of selected genes and immune cell fractions were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Significant relationships were visualized using scatter plots.

Functional enrichment analysis

Significant genes from SMR analysis (eQTL and pQTL) were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment (clusterProfiler) [31]. GO enrichment covered Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). In KEGG analysis, human disease pathways were excluded to focus on biological processes. Significance was defined as adjusted P < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg); top enriched terms were visualized with clusterProfiler.

PPI network analysis

To elucidate system-level functional relationships, a PPI network for candidate neutrophil-related genes was constructed using STRING (version 12.0; https://string-db.org/), limited to Homo sapiens and a minimum interaction confidence score of > 0.4 [32]. Both experimentally validated and computationally predicted interactions were included. The network was visualized in Cytoscape, and hub proteins were identified using the MCC algorithm in the cytoHubba plugin; the top 10 by MCC score were designated as hub nodes.

Construction and validation of the clinical diagnostic nomogram

To assess the clinical utility of identified diagnostic markers, a nomogram model was constructed using the GSE58294 dataset and the “rms” package in R, based on the expression levels of CCR1 and DEFA4. Model discrimination was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), reflecting the ability of the model to distinguish between ischemic stroke patients and controls. Model calibration was assessed using calibration curves, comparing predicted probabilities with observed outcomes. Clinical net benefit was evaluated by decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curves (CIC), providing insight into the potential utility of the model across various threshold probabilities. The nomogram was constructed in the training cohort (GSE58294) using the expression levels of CCR1 and DEFA4 to predict the probability of ischemic stroke. Finally, to test the robustness of these markers in stroke subtypes, CCR1 and DEFA4 expression was examined in cardioembolic stroke samples.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.0). Two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for group comparisons in transcriptomics and immune cell proportions. Pearson correlation was used for gene–immune cell associations. Enrichment analyses (GO, KEGG) were performed with clusterProfiler, with adjusted P < 0.05 indicating significance.

Results

Multi-omics SMR identifies neutrophil-related genes associated with ischemic stroke

We systematically integrated SMR analysis with functional validation and clinical model construction. A total of 949 neutrophil-related protein-coding genes were retrieved from the GeneCards database (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms of key prioritized targets, such as DEFA4, are conceptually illustrated to provide biological context (Fig. S1).

mQTL level

We identified 143 CpG sites mapping to 77 genes associated with IS risk (Table S2), of which 32 sites in 17 genes displayed colocalization evidence (PPH4 > 0.5, Figs. 2A, S2). For example, methylation at cg11589536 within CCR1 was negatively correlated with IS risk (OR = 0.954, 95% CI: 0.924–0.985, P = 0.004, P-multi = 0.00035), as was cg18146398 (OR = 0.859, 95% CI: 0.768–0.961, P = 0.008, P-multi = 0.008). Validation in FinnGen confirmed 14 CpG sites in eight genes (including CDKN1A, ITGAX, MMP13, NFATC1, PTK2, RUNX1, SFXN4, SH2B3), and 63 CpG sites in 37 genes (including ANXA3, CANT1, CCR1, CD34, CD40, CDH1, CDKN1A) were replicated in the UK Biobank (Table S2).

eQTL level

SMR analysis identified 21 genes associated with IS risk (Table S3). Among them, 4 core neutrophil-related genes (CCR1, DEFA4, SH2B3, and CDKN1A) were prioritized based on their association signals and biological relevance, with CCR1 expression positively associated with IS risk (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.019–1.103, P = 0.0036, P-multi = 0.0036, PPH4 = 0.56) (Table 1; Fig. 2B). These findings suggest a shared genetic basis between the expression of these genes and IS susceptibility (Fig. S2).

pQTL level

12 proteins were associated with IS risk (Fig. 2C, Table S4). Elevated SH2B3 protein levels were associated with increased risk (OR = 2.002, 95% CI: 1.427–2.809, P = 5.82 × 10− 5, P-multi = 5.82 × 10− 5, P-FDR = 0.023), whereas higher MMP12 abundance was protective (OR = 0.899, 95% CI: 0.864–0.936, P = 1.83 × 10⁻7, P-multi = 0.0017, P-FDR = 0.0004). Three proteins met colocalization criteria (PPH4 > 0.5). Importantly, MMP12 showed replication across both FinnGen and UK Biobank cohorts, while SH2B3 was successfully replicated in the UK Biobank (Table S4).

Network analysis (Fig. 2D–E) revealed that CCR1 serves as a central hub, exhibiting dense interactions with other prioritized immune-related genes. To elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms and determine whether the identified methylation changes and gene expression alterations are mechanistically connected, we further conducted SMR analysis treating mQTLs as the exposure and eQTLs as the outcome (Table 2). This analysis revealed significant regulatory relationships between specific methylation sites and the expression of core prioritized genes, indicating that they are interconnected rather than independent factors. Notably, for the central hub gene CCR1, genetic variants (SNPs) dictate methylation levels at specific CpG sites (e.g., cg10499974, cg12441928, and cg18146398), which in turn are strongly associated with CCR1 gene expression (PSMR < 1 × 10− 9). For instance, the methylation level at cg18146398 showed a significant causal effect on CCR1 expression (OR = 0.075, 95% CI: 0.034–0.167), linking the SNP at this methylation site directly to transcriptional regulation. Similar methylation-to-expression regulatory axes were confirmed for other key genes, including DEFA4 (cg07089660), CDKN1A (cg15474579), and SH2B3 (cg26359133 and cg01356824). Further analysis of eQTLs and pQTLs showed no significant overlapping effects (Table S5), while tissue-specific validation in brain cortex confirmed the associations of TDP2 and ANXA11 with IS (Table S6). This integrative multi-omics analysis established a cascading epigenetic-transcriptional mechanism for a panel of neutrophil-related genes in IS, laying the foundation for downstream exploration.

Immune infiltration and functional enrichment analysis reveal pro-inflammatory activation in IS

To explore the immune landscape and functional consequences of the identified genes, we performed immune cell infiltration analysis and functional enrichment (Fig. 3). Immune infiltration analysis using CIBERSORTx in the discovery cohort (GSE58294) revealed a marked alteration in the immune microenvironment, characterized by a significant increase in neutrophil proportions in IS patients compared to controls (P = 5.2 × 10− 12; Fig. 3A,B). Notably, as CIBERSORTx estimates relative cell fractions, this increase may partly reflect compositional shifts due to reductions in other immune cell populations rather than an absolute expansion of neutrophils. Furthermore, ssGSEA analysis indicated a heightened functional state of neutrophils in IS patients. We observed significant enrichment of pathways related to neutrophil recruitment and activity, including regulation of neutrophil chemotaxis (P = 3.00 × 10− 6), neutrophil clearance (P = 1.97 × 10− 5), and positive regulation of neutrophil migration (P = 7.25 × 10− 4).

Functional enrichment analysis (GO and KEGG) of the 21 SMR-prioritized genes underscored their specific involvement in neutrophil-mediated immunity (Fig. 3C–D). The most significantly enriched biological processes included neutrophil degranulation (P = 3.76 × 10− 18) and neutrophil activation involved in immune response (P = 4.14 × 10− 18), as well as leukocyte migration. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted the NF-kappa B signaling pathway and Osteoclast differentiation as key molecular mechanisms (P < 0.05; Fig. 3E–F).

Correlation analysis further validated the link between the prioritized gene CCR1 and neutrophil accumulation. In the GSE58294 cohort, CCR1 expression exhibited a positive correlation with neutrophil abundance (R = 0.31, P = 0.0024; Fig. 3G). Expanding on this, we further evaluated the associations of CCR1, DEFA4, and SH2B3 with a broader spectrum of immune cell subtypes (e.g., monocytes, B cells, and T cell subsets). The results demonstrated significant correlations between these core genes and specific immune infiltrates, reinforcing their extensive involvement in the post-stroke immune microenvironment (Fig. S3). Importantly, this positive association was successfully replicated in the independent validation cohort GSE16561 (P < 0.05; Fig. 3H), reinforcing the robustness of the CCR1-neutrophil relationship across different populations. Consistent with these findings, we also observed that both CCR1 (P < 0.001) and DEFA4 (P < 0.05) were significantly upregulated in IS patients compared to controls across both cohorts (Fig. 3G-H). Although SH2B3 presented a significant downregulated trend (P < 0.0001), the consistent upregulation of CCR1 and DEFA4 highlights their potential as reliable biomarkers.

Diagnostic nomogram based on CCR1 and DEFA4 expression demonstrates high clinical utility

Given the association of CCR1 and DEFA4 with IS, we constructed a diagnostic nomogram based on these two genes using the GSE58294 training cohort (Fig. 4A). The model demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability, achieving an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.796–0.954) (Fig. 4B). At the optimal threshold of 0.722, the model displayed a sensitivity of 84.1% and a specificity of 73.9%.

The calibration curve indicated good agreement between the predicted risks and actual observations (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) confirmed that the combined nomogram provided a higher net benefit compared to the “treat-all” or “treat-none” strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities. Notably, the combined model also outperformed the clinical utility of CCR1 or DEFA4 as single-gene predictors (Fig. 4D), highlighting the value of the multi-gene approach for IS screening.

These results indicate that the CCR1 plus DEFA4-based nomogram offers discrimination and calibration for IS diagnosis, with substantial potential for clinical application.

Discussion

Our multi-omics SMR framework prioritized 32 mQTLs, 11 eQTLs, and 3 pQTLs with strong colocalization evidence, with CCR1, CCR3, CDKN1A, DEFA4, SH2B3, and ANXA11 consistently highlighted. Among these, CCR1 emerged as the central hub, supported by colocalized methylation and expression signals, consistent SMR effect directions, upregulation in multiple IS cohorts, and a dominant position in immune-inflammatory PPI networks.

CCR1 functions as a critical mediator of leukocyte trafficking by sensing chemotactic gradients from ligands such as CCL3 and CCL5 [33]. In IS, excessive CCR1 signaling can facilitate the rapid influx of neutrophils into the ischemic territory, fueling an amplified inflammatory cascade. While most existing studies are based on observational or experimental models, our results provide genetic evidence supporting a role for CCR1 in IS susceptibility. In this study, our multi-omics framework provides convergent evidence supporting CCR1 across mQTL and eQTL levels. Crucially, our mQTL-to-eQTL SMR analysis confirms that these are not independent factors, but rather form a direct ‘SNP–methylation–expression’ mechanistic cascade. Specifically, genetic variants (SNPs) dictate the methylation levels at sites such as cg18146398, which in turn causally drive CCR1 upregulation. This indicates that genetic susceptibility alters local DNA methylation (potentially modulating chromatin accessibility) to directly promote CCR1 overexpression and increase IS risk. Moreover, CIBERSORT immune infiltration analysis confirmed a significant increase in peripheral neutrophil proportions in patients compared with controls. Correlation analysis further demonstrated a significant positive association between CCR1 expression levels and neutrophil abundance, suggesting CCR1 as a potential regulator associated with neutrophil recruitment in the acute phase. Mechanistically, CCR1 overactivation may heighten neutrophil chemokine sensitivity and motility, accelerating tissue infiltration and fostering a hyperactivated effector phenotype [34]. ssGSEA analysis revealed that these neutrophils were not in a resting state but functionally hyperactivated, exhibiting enhanced chemotaxis, activation, and cytotoxicity scores, indicating active participation in post-stroke neuroinflammation. Recruited neutrophils may then contribute to secondary injury through degranulation, oxidative burst, and NET/NETosis-mediated microvascular obstruction, thereby exacerbating ischemic damage [35]. Preclinical studies show that pharmacologic blockade or genetic deletion of CCR1 reduces neutrophil accumulation and improves neurological outcomes [36], further supporting its pathogenic role in post-stroke inflammation. Therefore, CCR1 upregulation and its strong correlation with neutrophil abundance indicate that CCR1 signaling may represent a key checkpoint in the post-stroke inflammatory cascade [37, 38]. Targeting CCR1 may potentially attenuate this hyperacute inflammatory surge, stabilize the neurovascular unit, and prolong the interval during which reperfusion can be applied more safely and effectively.

Other identified core genes may also contribute to IS pathophysiology through parallel or complementary pathways, collectively forming a neutrophil-centered inflammatory network, with CCR1 as a key upstream regulator. CDKN1A, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, regulates neutrophil apoptosis and inflammatory resolution, and its upregulation in neutrophils after IS has been linked to injury severity [39, 40]. DEFA4, an antimicrobial peptide, can exacerbate ischemic injury through endothelial toxicity, blood–brain barrier disruption, and promotion of NET formation [41]. SH2B3 (LNK) modulates hematopoietic and inflammatory signaling, with variants affecting neutrophil and platelet function, potentially influencing NET-mediated thrombosis [42, 43]. In this study, we observed a discrepancy for SH2B3 between SMR and transcriptomic results. SMR suggests that genetically predicted higher SH2B3 expression increases IS risk, whereas transcriptomic data show acute-phase downregulation. This likely reflects the distinction between lifelong genetic susceptibility and dynamic, stage-specific responses. While SMR captures baseline genetically regulated expression, transcriptomic changes reflect acute responses to ischemic injury. As a negative regulator of hematopoietic and inflammatory signaling, SH2B3 downregulation in the acute phase may represent a compensatory mechanism that releases inhibitory constraints to promote neutrophil activation. ANXA11, involved in calcium homeostasis and stress granule dynamics, may regulate neutrophil-driven thromboinflammation during ischemic injury [44]. Collectively, these data suggest that genetic predisposition may potentiate CCR1 signaling while synergizing with multiple neutrophil-associated effectors, thereby amplifying neuroinflammation and shaping stroke outcomes.

In our integrative SMR analysis, CCR1 emerged as a core neutrophil-related susceptibility gene for IS. Subsequent validation in multiple independent GEO cohorts confirmed that CCR1 expression is significantly elevated in the peripheral blood of IS patients. This transition, from a purely genetic finding to an experimentally validated expression change, positions CCR1 as a potential blood-based biomarker for early diagnosis or risk stratification. Beyond its diagnostic potential, CCR1 represents a therapeutically tractable target [35, 45]. As a cell-surface chemokine receptor, CCR1 already has small-molecule antagonists and monoclonal antibodies in clinical development for other inflammatory diseases [46]. Our study provides a rationale for repurposing these agents in IS. Importantly, the therapeutic goal would not be to simply reduce CCR1 expression, but rather to inhibit CCR1-mediated neutrophil recruitment and functional hyperactivation—thereby attenuating neuroinflammation while preserving essential immune defense. Such targeted modulation could mitigate secondary ischemic injury, while avoiding the risks associated with broad immunosuppression.

The methodological strength of this study lies in its use of a multi-omics SMR framework that integrates methylation QTL, expression QTL, and protein QTL datasets. By cross-validating genetic associations across multiple molecular layers, this approach offers a more comprehensive approach than single-omics or conventional association studies. The incorporation of colocalization analysis further mitigated the potential impact of horizontal pleiotropy, thereby enhancing the reliability of causal estimates. However, as an instrumental variable approach, SMR cannot fully establish definitive causality, and the observed associations require experimental validation.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the genetic datasets used were primarily derived from European ancestry populations, and the applicability of the conclusions to other ethnic groups requires cautious extrapolation. Second, although SMR and colocalization reduce confounding, complex mediation and residual pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded. Thirdly, acute-phase (3–24 h) human brain tissue data are unavailable due to ethical constraints, precluding direct CCR1 validation. We therefore relied on peripheral blood-based data, which may not fully recapitulate CNS-specific inflammatory processes. Fourthly, our study is primarily based on integrative association analyses and lacks direct functional validation. Therefore, although CCR1 and other identified genes are associated with IS and neutrophil-related processes, their roles and underlying mechanisms require further confirmation through in vitro and in vivo experimental studies. Fifthly, our study does not provide cell-type-specific evidence for CCR1. Future studies using single-cell transcriptomics or sorted-cell functional assays are needed to clarify the role of CCR1 specifically within neutrophils during ischemic injury. Sixthly, our validation datasets are derived from peripheral blood and may not fully capture gene expression changes within the ischemic brain. Consequently, observed increases in CCR1 and DEFA4 could reflect systemic inflammation rather than local neuroinflammatory processes at the site of injury. This study represents an initial step toward developing genetically informed diagnostic biomarkers. Future prospective, multi-center real-world clinical cohort studies are urgently needed to collect complete clinical scores and blood specimens for external validation, and to assess the potential clinical value of the nomogram centered on CCR1 and DEFA4 beyond conventional predictors.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this integrated multi-omics MR study establishes CCR1 not merely as a susceptibility gene for ischemic stroke, but as a pivotal regulatory hub that orchestrates neutrophil-mediated inflammation. Targeting CCR1 therefore holds transformative potential to modulate neuroinflammation. Collectively, these findings highlight CCR1 as both a mechanistic nexus and a high-priority therapeutic target, offering new prospects for precision intervention and the future development of stroke therapies.
