LEF1 gene mutation impairs the intestinal barrier and causes diarrhea
Authors: Jianan Jie, Mengling Qiu, Xing Liu, Qingqing Zhang, Jing Xie, Lanlan Geng, Sitang Gong, Yang Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whole-exome sequencing identified a novel de novo heterozygous LEF1 variant (c.880 C > T; p.Pro294Ser) in a male pediatric patient who presented with intractable chronic yellow-green watery diarrhea with onset at 18 months of age. The condition persisted for 4 years and was complicated by protein-energy malnutrition and growth failure refractory to standard therapy. Gastrointestinal endoscopy showed mucosal edema accompanied by mild chronic inflammation, and this variant was not present in public population genomic databases. Functional validation was performed in Lef1 (c.876 C > G; p.Pro292Ser) knock-in mice (the murine ortholog of the human LEF1 p.Pro294Ser variant), and revealed increased susceptibility to dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced diarrhea, intrinsic intestinal structural abnormalities, and impaired intestinal barrier integrity. Molecular and proteomic analyses revealed downregulated expression of tight junction proteins and aquaporin − 4 in both mutant mice and patient-derived intestinal tissues, concomitant with dysregulated ion transport, and aberrant inflammatory pathways. Mechanistically, the LEF1 variant enhances its own expression and nuclear accumulation, driving Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) and subsequent intestinal barrier disruption. Collectively, these findings establish LEF1 variant as a pathogenic driver of pediatric chronic diarrhea, broaden the functional role of LEF1 in intestinal homeostasis, and identifies its potential utility as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for this disorder.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40348-026-00240-5.

Introduction

Diarrhea ranks among the top ten causes of death in children worldwide, which is attributable to infectious agents, immune dysregulation, monogenic disorders, and environmental exposures [1]. In 2021, diarrheal diseases accounted for 59 million Disability-Adjusted Life-Years (DALYs) globally, of which 30.9 million (52.4%) affected children under 5 years of age [2, 3], imposing a substantial public health burden on the pediatric population. Monogenic variants are a key cause of intractable diarrhea in children, with over 40 genetic etiologies classified into five core pathogenic mechanisms [4–6]: (i) electrolyte transporter dysfunction (e.g., SLC26A3 [7]); (ii) epithelial polarity and interferon signaling defects (e.g., MYO5B, TTC7A [8, 9]); (iii) impaired epithelial proliferation and differentiation (e.g., WNT2B [10]); (iv) epidermal barrier and metabolic pathway mutations (e.g., SI); and (v) dysregulated mucosal immunity and inflammatory control (e.g., FOXP3 [11]). Importantly, intestinal barrier dysfunction is one of the key pathogenic mechanisms of congenital diarrhea; it directly disrupts both the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal epithelium, thereby enabling uncontrolled translocation of luminal antigens and sustaining a chronic state of intestinal inflammation. This pathological process often leads to severe protein-energy malnutrition and life-threatening complications refractory to standard therapy [12, 13].

Lymphoid enhancer-binding factor 1 (LEF1) is a core component of the TCF/LEF transcription factor family, which serves a pivotal role in governing embryonic development, intestinal homeostasis, as well as the proliferation and differentiation of epithelial cells [14, 15]. Beyond the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade, LEF1 is also implicated in multiple other signaling pathways. In the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling pathway, the phosphorylated Smad2/3-Smad4 complex upregulates LEF1 expression, and LEF1 subsequently cooperates with Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT)-related transcription factors such as Snail to mediate target gene transcriptional activation [16]. Within the Notch signaling pathway, LEF1 participates in epigenetic regulatory processes [17], whereas in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, NLK and ERK kinases phosphorylate LEF1, thereby dynamically modulating its transcriptional activity and protein stability [18]. Notably, the LEF1 mutant may downregulate the expression of downstream tight junction proteins (such as ZO-1 and occludin family members) and water channel proteins (such as AQP4) via disrupting their transcriptional regulation, ultimately culminating in chronic or intractable diarrhea [19, 20]. Moreover, LEF1 variants drive the tumorigenesis and progression of human colorectal cancer [21, 22]. For instance, mutations at the NLK-binding phosphorylation sites (Thr155/Ser166) of LEF1 result in aberrant activation of the Wnt pathway [18], while N-terminal truncating mutations abolish its binding capacity to β-catenin [23]. A heterozygous missense LEF1 variant (c.337G > A, p.G113R) has been identified in colorectal cancer, although its precise oncogenic mechanism remains to be fully elucidated [24]. Collectively, LEF1 acts not only as a core regulatory node in the fine-tuned control of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, but also as a central hub linking dysregulated epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) programs, intestinal barrier disruption, and a spectrum of human disorders encompassing congenital diarrhea and colorectal cancer [25–28].

In this study, we focused on a specific LEF1 mutation (c.880 C > T; p.Pro294Ser), and established a novel causal link between this variant and intractable diarrhea. Mechanistically, the LEF1 p.Pro294Ser mutation (LEF1 P294S) significantly impaired intestinal barrier function by driving EMT. Our research findings fill a critical gap in the understanding of LEF1 in the intestinal barrier regulation and expand the etiological spectrum of pediatric chronic diarrhea.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Antibodies against ZO-1 (21773-1-AP, WB 1:2,000; IHC 1:100), Occludin (27260-1-AP, WB 1:1,000; IHC 1:100), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, WB 1:1,000), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, WB 1:1,000), AQP4 (20874-1-AP, WB 1:1,000; IHC 1:100), SNAI1 (26183-1-AP, WB 1:1,000), Vimentin (10366-1-AP, WB 1:2,000), a-SMA (14395-1-AP, WB 1:2,000; IF 1:100), Fibronectin (15613-1-AP, WB 1:2,000), Beta Catenin (51067-2-AP, WB 1:3,000; IF 1:100), Lamin B1 ( 66095-1-Ig, WB 1:2,000), LEF1 (14972-1-AP, WB 1:1000; IP: 1:100) and GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, WB 1:1,000) were purchased from Proteintech (IL, USA). Anti-CD8+ (GB15068, 1:200) and F4/80 (GB113373, 1:200) were purchased from Service-Bi0 (Wuhan, China). Anti-Claudin-1 (A21971, WB 1:2,000) was obtained from ABclonal (Hubei, China). Peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit (111-035-003, WB 1:5000), Peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Mouse (115−035−003, WB 1:5000), Alexa Fluor® 594-AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Rabbit (711-585-152, IF 1:500) and Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated AffiniPure Donkey Anti-Mouse (715−546−150, IF 1:500) were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories (Pennsylvania, USA). Phenyl Methane Sulfonyl Fluoride (PMSF, P0100) and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (PIC, P6730) were acquired from Solarbio (Beijing, China). OB-Test Paper (BA2020B) was obtained from BASO (Guangdong, China). Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, 14040117) and EDTA (0.5 M, AM9260G) were provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, USA). Dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS, MP − 0216011010) was obtained from MP Biomedicals (CA, USA). FITC-dextran (MW 10,000, 0.6 mg/g) was supplied by MedChemExpress (NJ, USA).

Clinical data and colonic tissue samples

Colon tissue samples were obtained via endoscopy biopsy from a healthy volunteer and the pediatric patient, who presented with chronic diarrhea, both recruited through the Department of Gastroenterology at Guangzhou Medical University Affiliated Women and Children’s Medical Center. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from the patient’s guardians.

Cell lines

Stable Caco-2 cell lines overexpressing wild-type LEF1 (OE-WT) or the LEF1 P294S variant (OE-MUT) were established by lentiviral transduction. Recombinant lentiviruses were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the transfer vector carrying WT LEF1 or the LEF1 P294S variant, together with lentiviral packaging and envelope plasmids. Caco‑2 cells were then infected with the harvested viral supernatant. At 48 h post-infection, positive clones were selected with puromycin for at least 10 days. Monoclonal cell lines were obtained by limiting dilution in 96-well plates and verified by Sanger sequencing and Western blotting. All stable cell lines were cultured under standard conditions and used within 3–5 passages for subsequent experiments. Caco-2 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC® HTB-37™) and grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and routinely passaged at 90–100% confluence.

Lef1-p.P292S Cas9-PM mice

We generated Lef1 (c.876 C > G; p.Pro292Ser) knockin mice using the CRISPR-Cas9 system. Firstly, a gRNA targeting the genomic sequence adjacent to the intended insertion site was designed and transcribed in vitro. A donor vector containing the desired P292S substitution was also designed and constructed in vitro. Next, the CRISPR-Cas9 components and the donor vector were co-injected into C57BL/6J zygotes. Subsequently, the injected zygotes were transferred into the oviduct of pseudopregnant ICR females at 0.5 days post-coitum (dpc). F0 pups were born 19 ~ 21 days after embryo transfer; all offspring (F0 mice) were genotyped by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of tail-tip DNA. Positive F0 founders were then crossed with wild-type C57BL/6JGpt mice to establish heterozygous lines. C57BL/6JGpt mice served as wild-type (WT) controls, while B6/JGpt-Lef1em1Cin(p.Pro292Ser)/Gpt mice were the mutant (MUT) line. Specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice were housed under controlled conditions: 23 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All animals were maintained in the SPF facility of Guangzhou Medical University Affiliated Women and Children’s Medical Center, and all experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of this center.

DSS-induced Colitis

The acute DSS-induced colitis model was established as previously described [29]. Eight-week-old male wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups and weighed on day 1 (WTH2O; MUTH2O; WTDSS; MUTDSS; n = 3–5 per group). Mice in the DSS-treated group were given 2.5% DSS in drinking water, while the control groups were given sterile water. Body weight loss and stool consistency, were monitored daily. All mice were euthanized on the day 8, and intestinal tissues were collected for subsequent experiments. Diarrhea severity was scored based on fecal consistency (Table 1).

Isolation of intestinal epithelial cells (IECs)

Colonic tissues were rapidly excised, placed in ice-cold DPBS supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), and trimmed of mesenteric fat. Tissues were cut into 0.5–1 cm fragments, rinsed 3–5 times with ice-cold DPBS, and resuspended in EDTA-containing dissociation buffer for incubation at 37 °C with shaking for 30 min. The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and centrifuged. The cell pellet was resuspended in 30% Percoll solution, overlaid onto 70% Percoll, and subjected to density-gradient centrifugation. IECs at the 30%–70% Percoll interface were collected and either used immediately for downstream experiments or stored in liquid nitrogen.

RNA extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) preparation and real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from colonic IECs of mice using the Universal RNA Purification Kit (EZB - RN4). Total RNA was eluted with elution buffer. For reverse transcription, 1 µg of RNA was used: 2 µl of DNA removal reagent was added to the extracted RNA, followed by preparation of a 20 µl reaction mixture containing 4× EZscript RT Mix II, oligo dT18 (20×), and nuclease-free water. A 20 µl real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) reaction was then set up with cDNA, primers (Table 2), SYBR enzyme, and nuclease-free water. Gene expression differences between samples were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The control group’s relative quantification (RQ) value was set to 1, and mRNA levels in other groups were normalized to this value.

Proteomic analysis

To systematically analyze the proteomic changes in the intestinal tissues of wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) mice in a diarrhea model, this study employed data-independent acquisition (DIA) mass spectrometry technology. Intestinal tissues from WT and MUT mice were collected, ground in liquid nitrogen, lysed in buffer containing 1 mM PMSF, and centrifuged (4 °C, 12,000 × g, 10 min) to obtain total proteins. The protein concentration was determined by the BCA method, and samples from each group were adjusted to the same protein concentration and volume. The protein solutions were then reduced with DTT, alkylated with iodoacetamide, precipitated with acetone, and resolubilized in NH₄HCO₃. The samples were digested overnight at 37 °C with Trypsin-TPCK (1 mg/mL, 1:50, w/w), and the digestion products were desalted using SOLA™ SPE 96-well plates to obtain peptides. The peptides were separated by an EASY-nLC 1200 liquid chromatography system and injected into a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker) for analysis. Data were analyzed using Spectronaut Pulsar™ 18.4 (Biognosys) software. Differentially expressed proteins were defined as those with |log₂FC| ≥ 0.585 (i.e., FC ≥ 1.5 or FC ≤ 0.667) and a corrected P < 0.05. A total of 191 differentially expressed proteins in the small intestine and 160 in the colon were identified. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG pathway, and protein-protein interaction network (PPI) analyses were conducted.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Protein lysates from intestinal epithelial cells (IECs) were prepared. Following quantification, samples were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred into PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBS, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were added, and protein bands were visualized using the iBright Imaging Systems. Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Co‑immunoprecipitation (Co‑IP)

Cell lysates were pre-cleared by incubation with Pierce™ Protein A/G Plus Agarose beads at 4 °C for 30 min to reduce non-specific binding. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected and incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation at 20 rpm. Protein A/G magnetic beads were then added, and the mixture was incubated with gentle mixing at 4 °C for 2 h to capture antigen–antibody complexes. Beads were harvested by centrifugation and washed four times with pre-chilled lysis buffer to minimize background signals. Finally, bound proteins were eluted under denaturing conditions at 100 °C, and eluates were subjected to Western blotting to verify protein–protein interactions.

Immunofluorescence (IF) and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

IF staining of paraffin-embedded colon tissue sections was performed following standard protocols. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 3% BSA. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the sections were washed three times for 5 min each, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) containing anti-fade mounting medium (P0131, Beyotime, China) was used to stain nuclei for 5 min. To quantify the mean fluorescence intensity in intestinal tissue sections, 3 mice per group were randomly selected. Three high-power fields were randomly captured per group (n = 9 fields per group).

H&E staining was performed using the H&E Stain Kit (2305001, Solarbio, China) following standard protocols. Intestinal barrier integrity was assessed by two independent observers using the Quantitative-Mucosal Algorithmic Rules for Scoring Histology (Q-MARSH) system. Villus height (Vh) and crypt depth (Cd) were measured histomorphometrically, with a normal Vh: Cd ratio ≥ 3.0 [30]. Colonic morphology was further assessed by measuring crypt depth (from the base of the crypt to the luminal epithelial surface) and crypt density (number of intact crypts per millimeter in cross-sections of the colonic mucosa) [31, 32]. Briefly, intestinal tissues were harvested from 5 randomly selected mice per group. For each mouse, at least 10 well-oriented, intact intestinal villi and their corresponding crypts were randomly chosen for morphometric quantification using ImageJ software, and the Vh: Cd ratio was calculated accordingly. For the colon, only longitudinally sectioned, fully intact crypts with bilateral symmetry and no oblique cutting or fragmentation were evaluated. For each mouse, 10–20 such qualified crypts were assessed to determine crypt depth (measured from the crypt base to the mucosal surface) and crypt density (expressed as the number of intact crypts per unit area of colonic mucosa).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked for endogenous peroxidase activity. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000) for 20 min at room temperature, 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB, 10 s), and hematoxylin (1 min). Following final dehydration, paraffin sections were mounted and coverslipped using neutral balsam. For quantitative analysis of IHC staining, 3 mice were randomly selected from each experimental group, along with one control patient and the case patient. For each mouse or patient, three non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs) were randomly chosen. The number of target protein-positive cells was counted using ImageJ software. The average number of positive cells per subject was calculated and used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Intestinal tissues were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (4 °C, 4 h) and 1% osmium tetroxide (1–2 h), washed with PBS (3 times, 10–15 min each), dehydrated (alcohol gradient, 10–15 min each; absolute ethanol twice, 10–15 min each; 100% acetone twice, 10–15 min each), infiltrated with 100% acetone/embedding medium (3:1, 30 min, room temperature), embedded, and polymerized (37 °C, 24 h; 60 °C, 48 h). Ultrathin sections (~ 60–80 nm) were cut using a Leica UC7 ultramicrotome, stained with uranyl acetate (20 min) and lead citrate (12 min), and examined by transmission electron microscopy to assess microvilli length. The measurement standard was the straight-line distance from the distal tip of the microvillus to the intestinal epithelial cell membrane. For the measurement workflow, we acquired electron micrographs of the small intestine and colon from 5 mice, randomly selected 5 fields of view per sample, and measured the length of 10 microvilli per field. For statistical analysis, we calculated the average value of 50 microvillus measurements per mouse, followed by formal statistical analysis and plotting [33].

In vivo permeability assay

Eight-week-old WT and MUT mice were fasted (24 h, water ad libitum), administered 4 kDa FITC-dextran (0.6 mg/g body weight) via oral gavage, euthanized 4 h post-administration, and bled to collect plasma for fluorescence quantitative analysis to assess intestinal permeability [34, 35]. Additionally, the distribution of FITC-dextran in the intestinal lumen and mucosal layer was visualized in frozen intestinal tissue sections using fluorescence microscopy.

CUT&RUN

CUT&RUN experiments were performed using the CUT&RUN Assay Kit (#HD101) from Vazyme. Briefly, 1 × 10⁵ viable Caco‑2 cells were harvested and bound to ConA‑coated magnetic beads. Following cell permeabilization, an appropriate primary antibody against LEF1 was added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Next, pG‑MNase was introduced into the reaction mixture and incubated at 0 °C for 10 min. Chromatin digestion was initiated by addition of Ca²⁺ buffer and terminated using an EDTA‑containing stop buffer at 37 °C for 10 min. DNA was then purified using the DNA purification spin column provided in the kit. Enriched DNA fragments were analyzed by quantitative real‑time PCR (qPCR) to assess enrichment at target promoter regions, including SNAIL, LGR5, LGR6, and TCF7L1 (Table 3). Relative enrichment was calculated using the percent input method.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

To assess cytokine production in an ex vivo setting, full-thickness colonic tissue explants from mice were cultured in DMEM for 24 h. Following incubation, the supernatants were collected and analyzed for TNF-α and IL-6 concentrations using corresponding ELISA kits (E-HSEL-M0009 and E-HSEL-M0003; Elabscience, Wuhan, China) [36].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0), R (version 4.5.3), and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) software. Normality of the data distribution was evaluated by the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as the median (95% confidence interval [CI]) for non-normally distributed variables. For datasets meeting the assumptions of normality and equal variance, independent-samples t-test was used for two-group comparisons; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For single-factor comparisons across multiple groups, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction was used for normally distributed data, whereas non-normally distributed data were analyzed by the Kruskal–Wallis H test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. In multi-factorial experimental designs, factorial ANOVA was employed for normally distributed data, with post hoc analyses performed as described above. For non-normally distributed data in multi-factorial settings, Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA with corresponding simple-effects analysis and Bonferroni adjustment, or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Holm correction, was conducted. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Case presentation

A boy (born May 6, 2019; weight, 10 kg, height, 72 cm) presented with intractable chronic diarrhea with onset at 18 months of age. In November 2020, he developed diarrhea (yellow, loose stools, 4–5 times/day) during hospitalization for asthmatic bronchitis and exudative erythema multiforme at a local hospital. Despite treatment with azithromycin, cephalosporins, and an extensively hydrolyzed formula, his diarrhea and rash persisted. He was subsequently transferred to our hospital to receive cyclosporine for exudative erythema multiforme. Although the rash improved, his diarrhea worsened (5–7 times/day, yellow-green, loose stools with severe acidosis). Following etiological treatment, supportive care, and dietary modifications (meat congee, vegetable puree, rice paste), stool frequency decreased to 4–5 times per day with pasty consistency. On January 16, 2021, the patient developed fever, cough, severe dyspnea, and pyemias. After advanced life support, plasma exchange, and anti-infective therapy, multi-organ dysfunction improved but diarrhea recurred. On March 11, 2021, his daily stool volume reached 1,400–2,000 ml/day (dark green). Sequential interventions including somatostatin, mucosal repair agents, probiotics, infliximab, and donated breast milk failed to resolve his grayish watery stools, necessitating continuous pump feeding and parenteral nutrition.

Laboratory results

Fecal reducing substance test, negative; celiac disease antibodies, EMA-IgA: 163.54 ng/ml. The remaining test results are detailed in the Supplementary excel.

Past history

The patient was full-term firstborn, with no birth trauma or asphyxia. Non-consanguineous parents, no family metabolic disorder history. Delayed language development, allergies to eggs, milk and wheat.

Genetic testing

Whole exome sequencing identified a de novo heterozygous missense mutation in LEF1 exon 8 (NM_016269: c.880 C > T, p.Pro294Ser), confirmed by Sanger sequencing (parental wild-type, Figure S1A). This variant is absent from 1,000 Genomes and gnomAD databases; bioinformatics analysis predicted pathogenicity.

Prognosis

Alive, afebrile, with persistent diarrhea (approximately 10 watery stools/day), satisfactory mental state, and minimal nutritional improvement.

Results

The primary clinical phenotype is persistent diarrhea

A review of clinical data revealed that the child’s predominant clinical manifestation was persistent, high-volume diarrhea (1,400–2,000 mL/day; mean, 1718.75 ± 358.26 mL/day) (Fig. 1A), with recurrent diarrheal episodes averaging 11.75 ± 1.57 times per day (Fig. 1B). Etiological characterization demonstrated a significantly higher daily stool frequency (Fig. 1C) and longer disease duration (Fig. 1D) than those observed in common diarrheal disorders: bacterial infectious diarrhea [BID], viral infectious diarrhea [VID], antibiotic-associated diarrhea [AAD], irritable bowel syndrome [IBS], and inflammatory bowel disease [IBD]), resembling congenital diarrheal disorders such as congenital diarrhea and enteropathies (CoDEs) [37–42]. Correlation analysis between inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, CRP; procalcitonin, PCT) and diarrhea frequency showed no significant correlation, indicating that diarrhea persisted despite anti-infective therapy, consistent with a non-infectious etiology (Fig. 1E). The patient exhibited marked growth retardation, with body weight below − 3 SD according to WHO standards, accompanied by chronically decreased electrolyte levels. (Fig. 1F; Figure S1B). Additionally, gastrointestinal endoscopy showed both small intestine and colon with minor petechial hemorrhages (no erosions, ulcers, or masses) (Fig. 1G). Pathological staining revealed the intact epithelial structure, lymphocytic infiltration in the lamina propria, and eosinophil densities of 5–8/HPF (small intestine) and 7–8/HPF (colon) (no neutrophils), consistent with mild chronic mucosal inflammation (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that this case arises from a genetically driven form of chronic diarrhea.

LEF1 mutation in mice induces significant diarrhea

Multiple sequence alignment of LEF1 amino acid sequences across six mammalian species demonstrated evolutionary conservation of the P294S residue (Fig. 2A). To functionally validate the pathogenic relevance of the LEF1 P294S variant in diarrhea phenotypes, we administered 2.5% DSS to wild-type (LEF1 WT) and LEF1 P292S knock-in (mutation, MUT) mice, in which the murine P292S substitution is orthologous to human P294S mutation, while a water-treated group served as the control. The body weight changes, defecation patterns and stool characteristics were recorded. DSS treatment reduced colon length in both WT and MUT mice. MUT mice displayed significantly shorter colons compared with WT mice in both water-treated and DSS-treated groups (Fig. 2B–C). Diarrhea scoring showed greater severity and more pronounced weight loss in DSS-treated MUT mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 2D–E), indicating increased susceptibility to diarrhea. Collectively, these results support that the LEF1 P292S mutation enhances intestinal susceptibility to diarrhea.

Impaired intestinal structure and barrier function in LEF1 Mutant Mice

The integrity of the intestinal barrier is critical for maintaining normal intestinal physiological functions [43]. H&E staining revealed mild inflammatory infiltration, accompanied by marked morphological alterations (Fig. 3A–B). Control (water-treated) MUT mice exhibited spontaneous small intestinal villus damage, including villus shortening, as well as colonic epithelial atrophy and focal architectural disruption. DSS treatment markedly exacerbated these abnormalities, resulting in extensive loss of small intestinal microvilli and severe derangement of colonic structure. Quantitative-Mucosal Algorithmic Rules for Scoring Histology (Q-MARSH) analysis revealed a significant reduction in the villus height to crypt depth (Vh: Cd) ratios in the small intestine of MUT mice, irrespective of water or DSS treatment (Fig. 3C). Morphometric analysis of colonic tissue further revealed decreased crypt depth and crypt density in MUT mice (Fig. 3D) [31]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to evaluate microvillus length in intestinal tissues. As shown in Fig. 3E, TEM showed shorter microvilli, more severe damage to the epithelial brush border, and wider intercellular spaces at tight junctions in MUT mice. The FITC-dextran permeability assay showed that the fluorescence diminished fluorescence intensity in the intestinal lumen (Fig. 3F) and elevated serum fluorescence levels in MUT mice (Fig. 3G), confirming the increased intestinal epithelial permeability. Collectively, these findings indicate that the LEF1 P292S mutation exacerbates intestinal structural damage and impairs barrier function.

Altered intestinal proteome in LEF1 Mutant Mice

To comprehensively characterize the protein expression profile in the mouse diarrhea model, we performed proteomic mass spectrometry analysis on small intestinal and colonic tissues from WT and MUT mice (Fig. 4A–B). Through stringent screening, a total of 23 differentially expressed proteins were identified, which associated with diarrheal phenotypes (Fig. 4C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were predominantly enriched in functional categories including “ion transport,” “immune response,” and “secretion” (Fig. 4D–E). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis showed significant enrichment in pathways related to “glycoprotein biosynthesis,” “signal transduction,” and “inflammatory response” (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified key hub proteins, including Parvalbumin α (Pvalb), members of the solute carrier (SLC) family, and Mptx1 (Fig. 4G–H), which may contribute to mutation-driven intestinal barrier dysfunction. Multicolor immunofluorescence staining of intestinal tissues indicated that, following DSS treatment, the fluorescence signal intensity of CD8+ and F4/80+ cells in the colonic tissue of MUT mice was significantly increased in MUT compared to WT mice (Fig. 4I). ELISA of intestinal tissue supernatants demonstrated that DSS-treated MUT mice exhibited significantly higher levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in intestinal tissues compared with WT mice (Fig. 4J). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the LEF1 P292S mutant impaired the baseline intestinal barrier integrity and increased susceptibility to DSS-induced inflammation.

LEF1 mutation disrupts tight junctions and triggers diarrhea

The intestinal barrier, which serves as the first line of defense against invasion by external pathogen [44, 45], primarily relies on intact tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells (e.g., ZO-1, OCCLUDIN, CLAUDIN family), adherens junctions (e.g., E-CADHERIN), and normal intestinal mucosal architecture. We previously observed spontaneous intestinal structural defects in MUT mice. Subsequently, we isolated colonic epithelial cells and measured mRNA and protein levels of tight junction proteins and aquaporins by qPCR and Western blot analysis. Results showed elevated LEF1 mRNA and protein levels in MUT mice. In contrast, expression of ZO-1, OCCLUDIN, CLAUDIN-1, E-CADHERIN and AQP4 was decreased (Fig. 5A–B). IHC staining of mouse colon sections revealed lower expression of ZO-1, OCCLUDIN, CLAUDIN-1, and AQP4 expression in the MUT mice compared with WT mice (Fig. 5C). To further validate these findings, IHC staining was performed on human colon tissue sections, revealing significantly lower ZO-1 levels in the case than in the healthy control (Fig. 5D). These results indicated that LEF1 might regulate tight junctions and aquaporins in the pathogenesis of diarrhea.

LEF1 Mutant Promotes Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT)

Building on our identification of the LEF1 mutation as a negative regulator of intestinal barrier integrity, we next explored the mechanism by which this variant modulates tight junction protein expression. Given that LEF1 is a nuclear transcription factor in the canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, we first assessed LEF1 and β-catenin protein levels in cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions from stable Caco-2 cells expressing the LEF1 P294S mutation. We observed increased nuclear accumulation of mutant LEF1, whereas β-catenin levels were comparable between wild-type (WT) and mutant cells (Fig. 6A). Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays further showed no significant difference in binding affinity between WT or mutant LEF1 and β-catenin (Fig. 6B). We then performed immunofluorescence staining for β-catenin on intestinal tissue sections from LEF1 P292S knock-in mice and quantified nuclear β-catenin fluorescence intensity, confirming that the LEF1 mutation did not affect β-catenin nuclear translocation (Fig. 6C). CUT&RUN assays revealed that the LEF1 mutation did not alter its binding to the promoters of LGR5, LGR6, or TCF7L1 (classical Wnt/β-catenin target genes), but significantly enhanced its binding to the SNAIL promoter, a key regulator of EMT (Fig. 6D). These results aligned with previous reports demonstrating that LEF1 regulates tight junction protein expression via the EMT pathway, thereby compromising intestinal barrier function [18, 27]. In intestinal tissues from LEF1 mutant mice, mesenchymal markers including α-SMA and fibronectin were upregulated, while epithelial markers such as ZO-1 and E-CADHERIN were downregulated (Fig. 6E). These observations were further validated by immunofluorescence co-staining for E-CADHERIN and α-SMA (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these data demonstrated that the impairment of intestinal barrier function by the LEF1 P294S mutant was not mediated by dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Instead, this effect was driven by enhanced nuclear translocation of mutant LEF1, which promoted its binding to the SNAIL promoter and thereby accelerated EMT progression.

Discussion

Intestinal barrier integrity relies on epithelial renewal, tight junctions maintenance, mucus secretion, gut microbiota, and precise regulation of the lamina propria immune system; impairment of any of these factors ultimately manifests clinically as diarrhea [12, 13, 38, 46]. Recurrent diarrhea episodes correlate with long-term physical and psychological developmental disorders, resulting in approximately 1,400 healthy life-years lost per 100,000 people [1, 47]. Although the patient’s vital signs were stable, his weight remained below the WHO − 3 SD threshold, and electrolyte levels were low (Figure S1B), necessitating intravenous fluid replacement. Diarrhea can be classified into acute, persistent, and chronic categories, each showing distinct characteristics in terms of stool frequency and duration [48, 49]. This patient mainly presented with long-term, profuse diarrhea accompanied by growth retardation. WES identified a de novo LEF1 P294S mutation. Despite infection control, diarrhea persisted, excluding infectious etiologies. Unlike very early-onset inflammatory bowel disease (VEO-IBD) and other chronic diarrhea caused by inflammatory diseases, which typically present with endoscopic ulcers, erosions, small polyps, and extensive inflammatory exudation [50, 51], the endoscopic and pathological findings in this patient showed mucosal swelling and mild inflammation (no ulcers or erosions), supporting a gene-driven intestinal injury.

The LEF1 P294S mutation localizes to the conserved context-dependent regulatory domain (CDRD, aa68-295) [52], a previously unreported region whose 100% cross-species conservation indicates critical functional roles such as co-regulator binding and post-translational modification [53]. Rather than directly causing overt diarrhea, this variant probably induces intestinal inflammatory susceptibility, weakening the epithelium’s ability to tolerate inflammatory perturbations [54]. Inflammatory triggers such as microbial exposure and mucosal irritation act as a second hit [55], amplifying the severity of diarrhea, which is consistent with the exacerbated phenotype observed in mutant mice under DSS challenge. This explains the patient’s therapy-refractory course: conventional anti-inflammatory agents can alleviate acute attacks but fail to reverse the genetic predisposition. Our findings expand the etiological spectrum of pediatric chronic diarrhea, highlighting LEF1 as a key mediator of intestinal inflammatory tolerance and a potential diagnostic target for inflammation-sensitive, idiopathic cases.

Moreover, our results showed that the LEF1 P294S mutation drives inherent intestinal structural abnormalities and compromised barrier integrity, which directly constitute the core pathological basis for persistent diarrhea and growth retardation in affected individuals [56, 57]. Biologically, this finding establishes LEF1 as a key regulator of intestinal structural maintenance and barrier homeostasis, expanding our understanding of its functional repertoire beyond known roles in developmental and oncogenic pathways. The identified pathogenic loop, where baseline barrier defects enhance inflammatory susceptibility, and inflammation further exacerbates epithelial damage, provides a novel mechanistic framework for understanding inflammation-associated chronic diarrhea. Clinically, this insight explains the refractoriness of such diarrhea to conventional anti-infective therapies, as these interventions fail to target the underlying genetic-driven structural and barrier deficits. Moreover, it highlights LEF1 as a valuable diagnostic target for idiopathic pediatric chronic diarrhea characterized by structural barrier impairment, enriching the etiological spectrum and facilitating precise diagnosis for previously unexplained cases.

Proteomic analysis identified a LEF1 mutation-driven molecular network encompassing ion transport, epithelial adhesion, calcium signaling, and inflammatory-related pathways, providing a precise molecular basis for intestinal structural abnormalities, barrier dysfunction, and inflammatory susceptibility. Biologically, this discovery fills the gap in understanding the downstream effector pathways of LEF1 in intestinal homeostasis. Key differentially expressed proteins (e.g., SLC transporters, Pcdhb14, CLCA1 [58, 59]) and enriched pathways (cGMP-PKG [60], NF-κB [61, 62], glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis [63]) confirm that LEF1 orchestrates multidimensional intestinal function: nutrient transport, epithelial integrity, inflammation [64], and barrier dysfunction. Furthermore, upon DSS induction, marked infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and macrophages was observed in the colonic tissues of mutant mice, accompanied by elevated levels of IL‑6 and TNF‑α. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that the LEF1 P292S mutation impairs the intestinal immune barrier. Clinically, this molecular landscape explains refractory diarrhea: inflammation control fails to reverse dysregulated ion transport (e.g., Slc5a7) and epithelial adhesion, sustaining barrier leakage [65]. Additionally, these molecules, when combined with LEF1 mutation detection, enhance diagnostic specificity for idiopathic pediatric chronic diarrhea, avoiding misdiagnosis as inflammatory or infectious etiologies.

In animal models, we demonstrated that LEF1 P292S suppresses both transcriptional and protein expression of tight junction components. As a key transcription factor in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, LEF1 regulates intestinal homeostasis via its C-terminal DNA-binding domain and N-terminal β-catenin-binding domain [42, 52]. Since the mutation is localized near the main functional domain, we first assessed whether it would impair the normal function of LEF1, specifically its binding affinity for β-catenin and its capacity to activate downstream target genes (LGR5, LGR6, TCF7L1). The results showed no statistically significant changes. Previous literature has reported that LEF1 can act as a cofactor to directly bind to the promoter of the key EMT gene SNAIL, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and thereby inhibiting the expression of tight junction proteins [25]. Consistent with previous reports, we found that the increasing nuclear accumulation of mutant LEF1 directly binds to the SNAIL promoter to drive the EMT of intestinal epithelial cells. Besides, studies have shown that AQP1, AQP3, AQP4, AQP5, AQP8 and AQP9 are mainly involved in the secretion and absorption of intestinal water [66], among which AQP4 is mainly expressed in the crypt cells of the small intestine and the epithelial cells of the colon [67]. The down-regulation of water channel proteins such as AQP4 will further aggravate the intestinal water absorption disorder, ultimately leading to a diarrhea phenotype. Biologically, this finding establishes LEF1 as a dual regulator of intestinal barrier integrity and water-electrolyte balance: downregulated tight junctions compromise epithelial structural stability [68], while reduced AQP4 [66, 67] exacerbates fluid transport dysfunction, collectively underpinning persistent diarrhea and growth impairment. This expands our understanding of the functional repertoire of LEF1 beyond developmental and oncogenic roles to core intestinal physiological processes, linking transcriptional regulation to barrier structure and water homeostasis. Clinically, since conventional therapies fail to target LEF1-driven downregulation of tight junction proteins and AQP4, this dual molecular defect explains the refractoriness of the patient’s diarrhea. Moreover, our research highlights LEF1 mutation as a specific diagnostic marker for idiopathic pediatric chronic diarrhea linked to barrier and water transport deficits, and identifies downstream targets such as tight junction stabilizers or AQP4 modulators for precision therapies, offering a path to address the root cause rather than merely alleviating symptoms.

In summary, this study first establishes a pathogenic model: LEF1 P294S mutation promotes the occurrence of EMT, downregulates tight junctions (ZO-1, OCCLUDIN, CLAUDIN-1) and AQP4, impairs intestinal structure and barrier function, and enhances inflammatory susceptibility, leading to chronic refractory diarrhea (Fig. 7). Biologically, it expands the roles of LEF1 from development and carcinogenesis to the core regulation of intestinal homeostasis, filling critical research gaps. Clinically, the model explains the failure of conventional therapy, identifies LEF1 as a diagnostic marker for idiopathic pediatric chronic diarrhea, and provides new targets, advancing diagnosis and targeted treatment of unexplained childhood diarrhea.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, despite being a single-case report, we validated the reliability of our findings by establishing a mouse model and a cell line harboring the orthologous mutation. Second, in exploring the etiology of the patient’s diarrhea, the markedly elevated EMA-IgA level indicated a potential diagnosis of celiac disease. However, duodenal biopsy was not performed due to the patient’s young age; consequently, only a gluten-free diet intervention was administered for evaluation, yet no improvement in diarrheal symptoms was observed. Finally, the precise molecular mechanism by which this mutation influences binding to the SNAIL gene promoter remains elusive and warrants further investigation.
