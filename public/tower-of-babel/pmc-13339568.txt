A Review of Virtual Twins in Physiologically‐Based Pharmacokinetic Modeling and Simulation
Authors: Emily Mannix, Sam Mostafa, Amin Rostami‐Hodjegan, Thomas M Polasek, Carl M J Kirkpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe novel application of Virtual Twins (VT) in PBPK (VT‐PBPK) presents the opportunity to advance precision dosing and accelerate the shift from one‐size‐fits‐all to targeted, individualized treatments. This review aims to: (1) critically evaluate existing research on the use of VTs in PBPK, (2) develop a conceptual definition of VT‐PBPK, (3) describe and evaluate VT methodological diversity, (4) examine existing regulatory frameworks and guidance governing the integration of VT‐PBPK, and (5) identify opportunities and challenges for advancing next‐generation VTs. A structured literature search was conducted to identify studies describing VT‐PBPK of a whole human body for the purpose of predicting drug concentration and/or effect. Details of the VT‐PBPK models and VT design were extracted from each study. A framework assessing and categorizing methods of simulation and virtualization was applied to the extracted data. Twenty‐two (22) studies were included which demonstrated the application of VT‐PBPK across a range of populations, disease states, and drug classes. All studies applied VT‐PBPK to real‐world patient‐specific covariate data retrospectively for the purpose of PBPK model development and evaluation, or model‐informed precision dosing (MIPD). In the VT approaches, three levels of virtualization were identified; low, medium, and high, as determined by the number of covariates integrated into the model. To date there is no specific regulatory guidance on the appropriate use of VT‐PBPK. A shift in application of PBPK modeling from population‐based to specific, individualized predictions is required to advance VTs toward clinical implementation. Achieving rigorous design and evaluation of VT models will require strong interdisciplinary collaboration.

The application of personalized medicine through targeted, individualized treatments will increasingly become part of the standard of care as modern medicine departs from a one‐size‐fits‐all approach. 1 , 2 While personalized medicine has been defined in many different ways, it is fundamentally a healthcare model based on patient centricity. This involves a proactive approach to disease management to avoid predictable medication‐related harm and ensure “the right drug, with the right dose at the right time to the right patient”. 3

The majority of treatment guidelines and dosing recommendations currently represent either one‐size‐fits‐all, or population‐level recommendations that do not give full consideration to unique characteristics of each individual patient. For drugs with a wide margin between efficacy and toxicity, this is entirely safe and appropriate. However, drugs with a narrow therapeutic index or high risk of harm require a more precise and individualized approach. The first step toward personalization is to utilize population‐level pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) characteristics to inform dosing. For example, enoxaparin is routinely dosed according to covariates such as age, weight, and renal function, and subsequently optimized via the surrogate marker anti‐factor Xa. Combining such covariate‐guided dosing schedules with Bayesian dose optimization as part of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) or target concentration intervention (TCI) may be appropriate, 4 , 5 and show the greatest benefit in terms of maximized efficacy and reduced toxicity. Beyond this subgroup approach, there are a number of high‐risk drugs in which the application of modeling and simulation approaches as part of model‐informed precision dosing (MIPD) has great potential to support individual dose optimization. MIPD refers to the use of computer modeling and simulation to integrate vast amounts of data and characterize individual variability in drug PK and PD. This ability to account for the unique characteristics of an individual patient then subsequently informs individualized predictions of drug exposure that may be utilized to support the targeted, precision dosing of the drug. This is most applicable to high‐risk drugs with an established exposure–response relationship and an associated dosing challenge such as a narrow therapeutic index, cumulative toxicity, or significant risks associated with toxicity or therapeutic failure. Examples of ideal drug candidates include immunosuppressants (e.g., tacrolimus, cyclosporine), antipsychotics (e.g., clozapine, risperidone), and antineoplastics (e.g., busulfan), in which precision dosing may support a more effective treatment through minimizing adverse effects and optimizing drug exposure.

In this context, physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling and simulation has recently increased in popularity for its potential to support precision dosing, in addition to applications in drug development and regulatory assessments. 6 , 7 , 8 While PBPK has a significant history supporting the exploration of population PK variability, development of the virtual twin (VT) in PBPK (VT‐PBPK) exemplifies the shift in application from generating population‐based to specific, individualized predictions. 9 VT‐PBPK involves the use of dedicated software to create a digital representation of a person by integrating their unique data such as physiological, biochemical, environmental, and genetic covariates to produce patient‐specific PK predictions (Figures 1 and 2 ). When applied as part of MIPD (MIPD‐VT), VTs have the potential to equip clinicians with information to support decision making.

Like TDM and TCI, VTs aim to optimize drug exposure and clinical outcomes, one key difference being the potential to apply MIPD‐VTs prospectively, generating specific treatment recommendations at the point‐of‐care. Despite this great potential, there are many barriers to a successful and widespread implementation of MIPD‐VTs, including significant requirements of data integration, software and technology, and regulatory acceptance. 2 , 10 , 11

This review aims to: (1) critically evaluate existing research on the use of VTs in PBPK, (2) develop a conceptual definition of VT‐PBPK, (3) describe and evaluate VT methodological diversity, (4) examine existing regulatory frameworks and guidance governing the integration of VT‐PBPK, and (5) identify opportunities and challenges for advancing next‐generation VTs.

SEARCH STRATEGY

Studies were included that simulated a whole human body using PBPK modeling, integrated real‐world patient‐specific covariate data, and utilized VTs for the purpose of predicting drug concentration and/or effect. Studies involving virtualization of any system other than a whole human body, application beyond predicting drug concentration and/or effect, review articles, commentaries, editorials, and preprints were excluded. PK studies simulating clinical trials, drug–drug interactions (DDI), or bioequivalence studies were also excluded as these virtual populations are not patient‐specific.

Given the emerging nature and lack of universal definition of VTs, a Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) term in MEDLINE and similar repositories has yet to be defined. Thus, a typical systematic search strategy was not possible. Although the Emtree term “digital twin” exists, we observed that it is yet to be consistently used to index relevant publications; instead, publications are typically indexed by drug or population of interest. Therefore, we developed and evaluated a pragmatic purpose‐built, systematic search strategy in the endeavour to capture all relevant literature. MEDLINE and EMBASE (via Ovid) were searched in May 2025 with no date restrictions, using the following search string: (“PBPK” OR “physiologically based PK” OR “physiologically based pharmacokinetic*” OR “QSP” OR “quantitative systems pharmacology” OR “MIPD” OR “model informed precision dosing” OR “MBPD” OR “model based precision dosing” OR “model based personalized dosing”) AND (“digital twin*” OR “digital patient*” OR “digital population*” OR “virtual twin*” OR “virtual patient*” OR “virtual population*”). The Emtree term “digital twin” was exploded and added to the keyword search. To exhaust possible missed publications, hand‐searching was performed using Google Scholar, Perplexity AI, and citation‐searching. Finally, review of abstracts published from 2015 to 2025 by the American Conference on Pharmacometrics (ACoP, www.go‐acop.org) and Population Approach Group Europe (PAGE, www.page‐meeting.org) was undertaken.

Search results were imported into Covidence for review. Initial title and abstract screening in accordance with the above criteria was completed by E.M. Following this, full‐text review and data extraction was completed in consultation with a second reviewer (C.M.J.K.). In the case of disagreement during screening or data extraction, a further two experts were consulted (S.M., T.M.P.) for the final decision.

To inform the analysis of appropriate use of VTs, regulation, and future directions (aims 4 and 5), a separate search was conducted. This involved hand‐searching citations, as well as using Google Scholar and Perplexity AI to identify relevant guidelines, reviews, opinion articles, and regulatory white papers.

DATA EXTRACTION AND ANALYSIS

A purpose‐built and validated data extraction tool was utilized to extract details of VT‐PBPK model design including; software used, drug profile, model validation, study design, number of simulations per patient, number of patients simulated, and details of the model and patient population. Details of VT design were also extracted including; purpose of VT‐PBPK application, population/disease state, drug, whether the VT was dose‐matched to the actual dose administered to the patient, type of VT simulation, level of covariate virtualization (low, medium, high, as established by previously published VT approaches 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ), and exposure variable/s, for example, AUC, target concentration.

The drug profiles within these studies were evaluated as to whether they were either ‘system‐defined’ (SD) or ‘user‐built’ (UB). SD describes a profile provided and internally validated by a software system, and ‘system‐defined with modification’ (SDM) describes a profile from a software system that was modified to account for a specific population to describe VTs. UB refers to a new profile developed specifically within the study. ‘Previously published with modification’ (PPM) refers to a previously published UB model that was since modified, for example, scaling to pediatric ontogeny, or incorporating data representing functions of interest such as impaired hepatic or renal clearance. Models were considered validated if the study described a process of validation either in the publication itself, or referred to in a previous publication using the same model, or internally by the software company (in the case of a SD model).

By analyzing this extracted data, particularly considering simulation methodologies, VT design, and purpose of application, we developed a working definition of the VT in PBPK. In order to understand the diversity in existing methodologies, categories of VT simulation approaches were collated and visualized as a framework (Figure 3 ) which was then applied to the extracted data with a focus on representing the different approaches to virtualization of individuals and populations.

All 22 studies applied VT‐PBPK retrospectively. The commercial package Simcyp (12/22) and open‐source platform PK‐Sim (8/22) were most frequently used to build VTs, followed by MoBi (2/22), COMSOL Multiphysics (2/22), and MATLAB (1/22) (note that some studies utilized multiple platforms). All studies utilized models that were validated for use with the exception of two studies in which model validation was not described. 16 , 17 The number of real patients represented in VT simulations ranged from 1 to 200. Only 8/22 studies described the number of simulations computed for each VT, ranging from 10 to 1,000, with no discernible trend over time. One study 12 tested the number of simulations needed per VT to optimize and stabilize model predictions.

Through consolidation and analysis of the extracted data, our VT‐PBPK definition was:

Virtual Twin – an in silico, mathematical representation of a patient used to generate individualized, patient‐specific pharmacokinetic predictions. A high covariate virtualization VT will include sufficient real‐world patient‐specific covariate data (physiological, biochemical, demographic, environmental, and genetic) to most closely represent the patient.

In terms of virtualiation, patient‐specific covariates (e.g., sex, weight, renal function) must be integrated either through fixing input parameters or optimizing the model to intrinsically account for the characteristics of the target individual (Figure 1 ). We identified three levels of virtualization in the VT approaches; low, medium, and high. Low virtualization typically includes basic demographic data such as sex and age. Medium virtualization integrates further covariates specific to the individual such as weight, height, renal and hepatic function, and biochemistry. Lastly, high virtualization integrates many covariates to represent the individual as closely as possible, including smoking status, genetics, specific protein abundance (e.g., cytochrome P450 enzymes, P‐glycoprotein), organ blood flow, blood results or biomarkers of interest, and concomitant medications. Figure 2 gives examples from the literature of the covariates that may be virtualized across the three levels, according to drug specificity and data availability. For comparison, Bayesian approaches typically use prior models which contain covariates that fall somewhere between low and medium virtualization to predict first doses that will then be optimized once drug concentrations or PD endpoints are available.

Four distinct simulation styles emerged during analysis (Figure 3 ); Virtual Population (VPop), Individualized Virtual Population (IVPop, 5/22), Virtual Twin Population (VT‐Pop, 3/22), and Virtual Twin (VT, 15/22). Eight VT papers utilized high covariate virtualization, four used medium, and three used low.

We identified six studies 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 18 , 19 that systematically assessed the effects of progressive stepwise covariate virtualization: low, medium, and high. Radke et al.'s vancomycin model 19 is the first example of this method, which assessed the effects of base model predictions, low, and medium covariate virtualization. Despite some variability in predictive accuracy across different vancomycin concentration ranges, this work demonstrated minimizing mean absolute prediction error and mean prediction error with each step of increasing covariate virtualization. Similarly, Mseddi et al.'s fludarabine VT‐PBPK model 18 assessed the effect of integrating single covariates, and then multiple in combination, through fold error metrics and regression analyses. In this cohort, the combination of plasma protein scaling factor and nuclear GFR gave the best model predictions. Gasper et al.'s apixaban and rivaroxaban VT‐PBPK 14 study noted that high covariate virtualization integrating CYP3A4/5 and Pgp phenotyping did not further improve predictive performance beyond medium covariate virtualization, and resulted in slight overprediction. However, authors demonstrated that subgroup analysis stratified by age, kidney insufficiency, and treatment indication (venous thromboembolism or atrial fibrillation) may be useful to identify patients at high risk of bleeding. High covariate virtualization integrating CYP3A4/5 and Pgp phenotyping improved predictive accuracy and reduced mean fold error for 8 out of 10 patients with a measured C trough above the predefined threshold corresponding to increased bleeding risk. Fendt et al. similarly tested the effects of stepwise covariate virtualization using a caffeine VT‐PBPK model in a healthy adult population. 13 This work noted the percentage of PK predictions within the twofold range as 93.75 (low), 93.49 (medium), and 97.40 (high). Interestingly, this study tested the accuracy of covariate virtualization on an individual level via root mean square error, in which high virtualization produced the best fit for 23/48 individuals. In Mostafa et al.'s modeling of clozapine, 12 while the coefficient of determination and systematic bias were similar for medium and high covariate virtualization, both models demonstrated improved predictive accuracy compared with low covariate virtualization. Kangne et al.'s recent paper 15 implementing midazolam and digoxin VT‐PBPK noted that inclusion of protein expression data (Step 2 – proteomics/biochemistry) did not further improve PK predictions for IV midazolam beyond Step 1, and actually decreased prediction accuracy for oral dosing. However, increasing covariate virtualization was particularly successful for digoxin, as PK predictions within twofold of the observed data increased from 92% (Step 0) to 97% (Step 2).

During analysis, two key purposes emerged for the application of VT‐PBPK: MIPD and PBPK model development and evaluation. Fifteen studies used VT‐PBPK for MIPD, while 8 were as part of PBPK model development and evaluation (Table 2 ), note that one study demonstrates both applications. 20

As shown in Table 2 , VT‐PBPK is consistently applied to populations in which alterations in physiological parameters and PK are expected, such as critical illness, pregnancy, pediatrics, obesity and renal impairment. Results also indicate a substantial prevalence of high‐risk medicines that have narrow therapeutic indexes, high interindividual variability, or significant risk of harm associated with toxicity or medication error. Target concentration (14/22) and AUC (12/22) were the most commonly integrated exposure variables.

REGULATION AND VIRTUAL TWINS

There is currently a lack of guidance on the appropriate use of VTs despite the global trend of PBPK models supporting model‐informed drug development (MIDD) for regulatory submissions. Key regulatory bodies including the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA), as well as the International Council for Harmonization (ICH), explicitly support the implementation of MIDD approaches. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 The majority of submissions to the FDA and EMA use PBPK modeling to predict and evaluate the potential for DDIs at the population‐level. 6 , 7 , 25 Such modeling supports the prediction of information that is of high regulatory interest, with the intention that simulations will substantiate drug product information in lieu of some clinical trial data. The EMA's recent qualification of Simcyp for predicting CYP‐mediated DDIs in defined contexts of use underlines the value of such modeling in supporting this aspect of regulatory submission. 26 Despite the dearth of regulatory guidance specifically regarding the use of VTs, the principles of guidelines for industry use of PBPK models are generally applicable.

In summary, VT design and application will depend upon the intended purpose and target population to be simulated, that is, individual or population. The high covariate virtualization VT should be applied judiciously to specific scenarios of interest in which such high fidelity is expected to be beneficial. The patient populations and drugs most likely to benefit from VTs include those that are high‐risk, complex, or understudied, in which significant interindividual variability exists and is associated with a clinical challenge. The drug must have an established exposure–response relationship that can be captured through integration of an exposure variable. Whether the drug profile is UB or SD, it must be validated for use. A 1:1 high covariate virtualization VT must represent the patient as closely as possible through integration of many real‐world patient‐specific covariates, it should be dose‐matched to the actual dose administered to the patient, and ideally compute 1,000 simulations per VT to stabilize and optimize model predictions.

DISCUSSION

Using a pragmatic systematic literature search, 22 original research articles were identified. Analysis highlighted key differences in VT design, simulation methods, and application that determined definitions of current approaches. Unsurprisingly, results demonstrated application in high‐risk, vulnerable, and understudied populations, where the value of modeling and simulation is evident and longstanding. The drugs utilized in VT‐PBPK are typically those with high risk of harm or significant interindividual variability. We observed a wide diversity in the methodology of building and validating VTs. There is limited regulatory guidance for VTs, and we have identified some key challenges and opportunities for VT‐PBPK.

VT‐PBPK has been consistently applied to populations in which alterations in physiological parameters and PK are expected, such as critical illness, renal impairment, and obesity. Also represented are many high‐risk, vulnerable populations that would typically be excluded from clinical trials due to safety concerns, such as pediatrics, pregnant women, and those with organ impairment. These complex, comorbid populations inherently require treatment with polypharmacy, which further increases the risk of medication‐related harm including adverse drug reactions and DDIs. It is in these complex or understudied populations that VT‐PBPK is most applicable, as a high virtualization VT may complement standard of care and support clinical judgment.

While there was no discernible trend across the literature in the number of simulations per VT, notably, the second‐earliest publication in 2018 reported 10 simulations per patient. 20 Since then, advances in computational capabilities have substantially reduced the time to perform such simulations. In their 2023 paper, 12 Mostafa et al. tested the number of simulations needed per VT to optimize and stabilize model predictions of individual clozapine trough concentrations. After simulating two participants over 14 days at trials of 10, 100, 1,000, and 5,000, it was determined that 1,000 trials per VT captured a stable median trough concentration per individual, increasing the number of simulations beyond this did not significantly affect the accuracy of results. Of note, Mostafa et al.'s subsequent MIPD‐VT work 27 utilized this rigorous method of 1,000 trials per VT, ensuring reliability in this aspect of model predictions. Although it did not meet criteria for inclusion in this review, Susilo et al. 28 also demonstrated a rigorous application of VTs in quantitative systems pharmacology investigating biomarkers of mosunetuzumab treatment response. For each of the 140 patients, 5,000–20,000 dose‐matched VTs were simulated per individual, from which 25 with the lowest objective function values were selected. From here, a VT‐Pop was created that contained each set of 25 VTs that most accurately represented each patient. This VT‐Pop was then utilized for further simulations across different dosing regimens. Such examples represent the early development of the VT, and likely foreshadow an ongoing emergence of innovative approaches to increase methodological rigor, model robustness, and accuracy of model predictions. While there is currently no evidence of use of AI in any aspects of VT‐PBPK, we anticipate its eventual incorporation through data collection and transformation, simulations, or model optimization.

As VT methods continue to increase in sophistication, advancements in new technologies will further support a high virtualization, multi‐omics data integration approach, whereby it is possible to integrate an increasing amount of real‐world patient‐specific covariate data. Conceptually, increasing data integration should improve predictive accuracy and decrease residual error; however, implementation of stepwise virtualization method has shown inconsistent results so far. We emphasize the importance of judicious data integration according the covariate's anticipated relevance to drug‐specific PK/PD, and note that practical data integration will be dependent upon data availability in practice. An increasing ability to characterize covariate data is highlighted in Rostami‐Hodjegan et al.'s recent proof‐of‐concept paper using noninvasive liquid biopsy techniques to describe hepatic protein abundance for input into VT models within Simcyp. 29 This work is the first to demonstrate the success of liquid biopsy informed VT models, and greatly improves virtualization of the patient. To date there is limited integration of PD within VT‐PBPK, with only two examples which integrate PD; via respiratory rate (RR) and a visual analog scale (VAS) measuring pain score. 30 , 31 As an extension of these works, we also identified a pilot study protocol to prospectively validate this “physics‐based digital twin” to support individualized opioid dosing and switching opiates or formulations. 32 We anticipate that VT‐PBPK will eventually integrate more PD markers, providing expanded capabilities to generate PD predictions in the same manner as PK.

The high covariate virtualization VT extends current TDM/TCI PK monitoring approaches through incorporation of more comprehensive covariate data to better represent the patient. This enhanced granularity aims to account for more interindividual variability than previously possible, enabling more refined, individualized predictions, particularly in scenarios requiring high‐fidelity MIPD. Unlike current methods, future MIPD‐VTs will enable precise a priori prediction of complex clinical scenarios affecting drug PK such as DDIs, and organ impairment. One stark advantage of this approach is the potential for clinicians to optimize treatment prior to drug exposure. Such a priori predictions will not only support the use of high‐risk medicines, but also intricacies such as changing drug formulations, 31 , 33 rotating between medications, 31 or predicting first‐dose exposures. 17 , 34 Despite enhanced modeling capacity, real‐world variability means dosing must be adjusted based on PD endpoints and patient observations, and MIPD‐VT predictions will likely need ongoing optimization, similar to TCI. Additionally, we must also consider the targets for validation of such MIPD‐VT tools, and whether the standard fold error metrics and accepted predicted vs. observed thresholds of 1.25‐ and 2‐fold range can be considered appropriate in the context of MIPD‐VT for high‐risk, narrow therapeutic index drugs.

The future trajectory of VTs in MIPD will face challenges relating to both technological advancement and local acceptability, as innovation, potentially also including AI approaches, continues to accelerate despite a lack of industry and regulator guidance (Table 3 ). The foremost design stage demands the development of purpose‐built MIPD‐VT software. Such dedicated tools must be capable of managing significant computations, numerous interacting covariates, and providing end‐users with outputs that are both focused and interpretable. The next, broadest consideration is the overall readiness of the healthcare system to accept such a sophisticated and revolutionary solution as the MIPD‐VT. As highlighted above, a high covariate virtualization VT comes with significant data requirements. While the ongoing rollout of EMR systems will see an increase in the accessibility of data, gaps in data availability will remain, for example genotype testing, which is not yet widely implemented (with a few exceptions). Implementation of MIPD‐VTs will undoubtedly face challenges in local acceptability, including requirements for health economic evaluations, and demonstrating benefit as opposed to traditional dosing methods. Well‐designed data collection in the early design and evaluation stages will be required to support robust health economic model design and evaluation. As the body of evidence for use continues to grow, stakeholders must consider the significant burden of medication‐related harm that would hopefully lessen through the targeted use of MIPD‐VTs. To this point, we consider the education requirements in upskilling a clinical workforce to understand where a VT is applicable, data requirements, and how to interpret, apply, and understand the limitations of model predictions. Practically, a more likely solution is something akin to the recently proposed pharmacogenomics stewardship programs, as the complexity and expertise demanded by MIPD‐VTs exceed the capacity of busy clinicians, and necessitate specialized support for effective implementation. 35 , 36 Additionally, ‘willingness‐to‐use’ and attitudes toward VT‐PBPK must be considered ahead of the eventual prospective clinical implementation of MIPD‐VT. Koldeweij et al.'s recent European cross‐sectional study 37 indicated strong support and willingness of both clinicians and pregnant women to use model‐informed antenatal dosing. Such studies which highlight the clinician‐ and patient‐preferred features of practicable MIPD tools will be crucial in shaping future clinical implementation.

It is important to acknowledge that while this review focused on the application of VT‐PBPK to generate individualized, patient‐specific predictions, such a novel approach has undoubtedly arisen from the extensive history of population modeling and simulation in PBPK. VPop simulations remain integral to drug development, particularly in understanding factors that influence drug PK and exposure, and informing dosing regimens.

As described above, a systematic literature search was not possible for this novel topic given the challenge of inadequate digital indexing. Despite this, we employed a structured, systematic approach utilizing multiple search methods. Where there was confusion in categorizing study design or simulation methodology, corresponding authors were contacted for clarification. Despite these limitations, we believe this work represents the most realizable, structured analysis of VT‐PBPK to date.

CONCLUSION

Virtual Twins represent a significant opportunity for PBPK modeling and simulation to support the future of precision dosing. This evolving landscape demands its own regulatory roadmap toward standardization and acceptability of use, not only within MIDD but also as a foundation for integration into MIPD. Given the ‘proof‐of‐concept’ status of MIPD‐VTs, the next step is rigorous design and evaluation to enable routine use. Significant evidence will need to amass before prospective clinical implementation is achievable; development which will require interdisciplinary union between modelers, software developers, clinicians, industry, and regulators.
