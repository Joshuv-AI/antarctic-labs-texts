Precise combination therapy of radiotherapy and immunotherapy guided by joint PD-L1 imaging and T-cell imaging
Authors: Hui Yi, Qi Luo, Zixian Bai, Xiaoda Li, Yue Yu, Jiyun Shi, Xiaotu Ma, Fan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy is often used in combination with radiotherapy (RT) to enhance therapeutic efficacy in clinic. However, the effectiveness of the combination therapy is still unsatisfactory due to the blindness in the administration schedule. More seriously, multiple dosing-induced deaths of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade often occur. Considering the urgency to enhance the efficacy and safety of the combination therapy, we pioneered a strategy that used SPECT/CT imaging of tumor PD-L1 expression and tumor-infiltrating T cells to guide and predict the combination therapy toward precision cancer therapy.MethodsSingle-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)/CT imaging of a PD-L1-targeting probe (99mTc-MY1523) was employed to monitor the dynamic changes of tumor PD-L1 expression following RT, guiding the timing of αPD-1 therapy. SPECT/CT imaging of a T-cell-targeting probe (99mTc-sum IL-2) was employed to predict the prognosis of combination therapy. The findings were validated in both immune hot and cold tumor models, using high-dose single RT or fractionated RT.ResultsAs indicated by the dynamic imaging of tumor PD-L1 expression, the combination therapy achieved optimal antitumor efficacy, only when αPD-1 antibody was administrated during the window period of PD-L1 upregulation following RT. However, although PD-L1 imaging could indicate when the combination therapy was more effective, it could not accurately predict the prognosis of the combination therapy. The T-cell imaging showed that the changed number of tumor-infiltrating T cells, rather than the absolute number at a certain time, could predict the prognosis in the early stage. The single-cell RNA sequencing analysis showed that the αPD-1 therapy after RT during the time window could prevent or reverse the exhausted CD8+ T cell state and switched the tumor immune microenvironment to an antitumor state, only in ‘responsive tumors’ (imaging showed an increase in tumor-infiltrating T cells) rather than ‘non-responsive tumors’.ConclusionsPD-L1 imaging was suitable for guiding the timing of αPD-1 administration, while T-cell imaging was more suitable for predicting prognosis. The two imaging modalities jointly provide essential guidance to enhance the efficacy and safety of the combination therapy, facilitating individualized precision therapy. This imaging strategy can also be extrapolated to clinical practice for immunotherapy combined with other therapies to boost efficacy.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Combination therapy, Immune related adverse event - irAE, Nuclear medicine, Radiotherapy/radioimmunotherapy

Background

Cancer immunotherapies have transformed the landscape of cancer treatment over the past few decades. Among these, immune checkpoint inhibitors, including anti-PD-1 (αPD-1) and anti-PD-L1 (αPD-L1) antibodies, are the most frequently used in clinic settings. However, only a fraction of patients with cancer benefits from this therapy1; therefore, they are often used in combination with other treatments to enhance antitumor response.2

About half of all patients with cancer need to receive radiotherapy (RT). When RT is combined with αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies, it achieves a better therapeutic effect than when either therapy is used alone.3,5 RT reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment, such as promoting the cross-presentation of dendritic cells (DCs), recruiting and activating CD8+ T cells, and increasing the intratumoral PD-L1 expression. All of these changes can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies.6 7

However, the effectiveness of this therapeutic combination is often hindered, with mortality frequently resulting from multiple dosing of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in clinical scenarios.8 9 Moreover, about 20% of patients receiving αPD-1 treatment experience serious immune-related adverse events (irAEs) of grade ≥3.10 This is largely attributed to the absence of clear guidelines.11 Therefore, it is imperative to determine the optimal administration schedule to improve the efficacy and safety of the combined therapy.

The level of intratumoral PD-L1 expression is a crucial factor influencing the αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapeutic efficacy. Patients exhibiting high PD-L1 expression are more likely to benefit from αPD-1/αPD-L1 treatments.12 Additionally, the presence of tumor-infiltrating T cells is integral to the response to αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies.13 Currently, the most common method for detecting PD-L1 expression and T cell infiltration in tumors relies on the immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of biopsy specimens.14 15 However, due to the spatial heterogeneity and dynamic nature of intratumoral PD-L1 expression and tumor-infiltrating T cells, comprehensive and precise detection using this method can be challenging.16

Nuclear medicine molecular imaging, with its ability to provide non-invasive, dynamic, and comprehensive insights into intratumoral PD-L1 expression and T-cell infiltration, can overcome the limitations of IHC. In this study, our objective is to use PD-L1 imaging and T-cell imaging to guide the scheduling of combination therapy administration and predict the efficacy. We aim to enhance the effectiveness of the combination therapy and accomplish precise tumor treatment.

Methods

Cells and animal models

MC38 cells were kindly provided by the lab of Prof. Yangxin Fu at the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). B16F10 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, Virginia, USA). Cells were cultured in 5% CO2 at 37°C and maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS). Female C57BL/6 mice of 6–7 weeks were purchased from the Department of Laboratory Animal Science of Peking University. MC38 cells (1×106) and B16F10 cells (5×105) were inoculated subcutaneously into the front flanks of C57BL/6 mice. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions in the Department of Laboratory Animal Science of Peking University. The tumor volume was calculated as (length×width×height)/2.

Preparation of 99mTc-MY1523 and 99mTc-sum IL-2

The murine PD-L1-targeted nanobody MY1523 was provided by Novamab Biopharmaceuticals (Shanghai, China). The sum IL-2 was expressed and purified from Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells. Both MY1523 and sum IL-2 have an LPETG-His6 tag on the C-terminus for Ni-sepharose purification and site-specific transpeptidation reaction of Sortase-A. The two-step site-specific labeling approach was adopted to prepare 99mTc-MY1523 and 99mTc-sum IL-2. First, GGGGK-HYNIC peptide (GGGGK=Gly-Gly-Gly-Gly-Lys, HYNIC=6-hydrazinonicotinyl) was labeled with 99mTc to form 99mTc-HYNIC-G4K. Subsequently, 99mTc-HYNIC-G4K was conjugated to an LPETG tag on MY1523 or sum IL-2 with Sortase-A to form 99mTc-MY1523 or 99mTc-sum IL-2. His-tagged Sortase-A and unreacted sum IL-2 were removed by an Ni column (Cytiva, USA). The product was purified by high-performance size exclusion chromatography (Superdex-75TM column increase 10/300 GL, GE Healthcare Life Science) using PBS (pH 7.4) as the eluent. The radiochemical purity of 99mTc-MY1523 and 99mTc-sum IL-2 was determined using instant thin layer chromatography-silica gel (Agilent, USA) developed in saline.

SPECT/CT imaging

For small-animal SPECT/CT imaging, the mice bearing MC38 or B16F10 tumors were injected intravenously with 18 MBq 99mTc-MY1523 or 99mTc-sum IL-2. For 99mTc-MY1523, the mice were imaged 1 hour after injection. For 99mTc-sum IL-2, the mice were imaged 0.5 hour after injection using the NanoScan SPECT/CT system (Mediso, Hungary) following a standard protocol: pinhole SPECT (peak: 140 keV, 20% width; frame time: 25 s), helical CT (50 kVp, 0.67 mA, rotation 210°, exposure time: 300 ms). SPECT and CT images were merged using the Nucline software V.2.0 (Mediso). The regions of interest were drawn for determining tumor uptake calculated as the percentage injected dose per volume (%ID/cc).

RT, αPD-1, and bevacizumab therapy

Tumor-bearing mice were anesthetized and secured into custom lead-shielded jigs that exposed the tumor alone to radiation, and irradiated with 10 Gy or 2 Gy×5 using an X-ray irradiator (Rad Source Technologies, USA) at a dose rate of 3.5 Gy/min. Tumor-bearing mice received 5 mg/kg αPD-1 (clone RMP1-14, BioXCell, USA) via the tail vein. In the B16F10 mouse model, 10 mg/kg bevacizumab (AV, Roche Pharma, Switzerland) was administered via the tail vein 6 hours before RT.

Flow cytometry

The mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested, the tumor tissue digestive fluid (Cat. DHTE-5001, RWD Life Science, China) was added to the tumor tissue fragments for digestion at 37 ℃ for 1–2 hours. The single-cell suspension was separated by 40 μm sized cell sieves. 3 mL of Erythrocyte Lysis Solution (Cat. CC051, macgene, China) was added to the cell pellet and incubated on ice for 5 min, and then centrifuged at 300 g at 4°C for 10 min. Finally, 1×106 cells were resuspended with 100 µL buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS). Each sample was added with 1 µl CD16/32 (clone 2.4G2, BioXcell) and incubated for 10 min at 4°C for blocking Fc receptors on immune cells. LIVE/DEAD kit (Cat. L34959, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to distinguish alive cells from dead cells. To analyze PD-L1 expression on tumor cells (CD45-) and DCs (CD45+ CD11c+ MHCII+), the cells were incubated with anti-CD45 (Cat. 56-0451-82, eBio- science, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), anti-CD11c (Cat. 15-0114-82, eBioscience), anti-MHC-II (Cat. 25-5321-82, eBioscience) and anti-PD-L1 (Cat. 12-5982-82, eBioscience) antibodies at 4°C for 30 min. To analyze tumor infiltration numbers of CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells, the cells were incubated with anti-CD45, anti-CD3e (Cat. 25-0031-82, eBioscience), anti-CD4 (Cat. 45-0042-82, eBioscience) and anti-CD8a (Cat. 11-0081-82, eBioscience) antibodies at 4°C for 30 min. Foxp3 staining was performed using a transcription factor staining buffer kit (Cat. 00-5523-00, eBioscience) and Foxp3 antibody (1 µg/mL, Cat. 12-4771-82, eBioscience). Samples were analyzed via a Gallios (Beckman Coulter, USA) flow cytometer.

Single-cell sequencing

The tumors were prepared into cell suspension according to the above method, 2×107 cells were resuspended with 200 µL buffer (PBS containing 0.04% BSA and 2 mM EDTA). The dead cells were removed from the single-cell suspension using a dead cell separation kit (Cat. 130-090-101, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and finally formulated into a 90 µL cell suspension containing 1×107 live cells. CD4/CD8 (tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte, TIL) MicroBeads (Cat. 130-116-480, Miltenyi Biotec) were used to isolate TILs. Using the Chromium microfluidic system, individual TILs were wrapped and labeled with isolated oil droplets, in which the cells were ruptured to release mRNA and reverse-transcribed into labeled cDNA. cDNA released from ruptured oil droplets was collected, purified, amplified, and connected to sequencing splices to prepare a 10×Genomics single-cell transcriptome library. After the library was qualified, PE150 sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The TCR sequence data from 10×Genomics were processed using Cell Ranger software (V.3.1).

Statistical analysis

The sample size for analysis was as annotated in figure legends. FlowJo V.10.4 (BD Biosciences, USA) and ImageJ V.1.8.0. (National Institutes of Health, USA) were used to analyze the acquired data. Data were analyzed using Prism V.8 (GraphPad Software, USA) and expressed as the mean±SD. Significant differences between the two groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001 ****p<0.0001 were considered significant.

Results

PD-L1-targeted molecular imaging can guide combination therapy to maximize efficacy

To investigate whether PD-L1-targeted imaging could guide the combination of RT and αPD-1 therapy, we employed SPECT/CT imaging of a PD-L1-targeted probe (99mTc-MY1523) to monitor the dynamic changes of tumor PD-L1 expression following RT. 99mTc-MY1523 was developed in our previous study to visualize the PD-L1 expression in vivo, and its specificity for PD-L1 has been validated.17 When the MC38 tumors grew to around 150 mm3 bilaterally in C57 mice, the one side tumor was irradiated with 10 Gy of X-ray on day 0, and 99mTc-MY1523 SPECT/CT imaging was performed on days 0, 3, 6, and 9 (figure 1A). It was found that the uptake of 99mTc-MY1523 in the irradiated tumors significantly increased on day 3 and then decreased on days 6 and 9 (figure 1B, C and online supplemental figure S1A). Flow cytometry analyses showed that the mean fluorescence intensity of PD-L1 on tumor cells and immune cells, especially DCs within the tumors, was upregulated by RT intervention (figure 1D and online supplemental figure S1A-C), and the trend was consistent with the dynamic imaging of PD-L1 in vivo. Besides, the PD-L1 expression in the abscopal tumors was also highest on day 3 (figure 1B-D, B to D and online supplemental figure S1C). Therefore, the PD-L1 expression in tumors changed dynamically after RT, and there was an obvious time window of the upregulation of PD-L1 expression, which could be determined by 99mTc-MY1523 SPECT/CT.

Then, we evaluated the treatment efficacy of RT plus αPD-1 combination therapy. Mice-bearing bilateral MC38 tumors were treated with αPD-1 antibody administered prior to, concurrently with, or following RT. All combination therapies were well tolerated (online supplemental figure S1D). The combination therapy was most effective when αPD-1 treatment was performed on day 3 after RT, and 4/8 of the mice were completely cured (figure 1E, F). The cured mice were rechallenged with a secondary inoculation of 1×10⁶ MC38 cells on day 60 post-RT, and no tumor growth was observed up to day 90. This suggested that the optimized combination therapy induced a robust immune memory effect.

Based on the results of PD-L1 dynamic imaging, it was concluded that the combination of αPD-1 therapy during the window period of PD-L1 upregulation following RT was most effective, while the combination of αPD-1 therapy outside the window period of PD-L1 upregulation was less effective (figure 1F). However, even with the combined therapy of αPD-1 during the window period of PD-L1 upregulation, not all tumors could be cured due to individual differences (figure 1F). Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the causes of individual variances and assess the prognosis at an early stage in the course of combination therapy.

T-cell imaging can identify tumors that can be cured after combined therapy

The combination of 10 Gy RT and 5 mg/kg αPD-1 in MC38 tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice resulted in complete tumor regression in approximately half of the animals. This response rate provided a suitable model to investigate the key indicator of prognosis. To examine the differences in T-cell infiltration between response and non-response tumors during the combination therapy, we performed T-cell imaging using an IL-2 receptor-targeting probe (99mTc-sum IL-2) to monitor the dynamic changes of T-cell infiltration at tumor sites. 99mTc-sum IL-2 was developed in our previous study to track T cells in vivo, and it has been validated that sum IL-2 preferentially binds to CD8+ T cells, especially activated CD8+ T cells, and reduces binding to Treg cells.18 19 We validated in MC38 tumors that the signals from SPECT imaging and autoradiography coincided with the fluorescent signals of CD8+ T cells in serially sectioned samples (online supplemental figure S2A), while CD8+ T cells could be observed accumulating at the periphery of the tumor. Additionally, after treating MC38 tumor-bearing mice with graduated doses of RT, we observed a significantly positive correlation between the tumor uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 and the proportion of T cells detected by flow cytometry (online supplemental figure S2B-D). Next, 99mTc-MY1523 SPECT/CT and 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT were performed in mice bearing MC38 tumors treated with RT plus αPD-1 combination therapy (figure 2A). The imaging of the two tracers did not interfere with each other, because both tracers could be completely metabolized at the tumor sites within 2 days (online supplemental figure S3A). As shown in figure 2B and online supplemental figure S3B, PD-L1 SPECT/CT imaging basically showed an increasing trend of 99mTc-MY1523 uptake after RT; however, the changes in PD-L1 expression in tumors did not predict the treatment efficacy of combination therapy. The changes in 99mTc-sum IL-2 uptake in tumors varied greatly among different individuals after combination therapy (figure 2C and online supplemental figure S3B), when 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging was performed on days 1 and 6. The 99mTc-sum IL-2 uptake of some tumors significantly increased after combination therapy, whereas in others, it significantly decreased. Tumors were classified as responders or non-responders based on their status of cure or non-cure following combination therapy. As shown in figure 2D and online supplemental figure S3B, the treatment efficacy was not related to the static uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 at a certain time point. Importantly, we found that the treatment efficacy was closely related to the dynamic changes in tumor uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 following combination therapy, which referred to the changes in tumor-infiltrating T cells. The tumor uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 in responders obviously increased after combination therapy, while the tumor uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 in non-responders failed to increase (figure 2D). The growth of abscopal tumors was also correlated with the changes of tumor-infiltrating T cells after the combination treatment (figure 2E). In addition, the study showed that the changes in tumor-infiltrating T cells from day 0 to day 3 (before combining with αPD-1 therapy) could hardly achieve the prediction (online supplemental figure S4). Therefore, we suggested that the changes in tumor-infiltrating T cells following combination therapy were a potential indicator for predicting the prognosis of tumor treatment.

Nuclear imaging can guide and predict fractionated RT combined with αPD-1 therapy

Fractionated RT (FRT) is more commonly used than single high-dose RT in clinic.20 21 We fractionated a single dose of 10 Gy into the form of 2 Gy per day for 5 consecutive days. 99mTc-MY1523 SPECT/CT imaging was conducted before RT and on days 3, 6, and 9 after the first RT, which showed that PD-L1 expression in the tumors was significantly up-regulated on day 3 (online supplemental figure S5). We investigated the utility of T-cell imaging for prognosis evaluation of FRT combined with αPD-1 therapy. As illustrated in figure 3A, mice bearing MC38 tumors were treated with FRT (2 Gy×5), and αPD-1 treatment was performed on day 3 following FRT (the time window of PD-L1 upregulation). 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging was performed on days 0 and 6. The treatment efficacy of different individuals varied greatly, although the mice received the same treatment of FRT plus αPD-1 combination therapy (figure 3B). We also classified these tumors as responders and non-responders. The tumor uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 in responders obviously increased from day 0 to day 6, while the tumor uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 in non-responders failed to increase (figure 3C). Therefore, 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging can indeed predict the prognosis of FRT plus αPD-1 combination therapy early on, even before differences in tumor volume become apparent.

99mTc-sum IL-2 imaging can predict and guide the combination therapy for immuno-cold tumors

B16F10 tumors are referred to as ‘immuno-cold tumors’ due to their limited infiltration of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) and their poor response to immunotherapy.22 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging was performed on mice bearing MC38 or B16F10 tumors, and it was observed that the tracer uptake in B16F10 tumors was significantly lower than that in MC38 tumors (online supplemental figure S6A). To investigate whether 99mTc-sum IL-2 imaging could predict the prognosis of combination therapy for immuno-cold tumors, mice bearing B16F10 tumors were treated with 10 Gy of RT combined with αPD-1 therapy, and 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging was performed on days 0, 3, and 6 (figure 4A-C and online supplementalfigure S6B). Due to the extreme difficulty in curing B16F10 tumors, we defined the relatively slow-growing tumors as responders, and the tumors that grew rapidly were defined as non-responders. The treatment efficacy was also closely related to the changes of 99mTc-sum IL-2 uptake in tumors from day 0 to day 6 (figure 4C), but not from day 0 to day 3 (online supplemental figure S6B).

The disorganized vasculature in B16F10 tumors is one of the reasons to impede T-cell infiltration, and administering a low dose of bevacizumab (AV) can normalize the vasculature and promote T-cell infiltration into the tumors.23,25 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging showed that in B16F10 tumor-bearing mice, the administration of AV (10 mg/kg) before RT plus αPD-1 therapy resulted in more T-cell infiltration into the tumors compared with the RT plus αPD-1 therapy (figure 4D). The AV plus RT plus αPD-1 tri-combination therapy significantly prolonged the survival of mice bearing B16F10 tumors (figure 4E and online supplemental figure S6C). Moreover, 99mTc-sum IL-2 could accurately monitor the changes of tumor-infiltrating T cells following tri-combination therapy, which were closely related to the prognosis (figure 4E, F). IHC analysis confirmed that AV could reduce the immature blood vessels of tumors and promote T-cell infiltration into tumors (figure 4G, H and online supplemental figure S6D). Thus, it is demonstrated that for two different tumor models (MC38 and B16F10) and two combination therapy approaches (RT plus αPD-1 therapy and AV plus RT plus αPD-1 therapy), 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging can predict treatment efficacy by monitoring changes of tumor-infiltrating T cells.

Single-cell sequencing unveils mechanisms for responsive and non-responsive tumors identified by molecular imaging

99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging could distinguish responsive tumors from non-responsive tumors at the early stage of combination therapy. Then, we attempted to elucidate the early immune differences between responsive and non-responsive tumors by single-cell sequencing to investigate the mechanisms. As illustrated in figure 5A, the tumor-infiltrating T cells were sorted out, and after quality control, the dataset included 18,065 total cells covering the three groups.

We first performed unsupervised clustering analysis of CD8+ and CD4+ T cell subsets and labeled clusters based on marker gene expression (figure 5B, C and online supplemental figure 7). CD8+ T cells formed eight distinct clusters and could be divided into four states: naive, proliferative, terminal exhausted, and non-exhausted. CD4+ T cells formed seven distinct clusters. We found that the non-responsive tumors had a threefold increase in the proportion of terminal exhausted T (terminal Tex) cells after combination therapy, and in the responsive tumors, the proportion of effector memory T (Tem) cells and tissue-resident memory T (Trm) cells increased compared with pretreatment (figure 5B). The T-cell receptor (TCR) analysis also showed that the clonal expansion of terminal Tex cells was significantly increased in the non-responder tumors (figure 5D), which could be detrimental to antitumor immunity. The clonal expansion of Trm cells and Tem cells in the responsive tumors was increased, which has been reported to be related to treatment response.26 The proportion of CD8+ and CD4+ T subsets characterized by the Isg15 gene was increased in the responsive tumors (figure 5B, C), and this cell population may be either driven by TCR-triggered IFN-γ or directly driven by IFN-γ, representing an activated state of T cells.27

Immunosuppressive checkpoint genes (Pdcd1, Havcr2, Lag3)28 were significantly upregulated in CD8+ T cells of the non-responsive tumors (figure 5E). The molecular mechanism of αPD-1 therapy is to reverse the exhausted state of precursor exhausted T cells and restore their tumor-killing function.27 These data effectively suggested that the αPD-1 administration in non-responders did not relieve the suppressed state of T cell function.

Then, we conducted a bulk-RNAseq analysis of T cells between three groups and found that the non-responsive tumors had significantly higher expression of Pdcd1 (coding gene of PD-1) and Rgs16 compared with pre-treatment tumors (figure 5F). Rgs16 is a negative regulator of T-cell function, which expression can mark a subpopulation of terminal differentiation of T cells that have lost T-cell stemness and are less responsive to αPD-1 therapy.29 The responsive tumors showed higher expression of genes related to inflammation (Ly6c2), T cell retention marker genes (Itga1), co-stimulatory molecules (Klrk1, Irf7), and chemokine receptors (Cxcr6), compared with pretreatment tumors. These genes favor antitumor therapy.30 31

Therefore, we demonstrated that the TME of the responsive tumors identified by 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging was in an immune-activated state, which was conducive to the infiltration and function of T cells. However, in the non-responsive tumors, CD8+ T cells were rapidly exhausted, and the TME was in an immune-suppressed state.

Discussion

Cancer immunotherapies, especially αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies, are widely combined with RT in clinic. This combination strategy enhances the treatment efficacy through multiple synergetic mechanisms.1 3 32 33 Due to the severe consequences associated with irAEs such as fatal myocarditis, the dosage and frequency of αPD-1/αPD-L1 administration should be minimized while ensuring the combination therapy achieves optimal therapeutic efficacy. However, there is a lack of clear guidelines on the order and timing of the two treatments.

PD-L1 expression, as quantified by IHC assays, is currently the primary biomarker for guiding patient selection for αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies. Despite this, only 20%–40% of patients will benefit from these therapies and even fewer will have long-term disease remission.12 In addition, clinical evidence suggests that PD-L1 positive patients may not always respond to treatment, while some PD-L1 negative patients do,34,36 indicating the limitations of solely evaluating static tumor PD-L1 expression. Several studies have successfully used SPECT/CT or PET/CT to assess the intratumoral expression of PD-L1 and pointed out that these imaging techniques have potential in selecting suitable patients for αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies.37 38 Unlike previous studies, which performed PD-L1 imaging at a single time point to assess the static expression of PD-L1 in tumors, we used 99mTc-MY1523 SPECT/CT imaging to dynamically monitor tumor PD-L1 expression changes. We discovered that tumor PD-L1 expression fluctuated following RT, and administering a single dose of αPD-1 antibody during the PD-L1 upregulation window post-RT could optimize the therapeutic effect. The RT-induced dynamic upregulation of PD-L1 is possibly due to the activation of intrinsic signaling pathways in tumor cells (ATR-Chk1 DNA repair pathway) and remodeling of the immune microenvironment (IFN-γ produced by CD8+ T cells).39 40 Using 99mTc-MY1523 for the dynamic SPECT/CT imaging of tumor PD-L1 expression will provide new technical means for clinical practice to realize imaging-guided treatment of individual patients, thereby improving the efficacy of the combination therapy, while reducing the frequency and dosage of αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies to improve the safety.

Although PD-L1 imaging could guide the optimal order and timing of combination therapy, we found that it could hardly predict the prognosis in early stage, since not all tumors could be cured, even with the combined therapy of αPD-1 during the window period of PD-L1 upregulation. Infiltration and functionality of T cells within tumors are crucial for the response to αPD-1/αPD-L1 therapies.41 42 Previous studies suggested that using T-cell tracers to monitor tumor-infiltrating T cells at a certain time point (either before or after immunotherapy) could predict response to αPD-1 therapy.43 44 Different from these studies, our research used 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT for dynamic monitoring of tumor-infiltrating T cells during combination therapy. It was surprisingly found that the dynamic increase in T cell infiltration during combination therapy correlated positively with treatment efficacy, offering early therapeutic efficacy prediction. To further verify this conclusion, we conducted dynamic imaging of T cells following FRT combined with αPD-1 therapy, as well as following a tri-combination therapy in an immuno-cold tumor model. Experimental results consistently demonstrated that monitoring dynamic changes in tumor-infiltrating T cells during combination therapies using 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging could predict the prognosis in the early stage. By predicting the treatment outcomes through imaging at an early stage, patients can have the opportunity to receive effective therapy.

In this study, we pioneered a strategy of dual-tracer imaging to guide combination therapy and predict treatment response, which could facilitate more precise treatment. PD-L1 imaging was suitable for guiding the timing of αPD-1 administration. While ensuring maximum efficacy, the use of blind dosing could be reduced to alleviate the financial burden on patients and minimize the occurrence of serious irAEs. Meanwhile, T-cell imaging was more suitable for predicting prognosis, enabling the timely adjustment of treatment plans for non-responsive patients. The two imaging modalities are complementary and irreplaceable, providing essential guidance to enhance the efficacy and safety of the combination therapy. Moreover, the strategy that uses PD-L1 imaging and T-cell imaging to guide and predict the combination therapy is applicable not only to irradiated tumors but also to abscopal tumors in the bilateral-tumor mouse model.

To elucidate the mechanism for the individual differences in therapeutic efficacy observed in combination therapies, single-cell RNA sequencing analysis was performed on T cells from tumors identified by 99mTc-sum IL-2 SPECT/CT imaging as responders and non-responders. Overall, the decrease of T-cell infiltration in non-responsive tumors was due to the rapid exhaustion of T cells and the immunosuppressive TME, which inevitably led to rapid tumor growth. We also found that in T cells from the non-responsive tumors, the expression of immune-suppressive genes, such as the Rgs16 gene, significantly increased compared with pretreatment. Rgs16 expression can promote antitumor CD8+ T cell exhaustion and forebode poor response to αPD-1 therapy.29 This suggests that patients should not blindly receive more αPD-1 therapies when tumors have reduced the uptake of 99mTc-sum IL-2 during combination therapy. The treatment regimen of these patients should be timely adjusted. In contrast, the TME of the responsive tumors was in an immune-activated state, while some genes that were beneficial for anti-tumor therapy were highly expressed. Therefore, the single-cell RNA sequencing analysis has substantiated that dynamic imaging of T cells can be used to distinguish immuno-activated tumors from immuno-suppressed tumors, elucidating the mechanism for T-cell imaging to predict the prognosis of tumor treatment.

Conclusions

We have pioneered a strategy that employs SPECT/CT imaging of both tumor PD-L1 expression and tumor-infiltrating T cells to guide and predict combination therapy of RT and αPD-1 therapy. This is the first instance of two nuclear medicine imaging modalities being used to guide combination treatment, thereby facilitating individualized precision therapy. The results of this study can improve the administration tactics of PD-1 antibodies, reducing the number of doses while ensuring therapeutic effectiveness. This not only reduces toxicity but also saves costs for patients. By predicting the effectiveness of treatment through imaging at an early stage, the effectiveness of the treatment can also be improved. Moreover, compared with IHC, nuclear medical imaging is non-invasive, making it easier for patients to accept, while overcoming the issue of tumor heterogeneity. Overall, this research work holds significant clinical application value. Encouraged by the promising preclinical findings, we have initiated clinical translation research. Moreover, this strategy also possesses potential for clinical application in immunotherapy when combined with other therapies, such as chemotherapy and targeted RT, to boost efficacy.
