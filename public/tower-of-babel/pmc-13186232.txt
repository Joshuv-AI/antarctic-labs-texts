Domestic Violence and Quality of Life Among Pregnant Women During Covid-19 in Remote Areas of Indonesia
Authors: Triyana Harlia Putri, Suryani Suryani, Khatijah Lim Abdullah, Fitri Fujiana, Dwi Christina Rahayuningrum, Septian Andriyani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic not only caused psychological problems but also increased domestic violence (DV) against pregnant women due to uncertainty about the pandemic’s outcome, which could adversely affect their Quality of Life (QoL). Therefore, understanding how DV affects QoL during pregnancy is essential. Therefore, it is essential to understand how DV affects QoL during pregnancy remote areas.AimTo investigate the relationship between DV during pregnancy on QoL during the COVID-19 pandemic in remote area.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 647 out of 12,576 pregnant women attending six public health centers in remote areas of Pontianak, Indonesia. Convenience and snowball sampling techniques were employed due to limited access and geographical constraints in remote settings. Data were collected between in June-July 2022. The instruments included a sociodemographic questionnaire, the WHO Violence Against Women Instrument (VAWI), and the Quality of Life Gravidarum (QoL-GRAV) questionnaire. Spearman rank correlation test was used to assess the relationship between DV and QoL.ResultsA total of 47% of pregnant women experienced intimate partner violence, with physical violence being the most common form, followed by emotional violence and sexual violence. Among those exposed to DV, 24.4% had poor QoL. Women exposed to DV had significantly lower QoL scores compared to those who did not. A negative correlation was found between DV and QoL (r = –0.223) COVID-19 status and employment were also significantly associated with DV (p < 0.05).ConclusionDV negatively impacts the QoL of pregnant women during the COVID-19 pandemic These findings highlight the need for strengthened policies, including routine psychological screening, counseling services, and the integration of digital-based psychiatric mental health nursing interventions, particularly in remote areas with limited healthcare access.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has increased psychosocial stress among pregnant women worldwide. Previous studies have reported heightened psychological distress, fear, anxiety, and depression during the pandemic, which may lead to poor adherence to prenatal care and pose risks to both mother and fetus.1,2 In addition, infection during pregnancy increases the likelihood of severe disease.3 Consequently, adverse pregnancy outcomes may occur, including high mortality, peripartum complications, increased hospitalizations, caesarean section, preterm birth, and low birth weight.4 These serious problems during pregnancy can negatively impact quality of life (QoL),5 and high-risk pregnancies are associated with reduced QoL.6

QoL is defined as perceived satisfaction with various aspects of life,7 and QoL during pregnancy is particularly important,8 as low QoL in pregnant women increases the risk of serious consequences for the foetus and mother. Obstetric problems can subsequently impact postpartum QoL,9 negatively impacting the well-being of both mother and child.10 A systematic review prior to the pandemic reported that QoL during pregnancy was influenced by physical and psychological factors.11 However, during the pandemic, the QoL of pregnant and breastfeeding women decreased.12 Furthermore, low QoL is found in women who have experienced unpleasant events, unmarried women, and those in their second trimester.13 Intimate partner violence, as a major psychosocial stressor, has been consistently associated with lower QoL among pregnant women.14 Studies have reported that increased DV reduces QoL during pregnancy.15 Multiple stressors during pregnancy can substantially decrease QoL.16

DV during pregnancy has a significant impact on the health of women and their foetuses,17 including miscarriage, stillbirth, abortion, early foetal loss, poor child development after birth, anxiety and depression18 and other mental health problems.19 A significant absence of prenatal care visits during COVID-19 has been noted,20 leaving the world facing two health emergencies: COVID-19 and DV.21 DV against women during pregnancy and the postpartum period continues to increase,22 including psychological, sexual, and physical violence.23–25 Recent global evidence involving more than 600,000 women across 57 low- and middle-income countries has highlighted the high prevalence, disparities, and associated factors of intimate partner violence during pregnancy, while studies during the COVID-19 period have shown that women experiencing perinatal violence face barriers in accessing healthcare and support services26,27 However, a drastic increase in DV occurred during the COVID-19 era. Reports from 2019–2022 indicate a 23% increase in DV in pregnant women,28 including DV prevalence greater than 33% in rural areas,29 while the most recent report of the combined prevalence of total DV is greater than 36%.30 DV varies within and between continents, with the highest rates in Africa and the lowest in Europe.31 However, a 2.2% prevalence of DV has been reported in China.32 In Indonesia, DV during pregnancy has been reported,33 although province-specific data remain limited.

Although several studies have examined the relationship between DV and QoL among pregnant women, most evidence comes from urban or well-resourced settings. There remains a significant gap in empirical data from remote and underserved areas in developing countries, including Indonesia, where access to maternal and mental health services is limited. These contextual constraints may exacerbate the impact of DV on pregnant women’s QoL, yet they are rarely captured in existing research. In addition, socio-cultural factors such as gender norms, stigma surrounding DV, and limited help-seeking behavior may further influence both the occurrence and reporting of DV in these settings. Pontianak is one of the remote areas in Indonesia that has experienced the impacts of the pandemic, including poverty, maternal mortality, and violence.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, these disparities were likely intensified in remote areas due to service disruptions, mobility restrictions, and reduced social support. However, evidence on how these conditions affected the relationship between DV and QoL among pregnant women in such settings remains limited. Data from the West Kalimantan in 2021 shows that the poverty rate increased by 10% from 2019 to 2021 and 12% during COVID-19.34 Furthermore, there was an increase of 52 maternal deaths during COVID-19 in 2021, representing more than 50%.34 The rise in violence against women in Pontianak has also been associated with decreased household income and socio-economic vulnerability, influenced by diverse age, ethnic, and economic backgrounds.35

The increased prevalence of DV among pregnant women during the pandemic is alarming, resulting in significant changes during pregnancy, even having severe consequences throughout QoL. However, studies on the relationship between DV and QoL of pregnant women, especially in remote areas of developing countries, are still scarce. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship between DV and QoL among pregnant women in remote areas of Indonesia during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Method

Data were collected from June to July 2022, using a cross-sectional study design. A total of 12,576 pregnant women were recruited from six public health centersin the remote city of Pontianak. The Isaac and Michael table was used, with a 1% margin of error, resulting in a required sample of 647 respondents. Convenience and snowball sampling techniques were employed due to mobility restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic and limited access to pregnant women in remote areas. Snowball sampling was further used to reach additional eligible participants through participant referrals.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) pregnant women, (2) visiting public health centers located in various areas of Pontianak (3) not experiencing mental disorders, and (4) willing to participate in the study. Respondents were excluded if diagnosed with COVID-19. Data collection was conducted using a hybrid approach. Although the questionnaire was administered via a Google Forms link, participants were recruited in person during antenatal care visits at public health centers. Researchers provided direct assistance to participants who had difficulties using smartphones or accessing the online questionnaire, ensuring that all respondents could complete the survey properly. The public health centerswere located across Pontianak, specifically in the northern, southeastern, eastern, southern, and western parts of the city, as well as within the city center.

The instrument consisted of sociodemographic questions including age, education, occupation, COVID-19 status, gestational age, and parity. DV was assessed using the Indonesian version of the WHO Violence Against Women Instrument (VAWI), which has good internal consistency.36 The VAWI consists of 13 questions measuring psychological (four), physical (six), and sexual (three) violence, assessing partner-perpetrated violence in these three areas. Responses were recorded on a five-level Likert scale indicating frequency during the 12 months prior to the survey (0–5 times). These were then categorized as follows: 1–2 times, mild violence; 3–5 times, moderate violence; >5 times, severe violence.37–39 QOL-GRAV was developed by Vachkova et al.40 The Indonesian version has very high internal consistency.41 This questionnaire contains nine questions that address the QoL of individual women during pregnancy. A lower score indicates a higher QoL. The overall score categorizes the QoL-GRAV as excellent (9–18 points), moderate (19–27 points), mild (28–36 points), or low (37–45 points).42

The data obtained in this study were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23. Descriptive statistics are presented as frequency and percentage values for categorical variables and as mean, standard deviation, median, and minimum and maximum values for numeric variables. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In bivariate analyses, we assessed the correlation between DV with QoL and correlation type of DV and with QoL using Spearman’s rank correlation test. We examine the correlation between characteristics of gravida woman and obstetric information with DV using chi-square.

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine, Tanjungpura University (No. 780/UN.22.9/PG/2022). All respondents who completed the survey received a written information sheet and provided signed informed consent prior to participation. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Given the sensitive nature of DV, data collection was conducted in a manner that ensured participants’ privacy and confidentiality. Responses were anonymized, and participants were assured that their information would not be disclosed to any third parties. Researchers uphold the right to participate in research, the right to fair treatment, the right to refuse participation, and the right to privacy and confidentiality. Data collected will be stored and kept strictly confidential and deleted upon completion of the study.

The majority of respondents had a high level of education (583; 90.1%), while only a small proportion had a lower level of education. Nearly half of the participants were employed as traders (320; 49.5%), followed by other occupations. In terms of obstetric characteristics, 320 (49.5%) participants were in the second trimester of pregnancy, and more than half were multiparous (≥2 children) (381; 58.9%). Discussion

This study aimed to examine the impact of DV during pregnancy on QoL during the COVID-19 pandemic in a remote area of Indonesia. The findings indicate a statistically significant negative relationship between all types of DV (emotional, physical, and sexual) and QoL, suggesting that higher levels of DV are associated with poorer QoL. Previous research also reported a significant negative correlation between DV and QoL.15 In this study, although pregnant women reported varying levels of QoL, those who experienced DV were more likely to report poor QoL. This indicates that, on average, women exposed to DV had lower QoL scores compared to those who were not exposed, which is consistent with previous findings.43 These findings also highlight the importance of understanding domestic violence within underserved and remote settings, such as Pontianak, which remain underrepresented in the literature, particularly from mental health and gender perspectives. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of integrating psychosocial and gender-sensitive approaches into maternal healthcare policies, particularly in emergency and resource-limited settings.

An important finding of this study is that women who experienced DV were often exposed to multiple types of violence (two to three forms) within a 12-month period. This highlights the cumulative effect of violence, where overlapping forms of abuse may intensify its negative impact on QoL and should not be addressed in isolation in clinical practice. Similarly, other research highlights that most pregnant women do not experience multiple types of DV during their pregnancy.44 Among pregnant women, experience of DV was associated with decreased QoL scores across all dimensions (physical, psychological, and social) compared to those who did not experience DV.45

During the pandemic, the most frequently reported type of DV among pregnant women was physical violence, followed by emotional violence and then sexual violence. Nearly half of the respondents reported experiencing DV, indicating a substantial burden in this population. Another study found similarly high levels of DV with >36% reporting some form of violence, though the frequencies differed, with psychological violence being the most common, followed by sexual violence and physical.30 Psychological violence was the most common type with low scores, while physical violence gradually became apparent as scores increased.46 These differences suggest that the pattern of DV may be influenced by contextual and socio-cultural factors. A different study compared the QoL of women who experienced different types of violence, with each finding lower average scores for social functioning, emotional role, vitality, and mental health.47

Various factors influence the likelihood of pregnant women experiencing DV, which in turn can reduce their QoL. This study found that employment status, COVID-19 status, and residential context were significantly associated with DV. These findings suggest that both socio-economic and pandemic-related stressors play an important role in increasing women’s vulnerability to violence. Considering that the pregnant women in this study came from a remote city, other studies have reported that pregnant women in rural areas have a worse QoL than pregnant women in urban areas.48 This may be explained by limited access to healthcare services, social support, and protection systems in remote settings, which can delay detection and intervention of DV cases.

Our data also show that women who had previously contracted COVID-19 experienced DV more often than those who had never been infected. This may be related to increased physical, psychological, and financial strain during illness and isolation, which can heighten household tension. Pregnant women were at risk during the pandemic, quarantined or even resting at home, leading to prolonged exposure to partners and reduced external support. These conditions may have also affected household financial stability, particularly among women engaged in informal occupations such as trading. The pregnant women in this study were predominantly traders, which further highlights the role of economic vulnerability during the pandemic, where unstable income and financial stress may contribute to increased household tension and risk of DV. Pregnant women who work part-time are more exposed to DV than those who work full-time.46 These findings are consistent with the statistical results of this study, indicating that employment status and COVID-19 exposure are important factors associated with an increased risk of DV and poorer QoL among pregnant women.

The remote context of Pontianak further amplifies these challenges. Previous research has shown that pregnant women living in rural or remote areas tend to have lower QoL compared to those in urban settings.48 Limited access to healthcare services, psychosocial support, and protective systems may delay detection and intervention for DV, thereby worsening its impact. These findings are consistent with recent global evidence showing that intimate partner violence during pregnancy remains highly prevalent across low- and middle-income countries and is shaped by socio-economic inequalities,26 while barriers to healthcare access and help-seeking during the COVID-19 period further exacerbate its impact.27

Furthermore, social restrictions were implemented throughout the region and resulted in different responses from communities, especially in remote areas in Indonesia, such as Pontianak. This had a serious impact on the work of husbands who worked from home or were sent home or laid off. Stay-at-home orders imposed during the pandemic created numerous challenges in terms of gender inequality and personal, social, and family relationships, including statistical changes in DV.49 Economic disruptions, such as job loss or reduced income, may have further increased stress within households, thereby elevating the risk of DV. Partners were more likely to be at home, and women experienced more unintended pregnancies, ultimately impacting their QoL.50 Husbands were the primary source of support, which would impact women, especially their QoL during pregnancy. A significant positive relationship exists between QoL scores and social support.51

Another influencing factor is obstetric status, although this study did not find a relationship. This contrasts with previous research that highlighted pathological pregnancies as contributing to a greater decline in QoL between the 2–3 trimesters.52 Factors that significantly and negatively impact the QoL of pregnant women are depression and low socioeconomic status.53 QoL varies by trimester, and several sub-dimensions of QoL are negatively impacted across all three periods.54 Women in their third trimester and nulliparous women had the worst health-related QoL.48 The variables of physical violence, emotional violence, number of pregnancies, unintended pregnancies, and history of participation in childbirth preparation classes were significantly associated with pregnancy happiness scores.55

This study found several implications. Community mental health nurses in public health centers play a crucial role in the early detection, screening, and management of DV among pregnant women. These findings provide practical guidance for implementing integrated, community-based interventions in real-world healthcare settings. Providing counseling and mental health screening may help reduce the negative impact of DV on QoL. In addition, involving family members in care and improving their knowledge and skills in managing psychosocial problems is essential. Given the limited access to services in remote areas, digital-based interventions and telehealth approaches may offer practical solutions to improve access to care and support.

However, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single remote area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the use of convenience and snowball sampling may introduce selection bias, although these approaches were necessary due to access limitations during the pandemic. Furthermore, although validated instruments were used, self-reported data may be subject to response bias, particularly given the sensitive nature of DV. Despite these limitations, the findings remain relevant beyond the pandemic period, as DV among pregnant women continues to be a significant issue in remote areas with limited access to psychosocial and maternal health services, even in the post-pandemic period.

Conclusion

Nearly half of pregnant women experienced DV, with physical violence being the most common form. Women exposed to DV had a lower QoL than those not exposed, and a significant negative correlation was identified between DV and QoL during the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings reflect not only a clinical concern but also a broader social reality faced by pregnant women, particularly in remote settings such as Pontianak. DV during pregnancy should therefore be understood as a complex social issue, where limited access to formal support systems may increase women’s vulnerability. The findings also highlight the importance of strengthening psychosocial support systems, including the role of extended family and local community structures, which may serve as critical protective factors for women experiencing violence. In contexts where DV remains a sensitive and often underreported issue, these informal support networks can function as a “lifeline” for affected women. In addition, community health nurses in public health centers play a critical role in delivering sensitive, culturally appropriate, and holistic care, including early detection, counseling, and continuous support for pregnant women experiencing DV. Future studies are needed to further explore the influence of cultural norms, family dynamics, and health system responses on DV during pregnancy, as well as to evaluate the effectiveness of integrated and digital-based interventions in improving maternal QoL, particularly in resource-limited settings.
