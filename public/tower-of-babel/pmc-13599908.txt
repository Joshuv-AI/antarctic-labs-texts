Fragility fractures of the pelvis in the elderly: protocol for an international prospective multicentre observational cohort study
Authors: Pol Maria Rommens, Alexander Joeris, Mehdi Boudissa, Gregory J Della Rocca, Christian Fang, Maximilian J Hartel, Frank FA Ijpma, Björn Christian Link, Anser Nasir, Philipp Pieroh, Naofumi Shiota, Geoff Wilkin, Daniel Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFragility fractures of the pelvis (FFPs) are low-energy osteoporotic fractures that primarily affect elderly patients. They are associated with substantial morbidity, mortality and healthcare burden, and their incidence continues to rise as the global population ages. Despite the availability of dedicated classification systems and advances in surgical techniques, high-quality evidence to guide optimal management remains limited, and treatment algorithms are not standardised. To address these gaps, we established an international, observational cohort study designed to collect comprehensive data on patients with FFP and varying degrees of pelvic instability. The study aims to evaluate outcomes of non-operative and operative management and to identify predictors of favourable outcomes.Methods and analysisThis international, prospective, multicentre, observational cohort study will enrol approximately 420 patients aged ≥65 years with FFP (types II–IV) over a 4-year period at 10 large AO Trauma centres in the USA, Canada, Japan, Hong Kong, France, Switzerland, the Netherlands and Germany. Patients will be followed for up to 12 months. Data collected will include fracture characteristics, treatment details, radiological and functional outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, mortality and procedure-related adverse events. All treatments will adhere to the standard of care at each participating centre.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval was obtained from the responsible ethics committee at each participating site before recruitment. All patients will be enrolled following an informed consent process approved by the relevant ethics committee. Study results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications.Trial registration numberNCT04182776.

Introduction

Fragility fractures of the pelvis (FFPs) are osteoporotic or insufficiency fractures caused by low-energy trauma, typically a fall from standing or sitting height or in some cases repetitive minor stress without a recalled traumatic event. They predominantly affect elderly patients.1–3

FFPs account for approximately 7% of all fragility fractures.4 With the global population ageing, their incidence has steadily increased over the past few decades and is projected to rise further.5–8 In the USA, a national survey reported a 25% increase between 1996 and 2010.5 Similarly, Finnish projections indicate that, with continued incidence trends and population ageing, low-trauma pelvic fractures in adults aged ≥80 years could increase 2.4-fold by 2030.9 These fractures are associated with significant morbidity and mortality,10 as well as a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide.11 Recent data report a 1-year mortality rate of approximately 15.5%, and only a minority of patients regain sufficient mobility and independence to return to their previous living situation.12

The trauma mechanisms, clinical presentation, fracture patterns and natural history of FFP differ markedly from high-energy pelvic ring injuries in younger patients.3 13 Patients with FFP typically present with severe, immobilising pain in the groin, pubic region, and/or lower back and buttocks.11 FFPs show atypical fracture patterns and occur in areas of low bone resistance, corresponding to regions of reduced bone mineral density, with intact ligaments and minimal soft-tissue injury.14 Bilateral posterior pelvic fractures are common, whereas fractures of the posterior ilium, disruption of the iliosacral joint or pubic symphysis are less frequent.13 15 Overall, the fracture pattern resembles an implosion rather than an explosion of the pelvic ring, and fracture progression may occur, with additional fractures leading to increasing instability.7 16 17

Because of the distinct characteristics of FFP, Rommens and Hofmann developed a dedicated, comprehensive FFP classification and management protocol that accounts for the specific morphology and instability patterns of these fractures.7 16 The FFP classification distinguishes four categories of increasing instability based on fracture severity and location (figure 1). FFP type I includes isolated anterior fractures; type II, non-displaced posterior fractures; type III, displaced posterior unilateral fractures; and type IV, displaced posterior bilateral fractures. Subtypes of types II–IV further distinguish between iliac, iliosacral and sacral fractures.16 18 The FFP classification provides a framework for guiding treatment, but patient factors such as overall frailty, comorbidities, body habitus, preinjury activity levels and physiological status at presentation should also be considered.7

Patients with FFP are typically elderly and have multiple comorbidities. Due to their frailty, they are at increased risk from invasive treatments, so non-operative management—including pain control, mobilisation as tolerated, physiotherapy and walking aids—has traditionally been considered standard. However, non-operative treatment often leads to prolonged immobility in multimorbid patients, resulting in high complication and mortality rates. Retrospective studies report substantially higher 1-year to 5-year mortality rates in non-operatively treated patients compared with the general population, even for isolated pubic rami fractures.19 Additionally, patients managed non-operatively require ongoing follow-up (FU) to monitor for potential fracture displacement progression.11 17 18 20 Depending on the type of FFPs, surgical fixation of the posterior ring and/or of the anterior elements may be necessary. Recommendations for surgery are based on the FFP classification, the patient’s clinical condition at admission and the success of mobilisation efforts. When indicated, surgery should be minimal-invasive.21 Multiple techniques exist for surgical stabilisation using minimal invasive approaches and bridging implants,9 with the choice of technique adapted to the fracture type. Posterior ring fixation options include iliosacral screw osteosynthesis, cement-augmented iliosacral screws, transiliac trans-sacral screws, trans-sacral bars, transiliac internal fixators and lumbopelvic fixation,22 whereas the anterior ring can be stabilised using retrograde transpubic screws, pelvic bridge or internal fixator (INFIX).7

Despite recent efforts to define and classify FFP separately from general pelvic fractures, develop diagnostic algorithms and test advanced treatment strategies, there is still no consensus on an ideal treatment algorithm.23 This lack of guidance is particularly concerning given the current rise—and projected further increase—in FFP incidence.11 24 Apart from FFP type I fractures, which are usually treated non-operatively,19 it remains unclear which patients and fracture types benefit most from operative treatment, the optimal timing of intervention, the role of anterior pelvic ring fixation and the short-term and long-term outcomes, including pain, mobility, independence, quality of life (QoL) and mortality. Existing evidence is largely retrospective and of limited strength.

To address these gaps, the AO-FFPs cohort study was established as an international, prospective observational study to evaluate patient-related outcomes of non-operative and operative treatment in elderly patients with varying degrees of pelvic instability, and to compare complications, morbidity and mortality across treatment modalities over time. As an observational study, it captures real-world data on usual care without any study-driven therapeutic interventions.

Objectives

This study seeks to compare outcomes, complications, morbidity and mortality between operative and non-operative treatments in elderly FFP patients across varying levels of pelvic instability. Only FFP fracture types II–IV will be included, as type I fractures are managed conservatively and therefore fall outside the scope of this registry. Given the exploratory nature of the study, no primary or secondary objectives have been predefined.

Study procedures

Treatment and postoperative care will follow local standard of care, with the choice of non-operative and operative management left to the discretion of the treating physician. The study will not influence clinical decisions, materials or surgical/imaging techniques, except for standardised data collection in a customised database.

Participants

Patients presenting at the emergency department at participating centres will be screened for eligibility. All eligible participants will be approached by a study team member for potential participation. Eligible participants will be informed about the cohort study’s purpose, procedures, duration, risks, benefits, alternatives and data protection. Written informed consent form will be obtained before the patient is enrolled in the study and prior to any data entry into the electronic case report form (eCRF), unless waived by the local EC/IRB.

Each consented patient will be assigned a unique study number. Sites will maintain a secure identification list linking study numbers to personal data, which will not be shared with third parties except the sponsor, legal authorities or EC/IRB during authorised monitoring or audits. Consented patients will be followed within the registry unless participation is terminated early.

Recruitment will last up to 50 months, targeting 420 consecutively enrolled patients. Where feasible, the registry may remain open-ended for continued data collection.

Data collection

This observational registry follows standard of care, with FU visits, assessments and imaging determined by the treating physician. The registry schedule reflects routine management of FFP in elderly patients. All FU visits are scheduled from Day 0, defined as the treatment decision for non-operative patients or the day of surgery for operative patients. Patients are followed at hospital discharge and at 3, 6 and 12 months. Patients who switch from non-operative to operative treatment remain on the original 12-month FU timeline, with final outcomes assessed at 12 months. The maximum FU is 12 months. Unscheduled visits may occur as clinically indicated but are not documented. Table 2 summarises data collected at each visit.

Premature termination of participation

Participants may be terminated early due to consent withdrawal, investigator or sponsor decision, lost to FU, death, ineligibility or other reasons. Reasons for premature termination will be documented in a dropout form. Death and its circumstances will be specifically recorded. For these participants, data collected up to the termination date will be censored and included in the analyses.

Baseline information

Baseline data include demographics (gender, year of birth, height, weight, body mass index, ethnicity) and cognitive status assessed using the Mini-Cog test.25 Medical history will include fall history, comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index,26 osteoporosis status (diagnosis, year, T-score if available) and previous non-pelvic osteoporotic fractures (type, timing and treatment).

Injury and fracture details

Recorded injury data include date of injury, mechanism (fall from sitting or standing height, minor trauma or unknown) and, for atraumatic cases, symptom onset (≤1 week, 1–3 weeks, >3 weeks, >2 months or unknown) and date of confirmed diagnosis.

Pelvic fractures classification

Pelvic fracture will be classified according to the Rommens and Hofmann system,16 based on degree of instability and fracture location (figure 1). Diagnosis will be based on radiographs and pelvic CT scans assessed by local investigators; CT images will additionally undergo independent central review by the principal coordinating investigator (PCI) in collaboration with board-certified radiologists. In non-operatively treated patients, where fracture progression leads to an increase in FFP type, the initial classification category will be retained for data analysis throughout the study.

Radiological outcomes

Routine imaging will be assessed by the local investigator according to local standard of care to evaluate fracture fragment position and implant integrity including displacement, non-union (>6 months), loosening and failure.

Patient-reported outcomes

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs), assessed according to the registry schedule (table 2), include two scales from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information (PROMIS), the pain Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and the EuroQoL five dimensions questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L).

PROMIS

PROMIS, developed by the National Institutes of Health, standardises patient-reported health outcomes across physical, mental and social domains. In this registry, two short-form instruments will be used: (1) Pain Interference (PROMIS Pain Int 8a), assessing the impact of pain in daily activities, and (2) Physical Function (PROMIS PF 10b, V.2.0), evaluating self-reported physical functioning. Forms will be filled by patients when possible. Instruments will be used only with validated translations; new translations will be implemented following EC/IRB approval.

Pain intensity

Pain will be assessed using the NRS, 1–10, with higher scores indicating greater pain from the pelvic fracture.

EQ-5D quality of life questionnaire

The EQ-5D-3L assesses health-related QoL across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression, each rated at three levels: no, some or extreme problems. Response generate a 5-digit health profile.27 The questionnaire will be completed preoperatively and at 3-month, 6-month and 12-month post-treatment. Preinjury status will be assessed by adapting the item: ‘please indicate how you feel today’ to ‘please indicate how you felt before the injury’.

Health-related outcomes

Parker Mobility Score

The Parker Mobility Score evaluates preinjury and FU mobility across three activities—indoor walking, outdoor walking and shopping—scored from 0 (unable) to 3 (independent), for a total score of 0–9 points. Preinjury status is reported by the patient or proxy; FU will be assessed at 3-month, 6-month and 12-months post-treatment.

Modified Barthel Index

The Modified Barthel Index assesses functional independence across 10 activities of daily living28: feeding, bathing, grooming, dressing, bowel/bladder control, toilet use, chair/bed transfer, walking and stair climbing. Total scores range 0–100, with higher scores indicating greater independence.

Mortality

Mortality will be evaluated at four timepoints: perioperative (admission–72 hours postsurgery), early-term (≤7 days), mid-term (≤3 months) and long-term (≤12 months). In the event of death, the underlying adverse event (AE) and its outcome will be recorded using the standard AE form.

Adverse events

In this observational cohort study, only anticipated condition-related or treatment-related AEs will be documented from the time of consent. Over the 12-month observation period, all new medical conditions and AEs will be recorded, including infections (respiratory, urinary, peri-implant), cardiovascular events (cardiac failure, myocardial infarction, thrombosis, pulmonary embolism), gastrointestinal haemorrhage, stroke and pressure ulcers. Additional outcomes include surgical or treatment revisions, intraoperative and perioperative complications (bleeding, nerve or vessel injury, haematoma, neurological impairment), fracture progression, readmissions, delayed bone healing or non-union, prolonged pain, implant issues, secondary fracture dislocation, falls with injury and extended hospitalisation (>3 weeks).

This registry is considered minimal risk, given that participation poses a very low risk compared with the standard of care procedures.

Radiological parameters

Imaging will follow local standard of care protocols and be used to confirm diagnosis, classify fractures and assess fragment positioning and healing.

Statistical analysis

Since the objectives of this registry are descriptive and exploratory in nature, no formal hypothesis testing is planned and all analyses will be exploratory. Data will summarise demographics, fracture and surgical details, AEs and radiological and PROs. Accordingly, no power-based sample size calculation was performed, as the study is not designed to test a specific hypothesis. The target of 420 enrolled patients is based on expected enrolment over 50 months, anticipating a dropout rate of approximately 33%, based on experience from comparable long-term observational registries.

A detailed Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) will be finalised before the end of enrolment (last patient first visit).

As an observational cohort study, patient characteristics, clinical data and outcomes at standard of care FUs will be summarised using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and mean±SD, median, IQR and range for continuous variables. Additional analyses may be applied based on data quality and volume.

AEs will be summarised at both patient and AE levels, with rates calculated using the total enrolled population, including 95% CIs. Mortality and time-to-event outcomes will be analysed descriptively. Kaplan-Meier curves are planned for visual interpretation, with log-rank tests for descriptive comparisons of survival patterns across groups. Multivariable Cox regression will be considered if event numbers are sufficient (eg, minimum 10 events per covariate) and clinically meaningful covariates can be prespecified.

FFP classifications reported by participating sites will be compared with classifications derived from an independent central review of CT images by the PCI in collaboration with a board-certified radiologist; agreement will be expressed as a percentage, and potential sources of disagreement will be explored. Analysis populations, handling of missing data and protocol deviations will follow SAP specifications. Depending on the data volume and quality, additional exploratory analyses may be applied and relevant patient subgroups will be defined for specific research questions.

Informed consent process

Consent will be obtained from all participants before registry enrolment and data collection, in line with ISO 14155 and local EC/IRB requirements. If prior consent is not feasible (eg, emergency or mental incapacity), surrogate consent will be used per local regulations and patient consent will be sought as soon as possible. Oral consent is acceptable when written consent is temporarily impossible, with a witness signature and later confirmation if feasible.

Data management

Participant data are recorded in eCRFs using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) Cloud. CRFs are completed promptly and are password-protected, accessible only to authorised personnel. After registry completion, each site will receive an electronic copy of its own data. All images and clinical photos will be de-identified and sent digitally to the sponsor. As this is an observational registry, no data safety monitoring board or safety officer will be established.

Discussion

FFPs are an increasingly common clinical entity.6–9 Due to hospitalisation, medical and/or surgical treatment and the need for assistance from caregivers, they pose a growing burden on healthcare systems.11 Although this threat is increasingly recognised, a treatment algorithm of the different fracture types based on robust clinical evidence is still lacking.18 30

A growing number of clinical studies have been published on the treatment of FFP. However, most are retrospective, include small patient cohorts and focus on specific surgical techniques, resulting in a low level of evidence.11 17 20 There is an urgent need for higher-quality studies, including larger patient’s populations, prospective designs, comparison between non-operative and operative treatments, and evaluation of the drawbacks of specific surgical techniques. A prospective, double-blinded comparative trial between non-operative and operative treatment is considered unfeasible in this elderly population. Moreover, the wide variety of operative techniques—which differ significantly in invasiveness and achieved stability—makes consensus on an optimal approach challenging. A prospective observational study that explores the natural course of non-operative treatment versus outcomes of operative treatment, as determined by the treating physician, therefore appears to be the most feasible and appropriate design for generating evidence-based conclusions and recommendations.

Through this international study, we will gather data from patients across different countries and continents who are managed using diverse treatment algorithms. It will be highly interesting to see how regional practices influence patient-related outcomes following non-operative and operative treatment in individuals with varying degrees of pelvic instability.

Mortality rates will be compared between non-operatively and operatively treated patients. Secondary outcomes will also be analysed, with subgroup analyses according to FFP classification and treatment type.

While the international scope of the study enables inclusion of diverse healthcare settings and reflects region-specific treatment practices and policies, it also introduces important limitations. As an observational study, it captures real-world clinical practice but is inherently susceptible to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. Furthermore, variability in treatment protocols, surgical techniques and reporting practices across participating centres may influence the consistency, quality and comparability of the collected data. These factors will be carefully considered in the analysis through stratified and multivariable approaches. In addition, as the initial FFP-classification will be retained in case of fracture progression, a detailed analysis of change of instability will be limited. Nevertheless, fracture progression will be considered separately when interpreting the study findings.

An additional limitation relates to the level of detail captured for operative procedures. In particular, the use of imaging-assistance and surgical-assistance technologies—such as navigation, robotic assistance, augmented reality and patient-specific devices—has evolved considerably since the start of the registry and may have an impact on clinical practice but is not systematically captured in the present study. As these technologies become more widely available, their systematic evaluation will be important to strengthen clinical guidelines and healthcare policy and represent an important area for future research.31–34

Despite these limitations, this study represents a significant step forward. The AO-FFP data will provide high-quality evidence to address key clinical questions and support the development of an evidence-based treatment algorithm or decision framework for FFP. Such an approach could form the basis of clinical guidelines endorsed by national and international organisations in orthopaedic trauma, geriatrics and primary care. Ultimately, this work aims to endorse informed treatment decisions and contribute to standardised care pathways, improving survival, mobility and QoL in this vulnerable patient population.
