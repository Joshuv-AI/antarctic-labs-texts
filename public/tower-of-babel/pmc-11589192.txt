Guardians of the mind: Calvarial stem cells and brain border immunity
Authors: Bo Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryCalvarial bones safeguard the brain and are interconnected by immovable joints termed sutures, which function as growth centers for skull morphogenesis and stem cell niches. Recent years have witnessed paradigm shifts in this field, highlighting the essential roles of calvarial stem cells (CSCs), sutures, and surrounding structures in neuroimmune crosstalk and neurocognitive restoration.

Main text

The skull vault comprises separated calvarial bones, which accommodate and protect the vulnerable brain parenchyma, bound together by a band of dense fibrous tissue called sutures (Li et al., 2023a). These cranial sutures play a pivotal role in the growth and development of the cranium, functioning as growth centers for skull morphogenesis (Maruyama et al., 2021). In recent years, multiple independent groups have identified cranial sutures as niches for calvarial stem cells (CSCs) (Bok et al., 2023; Maruyama et al., 2021) (see Table S1), also known as suture stem cells (SuSCs) (Maruyama et al., 2021) (see Table S1). CSCs, or SuSCs, are closely involved in the pathological progression of craniosynostosis, a devastating condition with neurocognitive defects. Besides the sutures, the skull bone marrow also serves as a potential CSC niche (Li et al., 2023b; Rindone et al., 2021) (see Table S1). Recent works have unraveled that skull-meninges channels (SMCs) directly connect calvarial bone marrow and meninges (Kolabas et al., 2023) (see Table S1), and arachnoid cuff exit (ACE) points create connections between the dura mater and the brain (Smyth et al., 2024), which enable neuroimmune crosstalk and the trafficking of immune cells between the skull and the brain. Together with other immune compartments, such as dural sinuses (Rustenhoven et al., 2021) and arachnoid granulations (Shah et al., 2023), current findings explicitly suggest brain borders at the central stage of neuroimmunology, breaking the established dogma termed “immune privilege.”

Several studies have demonstrated potential markers of CSCs, including Gli1 (see Table S1), Axin2 (see Table S1), Prrx1 (see Table S1), Ctsk (see Table S1), Bmpr1a (Maruyama et al., 2021), and Ddr2 (Bok et al., 2023) (see Table S1), which have been reviewed in depth by our group elsewhere (Li et al., 2023a). These CSCs have been reported to reside in the cranial sutures and cranial bone marrow (Li et al., 2023b; Rindone et al., 2021) (Figure 1). To be noted, most recently, researchers have identified a population of teno- and ligamentocyte-like osteoprogenitor cells, Mkx+ cells, within the calvarial sutures (see Table S1). Specifically, Mkx+ cells display a teno-ligamentous transcriptomic profile, respond to mechanical stretch, and participate in calvarial bone turnover and repair (see Table S1). However, whether Mkx+ cells are CSCs remains to be further investigated. Overall, CSCs play critical roles in various aspects of skull bone growth and development, modeling and remodeling, injury repair, and tissue regeneration. The academic community recognizes that the misfunction or imbalance of CSCs is the primary factor resulting in craniosynostosis, a congenital disability in which the sutures prematurely fuse, causing severe problems with brain growth and neurocognitive functions (Ma et al., 2023; Menon et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2021). Consequently, the notion follows that replenishing CSCs within the fused sutures would restore their function and facilitate skull expansion, eventually improving neurocognition through CSC-driven meningeal lymphatic activation via vascular endothelial growth factor-C (Ma et al., 2023). Furthermore, a research group has refined this well-established viewpoint (Bok et al., 2023). Their study proposes a dual CSC interaction model, namely Ctsk+ and Ddr2+ CSCs, as the cellular mechanisms underlying premature suture fusion in craniosynostosis (Bok et al., 2023). Under normal conditions, these two populations of CSCs distribute within the sutures and self-renew and generate similar bone cells (osteoblasts). During the pathological process of suture fusion, apoptosis of Ctsk+ CSCs and the occurrence of endochondral ossification have been noted, which are rarely observed in normal sutures. Specifically, in the absence of Ctsk+ CSCs, Ddr2+ CSCs differentiate into chondrocytes and form cartilages, with insulin-like growth factor 1 signaling contributing to the formation of ectopic bone within the sutures (Bok et al., 2023). Taken together, these discoveries by several independent groups shed light on the CSC-based therapeutic strategies to reverse neurocognition defects and skull abnormalities (Bok et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2021).

Currently, multiple lines of evidence have emphasized the crucial contribution of the brain borders’ immune compartments to the central nervous system (CNS), including but not limited to skull bone marrow, dural sinuses, and meninges (Mazzitelli et al., 2023; Rustenhoven et al., 2021) (Figure 2). It is worth noting that the meninges have long been acknowledged to comprise three layers of membranes: the dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater. Recently, researchers have shown the existence of a fourth meningeal layer within the subarachnoid space, termed the subarachnoid lymphatic-like membrane (SLYM), situated between the arachnoid and pia mater. Of note, SLYM is conserved in both mouse and human brains and is phenotypically distinct from traditional meningeal layers, displaying lymphatic-like features (Møllgård et al., 2023). Besides harboring an abundance of immune cells, SLYM may directly participate in CNS immunosurveillance and is implicated in neuroinflammation following brain trauma or protein aggregation and cognitive decline in neurodegenerative disorders, such as multiple sclerosis (Møllgård et al., 2023). These immune cells include T cells, B cells, neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and others. As the neuroimmune interface, brain borders exhibit a remarkable diversity of immune cell types, armed with nearly all kinds of immune cells found throughout the body. Over the past few years, several previously overlooked structures, such as dural sinuses (Rustenhoven et al., 2021) and arachnoid granulations (Shah et al., 2023), have been delineated, shedding light on the structural basis of the interplay between the CNS and the immune system. These intricate structures challenge the conceptual framework that our brain is isolated and “immune privileged,” as once believed. Intriguingly, the calvarial bone marrow, once disregarded, is now recognized as one of the most critical immune compartments in this scenario, particularly with the discovery of SMCs (see Table S1). For one thing, the skull bone marrow is anatomically connected with the meninges through SMCs, which are bidirectional channels that permit molecular and cellular trafficking, and supplies adjacent meninges with a pool of innate immune cells under physiological and pathological conditions (Cugurra et al., 2021; Mazzitelli et al., 2023). For another, compared with bone marrow from other sites, the skull bone marrow displays a distinct molecular makeup and transcriptomic profile that holds relevance to neurological disorders (Kolabas et al., 2023). Our group has also identified age-related transcriptomic alterations in cranial bone marrow through single-cell RNA sequencing and found Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes terms of differentially expressed genes enriched in neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease (Li et al., 2023b).

Notably, the skull bone marrow serves as a putative niche for CSCs (Li et al., 2023b; Rindone et al., 2021) (see Table S1), which possess the potential to reverse neurocognitive defects through skull growth and expansion. Simultaneously, the skull bone marrow acts as a reservoir for immune cells that replenish meningeal and CNS immunity. Hence, we speculate that CSCs may interact with immune cells at the borders of the CNS parenchyma, and this interaction could potentially influence the progression of neurological disorders. Collectively, these recent findings indicate that molecular and cellular changes at brain borders may offer valuable insights for the diagnosis and treatment of neurological diseases. Besides, the hierarchy and heterogeneity of CSCs, distinguished by different markers, warrant further exploration and elucidation. In summary, both CSCs and CNS border immunity work together to protect the brain from perturbations and safeguard our cognitive function.
