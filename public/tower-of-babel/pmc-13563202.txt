Advanced Uterine Prolapse in a Primiparous Postmenopausal Woman Successfully Treated with Total Vaginal Hysterectomy: A Case Report
Authors: Frans Arvindo Irapanussa, Arif Satria Prabowo, Raden Kania Praharsini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPelvic organ prolapse (POP) results from weakness of pelvic floor support structures and may significantly impair quality of life. Multiparity and vaginal delivery are major risk factors for uterine prolapse. We report a rare case of stage IV uterine prolapse in a primiparous postmenopausal woman, highlighting the potential contribution of advanced age and postmenopausal status despite the absence of multiparity.Case PresentationA 58-year-old primiparous (P1A0) postmenopausal woman presented with a two-year history of a vaginal bulge that had progressively worsened over the preceding two months and became irreducible. She reported incomplete bladder emptying, straining during micturition, weak urinary stream, and persistent vaginal discomfort after pessary insertion. Pelvic examination revealed stage III anterior vaginal wall prolapse, stage IV uterine prolapse, stage III posterior compartment prolapse, and cervical elongation. The patient underwent total vaginal hysterectomy (TVH) with anterior colporrhaphy. Her postoperative course was uneventful, with stable clinical condition, adequate pain control, no significant bleeding, and satisfactory recovery.DiscussionSurgical management was selected because of advanced prolapse, persistent symptoms, and unsuccessful conservative treatment with a pessary. In this postmenopausal patient, uterine preservation was not a priority. The transvaginal approach enabled direct management of the prolapsed uterus while simultaneously restoring apical and anterior compartment support.ConclusionTotal vaginal hysterectomy with anterior colporrhaphy can effectively treat advanced uterine prolapse involving multiple compartments in postmenopausal women with persistent symptoms following unsuccessful conservative treatment.

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a common gynecological condition, particularly among older and postmenopausal women. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported an overall global prevalence of 30.9%, with a substantially higher prevalence identified through pelvic examination than questionnaire-based assessment (41.8% vs 25.0%).1 The incidence of POP increases progressively with age, reaching its highest rate among women aged 60–69 years. Uterine prolapse specifically has been reported to affect approximately 2–20% of women worldwide, with higher prevalence in developing countries.1,2 East Asian women appear to experience apical prolapse, including uterine prolapse, more frequently than Caucasian women, suggesting possible ethnic and anatomical differences in pelvic floor support.3

In Indonesia, epidemiological data on uterine prolapse remain limited. Hospital-based studies have reported a prevalence ranging from 3.4% to 56.4%, particularly among postpartum women.4 A retrospective study conducted at Dr. Mohammad Hoesin General Hospital, Palembang, reported that uterine prolapse accounted for 7.28% of all gynecological cases between 2020 and 2022, with most patients being older than 60 years (53.5%) and postmenopausal (87.1%).5

Uterine prolapse occurs when the uterus descends through the vaginal canal because of failure of the pelvic floor support system. Patients commonly present with a sensation of a vaginal bulge, pelvic pressure, discomfort during prolonged standing or walking, and urinary or bowel dysfunction.6 Pelvic organ prolapse is a multifactorial disorder resulting from the interaction of obstetric, hormonal, anatomical, and systemic factors that compromise pelvic floor integrity. Vaginal delivery and multiparity are considered the strongest risk factors because childbirth may directly injure the levator ani muscles, endopelvic fascia, and the uterosacral-cardinal ligament complex through mechanical stretching and compression.6 Advanced age and estrogen deficiency after menopause further contribute by reducing the strength and elasticity of connective tissues supporting the pelvic organs.7,8

Additional factors that increase chronic intra-abdominal pressure, including obesity, chronic cough, constipation, and repetitive heavy lifting, may further predispose women to POP or accelerate disease progression.6,7 Furthermore, intrinsic connective tissue abnormalities, particularly alterations in collagen composition and quality, as well as genetic susceptibility, have been implicated in women who develop prolapse despite having low parity.9 Previous pelvic surgery and poor nutritional status have also been reported as potential contributing factors.7 Consequently, uterine prolapse is most frequently encountered in multiparous, elderly, and postmenopausal women, especially those with established obstetric risk factors or underlying pelvic floor connective tissue weakness. Since symptoms often become clinically significant only in advanced stages, objective assessment using the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system is essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning.1,3,6

Although POP is frequently encountered in routine gynecological practice, reports of advanced uterine prolapse occurring in women with low parity remain uncommon. Such cases suggest that mechanisms other than obstetric trauma—including age-related degeneration, hypoestrogenism, and intrinsic connective tissue weakness—may play an important role in the pathogenesis of prolapse. This report describes a rare case of advanced uterine prolapse in a primiparous postmenopausal woman who was successfully treated with total vaginal hysterectomy and anterior colporrhaphy, highlighting the multifactorial nature of pelvic organ prolapse and the rationale for definitive surgical management.

Case Illustration

A 58-year-old postmenopausal woman (gravida 1, para 1, abortus 0) presented to the Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic on March 25, 2026, with a chief complaint of a vaginal bulge that had progressively enlarged over the previous two years. The protrusion became more prominent during straining, coughing, and walking and had become irreducible over the preceding two months. The patient denied vaginal bleeding or pelvic pain but reported a sensation of incomplete bladder emptying, weak urinary stream, and the need to strain during micturition. She had previously undergone conservative management with a vaginal pessary for approximately eight months; however, treatment failed because of persistent urinary symptoms, recurrent pessary expulsion, and ongoing vaginal discomfort, prompting her to seek definitive surgical management.

The patient had one previous full-term vaginal delivery and no history of miscarriage (P1A0). She had been menopausal for several years and had no history of obesity, chronic constipation, chronic cough, heavy lifting, previous pelvic surgery, or other medical conditions known to increase chronic intra-abdominal pressure. Her family history was unremarkable for pelvic floor disorders.

On physical examination, the patient appeared in good general condition. Her body weight was 52 kg, height was 150 cm, and body mass index (BMI) was 23.1 kg/m2. Abdominal examination was unremarkable, and no active vaginal bleeding or abnormal vaginal discharge was observed.

Pelvic examination revealed a large prolapsing mass protruding beyond the vaginal introitus. The cervix appeared smooth without ulceration or suspicious lesions, and the uterus was not palpably enlarged. Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) measurements were as follows: Aa +2, Ba +4, C +6, genital hiatus (GH) 5 cm, perineal body (PB) 1.5 cm, total vaginal length (TVL) 8 cm, Ap +1, Bp +2, and D −3 (Figure 1A–F). These findings were consistent with stage III anterior compartment prolapse (cystocele), stage IV uterine prolapse (apical prolapse), Stage III posterior vaginal wall prolapse, and cervical elongation. Routine laboratory investigations, including a complete blood count, were within normal limits. Hepatitis B surface antigen testing was negative. Chest radiography demonstrated no abnormalities. Pelvic ultrasonography was limited because the prolapsed uterus could not be adequately visualized, while both adnexa and kidneys appeared normal. Based on the patient’s history, physical examination, POP-Q assessment, and ancillary investigations, a diagnosis of advanced pelvic organ prolapse consisting of stage IV uterine prolapse with associated stage III anterior compartment prolapse, stage III posterior compartment prolapse, and cervical elongation was established.

Considering the advanced stage of prolapse, persistent symptoms, failure of conservative treatment, and the patient’s postmenopausal status, definitive surgical treatment was planned. On April 2, 2026, she underwent total vaginal hysterectomy (TVH) with anterior colporrhaphy under regional anesthesia. Following antiseptic preparation of the vulva and vagina, bladder catheterization was performed. Vaginal hysterectomy was completed uneventfully, followed by anterior colporrhaphy to restore anterior compartment support. Hemostasis was secured, and a vaginal pack was placed at the conclusion of the procedure (Figure 2A and B). No intraoperative complications were encountered. The postoperative course was uneventful. The vaginal tampon was subsequently removed 24 hours postoperatively. The patient remained hemodynamically stable, with no evidence of excessive vaginal bleeding or urinary retention. She was discharged on postoperative day 2 in good clinical condition. At the outpatient follow-up visit on April 13, 2026 (11 days after surgery), she reported complete resolution of the vaginal bulge and urinary symptoms. Both urinary and bowel functions had returned to normal, and the surgical wound demonstrated satisfactory healing without evidence of recurrence or postoperative complications.

Discussion

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a common pelvic floor disorder that predominantly affects multiparous and postmenopausal women. Pregnancy and vaginal delivery remain the strongest risk factors because they may cause irreversible injury to the levator ani muscles, endopelvic fascia, and the uterosacral-cardinal ligament complex. Additional factors, including aging, estrogen deficiency, obesity, chronic increases in intra-abdominal pressure, and connective tissue disorders, further contribute to the progressive weakening of pelvic floor support. Consequently, advanced uterine prolapse is typically encountered in elderly women with a history of multiple vaginal deliveries.7,10

The present case is noteworthy because advanced uterine prolapse developed in a primiparous postmenopausal woman. Although the patient had experienced only one vaginal delivery, she presented with stage IV uterine prolapse accompanied by stage III anterior compartment prolapse, stage III posterior compartment prolapse, and cervical elongation. This presentation suggests that factors other than parity may have played a substantial role in the pathogenesis of her condition.7,10

Previous studies have demonstrated that parity is positively associated with the risk of POP, with multiparity substantially increasing the likelihood of prolapse compared with nulliparity or primiparity. Nevertheless, several investigations have shown that pelvic floor dysfunction may also occur in women with low parity, indicating that aging, postmenopausal hypoestrogenism, and intrinsic connective tissue abnormalities may independently compromise pelvic floor integrity. Histological studies have demonstrated reduced collagen content, altered collagen type I-to-type III ratios, fragmentation of elastin fibers, and increased matrix metalloproteinase activity in women with POP. These structural changes reduce the tensile strength of pelvic connective tissues and may predispose susceptible women to prolapse despite limited obstetric exposure.7,10

Postmenopausal estrogen deficiency likely represented an important contributing factor in this patient. Estrogen plays a crucial role in maintaining collagen metabolism, tissue elasticity, vascularization, and neuromuscular function of the pelvic floor. Following menopause, decreased estrogen levels accelerate connective tissue degeneration and reduce pelvic floor muscle strength, thereby increasing the risk of progressive prolapse. The patient’s age of 58 years and postmenopausal status may therefore have amplified the cumulative effects of previous childbirth-related injury.7,10

Genetic susceptibility may also contribute to the development of POP through alterations in extracellular matrix composition and collagen remodeling. Polymorphisms involving collagen-related genes, particularly COL1A1 and COL3A1, have been investigated as potential genetic determinants of pelvic floor tissue weakness, although the reported associations remain inconsistent across populations.11–13

Conservative treatment using a vaginal pessary is recommended as first-line management for women who prefer nonsurgical treatment or are poor surgical candidates. In the present case, pessary treatment failed because urinary symptoms persisted and the patient experienced persistent vaginal discomfort after pessary insertion. These findings, together with the advanced stage of prolapse and the patient’s preference for definitive treatment, justified the decision to proceed with surgical intervention.7,14,15

Several surgical procedures have been described for the treatment of advanced uterine prolapse, including uterine-preserving reconstructive procedures and hysterectomy-based approaches. Uterine preservation may be appropriate for women wishing to maintain fertility or preserve the uterus. However, in postmenopausal women with advanced prolapse, total vaginal hysterectomy combined with pelvic floor repair remains one of the most widely accepted and effective treatment options. The vaginal approach offers several advantages, including avoidance of abdominal incision, shorter operative time, reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay, and faster recovery while allowing simultaneous correction of apical and anterior compartment defects.16–18

In this patient, total vaginal hysterectomy combined with anterior colporrhaphy resulted in complete resolution of the prolapse and urinary symptoms without perioperative complications. The favorable postoperative outcome supports previous evidence demonstrating high anatomical success rates and significant improvement in quality of life following vaginal reconstructive surgery for advanced POP. Furthermore, correction of the anterior compartment likely contributed to restoration of normal bladder emptying by reducing urethral kinking associated with severe cystocele.19,20

Based on the explanation of this case, primiparity in a postmenopausal woman may be considered a contributing factor to the development of POP in this patient. However, other factors may also have contributed, including postmenopausal hypoestrogenism, age-related changes, collagen and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, genetic susceptibility, and individual anatomical predisposition. Although the patient was 58 years old, we agree that age alone should not be considered sufficient to explain significant structural changes in the pelvic floor. Rather, the aging process may interact with various biological and mechanical factors in contributing to the development and progression of POP.

Furthermore, we acknowledge that the absence of documented evidence of levator ani muscle avulsion in this patient limits our ability to directly attribute the prolapse to vaginal delivery.11–13

Early diagnosis using the POP-Q system and individualized treatment selection are essential for optimizing functional outcomes and preventing disease progression.6,8,9

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that advanced uterine prolapse may develop even in primiparous postmenopausal women, emphasizing that pelvic organ prolapse is a multifactorial disorder rather than a condition solely attributable to multiparity. Clinicians should recognize other factors may also have contributed, including postmenopausal hypoestrogenism, age-related changes, collagen and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, genetic susceptibility, and individual anatomical predisposition. In appropriately selected patients, total vaginal hysterectomy with anterior colporrhaphy remains a safe and effective treatment, providing satisfactory anatomical correction, symptom resolution, and postoperative recovery. Reporting rare presentations such as this may improve clinicians’ awareness and contribute to a better understanding of the diverse pathophysiological mechanisms underlying pelvic organ prolapse.
