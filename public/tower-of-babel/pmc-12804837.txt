Leveraging TRPV1 for intracellular delivery of membrane impermeant compounds in the brain
Authors: José C Zepeda, Loren D Peeters, Pragnya S Adapa, Beimnet B Kassaye, Brad A Grueter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLarge-pore ion channels present a window of opportunity for cell-specific intracellular drug delivery. Here, we demonstrate that the capsaicin receptor, TRPV1, can be used for delivery of the membrane impermeant lidocaine derivative QX-314 into neurons in the brain in vivo by using drug-induced hyperlocomotion as a behavioral readout and we validated our findings with ex vivo electrophysiological recordings. Our studies present evidence that this strategy can be used to deliver membrane impermeant compounds in the brain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31484-2.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Drug discovery, Neuroscience

Conventional therapeutic strategies primarily focus on targeting tissue and cell-specific machinery using small molecules, but off-target and undesired side effects remain a significant barrier in the development of compounds with therapeutic potential1. Even more challenging has been targeting intracellular signaling mechanisms shared across tissues, in a tissue and cell specific manner2. One avenue that needs further exploring is the possibility of using natively expressed large-pore ion channels for intracellular drug delivery.

The transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) is a non-selective cation channel well characterized for its role in heat and pain detection3. TRPV1 pores dilate to approximately 12 angstroms when activated by capsaicin, a TRPV1 agonist, allowing for the influx of large cations4. In the dorsal root ganglion, QX-314, a membrane impermeant lidocaine derivative of 263 Da which can block voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSC) only from the intracellular domain5, can be selectively delivered into nociceptive neurons that express TRPV16,7. These properties make TRPV1 an attractive candidate for delivering membrane impermeant molecules into cells.

The nucleus accumbens (NAc) is a limbic-motor interface necessary for motivated behaviors and psychostimulant-induced locomotion8. TRPV1 is expressed in the medium spiny neurons (MSNs) of the NAc9, where TRPV1 activation can induce synaptic plasticity10. We postulated that we could leverage TRPV1 for intracellular delivery of QX-314 into NAc MSNs, which constitute ~90-95% of neurons in the NAc11. To test the applicability of this strategy, we investigated cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion12, a well-established behavioral paradigm dependent on NAc MSN function. Specifically, we delivered QX-314 into NAc MSNs through capsaicin-activated TRPV1 pores and used cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion as a behavioral readout for MSN VGSC inhibition.

To test the effects of infusion of capsaicin and QX-314 on cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion we first secured cannulas to the skulls of mice, to allow for the microinfusion of drugs into the NAc (Fig 1A). We recorded locomotor output during acclimation to an open-field chamber, following the response to a microinfusion of different combinations of capsaicin (1.5 µmol), QX-314 (2.5 mmol), and the highly selective TRPV1 antagonist AMG 9810 (0.5 µmol)13 , followed by an intraperitoneal (ip) injection of cocaine (15 mg/kg) (Fig. 1B,C). To control for effects caused by the microinfusion, we infused a vehicle containing saline with 0.1% DMSO into the NAc of mice. No differences in locomotor output were observed between the vehicle, capsaicin and QX-314, AMG 9810 groups (Fig. 1D). Following a cocaine injection, the group of mice infused with capsaicin and QX-314 exhibited a marked reduction in locomotor output when compared to the vehicle control group. Inclusion of AMG 9810 with capsaicin and QX-314 rescued the locomotor effect of cocaine (Fig. 1E). To test whether these drugs affected cocaine- induced hyperlocomotion on their own, we infused separate groups with only capsaicin, QX-314, or AMG 9810, and observed no differences with respect to the vehicle control group (Fig. s1B, C). To validate the necessity of the TRPV1 channel for the reduction in locomotion, we next microinfused mice which lack expression of the TRPV1 channel (TRPV1(-/-)) with capsaicin and QX-314. We observed no difference in cocaine-induced locomotor output with comparison to the control group (Fig. 1F–H). Together, these results demonstrate that TRPV1 is necessary for capsaicin and QX-314 to reduce cocaine-induced hyperlocomotion.

To explore the applicability of this strategy on hyperlocomotion stimulated by a drug with a different mechanism of action, we employed MK-801 as a hyperlocomotion-inducing agent (Fig. s2A)14,15 for which the NAc is an important mediator16,17. Again, locomotor output was unaffected by a microinfusion of capsaicin and QX-314 with respect to vehicle controls (Fig. s2B). However, MK-801 (0.3 mg/kg) locomotor activity was reduced following microinfusion of capsaicin and QX-314 (Fig. s2C). These findings broaden the applicability of our strategy.

Considering the effects the combined infusion of capsaicin and QX-314 had on behavior, we tested how this manipulation affects action potential generation in NAc MSNs. We assessed action potential generation using whole-cell patch clamp electrophysiology following current injections before and after perfusing brain slices with capsaicin and QX-314 in NAc MSNs (Fig 2a). We observed a reduction in action potential generation following the perfusion of capsaicin and QX-314 (Fig. 2b), that was prevented in the presence of AMG-9810 (Fig. 2c ,d, e). These results suggest that the reduction in locomotor output driven by capsaicin and QX-314 infusions is mediated by the inhibition of action potential generation in NAc MSNs. To validate that our manipulation reduced VGSC function, we used whole-cell voltage clamp and isolated VGSC currents, measuring the effects of capsaicin + QX-314. We find that bath applications of capsaicin and QX-314 did not reduce VGSC function independently, but their combined application reduced peak VGSC current amplitude (Fig. 1G–J). Together, these findings demonstrate that this manipulation is able to deliver QX-314 into the intracellular domain of NAc MSNs to inhibit VGSC function and consequently inhibit action potential generation in MSNs.

We present evidence that TRPV1 can be used for intracellular delivery of membrane impermeant compounds into neurons in the brain in vivo to inhibit two distinct types of drug-induced hyperlocomotion (Fig. s3). Interestingly, only evoked locomotion was affected by our manipulation while basal locomotion following infusions was spared (Fig. 1). This is similar to previous observations that infusion of the GABAAR agonist muscimol attenuates cocaine induced hyperlocomotion while not affecting saline induced locomotion, pointing to possible state-dependent effects18. Although not a statistically significant increase from our control, an increase in cocaine-induced locomotor activity upon blockade of TRPV1 activity would not have been surprising (Fig 1C–E), as responses to cocaine have been shown to be enhanced in TRPV1 KO mice10. Other brain areas have been shown to express TRPV1, such as the hypothalamus19 and the ventral tegmental area20. This strategy could be employed to selectively inhibit action potentials in TRPV1-expressing neurons in these brain areas. Importantly, our results advance the exciting possibility of targeting cells in the brain with membrane impermeant drugs by utilizing natively expressed large-pore ion channels. One limitation is that opening these channels may produce undesired effects of their own, such as the activation of TRPV1 channels on glia21, and cytotoxicity22, but the application is still conceivable within the appropriate context. One example of a clinical intervention which could benefit from such a strategy is the targeting of malignant gliomas, a brain cancer which has been shown to express TRPV1 and other TRP channels23. Current strategies include the use of Ommaya reservoirs used to infuse chemotherapeutics into the brain to target brain cancers24, but better targeting of specific cells is still necessary. Improved medicinal precision will improve outcomes, thus new strategies are necessary in bridging this gap and opening a new frontier in our struggle against disease.

Methods

Mice

Animal care and experimental protocols were approved and conducted in accordance with the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and reported in accordance to ARRIVE guidelines. Male C57BL/6J (Jackson Labs, strain #00664) or TRPV1-knockout (Jackson Labs, strain #003770) mice were used for behavioral and electrophysiological experiments and were 8-12 weeks of age. Mice were housed in groups of 3-5 and were maintained on a 12:12 light dark cycle.

Stereotaxic surgery

Mice were anesthetized using 1–3% isoflurane in oxygen (95% O2, 5% CO2). Under anesthesia, the scalp was shaved and cleaned using alcohol and iodine solution. The skull was exposed and a stainless-steel double (bilateral) 26-gauge guide cannula with a 2 mm gap and 3 mm projection (C235GS-5-2.0/SPC GUIDE 39622, Plastics One) was implanted into the brain through two craniotomies above the NAc (AP: +1.45 mm, ML: ±1 mm, relative to Bregma). The guide cannula was secured to the skull using Metabond (Parkell) and dental cement (A-M Systems). Dummy cannulas (Plastics One) were inserted then sealed using a cap to protect the guide cannula from occlusion. Mice were administered subcutaneous injections of 5 mg/kg ketoprofen (Zoetis) for two days post- surgery and kept on a warming pad until ambulatory.

Locomotor activity assay

Mice were habituated to open-field chambers (27 cm × 27 cm) and handled for three days before experimental tests. On the test day, gross locomotor activity was recorded and analyzed using Ethovision video acquisition software. Mice were first placed into open-field chambers and recorded for 10 min. This was followed by intra-NAc infusions of 0.5 µL of capsaicin (1.5 umol), QX-314 (2.5 mmol), and/or AMG-9810 (500 nmol), in vehicle saline with 0.1% DMSO at a rate of 150 nL/min through a cannula (C235IS-5/SPC, Plastics One) into the NAc (AP: + 1.45 mm, ML: ± 1 mm, DV: -4 mm, relative to Bregma). After intra-NAc infusions, animal locomotor activity was recorded for 20 min. Next, mice were injected with cocaine (15 mg/kg), or MK-801 (0.3 mg/kg) and their movement was recorded for an additional 30 min. Cannula infusion sites were validated by infusion of 500 nL of Fast Green FCF dye (Sigma-Aldrich F7252-5G; 0.25% in water) followed by acute brain slice imaging with a brightfield microscope (Fig. s1a).

Acute brain slice electrophysiology

Mice were decapitated under isoflurane anesthesia, their brains were extracted, and 250 μm thick coronal brain slices were collected using a Leica VT1200S Vibratome as described in detail previously25. Briefly, slices were prepared in oxygenated (95% O2; 5%CO2) ice-cold N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG)-based solution (in mM: 2.5 KCl, 20 HEPES, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 25 Glucose, 93 NMDG, 30 NaHCO3, 5.0 sodium ascorbate, 3.0 sodium pyruvate, 10 MgCl2, and 0.5 CaCl2-2H2O; pH 7.3–7.4, 295-300 mOsm) and recovered for 8-12 min in the same solution at 34 °C. Slices were then recovered for 1 hour in room temperature oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM: 119 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.3 MgCl2-6H2O, 2.5 CaCl2-2H2O, 1.0 NaH2PO4-H2O, 26.2 NaHCO3, and 11 glucose; pH: 7.3 – 7.4, 290-300 mOsm).

Whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiology was performed on MSNs in the NAc core as described previously21. Recordings were performed using a CV-7B headstage, Multiclamp 700B Amplifier, Axopatch Digidata 1550 digitizer, and 3-6 MΩ glass recording micropipettes (Sutter P1000 Micropipette Puller). Current clamp recordings were performed using a K+- based intracellular solution (in mM: 135 K+-gluconate, 5 NaCl, 2 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, 0.6 EGTA, 3 Na2ATP, 0.4 Na2GTP; pH: 7.36, 293 mOsm). Voltage clamp experiments were performed using a Cs+-based intracellular solution (in mM: 120 CsMeSO3, 15 CsCl, 8 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 10 TEA-Cl, 4.0 Mg-ATP, 0.3 Na-GTP, and 5.0 QX 314 bromide; pH: 7.32, 298 mOsm) and 100 µM cadmium chloride was added to the vehicle aCSF. During experiments, slices were continuously perfused with oxygenated aCSF at a rate of 2 mL/min and a temperature of 30-32 °C. All extracellular solutions were set to a pH between 7.3 and 7.4 using HCl or NaOH and an osmolarity between 290-300 mOsm and DMSO was limited to 0.1% v/v.

Pharmacology

(E)-capsaicin, QX-314, AMG 9810, and MK-801 were purchased from Tocris Biosciences. Cocaine hydrochloride, and Fast Green FCF were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Raw locomotor data were exported from Ethovision and analyzed using a custom Matlab script. Distance traveled was calculated and binned in Matlab then transferred to GraphPad Prism software for graph preparation and statistical analyses. Each dataset was tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test and was subjected to the appropriate parametric or nonparametric statistical test. Two-tailed t tests, Mann Whitney tests, one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis tests, and two-way ANOVA with Sidak multiple comparisons tests were used where appropriate and calculated using GraphPad Prism software. All data presented in bar or line graphs represents group average ± SEM. Dots represent an individual data point from one animal.
