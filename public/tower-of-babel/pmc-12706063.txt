Exploring the periodontal phenotype of Uygur adults in Xinjiang, China
Authors: Xiaohong Wu, Xianzhe Meng, Gulimire Abulimiti, Kexu Chen, Kai Luo, Xuejing Wang, Shiwei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to estimate the prevalence of thin and thick periodontal phenotypes in a sample of Uygur adults in Xinjiang, China, and their correlation with clinical parameters. A total of 50 healthy Uygur adult volunteers in Changji Hui Autonomous Prefecture, Xinjiang, China, were recruited. Gingival thickness (GT) and labial alveolar bone thickness (BT) were measured using CBCT at the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) level and at 1 mm below the alveolar crest, respectively. Additionally, the width of the keratinized gingiva (KGW) was measured using a periodontal probe. The crown width/length ratio (CW/CL), gingival papilla height (PH), and gingival curve (GC) were measured on a plaster model. Finally, correlations between parameters of the periodontal phenotype were analyzed. The mean GT of maxillary anterior teeth in the Uygur adults was 1.34 ± 0.30, with 85.3% having a thick gingival phenotype and the remaining 14.7% exhibiting a thin gingival phenotype. Additionally, the mean GT was 1.43 ± 0.21 for thick gingiva (≥ 1 mm) and 0.81 ± 0.08 for thin gingiva (< 1 mm). Significant differences in GT, BT, CW/CL, PH, and GC were noted between thin and thick gingival phenotypes (P < 0.05). Lastly, GT showed a strong positive correlation with CW/CL and GC (R > 0.8), a negative correlation with PH (R = −0.8022), and a moderate correlation with BT and KGW (0.80 > R > 0.30). In the current sample, a thicker gingival phenotype was more prevalent among Uygur adults. Introduction

As is well documented, the periodontal phenotype is vital for oral aesthetics, periodontal health, and dental treatment success1 and has garnered extensive attention from dental professionals. Seibert and Lindhe categorized gingival biotypes into “thick-flat” and “thin-scalloped” based on tissue morphology2, while Müller and Eger proposed the term “gingival phenotype” to better describe the morphological characteristics of gingiva3. Meanwhile, De Rouck’s study introduced a new crown and gingival morphology category, the “thick-scalloped type,” alongside the existing “thick-flat type” and “thin-scalloped type“4. The 2017 international classification recommends substituting “gingival biotype” with “periodontal phenotype”5, which encompasses both gingival and alveolar bone phenotypes and is characterized by factors such as gingival thickness (GT), the width of keratinized gingiva (KGW), the ratio of crown width to crown length (CW/CL), papilla height (PH), and alveolar bone thickness (BT). Among them, the gingival thickness is the primary factor for classifying periodontal phenotypes. Notably, the 2017 international consensus defines gingival thickness > 1 mm as thick and 1 mm or less as thin, a method commonly used in practice5.

The periodontal phenotype can be determined using methods such as puncture measurement6, periodontal probe fluoroscopy7, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT)8,9, ultrasound10, and digital registration11. While the simplest clinical method is visual inspection, it frequently requires the experience of clinicians to yield accurate results12. CBCT is an advanced imaging technique that uses a radiopaque object to measure gingival thickness by minimizing labial and buccal tissue interference. It is highly accurate and repeatable for evaluating the periodontal phenotype, especially gingival thickness. Indeed, previous studies identified no significant differences in measurements between CBCT and direct puncture detection8,9.

Gingival biotypes are influenced by race, ethnicity13–15, gender, age, and inflammation16,17. Currently, the majority of international research on periodontal biotypes focuses on Caucasian populations in Europe and America17,18. However, the periodontal tissues of Asian individuals may be thinner than those of Caucasians and Africans14,18. Xinjiang, China, is home to the Han, Uyghur, and Kazakh ethnic groups. Each ethnicity possesses unique genetic backgrounds and dietary habits that influence periodontal characteristics. Recently, the bulk of research on periodontal biotypes has concentrated on China’s Han population, with comparatively less focus on the Uyghurs19,20. Studies indicate that the Uyghurs have a mixed genetic background influenced by both Eastern and Western origins21.

Furthermore, various anatomical parameters, including CW/CL, attached gingival width, PH, and buccal BT, have been found to correlate with gingival thickness7.

This study utilized CBCT to assess labial GT and labial alveolar BT in the maxillary anterior teeth. The CW/CL ratio, KGW, PH, and GC were obtained through intraoral model measurements. The research aimed to achieve two primary objectives: determining the prevalence of thin versus thick periodontal phenotypes within this demographic and investigating potential relationships among these periodontal biotype traits in the maxillary anterior region.

Materials and methods

Participants

Fifty Uygur volunteers (20 males, 30 females; mean age 25.20 ± 4.65 years, ranging from 23 to 35 years), underwent oral exams at Changji People’s Hospital from Jan 2022 to Jun 2023. 300 maxillary anterior teeth were assessed. Inclusion criteria: Uygur residents of Xinjiang with no specific dietary habits; intact upper anterior dentition, free from supernumerary or missing teeth, no history of restorations, ortho, fillings, or wear; periodontal health (PD ≤ 3 mm, CAL ≤ 1 mm, GI ≤ 1 at teeth 13–23, no visible inflammation or BOP); proper alignment without crowding or ectopic teeth, stable occlusion. Exclusion criteria: systemic conditions, pregnancy or lactation, recent meds affecting gingiva, smokers, or habits like bruxism or mouth breathing.

Ethical consideration

This study followed the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and amendments, with all participants providing informed written consent. It was approved by the Ethics Committee of People’s Hospital of Changji Hui Autonomous Prefecture (Ethics No: 2022ZZQ-A-625), and all methods adhered to relevant guidelines. The study was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200064364; 2022-10-04).

Sample size calculation

The minimum sample size needed to ensure sufficient statistical power was determined through a method adapted from the research by Müller et al.22 and Kim et al.23 using PASS software. For the sample size calculation, an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.95 were applied. This resulted in a required minimum sample size of 50 subjects.

Research methods

At the start of the study, volunteers received guidance on oral hygiene and supragingival cleaning techniques. After a week, those with bleeding on probing rate under 10% qualified for the study. Data were collected from teeth No. 13, 12, 11, 21, 22, and 23.

CBCT measurements

Individual trays were crafted from silicone rubber to cover teeth No. 13–23, each exceeding 5 mm thick. After stabilization, the tissue surface was contoured with a spatula, creating an approximate 1 mm gap to the teeth. A mixture of ioversol and alginate was then loaded into each tray to form the final impression. The trays were carefully positioned in the mouth, ensuring the alginate impression material maintained full contact with the soft tissue surface of the upper anterior teeth. After stabilizing the impression, the CBCT (KaVo 3D eXam™ scanner) was utilized in this study. Scanning parameters included a reconstruction volume size of diameter 14 cm × height 8.5 cm, a resolution of 0.2 voxels, 30.89 mAs, 120 kVp, and an acquisition time of 23.0 s. We selected Sections 13–23 for parameter measurement. Using eXamVision KAVO software, we reconstructed the image data into detailed 3D models, achieving gingival images by precisely adjusting tooth positions. We oriented the sagittal and coronal views at cementoenamel junction (CEJ) level to bisect the long axis and meticulously adjusted the sagittal view at the cementoenamel junction.

GT was measured as the distance from CEJ to labial block perpendicular to tooth long axis, accurate to 0.01 mm. Repeated after one week and averaged (Fig. 1a) .

BT was determined by drawing a line perpendicular to the tooth’s long axis, 1 mm below the alveolar ridge crest. The length of this line within the bone was recorded as the bone thickness, measured to the nearest 0.01 mm, with values under 0.01 mm noted as zero (Fig. 1a).

Clinical measurement

Using a periodontal probe accurate to 0.5 mm, we measured KGW at the mid-labial point of the maxillary front teeth, spanning from the free gingival margin to the mucogingival junction. One week later, we repeated this measurement; the average of both readings was used as the final measurement (Fig. 1b).

Plaster model measurement

The maxillary dentition was modeled with alginate impression material, and the crowns and free gingiva in the anterior region were required to be clearly defined and free of air bubbles. Next, the model was filled with white stone and removed after setting. PH, CL and CW, and labial GC were measured on the stone model using vernier calipers with an accuracy of 0.01 mm (Fig. 1c).

PH: A line was drawn connecting the most convex gingival curvature points on the labial surfaces of adjacent teeth. A line parallel to the central tooth axis intersected the papilla apex. The distance from this intersection point to the apex was recorded as PH. Measurements were repeated after one week and averaged.

CW and CL: Following Jie Yuanet al.24, measured on maxillary anterior teeth labial surfaces. CL defined by perpendicular from gingival margin apex to incisal edge. CL divided into thirds; at cervical-middle junction, perpendicular to CL axis defined CW. Ratio CW/CL calculated. Vernier caliper (0.01 mm accuracy) used.

GC: Angle formed by the most concave labial free gingival margin point and both papilla tops. Represented the gingival curve. Measurement repeated after one week and averaged.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were conducted by a single calibrated examiner. Inter-examiner consistency (ICC = 0.87) and intra-examiner repeatability (ICC = 0.85) were confirmed. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0, including independent and paired t-tests for gingival thickness comparisons and Pearson correlation for variable relationships.

Differences between the characteristics of thick and thin gingival phenotypes

Based on CBCT results, GT exceeding 1 mm was classified as thick gingiva, while GT measuring 1 mm or less was designated as thin gingiva. The mean thickness measured 1.43 ± 0.21 mm for thick gingiva and 0.81 ± 0.08 mm for thin gingiva. As revealed in Table 2, the thick gingival phenotype exhibited significantly thicker labial alveolar bones, a wider keratinized gingiva, a more square-shaped crown, and a flatter free gingival margin relative to the thin gingival phenotype; these marked differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Multiple factors shape the periodontal phenotype, such as age, gender, inflammation, and lifestyle habits—particularly smoking25,26. This study concentrated on a healthy young population, deliberately excluding the influence of age, inflammation, and smoking. It comprised 50 healthy Uygur adults from the Changji Autonomous Region. Presently, reports detailing the periodontal phenotypes of Xinjiang’s Uygur population are scarce; understanding these traits is invaluable for guiding oral diagnosis and treatment within this group.

Periodontal phenotypes can be determined using methods such as visual assessment, periodontal probing, CBCT, and direct or ultrasound measurement. Periodontal probing is most common for its simplicity, but its accuracy depends on the clinician’s skill level27. CBCT is a minimally invasive, accurate technique for measuring maxillofacial tissues, matching periodontal probing without gingival pain. We employed ioversol with alginate to isolate mucosa and. In this study, the mean GT among Uygur adults was 1.34 ± 0.30 mm. GT ≥ 1 mm was classified as thick, whereas GT < 1 mm was considered thin. In the Changji Hui Autonomous Region, 14.7% of Uygur adults had a thin gingival phenotype, whereas the remaining 85.3% had a thick phenotype. In contrast, Liu et al. documented an average GT of 1.05 ± 0.31 mm in healthy Chinese adults24, whilst Shao et al. reported a mean thickness of 1.03 ± 0.24 mm in Chinese adults using CBCT measurements11. In their study, 40.32% of teeth exhibited thin gingival phenotypes (GPh), while 59.68% had thick GPh. In comparison, the GT observed in our study population is marginally greater. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the nationalities of the subjects. The aforementioned studies primarily recruited individuals from the Han nationality, whereas this study focused on the Uygur nationality. Uygurs possess a distinctive genetic and ethnic heritage, constituting a blend of Eastern and Western populations. Their ancestry includes 25–37% European, 12–20% South Asian, 15–17% Siberian, and 29–47% Asian components21. Most studies have concluded that the GT of Europeans and Americans is thicker than that of Asians17,18. However, periodontal phenotypes significantly vary across countries within Asia. Even within the same country, different ethnic groups and regions can exhibit distinct periodontal phenotypes. Nepalese scholar S. Shrestha et al.12 investigated the gingival phenotype of the Nepalese population and described that the proportion of the thick gingival phenotype in the Adivasi Janajati ethnic group was higher than that in the Brahmin/Chhetri ethnic group, highlighting the role of ethnic differences. Of note, genetic factors may also contribute to differences in periodontal phenotype. Moreover, the Uygur population prefers foods rich in fibers, such as beef and mutton, characterized by high protein, high fat, and high salt, which may also lead to thicker gingiva compared to the Han population. At the same time, an earlier study concluded that dietary intake and dental hygiene are not significantly correlated with gingival phenotypes28.

Variations in periodontal phenotypes have been observed across populations and within different regions of the dentition in the same individual. Within the same oral cavity, the GT is highest at the maxillary central incisors, followed by the lateral incisors, consistent with the findings of Kim’s and Amid’s studies23,29. The findings notably revealed that the labial gingival biotype in Uygur adults predominantly features a thick phenotype. This characteristic strongly suggests a relatively low aesthetic risk and a lower susceptibility to gingival recession following periodontal, implant, restorative, and orthodontic treatments. However, in cases involving periodontitis, the presence of thick gingival tissues can impede the elimination of periodontal pockets16.

Periodontal phenotypes are influenced by factors such as alveolar bone thickness. Younes et al. determined that over half of the central incisors and canines had a thin buccal bone wall (1 mm), with a moderate positive correlation with soft tissue thickness30. Similarly, Amid et al. noted a significant difference in alveolar bone thickness between the thin and thick gingival biotypes29. Fu et al. identified a moderate correlation between GT and BT using CBCT31. Other studies challenge the direct link between periodontal phenotype and bone thickness, showing thin alveolar bone can coexist with thick gingiva and vice versa30. Despite these discrepancies, numerous studies have documented that bone thickness significantly influences the aesthetic outcomes of anterior dental implants32,33. At least 2 mm bone thickness is recommended for implant stability and reduced bone resorption34, and is crucial in orthodontics to prevent gingival recession and bone resorption during tooth movement35. Additionally, the periodontal phenotype is linked to KGW and PH. A KGW greater than 2 mm is considered beneficial to periodontal and implant health. In patients with insufficient keratinized gingiva, gingival inflammation and recession are more likely to occur. Therefore, periodontists generally aim to increase KGW via gingival augmentation. DeRouck4 posits that the thin gingival phenotype is characterized by narrow keratinized gingiva and high gingival papillae, whereas the thick gingival phenotype is characterized by wide keratinized gingiva and low, flat gingival papillae. This study indicated that the Uygur adults predominantly exhibit a wide keratinized gingiva. In Uygur adults, a positive correlation was noted between GT and KGW, which may lead to favorable treatment outcomes.

Furthermore, the gingival biotype was associated with the CW/CL ratio, PH, and GC. The CW/CL ratio for Uighur adults was 0.78 ± 0.08 mm, which is marginally higher than the values reported by Chou et al. for Taiwanese subjects, 0.74 ± 0.07 mm36 and by Tsukiyama et al. for Japanese subjects, 0.72 ± 0.04 mm37. Conversely, this ratio is marginally lower than the values reported by Shao et al. in individuals residing in Nanjing, China, 0.82 ± 0.07 mm11 and by de Rouck et al., 0.81 ± 0.11 mm in Belgium4, potentially attributable to racial differences. In Uighur adults, GT was positively correlated with the CW/CL ratio. The Uighur adults showed a lower CW/CL ratio compared with de Rouck et al.‘s study4, particularly in those with thin gingiva, suggesting that Asians might have more elongated crowns compared to Caucasians. A study undertaken by Tsukiyama also pointed out that Asian subjects have more elongated crown shapes compared to white subjects37. Noteworthily, Malaysian researchers observed variations in crown morphology among different Asian races. Indeed, triangular crown shapes are prevalent in Malays, while the Chinese population displays both triangular and square crown shapes, and the Indian population primarily exhibits square crown shapes. The triangular crown shape is associated with the highest PH and the highest incidence of incomplete gingival papilla filling, suggesting that narrower crowns are more susceptible to gingival papilla recession38. According to Stellini’s analysis, significant differences were noted in buccal GT and PH across various crown shapes (triangular, oval, and square)39.

Notwithstanding, different studies reported conflicting results. Stein et al. discovered a positive correlation between CW/CL and GT and BT40. CW/CL and KGW can predict GT at the CEJ and serve as an indicator of BT. A meta-analysis uncovered that GT was significantly correlated with KGW, CW/CL, and PH, whilst KGW was also significantly correlated with the CW/CL ratio16. Fischer et al. conducted a study on Caucasian adults and found no apparent correlation between gingival biology and CW/CL, as well as no significant association between PH and crown shape. Nevertheless, they observed a significant increase in PH in the No. 21/22 tooth area in cases of thin periodontal biotypes41. Herein, the PH ranged from 1 to 6 mm, with most values between 3 and 4 mm, in line with the findings of Alhajj et al.16, Chang et al.42, and Chen MC et al.43. Furthermore, a positive correlation was observed between GT and PH, in agreement with the results of Yingzi’s studies44.

Thick gingival phenotype has rounder crowns, root contacts, short wide papillae, and gentle curves versus thin. In Uygur adults, anterior teeth are short and wide with low flat papillae and stable curves, promoting aesthetics. After periodontitis, recession risk is low, reducing aesthetic risk, but thick phenotype challenges pocket elimination.

Study limitations include small sample size, narrow age range, imbalanced sex ratio—compromising validity—and single-region subjects causing bias, necessitating future large-scale multicenter studies for validation.

Conclusion

The Uygur adults predominantly had thick gingival phenotypes and short-width crowns. Gingival thickness (GT) was strongly correlated with BT, CW/CL, PH, and GC, and moderately correlated with KGW. Thus, KGW, CW/CL, PH, and GC may serve as predictors of periodontal phenotypes.
