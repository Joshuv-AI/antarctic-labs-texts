Daily intake of a lycopene-rich juice is associated with reductions in inflammatory markers but not increases in skin carotenoids in a pilot study among participants with obesity
Authors: Stephanie B Jilcott Pitts, Qiang Wu, Melissa N Laska, Elizabeth Gates, Megumi H Seese, Katelyn E Senkus, Santiago Carrero Longlax, Alexandra Portillo-Varela, Andrew R DiNardo, Nancy E Moran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is associated with systemic inflammation, increasing chronic disease risk. Carotenoids possess anti-inflammatory properties, yet the short-term impact of carotenoid-rich juice intake on inflammation in individuals with obesity remains unclear. Therefore, the purpose of this paper was to examine: (1) participant adherence to a 3-week lycopene-rich juice intervention; (2) variance and effect sizes of responses to the dietary intervention; (3) changes in skin and plasma carotenoids, and inflammatory markers among adults with obesity.MethodsTwenty-five participants with body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2 were recruited and completed the study. Participants drank 4 ounces (118.3 mL) of study-provided tomato juice, providing 8400 µg lycopene daily, for 3 weeks. Participants were asked to keep their diets isocaloric and to continue with the habitual diet. Skin and plasma carotenoids were measured at baseline and at 3-weeks, using reflection spectroscopy and high-performance liquid chromatography, respectively. Thirteen inflammatory markers and C-reactive protein were quantified, and 3-week changes were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.ResultsMean age was 38.6 (10.7) years and mean BMI was 35.9 (4.1) kg/m2. Ten participants drank 83–99% and 15 participants drank 100% of the juice assigned. Effect sizes for inflammatory markers ranged from Cohen’s d = 0.07 to d = 0.57. There was a significant 3-week increase in plasma lycopene concentrations (9.72 (-1.04, 14.1) µg/dL, p = 0.029), but not in concentrations of other plasma carotenoids (p > 0.273). There was no change in skin carotenoid scores (p = 0.592); however, there was a reduction in IL-1β, TNF-⍺, IL-6, IL-17 A, IL-12p70, IL-23, IL-33 out of 13 inflammatory markers tested (p < 0.037).ConclusionsA 3-week lycopene rich juice intervention was associated with an increase in plasma lycopene and a reduction in inflammatory markers. There was not an increase in skin carotenoid scores. More research is needed to further understand the mechanisms by which carotenoid intake can influence inflammation among adults with obesity.Trial registrationThis trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT07120035 on August 12, 2025. The trial protocol and statistical analysis plan can be accessed by contacting the corresponding author.Keywords: Skin carotenoids, Plasma carotenoids, Inflammatory markers, Obesity, Nutrition

Background

A diet rich in phytonutrients found in fruits and vegetables (FV) is critical for optimal health [1, 2]. Diets rich in carotenoids are linked with positive health outcomes, including reductions in the risk of cardiovascular disease and certain cancers [3, 4]. One mechanism by which carotenoids may reduce cardiovascular disease and cancer risk is via reductions in lipid peroxidation and inflammation [3, 4]. Higher levels of inflammation are associated with lower plasma carotenoids concentrations [5–7]. For instance, large studies showed an inverse association between plasma carotenoids and a marker of systemic inflammation, C-reactive protein (CRP) [8–11]. However, in studies examining changes in inflammatory markers with increasing FV intake, results are mixed, with a number of studies demonstrating an increase in FV intake leads to reductions in markers of inflammation [12–14], whereas others showed no significant improvements in inflammatory markers with increased FV intake [15–17]. There is likely a complex interplay between dietary intake, epidemiologic and mechanistic drivers of inflammation, carotenoid status, and other variables.

Individuals with obesity have increased inflammation putting them at an increased risk of chronic diseases [18–20]. Carotenoids could offer some protection for obesity-associated inflammation, yet the relationship between dietary carotenoid intake, skin and plasma carotenoids, and inflammation among individuals with obesity is largely unknown. Prior studies examined the effects of tomato-based products, which are high in the carotenoid lycopene, on inflammation. For example, in a randomized trial of tomato juice (delivering 37 mg of lycopene) in women with overweight and obesity, the juice resulted in a decrease in interleukin (IL)-8 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α blood concentrations among all participants, and sub analyses of participants with obesity (n = 19) showed a significant decrease in IL-6 but not IL-8, TNF-α or high sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) concentrations in blood [21]. Although there is interconnectedness among cytokines (e.g. interleukins trigger CRP production in the liver), the latter study only assessed four inflammatory markers [21]. It is important to look at a broad panel of inflammatory biomarkers allowing for a more complete characterization of the chronic, low-grade inflammation associated with obesity [22]. In a meta-analysis of the effects of tomatoes and inflammatory markers, which included 465 participants from seven RCTs [23], the authors found that tomato intake did not significantly reduce CRP but did significantly reduce TNF-α, yet this meta-analysis did not examine effects specifically among individuals with obesity.

Feasible, cost-effective, and non-invasive measures of carotenoid intake are needed to guide counseling in the clinical setting, and for evaluation of public health nutrition initiatives in the community setting [24]. Plasma carotenoids are the current criterion standard biomarker to approximate FV intake for surveillance and in interventions to promote FV intake [8, 9]. Unfortunately, plasma carotenoids are expensive, invasive, and not practical for field-based public health nutrition interventions. Reflection spectroscopy (RS)-assessed skin carotenoids are emerging as valid and non-invasive, less expensive alternative measure of FV intake [10–13]. The Veggie Meter ® (Longevity Link, LLC) uses RS to quantify skin carotenoids, and has been used in several public health nutrition studies [14–17], and holds promise for application in both the clinical and public health nutrition settings.

The overall goals of this pilot study were to: examine protocol feasibility and adherence and to estimate variance and effect sizes to inform a future, fully powered trial. Optimizing a study design and assessing adherence to the tomato juice intervention are crucial to assess prior to conducting larger studies of the impact of carotenoids on obesity-related inflammation. A priori, we anticipated this pilot study would be underpowered to evaluate statistically significant changes in inflammatory markers over 3 weeks as a result of the juice, yet we still explored these changes with the goal of establishing effect sizes and directionality necessary for planning future studies. Furthermore, while Veggie Meter ®-assessed skin carotenoid scores (SCS) are sensitive to changes in dietary intake of carotenoids [25, 26], and have been validated against plasma carotenoids [27–29] SCS may be less sensitive to changes in dietary intake of lycopene [30]. Whether SCS can be used as a biomarker of lycopene intake in controlled studies, therefore, remains unclear. Therefore, the purposes of this pilot were to examine: (1) participant adherence to a 3-week lycopene-rich juice intervention; (2) variance and magnitude of effect size to use in sample size calculations for future, fully powered studies; and (3) 3-week changes in skin and plasma carotenoids, and inflammatory markers associated with daily intake of a lycopene-rich juice, among participants with obesity (n = 25). Examining the impact of a carotenoid-rich juice on skin and plasma carotenoids and inflammation among adults with obesity can provide evidence for precision nutrition and optimization of dietary guidance to mitigate obesity-related inflammation.

We sought to enroll a total of 30 individuals to complete the 3-week single arm pilot study of a lycopene-rich juice (commercially available tomato-vegetable blend juice). Participants were enrolled at the East Carolina University Diabetes and Obesity Institute. If eligible, participants reviewed the informed consent form with study staff, asked any questions, then signed to signify consent to participate. This trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT07120035.

Participants were enrolled from March 27, 2024, through September 16, 2024. Enrolled participants completed baseline measures, then were given the study juice and instructed to drink 4 fluid ounces daily. (More detail on the juice intervention is provided below.) Participants returned to the research office at 3 weeks to complete follow-up measures. Participants received a $100 incentive (Clincard) for both the baseline and 3-week follow-up visits ($200 total). The study was reviewed and approved by the East Carolina University Institutional Review Board (UMCIRB 23-002535). The following measures were completed at baseline and 3-weeks.

Skin and plasma carotenoid assessment

Skin carotenoid scores (SCS) were measured using the Veggie Meter ® (Longevity Link, LLC) which uses reflection-spectroscopy (RS) to quantify multiple skin carotenoids. Veggie Meter ®-assessed skin carotenoids have been validated against plasma carotenoids [27–29] and while the Veggie-Meter®-assessed SCS are sensitive to change in dietary intake of carotenoids [25, 26], SCS may be less sensitive to changes in dietary intake of lycopene than to other carotenoids [30]. The participants washed their hands with soap and water, then inserted and re-inserted the right index finger into the Veggie Meter ® three times. Scores range from 0 to 800 with a higher score indicating more skin carotenoids.

Each participant completed a 12-hour fasting blood draw, with 6 mL whole blood collected by venipuncture. Blood was centrifuged within 15 min of collection at 2500 xg for 10 min at 4 °C and aliquoted for analyses. Samples were frozen at -80 °C and stored until analysis. Plasma carotenoids were assessed using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with photodiode array detection as previously described [31, 32]. The major all-E α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lutein, and zeaxanthin isomers, the major all-E/5-Z lycopene isomers were quantified against external calibration curves of authentic standards (Millipore-Sigma).

Inflammatory marker assessment

Inflammatory markers were measured for all participants from the fasting blood draw. Inflammation was measured by examining basal (non-stimulated) levels of IL-1β, Interferon (IFN)-α2, IFN-𝛾, TNF-⍺, MCP-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17 A, IL-18, IL-23, IL-33 and CRP, which are all nonspecific measurements of inflammation that are increased with aging, cardiovascular disease, cancer, or associated with nonspecific morbidity [33–35]. CRP, a non-specific marker of inflammation, is associated with risk for both cancer and cardiovascular disease [36, 37]. These inflammatory markers were measured by ELISA as previously described [38, 39] [Biolegend Legendplex Multi-Analyte Flow Assay Kit (No. 740808 Human Inflammation Panel 1 (13-plex) with Filter Plate)].

Dietary intake

Self-reported dietary intake was assessed using the validated Diet History Questionnaire (DHQ)-III, which includes 135 foods and beverages as well as 26 dietary supplements [40]. Participants completed the questionnaire at the enrollment visit to provide information about their diet over the past month. We used the DHQ to obtain data on self-reported FV intake as well as total carotenoid intake, calculated as the sum of the amounts of individual carotenoids available in the DHQ-III output, including β-carotene, α-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lutein/zeaxanthin, and lycopene. The first version of the DHQ was validated for carotenoids with strong correlations between serum carotenoids and α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, low to modest correlations for lycopene, and low correlations for lutein [41]. Supplement use was quantified and tested as a potential covariate in statistical models.

BMI and body fat percentage

To compute BMI, height was measured twice using a stadiometer (SECA) and if the height measure was > 0.5 cm different, a third measure was taken. Weight was assessed using a weight scale (TANITA DC-430U Dual Frequency Total Body Composition Analyzer), which was also used to assess percent body fat via bioelectrical impedance. Participants’ weights and body fat percentages were measured twice. If the weight measures were > 0.2 kg different or if the body fat percentages were > 0.5% different, a third measure was taken. The mean of all measurements was used in analyses.

Population descriptors related to carotenoid outcomes

Because tobacco and alcohol use are associated with lowered carotenoid status [42], smoking and tobacco use was assessed among participants using validated, self-reported survey items [43, 44]. Alcohol intake was calculated using reported data from the DHQ-III. Because UV light exposure from the sun and tanning can influence skin carotenoids, we assessed participants’ self-reported UV light exposure from the sun and from tanning beds/lamps using validated survey items [45, 46].

Juice intervention

A tomato juice was selected for the dietary intervention because it (1) was logistically feasible to administer (2), could be purchased in a single lot to maintain consistency and (3) limited differences in carotenoid bioavailability inherent with varied food sources due to cooking and preparation. Furthermore, the juice used in our prior study [26] was well-tolerated by participants. After completing baseline measures, subjects received 3-weeks’ worth of the commercial tomato-based juice (Campbell’s Tomato Juice, Campbell’s Soup Company, Camden, New Jersey). We sought to deliver 8400 µg of lycopene per day, and so participants drank approximately 4 fluid ounces (118.3 mL) per day of the tomato-based, lycopene-rich juice. We did not test the chemical or carotenoid composition of the juice, but used the data provided to us by the supplier. This amount of lycopene per day was selected as it is similar to the amount of carotenoids provided in our prior study [26] to compare results across studies, to the extent possible. Adherence with intake of the assigned juice was assessed using simple intake logs, as in a prior study [26]. Participants were given graduated cups to measure the juice, and recorded daily intake of juice on a paper form and submitted the forms at the 3-week visit. Participants were instructed to maintain current diet and exercise levels during the phone screening process, informed consent process, and during the baseline study visit. Participants were instructed to drink the juice with a meal with fat or oil and told to consume the juice daily.

Participants were asked to record how much juice was consumed on each day, with options of “all juice,” “some or more than all juice,” or “no juice.” Each day “all juice” was marked, 4 ounces were consumed. If participants selected “some or more than all juice,” they recorded the amount in fluid ounces consumed for that day. If participants missed a day’s dose, they were instructed at the baseline visit to not take two doses the next day and to mark “no juice” on the juice log. Each log was assessed prior to the participant leaving the 3-week appointment for any discrepancies or missing information. The total number of fluid ounces consumed was calculated by adding the number of fluid ounces consumed each day over three weeks. This was divided by the total number of fluid ounces to be consumed, typically 84 ounces depending on scheduling, and multiplied by 100 to calculate percent adherence.

Statistical analyses

Adherence with the daily lycopene-rich juice intervention was examined using data from the daily juice log. Participants’ daily consumption of the juice was compared to the suggested amount of 4 ounces per day. Participants’ demographic data and their self-reported FV and carotenoid consumption at baseline were summarized in frequency (%) or mean (standard deviation). Basal (non-stimulated) levels of CRP, IL-6, and TNF-⍺ were summarized in median (inter-quartile-range) and their 3-week changes were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The data were analyzed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A significance level of 0.05 was used throughout the paper. Fig. 2 was created using GraphPad (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, California) and BioRender (Science Suite Inc., Ontario, Canada).

Changes in skin and plasma Carotenoids, and inflammatory markers

Changes in skin carotenoids and plasma carotenoids are shown in Table 3 and changes in inflammatory markers are shown in Table 4 and Fig. 2. Plasma lycopene concentrations changed from baseline to 3 weeks follow-up (median (IQR) change = 9.72 µg/dL (-1.04, 14.1), p = 0.029), but there were no changes in concentrations of other plasma carotenoids (Table 3). There were no significant changes in skin carotenoid scores, comparing baseline to 3 weeks follow-up (Median (IQR) change = 11.0 (-53.0, 56.3) in skin carotenoid score, p = 0.592) (Table 3). Table 4 and Fig. 2 demonstrate that there were statistically significant reductions in seven out of 13 inflammatory markers tested including reductions in IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-12p70, IL-17 A, IL-23, and IL-33. There were non-significant decreases in the rest of the inflammatory markers examined (IFN-α2, IFN-γ, IL-8, IL-10, IL-18, and CRP) (Table 4 and Fig. 2). Additionally, there was a non-significant increase in one inflammatory marker, MCP-1.

Discussion

In this pilot study, one overall goal was to determine if participants with obesity would adhere to the juice intervention. In our prior randomized control trial of a carotenoid-rich juice, there was high adherence; yet, the prior study used a sweeter, fruit-based juice (orange carrot juice) [26]. The current study sought to determine self-reported adherence and plasma carotenoid changes to establish adherence to a carotenoid-rich, savory vegetable juice (tomato juice). There was excellent adherence to the current tomato juice intervention, with all participants consuming 83% or greater of the juice, with the majority with 100% self-reported adherence, suggesting that this juice intervention is feasible to use in future studies with more participants.

We observed significant increases in plasma lycopene over a 3-week tomato juice intervention while other carotenoid species were not changed. There was a 28% increase in plasma lycopene in the current study, or a 7.98 ug/dL mean increase over baseline of 28.5 ug/dL. This compares to a 66.7% increase in plasma lycopene observed in a 4 week study delivering 7.5 mg/day of beta-carotene equivalents from mixed carotenoids [14]. Further, there were decreases in several inflammatory markers over the 3-week intervention period. This is in agreement with a prior randomized trial among women with overweight and obesity, which found a significant decrease in IL-6 among the subsample of 19 participants with obesity following tomato juice supplementation [21]. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis reported lycopene supplementation significantly reduced IL-6 [47]. Higher levels of circulating IL-6 have been associated with increased cardiovascular disease outcomes and overall greater mortality [48, 49]. Interestingly, recent studies showed a significant correlation between specific inflammatory markers, such as IL-6 and IL-1β, and cardiovascular disease [50–53]. IL-1β is also a key pro-inflammatory cytokine for the host-defense responses to pathogens or injury and a trigger to induce other cytokines [54]. Many anti-inflammatory therapies for atherosclerosis which target IL-1β are being tested and shown to be effective at reducing cardiovascular disease risk and improving outcomes [55]. Thus, it is noteworthy that the current study observed a decrease in IL-6 and IL-1β following a lycopene-rich juice intervention. The aforementioned proinflammatory cytokines, along with IL-33 and TNF-α which were significantly reduced, can be induced through the NF-κB pathway [56]. Mechanistic research suggests lycopene may suppress the NF-κB pathway both directly and indirectly through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [57]. Other cytokines related to chronic inflammation and amplification of the inflammatory response, IL-12p70, IL-17 A, and IL-23, were significantly reduced in this study. While these are not commonly evaluated in nutrition interventions, these changes represent a more comprehensive modulation of the inflammatory response and warrant further clinical investigation. The current results indicate a simple carotenoid-rich juice intervention may reduce inflammation.

Participants in the current study reported a mean of 1602 kilocalories per day, lower than the national average of 2068 kilocalories per day in a national sample of US adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2015–2018 [58], but similar to participants in the original DHQ validation study, where self-reported kilocalories per day were 1555–1630 kilocalories per day for women and 2084–2118 for men [40]. Participants in the current study also reported higher daily carotenoid and fruit/vegetable intake compared to nationally weighted estimates provided by NHANES (e.g.: carotenoids: 13255 mcg in in the current study v. 9687 in NHANES 2009–2018 [59]; Fruit/vegetable: 3.5 servings in the current study v. 2.5 servings in NHANES, 2013–2016 [60]). These differences could be due to dietary reporting biases or differences in the measure of self-reported diet used in each study (24-hour recalls versus a diet history questionnaire).

There was not an increase in skin carotenoid score accompanying the decreases in inflammatory markers. This may be because the intervention was a tomato juice, in which the major carotenoid is lycopene. A recent study found that the Veggie Meter ® does not appear to detect changes in lycopene intake as well as other carotenoid species [30]. Thus, future studies should ensure that a high-quality measure of self-reported FV intake, such as through the DHQ or 24-hour recalls, is included when the Veggie Meter ® is used, to overcome the limitation of limited detection of lycopene. For studies investigating lycopene, plasma carotenoids should be assessed. In addition, the time period of the 3-week intervention may not have been long enough to make a large enough change in skin carotenoid concentrations to be detected. However, even without changes in skin carotenoid scores, increased lycopene intake appeared to confer a beneficial reduction in inflammatory markers.

Study strengths include the use of valid measurement tools (the Veggie Meter®, plasma carotenoids, DHQ), biomarkers of inflammation, and studying skin carotenoid responses among individuals with obesity. This study was limited by the small sample size, lack of a control group, and inability to blind the participants and investigators.

Conclusions

One of the main purposes of the current study was to estimate effect sizes for future trials of carotenoid-rich juices and foods among participants likely to have chronic inflammation. Given that the current pilot study showed varying effect sizes for inflammatory markers ranging from Cohen’s d = 0.07 to d = 0.57, a sample size of n = 80/group would achieve 80% power to detect an effect size of d = 0.4 (i.e., the mean change as observed in IL-6) with a significance level of 0.05. Ultimately, fully powered, well-designed studies are needed to determine the downstream health effects of increasing plasma carotenoids and lower inflammatory markers, to determine if the effects persist after intervention completion. Future studies should also assess whether overall risk of chronic diseases is lowered with increased carotenoid intake.
