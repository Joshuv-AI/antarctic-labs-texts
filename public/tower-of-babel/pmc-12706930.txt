Qualitative parameters and deterioration kinetics of palm oil, shea butter and their blend use for frying cheese
Authors: Muftaudeen Taoheed Kolawole, Moses Dele Adams, Adio Tayo Ibrahim, Frank Abimbola Ogundolie, Temidayo Emmanuel Olajugbagbe, Gbadamosi Idayat Titilayo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Studies on the effect of deep frying of oils and their blend in specified frying cycles is unavailable in the open scientific literature. Thus, we investigated fluctuations in the quality of palm oil, shea butter, and their blend as affected by repeated usage in deep-frying of cheese. Crude palm oil, shea butter, and their blend (1:1 w/w) were subjected to deep frying of cheese at 150 °C for 15 minutes, repeated eight times, repeated for six (6) consecutive days. These oil quality indices such as free fatty acid (FFA), peroxide value, p-anisidine value, color index, iodine value, acid value, saponification value, specific gravity and refractive index changes in oil quality were determined. Kinetic rate and Arrhenius equation were used to determine deterioration rate and activation energy. Data were analyzed using ANOVA and regression at α= = 0.05. The FFA values of palm oil (6.09 - 8.03%), shea butter (2.12 - 2.91%), and their blend rate (3.30 - 4.50%) increased significantly (p < 0.05). Palm oil recorded a high value of peroxide (6.20 meqO2/kg), while the lowest value was recorded by shea butter (3.20 meqO2/kg). The p-anisidine value of palm oil was higher (91.77%) after the sixth frying cycle than shea butter (11.00%) and their blend (46.17%). A slight difference in iodine value was observed in the blend (4.89 - 3.82%), while a significant decrease (4.73 g − 0.90 g) was observed in palm oil. A high smoke point was recorded for shea butter (222.7 °C and 226.4°C), palm oil (204.3 °C and 218.2°C) and the blend oil (212.2 °C and 216.6°C) within the six frying cycles. The study indicated that the oils and their blend can be repeatedly used for six frying cycles of deep fat frying of cheese since all the quality parameters fall within the range recommended by the Codex Alimentarius Commission for edible oil. Blend oil exhibited best stability during repeated deep-frying while shea butter and palm oil were considerably less stable.

Introduction

Crude edible palm oil has an extensive history of direct use in Nigerian foods. It is easily accessible in the market and it serves either as a key component of some processed foods or as a minor ingredient in a variety of food products [1, 2]. Virgin palm oil is highly rich in tocotrienols, tocopherols and β-carotene, these antioxidants make the oil less prone to deterioration and oxidation [3].

The most desirable character of a deep-frying oil is its thermostability, this depends on several factors including the composition and type of oil, the nature of frying food pro-oxidants (e.g. water, oxygen, salt, iron, and copper) and anti-oxidants as well as the frying method employed [4]. This thermal oxidation mechanism principally comes from free radical-mediated breakages of the unsaturated sites of the triacylglycerol [5, 6]. Hydrolysis, isomerization, oxidation as well as polymerization happen during the degradation of frying oil, so that this leads to the generation of light molecular weight alcohols, free fatty acids, lactone, ketone, monoacylglycerols, aldehydes, polymerized diacylglycerols, and hydrocarbons [7, 8]. These degraded products have direct implications on quality and shelf life of the fried products [9].

Frying oil is reasonably stable to thermo-oxidation during extensive exposure to the high temperatures (150–190°C) [10]. Parameters such as the peroxide value (PV), p-anisidine value (p-AV), and free fatty Acid (FFA) are indicators used in monitoring frying fats and oils. Among the most reliable frying oil degradation indicators used in regulations by many countries are the total polar compound (TPC) and polymeric triacylglycerols [11]. The use of naturally stable oils has become the focal point of the frying industry. Palm oil and its fraction palm olein offer excellent frying characteristics [9, 11]. This substance, which is the richest source of β-carotene in nature, easily forms synergy with tocopherols as an antioxidant, gives natural color to foods and is a precursor of vitamin.

Shea butter is another natural oil with several traditional, medicinal, and industrial uses in many African countries [12]. It is mostly used as a cooking fat, hairdressing, waterproofing wax, candle, pomade, and as a component for medicinal ointments [13]. Environmental factors affect the composition and natural antioxidant level of shea butter [14]. Frying operators sometimes blend polyunsaturated fatty acids with a polysaturated or monounsaturated oil as a cost-effective way of reducing the amounts of linolenic and linoleic acids in the frying medium and thus increasing its thermostability [15]. Aside from oxidation control, blend can give better sensory properties in fried foods [16]. The maximum ‘fry life’ of oils, various chemical reactions and the extent of thermo-oxidative degradation during deep-fat frying have continued to engage the attention of researchers [17, 18].

Deep-fat frying using palm oil or shea butter decreases the oil’s unsaturated fatty acids and increases viscosity, color, foaming, specific heat, density, polar materials, contents of free fatty acids, and polymeric compounds [19]. Therefore, repeated deep-fat frying can produce constituents that not only influence food quality but also can induce the formation of compounds with adverse nutritional implications and potential hazards to human health [20, 21].

Studies by Akinrinlola et al. [22] analyzed fresh palm oil and shea butter that are commonly used in deep-frying of plantain chips. Mba et al. [3] reviewed documented reports on processing, refining, and use of palm oil for frying especially in combination with other vegetable oils. Omotosho et al. [23] studied the effect of deep-fat frying on vitamins, mineral and proximate composition on oils from Colocasia esculenta. Gouveia-de-Souza et al. [24] examined the thermo-analytic and kinetic parameters of sunflower oils via standard protocols. These studies did not however provide any information on the qualitative and deterioration status of crude palm oil, shea butter and their blend for repeated cheese-frying, creating opportunity to further advance the frontiers of knowledge in this regard. Therefore, in this study, we determined the effect of repeated use of crude palm oil, shea butter and their blend for frying cheese on quality indices and deterioration kinetics of the oils.

Methodology

Owing to the quality of research done in related research, we adopted largely the methodologies described by Mba et al. [25] with slight modifications. Briefly,

Oil sampling and frying procedures

Crude palm oil and shea butter were used for this research. The initial quality characteristics of the samples were determined using appropriate standard methods. A binary blend of palm oil and shea butter was formulated by mixing 1:1 (w/w) into a homogenous blend. Fresh cheese purchased at the point of production was cut into pieces (slab shape of approximately 10 cm long and 2 cm thick), and cheese of nearly similar sizes and shapes was selected to cushion the effect of size on the experimental results. The selected cheese (which is composed of casein, fat and water) was drained in a cabinet dryer at 60 °C for 30 min.

Following the methods described by Mba et al. [25], as slightly modified, 3 L of the binary were heated to a chosen humidity of 60 g/m3 which provide a high moisture condition; frying temperature of 150 °C in a 3.5 L capacity electric stainless deep fryer (Master Chef Model: MC-DF3138) for five minutes. Drained cheese slabs were fried in batches (10 slabs per batch) at a constant frying temperature of 150 °C for 15 minutes. The frying procedure was repeated eight times per day bringing the total usage of frying oil to 2 hours per day and was marked as a complete frying cycle. The frying cycle was repeated for six (6) days, giving a total frying time of 12 hours. At the end of each 2-hour frying cycle, used oil samples were cooled to room temperature, then aliquots of the fried and fresh oil samples (50 mL) were drained into dark amber bottles and kept frozen in the freezer until analyzed. Frying was done without oil replacement; however, the oil in the fryer was kept in a dark cold place and filtered at the beginning of each successive frying day to remove solid debris.

Determination of smoke-point

The procedure detailed by Wang [27] was adopted for the determination of smoke-point in the sample. Briefly, 15 ml of oil was placed in a beaker. The beaker was quickly heated with heating device at 40 °C. The temperature of the device was steadily increased at the rate of approximately 5 °C per minute. The point at which the oil begins to emit a thin, consistent, bluish stream of smoke was observed and recorded. Similarly, the temperature at which the smoke first appears (smoke-point of the oil) is recorded.

Determination of antioxidant status

The oil was tested using a 1,1-diphenyl-2-picryl hydrazyl (DPPH) protocol. A known quantity (24 mg) of DPPH was dissolved in 100 mL of methanol for making the stock solution. Filtration of DPPH stock solution using methanol yielded a usable mixture with an absorbance of around 0.861 at 550 nm. In a test tube, 2 mL DPPH working solution was combined with 100 µL of the oil. A known quantity (2 ml) of solution containing DPPH in 100 µL of methanol was used as a standard. Thereafter, the tubes were kept in a dark room for 25 min. Data analysis

Three replicates of the experiment were conducted and mean values were recorded as data. This was separated using ANOVA at 5% level of significance. Analysis of the kinetic data and statistical modelling were performed in regression procedures (SPSS Version 20). The fit of the equations correlating the kinetic data was evaluated by coefficient of determination (R2).

Results and discussion

Quality characteristics of virgin palm oil, shea butter and their blend

The qualitative characteristics of fresh palm oil, shea butter and their blend before frying process was shown in Table 1. The highest free fatty acid was observed in palm oil (5.65±0.06%), shea butter recorded the lowest (2.12±0.07%) and the blend recorded 3.28 ± 0.01% of free fatty acid. The acid value of the oil samples was 11.01 ± 0.12 mg KOH/g (palm oil), 4.13 ± 0.12 mg KOH/g (shea butter) and 6.40 ± 0.01 mg KOH/g (blend). These values were slightly above the acid value range of 0.60, 4.00 and 10.00 mg KOH/g recommended for crude/virgin palm oil edible by the CODEX commission Codex Alimentarius Commission [30]. The amount of free fatty acid in palm oil was higher than shea butter. This can be attributed to the harvesting method, storage condition of the oil seed, duration before processing and processing method [31, 32]. The highest iodine value (5.51±0.03 g/100 g) was recorded in palm oil while the lowest was recorded in share butter (4.42±0.03 g/100 g). The refractive index for palm oil, shea butter and blend was 1.4867 ± 0.03, 1.4580 ± 0.01 and 1.463 ± 0.02, respectively. The high refractive index of these oils was attributable to the high number of carbon atoms in their fatty acid composition [33]. Effect of repeated usage of the oils on peroxide value

The initial peroxide values of palm oil, shea butter and blend were 1.80 ± 0.02, 0.92 ± 0.01 and 1.20 ± 0.03 meq O2/g, respectively (Table 1). These changes in yellowness (b) values were found to depend on frying time, temperature and natural vegetable pigments.

High temperatures when used in frying foods sometimes introduce phosphates, carbohydrates, trace metals, and sulphur compounds, among others, into the oil. The formation of nonvolatile decomposition products is due primarily to thermal oxidation and polymerization of the unsaturated fatty acids in oil [51]. The yellow colour of palm oil after 6 hours of frying may be due to the type and number of metals, pigments and natural antioxidants present in the oil. Lalas and Tsaknis [52] reported that the colour value of cottonseed oil had rapidly increased and changed to a darker colour after frying due to oxidation. The smoke point of any oil is the temperature at which the oil begins to burn and produce smoke [55]. This is the point at which the oil begins to break down into glycerol and free fatty acids. The glycerol is then further broken down to acrolein, which is a component of the smoke; it causes the smoke to be extremely irritating to the eyes and throat. The smoke point also marks the beginning of both flavour and nutritional degradation [56]. Shea butter had significantly higher smoke points (226.69 °C and 222.91°C) recorded at first and sixth frying cycle respectively. The smoke point of palm oil recorded at the first and sixth frying cycles were 211.81 °C and 204.68 °C, while the smoke point of the blend sample recorded at the first and sixth frying cycles were 217.27 °C and 213.40 °C, respectively (Table 7). The smoke points recommended range (215 °C − 220°C) for palm oil is dependent on the content of some minor components especially the free fatty acids [58]. Based on our result, the shea butter and blend samples analyzed were suitable for the long frying process; they both show more stable smoke points during the frying cycles. Effect of repeated usage of the oils on iodine value

Iodine value is a simple chemical constant used to measure the average number of double bonds or unsaturation in any oil sample. It defines the grams of iodine that could be added to 100 g of oil [60]. The changes in the Iodine Value (IV) of palm oil shea butter and their blend during the deep frying of cheese for six consecutive days (2 hours per day) are shown in Table 8. Initial iodine values for Palm oil, shea butter and blend were 5.51 ± 0.02, 4.43 ± 0.02 and 4.57 ± 0.01 respectively (Table 1). Iodine value of the oil samples decreased exponentially during the frying cycle for Palm oil (0.90±0.06), shea butter (1.95±0.01) and blend (3.82±0.04) respectively (Table 8). The result shows that IV of these oil samples decreased progressively with frying time (inversely proportional to frying time).

As shown in Table 8, the highest significant (p < 0.05) change in the iodine value was recorded by the palm oil (0.90±0.06), thus indicating that the highest decrease in double bonds occurred due to oxidative rancidity in the proposed frying media. The higher the unsaturation rate (or iodine value) of the oil, the more rapid the oil tends to be oxidized, particularly during deep-fat frying. Although the lowest amount of unsaturated fatty acids was observed in blend, the protective role of the natural antioxidants induced by the presence of shea butter resulted in alteration in the double bonds. Among the three samples (palm oil, shea butter and blend), shea butter and blend showed the least significant (p < 0.05) changes in the iodine value, thus indicating that less oxidative rancidity occurred in these two oil samples. In contrast with the reports of Aboki [61] that reported a high iodine value for shea butter compared to palm oil and sunflower seed oil. The close range of iodine values in this study may indicate a non-significant degree of unsaturation of the examined samples, which could lead to non-reactivity and a non-potential for oxidation [62]. However, the more oxidized palm oil (rancid palm oil) has undergone chemical breakdown due to exposure to heat, light, and oxygen, leading to the generation of harmful reactive oxygen species and toxic byproducts. This oxidation occurs during prolonged heating or improper storage, transforming the fresh oil into an undesirable state that, when consumed, can lead to adverse health effects such as organ toxicity and adverse lipid-mediated and cardiac disorders [63].

Effect of repeated usage of the oils on refractive index

The refractive index of fresh palm oil, shea butter and blend sample were found to be 1.460, 1.458 and 1.463 respectively, all being within the Codex standard requirements, which range between 1.4589–1.4592, 1.454–1.480 and 1.4662–1.4755 for fresh palm oil, shea butter and blend, respectively. The average change in refractive index for palm oil, shea butter and blend exposed to constant frying temperature of 150 °C throughout the frying cycle was significantly different (p > 0.01) (Table 11). The results illustrated an increase in the refractive indices of oils with frying time. This could be attributed to the production of oxidation and polymerization products. Moreover, this increase is related to the molecular structure and degree of unsaturation. The results obtained from anisidine value, colour, and viscosity confirmed the increase in refractive index. Effect of repeated usage of the oils on specific gravity

Specific gravity is the weight of a substance compared to that of water, and it is usually expressed without units. The results indicated that the mean specific gravity of the frying oil decreased linearly with increase in frying time (Table 12). Average specific gravity of 0.87 ± 0.01 was obtained for palm oil before frying which decreased (0.82±0.01) after use at constant temperature (150°C). Shear butter had an initial specific gravity (0.95±0.01) which decreased progressively with frying time (0.85±0.05) after frying cycle. The same trend was observed in the blend, which exhibited an average specific gravity of 0.86 ± 0.01 before frying and recorded 0.83 ± 0.01 after frying (Table 12). All the oils examined, were stable with frying time. The activation energies Ea (kJ/mol) values obtained were 8.87 kJ/mol, 11.8 kJ/mol and 18.10 kJ/mol for palm oil, shea butter and blend, respectively (Table 13). The activation energy Ea for blend was the significantly highest. The quality of the blend oil sample (1:1 w/w) was not just additive of the qualities of palm oil and shea butter. Characteristically, the blend was more stable than palm oil and shea butter. This may be due to the dilution effect of the mixing process, which degraded the prooxidants likely present in palm oil and shea butter and caused a delay in the oxidation of the mixture during frying [76]. In addition, reduction in combined double bond strength with synergistic radical scavenging activity of both natural and synthetic antioxidants could have made the blend oil sample most stable [77].

The zero-order elimination kinetics model holds that a constant amount of substance is eliminated per unit time with the elimination rate being independent of concentration while a first order kinetics is a concentration-dependent process (i.e. the higher the concentration, the faster the clearance) [78].

In order to corroborate the virgin palm oil, shea butter, and their blend with additional information on their relationship with fatty-acid profile and antioxidant, in a bid to support their deterioration-kinetics claims, shea butter has greater oxidative/thermal stability (i.e. slower deterioration) than palm oil under comparable high heat frying or repeated thermal exposure. This is likely due to its higher content of certain saturated or less easily oxidizable fatty acids (stearic, oleic), and/or presence of antioxidant minor components. Blending palm oil with shea butter improves stability relative to palm oil alone, giving intermediate values for deterioration indices (FFA, peroxide, P-anisidine e.t.c), lowering the rate of degradation against pure palm oil. Kinetic modeling (reaction orders, rate constants, activation energies) is feasible for these systems, thereby quantifying how fast parameters like FFA or peroxide accumulate under repeated frying. Minor components (tocotrienols, phenolics, flavonoids, unsaponifiable) are reduced by refining/processing, and these reductions are associated with reduced antioxidant capacity and faster deterioration. Therefore, processing methods are important [10].

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this research, palm oil, shea butter and their blend can be repeatedly used for six frying cycles of deep fat frying of cheese since all the quality parameters fall within the range recommended by the Codex Alimentarius Commission for edible oil. Blend oil exhibited best stability during repeated deep-frying while shea butter and palm oil were considerably less stable.
