Role of CircRNA_0005075 in gastric cancer immune evasion through regulation of lactate metabolic reprogramming and histone lactylation
Authors: Jiaming Wu, Jin Jiang, Cong Chen, Guangjian Dou, Liyong Huang, Yi Zhu, Xiaoping Shen, Jie Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•circRNA_0005075 drives histone lactylation via lactate metabolism, establishing a novel metabolic-epigenetic-immune axis in gastric cancer.•circRNA_0005075 knockdown reduces H3K9la at PD-L1 promoter, suppressing PD-L1 and enhancing T cell cytotoxicity.•circRNA_0005075 knockdown potentiates anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo, suggesting a therapeutic target for gastric cancer.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains one of the most burdensome digestive system malignancies worldwide. According to the latest estimates from IARC/WHO's GLOBOCAN 2022, approximately 968,784 new cases and 660,175 deaths from gastric cancer occurred globally in 2022, placing it among the leading cancers in terms of both incidence and mortality, underscoring its continued status as a major public health concern [1,2]. Gastric cancer exhibits marked geographical disparities and heterogeneity, with the majority of patients diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting the overall therapeutic benefit [3]. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis have significantly advanced the systemic treatment landscape for advanced gastric cancer; however, clinical practice and large-scale randomized studies indicate substantial variability in efficacy across patient populations [4,5]. Beyond PD-L1 expression and MSI status, emerging evidence suggests that tumor metabolic states, particularly glycolytic activity and lactate accumulation, may serve as independent determinants of ICI responsiveness. Concurrently, novel systemic treatment strategies, including dual immune checkpoint blockade, antibody-drug conjugates, and metabolic modulators, are being actively explored in clinical trials for advanced gastric cancer [6]. In parallel, a growing body of evidence underscores that the inflammatory network within the tumor microenvironment profoundly influences therapeutic resistance, further highlighting the need for strategies that target both tumor cells and their surrounding stroma [7]. Although combination chemotherapy can improve objective response rates (ORR) and survival, a considerable proportion of patients display primary insensitivity or develop acquired resistance to immunotherapy [8]. This highlights an urgent need to dissect the intrinsic molecular mechanisms underpinning the limitations of immunotherapy efficacy.

Metabolic remodeling within the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) is recognized as a key determinant restricting the effectiveness of immunotherapy [9]. Metabolic reprogramming, characterized by enhanced glycolysis and lactate accumulation, is not only an adaptive response of tumor cells to hypoxia and proliferative demands but also facilitates immune evasion and attenuates the response to checkpoint blockade by creating a niche unfavorable to anti-tumor immunity. This is achieved through mechanisms including acidification of the local environment, competition for metabolic substrates, and functional reprogramming of immune cells [10,11]. Recent insights into the metabolic and immune adaptations in various pathophysiological states further emphasize that lactate is not merely a waste product but a central mediator of immune cell dysfunction [12]. Furthermore, upstream risk factors for gastric cancer underscore the importance of investigating the metabolism-immune interplay. Large-scale disease burden modeling suggests that Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection contributes significantly to the future burden of gastric cancer and is closely linked to chronic inflammation, mucosal microenvironmental alterations, and metabolic changes [[13], [14], [15]]. This provides a strong etiological and translational rationale for identifying intervenable nodes at the intersection of metabolism and immune regulation.

In recent years, lactate has evolved from being viewed merely as an end-product of glycolysis to a metabolite that can directly bridge metabolism and transcription through epigenetic regulation. Histone lactylation, a novel form of post-translational modification, is thought to translate intracellular lactate load into specific gene expression programs, influencing tumor progression and immune regulatory networks [16]. In addition to lactylation, dynamic regulation of histone methylation, particularly demethylation processes, has emerged as a critical determinant of gene expression programs in cancer [17]. Aberrant histone demethylation can drive tumor progression by modulating chromatin accessibility at promoters of oncogenes or tumor suppressors [18]. Accumulating evidence indicates that lactylation marks such as H3K9la and H3K18la are enriched at promoter or transcription start regions, correlate with transcriptional activation, and can participate in regulating the expression of genes involved in immune evasion [[19], [20], [21]]. For instance, recent studies have delineated a verifiable mechanistic link between H3K9 lactylation and an immunosuppressive phenotype as well as immunotherapeutic strategies within the context of tumor immune evasion, providing more direct experimental support for the "lactate-lactylation-immune checkpoint" axis [22]. Concurrently, clinical observations consistently associate PD-1/PD-L1 blockade efficacy with PD-L1 expression thresholds, suggesting that the transcriptional regulation of PD-L1 holds not only biological significance but also potential value for treatment prediction and combinatorial targeting [23].

Within this intricate network of metabolism-epigenetics-immune regulation, non-coding RNAs, particularly circular RNAs (circRNAs), may serve as crucial upstream regulatory nodes [24]. circRNAs, known for their high stability and tissue/cell-specific expression, have been increasingly implicated in gastric cancer development and progression, and are believed to influence tumor cell phenotypes and microenvironment remodeling through various mechanisms [25,26]. Notably, circRNA_0005075 has been previously reported to associate with gastric cancer biology, potentially affecting tumor progression via specific miRNA-mediated axes [27]. However, existing research has predominantly focused on its role in classic tumor phenotypes such as proliferation, migration, invasion, and EMT [28]. Whether circRNA_0005075 engages in lactate metabolic reprogramming, and further, whether it translates metabolic signals into the transcriptional program of the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 via histone lactylation, thereby affecting T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and the response to PD-1 antibody therapy, remains unclear, lacking systematic and mechanistically closed-chain evidence.

Concurrent efforts to remodel the tumor microenvironment through various strategies—including oncolytic virus-mediated immune cell recruitment—have underscored the importance of TME modulation as a key determinant of immunotherapeutic outcomes [29]. Based on this background, this study focuses on the highly relevant yet insufficiently elucidated mechanistic axis of “metabolism-epigenetics-immune checkpoint,” aiming to systematically dissect the immune-regulatory function and molecular basis of circRNA_0005075 in gastric cancer. The novelty of this work lies in three aspects: (1) we identify circRNA_0005075 as an upstream regulator of the lactate–lactylation–PD-L1 axis, a role not previously attributed to this circRNA; (2) we specifically demonstrate functional H3K9la enrichment at the PD-L1 promoter in gastric cancer, with metabolic dependency confirmed by sodium lactate rescue; and (3) we provide in vivo evidence that targeting this circRNA synergizes with PD-1 blockade, establishing a complete causal chain from metabolic alteration to epigenetic inscription to immune effector output. This work provides a theoretical foundation for future strategies combining metabolic-epigenetic regulation with immune checkpoint therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

Human gastric cancer cell lines AGS and MKN45 were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China) and resuscitated/expanded following standard provided protocols. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic mixture (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Routine culture was maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

All cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to experimental use to confirm origin. Mycoplasma contamination was regularly assessed via PCR to ensure the absence of cross-contamination or microbial infection during culture. All in vitro experiments utilized cells in the logarithmic growth phase to ensure consistency and reproducibility of results.

Small interfering RNA and plasmid transfection

Gene-specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting human circRNA_0005075 and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), along with corresponding negative control sequences (siNC), were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). siRNA sequences were designed based on the human circRNA_0005075 backsplice junction and PD-L1 coding sequence to enhance target specificity and minimize off-target effects. The circRNA_0005075 sequence and its backsplice junction were derived from a previously published study [30], in which the circular nature of this transcript was confirmed by RNase R resistance assay, divergent primer PCR, and Sanger sequencing. Plasmid constructs for circRNA_0005075 overexpression (Circ) and corresponding empty vector controls were generated by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). All plasmids were verified by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing to ensure correct sequence and circularization capability.

Cell transfection was performed using a lipid-mediated transient method with Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), strictly adhering to the manufacturer's recommended protocol. Prior to transfection, AGS and MKN45 cells were seeded in 6-well or 12-well plates and cultured to approximately 60–70% confluence under standard conditions to ensure cells were in the logarithmic growth phase with optimal transfection susceptibility. On the day of transfection, siRNA or plasmid DNA was mixed with the transfection reagent in serum-free medium, incubated at room temperature to form stable complexes, and then gently added to the cell culture.

The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 6–8 h post-transfection to reduce potential cytotoxicity from the transfection reagent. Cells were harvested 48 h later for subsequent molecular and functional analyses. All transfection experiments were independently repeated at least three times under identical conditions. Only samples with stable transfection efficiency and preserved cell morphology were included in downstream analyses to ensure reliability and reproducibility.

T cell isolation and co-culture system

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy volunteers from three independent donors, and experiments were repeated with cells from each donor. All donors provided written informed consent prior to blood collection, and the experimental protocol was approved by the institutional medical ethics committee. Following anticoagulant treatment, PBMCs were isolated from whole blood using density gradient centrifugation and immediately processed for T cell purification under sterile conditions.

CD3⁺ T cells were positively selected using human CD3 MicroBeads and magnetic separation columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, washed PBMCs were incubated with CD3 MicroBeads, and the labeled cell population was isolated using a magnetic field. The purity of sorted CD3⁺ T cells was assessed by flow cytometry on randomly selected samples, consistently exceeding 90%, ensuring a highly pure and consistent population for experiments. Post-sorting, T cell viability, assessed by Trypan blue exclusion, was consistently above 95%.

Isolated CD3⁺ T cells were maintained in short-term activation culture using complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and recombinant human interleukin-2 (IL-2; 50 U/mL, PeproTech, USA) to sustain baseline activity. T cells were activated for 24 h before being used to establish in vitro co-culture systems, minimizing potential alterations in T cell phenotype and function due to prolonged ex vivo culture.

For the co-culture experiments, transfected or otherwise treated gastric cancer cells (AGS or MKN45) served as target cells, while activated CD3⁺ T cells served as effector cells. Co-culture systems were established at a defined effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 10:1 and maintained under standard culture conditions for subsequent functional analyses. All co-culture experiments were performed in triplicate under identical conditions to ensure result reliability and reproducibility.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or mouse tumor tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured spectrophotometrically, with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 considered acceptable. RNA samples were stored at −80 °C, avoiding repeated freeze-thaw cycles prior to reverse transcription to minimize degradation.

Total RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan). For detection of circRNA_0005075, specific primers spanning the backsplice junction were designed to ensure amplification originated from the circular RNA rather than its linear counterpart. Standard primers were used for PD-L1 mRNA amplification. Reverse transcription reactions were performed strictly according to the kit protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Reaction mixtures and cycling parameters were set as recommended. Each sample was run with at least three technical replicates. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an endogenous reference gene to normalize for differences in RNA input across samples.

Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. All qPCR experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates. Negative controls (no-template controls) were included in each run to exclude non-specific amplification.

Colony formation assay

The colony formation assay was used to assess the long-term proliferative and survival capacity of gastric cancer cells following T cell-mediated immune killing under different treatment conditions. Transfected or treated gastric cancer cells (AGS or MKN45) were first co-cultured with activated CD3⁺ T cells for 24 h under standard conditions to allow for immune cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

After co-culture, the supernatant was discarded, and adherent cells were gently washed several times with pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove suspended T cells. Remaining adherent gastric cancer cells were examined microscopically to confirm the absence of significant residual T cells (suspended or attached) before proceeding. To further minimize potential influence from effector cells during the colony formation phase, adherent gastric cancer cells were trypsinized, counted, and re-seeded.

The washed gastric cancer cells were re-seeded into 6-well plates at a density of approximately 500–1000 cells per well and cultured under standard conditions for 10–14 days to allow surviving single cells to proliferate into colonies. Fresh medium was replenished periodically during this period.

At the endpoint, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution. Colonies were counted under an inverted microscope, with a colony defined as a cluster of at least 50 cells. All colony formation assays were performed in triplicate under identical conditions to ensure result stability and reproducibility.

Lactate measurement

To quantitatively assess changes in intracellular lactate levels in gastric cancer cells under different treatments, a commercial Lactate Assay Kit (BioVision, Milpitas, CA, USA) was used following the manufacturer's protocol. Due to the sensitivity of lactate to culture conditions, to minimize interference from exogenous lactate in the medium/serum, culture medium was rapidly aspirated from samples prior to analysis, and cells were washed at least twice with ice-cold PBS before immediate collection and lysis.

Transfected or treated gastric cancer cells were harvested at specified time points under identical culture conditions. Cell lysates were prepared using the kit-provided lysis buffer. Lysis was performed under cold conditions to prevent metabolite degradation/transformation during processing. Lysates were centrifuged at high speed to remove insoluble debris, and the clear supernatant was used for lactate quantification. All sample collection, lysis, and centrifugation steps were performed within a consistent time window to reduce metabolic bias introduced by processing time differences.

For lactate measurement, blank controls and a series of lactate standards were included to generate a standard curve, ensuring sample signals fell within the linear detection range. After incubating the reaction mixture in the dark for the specified duration, absorbance was measured at the designated wavelength using a multi-mode microplate reader. Lactate concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

To account for potential differences in cell number or total biomass across treatment groups, measured lactate levels were normalized to an internal reference. Specifically, lactate concentrations were normalized to the total protein content (determined by BCA protein assay) or cell count of the corresponding sample. All lactate measurement experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Targeted metabolomics analysis

Targeted metabolomics analysis was performed on MKN45 gastric cancer cells to systematically evaluate metabolic profile changes following circRNA_0005075 knockdown. Transfected or treated cells were harvested at specified time points under identical culture conditions. Culture medium was rapidly aspirated, and cells were washed at least twice with ice-cold PBS to remove residual medium metabolites. Cell samples were then immediately quenched at low temperature to minimize metabolite degradation/transformation during sample processing.

Metabolite extraction was performed using a pre-cooled methanol-water mixture (80:20, v/v) at −20 °C for metabolite quenching and protein precipitation. Briefly, the pre-cooled solvent was added to the cell pellet, vortexed thoroughly, and incubated under cold conditions. After sufficient incubation, samples were centrifuged at 4 °C to remove protein precipitate. The clear supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. All extraction, centrifugation, and transfer steps were performed consistently across samples to minimize batch effects and operational bias. Extracted samples were stored at low temperature prior to analysis to avoid freeze-thaw cycles.

Metabolite detection was performed using a liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) platform. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a reversed-phase LC system with a column suitable for polar metabolite analysis, employing gradient elution. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode to enhance sensitivity and specificity for targeted metabolite quantification. Metabolite identification and quantification were based on a pre-established and validated targeted metabolite database. Quality control (QC) samples were injected every 10 runs, and metabolites with RSD > 20% were excluded from downstream analysis. Raw MS data were processed with uniform normalization for subsequent statistical analysis.

Differential metabolites were identified by comparing the circRNA_0005075 knockdown group to the control group, applying thresholds of |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. The identified differential metabolites were further subjected to metabolic pathway enrichment analysis using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database to identify significantly affected pathways upon circRNA_0005075 knockdown.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using a commercial ChIP Assay Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, transfected or treated gastric cancer cells were cross-linked with formaldehyde at room temperature to fix protein-DNA interactions. The cross-linking reaction was terminated after a specified duration by adding glycine to avoid over-fixation affecting chromatin accessibility.

Cross-linked cells were lysed to release nuclei, and chromatin was sheared by sonication on ice to an average fragment size of 200–500 bp. Sonicated samples were centrifuged to remove debris, and the supernatant containing soluble chromatin was used for immunoprecipitation. An aliquot of each chromatin sample was set aside as an input control for subsequent qPCR normalization.

For immunoprecipitation, chromatin samples were incubated with a specific antibody against H3K9 lactylation (anti-H3K9la, rabbit polyclonal, PTM Bio, Hangzhou, China) to enrich for chromatin fragments carrying this modification. Negative controls included samples incubated without primary antibody or with an isotype control antibody to assess non-specific background. Antibody-chromatin complexes were captured using Protein A/G magnetic beads.

Following immunoprecipitation, beads were washed stringently to remove non-specifically bound chromatin. The immunoprecipitated complexes were then subjected to reverse cross-linking, proteinase K digestion, and DNA purification.

ChIP-qPCR analysis targeted the promoter region of the PD-L1 gene. Primers were designed to cover regions within the PD-L1 promoter rich in transcriptional regulatory elements to assess H3K9la enrichment. qPCR was performed using a SYBR Green system on a real-time PCR instrument. ChIP signals were calculated as the percentage of input DNA or as relative enrichment fold changes. All ChIP-qPCR experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer under cold conditions, supplemented with protease inhibitors to minimize degradation. Lysates were centrifuged at high speed to remove insoluble material, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and sample loading amounts were equalized based on these measurements.

Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After transfer, membranes were blocked at room temperature with 5% non-fat milk to reduce non-specific binding. Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-H3K18 lactylation (anti-H3K18la, rabbit polyclonal, PTM Bio, Hangzhou, China), anti-H3K9 lactylation (anti-H3K9la, rabbit polyclonal, PTM Bio, Hangzhou, China), and anti-Histone H3 (rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), the latter serving as a loading control for histone normalization. All primary antibodies were used at dilutions recommended by the manufacturers.

Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed thoroughly with TBST buffer and then incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. The secondary antibody used was HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), diluted as recommended. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. All Western blot experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

To assess the effect of circRNA_0005075-related regulation on PD-L1 promoter transcriptional activity, a firefly luciferase-based PD-L1 promoter reporter system was constructed. Briefly, the promoter region upstream of the human PD-L1 gene transcription start site was amplified and cloned into a luciferase reporter vector, positioning it upstream of the firefly luciferase gene to drive its expression. The constructed PD-L1 promoter reporter plasmid was verified by restriction enzyme digestion and Sanger sequencing to confirm accurate insert sequence and orientation.

Gastric cancer cells (AGS or MKN45) were seeded in culture plates and grown to appropriate density for transfection under standard conditions. Cells were co-transfected with equal amounts of the PD-L1 promoter-firefly luciferase reporter plasmid and a Renilla luciferase control plasmid (pRL-TK or similar) using lipid-based transfection methods consistent with previous nucleic acid transfections. The Renilla luciferase served as an internal control to normalize for variations in transfection efficiency, cell number, and viability.

Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested, and luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was measured first, followed by Renilla luciferase activity, using a multi-mode luminescence plate reader.

Promoter activity for each sample was expressed as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity, providing normalization for transfection and cell status variables. All dual-luciferase assays were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, with each replicate including parallel technical repeats.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry was used to analyze the expression of cell surface and intracellular immune-related molecules in in vitro cell samples and in vivo tumor tissue samples. For in vitro experiments, cells were collected after co-culture. For in vivo samples, excised mouse tumor tissues were minced and digested enzymatically to generate single-cell suspensions, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer to remove clumps, washed, and resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer.

Cell surface PD-L1 expression was assessed via surface staining. Single-cell suspensions were incubated with a fluorochrome-conjugated anti-PD-L1 antibody on ice in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed to remove unbound antibody and analyzed. The antibody used was anti-human PD-L1 (CD274) (mouse monoclonal, clone 29E.2A3, PE-conjugated, BioLegend, USA, catalog #329706), applied at the manufacturer's recommended dilution.

Intracellular staining was performed for effector molecules Granzyme B (GZMB) and Perforin. After surface staining (if applicable), cells were fixed and permeabilized using a commercial fixation/permeabilization kit (e.g., Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit, BD Biosciences, USA, catalog #554714). Cells were then incubated in the dark with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-GZMB and anti-Perforin antibodies. Antibodies used were: anti-human Granzyme B (mouse monoclonal, clone GB11, FITC-conjugated, BioLegend, USA, catalog #515403) and anti-human Perforin (mouse monoclonal, clone dG9, APC-conjugated, BioLegend, USA, catalog #308113), applied per manufacturer's instructions.

To assess non-specific background, appropriate fluorescence-matched isotype control antibodies (BioLegend, USA) were included in all flow cytometry experiments. For multicolor panels, single-color controls were used for compensation correction. A viability dye was used when necessary to exclude dead cells from analysis.

Data acquisition was performed on a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1, BD Biosciences, USA). During analysis, cell debris was excluded followed by exclusion of doublets using FSC-A versus FSC—H gating. The percentage of positive cells for PD-L1, GZMB, and Perforin was then determined within the gated CD3+ target cell population. All flow cytometry experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

Animal experiments

All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with national and institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the institutional animal ethics committee. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water.

To establish a gastric cancer xenograft model, we used female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, n = 6 per group). MKN45 human gastric cancer cells stably expressing shRNA targeting circRNA_0005075 (or control shRNA) were harvested at logarithmic growth phase and prepared as single-cell suspensions, then subcutaneously injected into the right flank of each mouse (5 × 10⁶ cells per mouse). PD-1 monoclonal antibody (clone RMP1–14, BioXCell) was administered intraperitoneally at 10 mg/kg every 3 days for a total of 4 doses. Tumor growth was monitored by caliper measurement every 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated as (length × width²)/2.

After counting and viability assessment, a defined number of cells were subcutaneously injected into mice to initiate tumor growth. Once tumors became palpable and reached a measurable size, mice were randomized into the following four groups using a computer-generated randomization list. Tumor volume measurements were performed by an investigator blinded to the group allocation. Control group, circRNA_0005075 knockdown group (siCirc), anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody treatment group (anti-PD-1), and the combination group (siCirc + anti-PD-1). Randomization ensured no significant initial difference in tumor volume distribution across groups. All mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, and tumor volume measurements were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. All in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates.

During the treatment phase, mice received interventions according to the pre-defined protocol. Control and siCirc group mice received equivalent volumes of vehicle/solvent. Anti-PD-1 and combination group mice received anti-PD-1 antibody injections at specified doses and intervals. Tumor growth was monitored regularly by measuring the longest and shortest diameters with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the standard formula (Volume = (length × width²) / 2).

At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized under anesthesia, and tumors were excised and weighed. A portion of each tumor was immediately processed for molecular and immunological analyses, while the remainder was preserved as required. Tumor samples were subsequently used for qPCR analysis of gene expression and flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cell phenotypes and functional states.

Importantly, because BALB/c nude mice are T-cell-deficient, the analyses of T cell effector molecules (GZMB and perforin) presented alongside the in vivo data in Fig. 5F–G were derived from independent in vitro co-culture experiments (as described in Methods 2.3 and Results 3.1 and 3.4), not from tumor-infiltrating T cells in the xenograft model. We have clarified this distinction in the Results section and Fig. 5 legend.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-sided Student‘s t-test. For multiple groups, one-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons when overall significance was detected. For data that did not pass normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) or equal-variance (Levene‘s test) assumptions, the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction (multiple groups) was used instead. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Knockdown of circRNA_0005075 enhances T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against gastric cancer cells

To delineate the functional role of circRNA_0005075 in gastric cancer immune evasion, an in vitro co-culture model of gastric cancer cells and T cells was established. Using both AGS and MKN45 cell lines, we systematically evaluated the impact of circRNA_0005075 knockdown on the anti-tumor effect of T cells.

Initially, knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR. Results demonstrated that transfection with two independent interfering sequences (siCirc-1 and siCirc-2) led to a significant reduction in circRNA_0005075 expression levels in both AGS and MKN45 cells compared to the negative control (siNC), with a consistent knockdown trend observed across both cell lines (P < 0.05, Fig. 1A). This confirmed the stability and reproducibility of the circRNA_0005075 knockdown model, providing a reliable foundation for subsequent functional assays. Colony formation assays were then employed to assess T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Under co-culture conditions with T cells, knockdown of circRNA_0005075 markedly reduced the number of colonies formed by both AGS and MKN45 cells, indicating a significant impairment in the long-term survival and proliferative capacity of tumor cells compared to the control (Fig. 1B). To further elucidate the mechanism underlying the altered T cell effector function, flow cytometry was used to measure the expression of granzyme B (GZMB), a key cytotoxic molecule in T cells. In co-culture systems with either AGS or MKN45 cells, knockdown of circRNA_0005075 significantly increased the proportion of GZMB-positive T cells (Fig. 1C), indicating enhanced cytotoxic activity. Concurrently, expression of another critical cytotoxic molecule, perforin, was assessed. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that following circRNA_0005075 knockdown, perforin expression levels in T cells were significantly elevated under co-culture conditions with both gastric cancer cell lines (Fig. 1D). This result, consistent with the trend observed for GZMB, further supports the notion that downregulation of circRNA_0005075 augments T cell-mediated cytotoxicity.

Collectively, these results from both molecular (GZMB, perforin) and functional (colony formation capacity) perspectives demonstrate that knockdown of circRNA_0005075 significantly enhances T cell killing of gastric cancer cells. These findings imply that circRNA_0005075 may promote immune evasion in gastric cancer by suppressing T cell effector function, laying a solid foundation for subsequent investigation into its molecular mechanisms involving metabolic reprogramming and immune checkpoint regulation.

Knockdown of circRNA_0005075 induces significant reprogramming of lactate metabolism in gastric cancer cells

Given the aforementioned finding that knockdown of circRNA_0005075 significantly enhances T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against gastric cancer cells, we sought to explore the underlying molecular basis. A targeted metabolomics strategy was employed to systematically analyze metabolic profile changes in MKN45 gastric cancer cells following circRNA_0005075 knockdown, with a focus on the reprogramming features of lactate-related pathways.

First, a volcano plot of differential metabolites provided an overview of the global metabolic alterations induced by circRNA_0005075 knockdown. The results revealed significant changes in the levels of numerous metabolites compared to the control group, with differentially abundant metabolites clearly distributed in both up- and down-regulated directions (Fig. 2A). This suggests that downregulation of circRNA_0005075 substantially perturbs metabolic homeostasis in gastric cancer cells. Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these differential metabolites was performed to identify the core affected metabolic networks. The enrichment results highlighted several pathways closely related to energy and carbon metabolism, including pyruvate metabolism, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and other central carbon metabolism pathways (Fig. 2B). To more intuitively depict the distribution characteristics of the significantly enriched pathways, a bar chart visualization of differential metabolic pathways was generated. The analysis showed that multiple pathways closely associated with lactate metabolism were prominent in terms of gene count and enrichment significance in the circRNA_0005075 knockdown group (Fig. 2C), further validating the broad and systemic impact of circRNA_0005075 downregulation on lactate-related pathways. For a comprehensive assessment of pathway enrichment levels and the contribution of differential metabolites, a bubble plot was used to integrate the significantly enriched metabolic pathways. The results positioned lactate-generating pathways and their upstream/downstream related metabolic pathways highly in terms of enrichment ratio and significance level (Fig. 2D), indicating that circRNA_0005075 knockdown induces a metabolic reprogramming centered on lactate metabolism at the network level.

Finally, to verify whether the lactate metabolic reprogramming suggested by targeted metabolomics was reflected at the level of key metabolites, we quantitatively compared representative metabolites related to lactate metabolism. Results showed that in MKN45 cells with circRNA_0005075 knockdown, key nodal metabolites of the glycolysis-lactate axis were significantly altered compared to controls. Pyruvate levels were significantly reduced (Fig. 2E, P < 0.05), and lactate levels also showed a marked decrease (Fig. 2E, P < 0.001). Concurrently, among TCA cycle intermediates closely linked to central carbon metabolism, α-ketoglutarate was significantly lowered (Fig. 2E, P < 0.01), and fumarate also exhibited a significant decline (Fig. 2E, P < 0.05). Beyond energy metabolism pathways, circRNA_0005075 knockdown was accompanied by synchronous changes in various metabolites related to redox homeostasis and amino acid/nucleotide metabolism: glutathione levels decreased significantly (Fig. 2E, P < 0.01), the γ-glutamyl-related metabolite H-gamma-Glu-Gln-OH was significantly reduced (Fig. 2E, P < 0.01), along with significant decreases in 2-Amino-1-phenylethanol (Fig. 2E, P < 0.01), Hydroxyphenyllactic acid (Fig. 2E, P < 0.001), and Inosinic acid (Fig. 2E, P < 0.05). Importantly, the most significantly enriched pathways, including pyruvate metabolism and the TCA cycle, consistently showed reduced levels of their key metabolites (lactate, pyruvate, α-ketoglutarate, and fumarate), indicating a coordinated reprogramming of lactate-centered metabolism upon circRNA_0005075 knockdown. These metabolite-level changes corroborate the pathway enrichment analysis, providing direct metabolomic evidence that circRNA_0005075 knockdown induces systematic reprogramming of a metabolic network centered on lactate metabolism.

CircRNA_0005075 indirectly modulates PD-L1 expression in gastric cancer cells via lactate metabolism–driven histone lactylation

Having established that circRNA_0005075 knockdown significantly alters lactate metabolism in gastric cancer cells, and considering that lactate serves not only as a metabolic end-product but also as a key substrate for histone lactylation, we next investigated whether circRNA_0005075 influences immune checkpoint molecule expression through a lactate metabolism–epigenetic regulation axis.

First, the direct impact of circRNA_0005075 on lactate homeostasis was verified at the metabolic level. In both AGS and MKN45 cells, knockdown of circRNA_0005075 significantly reduced intracellular lactate levels, while supplementation with exogenous sodium lactate effectively restored lactate content (Fig. 3A, P < 0.001). This confirms that circRNA_0005075 distinctly regulates lactate metabolism in gastric cancer cells, providing the necessary metabolic basis for subsequent epigenetic alterations. We then assessed whether changes in lactate metabolism were translated into histone lactylation signals. Western blot analysis revealed that circRNA_0005075 knockdown significantly decreased the lactylation levels at histone H3K18 and H3K9 sites in both cell lines, and sodium lactate supplementation concurrently restored these lactylation modifications (Fig. 3B, C). To determine whether histone lactylation directly acts on the transcriptional regulation of immune checkpoint genes, ChIP-qPCR was performed to assess the enrichment of H3K9 lactylation at the PD-L1 promoter region. The results showed that circRNA_0005075 knockdown significantly reduced H3K9la enrichment at the PD-L1 promoter, an effect that was markedly reversed by sodium lactate treatment (Fig. 3D, P < 0.001). This indicates that alterations in lactate metabolism can directly influence the PD-L1 transcriptional regulatory environment via epigenetic mechanisms. Consistent with these epigenetic changes, PD-L1 expression was significantly altered at both transcriptional and protein levels. qPCR analysis demonstrated that circRNA_0005075 knockdown significantly suppressed PD-L1 mRNA expression in AGS and MKN45 cells, and exogenous sodium lactate treatment partially restored its transcript levels (Fig. 3E, P < 0.001). Furthermore, flow cytometry confirmed that circRNA_0005075 knockdown significantly decreased the proportion of cells expressing surface PD-L1, and sodium lactate supplementation substantially reversed this change (Fig. 3F, P < 0.001).

In summary, these results systematically demonstrate, across multiple levels—from metabolic alteration and epigenetic modification to immune checkpoint expression—that circRNA_0005075, by regulating lactate levels, mediates dynamic changes in histone lactylation, thereby promoting the transcriptional activation and protein expression of PD-L1. This delineates a coherent and reversible metabolic-epigenetic regulatory pathway contributing to immune evasion in gastric cancer cells.

Subsequently, colony formation assays evaluated the killing capacity of T cells against gastric cancer cells under the different treatment conditions. Under T cell co-culture conditions, knockdown of PD-L1 significantly reduced the colony-forming ability of both AGS and MKN45 cells, indicating that PD-L1 downregulation enhances T cell-mediated anti-tumor effects. Notably, when circRNA_0005075 was overexpressed on the background of PD-L1 knockdown, the colony-forming capacity of gastric cancer cells was markedly restored (Fig. 4C). To further validate this phenomenon at the level of T cell effector function, flow cytometry was used to detect the expression of key cytotoxic molecules, granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin, in T cells. Results showed that when co-cultured with PD-L1-knockdown gastric cancer cells, the expression levels of both GZMB and perforin in T cells were significantly elevated. This finding further confirms, at the level of immune effector molecules, that circRNA_0005075 can suppress T cell cytotoxic function by upregulating PD-L1.

In conclusion, these systematic in vitro functional experiments demonstrate that circRNA_0005075 impairs T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses by regulating PD-L1 expression, thereby promoting immune evasion of gastric cancer cells. This provides direct causal evidence for its functional role within the tumor immune microenvironment.

Discussion

Gastric cancer continues to be one of the most lethal malignancies globally. Despite the significant advancements in systemic therapy for advanced gastric cancer driven by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis, clinical efficacy remains notably heterogeneous. A substantial proportion of patients exhibit either primary insensitivity or acquired resistance during immunotherapy [31]. Prior studies suggest that tumor metabolic reprogramming and its regulation of the immune microenvironment may constitute key intrinsic factors limiting ICI efficacy [32], yet the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. Within this context, the present study aimed to investigate whether circRNA_0005075 participates in the metabolic and immune regulatory processes in gastric cancer, with a specific focus on its potential roles in lactate metabolism remodeling, histone lactylation, and the transcriptional regulation of the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1, thereby providing new molecular-level evidence for understanding the intrinsic limitations of gastric cancer immunotherapy.

A noteworthy point of discussion is the apparent functional discrepancy between our findings and a previous report showing that circRNA_0005075 suppresses gastric carcinogenesis via the miR-431/p53/EMT axis [30]. This seeming contradiction may be explained by context-dependent functionality, which is increasingly recognized for circRNAs. The previous study focused on proliferation, migration, and EMT in different cell lines under standard culture conditions, whereas our study specifically addresses immune evasion in the context of the tumor–immune interface. It is plausible that circRNA_0005075 exerts pro-tumor effects within the immune microenvironment while simultaneously restraining intrinsic tumor aggressiveness—a phenomenon reminiscent of the dual roles described for other circRNAs in cancer. The multifaceted nature of circRNAs in cancer is increasingly recognized. For instance, while we position circRNA_0005075 as a promoter of immune evasion, other studies have shown that circRNAs or their interacting partners can profoundly influence DNA repair mechanisms and radioresistance [33,34]. Further mechanistic dissection using conditional knockout models or patient-derived samples is warranted to reconcile these observations.

Interestingly, earlier studies have demonstrated that the tumor suppressor p53 can counteract transcription factor downregulation induced by environmental stressors [35], suggesting that p53 pathway integrity may influence how cells respond to metabolic perturbations at the transcriptional level. Whether p53 or other transcription factors participate in circRNA_0005075-mediated metabolic reprogramming warrants further investigation. From a translational perspective, the circRNA_0005075–lactylation–PD-L1 axis may have dual clinical relevance. First, it could serve as a tissue-based biomarker for stratifying patients who are more likely to benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, particularly those without prominent inflammatory signatures. Second, targeting upstream regulators such as circRNAs, although still in early stages, might offer a strategy to “metabolically prime” tumors for improved checkpoint blockade responses. However, several hurdles remain, including delivery efficiency, off-target effects, and the dynamic nature of lactate metabolism in vivo. In our view, the most immediate clinical application lies not in direct circRNA targeting, but in using lactylation status as a companion diagnostic to guide combination therapy decisions.

In the in vitro co-culture system, knockdown of circRNA_0005075 significantly enhanced T cell-mediated killing of gastric cancer cells, concomitant with a marked upregulation of effector molecules GZMB and perforin. This finding implies that tumor cells are not merely passive targets of immune attack; rather, they can actively shape the magnitude of the immune response through their intrinsic molecular and metabolic states. Extensive research has established that tumor metabolic reprogramming, particularly changes characterized by enhanced glycolysis and lactate accumulation [36,37], can suppress anti-tumor immunity through multiple mechanisms, including lowering TME pH, limiting access to key nutrients like glucose for immune cells, and inducing CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction or exhaustion [38]. However, this paradigm largely positions lactate as a terminal metabolite of hyperactive glycolysis, with its immunosuppressive effects perceived as a passive consequence of aberrant tumor metabolism, thereby focusing research primarily on lactate's direct inhibitory effects on immune cell function [39]. Our results suggest that in gastric cancer cells, the lactate-associated immunosuppressive state is not entirely determined by passive changes in metabolic flux but is likely actively shaped by the tumor cell's endogenous regulatory network. The fact that knockdown of circRNA_0005075 alone significantly alleviated the suppression of T cell killing indicates that this molecule may operate at an upstream node within the lactate-mediated immunosuppressive pathway in tumor cells. By potentially regulating lactate production, utilization, or signal translation, circRNA_0005075 can influence the metabolic information tumor cells export to the immune microenvironment at its source. Conceptually, this expands the functional scope of circRNAs in tumor biology, demonstrating that circRNAs can influence not only classic phenotypes like proliferation or invasion but also indirectly determine the magnitude of immune effector response through metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, this finding offers a novel explanatory framework for understanding ICI efficacy heterogeneity: even within a similar immune cell context, intrinsic metabolic regulatory differences in tumor cells can lead to significant variations in immune killing efficiency. Thus, our functional findings lay a logical foundation for subsequent in-depth dissection of how circRNA_0005075 connects lactate metabolism to epigenetic regulation and suggest that targeting endogenous metabolic regulators in tumor cells could be a potential strategy to alleviate lactate-mediated immunosuppression and enhance immunotherapy efficacy. While the present study establishes circRNA_0005075 as an upstream regulator of lactate metabolism in gastric cancer cells, the direct molecular targets—whether specific metabolic enzymes (e.g., LDHA, PKM2), lactate transporters (e.g., MCT1/4), or RNA-binding proteins—remain to be experimentally identified. Existing literature suggests that circRNA_0005075 may function as a miRNA sponge. However, the specific interacting partners linking circRNA_0005075 to metabolic reprogramming warrant further mechanistic investigation.

Further metabolic and functional experiments revealed that circRNA_0005075 knockdown not only significantly reduced intracellular lactate levels. The ability of exogenous sodium lactate supplementation to partially restore these phenotypes functionally demonstrates that circRNA_0005075′s regulation of histone lactylation is metabolically dependent. Contrary to the traditional view of lactate as a mere metabolic end-product, this result suggests that endogenous molecular factors in tumor cells can indirectly determine how lactate is translated into specific epigenetic signals by modulating its generation and utilization. Since its initial characterization, research on histone lactylation has predominantly employed hypoxia or exogenous lactate treatments to reveal that H3K9la and H3K18la are enriched at promoters or transcription start sites of actively transcribed genes [[40], [41], [42]] and correlate positively with transcriptional activation, establishing lactylation as a crucial bridge connecting metabolic state and transcriptional regulation. However, such studies have largely focused on the existence and ubiquity of lactylation itself, with less exploration of whether it is finely regulated in different tumor types or molecular contexts, particularly lacking in-depth analysis of lactylation site selectivity and functional directionality. In our gastric cancer model, changes in circRNA_0005075 expression were found to synchronously influence lactate levels and H3K9la/H3K18la modification status, indicating that lactylation is not a random or passive global epigenetic response. Instead, it can be specifically shaped at defined sites by upstream regulatory factors. Importantly, both H3K9 and H3K18 reside in histone regions highly active in transcriptional regulation, and alterations in their lactylation status are more likely to directly participate in promoter opening and transcription complex recruitment. Therefore, the lactylation remodeling mediated by circRNA_0005075 possesses significant transcriptional regulatory potential. By linking circRNA-level regulation to site-specific lactylation, our study not only addresses a key missing mechanistic link—the upstream regulation of lactylation—but also offers a new perspective on how metabolic reprogramming achieves selective regulation of immune-related genes via epigenetic pathways. This elevates lactylation from a metabolic byproduct to a functional regulatory node actively involved in tumor immune evasion. We acknowledge that the targeted metabolomics profiling was performed in MKN45 cells as a representative gastric cancer model. While we validated the key finding of reduced lactate levels in both AGS and MKN45 cells by direct lactate assay (Fig. 3A), comprehensive metabolomic pathway analysis in additional gastric cancer cell lines and patient-derived organoids represents an important direction for future research. The current study establishes a proof-of-concept for circRNA_0005075-mediated metabolic-epigenetic regulation, with multi-cell line metabolomic validation and isotope tracing flux analysis planned as follow-up studies.

Focusing on immune checkpoints as critical clinical endpoints, our study systematically demonstrated, via ChIP-qPCR and dual-luciferase reporter assays, that the enrichment of H3K9la at the PD-L1 promoter region closely parallels changes in promoter activity and transcriptional output. Furthermore, circRNA_0005075 can indirectly dictate PD-L1 expression by regulating lactate metabolic status and the corresponding lactylation levels, thereby enabling metabolic signals to be epigenetically inscribed into the regulatory elements of an immune checkpoint gene promoter. Compared to prior research paradigms emphasizing exogenous stimuli like IFN-γ or hypoxia in driving PD-L1 expression, this mechanism provides a more endogenous molecular basis for explaining the high heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression observed clinically, even in the absence of overt inflammatory signals. In their 2024 study investigating primary cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF)-tumor cell interactions, Li et al. found that CAF-secreted LOX activates TGFβ-related signaling and enhances glycolysis, leading to increased lactate accumulation concomitant with H3K18la enrichment at the PD-L1 promoter. However, that study focused on stromal-derived signals as the upstream trigger, whereas our work demonstrates that an endogenous circRNA within tumor cells can intrinsically modulate the same lactylation-PD-L1 axis, suggesting that the regulation of PD-L1 via histone lactylation can occur independently of paracrine microenvironmental cues. They validated the transcriptional activation effect using ChIP-qPCR and promoter luciferase assays, proposing a model where microenvironment-derived factors impair PD-1/PD-L1 blockade efficacy via metabolic reprogramming [43]. In contrast, our work pushes this regulatory axis further upstream to the level of endogenous non-coding RNA within tumor cells and completes the evidence chain within a single system, linking lactate level changes, site-specific lactylation, promoter functional readout, and immune killing output. This establishes that the metabolic-epigenetic regulation of PD-L1 can operate independent of complex paracrine networks. Similarly, studies in hematological malignancies have reported that STAT5 promotes glycolysis and lactate accumulation to induce PD-L1 transcription, a mechanism also involving altered PD-L1 promoter lactylation status coupled with immunosuppressive function validation [44], although its upstream driver primarily involves a transcription factor signaling axis. Recent research in solid tumors involving circRNAs, such as the 2025 study by Xu et al., demonstrated that circDNER in lung cancer could reduce H3K18la enrichment at the PD-L1 promoter through metabolic reprogramming, thereby suppressing PD-L1 transcription and enhancing T cell killing [45]. Relative to these studies, the incremental contribution of our work lies primarily in the tumor type specificity and the lactylation site selectivity. Our results specifically point to the functional enrichment of H3K9la at the PD-L1 promoter in gastric cancer, and its metabolic dependency was confirmed through sodium lactate rescue experiments. This suggests that lactylation may exhibit site preferences and distinct regulatory pathways across different tumors and under varied upstream regulatory contexts. Collectively, our study not only further corroborates the general involvement of lactylation in PD-L1 transcriptional regulation but, by positioning circRNA_0005075 as an upstream regulatory node, constructs a complete mechanistic cascade—from metabolic alteration to epigenetic inscription to immune effector output—within the gastric cancer context. This significantly strengthens the translational interpretability and potential target value of this axis.

In the in vivo model, we observed that knockdown of circRNA_0005075 significantly potentiated the anti-tumor effect of PD-1 mAb, evidenced by markedly suppressed tumor growth accompanied by concurrent upregulation of cytotoxic effector molecules GZMB and perforin within tumor tissues. This indicates that the intervention not only reduced tumor burden but also substantively enhanced the effector immune response. Previous studies using various syngeneic transplant models have demonstrated that directly reducing tumor cell lactate production can enhance the in vivo efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade [46]. For instance, in the B16-F10 melanoma model by Daneshmandi et al., inhibiting LDH-A to lower lactate levels significantly augmented anti-PD-1 therapy-induced tumor growth inhibition, concomitant with increased tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cell proportions and granzyme B expression [47]. Similarly, in tumor models insensitive to PD-1 blockade, inhibiting lactate export or alleviating microenvironmental acidosis could partially restore responsiveness to immunotherapy, manifested as reduced tumor volume and improved effector immune cell function [48]. These studies collectively underscore the important role of lactate-associated metabolic states in shaping the response to checkpoint therapy, though their intervention targets primarily reside at the level of metabolic enzymes or transporters. In contrast, our in vivo results indicate that by modulating the endogenous circRNA_0005075 in tumor cells, it is possible to remodel lactate-related phenotypes without directly intervening in enzyme activity, and this significantly amplifies the anti-tumor effect of PD-1 mAb in vivo. Furthermore, building upon existing evidence showing that elevated H3K9 lactylation in tumor cells correlates with diminished immunotherapy response, and that intervention at this epigenetic state can improve CD8⁺ T cell function and enhance anti-PD-1 efficacy [49], our in vivo data further anchor the upstream regulatory node at the circRNA level. This integrates metabolic alteration, lactylation modification, and immunotherapy response into a continuous and verifiable mechanistic chain. Overall, these comparative findings support the notion that circRNA_0005075 and the lactylation state it mediates are not merely molecular features of immune evasion but may constitute important intrinsic determinants influencing the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy.

While our study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanism by which circRNA_0005075 influences PD-L1 expression and shapes immunotherapy response through the regulation of lactate metabolism and histone lactylation, several aspects warrant further investigation. First, the research is primarily based on in vitro cell systems and mouse xenograft models. Although these models offer strong controllability for mechanistic studies, they may not fully capture the complex metabolic and immune microenvironment heterogeneity inherent to human gastric cancer. Therefore, validating these conclusions in more physiologically relevant models, such as patient-derived organoids or humanized immune models, is necessary. Second, although circRNA_0005075 is positioned as an upstream regulatory node for lactate metabolism and lactylation, its precise mode of action remains to be fully deciphered. Based on our metabolomic profiling and published literature, we hypothesize several non-mutually-exclusive mechanisms: (1) circRNA_0005075 may sponge miRNAs that target glycolytic enzymes such as LDHA or PKM2; (2) it may interact with RNA-binding proteins (e.g., hnRNPs) to stabilize or destabilize mRNAs encoding lactate transporters (MCT1/MCT4); (3) it could modulate the activity of transcription factors governing metabolic gene expression; or (4) it might exert nuclear functions to directly influence chromatin accessibility at metabolic gene loci. These possibilities are currently under active investigation and will be reported in separate studies. For example, it is unclear whether it exerts its effects by influencing key metabolic enzymes, lactate transporters, or through interactions with RNA-binding proteins to modulate metabolic and epigenetic states. Additionally, our study focused on H3K9la and H3K18la, two lactylation marks closely associated with transcriptional activation. The potential roles of other histone sites or non-histone lactylation in immune regulation have not been systematically assessed. Future work integrating multi-omics analyses and validating the value of circRNA_0005075 and its associated lactylation signatures in predicting immunotherapy response using clinical samples will be crucial to advancing the translational application of this metabolic-epigenetic regulatory axis in gastric cancer immunotherapy.

From a therapeutic standpoint, targeting circRNA_0005075 directly remains challenging due to delivery and specificity issues. However, our findings suggest that downstream effectors, particularly histone lactylation, may be more amenable to pharmacological intervention. Small-molecule inhibitors of lactate metabolism (e.g., LDHA inhibitors) or epigenetic modulators could potentially be repurposed to disrupt this axis. Additionally, H3K9la enrichment at the PD-L1 promoter may serve as a tissue-based predictive biomarker for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade efficacy, especially in patients with low inflammatory infiltrates. Prospective clinical trials combining metabolic modulators with ICIs are warranted to test this hypothesis.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our findings are predominantly derived from cell lines and xenograft models, which may not fully recapitulate the complex metabolic and immune heterogeneity of human gastric cancer. Second, although we propose a mechanistic axis, the molecular mediator linking circRNA_0005075 to lactate metabolism—whether specific metabolic enzymes (e.g., LDHA, PKM2), lactate transporters (e.g., MCT1/MCT4), or RNA-binding proteins—remains unidentified. Third, while our metabolomics data reveal significant reprogramming of lactate-centered pathways, we did not perform detailed flux analyses (ECAR, glucose uptake, extracellular pH) or systematically evaluate the expression of glycolytic enzymes and lactate transporters. These mechanistic gaps will be addressed in future studies. Fourth, we did not validate circRNA_0005075, PD-L1, or histone lactylation levels in clinical gastric cancer samples, which limits the translational generalizability of our conclusions. Fifth, we focused on H3K9la and H3K18la, but other histone or non-histone lactylation sites may also contribute to the observed phenotypes. Sixth, protein-level validation of PD-L1 knockdown and rescue was not performed in the current study. Future studies using patient-derived organoids, clinical cohorts, and multi-omics approaches are needed to address these gaps.

Conclusion

This study systematically clarifies the function and mechanism of circRNA_0005075 in regulating immune evasion and immunotherapy response in gastric cancer. It reveals that circRNA_0005075 modulates lactate metabolic reprogramming in tumor cells, drives changes in histone lactylation, and consequently influences the transcriptional activation of the immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1. Through multi-layered evidence encompassing in vitro T cell co-culture systems, metabolic and epigenetic analyses, and in vivo tumor models combined with PD-1 monoclonal antibody treatment, our work establishes a complete mechanistic cascade from metabolic state alteration to promoter epigenetic inscription and ultimately to immune effector output. This is a significant departure from models that primarily focus on extrinsic inflammatory signals, and it underscores a concept previously observed in other contexts, where tumor-intrinsic non-coding RNAs dictate metabolic decisions to shape the outcome of anti-tumor immunity [17]. This delineates a pivotal role for metabolic-epigenetic regulation in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. In contrast to prior research predominantly focusing on inflammatory signals or microenvironmental stimuli driving PD-L1 expression, our study emphasizes the independent contribution of the tumor cell's intrinsic metabolic state and its regulation by upstream non-coding RNA in determining immune checkpoint expression. This provides a new molecular-level explanation for the significant heterogeneity observed in gastric cancer immunotherapy efficacy. Overall, these findings not only expand the functional boundaries of circRNAs in the realm of tumor metabolism and immune regulation but also suggest that circRNA_0005075 and the lactylation state it mediates hold potential value as nodes for indirect metabolic-epigenetic modulation and may serve as predictive biomarkers for treatment response. This work provides a theoretical foundation and research direction for future strategies combining metabolic-epigenetic regulation with immune checkpoint therapy.
