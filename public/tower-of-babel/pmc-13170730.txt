Pilot Study of Patient-Reported Outcomes in Stargardt Disease: Correlation With Functional and Structural Findings
Authors: Johnson Hoang, Iden Amiri, Lauren Dimalanta, Alessandro Iannaccone, Alice Yang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to assess the impact of Stargardt disease (STGD) on activities of daily living and mental health, correlating patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures from the Michigan Retinal Degeneration (MRDQ) and Vision-Related Anxiety Questionnaires (MVAQs) with clinical data.MethodsPatients with STGD with biallelic pathogenic ABCA4 mutations were recruited from the University of North Carolina (UNC) at Chapel Hill and Foundation Fighting Blindness (FFB) databases. Participants completed MRDQ and MVAQ testing. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), electroretinogram (ERG), and fundus autofluorescence (FAF) were retrospectively analyzed for UNC patients.ResultsFifty participants (mean age = 48.8 ± 18.5) were included. Median θ for MRDQ: central vision (0.138), color vision (0.143), contrast sensitivity (0.122), and photosensitivity (0.185). Median MVAQ θ scores for cone- and rod-related anxiety were 0.005 and −0.055, respectively. The following additional analyses were performed using the UNC subset of participants (n = 12): BCVA correlated with contrast sensitivity (P = 0.027). Rod ERG response correlated with scotopic vision (P = 0.044). Central FAF lesion extent correlated with central vision (P = 0.039) and contrast sensitivity (P = 0.032). Rod-related anxiety correlated with rod ERG response (P = 0.048), color vision (P < 0.001), contrast sensitivity (P < 0.001), photopic peripheral vision (P < 0.001), cone-related anxiety (P < 0.001), photosensitivity (P = 0.014), mesopic peripheral vision (P < 0.001), and scotopic vision (P < 0.001). Cone-related anxiety correlated with color vision (P = 0.026), contrast sensitivity (P = 0.013), photopic peripheral vision (P < 0.001), mesopic peripheral vision (P < 0.001), and scotopic vision (P < 0.001).ConclusionsIn patients with STGD, structural and functional measures correlated with reduced functional vision and increased vision-related anxiety per PRO criteria. These findings provide insight into patient-perceived disease burden and may help inform future STGD interventions.

First described by Dr. Karl Stargardt in 1909, Stargardt disease (STGD) is one of the most common inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) and is estimated to account for 7% of all retinal degenerative entities.1,2 The most typical form is inherited as an autosomal recessive disease, due to biallelic variants in the ABCA4 gene. The dysfunctional protein leads to the accumulation of visual cycle byproducts in the outer segments of both rod and cone photoreceptors and, ultimately, in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), resulting in photoreceptor and RPE dysfunction and loss. Affected individuals typically present with bilateral central vision loss, photophobia, and color vision impairment. Whereas STGD is typically diagnosed in childhood, it may manifest far later in life, and the clinical presentation of STGD depends on a variety of factors, including the severity of the two ABCA4 gene mutations and sex.2,3

Current therapeutic research focuses on three main approaches. The first involves gene therapy, aiming to introduce a functional copy of the ABCA4 gene or correct the existing mutation. The second targets disease progression by manipulating the visual cycle to slow outer retinal atrophic changes. The third is directed at visual restoration for the most severely visually impaired patients, relying on stem cell-based therapies, optogenetic approaches, or retinal prosthetics.3,4

Multiple established visual function tests and imaging modalities such as best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), kinetic and static visual fields, electroretinography (ERG), optical coherence tomography (OCT), fundus autofluorescence (FAF), and fundus photography are used routinely to objectively characterize IRDs, their progression, and response to treatments, but these outcomes fail to characterize the subjective patient experience of the disease.

Understanding the subjective patient impact of a disease on quality of life and affect is an area of study that has been poorly explored. These data are a critical part of natural history studies and may be the single most relevant outcomes for interventional clinical trials.5–7 The ensemble of these assessments is referred to as patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures, assessments on patient perspectives rather than clinician interpretation.8,9 In the context of a clinical trial, they can help assess the overall impact of a treatment not only based on measuring traditional functional or structural outcomes, but also based on the impact of disease on functional domains, activities of daily living (ADLs), and overall quality of life (QOL).5–7 Thus, to refine further our understanding of PROs of vision-related disability and anxiety more specifically in patients with IRD, the Michigan Retinal Degeneration Questionnaire (MRDQ) and Michigan Vision-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (MVAQ) were developed and validated.4–7 To date, several genotypes associated with retinitis pigmentosa (RP) have been characterized with these more IRD-specific PRO questionnaires.10–13 To the best of our knowledge, however, the impact of STGD on vision-related disability and anxiety has not been investigated to date. In this study, we utilized the MRDQ and MVAQ to assess the impact of ABCA4-related recessive STGD on vision- and anxiety-related PROs and correlated these outcome measures with established functional and clinical outcomes to examine the relationship between self-reported and objective measures of visual function in STGD.

Methods

Patients with STGD with confirmed biallelic pathogenic ABCA4 mutations were recruited from the Department of Ophthalmology at the University of North Carolina (UNC) at Chapel Hill and the Foundation Fighting Blindness (FFB) MyRetinaTracker databases. UNC patients were recruited using the Carolina Data Warehouse for Health, a central data repository containing clinical data from the UNC Health Care System, and the internal UNC electronic medical record. A total of 1675 individuals from the FFB MyRetinaTracker database were contacted via mass email by the FFB, and 38 participants responded and enrolled, yielding a response rate of 2.27%. Participants were consented verbally to complete the MRDQ and MVAQ. This study was approved by the UNC Institutional Review Board (IRB #24-0847) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The MRDQ and MVAQ questionnaires assess the functional impact of IRDs and vision-related anxiety, respectively.10–13 The MRDQ was used to assess the following domains: central vision, color vision, contrast sensitivity, scotopic function, photopic peripheral vision, mesopic peripheral vision, and photosensitivity.10 The MVAQ was used to assess two domains: rod-function related anxiety and cone-function related anxiety.11 For each domain, theta (θ) scores were generated using item response theory with a graded response model in R (version 4.4.2) using the mirt package. Scores were calculated using expected a posteriori (EAP) estimation and previously published item parameter calibrations from MRDQ/MVAQ validation studies.10–12 These domain scores were not simple summed raw scores, but latent trait estimates derived from each participant's pattern of responses within a given domain. Theta scores are mean-centered at zero and approximately normally distributed, with higher θ values indicating worse patient-reported impairment. For the MRDQ, higher scores indicate greater difficulty with the visual function being assessed in that domain. For the MVAQ, higher scores indicate greater vision-related anxiety, with higher rod- and cone-function-related anxiety scores reflecting greater anxiety associated with tasks dependent on those respective visual systems. The questionnaires were administered through a phone call, and questions were read aloud to participants by an interviewer (authors J.H. or L.D.) using a standardized script. Responses were documented as they were given to the interviewer.

BCVA, ERG, and FAF data were retrospectively available only for the UNC subset of participants; therefore, all structure-function and structure/PRO correlation analyses were restricted to this subset (n = 12). Given the limited sample size, these analyses were considered exploratory and intended to generate hypotheses for future study rather than establish definitive associations. Cone and rod response reduction on ERG were categorized as mild (up to 25% reduced compared to normal reference range), moderate (26%–50% reduced), severe (>50% up to non-recordable), or no reduction in response (normal range responses). FAF images from UNC participants were reviewed retrospectively by a single IRD specialist who was masked to PRO questionnaire scores at the time of image analysis. Because grading was performed by a single grader, inter-grader reliability was not assessed. Severity of the central lesion on FAF was categorized as within 10 degrees of the fovea, greater than 10 degrees from the fovea but within the macula, or extending outside the macula. Peripheral retinal lesions on FAF were categorized as mild (few, scattered hyper- and/or hypo-fluorescent changes), moderate (disseminated changes of either type but no widespread atrophy), severe (widespread extra-macular atrophy), or none (presence of neither hyper- nor hypo-fluorescent lesions) (Supplementary Fig. S1). In cases with more extensive disease, FAF assessment was limited to the acquired image field without stitched or expanded-field analysis.

Metrics were categorized as cone- or rod-dominant based on the photoreceptor function they were intended to reflect. Measures related to central vision, such as BCVA, ERG cone response reduction (mild to severe response reduction as defined above), and central lesion severity, were classified as cone metrics, whereas measures related to peripheral vision such as ERG rod response reduction (mild to severe as defined above) and peripheral lesion severity were classified as rod metrics.

Statistical Analyses

Spearman correlation coefficients were used to assess associations between MRDQ domains, MVAQ domains, and clinical measures. To reduce the risk of false-positive findings due to multiple testing, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately to prespecified families of tests: MRDQ-clinical correlations and MRDQ-MVAQ correlations. Adjusted P values are reported throughout.

Results

Fifty patients were included in the study, 12 of which were from the UNC database and 38 recruited from FFB's MyRetinaTracker database. The mean age was 48.8 ± 18.5 years, 29 participants were women (58%), and 40 reported their race as White (80%; Table 1). The mean BCVA of the better-seeing eye was 20/235 ± 20/91 (LogMAR 1.07 ± 0.66). Both MRDQ and MVAQ questionnaires were completed in their entirety by all participants. Mean duration of MRDQ and MVAQ administration were 33 and 7 minutes, respectively.

Discussion

This study evaluated PROs on vision-related disability and anxiety in patients with STGD. Patients reported higher difficulty with central vision, color vision, contrast sensitivity, and photosensitivity compared with scotopic vision and peripheral vision. The Spearman correlation analyses suggested a pattern of associations consistent with expected disease biology, including worse rod ERG dysfunction correlating with worse scotopic vision. These data provide preliminary insight into the structure-function correlation and the potential utility of PROs in measuring deficits in cone and rod function.

Interestingly, cone response on ERG was not strongly associated with any PROs. One possible explanation is that ERG measures global cone function across the entire retina, whereas functional domains may arise from specific areas. For instance, the domain of central vision localizes to foveal structure and function. Although the cones are most concentrated in the fovea, ERG also captures the function of photoreceptors outside this area. This broader measurement scope may contribute to the weaker correlation between cone ERG findings and PRO measures.

Additionally, higher levels of both cone- and rod-related anxiety were associated with greater reported functional impairments across most visual domains, regardless of whether the function was cone- or rod-dominant. This suggests that as general visual function worsens, anxiety levels also increase, underscoring the significant psychological burden experienced by individuals with STGD.

As with STGD, vision impairment due to other IRDs can result in both significant reduction in the QOL and increased vision-related anxiety and even depression.14,15 An established PRO questionnaire that measures the dimensions of self-reported vision-targeted health status that are most important for persons who have chronic eye diseases is the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ-25).16 Although the NEI-VFQ-25 is a robust and validated psychometric test which assesses all major domains of visual impairment, it is not designed for use in the severely visually impaired. Terheyden et al. assessed the utility of the NEI-VFQ-25 and Impact of Vision Impairment (IVI) profile in STGD.17 Although this study found these tools to have acceptable validity and reliability, they were less effective at distinguishing different levels of visual and emotional difficulties, particularly in earlier stages of STGD. Additionally, patients reported lower levels of difficulty than expected based on clinical severity, suggesting an underestimation of the functional and emotional impact of the disease. This may be due to the fact that these tools are designed for broad ophthalmic populations and general visual impairment rather than a specific disease such as STGD. In contrast, our study utilized the MVAQ and MRDQ, designed specifically for IRDs. Our results revealed preliminary correlations between PROs and clinical severity, providing a novel perspective on the patient experience.

Karuntu et al. assessed RP using the MRDQ, finding correlations between low-luminance visual acuity and central vision and contrast sensitivity domains, low-luminance deficit with scotopic and mesopic peripheral function domains.18 Parekh et al. reported patients with USH2A-associated retinal degeneration had higher disability in scotopic and mesopic peripheral vision domains on the MRDQ, consistent with the rod-cone degeneration pattern of USH2A-related disease.13 Similarly, our study found patients reported more difficulty in domains consistent with the clinical characteristics of STGD, such as central vision and photosensitivity, and less difficulty in domains such as peripheral vision. Unlike RP, which predominantly affects rod photoreceptors before cone photoreceptors, STGD primarily impacts cone more than rod function, leading to earlier central vision loss, consistent with our findings. However, our results suggest that rod-related dysfunction and anxiety still play a significant role in STGD, as evidenced by the correlations between reduced rod ERG response and vision-related anxiety. This underscores the importance of assessing both cone- and rod-related dysfunction structurally and in PRO measures for STGD. Furthermore, Gouveia et al. reported strong correlations between MRDQ scores and both rod- and cone-related anxiety in patients with RPGR-associated X-linked RP.19 These results are similar to those found in our study, highlighting the psychological impact of functional impairments in IRDs.

Despite the increasing recognition of the value of PROs, clinical trials for IRDs remain limited in characterizing the patient experience and how it affects their daily life.20 Utilization of PROs provides unique insight into the patient experience and correlates with objective measures as demonstrated by our study. Because of this important dimension that PROs can add to the understanding of disease and estimation of treatment effectiveness, instruments designed to assess them are becoming increasingly recognized by health authorities as important tools to collect patient-centered evidence.21–23 Thus, PROs may be a valuable tool in informing and evaluating future clinical trials for STGD as well as other IRDs.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Most notably, correlations between PROs and structural/functional biomarkers were limited to the UNC subset with available retrospective clinical data (n = 12), which substantially restricts statistical power and increases the possibility of unstable or spurious associations. Accordingly, these analyses should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating despite FDR adjustment, and confirmation in larger, prospectively characterized Stargardt cohorts is needed. Furthermore, FAF lesion grading was performed by a single masked IRD specialist, and formal inter-grader reproducibility was not assessed. Although masking reduced the potential for bias, the absence of inter-grader reliability testing may limit the reproducibility and generalizability of these imaging measurements. In addition, quantitative FAF assessment was limited to lesion extent visible within the acquired image field and did not involve stitched or expanded-field analysis; therefore, in eyes with more advanced Stargardt disease, particularly when lesions extended beyond the vascular arcades or outside the macula, this metric may not have fully captured total lesion burden, although none of the UNC patients had widespread severe atrophic phenotype extending into the mid or far periphery. Additional limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design, which does not provide information on disease progression by PRO criteria. Additionally, complete clinical data, such as specific ABCA4 variant classes, was unavailable for the patients recruited and interviewed through FFB's MyRetinaTracker, resulting in a small sample size for correlating PRO results and clinical measures. The FFB recruitment email had a low response rate (2.27%), which may be due to selection bias, as it is possible that those with greater visual impairments were likely more motivated to participate. Additionally, nonresponse bias may have been introduced if patients with severe visual impairment were less able to engage in email correspondence. Although interviewers used a standardized script for questionnaire administration, there may be variability between the two interviewers that could introduce bias into the final results. Finally, this study includes a predominantly White cohort (80%), which may limit the generalizability of our findings among more diverse populations. Future studies should prioritize recruitment strategies that engage patients of diverse racial and ethnic backgrounds to improve generalizability.

Conclusions

This pilot study provides an initial evaluation of IRD-specific PROs on vision-related disability and anxiety among patients with ABCA4-related STGD. These results suggest PRO measures may be useful for characterizing patient-perceived disease burden and for generating future hypotheses relevant to low vision rehabilitation, psychological intervention, and clinical management of these patients. Additionally, this study lays the foundation for further, longitudinal studies of PROs in STGD to determine their role in future patient-centric clinical trials for Stargardt disease.
