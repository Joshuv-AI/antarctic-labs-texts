Knowledge and practice of dental professionals regarding vertical teeth preparation techniques
Authors: Nasser M Alahmari, Mohammed A K Al-Haboob, Thrya S Gadah, Nassreen H Albar, Mohammed M Al Moaleem, Mohammed E Sayed, Ashraf Mohammed Alhumaidi, Taha M A S Al-Amri, Shehab S A Diaban, Abdulrahman A H S Tajedin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVertical preparation techniques (VPTs), particularly the biologically oriented preparation technique (BOPT), have gained prominence in restorative dentistry due to their conservative approach to tooth structure preservation and improved aesthetic outcomes. This study aims to assess the knowledge and clinical practices of VPTs, including BOPT, among dental professionals in Sanaa, Yemen, and explore the barriers to their widespread use.Materials and methodsAn online cross-sectional survey was conducted among general practitioners and specialists in Sanaa, Yemen using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The survey explored the participants’ knowledge, familiarity, and clinical use of VPTs and their perceptions of its advantages and challenges. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Chi-square tests to evaluate associations between professional roles and VPT knowledge and practice. P values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.ResultsOut of 285 participants, 76.8% were familiar with VPTs but only 11.6% reported using these approaches in clinical practice. The primary source of knowledge acquisition was informal, with 26.3% of the participants citing social media as their main resource. Specialists demonstrated significantly higher proficiency and confidence in applying VPTs compared with general practitioners (p < 0.05). Key challenges identified in clinical adoption were controlling gingival bleeding (32.28%) and achieving precise margins during tooth preparation (34.74%). The majority (55.8%) of participants acknowledged the potential of VPTs as the future of restorative dentistry. Despite these benefits, 36.1% of the respondents emphasized the need for further training and educational programs to enhance proficiency and overcome the perceived complexity of these techniques.ConclusionThis study highlights significant gaps in the knowledge and practice of VPTs, particularly BOPT, among the participants. While these techniques offer promising clinical and aesthetic benefits, their adoption is hindered by inadequate training and perceived complexity. Targeted educational interventions and further research are needed to support the broad implementation of VPTs in clinical practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06520-w.Keywords: Vertical teeth preparation, Biologically oriented preparation technique, Knowledge, Practice, Dental professional

Introduction

Tooth preparation is the removal of diseased and/or healthy enamel, dentin, and cementum to shape a tooth to receive a restoration [1]. It refers to the mechanical alteration of a defective, injured, or diseased tooth to receive a restorative material that restores a healthy state for the tooth, including aesthetic corrections and normal form and function [2]. The primary challenge of tooth preparation is how to obtain an excellent aesthetic outcome while preserving tooth structure through minimally invasive preparation techniques [3]. Tooth structure preservation enhances aesthetics and plays a crucial role in stress distribution, crack resistance, and long-term durability [4, 5].

Tooth preparation can be performed using either traditional horizontal preparation with a well-defined finish line or vertical preparation (VP) without a defined finish line [6]. Different from the traditional horizontal method that often requires the removal of a significant amount of tooth structure and may jeopardize the tooth’s long-term prognosis, VP is more conservative and defines the finish line as an area rather than a linear demarcation, with restoration margins determined by the laboratory technician based on gingival tissue evidence [7, 8]. VP are categorized into shoulderless and edgeless types, with the biologically oriented preparation technique (BOPT) being a popular edgeless method. Shoulderless preparations, also called bevel preparations, include feather edge, knife edge, and chisel edge types and have become less popular due to modern laboratory advancements and their inherent drawbacks [9, 10].

Noè et al. (2023) highlighted the advantages of the vertical edgeless preparation (VEP) technique, which involves preparing the tooth without a defined finishing line and enhances flexibility in prosthetic margin positioning and emergence profile [11]. The edgeless technique or BOPT involves a VPT that removes the anatomical emergence profile of the crown at the cement–enamel junction (CEJ) to create a new emergence profile with a prosthesis that mimics the natural tooth at the time of placement [12, 13]. The protocol for creating the provisional prosthesis is crucial because it establishes the new emergence profile at different levels within the gingival sulcus. This process allows the surrounding soft tissue to adjust its shape and position to fit the new prosthetic profile, promoting the healing, reinsertion, and thickening of the gingival tissues [14]. BOPT combined with a full digital workflow is a valid option for correcting and remodeling gingival architecture [11, 15].

VPTs are particularly suitable for patients with thick or medium periodontal phenotypes or those with periodontal attachment loss and probing depths greater than 2 mm because these conditions require significant tooth structure removal to create a horizontal finish line, potentially compromising the teeth’s long-term prognosis [11]. When retention and stability are needed, VPTs extend the prosthetically usable area to provide superior retention and stability [13]. In addition, VPTs help manage small carious lesions below the gingival margin and address morphological alterations in the gingival area by concealing the crown margin below the gingival level [2]. This conservative approach is ideal for teeth with short clinical crowns, such as mandibular incisors, where horizontal preparation would result in significant loss of dental tissues [16]. Moreover, VPTs, particularly BOPT, improve long-term gingival stability, enhance the prosthesis’ emergence profile, facilitate oral hygiene, and create a natural appearance, which is especially important in anterior regions where esthetics is crucial [17].

Research comparing horizontal and edgeless vertical techniques, specifically BOPT, has demonstrated that BOPT can match or surpass the effects of traditional methods on periodontal tissue health and clinical outcomes. Full coverage and anterior prostheses utilizing BOPT have been associated with high survival rates of over 4 and 6 years, excellent periodontal health, stable gingival margins, and increased gingival thickness [18, 19]. Similarly, posterior prostheses prepared with BOPT were linked to superior periodontal health with marginal stability, lower plaque and gingival index scores, and fewer pockets deeper than 3 mm compared with horizontal preparations (control group) after 5 years. In one study, the BOPT group showed better clinical response and similar complication rates to the control group [20].

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant differences in periodontal health status, including plaque index, gingival index, gingival recession, survival, and success rates, between BOPT and horizontal preparation techniques [17]. New research shows that vertical finish line preparations result in clinically acceptable marginal gaps across various ceramic materials, with vertical marginal gaps falling within the clinically acceptable range [7, 21, 22].

Research on zirconia crowns fabricated with minimally invasive techniques over 3 years indicated exceptional clinical quality and patient satisfaction, no crown replacements required, no caries, and no adverse soft tissue reactions [6]. Other studies highlighted the effectiveness of VP in achieving superior esthetic outcomes while preserving tooth structure, with gingival thickening, margin stability, and optimal aesthetics achieved over 2 years after VP [23].

Despite the recognized benefits of VPTs when appropriately indicated, their adoption and application among dental professionals exhibit significant variability stemming from differences in knowledge and practice attitudes of promising techniques. To our knowledge and during the literature search, the use of VPTs among dental professionals worldwide or locally has not been surveyed yet, and information about these techniques is lacking. Hence, the current study assessed and measured the knowledge and practices concerning VPTs among different dental professionals in Sanaa City, Yemen.

Participants and methods

Study design, ethical consideration, and setting

An online cross-sectional survey using a web-based study instrument, Google Forms (Google, Inc.), was conducted from 10 August to 25 September 2024. This study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration [24]. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Ibn al-Nafis for Medical Sciences (Re # 184 8-8-2024). The target population was general practitioners (GPs) and specialists at universities, private dental clinics, and governmental hospitals in Sanaa City, Yemen. Invitation to participate in this survey was circulated through social media channels (WhatsApp and Facebook). One follow-up link was re-sent to remind about participation in the study. This approach was considered a proven mode of collection that produces high response rates [25, 26].

Participation in the study was voluntary. Dental practitioners were provided a link; by ticking it, they approved to contribute voluntarily to this study and gave their consent to use their data for the survey. Furthermore, the confidentiality of the contributors’ data was maintained throughout the study by making their information anonymous. The setting of response was set to be one response to prevent multiple entries from the same participant [except in some questions]. All incomplete questionnaires were omitted from the data analysis.

Sample size determination

The sample size was estimated according to the dental professionals (general practitioners with 5 years after graduation and specialist) at Sanaa City, Yemen using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4, University of Dusseldorf). The effect size (d), α, and1-β (power) were 0.2, 0.05, and 0.80, respectively. A survey sample of 275 participants was required to accomplish statistically valid results, and this number was increased to 285 to make up for nonresponse through WhatsApp and Facebook [25].

Instrument development, validity and reliability, and pilot testing

A panel of experts (four prosthodontics and three periodontist consultants and college staff) assessed and evaluated the content validity of the questionnaire. Relevance, clarity, simplicity, and ambiguity for each question were assessed from a scale of 1 to 4, with 1 being the lowest and 4 being the highest [27]. The content validity index was 0.87, indicating that the questionnaire was valid. Reliability was determined by testing internal consistency, and the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.828. A web-based pilot study on a sample of 30 dental professional was then conducted to assess the questionnaire’s readability, clarity, simplicity, and the average time taken to complete it. Also, feedback from participants to improve and changes pointed issues raised in questionnaire. Those dental professionals were considered in the total number of the participants.

Questionnaire parts

An English version of the web-based, self-administrated questionnaire divided into three parts was distributed. The questionnaire consists of a brief explanation of the purpose of the study, the method of data collection, and close-ended questions. The questionnaire has two parts related to the knowledge and practice of VPTs, and it was attached as a separate file. The first part is related to the participant’s characteristics and includes questions on gender, level of education, years of experience, current professional role and position, and place of practice.

The second part consists of the following 17 questions related to the general knowledge of VPTs: ‘’Are you familiar with the concept of vertical preparation techniques?’’, ‘’How did you first learn about vertical preparation techniques?’’, ‘’Which of the following best describes vertical preparation?’’, ‘’ What are the types of vertical preparation techniques you are aware of?’’, ‘’ Why has shoulder less vertical preparation been practically abandoned?’’, ‘’ What is edgeless vertical preparation commonly known as?’’, ‘’What are the key steps involved in the BOPT technique?’’, ‘’What type of crowns is vertical edgeless preparation particularly indicated for?’’, ‘’In which clinical situations is vertical edgeless preparation indicated over horizontal techniques?’’, ‘’What are your primary concerns regarding the use of vertical preparation techniques?’’, ‘’ Why might vertical edgeless preparation be preferred for mandibular incisors?’’, ‘’ What are the advantages of edgeless vertical preparation (BOPT)?’’, ‘’ What are the advantages of edgeless vertical preparation (BOPT)?’’, ‘’ What are the disadvantages of edgeless vertical preparation (BOPT)?’’, ‘’ What is a common criticism of vertical preparation methods like BOPT?’’, ‘’What tool is recommended to reduce damage to the connective attachment during BOPT?’’, ‘’What did current analysis reveal about vertical (edgeless) vs. horizontal finishing lines regarding periodontal health?’’, and ‘’ Why is BOPT considered a promising technique in modern dentistry?”.

The third part of the questionnaire consists of questions related to the practice of VPTs during their daily practice: ‘’How often do you use vertical preparation techniques in your practice?’’, ‘’How confident are you in your ability to successfully perform vertical preparation techniques?’’, ‘’In what type of restorations do you most commonly use vertical preparation techniques?’’, ‘’How challenging do you find vertical preparation techniques compared to other techniques?’’, ‘’Have you observed any differences in patient outcomes when using vertical preparation techniques compared to horizontal techniques?’’, ‘’How important do you believe vertical preparation techniques are for the future of restorative dentistry?’’, ‘’How likely are you to recommend vertical preparation techniques to your colleagues’’?, ‘’What are your recommendations for improving the adoption of vertical preparation techniques in clinical practice?’’. Figure 1 shows the flow chart involved in instrument development. The answered questionnaires were received, directly recognized, and collected from the respondents, then numbered and arranged in an Excel sheet for statistical analysis.

Data processing and analysis

Data were analyzed using the SPSS program, version 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY., USA) and Microsoft Excel Sheet. Correct answers in each section were defined, and descriptive statistics (counts and percentage) were analyzed. The association among dental professionals, knowledge, and practice was analyzed using Chi-square. P values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

The number of participants who filled out and returned the questionnaire was 285, of which 159 (55.8%) were males. The participants were aged between 20 and 53 years, with a mean age and standard deviation of 38.31 ± 15.65 years. The majority of the participants received a Bachelor’s degree (210, 73.7%), had 1–4 years of clinical experience (153, 53.7%), and worked in private practice (186, 65.8%) (Fig. 2). Most of the participants were GPs (210, 73.7%), and only 75 (26.3%) were specialists in cosmetic dentistry (21, 28.0%), prosthodontics (18, 24.0%), and periodontics (12, 16.0%) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study is unique in its scope, being the first to evaluate the knowledge and practice of VPTs (particularly BOPT) among dental professionals. Although this research specifically targets a cohort of dental professionals in Sanaa City, Yemen, the significance of its findings extends beyond regional boundaries. This study provides a foundational understanding of how VPTs are perceived and utilized among GPs and specialists. The results may help bridge the gap between theoretical understanding and clinical application, thereby contributing to the advancement of evidence-based practices in restorative dentistry.

Knowledge and awareness of vertical preparation techniques

Results showed that although a significant majority of the participants (76.8%) were familiar with VPTs, their depth of knowledge considerably varied. Many dental professionals lacked a comprehensive understanding of various VPTs, especially VEP. Only 25.3% of the participants were aware of BOPT, and 32.6% were uncertain about the types of VPTs. This finding indicates a gap in the dental curriculum and continuing education programs, where emerging techniques such as BOPT are not sufficiently covered. Zitzmann et al. (2020) [27] highlighted similar gaps in digital education within dental curricula, underscoring the need for comprehensive coverage of emerging technologies in initial and continuing education. Specialists exhibited a higher level of familiarity with VPTs than GPs, which can be attributed to their advanced training and exposure to specialized concepts in restorative dentistry. In addition, specialists are likely to acquire their knowledge from formal sources such as professional journals (24% vs. 4.3% for GPs). By contrast, a notable portion of GPs (34.3%) first encountered VPTs through social media, indicating their reliance on less formal and potentially less reliable sources of information; this finding further underscores the need for structured educational interventions [28].

Application and challenges of BOPT in clinical practice

Despite the reported familiarity of VP, the practical application of BOPT among dental professionals remains limited. Nearly half of the respondents (47.4%) reported that they never use VPTs, and only 11.6% use them frequently. This low adoption rate, particularly among GPs, can be attributed to several factors, including the perceived complexity of BOPT and the demanding nature of the required instrumentation—such as operational microscopes, intraoral scanners, and dental model printers—which may limit its widespread adoption [10, 11].

The challenges associated with BOPT are well-documented. This technique requires meticulous execution, with specific demands such as the need for the precise management of the gingival sulcus, the elimination of the CEJ, and the creation of a new emergence profile [12, 13]. The inability to assess the final fit of a crown before cementation, difficulties in removing excess cement, and concerns about marginal seal and integrity add to its procedural complexity [18, 19]. In addition, the necessary waiting period for tissue healing during the temporary restoration phase (approximately 2 weeks) and the limited range of application, primarily with zirconium-oxide-based restorations, further complicate the use of BOPT in routine clinical practice [6].

These challenges possibly contribute to the hesitancy among GPs to adopt BOPT, despite its potential benefits. However, specialists reported better patient outcomes with VPTs more frequently than GPs, with 24% of the specialists observing significantly better outcomes compared with 8.57% of the GPs. This disparity is possibly due to the specialists’ higher proficiency and confidence in using the technique. With appropriate training and resources, the barriers to adopting BOPT could be mitigated, allowing many practitioners to leverage its advantages [20].

These findings indicate that VPTs achieve excellent marginal integrity comparable with horizontal preparations, and both techniques meet the stringent clinical requirements for marginal gaps. Thus, VP emerges as a viable alternative to horizontal preparation, offering reliable marginal integrity and expanding the options for clinicians in restorative dentistry. Recent published papers stated that VPTs provide esthetic benefits, minimize plaque accumulation, and improve periodontal health [29–31].

Clinical outcomes and efficacy of BOPT

The BOPT represents a significant advancement in prosthodontic procedures, particularly in VPTs. It has revolutionized the management of restorative margins, especially in cases requiring intricate gingival management and aesthetic outcomes. When successfully implemented, BOPT offers several clinical advantages. This technique is particularly suitable for patients with thick or medium periodontal phenotypes and those experiencing periodontal attachment loss with probing depths greater than 2 mm [11] as reported during follow-up of two cases [32, 33]. BOPT also allows for great flexibility in prosthetic design and enhances the gingival architecture, which is crucial for aesthetic outcomes, especially in anterior restorations. BOPT enhances periodontal health by maintaining or increasing gingival thickness and stability over time [16].

Several studies have documented the benefits of BOPT in maintaining and even improving periodontal health. Research comparing BOPT with traditional horizontal preparation methods has demonstrated that BOPT can either match or surpass the outcomes of these conventional techniques in terms of periodontal tissue health, gingival margin stability, and survival rates of restorations [17, 34]. In anterior teeth, BOPT has been associated with excellent periodontal health, increased gingival thickness, and high survival rates of 96.6% and 100% over 4 and 6 years, respectively [18, 19]. In posterior restorations, BOPT exhibited superior periodontal health compared with horizontal preparations after a 5-year follow-up, marked by 100% marginal stability and few pockets deeper than 3 mm [20]. However, higher bleeding on probing was reported with the use of BOPT compared with that in traditional finish lines [35].

Recent research further underscores the efficacy of VPT in achieving commendable marginal integrity for monolithic ceramic single-crown restorations. The recorded marginal gaps among the tested CAD/CAM materials were within the acceptable range of 120 μm [21, 22], with the highest value of 68.90 μm for zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate and the lowest of 63.90 μm for hybrid ceramic [36]. These findings indicate that VPTs achieve excellent marginal integrity comparable with horizontal preparations. They stand out as a viable alternative to horizontal preparation, offering dependable marginal integrity and broadening the choices available to clinicians in restorative dentistry.

Regarding mechanical resistance, the literature presents varying perspectives. Although some studies suggested that BOPT may result in lower fracture resistance compared with preparations with a defined finish line [37], others indicated that BOPT can achieve higher resistance, particularly when used with monolithic zirconia crowns [38]. The integration of CAD/CAM technology with BOPT further enhances its precision, allowing for accurate emergence profile creation and tooth structure preservation, which is essential for achieving optimal aesthetic and functional outcomes [11, 15].

Agustín-Panadero et al. (2018) [23] reported that zirconia full-coverage restorations on vertically prepared teeth resulted in gingival thickening, margin stability, optimal aesthetics, preservation of tooth structures, and no mechanical complications after 2 years. Noè et al. [11] highlighted the advantages of VEP for preparing teeth without a defined finishing line, enhancing flexibility in prosthetic margin positioning and emergence profile. The use of recent digital workflow allows for precise emergence profile creation and adjustment, which is crucial for aesthetic and functional success [11].

Recommendations for further adaptation

The results of this study indicate a strong demand for additional training and education programs, with 36.1% of the participants advocating for such initiatives to improve the adoption of VPTs. Other recommendations included advancement in tools and equipment (21.6%), improved materials and technology (16.4%), and increased research and evidence (25.9%). These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions to bridge the knowledge gap between GPs and specialists. Enhancing access to professional development opportunities, particularly those focusing on the practical application of VPTs and BOPT, could significantly improve the adoption of these techniques. Integrating a comprehensive coverage of VPTs into dental education curricula would help ensure that all practitioners are equipped with the necessary skills and knowledge to effectively implement these techniques.

Given the limited availability of studies on VPTs, particularly in the context of knowledge and practice, this study fills an important gap in literature. It also underscores the need for further research to validate these findings and explore the long-term outcomes associated with VPTs. The scarcity of long-term clinical studies on BOPT, especially in diverse clinical settings, emphasizes the pressing need for additional research. Future studies should focus on large and diverse populations and include longitudinal assessments to evaluate the impact of educational interventions on the adoption and efficacy of BOPT. Such research would provide valuable data to support the broad implementation of BOPT and other VPTs in dental practice.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the current state of knowledge and practice of VPTs among dental professionals in Sanaa City, Yemen. Despite the relatively high familiarity with VPTs, the actual use of these techniques, particularly BOPT, remains limited due to the perceived challenges and complexities of these approaches. The findings underscore the need for targeted educational interventions and further research to enhance the adoption and effective use of VPTs in clinical practice. By addressing these gaps, the dental community can ensure that all practitioners are equipped to provide the highest standard of care, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
