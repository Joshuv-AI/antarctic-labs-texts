Systematic review and meta-analysis of outcomes associated with incisional and organ/space surgical site infections in abdominal surgery patients
Authors: Shalini Elangovan, Shravya Murali, Amudha Aravindhan, Choon Sheong Seow, Nicholas Graves
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical site infections (SSI) are associated with worse healthcare outcomes and increased costs. Recent evidence suggests dissimilar outcomes for SSI subtypes. Abdominal surgery has the largest SSI incidence compared to other surgeries. However, outcomes are often aggregated, masking potential impact differences. This systematic review is the first to separately summarise outcomes associated with incisional and organ/space SSI in abdominal surgery.Materials and methodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and CINAHL was conducted for studies published between 1992 and 5th December 2024. Outcomes of interest were excess hospital length of stay (LOS), mortality risk, and proportions of readmitted and repeat surgery patients. Studies reporting at least one outcome for incisional or organ/space SSI in abdominal surgeries for adults were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for observational studies and randomized controlled trials respectively.ResultsTwenty studies were included, pooling 23,567 patients. Five reported outcomes for incisional, one for organ/space and 14 studies for both SSI subtypes. Patients with incisional SSI had an average excess LOS of 5.02 days (3.04–7.01), while organ/space SSI patients had a longer excess LOS of 14.40 days (10.11–18.69). Incisional SSI were associated with weakly increased mortality risk (1.62, 0.74–2.50), while organ/space SSI had increased mortality risk (2.75, 2.56–3.54). Proportions of readmissions were 16% (5–27%) for incisional and 37% (5–69%) for organ/space SSI. Proportions of patients who undergo repeat surgery were 4% (0– 9%) for incisional and 9% (4–14%) for organ/space SSI.ConclusionThis study revealed a significant burden of SSI, particularly organ/space SSI, which are associated with worse overall outcomes. Infection prevention and control strategies to reduce the impact and cost of SSI might be improved, with a need for targeted efforts against organ/space SSI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01656-w.

Background

Surgical site infections (SSI) occur when incisions at the surgical site become infected within 30 postoperative days. SSI are the most common postoperative complication, affecting approximately 3% to 20% of all surgeries depending on the surgical site, severity, and associated risk factors [1–3]. Patients with SSI suffer from overall worse health outcomes and increased costs, including higher mortality risks and longer hospital stays [4, 5]. Higher inpatient ward, treatment and surgery costs coupled with higher dissatisfaction with surgery results were also reported in elective laparotomy patients with SSI compared to non-SSI patients [6].

SSI are categorised into 3 types according to the depth of infection and anatomical structures affected by the infection [7]. Superficial incisional SSI involves an infection of the skin and subcutaneous tissue at the incision while deep incisional SSI involves muscle or fascial tissues at the incision [8]. Organ or space SSI refers to infection of any non-incisional anatomical structure involved in the surgery that occurs deeper than the muscular and fascial layer [8] including intra-abdominal abscesses, anastomotic leaks, or fistulas [9]. Following the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)/National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) definitions, superficial and deep incisional SSIs were combined as incisional SSI due to similarities in anatomic location and tissue involvement [10, 11].

SSI subtypes have been shown to be associated with different outcomes in some surgical fields [12, 13]. In abdominal surgery, which is associated with the highest SSI rates and the highest mortality than other surgery types [14, 15], risk factors are known to differ between incisional and organ/space SSI [16, 17] and organ/space SSI are associated with worse outcomes than incisional SSI [18]. Despite these distinctions, outcomes for SSI are generally aggregated for all types and the impact of incisional and organ/space SSIs as individual subtypes has not been described [19, 20]. This systematic review therefore aims to quantify and compare the length of excess postoperative hospital stay, mortality risk, and rates of hospital readmission and repeat surgery for both incisional SSI and organ/space SSI in adult abdominal surgery patients.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This review was conducted and reported according to both PRISMA and AMSTAR guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024599387) [21, 22]. A search was conducted of Pubmed, Embase, and CINAHL databases of studies reporting incisional or organ/space SSI outcomes in adult abdominal surgery from 1992 to 5th December 2024. Procedures included were abdominal, gastrointestinal, hernia, liver, small bowel, endocrine, rectal and colorectal surgeries.

The search strategy was restricted to English language studies. Prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case control studies, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included to maximize data availability of outcomes, regardless of study methodology. Studies reporting at least one SSI outcome of interest stratified into superficial or deep incisional SSI and organ/space SSI were included. SSI outcomes of interest included counts of 30-day all-cause mortality, postoperative hospital length of stays, counts of readmission and repeat surgery occurrences in the SSI population. Studies reporting combined SSI outcomes or studies where deep and organ/space SSI were combined were excluded. Studies without an appropriate control group for mortality and studies reporting 90-day mortality rates were disregarded.

Three independent reviewers (S.E., S.M. and A.A.) conducted abstract and full-test reviews using Rayyan [23]. Where decisions were not unanimous, it was resolved by discussion and consensus between the 3 reviewers.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed by three independent reviewers (S.E., S.M. and A.A.). The following data were extracted: year of publication, country in which study was conducted, duration, clinical setting, patient type, surgical procedure, surgical urgency, surgical approach, study design, reported SSI type, SSI definition source, sample size, outcomes of interest reported, follow-up duration, analytical approach, most common causative microorganisms and source of funding (Table 1).

Risk of Bias Assessment

The Newcastle Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2) tool were adapted for use for non-randomised studies and RCTs respectively [24, 25]. Domains of cohort and case-control studies evaluated included the cohort, cases, and controls selection methods, comparability of cohorts and efforts to control for confounders, as well outcome and exposure assessment methods. Each study was assigned a score out of a maximum of 9 stars. Studies with 5 stars or less were considered to have a high risk of bias, studies with 6 or 7 stars were considered to medium risk of bias and studies with 8 or 9 stars were considered to have a low risk of bias. For RCTs, the domains contributing to risk of bias assessed included the randomization process, participant and caregiver awareness of assigned intervention, data completeness, and outcome measurement methods.

Studies with low risk of bias in all domains were considered to have a low risk of bias, studies with at least one domain with some concerns is considered to have medium risk of bias and studies with at least one domain with high risk is considered to have a high risk of bias. Funnel plots were used to assess publication bias.

Data analysis

Incisional and organ/space SSI were defined by widely-used guidelines published by Horan [26]. Incisional SSI included superficial and deep incisional SSI, both of which do not affect organ tissue in the abdominal cavity. Combining superficial and deep incisional SSI allows us to capture differences in abdominal structures involved and treatment strategies between incisional and organ/space SSI, with organ/space SSI typically requiring more intensive medical management [27, 28].

Outcomes analysed included attributable excess hospital length of stay in days, mortality measured as log odds ratio, and proportions of hospital readmission and repeat surgery. A meta-analysis was conducted using the metafor package in R. Pooled estimates were reported separately for incisional and organ/space SSI and are reported as the estimate with 95% confidence interval in brackets. Where standard deviation for length of stay was not directly reported, it was derived using recommended methods [29–31]. Trends of outcomes for each SSI type over time were assessed. Scatter plots with trendlines were generated using the ggplot2 package in R and a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) regression model was used to model the underlying trend for each SSI type without assuming linearity [32, 33]. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and a random-effects meta-analysis model was used to account for high heterogeneity. Meta-regression analyses were conducted for outcomes with more than ten included studies to assess the relationship between SSI subtype and outcomes using random-effects models fitted using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method [34]. Bubble plots were generated to visualize associations, with point sizes reflecting inverse-variance weights and age included as a continuous covariate.

For mortality estimates, studies reporting single and double-zero mortality events were analysed using sensitivity analyses and Bayesian analyses using the rjags package as an alternative method to exclusion instead of fixed or non-fixed continuity corrections which are reported to produce biased odds ratio estimates [35–38]. The Bayesian meta-analysis modelled likelihood of death in the SSI and control group using a Binomial distribution and used a logit link function to transform probabilities of mortality to the log-odds scale. Uninformative priors were assigned from the Normal distribution centred at 0 and with low variance of 0.001 to allow study data to inform posterior estimates. The log-odds ratio was then derived for incisional SSI and organ/space SSI. Posterior distributions were estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling with 500,000 iterations using 3 chains. Autocorrelation in the MCMC chains was reduced using thinning, every 5th iteration was kept. Convergence was assessed using trace plots and the Gelman-Rubin diagnostic test [39].

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The search yielded a total of 3122 papers, after excluding 431 duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 873 papers underwent full-text screening. Of these, 20 studies were included (Fig. 1).

Five papers reported outcomes for incisional SSI only [40–44], one for organ/space only [45], and 14 papers for both SSI [10, 16, 46–57] (Table 1). Outcomes of 1541 patients with incisional SSI, 1435 with organ/space SSI, and 20,591 non-SSI patients were pooled. The most common countries of origin were the United States [10, 16, 41, 47, 53, 58, 59], Japan [44, 49, 51, 54, 55], and Spain [40, 48, 56]. Only one study was from a non-high income country, Thailand [43]. Colorectal patients were the most common population of interest, followed by hepatobiliary and pancreatic patients.

All but 6 studies used CDC definitions of SSI, with 1 study using definitions from the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP) [58] while 5 did not provide distinct definition sources. Most studies were focused on elective surgeries or both elective and emergent, with only one focusing solely on emergent surgeries [42] and four studies not reporting the type of surgery of interest. Most studies reported outcomes for both laparoscopic and open surgeries, with only 2 reporting outcomes for solely laparoscopic surgeries [49, 51], 1 focusing on open surgeries [45] and the remaining 5 not reporting the surgical approach of interest.

Risk of bias assessment

Seventeen non-randomised studies were assessed using the NOS scale (Supplementary Fig. 1) [10, 16, 42–45, 47–58]. Overall, study quality was high, with 12 studies scoring at least 7 out of 9 stars. Most studies received 7 stars [10, 16, 43, 45, 49, 50, 54, 58], while two scored 8 [48, 53] and another two scored 9 [52, 56]. Only two cohort studies scored 5 stars [55, 56] and two scored 6 [47, 57], primarily due to limitations in outcome assessment and lack of representativeness of the general population in the cohort. The single case-control study scored 5 out of 9 stars, as it provided limited detail on the definition and selection process of controls [42]. Two RCTs were assessed using the RoB 2 tool [40, 41] and both had some concerns in only one domain and a low risk of bias in all other domains. Funnel plots suggest possible publication bias for studies authored by Nanashima [55], which reported mortality outcomes for organ/space SSI, and Mazmudar [53] which reported readmission outcomes in organ/space SSI (Supplementary Fig. 2), but was found to be negligible following a post hoc trim-and-fill analysis [60]. Initial meta-analyses included all studies, followed by a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of high risk of bias studies.

Meta-analysis

Hospital LOS

Fifteen studies reported excess hospital LOS outcomes for incisional SSI patients [10, 16, 40, 42–45, 47–51, 54–56, 58] but 2 studies failed to report sufficient metrics to derive standard deviations and had to be excluded from the meta-analysis [10, 16]. Patients with incisional SSI spend on average an additional 5.02 (95% CI: 3.04, 7.01) days in hospital compared to non-infected patients (Fig. 2a). Twelve studies reported LOS outcomes for organ/space SSI [10, 16, 45, 47–51, 54–56, 58]. Patients with organ/space SSI spent on average 14.40 (95% CI: 10.11, 18.69) more days in hospital than non-infected patients (Fig. 2b). Colorectal studies and studies with large standard deviations accounted for high heterogeneity in both incisional and organ/space SSI groups, with I2 values of 94.0% and 93.2% respectively. Heterogeneity of the incisional colorectal subgroup reduced from 95.8% to a moderate 68.9% following the exclusion of a single outlier study with a small mean difference and large variance [43]. However, overall I² remained above 90% despite a leave-one-out analysis due to substantial variability in effect sizes and standard deviations across studies, combined with large weighting imbalances from differing sample sizes.

Mortality

Ten studies reported mortality outcomes for incisional SSI. A sensitivity analysis was conducted excluding five studies which reported zero mortality events in either the SSI or control populations [41, 51, 54, 55, 57]. The sensitivity analysis reported a log odds ratio of 1.62 (95% CI: 0.74, 2.50), suggesting a weak association between incisional SSI and increased risk of death (Fig. 3a). Seven studies reported mortality outcomes associated with organ/space SSI, and the sensitivity analysis excluded 3 studies with zero mortality events [51, 54, 57]. Patients with organ/space SSI are associated with a mortality log odds ratio of 2.75 (95% CI 1.31, 4.18) compared to non-infected patients (Fig. 3b). There was no observed heterogeneity for incisional SSI (I2 = 0%), suggesting that effect size variability is due to random sampling error, but high heterogeneity for organ/space SSI (I2 = 85.5%) which persisted despite leave-one-out sensitivity analyses. The substantial heterogeneity observed is likely due to large variation in effect and sample sizes [16, 48], wide confidence intervals [55], and few mortality events.

A Bayesian random-effects model was also used to estimate the association between mortality and SSI, to similar results. Incisional SSI had a log odds ratio of 1.01 (95% CI 0.07, 1.70) and organ/space SSI had a log odds ratio of 3.07 (95% CI 2.56, 3.54) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The Gelman-Rubin R-hat values and multivariate potential scale reduction factors (PSRF) were equal to 1.00 for all groups, indicating no evidence of lack of model convergence and well-mixed Markov chains with high agreement.

Readmission

Ten studies reported proportions of readmitted incisional SSI patients. The proportion of incisional SSI patients readmitted to hospital was 16% (95% CI 5, 27) (Fig. 4a). Seven studies reported proportions of readmitted for organ/space SSI patients and the pooled estimate for proportion of readmitted patients was 37% (95% CI 5, 69) (Fig. 4b). There was high heterogeneity for both incisional (I2 = 95.7%) and organ/space SSI (I2 = 99.3%), which dropped to 41.8% and 75.8% respectively when studies with outlier sample and effect sizes were excluded [41, 46, 51, 53].

Repeat surgery

Three studies reported proportion of incisional SSI patients who underwent repeat surgery. The proportion of patients who underwent repeat surgery was 4% (95% CI 0, 9) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Only two studies reported proportion of organ/space SSI patients who underwent repeat surgery and the proportion of patients who underwent repeat surgery was 9% (95% CI 4, 14). There was moderate heterogeneity for incisional SSI (I2 = 50.2%) and no observed heterogeneity for organ/space SSI (I2 = 0%).

Sensitivity analysis for risk of bias and study design

A sensitivity analysis was conducted where studies assessed to be at high risk of bias, scoring 5 or 6 stars on the NOS and assessed to be ‘High Risk of Bias’ on the RoB 2 were excluded from the meta-analysis. Six studies were therefore excluded [42, 44, 47, 51, 55, 57] and meta-analysis was carried for studies with low risk of bias only. Estimates of LOS for both incisional and organ/space SSI and the risk of mortality for incisional SSI decreased (Supplementary Fig. 5). However, mortality risk for organ/space SSI, proportion of readmission for both incisional and organ/space SSI, and proportion of repeat surgery for incisional SSI studies were all marginally increased. There were no studies with a high risk of bias among the studies reporting repeat surgery outcomes for organ/space SSI studies. A separate sensitivity analysis was conducted to exclude RCT studies from the meta-analysis. One study was removed from the LOS meta-analysis for incisional SSI [40], resulting in a pooled estimate of 5.21 (95% CI 3.11, 7.32) for non-randomised studies only. Similarly, a meta-analysis of readmission proportions found that the pooled estimate for RCTs only was 18% (95% CI -6, 42) [40, 41], and 16% (95% CI 4, 28) for non-randomized studies only. All other outcome measures did not include RCTs.

Meta-regression

There were insufficient studies to inform strong meta-regression models for all outcomes, hence mixed-effects models were fit only for LOS and mortality. The estimated mean LOS for incisional SSIs was 9.68 days (95% CI 6.55, 12.80; p < 0.001). Compared to incisional SSIs, organ/space SSIs were associated with an additional 7.15 days of hospital stay, although this difference was not statistically significant (95% CI − 2.21, 16.51; p = 0.1345). The model explained a substantial proportion of the variance in LOS (R2 = 78.96%) and included 19 studies.

Incisional SSI were not found to be statistically significant with an estimated risk of 0.67% (95% CI − 0.98 to 2.31; p = 0.4252). In contrast, organ/space SSI were statistically significant with an estimated risk of 5.27% (95% CI 0.0236, 0.0818; p = 0.0004), indicating a higher rate of mortality compared to incisional SSI. The model explained 52.15% of between-study heterogeneity and included 12 studies. These findings suggest that organ/space SSIs are associated with significantly worse mortality than incisional SSIs (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, due to the limited number of studies, these associations are not robust and should be interpreted with caution.

Trend of LOS, mortality, and readmission

From 2012 to 2023, hospital length of stay (LOS) for incisional SSI has shown a downward trend, whereas no clear trend is observed for organ/space SSI. Similarly, mortality rates and readmission proportions do not exhibit a distinct pattern over time for either SSI subtype. However, organ/space SSI consistently trends higher than incisional SSI across all outcomes, reinforcing its association with worse clinical consequences and supporting our findings (Supplementary Fig. 7). We recommend interpreting these findings with caution, as the trendline is based on a limited number of studies.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis conducted to summarise SSI impact, stratified by SSI type. Previous systematic reviews have focused on SSI incidence or the impact of interventions against SSI [61–63]. This study summarises the clinical and economic outcomes of incisional and organ/space SSI in abdominal surgery and reports longer LOS and worse mortality associated with both incisional and organ/space SSI compared to non-SSI patients (Supplementary Fig. 8). Additionally, organ/space SSI are associated with worse clinical outcomes compared to incisional SSI (Supplementary Fig. 9) and a longer postoperative hospital stay of 14 days compared to 5 days for incisional SSI. A previous study which quantified the costs associated with SSI in open surgical procedures in the United States reported an average incremental LOS of 9.3 days for pooled incisional and organ/space SSI, which falls between our reported numbers [64].

For the sensitivity analysis that excluded studies with single or double zero-events, we report an odds ratio of 2.75 for mortality risk associated with organ/space SSI, greater than the odds ratio of 1.62 associated with incisional SSI. Using Bayesian meta-analysis to account for studies with zero event, we report odds ratios of 3.07 and 1.01 for organ/space SSI and incisional SSI respectively. Although similar for organ/space SSI, the risk becomes negligible for incisional SSI when studies with zero events are estimated rather than excluded, suggesting that the impact of zero-event studies disproportionately reduces the estimated risk for incisional SSIs while having minimal influence on organ/space SSI odds ratios. We also found a higher mortality rate of 6.9% for organ/space SSI compared to 0.91% for incisional SSI and 0.31% for non-SSI patients, although the mortality risk estimates showed slight overlap in confidence intervals. Mortality attributable to SSI in general has been reported elsewhere to be between 1 and 3% [65, 66], although a study which pooled over a million ACS-NSQIP cases from 719 sites reported a significantly larger mortality rate of 34% for 16,751 patients with organ/space SSI [67]. Organ/space SSI may be less visibly apparent than incisional SSI, and patients with organ/space SSI generally present with systemic inflammatory and infectious symptoms, with the absence of infection at surgical incisions [8, 68]. Together with the need for more sophisticated diagnostic methods to confirm the presence of an organ/space SSI, there could be delayed diagnosis and treatment or organ/space SSI, especially if the infection is less severe, leading to higher mortality rates [9].

Readmission rates were greater for organ/space SSI patients at 37% compared to 16% for incisional SSI. This is proportionally similar to a previous study which reported a readmission rate of 92% for organ/space SSI, compared to 31% for superficial incisional SSI in general surgical procedures [69]. Readmission rates serve as quality indicators for hospitals as readmissions contribute significantly to healthcare costs, estimated by one meta-analysis to be approximately USD$16,000 per readmission, pooled across 5 distinct clinical conditions [70]. Readmittance within 30 days has also been associated with increased mortality rates and lower long-term survival probabilities [71, 72]. Finally, we report higher repeat surgery rates of 9% for organ/space SSI compared to 4% for incisional SSI. However, the number of included studies was limited (three for incisional SSI, two for organ/space SSI), and these findings should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the confidence intervals for both readmission and repeat surgery pooled estimates overlapped, further supporting cautious interpretation of these results. In abdominal surgery, the indication for repeat surgery is often unrelated to SSI and instead due to conditions such as perforated viscus, mesenteric ischemia, or bowel obstruction [73]. Conversely, repeat surgeries themselves contribute to an increased risk of organ/space SSI, further complicating patient outcomes [74].

There are useful insights from this study. Although SSI-related studies tend to aggregate findings across subtypes, recent evidence has suggested dissimilarities between different SSI types, which is demonstrated in this review [1, 67]. Findings from our study provide a unique overview of adverse outcomes associated with separate SSI types in abdominal surgery and emphasize the differences in severity between incisional and organ/space SSI. By quantifying the attributable health effects of SSI in terms of mortality risk and readmission and repeat surgery rates, and its financial burden as additional hospital length of stay, we provide reliable estimates of the costs and effectiveness outcomes associated with SSI, stratified by type and severity. These estimates are valuable for informing cost-effectiveness models of interventions targeting SSI and enable differentiation between SSI types within the model.

Importantly, the study indicates that organ/space SSI are associated with a greater burden of adverse outcomes compared to incisional SSI. Targeted intervention strategies against organ/space SSIs may provide substantial economic and clinical benefits given their higher rates of morbidity, longer hospital stays, and increased likelihood of readmission or repeat surgery. For example, perioperative bundles involving the use of oral antibiotics, mechanical bowel preparation and surgical gown usage have been found to be effective in reducing rates of organ/space SSI [75, 76], although it is recommended to limit the complexity of bundles for higher compliance rates [77].

All studies were from high-income countries (HICs) with the exception of two, one which utilised data from 44 countries [42] and one from Thailand, an upper middle income country [43] which may cause pooled estimates in this study to be primarily reflective of outcomes typical of high-resource settings, where SSI rates are lower and length of stays are shorter [78]. The scarcity of studies from low-income countries (LMICs) may be in small part due to differences in SSI subtype categorization. However, the overall lack of SSI-related data from LMICs remains a recognized challenge, with ongoing efforts to address it [79]. SSI are the most common healthcare acquired infection in LMICs and unlike in high-income countries, their rates have not declined [78]. Furthermore, the costs of SSI in LMIC are similar to Europe, although attributable mortality is much higher in LMICs [80]. Despite carrying a disproportionately high burden of SSIs compared to HICs, LMICs face gaps in infection prevention and control programs, as well as a lack of targeted research, as highlighted in our study. Proposed reasons for this include limited funding, a shortage of trained healthcare professionals, and inadequate infection protection and surveillance programmes, which contributes to the true impact of SSIs in LMICs remaining poorly documented [78, 81]. Our findings emphasize the urgent need for reliable studies on SSI outcomes to inform and support the implementation of effective infection control measures in these settings.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, infections were categorized by the involvement of incisional or non-incisional tissue. This led to the exclusion of some studies where SSI types were aggregated differently. However, the classification strategy aligned with studies that used a similar categorization, allowing for meaningful data aggregation. As the meta-analysis only included studies which separated outcomes based on SSI types, a pooled estimate of both subtypes was unavailable. Secondly, since the search strategy was not designed to identify or include studies that reported pooled outcomes for all SSI, pooling estimates across subtypes in our study would not have produced reliable results due to selection bias, as all relevant studies would not have been captured by the search. Lastly, there was high heterogeneity in some meta-analyses, which was reduced in most cases when sensitivity analyses were conducted. Clinical characteristics, for example studies reporting only laparoscopic surgeries, were not found to contribute substantially possibly contributing to the observed heterogeneity [82, 83]. Instead, the high heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis is more likely attributable to substantial differences in population sizes across studies, which in turn give rise to large standard deviations and imbalances in study weighting. Most studies in this meta-analysis are observational in nature, with participants enrolled opportunistically and in a nonrandomized fashion, resulting in varying sample distribution and high between-study variability. Other sources of heterogeneity include differences in surgical procedure type, surgical approach, urgency of operation, and differences in perioperative infection control practices across the 30-year study period. Given the substantial heterogeneity observed, the use of a random-effects model indicates that the pooled estimates reflect average effects across the populations represented by the included studies, and cannot be generalised as precise estimates applicable to any single setting [84].

Conclusion

Incisional SSI and organ/space SSI increase postoperative hospital LOS and mortality risks and contribute greatly to readmission and repeat surgery rates in abdominal surgery. Our findings highlight the need for strengthened SSI prevention and control strategies and provide usable estimates of health and cost outcomes for different SSI subtypes. The results from this study can support the optimisation of future infection prevention strategies and enable cost-effectiveness models to be developed with greater accuracy and stratified understanding of the distinct clinical and economic impact of different SSI subtypes. Although both SSI types are shown to contribute to worse clinical outcomes, investing in infection prevention strategies targeted at reducing organ/space SSI could yield the greatest clinical and economic benefits.
