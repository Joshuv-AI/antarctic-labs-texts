The time has come to reconsider the quantitative sugar guidelines and related policies
Authors: Jimmy Chun Yu Louie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This commentary critiques current sugar intake guidelines, suggesting they oversimplify a complex issue and may lead to unintended outcomes. It advocates for reducing sugar-sweetened beverages, promoting healthy dietary patterns, and recognizing the diverse roles of sugars in foods. The piece calls for a more practical and sustainable approach to public health messaging, aiming for more effective dietary strategies that reflect the complexity of nutritional needs and food sources.

In recent years, public health discourse has increasingly focused on added and free sugars, with widespread warnings about their potential dangers. Health organizations globally have responded by issuing guidelines that recommend strict limits on sugar intake (Table 1)1–9. A critical, often overlooked aspect of this debate that the same sugar (e.g., glucose) found naturally in foods and as added/free sugars is chemically identical. Total sugar is the sum of both naturally occurring sugars - like those in fruits and vegetables - and added/free sugars, which are introduced during processing or found in products like syrups, and fruit juices (including their concentrates, which are considered free sugars but not added sugars)9,10. Since they are chemically indistinguishable, no lab test can tell the difference between naturally occurring sugars (like those in an apple) and added/free sugars (like high-fructose corn syrup)10. Our bodies process them in exactly the same way11, which creates significant challenges for both research and policy implementation.

While methods for estimating added or free sugar content in foods have been developed, including some leveraging artificial intelligence, as well as biomarkers, they are not infallible10. These approaches often rely on subject expertise or the availability of gold standard training data, both of which can be difficult to obtain; and the biomarkers in development has their own significant drawbacks12,13. The recent introduction of added sugar labelling on US Nutrition Facts panels is a step forward, but this information remains unavailable in many other countries.

These complexities underscore the urgent need for a critical re-examination of our approach to sugar guidelines in public health messaging. We must strive to reflect the nuanced state of scientific evidence and address the practical challenges of implementation. This commentary aims to explore these issues and advocate for a more refined approach to sugar guidelines and policies that aligns more closely with current scientific understanding and practical realities.

Current sugar guidelines and policies: a broad-brush approach

Several major health organizations have issued guidelines on added/free sugar intake (Table 1). Some countries, such as Japan14,15, have not set specific quantitative limits but instead provide general advice to reduce sugar intake, while the European Union concluded there was insufficient evidence to set an upper limit for sugar consumption4. In contrast, the most cited guidelines from WHO recommends limiting free sugar intake to less than 10% of total energy intake, with a further reduction to below 5% for additional health benefits9. Many countries, including the United States8, Canada2, Australia3, and the Nordic countries5, have aligned with WHO’s primary recommendation of less than 10% of total energy intake.

However, some nations have adopted stricter stances. The United Kingdom recommends no more than 5% of total dietary energy from free sugars for adults and children over 2 years1. India has gone even further, advising less than 5% of total caloric intake from sugar, and notably recommending 0% for children under 2 years old7. China provides specific quantitative limits, recommending less than 50 g of sugar per day, with an ideal target of less than 25 g6.

These diverse guidelines have inspired various policies worldwide, often adopting a broad-brush approach to sugar reduction. However, such approach may oversimplify a complex nutritional issue. A key problem lies in treating all sources of added/free sugars as having equivalent health effects, despite evidence suggesting otherwise11. This has led to policies such as reformulation targets for a variety of foods, e.g., ref. 16, and popular opinion that sugar taxes should cover a broader range of unhealthy foods17. However, these policies often fail to distinguish between sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), consistently linked to adverse health outcomes18, and solid foods containing added/free sugars, where evidence is less conclusive18. They also overlook the functional roles of sugars in foods beyond sweetness19.

The evidence: not as sweet as it seems

While these guidelines and policies may seem straightforward, the scientific evidence supporting specific added/free sugar intake limits is less clear-cut than commonly assumed. The WHO guidelines9, often cited as authoritative, classify the evidence for their 10% recommendation as “moderate” quality. The evidence for the conditional 5% recommendation is considered “very low” quality. These classifications indicate significant uncertainty in the research findings. A key misunderstanding has persisted in public health discussions regarding these guidelines. They are frequently cited as relating to lower risks of both overweight/obesity and dental caries, e.g., ref. 20. However, in the original report accompanying the guideline release, the WHO itself stated that the 10% quantitative target was only supported by evidence related to dental caries risk, not overweight and obesity9. This miscommunication has gone largely unchallenged in subsequent years, with few questioning the evidence behind these quantitative targets since their introduction.

Much of our understanding of sugar’s metabolic effects comes from animal studies, but these often employ sugar intakes (e.g., 60% energy intake) far beyond typical human consumption (~15–20% energy intake)11. While these animal studies offer insights into biological mechanisms, their relevance to human health at realistic intake levels is questionable. A recent lifelong mouse study21 illustrates this point: consuming 25% of energy from sucrose (at the higher end of typical human intake) showed no significant effects on body composition, blood sugar, cholesterol, or lifespan compared to mice consuming 10% of energy from sucrose (the WHO recommended limit).

Human studies have yielded mixed results. While studies found weight gain when participants added SSBs to their diet, this does not necessarily reflect the impact of sugar within a calorie-controlled, balanced diet. In fact, a meta-analysis by Khan and Sievenpiper22 found no significant effect on body weight when sugar was swapped for other carbohydrates while maintaining constant total calorie intake.

Large-scale observational studies which form most of the evidence have found associations between higher added/free sugar intake from SSBs and increased risk of obesity, diabetes, and heart disease11. However, they cannot prove causation, as people consuming more added/free sugar may have other dietary and lifestyle habits influencing their health risks that could not be completely accounted for in statistical modelling18. Many also rely on self-reported dietary intake, which is often inaccurate due to memory lapses, misjudgement of portion sizes, and social desirability bias, where people underreport unhealthy foods and overreport healthier options23,24. This may lead to incomplete or distorted records of actual intake, affecting the validity of the findings.

Limitations of current guidelines

Current quantitative added/free sugar intake guidelines, while well-intentioned, have several significant limitations that warrant careful consideration. These guidelines often oversimplify a complex nutritional issue by focusing solely on a specific percentage of calories from added/free sugar, neglecting the broader context of overall dietary patterns and lifestyle factors. For example, a diet high in fast food that replaced SSBs with diet soft drinks could technically meet sugar guidelines while still being unhealthy overall. Additionally, these guidelines fail to distinguish between different food sources of added/free sugar, despite evidence suggesting that SSBs were more consistently associated with negative health effects than solid foods containing added/free sugars18.

Practical implementation of these guidelines also poses challenges for the average consumer. Quantitative targets, such as limiting added/free sugar to less than 10% of total calories, are difficult for most people to translate into everyday food choices. Most individuals lack the knowledge or resources to calculate their added/free sugar intake accurately, and the necessary information is often not readily accessible even for health professionals.

Moreover, strict added/free sugar limits may lead to unintended consequences. There is a growing trend of increased use of non-nutritive sweeteners25, which come with their own potential health concerns26. Recent studies have found these sweeteners in unexpected food sources27, raising concerns about underestimating their dietary exposure in current risk assessment methods.

Very low added/free sugar intake recommendations, such as less than 5% of calories, may be overly restrictive and difficult to achieve while maintaining a varied, palatable diet28. This could potentially lead to reduced intake of some nutritious foods that contain small amounts of added/free sugars, such as flavoured yogurt or whole grain cereals. In our modern society, with busy lifestyles and limited time for food preparation, it is unrealistic to expect most individuals to consume only minimally processed foods with little or no added/free sugar, as some actively advocate for, e.g. refs. 29,30.

Lastly, it is important to recognize that sugar serves functional purposes in many foods beyond sweetness, including texture, preservation, and fermentation19. Reformulating products to remove sugar is not always straightforward and may require the use of other processed ingredients or food additives to replicate sugar’s functions19. Furthermore, in products where the sugars removed are not replaced with a non-caloric component such as water in the case of SSBs, the caloric content (per 100 g) of the “reformulated” version may indeed increase when the product contains a considerable amount of fat, as the other caloric nutrients simply “concentrate” when sugars are removed (Fig. 1). On the other hand, although most packaged foods targeted for sugar reformulation by public health advocates were already classified as NOVA-4 ultraprocessed foods30, in non-ultraprocessed products where sugars serve only as a sweetening agent, such as canned fruits in syrups, replacing sugars with artificial sweeteners will push them into the ultraprocessed category31. The bigger picture: sugar in context

When considering the role of added/free sugars in our diet, it is crucial to view them within the broader context of overall dietary patterns and lifestyle factors. Most added/free sugars in typical diets come from discretionary foods and beverages that are low in essential nutrients. In Australia, for instance, nearly half of added sugar intake is derived from just SSBs, sugars and sweet spreads, and cakes, biscuits, pastries and batter-based products combined32. This suggests that focusing on reducing consumption of these specific sources, rather than targeting all added/free sugars indiscriminately, as implied in the current quantitative guidelines and sugar reduction policies, may yield more significant health benefits.

It is also important to recognize that added/free sugars can serve a purpose in making nutrient-dense foods more palatable33, potentially increasing the consumption of high fibre wholegrain and essential vitamins and minerals. It is idealistic yet naïve to expect most people would choose high-fibre products with little to no added sugars over a more palatable alternative.

Consumption patterns also play a crucial role. Most added/free sugar intake often occurs during snacks and non-main meal occasions34. Therefore, addressing overall eating patterns may prove more effective than solely focusing on added/free sugar content. Moreover, other dietary factors, such as overall calorie intake and consumption of whole plant foods, likely have a more significant impact on health than added/free sugar intake alone. The Mediterranean diet, widely associated with numerous health benefits and does not explicitly limit added sugars but instead emphasizes whole, minimally processed foods, serves as an excellent example35.

A more nuanced approach

Given the limitations of current evidence and the quantitative guidelines, a more nuanced approach to sugar recommendations is warranted. This approach should prioritize reducing intake of SSBs, which have the most substantial evidence for adverse health effects11. Furthermore, rather than focusing on specific nutrient targets, it is more effective to promote overall healthy dietary patterns, such as the DASH or Mediterranean diets, which have strong evidence for health benefits without requiring strict sugar counting. Foods should be evaluated based on their overall nutritional profile, not just added/free sugar content. For example, a yogurt with some added sugar may still be a healthy choice due to its protein, calcium, and probiotic content. It is important to offer simple, actionable strategies for reducing added sugars without resorting to artificial sweeteners or highly processed “low-sugar” alternatives that results from some of the current sugar reduction policies/strategies. These could include practical tips like choosing water over SSBs or gradually reducing sugar in coffee or tea.

A balanced message that acknowledges some added/free sugars can be part of a healthy diet, especially from nutrient-dense foods, is likely to be more sustainable and less prone to promoting extreme “sugar-free” diets. Ongoing research should be supported to better understand the health effects of different types and sources of added/free sugars within the context of overall dietary patterns, including studying the long-term effects of sugar substitutes and alternative sweeteners.

Conclusion

While reducing excessive added/free sugar intake remains an important public health goal, current guidelines oversimplify a complex nutritional issue. The evidence linking specific added/free sugar intake levels to health outcomes is less definitive than often portrayed in public messaging. A more effective approach should focus on overall dietary quality, emphasizing whole foods and providing practical strategies for reducing intake of SSBs and low-nutrient, high-sugar foods. This nuanced approach, rather than strict quantitative targets, is likely to be more achievable and beneficial for long-term public health. As with many aspects of nutrition, the reality is more complex than simple “good” vs “bad” labels.
