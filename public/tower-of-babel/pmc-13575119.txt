Interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 controls chemoresistance and immuno-killing in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Sabrina Digiovanni, Claudia Giannotta, Luca Alessandri, Simona Fontana, Angelo Meoli, Paolo Bironzo, Alessandra Merlini, Gabriella Doronzo, Parab Sushant, Elisa Balmas, Muhlis Akman, Joanna Kopecka, Maddalena Arigoni, Francesca Napoli, Julia Wens, Samuele Tarallo, Alessandro Bertero, Stefano Guglielmo, Barbara Rolando, Davide Palma, Luisella Righi, Massimo Massaia, Raffaele Calogero, Federico Bussolino, Silvia Novello, Giorgio Vittorio Scagliotti, Iris Chiara Salaroglio, Chiara Riganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) shows partial chemoimmunoresistance, driven by the upregulation of the drug efflux transporters ABCB1 and ABCC1 and the downregulation of the immunosensitizer protein ABCA1. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this chemoimmunoresistant phenotype, by performing a CRISPR-KO kinome screening, we identified interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1) as a key determinant of chemoimmunosensitive and chemoimmunoresistant phenotypes, defined as ABCB1/ABCC1lowABCA1high and ABCB1/ABCC1highABCA1low, respectively. IRAK1 expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort and in early-stage NSCLC patients. The IRAK1/ABCB1/ABCC1highABCA1low signature was predictive of poor response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy in advanced-stage patients. Mechanistically, IRAK1 silencing resensitized NSCLC cells to cisplatin by decreasing IL-1R/NFκB and IL-1R/AP-1 signaling, which in turn downregulated the drug efflux transporters ABCB1 and ABCC1. Concurrently, IRAK1 silencing activated the LXRα/ABCA1 axis, which enhanced Vγ9Vδ2 Tcell-mediated cytotoxicity and facilitated the recruitment of memory and effector CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in both ex vivo autologous cocultures and immunoxenografts, increasing the immuno-killing ability of tumor cells. Newly developed synthetic inhibitors of IRAK1 efficiently promoted chemo- and immunosensitization in immunoxenografts, resulting in good pharmacokinetic and safety profiles. These findings identify IRAK1 as a novel, druggable target that controls both chemoresistance and immune evasion in NSCLC. We suggest the pharmacological inhibition of IRAK1 as a future promising strategy to overcome resistance to chemotherapy and improve patient outcomes.Subject terms: Lung cancer, Tumour biomarkers, Target identification, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all lung cancers and is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. Among its subtypes, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) has a highly variable stage-dependent prognosis, ranging from a 5-year survival of 60–70% in early stages to less than 10% in metastatic cases.1,2 NSCLC treatment is multimodal. In patients with tumors characterized by driver mutations in EGFR or other druggable genes, target therapy is the first treatment option. In the remaining cases, neoadjuvant chemotherapy or immunotherapy followed by surgery is adopted in early stages. In advanced stages, chemoimmunotherapy, alone or associated with radiotherapy, is the current treatment scheme.1,2 The poor outcome still observed in advanced stages is caused by intrinsic or acquired drug resistance and immune evasion mechanisms.3 Several mechanisms are responsible for chemoresistance: low drug uptake, high drug efflux via ATP-binding cassette (ABC) membrane transporters, increased metabolic inactivation of chemotherapeutic drugs, increased drug sequestration within lysosomes followed by exocytosis, and mutations or amplification of the target of chemotherapeutic drugs that reduce their efficacy. One of the key mechanisms in NSCLC is the efflux of chemotherapeutic drugs by ABC transporters such as ABC subfamily B member 1 (ABCB1), the first drug efflux transporter discovered4,5 and ABC subfamily C member 1 (ABCC1). Both transporters, which are involved in the multidrug-resistant phenotype,6,7 bind and hydrolyze ATP, pumping chemotherapeutic drugs out of cells, against their concentration gradient. So doing, they reduce the intracellular accumulation of chemotherapeutic agents, including platinum-based agents used as first-line treatment in NSCLC.8

In addition to drug efflux transporters, the tumor immuno-microenvironment (TIME) is crucial for controlling chemoresistance. The intricate interplay among neoplastic cells, stromal cells, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), including CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, T-regulatory (Treg) cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, often skews the balance between immunosuppressive and antitumor activities toward a tumor-tolerant TIME.9 Moreover, it has been demonstrated that another member of the ABC transporter family, the ABC subfamily A member 1 (ABCA1), originally discovered as a transporter of cholesterol,10,11 is a modulator of the TIME. Besides cholesterol, ABCA1 effluxes isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP),12 an upstream metabolite of the biosynthetic pathway of cholesterol. IPP is an endogenous phosphoantigenic activator of Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes, an unconventional T cell subset endorsed of antitumor activity in solid and hematological tumors.12 ABCA1 is often downregulated in NSCLC, impairing the antitumor immune response of Vγ9Vδ2 T cells.13 Interestingly, aggressive NSCLC cells are characterized by high ABCB1 and ABCC1 levels, and low ABCA1 levels,13 displaying a simultaneous resistance to chemotherapy and immuno-killing.

Despite multiple attempts, pharmacological inhibitors of ABC transporters have always failed, because of structural complexity, broad substrate specificity and essential physiological role of ABC proteins14: for these reasons, most ABC transporter inhibitors tested have displayed poor specificity and high systemic toxicity.

Hence, reversing chemoresistance and immunoresistance mediated by ABC transporters requires alternative approaches: one option is targeting upstream networks that regulate ABC transporters. The expression, trafficking, stability and activity of ABC transporters are regulated by multiple kinases conserved across species.15,16 Among these kinases, some oncogenic drivers in NSCLC, such as EGFR/ERK1, EGFR/MAPK1, EGFR/PI3K/Akt, and VEGFR/Flk1/Src,17 can be druggable by small-molecule kinase inhibitors.18 However, the effects of these kinases on ABC transporter expression and activity, drug resistance and immunoresistance in NSCLC, are often controversial and depend on the kinase activated, on the cross-talk with other oncogenic pathways, and on the type of activating/inhibiting agent used.17 To investigate this intricate scenario, we applied a CRISPR-KO kinome library to the most chemoimmunoresistant human NSCLC cell line from a panel of 28 cell lines.13 We identified interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1) as a novel kinase associated with ABC transporter-mediated chemoresistance and immunoevasion. IRAK1 is a serine/threonine-protein kinase that initiates the innate immune response downstream of Toll-like receptor (TLR) and interleukin-1 receptor (IL1-R).19 When engaged by their respective ligands, the receptors recruit a protein complex, including MyD88 and IRAK2/3/4, termed the “Myddosome”. IRAK4 phosphorylates IRAK1 at Thr209; this step is required to induce IRAK1 autophosphorylation at Thr387, which in turn leads to the full activation of the kinase. By binding to tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor-activated factor 6 (TRAF6), IRAK1 activates NFκB, MAPK and interferon-dependent pathways, which are involved in inflammatory responses.20 IRAK1 has been implicated in chronic inflammatory processes underlying autoimmune and cardiovascular diseases, as well as in tumorigenesis.20 Previous findings reported that IRAK1 is deregulated in hematological21 and solid tumors.22,23 Moreover, IRAK1 activity has been linked to chemoresistance in breast cancer.22

The aim of this work was to investigate whether and how IRAK1 controls chemoresistance mediated by high levels of ABCB1 and ABCC1, and immune evasion mediated by low levels of ABCA1, in NSCLC. IRAK1 emerged as a new predictive biomarker and druggable target in chemoimmunoresistant NSCLCs.

Results

IRAK1 controls ABC transporter-mediated chemoresistance in NSCLC

As previously reported,13 human NSCLC cell lines present two distinct phenotypes, ABCB1/ABCC1highABCA1low and ABCB1/ABCC1lowABCA1high (Fig. 1a), with an inverse correlation between ABCB1/ABCC1 and ABCA1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). ABCB1/ABCC1highABCA1low cells had higher IC50 values for the ABCB1/ABCC1 substrates cisplatin (Pt) and docetaxel (DX) (Fig. 1b) and lower immuno-killing mediated by Vγ9Vδ2 T cells (Fig. 1c), indicating a chemoimmunoresistant phenotype. To identify the molecular drivers underlying this chemoresistance, we constructed a CRISPR-KO library targeting oncologically relevant kinases in the most chemoimmunoresistant cell line, NCI-H2228, to identify knockout (KO) clones that shifted from the ABCC1highABCA1low to the ABCC1lowABCA1high phenotype (high: ≥90% positive cells; low: ≤10% positive cells; Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 1d). The changes in kinases associated with the ABCA1high/ABCC1low phenotype were quantified by comparison of their log2-fold changes (Fig. 1e). Among the top 6 kinases significantly enriched in ABCA1high/ABCC1low cells (Table 1), we selected IRAK1 for its role in chemoresistance and inflammation24 as a potentially good candidate for understanding chemoimmunoresistance in NSCLC. We focused on the two most chemoimmunoresistant cells (ABCB1/ABCC1highABCA1low)—NCI-H2228 and Calu-3—and on the most chemoimmunosensitive (ABCB1/ABCC1lowABCA1high) NCI-H1650 cell line (Supplementary Fig. 3a). IRAK1 silencing (sh) in NCI-H2228 and Calu-3 cells decreased ABCB1 and ABCC1 expression and increased ABCA1 expression (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Figs. 3b, 4a, b), increased sensitivity to Pt and DX (Fig. 1g; Supplementary Fig. 4c, and Supplementary Table 1) and Vγ9Vδ2 T cell-mediated immuno-killing (Fig. 1h). In contrast, IRAK1 overexpression in the NCI-H1650 cell line produced the opposite effects (Fig. 1f–h, Supplementary Fig. 3b, and Supplementary Table 1). Overall, IRAK1 levels were directly correlated with ABCB1 and ABCC1 levels and inversely correlated with ABCA1 levels across the 28 NSCLC cell lines analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 5).

IRAK1 is a negative prognostic and predictive factor in NSCLC patients

To evaluate the prognostic significance of IRAK1, we analyzed the coexpression of IRAK1, ABCC1, and ABCA1 in the TCGA-LUAD dataset (n = 531; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-LUAD) and found that compared with IRAK1lowABCC1lowABCA1high patients, IRAK1highABCC1highABCA1low patients had worse overall survival (OS) (Fig. 2a). IRAK1 and ABCC1 were independent negative prognostic factors; conversely, ABCA1high patients had better OS, although the association was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). The transcriptomic profile of an internal cohort of treatment-naïve patients with early-stage NSCLC25 revealed that IRAK1 expression was higher in tumor tissue (n = 81) than in adjacent normal lung tissue (n = 48) and that IRAK1 clustered with ABCC1/ABCA1 (Fig. 2b), supporting the hypothesis of a molecular link between IRAK1 and these transporters. Next, we investigated whether IRAK1 had a clinical meaning in early-stage (n = 247)26 and advanced-stage (n = 77)27 patients followed at the home institution, with variable levels of intratumor IRAK1 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Advanced-stage patients received Pt-based chemotherapy (n = 36) or pembrolizumab-based immunotherapy (n = 41) (Supplementary Table 2). In both early-stage (Fig. 2c) and advanced-stage (Fig. 2d, e) patients, IRAK1high patients had the worst prognosis for OS and time to progression (TTP). In advanced-stage patients, IRAK1 levels were positively correlated with ABCB1/C1 levels and inversely correlated with ABCA1 levels before treatment (Fig. 2f). A significant direct correlation was obtained between IRAK1 and ABCB1/ABCC1 measured posttreatment in patients who experienced relapse (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Compared with the pretreatment levels, the level of IRAK1 increased or remained unchanged in all the patients reassessed after chemotherapy and in 11 out of the 13 patients reassessed after immunotherapy (Fig. 2g). The difference between pre- and posttreatment levels was not significant (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Persistently high IRAK1 levels after treatment were associated with worse OS (Fig. 2h). When patients were stratified according to the TTP as responsive (TTP value higher/equal to the median value) or resistant (TTP value lower than the median value) to treatment, we found that 63% of patients who were resistant to chemotherapy and 67% of patients who were resistant to immunotherapy had IRAK1high tumors (Supplementary Table 2).

IRAK1 regulates ABC transporters by inhibiting the NFκB and ERK/AP-1 pathways and activating LXRα

Next, we investigated the molecular mechanism linking IRAK1 to the expression of ABC transporters. Myddosome downstream IL-1R (MyD88/TRAF6) (Fig. 3a) was downregulated in shIRAK1 NCI-H2228 cells, except for IRAK2 and IRAK4, whose expression was unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 8a). IRAK1-downstream effectors, such as the IKK/NFκB and ERK1/2-AP-1 axes, which are known to upregulate ABCB1 and ABCC124 expression, were decreased. By decreasing IKKγ and active p(Ser176/180)IKKα/β and increasing p(Ser32)IkB (Fig. 3b), IRAK1 silencing impaired nuclear translocation (Fig. 3c), activity (Fig. 3d) and binding of p50/p65 NFκB at ABCB1/C1 promoters (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Moreover, IRAK1 silencing reduced ERK1/2 phosphorylation (Fig. 3e), AP-1 activity (Fig. 3f) and binding at ABCB1/C1 promoters (Supplementary Fig. 8b), resulting in the same effects of the NFκB/AP-1 inhibitor SN50 in NCI-H2228 wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c), and decreased ABCB1/ABCC1 mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. 9d). In parallel, IRAK1 silencing increased the expression of LXRα, a transcriptional inducer of ABCA1,28 and ABCA1 (Fig. 3g) by promoting LXRα binding to the ABCA1 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 8b), similar to the effects of the LXRα agonist T0901317 in wild-type cells (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f).

Since ABCA1 facilitates IPP-dependent activation of Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes,12 a tumor-infiltrating population associated with a favorable prognosis in NSCLC,13,29 we established cocultures of Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes and wild-type/shIRAK1 NCI-H2228 cells (Fig. 3h). Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes cocultured with shIRAK1 cells exhibited increased cytotoxic activation (CD107a+), INF-γ production and proliferation (Ki67+; Fig. 3i–k). Compared with wild-type cells, shIRAK1 cells were more susceptible to Vγ9Vδ2 T cell-induced killing (Supplementary Fig. 10a), were more likely to be necro-apoptotic (annexin V/propidium iodide PI+ cells; Fig. 3l), and were less likely to be viable (crystal violet staining; Fig. 3m). Interestingly, in cocultures of NSCLC cells and T lymphocytes (Fig. 3h), CD4+ and CD8+ T cells cocultured with shIRAK1 cells skewed toward a central memory (CM) + effector memory (EM) phenotype (Fig. 3n, o). This change may be due to the increase in CM coupled with the decrease in the number of terminal effector memory-reexpressing CD45RA T cells (TEMRA; representative images: Supplementary Fig. 10b), suggesting that shIRAK1 cells promote the differentiation of T cells toward memory subsets with antitumor immune activity.

These observations were further validated in an autologous system based on patient-derived NSCLC cells derived from tumors with high or low IRAK1 levels (Supplementary Table 2) and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the same patient. NSCLCs derived from IRAK1low tumors showed increased necro-apoptotic cell death and decreased viability (Fig. 3p, q), which were associated with an increased percentage of CD107a+INF-γ+CD8+ T cells in cocultures (Fig. 3r).

Hence, we investigated the role of IRAK1 in promoting chemoresistance and immune evasion via TIME-dependent mechanisms.

IRAK1 silencing delays tumor growth and reshapes the immune microenvironment

shIRAK1 and wild-type NCI-H2228 cells were injected into Hu-CD34+ mice, which have a reconstituted human immune system that includes γδ T cells,30 and treated with Pt at the maximally tolerable dose (2 mg/kg). shIRAK1 tumors, which presented lower intratumor ABCB1/ABCC1 and higher ABCA1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11a), had delayed tumor growth. The antitumor effects elicited by Pt were more pronounced in the silenced tumors (Fig. 4a–c and Supplementary Fig. 11b, c) and did not affect the weight of the mice (Supplementary Fig. 11d); moreover, the effects were associated with reduced tumor proliferation and increased apoptosis (Fig. 4d–f and Supplementary Fig. 11e), increased numbers of CD8+ TILs (Fig. 4d, g and Supplementary Fig. 11f) and Vγ9Vδ2 T cells (Fig. 4h). CD8+ TILs did not differ between wild-type and shIRAK1 tumors upon Pt treatment, whereas Vγ9Vδ2 T cells were significantly increased in shIRAK1 tumors after Pt (Fig. 4h), which is consistent with the strong increase in ABCA1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Single-cell RNA sequencing of wild-type (Fig. 4i) and shIRAK1 (Fig. 4j) tumors revealed increased numbers of CD8+ TILs (Fig. 4k), particularly CMs and EMs (Fig. 4l; GSE294588), in IRAK1-silenced tumors, suggesting a more active TIME, in line with the results of ex vivo cocultures.

Synthetic IRAK1 inhibitors are novel chemoimmuno-sensitizing agents in non-small cell lung cancer

Since IRAK1 silencing enhances sensitivity to Pt and promotes an antitumor-oriented TIME, IRAK1 appeared to be an attractive druggable target. To this aim, we screened a library of ten compounds (Table 2), selected after a virtual screening carried out through molecular docking of the library compounds against the IRAK1 crystal structure31 from the “Gold and Platinum collections of Asinex Corp.” (ASN and BAS series), or synthesized on the basis of pharmacophore screening (JW30 and SG518032; Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). The compounds were not cytotoxic to NCI-H2228 cells up to 1 µM (Supplementary Fig. 13a), but they enhanced the cytotoxicity of Pt at nanomolar concentrations (Fig. 5a). Among the three most promising compounds—BAS02137049, JW30 and SG5180—we selected JW30 because of its solubility, low cytotoxicity (Table 2), docking data (Supplementary Fig. 12c) and chemosensitizing effect; indeed, in wild-type cells, JW30 at 10 and 100 nM concentrations combined with Pt reduced cell viability to the same level as that of shIRAK1 cells treated with Pt (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 13b). Notably, JW30 restored Pt sensitivity in overIRAK1 NCI-H1650 cells at the same concentrations; it was less effective in shIRAK1 NCI-H2228 and parental NCI-H1650 cells, which have a low level of the target IRAK1 (Supplementary Fig. 13b). JW30 inhibited IRAK1 activity for up to 72 h (Fig. 5c) and demonstrated good stability in plasma after 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 13c). In Hu-CD34+ mice, JW30 alone decreased tumor growth (Fig. 5d, e and Supplementary Fig. 14a) without changing body weight (Supplementary Fig. 14b) and further decreased tumor volume when associated with Pt (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 14c). Compared with the vehicle, JW30 significantly decreased intratumor proliferation (Fig. 5g, h), increased apoptosis (Fig. 5g, i and Supplementary Fig. 14d) and enriched intratumor CD8+ (Fig. 5g, j and Supplementary Fig. 14e) and Vγ9Vδ2 (Fig. 5k) T cells, mimicking the findings observed in IRAK1-silenced tumors. Importantly, JW30, alone or combined with Pt, did not show signs of bone marrow, liver, lung or kidney toxicity, according to postmortem hematochemical parameters (Supplementary Table 3) and organ histopathological examination (Supplementary Fig. 15a), and showed a good pharmacokinetic profile following this order of biodistribution: liver>tumor>kidney>lungs>brain (Supplementary Fig. 15b).

Discussion

Despite the improvements achieved by chemoimmunotherapy across different stages of NSCLC, overcoming the poor response to chemotherapy and immuno-killing by the host immune system remains a significant challenge. As previously demonstrated, the high expression of ABCB1 and ABCC1, which mediate the efflux of chemotherapeutics, and the low expression of ABCA1, a transporter that promotes tumor immune recognition by Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes, indicate a chemoimmunoresistant phenotype in NSCLC.13,27

In the present study, we investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms of the balance between these ABC transporters. We focused on the kinome of cancer cells owing to the potential of kinases to drive initiation, progression, and recurrence and to the widespread clinical use of kinase inhibitors in oncological treatments, including NSCLC.18 We identified IRAK1 as one of the top kinases associated with the ABCC1highABCA1low phenotype, resistance to chemotherapy and immuno-killing. IRAK1 is upregulated in more than 20 cancer types and correlates with chemoresistance.33 Indeed, in breast cancer, dual inhibition of IRAK1/IRAK4 reduced ABCB1 expression and increased sensitivity to topotecan by inhibiting MAPK and NFκB signaling.34 The selective IRAK1 inhibitor TCS2210 decreased ABCC1 in ovarian cancer cells,35 whereas in hepatocellular carcinoma, IRAK1 silencing increased sensitivity to doxorubicin and sorafenib by suppressing IRAK4/IRAK1/AP-1/AKR1B10 signaling.36 The molecular mechanisms linking IRAK1 to chemoresistance are poorly understood. Moreover, while in cardiovascular diseases, IRAK1 inhibition increases ABCA1,37,38 a link between IRAK1, ABCA1 and immuno-killing has never been reported in cancer.

Our reverse genetic approach revealed that IRAK1 silencing decreased ABCB1/C1 expression and increased ABCA1 expression, shifting the phenotype from chemoimmunoresistance toward chemoimmunosensitivity across different NSCLC cell lines, whereas IRAK1 overexpression had the opposite effect. Since both IRAK1 detection and chemosensitivity assays can be performed in PDOs, they could serve as rapid readout tools for ex vivo drug screening in NSCLC patient-derived biobanks. In terms of clinical meaning, patients in the TGCA-LUAD cohort with the IRAK1lowABCC1lowABCA1high phenotype had better OS than IRAK1highABCC1highABCA1low patients did. IRAK1 was among the most differentially expressed genes in early-stage versus advanced-stage NSCLC.39 In two retrospective cohorts of early-stage and advanced-stage patients, high IRAK1 levels detected in biopsy-derived patient materials at diagnosis correlated with lower OS and TTP. Early-stage patients may represent a useful indication in cases of tumor recurrence treated with chemotherapy or immunotherapy. In advanced-stage patients, IRAK1 directly correlated with ABCB1 and ABCC1 and inversely correlated with ABCA1. In the small cohort analyzed, IRAK1 levels did not change after treatment, correlated with poor OS and were associated with resistance to chemotherapy/immunotherapy in more than 60% of patients. If further validated in larger and independent patient cohorts, IRAK1 detection may represent a strong predictive biomarker of poor response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Since IRAK1 was in the same transcriptomic cluster as ABCC1/ABCA1 in patient-derived material, we next investigated the molecular network linking IRAK1 and ABC transporters. Using genetic and pharmacological approaches, we revealed that IRAK1 upregulated ABCB1/ABCC1 via the IL1-R/NFκB and IL1-R/ERK/AP-1 axes and downregulated ABCA1 by inhibiting LXRα, which acted on key transcriptional inducers of ABCB1, ABCC1 and ABCA1, respectively.27,40 For the first time, our research identified IRAK1 as an upstream regulatory hub of these signaling axes in NSCLC. Consistently, shIRAK1 cells were sensitized to cisplatin and docetaxel, which are chemotherapeutic substrates of ABCB1 and ABCC1 and are commonly used in patients with advanced NSCLC. Moreover, shIRAK1 cells were more sensitive to immuno-killing by Vγ9Vδ2 T cells, which were activated by IPP efflux through ABCA1.12,13 The ex vivo cocultures used in this work did not reproduce exactly real tumors, in which the number of γδ T cells is less than 5%, although this population is characterized by strong antitumor activity41 and is associated with prolonged survival in NSCLC patients.42 Despite this limitation, the ex vivo systems allowed us to monitor the effects of intratumor IRAK1 levels on γδ T cells; indeed, in both cocultures and immunoxenografts, IRAK1 silencing was associated with increased abundance and activation of Vγ9Vδ2 T cells and tumor cell apoptosis. Since IRAK1 is a central mediator of inflammation and cardiovascular diseases,22,27 we further explored its potential role in shaping the TIME at the qualitative and quantitative levels. Single-cell RNA sequencing, immunophenotyping of TILs and ex vivo cocultures of T cells/NSCLC cells revealed that IRAK1 silencing increased CM and EM CD8+ TILs, two subsets associated with enhanced antitumor immunity and response to immune checkpoint inhibitors.43 Since CD8+ TILs are favorable prognostic markers in NSCLC patients39 and because intratumor γδ T cells synergize with EM CD8+ T cells within the lung cancer TIME,42 we suggest that low intratumor levels of IRAK1 promote an immunologically active TIME, as supported by the autologous patient-derived NSCLC cell/PBMC cocultures used in this study. Consistent with these findings, IRAK1 expression is negatively correlated with CD8 + T cell numbers across several cancer types,44 whereas inhibition of IL1-R/MyD88/IRAK1 expression enhances CD8 + T cell expansion.45

Although shRNAs/siRNAs33,46 and miRNAs47 targeting IRAK1 successfully chemosensitized melanoma48 and breast cancer cells,22,49,50 these tools are poorly applicable in the clinical setting. Pacritinib, a JAK2/FLT3 inhibitor, is currently the only indirect inhibitor of IRAK1 clinically available for myelofibrosis treatment.51 IRAK4 inhibitors are used in autoimmune diseases and hematological cancers,52 but no selective IRAK1 inhibitors currently exist for solid tumors. To fill this gap, we produced a library of computationally designed compounds active at nanomolar concentrations as inhibitors of IRAK1 and chemosensitizing agents. We prioritized JW30 as the most effective and stable IRAK1 inhibitor, capable of phenocopying IRAK1 silencing in terms of reduced tumor growth, rescue of chemosensitivity and TIME immuno-reshaping. The good pharmacological profile of JW30, which has an intratumor accumulation higher than that in other organs (except the liver), makes the inhibition of IRAK1 relatively safe in the short term, since neither hematochemical signs of bone marrow, liver, kidney or muscle/heart toxicity nor histopathological alterations in the lung, kidneys, liver and brain were detected. The absence of long-term chronic toxicity, on a broader spectrum of organs, remains a mandatory preclinical step.

In conclusion, this work identifies IRAK1 as a regulator of chemoresistance and immune resistance in NSCLC, with significant implications for biomarker discovery and drug development. First, IRAK1 could be a predictive factor of resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy and a negative prognostic factor in NSCLC patients. Second, given the historical failure of direct ABC transporter inhibition, targeting upstream regulatory pathways represents a promising therapeutic strategy to improve chemosensitivity. The development of IRAK1-targeted combination treatments warrants further investigation as a novel chemoimmuno-sensitizing strategy in NSCLC patients refractory to the current treatment options.

The collection and analysis of patient-derived NSCLC cells and PBMCs was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Comitato Etico Aziendale) of San Luigi Gonzaga Hospital, Orbassano, Italy (protocol: #16685; approval number: #73/2018; date: 11/15/2018; protocol renewal: #2866; approval number: #73/2018; date: 02/22/2023). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The experimental protocol on mice was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Torino (OPBA-Organismo per il Benessere Animale) and by the Italian Ministry of Health (approval number: #627/2018-PR; date: 08/10/2018; protocol renewal:798/2023-PR; date: 09/19/2023). The experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Chemicals and materials

Plasticware for cell culture was obtained from Falcon (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ), and the culture medium was from Invitrogen (Milan, Italy). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), cis-diammineplatinum (II) dichloride and all the reagents, unless otherwise specified, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich-Merck (St. Louis, MO).

Cells

Human NSCLC cells (NCI-H1650, NCI-H1385, NCI-H460, NCI-H522, NCI-H661, NCI-H2126, NCI-H23, NCI-H1703, NCI-H1435, NCI-H596, NCI-H2286, NCI-H1437, NCI-H1651, NCI-H2085, NCI-H2342, NCI-H2073, NCI-H1793, NCI-H2170, NCI-H1299, NCI-H2066, NCI-H2347, NCI-H1734, NCI-H1563, NCI-H441, NCI-H1975, A549, Calu-3, and NCI-H2228) were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA). Mycoplasma spp. contamination was checked every 4 weeks using a MycoStrip® 100 detection kit (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA). The contaminated cells were discharged. Primary human NSCLC cells (#24, #636, #778, #670, #206, and #607) were obtained from diagnostic thoracoscopies/pleuroscopies or surgical materials from San Luigi Gonzaga Hospital, Orbassano, Department of Oncology, University of Torino, Italy. Each sample was anonymized and indicated as an identification number. Tumor cells were obtained from enzymatically digested tissues or pleural effusion, grown in Ham’s F10 medium supplemented with 10% v/v FBS and 1% v/v penicillin‒streptomycin for 48 h,53 and vitally frozen in FBS supplemented with 10% DMSO. For the ex vivo coculture systems, 1 × 105 viable cells were used 24 h after being thawed. Patient characteristics are reported in Supplementary Table 2.

IRAK1 silencing and overexpression

A total of 1 × 106 NCI-H2228 cells were transfected with 1 μg of human green fluorescence protein (GFP)-IRAK1 shRNA plasmid (#abx952448; Abbexa Ltd., Cambridge, UK) with three different sequences targeting IRAK1 or scrambled shRNA (#abx991273; Abbexa). Stably silenced clones were generated by selecting cells with 1 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 weeks. For IRAK1 overexpression, 1 × 106 NCI-H1650 cells were transfected with an IRAK1 expression vector (#sc-400264-ACT, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) or the control CRISPR activation plasmid (#sc-437275, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Stably overexpressing clones were generated by selecting cells with 0.1 μg/mL puromycin for 4 weeks. The efficacy of silencing and overexpression was verified by RT‒PCR and immunoblotting.

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs)

Organoids HCM-WCMC-0789-C34 (ATCC PDM-685™) and HCM-WCMC-0749-C34 (ATCC PDM-683™) were purchased from ATCC and were cultured in organoid media formulation #10 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Up to five drops of Matrigel/cell suspension per well (100 μl volume) were distributed into a six-well ultralow-attachment culture plate. The drops were solidified by a 30-min incubation in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After solid drops formed, 2 mL of organoid medium was added. Fresh culture media was added to each well every three days, and 10 μM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632) was added for the first 3 days. Organoids ~300 to 500 μm in size were subcultured, and single cells and small cell clusters were replated as described above. Total RNA was extracted, and IRAK1 levels were measured. Organoids were fixed in 4% v/v paraformaldehyde for 1 h, permeabilized in PBS with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 4 h and stained with the following antibodies: anti-IRAK1 (Abcam; 1:100) for 18 h, followed by a secondary FITC-conjugated antibody for 18 h. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and analyzed by a Leica SP8 confocal microscope.

Flow cytometry

A total of 1 × 104 cells were washed in PBS (pH 7.2, 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 2 mM EDTA), centrifuged at 300×g for 10 min, incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark with 250 μL Inside Fix Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, washed with 1 mL Inside Perm (Miltenyi Biotec), centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and incubated 10 min at room temperature with the following antibodies (all from Miltenyi Biotec): anti-ABCB1 (PE-Vio® 770-conjugated); anti-ABCC1 (PE-conjugated); anti-ABCA1 (DyLight 488-conjugated); anti-TCR Vγ9 (REA470, APC-conjugated); anti-CD3 (REA613 VioBright B515-conjugated); anti-CD4 (REA623 PerCP-Vio700-conjugated); anti-CD45RA (REA1047 VioBright720-conjugated); and anti-CD62L (REA615 VioBright V600-conjugated). The cells were subsequently washed with 1 mL of Inside Perm reagent, centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and read using a BD FACSCelesta™ Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

Cell viability

A total of 5 × 103 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, incubated for 72 h with increasing concentrations (from 1 × 10−9 to 1 × 10−5 M) of cisplatin and docetaxel for 72 h and stained with a WST-1 kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). PDOs were seeded in a 24-well plate, incubated for 48 h with 100 μM cisplatin, and then treated with a 3D CellTiterGlo kit (Promega, Madison, WI). Plates were read using a Synergy HT Multi-Detection Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). The absorbance/chemiluminescence of the untreated cells/PDOs was considered to indicate 100% viability; the results are expressed as a percentage of viable cells versus control cells.

T lymphocyte activation and tumor killing

PBMCs were isolated by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation, used immediately or vitally frozen and used 24 h after thawing. For Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocyte activation, PBMCs were stimulated for 7 days with 1 μM zoledronic acid and 10 IU/mL IL-2 to expand Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes, after which Vγ9Vδ2 T cells were purified with a TCRγ/δ+T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec).54 The purity of Vγ9Vδ2 T lymphocytes was confirmed by staining 5 × 105 isolated cells with anti-TCR Vγ9 (REA470, APC-conjugated; Miltenyi Biotec) and anti-CD3 (REA613 VioBright B515-conjugated, Miltenyi Biotec) antibodies. Samples with >80% Vγ9+/CD3+ cells were cultured for 72 h with NSCLC cells at a 1:2 ratio. To analyze IFN-γ secretion and degranulation activity (CD107a level), brefeldin A (#420601; BioLegend, San Diego, CA) was added during the final 4 h of coculture. The cells were then stained with anti-Ki67 (REA183 PE-conjugated), anti-CD107a (REA792 PE-conjugated), and anti-INF-γ (REA600 APC-conjugated) antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec) and quantified with a BD FACSCelesta™ Cell Analyzer. The results are expressed as the fold change in Vγ9+Ki67+/CD107a+/IFNγ+ cells compared with Vγ9+ cells.

For T cell subset analysis and activation, T lymphocytes were isolated from PBMCs with a Human Pan T-Cell Isolation Kit (#130-096-535; Miltenyi Biotec). The purity of the T cells was confirmed by staining 5 × 105 isolated cells with an anti-CD3 (REA613 VioBright B515-conjugated, Miltenyi Biotec) antibody. T cell subset analysis was performed using anti-CD45RA (REA1047 VioBright R720-conjugated), anti-CD62L (REA615 VioBright R600-conjugated), anti-CD8 (REA734 VioBlue-conjugated), anti-CD4 (REA623 PerCP-Vio700-conjugated) and anti-CD3 (REA613 VioBright 515-conjugated) antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec). T cells were cultured for 72 h with NSCLC cells at a 1:2 ratio. To analyze proliferation and activation, the cells were stained with anti-Ki67 (REA183 PE-conjugated), anti-CD107a (REA792 PE-conjugated), and anti-INF-γ (REA600 APC-conjugated) antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec) and quantified with a BD FACSCelesta™ Cell Analyzer. The results are expressed as the fold change in the number of Ki67+/CD107a+/INFγ+ cells compared with that of CD3+ cells.

Tumor killing was evaluated by staining adherent NSCLC cells using a (1) MEBCYTO® Apoptosis Kit (MBL JSR Life Science Company, Sunnyvale, CA), and the percentage of Annexin V/PI+ cells was calculated by flow cytometry; (2) 0.1% v/v crystal violet dye was used to quantify the absorbance with a Synergy HT Multi-Detection Microplate Reader. The absorbance of the untreated cells was considered 100%; the results are expressed as a percentage of viable cells versus control cells.

CRISPR-KO kinome library

A total of 13 × 106 NCI-H2228 SpCas9 (BNT)_A6 clonal cells were expanded to obtain ≥30 × 106 cells for two transduction replicates. A total of 15 × 106 NCI-H2228 cells were transduced per replicate with 500 µL of the Kinome sgRNA Lentivirus Library (Oxford Genetics, Oxford, UK) using the spin-infection method. After 24 h, the cells were counted using a Vi-Cell XR cell counter and selected with 1 µg/mL puromycin for 6 days. Next, 5 × 106 cells per replicate were stored for next-generation sequencing (NGS), and 5 × 106 cells were expanded for 11 days for cell sorting. Cells were stained with PE-conjugated ABCC1 and DyLight 488-conjugated ABCA1 antibodies and sorted using a SONY Cell Sorter SH800S (Minato, Japan). PCR products were measured using a Nanodrop and Illumina NEBnext Library Quantification Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). PCR samples were sequenced by GENEWIZ (Azenta Life Sciences, Leipzig, Germany) using an Illumina HiSeq4000. Data were analyzed by MAGeck (Model-based Analysis of Genome-wide CRISPR. Cas9 knockout, version 0.5.9.2) at Oxford Genetics, Oxford, UK.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail set III (Sigma-Aldrich), 2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and 1 mM Na3VO4, sonicated (10 bursts of 10 s, 4 °C, 100 W, Labsonic sonicator, Hielscher, Teltow) and centrifuged at 13000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Twenty micrograms of protein was subjected to immunoblotting (4–20% gradient SDS‒PAGE) and probed with the following antibodies: ABCB1 (sc-13131, Santa Cruz), ABCC1 (sc-18835 Santa Cruz), ABCA1 (ab307534, Abcam, Cambridge), IL1-R (ab154524 Abcam), MyD88 (ab33995 Abcam), IRAK1 (ab180747, Abcam), IRAK2 (sc-515885, Santa Cruz), IRAK3 (sc-100389, Santa Cruz), IKKγ (#2695, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) phospho(Ser176/180)IKKβ/α (#2697, Cell Signaling Technology), phospho(Ser32)IkBα (#2859, Cell Signaling Technology), IkBα (sc-371, Santa Cruz), NFkBp50 (sc-8414, Santa Cruz), NFkBp65 (sc-8008, Santa Cruz), ERK1/2 (ABS44, Merck Rahway, New Jersey), phospho(Thr202/Tyr204, Thr185/Tyr187)ERK1/2. The proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ChemiDocTM Touch Imaging System device; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted and reverse-transcribed using the iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories). qRT‒PCR was performed with the IQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The primer sequences (Supplementary Table 4) of the ABCB1, ABCC1, ABCA1, IRAK1 and S14 (housekeeping genes) genes were designed using the qPrimerDepot database (http://primerdepot.nci.nih.gov). Their quantification was performed using the Bio-Rad Software Gene Expression Quantification.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed using 5 × 10⁶ cells as indicated.27 Promoter regions of the ABCB1, ABCC1, and ABCA1 genes were identified using the Eukaryotic Promoter Database (EPD; https://epd.expasy.org/epd/). Putative binding sites for AP-1, NFκB, and LXRα were predicted using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/). ChIP-enriched DNA was analyzed by qRT‒PCR using primer pairs designed by PrimerBlast (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast) (Supplementary Table 4).

NFκB and AP-1 transcriptional activity

NFκB and AP-1 transcriptional activity was quantified using TransAM® NFκB and TransAM® AP-1 Assay Kits (Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclear extracts were prepared using the Nuclear Extract Kit (Active Motif). For each assay, 10 µg of nuclear protein was incubated in 96-well plates precoated with consensus oligonucleotide sequences containing NFκB or AP-1 binding sites. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 655 nm using a BioTek Synergy HT plate reader. The data were normalized to the background signal and are expressed as optical density (OD450) values.

TCGA analysis and patient retrospective analyses

The transcriptome profile of the TCGA-LUAD cohort was imported to the R working environment (version 4.2.2) and analyzed as detailed previously.27 The distributions of IRAK1, ABCA1 and ABCC1 in tumor tissue were evaluated to estimate “high” and “low” gene expression. Patient OS was extracted and correlated with the coexpression of IRAK1, ABCA1 and ABCC1 as previously reported.27

The transcriptome profiles of 247 treatment-naïve, I-IIIA-stage NSCLC patients26 were analyzed for IRAK1 expression. According to the median value of IRAK1 expression, the patients were categorized into “high-” and “low-expressing” groups and analyzed for OS. Two retrospective analyses were conducted on patients enrolled at San Luigi Gonzaga Hospital, Department of Oncology, University of Torino, Italy. Each patient was anonymized and indicated by an identification number. First, IRAK1 expression was compared between 81 NSCLC tissues and 48 adjacent normal lung tissues from the same cohort. Patients’ clinical and molecular features are described previously25 and have been deposited in the European Genome-phenome Archive (EGA) under accession number EGAS00001007219. The datasets used for all analysis steps are available under accession number EGAD00001010838. Second, the levels of IRAK1, ABCB1, ABCC1, and ABCA1 were measured by qRT‒PCR and normalized to those of the housekeeping gene β-2-microglobulin (B2M) in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples from 77 treatment-naïve patients at stage IIIB-IV,27 36 patients received cisplatin as first-line therapy (chemotherapy group), and 41 patients received pembrolizumab as first-line therapy (immunotherapy group). IRAK1, ABCB1, ABCC1, and ABCA1 levels were reassessed in tumor cells obtained from the pleural effusion53 of patients who relapsed after treatment (8 patients in the chemotherapy group and 13 patients in the immunotherapy group). Patients were categorized into “high-” and “low-expressing” groups, and TTP and OS were analyzed. The pathological features, smoking habits, TTP and OS are reported in Supplementary Table 2. Patients’ FFPE samples at diagnosis were also stained with an antibody recognizing IRAK1 (Abcam), followed by a peroxidase-conjugated horseradish antibody (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich).

IRAK1 immunoprecipitation and enzyme activity

NCI-H2228 cells (2 × 106) were incubated with the IRAK1 inhibitor JW30 at concentrations of 10 and 100 nM for up to 72 h. One milligram of protein from whole-cell extracts was immunoprecipitated with an anti-IRAK1 antibody (ab180747; Abcam) using PureProteome protein A and protein G magnetic beads (Millipore, Billerica, MA). The eluted protein was subjected to immunoblotting analysis to verify the specificity for IRAK1. IRAK1 kinase activity was quantified using the ADP-Glo™ Kinase Assay (Promega, Madison, WI) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Recombinant IRAK1 protein (50 ng; SignalChem Lifescience, Richmond, Canada) was incubated with the JW30 inhibitor, and IRAK1 immunoprecipitated from cell lysates was incubated with the specific substrate (modified AKT, 0.2 µg/µL) and ATP (25 µM) in kinase reaction buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 20 mM MgCl₂, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 40 µM DTT). Reactions were performed in 384-well plates with a final volume of 20 µL and incubated at room temperature for 60 min. The kinase reaction was stopped by the addition of ADP-Glo reagent, which converted the generated ADP into a luminescent signal proportional to kinase activity. Luminescence was measured after a 30-min incubation with Kinase Detection Reagent at room temperature using a BioTek Cytation 3 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments). The results are expressed as a percentage of enzyme activity versus activity in treated cells, which was considered 100%.

Immunoxenografts

A total of 2 × 106 wild-type and shIRAK1 NCI-H2228 cells mixed with 100 μL of Matrigel were injected subcutaneously into female NOD SCID-γ (NSG) mice engrafted with human hematopoietic CD34+ cells (Hu-CD34+; The Jackson Laboratories, Bar Harbor, MA). The mice were housed (5 per cage) under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with food and water provided ad libitum. Tumor growth was measured daily by calipers according to the equation (LxW2)/2, where L = tumor length and W = tumor width. When the tumors reached a volume of 50 mm3, the animals (5/group) were randomized. In the first experiment, mice bearing wild-type and shIRAK1 NCI-H2228 tumors were treated for 3 weeks as follows: the vehicle group was treated with 0.1 mL of saline solution intraperitoneally (i.p.) once a week, and the cisplatin group was treated with 2 mg/kg cisplatin i.p. once a week. In a second experiment, mice bearing wild-type NCI-H2228 tumors were treated as follows: vehicle group, treated with 0.1 mL saline solution i.p., twice a week; cisplatin group, treated with 2 mg/kg cisplatin i.p., once a week; JW30 group, treated with 10 mg/kg IRAK1 inhibitor JW30 i.p., twice a week; and JW30+cisplatin group. Tumor volumes were monitored by calipers, and the animals were euthanized on day 28 after randomization with zolazepam (0.2 mL/kg) and xylazine (16 mg/kg). The tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% v/v paraformaldehyde and stained with hematoxylin/eosin and an anti-Ki67 antibody (AB9260; Merck), followed by a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Dako). The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. The tumor sections were also stained with an in situ cell death detection kit (TUNEL Assay; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) or an anti-CD8 antibody (MA5-44283; Invitrogen) followed by an Alexa Fluor488-conjugated secondary antibody (A32731TR; Invitrogen) and DAPI. Sections were examined with a Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader (BioTek Instruments). Immunostaining quantification was performed using ImageJ software, and the results are expressed as a percentage of Ki67/CD8- or TUNEL-positive nuclei/nuclei counted in each field. In each sample, ten fields were analyzed. To evaluate the intratumor infiltration, tumors were digested with 1 mg/mL collagenase and 0.2 mg/mL hyaluronidase for 1 h at 37 °C and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Infiltrating immune cells were collected, isolated and immunostained with anti-TCR Vγ9 (REA470 APC-conjugated, Miltenyi Biotec), washed with 1 mL of Inside Perm reagent, and centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, after which the cells were read using a BD FACSCelesta™ Cell Analyzer.

Immediately after euthanasia, 200 µL of blood was collected to measure the following hematochemical parameters: red blood cells (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs), hemoglobin (Hb), and platelets (PLTs), which are indicators of bone marrow function; lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (AP), which are indicators of liver function; creatinine, which is an index of kidney function; creatine phosphokinase (CPK), which is an index of muscle/heart damage using commercially available kits from Beckman Coulter, Inc. Heart, lungs, liver, kidneys and spleen were collected and fixed in 4% v/v paraformaldehyde. The sections were stained with hematoxylin‒eosin and examined with a Leica DC100 microscope.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq)

A total of eight freshly isolated samples, consisting of 2 replicates for each of the four experimental conditions (shIRAK1 untreated, shIRAK1 treated with Pt, wild-type untreated, and wild-type treated with Pt), were analyzed using microfluidic-based scRNA-Seq across eight batches. Excised tumor tissues were processed as described previously.27 The library pool was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) flow cell. Raw 10x reads were demultiplexed into FASTQ files for individual libraries using Cellranger (version 7.0.0). The same tool was used to demultiplex samples based on cell multiplexing oligos (CMOs), align unique reads to the reference genome (10x Genomics prebuilt combined human GRCh38 and mouse GRCm39 genomes), assign them to individual cells via 10x Genomics barcodes, remove duplicates, and filter them for valid cells. The sparse matrices were then converted into complete matrices using the h5tocsv function from the rCASC package.55 Using the human/mouse cell annotation provided by Cellranger, only human cells were retained. Quality control was subsequently performed by assessing the number of genes per cell and the percentage of reads mapped to mitochondrial and ribosomal genes, utilizing the mitoRiboUmi function from rCASC. Finally, only cells expressing at least 100 genes with more than 3 UMIs per cell were selected using the scannobyGtf function from rCASC. All samples were merged into matrices corresponding to the four experimental conditions: shIRAK1 untreated (shc1, shc2), shIRAK1 with Pt (shp1, shp2), wild-type untreated (wtc1, wtc2), and wild-type with Pt (wtp1, wtp2), while preserving, as part of the cell barcode, information on cell origin from each replicate and sequencing batch. Each matrix was analyzed separately using Louvain modularity clustering (seuratBootstrap function from the rCASC package) with a resolution of 0.3. The resulting clusters were then annotated using scATOMIC software56 integrated into the rCASC package. All analysis step data are available on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15205625), and the raw data are in the GEO dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds; GSE294588).

Design, stability, and biodistribution of IRAK1 inhibitors

IRAK1 inhibitors were designed by virtual screening and pharmacophore screening methods. JW30 and SG5180 compounds were chemically synthesized (Supplementary Fig. 18) at the Department of Drug Science and Technology, University of Torino, Italy. Purity (Supplementary Fig. 17), plasma stability and biodistribution of the lead compound JW30 were detected by reversed-phase-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) and ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC–MS/MS), respectively (see Supplemental material and methods).

Statistical analysis

All the data were presented as the means ± SDs. The results were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using GraphPad PRISM (v.10). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to calculate the TTP (time from the beginning of treatment to the first signs of disease progression) and OS (survival from the beginning of treatment until the patients’ death). The patient and animal sample sizes were calculated using G*Power (www.gpower.hhu.de), with α ≤ 0.05 and 1-β = 0.80.
