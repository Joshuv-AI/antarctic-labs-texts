Evaluating the Impact of a Mixed-reality Simulator for Point-of-care Echocardiography Training for Novice Trainees: A Parallel Group Feasibility Study
Authors: Arvind Rajamani, Daniel Bairamian, David Bairamian, Prashant Jedge, Jignesh Shah, Shivakumar Iyer, Sandip Kumar, Sasidaran Kandasamy, Pranav A Bharadwaj, Sriram Hariharan, Sam Orde, Amritha V Ragavan, Hemamalini Arvind, Stephen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsWhile learning point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), novice critical care trainees often face challenges in spatial understanding and image acquisition. This study evaluated the feasibility and user experience of an ultraportable mixed-reality (MR) echocardiography simulator as an adjunct to conventional patient-based teaching.Patients and methodsIn a parallel group feasibility study in two tertiary intensive care units (ICUs) in India, 14 novice intensive care trainees received a 45-minute didactic session and were randomized into two groups: Group I performed an initial echocardiogram on a patient and group II on the simulator. Following this, both groups performed a second scan on a different ICU patient in alternating sequence. Primary outcomes included trainee feedback on simulator design, comfort, usability, and image realism. Secondary outcomes included setup time, trainee handover and onboarding times, barriers to use, and the time required to acquire echocardiographic views.ResultsSimulator setup took 92 seconds; median handover and onboarding times were 45 (41.5–48) and 66 (54.5–69.5) seconds, respectively. All trainees reported satisfaction with simulator design and image realism; 10 felt it improved spatial understanding. Reported challenges included headset fit for spectacle wearers, battery life, and anatomical alignment. Group II acquired acceptable images faster than group I (8.1 vs 9.7 minutes; p = 0.002).ConclusionIn this pilot feasibility study, the simulator demonstrated operational feasibility and was well-received in a workshop setting. Its potential role as a training adjunct for POCUS education to enhance image acquisition skills in novice critical care trainees needs to be validated in larger studies with an adequate sample size and robust clinical and educational outcomes.Keywords: Cardiac point-of-care ultrasound, Education feasibility, Learning ultrasound in critical care, Simulator

Introduction

Most point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) programs rely on opportunistic bedside teaching with inconsistent exposure to echocardiographic pathology, which may provoke anxiety for novices in clinical settings.1 High-fidelity simulators offer a controlled environment where learners encounter simulated pathologies, make errors safely, standardize feedback, and shorten learning curves.2,3

Two authors invented an ultraportable mixed-reality (MR) echocardiography simulator to address challenges in early ultrasound education in acute and critical care. The simulator combines a Meta Quest 3™ headset, a silicone-coated thoracic manikin, and a three-dimensional (3D)-printed probe as a standalone system without an ultrasound device, weighing ~2.5 kg (Fig. 1). It is not based on commercial anatomy simulators such as HoloHuman. It is an adjunctive training aid to improve novices’ spatial understanding of echocardiographic anatomy, potentially enabling quicker acquisition of acceptable windows on patients. This aligns with evidence that ultrasound-supported anatomy teaching improves anatomical understanding.4,5 To maintain portability, the application runs entirely on-device, requiring no external ultrasound machines or computers. Low-cost, off-the-shelf components enable use across diverse settings.

Training follows a scaffolded progression. In initial modules, learners view digital overlays of internal anatomy and the ultrasound beam while scanning the chest, making visible the relationship between probe position, beam orientation, and image output to target spatial reasoning and psychomotor skills (Fig. 1D). Later modules replicate real patients by removing overlays and adding constraints such as limited acoustic windows and image noise. Immediate feedback supports deliberate practice, potentially improving learning efficiency and retention.6 Built-in guidance and self-assessment tools reduce supervisory demands and support structured learning, and a browser-based interface allows instructors to provide real-time and remote feedback in workshops (Fig. 1E).7 There are also integrated echocardiography lectures by experts and case studies with echocardiographic pathologies, although these educational components were not evaluated in this feasibility study.

We conducted this study to evaluate the feasibility, usability, and practical limitations of implementing the simulator in early critical care training.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

After ethics approval (Nepean Blue Mountains HREC, 2019/ETH01259), we conducted a parallel group feasibility study of novice critical care trainees attending a short basic echocardiography workshop within the learning ultrasound in critical care (LUCC) program in two tertiary ICUs in India. All were novice echocardiography trainees who had performed <10 echocardiograms and had no prior simulator exposure (Fig. 2).

Baseline Didactic

All trainees attended a 45-minute interactive lesson focused on helping them intuitively understand how ultrasound “slices” the heart to create two-dimensional images. To illustrate imaging planes, trainees used a blunt knife to slice a heart-shaped modeling clay model into sections corresponding to standard views: Parasternal long-axis (PLAx), parasternal short-axis (PSAx), apical four-chamber (A4C), and off-axis windows. They also received a lecture on “ideal” echocardiographic windows as defined in e-Table 6 of the LUCC consensus statement.8

Trainees were then randomly allocated to two groups of seven (group I—conventional; group II—simulator) and asked to perform two sequential basic echocardiograms to acquire PLAx, PSAx, A4C, and subcostal inferior vena cava (IVC) views.

For the first echocardiogram, trainees in group I performed the scan on a real ICU patient with cardiac failure while trainees in group II performed the scan on the simulator. The second echocardiogram was performed on a different ICU patient with sepsis, with trainees alternating in a predefined sequence—beginning with a group II trainee followed by a group I trainee—to partially balance immediate order effects between the groups. Both teaching patients were clinically stable ICU patients selected by convenience sampling based on their suitability for repeated educational imaging and their consent to participate. Body habitus, lung recruitment status, and probe accessibility were not systematically matched between the cardiac failure and sepsis cases.

For both echocardiograms, the image acquisition quality was done in real-time by a single unblinded, experienced echocardiography educator using predefined criteria from the LUCC consensus statement.8 Upon successful acquisition of a view considered sufficiently acceptable, the evaluating clinician permitted the trainee to proceed to the next view. The total time in minutes to obtain all views was recorded.

For their first echocardiogram, group I trainees scanned a real ICU patient with cardiac failure, while group II trainees used the echocardiography simulator. To address the suboptimal headset battery life reported in our preliminary evaluation in Vietnam, we kept the headset charged for extended use.

Following this, all trainees, including those from group I, were invited to use the simulator and provide feedback.

The primary outcome was the trainees’ feedback regarding the simulator's design, comfort, ease-of-use, software interface, and the quality of simulated echocardiographic images.

Statistical Analysis

The trainees’ feedback responses were grouped descriptively and are presented thematically in three broad categories: Perceived educational benefits, usability and comfort, and technical limitations. Error rates and Likert scores were not quantified. Total scan time in minutes for the two groups is reported as median (Q1–Q3) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, with a rank‑biserial correlation calculated as an effect size measure. Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05 (two‑tailed). Analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel™.

Results

Fourteen novice intensive care trainees participated in the workshop. Nine were male. The postgraduate year (PGY) level ranged from 3 to 6 years [median: 4 (4–5)]. All trainees had performed fewer than 10 echocardiograms and had no prior exposure to the simulator. The two teaching patients were male ICU patients aged 54 and 48 years, respectively, who were clinically stable and suitable for repeated educational imaging. The time to set up the simulator for first use in the workshop was 92 seconds. The median time to transfer from one trainee to the next trainee was 45 (41.5–48) seconds. The median time to familiarize a new trainee with the buttons and software interface of the simulator was 66 (54.5–69.5) seconds.

All 14 trainees reported satisfaction with the simulator's design, comfort, ease of use, software interface, and image quality.

Regarding the spatial understanding of echocardiographic window acquisition, 10 trainees reported that the simulator helped, 2 did not, and 2 were neutral. Helpful features included the simulation of the rib cage and acoustic shadow, and a dissection model displaying the anatomical plane alongside the echocardiographic image, particularly for the challenging A4C view.

Reported challenges included the need to troubleshoot alignment between the cardiothoracic anatomy and the simulator torso, as well as issues related to battery life and charging. One trainee who wore prescription spectacles experienced difficulty viewing the images and discomfort due to nasal bridge pressure, necessitating the removal of their glasses, which then resulted in suboptimal visual acuity. Except for this individual, all other trainees rated the quality of the echocardiographic views and pathological cases as “realistic” or “highly realistic.” The simulator battery lasted approximately 90 minutes before requiring continuous wall charging.

For patient 2, the median total time to obtain acceptable images of all four echocardiographic views was 9.7 minutes (9.55–9.9) for group I and 8.1 minutes (7.85–8.3) for group II. The between-group difference in total scan time was statistically significant on the Mann–Whitney U test (U = 49.0, p = 0.002) and corresponded to a rank-biserial correlation of r = −1.00, indicating a very large exploratory effect in this small feasibility sample; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, as image acceptability was judged in real time by an unblinded assessor.

Discussion

This study evaluates a low-cost echocardiography simulator as an adjunct to help trainees understand spatial cardiac anatomy and learn image acquisition skills. To evaluate the simulator's impact, we enrolled novice trainees with little or no ultrasound experience and used minimalist didactic teaching with modeling clay and a brief overview of ideal windows. Trainees did not receive any other assistance when they scanned the simulator or patient(s).

The short time taken to set up the simulator and transfer from one trainee to the next demonstrates its feasibility and simplicity, particularly since none of the trainees in our cohort had previously used a simulator. Trainees reported that spatial visualization features such as anatomical dissection and rib artifacts augmented their understanding of complex cardiac anatomy. This is an example of cognitive scaffolding, which refers to instructional techniques or tools that help learners build mental models and structures for understanding complex ideas.9 Just like scaffolding supports construction workers as they build or repair a structure, cognitive scaffolding supports students as they learn, making tough concepts more accessible until mastery is achieved. Trainees reported that practicing transducer maneuvers in a simulated environment was less stressful than on real patients; this perception requires confirmation in larger studies.10

While a range of simulator models, including physical phantoms, digital platforms, and fully virtual systems have demonstrated efficacy in improving ultrasound acquisition skills, few studies have explored MR echocardiography simulators comparable to ours.11–15 Unlike immersive virtual reality (VR) systems that may be disorienting in workshops, our MR design preserves spatial awareness and user orientation. This mitigates common VR-related side effects such as nausea and motion sickness, while allowing facilitators to maintain real-time engagement with participants, an essential feature in group-based training.16 The simulator also addresses limitations of other models, including excessive size, prohibitive cost, and low fidelity, which restrict portability and feasibility in low-resource settings. By combining high usability with scalable deployment, it offers a pragmatic solution for echocardiography education.

Also, by integrating echocardiography lectures by experts and case studies with echocardiographic pathologies (extendable by software updates), it also addresses the challenges of inconsistent exposure to echocardiographic pathology that prior studies have identified.7

Our study has several strengths. (1) The parallel group design with completely novice trainees enabled evaluation of the simulator's direct educational impact. The standardized image-quality assessment criteria reduced bias. (2) The portability may support scalability in resource-limited settings. (3) The shorter scan times observed in the simulator-exposed group on patient 2 suggest a possible educational benefit, but the study was not powered for definitive performance differences, and this between-group comparison should be regarded as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating finding rather than evidence of effectiveness. (4) Finally, the alternating sequence for the second echocardiogram on the patient with sepsis was intended to reduce immediate order effects. However, the two groups had different first‑scan experiences (real patient vs simulator), so residual carryover and practice effects cannot be excluded.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. (1) Due to limited time and funding, we restricted the study to a small cohort of trainees and a pair of teaching patients selected by convenience sampling without stratification by illness severity, which introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of patient‑related findings. Although trainee feedback was overwhelmingly positive, it may not fully evaluate all aspects of the simulator. The qualitative analysis did not assess thematic saturation, did not use formal Likert scale quantification, and did not triangulate perceptions with objective error rates, so these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. (2) Image quality assessments were performed by a single experienced evaluator who was unblinded to group allocation, and inter-rater reliability was not evaluated. This introduces potential assessor bias and reinforces that the observed difference in scan time should be regarded as an exploratory finding. (3) Although we used an alternating sequence during the second echocardiogram to partially balance order, performance was not stratified or adjusted by individual scan order, and unmeasured learning or practice effects across the two scans may have contributed to the observed difference in scan times. Future studies will incorporate trainee blinding to further minimize this effect and strengthen internal validity. (4) A key barrier to broader adoption was discomfort and reduced visual clarity in trainees wearing prescription glasses. We have partially addressed this by adding a headset spacer, allowing some spectacles to fit more comfortably. (5) Fidelity was assessed only through subjective ratings and comments, and no quantitative imagematching analysis between simulated and realpatient echocardiograms was performed, so a formal fidelityvalidation study will be required in future work. (6) Finally, we did not evaluate the simulator's scaffolded training model, the simulator's integrated lectures and echocardiographic case studies, or the trainees’ POCUS knowledge or image interpretation, since the current trainee cohort lacked sufficient background in echocardiographic pathology and image interpretation. Future studies will incorporate a larger and more appropriately trained cohort to assess these features and validate the simulator as a longitudinally effective tool for both instruction and competency evaluation.

Conclusion

In this pilot feasibility study, the simulator demonstrated operational feasibility and was well-received in a workshop setting. Its potential role as a training adjunct for POCUS education to enhance image acquisition skills in novice critical care trainees needs to be validated in larger studies with adequate sample sizes and robust clinical and educational outcomes.

Clinical Significance

This MR echocardiography simulator may offer a portable adjunct to conventional bedside teaching, enabling novices to practice image acquisition in a standardized, risk-free environment. Its potential effects on anatomical understanding, performance anxiety, and image acquisition speed are hypothesis-generating findings from this small feasibility study. If validated in larger studies, such simulators could help accelerate early echocardiography skill acquisition in critical care training programs, particularly where access to expert bedside supervision and pathology exposure is limited.
