Characterizing the Expressivity of Fixed-Precision Transformer Language Models
Jiaoda Li Ryan Cotterell

Abstract
Transformer-based language models (LMs) have achieved widespread empirical success, but their theoretical expressive power remains only partially understood. In this work, we analyze a restricted idealization of fixed-precision transformers with strict future masking, soft attention, and no positional encodings. We establish that this class of models is exactly as expressive as a specific fragment of linear temporal logic that contains only a single temporal operator: the past operator. We further connect this fragment to established classes in formal language theory, automata theory, and algebra, yielding a unified framework for understanding transformer expressivity under this idealization. Finally, we present empirical results that align closely with our theory: transformers trained on languages within their characterized expressive capacity generalize reliably across sequence lengths, while they consistently fail to generalize on languages beyond it. 1 1 1 Code available at GitHub repository .

2.3
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}

First-order logic
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
can also be used to describe formal languages.
In contrast to (linear) temporal logic, where formulas are interpreted at a single position,
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
formulas may contain multiple position variables, making it better-suited to simulating mechanisms in transformers that involve more than one position, such as attention; see
§§

3.2
and
B.2
.
In this paper, we introduce a fragment of
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
called
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
—the past fragment of first-order logic with two variables.
The atomic formulas of
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
consist of unary predicates
{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
for each
{\texttt{a}}\in{{\Sigma}}
and a binary numerical predicate
<
that can be used to determine the sequential order between variables.
Formulas in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can have at most two distinct variables,
x
and
y
. In addition to the usual Boolean connectives
\land
and
\lnot
, there is a bounded existential quantifier
\exists y<x
, where
y
is the variable bounded by the quantifier, and
x
is the free variable. In case there is no free variable, the bounded existential quantifier behaves like an ordinary existential quantifier.
The formulas in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
are constructed inductively as follows:

•

Every atomic formula is a formula;

•

A Boolean combination of formulas is a formula if it does not contain more than two variables;

•

If
{\phi}(x,y)
is a formula with two variables
x,y
,
\exists y<x:{\phi}(x,y)
and
\exists x<y:{\phi}(x,y)
are formulas;

•

If
{\phi}(x)
is a formula with one variable
x
,
\exists x<y:{\phi}(x)
is a formula;

•

If
{\phi}(x)
is a formula with one variable
x
,
\exists x:{\phi}(x)
is a formula.

The bounded universal quantifier
\forall y<x
can be defined using
\exists y<x
and
\lnot
. For instance:

\forall y<x:{\phi}(x,y)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\lnot\exists y<x:\lnot{\phi}(x,y).

(4)

A formula with no free variables is called a
sentence
.
We write
{{\textbf{{w}}}}\models{\phi}
if
{\phi}
is satisfied for
w
.
A sentence
{\phi}
defines a language, the set of strings over
{{\Sigma}}
satisfying it, denoted as
{\mathbb{L}}({\phi})
.

Example:

The sentence below defines all the strings that contain
a
b
c
as a subsequence (symbols appearing in order, possibly non-contiguously):

\exists x:\left({\pi}_{\texttt{c}}(x)\land\exists y<x:\left({\pi}_{\texttt{b}}(y)\land\exists x<y:{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x)\right)\right).

(5)

Note that the variable
x
is reused, as permitted in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
The formulas below, however, are not definable in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
:

•

x<y<z
(more than two variables);

•

\forall x:(\exists y\geq x:{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(y))
(disallowed use of the quantifier
\exists y\geq x
).

{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
is specifically designed to match the expressive power of
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
, allowing us to leverage both formalisms in our analysis of transformer expressivity.

3
Transformers

In this section, we formally define the transformer idealization under consideration and establish two central expressivity results: (i) every transformer can be translated into an equivalent
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formula, and (ii) every
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula can be simulated by a transformer. Combined with
Theorem

2.1
, these results imply that transformers are expressively equivalent to both
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
and
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

3.1
The Transformer Architecture

The transformer considered in this section follows the architecture described by
Yang et al. [50]
, with the exception that we use
soft attention
in place of unique hard attention (UHA). Before formally defining the architecture, we first state explicitly the assumptions that will govern our model as follows.

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that the transformer satisfies the following conditions:

1.

Fixed precision
: There exists a finite set of values that the floating-point numbers can assume.
We denote this set as
{\mathbb{F}}=\{{f}_{1},{f}_{2},\ldots\}
.

2.

No positional encoding
(NoPE): Positional encodings are omitted for now, but are addressed in
§

F.3
.

3.

Soft attention
: Attention weights are computed using the
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
function. We also treat average hard attention (AHA) in
§

F.1
, showing that AHA is equally expressive as soft attention.

4.

Strict future masking
: As in
Yang et al. [50]
, the attention is strictly future-masked. Strict masking is shown to be more expressive than non-strict masking in
§

F.2
.

5.

Recognition
: Following standard practice in expressivity studies
[
42
]
, the transformer is treated as a language recognizer. We will extend the analysis to transformer language models in
§

5
.

Transformers take strings as input.
Given a string
{{\textbf{{w}}}}={\texttt{w}}_{1}\cdots{\texttt{w}}_{N}
over an alphabet
{{\Sigma}}

{{\textsc{eos}}}\notin{{\Sigma}}
to form a

An initial embedding layer
{\mathbf{E}}\colon{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}^{{N}+1}\to{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
maps each input symbol including

We define the embedding layer as the

layer of the transformer:

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(0)}}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\mathbf{E}}.

(6)

The embedding is followed by a stack of

{\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}}\colon{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}

{\mathbf{F}^{(\ell)}}\colon{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}

These components are composed sequentially.
For each
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,{L}-1\}
, we define:

\displaystyle{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\text{LN}}\left({\mathbf{A}^{(\ell)}}\left({{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}}\right)+{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}}\right),

(7a)

\displaystyle{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+1)}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\text{LN}}\left({\mathbf{F}^{(\ell)}}\left({{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}}\right)+{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}}\right).

(7b)

where

Finally, a classification head

maps the representation at the

{o}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\mathrm{C}}\left({{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{({L})}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{:,{N}+1}}\right).

(8)

We use

Consequently, a transformer defines a function of type

3.2
From Transformers to
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}

In this subsection, we discuss the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

Every transformer can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

Proof.

See
§

B.2
.
∎

Our proof closely follows the approach of
Chiang et al. [9, Section 5]
, with one key difference: the simulation of summation in soft attention.
While prior work makes use of counting quantifiers to simulate summation,
Li et al. [27]
demonstrate that summation with iterative rounding can be simulated with
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
. We take this a step further by showing that the specific summation involved in soft attention can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
Here, we provide a high-level overview of the proof. We identify two summations in soft attention. The first occurs in the denominator of the
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
function, defined for a
{D}
-dimensional vector
{{{\mathbf{x}}}}
, as follows:

{{\mathrm{softmax}}}({{{\mathbf{x}}}})_{d}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{\exp(x_{d})}{\sum_{i=1}^{D}\exp(x_{i})},\text{ for }d\in\{1,\ldots,{D}\}.

(9)

\exp
is a deterministic function. Under the fixed precision assumption, the possible input and output values of
\exp
form a finite set, allowing
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formulas to encode
\exp
by explicit enumeration of these values.
Since
\exp
outputs only non-negative values, the summation in the denominator reduces to a sum of non-negative terms, which can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
(
Lemma

B.5
).
Additionally, the second summation is a weighted sum, in which the weights are produced by the
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
function. Under fixed precision, the output of a
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
contains a bounded number of non-zero entries (
Proposition

B.6
). Therefore, the weighted sum involves only a bounded number of terms and can similarly be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
(
Lemma

B.7
).

3.3
From
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
to Transformers

In this subsection, we discuss the following theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Every
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula can be simulated by a transformer.

Proof.

See
§

B.3
.
∎

Our proof is adapted from that of
Yang et al. [50, Appendix C.1]
. Intuitively, each
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula is encoded in a dedicated coordinate
d\in\{1,\ldots,{D}\}
of the transformer’s hidden state. At each position, this coordinate takes the value
1
if the formula is satisfied at that position, and
0
otherwise.
The key challenge in proving
Theorem

3.3
lies in simulating the temporal operator
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}
.
Prior constructions typically employ uniform attention, which assigns equal weight to all preceding positions, to simulate such operators.
However, under fixed precision, soft attention has a limited attention span, as the output of the
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
function contains only a bounded number of non-zero entries.
We refer to this bound as the maximum attention span, denoted by
{N_{\max}}
.
To overcome this limitation, previous work has relied on non-fixed numerical precision—such as arbitrary precision
[
9
,
49
]
or log precision
[
31
]
—to prevent attention weights from vanishing. In contrast, we present a construction that overcomes this issue without requiring increased numerical precision.

We begin by describing a base construction that applies when attention weights do not vanish, and then extend it to handle cases where vanishing weights may occur.

Base Construction.

Suppose a formula
{\psi}
is simulated by coordinate
d_{1}
at the
\ell^{\text{th}}
layer, i.e., by
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}_{d_{1},:}}
(omitting the input string
{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}
for brevity), and let
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}
be the formula we aim to simulate. We construct an attention sublayer that uniformly attends to all previous positions
m<n
such that
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}_{d_{1},m}}=1
.
If such positions exist, we set an unused coordinate
{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}}
to 1, i.e.,
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}_{{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}},n}}=1
and otherwise to
0
.

Handling Vanishing Weights.

However, when too many (more than
{N_{\max}}
) previous positions satisfy
{\psi}
, the resulting attention weights will underflow to
0
. This results in
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}_{{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}},n}}=0
even when
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}
is true, leading to incorrect behavior.
To address this, we first compute the logical conjunction of
d_{1}
and
{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}}
and store it in
{d_{\land}}
, i.e.,

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+1)}_{{d_{\land}},n}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}_{d_{1},n}}=1\text{ and }{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}_{{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}},n}}=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(10)

The number of positions
n
with
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+1)}_{{d_{\land}},n}}=1
is bounded by
{N_{\max}}
, since beyond the
({N_{\max}}+1)^{\text{th}}
position, the first attention sublayer will suffer from vanishing attention weights, causing
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+0.5)}_{{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}},n}}=0
.

Next, we construct a second layer in which the attention sublayer uniformly attends to all positions
m<n
where
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+1)}_{{d_{\land}},m}}=1
. This produces a coordinate
{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}}
that simulates
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}({\psi}\land{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi})
. Since the number of positions satisfying
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell+1)}_{{d_{\land}},m}}=1
is bounded, this second attention sublayer is not subject to vanishing attention weights and therefore computes its output reliably.

The formula
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}({\psi}\land{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi})
effectively asks whether there are at least two positions before
n
that satisfy
{\psi}
. Thus, if there is exactly one position
m<n
satisfying
{\psi}
, then
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}({\psi}\land{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi})
differs from the target formula
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}
. In this case, however, the first attention sublayer (producing
{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}}
) correctly simulates
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}
, since the attention only needs to cover one position.
We therefore take the logical disjunction of
{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}}
and
{d_{{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}}}
, implemented via the subsequent feedforward sublayer. The resulting coordinate
{d_{\lor}}
correctly simulates the formula
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}
. This completes the proof sketch.

Definition 4.2
.

A
deterministic finite automaton
(DFA) is a
5
-tuple
{{\mathcal{A}}}={{\left({{\Sigma}},{{Q}},{{q_{{{I}}}}},{{F}},{{\delta}}\right)}}
where

•

{{\Sigma}}
is an alphabet;

•

{{Q}}
is a finite set of states;

•

{{q_{{{I}}}}}\in{{Q}}
is the initial state;

•

{{F}}\subseteq{{Q}}
is the set of final states;

•

{{\delta}}\colon{{Q}}\times{{\Sigma}}\rightarrow{{Q}}

•

{{{q}}_{R}}\in{{Q}}\setminus{{F}}
is a rejecting sink state, i.e.,
{{\delta}}({{{q}}_{R}},{\texttt{a}})={{{q}}_{R}}
for every
{\texttt{a}}\in{{\Sigma}}
.

Given an automaton

is in state

such that

•

{{q}}_{0}={{q_{{{I}}}}}
;

•

{{q}}_{n+1}={{\delta}}({{q}}_{n},{\texttt{w}}_{n+1})
for

•

{{q}}_{N}={{q}}
.

A string

.

A DFA is said to be

In this section, we extend our analysis to transformer LMs. Formally, an LM constitutes a distribution over

6.1
Language recognition

We have shown that a transformer can recognize only left-deterministic polynomials. In this section, we conduct a series of language classification experiments to empirically validate this claim.
We consider five language classes, arranged in a strict inclusion hierarchy—each class is a proper subset of the one preceding it. For each class, we select one or more representative languages. These languages are listed in

6.1.1

We implement the transformer that we theorize about (
Assumption

). For comparison, we also train a long short-term memory (LSTM)
[
20
]
. Models are trained on strings up to length
40
, and tested on strings of length
41--500
.
Each experiment is run with
5
different random seeds and
3

We consider a transformer to have successfully recognized a language if it achieves
100\%

§

E.1.1
.

6.1.2
Results

We compute classification accuracy and report both the maximum and mean values across all runs in
Tab.

1
. The LSTM achieves perfect accuracy on all tasks, consistent with previous work showing that LSTMs can recognize regular languages
[
29
]
and implement counting mechanisms
[
48
]
. This confirms that the tasks are learnable given the available training data.
Results on transformers align precisely with our theoretical predictions: under fixed precision, transformers with soft attention and NoPE can recognize exactly the class of left-deterministic polynomials. They achieve perfect accuracy on all
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}

51

That is, attention can uniformly cover all prior positions without numerical underflow or overflow. Yet, despite these favorable conditions, the transformer exhibits no expressive power beyond what is predicted by our formal characterization. A more detailed breakdown of the results can be found in

.

A.1
Temporal logic

Temporal logic
[
37
]
is a special case of modal logic, with modalities referring to time.

A.1.1
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}

Linear temporal logic
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}
includes four temporal operators:
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}
(
past
),
{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}
(
future
),
{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}
(
since
), and
{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}
(
until
). Notably,
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}
,
{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}
, and one of
{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}
or
{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}
can be omitted without loss of expressive power
[
16
]
.
We write
{\top}
to denote
true
and
{\bot}
to denote
false
. The semantics of the formulas are defined inductively as follows:

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
iff the
n^{\text{th}}
symbol of
w
is an
a
.

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\psi}_{1}\land{\psi}_{2}
iff
{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\psi}_{1}
and
{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\psi}_{2}
.

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models\lnot{\psi}
iff
{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\not\models{\psi}
.

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}
iff there exists
m
such that
m<n
and
{{\textbf{{w}}}},m\models{\psi}
.

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}{\psi}
iff there exists
m
such that
m>n
and
{{\textbf{{w}}}},m\models{\psi}
.

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\psi}_{1}{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}{\psi}_{2}
iff there exists
m
such that
m<n
,
{{\textbf{{w}}}},m\models{\psi}_{2}
, and for every
k
such that
m<k<n
,
{{\textbf{{w}}}},k\models{\psi}_{1}
.

•

{{\textbf{{w}}}},n\models{\psi}_{1}{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}{\psi}_{2}
iff there exists
m
such that
m>n
,
{{\textbf{{w}}}},m\models{\psi}_{2}
, and for every
k
such that
n<k<m
,
{{\textbf{{w}}}},k\models{\psi}_{1}
.

Example:

•

{\texttt{a}}{\texttt{b}}{\texttt{b}},1\models{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
as the first symbol is
a
.

•

{\texttt{a}}{\texttt{b}}{\texttt{b}},3\models{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
as an
a
occurs before position 3.

•

{\texttt{a}}{\texttt{b}}{\texttt{b}},3\models{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
as
{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
holds at position 1 (before position 3), and
{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}
holds at every position between 1 and 3.

To say a string
w
of length
{N}
satisfies a formula
{\psi}
, written as
{{\textbf{{w}}}}\models{\psi}
, we imagine we start at a position outside the string, e.g., position
0
or
{N}+1
.

Example:

•

{\texttt{a}}{\texttt{b}}{\texttt{b}}\models{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
as the formula is satisfied at position
4
, outside the string.

•

{\texttt{a}}{\texttt{b}}{\texttt{b}}\models{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}
as the formula is satisfied at position
0
, outside the string.

The language defined by a formula
{\psi}
is the set of all strings that satisfy
{\psi}
, denoted by
{\mathbb{L}}({\psi})
:

{\mathbb{L}}({\psi})\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\{{{\textbf{{w}}}}\in{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}\mid{{\textbf{{w}}}}\models{\psi}\}.

(15)

Example:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{L}}\left({\pi}_{\texttt{b}}{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}\right)

\displaystyle={{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}{\texttt{a}}{{{\texttt{b}}^{*}}}

(16a)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{L}}\left({\pi}_{\texttt{a}}{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}\right)

\displaystyle={{{\texttt{a}}^{*}}}{\texttt{b}}{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}

(16b)

We define the
operator depth
of a formula
{\psi}
, denoted
{\text{od}}({\psi})
, as the maximum number of nested temporal operators in
{\psi}
. It is defined recursively as follows:

\displaystyle{\text{od}}({\pi}_{\texttt{a}})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{\text{od}}({\psi}_{1}\land{\psi}_{2})

\displaystyle=\max({\text{od}}({\psi}_{1}),{\text{od}}({\psi}_{2}))

\displaystyle{\text{od}}(\lnot{\psi})

\displaystyle={\text{od}}({\psi})

\displaystyle{\text{od}}({\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi})

\displaystyle={\text{od}}({\psi})+1

\displaystyle{\text{od}}({\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}{\psi})

\displaystyle={\text{od}}({\psi})+1

\displaystyle{\text{od}}({\psi}_{1}{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}{\psi}_{2})

\displaystyle=\max({\text{od}}({\psi}_{1}),{\text{od}}({\psi}_{2}))+1

\displaystyle{\text{od}}({\psi}_{1}{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}{\psi}_{2})

\displaystyle=\max({\text{od}}({\psi}_{1}),{\text{od}}({\psi}_{2}))+1

A.1.2
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}

Unary temporal logic
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
is the fragment of
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}
that excludes the binary operators
{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}}
and
{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}
. This restriction renders
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
strictly less expressive than
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}

[
13
,
14
]
.

Example:

Consider the language consisting of all words that begin with
a
and end with
b
. To enforce that a word starts with
a
, we require that
a
occurs at the beginning of the word, i.e., it is preceded by nothing. This can be expressed as
{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}({\pi}_{\texttt{a}}\land\lnot{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\top})
. Symmetrically, ending with
b
can be expressed using the dual condition involving
{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}
. Thus, the formula

{\mathbb{L}}({\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}({\pi}_{\texttt{a}}\land\lnot{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\top})\land{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}({\pi}_{\texttt{b}}\land\lnot{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}{\top}))={\texttt{a}}{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}{\texttt{b}}

(17)

defines the desired language.

A.1.3
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}

We define
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
as the past fragment of
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
, which excludes the
{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}
operator.

Proposition A.1
.

{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
is strictly less expressive than
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
.

Proof.

Since
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
is a syntactic fragment of
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
, every formula expressible in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
is also expressible in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
. To establish strictness, it suffices to exhibit a language definable in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
that is not definable in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
. Consider again the language
{\texttt{a}}{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}{\texttt{b}}
. It is not definable in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
, as expressing the end of a string requires the ability to look forward, which
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
lacks.
∎

A.2
First-order logic

The seminal work of
Büchi [5]
demonstrated how properties of languages can be described using logical formulas, while
McNaughton and Papert [28]
was the first to restrict these formulas to first-order logic (
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
).

A.2.1
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}

The atomic formulas of
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
consist of unary predicates
{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
for each
{\texttt{a}}\in{{\Sigma}}
and a binary numerical predicate
<
that can be used to determine the sequential order between variables.
Formulas in
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
may use arbitrarily many variables. However, it has been shown that three variables are both necessary and sufficient to define all first-order definable languages
[
25
,
24
]
.
In addition to the usual Boolean connectives
\land
and
\lnot
, there is an
existential quantifier

\exists
.
The formulas in
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
are constructed inductively as follows:

•

Every atomic formula is a formula;

•

A Boolean combination of formulas is a formula;

•

If
{\phi}(x,\ldots)
is a formula, so is
\exists x:{\phi}(x,\ldots)
;

The
universal quantifier

\forall
can be defined as follows:

\forall x:{\phi}(x,\ldots)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\lnot\exists x:\lnot{\phi}(x,\ldots).

(18)

A formula with no free variables is called a
sentence
. We write
{{\textbf{{w}}}}\models{\phi}
if
{\phi}
is satisfied for
w
under the interpretation, where variables range over the positions in
w
, unary predicates
{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x)
hold if the symbol at position
x
is
a
, and
<
is interpreted as the natural order on positions.
A sentence
{\phi}
can define a language, the set of strings over
{{\Sigma}}
satisfying it, denoted as
{\mathbb{L}}({\phi})
.

Example:

The formula below defines the language
{\texttt{a}}{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}{\texttt{b}}
:

\exists x\forall y\exists z:({\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x)\land x\leq y\land y\leq z\land{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}(z))

(19)

where
x\leq y
is shorthand for
x<y\lor x=y
.

The
quantifier depth
of a formula
{\phi}
, denoted
{\text{qd}}({\phi})
, is defined recursively as follows:

\displaystyle{\text{qd}}({\pi}_{\texttt{a}})

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle{\text{qd}}({\phi}_{1}\land{\phi}_{2})

\displaystyle=\max({\text{qd}}({\phi}_{1}),{\text{qd}}({\phi}_{2}))

\displaystyle{\text{qd}}(\lnot{\phi})

\displaystyle={\text{qd}}({\phi})

\displaystyle{\text{qd}}(\exists x:{\phi}(x,\ldots))

\displaystyle={\text{qd}}({\phi})+1

It is well known that
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
and
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}
are equivalent in expressive power
[
25
]
and they both define exactly the class of star-free languages
[
28
]
.

A.2.2
{\textup{{FO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}

{\textup{{FO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
is the fragment of
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
restricted to using only two distinct variables (typically reused via quantification).

Example:

The formula
Eq.

19
can be written as a formula in
{\textup{{FO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
as follows:

\exists x:\left({\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x)\land\forall y:\left(x\leq y\land\exists x:(y\leq x\land{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}(x))\right)\right),

(20)

which uses only two distinct variables
x
and
y
.

It is shown that
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}}]}
and
{\textup{{FO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
recognize precisely the same languages
[
14
]
.

A.2.3
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}

We define
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
as the past fragment of
{\textup{{FO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
, in which formulas are restricted so that whenever a free variable
x
is present, every existential quantifier must be of the form
\exists y<x
.. This ensures that all quantification is limited to positions at or before
x
, i.e., only past positions relative to the free variable are accessed.

{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
has precisely the same expressive power as
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
. We establish this equivalence by providing translations in both directions.

Lemma A.2
.

Every formula
{\psi}
in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
can be translated into an equivalent single-variable formula
{\phi}(x)
in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

Proof.

We proceed by structural induction on the formula
{\psi}
.

Base case.

If
{\psi}={\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
, we translate it to
{\phi}(x)={\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x)
.

Induction step.

Assume
{\psi}_{1}
and
{\psi}_{2}
can be translated into
{\phi}_{1}(x)
and
{\phi}_{2}(x)
respectively. We translate compound formulas as follows:

•

If
{\psi}={\psi}_{1}\land{\psi}_{2}
, then
{\phi}(x)={\phi}_{1}(x)\land{\phi}_{2}(x)
.

•

If
{\psi}=\lnot{\psi}_{1}
, then
{\phi}(x)=\lnot{\phi}_{1}(x)
.

•

If
{\psi}={\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}_{1}
, then
{\phi}(x)=\exists y<x:{\phi}_{1}(y)

In each case, the resulting formula
{\phi}(x)
belongs to
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
, since it uses at most two variables (the free variable
x
and a bound variable
y
), both of which can be reused.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Every single-variable formula
{\phi}(x)
in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can be translated into an equivalent formula
{\psi}
in
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the quantifier depth
{\text{qd}}({\phi})
.

Base case.

If
{\text{qd}}({\phi})=0
, then
{\phi}(x)
is a Boolean combination of atomic formulas such as
{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x)
, which are directly translatable into
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formulas.

Induction step.

Assume that every formula of quantifier depth at most
k
can be translated into an equivalent
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula. Consider a formula
{\phi}(x)
of depth
k+1
. The formula can be written as a Boolean combination of:

•

Subformulas of quantifier depth at most
k
, which can be translated into
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
by the inductive hypothesis;

•

Subformulas of the form
\exists y<x:{\phi}_{1}(y)
, where
{\phi}_{1}
has depth at most
k
. By the inductive hypothesis,
{\phi}_{1}(y)
translates to a
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula
{\psi}_{1}
, and the whole subformula translates to
{\psi}={\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}{\psi}_{1}
.

∎

Combining the two lemmas, we obtain the following theorem.
See
2.1

Proof.

The result follows directly from
Lemma

A.2
and
Lemma

A.3
. To define languages,
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formulas are interpreted at position
{N}+1
. Similarly,
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formulas with a single variable
x
can be converted into sentences by prefixing them with
\exists x
, which is semantically equivalent to
\exists x<{N}+1
.
∎

Appendix B
Transformers

In this section, we describe the transformer idealization under consideration in more detail. The formal proofs for the following two results are also given: (i) every transformer can be translated into an equivalent
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formula, and (ii) every
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula can be simulated by a transformer.

B.1
Transformer architecture

The assumptions we make are restated as follows:
See
3.1

We denote the set of non-negative floating-point numbers as
{\mathbb{F}_{\geq 0}}\subset{\mathbb{F}}
. Similar notation is used for positive numbers, negative numbers, and other subsets. The set
{\mathbb{F}}
includes two special values,
\infty
and
-\infty
, representing positive and negative infinity. Their behaviors are defined as follows:

•

\forall{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{-\infty\}
,
\infty+{f}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\infty
;

•

\forall{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{-\infty\}
,
-\infty+{f}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}-\infty
;

•

\forall{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{>0}
,
{f}\cdot\infty\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\infty
and
{f}\cdot(-\infty)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}-\infty
;

•

\forall{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{<0}
,
{f}\cdot\infty\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}-\infty
and
{f}\cdot(-\infty)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\infty
;

•

\forall{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{\infty,-\infty\}
,
{f}/\infty\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}0
;

•

\exp(\infty)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\infty
and
\exp(-\infty)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}0
.

Transformers take strings as inputs. Given a string
{{\textbf{{w}}}}={\texttt{w}}_{1}\cdots{\texttt{w}}_{N}
over an alphabet
{{\Sigma}}
, we append a special end-of-sentence symbol
{{\textsc{eos}}}\notin{{\Sigma}}
to form the extended string
{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\texttt{w}}_{1}\cdots{\texttt{w}}_{N}{{\textsc{eos}}}
over the extended alphabet
{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{{\Sigma}}\cup\{{{\textsc{eos}}}\}
.

The transformer consists of an input layer, followed by a stack of
{L}
hidden layers and a final output layer.

B.1.1
Input layer

An embedding function
{{{\mathbf{e}}}}
of type
{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{D}
maps each symbol to a
{D}
-dimensional column vector. The embedding layer applies
{{{\mathbf{e}}}}
to each symbol in
{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}
to produce the input representation:

{\mathbf{E}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
, i.e.,

{\mathbf{E}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{:,n}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{{{\mathbf{e}}}}({\texttt{w}}_{n}),\quad n\in\{1,\ldots,{N}+1\}.

(21)

where
{\texttt{w}}_{{N}+1}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{{\textsc{eos}}}
.

B.1.2
Hidden layers

Each hidden layer contains two sublayers: a self-attention mechanism and a feedforward network.

The self-attention mechanism is a function of type
{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
.
Given input
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
, we compute:

\displaystyle{{{\mathbf{Q}}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{Q}}}{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}),

(22a)

\displaystyle{{{\mathbf{K}}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{K}}}{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}),

(22b)

\displaystyle{{{\mathbf{V}}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{V}}}{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}),

(22c)

where
{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{Q}}},{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{K}}},{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{V}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times{D}}
are learnable parameter matrices.

A pairwise compatibility score
{\mathbf{S}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{({N}+1)\times({N}+1)}
is computed via scaled dot product:

{\mathbf{S}}_{n,m}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{{{{\mathbf{Q}}}}_{:,n}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\hbox to5.74991pt{\hfil\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{\lower 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}\hfil}}{\hbox to5.74991pt{\hfil\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{\lower 0.0pt\hbox{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}\hfil}}{\hbox to5.28671pt{\hfil\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{\lower 0.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}\hfil}}{\hbox to5.1909pt{\hfil\raise 0.0pt\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{\lower 0.0pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}\hfil}}}{{{\mathbf{K}}}}_{:,m}}{\sqrt{{D}}}.

(23)

We then compute attention weights
{\bm{\alpha}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{({N}+1)\times({N}+1)}
using
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
:

{\bm{\alpha}}_{n,m}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{\exp({\mathbf{S}}_{n,m})}{\sum_{i<n}\exp({\mathbf{S}}_{n,i})}.

(24)

In practice, the
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
is numerically stabilized by subtracting the maximum score from all scores before exponentiation:

{\bm{\alpha}}_{n,m}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{\exp({\mathbf{S}}_{n,m}-\max_{i<n}{\mathbf{S}}_{n,i})}{\sum_{j<n}\exp({\mathbf{S}}_{n,j}-\max_{i<n}{\mathbf{S}}_{n,i})}.

(25)

The attention output at position
n
is then computed as a weighted sum of the value vectors:

{\mathbf{A}}({{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}))_{:,n}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\sum_{m<n}{\bm{\alpha}}_{n,m}{{{\mathbf{V}}}}_{:,m}.

(26)

We assume a single attention head, since multiple heads do not increase expressive power
[
27
]
.

The feedforward sublayer is a function of type
{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
and consists of two linear transformations with a ReLU nonlinearity:

{\mathbf{F}}({{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}))_{:,n}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{F2}}}{{\mathrm{ReLU}}}\left({\bm{\Theta}^{\text{F1}}}{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{:,n}}+{\mathbf{b}^{\text{F1}}}\right)+{\mathbf{b}^{\text{F2}}},

(27)

where
{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{F1}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}^{\prime}\times{D}},{\mathbf{b}^{\text{F1}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}^{\prime}},{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{F2}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}^{\prime}\times{D}},{\mathbf{b}^{\text{F2}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{D}
are trainable parameters,
{D}^{\prime}
is the hidden size of the intermediate layer.

B.1.3
Output layer

The final output layer is a function of type
{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}
that computes a scalar score based on the representation at the final position (corresponding to
eos
):

{\mathrm{C}}\left({{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{({L})}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})}\right)=\left({{{\bm{\theta}}}}^{\text{C}}\right)^{\top}{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{({L})}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{:,{N}+1}}+b^{\text{C}}

(28)

where
{{{\bm{\theta}}}}^{\text{C}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}}
and
b^{\text{C}}\in{\mathbb{F}}
are learnable parameters.

B.2
From transformers to
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}

In this sub-section, we show that any transformer can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
We begin by formalizing what it means for a function to be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
With a slight abuse of notation, we write
{D}
to refer either to the model’s hidden dimensionality or to 1, in the case of a scalar classification output.

Definition B.1
.

A function
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}\colon{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}^{{N}+1}\to{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
is said to be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
if for every dimension
d\in\{1,\ldots,{D}\}
and every floating-point value
{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, there exists a single-variable formula
{\phi}(x)
in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
such that for every position
n\in\{1,\ldots,{N}+1\}
,

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d,n}}={f}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}},n\models{\phi}(x).

(29)

Similarly, a function
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}
of type
{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}^{{N}+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{({N}+1)\times({N}+1)}
is said to be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
if for every floating-point value
{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, there exists a two-variable formula
{\phi}(x,y)
in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
such that for every pair of positions
n,m\in\{1,\ldots,{N}+1\}
,

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{n,m}}={f}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}},n,m\models{\phi}(x,y).

(30)

We will prove the following proposition, which we will use repeatedly.

Proposition B.2
.

Let
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{1},{{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{2}
be functions of type
{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}^{{N}+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{D}
, and let
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}
be either a function
{\mathbb{F}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}^{\prime}}
(where
{D}^{\prime}
is not necessarily equal to
{D}
) or
{\mathbb{F}}^{D}\times{\mathbb{F}}^{D}\to{\mathbb{F}}
.
If both
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{1}
and
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{2}
can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
, then so can
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{1})
and
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{1},{{{\mathbf{H}}}}_{2})
.

Proof.

Since the inputs are fixed-dimensional vectors over
{\mathbb{F}}
, the number of possible value combinations is finite. For any given output value
{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, one can enumerate all input configurations for which the function yields
{f}
, and construct a
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formula expressing their disjunction.
∎

This proposition accounts for all components of the transformer architecture, such as the feedforward sublayers, projection operations, dot products, elementwise operations such as addition, scalar operations such as division, the
\exp
function, and the classification head—except for the embedding layer, the summations involved in the attention computation, and the operation of identifying the maximum score introduced by the stabilized softmax.

We handle the embedding layer as follows:

Lemma B.3
.

The embedding layer can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

Proof.

For any dimension
d
and position
n
, the embedding layer outputs a fixed value
{f}
if the symbol at position
n
is mapped to
{f}
by the embedding function
{{{\mathbf{e}}}}
in the
d
-th coordinate. Since
{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}
is finite and
{{{\mathbf{e}}}}
is fixed, we can express this using a disjunction over all symbols that are mapped to
{f}
in coordinate
d
:

{\mathbf{E}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d,n}={f}\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}},n\models\bigvee_{{\texttt{a}}\in\{{\texttt{a}}\mid{{{\mathbf{e}}}}({\texttt{a}})_{d}={f}\}}{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}(x).

(31)

∎

We next address the summations in the attention mechanism.
We begin by establishing the following result:

Proposition B.4
.

{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can count up to a threshold.

Proof.

The threshold counting quantifiers can be defined as follows.

•

The quantifier for “there exists at least one” coincides with the standard bounded existential quantifier:

\exists^{\geq 1}y<x:{\phi}(y)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\exists y<x:{\phi}(y).

(32)

•

“There exist at least two” can be defined as:

\exists^{\geq 2}y<x:{\phi}(y)\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\exists y<x:({\phi}(y)\land\exists x<y:{\phi}(x)).

(33)

•

We can define “exactly one” by combining the above:

\exists^{=1}y<x:{\phi}(y)\\
\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\exists^{\geq 1}y<x:{\phi}(y)\land\lnot\exists^{\geq 2}y<x:{\phi}(y).

(34)

•

Additional counting quantifiers up to a threshold can be defined in a similar fashion.

∎

Soft attention involves two types of summations, which we address in turn.
The first appears in the denominator of the
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
function, used to compute the attention weights
{\bm{\alpha}}
(
Eq.

24
).
Since
\exp
outputs non-negative values, this summation consists entirely of non-negative numbers and can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

Lemma B.5
.

{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can simulate a sum of non-negative fixed-precision floating-point numbers.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{F}_{\geq 0}}
is finite, there exists a finite set of possible sums.
Moreover, because all values are non-negative, for each such sum
there are only finitely many combinations of input values that yield it.
Each combination can be characterized using the threshold-counting
quantifiers introduced in
Proposition

B.4
:
Too many positive entries lead to overflow, while
0
’s do not affect the sum.
Taking a finite disjunction over all such combinations
defines a
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formula that holds exactly when
the prefix sum equals a given value.
∎

The second summation occurs in the computation of attention outputs (
Eq.

26
).
The value matrix
{{{\mathbf{V}}}}\in{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
may include both positive and negative values, so we cannot apply the previous approach directly. However, as pointed out by
Merrill and Sabharwal [31]
:

Proposition B.6
.

Under fixed precision, there exists an upper limit on the number of non-zero entries in the output of a
{{\mathrm{softmax}}}
function. We refer to the upper bound as the
maximum attention span

{N_{\max}}
.

Proof.

This upper bound is given by:

{N_{\max}}=\left\lfloor\frac{\min(1,\max({\mathbb{F}}\setminus\{\infty\}))}{\min({\mathbb{F}}_{>0})}\right\rfloor,

(35)

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is the floor function.
∎

Consequently, the number of nonzero attention weights is bounded. It follows that
Eq.

26
computes the sum over a bounded number of terms, which can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
:

Lemma B.7
.

{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can simulate a sum of a bounded number of fixed-precision floating-point numbers.

Proof.

Since the number of summands is bounded, we can enumerate all possible combinations of inputs. This behavior can be simulated using the threshold counting quantifiers introduced in
Proposition

B.4
.
∎

Finally, we account for the numerical-stability adjustment in
Eq.

25
.
The only additional operation introduced by this stabilization is the identification of the maximum score among a finite set of floating-point values, which can be expressed in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
as follows:

Proposition B.8
.

{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can identify the maximum score
\max_{i<n}{\mathbf{S}}_{n,i}
.

Proof.

Let the ordered set of floating-point values be

{\mathbb{F}}=\{-\infty,{f}_{1},{f}_{2},\ldots,{f}_{K},\infty\}
.
For each
{f}\in{\mathbb{F}}
, let
{\phi}_{f}(x)
be a
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
-formula that holds
iff the value at position
x
equals
{f}
.
Since the set of floating-point numbers is finite, we can enumerate all possible maximum values. For each candidate maximum value
{f}
, we can construct a
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
formula
{\phi}_{f}^{\max}(x)
that holds if and only if the scores among all positions
y<x
are less than or equal to
{f}
, and at least one value equals
{f}
.
They can be defined inductively over the total order of
{\mathbb{F}}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\phi}_{\infty}^{\max}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\exists y<x:{\phi}_{\infty}(y),

(36a)

\displaystyle{\phi}_{{f}_{K}}^{\max}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\left(\lnot\exists y<x:{\phi}_{\infty}^{\max}(y)\right)\land\exists y<x:{\phi}_{{f}_{K}}(y),

(36b)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

\displaystyle{\phi}_{{f}_{1}}^{\max}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\left(\lnot\exists y<x:\bigvee_{{f}\in\{\infty,{f}_{K},\ldots,{f}_{2}\}}{\phi}_{f}^{\max}(y)\right)\land\exists y<x:{\phi}_{{f}_{1}}(y),

(36c)

\displaystyle{\phi}_{-\infty}^{\max}(x)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\left(\lnot\exists y<x:\bigvee_{{f}\in\{\infty,{f}_{K},\ldots,{f}_{1}\}}{\phi}_{f}^{\max}(y)\right)\land\exists y<x:{\phi}_{-\infty}(y).

(36d)

∎

We have now completed the proof of the following theorem:
See
3.2

Proof.

By
Lemma

B.3
, the embedding layer can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
By
Proposition

B.2
, simulation by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
is closed under all subsequent operations in the transformer, except for summations.
By
Lemma

B.5
,
Proposition

B.6
, and
Lemma

B.7
, the summation operations involved in attention computation can also be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
Therefore, the entire transformer can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.
∎

B.3
From
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
to transformers

In this subsection, we show that every
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula can be simulated by a transformer. The central idea is to encode the truth value of the formula at each position as a dedicated coordinate within the transformer’s hidden state: the value
1
represents
{\top}
, and
0
represents
{\bot}
.

The formal definition is as follows.

Definition B.9
.

A
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula
{\psi}
is said to be simulated by a function
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}
of type
{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}^{{N}+1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}^{{D}\times({N}+1)}
if there exists a coordinate
d\in\{1,\ldots,{D}\}
such that for every position
n\in\{1,\ldots,{N}+1\}
and every input string
{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}\in{{{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}^{*}}}
,

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d,n}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }{{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}},n\models{\psi},\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(37)

We introduce a one-hot encoding function
{\llbracket\rrbracket}
, which maps an index to a column vector of zeros except for a single entry equal to
1
at that index. Let
{{\mathbf{0}}}
denote the all-zero column vector.

We first show that the atomic formulas can be translated into an embedding layer.

Lemma B.10
.

The atomic formulas
{\pi}_{\texttt{a}}
for
{\texttt{a}}\in{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}
can be simulated by an embedding layer.

Proof.

We assign a distinct coordinate
d
to every distinct symbol
{\texttt{a}}\in{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}
with a map
{g}\colon{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,{\left|{{\overline{{{\Sigma}}}}}\right|}\}
. Since the biases in attention mechanisms are sometimes omitted, we include a dedicated bias coordinate
{d_{b}}
that always holds a constant value
1
. The embedding layer is then defined as follows:

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(0)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d,n}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }d={d_{b}},\\

Lemma B.12

We start by introducing the base construction and then discuss two issues arising from fixed-precision arithmetic, together with their corresponding fixes.

Base construction.

When too many previous positions with

\min(\mathsf{round}\!\left(1\right))-{f}_{\text{small}}>0
. We rectify the value at

using:
Let
{f}_{\text{small}}
be a small positive floating-point number such that

is also representable,

, and
\min(\mathsf{round}\!\left(1\right))-{f}_{\text{small}}>0

(55)

Proposition B.13
.

If a
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula
{\psi}
can be simulated by a transformer without layer normalization, then it can also be simulated by a transformer with layer normalization.

Proof.

Let
{{{\mathbf{x}}}}
be a vector. Layer normalization is defined as follows:

\text{LayerNorm}({{{\mathbf{x}}}})=\frac{{{{\mathbf{x}}}}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\epsilon}}\cdot\gamma+\beta,

(56)

where
\mu
and
\sigma
are the mean and standard deviation of the elements in
{{{\mathbf{x}}}}

\gamma
and
\beta
are parameters, and
\epsilon
is a small constant for numerical stability.

We introduce, for every logical coordinate
d

{\widehat{d}}
and enforce

{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{{\widehat{d}},n}}=1-{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d,n}}

(57)

for all sublayers
\ell
and positions
n
. This guarantees that for every position
n
,

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}.

(58)

independently of the particular truth assignment.

With this structure, we can choose the parameters of layer normalization such that the operation becomes the identity function (i.e., has no effect):

\gamma=\sqrt{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\epsilon}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon},\quad\beta=\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{2}.

(59)

However, the attention sublayer may produce values close to
1
but not exactly equal to it. Consequently, layer normalization can slightly perturb these values away from the intended Boolean values. We denote by
\mathsf{round}\!\left(0\right)
and
\mathsf{round}\!\left(1\right)
the sets of floating-point numbers that deviate from
0
and
1

\delta
, which is again on the order of the machine precision. This deviation is harmless, since the subsequent feedforward sublayer can rectify the outputs using the following thresholding function: let

be the coordinate we hope to rectify and choose

with

\frac{1}{{f}_{2}-{f}_{1}}\left(\left({{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d_{1},:}}-{f}_{1}\right)-{{\mathrm{ReLU}}}\left({{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d_{1},:}}-{f}_{2}\right)\right).

(60)

To implement this with an FFN, select two hidden units
d^{\prime}_{1},d^{\prime}_{2}
and define:

{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{F1}}}_{d,:}=\begin{cases}{\llbracket d_{1}\rrbracket}^{\top}&\text{if }d=d^{\prime}_{1}\text{ or }d=d^{\prime}_{2},\\
{{\mathbf{0}}}^{\top}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad{\mathbf{b}^{\text{F1}}}=-{f}_{1}{\llbracket d^{\prime}_{1}\rrbracket}-{f}_{2}{\llbracket d^{\prime}_{2}\rrbracket}.

(61)

and

{\bm{\Theta}^{\text{F2}}}_{d,:}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{{f}_{2}-{f}_{1}}({\llbracket d^{\prime}_{1}\rrbracket}^{\top}-{\llbracket d^{\prime}_{2}\rrbracket}^{\top})&\text{if }d=d_{1},\\

(62)

This yields
1
if
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d_{1},n}}\in\mathsf{round}\!\left(1\right)
, and
0
if
{{{{\mathbf{H}}}}^{(\ell)}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}})_{d_{1},n}}\in\mathsf{round}\!\left(0\right)
.
∎

In this section, we introduce three alternative formalisms and show that they are equivalent in expressive power to

C.1
Left-deterministic polynomials

A
monomial
over an alphabet
{{\Sigma}}
is a language of the form:

{{\Sigma}}_{0}^{*}{\texttt{a}}_{1}{{\Sigma}}_{1}^{*}\cdots{\texttt{a}}_{n}{{\Sigma}}_{n}^{*},

(63)

where
{\texttt{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\texttt{a}}_{k}\in{{\Sigma}}

{{\Sigma}}_{0},{{\Sigma}}_{1},\ldots,{{\Sigma}}_{n}\subseteq{{\Sigma}}

A monomial is called

•

left-deterministic
if for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
{\texttt{a}}_{k}\not\in{{\Sigma}}_{k-1}
,

•

right-deterministic
if for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
{\texttt{a}}_{k}\not\in{{\Sigma}}_{k}
,

•

unambiguous
if it is either left-deterministic or right-deterministic.

A
polynomial
is a finite union of monomials, and it is said to be left-deterministic (resp. right-deterministic or unambiguous) if it is a finite disjoint union of left-deterministic (resp. right-deterministic or unambiguous) monomials.

Example:

The language
first

{\texttt{b}}{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}

{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
.

Let

Proof.

The case

We define a pair

Next, define the middle block of

{{{\mathbb{B}}}_{k}}=\left\{n\;\middle|\;\left|{{\textbf{{u}}}}({{\textbf{{s}}}}{{\textbf{{t}}}})^{k}\right|<n<\left|{{\textbf{{u}}}}({{\textbf{{s}}}}{{\textbf{{t}}}})^{K}{{\textbf{{s}}}}\right|\right\},

We proceed by induction on

Base case.

{\psi}
is a Boolean combination of atomic formulas

Induction step.

Assume the claim holds for depth

Let

•

Subcase 1.1:

E.1.3
Results

We compute classification accuracy and report both the maximum and mean values across all runs in
Tab.

1
. The LSTM achieves perfect accuracy on all tasks, consistent with previous work showing that LSTMs can recognize regular languages
[

and implement counting mechanisms

Counter languages.

While several papers have shown that counting quantifiers can be simulated by arbitrary-precision
[
9
,
49
]
or log-precision transformers
[
31
]
, our results indicate that a fixed-precision transformer cannot recognize

. This finding contrasts with
Bhattamishra et al. [3]
, who report that transformers can recognize
{\texttt{a}}^{n}{\texttt{b}}^{n}{\texttt{c}}^{n}
. The discrepancy may stem from our evaluation on longer input lengths.

Regular languages.

In line with
Hahn [17]
, we find that the transformer completely fails to learn the non-star-free regular language
parity
, reaching at most
52.1\%
accuracy. Although
Chiang and Cholak [8]
design a transformer with customized positional encodings capable of recognizing
parity

Star-free languages.

Yang et al. [50]
show that fixed-precision transformers with UHA recognize exactly the star-free languages. Similarly,
Yao et al. [51]
demonstrate that transformers with positional encodings of the form
n/{N}
—which are not always representable under fixed precision—can recognize bounded Dyck languages, a family of star-free languages. In contrast, we prove that transformers with soft attention and NoPE cannot recognize even the simplest bounded Dyck languages (
dyck
-
(1,2)
and
dyck
-
(1,1)
), nor the locally testable language
lt
-2. These results are corroborated by our experiments: the transformer fails to learn any of these three languages. The best performance is on
dyck
-
(1,1)

87.7\%
, which still indicates poor generalization, especially considering the simplicity of the task.

Unambiguous polynomials.

As predicted by our theory, the transformer fails to learn unambiguous polynomials that are not left-deterministic. The model achieves a maximum accuracy of
90.0\%
on
rdp
-1 and
64.8\%
on
last

Left-deterministic polynomials.

Although the transformer cannot learn

Summary

The empirical results align fully with our theoretical predictions. Importantly, we use single-precision (32-bit) floating-point numbers, and the string lengths never exceed the maximum attention span of the transformer. That is, attention can uniformly cover all prior positions without numerical underflow or overflow. Yet, despite these favorable conditions, the transformer exhibits no expressive power beyond what is predicted by our formal characterization.

Appendix F
Additional results

In this section, we present further theoretical results of potential interest.

F.1
Hard attention

Average hard attention (AHA) uniformly attends to positions
m<n
with the maximum score
{\mathbf{S}}_{n,m}
. Formally,
Eq.

24
is modified as follows:

{\bm{\alpha}}_{n,m}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}\left\{{\mathbf{S}}_{n,m}=\max_{i<n}{\mathbf{S}}_{n,i}\right\}}{\sum_{j<n}\mathbbm{1}\left\{{\mathbf{S}}_{n,j}=\max_{i<n}{\mathbf{S}}_{n,i}\right\}},

(76)

where
\mathbbm{1}\left\{\cdot\right\}
is the indicator function.

We now show that AHA is also definable in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

Theorem F.1
.

Every transformer with AHA can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

B.8
,
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
can identify the maximum score
\max_{i<n}{\mathbf{S}}_{n,i}
.
The denominator in
Eq.

76
is a sum of non-negative terms and can therefore be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
via
Lemma

B.5
.
As with the soft-attention case in
Eq.

24
, the resulting attention weights may vanish when the number of positions exceeds the model’s bounded attention span; hence,
Lemma

B.7
applies here as well.
The remaining steps of the construction follow identically to those in
§

B.2
.
∎

The other direction is straightforward.

Theorem F.2
.

Every
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formula can be simulated by a transformer with AHA.

Proof.

Recall that the attention mechanism we constructed in the proof of
Lemma

B.12
uniformly attends to all positions with the highest score
{\mathbf{S}}_{n,m}=0
, effectively making it equivalent to AHA. Therefore,
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
formulas can also be simulated by transformers with AHA.
∎

Now, we turn to UHA, another widely studied attention mechanism where each position
n
attends to a single position
m<n
with the highest
{\mathbf{S}}_{n,m}
. In the case of ties, the
rightmost
position is selected.
Yang et al. [50]
prove that transformers with UHA are as expressive as
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}
.

Combining the results from above, we have:

Corollary F.3
.

Transformers with AHA are as expressive as those with soft attention, which are strictly less expressive than those with UHA.

Proof.

The equality AHA
=
soft attention follows directly from
Theorems

F.1
and
F.2
.

The strict inequality soft attention
<
UHA follows directly from the fact that
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}}]}
is strictly less expressive than
{\textup{{LTL}}[{\mathrel{\textup{{P}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{F}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{S}}}},{\mathrel{\textup{{U}}}}]}
.
∎

F.2
Non-strict future masking

Most commonly used transformers adopt non-strict future-masked soft attention, where each position is allowed to attend to itself. In this case,
Eq.

24
and
Eq.

26
become:

{\bm{\alpha}}_{n,m}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\frac{\exp({\mathbf{S}}_{n,m})}{\sum_{i\leq n}\exp({\mathbf{S}}_{n,i})},

(77)

and

{\mathbf{A}}({{{\mathbf{H}}}}({{\overline{{{\textbf{{w}}}}}}}))_{:,n}\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\sum_{m\leq n}{\bm{\alpha}}_{n,m}{{{\mathbf{V}}}}_{:,m}.

(78)

respectively.

This modification, however, limits the expressiveness of the model.

Theorem F.4
.

Transformers with non-strict future masking are strictly less expressive than those with strict future masking.

Proof.

Since we have already established that transformers with strict masking are as expressive as
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
, it suffices to show that any transformer with non-strict masking is strictly less expressive than
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
.

We begin by observing that transformers with non-strict masking can be simulated by
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
. The required modification to
Proposition

B.4
involves including the current position in the threshold counting quantifiers:

\displaystyle\exists^{\geq 1}y\leq x:{\phi}(y)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\left(\exists^{\geq 1}y<x:{\phi}(y)\right)\lor{\phi}(x),

(79a)

\displaystyle\exists^{\geq 2}y\leq x:{\phi}(y)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{\tiny def}}}{{=}}}\left(\exists^{\geq 2}y<x:{\phi}(y)\right)\lor\left(\left(\exists^{\geq 1}y<x:{\phi}(y)\right)\land{\phi}(x)\right),

(79b)

\displaystyle\;\;\vdots

Next, we construct a language that is definable in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
but cannot be recognized by a transformer with non-strict masking. Consider the language
{\texttt{b}}{\texttt{b}}{{{{\Sigma}}^{*}}}
, which can be defined in
{\textup{{PFO}}^{2}[\mathord{<}]}
by the following sentence:

\exists x:({\pi}_{\texttt{b}}(x)\land\left(\exists^{=1}y<x:{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}(y)\right)\land\neg\exists y<x:\neg{\pi}_{\texttt{b}}(y)).

(80)

On the other hand, given any transformer
{{{\mathbf{H}}}}
with non-strict masking, and for any string

In formal logic, a numerical predicate is a predicate that depends solely on the positions in a string, not the symbols in those positions. Numerical predicates that depend on one (resp. two) position(s) are referred to as unary (resp. binary) numerical predicates. For instance, the relation
<
is a binary numerical predicate.

We now show that all absolute (resp. relative) positional encodings can be simulated by unary (resp. binary) numerical predicates.

Theorem F.5
.

Let
{{{{\mathbf{p}}}}}
be an absolute (resp. relative) positional encoding and

be a regular language. There exists a collection of unary (resp. binary) numerical predicates
\mathcal{P}
such that the following assertions are equivalent:

1.

{\mathbb{L}}
can be recognized by a transformer with positional encoding
{{{{\mathbf{p}}}}}
.

2.

{\mathbb{L}}
is definable by

, i.e.,

Positional encodings, under fixed precision, have a finite image. Therefore, for every dimension

or

r(n,m)={\top}\quad\text{ if }\quad{{{{\mathbf{p}}}}}(n,m)_{d}={f}.

(85)

Appendix G
Related work

The expressivity of transformers in the context of formal methods has been extensively studied in recent literature
[
42
]
. Various transformer variants have been explored, and different assumptions have been made to enhance the expressivity of transformers.

Chain of thought (CoT).

CoT reasoning, which involves generating intermediate steps before arriving at a final answer, has become a popular approach.
Pérez et al. [38]
demonstrated that a transformer with both an encoder and a decoder, when allowed a polynomial number of intermediate computation steps, is Turing complete. Similarly,
Merrill and Sabharwal [33]
,
Nowak et al. [35]
,
Li et al. [27]
show that the expressivity of transformers can be improved with various forms of CoT reasoning. In this work, along with many others, we do not allow intermediate steps, which restricts expressivity. In such a case,
Hao et al. [18]
,
Merrill et al. [34]
,
Merrill and Sabharwal [32]
,
Chiang et al. [9]
,
Yang and Chiang [49]
have established various upper bounds on expressivity.

Non-fixed precision.

If a transformer is allowed arbitrary precision, or if the precision increases with input length,
Bhattamishra et al. [3]
proved that it is more expressive than simplified and stateless counter machines. Additionally,
Chiang et al. [9]
and
Yang and Chiang [49]
showed that a transformer with such precision can recognize any language definable by temporal counting logic. In contrast, our work focuses on the scenario where precision is fixed, which imposes more limitations on expressivity.

Bespoke positional encodings.

Some prior studies have employed bespoke positional encodings, many of which cannot be represented under fixed precision, to overcome certain limitations of transformers. For example,
Chiang and Cholak [8]
used the encoding
n/{N}
to enable transformers to recognize parity, and
Barcelo et al. [2]
demonstrated that transformers with a specially designed positional encoding can achieve a lower bound of
{\textup{{FO}}[\mathord{<}]}
with unary numerical predicates. In contrast, our constructions and proofs do not rely on any form of positional encoding.

Hard attention.

Two forms of hard attention have been explored in the literature.
Yang et al. [50]
showed that masked fixed-precision transformers with UHA and NoPE recognize exactly the star-free languages.
Barcelo et al. [2]
established a similar lower bound for transformers with AHA and certain positional encodings. Prior to our work, it was unclear, under fixed precision, whether these two hard attention mechanisms were more or less expressive than, or comparable to, the standard soft attention. Surprisingly, we find that UHA is strictly more expressive than soft attention, while AHA is as expressive as soft attention.

Appendix H
Limitations

The primary limitation of this work lies in the omission of positional encodings. While we briefly discuss their role as numerical predicates, the exact numerical predicates simulated by commonly used positional encodings remain unknown. We hope to explore this in future work. Nevertheless, we believe it is important to first understand the expressivity of a barebones transformer architecture, as this forms the foundation for systematically incorporating various forms of positional encoding later on.

Another limitation—particularly from an empirical perspective—is our stringent evaluation criteria for transformer performance. While certain levels of accuracy might be considered successful in empirical studies, we require perfect accuracy. This is motivated by a formal perspective: a model (e.g., an automaton or logical formula) either recognizes a language or it does not; anything short of perfection is regarded as failure, suggesting that some form of approximation or shortcut has been employed. We interpret our results as identifying a class of tasks that transformers have the full capacity to solve.
