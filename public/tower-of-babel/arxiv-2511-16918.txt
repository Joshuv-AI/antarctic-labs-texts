Low-Sensitivity Matching via Sampling from Gibbs Distributions
Yuichi Yoshida, Zihan Zhang

Abstract
In this work, we study the maximum matching problem from the perspective of sensitivity. The sensitivity of an algorithm A A on a graph G G is defined as the maximum Wasserstein distance between the output distributions of A A on G G and on G − e G-e , where G − e G-e is the graph obtained by deleting an edge e e from G G . The maximum is taken over all edges e e , and the underlying metric for the Wasserstein distance is the Hamming distance. We first show that for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , there exists a polynomial-time ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm with sensitivity Δ O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)} , where Δ \Delta is the maximum degree of the input graph. The algorithm is based on sampling from the Gibbs distribution over matchings and runs in time O ε , Δ ​ ( m ​ log ⁡ m ) O_{\varepsilon,\Delta}(m\log m) , where m m is the number of edges in the graph. This result significantly improves the previously known sensitivity bounds. Next, we present significantly faster algorithms for planar and bipartite graphs as a function of ε \varepsilon and Δ \Delta , which run in time poly ⁡ ( n / ε ) \mathrm{poly}(n/\varepsilon) . This improvement is achieved by designing a more efficient algorithm for sampling matchings from the Gibbs distribution in these graph classes, which improves upon the previous best in terms of running time. Finally, for general graphs with potentially unbounded maximum degree, we show that there exists a polynomial-time ( 1 − ε ) (1-\varepsilon) -approximation algorithm with sensitivity n ⋅ ( ε − 1 ​ log ⁡ n ) O ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \sqrt{n}\cdot(\varepsilon^{-1}\log n)^{O(1/\varepsilon)} , improving upon the previous best bound of O ⁡ ( n 1 / ( 1 + ε 2 ) ) O(n^{1/(1+\varepsilon^{2})}) .

Introduction

In this work, we study the sensitivity of the maximum matching problem, which is recently proposed by Varma and Yoshida
[
40
]
.
Roughly speaking, the sensitivity of an algorithm quantifies how much its output changes, measured in Hamming distance, when a single edge is removed from the input graph.
For randomized algorithms, sensitivity is defined using the Wasserstein distance, where the underlying metric between outputs is the Hamming distance (see
Section

2
for details).
Note that the trivial upper bound on the sensitivity for the maximum matching problem is
O(n)
, where
n
is the number of vertices, because the matching size is
O(n)
.
Although sensitivity is an intriguing concept on its own, it is tightly linked to differential privacy
[
18
]
and the
non-signaling
model
[
1

3
,
15
,
20
]
(see
Section

1.2

Moreover, low sensitivity is crucial for enabling trustworthy decision-making and reliable knowledge discovery.
See

[

36

38

42

for algorithms with small average sensitivity, a slightly weaker notion of sensitivity, applied to data mining and machine learning problems.

Although maximum matchings can be computed in polynomial time, it is known that any such algorithm must have sensitivity
\Omega(n)

[
40
]
.
On the other hand, there exist algorithms with low sensitivity that achieve approximate solutions: specifically, a

In this work, we achieve a significant improvement in the sensitivity of a

We use

Then, we show the following:

For any

\min\left\{\exp(\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)})m\log n+O(n),\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n^{2}m\log n\right\}.

Our algorithm draws inspiration from statistical physics. Specifically, we consider the Gibbs distribution over matchings, where a matching

When

Hence, the exponential dependence on

Although the sensitivity in

To address this, we show that our algorithm can be significantly accelerated for graph classes where perfect matchings can be efficiently sampled.

1.1
Technical Overview

Bounded-Degree Graphs

We first discuss the approximation ratio of the matching sampled from the Gibbs distribution.
Let
m_{G}(\lambda)=\sum_{k\geq 0}m_{k}\lambda^{k}
be the
matching generating polynomial
of a graph
G
, where
m_{k}
denotes the number of matchings of size
k
in
G
. The expected size of a matching drawn from the Gibbs distribution with parameter
\lambda
is given by

\frac{\sum_{M}|M|\lambda^{|M|}}{\sum_{M}\lambda^{|M|}}=\frac{\lambda m_{G}^{\prime}(\lambda)}{m_{G}(\lambda)},

where
M
runs over matchings of
G
and
m_{G}^{\prime}
is the derivative of
m_{G}
.
Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\nu}
be the (negative) roots of
m_{G}
, where
\nu
is the size of a maximum matching in
G
.
Then, by a standard calculation, the expected matching size can be expressed as

\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}}.

Using the celebrated result of Heilmann and Lieb on the location of the roots of the matching polynomial in bounded-degree graphs
[
24
]
, we can show that a
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the roots
\lambda_{i}
satisfy
|\lambda_{i}|\leq\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}
, where
\Delta
is the maximum degree of
G
. Hence, by choosing
\lambda=\Delta^{\Theta(1/\varepsilon)}/\varepsilon=\Delta^{\Theta(1/\varepsilon)}
, we ensure that the expected matching size achieves a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation to the maximum matching size.

To bound the sensitivity of the Gibbs distribution, we establish a recursion on the sensitivity upper bounds for graphs of different sizes.
Similar techniques have been used to bound the spectral independence of the Gibbs distribution on matchings, as well as on other Holant problems and edge colorings
[
9
,
13
,
12
]
.

Before explaining the details, we introduce some notation.
Let
\mu_{E;G}
denote the Gibbs distribution on matchings of a graph
G=(V,E)
.
For any
F\subseteq E
, let
\mu_{F;G}
be the marginal distribution of
\mu_{E;G}
on
2^{F}
(see
Definition

2.9
for detail).
For an edge
i\in E
, we write
i\leftarrow+
to indicate that
i
is matched and
i\leftarrow-
to indicate that
i
is unmatched.
We call such an event a
pinning
.
It is not hard to show that bounding the Wasserstein distance
W_{1}\left(\mu_{E;G},\mu_{E;G-i}\right)
for an edge
i\in E
can be reduced to bounding
W_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)
.

A key property of the Gibbs distribution that yields low sensitivity is that conditioning on a pinning is equivalent to performing certain edge deletions on the underlying graph.
Hence, to bound the Wasserstein distance
W_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)
between the two distributions
\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow+}
and
\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow-}
, we expand the distributions by the law of total probability over the matched/unmatched edges in
N(i)
, which gives a weighted sum of Wasserstein distances on distributions conditioned on pinnings of
N(i)
, where
N(i)
is the set of edges incident to
i
.
Because of the equivalence of pinning and edge deletions, each of these conditional distributions is simply a Gibbs distribution on a smaller graph.
Thus, the Wasserstein distance between pinnings of the Gibbs distribution on
G
can be controlled by a weighted sum of Wasserstein distances on those smaller graphs.
If this sum has a sufficient decay property, we obtain a universal bound on the Wasserstein distance between pinnings.

Formally, to track the growth of the Wasserstein distance at each step of the recursion, we define

\kappa_{s,\Delta}=\max_{G,i\in E}W_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right),

where the maximum is taken over all graphs
G
with at most
s
edges and maximum degree at most
\Delta
.
Intuitively,
\kappa_{s,\Delta}
represents the worst-case Wasserstein distance when the graph has at most
s
edges.
Using our expansion into weighted sums, we obtain a recursion of the form

\displaystyle\kappa_{s,\Delta}\leq\begin{cases}0&\text{if }s=1,\\
\alpha(\kappa_{s-1,\Delta}+1)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\alpha=\lambda\Delta/(1+\lambda\Delta)
.
Solving this shows that

\kappa_{s,\Delta}\leq\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}=O(\lambda\Delta).

Because we choose
\lambda=\Delta^{\Theta(1/\varepsilon)}
, we obtain the desired sensitivity bound.

Planar and Bipartite Graphs

Recent work
[
9
]
showed that sensitivity can also be used to bound the spectral independence of the Gibbs distribution, which in turn yields a mixing-time bound for the Glauber dynamics.
An intriguing observation is that if we adopt the same recursion as in the bounded-degree setting, but measure the Wasserstein distance using the Hamming distance of matched
vertices
(as opposed to edges), then the sensitivity can be bounded by
2
, independent of the parameter
\lambda
and the maximum degree!

Motivated by this observation, we propose a new form of Glauber dynamics that operates on
matched vertices
.
In contrast to the standard edge-based Glauber dynamics, our approach employs a
2
-site update rule to maintain the connectivity of the Markov chain.
A similar Markov chain was studied in
[
2
]
to count the number of size-
k
matchings on planar graphs, and their method can be extended to approximately sample from the Gibbs distribution on planar graphs without dependence on
\lambda
.
But the time complexity of this sampling is high with respect to the size of the graph, as it requires a reduction from sampling to counting, followed by another reduction back to sampling.
We bypass these reductions, and our algorithm achieves significantly better time complexity with respect to the size of the graph, while maintaining only a logarithmic dependence on
\lambda
.

Concretely, we define a
2
-Glauber dynamics, denoted
\mathcal{P}_{G}
, on the distribution of matched vertices induced by the Gibbs distribution of matchings, which we denote by
\mu_{V;G}
.
We then show that
\mathcal{P}_{G}
is connected and converges to
\mu_{V;G}
.
By slightly modifying our earlier recursion for bounded-degree graphs, we derive an
O(1)
bound on the sensitivity of
\mu_{V;G}
and obtain a mixing time of

O\left(n^{2}\left(m\,\log\lambda+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

Although a similar spectral independence bound has been established in
[
2
]
, this work presents the first sensitivity bound for
\mu_{V;G}
. Notably, our proof is grounded in the combinatorial structure of the distribution, differing fundamentally from the zero-freeness-based approach employed in
[
2
]
.

Furthermore, if there exists a fully polynomial-time (deterministic or randomized) approximation scheme (FPTAS/FPRAS) for counting perfect matchings in subgraphs of
G
, as is known for planar and bipartite graphs, we can implement
\mathcal{P}_{G}
in polynomial time with sufficient accuracy to approximate
\mu_{V;G}
.
After obtaining a sample
U
from
\mu_{V;G}
, we can then sample from
\mu_{E;G}
by approximately sampling a perfect matching of
G[U]
, where
G[U]
is a subgraph of
G
induced by
U
, using a standard reduction that employs the approximate counting algorithm for perfect matchings on subgraphs of
G
.

General Graphs

To obtain a low-sensitivity algorithm for general graphs, we consider sparsifying the input graph into a low-degree subgraph that approximately preserves the maximum matching size, while ensuring the sparsification process itself has low sensitivity.
To this end, we leverage the recent notion of an
\varepsilon
-degree sparsifier

[
8
]
.
Intuitively, a subgraph
H
of
G=(V,E)
is an
\varepsilon
-degree sparsifier if its maximum degree is small, say,
O(\log n)
, and the maximum matching size in
H
approximates that in
G
within a factor of
\varepsilon
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be an optimal solution to the LP relaxation of the maximum matching problem with odd set constraints.
That is, we maximize
\sum_{e\in E}x(e)
subject to:

\sum_{e\ni v}x(e)\leq 1\quad\text{for all }v\in V,\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{e\in E(G[B])}x(e)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{|B|}{2}\right\rfloor\quad\text{for all odd-sized }B\subseteq V.

It was shown in
[
8
]
that sampling each edge
e\in E
independently with probability
\Theta(x(e)/\gamma)
yields an
\varepsilon
-degree sparsifier of
G
with maximum degree
\Theta(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)
with high probability, where
\gamma=\Theta(\varepsilon^{2}/\log n)
.

However, this sparsification process does not inherently have low sensitivity: small changes in the input graph can lead to large changes in the LP solution
x
.
To mitigate this, we introduce a entropy regularization term
\alpha\sum_{v\in V}H(\{x(e)\}_{e\in E:v\in E})
to stabilize the LP, where
H(\{x(e)\}_{e\in F})=\sum_{e\in F}x(e)\log(1/x(e))
is the entropy function and
\alpha=\Theta(\varepsilon/\log n)
.
This does not significantly degrade the maximum matching size of the sparsifier
H
.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
and
\tilde{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
denote the solutions to the regularized LPs for
G=(V,E)
and
\tilde{G}=G-e:=(V,E\setminus\{e\})
for some
e\in E
, respectively.
We can then show that
\|x-\tilde{x}\|_{1}=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n/\alpha})=\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon^{-1}n})
.
As a result, the Wasserstein distance between the edge sets of the corresponding sparsifiers
H
and
\tilde{H}
is also
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon^{-1}n}/\gamma)=\varepsilon^{-O(1)}\sqrt{n}
.
Finally, by applying our algorithm for low-degree graphs (
Theorem

1.1
) to the sparsifier, we obtain
Theorem

1.4
.

1.2
Related Work

Sensitivity

In addition to the sensitivity upper bounds for maximum matching discussed above, it is known that any deterministic constant-factor approximation algorithm must incur a sensitivity of
\Omega(\log^{*}n)
, even on constant-degree graphs
[
43
]
, where
\log^{*}n
denotes the iterated logarithm.
This result highlights the necessity of randomness in establishing
Theorem

1.1
, which achieves constant sensitivity for constant-degree graphs.

There are few problems for which algorithms with low sensitivity are known to exist.
One example is the minimum
s
-
t
cut problem, for which there is an algorithm that achieves an additive error of
O(n^{2/3})
with sensitivity
O(n^{2/3})

[
40
]
.
Another example is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the shortest path problem with sensitivity
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log^{3}n)
, though in this case, sensitivity is measured with respect to edge contractions rather than edge deletions
[
34
]
.
(Indeed, it is known that any algorithm that outputs an explicit
s
-
t
path must have sensitivity
\Omega(n)
with respect to edge deletions; see
[
40
]
.)

The idea of using the Gibbs distribution has previously been applied to design an algorithm for the minimum cut problem with low average sensitivity, where the average is taken over the choice of deleted edges
[
40
]
.
However, the analysis of the approximation ratio, sensitivity, and running time in that setting relies heavily on Karger’s cut-counting lemma, which states that the number of cuts of size at most
\alpha
times the minimum cut is at most
O(n^{2\alpha})

[
30
]
.
In contrast, for the matching problem, such structure does not hold: there exist graphs with a unique maximum matching, yet an exponential number of matchings of size just one less (see
Section

6
).
As a result, we take a completely different approach to analyzing the approximation ratio, sensitivity, and running time in our setting.

Sampling from the Gibbs Distribution over Matchings

We discuss the problem of sampling from the Gibbs distribution over matchings, where
\lambda
is the model parameter,
\delta
is the desired total variation distance, and
\Delta
denotes the maximum degree of the graph.
In their classical work
[
27
]
, Jerrum and Sinclair established the rapid mixing of Glauber dynamics for this model. The best known mixing time until recently was

O\left(nm\max\{1,\lambda\}\left(n(\log n+\log\max\{1,\lambda\})+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)

for arbitrary graphs
[
28
, Proposition 12.4]
.
Very recently, this was improved to
O(m\log n)
on all bounded-degree graphs
[
13
,
14
]
.
Based on our own calculation, the precise mixing time is

\exp\left(O(\lambda^{3}\Delta^{3}\log(\lambda^{2}\Delta^{3}))\right)\cdot m\left(\log n+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Our algorithm for bounded-degree graphs (
Theorem

1.1
) builds on this result.

For planar graphs, it has been shown that one can sample a matching in time
\mathrm{poly}(n,\log(1/\delta))
even from the monomer-dimer model
[
2
]
, where each matching
M
is sampled with probability proportional to
\prod_{e\in M}w(e)\cdot\prod_{v\in V\setminus V(M)}\lambda(v)
for weight functions
w:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\lambda:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Differential Privacy

Sensitivity is closely related to differential privacy
[
18
]
.
In the context of graphs, for
\eta>0
, a randomized algorithm
A
is said to be
(\eta,\delta)
-(edge) differentially private
if, for any graph
G,G^{\prime}
different by only one edge, and any set of outcomes
\mathcal{O}
of the algorithm, the following inequality holds:

\mathbf{Pr}\left[A(G)\in\mathcal{O}\right]\leq\exp(\eta)\cdot\mathbf{Pr}\left[A(G)\in\mathcal{O}\right]+\delta.

One way to design a differentially private algorithm for releasing statistics of a matching is to first compute a matching
M
using
Theorem

1.1
, then compute a statistic of interest on
M
, and finally add Laplace noise calibrated to its sensitivity.
To guarantee differential privacy in this way, we need the output of the algorithms to be stable not only in expectation but also with a high probability.
We can show that the algorithm presented in
Theorem

1.1
has such a guarantee (the proof is postponed to
Appendix

C
):

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
and let
G,G^{\prime}
be graphs different on only one edge with maximum degree
\Delta
.
Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
the algorithm presented in
Theorem

1.1
with parameter
\varepsilon
.
Then for any
c>0
, there exists a coupling between the output distributions
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(G)
and
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(G^{\prime})
such that, with probability at least
1-n^{-c}
, the Hamming distance between their outputs is bounded by
c\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\log n
.

For instance, this approach can be used to design an
(\eta,n^{-c})
-differentially private algorithm that releases the number of matched vertices with a given attribute with additive error of
c\eta^{-1}\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\log n
for graphs of maximum degree
\Delta
.

We mention that it is known that outputting any subgraph, including a matching, is impossible under standard differential privacy.
Consequently, previous works have explored relaxed notions, such as joint differential privacy
[
26
]
, or the release of an implicit representation of a matching, from which each node can locally recover its matched edge using its own private information
[
17
]
.

Non-Signaling Model

Although sensitivity is an intriguing concept on its own, it is tightly linked to the
non-signaling
model
[
1
,
3
,
15
,
20
]
.
In this framework, for a graph
G=(V,E)
, an algorithm may output any probability distribution, provided it respects the non-signaling principle: for every vertex set
S\subseteq V
, changes made to the input graph at distance
t+1
or more from
S
must leave the output distribution on
S
unchanged.
The integer
t
is called the model’s
locality
.
Because it imposes only this non-signaling constraint, the model is strictly more powerful than any “physical” synchronous distributed model with the same locality: it subsumes the classical
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model
[
37
]
, the quantum-
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model
[
20
]
, and the bounded-dependence model
[
25
]
, among others
[
1
]
.
It is recently observed in
[
19
]
that, if there is a distribution in the non-signaling model with locality
t
on graphs with maximum degree at most
\Delta
, then we can use it to design an algorithm sensitivity
\Delta^{O(t)}
.

Recent work has shown that the KMW lower bounds
[
16
,
32
]
, originally established for the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, also hold in the non-signaling model
[
4
]
.
3
3

3

They showed this fact for fractional problems, but it holds for constant-factor approximation to the maximum matching problem because the problem has an integrality gap of two, which is constant.

Consequently, any non-signaling distribution that achieves a
(1/\mathrm{polylog}\Delta)
-approximation for the maximum matching problem must have locality
\Omega(\log\Delta/\log\log\Delta)
.
Even if such an optimal-locality distribution existed, the general correspondence between the non-signaling model and sensitivity
[
19
]
would still give only the sensitivity bound
\Delta^{O(\log\Delta/\log\log\Delta)}
for
(1/\mathrm{polylog}\Delta)
-approximation.
By contrast,
Theorem

1.1
gives sensitivity
\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}
for
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation, which implies that the Gibbs distribution is strictly more powerful than any non-signaling distribution for the maximum matching problem in terms of the trade-off between approximation ratio and sensitivity.

in the Gibbs distribution must be at least
2^{\Omega(1/\varepsilon)}
to achieve a
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation.

2
Preliminaries

All the proofs in this section are postponed to
Appendix

B
.

2.1
Graph Theory

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, a matching is a set of edges in
E
such that no two edges share a common vertex.
We denote the set of all matchings in
G
by
\mathcal{M}(G)
.
Let
\nu(G)
denote the maximum matching size of
G
.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we denote its incident edges in
G
by
E_{G}(v)
.
We omit the subscript if it is clear from the context.
For a vertex set
S\subseteq V
, let
G[S]
denote the subgraph induced by
S
.
For a set of edges
F\subseteq E
, let
V(F)\subseteq V
denote the set of all vertices incident to at least one edge in
F
.
We denote by
G[F]
the subgraph of
G
consisting of
F
and vertices incident to
F
, i.e.,
G[F]=(V(F),F)
.
We denote by
G\setminus F
the subgraph obtained by deleting
F
from
G
.
When
F=\left\{e\right\}
,
G\setminus F
is also denoted by
G-e
.
Let
\Delta(v)
denote the degree of vertex
v\in V
.
We say an edge
e
in a graph
G
is
pendant
if one of its endpoints is of degree
1
.

2.2
Sensitivity

In this section, we formally define the sensitivity of a graph algorithm.

Definition 2.1

(Coupling between distributions)
.

For two distributions
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
on the same probability space
\Omega
, we say a distribution
\pi
on
\Omega\times\Omega
is a
coupling
between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
if the marginal distribution of
\pi
on the first and second coordinates are
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
, respectively.
We denote the set of all couplings between
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
by
\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
.

Definition 2.2

(Hamming distances on matchings)
.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
, we define two metrics on
2^{E}
:

d_{V}(F_{1},F_{2}):=|V(F_{1})\mathbin{\triangle}V(F_{2})|,\quad d_{E}(F_{1},F_{2}):=|F_{1}\mathbin{\triangle}F_{2}|,

for any
F_{1},F_{2}\in 2^{E}
,
where
\triangle
denotes the symmetric difference.

Definition 2.3

(Wasserstein distance)
.

For two distributions
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
on the same probability space
\Omega
with metric
d
, the (
1
-)Wasserstein distance is defined by

W^{d}_{1}\left(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\right):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathbf{E}_{(x,y)\sim\pi}\left[d(x,y)\right].

When
\Omega=\mathcal{M}(G)
for a graph
G=(V,E)
, we denote

W^{d_{E}}_{1}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
and
W^{d_{V}}_{1}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
by
W^{E}_{1}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
and
W^{V}_{1}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
, respectively.
We omit the superscript when the metric is clear from the context.

Definition 2.4

(Sensitivity)
.

For a (randomized) algorithm
A
that accepts a graph as an input
and outputs an edge set, we define the sensitivity of
A
on a graph
G
by

\max_{e\in E(G)}W^{E}_{1}\left(A(G),A(G-e)\right).

2.3
Wasserstein Distance

We regard a distribution
\mu
on a finite set
\Omega
as a function from
\Omega
to
\mathbb{R}
whose function value on
x
is
\mu(x)
.
Hence, we can perform addition and scalar multiplication on distributions on a common space (though the resulting function may not represent a distribution).
The following lemma plays a vital role in this work, whose proof can be found in e.g., Theorem 1.14 in
[
41
]
.

Lemma 2.5

(Kantrovich-Rubinstein duality theorem)
.

For any two distributions
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
on a metric space
\Omega
, we have

W_{1}\left(\pi,\pi^{\prime}\right)=\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\langle f,\pi-\pi^{\prime}\rangle=\sup_{f\in L^{1}(\Omega)}\sum_{x\in\Omega}f(x)(\pi(x)-\pi^{\prime}(x)),

where
L^{1}(\Omega):=\left\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}\;\middle|\;\forall x,y\in\Omega:|f(x)-f(y)|\leq d(x,y)\right\}
denotes the set of
1
-Lipschitz functions.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mu,\mu^{\prime}
be two distributions on the same finite metric space
\Omega
.
Suppose that there exist two sets
\left\{\mu_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
\left\{\mu^{\prime}_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
of distributions on
\Omega
and a set
\left\{\lambda_{i}\right\}_{1\leq i\leq n}

of non-negative real numbers such that

\mu-\mu^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\mu_{i}-\mu^{\prime}_{i}).

Then, we have

W_{1}\left(\mu,\mu^{\prime}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}W_{1}\left(\mu_{i},\mu^{\prime}_{i}\right).

One immediate corollary is the triangle inequality:

Corollary 2.7

(Triangle inequality)
.

Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
be distributions on a common finite metric space
\Omega
,
We have

W_{1}\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{n}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}W_{1}\left(\mu_{i},\mu_{i+1}\right).

2.4
Gibbs Distributions

We recall the definition of the Gibbs distribution for matchings, a special case of the monomer-dimer model, and derive its basic properties.

Definition 2.8

(Gibbs distribution on matchings)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
For a parameter
\lambda>0
, the Gibbs distribution
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
over matchings in
G
is defined as

\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(M):=\frac{1}{Z}\lambda^{|M|}

for every matching
M\in\mathcal{M}(G)
, where
Z:=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\lambda^{|M|}
is the partition function.
When
\lambda
is clear from the context, we sometimes omit
\lambda
and
simply write
\mu_{E;G}
.

Definition 2.9

(Marginal and conditional distribution)
.

The marginal distribution of
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
on a subset
F\subseteq E
, denoted
\mu_{F;\lambda,G}
, is defined as a distribution over
\mathcal{M}(G[F])
such that

\mu_{F;\lambda,G}(N):=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}(G)\\
M\cap F=N\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|M|}

for every matching
N\in\mathcal{M}(G[F])
, where
Z:=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\lambda^{|M|}
is the same partition function as
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
.

We denote the events that an
e\in E
is matched and is not matched by
e\leftarrow+
and
e\leftarrow-
, respectively, which are referred to as
pinnings
.
With a bit abuse of notation, for a subset
F\in E
, we denote by
F\leftarrow-
(resp.,
F\not\leftarrow-)
the event that none (resp., some) of the edges in
F
is matched.
For a vertex
v\in V
, we denote by
v\leftarrow+
(resp.,
v\leftarrow-)
the event that some (resp., none) of the edges incident to
v
is matched.

We write the event that a conditional distribution is conditioned on as
a superscript. For example
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{e\leftarrow+}
is the distribution
conditioned on
e
is matched. When we condition on multiple pinnings
at the same time, we simply stack the superscripts up.

One basic property of the Gibbs distribution is the following lemma that asserts the equivalence between graph modification and pinning.
For an edge
i\in E
(we often use
i
and
j
to denote edges when analyzing properties of the Gibbs distribution), let
N(i)
denote the set of edges that are incident to
i
, excluding
i
itself.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
i\in E
be an edge,
\lambda>0
,

E^{\prime}\subseteq E
and
F\subseteq N(i)
. Then

\mu_{E^{\prime};\lambda,G}^{\tau,i\leftarrow-}=\mu_{E^{\prime}-i;\lambda,G-i}^{\tau},\quad\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{\tau,i\leftarrow+}=\mu_{E\setminus F-i;\lambda,G\setminus F}^{\tau,i\leftarrow+},\quad\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{\tau,i\leftarrow+}=\mu_{E\setminus N(i)-i;\lambda,G\setminus N(i)-i}^{\tau}.

Since for a vertex
v\in V
there is at most one edge matched in
E(v)
,
we can decompose the Gibbs distribution according to the
matched edges in
E(v)
, resulting in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
v\in V
be a vertex, and
\lambda>0
be the parameter of the Gibbs distributions.
Then, we have

\mu_{E;G}=\sum_{i\in E(v)}\mu_{E;G}(i\leftarrow+)\mu_{E;G}^{i\leftarrow+}+\mu_{E;G}(E(v)\leftarrow-)\mu_{E;G}^{E(v)\leftarrow-}.

2.5
Glauber dynamics

To sample from the Gibbs distribution, a natural approach is to use a Markov chain whose stationary distribution coincides with the target distribution.
In this context, one of the most widely studied dynamics is the Glauber dynamics, a local Markov chain that updates the configuration by modifying a small part at each step:

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution over
2^{\Omega}
for some finite set
\Omega
and
1\leq k\leq|\Omega|
, we define the
k
-site Glauber dynamics
\mathcal{P}
of
\mu
as a Markov chain defined on the support of
\mu
as follows.
Let
\binom{\Omega}{k}
denote the family of subsets of size
k
.
For
A\in\binom{\Omega}{k}
and
x\in 2^{\Omega}
, define
B_{A}(x):=\left\{y\in 2^{\Omega}\;\middle|\;x\mathbin{\triangle}y\subseteq A\right\}
.
Then for any
x,y
with non-zero probability we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x\to y):=\binom{|\Omega|}{k}^{-1}\sum_{A\in\binom{\Omega}{k}:y\in B_{A}(x)}\frac{\mu(y)}{\sum_{z\in B_{A}(x)}\mu(z)}.

Intuitively, each step of
P
with the current state
x
does the following:

1.

uniformly sample a size-
k
subset
A
of
\Omega
;

2.

transit to the next state
y
with probability proportional to
\mu(y)
under the condition that
x\mathbin{\triangle}y\subseteq A
.

Notice that if
\mathcal{P}
is the
k
-site Glauber dynamics of
\mu
, then
\mu(x)\mathcal{P}(x\to y)=\mu(y)\mathcal{P}(y\to x)
for all
x,y\in 2^{\Omega}
, and hence if
\mathcal{P}
is irreducible, then
\mu
is its unique stationary distribution, and we have
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{0}\mathcal{P}^{t}=\mu
for any initial distribution
\mu_{0}
on the support of
\mu
.
What we care about is the speed of convergence.
Letting
\delta>0
, we define the
mixing time
of
\mathcal{P}
as

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathcal{P},\delta):=\min\left\{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\;\middle|\;\forall\mu_{0}:d_{\mathrm{tv}}(\mu_{0}\mathcal{P}^{t},\mu)<\delta\right\}.

3
Low-Sensitivity Algorithms for Bounded-Degree Graphs

In this section, we prove
Theorem

1.1
.

We consider an (inefficient) algorithm that, given a graph
G=(V,E)
, simply outputs a matching
M\subseteq E
sampled from
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
for
\lambda=\Delta^{\Theta(1/\varepsilon)}
.
We show that this algorithm has approximation ratio
1-\varepsilon
in Section
3.1
, and we analyze its sensitivity in Section
3.2
.
Then in Section
3.3
, we discuss a modification to the algorithm that enables it to run in polynomial time.

3.1
Approximation Guarantee

In this section, we show the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

We have

\mathbf{E}_{M\sim\mu_{\lambda}}\left[|M|\right]\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)\lambda}{\lambda+(4\Delta)^{2\varepsilon^{-1}}}\cdot\nu(G).

In particular, for
\lambda\geq 2\varepsilon^{-1}(4\Delta)^{2\varepsilon^{-1}}
, we have

\mathbf{E}_{M\sim\mu_{\lambda}}\left[|M|\right]\geq\frac{1-\varepsilon/2}{1+\varepsilon/2}\cdot\nu(G)\geq(1-\varepsilon)\nu(G).

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
n
vertices with maximum degree
\Delta
.
Let
m_{k}
be the number of matchings of size
k
in
G
.
The
matching polynomial

M_{G}
and
matching generating polynomial

m_{G}
are defined as

\displaystyle M_{G}(x):=\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{k}m_{k}x^{n-2k}\quad\text{and}\quad m_{G}(x):=\sum_{k\geq 0}m_{k}x^{k},

respectively.
Note that
M_{G}(x)=x^{n}m_{G}(-x^{-2})
holds and that
m_{G}(\lambda)
coincides with the partition function of the Gibbs distribution.
We start with the following simple fact:

Lemma 3.2
.

For the maximum matching size
\nu:=\nu(G)
, let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\nu}
be the roots of
m_{G}
.
Then, we have

\mathbf{E}_{M\sim\mu_{\lambda}}\left[|M|\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}}.

Proof.

Let
m^{\prime}_{G}(\lambda)
be the derivative of
m_{G}
at
\lambda
.
Then, we have

\mathbf{E}_{M\sim\mu_{\lambda}}\left[|M|\right]=\frac{\sum_{k\geq 0}km_{k}\lambda^{k}}{\sum_{k\geq 0}m_{k}\lambda^{k}}=\frac{\lambda m^{\prime}_{G}(\lambda)}{m_{G}(\lambda)}=\frac{\lambda m_{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda-\lambda_{i})}{m_{\nu}\prod_{i=1}^{\nu}(\lambda-\lambda_{i})}=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}}.

∎

Next, we show that the absolute values of the roots of the matching generating polynomial are bounded.
To this end, we use the following property of the matching polynomial.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
21
,
24
]
)
.

The matching polynomial is real-rooted.
Moreover, all the roots have absolute values at most
2\sqrt{\Delta-1}
.

Corollary 3.4
.

All the roots of generating matching polynomial are real and negative.
Moreover, all the roots have absolute value at least
1/(4(\Delta-1))
.

Proof.

Obvious from the formula
M_{G}(x)=x^{n}m_{G}(-x^{-2})
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, at least a
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of the roots of the matching generating polynomial has absolute value at most
(4\Delta)^{\varepsilon^{-1}}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\nu}
be the roots of
m_{G}
for
\nu:=\nu(G)
.
Then, we have
\prod_{i=1}^{\nu}\lambda_{i}=(-1)^{\nu}m_{0}/m_{\nu}=(-1)^{\nu}/m_{\nu}
by Vieta’s formula.
As all the roots are negative by
Corollary

3.4
, this implies
\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\log|\lambda_{i}|=-\log m_{\nu}
.
By
Corollary

3.4
, we have
\log|\lambda_{i}|\geq-\log(4(\Delta-1))\geq-\log(4\Delta)
for every
i\in[\nu]
.

Suppose that there are
\varepsilon\nu
many roots
\lambda_{i}
with
\log|\lambda_{i}|>\tau
for some
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, we have

-\log m_{\nu}=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\log|\lambda_{i}|\geq\varepsilon\nu\tau-(1-\varepsilon)\nu\log(4\Delta).

Hence, we have

\tau\leq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log(4\Delta)}{\varepsilon}-\frac{\log m_{\nu}}{\varepsilon\nu}\leq\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\log(4\Delta).

This implies that at least a
(1-\varepsilon)
-fraction of roots have absolute value at most
(4\Delta)^{(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}\leq(4\Delta)^{\varepsilon^{-1}}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\nu}
be the roots of
m_{G}
for
\nu:=\nu(G)
.
Then, the expected size of a matching sampled from the Gibbs distribution is

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{M\sim\mu_{\lambda}}\left[|M|\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{\nu}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\lambda_{i}}

(by
Lemma

3.2
)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{(1-\varepsilon/2)\nu}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+(4\Delta)^{2\varepsilon^{-1}}}

(by
Lemma

3.5
)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\varepsilon/2)\lambda}{\lambda+(4\Delta)^{2\varepsilon^{-1}}}\cdot\nu.

∎

3.2
Sensitivity Analysis

In this section, we prove the following theorem about sensitivity of the Gibbs distribution over matchings:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
,
i\in E
be an edge, and
\lambda>0
.
Then, we have

W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E;\lambda,G},\mu_{E;\lambda,G-i}\right)\leq 1+2\lambda\Delta.

We first consider sensitivity with respect to pinning in
Section

3.2.1
, using an auxiliary lemma that will be proved in
Section

3.2.2
. Then, we prove
Theorem

3.6
in
Section

3.2.3
.

3.2.1
Sensitivity with Respect to Pinning

In this section, we consider the Wasserstein distance with respect to pinning, and show the following:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
,
i\in E
be an edge, and
\lambda>0
.
Then, we have

W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)\leq 1+2\lambda\Delta.

We prove
Lemma

3.7
by a recursive coupling similar to that used in
[
9
,
13
]
, but
instead of using an algorithm that outputs the coupling then analyzing the output of the algorithm as they did, we construct the recursion of Wasserstein distance
directly with the help of
Lemma

2.6
.

For each
s\geq 1
, we define an upper bound on the Wasserstein distance on graphs with at most
s
edges, and put them into a recursion to derive a universal upper bound for graphs of any size.

Definition 3.8

(
\kappa^{E}_{s,\Delta,\lambda}
)
.

For each
s,\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
\lambda>0
, we define

\displaystyle\kappa^{E}_{s,\Delta,\lambda}

\displaystyle:=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}G=(E,V)\\
i\in E\\
i\text{ is pendant}\end{subarray}}W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right),

where
G=(V,E)
ranges over all graphs such that
|E|\leq s
and
\Delta(G)\leq\Delta
.

The following lemma shows the recurrence relation among the upper bounds.

Lemma 3.9
.

For any
s,\Delta\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
\lambda>0
, we have

\displaystyle\kappa^{E}_{s+1,\Delta,\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda\Delta}{1+\lambda\Delta}\left(\kappa^{E}_{s,\Delta,\lambda}+1\right).

We will prove
Lemma

3.9
in
Section

3.2.2
; here, we first use it to prove
Lemma

3.7
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.7
.

Notice that
\kappa^{E}_{1,\Delta,\lambda}=0
holds trivially.
By
Lemma

3.9
, we can prove by induction that for any
s\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\kappa^{E}_{s,\Delta,\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\left(\frac{\lambda\Delta}{1+\lambda\Delta}\right)^{k}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\lambda\Delta}{1+\lambda\Delta}\right)^{k}=\lambda\Delta.

By
Definition

3.8
, this indicates that for any graph
G=(V,E)
such that
\Delta(G)\leq\Delta
,
and any pendant edge
i\in E
, we have

\displaystyle W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)\leq\lambda\Delta.

(1)

To show the claim, it suffices to reduce deleting an arbitrary edge
e\in E
from a graph
G=(V,E)
to the case where pendant edges are deleted.
To this end, we define a new graph
G^{\prime}=(V,E^{\prime})
by breaking the edge
i
into two edges
i_{1},i_{2}
,
each incident to one of its endpoints. Then we have

\mu_{E-i;G,\lambda}^{i\leftarrow+}=\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow+\\
i_{2}\leftarrow+\end{subarray}},\qquad\mu_{E-i;G,\lambda}^{i\leftarrow-}=\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow-\\
i_{2}\leftarrow-\end{subarray}}.

Notice that
i_{1},i_{2}
are pendant edges in
G^{\prime}
.
By triangle inequality and
Equation

1
, we have

\displaystyle W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)

\displaystyle=W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow+\\
i_{2}\leftarrow+\end{subarray}},\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow-\\
i_{2}\leftarrow-\end{subarray}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow+\\
i_{2}\leftarrow+\end{subarray}},\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow-\\
i_{2}\leftarrow+\end{subarray}}\right)+W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow-\\
i_{2}\leftarrow+\end{subarray}},\mu_{E^{\prime}\setminus\left\{i_{1},i_{2}\right\};G^{\prime},\lambda}^{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leftarrow-\\
i_{2}\leftarrow-\end{subarray}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda\Delta.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+}\right)=1,\quad W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-},\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)=0.

Then by triangle inequality,

\displaystyle W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)

\displaystyle\leq W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+}\right)+W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow+},\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)+W^{E}_{1}\left(\mu_{E-i;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-},\mu_{E;\lambda,G}^{i\leftarrow-}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+2\lambda\Delta.

∎

3.2.2
Recursive Coupling under Edge Pinnings

In this section, we prove
Lemma

3.9
.
The following lemma states that the probability that a vertex is matched is bounded away from one.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
v\in V
be a vertex and
\lambda>0
.
Then
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow+)\leq\frac{\lambda\Delta(v)}{1+\lambda\Delta(v)}
.

Proof.

In this proof, we write
\mathcal{M}
instead of
\mathcal{M}(G)
for notational convenience.
For an edge
i\in E
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{i\leftarrow+}

\displaystyle:=\left\{M\in\mathcal{M}\;\middle|\;i\in M\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{N(i)\leftarrow-}

\displaystyle:=\left\{M\in\mathcal{M}\;\middle|\;i\notin M,N(i)\cap M=\varnothing\right\}.

Then, there is a bijection between
\mathcal{M}^{i\leftarrow+}
and
\mathcal{M}^{N(i)\leftarrow-}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{i\leftarrow+}\ni M

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow M-i\in\mathcal{M}^{N(i)\leftarrow-}.

Notice that if
v\leftarrow-
then we must have
E(v)\leftarrow-
, so
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow-)\geq\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(N(i)\leftarrow-)
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(i\leftarrow+)}{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow-)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(i\leftarrow+)}{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(N(i)\leftarrow-)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{i\leftarrow+}}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(M)}{\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{N(i)\leftarrow-}}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(M)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{i\leftarrow+}}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(M)}{\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}^{i\leftarrow+}}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(M)/\lambda}=\lambda.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow+)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in E(v)}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(i\leftarrow+)}{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow-)+\sum_{i\in E(v)}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(i\leftarrow+)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i\in E(v)}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(i\leftarrow+)/\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow-)}{1+\sum_{i\in E(v)}\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(i\leftarrow+)/\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(v\leftarrow-)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda\Delta(v)}{1+\lambda\Delta(v)}.

∎

3.3
Polynomial-Time Algorithm

There are two different bounds for the mixing time of the Glauber dynamics for
\mu
.
The first one is near-linear with the number of edges, while the second one is near-linear on the parameter
\lambda
.

Lemma 3.12

(
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Denote the (1-site) Glauber dynamics of
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
on edges by
\mathcal{P}
, then we have

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathcal{P},\delta)\leq\exp\left(O(\lambda^{3}\Delta^{3}\log(\lambda^{2}\Delta^{3}))\right)m\left(\log n+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right).

Lemma 3.13

(
[
27
, Proposition 12.4]
)
.

Denote the (1-site) Glauber dynamics of
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
on edges by
\mathcal{P}
, then we have

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathcal{P},\delta)=O\left(nm\max\{1,\lambda\}\left(n(\log n+\log\max\{1,\lambda\})+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right).

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Consider an algorithm that samples a matching by using the
1
-site Glauber dynamics
\mathcal{P}
in
Lemma

3.12
with the error parameter
\delta=1/n^{2}
and
\lambda=2(\varepsilon/2)^{-1}(4\Delta)^{1/\varepsilon}
, and outputs the sampled matching.
By
Theorem

3.1
, its approximation ratio is at least

\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)>1-\varepsilon.

By
Theorem

3.6
, the sensitivity is

\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)\cdot O(\lambda\Delta)+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot n=\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}.

By
Definition

2.12
, the
1
-site Glauber dynamics of
\mu_{E;\lambda,G}
is a Markov chain that does the transition as the following with current state
x\in\mathcal{M}(G)
:

1.

choose
i\in E
uniformly at random;

2.

transit to
x\mathbin{\triangle}\{i\}
with probability
\frac{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(x\mathbin{\triangle}\{i\})}{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(x\mathbin{\triangle}\{i\})+\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(x)}
, and stay at
x
otherwise.

Notice that

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(x\mathbin{\triangle}\{i\})}{\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(x\mathbin{\triangle}\{i\})+\mu_{E;\lambda,G}(x)}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1+\lambda}&i\in x\\
\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}&i\notin x,x\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{M}(G)\\
0&i\notin x,x\cup\{i\}\notin\mathcal{M}(G)\end{cases}.

By maintaining a list of matched vertices, we can check whether
x\cup\{i\}\in\mathcal{M}(G)
in constant time,
and hence each step of the Glauber dynamics can be run in
O(1)
time.
Moreover, we need
O(n+m)
time to read the input graph.
By
Lemma

3.12
,
the time complexity is

\displaystyle\exp(\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)})m\log n+O(m+n)=\exp(\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)})m\log n+O(n).

On the other hand,
Lemma

3.13
gives
the time complexity

\displaystyle nm\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}\left(n(\log n+\log\Delta^{1/\varepsilon})+\log n\right)+O(m+n)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}n^{2}m\log n.

∎

\mathcal{H}
of graphs implies an algorithm that approximately samples from the Gibbs distribution over matchings in time
\mathrm{poly}(nm)\cdot\log\lambda
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a subgraph-closed class of graphs and
Q_{\delta^{\prime}}
be a parametrized algorithm

\delta

Given the error parameter

and the parameter of Gibbs distribution

,
let

t:=\frac{n^{2}}{2}\left(m\log(1+\lambda)+\log\frac{3}{\delta}\right),\quad\delta^{\prime}:=\frac{\log(1+\frac{\delta}{3})}{t}=\frac{\delta}{O\left(n^{2}\left(m\log\lambda+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)\right)}.

Then, there exists an randomized algorithm
A
that samples from the Gibbs

\mathcal{H}
such that for any graph

\delta
of

, and makes

calls
of
Q_{\delta^{\prime}}
and
m
calls of
Q_{\frac{\delta}{3m}}

on subgraphs of
G
.

As a corollary, if the running time of
Q_{\delta^{\prime}}
is
\mathrm{poly}(nm,{\delta^{\prime}}^{-1})
, the running time of
A
is also
\mathrm{poly}(nm,\delta^{-1})
,
and

is a fully-polynomial time randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS).

is proved in

4.1
The
2
-vertex Glauber Dynamics

The key idea behind our algorithm is to sample the
matched vertices
instead of sampling directly from the Gibbs distribution over matchings. We then use the counting algorithm—whose existence is guaranteed by the theorem—to sample the matched edges.
We start with defining the Gibbs distribution of matched vertices.

Definition 4.4

(Gibbs distribution of matched vertices)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
\lambda>0
be a parameter and
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
be a subset of vertices.
We define the
Gibbs distribution of vertices
as the distribution on
2^{V^{\prime}}
such that, for any
U\subseteq V^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{V^{\prime};\lambda,G}(U):=\frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\mathcal{M}(G)\\
V(M)\cap V^{\prime}=U\end{subarray}}\lambda^{|M|},

where
Z:=\sum_{M\in\mathcal{M}(G)}\lambda^{|M|}
is the partition function.

Here we slightly abuse the notation to use
\mu
for both the Gibbs distribution of matched edges and vertices,
and we distinguish them by whether the first parameter in the subscript is an edge or vertex set.

The following Markov process is what we use to sample from
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
.

Definition 4.5

(
2
-vertex Glauber dynamics)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a parameter
\lambda>0
we define the
2
-vertex Glauber dynamics

\mathcal{P}_{G}
as a Markov chain on
\left\{U\subseteq V\;\middle|\;\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)>0\right\}\subseteq 2^{V}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{G}(U\to U^{\prime}):=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\frac{\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime\prime})}{\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)+\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime})}&\text{if }|U\mathbin{\triangle}U^{\prime}|=2,\\
\sum_{U^{\prime\prime}\in 2^{V},|U\mathbin{\triangle}U^{\prime\prime}|=2}\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\frac{\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)}{\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)+\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime\prime})}&\text{if }U=U^{\prime},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Intuitively, for the current state
U\subseteq V
, the dynamics does the following.

1.

Uniformly choose a random pair of vertices
\left\{v_{1},v_{2}\right\}
from
V
.

2.

Let
U^{\prime}=U\mathbin{\triangle}\left\{v_{1},v_{2}\right\}
, then transit to
U^{\prime}
with probability
\frac{\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime})}{\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)+\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime})}
and stay at
U
otherwise.

The reason for using the
2
-site Glauber dynamics instead of the
1
-site version is to ensure the connectivity of the chain.
We first verify that this chain actually converges to
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any graph
G=(V,E)
, the following detailed balance condition hold:

1.

\mathcal{P}_{G}
is connected;

2.

For any
U,U^{\prime}\in 2^{V}
:

\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)\mathcal{P}_{G}(U\to U^{\prime})=\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime})\mathcal{P}_{G}(U^{\prime}\to U)
.

As a corollary,
\mathcal{P}_{G}
converges to
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
(see, e.g.,
[
35
]
).

Proof.

We first prove the connectivity.
For any
U,U^{\prime}\in 2^{V}
, with non-zero probabilities in
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
,
we need to find a sequence
U_{0}=U,U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{k}=U^{\prime}\in 2^{V}
such that

\mathcal{P}_{G}(U_{j}\to U_{j+1})>0
for all
0\leq j\leq k-1
.

Since
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U),\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime})>0
, there exist
M,M^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}(G)
such that
V(M)=U,V(M^{\prime})=U^{\prime}
.
By deleting the edges in
M\setminus M^{\prime}
one by one then adding the edges in
M^{\prime}\setminus M
one by one
we get a sequence
M=M_{0},M_{1},\ldots,M=M_{k}\in\mathcal{M}(G)
such that each neighboring pair is different by a single edge.
Then we have a sequence
U=V(M),V(M_{1}),\ldots,V(M_{k}),U^{\prime}=V(M^{\prime})
such that

1.

\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(V(M_{j}))>0
for all
0\leq j\leq k
;

2.

|V(M_{j})\mathbin{\triangle}V(M_{j+1})|=2
for all
0\leq j\leq k-1
.

Then by
Definition

4.5
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{G}(V_{j}\to V_{j+1})>0
for all
0\leq j\leq k-1
, proving the connectivity.

Then we consider the second part. If either
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U)
or
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}(U^{\prime})
is zero then the equation trivially holds,
so we only need to consider the case that both of them have positive probability, which can be verified by
direct calculation.
∎

With the convergence guarantee, we can start with an arbitrary vertex set with positive probability in
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
,
say
\varnothing
,
and run
\mathcal{P}_{G}
for sufficiently many times, then the resulting distribution would be close to the limit
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
.
To obtain
Theorem

4.1
, we need to prove that

1.

each step of
\mathcal{P}_{G}
can be simulated by polynomially many calls of
Q_{\delta^{\prime}}
;

2.

it takes
\mathrm{poly}(nm,\delta)
many steps of
\mathcal{P}_{G}
to converge to
\mu_{V;\lambda,G}
within TV distance
\delta
.

The key component of this section is proving the second claim, which is presented in the next subsection.
The proofs of the remaining claims are postponed until the end of this section.

4.2.1
Spectral Independence and Mixing Time

The mixing time of Glauber dynamics is controlled by a property of the stationary distribution called
spectral independence
.
There are multiple variants of the definition, and here we follow the definition in
[
10
]
.

Definition 4.7

(Spectral independence)
.

Let
\mu
be a distribution on
2^{\Omega}
for some finite set
\Omega
. We say a event
\mathcal{F}
is a
pinning

if it is in the form of

\mathcal{F}=\left\{A\in 2^{\Omega}\;\middle|\;A\cap B_{-}=\varnothing,B_{+}\subseteq A\right\}

for some
B_{+},B_{-}\in 2^{\Omega}.

For a pinning
\tau
, we denote the conditional distribution of
\mu
on
\tau
by
\mu^{\tau}
.
We define the
influence matrix

\Psi^{\tau}_{\mu}(i,j)\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
of
\mu
under a pinning
\tau
as

\Psi^{\tau}_{\mu}(i,j):=\begin{cases}\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim\mu^{\tau}}\left[j\in A\;\middle|\;i\in A\right]-\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim\mu^{\tau}}\left[j\in A\;\middle|\;i\notin A\right]&\text{if }\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim\mu^{\tau}}\left[i\in A\right]\mathbf{Pr}_{A\sim\mu^{\tau}}\left[i\notin A\right]>0,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

for all

We say

-spectral independent if for any pinning

,
where

denotes spectral norm.

The following lemma characterizes the relation between spectral independence and mixing time.
The spectral gap of a Markov chain
\mathcal{P}
is defined as
1-\lambda_{2}(P)
, where
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{P})
is the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 4.8

(Theorem 24 in
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be an
\eta
-spectral independent distribution on
2^{\Omega}
for some finite set
\Omega
.
Then the

-site Glauber dynamics
\mathcal{P}
has spectral gap

\lambda(\mathcal{P})\geq\prod_{j=0}^{|\Omega|-l-1}\left(1-\frac{\eta}{|\Omega|-j},\right)

and mixing time

t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\mathcal{P},\delta)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\log\frac{1}{\mu_{\min}}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right),

where
\mu_{\min}=\min_{A\in 2^{\Omega},\mu(A)\neq 0}\mu(A)
is the minimum non-zero probability over
\Omega
.

The following shows that spectral independence is bounded by Wasserstein distance.

Lemma 4.9

(Proposition 4.1 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let

be a distribution on

for some finite set

and

0<\mu^{\tau}(i\leftarrow+)<1\end{subarray}}W_{1}\left(\mu^{\tau,i\leftarrow+},\mu^{\tau,i\leftarrow-}\right),

where the metric used in the Wasserstein distance is the Hamming distance.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let

be a distribution on

for some finite set

there is

\eta
-spectral independent.

5
Low-Sensitivity Algorithms for General Graphs

The algorithm presented in
Section

3
has high sensitivity and running time when the maximum degree is large.
In this section, we address this issue by sparsifying the input graph into one with bounded degree while approximately preserving the matching size.
We then prove
Theorem

1.4
by applying a version of
Theorem

1.1
obtained by using a slightly different bound on the mixing time of the Gibbs distribution to the resulting sparsified graph.

We begin in
Section

5.1
by showing how to solve the LP relaxation of the maximum matching problem with low sensitivity, which is a key step in constructing the sparsifier.
Then in
Section

5.2
, we describe the sparsification procedure using the obtained LP solution, and complete the proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

5.1
LP Relaxation

Our sparsification algorithm is based on linear programming, and we start with explaining the LP relaxation of the maximum matching problem.
For an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, the matching polytope
\mathscr{M}_{G}
of
G
is the convex hull of the indicator vectors of matchings in
G
.
Consider the following polytope, defined by constraints ensuring that the total weight of edges incident to any vertex in the matching is at most one:

\mathscr{P}_{G}:=\left\{\sum_{e\in E(v)}x(e)\leq 1,\forall v\in V\right\}\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}.

It is standard that
\mathscr{M}_{G}=\mathscr{P}_{G}
holds when
G
is bipartite.
When
G
is non-bipartite, we need to further consider odd-set constraints.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
and
B\subseteq V
, let
x(B):=\sum_{e\in E(G[B])}x(e)
.
Then, we let

\mathcal{O}_{G}:=\{B\subseteq V:|B|\geq 3\text{ and }|B|\text{ is odd}\}

be the collections of odd sets.
Then it is known that we have

\mathscr{M}_{G}=\mathscr{P}_{G}\cap\left\{x(B)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{|B|}{2}\right\rfloor,\forall B\in\mathcal{O}_{G}\right\}.

Hence, we can solve the maximum matching problem by maximizing
x(E)
subject to
x\in\mathscr{M}_{G}
.

To solve the LP relaxation in a stable fashion, we introduce an entropy function
H:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{F}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined as

H(x)=\sum_{e\in F}x(e)\log\frac{1}{x(e)}

It is well known that
H
is concave and
1
-strongly concave with respect to the
\ell_{1}
norm in the simplex
\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{F}:x(F)\leq 1\}
, where
x(F)=\sum_{e\in F}x(e)
.
Let
K_{V}
denote the complete graph on the vertex set
V
.
For a parameter
\alpha\geq 0
, we consider the following entropy-regularized LP:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\text{maximize}&\displaystyle x(E)+\alpha\sum_{v\in V}H(\{x(e)\}_{e\in E(v)})\\
\text{subject to}&x\in\mathscr{M}_{K_{V}}\\
&x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\binom{V}{2}}\end{array}

We consider the matching polytope over
K_{V}
, instead of
G
, to ensure that the constraints remain independent of the graph, which is crucial for the stability of the LP solution.
Note that
x(e)
is defined even for
e\not\in E
.
Since the objective is a concave function and we can construct a separation oracle for the constraints, the problem can be solved in polynomial time using the ellipsoid method.

For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{V}{2}}_{\geq 0}
and an edge set
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
, let
x|_{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{V}{2}}_{\geq 0}
denote the vector obtained by truncating
x
to
E
, i.e.,
x|_{E}(e)=x(e)
if
e\in E
and
x|_{E}(e)=0
otherwise.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

Appendix A
Sensitivity of Randomized Greedy upon Deleting a Vertex

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, the randomized greedy algorithm
A
for the maximum matching problem works as follows.
First, it chooses a random ordering
\pi
over edges.
Then starting with an empty matching
M
, it iteratively adds the
i
-th edge in the ordering
\pi
to
M
if it is not adjacent to any edge in
M
.
Then, it is claimed in
[
43
, Theorem 12]
that, for any graph
G=(V,E)
and a vertex
v\in V
, the sensitivity of
A
upon deleting
v
is one, that is,
W^{E}_{1}\left(A(G),A(G-v)\right)\leq 1
, and this claim was crucially used to bound the sensitivity of their
(1-\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the maximum matching.
Unfortunately, their proof has a flaw, which we describe below.

First, we review their argument.
For a permutation
\pi
over edges, let
M_{\pi}
be the (deterministic) matching constructed by the randomized greedy algorithm when it uses
\pi
as the ordering of edges.
For an edge
e\in E
, we call
\pi(e)
the

of
e
, and let
I_{\pi}(e)
be the set of edges sharing endpoints with
e
with smaller rank, that is,
I_{\pi}(e)=\left\{e^{\prime}\in N(e)\;\middle|\;\pi(e^{\prime})<\pi(e)\right\}
, where
N(e)
is the set of edges that share an endpoint with
e
.
Note that the matching
M_{\pi}
can be described by the following invariant:
An edge
e
is in
M_{\pi}
if and only if all of its neighbors
e^{\prime}\in N(e)\cap I_{\pi}(e)
are not in
M_{\pi}
.

Let
e_{\pi}\in E
be the edge incident to
v
with the smallest rank with respect to
\pi
.
We define

to intuitively be the set of edges in
G
that need to be changed to maintain the invariant. Formally, we set

if

and
S_{\pi,0}=\varnothing
otherwise.
Then for

, recursively set

We then define

However, this argument ignores the case that some edge incident to

is in

and deleting

A naive approach to address this issue is to define

Recall that, in that proof, we split an edge into two pendant edges and applied inductive coupling to each one.
For each pendant edge, at each inductive step, the probability that the discrepancy increasing is bounded by
\frac{\Delta\lambda}{1+\Delta\lambda}

Hence, the probability that total discrepancy exceeds
\frac{c\log n}{\log\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda\Delta}\right)}

\lambda=\Delta^{O(1/\varepsilon)}
