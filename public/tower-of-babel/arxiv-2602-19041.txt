Back to Blackwell: Closing the Loop on Intransitivity in Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning
Jiahao Zhang, Lujing Zhang, Keltin Grimes, Zhuohao Yu, Gokul Swamy, Zhiwei Steven Wu

Abstract
A recurring challenge in preference fine-tuning (PFT) is handling intransitive (i.e., cyclic) preferences. Intransitive preferences often stem from either (i) inconsistent rankings along a single objective or (ii) scalarizing multiple objectives into a single metric. Regardless of their source, the downstream implication of intransitive preferences is the same: there is no well-defined optimal policy, breaking a core assumption of the standard PFT pipeline. In response, we propose a novel, game-theoretic solution concept – the Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner ( MaxEntBW ) – that is well-defined under multi-objective intransitive preferences. To enable computing MaxEntBWs at scale, we derive PROSPER : a provably efficient PFT algorithm. Unlike prior self-play techniques, PROSPER directly handles multiple objectives without requiring scalarization. We then apply PROSPER to the problem of fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) from multi-objective LLM-as-a-Judge feedback (e.g., rubric-based judges), a setting where both sources of intransitivity arise. We find that PROSPER outperforms all baselines considered across both instruction following and general chat benchmarks, releasing trained model checkpoints at the 7B and 3B parameter scales.

Author Ted Chiang famously described large language models (LLMs) as a “
blurry JPEG of the web
”
(
Chiang 2023
)
. One way of interpreting his words is that after being trained on vast swathes of internet text, the choices of an LLM “judge”
(

)
are often inconsistent. Thus, when LLMs are used to rank a set of options
\{A,B,C\}

A\succ B
and
B\succ C
, but
A\not\succ C

(
Xu et al. 2025
)
.

By construction, there is no
total ordering
over options under intransitive preferences, as
every
option loses to another. Thus, the standard
preference fine-tuning
(PFT) pipeline
(
Stiennon et al. 2020
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2024
;
Swamy et al. 2026
)
, in which one attempts to learn such a total ordering via training a reward model, is fundamentally unequipped to robustly handle the intransitive preferences we see when attempting to learn from noisy LLM judge feedback
(
Procaccia & Rosenschein 2006
;
Boutilier et al. 2012
;
Sorensen et al. 2024
;
Gölz et al. 2025
)
.

In response, prior work has primarily explored two mitigatory strategies. First, to reduce the baseline inconsistency of LLM judge feedback, recent work has proposed to ask the LLM judge to explicitly evaluate candidate responses along multiple objectives, rather than making a single aggregate judgment
(
Viswanathan et al. 2025
;
Gunjal et al. 2025
)
. This echoes observations from psychology, where one of the most common root causes of intransitivity is
scalarization
of multiple objectives into a single metric
(
Tversky 1969
)
.

Second, to tackle the remaining per-objective inconsistency, a variety of authors have proposed to frame PFT as zero-sum game solving
(
Munos et al. 2023
;
Swamy et al. 2024
;
Rosset et al. 2024
)
, building on ideas from social choice theory on how to robustly handle intransitive preferences
(
Kreweras 1965
;
Fishburn 1984
)
. Intuitively, these approaches attempt to find a policy that the LLM judge prefers as often as possible against the worst-case comparator – a well-defined notion even under intransitive preferences.

Given the complementary nature of these two lines of work, one may naturally hope to combine them into a single, gestalt solution. However doing so is technically challenging for a rather subtle reason: when one splits an aggregate judgement into multiple objectives, the Minimax Theorem
(
von Neumann 1928
)
no longer holds, as famously observed by
Blackwell 1956
. The downstream implication of this fact is that standard game-solving machinery (e.g., in the proofs of
Swamy et al. 2024
) breaks down, making it unclear how to directly extend prior algorithms that build on said machinery to the multi-objective PFT setting.

Faced with this roadblock, we circle back and ask a first-principles question about the multi-objective PFT setting:
what should it mean to do well when evaluated under multiple objectives, each of which may be globally inconsistent?

Our answer is the
Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW):

a policy that is preferred to all other policies within its local neighborhood, regardless of the criteria it is judged under.
Remarkably, even though it is a strictly more general solution concept than those explored in prior work, the underlying structure of MaxEntBWs means one can efficiently compute them without requiring the Minimax Theorem to hold.
Although MaxEntBWs are defined via a game between two LLM players, their structure permits an efficient reduction to
a single-player optimization
problem: only the learner’s strategy must be maintained and updated, and the update can be implemented via square-loss regression. Thus, we can capitalize on the more accurate feedback we get from LLM judges by asking them to explicitly evaluate responses along multiple objectives without needing to complicate the policy optimization pipeline.
We call our algorithm
PROSPER
, for
PR
eference-based
O
ptimization with a
S
ingle
P
layer over
E
ntire
R
ubrics.

In greater detail, our contributions are threefold:

1. We propose the Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW) as a novel solution concept for multi-objective preference fine-tuning.
In contrast to classical solution concepts like the Minimax Winner that are only catered to single objectives
(
Kreweras 1965
;
Fishburn 1984
;
Dudík et al. 2015
)
, MaxEntBWs are well-defined under multiple objectives. MaxEntBWs also generalize the Blackwell Winners proposed by
Bhatia et al. 2020
.

2. We derive
PROSPER
, a provably efficient, regression-based algorithm for computing MaxEntBWs.
We leverage the underlying structure of MaxEntBWs to derive a simple and scalable algorithm for computing them. Doing so requires fusing insights from
Ziebart et al. 2008
and
Gao et al. 2024
to first eliminate the inner-loop optimization, before reducing the outer-loop optimization down to simple regression. We prove rigorous performance and efficiency guarantees for
PROSPER
.

3. We apply
PROSPER
to the problem of preference fine-tuning from multi-objective LLM-as-a-Judge feedback.
We first empirically verify that splitting up objectives reduces but does not completely eliminate intransitivity. We then apply
PROSPER
to instruction following and general chat and find that it outperforms all baselines considered.

Related Work

Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning.

A variety of approaches to multi-objective PFT have been explored in the literature, including minimax game formulations
(
Chakraborty et al. 2024
)
, distance-based optimization
(
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Xiong & Singh 2025
)
, policy aggregation
(
Zhou et al. 2024a
;
A Alamdari et al. 2024
;
Heymann 2025
)
, distributional reward modeling
(
Siththaranjan et al. 2023
;
Halpern et al. 2025
)
, explicit multi-objective reward modeling
(
Rame et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, multi-objective reinforcement learning
(
Zhong et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024c
)
, and multi-objective direct preference optimization
(
Chidambaram et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2024b
;
Shirali et al. 2025
;
Ramesh et al. 2024
)
. However, all of these approaches implicitly assume that along each objective, preferences are transitive and there is a well-defined optimal policy, making it difficult to directly apply them to the more general setting we study in our work.

Intransitive Preferences in Preference Fine-Tuning.
Drawing on ideas from social choice theory, several authors have pointed out that care is required when learning from intransitive,
aggregate
preferences to avoid distorting the preferences of the underlying population of raters
(
Conitzer et al. 2024
;
Gölz et al. 2025
;
Maura-Rivero et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Xiao et al. 2025
)
. However, even when preference feedback is sourced from an LLM rather than a population of raters, intransitivity still frequently arises
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
, and can therefore stymie policy training. To deal with the fact that there is no well-defined optimal policy under intransitive preferences, a variety of authors have proposed to reframe PFT as a zero-sum game, in which one attempts to find the policy that does the best against its worst-case comparator
(
Munos et al. 2023
;
Swamy et al. 2024
;
Rosset et al. 2024
;
Sutawika et al. 2026
)
. These ideas build on a rich history of similar techniques in the social choice theory
(
Kreweras 1965
;
Fishburn 1984
)
and bandit
(
Yue et al. 2012
;
Dudík et al. 2015
)
literature.
Wu et al. 2025
extend the two-player Nash-equilibrium formulation to the multi-player setting. While promising, all of these approaches assume the learner will be evaluated along a single objective. In contrast, we focus on the multi-objective setting, requiring nontrivial algorithmic innovation.

More closely to our work, several authors have explored handling multiple, potentially intransitive preferences.
Bhatia et al. 2020
pioneered the theoretical study of multi-dimensional intransitive preferences by proposing the Blackwell Winner, but stopped short of deriving a scalable algorithm capable of training modern LLMs as we do. One of the key technical innovations of the MaxEntBW is the use of entropy regularization to avoid the adversarial training
Bhatia et al. 2020
’s solution concept implicitly requires.
Agnihotri et al. 2025
take a constrained optimization perspective on multi-objective PFT, but essentially sidestep intransitivity by only optimizing the preference against a fixed reference policy, reducing the problem to standard multi-objective RL. In contrast, we directly confront the challenges of multiple intransitive preferences. Perhaps most similarly to our work,
Gupta et al. 2025
consider optimizing against multiple judges to deal with poor out-of-distribution judge generalization, deriving a scalable algorithm via the use of a variational relaxation. We implement a similar algorithm as one of our baselines and find that it performs worse than our proposed algorithm,
PROSPER
.

RL from AI Feedback.
Our work falls within a growing body of research that leverages AI-generated feedback to guide reinforcement learning. In particular, we elicit AI feedback from judge models that are fed collections of rubrics
(
Cui et al. 2023
;
Gunjal et al. 2025
;
Viswanathan et al. 2025
)
, similar to the “constitutional AI” paradigm used by frontier labs
(
Bai et al. 2022
)
. Among rubric-based methods,
Cui et al. 2023
evaluate responses using four global criteria rather than prompt-specific checklists, an approach shown to be less effective
(
Viswanathan et al. 2025
)
, and
Gunjal et al. 2025
focus on the RLVR setting, whereas our work targets general chat and instruction following. Consequently, we primarily compare against
Viswanathan et al. 2025
in our work. While
Viswanathan et al. 2025
employ prompt-specific checklists, they scalarize these per-item rewards into a single score with LLM-generated weights, which can lead to intransitivity. In contrast, we dynamically reweight checklist items based on where the current model is weakest and use game-theoretic techniques to more robustly handle per-item intransitivity. We find that these differences lead to better model performance across the board on both instruction following and general chat benchmarks.

Back to Blackwell: A New Solution Concept for Multi-Objective Preference Fine-Tuning

3.1
Preliminaries

We begin by discussing relevant background information.

Notation.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be the space of prompts and
\mathcal{Y}
the space of responses.
We consider a class of language models (policies)

\Pi\subseteq\{\mathcal{X}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\}
, which we assume is convex and compact. For every prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
, a policy
\pi\in\Pi
induces a distribution over responses
\pi(x)\in\Delta(Y)
.

We assume access to a
multi-objective pairwise judge
,

\mathcal{P}:\ \mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{X}\;\to[0,1]^{m(x)},

(1)

which compares responses
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}
along
m(x)
objectives, returning a vector of size
m(x)
. Rather than assuming a fixed set of criteria for every prompt (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness), this formulation accounts for the diversity of possible prompts fed to today’s LLMs: each prompt may be associated with different (and different numbers of) relevant criteria. For each
k\in[m(x)]
,
\mathcal{P}^{k}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)
denotes the probability that
y\succ y^{\prime}
, conditioned on prompt
x
and based on criterion
k
. We emphasize that we make no assumptions about the transitivity of preferences along each objective. With a slight abuse of notation, we can extend this to a preference function between two policies
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi\times\Pi
:

\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\!\triangleq\!\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\pi\left(x\right),\ y^{\prime}\sim\pi^{\prime}\left(x\right)\end{subarray}}\!\left[\mathcal{P}\left(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x\right)\right]\!\in\!\left[0,1\right]^{m(x)}.

von Neumann Winner.
Under intransitive preferences, there is often no well-defined optimal policy (more formally, no
Condorcet Winner
,
Brandt et al. 2016
), as every response
y
loses to some other response
y^{\prime}
. Thus, we need to consider alternative solution concepts. In the single-objective setting, a popular choice is the
Maximal Lottery

(
Kreweras 1965
;
Fishburn 1984
)
or
von Neumann Winner

(
Dudík et al. 2015
)
, which is the policy that is as preferred as possible against its worst-case opponent. More formally, a von Neumann Winner is a Nash Equilibrium of the zero-sum game with
\mathcal{P}
as the payoff matrix. Due to the symmetry of the payoff matrix, simple self-play is provably efficient for computing such equilibria
(
Swamy et al. 2024
)
.

Blackwell Winner.
A
Blackwell Winner

(
Bhatia et al. 2020
)
is the natural extension of the von Neumann Winner to the multi-objective setting, drawing on Blackwell’s notion of a
target set

(
Blackwell 1956
)
. For simplicity, we present this solution concept in the promptless setting. For vector
z
and set
C
, define the
\ell_{\infty}

distance function
as

\operatorname{dist}_{\infty}(z,C)=\min_{c\in C}||z-c||_{\infty}.

(2)

Suppose we want our policy
\pi
to be preferred with at least probability
p
to any policy
\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
along all
m
objectives:

\forall\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\in[p,\infty]^{m},

(3)

where
C(p)\triangleq[p,\infty]^{m(x)}
is the corresponding target set. For any
p\geq 1/2
, we can
linearize
the distance function as

\displaystyle\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\text{dist}_{\infty}(\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}),C(p))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\big(p-\min_{w\in\Delta_{m}}\langle w,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\rangle\big),

since
\pi^{\prime}
can ensure that any coordinate of
\mathcal{P}^{k}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\leq 1/2
(e.g., by setting
\pi^{\prime}=\pi
). In this case, we can define the
\ell_{\infty}
Blackwell Winner
(
Bhatia et al. 2020
)
as

\argmin_{\pi\in\Pi}\max_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\big(p-\min_{w\in\Delta_{m}}\langle w,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime})\rangle\big).

(4)

3.2
The Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner

Note that removing the constant shift
p
in
Equation

4
does not affect the solution. Then, reintroducing the prompts
x
, we can express the
\ell_{\infty}
Blackwell Winner as the solution to

\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\quad\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\left\langle w(x),\,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle\right].

While perhaps reasonable for small-scale problems, performing a
best response
over adversary policies
\pi^{\prime}
as in the above is infeasible at the scale of today’s foundation models. Furthermore, adversarially selecting both the criteria and comparator policy could lead to situations where
all
policies look bad, even though some are qualitatively better than others. Thus, to control the strength of the adversary, we propose to KL regularize it to a reference policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\langle w(x),\,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\left(x\right)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\left(x\right)\right)],

(5)

where
\beta>0
controls the strength of the regularization.
Beyond being perhaps the natural choice, the use of KL regularization on the adversary will allow us to elide adversarial training, as we discuss in greater detail below. For now though, let us define the game value of a policy
\pi
as

\begin{split}V(\pi)\ \triangleq\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ &\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle\\
&+\ \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}(x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\right)\big].\end{split}

We are now equipped to define our core solution concept, the
Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW)
:

Definition 3.1

(Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW))
.

A policy
\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}V(\pi)
is called a
Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner (MaxEntBW)
.

In words, a MaxEntBW is a policy that compares favorably to policies in its local neighborhood, regardless of the objective it is evaluated under – a nuanced form of robustness. Observe that this solution concept is well-defined even under multiple, globally inconsistent sets of preferences.

In contrast to the single-objective setting, the Minimax Theorem
(
von Neumann 1928
)
doesn’t hold in the multi-objective setting when we consider the worst case over objectives
(
Blackwell 1956
)
. Thus, the
order of play
matters: the value of the game is different depending on whether learner
\pi
or adversary
\pi^{\prime}
moves first
(
Roth 2023
)
. This means that the self-play approaches
(
Swamy et al. 2024
)
that were sufficient in the single-objective setting cannot be directly extended to the multi-objective setting, seemingly indicating that adversarial training may be necessary.

Key Step #1: Entropy Regularization Ensures a Closed-Solution for

.
Fix a
\pi
and
w
. Then, we need to solve

\displaystyle\min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\langle w(x),\,\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle+\beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi^{\prime}\left(x\right)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\left(x\right)\right)].

Using
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi(x)}[\mathcal{P}(y\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)]
, we know from
Ziebart et al. 2008
that this problem has solution

\pi^{\prime}_{\star}(y^{\prime}\mid x)=\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}}/\beta)}{Z(w,\pi\mid x)},

where

Z(w,\pi\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}}/\beta)]

is the
partition function
that ensures the above sums to 1 across
\mathcal{Y}
. As in
Gupta et al. 2025
, we can then plug this closed-form expression back into
Equation

5
to arrive at

\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\min_{w:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{m(x)}}\mathbb{E}_{x}[-\beta\log Z(w,\pi|x)].

(6)

Observe that this optimization problem does not require adversarial training between a pair of policies to solve.

Key Step #2: Simplifying
w
down to a Prompt-Wise Minimum.
Next, we observe that
Equation

6
is
concave
in
w(x)
. Thus, for
\forall x\in\mathcal{X}
, we know that
w^{\star}(x)
must be a vertex of the simplex
\Delta_{m(x)}
(i.e., a standard basis vector). As the set of objectives is usually relatively small in practice, we can simply take a minimum prompt-wise across different objectives to eliminate the optimization over
w
. More formally, we can define the
k
th partition function

Z^{k}(\pi\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}\!\left[\exp\!\Big(-\mathcal{P}_{\pi}^{k}(y^{\prime}\mid x)/\beta\Big)\right],

(7)

and the worst-case coordinate as

k^{\star}(x)\;=\;\argmin_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi\mid x).

(8)

Then, we can further simplify down
Equation

6
to:

\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}_{x}{\mathchoice{\left[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)\right]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}{[-\beta\log Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)]}}.

(9)

We have now reduced our optimization problem, which initially had three players, to a single-player formulation.

Key Step #3: Using Regression to Approximate Online Mirror Descent.
We now observe that
Equation

9
is concave in
\pi
. This is because each
-\beta\log Z^{k}(\pi\mid x)
is concave in
\pi
(see Lemma
B.1
in Appendix
B
) and the minimum of concave functions is also concave. We can therefore reduce this problem to online convex optimization
(
Hazan 2023
)
and use Online Mirror Descent (in particular, with KL as the Bregman Divergence) for policy optimization.

The first step of a descent algorithm is calculating the gradient. Algebra tells us that the (functional) gradient with respect to
\pi
at some response
z\in\mathcal{Y}
of
Equation

9
is

\displaystyle g(z\mid\pi,x)=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}[\mathcal{P}^{k^{\star}(x)}(z\succ y^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-\mathcal{P}_{\pi}^{k^{\star}(x)}(y^{\prime}\mid x)/\beta)]}{Z^{k^{\star}(x)}(\pi|x)}.

(10)

On small-scale problems, we could follow this gradient by performing mirror descent independently at each prompt
x\in\mathcal{X}
and response
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. Unfortunately, both
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are too large in modern foundation model training for doing so to be practically feasible. In response, we use the regression-based reformulation of mirror descent proposed by
Gao et al. 2024
;
Gao et al. 2025
;
Brantley et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2024a
to arrive at the following update:

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,z\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(x),\,z^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{1}{\eta}\Big(\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z\mid x)}

\displaystyle-\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta}(z^{\prime}\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z^{\prime}\mid x)}\Big)-\big(g(z\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)-g(z^{\prime}\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)\big)\Big)^{2}.

As proved by
Gao et al. 2024
, solving the above optimization problem would correspond to exact mirror descent. While we are unlikely to be able to do this in practice, we can still learn a policy with strong performance guarantees under approximation error, as we show in Section
4.1
.

Putting It All Together in the Finite-Sample Setting.
In practice, we also need to estimate the infinite-sample gradient (Eq.
10
) based on samples from the current policy and the reference policy. Thus, we draw
M
samples
y_{1:M}\sim\pi(x)
and another
M
samples

y^{\prime}_{1:M}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)
independently. We then approximate the partition function

\widehat{Z}^{k}(x):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}{\mathchoice{\left[\exp{{\mathchoice{\left(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x)\right)}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}}}\right]}{[\exp{{\mathchoice{\left(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x)\right)}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}}}]}{[\exp{{\mathchoice{\left(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x)\right)}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}}}]}{[\exp{{\mathchoice{\left(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x)\right)}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}{(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{k}(y_{i}\succ y^{\prime}_{j}\mid x))}}}]}},

\widehat{k}(x)=\argmin_{k\in[m(x)]}-\beta\log\widehat{Z}^{k}(x),

(11)

and arrive at an estimate of the gradient
\widehat{g_{t}}(x,z)
:

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\widehat{k}(x)}(z\succ y_{j}^{\prime}\mid x)\exp(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\widehat{k}(x)}(y_{i}\succ y_{j}^{\prime}\mid x))}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp(-\frac{1}{M\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{P}^{\widehat{k}(x)}(y_{i}\succ y_{j}^{\prime}\mid x))}.

We then regress this gradient estimate in terms of the policy to perform updates as in
Gao et al. 2024
. We call this approach
PROSPER
: PReference Optimization with a Single Player over Entire Rubrics and outline it in Algorithm
1
.

4.1
Performance Guarantee for
PROSPER

Recall that the game value of a policy
\pi
is denoted by
V(\pi)
. Let
V(\pi^{\star})
be the optimal value. We now prove that under the assumption that the regression problem can be solved accurately in-distribution,
PROSPER
can efficiently learn a policy
\hat{\pi}
such that
V(\hat{\pi})
is close to
V(\pi^{\star})
. Intuitively, this means
\hat{\pi}
competes around as favorably as possible with policies in its local neighborhood, regardless of the objective it is judged under. More formally, we assume that

Assumption 4.1
.

For all
t\in[T]
, we have that for some
\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,\\
z\sim\pi_{\theta_{t}}(x),\\
z^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\end{subarray}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\eta}

\displaystyle\Bigg(\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(z|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z|x)}-\ln\frac{\pi_{\theta_{t+1}}(z^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\theta_{t}}(z^{\prime}|x)}\Bigg)

\displaystyle-\left(g(z\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)-g(z^{\prime}\mid\pi_{\theta_{t}},x)\right)\Bigg)^{2}\leq\epsilon.

Intuitively, this assumption says that there is a policy in our policy class that is able to accurately fit the difference of gradients. We believe this is a reasonable assumption for modern deep networks. Next, given some policy
\pi
, we denote the
concentrability coefficient
as
C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi}=\max_{x,y}\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(y\mid x)}

(
Kakade & Langford 2002
)
, which quantifies how well the reference policy
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
covers
\pi
. We are now ready to state our main theoretical result:

Theorem 4.2
.

Under
4.1
, after
T
iterations, there exists some
\hat{\pi}\in\{\pi_{\theta_{1}},\cdots,\pi_{\theta_{T}}\}
such that
V(\pi^{\star})-V(\hat{\pi})\leq O{\mathchoice{\left(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon}\right)}{(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon})}{(\sqrt{1/T}+\sqrt{C_{\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}\rightarrow\pi^{\star}}\epsilon})}}
, where

\pi^{\star}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi}V(\pi)
denotes an arbitrary MaxEntBW.

In words, this theorem says that as long as supervised learning works (i.e.,
\epsilon
is small), then
PROSPER
can efficiently compute an approximate MaxEntBW. We defer the detailed proof to Appendix
B
and turn our attention to empirically validating our approach on LLM fine-tuning problems.

5
LLM Fine-Tuning Experiments Setup

We now outline the setup used for our LLM experiments. We defer all training hyperparameters to Appendix
C.1
.

Training Dataset.

We train on the
WildChecklists
dataset
(
Viswanathan et al. 2025
)
.
WildChecklists
is based on
WildChat
, a collection of conversations between users and AI language models
(
Zhao et al. 2024
)
. As described in
Viswanathan et al. 2025
,
WildChecklists
was created by using Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct to generate
prompt-specific
rubrics to guide LLM judge evaluation of candidate responses. Note that our framework – which does not assume a fixed set of criteria across all prompts – is able to flexibly accommodate this more nuanced feedback. One can view each checklist item as one of the multiple objectives discussed in our preceding theoretical formulation.

Policy and Judge Models.

We fine-tune the 3B and 7B parameter Qwen2.5-Instruct models
(
Qwen et al. 2025
)
and use Qwen3-14B as our judge model
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
. We selected Qwen3-14B due to the relatively high agreement it demonstrated with author judgments across multiple criteria. We include the full prompt templates in Appendix
C
and use the standard averaging over both input orderings to mitigate the positional bias of pairwise judges
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
. All LLM judges score response pairs along a checklist item using a 5-point Likert scale. We query the judge 5 times and average to reduce variance before normalizing to
[0,1]
.

Benchmarks.
We primarily evaluate our trained models on
AlpacaEval

(
Dubois et al. 2024
)
and
Arena-Hard

(
Li et al. 2024
)
, as these benchmarks are in-domain for the
WildChecklists
training dataset. Additionally, to ensure our training procedure doesn’t cause regression on other capabilities, we also report results on multiple-choice QA and reasoning benchmarks:
IFEval

(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
,
MMLU

(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
,
ARC

(
Clark et al. 2018
)
,
HellaSwag

(
Zellers et al. 2019
)
, and
TruthfulQA

(
Lin et al. 2021
)
.

Baselines.
Given the similarity of the training data, we primarily evaluate our methods against the
RLCF
(RL from Checklist Feedback) method of
Viswanathan et al. 2025
. For each prompt, candidate response, and checklist item,
RLCF
generates a score by querying the LLM judge. Another LLM (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct in our experiments) then generates a set of weights used to combine the per-item scores into a per-response reward. Thus, the primary distinction between our method and RLCF is the use of a more principled, game-theoretic aggregation of per-item scores. Less important distinctions include the use of DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
on the highest and lowest scoring responses for a prompt rather than gap size-sensitive REBEL
(
Gao et al. 2024
)
for policy optimization and our use of
pairwise
judges rather than a judge that evaluates responses in isolation. For the 7B scale, we evaluate the author’s released checkpoints. For the 3B scale, as no models were released by the authors, we re-implement
RLCF
with REBEL as the policy optimizer, using our existing optimization pipeline.

Ablations.
In addition to evaluating our full method,
PROSPER
, we evaluate two simplifications, each of which correspond to dropping one of the inner loop optimizations.

Ablation #1: Single-Objective Optimization.
We ablate the importance of explicitly optimizing over multiple objectives by instead asking the LLM judge for a single, aggregate score (i.e.,
\forall x\in\mathcal{X},m(x)=1
that reflects all checklist items
jointly
). We refer to this variant as
PROSPER-JC
.

Ablation #2: Fixed Competitor Policy.
Rather than adversarially selecting a competitor policy, we can instead compare our policy to
\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}
. This corresponds to taking
\beta\to\infty
or a
variational bound
on our full objective
(
Gupta et al. 2025
)
. Thus, we refer to this variant as
PROSPER-VB
.

Across all variants of our method, we use
M=2
samples for gradient estimation and sample
4
responses per prompt, rather than a single pair
(z,z^{\prime})
as in Algorithm
1
. We also pool pairs across prompts and only train on the response pairs with the largest gaps in gradient vectors, which we find improves the quality of the final learned model. Intuitively, one can view this process as filtering out response pairs where the LLM judge was not confident in its choice.

6
PROSPER
Enables Efficient Multi-Objective PFT From LLM Judge Feedback

Our experiments focus on answering four key questions:

1. How much of an issue is LLM judge intransitivity in practice?
We compute the fraction of prompts for which LLM judges have intransitive preferences and for which Condorcet Winner exists. We also evaluate how this fraction changes if we decompose a checklist into individual items and ask the judge to evaluate each independently.

2. Is
PROSPER
able to optimize multi-objective judge preferences?
We compute the pairwise win-rate between all trained pairs of trained policies as in
Munos et al. 2023
to quantify how well the policies produced by
PROSPER
optimize nuanced, multi-criteria LLM judge preferences.

3. Do the policies trained via
PROSPER
exhibit qualitatively strong behavior?
Beyond optimizing the MaxEntBW objective, we also evaluate how well the policies produced by
PROSPER
perform on the
AlpacaEval
(i.e., instruction following),
Arena-Hard
(i.e., general chat),
IFEval
,
MMLU
,
ARC
,
HellaSwag
, and
TruthfulQA
benchmarks. We primarily evaluate on the first 2 benchmarks as they are in-domain for our training set
WildChecklists
, with the rest serving as regression tests.

4. How important are the inner loop optimizations for
PROSPER
’s practical performance?
We ablate the effect of the adversarially chosen competitor (
PROSPER-VB
) and adversarially weighted objectives (
PROSPER-JC
) on trained policy performance across all seven benchmarks.

) and there is no Condorcet Winner (i.e., those where no fixed response is preferred to all others). We note that these two quantities are not the same, as cycles may only occur across relatively weak responses. We compare two judges:
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}
(joint check) and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}
(single check). For
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}

In Figure
2
, we see that both judges frequently have intransitive preferences for large enough batch size

, echoing the findings of
Xu et al. 2025
. This underscores the importance of robust algorithms like
PROSPER
for learning from such inconsistent feedback. We also see that splitting up a checklist into multiple items (i.e., using
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{SC}}
rather than
\mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{JC}}
) improves both metrics but does not completely eliminate the problems of having no Condorcet Winner and intransitive preferences. Thus, while checklist / rubric rewards are an important first step, we still need to take care in the multi-objective setting to handle LLM judge intransitivity.

6.2
PROSPER
Effectively Optimizes Multi-Objective LLM Judge Preferences

Next, we use our LLM judge model to compare all trained 7B policies (
PROSPER
,
PROSPER-JC
,
PROSPER-VB
, RLCF, and the base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) on a set of 500 held-out prompts from the test set. For each pair of policies and prompt, we sample responses from each policy. We then ask our judge to evaluate each pair of responses along all criteria and pick a winning policy for each checklist item. We then average over checklist items before performing a final average over prompts to compute a
win rate
.

In Figure
3
, we see that
PROSPER
produces policies that better optimize for judge preferences than the base policy and RLCF baseline. Zooming in on the top row, we see that
PROSPER
produces policies that generate responses preferred to the base policy responses roughly
3/4
of the time and the RLCF policy responses roughly
2/3
of the time. Furthermore, we see that the full version of
PROSPER
outperforms both the
PROSPER-JC
and
PROSPER-VB
ablations, indicating that both inner loop optimizations improve efficacy at optimizing rubric-based LLM judge preferences.

7
Conclusion

We introduce the
Maximum Entropy Blackwell Winner
: a solution concept for learning from multiple, potentially intransitive preferences. This is frequently the case when learning from rubric-based LLM judges. We then derive
PROSPER
, a provably efficient algorithm for computing MaxEntBWs, before using it to fine-tune LLMs at the 3B and 7B parameter scales. Across multiple benchmarks, we find that
PROSPER
produces performant policies.

B.1
Concavity of the Optimization Objective

Recall that the optimization objective is

\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \min_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\big[\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle+\ \beta\,\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi^{\prime}(x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)\big)\big].

Using the results from
Ziebart et al. 2008
,
\pi^{\prime}
has a closed-form solution, and we can then simplify the objective to

\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\ \min_{w:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta_{m(x)}}\ \mathbb{E}_{x}[-\beta\log Z(w,\pi|x)],

where

Z(w,\pi\mid x)=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}(x)}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}}/\beta)].

To further simplify the objective, we invoke the following lemma:

Lemma B.1
.

-\beta\log Z(w,\pi\mid x)
is concave in
w(x)
and
\pi
.

Proof.

By Holder’s inequality, for any
w_{1},w_{2}
and
\theta\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle Z(\theta w_{1}+(1-\theta)w_{2},\pi\mid x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})^{\theta}\cdot\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})^{1-\theta}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{1}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})]^{\theta}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{y^{\prime}\sim\pi_{\mathsf{ref}}}[\exp(-{\mathchoice{\left\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\right\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}{\langle w_{2}(x),\mathcal{P}_{\pi}(y^{\prime}\mid x)\rangle}})]^{1-\theta}

\displaystyle=Z(w_{1},\pi\mid x)^{\theta}Z(w_{2},\pi\mid x)^{1-\theta}.

Taking
-\beta\log(\cdot)
on both sides, we have

-\beta\log Z(\theta w_{1}+(1-\theta)w_{2},\pi\mid x)\geq\theta(-\beta\log Z(w_{1},\pi\mid x))+(1-\theta)(-\beta\log Z(w_{2},\pi\mid x)).

Thus,

is concave in
w(x)
. A similar argument applies for
\pi

(Bauer’s maximum principle, concave form)
.

Let

be the set of extreme points in

be concave and lower semicontinuous (in particular, continuous suffices).
Then

attains its minimum at an extreme point of

\min_{x\in C}f(x)\;=\;\min_{x\in\mathcal{C}}f(x).

By
