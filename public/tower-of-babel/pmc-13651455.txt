Association between socioeconomic status and dental caries in adolescents – a systematic review of cohort studies
Authors: Marit Slåttelid Skeie, Kristin Solveig Klock, Göran Dahllöf, Hedda Høvik, Ida Sofie Karlsen Sletten, Tone Natland Fagerhaug, Abhijit Sen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEarly adolescence is a critical period for caries initiation, underlining the need for updated knowledge on effective caries indicators. This systematic review of cohort studies aimed to assess the association between socioeconomic status (SES), involving a wide range of indicators, and dental caries in adolescents aged 12–19.Materials and methodsFour databases (MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science) were systematically searched for relevant publications from January 1, 2000, through December 5, 2025. SES and SES proxy indicators were parental education, income, and occupation, and adolescents’ school type, their social capital, and immigrant background. Composite SES indicators combined the aforementioned variables. The risk of bias was evaluated using a modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale.ResultsStudies (n = 21) from 11 countries across five continents were included, covering approximately 66,000 adolescents. An inverse relationship was documented between caries and mothers’ education (six of seven studies), fathers’ education (three of four studies), and parental income (three of six studies). School type (three studies), social capital (two studies), immigrant background (four studies), and composite SES indicators (five of six studies) were also linked to caries. However, the effect measures and definitions of exposures and outcomes were not consistent.ConclusionsAn inverse association was observed between maternal education and caries, while corresponding associations with fathers’ education and parental income were weaker and less consistent.This type of association was also observed for adolescents’ school types, their social capital, and composite SES indicators. Immigrant background served as a caries predictor. However, the substantial heterogeneity in the included studies did not allow for meta-analyses and limited definite conclusions. The findings underscore the need to develop and validate robust composite SES measures applicable across diverse global contexts.KEYWORDS: Adolescent, socioeconomic factors, dental caries, epidemiology

Introduction

Socioeconomic health disparities have been a topic of interest for centuries [1]. Numerous studies have consistently reported a socioeconomic gradient in both general and oral health [2, 3]. Efforts in various welfare states have also been made to address this socioeconomic gradient in oral health [4]. Despite these initiatives, a global systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies from 2000 to 2013 reported that low socioeconomic position (SEP) remained associated with a higher caries risk than high SEP, and that the SEP gradient in caries appeared especially strong in the most developed countries [5].

In the literature, the terms socioeconomic status (SES) and SEP are often used interchangeably. While SEP primarily refers to an individual’s employment status, occupation, or occupational class [6], SES is considered a more multidimensional measure that also includes education and income in addition to occupation. Nevertheless, the influence of SES or SEP on caries outcomes is not straightforward and is mediated by multiple factors. One such contextual mediating factor is the existing system for providing and utilizing oral healthcare [7]. Oral health-related attitudes exemplify individual attributes that affect this association. To better understand the complexity of how SES is linked to oral health, published theoretical explanations are useful. One such publication [8] has given four current explanations: the materialist explanation (factors beyond individual control), cultural/behavioral explanations (behaviors influenced by cultural norms of behavior), the psychosocial perspective (psychosocial level of stress), and the life course perspective (embodiment of prior living conditions). Another publication [9] gives a conceptual framework in which social and commercial determinants of oral health are combined. This framework highlights interacting influences and processes.

Socioeconomic disadvantage is particularly harmful when experienced early in life and is known to be a key risk factor for early childhood caries (ECC) [10]. However, adolescence marks a period of increased independence [11], and during this period, personal behavioral and dietary habits may influence the impact of SES on caries [12]. A lower association between SES and caries during early adolescence is reported [13], which may partly be explained by fewer teeth due to the exfoliation of primary molars or the lack of proximal contact in erupting permanent teeth. Another aspect is the longstanding practice in caries epidemiology of omitting enamel caries when measuring caries. This is problematic because enamel caries occurring between the ages of 12 and 15 accounts for half of the total caries experience [14], suggesting that without including enamel caries, the point estimates for the association between SES and caries may be biased.

Generally, systematic reviews and meta-analyses have, over the years, established solid evidence for a pronounced association between SES and caries. As caries is cumulative throughout life, analyzing this impact in a group of adults will not give a distinct description for adolescents [15]. Only a few systematic reviews have addressed this relationship by restricting the SES data to adolescents alone, without the influence of other age groups. In those reviews [16, 17], the scientific evidence supporting an inverse relationship was found to be relatively weak. Questions remain about the extent to which reported disparities in caries among adolescents are truly linked to SES, and whether commonly used parental SES indicators are appropriate. Traditional SES indicators have also been reported inconsistently related to adolescents’ health outcomes [18, 19]. The rationale for this systematic review is the uncertainties and missing knowledge in research literature about the SES-caries association during adolescence. Increasing knowledge in this field is important for selecting effective adolescent-focused promotive and preventive strategies.

In this systematic review of cohort studies, we therefore wanted to assess commonly used and other less commonly used SES indicators to examine their influence on caries in adolescents. The SES indicators included were parental education, adolescents’ type of school attended, and parental income and occupation. We also wanted to assess SES proxies such as adolescents’ social capital and immigrant background, and SES indicators that encompassed composite variables, consisting of different types. Only cohort design studies were selected because they are considered less susceptible to selection and recall bias and provide stronger evidence of temporal relationships.

The research question was: Is there an association between SES, across a range of indicators, and dental caries among adolescents aged 12–19 years?

Materials and methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [20] and is registered in the international database of prospectively registered systematic reviews, PROSPERO 2024 (CRD42024542582). Amendments to the protocol included extending the search period and omitting meta-analysis. The decision not to perform meta-analysis was made post hoc because, generally, the number of eligible studies was too low, and a wide spectrum of heterogeneity prevented quantitative synthesis. The sources of heterogeneity were different types of SES indicators, inconsistencies in the defined caries outcome, and variability of effect measures used. Two previous attempts were made to do a meta-analysis. Effect of mother’s education on caries incidence with its associated funnel plot to assess publication bias are presented in Appendix 1. 1a and Appendix 1. 1b. Effect of parental income on caries incidence is shown by Appendix 1. 1c.. In these analyses, the concerns were about a too low number of studies and inconsistencies in caries outcome.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were publications in Scandinavian or English that reported a possible association between SES (the exposure variable) and caries incidence or caries increment in the permanent dentition of 12–19-year-olds. Studies in which SES was not the primary exposure were also included. The study design was limited to cohort studies (prospective or retrospective). If a study was published in multiple papers, the most recent, the one with the largest sample size, or the most relevant to this topic was selected. Exclusion criteria encompassed reviews, meta-analyses, theses, ecological studies, ‘grey’ literature (conference abstracts, dissertations, reports), and studies involving specific, non-representative populations such as socioeconomically deprived groups (including Indigenous or tribal populations), dental students, or groups with various medical conditions. Studies that relied solely on neighborhood or geographic information without additional SES evaluations, as well as intervention studies that could influence the described association, were also excluded. Finally, we excluded studies that used SES estimates based on self-estimation (e.g. satisfaction with their SES or their perception of SES) or self-reported caries status. Appendix 1. 2 outlines a table for exclusion criteria, while the potential impacts of these exclusions on the results are evaluated in the discussion section.

Information sources

Studies published in peer-reviewed journals from January 1, 2000, until the final search on December 5, 2025, were retrieved from four databases (MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science) with the assistance of an academic librarian (ISKS) and peer-reviewed by another academic librarian. Furthermore, hand searches were conducted for publications in Scandinavian (Norwegian, Swedish, or Danish) dental journals and by using the reference lists of all included publications and other systematic reviews related to the topic.

Search strategy

The search terms are presented in Appendix 1. 3. The publications were imported into the Rayyan Systematic Review Software [21], but the AI component was not used. Duplicates were removed using Bramer’s method [22] in the EndNote reference manager.

Selection process

One reviewer (MSS) screened all publications by title and abstract (phase 1) three times, while another reviewer (KSK) screened once, covering 10% of the publications (every tenth publication). The reviewers’ selection agreement was 97.3%, and any disagreement was resolved through a consensus decision. In the further screening process (phase 2), both reviewers independently read the full text of the publications marked with the ‘Maybe’ button in Rayyan.ai. This functions as an intermediate decision between ‘Included’ and ‘Excluded’. Potentially eligible studies were re-examined, and when uncertainty remained, a third reviewer (AS) assessed the full text and contributed to the final inclusion decision.

Data collection process

Three reviewers (MSS, AS, KSK) collaborated to extract the following data from the included studies: study ID, first author’s name, publication year, country and its Human Development Index (HDI) [23], cohort study type, study name or description, study period, follow-up duration, baseline sample size, child age at baseline, number of participants at follow-up, caries diagnostic method, use of bitewing (BW) radiographs, whether enamel caries were included, examiner calibration, number of examiners, exposure(s), outcomes (caries incidence/mean caries increment), and confounders.

Data items

Outcome

The outcome was presented using the DMFT/S index (Decayed, Missing, and Filled Tooth/Surfaces) [24] to indicate either caries incidence (binary count of new caries cases) or the mean caries increment (increase in the number of caries-affected teeth/surfaces, ΔDMFT/ΔDMFS). The ‘D’ component of DMFT/S was defined either to include enamel caries (enamel level) or to be restricted to dentin caries (dentin level, excluding enamel caries). Caries risk was thus, over a specific period, defined as caries incidence or as caries increment. Additionally, caries severity was defined as an outcome when the caries increment exceeded a specified threshold.

The SES variable (exposure)

SES exposures included parental education, the adolescents’ type of school attended, and parental income and occupation, whereas SES proxies encompassed the adolescents’ social capital and immigrant background. SES exposures were also treated as composite variables, combining the aforementioned variables. Additionally, the HDI [23] of the respective country was evaluated based on the year the participants were first included in the study, classified as ‘Very high’, ‘High’, ‘Medium’, and ‘Low’.

Effect measures

Point estimates used to measure the association between SES and outcome were odds ratio (OR), incidence rate ratio (IRR), and relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). When beta (β) coefficients were applied, the standard error (SE) was reported. If the CIs were lacking, the authors of the study were contacted and asked if they could provide this information. Criteria for conducting meta-analyses included a minimum of three studies and consistent definitions of SES exposure variables and outcome measures.

Study risk of bias assessment

To evaluate the quality of the included studies, modifications were made to the three categories (Selection, Comparability, Outcome) of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [25] for cohort studies (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK115843/bin/appe-fm3.pdf). The modified NOS is presented in Appendix 1. 4 (stars in the standard NOS version are replaced by points). Total scores could range from 0 to 10 points, categorized as low (0–3), medium (greater than 3–6), and high (greater than 6–10) study quality. For caries at the dentin level, the decision regarding the question, ‘Was the follow-up long enough for outcomes to occur?’ was set at a minimum of 4 years of follow-up for 10–11-year-olds and up to 17 years of age, and at more than 7 years of follow-up for older participants. These limits were based on research on the median time for caries progression by Mejàre et al. [26]. No limit was set for enamel lesions. In the modified NOS, a study could be awarded two points in the Outcome category instead of one. This was done because in caries epidemiology, the caries assessment is crucial and especially relevant for study quality. As this part in the standard NOS (a, b) was not suitable for this study, the text was changed to a) calibration of examiners (0.5 point), and with reliability caries tests (0.5 point), b) use of BW radiographs (1 point). In the Selection category, part 3, ‘Ascertainment of the exposure’, the text in the standard NOS (a and b) was modified to a) standardized record/use of known measurements (1 point), b) validated questionnaires/structured interviews (1 point). Lastly, in the category ‘Comparability’, the text was altered from standard NOS to 0.25 points for each confounder. This was done because in the standard NOS, mentioning adjustment for only age and sex would qualify for the maximum value, 2 points. Two reviewers (MSS, KSK) independently assessed each included study.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1, the flow diagram based on the PRISMA 2020 statement [20], outlines the results of the search and selection process. Initially, 2817 publications were identified (MEDLINE: 850, Embase: 1016, Cochrane Library: 348, Web of Science: 603). After excluding publications based on title and/or abstract and publications not written in English or Scandinavian languages (n = 31), 80 publications were considered potentially relevant, of which 5 were identified from reference lists. After the exclusions described in detail in Appendix 1. 5, 34 publications remained. Of these, 13 publications were excluded because they reported the same studies as included publications. The respective publications and the reasons for selecting them are presented in Appendix 1. 6.

Flow chart based on the PRISMA 2020 statement.

Study characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the 21 cohort studies (18 prospective and three retrospective) included data from approximately 66,000 adolescents aged 12–19 years, representing 11 countries across five continents (Asia, North America, South America, Europe, and Oceania). Most studies were published between 2010 and 2019 (n = 10), and between 2020 and the final search date, December 5, 2025 (n = 8). The majority of studies were from Brazil (n = 6) and Sweden (n = 4). Using HDI [23], 13 studies reported a very high HDI and five a high HDI, while three reported a medium HDI. Studies on caries incidence and increment were reported more frequently at the tooth (T) level (n = 14) than at the surface (S) level (n = 7). Ten studies used the World Health Organization (WHO) caries diagnostic criteria [27], but five- and six-grade/code caries diagnostic systems were also applied. However, only three studies included enamel caries in analyses of the association between SES and caries. Calibration of the examiners was performed in 17 studies, although not all provided results from caries reliability tests. Seven studies reported using BW radiographs. Additionally, the duration of the follow-up periods varied.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized evidence from 21 cohort studies across 11 countries to evaluate the association between SES and dental caries in adolescents. By restricting inclusion to cohort designs and to adolescents aged 12–19 years, the review provides age-specific insights into socioeconomic gradients in caries without dilution from childhood or adult data. Across most analyses, SES indicators were associated with caries outcomes, supporting the relevance of socioeconomic conditions for oral health during adolescence.

Interpretation of main findings

Among the SES indicators assessed, maternal education showed the most consistent association with caries outcomes, with six of seven studies reporting an inverse relationship [34, 36, 38, 43, 44, 47]. Associations with fathers’ education and parental income were observed less consistently, with an inverse association of fathers’ education reported in three out of four studies [3, 34, 43] and of parental income in three out of six studies [34, 38, 40]. These findings contrast with earlier research suggesting that traditional parental SES indicators are weakly or inconsistently related to adolescent health outcomes [18, 19], but align with evidence indicating that parental SES indicators influence caries into adolescence.

Composite SES measures consistently demonstrated socioeconomic gradients in caries across five out of six included studies [30–33, 39], supporting the notion that multidimensional indicators may better capture socioeconomic influences than single variables. SES indicators linked directly to adolescents – such as school type [3, 43, 45] and social capital [29, 31] – also showed meaningful associations, albeit in fewer studies. These findings are particularly relevant given adolescents’ increasing independence and the growing influence of peer, school, and neighborhood contexts on health behaviors [11, 12].

Methodological heterogeneity and certainty of evidence

The initial stage of the screening process, phase 1 (title/abstract screening), followed best-practice guidance of full dual-review only in 10% of the publications. Although one reviewer screened the publications three times, and all publications classified as ‘maybe’ in phase 2 (further screening process) were read and assessed by both reviewers, this constituted a methodological limitation and a risk that some publications could have been overlooked.

A major challenge in interpreting the evidence was the substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies. Differences in caries outcome definitions (incidence versus increment, variation in the use of DMFT or DMFS index, inclusion or exclusion of enamel lesions), SES operationalization, and effect measures (OR, RR, IRR, β coefficients) limited comparability and precluded meta-analysis. Although exclusion of the ‘M’ component likely had minimal impact because extractions due to caries are generally rare among adolescents [48], inconsistent handling of enamel caries, which accounts for a large proportion of adolescent caries experience [14], may have biased estimates toward underestimation. Concerning a lacking ‘F’ component, it may have introduced bias mostly restricted to data in late adolescence, as the median time for caries progression from the onset of caries into the dentin layer in permanent teeth is 4 years [26]. This component is also ambivalent. In countries without free dental health care, restorative treatment might be limited to those whose parents can afford it.

Heterogeneity was further compounded by variation in follow-up duration, examiner calibration, and use of BW radiographs. These methodological inconsistencies, together with relatively small sample sizes in many studies (only two individual studies had a baseline sample size of more than 10,000), reduced the precision of effect estimates and the overall certainty of evidence.

Residual confounding is also likely to have influenced observed associations. The confounders most often adjusted for were sex, ethnicity, and baseline caries. Contextual factors such as water fluoridation, access to dental care, and broader oral healthcare systems were less frequently considered.

The use of a modified NOS for quality assessment made it impossible to compare our scores with other systematic reviews using the standard NOS. Therefore, the scores of two identified systematic reviews targeting adults, using the standard NOS [15, 49], could not be compared with our review, although their scores were approximate.

Equity, generalizability, and external validity

The generalizability of the findings is limited by the geographical and socioeconomic distribution of included studies. Most were conducted in very high-HDI countries, and none of the studies was conducted in countries with a low HDI. Given documented differences in healthcare systems, preventive measures (e.g. fluoridation), and social determinants of health, the SES–caries associations observed here may not directly apply to lower-income contexts.

Additionally, the exclusion of socioeconomically deprived groups, including Indigenous and tribal populations, restricts external validity and may underestimate the true magnitude of socioeconomic inequalities in adolescent caries. Similarly, the exclusion of grey literature [50] may have contributed to publication bias. While these decisions enhanced methodological comparability, they simultaneously highlighted persistent inequities in the available evidence. Inclusive research strategies should therefore be promoted.

Potential mechanisms through which SES indicators affect caries in adolescents

It is acknowledged that a broad range of underlying determinants interact when assessing for association between SES and caries, for example, biological, behavioral, psychosocial, and economic [9].

Behavioral pathways

Compared to having a low educational background, individuals with a higher educational background are more easily able to make healthier diet choices and understand the importance of oral hygiene and preventive oral care, which means a higher level of oral health literacy. Adolescents’ diet is higher in sugars compared with the diet of children and adults [51], has a pattern of frequent snacking between meals [52], and adolescents’ oral hygiene is occasionally poorer than that of the other age groups [53]. SES indicators targeting these challenges in adolescents by using behavioral pathways should be widely adopted. Adolescents living in low socioeconomic environments also have an elevated risk of additional unhealthy behaviors, such as early initiation of smoking and involvement in drug abuse [54]. The mechanisms through which SES indicators affect caries in adults and children may differ due to other aspects of everyday life, but also, for example, in the case of adults, the enamel of permanent teeth is now fully matured and not to the same extent prone to caries. Regarding future intervention for adolescents, oral health education must be adjusted and based on credibility and confidence [55].

Access pathways

Across the world, not all adolescents live in societies with access to preventive and restorative dental care, and if such access exists, not all can afford services, such as access to oral health literacy skills and fluoridation. However, in some areas, water fluoridation is available, reducing the caries risk.

Psychosocial pathways

Adolescents, characterized by being in psychological and social transitions, are vulnerable to different psychosocial challenges, including depression [11], and are more often affected than children and adults. SES proxies for caries, such as social capital or social support, may help to uncover those who struggle, which is important as poor oral health is associated with low SES [56]. Living in countries without a functioning oral healthcare system may be detrimental to the oral health of those adolescents.

Considering our study, unmeasured behavioral and psychosocial factors, such as oral health literacy or oral health-seeking behavior, may have resulted in both over- and underestimation of SES–caries associations, reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation and non-causal language.

Implications for research and practice

Adolescence is a critical period for caries initiation, particularly in the context of enamel lesions [57]. Therefore, when enamel caries is included in the caries diagnosis, the conclusions drawn by researchers that new caries occurs at a relatively constant rate and that adolescence is not a period of heightened caries risk [58] may not hold. Evidence suggests that SES indicators are most informative during this stage, when newly erupted permanent teeth are highly susceptible [59]. The consistent performance of composite SES indicators in predicting caries supports calls for developing standardized, adolescent-specific SES measures, as single indicators may insufficiently capture complex social exposures [60].

Existing composite SES scales for children [61] and adults [62] provide a foundation, but importantly, adolescent-specific scales should incorporate both parental factors and adolescent-contextual indicators, including school environment, social capital, and peer influences. Or, in other words, such scales should reflect how adolescents live their lives in different regions of the world today. For adolescents living in socioeconomically disadvantaged and vulnerable populations, some scale adjustments may be considered, for example, to clarify whether they have the financial means to pay for dental treatment and oral hygiene cleaning products, such as toothbrushes and fluoride toothpaste. Future research should prioritize prospective cohort designs, include disadvantaged socioeconomic populations, and apply multilevel analytical approaches to disentangle individual and contextual effects [15]. To address the multidimensional, latent, and context-dependent nature of SES, no single method is sufficient on its own.

Conclusions

Our findings revealed considerable heterogeneity across the included studies, which precluded meta-analysis and limited the strength of definitive conclusions. As a result, the overall certainty of evidence regarding the association between SES and caries in adolescents remains low. Nevertheless, the results consistently suggested an inverse association between maternal education and caries, while inverse associations with fathers’ education and parental income were weaker and less consistent. This type of association was also observed for adolescents’ school type, their social capital, and composite SES indicators. Immigrant background served as a caries predictor. The findings underscore the need to develop and validate robust composite SES measures applicable across diverse global contexts.
