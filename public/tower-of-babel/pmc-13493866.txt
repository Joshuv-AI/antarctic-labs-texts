HOG-supervised compact CNNs for real-time visual place recognition
Authors: A H Abdul Hafez, Imad Odeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents HOGNet, an efficient deep learning framework aimed at replicating and improving histogram of oriented gradients (HOG) features for visual place recognition (VPR). HOGNet distinguishes itself from traditional deep models by utilizing handcrafted descriptors for efficiency and interpretability, while simultaneously harnessing the representational capabilities of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), thus reducing the need for extensive labeled datasets and significant computational resources. Two variants of the model, HOGNet_441 and HOGNet_1764, are developed to predict HOG descriptors of varying lengths, facilitating adaptable implementation in diverse computational environments. We assess HOGNet using two challenging datasets, the Nordland dataset and the India driving dataset (IDD-VPR), and perform thorough comparisons with established architectures such as VGG16, ResNet101, and MobileNet. HOGNet_441 achieves 67.7% validation accuracy and 90.76% Recall@20, outperforming VGG16 (88.00% at Recall@20) while being 36x faster (3.44 ms vs. 124.17 ms). An ablation study confirms that HOG supervision provides a +5.55% accuracy improvement over a baseline CNN without HOG targets. Intermediate convolutional layers (Pool2, Pool3) are shown to be more effective for VPR than output layers. HOGNet achieves approximately 290 FPS, making it suitable for real-time and resource-constrained applications. The findings underscore the potential of incorporating handcrafted feature descriptors into deep learning models to attain an optimal balance among accuracy, interpretability, and computational efficiency.Keywords: HOGNet, Histogram of Oriented Gradients, Deep Learning, Visual Place Recognition, Lightweight Models, Handcrafted FeaturesSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Background and motivation

Visual place recognition (VPR) is a fundamental problem in computer vision and robotics, where the objective is to recognize previously visited locations using visual inputs. It is essential for robot localization, autonomous navigation, and simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM)1,2.

The aim of this work is to develop a lightweight deep learning model that can efficiently replicate Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) descriptors for Visual Place Recognition. The main objectives are to achieve competitive recognition accuracy compared to state-of-the-art CNNs while significantly reducing computational cost and inference time. The motivation stems from the observed gap between handcrafted descriptors (efficient but less discriminative) and deep features (powerful but computationally expensive). The scope of this work is limited to VPR tasks in real-time and resource-constrained applications, such as embedded robotic systems and autonomous navigation.

Traditional Visual Place Recognition (VPR) methods predominantly utilized manually designed descriptors, including Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT)3, Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF)4, and Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG)5. The descriptors, grounded in domain knowledge, exhibit efficiency and robustness to particular transformations, rendering them appropriate for tasks including object detection, human recognition, and visual matching. HOG features have been notably impactful because they effectively capture edge orientations and local structural patterns5. Handcrafted features frequently demonstrate restricted generalization when confronted with real-world challenges, including illumination shifts, seasonal variations, dynamic objects, and significant viewpoint changes6.

On the other hand, deep learning-based methods, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in a variety of visual recognition tasks, such as VPR7–10. CNN-based descriptors are used by models like EigenPlace11, CosPlace12, MixVPR13, DenseVLAD6, and NetVLAD14 to generate compact and discriminative representations. Recent advances in deep ensemble methods15 and feature fusion techniques16 further highlight the potential of hybrid architectures for vision-based recognition tasks. Advanced optimization techniques have also been successfully applied to deep learning models for time series forecasting and energy systems17,18, while nature-inspired metaheuristic algorithms continue to evolve for global optimization problems19. However, these models usually need large annotated datasets, powerful hardware, and long inference times, which limits their use in resource-constrained and real-time scenarios like embedded robotic systems20. Furthermore, compared to handcrafted descriptors, the interpretability of deep features is frequently diminished due to their black-box nature21.

Hence, combining the flexibility of deep networks with the interpretability and effectiveness of handcrafted descriptors has become a promising approach. In order to improve performance and robustness, recent studies have investigated hybrid models in which handcrafted descriptors are repeated using neural architectures or fused with deep features22–28.

Contributions

In light of previous observations, this paper presents HOGNet, a lightweight deep learning model that has been specifically trained to predict HOG descriptors from input images. HOGNet achieves high recognition accuracy at a lower computational cost than traditional deep models because it incorporates handcrafted knowledge into a trainable architecture. The main contributions of this work are multifaceted.

HOGNet is novel in three aspects. First, it is the first lightweight CNN explicitly trained to replicate dense HOG descriptors for Visual Place Recognition. Second, it provides a systematic analysis of two descriptor dimensionalities (441 and 1,764) for VPR, revealing trade-offs between accuracy and speed. Third, it demonstrates that a model trained solely on 32×32 CIFAR-10 images can generalize effectively to large-scale VPR datasets (Nordland, IDD-VPR) without fine-tuning.

First, a novel CNN-based feature replication mechanism is presented using the proposed HOGNet model, which learns to map input images to their corresponding HOG feature vectors. The long-standing gap between learned deep representations and manually created descriptors is successfully closed by this creative design. In addition, this study offers two scalable versions of the model, called HOGNet_441 and HOGNet_1764, which correlate to various HOG descriptor lengths, to accommodate a range of application constraints. These variations are made and thoroughly tested to allow for flexible deployment according to the computational resources that are available. A comprehensive evaluation on two difficult datasets, Nordland and IDD-VPR, which cover a broad range of environmental conditions, is also provided and demonstrated the robustness of the suggested HOGNet model in this paper. The evaluation compares HOGNet to a number of popular architectures, such as MobileNet, ResNet101, and VGG16. A key finding of our analysis pertains to the model’s efficiency and its performance in retrieval tasks. Empirically, we demonstrate that HOGNet achieves accuracy comparable to much heavier CNNs while drastically cutting down on inference time and parameter count. Interestingly, in high-recall retrieval tasks, HOGNet’s intermediate feature layers demonstrate strong discriminative power.

Related work

In the fields of computer vision and robotics, Visual Place Recognition (VPR) has been thoroughly studied. Research efforts have generally gone in three main directions: using handcrafted features, deep learning-based techniques, or hybrid approaches that combine the two.

Handcrafted features in computer vision and VPR

In order to capture prominent local structures, gradients, and textures, popular techniques like SIFT3, SURF4, and HOG5 were specifically created. HOG is excellent at encoding edge orientations and local shapes by splitting an image into cells and computing gradient histograms within them, while SIFT and SURF in particular are prized for their resilience to scale and rotation changes. These descriptors are widely used in applications like face recognition29, vehicle recognition30, pedestrian detection5,30, and more. Recent advancements have demonstrated the effectiveness of optimal deep learning-based vehicle detection and classification in remote sensing imagery, leveraging chaotic equilibrium optimization algorithms31. Whereas SeqSLAM32 showed that patch-normalized gradient-based features could even achieve robustness to extreme seasonal variations, global scene representations like Gist33 and probabilistic appearance-based approaches like FAB-MAP34 allowed recognition across moderate viewpoint and illumination changes in the context of VPR. However, the drawbacks of manually created descriptors quickly became evident. The effectiveness of handcrafted features is limited in complex real-world environments due to their incapacity to capture high-level semantic information, and they typically fail when faced with severe appearance changes, occlusions, or dynamic objects, despite attempts by variants like RootSIFT35 to improve robustness to illumination and noise.

Deep learning approaches in VPR

By allowing models to directly learn task-specific features from data, deep learning revolutionized VPR. Large-scale recognition tasks have seen the widespread adoption of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)7–10. A number of these architectures were later modified for place recognition. While DenseVLAD6 used dense feature sampling to increase discriminative power, NetVLAD14 combined CNN activations into compact global descriptors using a trainable VLAD layer. Additional research revealed that pre-trained network features, like those of AlexNet and VGG, could be used for VPR without requiring fine-tuning36,37. More recent contributions include SqueezeNetVLAD38, which optimized the VLAD framework for resource-constrained platforms, Patch-NetVLAD39, which introduced multi-scale local-global fusion, CosPlace12, which used a novel metric learning strategy for large-scale geo-localization, MixVPR13, which enhanced generalization through feature mixing, and EigenPlaces11, which trained viewpoint-robust descriptors that set new state-of-the-art results. Advanced training strategies for image understanding have also been explored in related domains such as monocular depth estimation40. Furthermore, deep learning architectures have been effectively applied to enhance accuracy in image recognition tasks, including the detection of deepfaked images, where convolutional networks have demonstrated superior performance compared to LSTM-based approaches41. In contrast to handcrafted descriptors, deep learning-based approaches have limited interpretability, involve millions of parameters that impose high memory and computational demands, and frequently require large annotated datasets42 because of their impressive performance21. Their direct implementation in embedded robotic systems or real-time systems is limited by these difficulties.

Hybrid models: combining handcrafted and deep features

Hybrid approaches have become popular as a way to balance the complementary strengths of deep and handcrafted features. HOG descriptors, for example, were integrated into a CNN framework for object detection by Cai et al.24, demonstrating that combining edge-based information with learned features enhances detection accuracy. Similarly, in classification tasks, Khan et al.43 and Xu et al.44 showed that hybrid descriptors frequently perform better than either handcrafted or deep features alone because the former offer local structural cues that improve the latter’s discriminative ability. Khaliq et al.26 created lightweight CNNs enhanced with handcrafted information to handle drastic viewpoint changes, while Arroyo et al.25 suggested combining handcrafted descriptors with CNN activations for seasonal-invariant topological localization in VPR specifically. A related study by Simo-Serra et al.27 demonstrated that deep networks can learn to mimic and even surpass traditional features in discriminative capacity by training CNNs to replicate handcrafted descriptors like SIFT. These hybrid studies demonstrate how handcrafted knowledge can greatly improve robustness, interpretability, and efficiency when incorporated into a learning framework.

Research gap

Despite the benefits of hybrid approaches, several research gaps remain unaddressed. First, while fusion-based methods have been explored, the explicit design of lightweight CNNs trained to replicate HOG descriptors for VPR tasks remains underexplored. Second, most existing hybrid models are computationally demanding and unsuitable for embedded or real-time robotic systems. Third, the relationship between HOG descriptor dimensionality and VPR performance across different environmental conditions (seasonal, urban, unstructured) has not been systematically analyzed. Additionally, real-time navigation systems for indoor environments, such as those combining augmented reality with A* search algorithms, highlight the need for lightweight yet accurate localization methods45. This work addresses these gaps by proposing HOGNet, a lightweight CNN trained to replicate HOG descriptors of varying lengths, evaluated on diverse datasets representing different environmental challenges.

Proposed method: HOGNet

This section introduces HOGNet, a deep learning model created especially to forecast input image Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) features. In order to leverage the interpretability and efficiency of HOG while taking advantage of CNNs’ representation power, the main concept is to incorporate manually created HOG descriptors into a learnable CNN. Two HOGNet architectures, HOGNet_441 and HOGNet_1764, are suggested; they are each designed to produce HOG feature vectors with varying lengths.

Training HOGNet to represent HOG features

In the training process, the CNN learns to map input images to their corresponding HOG vectors by framing the task as a regression problem. The training dataset is CIFAR-10, which comprises 60,000 color images of size 32 × 32 in 10 classes. 10,000 images are used for validation, and 50,000 images are used for training. Utilizing the HOG parameters unique to each architecture, the HOG feature vector is calculated for every input image and set as the ground-truth target. The images are resized to fit the input size that each network architecture requires. The models were trained on Google Colab Pro with access to powerful GPUs like the A100 and L4, and they were implemented using PyTorch.

It is important to note that the primary goal of training HOGNet on CIFAR-10 is to learn the general mapping from input images to HOG feature vectors, not to learn VPR-specific scene semantics. HOG descriptors are general-purpose features that capture edge orientations and local structures, which are universally applicable across different image domains. Training on CIFAR-10 allows rapid prototyping and experimentation (60,000 images, 15–20 epochs). The successful transfer of HOGNet to Nordland and IDD-VPR datasets (achieving 67.7% accuracy and 90.76% Recall@20) validates that the HOG replication capability learned on CIFAR-10 transfers effectively to VPR tasks.

HOGNet_441 architecture

HOGNet_441 is intended to forecast HOG feature vectors with a length of 441. The HOG parameters used are orientations = 9, pixels_per_cell = (32, 32), and cells_per_block = (1, 1). The input images are resized to 224 × 224 pixels in order to calculate the HOG descriptor of this length.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the network follows a sequential structure comprising convolutional, pooling, and fully-connected layers. The feature extraction process begins with a first convolutional block (Conv1), which employs 6 filters with a 5 × 5 kernel and a stride of 1. This layer is immediately followed by a MaxPooling layer with a 2 × 2 kernel and a stride of 2 to reduce spatial dimensionality. A second, identical convolutional block (Conv2) follows, this time utilizing 16 output channels to capture higher-level features, again succeeded by a MaxPooling operation. The resulting feature maps are then flattened into a one-dimensional vector to transition into the classification stage. This vector is fed into a fully-connected layer (FC1) containing 4,096 neurons for non-linear transformation, before being projected onto the final output layer (FC2). The FC2 layer consists of precisely 441 neurons, corresponding directly to the dimensionality of the target HOG descriptor.

Architecture of HOGNet_441.

The hidden layers are activated using ReLU. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function and an optimizer like Adam with a suitable learning rate are used to train the model over 15 epochs. Following training, HOGNet_441 attains a validation loss of 0.0162. There is a warning that prolonged training may result in overfitting and impair feature performance at specific intermediate layers, even though training for more epochs may further lower the validation loss.

HOGNet_1764 architecture

HOGNet_1764 seeks to forecast HOG feature vectors of length 1764. For this variant the input images are resized to 256 × 256 pixels. The HOG parameters set are: orientations = 9, pixels_per_cell = (32, 32), and cells_per_block = (2, 2).

As depicted in Fig. 2, this variant employs a deeper and more capacious network. The feature extraction backbone consists of three sequential convolutional blocks. Each block is structured identically, comprising a convolutional layer with 32 output channels, a 3 × 3 kernel, and a stride of 1, followed immediately by a MaxPooling layer with a 2 × 2 kernel and a stride of 2. This triple-stacked design enables the network to learn a richer hierarchy of features. Following the final pooling operation, the resulting feature maps are flattened into a one-dimensional vector with 28,800 elements. This vector is subsequently processed through a series of fully-connected layers for high-level reasoning. It first passes through two large hidden layers (FC1 and FC2), each containing 4,096 neurons, which progressively refine the representation. The final output layer (FC3) then projects this onto a 1,764-dimensional space, thereby generating the target HOG descriptor.

Architecture of HOGNet_1764.

The model is trained for 20 epochs with the same optimizer settings as HOGNet_441 and the MSE loss. The validation loss of HOGNet_1764 is 0.0042. Similar to the other variation, the number of epochs is selected to strike a balance between generalization and training efficiency.

Discussion on model design and training

The integration between handcrafted HOG features and the network’s learning capacity operates as follows. No explicit balancing mechanism is used; instead, the network is trained end-to-end to minimize MSE loss between its output and the ground truth HOG descriptor. The network does not use HOG features internally; HOG features are only used as training targets (supervision). During inference, HOGNet operates as a standard CNN that directly outputs HOG-like descriptors without any HOG computation. The learning capacity is preserved through the fully-connected layers (4,096 neurons), which provide sufficient capacity to learn complex transformations beyond simple HOG replication.

Practical applications and trade-offs: HOGNet_441 is recommended for applications where recognition accuracy is the primary concern, such as autonomous navigation in complex environments or safety-critical robotics. HOGNet_1764 is recommended for applications where real-time performance is critical, such as high-speed drone navigation or embedded systems with strict latency requirements.

Iterative experimentation was used to make architectural decisions that balanced performance and complexity. Larger models, like HOGNet_1764, inherently require more processing power and memory; training them necessitates having access to strong GPUs and enough RAM. Increasing the number of epochs during training was found to reduce validation loss, but it can also result in overfitting or diminishing returns. In order to balance generalization and training efficiency, the ideal number of epochs was chosen.

Effective learning required the use of a large and diverse dataset (CIFAR-10); robust representations could not be produced with smaller datasets. In order to show that CNNs can be trained to represent handcrafted features, we introduced two HOGNet variants. While HOGNet_1764 offers a deeper architecture that can generate higher-dimensional HOG features, HOGNet_441 offers a simpler architecture with lower-dimensional output. Both show how effective learning can be facilitated with potentially lower computational costs by incorporating handcrafted features into a trainable deep framework.

HOGNet capitalizes on the advantages of both approaches by incorporating handcrafted features into a deep learning framework. This integration may lead to improved learning with reduced computational complexity and cost, providing a promising path for fusing contemporary deep learning methodologies with conventional computer vision techniques.

Experimental setup and results

This section presents the experimental evaluation of the suggested HOGNet models for the Visual Place Recognition (VPR) task in comparison to well-known architectures like VGG16, ResNet101, and MobileNet. We give a thorough analysis of the outcomes of numerous experiments, as well as a description of the datasets and experimental methodology.

Two benchmark datasets were chosen, and a set of manually created and deep learning baselines were put into place for comparison in order to assess the efficacy of the suggested HOGNet models. To guarantee a fair analysis of accuracy and computational efficiency, the experiments were carried out using a unified evaluation protocol.

Experimental setup

Datasets

In this paper, two publicly available benchmark datasets—the Nordland and IDD-VPR datasets—that present distinct challenges to VPR systems and are complementary in nature are employed.

The Nordland dataset32 consists of four long video traversals taken from a train traveling the same 728-kilometer route in four distinct seasons—spring, summer, fall, and winter. Seasonal variations create dramatic changes in visual appearance, making the dataset especially difficult to work with. For example, when snow covers a place in the winter, it may look entirely different from when it is covered in lush greenery in the summer. As is customary in VPR literature, query images are provided by one traversal, and the reference database is identified by another. Samples from the Nordland dataset are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Samples from the Nordland dataset32.

On the other hand, the India Driving Dataset (IDD-VPR)46 is a comprehensive dataset created to illustrate the difficulties associated with unstructured traffic in Indian cities. It includes roughly 10,000 photos that were taken from videos that were shot in a variety of scenarios, including busy streets, cluttered scenes, occlusions, changing lighting, and moving objects. In contrast to Nordland, which stresses seasonal variation, IDD-VPR concentrates on real-world urban driving situations with a lot of visual noise and dynamic objects. One part of the dataset serves as the reference database for evaluation, and query images are provided by a disjoint subset. This dataset evaluates how well VPR techniques hold up in difficult urban environments with changing lighting. Samples from the IDD-VPR dataset are provided in Fig. 4.

Samples from the IDD-VPR dataset46.

In order to provide a thorough evaluation, we combine these two datasets to test the suggested models in both real-world urban driving conditions (IDD-VPR) and extreme environmental changes (Nordland).

Baseline methods

To establish a meaningful comparison, the proposed HOGNet models are evaluated against both handcrafted descriptors and deep learning approaches commonly used for VPR. The classical baseline is represented by the Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG)5. As the direct, non-trainable precursor to the features that HOGNet aims to replicate, it provides a critical performance floor and validates the premise of learning an approximation of this handcrafted descriptor. For comparison within the deep learning paradigm, several influential convolutional neural networks are selected: VGG1647, ResNet1018, and MobileNet20. This selection is strategic, encompassing a broad spectrum of model complexity and representational power. It ranges from the lightweight and efficiency-optimized MobileNet, suitable for real-time applications, to the deeper, more computationally intensive backbones like VGG16 and ResNet101, which offer high feature representation capacity. This contrast allows for a thorough assessment of HOGNet’s performance against both efficient and powerful deep learning alternatives.

The experimental evaluation encompasses the full range of VPR approaches by contrasting HOGNet with these various baselines, ranging from conventional handcrafted methods to large-scale CNN architectures and the most recent purpose-built descriptors.

Evaluation protocol

Image retrieval performance serves as the basis for the evaluation. The system finds the most visually similar images in the reference database for each query image, and if the retrieved image is within ±5 frames of the ground-truth location, the recognition is deemed successful. This is in accordance with the standard evaluation procedure used in earlier VPR studies11,14.

The evaluation protocol employs a comprehensive set of metrics to ensure a thorough assessment of performance across multiple dimensions. Recall@K measures the percentage of queries for which at least one correct match is retrieved within the top K results, effectively capturing each model’s ability to identify correct locations among its highest-ranked candidates. Feature extraction layers

To determine which layers in the networks offer the most discriminative features for the VPR task, the performance of the various layers is assessed. The feature representations were evaluated from specific layers of each model: for VGG16, layers Pool4, Pool5, FC6, and FC7 were tested; for HOGNet_441, layers Pool2 and FC1 were examined; and for HOGNet_1764, features from Pool2, Pool3, FC1, and FC2 were analyzed. Essentially, we seek to balance feature richness and computational efficiency by examining performance at different layers in order to identify the ideal depth at which the features are most useful for place recognition.

Analysis of exhaustive training and overfitting mitigation

To evaluate the effect of prolonged training epochs on performance, extensive training is performed on both HOGNet models. For HOGNet_1764, more training epochs increased the predicted HOG vector accuracy from 61.45% to 67.51% and decreased the validation loss from 0.0042 to 0.0038. The accuracy of the Pool3 layer, however, decreased slightly from 77.71% to 75.48%, indicating that although the replication of HOG features improved, some overfitting might have taken place, slightly impairing the generalization of intermediate features.

HOGNet_441, on the other hand, behaved differently after prolonged training. While the validation loss dropped from 0.0162 to 0.0159, the accuracy of the Pool2 layer (marginally dropping from 81.25% to 81.19%) and the predicted HOG vector (dropping from 37.61% to 30.2%) also declined. This suggests that more training didn’t improve performance but instead probably caused overfitting, which had an impact on the output layer’s capacity for prediction.

The Nordland dataset is used to test the HOGNet models in a VPR classification task. The feature extraction backbones evaluated for comparison were: the Pool3 layer (28,800 features) from HOGNet_1764, the Pool2 layer (44,944 features) from HOGNet_441, and the Pool4 layer (100,352 features) from VGG16.

Discussion

The experimental findings show that HOGNet_441 provides a good trade-off between computational efficiency and performance, whereas VGG16 attains higher accuracy at lower Recall@K values. Because of its lightweight architecture, which enables faster inference times, HOGNet_441 is appropriate for real-time applications or deployment on devices with limited resources.

Additionally, compared to deeper fully connected layers or the final output layers, the analysis shows that intermediate convolutional layers offer more useful features for VPR tasks. This is true for various models and datasets, highlighting how crucial it is to choose the best feature extraction layer depending on the demands of the task.

Why intermediate layers outperform output layers: Intermediate layers (Pool2, Pool3) retain spatial layout information (feature maps of size 50×50 to 100×100). Output layers flatten and compress features, losing spatial relationships. HOG features are inherently edge-based and structural, and intermediate layers preserve these representations. VPR benefits from spatial information (e.g., “the building is on the left”) more than semantic information.

Ablation Studies: Two ablation studies were conducted. First, a baseline CNN (no HOG supervision) was trained and compared to HOGNet_441. HOG supervision provided a +5.55% absolute improvement in validation accuracy. Second, comparing HOGNet_441 and HOGNet_1764 reveals an accuracy vs. speed trade-off.

Furthermore, the effectiveness of HOGNet models indicates that handcrafted features can be incorporated into deep learning architectures to improve discriminative power without sacrificing efficiency. The potential of this method for real-world VPR applications is highlighted by HOGNet_441’s capacity to attain high recall rates with fewer parameters.

Conclusion

This paper presented HOGNet, a lightweight deep learning model trained to replicate Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) features for efficient Visual Place Recognition. Two variants were developed: HOGNet_441 (35.96M parameters, 3.44 ms inference) and HOGNet_1764 (46.10M parameters, 2.70 ms inference). Experimental evaluation on Nordland and IDD-VPR datasets demonstrated that HOGNet achieves competitive recognition accuracy (67.7% for HOGNet_441) while significantly reducing computational cost compared to VGG16 (36x faster inference, 59% fewer parameters). At Recall@20, HOGNet_441 achieved 90.76%, outperforming VGG16 (88.00%). Intermediate convolutional layers (Pool2, Pool3) were found to be more effective for VPR than output layers, as they retain spatial information crucial for place recognition. The ablation study confirmed that HOG supervision provides a +5.55% accuracy improvement over a baseline CNN without HOG targets. Future work includes: (1) extending HOGNet to multi-modal inputs (LiDAR, radar) for robust VPR in adverse conditions, (2) deploying and benchmarking on embedded hardware (NVIDIA Jetson, Raspberry Pi), (3) exploring alternative handcrafted descriptors (SIFT, SURF) within the same framework, (4) evaluating on additional urban datasets (Oxford RobotCar), (5) exploring lightweight attention mechanisms, and (6) conducting security analysis against adversarial attacks.
