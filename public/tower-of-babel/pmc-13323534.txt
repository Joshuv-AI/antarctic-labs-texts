Direct viral invasion and tumor-like pulmonary nodules: A fatal case of mpox in a patient with advanced HIV disease
Authors: Sen Yang, Qing Yu, Yanming Zeng, Yanqiu Lu, Chao Xia, Fang Cheng, Yan Liu, Min Liu, Yemiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This is the first reported fatal Mpox case with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) co-infection in Chongqing, China.•Mpox is an independent high-risk factor for immunocompromised HIV-infected patients, warranting timely screening.•Mpox pneumonia produces lung lesions radiologically mimicking tumors, emphasizing the importance of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) testing in differential diagnosis.Keywords: Mpox, Mpox virus (MPXV), Human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), Pneumonia

Introduction

Since May 2022, mpox (predominantly clade IIb) has evolved from a geographically restricted disease to a global public health concern, with documented spread to 110 countries across Europe, the Americas, and Asia [1]. In China’s mainland, following the first reported case in Chongqing in September 2022 [2], [3], the cumulative number of confirmed cases had exceeded 2,000 by December 2024. More recently, in early 2025, clusters involving the more virulent clade Ib strain have been identified [4]. Epidemiological analyses indicate that the vast majority of cases occur in males, particularly men who have sex with men (MSM), with the prevalence of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) coinfection ranging from 30% to 50% [5].

Mpox typically presents as a self-limiting zoonosis, with a case fatality rate (CFR) below 0.1% in the current clade IIb outbreak [6]. However, the clinical course is markedly more severe in individuals with advanced HIV disease, particularly those with a CD4+ T-cell count below 200 cells/µL, with recent case series reporting CFRs as high as 20%–26% [7]. Classic manifestations include vesiculopustular cutaneous lesions accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever and lymphadenopathy. Nonetheless, evidence emerging from the 2022–2025 outbreaks has highlighted rare but critical visceral involvement, including pulmonary, neurological, and pleural complications [8], [9], [10]. These complications pose a severe diagnostic challenge in immunocompromised hosts. Notably, pulmonary involvement frequently manifests as parenchymal nodules on computed tomography (CT) imaging, which are easily mistaken for as secondary bacterial or fungal infections or, due to radiological mimicry, misidentified as malignancies −leading to critical delays in appropriate management.

The inherent compromise of cellular immunity in people living with HIV (PLWH) is further exacerbated by concurrent mpox, creating a synergistic vulnerability that increases morbidity and mortality. Recent studies have highlighted prolonged viral shedding, increased hospitalization rates (up to 56% in HIV-coinfected individuals) [12], and severe manifestations such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [13] in this population. Here, we describe a fatal case of a severely immunocompromised patient with advanced HIV infection who succumbed to mpox-driven polymicrobial multifocal pneumonia. Notably, the pulmonary radiological features in this case strongly mimicked a pulmonary tumor. This case highlights that mpox should be considered in the differential diagnosis of pulmonary nodules in HIV-positive patients, especially those with severe or disseminated infection. Early nucleic acid testing of respiratory samples or tissues is crucial for accurate diagnosis and timely intervention.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old man presented to a local hospital on May 31, 2025, with bilateral inguinal pain and a generalized rash. A preliminary screening test for HIV-1 was positive, prompting his referral to the Chongqing Public Health Medical Center for further management.

On June 2, 2025 (designated as Hospital Day 1), the patient was admitted with a chief complaint of facial rash and inguinal pain (Fig. 1). Physical examination revealed a disseminated rash, predominantly affecting the face and abdomen, characterized by umbilicated lesions, some of which were ulcerated (Fig. 2). Significant perineal swelling was noted (Fig. 2). Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. Laboratory investigations revealed an HIV-1 viral load of 8.94 × 105 copies/mL and a CD4+ T-cell count of 76 cells/µL (Table 1). Testing for the mpox virus (MPXV) was positive. Hepatitis B serology indicated positive HBsAg, HBeAg, and HBcAb, with an HBV- deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) load of 4.08 × 104 IU/mL. Inflammatory markers were elevated (Table 1). Ultrasound demonstrated enlarged lymph nodes with uneven cortical thickening in the left inguinal region (largest 14.7 mm × 8.9 mm) and diffuse inflammatory changes in the scrotum with a right hydrocele. A hyperechoic focus of undetermined nature was also noted in the left testis. Albumin was low (25.2 g/L); renal function (Table 1) and electrolytes were normal. The patient was diagnosed with mpox, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), chronic hepatitis B, hypoalbuminemia, and secondary bacterial skin and soft tissue infection with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). As the specific anti-orthopoxvirus agent tecovirimat was unavailable, the initial management plan focused on supportive care and symptomatic treatment. This included empirical antimicrobial therapy (intravenous oxacillin for suspected skin infection), adjunctive therapy (Xuebijing injection, a traditional Chinese medicinal preparation used adjunctively in sepsis), analgesia/antipyresis (oral ibuprofen), and volume/colloid support (albumin infusion for hypoalbuminemia) (Table1).

From Day 3, the patient developed a paroxysmal cough and intermittent fever peaking at 38.5 °C. Chest CT showed thickened bronchovascular bundles and diffuse, inhomogeneous nodules and patchy opacities with halo signs in both lungs (Fig. 3). Bronchiectasis was noted in the right middle and lower lobes, along with hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Sputum smears and DNA tests for tuberculosis (TB) were negative. Culture of skin lesion secretions yielded methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), prompting a switch to vancomycin on Day 4.

C–D) Photographs taken on Day 22, demonstrating significant worsening of the lesion, characterized by diffuse multiple parenchymal nodules of varying sizes, patchy opacities, and partial pulmonary artery vascular filling defects.

By Day 8, the rash and ulceration had progressed, the inguinal pain worsened, and respiratory symptoms persisted. Culture of skin secretions revealed Pseudomonas luteola (sensitive to ceftazidime). Sputum analysis indicated Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). Bedside bronchoscopy revealed scattered white necrotic tissue and circular ulcers in the trachea and bronchi (Fig. 4). Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was performed on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and subsequent lung tissue samples. DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing were conducted using the Illumina sequencing platform by Dian Diagnostics (Hangzhou, China), a commercial clinical laboratory accredited by the China National Accreditation Service for Conformity Assessment (CNAS). Negative and positive controls were included in each sequencing run to monitor potential contamination. mNGS of BALF identified Streptococcus constellatus (42 reads), human herpesvirus 1 (228 reads), human herpesvirus 7 (51 reads), Pneumocystis (85 reads), and Ureaplasma urealyticum (85 reads). The diagnosis was revised to mpox with bacterial pneumonia, PJP, AIDS, and chronic hepatitis B. The regimen was adjusted to ceftazidime plus vancomycin, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (TMP-SMX) for PJP, and escalated analgesia. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) with an ainuovirine-based regimen (ainuovirine/lamivudine/tenofovir disoproxil fumarate) was initiated on Day 13.

Bedside bronchoscopic findings. A schematic diagram of the tracheobronchial tree depicts the endoscopic observations.

Following these interventions, the skin lesions began to crust, perineal swelling subsided, and the patient became afebrile by Day 11. Concurrently, on Day 11, the patient developed ocular swelling and discomfort. He was treated with gatifloxacin ophthalmic solution, which resulted in significant symptom resolution after four days. Antibiotics were discontinued sequentially by Day 23. However, despite the clinical improvement in skin and ocular symptoms, mpox viral shedding persisted in skin and pharyngeal swabs, and the cough remained.

On Day 24, the patient developed progressive dyspnea. A repeat chest CT showed significant progression of bilateral pulmonary lesions compared to admission, with diffuse nodules, patchy densities, and increased lymphadenopathy (Fig. 3). Tumor markers (CA15-3, NSE, CYFRA21-1) were mildly elevated. A lung biopsy showed no evidence of malignancy or granulomas (Fig. 5). However, mNGS of the lung tissue revealed an extremely high load of MPXV (260,840 reads), alongside CMV (1001 reads) and EBV (138 reads); TB-DNA was negative. A supplemental diagnosis of mpox pneumonia was made. Given the unavailability of tecovirimat, foscarnet sodium was added for preemptive anti-CMV therapy and potential broad-spectrum antiviral effect, alongside immunomodulators and high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) oxygen therapy.

Histopathological examination of lung tissue, 100 ×. The image shows alveolar spaces filled with foam cells and lymphocytes. There is chronic inflammatory cell infiltration within the alveolar septa, along with focal atypical hyperplasia of the alveolar epithelium.

By Day 29, the patient’s condition deteriorated critically with severe cough, dyspnea, and palpitations. Examination revealed oral leukoplakia and bilateral lung crackles. Inflammatory markers rebounded. Despite the addition of caspofungin and piperacillin-tazobactam and escalation to non-invasive ventilation (NIV), the patient declined further. He refused intensive care unit (ICU) admission and succumbed to progressive respiratory failure on Day 31.

Discussion

Since May 2022, mpox (predominantly clade IIb) has spread rapidly worldwide, disproportionately affecting MSM, among whom coinfection with HIV is one of the most common comorbidities [13], [14]. The patient described here—a 38-year-old HIV-positive MSM—aligns with the epidemiological characteristics of the current outbreak, where transmission in HIV-positive individuals occurs primarily through close sexual contact [15], [16]. These findings underscore the necessity of continuous MPXV surveillance within the MSM population, particularly among those living with HIV, to facilitate early intervention and improve outcomes.

While mpox is typically a self-limiting disease, clinical outcomes rely heavily on viral exposure levels, baseline host immune status, and complications. In immunocompetent individuals, the CFR for the circulating clade IIb strain is below 0.1% [6]. However, in people with advanced HIV disease, specifically those with a CD4+ T-cell count <100 cells/µL, mortality is significantly elevated. A global case series of 382 patients with advanced HIV and mpox coinfection reported a mortality rate of 15.1% among hospitalized patients; notably, in cases with lung involvement, mortality rose to 27% [6]. This case report illustrates the rapid clinical deterioration of a patient with advanced AIDS following pulmonary invasion by MPXV, complicated by superinfections and ultimately leading to death. This is the first fatal case of HIV and mpox coinfection reported in Chongqing, China. It provides compelling evidence that pulmonary MPXV invasion is a critical risk factor for mortality in HIV-infected individuals.

The radiological progression in this case is of particular diagnostic interest. Initially presenting with mild pulmonary lesions and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, the patient rapidly developed diffuse, variable-sized solid nodules and patchy consolidations throughout the lung parenchyma, predominantly in a perivascular distribution. These findings mimic pulmonary malignancies, such as metastatic disease or early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, which often present as well-circumscribed solid nodules or ground-glass opacities [17], [18], [19]. However, distinct pathophysiological mechanisms differentiate these entities: whereas tumor nodules are typically vascularized solid lesions, mpox-associated pulmonary nodules are characterized by ischemia and necrosis [21]. In this case, mNGS of lung tissue revealed an overwhelming MPXV viral load, confirming direct viral invasion of the lung parenchyma. This aligns with previous reports of mpox in advanced HIV, describing diffuse, well-defined, non-cavitating perivascular nodules indicative of hematogenous dissemination [6]. While MPXV was the dominant pathogen detected by mNGS (260,840 reads), concurrent detection of CMV and EBV, along with clinical evidence of bacterial and fungal superinfections, suggests a polymicrobial process in this severely immunocompromised host. It is plausible that MPXV initiated the pulmonary pathology, while profound immunosuppression permitted opportunistic pathogens to contribute to disease progression. Given the absence of autopsy confirmation, the precise contribution of each pathogen cannot be definitively determined. Nevertheless, the overwhelming MPXV viral burden, together with previous autopsy studies demonstrating MPXV-associated necrotizing pneumonia in patients with advanced HIV [21], supports a critical role of direct MPXV invasion. Furthermore, the presence of patchy consolidation in our patient suggested concurrent bacterial or fungal superinfection, highlighting the need for comprehensive molecular testing to distinguish etiology. Consequently, we recommend that routine pulmonary imaging and targeted MPXV testing be considered mandatory in HIV-infected patients with severe immunosuppression (e.g., CD4+ T-cell count <200 cells/µL) to facilitate the early recognition of mpox pneumonia.

Furthermore, studies indicate that among HIV and mpox coinfected patients with CD4+ T-cell counts <200 cells/µL, 62% present with genital lesions and 5% with ocular involvement; in fatal cases with CD4+ T-cell counts <100 cells/µL, ocular involvement increases to 48% [6]. While genital lesions suggest transmission via direct contact, ocular involvement is often a marker of disseminated systemic infection [22]. The presentation of significant perineal swelling and ocular symptoms in our patient serves as a clinical indicator of viral dissemination and high mortality risk.

The absence of tecovirimat, the anti-orthopoxvirus agent, undoubtedly constituted a therapeutic gap in this case. Its potential impact, however, must be contextualized within the disease stage and host immunity. A large global series of mpox in advanced HIV revealed high mortality (27% in cases with lung involvement) despite antiviral use in some patients, underscoring the therapeutic challenges in this population and the emergence of drug resistance [6]. These clinical observations are mechanistically informed by recent preclinical evidence demonstrating that the efficacy of tecovirimat is critically time-dependent. Studies in immunocompromised mouse models show that early administration (within 2 days post-exposure) effectively curtails viral dissemination, whereas treatment initiated during late-stage disease—characterized by systemic involvement—fails to resolve established local pathology, even with prolonged courses [23]. Our patient developed respiratory symptoms early during hospitalization (Day 3), and by the time MPXV pneumonia was confirmed by lung biopsy (Day 24), he had already progressed to advanced pulmonary disease with severe imaging progression and hypoxemia. This trajectory suggests that by the time a definitive diagnosis was established, the window for highly effective antiviral monotherapy might have already narrowed substantially. This case, therefore, amplifies two critical imperatives: first, the urgent need to improve access to effective antivirals in all regions; and second, the paramount importance of our earlier diagnostic argument—that early detection through vigilant imaging and molecular testing in high-risk individuals is not merely diagnostic but fundamentally therapeutic, as it identifies the narrow window where antiviral intervention can be most effective.

A limitation of this case report is the absence of autopsy confirmation, as the patient's family declined post-mortem examination. Therefore, while the clinical presentation and mNGS findings strongly support MPXV as the primary driver of pulmonary pathology, the definitive attribution of the fatal outcome to specific pathogens remains limited.

In conclusion, this case emphasizes the integration of systematic mpox screening into routine sexually transmitted infection (STI) surveillance. For HIV-positive MSM, mpox testing in asymptomatic individuals is indispensable for early case detection and outbreak control. For severely immunocompromised hosts (unsuppressed HIV viremia with CD4+ T-cell <100 cells/µL), even mild respiratory symptoms warrant immediate chest imaging and molecular testing to rule out fulminant mpox pneumonia − a life-threatening yet potentially reversible complication.
