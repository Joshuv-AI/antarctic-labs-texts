Development of a case definition for polycystic ovary syndrome using administrative health data: a validation study
Authors: Jenna N Salem, Robyn Vettese, Jennifer M Yamamoto, Sheffinea Koshy, Tyrone Harrison, Nikki Stephenson, Paul E Ronksley, Amy Metcalfe, Erin A Brennand, Jamie L Benham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo develop and validate a polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) case definition using administrative health data sources.DesignA validation study.SettingSecondary care centre outpatient gynaecology clinic in Calgary, Alberta, Canada.Participants3951 electronic health records of women aged 18–45 years who presented to a gynaecology clinic in Calgary, Canada, between January 2014 and December 2019 were reviewed. We identified 180 patients with PCOS using the Rotterdam criteria. Participants were excluded if they were biologically male, pregnant at the time of the consultation, did not meet the date criteria or if their consultation note was missing. The chart data were connected to the Practitioner Claims and the Discharge Abstract Database by personal health number.Primary and secondary outcome measuresSensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of 68 case definitions for PCOS were estimated. Case definition performance was graded.ResultsOf the 68 case definitions tested, none had high validity. The best performing case definitions were: (1) ≥3 instances of International Classification of Diseases-9 code 256.4 (polycystic ovaries) with exclusion codes (sensitivity 23.89%, specificity 99.59%, PPV 74.14%, NPV 96.35%) and (2) 626.X (irregular menstruation), 704.1 (hirsutism) and ≥3 instances of code 256.4 with exclusion codes (sensitivity 2.78%, specificity 99.97%, PPV 83.33%, NPV 95.40%).ConclusionsWe identified several case definitions for PCOS of moderate validity with high PPV (>70%) for case ascertainment in PCOS research in jurisdictions with similar administrative health data. These case definitions are limited by low sensitivity, which should be considered when interpreting research findings.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common and complex endocrine disorders affecting 8%–13% of women. Yet, 75% of women with PCOS are unaware they have the condition.1 2 While PCOS has heterogenous clinical presentations, it is characterised by irregular or absent menstruation, biochemical or clinical hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovary morphology on ultrasound.3 4 Symptoms of PCOS are among the most frequent reasons reproductive aged women seek medical attention.5 However, many women with PCOS experience barriers to receiving a diagnosis. It can take three or more visits to a healthcare professional and two or more years before women are diagnosed with PCOS.6 Women with PCOS report that their symptoms are dismissed, and that they do not receive enough information from their medical providers to manage their diagnosis.6 Moreover, women with PCOS have an increased risk of developing comorbidities, including type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, obesity and other cardiometabolic disease risk factors.7 8 Considering the distress that women with PCOS experience in managing their diagnosis and risk of comorbidities, there is a clear need to surveil PCOS in the population and to increase awareness about the condition.9

Administrative health datasets are comprehensive collections of information that capture patient interactions with the healthcare system.10 Although these data are typically collected for administrative purposes, administrative health data are useful for researchers because of the longitudinal follow-up, large sample sizes and comprehensive coverage.11 Thus, administrative health data provide the opportunity to assess health outcomes for various conditions within the population. However, because these datasets are not inherently developed for research purposes and there is no verification of the codes used across patients, the case definitions used to identify conditions from these datasets should be validated to ensure that the group of interest is accurately represented.12

PCOS is a common and complex condition that is under-recognised and underdiagnosed, so longitudinal surveillance of the management of PCOS and its associated comorbidities is essential to addressing the burden of PCOS.3 5 8 9 Globally, epidemiologic studies that identify women with PCOS from administrative health datasets are being conducted using various case definitions, many of which have not been validated.13–23 Because most studies are not using validated case definitions, it is unclear whether women with PCOS are accurately being identified if these definitions are used, which obscures the reliability of any evidence derived and conclusions drawn. We therefore aimed to develop and validate a case definition that would accurately capture PCOS in the population for epidemiologic research. Overall, a validated case definition for PCOS will help standardise future PCOS epidemiology research and ensure that the evidence being derived from these types of studies accurately describes the health risks that women with PCOS face.24–27

Methods

In this retrospective cohort study, we validated the accuracy of administrative health data case definitions for PCOS using medical charts. We followed the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data (RECORD) statement.28

Patient and public involvement

Patient collaborators were integral in developing and executing this study, including formulating the research question, study design and choice of outcome measures. They have continued to be engaged throughout and contributed to data collection and interpretation, and one is a coauthor of the manuscript. Patient collaborators have been able to highlight the information they would like returned to clinicians and scientists and continue to make high-value contributions to how the team will work with knowledge translation and dissemination of this project.

Chart data collection and defining PCOS

We reviewed medical charts from a random sample of 3951 people assessed at an ambulatory Gynecology clinic at the Foothills Medical Centre in Calgary, Alberta between 1 January 2014 and 31 December 2019, for a PCOS diagnosis or PCOS signs and symptoms. Participants were included in the cohort if they were assigned the female sex at birth and were between the ages of 18 and 45 years at the time of the first appointment. All charts were assessed by reviewers (JNS, RV, SK), and any cases with PCOS identified were confirmed to have PCOS by a medical endocrinologist (JLB) regardless of diagnostic status. Participants were classified as having PCOS if they met at least two of three Rotterdam criteria, including (a) clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, (b) oligo-ovulation and (c) polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound.29 To create the gold standard cohort, participant charts were classified as: (1) no PCOS; (2) PCOS; (3) not eligible for inclusion or (4) missing chart. Patients were excluded from the cohort if they (1) did not meet the inclusion criteria or (2) their charts were missing.

Defining PCOS for administrative health data usage

Two administrative datasets, the Practitioner (Physician) Claims and the Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), were connected to the chart data by personal health number. The Physician Claims dataset captures most of the inpatient and outpatient services provided by physicians who submit their claims to the government insurance programme.30 The Physician Claims dataset for Alberta includes up to three International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 diagnosis codes and one procedure code per clinical visit. The DAD captures administrative and clinical information on hospitalisations from all hospitals in Alberta with up to 25 ICD-10-CA (Canada) diagnostic codes and 20 procedure codes.31 The data accessed ranged from 1 January 2012 until 31 December 2022. This date range includes the clinical dates of the reference cohort with a 2-year lookback and 3-year outcome ascertainment periods to include additional clinical encounters and investigations.

Our aim was to develop and validate a case definition that could be applied to readily available and accessible data. Four previously published validated case definitions informed our case definition development.24–27 68 case definitions for PCOS using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes were systematically developed including three previously validated case definitions developed for other regions (online supplemental table 1).24 25 27 32 We chose to use ICD-9 and ICD-10-CA codes in the development of our case definition, as the administrative health data are readily available in Canadian provinces and territories, and therefore, an ICD-based case definition can be used for ongoing PCOS outcome ascertainment and surveillance. Case definitions were applied at any time over the study period. Some published case definitions have included keyword searches, medications and laboratory test findings, but we chose not to include these additional components as they are not available across all regions in Canada.24–26

Symptoms of PCOS, including polycystic ovaries, hyperandrogenism and irregular menstruation, were combined to build the inclusion criteria for case definitions to identify participants with PCOS. Presence of polycystic ovaries on ultrasound was classified by ICD-9 code 256.4. Clinical hyperandrogenism was characterised by both acne, ICD-9 code 706.X, and hirsutism, ICD-9 code 704.1. Irregular menstruation was identified by ICD-9 codes 626.1, 626.2, 626.4, 626.9 and 628.0. The ICD-10-CA code for PCOS, E28.2, was also used. Case definitions also included exclusion codes for conditions that could mimic PCOS signs and symptoms, such as thyroid dysfunction (online supplemental table 2). While other PCOS case validation studies, like Actkins et al and Castro et al, have excluded patients with conditions like eating disorders and fibroids, we chose not to exclude them because it is known that women with PCOS have an increased risk of eating disorders, and that PCOS and fibroids can coexist.24 25

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of PCOS in the gold standard cohort was determined. The validity of each case definition in comparison to the gold standard reference cohort was assessed by computing the following validity indices: sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV). The 95% CI were estimated for these statistics.

Grading of case definitions

All case definitions were graded as per other case definition validation studies using administrative health data.33 34 Validity was defined as: low (PPV <70%, sensitivity <70%), moderate (PPV ≥70%, sensitivity <70% or PPV <70%, sensitivity ≥70%) or high (PPV ≥70%, sensitivity ≥70%). We expanded the definition of moderate validity to include PPV <70%, sensitivity ≥70% to allow for others to choose the case definition that best suits their research objectives. Like other case definition studies, we chose not to grade definitions based on NPV or specificity since these indices are typically >90% for chronic conditions, and PCOS is a chronic condition that affects women across the lifespan.33 35

Results

Of 3951 randomly selected medical charts, 140 were excluded. Reasons for exclusion included a missing physician assessment note (n=116) and not being eligible for inclusion (n=24). The gold standard reference cohort was, therefore, comprised of 3811 participants (figure 1). Of those, 3631 (95%) participants did not have a diagnosis of PCOS, while 180 (5%) participants had PCOS. The mean age of participants in the cohort was 34.4 years (SD 7.95 years).

Cohort selection. PCOS, polycystic ovary syndrome.

Of the 68 case definitions that we systematically evaluated, the case definitions with the highest sensitivity were: (1) irregular menstruation or hyperandrogenism or at least two instances of polycystic ovaries (sensitivity 82.78%, specificity 55.33%, PPV 8.41%, NPV 98.48%); (2) irregular menstruation or hyperandrogenism or at least three instances of polycystic ovaries (sensitivity 81.11%, specificity 55.66%, PPV 8.31%, NPV 98.35%) and (3) irregular menstruation or hirsutism, or at least one instance of polycystic ovaries (sensitivity 79.44%, specificity 66.07%, PPV 10.40%, NPV 98.48%) (table 1). The least sensitive case definition was irregular menstruation or hyperandrogenism or at least one instance of polycystic ovaries (sensitivity 0.56%, specificity 99.70%, PPV 8.33%, NPV 95.29%).

The three case definitions with the highest PPV included: (1) irregular menstruation and hirsutism, and at least three instances of polycystic ovaries, with exclusion codes (sensitivity 2.78%, specificity 99.97%, PPV 83.33%, NPV 95.40%); (2) irregular menstruation or hirsutism, and at least three instances of polycystic ovaries, with exclusion codes (sensitivity 15.00%, specificity 99.78%, PPV 77.14%, NPV 95.95%) and (3) irregular menstruation or hyperandrogenism, and at least three instances of polycystic ovaries, with exclusion codes (sensitivity 17.78%, specificity 99.72%, PPV 76.19%, NPV 96.07%). The case definition with the lowest PPV was irregular menstruation or hyperandrogenism, with exclusion codes (sensitivity 52.22%, specificity 64.86%, PPV 6.86%, NPV 96.48%).

Grading of PCOS case definitions

Of the 68 case definitions tested, none had high validity with both a sensitivity and PPV >70%. Six case definitions were of moderate validity and had a sensitivity >70%, and another six had a PPV >70% (table 1). Of the six case definitions with a sensitivity >70%, only two included the stipulation of three or more instances of ICD-9 code 256.4; whereas the six case definitions with a PPV >70% all included at least three or more instances of the ICD-9 code 256.4. The best performing case definition to identify PCOS included searching for three or more instances of polycystic ovaries (ICD-9 256.4) with exclusion codes (sensitivity 23.89%, specificity 99.59%, PPV 74.14%, NPV 96.35%). The case definition that returned the highest PPV searched for irregular menstruation and hirsutism, and at least three instances of polycystic ovaries (ICD-9 256.4), with exclusion codes (sensitivity 2.78%, specificity 99.97%, PPV 83.33%, NPV 95.40%). Seven of the 12 case definitions of moderate validity were run without exclusion codes, and all searched for irregular menstruation. Of these seven case definitions, six had a sensitivity >70% with only one having a PPV >70%. The remaining five case definitions of moderate validity all searched for exclusion codes and had a PPV >70%. The three case definitions that included the ICD-10 code E28.2 were all low validity. Searching for only polycystic ovaries (ICD-9 code 256.4) on at least one occasion resulted in a sensitivity of 55.56%, specificity of 96.89%, PPV of 46.95% and an NPV of 97.78%, which was of low validity.

Discussion

We assessed the validity of case definitions for PCOS using administrative health data from Alberta, Canada, for identifying cases with PCOS. Of the 68 case definitions developed, the case definition with the highest sensitivity and PPV identified PCOS by at least three instances of polycystic ovaries with exclusion codes. This definition returned a sensitivity of 23.89% and a PPV of 74.14% and was of moderate validity. 12 case definitions were found to be of moderate validity, and no case definitions of high validity were found. Most case definitions evaluated in this study performed poorly with low validity.

Globally, PCOS case definitions are being used in epidemiological research. Four other studies have validated a PCOS case definition for use in administrative health datasets; however, similar to our findings, none of the proposed case definitions were reported to have high validity in a recent systematic review.36 In the USA and South America, several studies have identified women with PCOS by using various combinations of ICD-9 code 256.4 alone or by including codes for symptoms like anovulatory infertility or irregular menstruation and hirsutism.13–20 In our dataset, we found six case definitions that included PCOS symptoms or the ICD-9 code 256.4 with high sensitivity (>70%); however, these had low PPV. Conversely, when PCOS symptoms and the ICD-9 code 256.4 for polycystic ovaries were combined, we found six case definitions that had high PPV (>70%) but had low sensitivity. Oliver-Williams et al identified PCOS only by using the ICD-10 code E28.2 for PCOS.21 While our study used the Practitioner Claims dataset and the DAD, we found that the DAD did not accurately capture cases with PCOS. All case definitions using ICD-10 codes were of low validity, thus applying case definitions in the DAD alone is insufficient to identify PCOS in our dataset. Overall, these findings emphasise the need to promote the use of locally validated case definitions that are capable of sufficiently capturing women with PCOS from administrative health data.

Machine learning is another tool that can be applied to electronic medical records (EMRs) for PCOS case ascertainment. Zad et al developed a machine learning algorithm using EMR to predict PCOS risk and diagnose the condition.32 Within their sample of 30 601 patients, 4.5% had a PCOS ICD-9 code of 256.4. They developed four different predictive models which considered outcomes like ICD-9 code 256.4, biochemical and hormonal values, imaging and Rotterdam criteria. All four models returned area under the curve values ranging from 80% to 85% and were described as highly accurate and robust in predicting a PCOS diagnosis. Additionally, Castro et al developed a natural language processing algorithm using EMR data and ICD-9 codes.25 Their algorithm was able to positively predict a PCOS diagnosis at 66% accuracy. Considering the results of Zad et al and Castro et al, machine learning has the potential to be a useful diagnostic tool for PCOS and complements the development of case definitions from administrative health data to improve accuracy and generalisability in some jurisdictions25 32; however, machine learning algorithms have limited applicability in other jurisdictions like provinces in Canada where many different EMRs are used.37

This study has many notable strengths. Our study is the first to systematically develop and validate extensive variations of administrative health case definitions for PCOS using ICD codes in Canada. In this process, we developed a large gold standard reference cohort which included a considerable number of participants without PCOS, allowing us to estimate the validity indices. There are also limitations to consider. First, patients who present to an outpatient gynaecology clinic are not representative of the entire PCOS population, especially considering that up to 75% of women with PCOS are estimated to be unaware of their condition.2 5 As discussed by Cadarette and Wong, conditions that are clinically heterogenous or non-specific may be prone to misclassification and may not be accurately captured in health datasets.10 The nature of PCOS, being a heterogeneous condition that is poorly understood and underdiagnosed, likely contributed to our inability to find a case definition of high validity and presents the risk of misclassification bias.9 38 Second, the gold standard cohort was created through chart review of an EMR, and therefore, data could be incomplete or inaccurate. Finally, the prevalence of PCOS in our gold standard reference cohort (4.72%) was less than expected. Actkins et al and Castro et al both reported low prevalences of PCOS in their respective studies (<1.2%). These low prevalences may reflect the proportion of women with PCOS who have not been diagnosed, as well as differences in coding practices among practitioners.24 25 In our dataset, this low prevalence could also stem from the participant sample only including those who presented to a gynaecologist as patients with PCOS could also be diagnosed or treated by family physicians or other specialist physicians like endocrinologists and dermatologists.

Conclusion

The majority of the case definitions we analysed were of low validity, and none were of high validity. We identified 12 case definitions of moderate validity in our dataset with either PPV >70% and sensitivity <70% or PPV <70% and sensitivity >70%. The high specificity suggests these moderate validity case definitions may be used for case ascertainment in Canadian PCOS research; however, the case definitions are limited by either low sensitivity that will result in many cases with PCOS being missed, or low PPV, which must be considered in the interpretation of study findings using these case definitions.
