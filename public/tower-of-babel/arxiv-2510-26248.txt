Integration in finite terms and exponentially algebraic functions
Rémi Jaoui, Jonathan Kirby

Abstract
. We develop techniques at the interface between differential algebra and model theory to study the following problems of exponential algebraicity: (1) Does a given algebraic differential equation admits an exponentially algebraic solution , that is, a holomorphic solution which is definable in the structure of restricted elementary functions? (2) Do solutions of a given list of algebraic differential equations share a nontrivial exponentially algebraic relation , that is, a nontrivial relation definable in the structure of restricted elementary functions? These problems can be traced back to the work of Abel and Liouville on the problem of integration in finite terms. This article concerns generalizations of their techniques adapted to the study of exponential transcendence and independence problems for more general systems of differential equations. As concrete applications, we obtain exponential transcendence and independence statements for several classical functions: the error function, the Bessel functions, indefinite integrals of algebraic expressions involving Lambert’s W W -function, the equation of the pendulum, as well as corresponding decidability results.

1.
Introduction

In this article, we study the following “functional” problems of exponential algebraicity. Consider the o-minimal structure of
restricted elementary functions

\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}:=(\mathbb{R},0,1,+,\times,<,\text{restricted complex exp})

obtained by expanding semialgebraic geometry by the complex exponential function restricted to some (equivalently all) compact box
[
42
]
.

(1)

Does a given algebraic differential equation admits an
exponentially algebraic solution
, that is, a holomorphic solution which is definable in the structure
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
of restricted elementary functions?

(2)

Do solutions of a given list of algebraic differential equations share a nontrivial
exponentially algebraic relation
, that is, a nontrivial relation definable in the structure
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
of restricted elementary functions?

They are the natural respective analogues of the problem of finding the algebraic solutions and the algebraic relations between solutions of algebraic differential equations for the structure
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sa}}=(\mathbb{R},0,1,+,\times,<)
. It is worth stressing that despite the appearance of the field of real numbers, the problems (1) and (2) are concerned with the
holomorphic solutions
of complex algebraic equations and hence with the definability properties of holomorphic functions in certain reducts of the o-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{an}
of restricted analytic functions
[
45
]
.

In the
semialgebraic
case, decidability of (1) is a famously open problem known as Poincaré’s problem
[
30
]
. For linear differential equations, the problems (1) and (2) are the subject of several important number-theoretic conjectures and
Picard-Vessiot differential Galois theory

[
43
]
provides effective tools to study linear versions of these problems in the semialgebraic case. The aim of this article is to develop a parallel Galois-theoretic framework, Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
below, to study the analogous problems of exponential algebraicity.

Applications of this framework to the study of classical functions which already appears in Liouville’s work
[
25
,
26
]
are discussed in the introduction and described in greater details in the fifth section of the text.

1.1.
Some history

The study of the problems (1) and (2) in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
can be traced back to the problem of integration in finite terms studied by Abel and Liouville. The classical framework focuses on the case of indefinite integrals and on a
subclass
of the class of exponentially algebraic functions, the class of
elementary functions
. They are the analytic branches of the meromorphic functions which can obtained by iterated compositions of the complex exponential, the branches of the complex logarithm and algebraic functions involving possibly several complex variables.

A predecessor of the larger class of
exponentially algebraic functions
is what Liouville calls “les fonctions finies implicites” in
[
26
]
. They are the functions which can be implicitly defined from the class of elementary functions by “using the implicit function theorem”. We refer to the main theorem of
[
45
]
for a precise version of this equivalence. A prototype studied by Liouville is the Lambert
W
-function given by

(1)

W(z)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-m)^{m-1}}{m!}z^{m}

which appears in the solutions of many problems in enumerative combinatorics, biology and physics
[
6
,
44
]
. The ubiquity of (
1
) comes from its definition as a solution of the functional equation
w\cdot e^{w}=z
which provides its implicit definition as an exponentially algebraic function. Liouville
[
26
]
showed however that (
1
) can not be explicitly defined within the smaller class of elementary functions.

The starting point of Liouville’s theory of integration
[
25
]
motivated by the integration of the pendulum concerns the
exponential transcendence
of the elliptic integrals:

(2)

\mathrm{sn}(z)=\int\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{3}+az+b}}\text{ where }4a^{3}+27b^{2}\neq 0.

Liouville showed that this function is not elementary. As described in Ritt’s monograph
[
35
]
on Liouville’s theory and in
[
41
]
, the fact that (
2
) is not even exponentially algebraic then follows from a statement written by Abel a few years before, which in the terminology of this paper can be reformulated as:

if the indefinite integral of an algebraic function is exponentially algebraic then it is an elementary function
.

Although there is no trace of Abel’s proof of this statement, Ritt
[
35
]
and then Risch
[
34
]
have both established rigorous forms of Abel’s statement.

Since the seventies, the scope of Liouville’s theory of integration has been greatly expanded in connection with Ax’s proof of the functional version of Schanuel conjecture
[
1
]
. The connection between these two problems already appears implicitly in Risch’s “solution of the problem of integration in finite terms”
[
33
]
and was made fully explicit by Rosenlicht in
[
36
]
. These developments led to the development of complete decision procedures
[
33
,
3
,
38
,
31
]
to decide if
the indefinite integral of an arbitrary elementary function

f(z)
is elementary and to compute it explicitly when it is.

1.2.
A generalization of Abel’s result

The first result of this article is a generalization of Abel’s result which is missing from Liouville’s theory of integration. For instance, it is known since Liouville’s work that the
error function

\mathrm{erf}(z)=\int e^{-z^{2}}dz

is not elementary but is it exponentially algebraic? In other words, can it be
implicitly
constructed from the class of elementary functions? This strengthening is in general not automatic. For instance, all the solutions of the first-order equation associated to the Lotka-Volterra systems:

(3)

\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{\alpha x-\beta xy}{\gamma y-\delta xy}\text{ where }\alpha,\beta,\delta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\text{ and }\alpha\neq\gamma

are exponentially algebraic but (
3
) admits no elementary solutions, see
[
10
,
9
]
. The following theorem provides a general dividing line which separates the algebraic differential equations which satisfy Abel’s statement from the other ones.

Theorem A

(Theorem
4.19
)
.

Consider an algebraic differential equation

(4)

F(y,y^{\prime},\ldots,y^{(n)})=0

whose coefficients are elementary functions and assume that the equation is internal to the constants. Then every exponentially algebraic solution of the equation is an elementary function (in the sense of Liouville).

The assumption that the equation (
4
) is internal to the constants is an important model-theoretic assumption in differential algebra which allows for the development of a differential Galois theory
[
13
,
29
,
27
]
. The differential equation (
3
) witnesses that it can not be avoided. An algebraic differential equation is
internal to the constants
if from finitely many particular solutions, one can express any other solution (in any differential field extension) as an algebraic combination of these particular solutions, their derivatives and constants. In particular, this theorem applies to many classical families of examples such as
for the indefinite integrals of elementary functions, for the solutions of (possibly inhomogeneous) linear differential equations, Riccati equations and elliptic differential equations
.

Using a combination of classical results and techniques from the problem of integration in finite terms, Theorem A implies the exponential transcendence of several important classical functions such as the expressions of the form

(5)

\mathrm{Ei}(z)=\int\frac{e^{z}}{z}dz,\mathrm{Ai}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{cos}(t^{3}/3+zt)dt\text{ and }\int\frac{W(z)}{z^{2}}dz.

The first two examples can be studied inside decidable frameworks: Risch’s solution of the problem of integration in finite terms
[
38
]
and Hrushovski’s computation of differential Galois groups
[
13
]
. Corollary
5.5
explains how to extend Risch’s decidable framework to cover certain indefinite integrals involving local analytic inverses of elementary functions such as the last example in (
5
).

Another meaningful application of Theorem
A
is the exponential transcendence of the indefinite elliptic integrals also proved in
[
17
]
studied in our setting using elliptic differential equations.

Corollary

(Corollary
5.7
)
.

Consider
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
and the equation of the pendulum

(6)

\theta^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}\cdot\mathrm{sin}(\theta)=0.

The exponentially algebraic solutions of (
6
) are exactly the solutions with energy

H=1/2(\theta^{\prime})^{2}-\omega^{2}\mathrm{cos}(\theta)=\pm\omega^{2}.

The restriction to the real locus of the exponentially algebraic solutions of (
6
) are easy to detect on the usual phase portrait of the pendulum: they are the so-called
homoclinic solutions
which separate the bounded solutions oscillating around the stable equilibrium positions from the unbounded ones. They can indeed be expressed using inverse trigonometric functions
[
7
]
.

We give two proofs of this result. The first proof based on Theorem
A
provides a
decision procedure
for finding the exponential algebraic solutions of one-dimensional Hamiltonian systems of the form

(7)

H=\frac{y^{2}}{2}+V(x)\text{ where }V(x)\text{ is an elementary function.}

This appears as Corollary
5.8
below. This procedure involves one application of the implicit function theorem and the computation of an indefinite integral. In the case of (
6
), the computation reduces to the computation of the Legendre’s elliptic integral which were already known to Liouville.

The second Galois-theoretic proof uses Theorem
B
below. Using the change of variables
u=\mathrm{cos}(\theta/2)
which preserves exponential algebraicity, (
6
) can be transformed into an autonomous algebraic differential equation given as a
nonisotrivial family of elliptic differential equations

(8)

\Big(\frac{du}{dz}\Big)^{2}=\omega^{2}(1-u^{2})\Big(u^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}-h_{0}}{2\omega^{2}}\Big).

The “nondegeneracy condition”
h_{0}\neq\pm\omega^{2}
describe the smooth fibers of this elliptic fibration, that is, those which do not degenerate as rational curves. Under this condition, (
8
) is an
elliptic differential equation
, that is, a differential equation which is internal to the constants and with Galois group an elliptic curve. In fact, Theorem
B
leads to a stronger result namely that:

whenever
(\omega_{i},h_{i})
are chosen so that
h_{i}\neq\pm\omega_{i}^{2}
and the elliptic curves

v^{2}=\omega_{i}^{2}(1-u^{2})\Big(u^{2}-\frac{\omega_{i}^{2}-h_{i}}{2\omega_{i}^{2}}\Big)

are pairwise nonisogeneous then corresponding solutions of (
6
) are exponentially algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
.

The content of such exponential algebraic independence statements is described below before the statement of Theorem B.

1.3.
Exponential algebraic independence and differential Galois theory

We say that meromorphic functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{M}(U)
are
exponentially algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
if they satisfy no nontrivial exponentially algebraic relations in the sense that:

whenever
G
is a holomorphic function definable on a connected open subset of
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
such that the function

z\mapsto G(z,f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{n}(z))
is well-defined and vanishes on a nontrivial open subset of
U
then
G
vanishes identically on
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.

Natural problems of exponential algebraicity can be built from the same classical examples studied by Liouville: For instance, are
\mathrm{sn}(z)
and
\mathrm{erf}(z)
exponentially algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
? What about
\mathrm{Ai}(z)
and
\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(z)
?

We describe of framework using definable Galois theory
[
29
,
27
]
which applies to (complete) types which are internal to the constants in the theory
\mathrm{DCF}
of differentially closed fields. This generalizes Picard-Vessiot differential Galois theory to include elliptic equations such as (
8
). Following the model-theoretic terminology, we refer to the Galois groups as
binding groups
. Two essential ingredients of the Galois-theoretic treatment of the algebraic relations between solutions of linear differential equations are:

(a)

the field-theoretic algebraic closure
\mathrm{acl}(-)
is a combinatorial
pregeometry
, that is, it is a monotone closure operator with finite character that satisfies the
exchange property
:
a\in\mathrm{acl}(A,b)\setminus\mathrm{acl}(A)\Rightarrow b\in\mathrm{acl}(A,a)
.

(b)

a Galois-theoretic characterization of algebraicity: whenever
p=\mathrm{tp}^{D}(f/k)
is a type internal to the constants realized in a differential field
without new constants
then
p
is an algebraic type if and only if its Galois group is trivial.

In practice, these properties are often used together with a group-theoretic lemma,
Goursat lemma
which describes the proper algebraic subgroups of a product
G_{1}\times G_{2}
projecting surjectively on both factors. This leads to automatic algebraic independence statements, see
[
23
]
.

In the exponential algebraic case, the first step (a) was adapted to the study of exponential algebraicity in
[
19
]
where a pregeometry called
exponential algebraic closure

(9)

\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(C,-):\mathcal{P}(K)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(K)

is defined on any differentially closed field
K
with field of constants
C
.
This pregeometry will be described in greater details in subsection 1.4 of the introduction. Regarding the second step (b), we prove the following theorem:

Theorem B

(Theorem
4.21
)
.

Let
k
be a differential field with an algebraically closed field of constants and
f
an element of a differential field extension of
k
without new constants. Assume that the algebraic closure of
k
is self-sufficient and that
f
satisfies a differential equation internal to the constants with parameters in
k
. Then

if
f\in\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(k)
then the binding group of
\mathrm{tp}^{D}(f/k)
is linear and abelian-by-finite.

Here, an algebraically closed subfield
k
extending the field
C
of constants of some differentially closed field
K
is
self-sufficient
if the analogue of the Ax-Schanuel Theorem holds over
k
in the sense that:

whenever
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in K
and
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in K
are exponentials of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
respectively then

\mathrm{td}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}/k)\geq n

provided that the
(f_{i},e_{i})
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent in
(\mathrm{G}_{a}\times\mathrm{G}_{m})(K)/(\mathrm{G}_{a}\times\mathrm{G}_{m})(k)
.

Corollary
2.20
shows that this condition does not depend on the choice of the differentially closed field
K
extending
k
.

As applications of Theorem
B
, we obtain the exponential algebraic independence of the solutions of (
6
), as well as the exponential independence of
\mathrm{sn}(z)
,
\mathrm{erf}(z)
and
\mathrm{Ai}(z)
, see Corollary
5.9
. In the case of the Bessel functions given by

J_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!\cdot\Gamma(\alpha+m+1)}(\frac{z}{2})^{2m+\alpha},

we get the following generalization of Kolchin’s result
[
23
]
.

Corollary

(Corollary
5.11
)
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
be complex numbers such that

\alpha_{i}\notin\mathbb{Z}+1/2
and
\alpha_{i}\pm\alpha_{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for
i\neq j
.

Then the Bessel functions
J_{\alpha_{1}}(z),\ldots,J_{\alpha_{n}}(z)

and their first derivatives are
2n
exponentially algebraically independent holomorphic functions over
\mathbb{C}(z)
.

1.4.
Relation with the model theory of the blurred complex exponential

A fundamental model-theoretic input of our framework is the first-order theory
\mathrm{BE}
of the
blurred complex exponential

[
19
,
21
]
. We briefly describe the central role of this theory in the rest of the paper.

While Zilber’s first-order treatment of complex exponential algebra is conditional to the validity of several number-theoretic conjectures, this can be avoided for the functional problems (1) and (2) following the approach of
[
19
,
21
]
. Roughly speaking, it consists in “adding a field of constants to exponential algebra”:

A
blurred exponential field

(k,C,\Gamma)
is a pair
k/C
of algebraically closed fields equipped with a divisible subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{G}_{m}(k)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(k)
extending the trivial correspondence
\mathrm{G}_{m}(C)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(C)
on the field
C
of constants.

This approach comes with a direct connection with differential algebra: every algebraically closed differential field
(k,\partial)
carries such a structure obtained by setting

(10)

\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}}:=\{(y,x)\in\mathrm{G}_{m}(k)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(k)\mid\partial(y)/y=\partial(x)\}.

The field
C
is then the field of constants of
k
. A fundamental result relates the reduct of the theory
\mathrm{DCF}
of ordinary differentially closed fields in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}=\{0,1,+,-,\times,\Gamma\}
where the interpretation of
\Gamma
is given by (
10
) to the theory deduced from
(\mathbb{C},\mathrm{exp})
by “blurring” the graph of the exponential function. In the second case, one first constructs a countable “field of constants”
k_{0}
by taking intersection of constants fields of
E
-derivations
lying in

\mathrm{EDer}(\mathbb{C})=\{D\in\mathrm{Der}(\mathbb{C})\mid\forall a\in\mathbb{C},D(e^{a})=e^{a}\cdot D(a)\}

and then interprets the correspondence
\Gamma
as

\Gamma_{0}=\{(y,x)\in\mathrm{G}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(\mathbb{C}\mid\exists c\in k_{0}\mid y=c\cdot\mathrm{exp}(x)\}.

The main result of
[
21
]
states the two constructions lead to elementary substructures in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
. The common
\omega
-stable theory
\mathrm{BE}
is called the theory of the
blurred complex exponential
.

We refer the reader to Section 2 for more details on this theory and in particular for the construction of the pregeometry
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(C,-)
presented in (
9
) on any model
K
of the theory
\mathrm{BE}
. On the standard models
K_{0}=(\mathbb{C},k_{0},\Gamma_{0})
, the pregeometry
\mathrm{ecl}^{K_{0}}(k_{0},-)
admits a concrete description based on the o-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
. As a consequence of
[
45
,
20
]
, it can be phrased as an equality of two pregeometries over the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers

(11)

\mathrm{ecl}^{K_{0}}(k_{0},-)=\mathrm{hcl}_{\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}}(-)

where
\mathrm{hcl}
is a pregeometry called
holomorphic closure
associated to certain reducts of the o-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{an}
, see also
[
17
,
16
]
. In the case of
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
, this second pregeometry is defined by closing a subset
A
of
\mathbb{C}
under the evaluation of
\mathrm{RE}
-definable holomorphic functions, that is,

\mathrm{hcl}(A)=\left\{f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\mid\begin{cases}a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A\\
f\text{ is a }k_{0}\text{-definable holomorphic function}\\
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathrm{dom}(f)\end{cases}\right\}.

The main result of Section 3 is a functional variant of (
11
) which describes the pregeometry
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},-)
on differentially closed fields
K
with field of constants
\mathbb{C}
.

Theorem C

(Theorem
3.1
)
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
be holomorphic functions of one variable defined on some complex domain
U
. Then
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
are exponentially algebraically dependent over
\mathbb{C}
if and only if there exist a subdomain
V
of
U
, a connected open subset
W
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
containing the analytic curve
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})(V)
and a nonzero holomorphic function
F
of
n
variables definable in
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
such that

F(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{n}(z))=0\text{ for all }z\in V.

Furthermore, if
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1}
are exponentially algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}
, the previous condition is also equivalent to the existence of a subdomain
V
of
U
, a connected open subset
W
of
\mathbb{C}^{n-1}
containing the analytic curve
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n-1})(V)
and a holomorphic function
G
of
n-1
variables definable in
\mathbb{R}_{RE}
such that

f_{n}(z)=G(f_{1}(z),\ldots,f_{n-1}(z))\text{ for all }z\in V.

Here, the meromorphic functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
are exponentially algebraically dependent over
\mathbb{C}
if they are exponentially algebraically independent in the sense of
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},-)
in some/any differentially closed field
K
extending
\mathcal{M}(U)
without new constants.

Hence, Theorem C provides
a differential algebraic formulation
of the complex-analytic problems (1) and (2) concerning the definability properties of holomorphic functions in the o-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{RE}}
. Replacing formulas with complete types, these problems are fully encoded in the
reduct map

(12)

\mathrm{red}:S_{n}^{D}(\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg})\rightarrow S_{n}^{\mathrm{BE}}(\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg})

defined by
tp^{D}(a/\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg})\mapsto tp^{\mathrm{BE}}(a/\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg}
using the construction of
\mathrm{BE}
as a
\mathrm{DCF}
-reduct. The study of the
stability-theoretic
description of (
12
) was our initial motivation to study the problem of integration in finite terms and Theorem A and Theorem B achieve an effective description of (
12
) for types which are
internal to the constants
.

It is natural to expect interesting counterparts for these results at the other ends of Zilber’s trichotomy, as well, such as in the description of
strongly minimal sets with exponentially algebraic solutions
. Classical examples of completely disintegrated strongly minimal equations connected to Liouville’s work include:

Corollary

(Corollary
5.13
)
.

Any distinct solutions
W_{1}(z),\ldots,W_{n}(z)
of Kepler’s equation and Lambert’s equation respectively

w-\mathrm{sin}(w)=z\text{ and }w\cdot e^{w}=z

are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
.

The investigation of these other stability-theoretic properties of the reduct map (
12
) is postponed until a future work. This includes important questions about the theory
\mathrm{BE}
such as: are the solutions of minimal locally-modular and not geometrically trivial types
exponentially transcendental
? Can exponentially algebraic functions satisfy
strongly minimal
algebraic differential equations of arbitrary high order? Are strongly minimal systems with exponentially algebraic solutions necessarily
\omega
-categorical
?

2.
Preliminaries on the blurred exponential reduct

In this section, we recall several results from
[
19
]
concerning exponential algebraicity in differential fields and how it is encoded in the blurred exponential reduct
\mathrm{BE}
of the theory
\mathrm{DCF}
of differentially closed fields of characteristic zero. Here, we only consider the exponential map of the multiplicative group
\mathrm{G}_{m}
but the set up of
[
19
]
is more general allowing for semiabelian varieties defined over the constants. We refer the reader to
[
36
]
for background on differential forms.

2.1.
Blurred exponential fields

Definition 2.1
.

A
blurred exponential field

(k,C,\Gamma)
is a pair
k/C
of algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero and equipped with a
2
-ary predicate
\Gamma
satisfying

(i)

\Gamma
is a divisible subgroup of
T\mathrm{G}_{m}(k)=\mathrm{G}_{m}(k)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(k)
,

(ii)

\Gamma\cap(\mathrm{G}_{m}(k)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(C))=\mathrm{G}_{m}(C)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(C)
and symmetrically
\Gamma\cap(\mathrm{G}_{m}(C)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(k))=\mathrm{G}_{m}(C)\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(C)
.

The second property ensures that the algebraically closed field
C
which we call the
field of constants of

(k,\Gamma)
is definable from the
2
-ary predicate
\Gamma
as

C=\{x\in k\mid(1,x)\in\Gamma\}.

For this reason, we consider blurred exponential fields as structures in the language
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}=\{0,1,+,-,\times,\Gamma\}
. Morphisms of blurred exponential fields are morphisms of
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
-structures.

Example 2.2
.

The correspondence

(k,\Delta)\mapsto(k^{alg},C(k^{alg}),\Gamma_{BE})

where
\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}}:=\{(y,x)\in T\mathrm{G}_{m}(k^{alg})=\mathrm{G}_{m}(k^{alg})\times\mathrm{G}_{a}(k^{alg})\mid\frac{\partial(y)}{y}=\partial(x)\text{ for all }\partial\in\Delta\}

defines a functor for the category of differential fields with
s
commuting derivations to the category of blurred exponential fields.

We shall focus on the case of ordinary differential fields and on models of the complete theory
\mathrm{DCF}
of ordinary differentially closed fields of characteristic zero.

Definition 2.3
.

The
theory
\mathrm{BE}
of the blurred exponential reduct
is the common and complete
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
-theory of all blurred exponential fields
(K,C,\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}})
obtained by applying the functor from Example
2.2
to ordinary differentially closed fields
(K,\partial)\models\mathrm{DCF}
.

In the language
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
, an axiomatization of the theory of
(K,C,\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}})
is presented in
[
21
, Fact 2.3]
as follows: the universal part of the theory contains a countable scheme of axioms expressing the
Ax-Schanuel property
:

If
a\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}}^{n}
satisfies
\mathrm{td}(a/C)\leq n
then there is a proper algebraic subgroup
J
of
\mathrm{G}_{m}^{n}
such that
a\in TJ+T\mathrm{G}_{m}(C)
.

The existential part of
\mathrm{BE}
expresses that
(K,C,\Gamma)
a
is a nontrivial blurred exponential field and contains additional
existential closedness axioms

for every free and rotund subvariety
V
of
(\mathrm{G}_{m}\times\mathrm{G}_{a})^{n}
, the intersection
V\cap\Gamma^{n}
is Zariski-dense in
V
.

See
[
21
]

2.2.
Reformulation of the Ax-Schanuel Theorem

Since the extensions of blurred exponential fields are algebraically closed, we say that they are

Definition 2.9

Let

be a finite-dimensional extension of blurred exponential fields with the same field of constants. The

\delta(L/k)=\mathrm{td}(L/k)-\mathrm{ldim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\Gamma(L)/\Gamma(k))\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\}.

Let

for all finite-dimensional subextensions

.

Corollary

below states that this condition does not depend on the choice of the elementary submodel

extending
k
without new constants.

Example 2.10

\delta(M/L)=\delta(M/k)-\delta(M/L)=\delta(M/k)\geq 0

In particular, every elementary extension of

For the first part, we have that

so that

\delta(L/k)\leq\mathrm{ldim}_{L}(\Omega^{1}(L/k))-\mathrm{ldim}_{L}(L\cdot\Theta_{\mathrm{BE}}(L/k)).

Since
\mathrm{Der}_{\mathrm{BE}}(L/k)

To prove the second part, consider a nontrivial

-derivation

2.3.
Exponential hulls

Fix

a self-sufficient substructure contained in a model

without new constants.

Lemma 2.15

(
[
19

)
.

The exponential predimension is submodular , that is,

\delta(M\cap N/k)+\delta((M\cdot N)^{alg}/k)\leq\delta(M/k)+\delta(N/k)

for all finite-dimensional blurred exponential field subextensions
M/k
and

in

of finite transcendence degree.

Importantly for the validity of this lemma, all the extensions of blurred exponential fields are assumed to be algebraically closed. Note also that taking

M=\mathbb{Q}(z+\pi)^{alg}\text{ and }N=\mathbb{Q}(z)^{alg}

then both
M/k
and
N/k
have no new constants but

has

as a new constant, hence working inside an elementary submodel

without new constants is required.

Let
L/k
be a finite-dimensional extension of substructures in
K
. As a consequence of Lemma
2.15
, the set of finite-dimensional extensions

in
K
satisfying that:

M
extends
L
and
\delta(M/k)
is minimal among the finite-dimensional extensions of
L
in
K
,

is stable under finite intersections and algebraic closure of the compositum.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
L/k
be a finite-dimensional extension of substructures in
K
. The

L/k
in
K

is the smallest (necessarily finite-dimensional) extension of
k
in
K
containing
L
with minimal predimension over
k
. When

, we also write
\mathrm{Hull}_{K}(a/k)
for
\mathrm{Hull}_{K}(k(a)^{alg})
.

The following lemma shows that
\mathrm{Hull}_{K}(L)
does not depend on

. More generally, for a (not necessarily finite-dimensional) substructure
L
extending the field
C
of constants, one can define its hull as the smallest self-sufficient extension of
L
in
K
.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
L/k
be a finite-dimensional extension in
K
. Then
\mathrm{Hull}_{K}(L)
is the smallest self-sufficient extension of
L
in
K
.

In particular,

is self-sufficient in

if and only if for all tuple

from

\mathrm{Hull}(a/k)\subset L
.

Proof.

Certainly,

is self-sufficient in
K
since
\delta(M/N)\leq 0
implies that

\delta(M/k)=\delta(M/N)+\delta(N/k)\leq\delta(N/k).

Hence, by minimality of
\delta(N/k)
, we must have
\delta(M/N)=0
. Conversely, consider
M/L
an algebraically closed extension of
L
self-sufficient in
K
. By submodularity, we have:

\delta(M\cap N/k)\leq\delta(M/k)+\delta(N/k)-\delta(M\cdot N/k)=\delta(N/k)-\delta(M\cdot N/M)

Since
M
is self-sufficient in
K
, the latter is bounded from above by
\delta(N/k)
. By minimality of
\delta(N/k)
, we must that
\delta(M\cap N/k)=\delta(N/k)
and by minimality of

, we have

as required.

The second part follows from the fact that an increasing union of self-sufficient substructures is self-sufficient in
K
.
∎

4.1.
The Maurer-Cartan equation

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and let
G
be a connected algebraic group over
k
of dimension
n
and let
\mathfrak{g}
be its Lie algebra.

Definition 4.1
.

An
invariant one-form
on
G
is a rational one-form
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(k(G)/k)
which is right-invariant in the sense that
R_{g}^{\ast}\omega=\omega
for all
g\in G
where
R_{g}:x\mapsto x\cdot g
denotes the right-translation by
g
on
G
.

It is well-known that every invariant one-form on
G
is regular at every point
g
of
G
and that the space of invariant one-forms on
G
is a
k
-vector space of dimension
n
, see
[
22
]
for example. When
G
is commutative, a fundamental property is that every invariant one-form is
closed
. In general, this condition is replaced by the
Maurer-Cartan equation
(
17
) described below.

Definition 4.2
.

Fix
M
a smooth algebraic variety over
k
. A
rational one-form on
M
with values in
\mathfrak{g}
is an element
\theta\in\Omega^{1}(k(M)/k)\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{g}
.

Choosing a basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
of the Lie-algebra
\mathfrak{g}
and writing
\mathfrak{g}=ke_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus ke_{n}
then we can decompose any rational one-form on
M
with values in
\mathfrak{g}
as:

\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta^{i}\otimes e_{i}\text{ where }\theta^{1},\ldots,\theta^{n}\in\Omega^{1}(k(M)/k).

We say that
\theta
is regular at some point

if the one-forms
\theta^{1},\ldots\theta^{n}
are all regular at
p
. For all the points
p
in the Zariski-open subset of regular points of
\theta
, the induced map

\theta_{p}:T_{p}M\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}

given by
v\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta^{i}(v)\cdot e_{i}
is
k
-linear.

Definition 4.3
.

Assume that
\mathrm{dim}(M)=n
and let
\theta
be a rational one-form on
M
with values in
\mathfrak{g}
. We say that
\theta
is a
rational coparallelism
if for all points
p
in a Zariski-dense open subset of
M
,
\theta_{p}:T_{p}M\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
is a
k
-linear isomorphism.

Example 4.4
.

Assume that we are given a rational parallelism of
M
, that is,
n
derivations
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
which form a
k(M)
-basis of
\mathrm{Der}(k(M)/k)
and denote by
\theta^{1},\ldots,\theta^{n}
the dual basis of
\Omega^{1}(k(M)/k)
. Then

\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta^{i}\otimes e_{i}

is a coparallelism on
M
.

Conversely, if a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued one-form
\theta
is a coparallelism then the one-forms
\theta^{1},\ldots,\theta^{n}
form a
k(M)
-basis of
\Omega^{1}(k(M)/k)
. Hence, every rational coparallelism is associated with the rational parallelism of
M
given by the dual basis
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
of
\mathrm{Der}(k(M)/k)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Denote by
c_{i,j}^{l}\in k
the structure constants of the Lie-algebra
\mathfrak{g}
defined by

[e_{i},e_{j}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}c_{i,j}^{l}\cdot e_{l}.

With the notation of Example
4.4
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
, we have
[D_{i},D_{j}]=\sum_{l=1}^{n}c_{i,j}^{l}\cdot D_{l}
where
[-,-]
denotes the Lie-bracket of derivations on
\mathrm{Der}(k(M)/k)
,

(ii)

the coparallelism
\theta
satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation

(17)

d\theta^{i}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,l=1}^{n}c_{j,l}^{i}\cdot\theta^{j}\wedge\theta^{l}\in\Omega^{2}(k(M)/k).

Here,

denotes the exterior derivative of one-forms and
\wedge:\Omega^{1}(k(M)/k)\times\Omega^{1}(k(M)/k)\rightarrow\Omega^{2}(k(M)/k)
the wedge-product of one-forms. See
[
4
, Section 1]
and also
[
39
, Chapter 3, section 3]
for a proof of this lemma as well as a coordinate-free expression of the equation (

).

Definition 4.6
.

Assume that
\mathrm{dim}(M)=n
and let
\theta
be a rational one-form on
M
with values in
\mathfrak{g}
. We say that
\theta
is a
coparallelism of type

\mathfrak{g}
if it is a rational coparallelism which satisfies the Maurer-Cartan equation of Lemma

.

Example 4.7
.

The coparallelism
\theta_{G}\in\Omega^{1}(k(G)/k))\otimes\mathfrak{g}
associated with the parallelism

of

by right-invariant vector fields is a parallelism of type
\mathfrak{g}
. It is the called the (right)

of

For

with Lie-algebra

. In the coordinates

such that

, the Maurer-Cartan form of

\theta_{G}=(dX_{i,j})_{i,j}\cdot(X_{i,j})_{i,j}^{-1}\in\Omega^{1}(G/k)\otimes\mathrm{Mat}_{n}(k).

If

is a connected closed subgroup of

\phi^{\ast}\theta_{G}
of Maurer-Cartan form of

\mathfrak{g}

As a nonlinear example, if

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
M
be a smooth algebraic variety over
k
of dimension
n
and let
\theta
be a coparallelism of type
\mathfrak{g}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}
is abelian if and only if

d\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\theta^{i}\otimes e_{i}=0\in\Omega^{2}(k(M)/k)\otimes\mathfrak{g}.

Proof.

Denote by
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
the parallelism associated
\theta
. By definition of the wedge-product of one-forms if

then

(\theta^{j}\wedge\theta^{l})(D_{s},D_{t})=\theta^{j}(D_{s})\theta^{l}(D_{t})-\theta^{j}(D_{t})\theta^{l}(D_{s})=\begin{cases}1\text{ if }j=s\text{ and }l=t,\\
-1\text{ if }j=t\text{ and }l=s,\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

It follows from the Maurer-Cartan equation above that for

d\theta^{i}(D_{s},D_{t})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,l}c^{i}_{j,l}(\theta^{j}\wedge\theta^{l})(D_{s},D_{t})=-\frac{1}{2}(c^{i}_{s,t}-c^{i}_{t,s})=-c^{i}_{s,t}

Hence

is abelian if and only if all the structure constants of

are equal to zero if and only if

∎

4.2.
Abelian invariant differentials

Let

Definition 4.9
.

Let

The induced one-form

This notion coincides with the notion used by Kolchin in
[

and by the second author in
[

[

Proposition 4.10
.

be a field extension, let

An easy reduction shows that it is enough to prove the statement assuming that

and

Set

an irreducible subvariety of

The equality in the assumption of the lemma states that

∎

4.3.
Relation with differential Galois theory

Assume now that
k
is an algebraically closed differential field with field of constants
C
and let
(L,\partial)/k
be a differential field extension of finite transcendence degree. The Lie derivative:

\mathcal{L}_{\partial}:\Omega^{1}(L/k)\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(L/k)

defines a differential module structure on
\Omega^{1}(L/k)
over
L
.

Lemma 4.11
.

The differential module
(\Omega^{1}(L/k),\mathcal{L}_{\partial})
is the dual of the differential module on
\mathrm{Der}(L/k)
defined by the Lie bracket with the derivation
\partial
given by

D\mapsto[\partial,D]=\partial\circ D-D\circ\partial.

Proof.

First note that since
\partial(k)\subset k
,
D\mapsto[\partial,D]=\partial\circ D-D\circ\partial
is well-defined and satisfies the Leibniz rule of differential modules. As defined in
[
36
]
, the Lie-derivative is the unique differential module structure on
\Omega^{1}(L/k)
satisfying

\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(df)=d(\partial(f))\text{ for all }f\in L.

Therefore it is enough to see that the dual
(\Omega^{1}(L/k),L_{\partial})
of
(\mathrm{Der}(L/k),[\partial,-])
satisfies this condition. Denote by
\langle-,-\rangle:\Omega^{1}(L/k)\times\mathrm{Der}(L/k)\rightarrow L
the duality pairing and let
f\in L
. For every derivation
D\in\mathrm{Der}(L/k)
, we have

\partial(\langle df,D\rangle)=\langle L_{\partial}(df),D\rangle+\langle df,[\partial,D]\rangle

Since
\langle df,D\rangle=D(f)
for every
D\in\mathrm{Der}(L/k)
, we conclude that

\langle L_{\partial}(df),D\rangle=\partial(D(f))-[\partial,D](f)=D(\partial(f))=\langle\partial(f),D\rangle.

Since this is true for any
D\in\mathrm{Der}(L/k)
, we obtain
L_{\partial}(df)=d(\partial(f))
as required. This finishes the proof of the lemma.
∎

In the case where

is a

, we have the following description of the solution sets of these differential modules.

.

the Lie-algebra of

Proof.

Note first that standard results of differential Galois theory imply that
\mathrm{td}(L/k)=\mathrm{dim}(G)
so that
\mathrm{dim}(M)=\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{g}(k))=n
and that

Fact 4.13

(
[

Let

V=\{D\in\mathrm{Der}(L/k)\mid D\circ\partial=\partial\circ D\}

Denote by

are

. Indeed, otherwise consider a nontrivial

\lambda_{1}D_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}D_{m}=0\text{ where }\lambda_{i}\in L

Applying

leads to

\partial(\lambda_{1})D_{1}+\cdots+\partial(\lambda_{m})D_{m}=\partial(\lambda_{2})D_{2}+\cdots+\partial(\lambda_{m})D_{m}=0.

By minimality of our choice, this must be the trivial
L
-linear relation so that the
\lambda_{i}
are constants. This contradicts the

-linear independence of

.

By comparing the dimension, it follows from the claim that

form a
L
-linear basis of
\mathrm{Der}(L/k)
and therefore a rational parallelism of

of type
\mathfrak{g}(k)
. Hence, they define a coparallelism
\theta
of type
\mathfrak{g}(k)
on

by Lemma

. Finally, it follows from the duality that for all

0=\partial(\theta^{i}(D_{j}))=\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\theta^{i})(D_{j})+\theta^{i}([\partial,D_{j}])=\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\theta^{i})(D_{j}).

Hence, the one-form
\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\theta^{i})
vanishes on the
L
-basis

and is therefore identically equal to zero. This finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

In the case of an

Let

\omega(\alpha)\neq 0\in\Omega^{1}(L/k)\text{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\omega(\alpha))=0\in\Omega^{1}(L/k).

Proof.

Denote by
\omega^{1},\ldots,\omega^{n}
a

-basis of invariant one-forms on

. Recall the terminology of
[

]
: an

-primitive element

with the property that:

\langle\partial,\omega^{i}_{L/C}(\alpha)\rangle\in k\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,n

where

is the derivation on

,

(

)

There exists an

Denote by

4.4.
Binding groups of exponentially algebraic functions

Let
k
be a self-sufficient algebraically closed differential field with field of constants
C
and let
K
be a differentially closed field extending
k
without new constants.

Lemma 4.16
.

Consider
L/k
a finite dimensional blurred exponential field extension in
K
satisfying
\delta(L/k)=0
. Then
L
is a differential subfield of
K
.

In particular, if
k
is a differential subfield of
K
then
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(k)
is also a differential subfield of
K
.

Proof.

Since
L/k
satisfies
\delta(L/k)=0
,
L
does not contain any differentially transcendental element over
k
and is contained in a differential field
M
of finite transcendence degree over
k
. Consider the exact sequence

0\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(L/k)\otimes_{L}M\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(M/k)\overset{\pi}{\rightarrow}\Omega^{1}(M/L)\rightarrow 0

and the action of the Lie-derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\partial}:\Omega^{1}(M/k)\rightarrow\Omega^{1}(M/k)
. We claim that

\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\Omega^{1}(L/k)\otimes_{L}M)\subset\Omega^{1}(L/k)\otimes_{L}M

if and only if
L
is a differential subfield of
M
.
Indeed, the only thing required to see this is the formula
\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(df)=d(\partial(f))
for
f\in L
. For instance, if
f\in L
then
df\in\Omega^{1}(L/k)
and stability under the Lie-derivative and the previous exact sequence implies that
d(\partial(f))=0\in\Omega^{1}(M/L)
which means that
\partial(f)\in L
. The direct implication of the previous equivalence follows and the proof of the converse is similar. Now, since
\delta(L/k)=0
, Proposition
2.13
implies that

(19)

L\cdot\Theta_{BE}(L/k)=\Omega^{1}(L/k).

Hence, any one-form
\omega\in\Omega^{1}(L/k)\otimes_{L}M
can be written as

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}

where
\lambda_{i}\in M
,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{BE}(L/k)
. This means that each
\theta_{i}
is of the form
dy_{i}/y_{i}-dx_{i}
for some
x_{i},y_{i}\in L
satisfying
\partial(y_{i})/y_{i}=\partial(x_{i})
. Hence

\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\omega_{i})=\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(dy_{i}/y_{i}-dx_{i})=d(\partial(y_{i})/y_{i}-\partial(x_{i}))=0

Using the Leibniz rule, it follows that
\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\Omega^{1}(L/k)\otimes_{L}M)\subset\Omega^{1}(L/k)\otimes_{L}M
as required. SInce any exponentially algebraic element is contained in an extension of predimension zero, the second clause of the lemma follows.
∎

Proposition 4.17
.

Consider
q\in S^{D}(k)
a
\mathcal{C}
-internal type which is exponentially algebraic. Then the binding group
\mathrm{Aut}^{D}(q/\mathcal{C})
of

is isomorphic to a linear algebraic group.

Here, we say that
q
is
exponentially algebraic type
to mean that

is weakly orthogonal to the constants and that some/any realization of

in a differentially closed field

extending

without new constants belongs to
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(k)
. The additional assumption that

is internal to the constants implies that in the saturated model

with field of constants
\mathcal{C}
, the action of

on

coincides with the action of a definable permutation group

of

. The binding group theorem
[
13
, Appendix B]
states that we have a definable isomorphism of permutation groups:

(G(\mathcal{U}),q(\mathcal{U}))\simeq(H(\mathcal{C}),X(\mathcal{C}))

where
(H,X)
is an algebraic permutation group defined over
C
. The content of the proposition is that, under the assumption that

is exponentially algebraic, the algebraic group

is linear.

Proof.

For the sake of a contradiction, assume that we can find an exponentially algebraic type whose binding group is not linear in the previous sense. Since
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(k)
is a differential field by Lemma
4.16
, the main theorem of
[
18
]
ensures the existence of an exponentially algebraic type

whose binding group is isomorphic to a simple abelian variety

over the field of constants

of

.

Consider
a\models q
. Since the binding group of
q
is commutative,

is the strongly normal extension associated to
q
. By Proposition
4.14
, we can find a point
\alpha\in A(L)
and an invariant differential
\omega
on
A
such that the induced differential
\omega(\alpha)\in\Omega^{1}(L/k)
satisfies

\omega(\alpha)\neq 0\text{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\omega(\alpha))=0.

Now, by exponential algebraicity of
x
over
k
, we can find a finitely generated
M/k
satisfying
\delta(M/k)=0
and containing
L
. Proposition
2.13
implies that

. Hence
\omega
can be decomposed as

\omega(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(M/k)

where

and
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{BE}}(M/k)
are

-linearly independent. Using that

, the properties of the Lie-derivative gives:

0=\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\omega(\alpha))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\partial(m_{i})\cdot\theta_{i}+m_{i}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\partial}(\theta_{i}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\partial(m_{i})\cdot\theta_{i}.

Since
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
are

-linearly independent, it follows that

and hence that

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\cdot\theta_{i}\in\Omega^{1}(M/k)

where the
\lambda_{i}\in C
and
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{\mathrm{BE}}(M/k)
.
Expanding each

, we can rewrite the previous equality as

\omega(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(dy_{i}/y_{i}-dx_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}dy_{i}/y_{i}-d\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}x_{i}\Big)

which is an equality between two induced forms of the form described by Proposition
4.10
. It follows from Proposition

that

and therefore in
\Omega^{1}(L/k)
too. This is a contradicts our assumption on
\omega(\alpha)
and finishes the proof.
∎

.

4.5.
Proofs of Theorem A and Theorem B

Let
k
be a differential field. Recall that a type
p\in S^{D}(k)
is
analyzable in the constants
(or
\mathcal{C}
-analyzable) if it is a definable image of a type
p_{n}
obtained from a sequence of definable maps

p_{n}\rightarrow p_{n-1}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow p_{1}

where at each step the type of the fiber is either algebraic or stationary and internal to the constants. The elimination of imaginaries in the theory
\mathrm{DCF}
ensures that the this condition is equivalent to the seemingly stronger condition asserting that
p
itself can be obtained from such a sequence of definable maps. See
[
15
]
for background on analyzability in the constants.

Theorem 4.19

(Theorem A)
.

Let
k
be a self-sufficient differential field and assume that
p\in S^{D}(k)
is a type which is weakly orthogonal to the constants. Then

some/any realization is realized in an elementary differential field extension of
k
without new constants if and only if
p
is both analyzable in the constants and exponentially algebraic.

As stated in the introduction, this theorem can be thought as a model-theoretic generalization of a statement of Abel concerning the indefinite integral of algebraic functions:

if the integral of an algebraic function can be expressed in terms of implicitly or explicitly defined elementary functions then it is elementary.

Indeed, an equivalent form of Theorem
4.19
is the following corollary stated in the introduction, from which one recovers Abel’s claim using that the equations
y^{\prime}=a
are internal to the constants.

Corollary 4.20
.

Assume that
y(z)
is an holomorphic solution of an algebraic differential equation

F(y,y^{\prime},\ldots,y^{(n)})=0

whose coefficients are elementary functions and assume that the equation is internal to the constants. Then
y(z)
is exponentially algebraic if and only if it is an elementary function.

Proof.

Since the coefficients
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}
of
F
are elementary functions,
\mathrm{tp}^{D}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}/k)
is analyzable in the constants. Moreover, by assumption,
\mathrm{tp}^{D}(y/a_{0},\ldots,a_{n},k)
is internal to the constants, it follows by transitivity that
\mathrm{tp}^{D}(y,a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}/k)
and hence
\mathrm{tp}^{D}(y/k)
are analyzable in the constants. Under this assumption, Theorem
4.19
states the
y
is exponentially algebraic if and only if
y
is contained in an elementary extension of
\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg}
, that is, if and only if
y
is an elementary function.
∎

Before proving Theorem
4.19
, we state the second theorem which is a slightly refined version of Theorem
B
from the introduction. Recall that self-sufficient differential fields are algebraically closed by convention.

Theorem 4.21

(Theorem B)
.

Let
k
be a self-sufficient differential field and let
p
be an exponentially algebraic type which is
\mathcal{C}
-internal. Then:

(1)

the binding group
\mathrm{Aut}^{D}(p/\mathcal{C})

\mathrm{G}_{a}^{r}(C)\times\mathrm{G}_{m}^{s}(C)
for some

,

(2)

the type
p
is interdefinable with a product

p_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes p_{r}\otimes q_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes q_{s}

where the
p_{i}
are elementary antiderivatives of elements of
k
and the
q_{j}
are exponentials of elementary antiderivatives,

(3)

in the decomposition given by (2), the realizations of the
p_{i}
and of the

are respectively of the form

(20)

a_{i}+\sum_{s=1}^{n_{i}}\lambda_{i,s}\cdot\mathrm{ln}(b_{i,s})\text{ and }e^{a_{j}}\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{m_{j}}(b_{j,t})^{\lambda_{j,t}}

where the
\lambda_{i,s}
and the

are constants and the

and the

.

Note that for some element

(b,y)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}}(K),(y,b)\in\Gamma_{\mathrm{BE}}(K)\text{ and }y^{\prime}/y=\lambda\cdot b^{\prime}/b.

and any

, the derivative

of

When

, we obtain that

t^{\prime}/t=s^{\prime}=a+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\cdot\frac{b^{\prime}_{i}}{b_{i}}.

As noticed after the statement of Theorem
4.21
, this is equivalent to the integral form given by (
20
). This finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5.
Applications

5.1.
The problem of integration in finite terms

In the terminology of Bronstein
[
3
]
, the problem of
(A,B)
-integration (in finite terms)
is to determine whether a given expression in the class
A
has an antiderivative in the class
B
and to calculate it if it is. We first describe Risch’s solution of the classical problem of Liouville where
A=B
is the class of
elementary functions
: there are two obvious number-theoretic obstruction to decidability:

(1)

we can not decide whether an indefinite integral of the form

\int P(e,\pi)\cdot e^{z^{2}}dz\text{ with }P\in\mathbb{Q}[X,Y]

is elementary or not since we do not know whether
e
and
\pi
are algebraically independent,

(2)

indefinite integrals of the form

\int(\mathrm{ln}(e^{z})-z)e^{z^{2}}dz

are ambiguous since the answer depends on the chosen branch of the logarithm.

It can be shown that both phenomena provide a source of undecidability for the problem of integration in finite terms (see
[
32
, Proposition 2.2]
and the introduction of
[
8
]
). To circumvent these problems, we work under the following natural assumptions:

Assumption 5.1

(Risch)
.

(1)

The field of constants
C_{0}
is an algebraically closed extension of
\mathbb{Q}
of finite transcendence degree equipped with an explicit transcendence basis
\mathcal{B}
. Each constant from
C_{0}
will be prescribed by giving its minimal polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}(\mathcal{B})
.

(2)

Every elementary function is presented as an element
f
belonging to an elementary extension
L=C_{0}(z,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
where for each
i\geq 1

–

either
\theta_{i}
is algebraic over
K_{i-1}=C_{0}(z,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{i-1})
and its minimal polynomial is explicitly given,

–

or
\theta_{i}
is the exponential or the logarithm of an element in
K_{i-1}
which does not already have an exponential or a logarithm in
K_{i-1}^{alg}
.

Under these assumptions, one can compute effectively with the elements of
L
: assign distinct symbols to the elements of the field, construct bases for finitely generated subfields, tell when two elements are equal and factor polynomials over it.
Risch’s solution of the problem of integration in finite terms asserts that this problem becomes decidable in this setting.

Fact 5.2

(Risch’s algorithm)
.

Let
L/C_{0}(z)
be an elementary differential field extension of
C_{0}(z)
satisfying Assumption
5.1
. Then the problem of integration in finite terms for elements of
L
is decidable.

We refer to
[
38
]
for Risch’s proof of this result and to
[
3
]
for another version valid at this level of generality. In the case of algebraic functions, practical implementation of Risch’s algorithm are described in Davenport’s book
[
8
]
. See also
[
31
]
for a description of this algorithm based on the Ax-Schanuel Theorem. These results provide an important source of (practical) decidability for the problems of exponential algebraicity. For instance, the following corollary states the
(A,B)
-problem of integration where
A
is the class of elementary functions and
B
is the class of exponentially algebraic functions is decidable.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
L/C_{0}(z)
an elementary differential field extension satisfying Assumption
5.1
, then

the problem of integration in finite terms for elements of
L
by exponentially algebraic functions is decidable.

This is an immediate consequence of Fact
5.2
and Theorem
4.19
.

Example 5.4
.

Classical examples of this decision procedure going back to Liouville include the
elliptic
(indefinite)
integrals
and the
Chebychev integrals
given respectively by

\int\frac{dz}{\sqrt{z^{3}+az+b}}\text{ with }a,b\in C\text{ and }\int z^{p}(1-z)^{q}dz\text{ with }p,q\in\mathbb{Q}.

They are exponentially algebraic if and only if respectively
4a^{3}+27b^{2}=0
and at least one of
p,q
and
p+q
are integers respectively, see
[
35
, p.37]
. Other classical examples from statistics and optics are the
error function
and the
Fresnel integrals
:

\mathrm{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int e^{-z^{2}}dz\text{, }C(z)=\int\mathrm{cos}(z^{2})dz\text{, }S(z)=\int\mathrm{sin}(z^{2})dz

which are all exponentially transcendental.

In some cases, we show that decidability of the problem of integration by exponentially algebraic functions remains even after allowing in the integrand other exponentially algebraic functions such as the solutions of Lambert’s and Kepler’s equations:

w\cdot e^{w}=z\text{ and }w-\mathrm{sin}(w)=z.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
F(w)
be an elementary function presented as in Risch’s algorithm and consider
W(z)
a local inverse of
F(w)
, that is, a solution of the functional equation

F(w)=z.

The
(A,B)
-integration problem where
B
is the class of exponentially algebraic functions and
A
is the set of expressions of the form

\int G(z,W(z))dz

with
G\in C_{0}(x,y)^{alg}
an algebraic function of two variables is decidable.

Proof.

After the change of variable
z=F(w)
the indefinite integral becomes:

(21)

\int G(F(w),w)\cdot F^{\prime}(w)dw

Following Assumption
5.1
, we can write
F\in C_{0}(w,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
satisfying the properties (1) and (2) of Assumption
5.1
. The element

h(w)=G(F(w),w)\cdot F^{\prime}(w)\in C_{0}(w,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})

belongs to the elementary differential field
L=C_{0}(w,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n+1})
where
\theta_{n+1}=G(F(w),w)
. It follows from Corollary
5.3
that the problem of integration in finite terms for the indefinite integral (
21
) by exponentially algebraic functions is decidable.

It remains to relate exponential algebraicity of (
21
) and exponential algebraicity of
\int G(z,W(z))dz
independently of the choice of the solution
W(z)
. This uses the
change of variables
formula:

(22)

\Big(\int G(z,W(z))dz\Big)\circ F=\int G(F(w),w)F^{\prime}(w)dw.

We give two different proofs of this result. The first one uses Theorem C and the properties of the o-minimal structures. Restricting if necessary the domain of definitions, we may assume that

U_{w}\underset{F}{\overset{W}{\leftrightarrows}}U_{z}

are inverse biholomorphisms where
U_{w}\subset\mathbb{C}
and
U_{z}\subset\mathbb{C}
are complex disks. On general grounds, the properties of o-minimal structures imply that:

•

the compositional inverse of an exponentially algebraic biholomorphism is exponentially algebraic, and

•

the class of exponential algebraic functions is stable under composition.

The statement then follows from the formula (
22
) of change of variables. A differential algebraic proof accounts of the formula (
22
) as follows: the precomposition
f\mapsto f\circ F
defines an isomorphism

\mathcal{M}(U_{z})^{alg}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(U_{w})^{alg}

of
blurred exponential fields
. Note that however, this is
not a morphism of differential fields
. Setting
i_{z}=\int G(z,W(z))dz
and
i_{w}=\int G(F(w),w)F^{\prime}(w)dw
, the formula of change of variables implies that

\mathrm{tp}^{\mathrm{BE}}(z,i_{z}/C)=\mathrm{tp}^{\mathrm{BE}}(w,i_{w}/C)

and hence that
\mathrm{etd}(w,i_{w}/C)=\mathrm{etd}(z,i_{z}/C)
. Since moreover
W(z)
is exponentially algebraic, we also have that
\mathrm{etd}(w,z/C)=1
. Using the exchange property, we get

i_{w}\in\mathrm{ecl}(C,z)\Leftrightarrow i_{w}\in\mathrm{ecl}(C,w)\Leftrightarrow i_{z}\in\mathrm{ecl}(C,z).

This concludes the proof of the corollary.
∎

Example 5.6
.

For the classical
W
-function, this algorithm applied to the indefinite
\int\frac{W(z)}{z}dz
uses the change of variable
z=we^{w}
and computes that

\frac{W(z)}{z}\cdot dz=\frac{w}{we^{w}}\cdot e^{w}(w+1)dw=(w+1)dw

admits
w^{2}/2+w
as an indefinite integral and it follows that

\int\frac{W(z)}{z}dz=W(z)^{2}/2+W(z)

which is an expression of the indefinite integral as an exponentially algebraic function. On the other hand, applying the same change of variables to
\int\frac{W(z)}{z^{2}}dz
leads to

\frac{W(z)}{z}^{2}\cdot dz=\frac{w}{(w^{2}e^{2w})}\cdot e^{w}(w+1)dw=e^{-w}+\frac{e^{-w}}{w}.

In that case, the algorithm concludes that the indefinite integral
\int\frac{W(z)}{z^{2}}dz
is not exponentially algebraic since Liouville showed that
\int\frac{e^{-w}}{w}dw
is not elementary.

5.2.
The equation of the pendulum

One of Liouville’s motivations to study (pseudo)-elliptic integrals comes from their importance in the resolution of important problems in classical mechanics. In the resolution of the equation of the pendulum:

(23)

\theta^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}\cdot\mathrm{sin}(\theta)=0

the elliptic integrals play the role of the inverse trigonometric functions in the resolution of the harmonic oscillator
\theta^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}\cdot\theta=0
. The solutions of the harmonic oscillator

c_{1}\cdot\mathrm{cos}(\omega z)+c_{2}\cdot\mathrm{sin}(\omega z)

are (obviously) exponentially algebraic.

Corollary 5.7
.

Consider the equation (
23
) of the pendulum given by
\theta^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{2}\cdot\mathrm{sin}(\theta)=0
where
\omega
is a nonzero complex number. The exponentially algebraic solutions of
(E)
are precisely the solutions with energy

H=\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}-\omega^{2}\cdot\mathrm{cos}(\theta)=\pm\omega^{2}.

Proof.

The equation (
23
) and its first integral
H=\frac{1}{2}(\theta^{\prime})^{2}-\omega^{2}\cdot\mathrm{cos}(\theta)=\pm\omega^{2}
are both nonalgebraic. Making the change of variables
u=\mathrm{cos}(\theta/2)
sends the solutions of
(\ref{equation-pendulum})
with energy
h_{0}
to solutions of the algebraic differential equation:

(24)

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{du}{dz}\Big)^{2}=\omega^{2}(1-u^{2})\Big(u^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}-h_{0}}{2\omega^{2}}\Big).

Using the method of separation of variables and solving for
z
in terms of
u
, we obtain

z=\int\frac{du}{\omega\sqrt{(1-u^{2})(u^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}-h_{0}}{2\omega^{2}})}}

which is known as Legendre’s elliptic integral of the first kind. Such indefinite integrals were studied by Liouville. He showed (see
[
35
, p.35]
and below) that they are elementary if and only if:

(25)

\frac{\omega^{2}-h_{0}}{2\omega^{2}}\in\{0,1\}

which gives
h_{0}=\pm\omega^{2}
. Theorem A states that they are elementary if and only if they are exponentially algebraic and since the class of exponentially algebraic functions is stable under taking local compositional inverse and composition, the corollary follows.
∎

The proof presented here is based on Liouville’s work and our reformulation of Abel’s result on the indefinite integrals of algebraic functions (Theorem A). It was therefore fully accessible at the times of Liouville but we did not find this formulation in Liouville’s work. A second proof of this result can be deduced from the results of
[
17
]
. Finally, a third possibility is a Galois-theoretic proof based on Theorem B presented as Corollary
5.10
below. In the Galois-theoretic proof, it is the
elliptic equation
(
24
) which plays the important role. In that case, the condition (
25
) is interpretated as the set of parameters where the elliptic curve

v^{2}=\omega^{2}(1-u^{2})(u^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}-h_{0}}{2\omega^{2}})

degenerate as a rational curve..

From the first proof, we deduce a general decision procedure for Hamiltonian systems with one degree of freedom.

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
V(x)
be an elementary function described as in Risch’s algorithm. Consider the algebraic Hamiltonian system with one degree of freedom

(26)

x^{\prime}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial y},y^{\prime}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\text{ where }H=\frac{y^{2}}{2}+V(x)

Given
h_{0}\in C_{0}
in a computable field of constants, one can decide whether all the solutions of the Hamiltonian system with energy
h_{0}
are exponentially algebraic and compute them if they are.

Proof.

The solutions of (
26
) with energy
h_{0}
satisfies the differential equation

\Big(\frac{dy}{dx}\Big)^{2}=h_{0}-V(x).

Given the potential
V(x)
as an element
C(x,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
satisfying Assumption
5.1
, one can determine whether
U(x)=\sqrt{h_{0}-V(x)}
lives in
C(x,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
If it does, the algorithm applies Risch’s decision procedure to the integration of

\frac{dy}{dx}=\pm U(x)

and otherwise applies Risch’s decision procedure to
C(x,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n},\sqrt{h_{0}-V(x)})
satisfying (1) and (2) in Assumption
5.1
.
∎

5.3.
Exponential algebraic independence through differential Galois theory

We now describe applications of Theorem B which treat of the
exponential algebraic independence
of several holomorphic functions.

Corollary 5.9
.

The holomorphic functions

\mathrm{sn}(z)=\int\frac{dz}{z^{3}+az+b},\mathrm{erf}(z)=\int e^{-z^{2}}dz\text{ and }\mathrm{Ai}(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{cos}(tz+t^{3}/3)dz

are exponentially algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
.

Proof.

Fix
K
a differentially closed field containing these three meromorphic functions with field of constants
\mathbb{C}
. Using that
\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},-)
is a pregeometry, we want to show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{sn}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z),\mathrm{erf}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z,\mathrm{sn}(z)),\mathrm{Ai}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z,\mathrm{sn}(z),\mathrm{erf}(z)).

Since the three functions satisfy order two homogeneous linear differential equations, classical computations following Kovacic’s algorithm
[
24
]
show that the types

p=\mathrm{tp}^{D}(\mathrm{sn}(z)/\mathbb{C}(z)),q=\mathrm{tp}^{D}(\mathrm{erf}(z)/\mathbb{C}(z))\text{ and }r=\mathrm{tp}^{D}(\mathrm{Ai}(z)/\mathbb{C}(z))

are internal to the constants and that their binding groups are respectively the additive group
\mathrm{G}_{a}(\mathbb{C})
, the group
\mathrm{Aff}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
of affine transformations of the affine line and the special linear group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
respectively.

Two different proofs of the first statement
\mathrm{sn}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z)
were described after Corollary
5.7
. We now claim that
\mathrm{erf}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z,\mathrm{sn}(z))
. Indeed, set
k=\mathbb{C}(z,\mathrm{sn}(z))^{alg}
and observe that
k
is a self-sufficient differential field because
\mathrm{sn}(z)
is exponentially transcendental, see Example
2.10
. Furthermore, Goursat’s lemma implies that no nontrivial subgroup of

\mathrm{G}_{a}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{Aff}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

projects surjectively on both factors. This expresses that the
\mathrm{PV}
-extensions generated fundamental solutions of these types are linearly disjoint over
k
in
K
. It follows that
q
has a unique extension to
k
, which is its nonforking extension
q\mid k
, and that its binding group is equal to the binding group as
q
. This means that the binding group of

is
\mathrm{Aff}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and since

is a self-sufficient differential field, Theorem B ensures that
\mathrm{erf}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(k)=\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(z,\mathrm{sn}(z))
.

Finally, we claim that
\mathrm{Ai}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z,\mathrm{sn}(z),\mathrm{erf}(z))
follows from a similar proof. Indeed, since
\mathrm{erf}(z)
is exponential transcendental and its derivative in the exponential of an algebraic function, the differential field

is self-sufficient, see Example
2.10

\mathrm{Aff}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})\text{ nor of }\mathrm{G}_{a}(\mathbb{C})\times\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

projects surjectively on both factors. As previously, this means that
r
admits a unique extension to
l
and that its binding group is
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})

l
is a self-sufficient differential field and
tp^{D}(\mathrm{Ai}(z)/l)
as binding group
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, Theorem B ensures that
\mathrm{Ai}(z)\notin\mathrm{ecl}^{K}(\mathbb{C},z,\mathrm{sn}(z),\mathrm{erf}(z))
. This finishes the proof.
∎

The second application treats of the solutions of the pendulum.

.

Let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\neq\pm\omega^{2}

5.4.
Lambert’s and Kepler’s equations

Finally, we end this article with two algebraic independence statements for nonelementary exponentially algebraic functions. The first one concerns the solutions
w=W(z)
of Lambert’s equation:

w\cdot e^{w}=z.

Corollary 5.12
.

Any distinct solutions
W_{1}(z),\ldots,W_{n}(z)
of Lambert’s equation are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
.

Proof.

The functional equation written as
e^{w}=z/w
implies that the
W_{i}(z)
are solutions of the
\mathcal{L}_{\Gamma}
-formula

(28)

\phi(w):=(w,z/w)\in\Gamma

which has coefficients in
\mathbb{C}(z)
. We will show that its solution set is a strongly minimal completely disintegrated set in the theory
\mathrm{DCF}
which implies the desired algebraic independence statement.

Indeed, first note the Ax-Schanuel Theorem implies that
\phi(w)
has no algebraic solutions since
w
and
z/w
can not be both constants. Expanding
\Gamma
explicitly, we obtain the differential equation

(29)

w^{\prime}=\frac{1}{(1+1/w)z}

as an equivalent form of (
28
). Since (
29
) has no algebraic solution, this formula isolates a complete type
p\in S^{D}(\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg})
and Zilber’s trichotomy
[
14
]
for order one differential equations implies that either
p
is almost internal to the constants or
p
is geometrically trivial.

In the first case, since
p
is an exponentially algebraic type, it follows from Theorem
A
that any realization of
p
in a field of meromorphic functions is an elementary function. This contradicts Liouville’s result that Lambert’s functional equation has no elementary solution. We are therefore in the second case and
p
is geometrically trivial.

It follows that it is enough to prove that any two distinct realizations of
p
are algebraically independent over
\mathbb{C}(z)
to conclude that the solution set of
\phi(w)
is completely disintegrated. To see this, consider
w_{1},w_{2}\models p
which satisfy a nontrivial algebraic relation. Hence,

\mathrm{td}(w_{1},z/w_{1},w_{2},z/w_{2}/\mathbb{C}(z))\leq 1.

It then follows from the Ax-Schanuel Theorem that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly dependent modulo
\mathbb{C}
that is

q_{1}w_{1}+q_{2}w_{2}=c

for some nonzero
q_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Taking the derivative and using (
29
), we also obtain

q_{1}\cdot{w_{1}}\cdot(1+w_{2})+q_{2}\cdot{w_{2}}\cdot(1+w_{1})=0.

These two equations together lead to

q_{1}w_{1}(q_{2}+c-q_{1}w_{1})+q_{2}\cdot(c-q_{1}w_{1})(1+w_{1})=0

which must vanishes identically since
w_{1}
transcendental over
\mathbb{C}
. It follows that
c=0
and
q_{1}=-q_{2}
so that
w_{1}=w_{2}
as required. This finishes the proof of the corollary for Lambert’s equation.
∎

An even older example is Kepler’s equation (1609) which relates the mean anomaly to the eccentric anomaly in orbital mechanics

w-\mathrm{sin}(w)=z.

After writing this equation, Kepler writes:

I am sufficiently satisfied that it cannot be solve a priori, on account of the different nature of the arc and the sine. But if I am mistaken, and any shall point out the way to me, he will be in my eyes the great Apollonius.

Kepler’s expectation was made precise by Liouville

by showing that Kepler’s equation admits no elementary solution.

By quantifier elimination in the theory
\mathrm{DCF}

\mathcal{L}_{D}

w^{\prime}-w^{\prime}\mathrm{cos}(w)=1

\mathrm{cos}^{2}(w)+\mathrm{sin}^{2}(w)=1

(1/w^{\prime}-1)^{2}+(w-z)^{2}=1.

After some simplification, we reach

p\in S^{D}(\mathbb{C}(z)^{alg})
of this equation is exponentially algebraic. The same reasoning as for Lambert’s equation implies that
p
is a minimal geometrically trivial type.

w_{1},w_{2}
