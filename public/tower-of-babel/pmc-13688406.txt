The Clinicopathological Characteristics and Prognosis of Cholangiolocarcinoma: A Multicenter Study With a Central Pathology Review

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCholangiolocarcinoma (CLC) is a rare liver cancer with ≥ 80% ductular configuration (DC). Given its rarity, its clinicopathological characteristics remain poorly understood, and its status as a distinct disease entity remains controversial. We conducted a nationwide multicenter study with centralized pathological review to investigate the features of CLC.MethodsWe included patients with a postoperative diagnosis of CLC at 49 participating institutions (2005–2018). All cases were re‐evaluated by centralized pathology according to the WHO classification.ResultsAmong 196 re‐evaluated patients, 56 tumors were classified as CLC, 69 as intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) with < 80% DC, 53 as small‐duct type iCCA with DC, and 16 as mixed small‐ and large‐duct type (SD/LD‐iCCA with DC). CLC showed a low prevalence of viral hepatitis (23.2%), frequent arterial enhancement (83.9%), and characteristic apical EMA positivity. The median overall survival (OS) was 10.8 years, with 5‐ and 10‐year OS rates of 85.0% and 73.7%, respectively, which were significantly better than those of SD/LD‐iCCA with DC. Recurrence occurred in 38.2% of CLC cases with favorable outcomes following surgical resection.ConclusionsCLC exhibits distinct pathological and prognostic features, including favorable long‐term survival, supporting CLC as a distinct clinicopathological entity. Further molecular research is needed to refine the classification.Trial RegistrationThis study was registered with the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000057919)

Introduction

Although Steiner et al. first proposed cholangiolocarcinoma (CLC) as a distinct disease entity in 1959 [1], its nature remains poorly understood, and it has been regarded as a special subtype of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) [2, 3, 4]. CLC is a rare primary liver cancer that accounts for less than 1% of all cases [5]. In addition to its rarity, the absence of characteristic imaging findings or specific laboratory abnormalities makes the noninvasive diagnosis of CLC extremely difficult. Consequently, a definitive diagnosis of CLC is generally achievable only by a pathological evaluation of surgical resections. CLC is known to exhibit diverse pathological features, occasionally containing areas resembling conventional cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) or demonstrating hepatocytic differentiation within the same tumor nodule [6, 7].

According to the fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of the digestive system, the diagnosis of CLC requires that ≥ 80% of the tumor be composed of CLC components; namely, a ductular configuration (DC) that mimics reactive bile ductular proliferation [8, 9]. However, whether or not CLC should be classified as a distinct disease entity remains controversial [10, 11, 12]. Under the current WHO classification, CLC is no longer categorized as a distinct disease entity, and tumors without hepatocytic differentiation are classified as small‐duct type iCCA, while those with hepatocytic differentiation are classified as combined hepatocellular cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC‐CCA) [8, 13]. In contrast, CLC is maintained as a distinct disease entity alongside hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), iCCA, and cHCC‐CCA in the Japanese general rules of clinical and pathological study [14].

Clinically, CLC often arises in the context of chronic liver disease [3, 7, 15, 16], and its prognosis tends to be more favorable than that of iCCA, resembling that of HCC [4, 15, 17]. However, from a molecular standpoint, CLC has been reported to harbor genetic alterations similar to those observed in iCCA [2, 4, 11, 12]. Given its rarity, further studies are warranted to refine the pathological classification of CLC and to elucidate its clinical and molecular characteristics.

The present study identified the clinicopathological features of CLC and assessed whether CLC should be regarded as a distinct clinicopathological entity through a large‐scale nationwide case collection and centralized pathological assessment in Japan.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study. Clinical data and pathological specimens were collected from board‐certified training institutions of the Japanese Society of Hepato‐Biliary‐Pancreatic Surgery. Eligible cases included patients who underwent surgical resection for primary liver cancer from January 2005 to December 2018 and were diagnosed with CLC at each participating institution. A central pathological review was conducted. For tumors measuring 4 cm or larger in diameter, both the central and peripheral tumor sections were used for the pathological evaluation, and cases in which ≥ 80% of the tumor consisted of DC [9], as defined by the diagnostic criteria of the current WHO classification [8], were included as CLC in subsequent analyses. In addition, iCCA that contained DC in < 80% of the tumor was separately analyzed as “iCCA with DC”. Furthermore, iCCA with DC was subclassified into small‐duct‐type iCCA with DC components (SD‐iCCA with DC) and mixed small‐ and large‐duct‐type iCCA with DC components (SD/LD‐iCCA with DC) based on the histological characteristics of the iCCA tumor cells.

All pathological specimens collected were independently assessed by two experienced hepatopathologists (M.K. and J.A.) who were blinded to the clinical data. In cases of disagreement, consensus was achieved through a joint review.

Endpoints

The overall survival (OS) and recurrence‐free survival (RFS), calculated from the date of surgery, were designated as primary endpoints. In addition, descriptive statistical analyses were conducted to examine the patient demographics and recurrence patterns. Immunohistochemical analyses were also performed on CLC and iCCA with DC.

Pathological Evaluations

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained and unstained slides were collected from each participating institution. Standard protocols were used for H&E and Alcian Blue staining, with the latter used to assess mucin production. Immunohistochemical staining was performed with the following Ready‐to‐Use primary antibodies: keratin 19 (K19, clone A53‐B/A2.26; Roche Diagnostic GmbH, Austria), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA, clone E29; Roche Diagnostic GmbH), HepPar‐1 (clone OCH1E5; Roche Diagnostic GmbH), and arginase‐1 (clone EP261; Roche Diagnostic GmbH). K19 was used as a cholangiocyte marker; EMA for iCCA subtyping (apical, small‐duct phenotype; cytoplasmic, large‐duct phenotype); and both HepPar‐1 and arginase‐1 as hepatocyte markers [8]. Antibody labeling was visualized using a standard detection system. The immunohistochemical positivity rate for each marker was determined by estimating the percentage of tumor cells showing specific staining in representative fields. Two experienced hepatopathologists (M.K. and J.A.) independently assessed the staining, and in cases of discrepancy, a consensus was reached through a joint review.

Statistical Analyses

Cases with missing data were excluded from statistical analyses. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables in the patient background data. In the pathological assessments, continuous variables were expressed as medians with ranges. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test for categorical variables to evaluate the differences across the groups, and multiple comparisons were evaluated using pairwise Wilcoxon's rank‐sum tests with Bonferroni correction. The OS and RFS were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and group comparisons were performed using the log‐rank test. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software program. Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05. All analyses were supervised by a statistician (A.N.).

Results

Clinical and pathological specimens were obtained from 196 patients at 49 institutions. According to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the 5th edition of the WHO classification, 56 cases were diagnosed with CLC, defined as a tumor composed of ≥ 80% DC. A DC was identified in another 69 cases, although it constituted < 80% of the tumor component; these were classified as iCCA with DC. Among these 69 cases, 53 were categorized as SD‐iCCA, while the remaining 16 exhibited SD/LD‐iCCA (Figure 1). Conversely, 71 cases displayed no DC and therefore lacked a CLC component. The details of these 71 cases are summarized in Table S1. For subsequent analyses, we focused on three groups: (1) CLC, (2) SD‐iCCA with DC, and (3) SD/LD‐iCCA with DC.

Patient Background and Tumor Factors

Patient backgrounds and tumor‐related characteristics for CLC, SD‐iCCA with DC, and SD/LD‐iCCA with DC are summarized in Table 1. The mean age at surgery in the CLC group was 69.4 years, and the male‐to‐female ratio was 37:19. Hepatitis B surface antigen was positive in 5 patients (8.9%), and anti‐HCV antibody was positive in 8 patients (14.3%), indicating a low prevalence of viral hepatitis in this cohort. The positivity rates of preoperative tumor markers in the CLC group were 5.8% for alpha‐fetoprotein (AFP; > 15 ng/mL), 8.0% for des‐gamma‐carboxy prothrombin (DCP; > 40 mAU/mL), 11.1% for carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA; > 5 ng/mL), and 22.6% for carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19‐9; > 37 U/mL). Although CA19‐9 tended to be elevated in a subset of patients, no specific tumor marker expression was identified as a characteristic of CLC.

Clinical background of CLC, SD‐iCCA with DC, and SD/LD‐iCCA with DC patients.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed arterial phase hyperenhancement in 47 patients (83.9%) and delayed washout in 16 patients (28.6%). The hepatectomy procedures included non‐anatomical resection/anatomical resection/other in 10/44/2 cases. Lymph node dissection was performed significantly more frequently in the CLC group (16 patients, 29.1%) than in the other two groups. Lymph node dissection was performed according to the standard procedure for iCCA, given the difficulty in preoperatively distinguishing CLC from conventional iCCA.

The mean tumor diameter was 4.6 cm in CLC. Macrovascular invasion was defined according to the Japanese staging system [14], based on its location. Portal vein tumor thrombi are classified into four categories: Distal to the secondary portal branches (vp1), secondary portal branches (vp2), primary portal branches (vp3), or main trunk or bilateral portal branches (vp4). Biliary tract tumor thrombus is similarly classified into four categories: b1 to b4. Hepatic vein tumor thrombi are classified into three categories: Peripheral hepatic veins (vv1), major hepatic veins (vv2), and inferior vena cava (vv3). Portal vein invasion was classified as vp0/vp1/vp2 in 39/16/1 cases; hepatic vein invasion as vv0/vv1/vv2/vv3 in 46/6/2/2 cases; and biliary tract invasion as b0/b1/b2/b3 in 47/7/1/1 cases, respectively. The lymph node metastasis status was classified as n+/n−/nx in 4/30/22 patients. Regarding background liver histology, 32.2% of patients (excluding those with missing data) had fibrosis stage f3 or greater, indicating a relatively high prevalence of chronic liver disease in this population.

Pathological Findings

Representative pathological features of CLC (Figure 2), SD‐iCCA with DC, and SD/LD‐iCCA with DC (Figure S1) are shown. The proportion of DC in the DC is illustrated in a histogram and scatter plot (Figure 3A,B). The scatter plot shows the percentage of tumor cells displaying DC in each case. By definition, CLC cases exhibited ≥ 80% DC and thus had a significantly higher proportion of DC components than both SD‐iCCA with DC and SD/LD‐iCCA with DC. An immunohistochemical analysis showed that K19, a marker specific to bile ducts [18], was expressed in nearly all cases, with no significant differences observed among the groups (Figure 3C). Conversely, markers indicative of hepatocytic differentiation, including HepPar‐1 and arginase‐1 [19, 20, 21], were scarcely detected in the tumor cells (data not shown). Alcian Blue staining showed minimal mucin production in both CLC and SD‐iCCA with DC, whereas SD/LD‐iCCA with DC exhibited significantly greater mucin production (Figure 3D). Regarding EMA staining patterns, CLC and SD‐iCCA with DC displayed predominantly apical staining, with infrequent cytoplasmic expression. However, this pattern was altered in SD/LD‐iCCA with DC, where apical expression was reduced, and cytoplasmic staining was more prominent (Figure 3E,F).

Although no immunohistochemical marker was found to be specific to CLC, the absence of mucin production and the tendency of EMA expression to be localized to the apical side with minimal cytoplasmic staining may aid in the pathological diagnosis of CLC.

The OS and RFS

The median follow‐up period was 5.3 years in the CLC group, 4.9 years in the SD‐iCCA with DC group, and 3.5 years in the SD/LD‐iCCA with DC group. The median OS in the CLC was 10.8 years, with 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year OS rates of 98.2%, 85.0%, and 73.7%, respectively. In comparison, the SD‐iCCA with DC had a median OS of 10.7 years, with 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year OS rates of 96.0%, 69.1%, and 59.3%, respectively. SD/LD‐iCCA with DC showed a markedly shorter median OS of 4.5 years, with 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year OS rates of 87.5%, 46.4%, and 38.7%, respectively. There was no significant difference in the OS between the CLC and SD‐iCCA with DC groups (HR, 1.71; 95% CI, 0.78–3.76); however, the OS in the CLC group was significantly better than that in the SD/LD‐iCCA with DC (p = 0.004; HR: 3.48, 95% CI, 1.40–8.65) (Figure 4A). The median RFS in the CLC was 10.8 years, with 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year RFS rates of 90.6%, 55.0%, and 52.4%, respectively. While CLC tended to show better RFS compared to both SD‐iCCA with DC (HR, 1.17; 95% CI, 0.68–2.02) and SD/LD‐iCCA with DC (HR, 2.13; 95% CI, 1.04–4.36), the differences were not statistically significant (Figure 4B).

Patterns of Recurrence and Post‐Recurrence Treatment

During the follow‐up period, 21 patients (38.2%) with CLC experienced tumor recurrence (Table 2). The most common sites of recurrence were the liver (n = 12), lungs (n = 5), bones (n = 4), and lymph nodes (n = 4), with some patients presenting multiple sites. Among the 21 recurrent cases, pathological confirmation of recurrent lesions was available for 11 cases. Although the pathological diagnoses for recurrent lesions were made at each participating institution and no central review was conducted, the pathological subtypes were reported as CLC in six cases, HCC in four cases, and iCCA in one case. The 5‐year post‐recurrence survival rate in the CLC was 64.2%, which was significantly better than that observed in patients with SD‐iCCA with DC (p = 0.04, HR: 3.28, 95% CI; 1.16–9.28) and those with SD/LD‐iCCA with DC (p = 0.0004, HR: 8.74, 95% CI; 2.20–34.69) (Figure S2A). Of the 21 patients with recurrence, 20 received treatment for the initial recurrence. Treatments included hepatic resection (n = 7), transarterial (chemo) embolization (TA[C]E) (n = 3), radiation therapy (n = 4), and systemic chemotherapy (n = 11). Some patients received multiple modalities. All systemic chemotherapy regimens included gemcitabine, cisplatin, or S‐1 in accordance with the standard treatment for biliary tract cancer. Notably, among the patients who underwent surgical resection of recurrent lesions, no deaths were observed during the observation period (Figure S2B), suggesting that surgical resection may be an effective option in selected cases, even after recurrence.

Discussion

This study is the first large‐scale, nationwide, multicenter investigation of CLC. A major strength of this study lies in the centralized pathological re‐evaluation of a substantial number of tissue specimens, which significantly enhanced diagnostic accuracy. In accordance with the current 5th edition of the WHO classification, tumors with ≥ 80% DC were defined as CLC [8, 9]. CLC exhibit distinct pathological features and favorable long‐term outcomes. These results suggest that the prognosis of CLC is markedly superior to that of other primary liver cancers. These findings support the classification of CLC as a distinct clinicopathological entity.

Clinically, CLC is infrequently associated with viral hepatitis [3, 7, 15, 16], and 32.2% of patients have underlying chronic liver disease. Unlike HCC or iCCA, no specific tumor marker was characteristic of CLC, although a tendency toward CA19‐9 elevation was observed. Arterial phase hyperenhancement on contrast‐enhanced CT was observed in 83.9% of CLC cases, which appeared to be a distinctive feature. This finding was also present in 69.8% of SD‐iCCA with DC and 62.5% of SD/LD‐iCCAs with DC, which is consistent with previous reports suggesting that iCCA with arterial enhancement tends to have a more favorable prognosis than iCCA without arterial enhancement [22]. The hypervascularity observed in the arterial phase of CLC likely reflects the presence of abundant vessels interspersed between the tumor cells [23]. Owing to the limited number of patients who underwent lymph node dissection during surgery, it remains unclear whether CLC commonly involves lymphatic spread. Nevertheless, given the favorable prognosis and previous reports indicating that lymphadenectomy may increase postoperative complications [24], it may be reasonable to omit lymph node dissection in patients without clinically suspicious lymphadenopathy. The liver was the most common site of recurrence, followed by the lungs, bones, and lymph nodes, which were similar to those observed in HCC and iCCA [17]. Although pathological confirmation of recurrent lesions was obtained in only 11 of 21 recurrent cases at each participating institution, more than half of the patients were diagnosed with recurrent CLC. In this cohort, patients with CLC demonstrated favorable survival even after recurrence. These findings suggest that accurate imaging and a pathological evaluation of recurrent lesions followed by appropriate therapeutic intervention may contribute to favorable post‐recurrence survival outcomes in patients with CLC.

In the present study, 56 (28.6%) out of 196 cases with available pathological specimens were diagnosed with CLC upon centralized pathological review. This relatively small number may be attributed to the case accumulation period (2005–2018), during which the previous version of the WHO classification [25], which lacked a precise definition of CLC, was still used. However, in this study, all diagnoses were re‐evaluated according to the updated criteria of the 5th edition WHO classification (2019), which defines CLC as a tumor composed of ≥ 80% DC [8, 9]. This likely accounts for the limited number of cases that met the current diagnostic threshold for CLC. The majority of cases were re‐categorized from CLC to SD‐iCCA based on this revised criterion. Pathological findings revealed that nearly all tumor cells in the CLC were negative for hepatocellular markers and positive for K19, supporting a biliary epithelial origin. Furthermore, consistent with previous reports [4, 26, 27], CLCs exhibited low mucin production and an apical EMA expression pattern. In contrast, cytoplasmic EMA expression and mucin production were frequently observed in SD/LD‐iCCA with DC but not in CLC. These findings suggest that the combined assessment of morphological features with EMA and mucin staining may aid in distinguishing CLC from the other iCCA subtypes. At present, the diagnosis of CLC relies largely on morphological criteria; therefore, further identification of robust biomarkers is warranted.

Another interesting finding was that 16 cases in this series exhibited both SD and LD features within the same tumor (SD/LD‐iCCA with DC). These tumors demonstrated cytoplasmic EMA expression and mucin production, reflecting the presence of LD components. This may indicate a biologically distinct subtype from “pure” SD‐iCCA, potentially originating from interlobular bile ducts, which are anatomically closer to the large bile duct. Importantly, SD/LD‐iCCA with DC was associated with a poorer prognosis than both CLC and SD‐iCCA with DC, underscoring the clinical importance of identifying this subgroup. Although similar features have been reported in previous studies [26, 28], the precise pathological characteristics and clinical implications of SD/LD‐iCCA remain unclear. Further investigation is warranted to better define and understand this entity.

According to a nationwide follow‐up survey by the Japan Liver Cancer Association, the 5‐year OS rates after hepatic resection for HCC, iCCA, and cHCC‐CCA are 70.1%, 49.6%, and 50.1%, respectively [29]. In comparison, the prognosis of CLC is markedly more favorable. Although the 5th edition of the WHO classification currently categorizes CLC as a subtype of iCCA or cHCC‐CCA [8], our data suggest that, from a prognostic standpoint, CLC should be recognized as a distinct disease category. In the present study, we further investigated the prognostic impact of the specific proportion of the DC component. When analyzing its quantitative influence, we found that tumors with ≥ 80% DC exhibited a significantly better OS compared to those with < 80% DC (p = 0.04; data not shown). However, no significant differences in the OS or RFS were observed between tumors containing 30%–79% DC and those with < 30% DC (Figure S3). In other words, among the tumors with less than 80% DC, the degree of DC involvement did not appear to further influence the survival outcomes. These findings support the clinical and prognostic validity of the current WHO diagnostic threshold of ≥ 80% DC for the diagnosis of CLC. Previous studies have demonstrated that the presence of DC is associated with a favorable prognosis in iCCA. Our findings are consistent with this observation. While tumors with a predominant DC component (≥ 80%) exhibited the most favorable outcomes, no clear prognostic gradient was observed according to the proportion of DC among the tumors containing < 80% DC. These findings suggest that the presence of DC itself may confer a favorable biological behavior, whereas increasing DC proportions below the 80% threshold do not appear to further stratify the prognosis [28, 30]. Consistently, in our cohort, SD‐iCCA with DC demonstrated a 5‐year OS of 63.5%, which is better than that typically observed in general iCCA populations. In contrast, SD/LD‐iCCA with DC showed a relatively poor prognosis, suggesting that the presence of LD components may negatively affect outcomes.

Several limitations associated with the present study warrant mention. First, the diagnosis of CLC can only be confirmed after surgical resection; thus, the clinicopathological features analyzed in this study were limited to resected cases. The clinical characteristics of unresectable or advanced CLC at the initial diagnosis remain unknown. Second, direct comparisons with other types of primary liver cancer were not performed. In particular, because cases diagnosed as iCCA without DC components at each participating institution were excluded from the study, the differences in clinicopathological features between CLC and iCCA without DC components remain an important and intriguing area for future research. Third, although this was a nationwide, multicenter study, the number of CLC cases was limited to 56, and the total number of cases, including iCCA with DC, was 125. Given the rarity of CLC, this limitation was unavoidable. A fourth limitation is that the multicenter design limited our ability to ascertain the cause‐specific mortality in older patients. Therefore, deaths occurring more than 10 years after resection may include non‐cancer‐related events, thus potentially affecting the long‐term OS analysis.

In conclusion, this is the first nationwide study of CLC with a centralized pathological review, providing robust and uniform diagnostic data. Our findings suggest that CLC is a distinct clinicopathological entity with unique clinicopathological features and a favorable prognosis. International collaboration is essential to further clarify the biological and clinical characteristics of this disease.
