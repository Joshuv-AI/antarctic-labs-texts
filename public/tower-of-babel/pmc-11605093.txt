Layered Babinet complementary patterns acting as asymmetric negative index metamaterial
Authors: Emese Tóth, Olivér A. Fekete, Balázs Bánhelyi, Maxim Durach, Zsolt Szabó, Mária Csete
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Azimuthal orientation and handedness dependence of the optical responses, accompanied by asymmetric transmission and asymmetric dichroism, were demonstrated on multilayers constructed with subwavelength periodic arrays of Babinet complementary miniarrays, illuminated by linearly and circularly polarized light. In case of single-sided illumination asymmetric optical responses were observed at the spectral location of maximal cross-polarization that is accompanied by radiative electric dipoles and weak, slowly-rotating in-plane magnetic dipoles on the nano-objects; where the outgoing waves are elliptically (almost circularly) polarized. The negative index material phenomenon was demonstrated, where the electric and magnetic dipoles overlap both spatially and spectrally. The negative index material (NIM) phenomenon is accompanied by electric multipoles that add up non-radiatively and correlates with the strong, pronouncedly-tilted, rotating magnetic dipoles characteristic on the nano-entities, where the outgoing waves are linearly (slightly elliptically) polarized. By illuminating the multilayer with two counter-propagating circularly polarized beams it was proven that asymmetrical normal component displacement currents at the bounding interfaces, arising along flat and tilted bands, accompany the asymmetric copolarized and cross-polarized transmission. The latter correlates with the asymmetric dichroism in the cross-polarized signal observed in case of single-sided circularly polarized light illumination. The dispersion maps in the single-sided asymmetrical co-polarized reflectance and absorptance indicate flat bands of analogous and complementary extrema, proving the partially dichroic nature of the observed asymmetric phenomena. The Tellegen (chirality) coefficients exhibit a maximum in a spectral region coincident with the asymmetric transmission (maximal polarization rotation in the 90° azimuthal orientation). The multilayer is proposed as an ultrathin NIM and nonreciprocal nanophotonic element.

Introduction

Metamaterials constructed with sub-wavelength patterns of plasmonic nano-objects allow for unique optical properties, including the negative index of refraction1. Negative index material (NIM) behaviour is achievable via spectrally overlapping electric dipoles excitable e.g. on wires and magnetic dipoles excitable on e.g. split ring resonators (SRR), that result in negative permittivity and permeability, respectively2. This explains, why primary attempts in metamaterial designs were focused on the creation of artificial magnetism via electric dipoles on circular arrays of nanoparticles or via circulating currents on SRRs3. Based on conformal mapping it was predicted that SRRs, acting as transformation optics analogue of infinite waveguides and supporting degenerate electric and magnetic dipoles, can result in NIM behaviour in wide optical wavelength regions4. NIM achievable in the near-IR via bi-layers of parallel nanorods and nanoholes originates from electric and magnetic resonances excited at overlapping spectral locations and is accompanied by considerable loss5,6. NIM reachable in near-IR via multilayers forming a fishnet that supports overlapping forbidden gaps of electric and magnetic media allows for better FOM = − nreal/nim7. Unusual NIM is attainable via HMM, where the opposite sign of negative group index originates from the hyperbolic dispersion8–11.

Type-I/II HMMs exhibiting negative permittivity or permeability for the out-of-plane/in-plane direction, are typically constructed with horizontally/vertically stacked anisotropic media of multilayers, and type-II HMMs allow NIM throughout wide bands of optical frequencies accompanied by low loss.

Metamaterials are important tools in the creation of artificial chirality that can manifest itself in optical activity (OA), which is polarization rotation; and circular dichroism (CD), which is polarization selective absorption; that are comparable or outperform those reachable via natural materials12. This can be achieved via e.g. three-dimensional arrays of twisted plasmonic antennas13. Three-dimensional chiral arrays of spherical SRRs can be used also for information encoding14.

Asymmetric transmission (AT) on single layers was attributed to different polarization conversion efficiencies along forward and backward propagation directions for linearly or circularly polarized waves (the latter was nominated as “circular conversion dichroism”). In 2D anisotropic and lossy chiral metamaterials well-defined AT was achieved in the cross-polarized component, and it was shown that the enantiomers exhibit analogous effect with the same amplitude but of the reversal sign15,16. Coexistent asymmetric reflection (AR) and absorption (AA) was also observed on planar arrays of asymmetric SRRs17,18. AT initialization via tilting along with extrinsic OA and CD was also demonstrated19. The theoretical AT limit (25%) was approached in an optical spectral interval that was gradually broadened by increasing the number of circles in a spiral-shaped meta-atom20.

Considerable asymmetric transmission in the cross-polarized state is accessible via bilayers of chiral metamaterials. Moreover, meta-atoms ensuring complete symmetry breaking arranged in bilayers can result in AT in any polarization base21. Twisted Babinet pattern of nanoslits and nanorods allows for AT and CD effects simultaneously22. Via orthogonally chained S-shaped resonator patterns with broken-symmetry along the propagation direction dual band dichroic AT effect was demonstrated in the considerable cross-polarization conversion23.

Trilayer of bianisotropic metasurfaces constructed with patterned gold films at sub-wavelength distances allow for extremely large AT and coexistent extinction ratio at the telecommunication wavelength24. Circularly polarized light illumination of three layers of gold nanorods acting as anisotropic metamaterial resulted in dual-band AT with mutual polarization conversion25. The application of a permittivity profile possessing real and imaginary parts related by the Kramers–Kronig relation, allows for achieving unidirectional reflectance suppression26.

Non-reciprocity was achieved via traveling-wave resonant ring-shaped resonators acting as magnetless nonreciprocal metamaterials coupled to microstripe-lines that can be used as circulators and isolators27. The key role of out-of-plane surface currents in the achievement of non-reciprocity was identified28. Multilayer Faraday isolator based on a resonant multi-layer cavity constructed with dichroic nanolayers and a magnetic layer was demonstrated29.

Novel classes of Tellegen-coupling and artificial-moving type metamaterials with non-reciprocal responses were developed30. Unidirectional as well as non-reciprocal responses were demonstrated on layered and strongly-coupled non-Hermitian plasmonic waveguides31. It was proven, that chirality can be combined with PT-symmetry, and may result in advanced polarization control32.

To achieve these unique properties, metamaterials consisting of plasmonic nano-objects are promising, due to the phenomena that can result in coupled modes on miniarrays33, strong-coupling of localized and propagating modes on hole-arrays in metal films34. The Babinet complementary patterns are exceptionally interesting, as they exhibit complementary near-field and far-field responses, as well as effective optical properties35,36. Such structure can be fabricated via integrated lithography described in our previous papers37–40.

In our previous studies we have shown that a multilayer of complex patterns in convex-concave-convex succession constructed with miniarrays of spherical nano-objects results in azimuthal orientation (handedness) dependence, asymmetric transmission (as well as asymmetric dichroism (AD)) and NIM in case of illumination with linearly (circularly) polarized light41. Similar multilayer of complex patterns in concave-convex-concave succession was the subject of present study in order to demonstrate that the AT is accompanied by radiative electric dipoles and asymmetric displacement currents, is related to weak, slowly-rotating in-plane magnetic dipoles, whereas the NIM appearance is accompanied by non-radiative multipoles and is due to the overlapping electric and strong, pronouncedly-tilted, quickly-rotating magnetic dipoles.

Methods

The inspected multilayer is constructed with three succeeding layers of 300 nm periodic Babinet complementary patterns consisting of miniarrays of spherical nano-objects in concave-convex-concave succession. In the concave boundary layers the nano-objects are spherical holes in 39 nm thick gold films, whereas in the convex middle layer the nano-objects are convex gold nano-antennas of the same shape.

Namely, the central nano-object is a nanoring with R = 25 nm outer and r = 5 nm inner radius, this is surrounded by a nanocrescent quadrumer, the composing nanocrescents are created as an overlap of two cylinders with 0.4/5 × R radius. The interlayer distance is 5 nm, in accordance with the previously inspected multilayer of convex-concave-convex patterns41.

The unit cell of this pattern was illuminated by linearly and circularly polarized plane wave by using Floquet boundary condition in COMSOL Multiphysics (insets in Figs. 1, 2). The complete optical responses were determined first by polarization unspecific power-outflow read-out (Figs. 1a, 2a).

The polarization specific read-out was used thereafter to determine the co-polarized and cross-polarized linearly polarized components (Figs. 1b, 2b, Table S1). The co-polarized and cross-polarized circularly polarized components were determined via Jones matrix elements41,42. The azimuthal orientation and handedness dependence was inspected in case of linearly and circularly polarized light illumination, respectively (Figs. 1c, 2c).

The asymmetric transmission was considered as the propagation direction dependence for the same original polarization of co-polarized and cross-polarized components selectively23. In case of circularly polarized light the asymmetric dichroism qualifying the direction dependence of the conversion into the cross-polarized states for opposite original polarization states was analysed as well (Figs. 1d, 2d). To determine the effective optical parameters of the slabs, the retrieval method relaying on the closed form Kramers–Kronig relations was used43–45 (Figs. 1e,f, 2e,f). The bianisotropy of the multilayer was considered by utilizing the Fresnel–Airy equations developed by Durach et al.46. Result

Extrema in optical responses

Considering that the plasmonic resonances are accompanied by absorptance extrema, the rectified absorptance was inspected first, then the rectified transmittance and reflectance determined by polarization unspecific power-outflow read-out were analysed, both for linearly and circularly polarized light illumination (Figs. 1a, 2a).

In the rectified absorptance there are well-defined maxima corresponding to the U resonance on the convex/concave pattern and to the mixed C1–C2 resonance on the concave/convex pattern in the 0°/90° azimuthal orientation (570 nm/560–600 nm).

These are followed in the 0°/90° azimuthal orientation by a tiny peak/shoulder related to the C1 resonance on the concave/convex pattern overlapping with the SPP coupled on the concave pattern (640 nm). Novel extrema appear, governed by the interlayer coupling at 680 nm and 730 nm/720–750 nm. These rectified absorptance extrema are accompanied by slightly shifted rectified transmittance maxima. An important difference in the rectified transmittances is that a global maximum and a shoulder appears at 690 nm and 730 nm in the 0° azimuthal orientation, while after a global maximum at 620 nm, local maxima develop at 680 nm and 730 nm in the 90° azimuthal orientation (Fig. 1a).

When circularly polarized light illumination is applied, the fingerprints of the analogous rectified absorptance maxima are observable that are coincident independent of handedness. Namely, a global maximum and shoulders appear at 570 and 610–640 nm, which are followed by larger and smaller local maxima at 680 nm and 730 nm. The novel extrema manifest themselves in the global rectified transmittance maximum at 690 nm and in the shoulder at 730 nm (Fig. 2a). The rectified reflectance is complementary to the rectified absorptance with inverted extrema at the same spectral locations, both for linearly and circularly polarized illumination (Figs. 1a, 2a). The propagation direction dependence of the optical response from polarization unspecific read-out was also determined (see Supplementary material Figs. S1a, S2a).

In the co-polarized transmission taken in the 0° azimuthal orientation (Ty) shoulders appear in the interval of the single layer related resonance extrema (570 nm and 640 nm), which are followed by a well-defined local maximum and a shoulder (690 nm and 720 nm), before the global minimum observable at 750 nm. The transmittance signals computed by using the Jones matrix elements exhibit more pronounced handedness dependence, than the transmission computed from the polarization unspecific power-outflow read-out for LCP and RCP light illumination.

The handedness dependence can be attributed dominantly to the co-polarized light component, similarly to the previously investigated convex-concave-convex multilayer41, and analogously with the co-polarized component’s dominance in case of linearly polarized light illumination (Fig. 2b). The T++forward and T−−backward transmission signals are larger, and exhibit shoulders analogous to the single layer related resonance extrema (610 nm and 640 nm), which are followed by a multilayer specific well-defined local maximum and a shoulder (680 nm and 720 nm), before the global minimum is reached at 760 nm. In comparison, the T−−forward and T++backward transmission signals are smaller, and possess similar shoulders (610 nm and 640 nm) that correspond to single layer related extrema, which are followed by a local maximum and a shoulder (690 nm and 730 nm), before the global minimum is reached at the same spectral location (760 nm). The maximal handedness-related difference is 5.823 × 10–3/5.399 × 10–3 in the forward/backward propagation direction that is reached at 680 nm (Fig. 2b,c, Table S1).

In contrast, the cross-polarized signal is tiny and shows only small local maximum (560 nm) and a shoulder (620 nm), preceding the global maximum appearing at 680 nm and a local maximum (750 nm) handedness independently. Accordingly, the maximal original handedness-related difference in the cross-polarized signals is 3.468 × 10–5/2.097 × 10–5 at 670 nm in the forward/backward propagation directions (Table S1). The signals and the maximal handedness-related difference are two-orders of magnitude smaller compared to the co-polarized signals, similarly to the linearly polarized light illumination. The global maximum (680 nm)—minimum (740 nm)—local maximum (750 nm) in the cross-polarized signal corresponds to local maximum—(680 nm/690 nm)—shoulder (720 nm/730 nm)—global minimum (760 nm) in the co-polarized signal for RCP/LCP illumination (Fig. 2b,c).

Although, the forward and backward propagating signals overlap, there is a noticeable difference between the forward and backward propagating co-polarized light components. The maximal asymmetrical dichroism of 2.461 × 10–4/6.697 × 10–4 is reached at 680 nm/680 nm, when the T++forward − T−−backward/T−−forward − T++backward signal is analysed. In comparison, there is a tiny difference in the forward and backward propagating cross-polarized light components that are analogous by means of reciprocity. Namely, 1.791 × 10–4/2.347 × 10–4 is reached at 670 nm/670 nm, when the T−+forward − T+−backward/T+−forward − T−+backward signals are compared, proving slightly smaller AD in the cross-polarized transmission. The maximal difference is in the same order of magnitude (0.728 and 0.351-times smaller for RCP-LCP and LCP-RCP original polarizations in the forward–backward direction) compared to the AD reached in case of the co-polarized signals (Fig. 2b,d, Table S1).

When the signals for the same original polarization are compared, significantly larger difference arises between the forward and backward propagating co-polarized components. This proves the capability for achieving non-reciprocal responses. The maximal asymmetric transmission of 5.153 × 10–3/6.069 × 10–3 is reached at 680 nm/680 nm, when the T++forward − T++backward/T−−forward − T−−backward co-polarized signal is analysed. In comparison, there is a tiny difference in the forward and backward propagating cross-polarized components.Namely, 2.000 × 10–4/2.137 × 10–4 is reached at 670 nm/670 nm, when the T−+forward − T−+backward/T+−forward − T+−backward signals are compared, proving a significantly smaller cross-polarized AT. The maximal AT in cross-polarized signals is one-order of magnitude smaller compared to the AT reached in the co-polarized signals (Fig. 2b,d, Table S1).

At the global minima, that appear at 750 nm/760 nm in the 0°/90° azimuthal orientation and at 760 nm both for RCP and LCP illumination in the co-polarized signal, there is a tiny local maximum in the cross-polarized transmittance signal. However, not only the transmittance approaches zero, but also the azimuthal orientation and handedness dependence, as well as the AT and AD show close-to-zero values both for the co-polarized and cross-polarized components (Figs. 1b–d, 2b–d). This predicts that the spectral location of the global minimum is exceptional.

Effective optical parameters

The retrieval procedure resulted in NIM characteristic in all inspected configurations (Figs. 1e,f, 2e,f). On the differential dispersion maps taken in the T++forward − T++backward and T−−forward − T−−backward co-polarized AT signals a well-defined flat band of maxima/minima appears at 680 nm through 15° tilting. Interestingly the width/extension of the band is larger/smaller for both cases of RCP/LCP illumination, compared to the band in δDnorm (Figs. 10b–e, 11b–e). In contrast, on the differential dispersion maps taken in the T++forward − T−−backward and T−−forward − T++backward co-polarized AD signal the corresponding flat band of extrema has a global maximum at larger tilting (~ 15°) rather than at perpendicular incidence (Figs. 10f, 11f). At the tilted band corresponding to the propagating SPP modes, the AT (AD) differential co-polarized transmittance signal exhibits minima (maxima). These results prove that well-defined co-polarized bands correspond to the AT phenomenon for both handednesses and the differential δDnorm signal is related unambiguously to co-polarized AT rather than to AD (Figs. 9e,f, 10e,f). However, the co-polarized AT extrema are reversed compared to the extrema appearing along the flat-and-tilted bands in the δDnorm.

The characteristic of the δDnorm can be better explained by considering the cross-polarized AT and AD signals, as on all differential dispersion maps taken in the T+−forward − T+−backward and T−+forward − T−+backward cross-polarized AT signals and in the T+−forward − T−+backward and T−+forward − T+−backward cross-polarized AD signals there are maxima along the flat and tilted bands (Figs. 10d,i,l, 11d,i,l). However, the degree of modulation in the transmittance signals is very small, therefore it is reasonable to inspect the reflectance and absorptance of light with specific polarization as well. The map taken in the co-polarized asymmetric reflectance indicates a flat band of maxima/minima, while the asymmetric absorptance shows a flat band of minima/maxima at the AT phenomenon (Figs. 10g,h,j,k, 11g,h,j,k). This unambiguously proves that the AT is partially of dichroic origin, and the differential absorption is divided into two channels of asymmetric transmission and reflection.

The spectra of Tellegen coefficient and chirality factor show a well-defined extrema in the interval of the asymmetric transmission that depend on the propagation direction. This proves that both strong circular dichroism and moderate optical activity is at play at the AT (Fig. 2f,i, Fig. S4). Even though the difference of the outflows exhibits a minimum, the polarization ellipses are distinctly different for the two propagation directions (see Supplementary material Fig. S5).

Electric modes on the multilayer: AT for double-sided circularly polarized light illumination

In case of double-sided RCP and LCP illuminations of the multilayer at 680 nm perfect 3D quadrupoles rotate clockwise (counter-clockwise) and counter-clockwise (clockwise) on the bottom (top) concave nanorings and parallel 3D dipoles appear on the concave nanocrescents; while the twisted multipole continuously varies between a dipole and a quadrupole on the convex nanoring, but the convex nanocrescents support 3D dipoles that are antiparallel compared to the parallel ones on the concave nanocrescents (Fig. 6e,f, Videos S9, S10).

On the concave nanorings the relative orientation of the quadrupoles continuously varies in the [0°, 180°], due to the counter-varying phases, that is mediated by the continuously varying multipole on the convex nanoring. The predominant 3D dipoles on the concave nanocrescents are parallel due to the interleaved antiparallel 3D dipole on the convex nanocrescents.

On the concave nanocrescents transiently quadrupoles appear due to horizontal charge separation, but the 3D dipoles on the convex nanocrescents quickly switch their orientation, therefore higher order multipoles cannot appear. The surface dipoles are perfectly in-phase in the quadrumer in each specific layers. In contrast, the surface multipoles on the nanocrescents exhibit a small phase delay in the quadrumers on the lower and upper surfaces of each layers.

Magnetic dipoles on the multilayer at AT for double-sided circularly polarized light illumination

In case of double-sided illumination, the amplitude increases slightly on all nano-objects compared to the AT observed via single-sided illumination, moreover it becomes larger compared to the NIM on the top and bottom quadrumer for both polarizations, on the middle convex quadrumer and miniarray/top and bottom concave miniarray. The inclination is enhanced (except the top and bottom quadrumer and top/bottom miniarray), though it remains still smaller than at the NIM (except the top-ring, LCP illuminated middle and bottom ring and top miniarray); the rotation is shortened, (except the top quadrumer, top miniarray/bottom quadrumer), moreover it remains shorter than at the NIM (except the top miniarray and quadrumer, top-middle-bottom ring/top ring and bottom quadrumer) (see Supplementary material, Fig. S6, Tables S2–S5).

Conclusion

The multilayer illuminated by linearly polarized light exhibits azimuthal orientation dependence and asymmetric transmission, these are two and one oder/less than one order of magnitude larger for y/x original polarization, in the co-polarized components, respectively. The AT is coincident with the spectral location of the maximum in the cross-polarized signals, except the Txx co-polarized component.

The multilayer illuminated by circularly polarized light exhibits handedness and propagation direction dependence that manifests itself in asymmetric transmission and dichroism as well. These manifest themselves in two orders of magnitude larger handedness dependence, one oder and less than one order of magnitude larger AT and AD in the co-polarized components. The AT and AD is coincident with the spectral location of the maximum in the cross-polarized signals, and the co-polarized AT and AD inherit the location of the global maximum in the co-polarized AT in the 0° azimuthal orientation.

NIM is achieved both with linearly and circularly polarized light illumination at the spectral locations, where electric and magnetic dipoles overlap. However, all signals, as well as their azimuthal orientation and handedness dependence exhibit a minimum close to the NIM phenomenon. This already predicts the non-radiative nature of the multipoles that are excitable at the NIM.

The dispersion characteristics taken via single-sided illumination exhibits a flat band of extrema at the AT phenomenon and a gap between side-bands at the NIM phenomenon, respectively. In case of single-sided linearly polarized light illumination at the AT phenomenon the predominant dipole on the convex nanoring allows for transmission that is larger due to the accompanying synchronized nanocrescent dipoles in the 0° azimuthal orientation, while in the 90° azimuthal orientation 3D dipoles are observable only on the nanorings. In contrast, at the NIM phenomenon zero transmittance is caused by the reversal predominant dipoles on the nanorings in the bounding concave layers accompanied by partially synchronized dipoles on the nanocrescents. The large (considerable) fraction of the reversal dipoles on the nanoring (nanocrescents) in both concave layers/middle convex and the top bonding concave layers as well as the quadrupoles on the individual nanocrescents/complete quadrumer in the middle convex layer is not advantageous for transmission. In case of single-sided circularly polarized light illumination the predominant and cyclical twisted dipole on the convex and upper concave nanoring accompanied by well-synchronized nanocrescent dipoles allow for finite transmission at the AT phenomenon. In contrast, at the NIM phenomenon the zero transmittance is caused by predominant twisted dipoles on the nanorings in the bounding concave layers with continuously varying phase accompanied by less synchronized twisted dipoles on the nanocrescents. The larger fraction of the (perpendicular) reversal dipoles on the nanoring and nanocrescents in the (bottom) top concave and middle convex layer is also not advantageous for transmission.

At the spectral location of the AT phenomenon in case of linearly (circularly) polarized illumination the charge distribution is localized, and interlayer-coupling occurs between individual nano-objects. The transmittance maximum/shoulder (maximum) is due to the more/less significant fraction of radiating dipoles in the 0°/90° azimuthal orientation (including the (strongly/slightly) twisted dipoles on the convex and (bottom/top) concave nanoring and the synchronized dipoles on the nanocrescents for both handednesses). Compared to the modes localized on the nano-objects, the propagating grating-coupled SPPs are intermittently in phase, i.e. the transmission is cyclically promoted in case of linearly polarized light illumination in the 90° azimuthal orientation, whereas they are permanently in phase for both handednesses, i.e. the transmission is promoted in case of circularly polarized light illumination for both handednesses.

The NIM differs from the AT location in the sense that the charge distribution is more extended, interlayer and inter-unit cell coupling also appears due to the synchronized surface monopoles on individual nano-objects, that are capable of preventing the transmission. In addition to this, the localized modes are out-of-phase with the propagating SPPs, and the quadrupoles appearing on the complete quadrumer in the middle convex and both (top) concave layers are non-radiative in the 90° azimuthal orientation (for both handednesses), thereby forward radiation, i.e. transmission is prevented.

Considering all of the three layers, the magnetic dipoles characteristic predicts different NIM in the 0° and 90° azimuthal orientations. In comparison, the balanced magnetic dipoles characteristic predicts similar intermediate NIM for the two handednesses, which correlates with the extracted effective parameters. At all types of inspected extrema the magnetic dipoles are stronger on the nanoring in the concave layer (except the top layer for LCP at AT), but larger magnetic dipoles develop on the quadrumer in the convex layer. The NIM results in the largest amplitude magnetic dipole—but with the largest tilting, and exhibits intermediate rotation characteristic; at single-sided AT the smallest amplitude magnetic dipole remains in the plane of the multilayer—i.e. exhibits the smallest tilting, and rotates the most continuously. The main difference between the NIM and AT is that the amplitude and projection on all nano-objects remains considerably smaller at the AT, except on the quadrumer in the top and bottom concave layer. The ring is relatively the most dominant at the NIM, while the quadrumer is relatively the most dominant at the single-sided AT on all layers—except the bottom layer in case of RCP. The single-sided AT is an example of in-plane magnetic field emulation and the initialization of non-reciprocal phenomena, namely asymmetric co-polarized and cross-polarized transmission.

The double-sided illumination results in optical response modulation in the interval of single and multilayer specific extrema, but in the differential outflow signal inflections are coincident with the spectral locations of AT that were observed in case of single-sided illumination. Even though in case of the double-sided illumination an intermediate amplitude magnetic dipole appears with intermediate tilting, which exhibits the quickest turning, a well-defined asymmetry persists, not only in the transmission, but also in the displacement current distribution. The dispersion maps taken via double-sided illumination in the relative difference of normal-component displacement currents and in the single-sided differential transmittance signals indicate flat and tilted bands corresponding to the co-polarized AT and AD phenomenon, that are accompanied by weak flat and similar tilted bands in the differential signals corresponding to the cross-polarized AT and AD phenomenon. The dispersion maps taken in the single-sided differential co-polarized reflectance and absorptance indicate well-defined flat bands of analogous and complementary extrema proving the partially dichroic origin of the observed AT phenomena.

In case of double-sided RCP and LCP illumination at the AT location the charge distribution is localized, interlayer-coupling occurs between individual nano-objects. The outflow drop is due to the nearby absorptance related to the coexistence of radiating dipoles on the nanocrescents and convex nanoring as well as of non-radiating quadrupoles on the concave and convex nanorings. The propagating SPPs are cyclically in phase, i.e. the transmission is promoted.

The multilayer exhibits non-reciprocal AT and NIM characteristic as well, the users have to define, which phenomenon is preferable for a specific application. Considering that the NIM and AT phenomena prefer different complex structure parameters, optimization of similar multilayers for one of these phenomena separately is a subject of ongoing studies.
