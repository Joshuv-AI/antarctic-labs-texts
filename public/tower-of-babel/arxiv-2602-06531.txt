Partial fraction decompositions on hyperplane arrangements
Claire de Korte, Teresa Yu

Abstract
. We initiate the study of partial fraction decompositions (PFDs) in several variables using tools from commutative algebra. We give criteria for when a rational function with poles on a hyperplane arrangement has a desirable PFD. Our criteria are obtained by examining the primary decomposition of ideals coming from hyperplane arrangements. We then present an algorithm for finding a PFD that satisfies properties desired by physicists, and demonstrate the effectiveness of this algorithm for computing large examples coming from Feynman integrals.



1.
Introduction

Partial fraction decompositions (PFDs) of rational functions in several variables appear frequently in physics. Examples include abstract applications, such as the re-casting of scattering amplitudes as canonical forms of polytopes
[
ABL
]
, and computational applications like developing efficient algorithms to compute Feynman integrals
[
HvM
]
. Despite their ubiquity in physics, studies of PFDs seldom appear in mathematical literature
[
BBD+
,
Bec
,
Lei
]
. There has been interest from physicists to utilize algebro-geometric techniques in order to compute certain PFDs
[
BW+
,
CDE
,
DP
]
.
In this paper we study PFDs from the perspective of commutative algebra, by using primary decomposition and algorithms based upon Gröbner bases.

Let
R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
be a polynomial ring over a field
K
, and let
f,g\in R
be nonzero polynomials. We are interested in
partial fraction decompositions (PFDs)
of the rational function
f/g
; these are expressions of the function as:

\frac{f}{g}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{f_{i}}{g_{i}},\qquad\deg(f_{i})<\deg(f),\,\deg(g_{i})<\deg(g).

Example 1.1
.

To see where the complexity in PFDs can arise, consider the following example of a rational function with two different PFDs:

\begin{split}\frac{13y-6x}{(y-3x)(x+y)(x-2y)}&=\frac{19}{12x(x+y)}-\frac{2}{15x(x-2y)}-\frac{33}{20x(y-3x)}\\
&=\frac{1}{(x+y)(x-2y)}+\frac{2}{(y-3x)(x-2y)}-\frac{5}{(y-3x)(x+y)}.\end{split}

This example demonstrates that:

(1)

rational functions do not necessarily have a unique PFD; and

(2)

certain PFDs of rational functions can introduce
spurious poles
, i.e., poles in the terms of the PFD that do not appear in the rational function itself (see Definition
5.4
for a precise definition).

In applications to physics, one would like to know when a rational function
f/g
has a PFD, and if so, how to make the PFD “optimal.” Here, optimal can refer to a number of parameters, including minimizing the number of terms
m
in the PFD, maximizing the degrees of the numerators
f_{i}
, and avoiding spurious poles (the introduction of such poles can complicate calculations and obscure physical properties and symmetries
[
Hod
]
).

In this paper, we ask the following question: given a rational function
f/g
such that
g=\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
can be factored into linear polynomials in
R
, can we determine when an optimal PFD for
f/g
exists? We desire a combinatorial answer in terms of the hyperplane arrangement associated with the linear polynomials
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
. We say that such a rational function
f/g
has a
PFD (of degree
d
)
if it can be expressed as:

(1)

\frac{f}{g}=\frac{f}{\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}}=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n-d}\}\subset[n]}\frac{h_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n-d}}}{\ell_{j_{1}}\cdots\ell_{j_{n-d}}},\qquad\deg(\ell_{i})=1,\,\deg(h_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n-d}})\leq\deg(f)-d.

The main results (Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
) of this paper characterize the answer to this question in terms of the numerator
f
’s geometric properties relative to the hyperplane arrangement; the main result for hyperplane arrangements in projective space is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
\deg(f)\geq d\geq 1
, and let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
be nonzero linear forms with
n\geq d
.
Then
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
has a PFD of degree
d
as in (
1
) if and only if
f
vanishes to order at least
d-n+|S|
on all flats
S
of size at least
n-d+1
of the hyperplane arrangement associated with the linear forms.

Our approach to proving Theorem
1.2
is with commutative algebra: given linear forms
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
of the polynomial ring
R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
, we study the primary decomposition of the following ideal:

(2)

I_{L,d}=\langle\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}:1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq n\rangle.

Existence of a PFD of order
d
for the rational function
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
is equivalent to the numerator
f
being in the ideal
I_{L,d}
, and our primary decomposition gives an equivalent geometric condition for when this occurs.

One can use
Macaulay2

[
M2
]
to determine the existence of and algorithmically compute PFDs of the form in
(
1
). Our algorithm (Algorithm
2
) for finding a PFD is based upon Gröbner basis algorithms. It satisfies conditions
(i)
,
(ii)
,
(iii)
, and
(iv)
of the “wishlist” for a good partial fraction algorithm, described by Heller and von Manteuffel in
[
HvM
, §2.3]
and based upon work of Leĭnartas
[
Lei
]
:

(i)

the algorithm provides a unique answer;

(ii)

the algorithm does not introduce spurious denominator factors;

(iii)

the algorithm commutes with summation;

(iv)

the algorithm eliminates any spurious poles that are present in the input.

We now discuss the organization and contributions of this paper. In §
2
, we study the ideals
I_{L,d}
in (
2
) defined from hyperplane arrangements. We give primary decompositions for these ideals (Corollary
2.9
and Theorem
2.12
), which are presented in terms of the matroid associated with the hyperplane arrangement. Our primary decomposition also allows us to see that the ideals
I_{L,d}
have linear resolutions (Corollary
2.11
).
We then apply our decomposition in §
3
to study the ideals
I_{L,d}
where
L
is the braid arrangement. In §
4
, we also use our primary decomposition to deduce vanishing behavior of adjoint polynomials arising from polytopes; these polynomials are objects of much study in physics, combinatorics, and algebraic geometry. We then return to our main motivation of PFDs in §
5
: in this section, we give criteria for when PFDs of degree
d
exist (Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
). We present Algorithms
1
and
2
to determine the existence of and compute PFDs; we also demonstrate how our algorithms satisfy all four conditions
(i)
–
(iv)
of a good PFD algorithm. In §
6
, we then show how our criteria and algorithm can be applied to PFDs coming from two different contexts from physics: Feynman integrals (§
6.1
) and flat-space wavefunction coefficients (§
6.2
). We conclude in §
7
with remarks on the outlook and directions for future investigation for the algebraic study of PFDs.

Code for the algorithms and examples presented in this paper are publicly available at:

https://github.com/CJMDK/PFDs
.

2.
Primary decompositions

We fix a field
K
, let
r,n\geq 1
be arbitrary positive integers, and let
R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
denote the polynomial ring in
r
variables. The central geometric objects of this paper are hyperplane arrangements, defined by
n
linear polynomials
L=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
,
\ell_{i}\in R
; we consider arrangements in both affine and projective spaces, with the
\ell_{i}
homogeneous in the latter setting.

Let
d
be a positive integer with
1\leq d\leq n
. Given a hyperplane arrangement
L=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
, we study the ideal
I_{L,d}\subset R
defined by
d
-fold products of the linear forms:

I_{L,d}=\langle\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}:1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq n\rangle.

We allow for the cases that some of the
\ell_{i}=0
are zero, or that the
\ell_{i}
may not all be distinct, i.e.,
\ell_{i}=\ell_{j}
for for some
i\neq j
. However, we assume that
I_{L,d}\neq 0
is not the zero ideal, so not all of the
\ell_{i}=0
are zero. The main results of this section give a primary decomposition for
I_{L,d}
.

2.1.
Generic hyperplane arrangements

Before tackling the case of an arbitrary hyperplane arrangement, we focus first on
generic
arrangements in projective space, i.e., the hyperplanes are in linearly general position; the matroid corresponding to the arrangement is the
uniform matroid
(see Remark
2.4
).
Here, we let
n\geq r
be arbitrary positive integers, and fix
d=n-r+1
.

To state the following proposition, we introduce some notation. Write each linear form
\ell_{i}\in R
in the form
\ell_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{ij}x_{j}
, with
a_{ij}\in K
. Define
A=(a_{ij})
to be the
(n\times r)
-matrix of these coefficients. For
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
, denote by
A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]
the
r
-minor of
A
obtained by taking the submatrix of
A
with rows labeled by
\ell_{i_{1}},\ldots,\ell_{i_{r}}
. Now, define
\mathcal{M}
to be the
(\binom{n}{d}\times\binom{n}{d})
-matrix of coefficients, with rows labeled by
d
-fold products
\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}
of the linear forms, and columns labeled by degree
d
monomials of
R
.

Proposition 2.1
.

With the notation set above, we have:

(3)

\det(\mathcal{M})=\pm\prod_{1\leq i_{i}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}].

In particular, if every
r
-subset of the linear forms
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
is linearly independent, then the set of
d
-fold products of the linear forms is also linearly independent.

Proof.

We may assume that
K
is algebraically closed. Note that each
A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]
is irreducible, and so by Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz,

A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]\text{ divides }\det(\mathcal{M})\ \Leftrightarrow\ \mathcal{V}(A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}])\subset\mathcal{V}(\det(\mathcal{M})).

We now show that
\mathcal{V}(A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}])\subset\mathcal{V}(\det(\mathcal{M}))
. Suppose that there exists some relation between the
\ell_{i}
, i.e., for some
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\subset[n]
, there exists
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in K
not all zero such that:

(4)

\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{i}\ell_{i_{j}}=0.

The existence of such a relation is equivalent to
A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]
being identically zero. In other words, the tuples
(a_{11},\ldots,a_{nr})\in K^{n\times r}
for which such a relation (
4
) exist form
\mathcal{V}(A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}])
.
Define the set
\{i^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,i^{\prime}_{d-1}\}=[n]\setminus\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}
. Multiplying each term in (
4
) by
\ell_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i^{\prime}_{d-1}}
, we find that:

(5)

\sum_{j=1}^{r}c_{i}\ell_{i_{j}}\ell_{i^{\prime}_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i^{\prime}_{d-1}}=0.

Each
c_{i}
in (
5
) is multiplied by a unique collection of
d
linear forms.
But then we see that the set of
d
-fold products of the
\ell_{i}
is linearly dependent, and so
\det(\mathcal{M})
vanishes on
\mathcal{V}(A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}])
. Thus
\mathcal{V}(A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}])\subset\mathcal{V}(\det(\mathcal{M}))
. This implies that:

\det(\mathcal{M})=h\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\subset[n]\\
i_{i}<\cdots<i_{r}\end{subarray}}A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}],\qquad h\in K[a_{11},\ldots,a_{nr}].

Each
A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]
is a degree
r
polynomial, and so their product has degree
\binom{n}{r}\cdot r
in the
a_{ij}
.
The entries of the matrix
\mathcal{M}
are each of degree
d
in the
a_{ij}
, and so
\det(\mathcal{M})
has degree
\binom{n}{d}\cdot d
. For
n=r+d-1
, we have
\binom{n}{r}\cdot r=\binom{n}{d}\cdot d
, so
\det(\mathcal{M})
and the product of
A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]
are polynomials of the same degree. This is only possible if
h\in K
. The fact that
h=\pm 1
is proven in the
following lemma (Lemma
2.2
).
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Following the notation of the proof of Proposition
2.1
, we have that
h=\pm 1
:

(6)

\det(\mathcal{M})=\pm\prod_{1\leq i_{i}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n}A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}].

Proof.

Consider a diagonal term order on the polynomial ring
K[a_{11},\ldots,a_{nr}]
. Then for any
r
-subset
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}\}\subset[n]
, the leading term (with coefficient) of
A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}]
is

\LT(A[i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}])=\pm a_{i_{1}1}\cdots a_{i_{r}r}.

Therefore, the leading term of the RHS of (
6
) is
\pm m
, where
m
is the monomial with the exponent of the variable
a_{ij}
given by:

•

if
i\geq j
:
\binom{i-1}{j-1}\binom{n-i}{r-j}
, which is the number of
r
-sequences
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{r})
where
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq n
and
i
appears in the
j^{\text{th}}
spot;

•

if
i<j
:
0
.

Now consider the matrix
\mathcal{M}
. Its columns are indexed by degree
d
monomials in
R
, so order its columns by lexicographic order. Its rows are indexed by
r
-subsets of
[n]
, so order these by lexicographic order on
[n]^{r}
. Then the leading term
m^{\prime}
of
\det(\mathcal{M})
is the product of terms appearing in the diagonal of
\mathcal{M}
. Using the “stars and bars” combinatorial argument, i.e., the correspondence between monomials and
n
-tuples of stars and bars, with
i_{1},\ldots,i_{r}
corresponding to the indices where stars are placed, one sees that
m^{\prime}
is the monomial with the exponent of the variable
a_{ij}
given by:

•

if
i\geq j
:
\binom{i-1}{j-1}\binom{n-i}{r-j}
, which is the number of
n
-tuples of stars and bars where the
i^{\text{th}}
position is a star appearing after
j-1
bars and before
r-j
bars;

•

if
i<j
:
0
.

Thus, we see that the leading terms of both sides of (
6
) are the same, up to multiplication by
\pm 1
. This implies the desired result.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
L=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
be
n
linear forms in
R
such that every
r
-subset of the
\ell_{i}
is linearly independent, and let
d=n-r+1
.
Then
I_{L,d}=\mathfrak{m}^{d}
, where
\mathfrak{m}=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\rangle
is the homogeneous maximal ideal. In particular, the set
\{\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}:1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq n\}
forms a
K
-basis of the vector space of degree
d
homogeneous polynomials in
R
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.1
, the set
\{\ell_{i_{1}}\ell_{i_{2}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}:1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq n\}
is linearly independent, and has cardinality
\binom{n}{d}
. The vector space of homogeneous degree
d
polynomials in
R
also has dimension
\binom{n}{d}
, and so the set of
d
-fold products of the
\ell_{i}
forms a basis for this vector space.
Consequently, the ideal
I_{L,d}
is the same as the ideal generated by all degree
d
polynomials of the polynomial ring; this latter ideal is exactly
\mathfrak{m}^{d}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The description of the linear forms
\ell_{i}
in Theorem
2.3
can be reformulated from a matroid-theoretic perspective, with the configuration of the hyperplane arrangement corresponding to the uniform matroid of rank
r
on
n
elements. Explicitly, setting
\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\}
as the ground set, the collection of independent sets is:

\{\{\ell_{i_{1}},\ldots,\ell_{i_{m}}\}:\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}\subset[n],\ m\leq r\}.

The flats of this matroid are the subsets of the ground set of cardinality at most
r
, together with the ground set itself. This foreshadows Corollary
2.9
in the following subsection, where we consider arbitrary hyperplane arrangements.

2.2.
Arbitrary projective case

In this subsection, let
r,n,d
be positive integers with
n\geq d
. We consider arbitrary hyperplane arrangements determined by
n
linear forms
L=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})
, with
\ell_{i}\in R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
. Our main result (Corollary
2.9
) is a primary decomposition for the ideal
I_{L,d}
.

Our argument follows that of
[
CH
, §3]
, in which the authors study products of ideals generated by linear forms; we note that although of a similar flavor, our ideal
I_{L,d}
cannot be written as the product of such ideals.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
I
be a homogeneous ideal in the polynomial ring
R
, and let
\mathfrak{m}=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\rangle
be the homogeneous maximal ideal. We define the
saturation

I^{\sat}
of
I
to be the ideal:

I^{\sat}=\{f\in R:f\cdot\mathfrak{m}^{p}\subset I\text{ for some }p\in\mathbb{N}\}.

The
saturation degree

\mathrm{sat}(I)
of
I
is then defined to be the smallest integer
q\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
f
homogeneous with
\deg(f)\geq q
:

f\in I\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad f\in I^{\sat}.

For
S\subset[n]
, let
I_{S}=\langle\ell_{i}:i\in S\rangle
be the prime ideal generated by a subset
S
of the linear forms. Notice that any power of
I_{S}
is an
I_{S}
-primary ideal; our primary decomposition of
I_{L,d}
consists of these
I_{S}
-primary ideals.

Lemma 2.6
.

We have the following inclusion of ideals:

I_{L,d}\subset\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}.

Proof.

Fix
S\subset[n]
with
|S|\geq n-d+1
. It suffices to show that
I_{L,d}\subset(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}
; in particular, it is enough to show that each generator
\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}
of
I_{L,d}
is in
(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}
. Since
S
and
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}
are subsets of
[n]
, we have that
|S\cup\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}|\leq n
. Also, since
|S|\geq n-d+1
, we have that
n\leq d+|S|-1<d+|S|
. Applying these two facts in succession, we find that:

|S\cap\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}|=|S|+d-|S\cup\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}|\geq|S|+d-n>0.

But then
S\cap\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}
is non-empty and has cardinality at least
d-n+|S|
. Choose any subset of this intersection that has cardinality
d-n+|S|
, say
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d-n+|S|}\}
. Then each
\ell_{j_{m}}
is a generator in
I_{S}
and so the products
\ell_{j_{1}}\cdots\ell_{j_{d-n+|S|}}
along with
\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}
belong in
(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}
.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
I
be the following ideal:

I=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}.

Then
\sat(I)\leq d
.

Proof.

We may assume that
I_{[n]}=\mathfrak{m}
; otherwise, one may work in a polynomial ring with fewer variables.
Each term appearing in the decomposition of
I
is primary. In particular,
(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}
is
I_{S}
-primary. Then, a primary decomposition for the saturation
I^{\sat}
is exactly the same as that of
I
, except for the removal of ideals that are
\mathfrak{m}
-primary. We have that
\mathfrak{m}^{d}
appears in the decomposition, since
I_{[n]}=\mathfrak{m}
. Furthermore, the exponent
d-n+|S|=d-n+n=d
is the largest exponent appearing in the decomposition of
I
. Thus, we see that:

I=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}=\mathfrak{m}^{d}\cap\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1,\\
I_{S}\neq\mathfrak{m}\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}=\mathfrak{m}^{d}\cap I^{\sat}.

This implies that
I
and
I^{\sat}
agree in degrees at least
d
, and so
\sat(I)\leq d
.
∎

Theorem 2.8
.

We have the following equality of ideals:

I_{L,d}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on both
d
and
\dim(R)=r
. We may assume that
K
is algebraically closed and therefore infinite, as well as that
r
is minimal so that
I_{[n]}=\mathfrak{m}
.

We first consider the base cases for the induction. Assume that
d=1
. Then
I_{L,d}=\langle\ell_{i}:i\in[n]\rangle=I_{[n]}=\mathfrak{m}
and:

\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{1-n+|S|}=I_{[n]}=I_{L,d}.

Now suppose
d
is arbitrary and
r=1
. Then
\ell_{i}=a_{i}x_{1}
with
a_{i}\in K
not all zero. We then see that
I_{L,d}=\langle x_{1}\rangle^{d}=I^{d}_{[n]}
, and so the claim follows.

We now consider
d,r\geq 2
. In Lemma
2.6
, we have shown one of the inclusions, and so the remainder of this proof is dedicated to showing the reverse inclusion.
For each
j\in[n]
, define the ideal
I_{j}
by:

I_{j}=\langle\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d-1}}:\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d-1}\}\subset[n]\backslash\{j\},i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d-1}\}.

The induction assumption on
d
allows us to write the following decomposition for each
I_{j}
:

I_{j}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n]\backslash\{j\},\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}.

Then:

\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}|S|\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}=\mathfrak{m}^{d}\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}.

To prove the reverse inclusion, it therefore suffices to show that if
f\in\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}
is homogeneous of degree at least
d
, then
f\in I_{L,d}
.

Since
K
is infinite, there exists a linear form
y\in R
such that

•

y
is a nonzerodivisor on each
I_{j}^{\sat}
for which
I_{j}
is positive-dimensional; and

•

y
is not in the
K
-span of the linear forms
\ell_{j}
for all
j
.

Consider the ideals
(I_{L,d}+\langle y\rangle)/\langle y\rangle
and
(I_{j}+\langle y\rangle)/\langle y\rangle
in the quotient ring
R/\langle y\rangle
, which has dimension
r-1
. These ideals are generated by
d
and
(d-1)
-fold products of the images of the
\ell_{i}
.
One can then invoke the induction hypothesis on
\dim R
to see that the image of
f
in
R/\langle y\rangle
is in
(I_{L,d}+\langle y\rangle)/\langle y\rangle
. Therefore,
f
can be written as
f=h+yg
, where
h\in I_{L,d}
and
g\in R
is of degree at least
d-1
, and so we may assume that
f=yg
. Then,
f=yg\in I_{j}\subset I_{j}^{\sat}
for each
j
. Since
y
is a nonzerodivisor on
I_{j}^{\sat}
, we have that
g\in I_{j}^{\sat}
as well. The preceding argument holds for all
j
with
I_{j}
positive-dimensional; if
I_{j}
has dimension zero, then
I_{j}^{\sat}=R
, and so
g\in I_{j}^{\sat}
for all
j
.
Applying the induction hypothesis along with Lemma
2.7
(so
\sat(I_{j})\leq d-1
), we have that
g\in I_{j}
. Write
y\in\mathfrak{m}=I_{[n]}
as
y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}
, where
y_{j}\in\langle\ell_{j}\rangle
. Then
f=yg=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}g\in I_{L,d}
, as
y_{j}g\in\langle\ell_{j}\rangle\cdot I_{j}\subset I_{L,d}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We are now able to give a combinatorial description for a primary decomposition of
I_{L,d}
in terms of the matroid associated with the subspace arrangement, which we now define. The matroid
M
is on the ground set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The independence sets of this matroid are given by subsets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}\subset[n]
such that the linear forms
\ell_{i_{1}},\ldots,\ell_{i_{m}}
are linearly independent in the vector space of linear forms in
R
.

Given such a matroid
M
, we have an associated
rank function

\mathrm{rk}(-)
on
M
: for
S\subset[n]
, we define
\mathrm{rk}(S)
to be the rank of the
K
-vector space spanned by
\{\ell_{i}:i\in S\}
. For a subset
S\subset[n]
, we define the
closure

\mathrm{cl}(S)
of
S
to be:

\mathrm{cl}(S)=\{i\in[n]:\mathrm{rk}(S)=\mathrm{rk}(S\cup\{i\})\}.

A
flat
of the matroid is defined to be a subset
S\subset[n]
such that
S=\mathrm{cl}(S)
.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
M
denote the matroid corresponding to the linear forms
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
. Then we have the following primary decomposition of the ideal
I_{L,d}
:

I_{L,d}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[n],\\
|S|\geq n-d+1,\\
\text{$S$ a flat of $M$}\end{subarray}}(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}.

Proof.

Let
S\subset[n]
with
|S|\geq n-d+1
.
Then
I_{S}=I_{\mathrm{cl}(S)}
, and
|\mathrm{cl}(S)|\geq|S|
, so:

(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|}\supset(I_{\mathrm{cl}(S)})^{d-n+|\mathrm{cl}(S)|}.

Therefore, it is enough to only take the closed sets in the intersection of Theorem
2.8
; the closed sets are exactly the flats of
M
.
∎

We note that the primary decomposition in Corollary
2.9
is not necessarily minimal, as the following example illustrates. One may try to use more refined data from the associated matroid to determine the associated primes and minimal primary decomposition of
I_{L,d}
, such as in

.

Example 2.10

Let

Then a minimal primary decomposition of the corresponding ideal

Since

is always a flat, the ideal

appears in the primary decomposition given in Corollary

. However, in this example it is redundant and does not appear in the minimal primary decomposition.

2.3.
Affine case

From our work in the homogeneous case, we may now deduce results for primary decompositions of hyperplane arrangements in affine space.

Consider linear polynomials
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\in R
. By homogenizing, we get linear forms
\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\ell_{n}^{\prime}\in S=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1}]
. Let
I_{L,d}\subset R
denote the ideal of
d
-fold products of the
\ell_{i}
, and let
J_{L,d}\subset S
denote the ideal of
d
-fold products of the
\ell_{i}^{\prime}
. Then:

I_{L,d}=(J_{L,d}+\langle x_{r+1}-1\rangle)\cap R.

Therefore, we use Corollary
2.9
to obtain the following primary decomposition of
I_{L,d}
in
R
:

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\in R
be linear polynomials. Then we have the following primary decomposition of
I_{L,d}
:

I_{L,d}=\bigcap(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|},

where the intersection is over subsets
S\subset[n]
such that:

(1)

S
indexes a flat of the hyperplane arrangement associated with the homogenizations of the
\ell_{i}
;

(2)

the hyperplane at infinity is not in the span of this associated flat, i.e.,
x_{r+1}\notin I_{S}
; and

(3)

|S|\geq n-d+1
.

Proof.

We retain the notation introduced in the paragraph preceding the statement of the theorem. By Corollary
2.9
, we have that:

\displaystyle I_{L,d}

\displaystyle=(J_{L,d}+\langle x_{r+1}-1\rangle)\cap R

\displaystyle=\left(\left(\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$S$ a flat for $\ell^{\prime}_{i}$},\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}(J_{S})^{d-n+|S|}\right)+\langle x_{r+1}-1\rangle\right)\cap R

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{$S$ a flat for $\ell_{i}^{\prime}$},\\
|S|\geq n-d+1\end{subarray}}\left((J_{S})^{d-n+|S|}+\langle x_{r+1}-1\rangle\right)\cap R.

If
J_{S}
is supported on the hyperplane at infinity, i.e.,
x_{r+1}\in J_{S}
, then:

\left((J_{S})^{d-n+|S|}+\langle x_{r+1}-1\rangle\right)\cap R=R,

and so the term is redundant in the intersection. Otherwise:

\left((J_{S})^{d-n+|S|}+\langle x_{r+1}-1\rangle\right)\cap R=(I_{S})^{d-n+|S|},

which gives the desired equality.
∎

We demonstrate our primary decomposition (Corollary

) on

, which form an important family of hyperplane arrangements arising across algebra, geometry, topology, and combinatorics (see, e.g.,
[

). Braid arrangements also appear in physics via denominators of

[

and

[

Definition 3.1
.

The

consists of the following set of

\{x_{i}-x_{j}:\text{$i<j$ for which $i,j\in B_{k}$ are in the same subset of the partition}\}.

For each flat of

where
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}=r
and
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\lambda_{r}\geq 0
. Then, for a flat associated with the partition

One may notice from Table

and Example

that if
\lambda\succeq\mu
under the dominance order on partitions (recall that
\lambda\succeq\mu
under the
dominance order
if and only if
\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{i}\geq\mu_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{i}
for all
i\in[r]
), then the size of a flat associated with
\lambda
is larger than that of
\mu
. The following result shows that this holds more generally, thus showing that the set of partitions associated with flats appearing in our primary decomposition (Corollary
2.9
) is a filter in the poset of partitions.

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose
\lambda,\mu\vdash r
be partitions, with
\lambda\succeq\mu
under the dominance order. Then
|S_{\lambda}|\geq|S_{\mu}|
, where
S_{\lambda}
(resp.
S_{\mu}
) denotes any flat associated with
\lambda
(resp.
\mu
). In particular, if flats corresponding to the partition
\mu
appear in the primary decomposition of Corollary
2.9
, then so do flats corresponding to the partition
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
and
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{r})
, with
\lambda_{i},\mu_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
. It suffices to assume that
\lambda\succeq\mu
is a cover relation. By
[
Bry
, Proposition 2.3]
, this implies that there exists
i,j\in[r]
with
i<j
such that
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}+1
,
\lambda_{j}=\mu_{j}-1
,
\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\geq 2
, and
\lambda_{k}=\mu_{k}
for all
k\neq i,j
. Then
|S_{\lambda}|\geq|S_{\mu}|
if and only if:

\binom{\lambda_{i}}{2}+\binom{\lambda_{j}}{2}\geq\binom{\mu_{i}}{2}+\binom{\mu_{j}}{2}=\binom{\lambda_{i}-1}{2}+\binom{\lambda_{j}+1}{2}.

Consequently:

\displaystyle\left(\binom{\lambda_{i}}{2}+\binom{\lambda_{j}}{2}\right)-\left(\binom{\lambda_{i}-1}{2}+\binom{\lambda_{j}+1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}-1\geq 1,

and so the desired conclusion holds.
∎

4.
Adjoint polynomials

A rapidly developing field at the intersection of physics, combinatorics, and algebraic geometry is the study of positive geometries and canonical forms
[
Lam
,
RST
]
. Central mathematical objects in this study are projective polytopes and their
adjoint polynomials
. In this section, we highlight how the primary decomposition in Corollary
2.9
can be used to easily deduce vanishing properties of adjoint polynomials. We define this polynomial, and refer the reader to the paper
[
Gae
]
for further introduction to the relevant terminology.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
P
be a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{r-1}
. Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
be the cone over
P
with rays
V(C)
corresponding to vertices of
P
.
For each simplicial cone
S\in T
of a triangulation
T
of
C
, denote by
a_{S}
the volume of the parallelepiped whose edges are defined by the rays
V(S)
of
S
. The
adjoint polynomial
of
C
is defined as:

\mathrm{adj}_{C}(x)=\sum_{S\in T}\left(a_{S}\prod_{v\in V(C)\backslash V(S)}\ell_{v}(x)\right).

Here,
\ell_{v}(x)=v\cdot x
, where
\cdot
denotes scalar product.

The form of
\mathrm{adj}_{C}
is independent of the choice of triangulation of
C
, and
\mathrm{adj}_{C}
is homogeneous and of degree
|V(C)|-\dim(C)

[
Gae
,
War
]
.
Furthermore, Kohn–Ranestad showed that the residual arrangement of the
hyperplane arrangement defining the facets of the dual cone
C^{\vee}
must vanish on the adjoint polynomial; the adjoint polynomial is also uniquely characterized by certain vanishing conditions on this residual arrangement
[
BW
,
KR
]
.

The above definition of adjoint polynomial makes it clear that adjoint polynomials are in ideals of the form
I_{L,d}
, where in this case the hyperplanes defining the ideal are defined by the
\ell_{v}
from Definition
4.1
.
Consequently, using the primary decomposition of Corollary
2.9
, one can determine certain properties about where adjoint polynomials vanish, without needing to compute any of the
a_{S}
coefficients or the residual arrangement of the dual cone.
Corollary
2.9
implies the following vanishing behavior of adjoint polynomials.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{r-1}
be a polytope, and let
C
be the cone over
P
. Then the adjoint polynomial
\mathrm{adj}_{C}
vanishes to order at least
d-n+|S|
on all flats
S
of size at least
n-d+1
of the hyperplane arrangement given by the homogenized facet hyperplanes of
P^{\vee}
.

This vanishing condition is different from that of
[
KR
, Theorem 1.5]
, which guarantees vanishing of
\mathrm{adj}_{C}
on the residual arrangement of the dual polytope.

4.3
.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the polytope
P
shown in Figure
1
. Its homogeneous facet hyperplanes can be given by:

\begin{aligned} \ell_{1}&=x_{2}-x_{4},\\
\ell_{3}&=-2x_{1}+x_{2}+2x_{4},\\
\ell_{5}&=-2x_{1}+x_{2}-2x_{4},\\
\ell_{7}&=x_{2}-2x_{3}+x_{4}.\end{aligned}\quad\quad\begin{aligned} \ell_{2}&=2x_{1}+x_{2}-2x_{4},\\
\ell_{4}&=2x_{1}+x_{2}+2x_{4},\\
\ell_{6}&=x_{3}\end{aligned}

Each of these can be written in the form
v_{i}\cdot x
where the
v_{i}
are the set of vertex rays of a pointed polyhedral cone
C
(the dual cone associated with
P
). The adjoint polynomial of this cone is thus of the form:

\text{adj}_{C}(x)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}\leq 7}a_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}\ell_{i_{1}}\ell_{i_{2}}\ell_{i_{3}},\qquad a_{i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}}\in\mathbb{R}.

This polynomial is an element of the ideal:

I_{L,3}=\langle\ell_{i_{1}}\ell_{i_{2}}\ell_{i_{3}}:1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}\leq 7\rangle,

which, using Corollary
2.9
, can be expressed as the primary decomposition:

I_{L,3}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subset[7],\,|S|\geq 5,\\

Here, we have used that the matroid defined by the facet hyperplanes only has two flats containing more than four elements, those being
\{\ell_{i}:i\in[5]\}
and

. This result tells us that the adjoint polynomial associated with the polytope

all vanish.

Remark 4.4
.

It was shown in
[
Gae
]
that canonical forms of polytopes
P
can be expressed as rational functions, the numerator being the adjoint polynomial associated with the polytope
P^{\vee}
dual to
P
, and the denominator being a product of facet hyperplanes of
P^{\vee}
. This means that canonical forms are exactly a type of rational function with a PFD of degree
d
.

5.
Partial fraction decompositions and algorithmic aspects

We now return to the problem of existence and computation of PFDs. Let
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
be a rational function, where
f,\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\in R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
are nonzero polynomials such that
\deg(\ell_{i})=1
. Recall that we are interested in when this function has a PFD of degree
d
, meaning that it can be expressed as:

(7)

\frac{f}{\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}}=\sum_{\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n-d}\}\subset[n]}\frac{h_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n-d}}}{\ell_{j_{1}}\cdots\ell_{j_{n-d}}},\qquad\deg(h_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n-d}})\leq\deg(f)-d.

Generally, the least common multiple of all of the terms in the PFD expression is exactly
\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}
. By putting all of the terms of the PFD under this common denominator, we see that such a PFD exists exactly when the polynomial
f
can be expressed as an
R
-linear combination of
d
-fold products of the
\ell_{i}
’s. In other words, a PFD of degree
d
exists for
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
if and only if
f
lies in the ideal
I_{L,d}
studied in §
2
. We can thus interpret our results on primary decompositions of
I_{L,d}
as vanishing criteria for existence of PFDs.
We first consider the case of hyperplane arrangements in linearly general position.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
be
n
linear forms in
R=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}]
such that any
r
-subset of the
\ell_{i}
are linearly independent. Set
d=n-r+1
, and let
f\in R
be any homogeneous degree
d
polynomial. Then the rational function
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})

has a unique PFD of degree
d
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.3
, the polynomial
f
can be written uniquely as a
K
-linear combination of the elements of
\{\ell_{i_{1}}\ell_{i_{2}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}}:1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq n\}
:

f=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}\subset[n],\\
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\end{subarray}}a_{i_{1}\ldots i_{d}}\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}},\qquad a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}\in K.

Thus, we can write the rational function as:

\frac{f}{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\cdots\ell_{n}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}\subset[n],\\
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\end{subarray}}\frac{a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}}{(\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\cdots\ell_{n})/(\ell_{i_{1}}\cdots\ell_{i_{d}})}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n-d}\}\subset[n],\\
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{n-d}\end{subarray}}\frac{b_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n-d}}}{\ell_{j_{1}}\cdots\ell_{j_{n-d}}},

where we set
b_{j_{1}\cdots j_{n-d}}
to be equal to
a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}
with
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}=[n]\setminus\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n-d}\}
.
∎

We now turn to the case of arbitrary hyperplane arrangements, both in the projective and affine settings. The following results on PFDs follow from Corollary
2.9
and Theorem
2.12
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
be
n
nonzero linear forms in
R
, let
d\leq n
, and let
f\in R
be a homogeneous polynomial with
\deg(f)\geq d
. Then
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
has a PFD of degree
d
if and only if
f
vanishes to order at least
d-n+|S|
on all flats
S
of size at least
n-d+1
of the hyperplane arrangement associated with the linear forms.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}
be
n
nonzero linear polynomials in
R
, let
d\leq n
, and let
f\in R
be a polynomial with
\deg(f)\geq d
. Then
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
has a PFD of degree

if and only if
f
vanishes to order at least
d-n+|S|
on all flats

, where:

•

S

•

the size of

; and

•

We now turn to the matter of computing PFDs. Recall the wishlist for a good PFD algorithm outlined in §
1
. We first focus on the notion of spurious poles.

Definition 5.4
.

A
spurious pole
of a rational function is a denominator factor that can be removed upon a re-writing of the rational function.

In Example
1.1
, the first decomposition of our rational function introduces a spurious pole at
x=0
; this is undesirable. Our notion of a PFD of degree
d
guarantees that spurious poles are not introduced, as all of the poles in (

) appear in both the original rational function and its PFD. However, before beginning any computation of PFDs of this form, we must ensure that spurious poles do not already exist in the input of
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
.
To address this, consider the following definition: we say that a rational function
f/g
with
f,g\in R
nonzero polynomials is
reducible
if
f,g
share a common irreducible factor. Then, the input rational function
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
already having a spurious pole is equivalent to it being reducible. One also sees immediately that this is equivalent to
f\in\langle\ell_{i}\rangle
for some
i\in[n]
. Algorithm
1
below constructs a reduced expression for the rational function by finding and eliminating these spurious
\ell_{i}
(if they exist).

We now explain why our algorithms satisfy the wishlist from §

(i)

Given a polynomial

Remark 5.6
.

The algorithm above is highly customizable. Below we list examples of small changes that can be made to hone the algorithm for certain applications.

(1)

Large
n
or
r
:
Construction of the ideals
I_{L,d}
in
Macaulay2
can take a long time when
n
or
r
is large. Below are two ways these large computation times can be circumvented.

(a)

For a given choice of
d
, check if the numerator is in an ideal generated by some subset of the generators of
I_{L,d}
. If this is true, a PFD of degree
d
is then obtainable with this restricted set of generators. This strategy is used below for the calculation of a “large” coefficient coming from Feynman integrals (§
6.1.2
).

(b)

Suppose the numerator is in the ideal
I_{L,d}
for some small choice of
d
. Rather than checking if the same numerator is in
I_{L,d}
for larger
d
, consider the numerators in the PFD expansion for small
d
and apply the PFD algorithm on them, also restricting to small choices of
d
(if need be). Proceed iteratively. This iterative procedure will produce the same output as the original algorithm as a consequence of the fact that the algorithm commutes with summation
(iii)
.

(2)

Choosing PFD denominators:
Suppose one has a specific PFD form in mind, e.g. that does not have certain denominator factors. This can be achieved by restricting the generators of
I_{L,d}
to the subset of them that do not produce the undesired denominator factors. If the numerator is a member of this restricted ideal, then the desired PFD can be generated.

(3)

Nonlinear denominator factors:
The algorithm above can readily be applied to the case where the
\ell_{i}
are not linear. Unlike when all the
\ell_{i}
are linear, the resulting PFD may contain terms with denominators of differing degrees.

6.1.
Feynman integrals

In preparation for the approaching upgrade to the Large Hadron Collider
[
AAA+
]
, much work has been done in developing efficient algorithms to aid the computation of Feynman integrals
[
HvM
,
BFP
,
CGM
]
. Many of these algorithms are based on
integration-by-parts
(IBP)
reductions
, in which a Feymann integral is decomposed as a linear combination of
master integrals
(MIs). These MIs are a choice of basis for the family of Feynman integrals under consideration. The coefficients of the linear combination are rational functions in the external kinematics and the dimensional regularization parameter
\epsilon
. These rational functions are often very large, and can make calculations cumbersome. The authors of
[
BW+
]
suggest storing the coefficients of IBP reductions in the form of a PFD, and introduce their own PFD algorithm as a “powerful method to reduce the byte size of the analytic IBP reduction coeﬃcients”. Their paper includes an application to IBP reduction coefficients arising from the Feynman integral associated with the the
two-loop five-point non-planar double pentagon diagram
shown in Figure
2
. These coefficients were calculated (with respect to a specific choice of MIs) in
[
BBD+
]
.
The authors of
[
HvM
]
suggest using these coefficients as a benchmark for testing PFD algorithms for Feynman integrals. In this section we naively apply our PFD algorithm to two of these coefficients, a “small” and a “large” example, and discuss the results. We hope this may serve as a starting point for further refinement by physicists.

7.
Future directions and outlook

We present some potential directions for future work regarding PFDs.

In the generic case, PFDs of degree
d
are unique (Theorem
5.1
). But given arbitrary
f\in R
and an arbitrary hyperplane arrangement
L
, there can be be many different such PFDs. This is because the products of
d
of the
\ell_{i}
need not be algebraically independent. To see this explicitly, consider the following example.

Example 7.1
.

Define the hyperplanes
\ell_{1}=x-y,\ \ell_{2}=y+z,\ \ell_{3}=z,\ \ell_{4}=x+y\in K[x,y,z]
, and the polynomial
f
:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=2x^{2}y^{2}-2y^{4}+12x^{2}yz+4xy^{2}z-2y^{3}z+10x^{2}z^{2}+4xyz^{2}

\displaystyle=3x\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}+2y\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{4}+7x\ell_{2}\ell_{3}\ell_{4}

\displaystyle=(-2x-5y)\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}+2y\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{4}+10\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}\ell_{4}+(2x+5y)\ell_{2}\ell_{3}\ell_{4}.

We then have the following two PFDs of degree
3
:

(8)

\frac{f}{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}\ell_{4}}=\frac{3x}{x+y}+\frac{2y}{z}+\frac{7x}{x-y}=-\frac{2x+5y}{x+y}+\frac{2y}{z}+10+\frac{2x+5y}{x-y}.

Our algorithm gives a unique partial fraction decomposition, but changing the choice of labeling or ordering of the
\ell_{i}
could result in different PFDs.

Question 7.2
.

Given a hyperplane arrangement
L
and a polynomial
f\in I_{L,d}
, can one determine the number of unique PFDs of degree
d
?

Another question one can ask concerns when a term in a PFD is reducible, such as in the second PFD of (
8

10

Question 7.3
.

What are necessary or sufficient conditions for when a term of a PFD of degree
d
is reducible, or even a polynomial?

A first step in answering this question is the following result.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose
d=n-1\geq 1
, all of the
\ell_{i}
are pairwise prime linear polynomials, and
f\in I_{L,d}
is a polynomial. Then a PFD of degree
d
for
f/(\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n})
has a reducible term if and only if
f\in\langle\ell_{i}\rangle
for some
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

Since
f\in I_{L,d}
, it can be written in the form
f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\ell_{1}\cdots\hat{\ell}_{j}\cdots\ell_{n}
for some
a_{j}\in R
. Then we have the following chain of equivalences:

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ f\in\langle\ell_{i}\rangle\text{ for some }i\in[n]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow f=\ell_{i}g\text{ for some }g\in R

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\ell_{1}\cdots\hat{\ell}_{j}\cdots\ell_{n}=\ell_{i}g

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow a_{i}=\ell_{i}h\text{ for some }h\in R,\text{ since the $\ell_{i}$ are pairwise prime}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\frac{f}{\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{n}}=h+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}\frac{a_{j}}{\ell_{j}}.

∎

Through our study of ideals of the form
I_{L,d}
, we have proven a number of results about PFDs of rational functions on hyperplane arrangements. This led to new ways of understanding the geometry of adjoint polynomials and canonical forms, as well as a novel algorithmic approach for constructing PFDs of a tailored form. We have demonstrated how our findings have direct applications to a variety of problems in physics and hope that this paper inspires further study of PFDs by algebraists.
