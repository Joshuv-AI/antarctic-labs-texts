Overlapped Arithmetic Codes
Fang, Yong

Abstract
In his seminal paper giving birth to information theory, Shannon raised two fundamental coding problems in modern communications systems, i.e. , source coding for data compression and channel coding for error correction, which form the two main branches of information theory. Since then, many new coding problems appeared. For example, in the 1970s, Slepian and Wolf created a new branch for information theory, i.e. , distributed source coding , which is also called multiple-terminal source coding. Arithmetic codes are usually deemed as the most important means to implement lossless source coding, whose principle is mapping every source symbol to a sub-interval in [ 0 , 1 ) [0,1) . For every source symbol, the length of its mapping sub-interval is exactly equal to its probability. With this symbol-interval mapping rule, the interval [ 0 , 1 ) [0,1) will be fully covered and there is neither overlapped sub-interval (corresponds to more than one source symbol) nor forbidden sub-interval (does not correspond to any source symbol). It is well-known that there is a duality between source coding and channel coding, so every good source code may also be a good channel code meanwhile, and vice versa. Inspired by this duality, arithmetic codes can be easily generalized to address many coding problems beyond source coding by redefining the source-interval mapping rule. If every source symbol is mapped to an enlarged sub-interval, the mapping sub-intervals of different source symbols will be partially overlapped and we obtain overlapped arithmetic codes , which can realize distributed source coding . On the contrary, if every source symbol is mapped to a narrowed sub-interval, there will be one or more forbidden sub-intervals in [ 0 , 1 ) [0,1) that do not correspond to any source symbol and we obtain forbidden arithmetic codes , which can implement joint source-channel coding . Furthermore, by allowing the coexistence of overlapped sub-intervals and forbidden sub-intervals, we will obtain hybrid arithmetic codes , which can cope with distributed joint source-channel coding . The aim of this monograph is to review recent advances in overlapped arithmetic codes during the past decade. This monograph includes six sections. Section 1 introduces the principle of arithmetic codes by emphasizing two implementation issues— underflow handling and bitstream termination . Section 2 reveals that overlapped arithmetic codes are actually a kind of nonlinear coset codes and hence possess two fundamental properties— Coset Cardinality Spectrum (CCS) and Hamming Distance Spectrum (HDS). Then Section 2 shows how to calculate the CCS of overlapped arithmetic codes theoretically or numerically. On the basis of CCS, Section 2 also derives the decoding algorithm of overlapped arithmetic codes. Section 3 introduces a powerful analysis tool— coexisting interval to calculate the error rate of overlapped arithmetic codes. The topic of HDS is addressed in Section 4 , where a tight connection between CCS and HDS is also built. Then aided by HDS, Section 5 explains why overlapped arithmetic codes can be improved by mapping the last few symbols of a block to non-overlapped intervals. Finally, Section 6 concludes this monograph.

1.1
Theoretical Foundation

This subsection will briefly review the main theoretical results of four coding problems. We begin with some notation conventions. Let
(X,Y)
be a pair of discrete random variables defined over the alphabet
{\cal X}\times{\cal Y}
. The joint
probability mass function
(pmf) of
X
and
Y
is denoted as
p(x,y)
. The marginal pmfs of
X
and
Y
are denoted as
p(x)
and
p(y)
, respectively. Let
p(x|y)
be the conditional pmf of
X
given
Y
, and
p(y|x)
that of
Y
given
X
. These pmfs are related by

\displaystyle p(x,y)=p(x)p(y|x)=p(y)p(x|y).

(1.1)

Definition 1.1.1

(Entropy, Conditional Entropy, Joint Entropy, and Mutual Information)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be two discrete random variables with finite alphabets
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
, respectively. The entropy of
X
is defined as

\displaystyle H(X)\triangleq-\sum_{x\in{\cal X}}p(x)\log_{2}{p(x)},

(1.2)

and the entropy of
Y
, denoted by
H(Y)
, is defined in a similar way. The conditional entropy of
X
given
Y
is defined as

\displaystyle H(X|Y)\triangleq-\sum_{x\in{\cal X}}\sum_{y\in{\cal Y}}p(x,y)\log_{2}{p(x|y)},

(1.3)

and the conditional entropy of
Y
given
X
, denoted by
H(Y|X)
, can be defined in a similar way. The joint entropy of
X
and
Y
is defined as

\displaystyle H(X,Y)\triangleq-\sum_{x\in{\cal X}}\sum_{y\in{\cal Y}}p(x,y)\log_{2}{p(x,y)},

(1.4)

and the mutual information between
X
and
Y
is defined as

\displaystyle I(X;Y)\triangleq-\sum_{x\in{\cal X}}\sum_{y\in{\cal Y}}p(x,y)\log_{2}\frac{p(x)p(y)}{p(x,y)}.

(1.5)

According to the above definitions, it is easy to obtain

\displaystyle H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y|X)=H(Y)+H(X|Y)

(1.6)

and

\displaystyle I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y).

(1.7)

Now we give Shannon’s First Theorem
[
30
]
, which lays the theoretical foundation for lossless coding of memoryless discrete sources.

Theorem 1.1.2

(Shannon’s First Theorem)
.

Let
X
be a discrete random variable with finite alphabet

{\cal X}
. Let
x^{n}\triangleq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be
n
independent realizations of
X
. To compress
x^{n}
with zero loss, the achievable rate
R
(bits/symbol) is lower bounded by
H(X)
as block length
n\to\infty
.

We call the random variable
X
to be compressed as the
source
. Since the achievable rate
R\geq H(X)
, lossless source coding is also called
entropy coding
. Note that
Theorem

1.1.2
considers only memoryless sources. As for lossless coding of sources with memory, the reader may refer to
Chapter 4
of Cover’s textbook
[
7
]
.

Then we consider the problem of channel coding. A discrete memoryless transmission
channel
can be described by the conditional pmf
p(y|x)
of the discrete output
Y
given the discrete input
X
. The most important property of a channel is its

.

Definition 1.1.3

(Channel Capacity)
.

Let
p(y|x)
be a discrete memoryless channel with finite alphabet. Its capacity is defined as

\displaystyle C\triangleq\max_{p(x)}I(X;Y).

(1.8)

Following is Shannon’s second theorem, which lays the theoretical foundation for channel coding
[
30
]
.

Theorem 1.1.4

(Shannon’s Second Theorem)
.

Let
(x,y)^{n}
be
n
independent uses of a discrete memoryless channel with finite alphabet. The information amount conveyed by one use of the channel (bits/use) is upper bounded by
C
as block length
n\to\infty
.

The third coding problem is

Theorem 1.1.5

(Slepian-Wolf Theorem)
.

Let

and

After comparing

1.1.5

[
2
]
, and its asymmetric form with decoder side information is called

[
35
]
.

Finally, we consider the noisy-channel Slepian-Wolf coding problem, which is the lossless version of the

(DJSCC) problem (cf.
Fig.

1.2
). According to the Source-Channel Separation Theorem, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 1.1.6

(Noisy-Channel Slepian-Wolf Coding)
.

If two discrete sources
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are compressed independently and their bitstreams are conveyed over two noisy channels with capacities

and
C_{2}
, respectively, then the achievable rate region is given by
R_{1}\geq H(X_{1}|X_{2})/C_{1}
,
R_{2}\geq H(X_{2}|X_{1})/C_{2}
, and
C_{1}R_{1}+C_{2}R_{2}\geq H(X_{1},X_{2})
.

The low efficiency of Huffman codes comes from the property that Huffman codes always map every source symbol to a bit string, so that at least one bit is needed for one source symbol. To allow a fractional number of bits for one source symbol, we must map a block of source symbols as a whole to a bit string, which is the idea of arithmetic codes. Though the idea of arithmetic codes can date back to 1950s, Rissanen is widely known as the inventor of arithmetic codes
[
29
]
. For the history of arithmetic codes, the reader may refer to
[
25
]
. In the following, we will first introduce the primitive idea behind arithmetic codes, and then discuss two implementation issues,
i.e.
,
finite-precision
issue and
prefix-code
issue, encountered in practice, which were comprehensively discussed in Witten’s classical article
[
34
]
.

Let
X
be a binary source with bias probability
\Pr(X=1)=p
. Let
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
be
n
independent realizations of
X
. The arithmetic encoder maps
x^{n}
onto an interval
[l,h)\subset[0,1)
. Then from
[l,h)
, the number that can be represented with the fewest bits is binarized as the bitstream of
x^{n}
. Concretely speaking, the mapping interval
[l,h)
of
x^{n}
is obtained via a recursion, and for every source symbol, the length of its mapping interval is proportional to its probability (cf.
Fig.

1.3(a)
). Let
[l_{i},h_{i})
denote the mapping interval of
x^{i}\in\mathbb{B}^{i}
. Initially,
[l_{0},h_{0})=[0,1)
. According to
Fig.

1.3(a)
, on knowing
[l_{i},h_{i})
, we can derive
[l_{i+1},h_{i+1})
by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}l_{i+1}=l_{i}+x_{i+1}\cdot(1-p)(h_{i}-l_{i})\\
h_{i+1}=h_{i}-(1-x_{i+1})\cdot p(h_{i}-l_{i}).\end{cases}

(1.9)

Finally, we obtain the mapping interval
[l_{n},h_{n})
for
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Clearly,

\displaystyle[l_{n},h_{n})\subset[l_{n-1},h_{n-1})\subset\dots\subset[l_{1},h_{1})\subset[l_{0},h_{0})=[0,1).

(1.10)

For conciseness, we shorten
[l_{n},h_{n})
to
[l,h)
, whose length is

\displaystyle(h-l)=p(x^{n})\triangleq\Pr(X^{n}=x^{n})=(1-p)^{n-|x^{n}|}\cdot p^{|x^{n}|},

(1.11)

where
|x^{n}|
is the support (the number of nonzero terms) of
x^{n}
. There are infinitely many real numbers in
[l,h)
, among which the real number that can be represented by the fewest bits is binarized as the bitstream of
x^{n}
. Obviously, in any interval, integers can be represented by fewer bits than floating-point numbers. However, there is no integer in
[l,h)
. To solve this problem, we introduce the following concept.

Definition 1.3.1

(Normalized Interval)
.

Let
[l,h)\subset[0,1)
be the mapping interval of
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. The normalized mapping interval of
x^{n}
is defined as
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
, where

\displaystyle m\triangleq\lceil-\log_{2}(h-l)\rceil=-\lfloor\log_{2}(h-l)\rfloor.

(1.12)

Lemma 1.3.2

(Properties of Normalized Interval)
.

The following properties of
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
hold obviously.

•

1\leq(h2^{m}-l2^{m})=(h-l)2^{m}<2
.

•

l2^{m}
and
h2^{m}
are constrained by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil-1)<l2^{m}\leq\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil\\
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil<h2^{m}<(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+2).\end{cases}

(1.13)

•

If
\log_{2}(h-l)\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
(h-l)2^{m}=1
and there is one and only one integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
in
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
.

•

If
\log_{2}(h-l)\notin\mathbb{Z}
, then
1<(h-l)2^{m}<2
and there may be one or two integers in
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
. Further,

–

if there are two integers in
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
, they must be
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
and
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1
; and

–

if there is only one integer in
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
, it must be
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
.

According to the above properties, the integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
always belongs to
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
, while the integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1
may or may not belong to
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
. The integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
in the normalized interval
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
corresponds to the real number
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil 2^{-m}
in the original interval
[l,h)
. By binarizing
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
into
m
bits, we obtain a bitstream of
x^{n}
. If the integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1
belongs to
[l2^{m},h2^{m})
, the corresponding real number
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)2^{-m}
must also belong to
[l,h)
. By binarizing
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1
into
m
bits, we obtain another bitstream of
x^{n}
.

Definition 1.3.3

(Raw Arithmetic Bitstream)
.

Let
[l,h)\subset[0,1)
be the mapping interval of
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. Let
m=-\lfloor\log_{2}{(h-l)}\rfloor
. Apparently,
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil 2^{-m}\in[l,h)
holds always, but
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)2^{-m}\in[l,h)
may or may not hold. The integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
can be represented by a string of
m
bits, which is called a raw arithmetic bitstream of
x^{n}
. If
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)2^{-m}\in[l,h)
, the string of
m
bits representing the integer
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1
is also a raw arithmetic bitstream of
x^{n}
.

According to the definition, for
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, if
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)2^{-m}\notin[l,h)
, there is only one raw arithmetic bitstream; if
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)2^{-m}\in[l,h)
, there are two raw arithmetic bitstreams.

1.5
Prefix-Code Issue

Even though the finite-precision issue has been perfectly solved, there is still another issue. Actually, when we decode a raw arithmetic bitstream, we implicitly assume that it is followed by an all-zero bitstring. However in practice, a raw arithmetic bitstream may be followed by arbitrary bitstrings, and some special following bitstrings may cause decoding failure. For conciseness, we refer to the string of following bits as
tail
. Let us define different kinds of bitstreams.

Definition 1.5.1

(Prefix Bitstream, Zero-tail Bitstream, and Half-tail Bitstream)
.

Let
[l,h)\subset[0,1)
be the mapping interval of
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, and the arithmetic bitstream of
x^{n}
is denoted by
z^{m}\triangleq(z_{1}\cdots z_{m})
.

•

If
(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m}z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}\in[l,h)
holds for every tail
(.z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}\in[0,1)
, we say that
z^{m}
is a prefix bitstream of
x^{n}
.

•

If
(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m}z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}\in[l,h)
holds for the zero tail
(.z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}=(.00\cdots)_{b}=0
but not for all tails, we say that
z^{m}
is a zero-tail bitstream of
x^{n}
.

•

If
(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m}z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}\in[l,h)
holds for the half tail
(.z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}=(.100\cdots)_{b}=(.011\cdots)_{b}=0.5
but not for all tails, we say that
z^{m}
is a half-tail bitstream of
x^{n}
.

If
z^{m}
is a prefix bitstream of
x^{n}
, then
x^{n}
can always be correctly decoded, no matter what tail
(z_{m+1}\cdots)
follows
z^{m}
. On the contrary, if
z^{m}
is a zero-tail or half-tail bitstream of
x^{n}
, there will be a risk of decoding failure for those tails causing
(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m}z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}\notin[l,h)
. Now we wonder whether raw arithmetic bitstreams are prefix bitstreams.

Theorem 1.5.2

(Raw Arithmetic Bitstream)
.

Let
[l,h)\subset[0,1)
be the mapping interval of
x^{n}
and
m\triangleq-\lfloor\log_{2}{(h-l)}\rfloor
. If
\frac{\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1}{2^{m}}\geq h
, there is only one raw arithmetic bitstream, which is a zero-tail bitstream representing
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
; if
\frac{\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1}{2^{m}}<h
, there are two raw arithmetic bitstreams, among which the raw arithmetic bitstream representing
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
is a prefix bitstream, while the raw arithmetic bitstream representing
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)
is a zero-tail bitstream.

Proof 1.5.3
.

Since
l2^{m}\leq\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil<l2^{m}+1
, we have
l\leq\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil 2^{-m}<l+2^{-m}
. Since
2^{-m}=2^{\lfloor\log_{2}{(h-l)}\rfloor}\leq 2^{\log_{2}{(h-l)}}=(h-l)
, we have

. Since
l\leq\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil 2^{-m}<h
, the string of
m
bits for
\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil
is always a raw arithmetic bitstream of
x^{n}
.

Then we proceed to the string of
m
bits representing
(\lceil{l2^{m}}\rceil+1)
. Since
2^{-m}=2^{\lfloor\log_{2}{(h-l)}\rfloor}>2^{\log_{2}{(h-l)}-1}=(h-l)/2
, we have

. In a word,
l+2^{-m}\leq h<l+2^{1-m}
. As we know,

\displaystyle 0\leq(.\underbrace{0\cdots 0}_{m}z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}<2^{-m},

we have

\displaystyle(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m})_{b}\leq(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m}z_{m+1}\cdots)_{b}<(.z_{1}\cdots z_{m})_{b}+2^{-m},

where

•

If

1.7
Arrangement of Remaining Sections

DSC is an important branch of network information theory featured by separate encoding and joint decoding. Compared with conventional or centralized source coding, DSC has mainly two advantages: (a) The heavy computation burden is shifted from encoder to decoder, making DSC suited to those scenarios requiring lightweight terminals,
e.g.
, wireless sensor network
[
36
]
; (b) The flow of data across terminals is avoided for privacy protection, making DSC suited to those scenarios where the data to be compressed are highly confidential,
e.g.
, distributed genome data compression
[
33
]
.

In essence, lossless DSC, also known as Slepian-Wolf coding, is equivalent to channel coding
[
5
,
6
]
and thus naturally implemented with channel codes
[
28
]
,
e.g.
, turbo codes
[
19
]
, LDPC codes
[
26
]
, and polar codes
[
3
]
. However, channel codes are originally designed for the purpose of error correction, so may not be well suited to data compression for several reasons: (a) Most channel codes are binary codes, while real-world sources,
e.g.
, images and videos, are typically nonbinary; (b) The statistical features of real-world sources are usually time-varying, while it is difficult for block channel codes to adapt to nonstationary source characteristics.

As the most famous method for lossless source coding, arithmetic codes can also be adapted to other coding problems by simply redefining symbol-interval mapping rules. For example, Slepian-Wolf coding can be implemented with overlapped arithmetic codes, a variant of arithmetic codes mapping source symbols to partially overlapped intervals. The following sections of this monograph will review recent advances in
overlapped arithmetic codes
during the past decade, while leaving the discussion on
forbidden arithmetic codes
and
hybrid arithmetic codes
as future work. Besides overlapped arithmetic codes, overlapped

for the same purpose. In addition, there is another similar scheme that makes use of arithmetic codes to implement Slepian-Wolf coding by bit puncturing rather than interval overlapping, which is named as
punctured arithmetic codes

[
27
]
. This monograph will discuss only overlapped arithmetic codes with half-tail bitstreams, while ignoring other similar works. Finally, we would like to remind the reader of that for clarity, this monograph treats with only uniformly distributed binary sources. As for the works on non-uniformly distributed binary sources or non-binary sources, the reader may refer to the references recommended in
Section

Chapter 2
Coset Cardinality Spectrum

Note that this and the following sections will treat with only uniformly distributed binary sources for clarity and conciseness.

This section will utilize probability theory to build a mathematical model for overlapped arithmetic codes. It will be found that overlapped arithmetic codes are actually a kind of nonlinear coset codes partitioning source space into unequal-sized cosets. Hence the distribution of coset cardinality, or the so-called
Coset Cardinality Spectrum
(CCS), is a fundamental property of overlapped arithmetic codes. This section will give methods to calculate the CCS of overlapped arithmetic codes theoretically or numerically. On the basis of CCS, this section will also derive the decoding algorithm of overlapped arithmetic codes. Experimental results will show that aided by CCS, the performance of low-complexity decoder can be significantly improved.

2.1
Math Foundation

To begin with, let us introduce an important theorem, which will lay the math foundation for the rest of this monograph.

Definition 2.1.1

(Counting Function)
.

Let
\wedge
denote boolean AND. Let
\omega=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of real numbers. The counting function is defined as the number of terms
a_{i}
’s, where
1\leq i\leq n
, for which
\{a_{i}\}\triangleq a_{i}-\lfloor{a_{i}}\rfloor\in{\cal I}\subseteq[0,1)
. In mathematical language,

\displaystyle c({\cal I};n;\omega)\triangleq|\{a_{i}:(\{a_{i}\}\in{\cal I})\wedge(1\leq i\leq n)\}|.

(2.1)

With the help of counting function, we introduce the following concept (cf.
Definition 1.1
of
[
24
]
).

Definition 2.1.2

(u.d. mod 1 Sequence)
.

The sequence
\omega=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
is uniformly distributed modulo 1 (u.d. mod 1) if for every pair of

and

with

, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c([l,h);n;\omega)}{n}=h-l.

(2.2)

An interesting finding is that almost every geometric sequence is a u.d. mod 1 sequence, as stated by the following lemma (cf.
Corollary 4.1
and
Corollary 4.2
of
[
24
]
).

Lemma 2.1.3

(Property of Geometric Sequence)
.

The geometric sequence
(a,ar,ar^{2},\dots)
, where
a\neq 0
, is u.d. mod 1 for almost every non-integral
r>1
, and the exceptional set has Lebesgue measure zero.

Lemma 2.1.4

(u.d. mod 1 Sequence)
.

Let
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)
be a u.d. mod 1 sequence and
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) discrete random variables. Let

, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part of a real number. Then
E_{n}
will be uniformly distributed (u.d.) over
[0,1)
as

.

Theorem 2.1.5

(Weighted Sum of Geometric Sequence)
.

Let
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
be a sequence of i.i.d. discrete random variables. Let
E_{n}\triangleq\{aX_{1}+arX_{2}+\cdots+ar^{n-1}X_{n}\}
, where
a\neq 0
and
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part of a real number. Then for almost every non-integral
r>1
,
E_{n}
will be u.d. over
[0,1)
as
n\to\infty
.

2.2
Asymptotic Spectrum

We begin with infinite-length overlapped arithmetic codes. Let
X
be a binary random variable with bias probability
\Pr(X=1)=1/2
and
X^{n}=X_{1}^{n}\triangleq(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be
n
independent copies of
X
. The encoder recursively shrinks the initial interval
[0,1)
according to
X^{n}
. Let
[l(X^{i}),h(X^{i}))
be the mapping interval of
X^{i}
, and initially,
[l(X^{i}),h(X^{i}))=[0,1)
. According to
Fig.

1.3(b)
, for a rate-
r
overlapped arithmetic code, we have
h(X^{i})-l(X^{i})=2^{-ir}
and

\displaystyle l(X^{i})

\displaystyle=l(X^{i-1})+X_{i}\cdot(1-2^{-r})\cdot\left(h(X^{i-1})-l(X^{i-1})\right)

\displaystyle=l(X^{i-1})+X_{i}\cdot(1-2^{-r})\cdot 2^{-(i-1)r}.

(2.3)

After rearrangement, we obtain

\displaystyle l(X^{i})

\displaystyle=(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}{\left(X_{i^{\prime}}\cdot 2^{-(i^{\prime}-1)r}\right)}

\displaystyle=(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}{\left(X_{i^{\prime}}\cdot 2^{-i^{\prime}r}\right)}.

(2.4)

For
r>0
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{(h(X^{n})-l(X^{n}))}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{2^{-nr}}=0.

(2.5)

In other word, as
n
increases, the mapping interval of
X^{n}
will converge to a real number
U
in
[0,1)
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}l(X^{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}h(X^{n})

\displaystyle=(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{X_{i}2^{-ir}}.

(2.6)

A natural problem is: How is the random variable
U
distributed over
[0,1)
? Let
f(u)
be the
probability density function
(pdf) of
U
. We temporarily name
f(u)
as the
Asymptotic Spectrum
of overlapping arithmetic codes. According to the properties of pdf, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}f(u)\geq 0,&0\leq u<1\\
f(u)=0,&u\notin[0,1)\end{cases}

(2.7)

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{f(u)\,du}=\int_{0}^{1}{f(u)\,du}=1.

(2.8)

Theorem 2.2.1

(Implicit Form of Asymptotic Spectrum)
.

For a rate-
r
overlapped arithmetic code defined by
Fig.

1.3(b)
, its asymptotic spectrum, denoted as

, is given by

\displaystyle 2^{1-r}f(u)

\displaystyle=f(u2^{r})+f((u-(1-2^{-r}))2^{r})

(2.9)

\displaystyle=f(u2^{r})+f(1-(1-u)2^{r}).

(2.10)

(a)

q=1/\sqrt{2}

(b)

1/2<q\leq(\sqrt{5}-1)/2

(c)

(\sqrt{5}-1)/2<q<1/\sqrt{2}

(d)

q>1/\sqrt{2}

Proof 2.2.2
.

If
X_{1}=0
, then

will be mapped to a real number in

will be mapped to a real number in

be the conditional pdf of
U
given

. According to

\displaystyle f(u)

\displaystyle=\Pr(X_{1}=0)f_{0}(u)+\Pr(X_{1}=1)f_{1}(u)

\displaystyle=(f_{0}(u)+f_{1}(u))/2.

Since both

,

is defined over

\displaystyle f_{0}(u)=\frac{f(u2^{r})}{\int_{0}^{2^{-r}}{f(v2^{r})dv}}=2^{r}f(u2^{r}).

(2.12)

Similarly, we have
f_{1}(u)=f_{0}(u-(1-q))=f_{0}(u-(1-2^{-r}))
and thus

\displaystyle f_{1}(u)=2^{r}f((u-(1-2^{-r}))2^{r}).

(2.13)

Finally, substituting (

\displaystyle 2^{1-r}f(u)=\begin{cases}f(u2^{r}),&0\leq u<(1-2^{-r})\\

goes to

\displaystyle\mathrm{sinc}(\omega)=\frac{\sin(\omega)}{\omega}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}{\cos(\omega/2^{k})}.

From

2.3
Source Space Partitioning

Now we proceed to finite-length overlapped arithmetic codes. Still consider uniform binary sources. For simplicity, we assume
nr\in\mathbb{Z}
below. To generate the bitstream, the final mapping interval
[l(X^{n}),h(X^{n}))
is enlarged by
2^{nr}
times to obtain a normalized interval
[s(x^{n}),s(x^{n})+1)
with unit length, where

\displaystyle s(x^{n})

\displaystyle\triangleq 2^{nr}l(X^{n})=(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}2^{(n-i)r}}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}2^{ir}},

(2.21)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes two random variables equal in distribution. Clearly, there is one and only one integer in
[s(x^{n}),s(x^{n})+1)
, which is just

\displaystyle M=m(X^{n})\triangleq\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil.

(2.22)

Obviously,
0\leq s(x^{n})\leq(2^{nr}-1)
and
M
is drawn from
[0:2^{nr})\triangleq\{0,1,\dots,(2^{nr}-1)\}
, so
M
can be represented by
nr\leq n
bits, which is just the raw arithmetic bitstream of
X^{n}
. In this sense, overlapped arithmetic codes realize a many-to-one nonlinear mapping from
\mathbb{B}^{n}
to
[0:2^{nr})
or
\mathbb{B}^{nr}
, which partitions source space
\mathbb{B}^{n}
into
2^{nr}
cosets, and
M
is just the index of the coset containing
X^{n}
. Note that source space partitioning by overlapped arithmetic codes differs from source space partitioning by linear block codes in that the former results in unequal-sized cosets, while the latter results in equal-sized cosets.

As an analogy, each source block
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
can be taken as a ball and each coset can be taken as a bin. Clearly, source space partitioning is just a procedure of ball binning. Let us give a simple example to demonstrate this analog. Assume
n=4
and
r=0.5
. For every
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, we calculate
s(x^{n})
according to (
2.21
). The location of
s(x^{n})
along the real number axis is illustrated by
Fig.

2.2
. As shown by
Fig.

2.2
, all source blocks (balls) falling into the interval
(m-1,m]
will be classified into the
m
-th coset/bin
{\cal C}_{m}
. The codewords in each coset are structured into an incomplete binary tree, as shown by
Fig.

2.2
.

and

. (a) Mapping from

2.4
Definition of CCS

As we know, linear block codes always partition source space into equal-sized cosets so that the distribution of coset cardinality is never a problem. On the contrary, as shown by
Fig.

2.2
, overlapped arithmetic codes partition source space into unequal-sized cosets, so the distribution of coset cardinality is an important problem. Let
|{\cal C}_{m}|
be the cardinality of
{\cal C}_{m}
, where
0\leq m<2^{nr}
. For example, in

2.2

|{\cal C}_{0}|=1
,
|{\cal C}_{1}|=|{\cal C}_{3}|=4
, and
|{\cal C}_{2}|=7
. It is very difficult to deduce the distribution of

for finite

is a discrete random variable. To solve this problem, we introduce the following concept.

Definition 2.4.1

(Bitstream Projection)
.

Let
M=m(X^{n})=\lceil s(X^{n})\rceil\sim p(m)
for
m\in[0:2^{nr})
, where
s(X^{n})
is defined by (
2.21
), be the bitstream of a length-
n
and rate-
r
overlapped arithmetic code. The projection of
M
onto the interval

) and

\displaystyle U_{i,n}\triangleq\frac{\overbrace{2^{-nr}M}^{U_{0,n}}-l(X^{i})}{\underbrace{h(X^{i})-l(X^{i})}_{2^{-ir}}}=2^{ir}\left(U_{0,n}-l(X^{i})\right).

(2.23)

Evidently,

is defined over
[0,1)
for every

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}{U_{0,n}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{l(X^{n})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}{h(X^{n})}

\displaystyle=(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{X_{i}2^{-ir}}.

(2.24)

For conciseness,
U_{i,n}
can be shortened to

without causing ambiguity. Especially,

is called the
initial
projection and
U_{n}
is called the
final
projection. It is easy to obtain

\displaystyle U_{n}=M-2^{nr}l(X^{n})=\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil-s(x^{n}),

showing that

is the ceiling error of

. From (

\displaystyle U_{0}

\displaystyle=2^{-ir}U_{i}+l(X^{i})=2^{-(i+1)r}U_{i+1}+l(X^{i+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}2^{-(i+1)r}U_{i+1}+l(X^{i})+X_{i+1}(1-2^{-r})2^{-ir},

(2.26)

where

\displaystyle U_{i-1}=2^{-r}U_{i}+X_{i}(1-2^{-r}),

\displaystyle U_{i}=2^{r}\left(U_{i-1}-X_{i}(1-2^{-r})\right).

If

is decoded along the path

Theorem 2.4.2

(Markov Chain)
.

The sequence
(U_{0},\dots,U_{n})
forms a Markov chain, i.e.,

. The sequence
(U_{0},\dots,U_{i-1},X_{i})

. The relation between
U_{i-1}

, and

can be summarized by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{i-1}=2^{-r}U_{i}+X_{i}(1-2^{-r}),\\
U_{i}=2^{r}\left(U_{i-1}-X_{i}(1-2^{-r})\right).\end{cases}

(2.27)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ccccccccccc}U_{0}&\leftarrow\cdots\leftarrow&U_{i-1}&\leftarrow&U_{i}&\leftarrow&U_{i+1}&\leftarrow\cdots\leftarrow&U_{n-1}&\leftarrow&U_{n}\\
\uparrow&\cdots&\uparrow&&\uparrow&&\uparrow&\cdots&\uparrow\\
X_{1}&\cdots&X_{i}&&X_{i+1}&&X_{i+2}&\cdots&X_{n}\\
\end{array}

We define

as (
2.4
). Let

To deduce

X_{j}=x\in\mathbb{B}
is called the conditional CCS and denoted by
f_{i|j,n}(u|x)
, or just
f_{i|j}(u|x)
for conciseness.

Lemma 2.4.7

(Conditional CCS)
.

The conditional CCS is given by
[
16
]

\displaystyle f_{i-1|i}(u|x)=2^{r}f_{i}((u-x(1-2^{-r}))2^{r}).

(2.33)

Theorem 2.4.8

(Backward Deduction of CCS)
.

The level-
i
CCS
f_{i,n}(u)
can be deduced via the following backward recursion

\displaystyle f_{i-1}(u)

\displaystyle=\Pr(X_{i}=0)f_{i-1|i}(u|0)+\Pr(X_{i}=1)f_{i-1|i}(u|1)

\displaystyle=2^{r-1}\left(f_{i}(u2^{r})+f_{i}((u-(1-2^{-r}))2^{r})\right).

(2.34)

Corollary 2.4.9

(Conditional Probability of Source Symbol Given CCS)
.

Let
p_{j|i}(x|u)\triangleq\Pr(X_{j}=x|U_{i}=u)
. Then we have

\displaystyle p_{i|i-1}(x|u)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Pr(X_{i}=x)f_{i-1|i}(u|x)}{f_{i-1}(u)}=\frac{f_{i-1|i}(u|x)}{2f_{i-1}(u)}

\displaystyle=2^{r-1}\frac{f_{i}((u-x(1-2^{-r}))2^{r})}{f_{i-1}(u)}.

(2.35)

2.4.8

2.5
Numerical Algorithm of CCS

For conciseness,
f_{i,n}(u)
will be abbreviated to
f_{i}(u)
below. It is usually very hard to deduce the exact closed form of
f_{i}(u)
according to (
2.34
), so we have to resort to a numerical algorithm.

2.5.1
Rounding Numerical Algorithm

Let us discretize the interval
[0,1)
into
N
segments of equal length. We take
\hat{f}_{i}(j)
as the approximation of
f_{i}(j/N)
. Initially,
\hat{f}_{n}(j)=1
for every
j\in[0:N)
(cf.
Theorem

2.4.5
). Let us define

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\lambda_{0}\triangleq j2^{r}\\
\lambda_{1}\triangleq(j-N(1-2^{-r}))2^{r}.\end{cases}

(2.39)

Let
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
denote the rounding function. Then (
2.34
) can be discretized by

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i-1}(j)=2^{r-1}\left(\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\lambda_{0}\rceil)+\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\lambda_{1}\rceil)\right).

(2.40)

Sometimes we have
\lfloor\lambda_{0}\rceil\notin[0:N)
or
\lfloor\lambda_{1}\rceil\notin[0:N)
. It does not matter because
\hat{f}_{i}(j)\equiv 0
for any
j\notin[0:N)
according to the definition of CCS. Since the core of (
2.40
) is the rounding function, this method is referred to as
rounding
numerical algorithm.

2.5.2
Linear Numerical Algorithm

Let
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
and
\lceil\cdot\rceil
denote the floor and ceiling functions, respectively. The rounding numerical algorithm (
2.40
) can be slightly improved by

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i-1}(j)=2^{r-1}\left(g_{0}(\lambda_{0})+g_{1}(\lambda_{1})\right),

(2.41)

where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}g_{0}(\lambda_{0})=(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-\lambda_{0})\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\lambda_{0}\rfloor)+(\lambda_{0}-\lfloor\lambda_{0}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil)\\
g_{1}(\lambda_{1})=(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil-\lambda_{1})\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\lambda_{1}\rfloor)+(\lambda_{1}-\lfloor\lambda_{1}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil).\end{cases}

(2.42)

Especially, if
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
g_{0}(\lambda_{0})=\hat{f}_{i}(\lambda_{0})
; if
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
g_{1}(\lambda_{1})=\hat{f}_{i}(\lambda_{1})
. Since (
2.42
) is similar to the linear interpolation formula, (
2.41
) is named as
linear
numerical algorithm.

2.5.3
Fine Numerical Algorithm

It must be pointed out that for both rounding and linear numerical algorithms, a renormalization step is needed after (
2.40
) and (
2.41
):

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}(j)=\left.\hat{f}_{i}(j)\middle/\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{N-1}\hat{f}_{i}(j^{\prime})\right..

(2.43)

To remove this renormalization step, the
fine
numerical algorithm was proposed in
[
8
]
. Now let
\hat{f}_{i}(j)
be an approximation of the scaled-up probability of
U_{i}
falling into the interval
[j/N,(j+1)/N))
,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i}(j)\approx N\int_{j/N}^{(j+1)/N}f_{i}(u)\,du.

(2.44)

According to (
2.34
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{j/N}^{(j+1)/N}{f_{i-1}(u)\,du}=\;

\displaystyle 2^{r-1}\int_{j/N}^{(j+1)/N}{\left(f_{i}(u2^{r})+\right.}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\;\;{\left.f_{i}((u-(1-2^{-r}))2^{r})\right)\,du}.

(2.45)

Besides
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
defined by (
2.39
), we also define

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\eta_{0}\triangleq(j+1)2^{r}=\lambda_{0}+2^{r}\\
\eta_{1}\triangleq(j+1-N(1-2^{-r}))2^{r}=\lambda_{1}+2^{r}.\end{cases}

(2.46)

Let
v\triangleq u2^{r}
and
w\triangleq(u-(1-2^{-r}))2^{r}
. Then (
2.45
) will become

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{j}{N}}^{\frac{j+1}{N}}{f_{i-1}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\frac{\lambda_{0}}{N}}^{\frac{\eta_{0}}{N}}{f_{i}(v)\,d{v}}+\int_{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{N}}^{\frac{\eta_{1}}{N}}{f_{i}(w)\,d{w}}\right)/2

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\frac{\lambda_{0}}{N}}^{\frac{\eta_{0}}{N}}{f_{i}(u)\,d{u}}+\int_{\frac{\lambda_{1}}{N}}^{\frac{\eta_{1}}{N}}{f_{i}(u)\,d{u}}\right)/2,

(2.47)

which can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{i-1}(j)=\left(g_{0}(j)+g_{1}(j)\right)/2,

(2.48)

where
\hat{f}_{i-1}(j)
is defined by (
2.44
) and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}g_{0}(j)\approx N\int_{\lambda_{0}/N}^{\eta_{0}/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}\\
g_{1}(j)\approx N\int_{\lambda_{1}/N}^{\eta_{1}/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}.\end{cases}

(2.49)

In plain words,
g_{0}(j)
is an approximation of the scaled-up probability of
U_{i}
falling into
[\lambda_{0}/N,\eta_{0}/N)
and
g_{1}(j)
is an approximation of the scaled-up probability of
U_{i}
falling into
[\lambda_{1}/N,\eta_{1}/N)
.

For
0<r<1
, we have
1<2^{r}<2
. Hence,
\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor-\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil=0
or
1
. It is easy to obtain

\displaystyle g_{0}(j)=\;

\displaystyle N\int_{\lambda_{0}/N}^{\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}+N\int_{\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil/N}^{\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}\;+

\displaystyle N\int_{\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor/N}^{\eta_{0}/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}.

(2.50)

For
N
sufficiently large,
f_{i}(u)
will be approximately uniform over the interval
[j/N,(j+1)/N)
. Hence we have

\displaystyle N\int_{\lambda_{0}/N}^{\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle\approx(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-\lambda_{0})N\int_{(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-1)/N}^{\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle\approx(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-\lambda_{0})\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-1)

(2.51)

and

\displaystyle N\int_{\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor/N}^{\eta_{0}/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle\approx(\eta_{0}-\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor)N\int_{\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor/N}^{(\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor+1)/N}{f_{i}(u)\,du}

\displaystyle\approx(\eta_{0}-\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor).

(2.52)

According to the above analysis, we can obtain

\displaystyle g_{0}(j)=\;

\displaystyle(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-\lambda_{0})\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-1)+(\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor-\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil)\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil)\;+

\displaystyle(\eta_{0}-\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor).

(2.53)

Especially, if
\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor=\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil
,

\displaystyle g_{0}(j)=(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-\lambda_{0})\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{0}\rceil-1)+(\eta_{0}-\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\eta_{0}\rfloor).

(2.54)

Similarly,

\displaystyle g_{1}(j)=\;

\displaystyle(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil-\lambda_{1})\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil-1)+(\lfloor\eta_{1}\rfloor-\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil)\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil)\;+

\displaystyle(\eta_{1}-\lfloor\eta_{1}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\eta_{1}\rfloor),

(2.55)

and especially, if
\lfloor\eta_{1}\rfloor=\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil
,

\displaystyle g_{1}(j)=(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil-\lambda_{1})\hat{f}_{i}(\lceil\lambda_{1}\rceil-1)+(\eta_{1}-\lfloor\eta_{1}\rfloor)\hat{f}_{i}(\lfloor\eta_{1}\rfloor).

(2.56)

2.6
Information Theoretic Understanding

With the asymptotic initial CCS
f(u)
, which is given by (
2.9
), we can have a better understanding of overlapped arithmetic codes.

2.6.1
Expected Coset Cardinality

Let
M=\lceil s(X^{n})\rceil\sim p(m)
, where
m\in[0:2^{nr})
, be the coset index of
X^{n}
.
Let
{\cal C}_{m}
be the
m
-th coset, whose cardinality is denoted by
|{\cal C}_{m}|
. Since
\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{|{\cal C}_{m}|}=|\mathbb{B}^{n}|=2^{n}
and
U_{0,n}=2^{-nr}M
, where
U_{0,n}
is the initial bitstream projection, we have

\displaystyle f_{0,n}(u)=2^{-n}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}|{\cal C}_{m}|\cdot\delta(u-m2^{-nr}),

which is followed by

\displaystyle|{\cal C}_{m}|=2^{n}\int_{(m2^{-nr})_{-}}^{((m+1)2^{-nr})_{-}}f_{0,n}(u)\,du=2^{n}\int_{(m2^{-nr})_{-}}^{(m2^{-nr})_{+}}f_{0,n}(u)\,du.

For
n
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle 2^{n}\int_{(m2^{-nr})_{-}}^{((m+1)2^{-nr})_{-}}f_{0,n}(u)\,du

\displaystyle\approx 2^{n}\int_{m2^{-nr}}^{(m+1)2^{-nr}}f(u)\,du

\displaystyle\approx 2^{n}\cdot f(m2^{-nr})\cdot 2^{-nr},

where
f(u)
is the asymptotic initial CCS defined by (
2.9
), and thus

\displaystyle|{\cal C}_{m}|\approx f(m2^{-nr})\cdot 2^{n(1-r)}.

(2.57)

The probability of
{\cal C}_{m}
is

\displaystyle p(m)=\Pr(X^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m})=\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{p(x^{n})}\overset{(a)}{=}|{\cal C}_{m}|\cdot 2^{-n},

where
(a)
comes from
p(x^{n})\equiv 2^{-n}
for uniform binary sources. From (
2.57
), for
n
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle p(m)\approx f(m2^{-nr})\cdot 2^{-nr}.

(2.58)

Though the accurate value of
p(m)
changes with
n
and thus is hard to find, it can be well approximated by
f(m2^{-nr})2^{-nr}
for large
n
.

Definition 2.6.1

(Expected Coset Cardinality (ECC))
.

We refer to
\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]
, the expectation of
|{\cal C}_{M}|
, as the ECC.

It is easy to find that the ECC is actually equal to the expected number of codewords searched by a full-search decoder.
According to the definition of ECC, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]=\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{p(m)\cdot|{\cal C}_{m}|}.

From (
2.57
) and (
2.58
), for
n
sufficiently large, we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]

\displaystyle\approx 2^{n(1-r)}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{f^{2}(m2^{-nr})\cdot 2^{-nr}}

\displaystyle\approx 2^{n(1-r)}\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}.

(2.59)

Given
f(u)\geq 0
and
\int_{0}^{1}{f(u)\,du}=1
, we have
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}\geq 1
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]\geq 2^{n(1-r)}.

This point can be explained in an intuitive way: The cosets of larger cardinalities are more likely to appear and further, more codewords must be searched for those cosets of larger cardinalities, so the complexity of full-search decoder should be scaled by
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du}\geq 1
.

Definition 2.6.2

(Normalized Expected Coset Cardinality)
.

We refer to
2^{-n(1-r)}\cdot\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]
as the normalized ECC.

According to (
2.59
), it is easy to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]}{2^{n(1-r)}}=\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}\geq 1.

(2.60)

For
r=1
, we have
f(u)=\Pi(u)
, where
\Pi(u)
is defined by (
2.32
), and
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}=1
.
As
r\to 0
,
f(u)
will converge to
\delta(u-1/2)
, where
\delta(u)
is the Dirac delta function, and
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}\to\infty
. In a word, as
r
decreases from
1
to
0
, the normalized ECC will increase from
1
to
\infty
.

2.6.2
Block-wise Rate Loss

As we know, the asymptotic initial spectrum
f(u)
is not uniform over
[0,1)
for
r<1
, hence the coset index
M
is not uniformly distributed over
[0:2^{nr})
, which implies a possible rate loss.

(a)

H(X^{n})=n

(b)

H(X_{i})=1

Figure 2.5:
(a) The entropy of
X^{n}
is
n
. Though
X^{n}
is encoded at rate
r
, the information amount of
X^{n}
provided by
M
is smaller than
nr
and the remaining uncertainty of
X^{n}
is higher than
n(1-r)
. The block-wise rate loss will converge to
-h(U)
as
n\to\infty
. (b) The entropy of
X_{i}
is
1
. Though
X_{i}
is encoded at rate
r
, the information amount of
X_{i}
provided by
M
is smaller than
r
and the remaining uncertainty of
X_{i}
is larger than
(1-r)
. The symbol-wise rate loss is
(h(U_{i})-h(U_{i-1}))
.

Since
U_{0}=2^{-nr}M
is a bijective function, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}H(X^{n}|M)=H(X^{n}|U_{0})\\
I(X^{n};M)=I(X^{n};U_{0})=I(M;U_{0})=H(M)\end{cases}

(2.61)

and

\displaystyle H(X^{n})

\displaystyle=H(M)+H(X^{n}|M)

\displaystyle=I(X^{n};M)+H(X^{n}|M)

\displaystyle=I(X^{n};U_{0})+H(X^{n}|U_{0})=n,

(2.62)

just as illustrated by
Fig.

2.5(a)
.

Theorem 2.6.3

(Block-wise Rate Loss)
.

The rate loss of coding
X^{n}
with a rate-
r
overlapped arithmetic code is

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(nr-H(M))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}(H(X^{n}|M)-n(1-r))

\displaystyle=-h(U),

(2.63)

where
U
is the asymptotic initial projection defined by (
2.6
).

Proof 2.6.4
.

The key is to compute the entropy of
M
:

\displaystyle H(M)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{p(m)\log_{2}{p(m)}}

\displaystyle\to-\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{p(m)\log_{2}{(f(m2^{-nr})2^{-nr})}}

\displaystyle=nr-\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{f(m2^{-nr})\log_{2}{f(m2^{-nr})}\cdot 2^{-nr}}.

(2.64)

Since
H(X^{n})=H(M)+H(X^{n}|M)=n
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(H(X^{n}|M)-n(1-r))

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{(nr-H(M))}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}{f(u)\log_{2}{f(u)}\,du}=-h(U).

(2.65)

Because
m\in[0:2^{nr})
, we have
H(M)\leq nr
and the equality holds iff
M
is uniformly distributed over
[0:2^{nr})
. As
n\to\infty
, the block rate loss will tend to a constant
-h(U)
, where
U
is the asymptotic initial bitstream projection defined by (
2.6
). As we know,
h(U)\leq 0
, where the equality holds iff
f(u)
is uniform over
[0,1)
. The rate loss per source symbol is
\frac{nr-H(M)}{n}
. For
r>0
, we have
h(U)>-\infty
and
\frac{nr-H(M)}{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, so the rate loss per source symbol will vanish as code length increases. As for
r=0
, we have
f(u)=\delta(u-1/2)
and
h(U)=-\infty
, so the rate loss per source symbol is unknown.

2.6.3
Symbol-wise Rate Loss

Now we consider the symbol-wise rate loss of overlapped arithmetic codes. As shown by
Theorem

2.4.2
,
U_{0}\to\dots\to U_{i-1}\to X_{i}
, so

\displaystyle\begin{cases}H(X_{i}|M)=H(X_{i}|U_{0})=H(X_{i}|U_{i-1})\\
I(X_{i};M)=I(X_{i};U_{0})=I(X_{i};U_{i-1})\end{cases}

(2.66)

and

\displaystyle H(X_{i})

\displaystyle=I(X_{i};M)+H(X_{i}|M)

\displaystyle=I(X_{i};U_{0})+H(X_{i}|U_{0})

\displaystyle=I(X_{i};U_{i-1})+H(X_{i}|U_{i-1})=1,

(2.67)

as illustrated by
Fig.

2.5(b)
. By the chain rule, we can get

\displaystyle H(X^{n}|U_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{H(X_{i}|U_{0},X^{i-1})}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{H(X_{i}|U_{0})}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{H(X_{i}|M)},

(2.68)

where
(a)
is because
X^{n}
is an i.i.d. random process. Therefore,

\displaystyle H(X^{n}|M)=H(X^{n}|U_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{H(X_{i}|M)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{H(X_{i}|U_{i-1})}.

(2.69)

Theorem 2.6.5

(Symbol-wise Rate Loss)
.

For a rate-
r
overlapped arithmetic code, as
n\to\infty
, the rate loss of coding
X_{i}
is

\displaystyle r-I(X_{i};M)

\displaystyle=H(X_{i}|M)-(1-r)

\displaystyle=H(X_{i}|U_{i-1})-(1-r)

\displaystyle=h(U_{i})-h(U_{i-1}).

(2.70)

Proof 2.6.6
.

According to (
2.33
) and the properties of differential entropy,
h(U_{i-1}|X_{i})=h(U_{i})-r
as
n\to\infty
. Hence,

\displaystyle H(X_{i}|U_{i-1})

\displaystyle=H(X_{i})-I(X_{i};U_{i-1})

\displaystyle=1-(h(U_{i-1})-h(U_{i-1}|X_{i}))

\displaystyle=1-h(U_{i-1})+h(U_{i})-r

\displaystyle=(1-r)+h(U_{i})-h(U_{i-1}).

(2.71)

Since
X_{i}
is coded at rate
r
, the rate loss is
(h(U_{i})-h(U_{i-1}))
.

Corollary 2.6.7
.

The sequence
(h(U_{0}),\dots,h(U_{n}))
is a monotonously increasing sequence, i.e.,
h(U_{0})\leq\cdots\leq h(U_{n})\to 0
, where
h(U_{n})\to 0
is because
f_{n}(u)
tends to be uniform over
[0,1)
as
n\to\infty
.

Remark 2.6.8

(A Neat Proof of
Theorem

2.6.3
)
.

According to
Theorem

2.6.5
, we can obtain a neat proof of
Theorem

2.6.3
. The block rate loss is equal to the sum of symbol losses, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(h(U_{i})-h(U_{i-1}))=h(U_{n})-h(U_{0})\overset{(a)}{\to}-h(U),

(2.72)

where
(a)
is because
h(U_{n})\to 0
and
U_{0}\to U
as
n\to\infty
.

As
(n-i)\rightarrow\infty
, both
f_{i-1,n}(u)
and
f_{i,n}(u)
will converge to
f(u)
and thus
(h(U_{i-1,n})-h(U_{i,n}))\to 0
. Hence, there is hardly any symbol-wise rate loss for those symbols far away from the end of a block, and the block-wise rate loss comes mainly from ending symbols of a block.

Remark 2.6.9

(An Intuitive Explanation of Rate Loss)
.

The rate loss of overlapped arithmetic codes can be explained intuitively with an example. We first compress
X^{n}
with a rate-
r
overlapped arithmetic encoder to obtain bitstream
M
and then compress
X^{n}
with a rate-
1
standard arithmetic encoder parameterized with the probability sequence
(p_{1|0}(x|u),\dots,p_{n|n-1}(x|u))
to obtain another bitstream
\hat{M}
. Obviously, the length of
M
is always
nr
and the expected length of
\hat{M}
is about
n(1-r)-h(U_{0})
. Thus, the total length of
M
and
\hat{M}
is
n-h(U_{0})\geq n
. It is easy to see that the error-free recovery of
X^{n}
is achievable by the interaction between the overlapped arithmetic decoder of
M
and the standard arithmetic decoder of
\hat{M}
. First, according to
U_{0}
, the overlapped arithmetic decoder can obtain
p_{1|0}(x|u)
, which is then used by the standard arithmetic decoder to recover
X_{1}
exactly. Next according to
U_{0}
and
X_{1}
, the overlapped arithmetic decoder can obtain
U_{1}
and
p_{2|1}(x|u)
, which is then used by the standard arithmetic decoder to recover
X_{2}
exactly. Such operations are repeated until
X_{n}
is recovered. This example shows that
X^{n}
can be exactly represented by two bitstreams
M
and
\hat{M}
, whose total length is slightly larger than
H(X^{n})=n
, and the rate loss tends to
-h(U)
as
n\to\infty
.

2.7
Decoding Algorithm

Figure 2.6:
An incomplete binary tree generated by the decoder.

Up to now, the reader may wonder why we need to study CCS or what we can gain from CCS? One reason is that the decoder can make use of CCS to achieve a better performance.

Let
u_{0}
be a realization of
U_{0}
. From
u_{0}
, a depth-
n
incomplete binary tree
{\cal T}(u_{0},n)
will be created by the decoder
[
10
]
, as shown by
Fig.

2.6
. In
{\cal T}(u_{0},n)
, each length-
i
path corresponds to a length-
i
binary block. If
i=n
, it is called a
full
path; otherwise,
i.e.
, if
i<n
, it is called a
partial
path. We refer to the path corresponding to binary block
x^{i}\in\mathbb{B}^{i}
as the
x^{i}
-path, and its end node is called the
x^{i}
-node. Let us define

u(x^{i})\triangleq\frac{u_{0}-l(x^{i})}{h(x^{i})-l(x^{i})}=2^{ir}(u_{0}-l(x^{i})).

(2.73)

Initially,
u(x^{0})=u_{0}
. By comparing (
2.73
) with (
2.23
), it can be found that
u(x^{i})
is actually a realization of
U_{i}
. From (
2.27
), we can obtain

\displaystyle u(x^{i})=2^{r}\left(u(x^{i-1})-x_{i}(1-2^{-r})\right),

(2.74)

showing that
u(x^{i})
is a function w.r.t.
x_{i}
and
u(x^{i-1})
. With the help of side information
y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, the decoder searches through the incomplete binary tree
{\cal T}(u_{0},n)
to find the best binary block
\hat{x}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\hat{x}^{n}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{x^{n}\in{\cal T}(u_{0},n)}p(y^{n}|x^{n})

(2.75)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\arg\max_{x^{n}\in{\cal T}(u_{0},n)}p(x^{n}|y^{n}),

(2.76)

where
(a)
holds because the maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) decoder (
2.76
) is equivalent to the maximum likelihood (ML) decoder (
2.75
) for uniformly distributed
X
.

There are mainly two tree searching strategies:
breath
-first search,
i.e.
, the
M
-algorithm, and
metric
-first search,
i.e.
, the stack algorithm. This monograph omits the details of these two algorithms, as they can be found in many textbooks. Instead, we put an emphasis on how to calculate path metrics. We model the correlation between
X
and
Y
as a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability
\epsilon=\Pr(X\neq Y)
. Let
\oplus
denote
eXclusive OR
(XOR). By intuition, the metric of the
x^{i}
-path should be calculated by

\displaystyle\varsigma(x^{i})

\displaystyle\triangleq p(x^{i}|y^{n})\overset{(a)}{=}p(x^{i}|y^{i})\overset{(b)}{=}\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}{p(x_{i^{\prime}}|y_{i^{\prime}})}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{|z^{i}|}\cdot(1-\epsilon)^{i-|z^{i}|},

(2.77)

where
(a)
and
(b)
are because both
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
are i.i.d. random processes, and
|z^{i}|
denotes the number of nonzero terms in
z^{i}=x^{i}\oplus y^{i}
. Equivalently in the log domain,

\displaystyle\varsigma(x^{i})=|z^{i}|\cdot\log{\epsilon}+(i-|z^{i}|)\cdot\log(1-\epsilon).

(2.78)

However, (
2.77
) totally ignores the properties of
M
. As we know, the nonuniform distribution of
M
will cause rate loss. Hence, a gain can be achieved if
M
is also taken into consideration during decoding. Now we define the
overall
metric of the
x^{i}
-path as

\displaystyle\sigma(x^{i})

\displaystyle\triangleq p(x^{i}|y^{n},m)=p(x^{i}|y^{i},m)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\propto}p(x^{i}|y^{i})\cdot p(x^{i}|m)=\varsigma(x^{i})\cdot p(x^{i}|m),

(2.79)

where
(a)
is due to the Markov chain
Y^{n}\to X^{n}\to M
. We call
\varsigma(x^{i})
the
extrinsic
metric and
p(x^{i}|m)
the
intrinsic
metric.

Lemma 2.7.1

(Intrinsic Metric)
.

The intrinsic metric of the
x^{i}
-path is

\displaystyle p(x^{i}|m)=2^{i(r-1)}\frac{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}{f_{0}(u_{0})}\propto f_{i}(u(x^{i})).

(2.80)

Proof 2.7.2
.

Due to the i.i.d. nature of
X^{n}
and
U_{0}\to\dots\to U_{i-1}\to X_{i}
,

\displaystyle p(x^{i}|m)=p(x^{i}|u_{0})=\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p(x_{i^{\prime}}|u_{0})=\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i}p(x_{i^{\prime}}|u(x^{i^{\prime}-1})).

According to (
2.35
) and (
2.74
), we have

\displaystyle p(x_{i}|u(x^{i-1}))=2^{r-1}\frac{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}{f_{i-1}(u(x^{i-1}))}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle p(x^{i}|m)

\displaystyle=2^{i(r-1)}\frac{f_{1}(u(x_{1}))}{f_{0}(u_{0}))}\times\cdots\times\frac{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}{f_{i-1}(u(x^{i-1}))}

\displaystyle=2^{i(r-1)}\frac{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}{f_{0}(u_{0})}\overset{(a)}{\propto}f_{i}(u(x^{i})),

where
(a)
is because
2^{i(r-1)}/f_{0}(u_{0})
is a constant for every path.

Theorem 2.7.3

(Overall Metric)
.

The overall metric of the
x^{i}
-path is

\displaystyle\sigma(x^{i})\propto\epsilon^{|z^{i}|}\cdot(1-\epsilon)^{i-|z^{i}|}\cdot f_{i}(u(x^{i})),

(2.81)

where
z^{i}=x^{i}\oplus y^{i}
, and equivalently in the log domain,

\displaystyle\sigma(x^{i})=|z^{i}|\cdot\log{\epsilon}+(i-|z^{i}|)\cdot\log(1-\epsilon)+\log{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}.

(2.82)

Remark 2.7.4

(An Intuitive Explanation on Intrinsic Path Metric)
.

Let us rewrite (
2.80
) as

\displaystyle p(x^{i}|m)={2^{i(r-1)}}\frac{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}{f_{0}(u_{0})}=\frac{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))\cdot{2^{(n-i)(1-r)}}}{f_{0}(u_{0})\cdot{2^{n(1-r)}}}.

(2.83)

The denominator of (
2.83
) is in fact the total number of leaf nodes of the tree
{\cal T}(u_{0},n)
, and the numerator of (
2.83
) is in fact the number of leaf nodes of the subtree grown from the
x^{i}
-node given
U_{i}=u(x^{i})
. For uniform binary sources, all full paths are actually of the same probability
2^{-n}
, so the intrinsic metric of the
x^{i}
-path must be equal to the ratio of the number of leaf nodes of the subtree grown from the
x^{i}
-node to the total number of leaf nodes of the tree grown from the root node, which perfectly coincides with our intuition.

Continue the above analysis. It has been proved that as
n\to\infty
, the final CCS
f_{n}(u)
will become uniform over
[0,1)
for uniform sources
[
16
]
. Thus, (
2.83
) will become

\displaystyle p(x^{n}|m)=\frac{f_{n}(u(x^{n}))}{f_{0}(u_{0})\cdot 2^{n(1-r)}}=\frac{1}{f_{0}(u_{0})\cdot 2^{n(1-r)}},

(2.84)

i.e., all full paths in the tree
{\cal T}(u_{0},n)
have the same intrinsic metric. This is because for uniform binary sources, all codewords
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
are of the same probability and the tree
{\cal T}(u_{0},n)
contains
f_{0}(u_{0})2^{n(1-r)}
leaf nodes. It again perfectly coincides with our intuition.

Remark 2.7.5

(Breath-First Search)
.

In the original papers on overlapped arithmetic codes
[
20
,
21
]
, the decoder was realized with the breadth-first search, i.e., the
M
-algorithm. Let
n
be source block length. The decoding process includes
n
stages and the decoder maintains a list of paths. At each stage, the list of paths is traversed and one source symbol is decoded for each path. After each stage, at most
M
optimal paths with larger metrics are retained by pruning others, and then at the next stage, at most
2M
branches are created. Hence the decoder should allocate a memory block of
2M
nodes for each stage to avoid memory overflow, and the order of memory size of the
M
-algorithm is
{\cal O}(2nM)
. Such process is repeated and finally after the
n
-th stage, the best length-
n
path is selected. It can be found that the
M
-algorithm decoder of overlapped arithmetic codes is quite similar to the famous Successive-Cancellation List (SCL) decoder of polar codes
[
32
]
.

Figure 2.7:
The interval
[0,1)
is divided into
N=2^{16}
segments. (a) and (b) are some examples of the rounding numerical algorithm. It has two spikes when
(n-i)=8
and dithers along the real CCS when
(n-i)=16
. (c) and (d) are some examples of the linear numerical algorithm. It has one spike when
(n-i)=2
and dithers when
(n-i)=8
. (e) and (f) are some examples of the fair numerical algorithm. It overcomes all weaknesses of the rounding and linear numerical algorithms.

Remark 2.7.6

(Backward-Replacing Algorithm)
.

As we know, the decoding tree of overlapped arithmetic codes is incomplete, so fewer than
2M
branches are usually created at each stage, which means that partial memory will be wasted unavoidably. In
[
10
]
, the concept of Expansion Factor is defined as
\mu_{i}\triangleq 1+\int_{1-2^{-r}}^{2^{-r}}{f_{i}(u)}\,du\in[1,2)
. If
M
paths are retained after the
i
-th stage, then the number of branches created at the
(i+1)
-th stage is
M\mu_{i}
. For example, if
r=0.5
, we have
\mu_{i}=2-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\approx 1.65
and
M\mu_{i}\approx 1.65M
, meaning that
(2-1.65)/2>1/6
of the memory is wasted. If
r=1
, we have
\mu_{i}=1
, and as
r\to 0
, we have
\mu_{i}\to 2
. Hence, the problem of memory waste will become especially serious at high rates. To solve this problem, we propose the Backward-Replacing Algorithm: The decoder allocates a block of memory for
nM
nodes in total and maintains at most
M
paths at each stage.

The following example shows how the backward-replacing algorithm works. Let
M=6
. After the
i
-th stage, we have only
5
paths, sorted in the descending order of metric and numbered from
1
to
5
. These
5
paths are stored as a list
\{1,2,3,4,5,\_\}
, where
\_
denotes an unoccupied cell. The decoder begins with the
1
-st path, which has two children. After the
1
-st path is coped with, the list becomes
\{1a,2,3,4,5,1b\}
, where
1a
and
1b
denote the two children of the
1
-st path. Then the
2
-nd path is dealt with, which has only one child. Thus the list becomes
\{1a,2*,3,4,5,1b\}
, where
2*
denotes the sole child of the
2
-nd path. Then the
3
-rd path is tackled, which has two children and the list becomes
\{1a,2*,3a,4,3b,1b\}
, i.e., the
5
-th path is replaced directly without being handled. In turn, the
4
-th path is treated, which has two children. However, there is no more space, so only the better child is retained, while the other is discarded. If the
4b
child is better than the
4a
child, the list finally becomes
\{1a,2*,3a,4b,3b,1b\}
. This list is sorted and the above actions are repeated for the next stage.

With

(CCS), we have known how overlapped arithmetic codes partition source space into unequal-sized cosets. This section will introduce the concept of
Coexisting Interval
, which is a powerful analysis tool of overlapped arithmetic codes and has found applications to the deductions of
Hamming Distance Spectrum
(HDS)
[
17
,
16
]
and block error rate
[
18
]
. This section will also show how to deduce the block error rate of overlapped arithmetic codes with coexisting interval, while the next section will make use of coexisting interval to deduce the HDS of overlapped arithmetic codes. Note that though coexisting interval was formally defined in
[
16
]
, it is actually originated from
Risky Interval
defined in
[
17
]
.

3.1
Definition of Coexisting Interval

For conciseness, we will not distinguish random variables equal in distribution below. If
X^{n}
is an i.i.d. random process, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}\cdot 2^{(n-(i-1))r}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}2^{ir}},

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
means that two random variables are equal in distribution. Therefore, according to (
2.21
),

\displaystyle s(x^{n})\overset{d}{=}(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}2^{ir}}=(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{X_{i+1}2^{ir}}.

(3.1)

For any
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, it is easy to know

\displaystyle 0=s(0^{n})\leq s(x^{n})\leq s(1^{n})=(2^{r}-1)\frac{2^{nr}-1}{2^{r}-1}=2^{nr}-1.

The following lemmas hold obviously.

Lemma 3.1.1

(Relation Between
s
-Function and Coset)
.

The necessary and sufficient condition for the event that
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
belongs to the
m
-th coset
{\cal C}_{m}
is
s(x^{n})\in(m-1,m]
, where
m\in[0:2^{nr})
. Let
\{\cdot\}\leftrightarrow\{\cdot\}
denote the equivalence between two events. Then

\displaystyle\{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}\}\leftrightarrow\{s(x^{n})\in(m-1,m]\}.

(3.2)

Lemma 3.1.2

(Coexistence of Codewords)
.

The necessary and sufficient condition for the event that two codewords
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
coexist in the same coset
{\cal C}_{m}
is
\lceil{s(x^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(y^{n})}\rceil=m
.

For
0\leq d\leq n
, we define
b^{d}\triangleq(b_{1},\dots,b_{d})\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
and

\displaystyle j^{d}\triangleq\{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}\}\subseteq[n]\triangleq\{1,\dots,n\},

(3.3)

where

\displaystyle 1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{d}\leq n.

Especially,
j^{0}\equiv\emptyset
and
j^{n}\equiv[n]
. Further, we define

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{n,d}\triangleq\{j^{d}:1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\dots<j_{d}\leq n\}.

(3.4)

The following properties of
{\cal J}_{n,d}
are obvious.

•

{\cal J}_{n,0}=\{\emptyset\}
,
{\cal J}_{n,1}=\{\{1\},\dots,\{n\}\}
, and
{\cal J}_{n,n}=\{[n]\}
.

•

The cardinality
|{\cal J}_{n,d}|=\binom{n}{d}
. Especially,
|{\cal J}_{n,0}|=|{\cal J}_{n,n}|=1
.

•

\sum_{d=0}^{n}{|{\cal J}_{n,d}|}=2^{n}
and
\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\left(2^{d}|{\cal J}_{n,d}|\right)}=3^{n}
.

Definition 3.1.3

(Shift Function)
.

For
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
and
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
, we define the shift function as
[
16
]

\displaystyle\tau(j^{d},b^{d})\triangleq(1-2^{-r})\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}{(1-2b_{d^{\prime}})2^{rj_{d^{\prime}}}}\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.5)

Lemma 3.1.4

(Properties of Shift Function)
.

For
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
and
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
,

•

\tau(j^{d},b^{d}\oplus 1^{d})=-\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
; and

•

|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\leq(2^{nr}-1)<2^{nr}
.

Proof 3.1.5
.

Since
(1-2b)=-(1-2(b\oplus 1))
for
b\in\mathbb{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(j^{d},b^{d}\oplus 1^{d})=-\tau(j^{d},b^{d}).

We rewrite (
3.5
) as

\displaystyle\tau(j^{d},b^{d})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(1-2^{-r})\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}{2^{rj_{d^{\prime}}}}}_{c(j^{d})}-2\underbrace{(1-2^{-r})\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}{b_{d^{\prime}}2^{rj_{d^{\prime}}}}}_{v(j^{d},b^{d})}

\displaystyle=c(j^{d})-2v(j^{d},b^{d}).

(3.6)

Since
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle 0=v(j^{d},0^{d})\leq v(j^{d},b^{d})\leq v(j^{d},1^{d})=c(j^{d}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle-c(j^{d})=\tau(j^{d},1^{d})\leq\tau(j^{d},b^{d})\leq\tau(j^{d},0^{d})=c(j^{d}).

Since
(1-2^{-r})>0
for
0<r<1
, we have

\displaystyle c(j^{d})\leq c(j^{n})=s(1^{n})=(2^{nr}-1).

Therefore,

\displaystyle-(2^{nr}-1)=\tau(j^{n},1^{n})\leq\tau(j^{d},b^{d})\leq\tau(j^{n},0^{n})=(2^{nr}-1).

Hence,
|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\leq(2^{nr}-1)<2^{nr}
.

Lemma 3.1.6

(Physical Meaning of Shift Function)
.

Let
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
,
y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, and
z^{n}=x^{n}\oplus y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. We define
x_{j^{d}}\triangleq(x_{j_{1}},\dots,x_{j_{d}})\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
. In a similar way,
y_{j^{d}}
and
z_{j^{d}}
are defined. If
z_{j^{d}}=1^{d}
and
z_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}=0^{n-d}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{cases}s(y^{n})=s(x^{n})+\tau(j^{d},x_{j^{d}})\\
s(x^{n})=s(y^{n})+\tau(j^{d},y_{j^{d}}).\end{cases}

(3.7)

Proof 3.1.7
.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, the following two branches hold obviously:

•

If
(x_{i},y_{i})=(0,1)
, then
x_{i}2^{ir}=0
and
y_{i}2^{ir}=2^{ir}=x_{i}2^{ir}+2^{ir}
;

•

If
(x_{i},y_{i})=(1,0)
, then
x_{i}2^{ir}=2^{ir}
and
y_{i}2^{ir}=0=x_{i}2^{ir}-2^{ir}
.

The above two branches can be merged as

\displaystyle y_{i}2^{ir}=x_{i}2^{ir}+(1-2x_{i})2^{ir}.

Then (
3.7
) follows immediately.

Definition 3.1.8

(Coexisting Interval)
.

Let
\{(a,b]+\tau\}\triangleq(a+\tau,b+\tau]\subset\mathbb{R}
. For
m\in[1:2^{nr})
, the
m
-th coexisting interval associated with
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
and
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{m}{(j^{d},b^{d})}

\displaystyle\triangleq\{(m-1,m]-\tau(j^{d},b^{d})\}\cap(m-1,m]

\displaystyle\subseteq(m-1,m].

(3.8)

We refer to
(m-1,m]
as the

3.2
Probability of Coexisting Interval

Now we ponder over such an important problem: Given

, how possible will

fall into

Definition 3.2.1

(Conditional Value of
s(X^{n})
)
.

For

and

, we define the conditional value of

given

as

\displaystyle E{(j^{d},b^{d})}\triangleq s(X^{n}|X_{j^{d}}=b^{d}).

(3.17)

According to (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle E{(j^{d},b^{d})}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\underbrace{(1-2^{-r})\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}{b_{d^{\prime}}2^{rj_{d^{\prime}}}}}_{{\rm deterministic\penalty\ constant}\penalty\ c(j^{d},b^{d})}+\underbrace{(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus j^{d}}{X_{i}2^{ir}}}_{{\rm random\penalty\ variable}\penalty\ V([n]\setminus j^{d})}

\displaystyle=c(j^{d},b^{d})+V([n]\setminus j^{d}),

(3.18)

where
c(j^{d},b^{d})
is a deterministic constant parameterized by
j^{d}
and
b^{d}
, while
V([n]\setminus j^{d})
is a function w.r.t.
(n-d)
random variables
X_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}
. Consequently,
E{(j^{d},b^{d})}
is also a function w.r.t.
(n-d)
random variables
X_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}
. If

, then

is itself a random variable and in turn

is also a random variable; otherwise, if

, then

and

actually degenerates into a deterministic constant parameterized by

:

\displaystyle E{(j^{n},b^{n})}=s(b^{n})=c(j^{n},b^{n})=(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{b_{i}2^{ir}}.

Remark 3.2.2

(Comparison of
E(j^{d},b^{d})
with
\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
)
.

After observing (
3.6
) and (
3.18
), the reader may find that

and

are very similar to each other, where

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tau(j^{d},b^{d})=c(j^{d})-2v(j^{d},b^{d})\\
E(j^{d},b^{d})=c(j^{d},b^{d})+V([n]\setminus j^{d}).\end{cases}

However, the reader should notice the differences between them.

•

c(j^{d})
is a special case of
c(j^{d},b^{d})
when
b^{d}=1^{d}
.

•

v(j^{d},b^{d})
is a deterministic constant, while
V([n]\setminus j^{d})
is a random variable (for
d<n
).

•

v(j^{d},b^{d})
is the sum of
d
terms, while
V([n]\setminus j^{d})
is the sum of
(n-d)
terms.

•

\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
is a deterministic constant over
(-2^{nr},2^{nr})
, while
E(j^{d},b^{d})
is a random variable (for
d<n
) over
[0,2^{nr})
.

Theorem 3.2.3

(Asymptotic Probability of Coexisting Interval)
.

For almost every

, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{(n-d)\to\infty}\Pr\left\{E{(j^{d},b^{d})}\in{\cal I}(j^{d},b^{d})\right\}=\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)^{+}.

(3.19)

.

As shown by (

),

is the sum of a deterministic constant

and a random variable

(for

). Let us pay attention to

will go to infinity as

. According to

, for almost every

will be u.d. mod 1 as

will also be u.d. mod 1. In other words, if

will be u.d. over

\displaystyle\lim_{(n-d)\to\infty}\Pr\left\{E{(j^{d},b^{d})}\in{\cal I}_{m}(j^{d},b^{d})\middle|E{(j^{d},b^{d})}\in(m-1,m]\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{|{\cal I}_{m}(j^{d},b^{d})|}{|(m-1,m]|}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)^{+},

Remark 3.2.5

(A Note about the Proof of

If

3.3
Error Rate for One Unknown Ending Symbol

Equipped with coexisting interval, we are now ready to compute the frame error rate of overlapped arithmetic codes. Due to the difficult nature of this problem, we will start with some simple cases. It was found in
[
17
]
that the residual symbol errors of overlapped arithmetic codes after decoding usually happen at the end of each block, so we will begin with the simplest case that
all but the last
symbols of each block are known at the decoder.

Let
X^{n}
be the source and
Y^{n}
be the side information known only at the decoder. The correlation between
X^{n}
and
Y^{n}
is modeled as a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability
\epsilon
. Let
Z^{n}=X^{n}\oplus Y^{n}
. Then the event
X_{i}\neq Y_{i}
is equivalent to the event
Z_{i}=1
. On receiving the bitstream
M=\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil
, the decoder will search through the coset
{\cal C}_{M}
to find the codeword closest (in Hamming distance) to
Y^{n}
, which is denoted by
\hat{X}^{n}
, and take it as the estimate of
X^{n}
. The
frame-error-rate
(FER) after decoding is denoted by
\Pr(e)
, where
e
denotes the event
\{\hat{X}^{n}\neq X^{n}{|Y^{n}}\}
. If
X^{n-1}
is known at the decoder, then
\hat{X}^{n-1}\equiv X^{n-1}
. The conditional FER given
X^{n-1}
known at the decoder is

\displaystyle\Pr(e|X^{n-1})=\Pr(\hat{X}_{n}\neq X_{n}{|Y_{n}})<\Pr(e).

Theorem 3.3.1

(FER Given
X^{n-1}
)
.

For almost every
0<r<1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\Pr(e|X^{n-1})}=(2-2^{r})\epsilon.

(3.21)

Proof 3.3.2
.

Let us define
\tilde{X}^{n}\triangleq(X^{n}\oplus(0^{n-1}1))
, which is called a spare codeword of
X^{n}
. From
Lemma

3.1.2
, we have the following findings.

•

If
\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil\neq\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil
, then
X^{n}
and
\tilde{X}^{n}
do not coexist in the same coset, so decoding will always succeed, regardless of
Y^{n}
.

•

If
\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil
, then
X^{n}
and
\tilde{X}^{n}
coexist in the same coset, so decoding correctness purely depends on
Y_{n}
.

Now it is obvious that

\displaystyle\Pr(\hat{X}_{n}\neq X_{n}{|Y_{n}})

\displaystyle=\Pr(\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil)\cdot\Pr(Z_{n}=1)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\cdot\Pr(\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil).

The key is how to calculate
\Pr(\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil)
. Since
X^{n}\oplus\tilde{X}^{n}=(0^{n-1}1)
, the Hamming distance between
X^{n}
and
\tilde{X}^{n}
is
d=1
. By (
3.16
) of
Theorem

3.1.16
, the equivalent event of
\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil
is
s(X^{n})\in{\cal I}(j^{d},X_{j^{d}})
, where
j^{d}=\{n\}
and
X_{j^{d}}=X_{n}
. That is,

\displaystyle\{\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil=\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n})}\rceil\}\leftrightarrow\{s(X^{n})\in{\cal I}(n,X_{n})\},

where
{\cal I}(n,X_{n})={\cal I}(\{n\},X_{n})
for conciseness. In turn,

\displaystyle\Pr(s(X^{n})\in{\cal I}(n,X_{n}))

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{B}}\Pr(X_{n}=b)\cdot\Pr(s(X^{n}|X_{n}=b)\in{\cal I}(n,b))

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(a)}{=}\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{b\in\mathbb{B}}\Pr(E(n,b)\in{\cal I}(n,b)),

where
E(n,b)=E(\{n\},b)
for conciseness and
(a)
comes from (
3.17
). By (
3.19
) of
Theorem

3.2.3
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\Pr\left\{E{(n,b)}\in{\cal I}(n,b)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left(1-|\tau(n,b)|\right)^{+}

\displaystyle=1-(1-2^{-r})2^{r}=2-2^{r},

where
\tau(n,b)=\tau(\{n\},b)
for conciseness. Hence (
3.21
) holds
[
14
]
.

3.4

After understanding the simplest case, now we consider a slightly more complex case:
All but the last two
symbols of each block are known at the decoder. It will be seen that the analysis in the second simplest case is much more difficult than that in the simplest case, and the extension is not straightforward. Let
\Pr(e|X^{n-2})
be the conditional FER given
X^{n-2}
at the decoder, then

\displaystyle\Pr(e|X^{n-2})=\Pr(\hat{X}_{n-1}^{n}\neq X_{n-1}^{n}{|Y_{n-1}^{n}}),

where
X_{n-1}^{n}=(X_{n-1},X_{n})
. Let us define
(2^{2}-1)=3
spare codewords of
X^{n}
as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{X}^{n}_{01}=(X^{n}\oplus(0^{n-2}01))\\
\tilde{X}^{n}_{10}=(X^{n}\oplus(0^{n-2}10))\\
\tilde{X}^{n}_{11}=(X^{n}\oplus(0^{n-2}11)).\end{cases}

Let
{\cal E}_{01}\triangleq\{\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n}_{01})}\rceil=\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil\}
and
\bar{{\cal E}}_{01}\triangleq\{\lceil{s(\tilde{X}^{n}_{01})}\rceil\neq\lceil{s(X^{n})}\rceil\}
. In a similar way,
{\cal E}_{10}
,
\bar{{\cal E}}_{10}
,
{\cal E}_{11}
, and
\bar{{\cal E}}_{11}
are also defined. Then

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\cal E}_{01}\leftrightarrow\{s(X^{n})\in{\cal I}(n,X_{n})\}\\
{\cal E}_{10}\leftrightarrow\{s(X^{n})\in{\cal I}(n-1,X_{n-1})\}\\
{\cal E}_{11}\leftrightarrow\{s(X^{n})\in{\cal I}(\{n-1,n\},X_{n-1}^{n})\}.\end{cases}

(3.22)

3.4.1
Enumeration of Event Combinations

There are
2^{2^{2}-1}=2^{3}=8
event combinations in total.

1.

\bar{\cal E}_{01}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{10}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{11}
: The decoding succeeds always.

2.

{\cal E}_{01}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{10}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{11}
: If
Z_{n}=X_{n}\oplus Y_{n}=1
,
i.e.
,
X_{n}\neq Y_{n}
, the decoding will fail, so the failure probability is
\epsilon
.

3.

\bar{\cal E}_{01}\wedge{\cal E}_{10}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{11}
: Just as case 2, the failure probability is
\epsilon
.

4.

\bar{\cal E}_{01}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{10}\wedge{\cal E}_{11}
: If
Z_{n-1}^{n}=(X_{n-1}^{n}\oplus Y_{n-1}^{n})=1^{2}
, the decoding will fail; if
Z_{n-1}^{n}=10
or
01
, since
|Y^{n}\oplus\tilde{X}_{11}^{n}|=|Y^{n}\oplus X^{n}|=1
, the failure probability is
0.5
. The overall failure probability is

\displaystyle\Pr(Z_{n-1}^{n}=1^{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\Pr(Z_{n-1}^{n}=10)+\Pr(Z_{n-1}^{n}=01)\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\epsilon(1-\epsilon)+(1-\epsilon)\epsilon)=\epsilon.

5.

{\cal E}_{01}\wedge\bar{\cal E}_{10}\wedge{\cal E}_{11}
: If
Y_{n}\neq X_{n}
, the decoding will fail; if

Remark 3.4.8

Chapter 4
Hamming Distance Spectrum

We have known in
Section

2
that overlapped arithmetic codes are actually a kind of nonlinear coset codes partitioning source space into unequal-sized cosets, and
Coset Cardinality Spectrum
(CCS) is a fundamental property of overlapped arithmetic codes that can describe the distribution of coset cardinality. However, this is not enough. Just as channel codes, another fundamental property of overlapped arithmetic codes is how far (in Hamming distance) the codewords within the same coset keep away from each other. To avoid verbosity, we introduce the terminology of
mate codewords
as below.

Definition 4.0.1

(Mate Codeword)
.

We refer to the codewords belonging to the same coset as mate codewords, or just mates for short. In mathematical language, if
m(x^{n})=m(y^{n})
, where

\displaystyle m(x^{n})=\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil=\lceil(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}2^{ir}\rceil,

as defined by (
2.21
) and (
2.22
), then
x^{n}
and
y^{n}
are mates.

We introduce the
Hamming Distance Spectrum
(HDS) to quantitatively measure the distribution of inter-mate Hamming distance, which will be helpful for us to understand overlapped arithmetic codes.

4.1
Definition of HDS

Definition 4.1.1

(Codeword HDS)
.

Let
n
be code length. For
d\in[0:n]\triangleq\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, the HDS of codeword
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle k_{d}(x^{n})\triangleq\left|\left\{y^{n}:(y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n})\wedge(m(x^{n})=m(y^{n}))\wedge(|x^{n}\oplus y^{n}|=d)\right\}\right|,

where
|x^{n}\oplus y^{n}|=d
means that
y^{n}
is
d
-away from
x^{n}
.

In plain words,
k_{d}(x^{n})
is the number of mate codewords
d
-away from
x^{n}
. Actually, for linear block codes, every codeword has the same HDS,
i.e.
,
k_{d}(x^{n})\equiv k_{d}(0^{n})
for any
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
. However, for overlapped arithmetic codes, every codeword may have different HDS.

Lemma 4.1.2

(Properties of Codeword HDS)
.

Let
{\cal C}_{m}
be the coset containing
x^{n}
, whose cardinality is denoted by
|{\cal C}_{m}|
, where
m\in[0:2^{nr})
. It is easy to obtain the following properties of
k_{d}(x^{n})
.

•

0\leq k_{d}(x^{n})\leq\binom{n}{d}
.

•

For
1\leq d\leq n
, the sum
\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{k_{d}(x^{n})}
is twice the number of
d
-away codeword-pairs in
{\cal C}_{m}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{n}{\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{k_{d}(x^{n})}}=2\binom{|{\cal C}_{m}|}{2}=|{\cal C}_{m}|^{2}-|{\cal C}_{m}|.

(4.1)

•

If we define
k_{0}(x^{n})=1
, then
\sum_{d=0}^{n}{k_{d}(x^{n})}=|{\cal C}_{m}|
and further

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{\sum_{d=0}^{n}{k_{d}(x^{n})}}=|{\cal C}_{m}|^{2}.

(4.2)

Definition 4.1.3

(Coset HDS)
.

The HDS of the
m
-th coset is

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(d)\triangleq\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{\Pr(X^{n}=x^{n}|X^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m})\cdot k_{d}(x^{n})}.

Definition 4.1.4

(Code HDS)
.

For
0\leq d\leq n
, the HDS of an overlapped arithmetic code is defined as

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)\triangleq\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}{\Pr(X^{n}=x^{n})\cdot k_{d}(x^{n})}.

For uniform binary sources,
i.e.
,
\Pr(X^{n}=x^{n})\equiv 2^{-n}
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(d)=\tfrac{1}{|{\cal C}_{m}|}\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{k_{d}(x^{n})}

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)=2^{-n}\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}{k_{d}(x^{n})}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}\sum_{x^{n}\in{\cal C}_{m}}{k_{d}(x^{n})}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}|{\cal C}_{m}|\cdot\phi_{m}(d).

(4.4)

From (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we have
\sum_{d=0}^{n}\phi_{m}(d)=|{\cal C}_{m}|
.

Definition 4.1.5

(Asymptotic Code HDS)
.

For
0\leq d\leq n
, the asymptotic HDS of an overlapped arithmetic code is

\displaystyle\psi(d)\triangleq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\psi(d;n)}.

For linear block codes, since
k_{d}(x^{n})=k_{d}(0^{n})
for every
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, it is easy to obtain
\psi(d;n)=k_{d}(0^{n})
for every
d\in[0:n]
. That is, the HDS of a linear block code is equivalent to its weight spectrum. However, for overlapped arithmetic codes, the problem is much more complex.

,

,

, and

;

,

\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\psi(d;n)}>2^{n(1-r)}
, which is not a coincidence, just as shown below.

Lemma 4.1.7

(Sum of HDS)
.

Let
f(u)
be the asymptotic initial CCS given by (
2.9
). Then

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\psi(d;n)}}{2^{n(1-r)}}=\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du}.

(4.5)

Proof 4.1.8
.

According to (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), we will obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\psi(d;n)}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{|{\cal C}_{m}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=2^{n(1-r)}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{\left(\frac{|{\cal C}_{m}|}{2^{n(1-r)}}\right)^{2}}2^{-nr}.

It can be written as

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\psi(d;n)}}{2^{n(1-r)}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{2^{nr}-1}{\left(\frac{|{\cal C}_{m}|}{2^{n(1-r)}}\right)^{2}}2^{-nr}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du},

where
(a)
comes from the definition of
f(u)
.

Lemma 4.1.9

(Convexity of HDS)
.

For an overlapped arithmetic code with length
n
and rate
r
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\psi(d;n)}\geq 2^{n(1-r)},

(4.6)

where the equality holds iff source space
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is equally partitioned into
2^{nr}
cosets of cardinality
2^{n(1-r)}
.

Proof 4.1.10
.

Since
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du}
is a nonnegative and convex function in
f(u)
, we have
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du}\geq 1
and the equality holds iff
f(u)
is uniform over
[0,1)
, i.e., source space
\mathbb{B}^{n}
is equally partitioned into
2^{nr}
cosets of cardinality
2^{n(1-r)}
. This lemma then follows
Lemma

4.1.7
immediately.

4.2
Two Toy Methods to Calculate HDS

Remark 4.2.1

(Exhaustive Enumeration)
.

As shown by (
4.4
), to obtain
\psi(d;n)
for all
d\in[0:n]
, we should try every
x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and every
y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, so the total complexity is
{\cal O}(2^{n}\times 2^{n})={\cal O}(4^{n})
. More concretely, the computing complexity of
\psi(d;n)
varies for different
d
. Let
z^{n}=x^{n}\oplus y^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
|x^{n}\oplus y^{n}|=|z^{n}|=d
. For convenience, we define
z_{j^{d}}^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
as a length-
n
binary block with
z_{j^{d}}=1^{d}
and
z_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}=0^{n-d}
, where
j^{d}
and
[n]
are defined by (
3.3
). Let
{\bf 1}_{A}
be the well-known indicator function equal to either
1
if
A
is true, or
0
if
A
is false. From (
4.4
), we have

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)=2^{-n}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\sum_{x^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}{\bf 1}_{m(x^{n})=m(x^{n}\oplus z_{j^{d}}^{n})},

(4.7)

where
{\cal J}_{n,d}
is defined by (
3.4
). Obviously, the computing complexity of (
4.7
) is
{\cal O}(2^{n}\binom{n}{d})
, following the binomial distribution w.r.t.
d
, extremely huge and unacceptable for every
d\in[0:n]
. Hence, we are badly in need of a fast method to calculate
\psi(d;n)
.

Theorem 4.2.2

(Binomial Approximation of HDS)
.

If we take overlapped arithmetic codes as random codes, then
\psi(d;n)
for
0\leq d\leq n
obeys the following binomial distribution

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)\approx\binom{n}{d}\cdot 2^{-nr}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}.

(4.8)

Proof 4.2.3
.

As shown by
Lemma

4.1.7
,

\displaystyle\sum_{d=0}^{n}{\psi(d;n)}\approx 2^{n(1-r)}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du}.

If
\psi(d;n)
for
0\leq d\leq n
obeys the binomial distribution, then

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)\approx\frac{\binom{n}{d}\cdot 2^{n(1-r)}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)du}}{\sum_{d=0}^{n}\binom{n}{d}}.

As we know,
\sum_{d=0}^{n}\binom{n}{d}=2^{n}
. Now (
4.8
) follows immediately.

According to (
4.8
), the complexity of
\psi(d;n)
is
{\cal O}(1)
, hence the total complexity is
{\cal O}(n)
for all
d\in[0:n]
. Despite its low complexity, one serious drawback of (
4.8
) is its low accuracy. Because overlapped arithmetic codes are not random codes,
\psi(d;n)
for
0\leq d\leq n
does not strictly follow the binomial distribution, especially at the two ends of
[0:n]
. Hence with (
4.8
), we can obtain only a coarse approximation of
\psi(d;n)
. For example, (
4.8
) returns
2^{-nr}\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}
for
d=0
, while
\psi(0;n)=1
actually. It will be verified in
Sect.

4.9
that (
4.8
) works well only for
d\approx n/2
, while performs poorly in other cases, which motivates us to look for a more accurate method.

4.3
Soft Approximation of HDS for
d<n

Theorem 4.3.1

(Soft Approximation of HDS for
d<n
)
.

Let us define
j^{d}
as (
3.3
) and
{\cal J}_{n,d}
as (
3.4
). For almost every
0<r<1
and for every
d<n
, as
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)\to 2^{-d}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}{\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)^{+}},

(4.9)

where
\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
is defined by (
3.5
) and
(\cdot)^{+}\triangleq\max(0,\cdot)
.

Proof 4.3.2
.

Let us define the conditional indicator function as

\displaystyle{\bf 1}_{A|B}\triangleq\begin{cases}1,&A{\rm\penalty\ is\penalty\ true\penalty\ given}\penalty\ B\\
0,&A{\rm\penalty\ is\penalty\ false\penalty\ give}\penalty\ B.\end{cases}

Then (
4.7
) can be written as

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)=2^{-d}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{\psi(d;n|j^{d},b^{d})},

where

\displaystyle\psi(d;n|j^{d},b^{d})\triangleq 2^{-(n-d)}\sum_{x_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}\in\mathbb{B}^{n-d}}{\bf 1}_{m(x^{n})=m(x^{n}\oplus z_{j^{d}}^{n})|x_{j^{d}}=b^{d}}.

Let us define the following binary random variable

\displaystyle V(j^{d},b^{d})\triangleq{\bf 1}_{m(X^{n})=m(X^{n}\oplus z_{j^{d}}^{n})|X_{j^{d}}=b^{d}}.

Then
{\bf 1}_{m(x^{n})=m(x^{n}\oplus z_{j^{d}}^{n})|x_{j^{d}}=b^{d}}
is a realization of
V(j^{d},b^{d})
. According to
Theorem

3.1.16
, we have

\displaystyle\left\{m(x^{n})=m(x^{n}\oplus z_{j^{d}}^{n})\middle|x_{j^{d}}=b^{d}\right\}\leftrightarrow\left\{s(x^{n}|x_{j^{d}}=b^{d})\in{\cal I}(j^{d},b^{d})\right\},

where
\{\cdot\}\leftrightarrow\{\cdot\}
denotes two equivalent events, so
V(j^{d},b^{d})
is a binary random variable with bias probability
\Pr\left\{E(j^{d},b^{d})\in{\cal I}(j^{d},b^{d})\right\}
, where
E(j^{d},b^{d})\triangleq s(X^{n}|X_{j^{d}}=b^{d})
, as defined by (
3.17
).

•

As
(n-d)\to\infty
, we have infinitely many independent realizations of
V(j^{d},b^{d})
and thus according to the law of large numbers,

\displaystyle\lim_{(n-d)\to\infty}\psi(d;n|j^{d},b^{d})

\displaystyle=\lim_{(n-d)\to\infty}\Pr\left\{E(j^{d},b^{d})\in{\cal I}(j^{d},b^{d})\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)^{+},

(4.10)

where
(a)
comes from
Theorem

3.2.3
.

•

For
1\leq(n-d)<\infty
, there are only finitely many realizations of
V(j^{d},b^{d})
, so (
4.10
) cannot be applied. However, by the generalized law of large numbers, for every
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{\psi(d;n|j^{d},b^{d})}}{\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)^{+}}}=1.

(4.11)

However, note that
V(j^{d},b^{d})
is a function w.r.t.
(n-d)
random variables
X_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}
and will degenerate into a deterministic constant for
d=n
. Therefore, (
4.11
) does not hold for
d=n
.

We name (
4.9
) as the
Soft Approximation
of
\psi(d;n)
, to distinguish it from the
Hard Approximation
of
\psi(d;n)
named in the next sub-section.

Remark 4.3.3

(Accuracy)
.

Though (
4.9
) is accurate for almost every
d
, there is an exception
\psi(n;n)
, which cannot be approximated by (
4.9
) otherwise there will be a large deviation.

Remark 4.3.4

(Complexity)
.

As we know, the cardinality of
{\cal J}_{n,d}
is
\binom{n}{d}
, so the complexity to calculate
\psi(d;n)
by (
4.9
) is
{\cal O}(2^{d}\binom{n}{d})
. Compared with (
4.7
), whose complexity is
{\cal O}(2^{n}\binom{n}{d})
, the complexity of (
4.9
) is reduced by
2^{n-d}
times. However, the complexity of (
4.9
) goes up hyper-exponentially as
d
increases. Therefore, even though (
4.9
) is accurate for almost every
d
, it is feasible only for small
d
in practice. It will be very desirable if the complexity of (
4.9
) can be reduced for large
d
. Since
\sum_{d=0}^{n}2^{d}\binom{n}{d}=3^{n}
, the total complexity to calculate
\psi(d;n)
for all
d\in[0:n]
by (
4.9
) is
{\cal O}(3^{n})
. As a comparison, remember that the total complexity of the exhaustive enumeration (
4.7
) is
{\cal O}(4^{n})
, and the total complexity of the binomial approximation (
4.8
) is
{\cal O}(n)
.

4.4
Hard Approximation of HDS for
1\ll d<n

This sub-section will propose a variant of (
4.9
) to calculate
\psi(d;n)
, which is called
Hard Approximation
, just to distinguish it from the
Soft Approximation
defined by (
4.9
). In essence, the
Hard Approximation
is an approximation of the
Soft Approximation
for
d\gg 1
. However, there is still an exception when
d=n
, which will be particularly discussed in the next sub-section.

Definition 4.4.1

(
j^{d}
-Active Set)
.

The active set associated with
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{j^{d}}\triangleq\{b^{d}:(b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d})\wedge(|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1)\}\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{d}.

(4.12)

Definition 4.4.2

(
j^{d}
-Universal Sequence and
j^{d}
-Active Sequence)
.

For every
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
, we define two sequences. One is the
j^{d}
-universal sequence

\displaystyle\omega_{j^{d}}\triangleq\left(\tau(j^{d},b^{d})\right)_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}\in(-2^{nr},2^{nr})^{|\omega_{j^{d}}|},

(4.13)

and the other is the
j^{d}
-active sequence

\displaystyle\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}\triangleq\left(\tau(j^{d},b^{d})\right)_{b^{d}\in{\cal B}_{j^{d}}}\in(-1,1)^{|\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}|},

(4.14)

where

\displaystyle|\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}|=|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|=\left(\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{{\bf 1}_{|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1}}\right)\leq|\omega_{j^{d}}|=2^{d}.

Definition 4.4.3

(
d
-Universal Sequence and
d
-Active Sequence)
.

For every
d\in[0:n]
, we define two sequences. One is the
d
-universal sequence

\displaystyle\omega_{d}\triangleq\left(\omega_{j^{d}}\right)_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\in(-2^{nr},2^{nr})^{|\omega_{d}|},

(4.15)

and the other is the
d
-active sequence

\displaystyle\omega_{d,{\cal B}}\triangleq\left(\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}\right)_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\in(-1,1)^{|\omega_{d,{\cal B}}|},

(4.16)

where

\displaystyle|\omega_{d,{\cal B}}|=\left(\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|\right)\leq|\omega_{d}|=\binom{n}{d}2^{d}.

We use
\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}\subseteq\omega_{j^{d}}
to denote that
\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}
is a sub-sequence of
\omega_{j^{d}}
. Similarly,
\omega_{d,{\cal B}}\subseteq\omega_{d}
denotes that
\omega_{d,{\cal B}}
is a sub-sequence of
\omega_{d}
. Clearly, iff
d=n
, we have
\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}=\omega_{j^{d}}
and
\omega_{d,{\cal B}}=\omega_{d}
. In addition,
|\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}|\to\infty
and
|\omega_{d,{\cal B}}|\to\infty
as
d\to\infty
. Since
\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
is defined by (
3.5
), the following lemmas hold obviously.

Lemma 4.4.4

(Conditional Distribution of Shift Function)
.

For almost every
0<r<1
, as
d\to\infty
, both
\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}
and
\omega_{d,{\cal B}}
will be u.d. over
(-1,1)
.

Lemma 4.4.5

(Average Length of Coexisting Interval)
.

For almost every
0<r<1
and for every
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{b^{d}\in{\cal B}_{j^{d}}}{\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)}}{|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|}=1/2.

(4.17)

Theorem 4.4.6

(Hard Approximation of HDS for
1\ll d<n
)
.

For almost every
0<r<1
and for
d<n
, as
d\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)\to 2^{-(d+1)}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{{\bf 1}_{|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1}}.

(4.18)

Proof 4.4.7
.

As
d\to\infty
, by using
{\cal B}_{j^{d}}
, we can rewrite (
4.9
) as

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)

\displaystyle\to 2^{-d}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}{\sum_{b^{d}\in{\cal B}_{j^{d}}}\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\to}2^{-(d+1)}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|,

where
(a)
comes from (
4.17
). Then (
4.18
) follows immediately.

Remark 4.4.8

(Comparison of
Theorem

4.4.6
with
Theorem

4.3.1
)
.

It can be seen that (
4.9
) and (
4.18
) are very similar to each other, and (
4.18
) can be taken as a binary approximation of (
4.9
). We would like to highlight the following points.

•

They have the same complexity
{\cal O}(2^{d}\binom{n}{d})
.

•

As
d
increases, (
4.9
) and (
4.18
) will approach each other gradually, according to the generalized law of large numbers.

•

Neither (
4.9
) nor (
4.18
) applies to
d=n
because
E{(j^{d},b^{d})}
will degenerate into a deterministic constant.

•

The condition of (
4.9
) is
n\to\infty
, while the condition of (
4.18
) is
d\to\infty
. Since
d<n
, it is obvious that
d\to\infty
is a stricter condition than
n\to\infty
. Hence, (
4.9
) has a broader scope of application than (
4.18
).

•

For small
d
, the accuracy of (
4.18
) is poor because its cornerstone (
4.17
) does not hold; while (
4.9
) is always accurate no matter for small
d
or large
d
(but except
d=n
).

4.5
Discussion on
d=n

As analyzed before, neither (
4.9
) nor (
4.18
) applies to
d=n
because
E{(j^{d},b^{d})}
will degenerate into a deterministic constant. Now we want to see what will happen when
d=n
.

Lemma 4.5.1

(
n
-away Codeword Pairs)
.

Let
n
be code length. If a pair of
n
-away codewords coexist in the same coset, then this pair of codewords must belong to the
2^{nr-1}
-th coset. Conversely speaking, the
2^{nr-1}
-th coset
{\cal C}_{2^{nr-1}}
includes all pairs of
n
-away codewords.

Proof 4.5.2
.

According to (
3.5
), the definition of
\tau
-function, we have

\displaystyle\tau(j^{n},x^{n})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{2^{ir}}}_{2^{nr}-1}-2\underbrace{(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{x_{i}2^{ir}}}_{s(x^{n})}

\displaystyle=(2^{nr}-1)-2s(x^{n}),

which is followed by

\displaystyle s(x^{n})=2^{nr-1}-\frac{1+\tau(j^{n},x^{n})}{2}.

For
y^{n}=x^{n}\oplus 1^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle s(y^{n})

\displaystyle=s(x^{n}\oplus 1^{n})=s(x^{n})+\tau(j^{n},x^{n})

\displaystyle=2^{nr-1}-\frac{1-\tau(j^{n},x^{n})}{2}.

If
|\tau(j^{n},x^{n})|<1
, then
0<\frac{1\pm\tau(j^{n},x^{n})}{2}<1
and

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(2^{nr-1}-1)<s(x^{n})<2^{nr-1}\\
(2^{nr-1}-1)<s(y^{n})<2^{nr-1},\end{cases}

implying
\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil\equiv\lceil s(y^{n})\rceil\equiv 2^{nr-1}
. In other words,
x^{n}
and
y^{n}=x^{n}\oplus 1^{n}
must coexist in the
2^{nr-1}
-th coset
{\cal C}_{2^{nr-1}}
.

Lemma 4.5.3

(Particularity of
d=n
)
.

The necessary and sufficient condition for the event that
x^{n}
and
(x^{n}\oplus 1^{n})
coexist in the same coset is
|\tau(j^{n},x^{n})|<1
.

As a comparison, let us recall
Corollary

3.1.18
, which states that
|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1
is the necessary condition for the coexistence of
x^{n}
and
y^{n}=x^{n}\oplus z^{n}
in the same coset, where
x_{j^{d}}=b^{d}
,
z_{j^{d}}=1^{d}
, and
z_{[n]\setminus j^{d}}=0^{n-d}
. Especially,
Lemma

4.5.3
says that, if
d=n
, the necessary condition
|\tau(j^{n},b^{n})|<1
is also the sufficient condition for the coexistence of
x^{n}=b^{n}
and
y^{n}=x^{n}\oplus 1^{n}
in the same coset.

Theorem 4.5.4

(
\psi(n;n)
)
.

Let
j^{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\psi(n;n)=2^{-n}\cdot|{\cal B}_{j^{n}}|=2^{-n}\sum_{b^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}{{\bf 1}_{|\tau(j^{n},b^{n})|<1}}.

(4.19)

Proof 4.5.5
.

There is only one element
j^{n}
in the set
{\cal J}_{n,n}
. For every
b^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, if
|\tau(j^{n},b^{n})|<1
, then both
b^{n}
and
b^{n}\oplus 1^{n}
coexist in the same coset. Hence, (
4.19
) holds naturally.

Let us compare (
4.19
) with (
4.18
). By (
4.18
), as
d=n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\psi(n;n)\to 2^{-(n+1)}\cdot|{\cal B}_{j^{n}}|=2^{-(n+1)}\sum_{b^{n}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}}{{\bf 1}_{|\tau(j^{n},b^{n})|<1}},

(4.20)

which is just half of (
4.19
)! The definition of
E(j^{d},b^{d})
explains why this phenomenon happens. According to (
3.17
), if
d<n
,
E(j^{d},b^{d})
is a random variable; while when
d=n
,
E(j^{n},b^{n})
will degenerate into a deterministic constant, and hence (
4.18
) does not apply.

In addition, also please notice another subtle difference between (
4.19
) and (
4.18
). That is, (
4.18
) gives only an asymptotic value of
\psi(d;n)
for
d<n
, while (
4.19
) gives an exact value of
\psi(n;n)
.

For clarity, we integrate
Theorem

4.4.6
and
Theorem

4.5.4
into the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5.6

(Hard Approximation of HDS)
.

Let
\alpha\triangleq{\bf 1}_{(d=n)}
. For almost every
0<r<1
and for
1\ll d\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)

\displaystyle\approxeq 2^{\alpha-d-1}|\omega_{d,{\cal B}}|=2^{\alpha-d-1}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}|\omega_{j^{d},{\cal B}}|

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha-d-1}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha-d-1}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{{\bf 1}_{|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1}},

(4.21)

where the approximation becomes equality if
d=n
.

Meanwhile, by taking the case of
d=n
into consideration, we will get a general form of
Theorem

4.3.1
as below.

Theorem 4.5.7

(Soft Approximation of HDS)
.

Let
\alpha\triangleq{\bf 1}_{(d=n)}
. For almost every
0<r<1
and for
1\leq d\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)

\displaystyle\approx 2^{\alpha-d}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\sum_{b^{d}\in{\cal B}_{j^{d}}}{\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)}

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha-d}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}\sum_{b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}{\left(1-|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|\right)^{+}}.

(4.22)

4.6
Fast Approximation of HDS for
d\approx n

As show by
Theorem

4.5.6
and
Theorem

4.5.7
, for both
Hard Approximation
and
Soft Approximation
, the complexity is too high to be acceptable for large
d
. This section will derive a fast method to calculate
\psi(d;n)
for
d\approx n
based on the close affinity between CCS and HDS, which is named as
Fast Approximation
, whose complexity is
{\cal O}(1)
, the same as that of the
Binomial Approximation
defined by (
4.8
). Through this work, we bridge HDS with CCS
[
13
]
.

4.6.1
Normalized Shift Function

It can be seen from (
4.21
) that to calculate
\psi(d;n)
, the key is to find
|\omega_{d,{\cal B}}|
, the number of codewords making the shift function
\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
fall into the interval
(-1,1)
. Actually, (
4.21
) suggests to calculate
\psi(d;n)
with exhaustive enumeration, whose complexity is
{\cal O}(2^{d}\binom{n}{d})
, very high for large
d
. In the following, instead of exhaustive enumeration, we try to find a simple method to calculate
\psi(d;n)
for large
d
. Our analysis is based on the close affinity between CCS and HDS. More concretely, for every
d\approx n
, we can derive the asymptotic distribution of
\omega_{d}
according to CCS. Further, according to the distribution of
\omega_{d}
, we can obtain
|\omega_{d,{\cal B}}|
. This is a very interesting finding.

Before deriving the distribution of
\omega_{d}
, we should know the distribution of
\omega_{j^{d}}
. To derive the distribution of
\omega_{j^{d}}
, let us define a random variable
\tau(j^{d},X^{d})
according to (
3.5
). Obviously, for uniform binary sources, the distribution of the random variable
\tau(j^{d},X^{d})
is just the distribution of the sequence
\omega_{j^{d}}
.

However, note that
\tau(j^{d},X^{d})
is discretely distributed over the interval
(-2^{nr},2^{nr})
. As
n\to\infty
, the shift function
\tau(j^{d},X^{d})
will not be well defined because the interval
(-2^{nr},2^{nr})
will become the real field
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, it is very difficult to derive the distribution of
\tau(j^{d},X^{d})
directly. To overcome this difficulty, for a given
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
, according to (
3.5
), we define a normalized random variable for
\tau(j^{d},X^{d})
.

Definition 4.6.1

(Normalized Shift Function)
.

For every
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
, we define the normalized shift function as

\displaystyle W(j^{d})\triangleq 2^{-nr}\tau(j^{d},X^{d})=c(j^{d})-2V(j^{d}),

(4.23)

where

\displaystyle c(j^{d})\triangleq 2^{-nr}(1-2^{-r})\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}{2^{rj_{d^{\prime}}}}

and

\displaystyle V(j^{d})\triangleq 2^{-nr}(1-2^{-r})\sum_{d^{\prime}=1}^{d}{X_{d^{\prime}}2^{rj_{d^{\prime}}}}.

(4.24)

It can be seen that
c(j^{d})
is a constant, while
V(j^{d})
is a random variable for
d\geq 1
. Clearly,
0\leq V(j^{d})\leq c(j^{d})
and
-c(j^{d})\leq W(j^{d})\leq c(j^{d})
. It is easy to know
0\leq c(j^{d})<1
. Therefore,
V(j^{d})
is defined over
[0,1)
and
W(j^{d})
is defined over
(-1,1)
.

Let
f_{W|j^{d}}(w)
, where
-1<w<1
, be the pdf of
W(j^{d})
, and
f_{V|j^{d}}(v)
, where
0\leq v<1
, be the pdf of
V(j^{d})
. According to the property of pdf, it is easy to obtain

\displaystyle f_{W|j^{d}}(w)=\tfrac{1}{2}f_{V|j^{d}}(\tfrac{c(j^{d})-w}{2}).

(4.25)

Both
V(j^{d})
and
W(j^{d})
are tractable because they are well defined. Once
f_{W|j^{d}}(w)
is obtained, the distribution of
\omega_{j^{d}}
can be easily derived. Further, we define

\displaystyle f_{W|d}(w)\triangleq\frac{\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}{f_{W|j^{d}}(w)}}{\binom{n}{d}}.

(4.26)

Once
f_{W|d}(w)
is obtained, the distribution of
\omega_{d}
can be easily derived.

4.6.2
Distribution of Normalized Shift Function

We begin with
d=n
. There is only one element
j^{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
in
{\cal J}_{n,n}
, so we abbreviate
V(j^{n})
to
V
, and
W(j^{n})
to
W
for conciseness. Similarly,
f_{W|j^{n}}(w)
is shortened to
f_{W}(w)
, and
f_{V|j^{n}}(v)
to
f_{V}(v)
.

Theorem 4.6.2

(Asymptotic Distribution of Normalized Shift Function for
d=n
)
.

As
n\to\infty
, the pdf of
V
is
f_{V}(v)=f(v)
, where
f(u)
is the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
), and the pdf of
W
is

\displaystyle f_{W}(w)=f_{V}(\tfrac{1-w}{2})/2=f(\tfrac{1-w}{2})/2.

(4.27)

Proof 4.6.3
.

For
j^{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}\in{\cal J}_{n,n}
, we have

\displaystyle V=(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}2^{(i-n)r}}\overset{d}{=}(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}2^{-ir}}.

(4.28)

Surprisingly, by comparing (
4.28
) with (
2.6
), we find
\lim_{n\to\infty}{V}=U
, where
U
is the asymptotic projection. Thereby, as
n\to\infty
, the pdf of
V
will be exactly equal to the asymptotic CCS
f(u)
defined by (
2.9
). On knowing the pdf of
V
, the pdf of
W
can be easily obtained from (
4.25
). Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}c(j^{n})=1
, we have
f_{W}(w)\to f(\frac{1-w}{2})/2
.

We proceed to the case of
d=(n-1)
. There are
n
sequences in
{\cal J}_{n,n-1}
, and for every sequence, the pdf of
W(j^{n-1})
is different. We have the following theorem and corollaries (see
[
13
]
for the proofs).

Theorem 4.6.4

(Asymptotic Distribution of Normalized Shift Function for
d=(n-1)
)
.

For
1\leq k\leq n
, we define

\displaystyle j^{n-1}

\displaystyle=[n]\setminus(n-k+1)

\displaystyle=\{1,\dots,n-k,n-k+2,\dots,n\}\in{\cal J}_{n,n-1}.

(4.29)

Let
f(u)
be the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
).

•

For
k<\infty
, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle f_{V|j^{n-1}}(v)\to 2^{1-k(1-r)}\sum_{x^{k-1}\in\mathbb{B}^{k-1}}f((v-l(x^{k-1}))2^{kr})

(4.30)

and

\displaystyle f_{W|j^{n-1}}(w)\to 2^{-k(1-r)}\sum_{x^{k-1}\in\mathbb{B}^{k-1}}f((\tfrac{c(j^{n-1})-w}{2}-l(x^{k-1}))2^{kr}),

(4.31)

where
c(j^{n-1})=1-(2^{r}-1)2^{-kr}
and
l(x^{i})
is defined by (
2.4
).

•

f_{V|j^{n-1}}(v)\to f(v)
and
f_{W|j^{n-1}}(w)\to f(\frac{1-w}{2})/2
as
k\to\infty
.

Corollary 4.6.5

(Simple Relation Between
f_{V|j^{n-1}}(v)
and
f(u)
)
.

Let
f(u)
be the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
). Let
j^{n-1}=[n]\setminus(n-k+1)\in{\cal J}_{n,n-1}
, where
1\leq k\leq n
. Then

\displaystyle f(v)=\tfrac{1}{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(f_{V|j^{n-1}}(v)+f_{V|j^{n-1}}(v-(2^{r}-1)2^{-kr})\right).

(4.32)

Finally, let us discuss
f_{W|(n-1)}(w)
,
-1<w<1
, which is defined by (
4.26
), where
d=(n-1)
. The following corollary gives an interesting result about
f_{W|(n-1)}(w)
.

Corollary 4.6.6

(Asymptotic Form of
f_{W|{(n-1)}}(w)
)
.

As
n\to\infty
, we have
f_{W|(n-1)}(w)\to f(\frac{1-w}{2})
, where
f(u)
is the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
). In other words, for large
n
, we have
f_{W|(n-1)}(w)\approx f(\frac{1-w}{2})
.

Proof 4.6.7
.

There are
n
sequences in
{\cal J}_{n,n-1}
, and each sequence can be written as
j^{n-1}={[n]\setminus(n-k+1)}\in{\cal J}_{n,n-1}
, where
1\leq k\leq n
. As stated by
Theorem

4.6.4
, as
k\to\infty
, we have
f_{W|j^{n-1}}(w)\to f(\frac{1-w}{2})/2
. According to the definition (
4.26
),
f_{W|(n-1)}(w)
is actually the average of
n
functions, and these functions converge to
f(\frac{1-w}{2})/2
. Hence, this corollary holds obviously.

So far, we have solved the asymptotic distribution problem of
W(j^{d})
for
d=n
and
(n-1)
. It is certain that the above methodology can be easily extended to the general case of
d\approx n
. There are
\binom{n}{d}
sequences in
{\cal J}_{n,d}
. For each sequence
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
, we calculate the asymptotic pdf of
V(j^{d})
and then derive the asymptotic pdf of
W(j^{d})
. Since the procedure is very complex and boring, we would like to stop here for this issue. However, it deserves being spotted that
Corollary

4.6.6
can be extended to the general case
d\approx n
.

Corollary 4.6.8

(Asymptotic Form of
f_{W|d}(w)
)
.

Given
(n-d)<\infty
, as
n\to\infty
, we have
f_{W|d}(w)\to f(\frac{1-w}{2})
, where
f(u)
is the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
). In other words,
f_{W|d}(w)\approx f(\frac{1-w}{2})
for
d\approx n
.

4.6.3
Bridging HDS with CCS

Given the pdf of
W(j^{d})
, we can easily derive how the sequence
\omega_{j^{d}}
is distributed over
(-2^{nr},2^{nr})
. The following lemma answers how many terms in the sequence
\omega_{j^{d}}
fall into the interval
(-1,1)
.

Lemma 4.6.9

(Length of
j^{d}
-Active Sequence)
.

Given
d\approx n
, for
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
, the number of codewords
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
making
|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1
is
|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|\approx 2^{d+1-nr}f_{W|j^{d}}(0)
, where
f_{W|j^{d}}(w)
is the pdf of
W(j^{d})
.

Proof 4.6.10
.

Every
j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}
corresponds to
2^{d}
codewords in total, and for every
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
, the shift function
\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
is distributed over
(-2^{nr},2^{nr})
. According to the definition of
W(j^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|

\displaystyle=2^{d}\int_{-2^{-nr}}^{2^{-nr}}{f_{W|j^{d}}(w)}\,dw

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\approx}2^{d}\cdot 2^{1-nr}\cdot f_{W|j^{d}}(0)

\displaystyle=2^{d+1-nr}f_{W|j^{d}}(0),

(4.33)

where
(a)
comes from the fact that
f_{W|j^{d}}(w)
tends to be uniform over
(-2^{-nr},2^{-nr})
as
n\to\infty
.

In turn, given
f_{W|d}(w)
, we can easily derive how the sequence
\omega_{d}
is distributed over
(-2^{nr},2^{nr})
. The following lemma answers how many elements in the sequence
\omega_{d}
fall into the interval
(-1,1)
.

Lemma 4.6.11

(Length of
d
-Active Sequence)
.

For large
n
, given
d\approx n
, the number of codewords
b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
making
|\tau(j^{d},b^{d})|<1
is

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}{|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|}

\displaystyle\approx\binom{n}{d}2^{d+1-nr}f_{W|d}(0)

\displaystyle\approx\binom{n}{d}2^{d-nr}f(1/2),

(4.34)

where
f_{W|d}(w)
is defined by (
4.26
) and
f(u)
is the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
).

Theorem 4.6.12

(Fast Approximation of HDS)
.

For
d\approx n
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)\approx\binom{n}{d}2^{\alpha-nr-1}f(1/2),

(4.35)

where
\alpha={\bf 1}_{(d=n)}
and
f(u)
is the asymptotic CCS defined by (
2.9
).

Proof 4.6.13
.

According to (
4.19
), we have

\displaystyle\psi(n;n)

\displaystyle\approx 2^{-n}\cdot|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\approx}}2^{-n}\cdot 2^{n+1-nr}f_{W}(0)

\displaystyle=2^{1-nr}f_{W}(0)=2^{1-nr}f_{V}(1/2)/2

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}2^{-nr}f(1/2),

(4.36)

where
(a)
comes from
Lemma

4.6.9
and
(b)
comes from
Theorem

4.6.2
. According to (
4.18
), for
d<n
,

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)

\displaystyle\approx 2^{-(d+1)}\sum_{j^{d}\in{\cal J}_{n,d}}{|{\cal B}_{j^{d}}|}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\approx}}2^{-(d+1)}\binom{n}{d}2^{d-nr}f(1/2)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{d}2^{-nr-1}f(1/2),

(4.37)

where
(a)
comes from
Lemma

4.6.11
. Combining (
4.36
) with (
4.37
), we will obtain (
4.35
).

After comparing
Theorem

4.5.7
and
Theorem

4.5.6
with
Theorem

4.6.12
, it can be found that the computing complexity of
\psi(d;n)
is reduced from
{\cal O}(2^{d}\binom{n}{d})
to
{\cal O}(1)
.

Finally, we give a summary on the approximate formulas of
\psi(d;n)
. In

4.7.1
\psi(1)
and
\psi(2)
are Convergent

Corollary 4.7.1

(Convergence of
\psi(1)
)
.

As
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\psi(1)=\sum_{i=1}^{J_{1}}{\left(1-(1-2^{-r})2^{ir}\right)}<\infty,

where

\displaystyle J_{1}\triangleq-\left\lfloor\tfrac{1}{r}\log_{2}{(2^{r}-1)}\right\rfloor<\infty.

(4.39)

Proof 4.7.2
.

As we know,
{\cal J}_{n,1}=\{\{1\},\dots,\{n\}\}
, so when
d=1
,

\displaystyle\psi(1)=\left(\psi(1|0)+\psi(1|1)\right)/2,

where

\displaystyle\psi(1|b)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\left(1-|\tau(i,b)|\right)^{+}}.

Due to the symmetry,
\psi(1|0)=\psi(1|1)
, so we consider only
\psi(1|0)
below. According to (
3.5
), we have
\tau(i,0)=(1-2^{-r}){2^{ir}}>0
, which is monotonously increasing w.r.t.
i
. After solving

, we obtain

\displaystyle i\leq J_{1}

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\lceil-\tfrac{1}{r}\log_{2}{(1-2^{-r})}\right\rceil-1

\displaystyle=-\left\lfloor\tfrac{1}{r}\log_{2}{(2^{r}-1)}\right\rfloor<\infty.

Finally,
\psi(1)=\psi(1|0)=\psi(1|1)
.

(Convergence of

.

The closed form of

4.7.2
Convergence of
\psi(3)

In theory, if only
\psi(d)<\infty
, it can be calculated following the same methodology developed for
\psi(1)
and
\psi(2)
. However, the procedure will be more and more complex. What’s worse,
\psi(d)=\infty
often happens for
d\geq 3
, as observed in
[
17
]
. The following theorem gives the necessary and sufficient condition for the convergence of
\psi(3)
.

Theorem 4.7.8

(Necessary and Sufficient Condition for the Convergence of
\psi(3)
)
.

If there is no pair of integers
i\geq 1
and
j\geq 1
such that
2^{ir}(2^{jr}-1)=1
, then
\psi(3)<\infty
; otherwise,
\psi(3)=\infty
.

Proof 4.7.9
.

Since the proof is overly long, it is not included in this monograph. The reader may refer to
[
13
]
for a detail proof.

Corollary 4.7.10

(Sufficient Condition for the Convergence of
\psi(3)
)
.

If
2^{r}
is a transcendental number,
\psi(3)<\infty
.

Proof 4.7.11
.

This is a direct result of
Theorem

4.7.8
.

We have given the necessary and sufficient condition for the convergence of
\psi(3)
. Similarly, it is possible to deduce the necessary and sufficient condition for the convergence of
\psi(d)
for
d>3
. However, the procedure will become more and more complex. Actually, we strongly believe that if
2^{r}
is a transcendental number, then
\psi(d)<\infty
for any
d
, not just for
d=3
. However, we are not able to provide a strict proof at present, so we remain it as future work.

4.8

\psi(3;n)

For every pair of
i\geq 1
and
j\geq 1
, there must be an overlapping factor
r\in[0,1]
making
2^{ir}(2^{jr}-1)=1
. Some examples of
(i,j)
-pairs and the corresponding overlapping factors satisfying
2^{ir}(2^{jr}-1)=1
are given in
Fig.

4.2
. It can be seen that when
2^{r}
is the golden ratio,
i=j=1
will make
2^{ir}(2^{jr}-1)=1
. Actually, this is the largest
r
making
\psi(3)=\infty
, as proved by the following lemma. As
i
or
j
increases, the overlapping factor making
2^{ir}(2^{jr}-1)=1
will be smaller.

Lemma 4.8.1

(Sign of
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})
)
.

Let
\varphi
denote the golden ratio. Depending on the relation between
2^{r}
and
\varphi
, the value of
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})
will have different signs.

•

2^{r}=\varphi
: We have
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})\geq 0
and the equality holds iff
(j_{3}-j_{2})=(j_{2}-j_{1})=1
.

•

2^{r}>\varphi
: We have
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})>0
for every
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}
and hence
\psi(3)<\infty
.

•

2^{r}<\varphi
: The value of
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})
can be positive or negative, and for some special
2^{r}
, may be
0
.

Proof 4.8.2
.

We can write
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})
as

\displaystyle(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})=2^{j_{1}r}(2^{(j_{3}-j_{1})r}-2^{(j_{2}-j_{1})r}-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2^{j_{1}r}(2^{(j_{2}-j_{1})r}(2^{((j_{3}-j_{1})-(j_{2}-j_{1}))r}-1)-1)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2^{ir}(2^{jr}(2^{kr}-1)-1),

where
i=j_{1}\geq 1
,
j=(j_{2}-j_{1})\geq 1
, and
k=((j_{3}-j_{1})-(j_{2}-j_{1}))=(j_{3}-j_{2})\geq 1
. Clearly,
2^{jr}(2^{kr}-1)>0
and it is strictly increasing w.r.t. both
j
and
k
. Thus
2^{jr}(2^{kr}-1)\geq 2^{r}(2^{r}-1)
. After solving
2^{r}(2^{r}-1)=1
, we obtain
2^{r}=\varphi\approx 1.618
and
r\approx 0.6942
. Hence, if
2^{r}=\varphi
, we have

\displaystyle(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})

\displaystyle=2^{ir}(2^{jr}(2^{kr}-1)-1)

\displaystyle\geq(2^{jr}(2^{kr}-1)-1)

\displaystyle\geq(2^{r}(2^{r}-1)-1)=0,

where the equality holds iff
k=(j_{3}-j_{2})=j=(j_{2}-j_{1})=1
.

Since
2^{r}(2^{r}-1)
is strictly increasing w.r.t.
2^{r}
and
r
, if
2^{r}>\varphi
, then
(2^{j_{3}r}-2^{j_{2}r}-2^{j_{1}r})\geq(2^{r}(2^{r}-1)-1)>0
for any
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<j_{3}
and thus
\psi(3)<\infty
. Similarly, the third bullet point also holds.

Now we wonder whether it is possible to find the analytical form of
\psi(3;n)
even if
\psi(3;n)
does not converge? Below we first give the general form of
\psi(3;n)
when it does not converge, and then derive the concrete forms of
\psi(3;n)
in two special cases. The proofs of the following theorem and corollaries can be found in
[
13
]
.

Theorem 4.8.3

(Linearity of
\psi(3;n)
for Large

)
.

If there exist one or more pairs of integers

and
j\geq 1
such that

, then for large

will increase linearly. Let

denote the set of all pairs of integers

and

satisfying

. Every

will cause a divergent term

, and

\displaystyle\psi(3;n)

\displaystyle\approx c_{0}+(1/4)\sum_{(i,j)\in{\cal P}}{(n-(i+j))}

\displaystyle=c_{1}+n|{\cal P}|/4,

(4.43)

where

is the sum of convergent terms,

is the cardinality of

, and

.

As shown by (

for a specific

includes only one

Corollary 4.8.4

(

.

If

Remark 4.8.6

(Propagation of Divergence)
.

Finally, we would like to end this sub-section with an interesting phenomenon. If for some overlapping factor
r
, there are two or more pairs of
i\geq 1
and
j\geq 1
such that
2^{ir}(2^{jr}-1)=1
, then
\psi(4;n)
and
\psi(6;n)
may not converge. For example, if

x^{3}-x-1=x^{5}-x^{4}-1=0
, then

\displaystyle x^{2}(x^{5}-x^{4}-1)+(x^{3}-x-1)=x^{7}-x^{6}+x^{3}-x^{2}-x-1=0,

which will cause a divergent term
(n-7)
and further make
\psi(6;n)
divergent. Meanwhile, we have

\displaystyle(x^{5}-x^{4})-(x^{3}-x)

\displaystyle=x^{5}-x^{4}-x^{3}+x

\displaystyle=x(x^{4}-x^{3}-x^{2}+1)=0,

and thus
(x^{4}-x^{3}-x^{2}+1)=0
, which will cause a divergent term
(n-4)
and further make
\psi(4;n)
divergent. By repeatedly combining the above zero elements, we can even observe that
\psi(d;n)
is divergent for other

. This phenomenon is coined as Propagation of Divergence in
[
13
]
and still remains a difficult open question.

Chapter 5
Tailed Overlapped Arithmetic Codes

From
Fig.

2.8
, the reader may have found that the performance of overlapped arithmetic coeds is very poor and the residual decoding errors cannot be removed even though the source and side information are strongly correlated
[
17
]
. This finding is very similar to the well-known error-floor phenomenon of LDPC codes, and can be theoretically explained with HDS. From
Tab.

4.3
, the reader may have found an important property of overlapped arithmetic codes: For small Hamming distance
d
,
e.g.
,
1
,
\psi(d;n)
does not converge to
0
as block length
n\to\infty
. Since the minimum Hamming distance of overlapped arithmetic codes is always
1
, closely-spaced codewords in the same coset cannot be removed by increasing code length, which explains why the performance of overlapped arithmetic codes is so poor.

The performance of overlapped arithmetic codes can be improved by mapping the last few source symbols of a block, called
tail
, to non-overlapped intervals
[
20
,
21
]
. This improvement can be theoretically explained from the viewpoint of HDS: By mapping the tail of a block to non-overlapped intervals, the minimum Hamming distance of overlapped arithmetic codes may be increased and closely-spaced codewords in the same coset can be removed
[
16
]
.

5.1
Principle

Now we divide each source block
x^{n}
into body
x^{n-t}
and tail
x_{n-t+1}^{n}
. Every tail symbol is always coded at rate
1
, so the mapping rule is:
0\rightarrow[0,2^{-1})
and
1\rightarrow[2^{-1},1)
. To compress
x^{n}
at the average rate
R
, every body symbol should be coded at rate

\displaystyle r=\frac{nR-t}{n-t}\leq R,

(5.1)

so the mapping rule is:
0\rightarrow[0,2^{-r})
and
1\rightarrow[(1-2^{-r}),1)
. Obviously,
r
is strictly decreasing w.r.t.
t
. To guarantee
r\geq 0
,
t
can not be larger than
nR
, so
t\in[0:nR]
, where
nR
is assumed to be an integer for simplicity. If
t=0
and
r=R
, it is called a
tailless
overlapped arithmetic code; while if
t>0
and
r<R
, it is called a
tailed
overlapped arithmetic code. According to (
2.4
), the final interval after coding
x^{n}
is
[l(x^{n}),l(x^{n})+2^{-nR})
, where

\displaystyle l(x^{n})

\displaystyle=(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n-t}{x_{i}2^{-ir}}+2^{-(n-t)r}\sum_{i=1}^{t}{x_{i+(n-t)}2^{-i}}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}(2^{r}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n-t}{x_{i}2^{-ir}}+2^{n(1-R)}\sum_{i=n-t+1}^{n}{x_{i}2^{-i}},

(5.2)

where
(a)
comes from
(n-t)(1-r)=n(1-R)
. Since
nR=(n-t)r+t
, according to (
2.21
), we have

\displaystyle s(x^{n})=2^{nR}l(x^{n})\overset{d}{=}2^{t}(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n-t}{x_{i}2^{ir}}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{x_{i+(n-t)}2^{i-1}}.

(5.3)

The normalized final interval is
[s(x^{n}),s(x^{n})+1)
and
s(x^{n})\leq(2^{nR}-1)
. It can also be seen that
m(x^{n})\triangleq\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil\in[0:2^{nR})
.

5.2
Hamming Distance Spectrum

Let
x_{i}\in\mathbb{B}
,
y_{i}\in\mathbb{B}
, and
z_{i}=x_{i}\oplus y_{i}\in\mathbb{B}
. Let
\{\pm 1\}\triangleq\{-1,1\}
and
\{0,\pm 1\}\triangleq\{-1,0,1\}
. We define the following ternary variable

\displaystyle w_{i}\triangleq(x_{i}\oplus y_{i})(1-2x_{i})=z_{i}(1-2x_{i})\in\{0,\pm 1\}.

Clearly,
w_{i}=0
if
z_{i}=0
and
w_{i}=\pm 1
if
z_{i}=1
. Conversely, if
w_{i}=\pm 1
, we have
z_{i}=1
and
x_{i}=(1-w_{i})/2
; otherwise,
i.e.
,
w_{i}=0
, we can get
z_{i}=0
, but
x_{i}
is unknown. Thus, given
w^{n}
,
z^{n}
is fully determined, but
x^{n}
is partially determined. Let
d=|w^{n}|
be the support (number of nonzero elements) of
w^{n}
and let

\displaystyle j^{d}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{d}\}\subseteq[n]\triangleq\{1,\dots,n\},

where
1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{d}\leq n
, be the set of the indices of nonzero elements of
w^{n}
. In other words,
w_{i}=\pm 1
if
i\in j^{d}
and
w_{i}=0
if
i\in[n]\setminus j^{d}
. Actually, every
w^{n}
leads
2^{n-d}
different
x^{n}
’s. From
x^{n}
, we draw all elements indexed by
j^{d}
to form a sub-vector
x_{j^{d}}=b^{d}\in\mathbb{B}^{d}
. Then
w_{j^{d}}=z_{j^{d}}(1-2x_{j^{d}})=(1-2x_{j^{d}})\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
. Now we define

\displaystyle\tau_{n,r}(w^{n})

\displaystyle\triangleq(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n}{w_{i}2^{ir}}=(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i\in j^{d}}{w_{i}2^{ir}}

\displaystyle=(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i\in j^{d}}{(1-2x_{i})2^{ir}}=\tau(j^{d},b^{d}),

(5.4)

where
\tau(j^{d},b^{d})
is defined by (
3.5
). Following (
5.3
), we define

\displaystyle\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)

\displaystyle\triangleq 2^{t}(1-2^{-r})\sum_{i=1}^{n-t}{w_{i}2^{ir}}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}{w_{i+(n-t)}2^{i-1}}

\displaystyle=2^{t}\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})+\tau_{t,1}(w_{n-t+1}^{n}).

(5.5)

According to (
4.9
), the following theorem holds obviously.

Theorem 5.2.1

(HDS of Tailed Overlapped Arithmetic Codes)
.

Let
t
be tail length of an overlapped arithmetic code. If body symbols are coded at rate
r
, then as code length
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)

\displaystyle\to 2^{\alpha-d}\sum_{w^{n}:|w^{n}|=d}{\left(1-|\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)|\right)^{+}}

\displaystyle=2^{\alpha-d}\sum_{w^{n}:|w^{n}|=d}{\left(1-|2^{t}\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})+\tau_{t,1}(w_{n-t+1}^{n})|\right)^{+}},

(5.6)

where
\alpha={\bf 1}_{d=n}
and
(\cdot)^{+}=\max(0,\cdot)
.

5.3
Closely-Spaced Mate Codewords

Experiments show that tailed overlapped arithmetic codes are better than tailless overlapped arithmetic codes
[
20
,
21
]
. Below, we will reveal that closely-spaced mate codewords can be removed by increasing tail length, which explains the superiority of tailed overlapped arithmetic codes over tailless overlapped arithmetic codes. Due to computation complexity and to keep the exposition simple, we consider below only
\psi(1;n)
and
\psi(2;n)
. To begin with, we give the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3.1
.

If
|z^{n-t}|=0
and
|z_{n-t+1}^{n}|>0
, then
m(x^{n})\neq m(x^{n}\oplus z^{n})
. Conversely, if
|z^{n}|>0
and
m(x^{n})=m(x^{n}\oplus z^{n})
, then
|z^{n-t}|>0
.

Proof 5.3.2
.

If
|z^{n-t}|=0
, then
\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)=\tau_{t,1}(w_{n-t+1}^{n})\in\mathbb{Z}
, implying
m(x^{n})\neq m(x^{n}\oplus z^{n})
for any
|z_{n-t+1}^{n}|>0
.

In plain words, if a pair of codewords coexist in the same coset, it is impossible that these two codewords differ from each other only in tails. With the help of
Lemma

5.3.1
, we explain below why
\psi(1;n)
and
\psi(2;n)
will tend to
0
as
t
increases.

Theorem 5.3.3

(
1
-Away Mate Codeword Pairs)
.

As tail length
t
increases,
1
-away mate codeword pairs will be removed and hence the minimum Hamming distance of overlapped arithmetic codes is at least
2
.

Proof 5.3.4
.

Given
|w^{n}|=1
, there are only two cases:

•

|w^{n-t}|=0
and
|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=1
;

•

|w^{n-t}|=1
and
|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=0
.

According to
Lemma

5.3.1
, in the former case,
m(x^{n})\neq m(x^{n}\oplus z^{n})
holds always. Hence, we need to consider only the later case, which is followed by
\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)=2^{t}\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})
. Given
|w^{n-t}|=1
, we can obtain from (
5.5
) that

\displaystyle|\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})|\geq(1-2^{-r})2^{r}=(2^{r}-1)

and further
|\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)|\geq 2^{t}(2^{r}-1)
. If we suppose
t\leq{nR\over 2}
, it can be deduced from (
5.1
) that
r\geq{R\over 2-R}
and thus

\displaystyle\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)=2^{t}(2^{r}-1)\geq 2^{t}(2^{R/(2-R)}-1).

(5.7)

Increasing
t
will certainly make the right hand side of (
5.7
) greater than
1
. An example of
2^{t}(2^{r}-1)
is given in
Fig.

5.1
for
n=64
and
R=0.5
, which shows that
2^{t}(2^{r}-1)
increases monotonously for small

. Hence, it is possible to make
|\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)|\geq 1
hold always for all
w^{n}
’s satisfying
|w^{n}|=1
by increasing
t
. In other words,
1
-away mate codeword pairs can be removed by simply increasing tail length
t
, even for finite code length
n
.

Theorem 5.3.5

(
2
-Away Mate Codewords Pairs)
.

Given
n/t=\infty
, at least for
R>\log_{2}\frac{3}{2}\approx 0.585
,
2
-away mate codeword pairs can be removed by simply increasing tail length
t
and the minimum Hamming distance of overlapped arithmetic codes is at least
3
.

Proof 5.3.6
.

Given
|w^{n}|=2
, there are only three cases:

•

|w^{n-t}|=0
and
|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=2
;

•

|w^{n-t}|=2
and
|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=0
;

•

|w^{n-t}|=|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=1
.

According to
Lemma

5.3.1
, in the first case,
m(x^{n})\neq m(x^{n}\oplus z^{n})
, so we need to consider only the later two cases.

In the case of
|w^{n-t}|=2
and
|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=0
, we have
\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)=2^{t}\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})
. It is easy to get from (
5.5
) that

\displaystyle|\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})|

\displaystyle\geq(1-2^{-r})(2^{2r}-2^{r})

\displaystyle=(2^{r}-1)^{2}.

Still suppose
t\leq{nR\over 2}
, which is followed by

\displaystyle 2^{t}(2^{r}-1)^{2}\geq 2^{t}(2^{R/(2-R)}-1)^{2}.

(5.8)

Increasing
t
will make the right hand side of (
5.8
) not smaller than
1
and in turn
|\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)|\geq 2^{t}(2^{r}-1)^{2}\geq 1
holds always for all
w^{n}
’s satisfying
|w^{n-t}|=2
and
|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=0
. An example of

is shown in
Fig.

5.1
, where
n=64
and

.

Finally, we consider the case of
|w^{n-t}|=|w_{n-t+1}^{n}|=1
. We have

•

|\tau_{n-t,r}(w^{n-t})|=(2^{r}-1)2^{ir}
, where
i\in[0:(n-t))
; and

•

|\tau_{t,1}(w_{n-t+1}^{n})|=2^{j}
, where
j\in[0:t)
.

For simplicity, let
\gamma_{i}\triangleq(2^{r}-1)2^{t+ir}
. Then
|\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)|=|\gamma_{i}\pm 2^{j}|
, where
(i,j)\in[0:(n-t))\times[0:t)
.

•

Since
\gamma_{i}>0
and
2^{j}\geq 1
, we have
|\gamma_{i}+2^{j}|>1
.

Since

5.4
Effects of Tail Length

Following (
2.23
), we define the bitstream projection of tailed overlapped arithmetic codes. According to (
2.27
), we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}U_{i-1}=2^{-r}U_{i}+X_{i}(1-2^{-r}),&i\in[1:(n-t)]\\
U_{i-1}=(U_{i}+X_{i})/2,&i\in[(n-t+1):n].\end{cases}

Especially,
U_{0}=2^{-nR}\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil
and
U_{n}=\lceil s(x^{n})\rceil-s(x^{n})
. We also define CCS for tailed overlapped arithmetic codes. Following the analyses in
Sect.

2.4
, we can easily know
f_{i}(u)=\Pi(u)
for
i\in[(n-t):n]
and

\displaystyle f_{i-1}(u)=2^{r-1}\left(f_{i}(u2^{r})+f_{i}((u-(1-2^{-r}))2^{r})\right)

(5.10)

for
i\in[1:(n-t)]
. The overall metric of the
x^{i}
-path can still be given by (
2.82
). Especially for
i\in[(n-t):n]
, the term
\log{f_{i}(u(x^{i}))}
can be ignored because
f_{i}(u)=\Pi(u)
, and hence (
2.82
) can be reduced to

\displaystyle\sigma(x^{i})=|x^{i}|\cdot\log{\epsilon}+(i-|x^{i}|)\cdot\log(1-\epsilon).

Similarly, following the analyses in
Sect.

2.6
, the block rate loss of tailed overlapped arithmetic codes is

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}(nR-H(M))=-h(U)=\int_{0}^{1}f(u)\log_{2}{f(u)}\,du\geq 0,

where
U
is the asymptotic initial projection defined by (
2.6
) and
f(u)
is the asymptotic initial CCS defined by (
2.9
). For
n
sufficiently large, the cardinality of
{\cal C}_{m}
is

\displaystyle|{\cal C}_{m}|\approx f(m2^{-nR})\cdot 2^{n(1-R)}=f(m2^{-nR})\cdot 2^{(n-t)(1-r)}

and the
Expected Coset Cardinality
(ECC) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]\approx 2^{n(1-R)}\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}=2^{(n-t)(1-r)}\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}.

The normalized ECC is

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]}{2^{n(1-R)}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[|{\cal C}_{M}|]}{2^{(n-t)(1-r)}}=\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}\geq 1.

We have learned that from the viewpoint of HDS, larger tail length
t
has a positive effect of removing closely-spaced mate codewords. Now we discuss the effect of tail length
t
from the viewpoint of CCS. For fairness, the average rate
R
of
x^{n}
should be fixed by coding the body
x^{n-t}
at rate
r=\frac{nR-t}{n-t}
, which is monotonously decreasing w.r.t.
t
. As analyzed in
Sect.

2.4
, the asymptotic initial CCS
f(u)
will be spikier and spikier as
r
decreases. Two extreme cases are
f(u)=\Pi(u)
for
r=1
and
f(u)=\delta(u-0.5)
for
r=0
. Hence both
-h(U)
and
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}
are monotonously decreasing w.r.t.
r
and in turn monotonously increasing w.r.t.
t
. That means, greater
t
will lead to larger rate loss and higher decoding complexity (as we know, the expected number of codewords searched by a full-search decoder is equal to the ECC). Thus from the viewpoint of CCS, larger tail length
t
has a negative effect of increasing rate loss and decoding complexity. In a word,
t
should be carefully selected to reach a compromise between HDS and CCS.

Remark 5.4.1

(A Weird Phenomenon When
t=nR
)
.

If
t=nR
, we have
r=(1-2^{-r})=0
. In this extreme case,
x_{n-t+1}^{n}
is transmitted directly without coding, while
x^{n-t}
is not transmitted. Now (
5.10
) becomes
f_{i-1}(u)=f_{i}(u)
and thus
f_{0}(u)=\dots=f_{n}(n)=\Pi(u)
, which leads to
h(U)=0
and
\int_{0}^{1}{f^{2}(u)\,du}=1
. Hence, there is no rate loss and the complexity of full-search decoder is minimized.

Meanwhile, since
r=0
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)=\tau(w^{n};n,nR,0)=\tau_{t,1}(w_{n-t+1}^{n})\in\mathbb{Z},

showing that
\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)
purely depends on
w_{n-t+1}^{n}
and has nothing to do with
w^{n-t}
. In addition, since
\tau(w^{n};n,t,r)
is always an integer,
x^{n}
and
y^{n}=(x^{n}\oplus z^{n})
cannot coexist in the same coset if
z_{n-t+1}^{n}\neq 0^{t}
. In other words,
\psi(d;n)=0
for
d>(n-t)=n(1-R)
. Finally,

\displaystyle\psi(d;n)=\begin{cases}\binom{n(1-R)}{d},&d\in[0:n(1-R)]\\
0,&d\in(n(1-R):n],\end{cases}

(5.11)

showing that
\psi(d;n)
for small
d
does not converge to
0
, i.e., closely-spaced mate codewords cannot be removed.

Chapter 6
Conclusion

This monograph begins with arithmetic codes by emphasizing two implementation issues—
underflow handling
and
bitstream termination
. After a brief description of overlapped arithmetic codes, this monograph introduces related theoretical advances. It is revealed that overlapped arithmetic codes are actually a kind of nonlinear coset codes partitioning source space into unequal-sized cosets, and hence have two fundamental properties—
Coset Cardinality Spectrum
(CCS), which is a continuous function describing how coset cardinality is distributed, and
Hamming Distance Spectrum
(HDS), which is a discrete function describing how inter-mate Hamming distance is distributed, where mates refer to codewords belonging to the same coset. For both CCS and HDS, theoretical formulas are derived, and their tight connection is found; while for CCS, numerical algorithms are also given. On the basis of CCS, the decoding algorithm of overlapped arithmetic codes is derived. Experimental results show that aided by CCS, the performance of low-complexity decoder can be significantly improved. To deduce HDS, a powerful analysis tool—
Coexisting Interval
is developed, which can also be used to deduce the frame error rate of overlapped arithmetic codes.

The performance of overlapped arithmetic codes is very poor, if all source symbols of a block are mapped to partially overlapped intervals, because the minimum Hamming distance is always 1. The performance of overlapped arithmetic codes can be improved by mapping the last few source symbols of a block, called
tail
, to non-overlapped intervals. This improvement can be theoretically explained from the viewpoint of HDS,
i.e.
, the minimum Hamming distance of overlapped arithmetic codes may be increased by mapping the
tail
of a block to non-overlapped intervals. This monograph discusses the effect of tail length on overlapped arithmetic codes from the aspects of both HDS and CCS. It is shown that closely-spaced mate codewords can be removed by increasing tail length, which perfectly explains the superiority of tailed overlapped arithmetic codes over tailless overlapped arithmetic codes. It is also shown that increasing tail length will cause higher decoding complexity and larger rate loss meanwhile. These findings indicate that increasing tail length usually brings both positive (removing closely-spaced mate codewords) and negative (higher decoding complexity and larger rate loss) effects, so tail length should be tuned carefully to optimize the overall performance. At the same time, it is also found that the effects of tail length are sometimes weird, so how to optimize overlapped arithmetic codes still remains a very difficult open question.

However, it should be pointed out that this monograph is limited to uniformly distributed binary sources. For overlapped arithmetic codes, there have been some advances on non-uniform binary sources
[
15
,
11
]
and uniform non-binary sources
[
12
]
.

All theoretical analyses of this monograph are perfectly verified by experiments. A software package of this monograph for result reproduction is available on
https://github.com/fy79/NOW-FTCIT
.
