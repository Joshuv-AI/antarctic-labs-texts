Clinical profiles, management, and outcomes of acute coronary syndrome patients in hospitals without reperfusion therapy: a multicentre study in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia
Authors: Begashaw Belay Abichu, Gashaw Solela, Samson Mulugeta, Nahom Addisu Bekele, Amanuel Zeleke, Roman Negewo, Chala Fekadu Oljira, Yidnekachew Asrat Birhan, Sintayehu Abebe, Dejuma Yadeta, Bekele Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries. Although ACS has been studied in Ethiopia, there is limited evidence on patients managed without reperfusion therapy, particularly across multiple tertiary public hospitals. This study aimed to assess the clinical profiles, in-hospital management, and outcomes of non-reperfused ACS patients in a multicentre Ethiopian setting.MethodsWe conducted a multicentre retrospective study of adult ACS patients admitted to three tertiary public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, between February 25, 2023, and August 31, 2024, where reperfusion therapy was unavailable. All eligible patients were consecutively included. ACS included unstable angina, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, diagnosed based on ischemic symptoms, elevated troponins, ECG changes (new ST/T-wave changes, new left bundle branch block, or pathological Q-waves), and/or imaging evidence of myocardial injury or regional wall motion abnormalities. Clinical characteristics, management, and outcomes were extracted from electronic health records. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 and multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality (P < 0.05).ResultsA total of 154 ACS patients were included (mean age 58.4 ± 12.2 years; 63.6% male), with STEMI accounting for 79.9%. The median time from symptom onset to hospital arrival was 24 h (IQR 11.5–72.0). Chest pain (87.7%, typical in 53.2%) and shortness of breath (50.6%) were the most common presenting symptoms. Hypertension (55.2%) and diabetes (44.2%) were the most frequent comorbidities. Most patients received dual antiplatelet therapy (98.7%), statins (94.8%), and therapeutic anticoagulation (90.3%). The in-hospital mortality rate was 26.6%, with a median hospital stay of 8 days (IQR 4–10). Independent predictors of mortality were older age (AOR 1.04; 95% CI 1.00–1.09), female sex (AOR 3.90; 95% CI 1.40–10.93), higher respiratory rate (AOR 1.18; 95% CI 1.04–1.35), and lower systolic blood pressure (AOR 0.97; 95% CI 0.95–0.99).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated a high rate of in-hospital mortality among ACS patients in hospitals without reperfusion therapies. Older age, female sex, higher respiratory rate, and lower systolic blood pressure were independently associated with mortality, emphasizing the importance of early risk assessment and vigilant monitoring of vital signs. Expanding the availability of thrombolysis, implementing structured prehospital ACS education programs, and establishing standardized ACS care pathways are imperative to achieving optimal outcomes in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Clinical profiles, In-hospital management, Outcomes, Without reperfusion therapy

Introduction

Coronary artery disease is the leading contributor to cardiovascular mortality and disability, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), a major clinical manifestation resulting from sudden reduction in coronary blood flow, includes unstable angina (UA), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) [3]. It accounts for millions of cases globally each year and substantial healthcare utilization, particularly in high-income settings [4]. Despite this, outcomes remain significantly worse in resource-limited settings where access to timely and effective therapies is limited.

A 10-year systematic review in SSA reported a rising incidence of ACS, driven by poorly controlled cardiovascular risk factors, rapid urbanization, changes in lifestyle and diet, and an aging population [5]. In Ethiopia, a systematic review of eight studies found hypertension and diabetes mellitus to be the most prevalent risk factors, affecting 54.8% and 38.5% of patients, respectively [6].

Delays in seeking medical attention are a significant challenge in the management of ACS in Ethiopia. Previous studies have reported that the average time from symptom onset to hospital presentation ranges from 3 to 3.8 days, with most patients arriving 12 to 48 h after symptom onset. Common presenting symptoms included chest pain, shortness of breath, diaphoresis, and nausea or vomiting [7–11].

In-hospital mortality related to ACS varies widely across regions, and data from sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remain limited. A systematic review of observational studies in the region reported hospital mortality ranging from 1.2% to 24.5%, depending on patient characteristics and the availability of revascularization procedures [5]. In Ethiopia, in-hospital mortality rates from ACS range from 3.8% to 27.4%, with independent predictors including older age, delayed presentation, advanced Killip class, STEMI, absence of beta blocker therapy, cardiogenic shock, and reduced left ventricular function [7–10, 12, 13].

Despite the growing burden of ACS in Ethiopia, multicentre evidence on clinical characteristics, management, and outcomes in settings without reperfusion therapy remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes of ACS patients admitted to selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, where reperfusion therapy was not available.

Methods and materials

Study design, period, and area

A multicentre retrospective study was conducted among ACS patients admitted between February 25, 2023, and August 31, 2024, to three public referral hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: Yekatit 12 Hospital Medical College (Y12HMC), Menelik II Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (MIICSH), and Ras Desta Damtew Hospital (RDDH). These hospitals serve as major referral centres for cardiovascular emergencies, including ACS, receiving patients from surrounding health facilities. Reperfusion services for ACS, such as percutaneous coronary intervention or thrombolytic therapy, were not available at any of the participating hospitals during the study period, and all patients were managed according to available guideline-directed medical therapy.

Population and eligibility criteria

The source population consisted of all adult patients aged 18 years and older who presented to the emergency department or were admitted to the medical intensive care unit or wards of the participating hospitals. The study population included those with a confirmed diagnosis of ACS during the study period. Diagnosis of ACS was based on clinical symptoms, electrocardiographic findings, and biomarker testing, with troponin assays available in all centres. Cases of UA were included when biomarkers were negative but clinical and ECG findings supported the diagnosis.

Only patients with complete data for predefined essential variables were included. These variables included age, sex, presenting symptoms, admission vital signs, electrocardiographic findings, cardiac biomarker results, and in-hospital outcomes such as mortality, complications, and discharge status. Patients with missing data in any of these variables were excluded. Non-essential variables, including lipid profile, renal function tests, and imaging findings such as echocardiography and chest X-ray, were included when available and analyzed using available case analysis without affecting eligibility.

Operational definitions

ACS includes STEMI, NSTEMI, or UA [3]. Diagnosis was based on ischemic symptoms, elevated cardiac biomarkers (particularly troponins), electrocardiographic changes such as new ST-segment or T-wave alterations, new left bundle branch block, or pathological Q-waves, or imaging evidence of new myocardial injury or regional wall motion abnormalities [3].

Killip classification stratifies ACS patients according to the severity of heart failure at hospital admission [14].

Without reperfusion therapy refers to the lack of both PCI and thrombolytic treatment, which are essential for timely restoration of coronary blood flow in ACS patients [3].

In-hospital outcomes include complications, death, length of hospital stay, and discharge status.

Sample size and study population

The minimum required sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, based on a reported in-hospital mortality rate of 7.5% [13], with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error. This yielded a minimum of 107 patients, which was increased to 118 after adding a 10% contingency for incomplete or missing data. All adult patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period were included, resulting in a total of 154 participants. Thus, the study employed a census-based design, capturing the entire target population and eliminating the need for sampling.

Study variables

The dependent variable was in-hospital mortality (yes/no).

Data collection procedure and data quality management

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire designed to extract relevant information from patients’ electronic health records. Three trained general practitioners served as data abstractors. They received a two-day training on the study objectives, questionnaire content, and standardized procedures for data abstraction. A pilot test was conducted on 10 patient records to ensure clarity and feasibility of the data collection tool. Inter-abstractor agreement was assessed, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the principal investigator to ensure consistency. The principal investigator also closely supervised the data collection process and performed periodic checks to validate the accuracy and completeness of the extracted data.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarized clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and in-hospital outcomes of ACS patients. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± SD or median (IQR), depending on distribution. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square (χ²) test. Variables with substantial missing data, including lipid profiles, renal function tests, and echocardiography, were excluded from inferential analyses. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality. Variables with P < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model to control for confounders, following established guidance on purposeful variable selection [15]. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF). Model fit was evaluated with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P = 0.772). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of ACS patients

Out of 166 patients diagnosed with ACS, data from 154 eligible patients were included in the final analysis, while 12 patients were excluded due to missing the predefined essential variables. Of these, 79 patients were from Y12HMC, 42 from RDDH, and 33 from MIICSH. The study population had a mean age of 58.4 ± 12.2 years (range: 32–93), with most patients aged 40–60 years, and 63.6% were male. Age and sex distributions were comparable between STEMI and NSTE-ACS groups (Table 1).

At presentation, the mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) was 129.1 ± 31.4 mmHg, with 7.8% of patients exhibiting SBP < 90 mmHg, indicative of hypotension, and the mean diastolic blood pressure (DBP) was 76.3 ± 16.2 mmHg. Laboratory and imaging results of ACS patients

All patients underwent troponin testing, with 97.9% demonstrating elevated levels, confirming myocardial injury. Renal function was assessed in 93 patients (60.4%), of whom 21.5% had elevated creatinine (≥ 1.2 mg/dL). Lipid profiles were available in a smaller subset: HDL cholesterol for 49 patients (31.8%), of whom 57.1% had low HDL (< 40 mg/dL), and LDL cholesterol for 51 patients (33.1%), with 67% showing elevated LDL (≥ 100 mg/dL). These results should be interpreted cautiously due to the substantial proportion of missing data.

Electrocardiography (ECG) was performed in all patients. The most common findings were significant ST-segment elevation (79.9%) and ST-segment depression (13%), followed by tachyarrhythmias (5.2%), bradyarrhythmias (4.5%), T-wave inversion (4.5%), new left bundle branch block (1.9%), and Q waves (1.3%). Other ECG abnormalities were observed in 3.9% of patients, while 1.9% had non-remarkable findings. Chest X-rays were performed in 80 patients (52%), most commonly showing pulmonary edema (26.3%), cardiomegaly (20%), and pleural effusion (13.8%). Transthoracic echocardiography was performed in 148 patients (96%), with regional wall motion abnormalities being the most frequent findings (Fig. 1).

In-hospital management of ACS

Within the first 24 h of presentation, 124 patients (80.5%) received a loading dose of aspirin and 120 (77.9%) received a loading dose of clopidogrel. Overall, 152 patients (98.7%) were maintained on dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), with 153 (99.4%) receiving a maintenance dose of aspirin and 152 (98.7%) receiving a maintenance dose of clopidogrel. Two patients could not receive DAPT due to bleeding events. Statin therapy was initiated in 146 patients (94.8%), and therapeutic anticoagulation was administered in 90.3% of cases.

Beta-blockers were prescribed in 64.3% of patients, while angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) were administered in 42.2%. Morphine was used in 34.4% of patients, and vasoactive agents (vasopressors or inotropes) in 23.4%. Notably, nitrates were used in only 1.9% of patients. Sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors and calcium channel blockers were prescribed in 5.8% and 3.9% of patients, respectively. Cardioversion or defibrillation was performed in seven patients, and one patient required mechanical ventilation. No patients received fibrinolytic therapy or primary percutaneous coronary intervention during hospitalization.

In-hospital outcomes of ACS patients

Overall, in-hospital mortality was 26.6%, with 90.2% of deaths due to cardiovascular causes. Mortality by ACS subtype and Killip class was assessed using the chi-square test. Among 123 STEMI patients, 34 (27.6%) died, compared with 7 of 31 (22.6%) NSTE-ACS patients (χ² = 0.325, P value = 0.569). Mortality differed markedly by Killip class: 22 of 116 patients (18.9%) with Killip I–II died versus 19 of 38 (50.0%) with Killip III–IV (χ² = 14.112, df = 1, P value < 0.001), indicating higher Killip class was strongly associated with increased mortality. The median hospital stay was 8 days (IQR: 4–10; range: 1–37). The most frequent ACS-related complications were cardiogenic shock (16.2%), heart failure (15.6%), and major arrhythmias (10%). Most patients improved by discharge, though a few required referral to other facilities (Table 4).

Predictors of in-hospital mortality among ACS patients

The bivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that several factors were significantly associated with increased risk of in-hospital mortality among patients with ACS. These included female sex (COR, 2.69; 95% CI, 1.29–4.69; P value = 0.008), presence of orthopnea (COR, 3.25; 95% CI, 1.19–8.88; P value = 0.022), and symptoms of nausea and/or vomiting (COR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.00–4.28; P value = 0.049). Additionally, higher HR (COR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.05; P value = 0.002), increased RR (COR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.10–1.34; P value < 0.001), advanced Killip class [III/IV] (COR, 4.27; 95% CI, 1.94–9.39; P value < 0.001), and lower SBP (COR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–0.98; P value < 0.001) were associated with in-hospital mortality (Table 5). Variables such as time to presentation and ACS subtype were not included in the multivariable analysis, as they did not meet the pre-specified statistical inclusion criterion in the bivariate analysis (p < 0.25).

In the multivariable logistic regression model, older age (AOR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.00–1.09; P value = 0.046), female sex (AOR, 3.90; 95% CI, 1.40–10.93; P value = 0.009), higher RR (AOR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.04–1.35; P value = 0.013), and lower SBP (AOR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–0.99; P value = 0.018) were independently associated with increased odds of in-hospital mortality among patients with ACS (Table 5). Female patients had approximately fourfold higher odds of death compared with males. Each one breath/minute increase in RR was associated with an 18% increase in the odds of death, while each 1 mmHg decrease in SBP corresponded to a 3% increase. Each one-year increase in age was associated with a 4% increase in the odds of death; however, this should be interpreted cautiously given the borderline confidence interval. Although advanced Killip class and elevated HR showed strong associations with mortality in crude analysis, they did not retain statistical significance in the adjusted model.

Discussion

This study highlights the high in-hospital mortality burden of ACS in selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa, where reperfusion therapy is unavailable. The predominance of STEMI and prolonged delays to admission reflect critical gaps in early recognition and access to timely care. Advanced age, female sex, hypotension, and tachypnea were independent predictors of in-hospital death, underscoring the need for early risk stratification and aggressive supportive management in resource-limited settings. These findings emphasize the urgent requirement for systems-level interventions to improve ACS outcomes in Ethiopia.

Males constituted the majority of the patients with ACS (63.6%) in this study. A similar pattern of male predominance was observed in other retrospective studies conducted at Ayder Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (ACSH) in northern Ethiopia (72.2%) and at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) in central Ethiopia (75.8%), as well as in the Saudi Project for Assessment of Coronary Events (SPACE) registry (77%) [7, 12, 16]. The mean age of ACS patients in this study was 58.4 ± 12.2 years, which was in line with that of the studies conducted in TASH (56.3 ± 13.7 years), ACSH (59 ± 13), Kenya (59.7 ± 3.8 years), and India (60.1 ± 11.2 years) [7, 12, 17, 18], but approximately a decade lower than that reported in the BLITZ survey conducted in Italy [19]. This age disparity may reflect the relatively younger population demographics in developing countries and could partly explain the earlier onset of ACS in these settings.

The most common presenting symptoms in the present study were chest pain (87.7%) and shortness of breath (50.6%). Similar findings were reported in a multicentre study conducted at Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College (SPHMMC) and Saint Peter’s Specialized Hospital (SPSH) in Ethiopia, where 90.5% of ACS patients had presented with chest pain and 53.6% with shortness of breath [6]. Additionally, comparable prevalence of chest pain was observed in studies conducted at ACSH and the University of Gondar (UOG) in Ethiopia, as well as in the Acute Coronary Events registry in Nigeria (RACE-Nigeria) [11, 12, 20].

A considerable delay in presentation was observed in this study, with a median time of 24 h and only 26.6% of patients arriving within 12 h of symptom onset. This is notably longer than the delay reported in Kenya, where 78% of ACS patients presented within 12 h [17], and in Senegal, which reported a mean delay of 14.5 h [21]. However, it was shorter than the delay reported at the UOG in Ethiopia, where the median time to presentation was 72 h [11]. Contributing factors likely included atypical ACS symptoms, poor public awareness, limited access to emergency transport, and delayed referrals. These findings highlight the need for public health education and system-level improvements to promote early recognition and timely management of ACS.

In this study, hypertension (55.2%) and diabetes mellitus (44.2%) were identified as the leading risk factors among patients with ACS. These findings were broadly consistent with the study conducted at SPHMMC and SPSH, where hypertension and diabetes mellitus were present in 55.2% and 35.4% of ACS patients, respectively [9]. On the other hand, the SPACE registry reported diabetes mellitus in 56% and hypertension in 48% of ACS patients [16]. These observations highlight the urgent need for effective preventive strategies and risk factor modification in high-risk populations.

In the present study, STEMI was the most frequently identified subtype of ACS, accounting for 79.9% of cases. This proportion is consistent with findings from other studies conducted in Ethiopia, including TASH (72.6%) and ACSH (72.8%) [7, 12]. However, the prevalence of STEMI in our study was substantially higher than that reported in several international registries, such as the GRACE registry (38.8%) [22], the SPACE registry (45%) [16], and the RACE-Nigeria registry (48.7%) [20]. Moreover, a reversed pattern was observed in high-income settings, where non-ST-elevation ACS predominated. For example, only 38.5% of ACS cases were STEMI in a central European registry [23], and 43.7% in a Japanese study [24]. Delayed presentation, limited recognition of NSTE-ACS, and limited access to advanced diagnostics, such as high-sensitivity troponin, may contribute to this pattern, though these factors were not directly measured and should be considered plausible rather than definitive explanations.

In the current study, 24.7% of patients presented with advanced Killip class (III or IV), which is comparable to findings from a multicentre study conducted at Jimma University Medical Centre (JUMC) and SPSH in Ethiopia (23.8%) [25]. However, this proportion was considerably higher than that reported in studies from Japan (10.9%) and SPHMMC (3.8%) [8, 24]. The most frequent ACS-related complications in our study were cardiogenic shock (16.2%) and heart failure (15.6%), findings that align with previous reports from TASH and UOG [7, 11]. The high proportion of patients presenting in higher Killip class and the associated complications highlight potential delays in presentation and diagnosis, underscoring the need to strengthen early recognition and timely management of ACS in resource-limited settings.

In terms of treatment initiated during hospitalization, 80.5% of patients were administered a loading dose of aspirin and 77.9% received a loading dose of clopidogrel, both higher than the proportions reported from ACSH, where 63.8% and 62.8% received loading doses of aspirin and clopidogrel, respectively [22]. The prescription rate for lipid-lowering medication in this study (94.8%) was comparable to that reported in the ACCESS registry from South Africa (91%), whereas the use of ACEis or ARBs was lower (42.2% vs. 66%) [26]. Beta blocker utilization was 64.3%, comparable to the rate reported in the ACCESS registry (69%) [26] but lower than those observed in the study from UOG (82.4%) [11], the Kenyan registry (72%) [27], and the Euro Heart Survey ACS (77.8%) [28]. The suboptimal use of ACEis/ARBs and beta-blockers underscores key opportunities to improve adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy.

The in-hospital mortality rate among ACS patients in this study was high (26.6%), comparable to that reported from TASH (27.4%) and slightly higher than that of the study conducted at JUMC and SPSH (20.4%), where reperfusion therapy was not available, as in the current study setting [7, 10]. A comparable mortality rate of 24.5% was reported at ACSH, where only 6.3% received streptokinase and 3.9% underwent primary PCI [12]. In contrast, the study conducted at SPHMMC and SPSH, reported a lower mortality rate of 8.8%, with 12.3% of patients undergoing PCI [9]. Lower mortality rates were also observed in high-resource settings, such as a Japanese study reporting 8.9% and the Euro Heart Survey, which found mortality rates of 7% for STEMI and 2.4% for NSTEMI, where the majority of patients underwent PCI [24, 28]. Notably, the study hospitals do not provide reperfusion therapy, reflecting infrastructural and human resource limitations typical of tertiary care centres in resource-limited settings. Limited access to reperfusion is likely a major contributing factor to poor ACS outcomes in Ethiopia, with patient-level factors further affecting mortality, highlighting the need to expand interventions and optimize care.

This study identified older age, female sex, lower SBP, and higher RR as predictors of in-hospital mortality among ACS patients. Older age was found to be a predictor of in-hospital mortality in several studies conducted in both developed and developing nations [10, 24, 28, 29]. A 20-year registry from Qatar showed that female gender was an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality [30]. A number of studies in ACS patients have shown an inverse association between in-hospital mortality and levels of SBP [27, 31, 32]. Consistent with our findings, a retrospective cohort study from Turkey revealed that each incremental unit rise in RR was associated with an 8% increase in the risk of mortality for patients with acute MI [33]. Nocturnal RR has also been identified as a mortality predictor in ACS [34]. Moreover, a study from Boston found that elevated RR at admission independently predicted worse outcomes, highlighting its value for early risk stratification and guiding timely interventions [35]. Close monitoring of RR and BP may improve early risk stratification and management, potentially reducing in-hospital mortality in ACS.

This study has several important limitations. First, although it involved multiple centres, the relatively small sample size, particularly of NSTEMI patients, may limit generalizability to the broader ACS population. Second, reliance on electronic health records led to incomplete or missing clinical, laboratory (notably lipid profiles and renal function tests), and imaging data, which may have introduced information bias. Third, data on treatment adherence were not available, limiting assessment of its impact on outcomes. Fourth, the retrospective observational design precludes establishing causal relationships, as unmeasured confounding may have influenced the results. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up restricts understanding of post-discharge outcomes and survival.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated a high rate of in-hospital mortality among ACS patients in hospitals without reperfusion therapy. Older age, female sex, higher respiratory rate, and lower systolic blood pressure were independently associated with mortality, emphasizing the importance of early risk assessment and vigilant monitoring of vital signs. Expanding the availability of thrombolysis, implementing structured prehospital ACS education programs, and establishing standardized ACS care pathways are imperative to achieving optimal outcomes in resource-limited settings.
