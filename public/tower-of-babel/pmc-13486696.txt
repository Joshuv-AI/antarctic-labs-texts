Association of early probiotic exposure with age at NEC onset in preterm Infants: A retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Yixian Chen, Mingyun Tang, Yuhui Duan, Ba Wei, Shuangjing Pan, Xuemei Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of probiotics for preventing necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) in preterm infants remains controversial. Specifically, whether the outcomes of infants with NEC are associated with prior probiotic administration remains unknown. This study aimed to examine the association between early supplementation with mixed-species probiotics and the timing of NEC onset as well as clinical outcomes in preterm infants who subsequently developed NEC. This retrospective study enrolled 216 preterm infants (gestational age < 35 weeks) diagnosed with stage II/III NEC from two hospitals in China. The patients were divided into a probiotics group and a nonprobiotics group. The timing of NEC onset, disease characteristics (intestinal perforation, surgery, mortality), and the occurrence of other preterm comorbidities were compared between the two groups. Among the infants who developed NEC, the median age at onset was observed to be later in the probiotic-exposed group compared with the unexposed group (22.5 days vs. 12 days). The probiotics group had a shorter duration of fasting after NEC onset (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of rates of surgery, intestinal perforation, mortality, shock, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), or extrauterine growth restriction (EUGR). Among infants who developed NEC, early mixed-species probiotic exposure was associated with later NEC onset. This association should be interpreted cautiously because probiotic exposure was non-protocolized and time-dependent. No clear evidence was observed for an association with reduced disease severity or lower rates of major complications, including surgery, bowel perforation, or mortality. These associational findings are exploratory and require further investigation.

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a detrimental gastrointestinal disease that affects preterm infants and is caused by multiple factors, including intestinal immaturity, formula feeding, microbial dysbiosis, and infection1. Postnatally, the diversity and abundance of microorganisms in the digestive tract gradually increase, establishing a new microbial equilibrium. However, the gut microbiota of preterm infants is particularly vulnerable to dysbiosis caused by alterations in colonization patterns resulting from intestinal immaturity, caesarean delivery, antibiotic exposure, delayed enteral feeding, and formula feeding2. On the basis of evidence that probiotics modulate microbiome composition and promote microbial maturation in preterm infants, they are increasingly being administered to prevent NEC and late-onset neonatal sepsis2. Studies have demonstrated that probiotic administration significantly reduces the incidence of stage II/III NEC and associated morbidity3. Compared with single-strain preparations, mixed-strain probiotics show superior efficacy in preventing NEC, mortality, and late-onset sepsis and reducing the length of hospital stay and the time to achieve complete enteral feeding4. Despite these findings, the adoption of probiotics remains limited because of safety concerns, particularly the development of probiotic-associated sepsis in preterm infants5. Some studies have reported inconsistent efficacy of probiotics against NEC, further compounded by heterogeneous regimens regarding strain selection, timing of initiation, dosing, and treatment duration6,7. Most existing studies have focused on the preventive effect of probiotics on the incidence of NEC in preterm infants, whereas the clinical characteristics, disease course, and short-term outcomes of NEC cases that occur despite early probiotic exposure remain unclear. This retrospective study aimed to investigate, among preterm infants who ultimately developed stage II/III NEC, the associations between prior mixed-species probiotic exposure and the age at NEC onset, disease severity, and short-term clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This retrospective, multicentre analysis included neonates who were diagnosed with NEC at Liuzhou Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital and The First People’s Hospital of Yulin, China, between 2013 and 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows1: gestational age (GA) < 35 weeks and2 a diagnosis of Bell’s stage II or higher NEC. The exclusion criteria were1presence of chromosomal, genetic, or metabolic disorders2; presence of congenital intestinal malformations or other major congenital anomalies; and3 incomplete medical records. Related definitions

NEC was defined according to modified Bell’s criteria11. Early-onset NEC was defined as NEC that occurred at < 14 days post-natal age, whereas late-onset NEC was defined as NEC that occurred at ≥ 14 days, which is consistent with the findings of Yee et al.12. During the study period, the administration of probiotics in the NICU was not based on a unified prospective research protocol. In the absence of clear clinical practice guidelines at the time, probiotic administration was primarily guided by prevailing research trends and clinical experience. Specifically, probiotics were administered as an individualized clinical management approach when preterm infants exhibited signs of feeding intolerance (such as frequent vomiting or abdominal distension) or abnormal bowel movements, following assessment by the attending physician and obtaining written informed consent for clinical treatment from the family. Based on probiotic exposure before NEC diagnosis, infants were categorized into two groups1: the probiotics group (infants who had initiated supplementation with the mixed-species probiotics, which contained Bifidobacterium longum, at a dose of ≥ 2.1 × 10⁸ CFU/day), and2 the control group (infants never exposed to any probiotics before diagnosis). Small for gestational age (SGA) was defined as a birth weight below the 10th percentile for sex and GA according to standard growth charts. Extrauterine growth restriction (EUGR) was defined as a weight < 10th percentile for postmenstrual age and sex at discharge13. The growth of preterm infants was evaluated by plotting the Fenton normal curve. BPD was defined as oxygen dependency (FiO₂ > 21%) lasting for ≥ 28 days in any neonate14. Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) was diagnosed according to the International Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity (1984)15. Bacteraemia was defined as a positive blood culture. Bowel perforation was confirmed by surgery or postmortem. Cholestasis was defined as serum total bilirubin > 85.5 µmol/L with a direct bilirubin fraction > 20% of total bilirubin16. Shock was defined as a clinical syndrome of acute circulatory dysfunction characterized by hypoperfusion and metabolic abnormalities, diagnosed by the attending physician according to the diagnostic criteria of Practice of Neonatology (5th edition)17. Surgery referred to any surgical intervention (laparotomy or peritoneal drainage) performed for NEC.

Data collection

The clinical data were collected from the electronic medical records of the patients created during hospitalization. Collected variables included: demographic and perinatal variables (GA, BW, sex, Apgar scores, caesarean section, GDM); postnatal interventions (oxygen therapy, IMV, antibiotics, PS therapy, blood transfusion, ibuprofen, probiotic); NEC-related variables (age at onset, intraoperative bowel integrity, Bell stage); and outcomes (fever, bacteraemia, shock, bowel perforation, surgery, mortality, BPD, ROP, cholestasis, EUGR).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26) and R (version 4.4.1). Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations when normally distributed, with between-group comparisons analysed using Student’s t test. Nonnormally distributed variables are reported as medians (interquartile ranges) and were compared using the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were analysed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test (where appropriate). Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to describe the distribution of age at NEC onset among infants who developed NEC. The log-rank test was used to compare the distribution of age at NEC onset between infants who received probiotics before NEC diagnosis and those who did not. This analysis is descriptive only and does not imply a causal effect of probiotics on NEC timing, given the time-dependent nature of exposure and the risk of immortal time bias. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. For multivariable analysis, due to the limited sample size and to avoid overfitting, we prespecified a parsimonious model based on clinical relevance and collinearity assessment.

Results

A total of 216 infants with NEC were included in this study, with 172 in the nonprobiotics group and 44 in the probiotics group. The baseline characteristics of patients before NEC onset did not differ significantly between the probiotics group and nonprobiotics group, except for the initiation of antibiotics within one week after birth (93.18% vs. 76.16%, p = 0.012) and the proportion of infants from Center A (66.86% vs. 43.18%, p = 0.004) (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics and outcomes of neonates with NEC exposed to probiotics pre-NEC

Neonates who received probiotics before NEC onset had a later median age at NEC onset than the unexposed neonates did (22.5 vs. 12 days, p < 0.001). Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank testing further confirmed that the probiotic group had a significantly longer time to NEC onset compared with the nonprobiotic group (log-rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). The probiotic group also had a significantly greater incidence of fever (50% vs. 29.65%, p = 0.011). The surgery and mortality rates in the probiotics group were 27.27% (12/44) and 2.27% (1/44), respectively, numerically lower than the rates observed in the nonprobiotics group (28.49% [49/172] and 10.47% [18/172]) (for surgery: χ² = 0.026, p = 0.873; for mortality: χ² = 1.999, p = 0.157). Additionally, the incidences of IMV, septic shock, and bowel perforation are presented in Table 2 (all p > 0.05).

Among survivors, the probiotics group had shorter fasting durations (8 vs. 11 days, p = 0.032). The duration of antibiotic therapy, BPD, ROP, cholestasis, and EUGR are presented in Table 3 (all p > 0.05).

Univariate Analysis Between Early-Onset NEC and Late-Onset NEC.

The late-onset group had significantly lower GA and BW (30.11 ± 2.34 vs. 31.36 ± 2.46 weeks; 1333.38 ± 393.07 vs. 1519.10 ± 432.20 g, both p < 0.01), as well as lower rate of cesarean Sect. (45.95% vs. 61.90%, p < 0.05). The late-onset group had significantly higher rates of blood transfusion, ibuprofen, and probiotic administration (all p < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression

Due to the limited sample size and to avoid overfitting, we prespecified a parsimonious model based on clinical relevance and collinearity assessment. The following variables were included in the multivariable logistic regression model: GA, initiation of antibiotics within 1 week after birth, blood transfusion, initiation of IMV within 24 h after birth, and center. Probiotic exposure was also forced into the model as the primary variable of interest. (Table 5). After adjustment, lower GA (OR 0.841, 95% CI 0.724–0.977, p = 0.024) and early antibiotic use (OR 2.478, 95% CI 1.051–5.840, p = 0.038) were associated with late-onset NEC. The OR for probiotic exposure was 3.876 (95% CI 1.665–9.024, p = 0.002).

Discussion

NEC results from a combination of multiple factors. An immature gut, hypoxia, infection, formula consumption, and the gut microbiota can work together to cause NEC18. Beyond these established etiological factors, our study observed associations between several clinical exposures and the timing of NEC onset. Specifically, probiotic exposure, lower gestational age, and early antibiotic use were associated with late-onset NEC.

In this retrospective cohort of preterm infants who developed stage II/III NEC, we observed a statistical association between prior mixed-species probiotic exposure and a later age of NEC onset (median 22.5 days vs. 12 days). However, this finding is subject to confounding by indication (probiotics were given to symptomatic infants) and immortal time bias (earlier NEC allowed less opportunity for exposure). Regarding the higher rate of probiotic administration in the late-onset NEC group, this finding is structurally inevitable rather than indicative of a protective effect. Infants who develop NEC later have a longer pre-diagnosis window to receive probiotics, introducing survival bias or reverse causality. Therefore, this association should not be interpreted causally. Preclinical studies have suggested that probiotics can modulate gut microbiota composition and intestinal barrier function19,20, and prior work has identified distinct microbial patterns preceding early- versus late-onset NEC21,22. Taken together, these findings raise a hypothesis that warrants future investigation: probiotics might shift the timing of NEC onset toward a later window, but our study design cannot test this hypothesis. Our study did not reveal that the combination of probiotics can alleviate the severity of stage ≥ II NEC, including IMV, shock, surgical intervention, bowel perforation, and death.

Recently, increasing attention has been given to the so-called “gut–lung axis”23, “gut–brain axis”24, “gut–eyes axis”25, etc. Garg et al.8 reported that approximately three-fourths of infants who underwent surgery for NEC had moderate to severe BPD. In addition, infants with NEC who underwent surgery and had moderate to severe BPD were more likely to experience longer postoperative morbidity, greater surgical complications, poorer linear growth, greater respiratory support, and longer hospitalization. A study of 7,483 preterm infants revealed that infants with NEC who underwent surgery26, particularly those with early-onset NEC (8 to 28 days) who required surgery, had a significantly increased risk of ROP and severe ROP (adjusted OR: 3.1 and 3.3, respectively). A few studies have shown that probiotics can prevent and alleviate BPD in clinical and animal models27,28. However, no benefits of probiotics in alleviating BPD, ROP or intraventricular haemorrhage in preterm infants were apparent in the latest meta-analysis4. Our study did not find the effects of mixed bifidobacteria-containing probiotics, which are commonly used in clinical practice, in alleviating the incidence of BPD and ROP in infants with NEC. This lack of effect may be explained by early probiotic supplementation in preterm infants may selectively modulate colonization by certain bacterial taxa without fundamentally altering the overall longitudinal succession of the gut microbiota during the neonatal period29. Furthermore, the strain-specific properties of the probiotics, as well as the timing and duration of administration, could also influence their systemic efficacy.

It has been well described that infants born at lower GA develop NEC at a later chronological age30. The inverse association between GA and late-onset NEC in our study is consistent with existing literature. A large population-based cohort study by Yee et al. demonstrated that NEC presents earlier (mean 7 days) in more mature preterm infants, whereas onset is delayed to 32 days in smaller, lower GA infants12. These findings collectively support our observation that lower GA is associated with later NEC onset, likely reflecting the complex interplay between intestinal maturity, immune development, and the timing of NEC pathogenesis.

Early antibiotic use has been implicated as a risk factor for NEC31, but its association with the timing of NEC onset is less clear. In our study, early antibiotic use (within the first week after birth) was associated with late-onset NEC (OR 2.478, 95% CI 1.051–5.840, p = 0.038). In a previous study on early empiric antibiotic exposure in preterm infants, the median age of NEC onset was 11 days in the no antibiotic group, 13 days in the short course group (≤ 72 h), and 14 days in the prolonged course group (> 72 h), with no significant difference between groups32. A systematic review identified that the choice of antibiotic regimen for suspected late-onset sepsis may influence NEC risk, though well-designed studies remain limited33. These observations collectively suggest that antibiotic exposure, including both early empiric use and specific regimen selection, may play a role in shaping NEC onset timing, though further studies are needed.

Several limitations should be considered. First and most importantly, two methodological issues fundamentally constrain causal interpretation. Probiotics were given based on clinical judgment (feeding intolerance, abnormal bowel movements), not a standardized protocol, introducing confounding by indication. Probiotic exposure was time-dependent, so infants with earlier NEC had less opportunity to receive probiotics, creating immortal time bias. Because only infants who developed NEC were included, this study cannot assess the potential preventive effect of probiotics on the overall incidence of NEC. If probiotics do have a protective effect, our cohort may have systematically excluded “responders” who avoided NEC due to probiotic supplementation. Therefore, our findings are strictly applicable to the question of whether early probiotic exposure is associated with the disease course among infants who progress to NEC. Other limitations include the small sample size, lack of standardized probiotic regimens, missing feeding-related variables (timing of enteral feeding initiation, advancement of feeds, time to full feeds), and an exploratory multivariable analysis with limited model stability. Future large-scale, prospective randomized controlled trials conducted in unselected at-risk neonatal populations are needed to further evaluate the preventive efficacy of probiotics against NEC incidence and their impact on disease progression.

Conclusion

In this cohort of infants who developed NEC, early mixed-species probiotic exposure was associated with later NEC onset. This association should be interpreted cautiously because probiotic exposure was non-protocolized and time-dependent. No clear evidence was observed for an association with reduced disease severity or lower rates of major comorbidities. These associational findings are exploratory and require further investigation.
