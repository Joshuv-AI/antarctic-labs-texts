The association of health anxiety with quality of life among medical students in Egypt: Myth or Menace?
Authors: Mohamed Terra, Mohamed Baklola, Lena Said Mansour, Nour Edin Darwish, Mennatallah Elsayed Badr, Amira A Alghazali, Jinan Ali A Almumtin, Fayrouz Mohamed Fahmy, Abdel-Hady El-Gilany
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth anxiety (HA) is a prevalent concern among medical students, potentially impacting their academic performance and Quality of Life (QoL). This study aims to assess the prevalence of HA and its predictors among medical students in Egypt and its correlation with the Quality of Life.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted during the 2022–2023 academic year across ten Egyptian medical schools. A total of 1,173 students participated by completing an online questionnaire, which included the Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI) and the 12-Item Short Form Survey (SF-12). Data were analyzed using SPSS 25. Chi-square tests, crude odds ratios (COR) with 95% confidence intervals, and Pearson’s correlation coefficients were utilized for statistical analysis. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThis study found that 15.7% of participants experienced clinically significant HA. Female students (17.5%) and students dissatisfied with their academic performance (18%) exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of HA. A significant negative correlation was observed between HA and QoL (r = -0.393, p ≤ 0.05), with students experiencing HA reporting lower QoL scores. Key predictors of HA included female gender (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.01–1.96) and lack of academic satisfaction (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.04–1.96).ConclusionHealth anxiety (HA) is a notable issue among Egyptian medical students, particularly affecting females and those dissatisfied with their academic performance and is closely linked to reduced quality of life. Effective interventions could enhance the overall educational experience, helping students improve their well-being and manage HA’s impacts on their personal and academic lives.Keywords: Health Anxiety, Medical Students, Quality of Life, Egypt

Introduction

Anxiety disorders are the most common psychiatric illnesses, with a global prevalence of 7.3% [4.8%–10.9%] [1]. Specific (isolated) phobias are the most frequent anxiety disorders, with a prevalence of 10.3%, followed by panic disorder, which has a frequency of 6.0%, social phobia (2.7%), and generalized anxiety disorder (2.2%). Women are more likely than males to have emotional disorders during adolescence; they are 1.5–2 times more likely than men to have an anxiety condition [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), anxiety disorders were the sixth leading cause of years lived with disability (YLD) worldwide in 2015 and the fourth leading cause in highly developed nations; they are thus among the chronic illnesses that have the strongest impact on patients’ lives [3].

Health anxiety (HA) is a specific type of anxiety, defined as an obsession with or worry about having a major illness, which is frequently provoked by a misperception of body feelings and symptoms and is accompanied by recurrent medical examinations, which usually only provide temporary reassurance.[4, 5]. Recent surveys indicate an HA lifetime prevalence of 5.7% and a current rate of 3.4% [6]. The high-stress environment of medical school, with its academic demands and clinical experiences, increases students’ vulnerability to HA, commonly referred to as “medical student syndrome” or “nosophobia” [7]. HA includes two main components: anxious thoughts about illness and the distress these thoughts create [8].

The terms “health anxiety” and “hypochondriasis” are often used interchangeably, with health anxiety seen as a more anxious, less severe form of hypochondriasis, which may also involve depressive symptoms and even delusional beliefs [9, 10]. Despite medical reassurance, medical students frequently experience persistent health-related worries, often adjusting their self-diagnoses based on current clinical rotations, which can interfere with their focus and academic performance [11]. Additionally, the rise of health-monitoring apps and internet-based symptom-checking (cyberchondria) has been linked to increased HA severity and persistence, as students are more exposed to self-diagnosis and medical information online [12].

Globally, a study in India found that one in seven medical students experienced health anxiety (HA), with preclinical students being the most affected; HA was higher in medical students than in the general population, regardless of personal or family psychiatric history [8]. In the UAE, nearly one in ten medical students reported HA, with personal experiences of physical or mental illness potentially influencing their choice to pursue medicine [13]. In Egypt, a study at Tanta University reported a 78.8% prevalence of hypochondriasis among medical students, affecting females, fourth-year students, and those from rural areas most significantly [14]. Further studies in 2023 and 2024 highlighted a high prevalence of psychological distress among undergraduates and noted that medical students demonstrated higher mental health literacy and recognition of disorders compared to non-medical students [15, 16]. However, 90.3% of distressed students needed mental health care, with common barriers including a preference for self-management.

Given the limited research on health anxiety (HA) among medical students in Egypt, this study aims to assess the prevalence and patterns of HA within this population and identify key predictors of clinically significant HA. By examining demographic factors, academic year, and personal or family history of mental health conditions, we seek to better understand the characteristics associated with HA in medical students. This research will provide valuable insights into the factors contributing to HA in this group, informing targeted interventions to support student well-being.

Methods

Study locality and duration

This study was conducted in ten various Egyptian medical schools during the academic years 2022–2023 and 2023–2024.

Study design

This is a descriptive cross-sectional study with an analytical component.

Target population

The target population consisted of undergraduate medical students from different Egyptian universities. Students who were willing to participate and enrolled in years one through six of the medical programs were included in the study.

Study tools

A self-administered online questionnaire was used that included four sections. The first sociodemographic section captured pertinent factors such as age, gender, place of residence, academic performance, psychiatric history, and external pressures to enroll in medical school.

The second Short Health Anxiety Inventory (SHAI) section utilized a validated and reliable tool to measure health anxiety, which differentiated between mild health anxiety and the severe form of hypochondriasis. It comprised two subsections, one of which evaluated health anxiety, while the other assessed the extent of distress about illness. The 18-item version of SHAI was utilized, with each question being self-rated on a score range of 0 to 3, and the total score range being 0 to 54. A cut-off score of 27 was employed to indicate clinically significant health anxiety [17].

The third section of the survey included a single question aimed at understanding the perception of the participants regarding their health conditions. Specifically, participants were asked to indicate which types of diseases they believed they had.

The fourth section of the survey evaluated the quality of life of the participants using the 12-item Short Form Survey (SF-12) by Ware, Kosinski, and Keller [18]. The second version of this survey was utilized, composed of eight domains that encompassed both physical and mental aspects of health. These domains included general health, physical role, physical health, physical pain, vitality, mental health, emotional role, and social functioning. A higher score on the scale indicated a better quality of life in the respective domain.

Sampling method

A convenience sample was drawn from ten Egyptian medical schools.

Data collection approach

Participants accessed the questionnaire online via Google Forms. These forms were disseminated through official Telegram channels and other social media platforms of Egyptian medical schools, allowing participants to answer the questions freely and anonymously.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the prevalence of health anxiety among medical students in a prior study, which reported a rate of 9.3% [19]. To achieve a study power of 80%, alpha error of 5%, and 5% precision, with a design effect of 10, a sample size of 1300 participants was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Science Program (SPSS 25 for macOS). Quantitative data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and median for non-parametric data. Qualitative data were summarized using numbers and percentages. For univariate analysis, Chi-square tests were applied to evaluate associations between categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare non-parametric distributions across groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated to examine correlations between health anxiety and quality of life. For the multivariate analysis, binary logistic regression was conducted to identify independent predictors of health anxiety, adjusting for variables that were significant in the univariate analysis. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant demographics and health anxiety prevalence

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the one thousand one hundred seventy-three medical students who participated in the study, alongside the prevalence of health anxiety (HA) among different groups. Female participants comprised the majority (58.9%), with a significantly higher prevalence of HA (17.5%) compared to male participants (13.1%) (p ≤ 0.05). Regarding age, participants aged between 20 to 22 had the highest percentage of health anxiety (17.1%), but it is not statistically significant. As for academic level, second-year students had the highest prevalence of health anxiety (17.9%), but it is statistically not significant. Rural students with health anxiety (15.8%) were slightly higher than urban students (15.6%), but it is not statistically significant. Participants with academic performance satisfaction had a lower prevalence of health anxiety (13.3%) compared to those without academic performance satisfaction (18%) which is statistically significant. Students with family members working in the medical field and those with relatives who had psychiatric disorders showed less prevalence of health anxiety compared to others, but this is not statistically significant.

Demographic characteristics and quality of life

Table 2 presents the relationship between demographic characteristics and quality of life (QoL). The median QoL score for males was higher at 37 (IQR 32–44) compared to females at 35 (IQR 30–40), a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Regarding place of residence, urban students had a median QoL score of 36 (IQR 31–42), which was significantly higher than that of rural students, who had a median QoL score of 35 (IQR 31–40) (p = 0.004). Students who reported overall academic performance satisfaction had a higher median QoL score of 38 (IQR 32–43) compared to those without satisfaction, whose median score was 34 (IQR 30–39), also statistically significant (p < 0.001). Participants with relatives who had a history of psychiatric disorders and those with health anxiety had significantly lower median QoL scores: 34 (IQR 29–39) for those with a family psychiatric history (p < 0.001) and 32 (IQR 28–36) for those with health anxiety (p < 0.001). The rest of the demographic variables did not show statistically significant differences in QoL scores.

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the correlation between quality of life (QoL) and health anxiety (HA) among Egyptian medical students, focusing on prevalence, predictors, and patterns of clinically significant HA. According to the SHAI, we found that 15.7% of the participating students exhibited clinically significant HA, indicating a noteworthy level of HA among medical students in Egypt.

According to this study, females have a slightly higher prevalence of health anxiety than males when measured with the SHAI. Furthermore, it reveals that HA is associated with academic performance satisfaction, with participants who are satisfied with their academic performance having a lower prevalence of health anxiety than those who are not satisfied with their academic performance.

There was no significant association between place of residence, academic level, having relatives or family members with mental health disorders, or having family members working in the medical field, and clinically significant health anxiety.

According to early studies conducted in the 1960s, 70% and 78.8% of medical students experienced significant health anxiety [20, 21]. However, a later study suggested that these initial rates were likely overestimations, reporting a revised prevalence of 8.3% [22], other studies done in the MENA region reported findings that are comparable with our study results, 9.3% in the United Arab Emirate (UAE) [19] and 16.1–17.4% in Saudi Arabia [11]. Across the globe, other studies reported prevalence rates of 11% in Iran [23], 11.9% in Pakistan [24], and 14.7% in India [8], which are comparable to our study’s rate of 15.7%. The variation in these rates likely reflects methodological differences, such as variations in sample sizes and measurement tools used across studies. Notably, most HA prevalence rates in medical students are similar to those observed in the general population, where estimates range from 2.1% to 13.1% [4]

According to the results of our study, women are slightly more likely than men to have health anxiety when using the SHAI, which suggests that women may need to take the condition more seriously. This is in line with other epidemiological studies, which found that Egyptian women were more prone than men to suffer from anxiety disorders [25, 26]. Our findings are compatible with those of a study that was conducted in Pakistan [24]. This can be explained by the fact that there are typically more female students than male students enrolled in medical colleges. Another reason for this gender disparity could be that women in the MENA region are more likely than men to worry about the challenges of their educational programs and to have fewer employment opportunities [26].

Our study additionally revealed that HA is linked to academic performance satisfaction, with participants who are happy with their academic achievement showing less evidence of health anxiety than those who are not. It is noteworthy since medical students’ academic performance may be negatively affected by health anxiety [27]. As for the association between HA and a student’s year of study, specifically second-year medicine, where the highest levels of HA were reported but the association was not significant, a study of Pakistani Medical Students found that second-year students had the highest HA levels and fifth-year students, the lowest [24]. Our results were in accordance with theirs, and their results were also statistically insignificant.

In this study, logistic regression adjusted for potential significant variables revealed that gender and overall academic performance satisfaction were the most powerful predictors of medical students’ health anxiety. Furthermore, the results of our study suggest that health anxiety negatively affects quality of life, students experiencing health anxiety have a significant reduction in quality of life. This could be explained by the possibility that health anxiety can result in depression and sleep issues, both of which have a negative impact on quality of life, as they can lead to mental, psychological, and physical morbidities [28–31].

Participants with relatives with a history of psychiatric disorders, females, rural students, and students with low academic performance satisfaction experienced lower levels of quality of life. Being the first study to examine HA and its impact on quality of life among Egyptian medical students, the study’s information is novel, which is one of its many strengths.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of a cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causality between health anxiety and quality of life or other associated factors. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to response bias, as participants might underreport or overestimate their symptoms. Third, the sample was drawn from a limited number of Egyptian medical schools and was recruited through convenience sampling, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings to all medical students in Egypt or other regions. Finally, factors such as social desirability bias and the online nature of the survey may have influenced responses. Future research with longitudinal designs and more diverse sampling methods would help validate these findings across broader contexts.

Recommendations for future research

To address health anxiety (HA) among medical students, future research should focus on several key areas. Longitudinal studies are essential to understanding how HA develops over time in medical school, particularly during periods of high academic or clinical stress. Controlled intervention trials could assess the effectiveness of methods like cognitive-behavioral therapy and mindfulness-based approaches, as recent meta-analyses indicate these interventions can effectively reduce HA symptoms. Additionally, integrating mental health education within the medical curriculum may help students develop resilience and coping skills; future studies could evaluate whether these changes result in lower rates of HA and enhanced quality of life. Multi-center, cross-cultural research will clarify how HA presents across diverse educational and cultural settings, offering insights for more universally effective interventions. Finally, studies examining the impact of digital symptom-checking, or cyberchondria, on HA could help develop guidelines around responsible health information use, potentially reducing HA severity among students.

Conclusion

This study represents the first investigation into the correlation between health anxiety (HA) and quality of life among medical students in Egypt, providing valuable insights into the impact of HA on quality of life (QoL). The results reveal that 15.7% of the medical students surveyed experienced clinically significant HA. Key findings indicate that female students and those dissatisfied with their academic performance are at higher risk for HA. Furthermore, HA is negatively correlated with QoL, emphasizing the importance of addressing HA to improve the well-being of medical students. These findings emphasize the necessity for targeted interventions to reduce health anxiety (HA) and support academic performance among Egyptian medical students. Effective measures include offering mental health support services, incorporating mental health education into the curriculum, creating peer support networks, monitoring academic progress, and launching awareness campaigns.
