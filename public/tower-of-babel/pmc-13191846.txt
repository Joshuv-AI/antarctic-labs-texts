Does congenital cervical fusion predispose to adjacent segment degeneration? A retrospective cohort study

Background

Congenital cervical fusion is associated with adjacent segment degeneration (ASD). Some argue that spinal fusion could trigger a more rapid deterioration of intervertebral discs in the levels adjacent to the fusion site, both above and below [1–3]. On the other hand, there is a viewpoint that considers ASD as an unavoidable aspect of the natural aging process [4, 5]. For now, there remains an absence of definitive evidence to suggest that the rate of ASD progression is more rapid than that of the natural aging process [6].

Klippel-Feil Syndrome, characterized by the congenital fusion of cervical vertebrae, serves as an exemplary model for studying ASD post congenital cervical fusion [7]. Utilizing Type I KFS as a research template offers several unique advantages: (1) The cervical vertebrae have been fused at birth, allowing the age at MRI examination to directly correlate with the duration of the fusion’s impact on cervical degeneration. (2) With no degeneration present at birth, retrospective studies can be anchored from this uniform baseline, standardizing the starting point for degenerative changes across the cervical spine. (3) Type I KFS, characterized by a single segment fusion [8], simplifies the analysis by reducing the variability in fusion patterns that could otherwise confound the study. (4) The relative uniformity in the morphology of Type I KFS patients contributes to a more controlled study environment, minimizing the influence of individual anatomical differences [9]. (5) Focusing on Type I KFS patients with fusion at C5-6, the most common site for cervical degeneration [10], further narrows down the confounding effects of different fusion sites. In this study, we have assembled the largest cohort to date, encompassing 70 patients with single cervical fusions at the C5-6 level. Our objective is to delineate the influence of congenital cervical fusion on degenerative processes of cervical spine.

Methods

Study data

The study cohort was derived from an extensive imaging database, encompassing more than 100,000 cervical spine MRI examinations. A flowchart was utilized to guide the study’s methodology (Fig. 1). The diagnosis of KFS was confirmed by the presence of fused vertebrae at levels characterized by either completely or partially fused vertebral bodies with an absent or incomplete disc space. A consecutive series of 70 patients with type I KFS (C5-6 fusion) were selected and matched in a 1:2 case-control ratio. Participants were stratified into three distinct age groups: the younger group (under 40 years), the middle-age group (40 to 60 years), and the older-age group (over 60 years). Sagittal and axial (T2-weighted) MRI scans were utilized for measurements.

Pfirrmann grading

The prevalence of disc degeneration was evaluated using the Pfirrmann grading system (Table 1) [11], which was conducted by a spinal surgeon and a radiologist. In cases of disagreement, the final grade was determined through consensus. In an effort to quantify the degree of disc degeneration, we have grouped Pfirrmann grades 4 and 5 into a single category, termed ‘severe degeneration’, and grades 1, 2, and 3 into another category, termed ‘mild degeneration’ [12].

Cervical spine biological age scale

We evaluated the whole cervical spine degeneration situation by cervical spine ‘biological age scale’ (Table 2) [13]. In the KFS group, the C5-6 level is characterized by fusion, which necessitates an adjustment in the scoring for the control group. To address this, we have adopted a modified scoring approach for the control group. Specifically, we assign the C5-6 level in the control group the same score as observed in the KFS group, regardless of the degree of degeneration present at that level. Furthermore, when calculating the diameter of the spinal canal at the level of greatest severity, the C5-6 level is intentionally excluded from consideration, even if it represents the most severely affected region.

Dural sac compression index

The dural sac compression was calculated as the ratio of the dural sac diameter to the spinal canal diameter, thereby quantifying the degree of disc space constriction as a percentage (Fig. 2). The formula for this calculation is simple: [(Dural sac diameter / Spinal canal diameter) * 100%].

Statistics

The analysis was completed using IBM SPSS 24.0 statistical software for database organization and statistical analysis. Statistical inferences were made using t-tests, analysis of variance, Mann-Whitney U test, rank sum tests, One-Way ANOVA, chi-square tests, and Mantel-Haenszel chi-square test for trend. The P value was taken for two-tailed tests, and a difference was considered statistically significant if P < 0.05. Within the same group, multiple pairwise comparisons are conducted using the Bonferroni correction.

Power analysis

The ratio of case numbers to control numbers is 1:2. α = 0.05, β = 0.10, Zα=1.64, Zβ=1.28. According to literature reports, the rate of cervical spine degeneration in KFS patients is P1 = 68.6%, and the rate of cervical spine degeneration in the general population is P0 = 59.6%. Then‾p = (P1 + cP0) / (1 + c) = 0.626, N = (1 + 1/c) ‾P (1 - ‾P) (Zα + Zβ)2 / (P1 - P0) ≈ 33. The control group = c * N = 2 * 33 = 66. With 90% power and a confidence level of 95%, at least 33 individuals were needed in KFS group and 66 individuals were needed in control group.

Results

Patient demographics

KFS cohort is composed of 70 patients, featuring a gender distribution that includes 25 males (35.7%) and 45 females (64.3%), resulting in a male-to-female ratio of approximately 1:1.8. The participants are categorized into three distinct age brackets: the younger group, encompassing individuals under 40 years of age with a count of 14; the middle-age group, ranging from 40 to 60 years with 30 participants; and the older-age group, including those above 60 years with 26 individuals. The control group is meticulously matched to the KFS group at a ratio of 1:2, ensuring equivalence in age and gender distribution (Table 3).

Disc degeneration

The Pfirrmann grade across different age groups in both the KFS and control groups is elegantly captured (Fig. 3). A parallel trend of cervical disc degeneration is observed between the control and KFS groups, with the severity of degeneration escalating in direct proportion to age. Notably, the C4-5 and C6-7 segments are identified as the most severely affected, with C3-4, C2-3, and C7-T1 following in descending order of degeneration severity.

Pfirrmann Grade for KFS and Control groups. Legends: A, Younger group. B, Middle-age group. C, Older-age group

A comparative analysis of Pfirrmann grade of the KFS and control groups revealed a significant divergence at the C6-7 (p = 0.03) within the KFS group. Conversely, no statistically significant variations were detected at the C2-3 (p = 0.87), C3-4 (p = 0.05), C4-5 (p = 0.06), and C7-T1 (p = 0.12) levels when juxtaposed with the control group.

A deeper dive into age-specific cohorts unveiled that the C4-5 (p = 0.01) showed a statistically significant deviation from the control group among the younger cohort. Similarly, the C6-7 (p = 0.02) marked a statistically significant distinction in the middle-age group. However, within the older-age group, no such significant differences were observed across all levels of the KFS group when compared to the control group.

In the control group, a longitudinal comparison of the proportion of severe degeneration reveals no statistically significant differences across various cervical spine segments in the young cohort. In the middle-age group, older-age group and all ages, although there are numerical variations in the proportion of severe degeneration at the C4-5, C6-7, and C3-4 levels, these differences do not reach statistical significance when compared with one another. Similarly, the C2-3 and C7-T1 levels exhibit numerical differences that do not translate to statistical significance. Furthermore, a significant difference is observed between the groups ‘C4-5, C6-7, C3-4’ and ‘C2-3, C7-T1’ (Fig. 4). In the KFS group, the statistical profiles closely mirror those of the control group, with the exception of the lack of significant differences between the C3-4 and C7-T1 segments within the elderly subgroup, underscoring a remarkable consistency in the observed trends (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The idea that post-fusion disc degeneration is accelerated compared to natural aging is difficult to prove experimentally. A more realistic strategy is to compare preoperative and postoperative conditions, or to compare the degeneration of intervertebral discs between adjacent and non-adjacent segments [14]. However, it is difficult for us to say that these exacerbated results are not part of the natural aging process. What is more, many studies show that the adjacent segments, often located in the lower cervical region, are naturally more prone to faster degeneration [10, 15]. Therefore, we need to address: What is the inherent progression of cervical spine degeneration, and to what extent does cervical fusion expedite this process?

Our findings show that in the control group, a clear age-related trend in cervical spine degeneration, with the severity increasing significantly as age progresses (Figs. 3 and 6), which aligns with previous research [16, 17]. Young individuals exhibit no significant differences, middle-aged and older individuals show higher rates at C6-7, C4-5, and C3-4 compared to C2-3 and C7-T1. Although the numerical order of degeneration severity is C6-7 > C4-5 > C3-4, these differences are not statistically significant (Fig. 4). These suggest a predisposition for middle and lower cervical segments to degenerate [10, 18].

When comparing the KFS and control groups, we found: (1) The Pfirrmann grade in the KFS group shows that C4-5 in young individuals and C6-7 in middle-age individuals and overall patients are significantly higher than in the control group. This indicates that cervical fusion has accelerated the ASD particularly during early to middle adulthood. (2) The proportion of severe degeneration in the KFS group, like in the control group, significantly increases with age. And our results show that the trend of the proportion of severe degeneration increasing with age also has a significant difference. However, within each segment and age group, there is no difference between the KFS group and the control group (Fig. 6A-E). This suggests that cervical fusion only moderately increased the severe degeneration of cervical discs. (3) In both the control group and the KFS group, a similar trend in the severity of cervical disc degeneration is observed, with the order of degeneration being C6-7, C4-5, C3-4, C2-3, and C7-T1, with no statistical difference between C6-7, C4-5, and C3-4; and no statistical difference between C2-3 and C7-T1. This consistency across KFS and control groups suggests a common pattern in the progression of disc degeneration, highlighting the comparable nature of these cervical degeneration regardless of whether the cervical spine has undergone fusion. In summary, when considering cervical disc degeneration, the fusion of C5-6 appears to hasten the natural aging process of lower cervical spine (C3-4, C4-5, and C6-7), which include the adjacent segments (C4-5 and C6-7), particularly during early to middle adulthood. Nonetheless, the extent and duration of this accelerated effect are confined and do not persist indefinitely.

The impact of congenital cervical fusion on cervical degeneration is not confined to the intervertebral discs, it also accelerates the overall aging of the cervical spine [19]. To describe the degeneration across the entire cervical spine, we employ the biological age scale (Table 2). Our results indicated that the biological age scale increases with age in both the KFS (Fig. 7A) and control groups (Fig. 7B), aligning with the general understanding of the aging process. In the younger and middle-age groups, the KFS group exhibited a significantly higher biological age scale compared to the control group. However, in the older age group, no significant differences were observed between the two groups (Fig. 8). When examining the overall situation, the KFS group’s biological age scale was significantly higher than that of the control group (P < 0.001). This suggests that the fusion at the C5-6 level accelerates the degeneration of the entire cervical spine during the young and middle-aged periods. Yet, as the control and KFS group ages, the acceleration effect becomes indistinguishable from the natural progression of cervical degeneration, resulting in no differences observed in the older-age group.

We employ the “dural sac compression index” (DSCI) as a metric to assess the severity of spinal dural sac compression. This compression can arise not only from intervertebral disc degeneration but also from other contributing factors, such as hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum. Overall, in both the control and KFS groups, the DSCI increases with age across all cervical spine segments (Fig. 10). Notably, the DSCI at C3-4, C4-5, and C6-7 in the KFS group is significantly higher than in the control group (Fig. 9). Particularly at C4-5 (p < 0.001) and C6-7 (p < 0.001), the disparity between the two groups is markedly pronounced. This suggests that cervical fusion accelerates the degenerative process of the cervical spine, significantly elevating the degree of nerve compression and, by extension, the likelihood of patients experiencing neurological damage symptoms.

When comparing different age groups, in the younger cohort, the DSCI for C3-4 and C4-5 in the KFS group is significantly larger than in the control group. In the middle-aged group, the DSCI for C2-3, C3-4, C4-5, and C6-7 in the KFS group is significantly larger than in the control group. In the older age group, only the DSCI for C6-7 in the KFS group is significantly larger than in the control group (Fig. 10). From the perspective of potential neurological compression, congenital fusion at C5-6 hastens cervical dural sac compression, making young and middle-age individuals more susceptible to neurological symptoms at lower cervical levels (C3-4, C4-5, and C6-7). In the elderly group, this accelerated degeneration process essentially mirrors the natural aging process, with only the C6-7 being more prone to exhibit neurological symptoms.

In summary, congenital cervical fusion not only moderately accelerates degeneration in adjacent segments but also extends its impact to non-adjacent segments. However, the extent of this acceleration is constrained. Concurrently, cervical fusion significantly amplifies the overall degeneration of cervical spine, contributing to an increased incidence of dural sac compression. This, in turn, may heighten the risk of neurological symptoms. The pronounced acceleration effects of cervical fusion are primarily evident in younger and middle-aged populations. In contrast, among older individuals, the accelerated degenerative process essentially parallels the natural aging trajectory. The insights gleaned from these findings are thought-provoking for enhancing our comprehension of ASD following ACDF-surgery.

Limitations

As the patients in the control group are not healthy volunteers, the baseline level of cervical degeneration in the control group is potentially higher than that of the general population.
