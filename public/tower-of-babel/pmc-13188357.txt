The impact of adverse childhood experiences on phubbing among college students at a medical university in China: the mediating role of depression and the moderating role of personal social capital
Authors: Yongfu Yang, Xinxin Lai, Yuxin Zhang, Zhenkun Shi, Honglin Yan, Jinghong Huang, Xiaojun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe phenomenon of “screen-addicted youth” is becoming increasingly prevalent among Chinese college students. This study examines how Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), Personal Social Capital (PSC) and depression influence phubbing behavior in today’s global digital environment.MethodsA stratified and convenience sampling method was used to survey 1918 college students at a comprehensive medical university in China using the Chinese version of the Revised Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire, the Chinese version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9, the Chinese version of the Generic Scale of Phubbing, and the Chinese version of the Personal Social Capital Scale 8 from October 8, 2024 to November 8, 2024. A moderated mediation model was employed to analyze the mediating effect of depression and the moderating effect of PSC.ResultsThere were significant positive correlations of phubbing with ACEs (r = 0.433, P < 0.001) and depression (r = 0.531, P < 0.001), while PSC was negatively correlated with phubbing behavior (r = -0.572, P < 0.001). Depression partially mediated the relationship between ACEs and phubbing behavior, with an indirect effect 0.126 (95% CI = 0.111–0.164), accounting for 36.84% of the total effect. PSC not only moderated the direct effect of this relationship (β = -0.087, t = -5.466, P < 0.001), but also moderated the first half of the indirect effect (β = -0.041, t = -2.148, P = 0.026).ConclusionsDepression partially mediates the association between ACEs and phubbing among students at a medical university in China, PSC was negatively associated with phubbing behavior and was linked to a weaker association between ACEs and depression, thereby being indirectly associated with lower levels of phubbing behavior. This study highlights the need for enhanced mental health interventions for Chinese students with high ACEs exposure and the importance of improving PSC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04486-6.

Introduction

According to data from the China Internet Network Information Center, China’s smartphone user base has reached 1.105 billion [1]. The widespread adoption of internet technology has given rise to the phenomenon of phubbing, where people neglect those around them while being fixated on their smartphones [2, 3]. Phubbing behavior disrupts face-to-face communication, leading to social withdrawal, interpersonal conflicts, and communication barriers [3–6]. Furthermore, the phubbing behavior has been shown to correlate positively with excessive smartphone dependency [7]. Without intervention, this may progress to clinically significant smartphone addiction [8, 9]. This not only leads to physical discomfort such as eye strain, hearing loss, arm numbness, wrist swelling, and neck pain [10], but is also associated with psychological issues like depression and anxiety [11, 12]. Research indicates that phubbing is moderately prevalent among college students, with 67.3% of participants identified as frequent phubbers [13]. Phubbing is increasingly becoming a prominent social issue, particularly among Chinese university students, who are highly immersed in digital environments [14]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms underlying phubbing behavior is crucial for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies. Existing research indicates that both social and psychological factors jointly contribute to phubbing behavior [5, 9]. Particularly, negative emotional states like depression and anxiety, as well as adverse environmental conditions have been identified as key predictors [15]. However, few studies examined the impact of adverse events during the developmental process on phubbing behavior among university populations, particularly the role played by individual social capital and depression.

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) refer to harmful events encountered before age 18, including abuse, neglect, and family dysfunction [16]. ACEs are now widely recognized as a significant global public health concern. Research among Chinese college students reveals that approximately 91.1% of respondents experienced at least one ACE [17]. Empirical studies have demonstrated that ACEs significantly increase the risk of depressive symptoms in adolescents by disrupting the development of adaptive emotional regulation and fostering negative cognitive schemas about the self and the world [18]. From a social cognitive theory perspective [19, 20], ACEs may reshape individuals’ cognitive evaluation processes and coping strategies, making them more susceptible to psychological distress and maladaptive behavioral responses. These psychological mechanisms may further exacerbate individuals’ emotional regulation or avoidance dependence on smartphones, thereby contributing to the occurrence of phubbing behavior. Additionally, ACEs also correlate with problematic smartphone use [21]. Adolescents experiencing four or more ACEs exhibit a smartphone addiction prevalence rate approximately 1.6 times higher than those without ACEs [22].

Depression is highly prevalent among college students, with nearly one-quarter of respondents experiencing depressive symptoms before the age of 19 [23]. As adolescence is a critical stage for psychological and cognitive development, depression distorts thought patterns and impairs mental health [24, 25]. Depression consistently correlates with adverse life events [26] and high-frequency phubbing behavior [27, 28]. Individuals experiencing depressive symptoms may use smartphones to escape real-world pressures, thereby reinforcing screen dependency [28, 29]. Personal social capital (PSC), a resource embedded within an individual’s personal social relationships, has been widely recognized as a critical factor influencing mental health and behavioral outcomes [30]. According to social capital theory [31, 32], social resources derived from interpersonal networks provide emotional support, informational assistance, and a sense of belonging, thereby helping individuals cope with stress and adversity [33]. Empirical evidence indicates that PSC exerts a protective effect on mental health by providing emotional support and facilitating access to social resources [33, 34]. Individuals with higher levels of social capital are more likely to receive substantial social support, which may reduce their reliance on smartphones for emotional compensation and consequently decrease phubbing behavior [35].

Although prior studies have examined the independent effects of ACEs, depression, and social capital on phubbing behavior, few studies have explored how these factors interact to shape such behavior [36–38]. Furthermore, existing research has examined the mediating role of depression in the association between bullying victimization and phubbing behavior, as well as the moderating role of social support in this relationship. Building upon this research framework, the present study broadens the literature perspective by investigating the roles of ACEs and PSC, offering a more comprehensive view of how ACEs and social resources influence phubbing behavior [28].

Overall, social cognitive theory and social capital theory provide a useful framework for understanding the mechanisms underlying phubbing behavior [19, 20, 31, 32, 39]. Social cognitive theory [19, 20] posits that individual behavior is shaped by the interplay between environmental experiences, cognitive processes, and psychological states. Within this framework, ACEs may impact an individual’s emotional regulation and cognitive patterns, increasing their susceptibility to depression and maladaptive coping behaviors such as excessive smartphone use in social settings. Concurrently, social capital theory [31, 32] emphasizes the protective role of social resources embedded within interpersonal networks. An individual’s social capital can mitigate the negative psychological consequences of ACEs by providing emotional support and social connections, thereby reducing the propensity to engage in phubbing behavior [39]. Thus, PSC may serve as a moderator in the pathway linking ACEs, depression, and phubbing behavior.

In summary, this study proposes the following hypotheses:H1. ACEs are positively associated with phubbing behaviorH2. ACEs are positively associated with depressionH3. Depression is positively associated with phubbing behaviorH4. Depression mediates the relationship between ACEs and phubbing behaviorH5. PSC moderates both the direct and indirect associations between ACEs and phubbing behavior. Materials & methods

Study design and participants

This study employed stratified sampling based on major level. Participants were selected from nine colleges and 24 majors at a comprehensive medical university in Fujian, China. The enrollment quota for each major is determined based on the proportion of first-year students enrolled in that major. Within each major, participants were recruited through convenience sampling at class level, and then, a full survey was conducted on the selected classes. Data collection occurred from October 8, 2024 to November 8, 2024, utilizing both online (via the Questionnaire Star platform) and offline (paper questionnaires in classrooms) methods. Participation was entirely voluntary. To acknowledge respondents’ time investment, researchers provided low-value non-monetary tokens of appreciation (e.g., candy). A total of 1999 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding incomplete or invalid responses, 1918 valid questionnaires were retained (valid response rate = 95.95%). Among these, 888 were collected online and 1030 offline. To enhance quality control, we incorporated simple calculation questions into the questionnaire, implemented double data entry during data input, and conducted manual screening during data processing. Measures

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)

ACEs were assessed using the Chinese version of the Revised Adverse Childhood Experiences Questionnaire (ACEQ-R) [40, 41]. This scale comprises 14 dichotomous items (0 = absent, 1 = present), each representing a type of ACE. The total score ranges from 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating a greater variety of ACEs. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in the Chinese population [42, 43]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.862.

Depression

The Chinese version of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used to assess depression [44], which consists of 9 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = some days, 2 = more than half the time, 3 = nearly every day). The total score ranges from 0 to 27; the higher the score represents the more serious the depression [45]. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in the Chinese population and has been widely used for depression screening [46, 47]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.936.

Phubbing behavior

Phubbing behavior was assessed using the Chinese version of the Generic Scale of Phubbing (GSP) [48]. This scale contains 4 dimensions and consists of 15 items using a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree). A total score ranges from 15 to 75, with higher scores reflecting more severe phubbing [49]. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in the Chinese population [50]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.947.

Personal social capital (PSC)

The Chinese version of the Personal Social Capital Scale 8 (PSCS-8) was used to assess PSC [51, 52], the scale applies a 5-point Likert rating system (from 1 = very few to 5 = very many). The total score ranges from 8 to 40, with higher scores representing higher levels of PSC [33]. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in the Chinese population [53]. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale in this study was 0.944.

Covariates

Gender (male and female), grade (freshman year, sophomore, junior year, and senior year and above), major (medicine, science and engineering, and others), monthly living expenses (≤ 1000 yuan, 1001-1500 yuan, 1501-2000 yuan, 2001-2500 yuan, and > 2500 yuan), and area of residence (natural village, township, county seat, and central urban area).

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were presented as frequency (n) and percentage (%), and continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Independent samples t-tests and ANOVA were conducted to compare differences in phubbing behavior scores across groups with different sociodemographic characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine associations between ACEs, depression, PSC, and phubbing. Prior to conducting the regression analyses, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The results showed that all VIF values ranged from 1.239 to 1.319, which are well below the commonly recommended threshold of 5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious concern in this study. Before testing the moderated mediation model, all continuous variables were mean-centered to enhance the interpretability of the interaction terms. Confirmatory factor analysis using AMOS and the model fit was assessed using multiple indices: χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA, and SRMR. Hayes’ PROCESS macro was employed to examine the mediating role (Model 4) and the moderated mediation (Model 8), followed by simple slope analysis. The bias-corrected percentile bootstrap method with 5000 resamples was applied to test. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 with a significance test level α = 0.05. For the mediation and moderated mediation analyses, only standardized regression coefficients are reported in the main text, the unstandardized coefficients are presented in Table S1 and S2.

Results

Common method bias

To address the potential risk of common method bias (CMB) due to the self-reported data, we conducted Harman’s single-factor to test. The results of the exploratory factor analysis showed that six factors had eigenvalues greater than 1. The first factor accounted for 34.147% of the total variance, which is below the recommended threshold of 40%. Therefore, CMB was not a serious concern in this study.

Sociodemographic characteristics

This study included a total of 1918 participants, with a higher proportion of females (61.1%, n = 1172) than males (38.9%, n = 746). In terms of grade, freshmen constituted the largest group (35.8%, n = 686), while seniors and above represented the smallest (18.0%, n = 345). Among majors, medicine major accounted for the majority (55.0%, n = 1055), whereas other major was the minority (13.1%, n = 251). In monthly living expenses, only 129 participants (6.7%) reported expenditures ≤ 1000 Yuan, in contrast to the largest group, which fell in the 1501–2000 Yuan (34.4%, n = 660). The highest proportion of respondents resided in central urban areas (31.4%, n = 602), while the smallest proportion came from natural villages (16.3%, n = 313).

Independent t-tests and ANOVA results demonstrated significant differences (P < 0.001) in phubbing behavior score across gender, grade, major, monthly living expenses, and area of residence (Table 1).

The results showed that the independent variables were significantly correlated with the dependent variables in the expected directions. Specifically, ACEs were positively correlated with depression (r = 0.392, P < 0.001) and phubbing behavior (r = 0.433, P < 0.001). Depression was positively correlated with phubbing behavior (r = 0.531, P < 0.001). In contrast, PSC was negatively correlated with ACEs (r = − 0.342, P < 0.001), depression (r = − 0.412, P < 0.001), and phubbing behavior (r = − 0.572, P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Confirmatory factor analysis

The results of confirmatory factor analysis showed that: χ²/df = 14.20, CFI = 0.80, TLI = 0.78, RMSEA = 0.083, SRMR = 0.048. The elevated χ²/df ratio is expected given the sensitivity of the chi-square statistic to large samples (N = 1918). Although the CFI and TLI were slightly below the conventional cutoff of 0.90, the RMSEA was within an acceptable range (< 0.10), and the SRMR indicated good fit (< 0.08) (Table S3).

All standardized factor loadings were significant (P < 0.001). For depression, phubbing, and PSC, the factor loadings ranged from 0.575 to 0.886, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.50. CR values for all variables ranged from 0.876 to 0.945, indicating good internal consistency. AVE values for depression (0.622), phubbing (0.530), and PSC (0.683) exceeded 0.50, supporting adequate convergent validity, while the AVE for ACEs (0.350) was slightly below this threshold (Table S3).

Mediation analysis

ACEs were significantly and positively associated with phubbing behavior (β = 0.342, t = 16.678, P < 0.001), with the model explaining 29.4% of the variance in phubbing behavior(R² = 0.294, F = 132.570, P < 0.001). When depression was included as a mediator, the overall model remained significant and explained 41.8% of the variance (R² = 0.418, F = 190.263, P < 0.001). The mediating role of depression was significant, with an indirect effect of 0.126 (95% CI = 0.111–0.164), accounting for 36.84% of the total effect (Table 3 and 4). Discussion

The relationship between ACEs and phubbing behavior

This study found that ACEs were positively associated with Phubbing behavior among students at a medical university in China, consistent with previous findings [22, 54–56]. Chinese college students exposed to ACEs may exhibit poorer social and cognitive functioning, increasing their likelihood of seeking emotional compensation through virtual interactions, which may reinforce phubbing and risk problematic smartphone use [56, 57]. Specifically, ACEs may impair social cognition and communication skills, making real-world interactions more challenging and driving individuals to rely on smartphones to fulfill emotional needs [55]. Furthermore, increased autonomy, interpersonal challenges, and academic pressures during university life may exacerbate vulnerability among individuals with ACEs, prompting them to escape stressors and negative emotions through excessive smartphone use [57, 58]. Therefore, in university mental health initiatives, early identification and social-emotional skill training for students with ACEs may enhance real-world interaction resilience, reducing compensatory smartphone use.

Mediating effect of depression

The results also indicated that depression was significantly associated with the relationship between ACEs and phubbing behavior, with a significant indirect association observed. Beyond the direct association, ACEs were linked to phubbing behavior through higher levels of depression. This finding reflects the psychological mechanisms that link early adversity to later behavioral outcomes and aligns with the “environment-cognition-behavior” framework of social cognitive theory. Concurrently, ACEs may disrupt cognitive development, emotional regulation, and coping abilities throughout adolescence [59, 60], leading to cognitive distortions and the formation of negative core beliefs such as feelings of inferiority or lack of belonging, such as inferiority or unworthiness, which further heighten vulnerability to depression [61]. Consistent with earlier studies, our results suggest that the detrimental influence of ACEs on mental health may extend to maladaptive behavioral responses, including phubbing [28]. This suggests that interventions should combine symptom relief with cognitive restructuring to address negative self-schemas and break the depression-phubbing cycle [62].

Moderating role of personal social capital

First, PSC was found to moderate these associations, such that the positive associations between ACEs and both depression and phubbing behavior were weaker among individuals with higher levels of PSC. According to social capital theory [31, 32], individuals with higher social capital can more effectively access emotional and instrumental resources to help them cope with adversity and regulate their behavior [39]. Thus, these findings support existing evidence that higher social capital reduces negative behavioral consequences by providing meaningful real-world social support [34, 63]. Furthermore, resilience-related concepts emphasize that an individual’s adaptive capacity in facing early adversity is not determined by a single trait but rather results from the synergistic interaction between internal characteristics and external protective resources [64]. As a key external protective resource, PSC may enhance psychological resilience in coping with adversity by compensating for the psychological resource deficits influenced by ACEs, thereby reducing the risks of depression and maladaptive behaviors.

On the other hand, various social networks within individual social capital may enhance the perceived value and feasibility of real-world social interactions by strengthening social support [65]. This, in turn, weakens individuals’ motivation to escape reality through smartphones and reduces screen-gazing behavior. This complements findings from some studies suggesting that social capital may increase screen-gazing by expanding online social networks [66]. This further highlights the protective role played by the social capital embedded within an individual’s social network. Importantly, the aforementioned research findings can be interpreted within the context of Chinese culture. Chinese society exhibits collectivist characteristics and a tightly knit social structure, where close ties with family members, classmates, and neighbors form the core of PSC [67] Within this relational framework, frequent emotional exchanges, mutual support, and the practice of collective problem-solving may help mitigate psychological distress associated with ACEs. This culturally embedded pattern of interaction may enhance individuals’ sense of belonging, relational obligations, and perceived social value, while simultaneously reinforcing self-efficacy through positive group feedback [68, 69]. Consequently, within this cultural context, PSC may exert a particularly significant protective effect in mitigating depression and subsequent tendencies toward smartphone addiction.

Strengths

This study makes several contributions to the existing literature. First, while prior research has primarily focused on proximate psychological factors associated with mobile phone cold-shouldering behavior, this study broadens the perspective by examining ACEs as an early-life contextual factor related to such behavior. Second, it explores the mediating role of depression, revealing the underlying psychological pathway linking ACEs to smartphone neglect. Third, by introducing PSC as a moderator, the study highlights the potential buffering effect of social resources. These findings contribute to the literature by integrating early-life environmental factors, psychological mechanisms, and social resources into a unified framework for explaining phubbing behavior.

Limitations

Although the mediation intermediary model has theoretical justification, cross-sectional designs cannot draw causal conclusions. Longitudinal or experimental studies are required to verify the temporal sequence. Furthermore, this study relies on self-administered questionnaire data, which may introduce recall bias and self-report bias. The sample was drawn from a specific population (a medical university in China), potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to entire Chinese college students and other college student in different countries or areas. Although depression was examined as the primary psychological mechanism in this study, other potential psychological mediators linking ACEs and phubbing behavior were not explored. Prior research suggests that factors such as anxiety, stress, loneliness, and low self-esteem may also play a role. Future studies should examine these additional psychological pathways and incorporate broader psychological variables to better understand the mechanisms underlying this association. Another limitation of this study lies in the use of a blended online-offline survey method for data collection. Supplementary analyses revealed significant differences between the two groups in their ACEs and phubbing behavior scores: offline participants reported higher ACE scores (1.14 > 0.63), while online participants reported slightly higher mobile phone neglect behavior ratings (41.92 > 40.54) (Table S4). These differences may be attributable to variations in survey contexts or response environments associated with different data collection methods. Therefore, the possibility that survey method influenced respondents’ answers cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies are advised to adopt more uniform data collection methods to minimize potential survey method-related bias.

Conclusion

Depression partially mediates the relationship between ACEs and phubbing behavior students at a medical university in China. These findings highlight the importance of targeted screening and early identification of both ACE exposure and problematic smartphone use. For Chinese students who have experienced ACEs, mental health support and strategies to enhance PSC are crucial for reducing phubbing behavior and promoting healthier social functioning.
