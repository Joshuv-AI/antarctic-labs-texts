Dihedral solutions of the set theoretical Yang-Baxter equation
Alex W. Nowak, Anna Zamojska-Dzienio

Abstract
. We introduce the notion of a braided dihedral set (BDS) to describe set-theoretical solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation (YBE) that furnish representations of the infinite dihedral group on the Cartesian square of the underlying set. BDS which lead to representations of the symmetric group on three objects are called braided triality sets (BTS). Basic examples of BDS come from symmetric spaces. We show that Latin BDS (LBDS) can be described entirely in terms of involutions of uniquely 2-divisible Bruck loops. We show that isomorphism classes of LBDS are in one-to-one correspondence with conjugacy classes of involutions of uniquely 2-divisible Bruck loops. We describe all LBDS of prime, prime-square and 3 times prime-order, up to isomorphism. Using GAP , we enumerate isomorphism classes of LBDS of orders 27 and 81. Latin BTS, or LBTS, are shown to be in one-to-one correspondence with involutions of commutative Moufang loops of exponent 3 (CML3), and, as with LBDS, isomorphisms classes of LBTS coincide with conjugacy classes of CML3-involutions. We classify all LBTS of order at most 81.

1.1.
Background and motivation

The quantum Yang-Baxter equation, which first appeared in the field of statistical mechanics, has proven to be an object of interest in many areas of mathematics, including the representation theory of quantum groups
[
8
]
and knot theory
[
18
,
42
]
. We study its set-theoretical articulation, which was first proposed by Drinfeld
[

]
. Namely, a function of sets
r:Q^{2}\to Q^{2}
is a
set-theoretical solution to the quantum Yang-Baxter equation
if

(1.1)

(r\times 1_{Q})(1_{Q}\times r)(r\times 1_{Q})=(1_{Q}\times r)(r\times 1_{Q})(1_{Q}\times r).

The term

refers to a set-map pair
(Q,r)
such that

adheres to (
1.1

to refer to such a pair. We call a solution
(Q,r)

bijective
if

is bijective. A solution is

if

. It is

if
r^{2}=r
. More generally,
(Q,r)
has

if there is a positive integer

r^{n+i}=r^{i}

With respect to a binary operation

on

\displaystyle L^{*}_{q}

\displaystyle:x\mapsto q*x,

\displaystyle R^{*}_{q}

\displaystyle:x\mapsto x*q.

Now, given a solution

, define binary operations

and

on

such that

. If for each

is

is

. A solution is

if it is left and right nondegenerate. If

While nondegenerate, involutive solutions have garnered the most interest (

For a function

2.
Quasigroups, loops, and an alternative formulation of the SYBE

2.1.
Quasigroups

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Q
be a set, and consider three binary operations on
Q
:
*
denoting
multiplication
,
/^{*}

right division
, and
\backslash_{*}

left division
. Consider also the following identities in these operations:

\displaystyle\text{(IL)}\phantom{A}y\backslash_{*}(y*x)=x,

\displaystyle\text{(IR)}\phantom{A}x=(x*y)/^{*}y,

\displaystyle\text{(SL)}\phantom{A}y*(y\backslash_{*}x)=x,

\displaystyle\text{(SR)}\phantom{A}x=(x/^{*}y)*y.

If (IL) and (SL) hold, then
(Q,*,\backslash_{*})
is a
left quasigroup
. If (IR) and (SR) hold, then
(Q,*,/^{*})
is a
right quasigroup
. If all four identities hold, then
(Q,*,\backslash_{*},/^{*})
is a
quasigroup
.

Note that if we divide (SL) on the right by
y\backslash_{*}x
and (SR) on the left by
x/^{*}y
we get two identities:

(2.1)

x/^{*}(y\backslash_{*}x)=(x/^{*}y)\backslash_{*}x=y.

Remark 2.2
.

(a)

Given a magma (set under binary operation)
(Q,*)
,
*
is a left (resp. right) quasigroup multiplication if and only if, for all
q\in Q
, the left (right) translation map
L^{*}_{q}:Q\to Q;x\mapsto q*x
(
R^{*}_{q}:Q\to Q;x\mapsto x*q
) is invertible. Axiom (IL) (resp. (IR)) says that each left (right) translation is injective, while (SL) ((SR)) ensures surjectivity of each left (right) translation.

(b)

Going forward, it will be convenient to use concatenation to denote certain instances of the multiplication,
*
, in a quasigroup
(Q,*,\backslash_{*},/^{*})
. We will abbreviate
x*y
to
xy
, and concatenation will bind more tightly than
*
. For example,
(x*y)*z
will abbreviate as
xy*z.

(c)

A mapping
h\colon(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ},/^{\circ})\to(P,\bullet,\backslash_{\bullet},/^{\bullet})
between two quasigroups is a
quasigroup homomorphism
if it preserves the three quasigroup operations. However, preserving one operation implies that the property holds for the other two.

(d)

A triple of maps between quasigroups
(f,g,h):(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ},/^{\circ})\to(P,\bullet,\backslash_{\bullet},/^{\bullet})
is called a
homotopy
if
f(x)\bullet g(y)=h(x\circ y)
for all
x,y\in Q
. If each of the three maps is a bijection,
(f,g,h)
is called an
isotopy
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
Q
be a nonempty set, and

r:Q^{2}\to Q^{2};(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x\bullet y)

an invertible map. Let
\tau(x,y)=(y,x)
. The pair
(Q,r)
is said to be

(a)

a
braided set
, or a
solution
if
r
adheres to (
1.1
); that is, if

(2.2)

x\circ(y\circ z)=(x\circ y)\circ((x\bullet y)\circ z),

(2.3)

(x\circ y)\bullet((x\bullet y)\circ z)=(x\bullet(y\circ z))\circ(y\bullet z),

(2.4)

(x\bullet y)\bullet z=(x\bullet(y\circ z))\bullet(y\bullet z),

hold in Q;

(b)

left nondegenerate
if
\circ
gives rise to a left quasigroup
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ})
,
right nondegenerate
if
\bullet
furnishes a right quasigroup
(Q,\bullet,/^{\bullet})
, and
nondegenerate
if it is both left and right nondegenerate;

(c)

Latin
if
\circ
gives rise to a quasigroup
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ},/^{\circ})
;

(d)

dihedral
if
(\tau r)^{2}=1_{Q^{2}}
; that is, if

(2.5)

(x\bullet y)\bullet(x\circ y)=x,

(2.6)

(x\bullet y)\circ(x\circ y)=y,

each hold in
Q
;

(e)

a
triality set
if
(\tau r)^{2}=r^{3}=1_{Q^{2}}
; that is, if (
2.5
)-(
2.6
) hold, and

(2.7)

(x\circ y)\circ(x\bullet y)=y\bullet x,

(2.8)

(x\circ y)\bullet(x\bullet y)=y\circ x,

each hold in
Q
.

Remark 2.4
.

Nondegenerate braided sets are known as
biracks
and they were introduced in
[
11
]
in the context of the virtual knot theory
[
20
]
. It was proved in
[
39
]
that the following identities:

(2.9)

(x\setminus_{\circ}x)/^{\bullet}(x\setminus_{\circ}x)=x.

(2.10)

(x/^{\bullet}x)\setminus_{\circ}(x/^{\bullet}x)=x.

are equivalent in any birack. Then, a birack which satisfies (
2.9
) or (
2.10
) is called a
biquandle
.
If one considers the squaring mappings with respect to operations
\setminus_{\circ}
and
/^{\bullet}
(also known as

), then identities (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) say that in any biquandle mappings
\text{Sq}_{\setminus_{\circ}}
and
\text{Sq}_{/^{\bullet}}
are mutually inverse. In
[
17
]
it was shown that in any nondegenerate solutions of (

) diagonal mappings are commuting bijections, but not necessarily mutually inverse. However, Latin braided dihedral sets are biquandles satisfying, in addition,
\text{Sq}_{\setminus_{\circ}}=\text{Sq}_{/^{\bullet}}
(see Lemmas

and
A.3
in the Appendix).

There are some so-called
symmetry classes
of left and right quasigroups that will come up repeatedly that we shall now review. A magma satisfying

(2.11)

x*xy=y

is said to be
left symmetric
. A left symmetric magma is necessarily a left quasigroup, for
(L^{*}_{x})^{-1}=L^{*}_{x}
. Dually, magmas in which the right translations are involutive, so that

(2.12)

yx*x=y

holds, are

right quasigroups. Any magma in which

(2.13)

x*yx=y

holds is a quasigroup, for (
2.13
) implies
L^{*}_{x}=(R^{*}_{x})^{-1}
. Equivalently,
R^{*}_{x}=(L^{*}_{x})^{-1}
translates to

(2.14)

xy*x=y.

Quasigroups satisfying the equivalent identities (
2.13
)-(
2.14
) are

.
Note that Lemma

below may be used to show that any two of (
2.11
)-(
2.13
) imply the third. Quasigroups in which these three identities hold are called

quasigroups. Totally symmetric quasigroups are also commutative.

We conclude this subsection by collecting a couple of elementary quasigroup-theoretic results that will serve us later. The first relates the potential commutativity of a quasigroup to the potential right symmetry of the left division operation.

Lemma 2.5
.

With respect to a quasigroup
(Q,*,\backslash_{*},/^{*})
, all of the following identities are equivalent:

(a)

xy=yx
;

(b)

x/^{*}y=y\backslash_{*}x
;

(c)

(y\backslash_{*}x)\backslash_{*}x=y
.

Proof.

\text{(a)}\implies\text{(b)}
By (IL) and (SR),
x/^{*}y=y\backslash_{*}(y*(x/^{*}y))=y\backslash_{*}((x/^{*}y)*y)=y\backslash_{*}x.

\text{(b)}\implies\text{(a)}
By (SL) and (IR),
xy=y*(y\backslash_{*}(xy))=y*(xy/^{*}y)=yx
.

\text{(b)}\implies\text{(c)}
By (
2.1
),
(y\backslash_{*}x)\backslash_{*}x=x/^{*}(y\backslash_{*}x)=y.

\text{(c)}\implies\text{(b)}
By (
2.1
),
x/^{*}y=x/^{*}((y\backslash_{*}x)\backslash_{*}x)=y\backslash_{*}x.

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(Q,*,\backslash)
be a left quasigroup, and define
x^{S}:=x\backslash_{*}x
. If
x^{S}*xy=y
, then the following identities hold in
(Q,*,\backslash_{*})
:

(a)

S^{2}=1_{Q}

(b)

x*x^{S}y=y.

Proof.

Note
x^{S}\backslash_{*}y=x^{S}\backslash_{*}(x^{S}*xy)=xy
for any
x,y\in Q
. In particular
x^{S^{2}}=x^{S}\backslash_{*}x^{S}=x*x^{S}=x(x\backslash_{*}x)=x
, proving (a). To see that (b) holds, note
x^{S}y=x^{S}(x(x\backslash_{*}y))=x\backslash_{*}y

x*x^{S}y=x(x\backslash_{*}y)=y.

∎

2.2.
Idempotence and distributivity of operations

We shall denote the squaring map with respect to an operation
*
by
\text{Sq}_{*}(x)=x*x
. When context permits, we may also use exponent notation
x^{2}=x*x
. When exponentiation appears in an expression involving multiple quasigroup operations, it is understood that these powers are with respect to the multiplication. For example,
x^{2}/y=(x*x)/y
in
(Q,*,\backslash_{*},/^{*})
.
An element
x
of a magma
(Q,*)
is called an
idempotent
of the magma if

(2.15)

x^{2}=x.

If each element of
Q
is an idempotent, then
(Q,*)
is said to be
idempotent
.

If one expresses the quasigroup axioms (IL)-(SR) in terms of a single variable
x
, the next lemma follows.

Lemma 2.7
.

If an element
x
in a quasigroup
(Q,*,\backslash_{*},/^{*})
is idempotent with respect to one of the operations, then it is idempotent with respect to all of them.

A multiplication
*
is a
left distributive
operation if for all
x,y,z\in Q
,

(2.16)

x*(y*z)=(x*y)*(x*z).

A left distributive, left quasigroup is called a
rack
. Note that a map
r(x,y)=(x\circ y,x)
is a left nondegenerate, derived solution (cf. Section
1.1
) if and only if
(Q,\circ)
is a rack. Moreover, to any left nondegenerate solution
r:(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x\bullet y)
, one may associate the
derived solution

(x,y)\mapsto(x\triangleright^{r}y,x)
, where

(2.17)

x\triangleright^{r}y=x\circ(y\bullet(y\backslash_{\circ}x)),

and
(Q,\triangleright^{r})
is called the
derived rack
of the solution
[
38
, Thm. 1.1]
.

An idempotent rack is called a
quandle
. Quandles and racks which are two-sided quasigroups are referred to as
Latin
. A left symmetric quandle is often referred to as an
involutive quandle
or a
kei
. Whereas quandles provide algebraic invariants of oriented knots, the left-symmetric identity makes keis suitable for producing algebraic invariants of unoriented knots
[
19
]
.

2.3.
SYBE solutions coming from LF-left quasigroups

Until this point, we have been reviewing some standard terms and proving folkloric results from quasigroup theory. In this section, however, we will provide some original observations and new results that clarify the role that LF-left quasigroups play in generating braided sets.

For a left nondegenerate map
r:(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x\bullet y)
, it will be convenient for us to abbreviate
\text{Sq}_{\backslash_{\circ}}(x)=x\backslash_{\circ}x
to simply
S
. Moreover, we employ the exponential notation
x^{S}:=x\backslash_{\circ}x.
The variety of
LF-left quasigroups
is generated by the following variation of (
2.16
):

(2.18)

x\circ(y\circ z)=(x\circ y)\circ(x^{S}\circ z).

Remark 2.8
.

A (two-sided) quasigroup satisfying (
2.18
) is called a left F-quasigroup (or an LF-quasigroup). Dually, one defines right F-quasigroups via

(2.19)

(z*y)*x=(z*(x/^{*}x))*(y*x).

If a quasigroup satisfies both (
2.18
) and (
2.19
), it belongs to the variety of F-quasigroups, which is one of the oldest studied varieties of quasigroups, introduced already by Murdoch in 1939, although he did not use this name. For details and comprehensive references, see
[
34
]
or
[
35
, Chapter 6]
.

The following lemma generalizes a known property of LF-quasigroups to LF-left quasigroups. In fact, the proof given in
[
34
, Lmm. 2.1]
does not rely on a right division, so we will simply reproduce it below.

Lemma 2.9
.

In an LF-left quasigroup,
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ})
, the map
x^{S}=x\backslash_{\circ}x
is an endomorphism.

Proof.

Since
y\circ y^{S}=y,
(
2.18
) implies
x\circ y=x\circ(y\circ y^{S})=(x\circ y)\circ(x^{S}\circ y^{S})
, and, thus,
(x\circ y)^{S}=(x\circ y)\backslash_{\circ}(x\circ y)=(x\circ y)\backslash_{\circ}((x\circ y)\circ(x^{S}\circ y^{S}))=x^{S}\circ y^{S}
.
∎

The following proposition illustrates that LF-left quasigroups are to left nondegenerate solutions of the form
(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x^{S})
as racks are to left nondegenerate derived solutions.

Proposition 2.10
.

A left nondegenerate map of the form
(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x^{S})
is a braiding if and only if
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ})
is an LF-left quasigroup.

Proof.

If
(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x^{S})
is a left nondegenerate braiding, then (
2.18
) follows from (
2.2
).

Conversely, assume
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ})
is an LF-left quasigroup and define
r:(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x^{S})
. Then (
2.2
) follows by definition. Moreover,
(
2.4
)
holds trivially. Finally, (
2.3
) translates to
(x\circ y)^{S}=x^{S}\circ y^{S}
, which is established in Lemma
2.9
Hence, (
2.2
)-(
2.4
) are satisfied and
(Q,r)
is braided.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Assume that
(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x\bullet y)
is a Latin braiding. Then
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ},/^{\circ})
is an LF-quasigroup if and only if
x\bullet y=x^{S}
.

Proof.

Suppose
(x,y)\mapsto(x\circ y,x\bullet y)
is a Latin braiding and that
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ},/^{\circ})
is an LF-quasigroup.
Upon dividing (
2.18

, we have

\displaystyle x^{S}\circ z

\displaystyle=(x\circ y)\backslash_{\circ}(x\circ(y\circ z))

\displaystyle=(x\circ y)\backslash_{\circ}((x\circ y)\circ((x\bullet y)\circ z))

\displaystyle=(x\bullet y)\circ z,

where the second equality comes from (
2.2
). Since
\circ
accompanies a right division, we can cancel the

in the first and last equalities above, yielding

The converse follows directly from Proposition

∎

Remark 2.12

We remark on the necessity of the Latin property in Proposition

2.4.
Loops

A quasigroup with a multiplicative identity is called a
loop
. We write
(Q,*,e)
to describe the structure of a loop. Note that the loop axioms are not enough to ensure the existence two-sided inverses. That is, for some
x\in Q
there may exist
y\neq z
such that
xy=zx=e
. When a loop does have two-sided inverses, we write
x^{-1}
to denote that unique element for which
x^{-1}x=xx^{-1}=e
. A loop with two-sided inverses in which

(2.20)

(xy)^{-1}=x^{-1}y^{-1},

holds for all
x
and
y
is called an
automorphic inverse property loop
(AIP).
A loop
Q
is said to have the
left inverse property
(we call
Q
an LIP loop) if for every
x\in Q
, there is a two-sided inverse,
x^{-1}
such that for all
y\in Q
,

(2.21)

\displaystyle x^{-1}(xy)=x(x^{-1}y)=y.

The two-sided inverse of a
right inverse property loop
(RIP loop) adheres to

(2.22)

\displaystyle y=(yx^{-1})x=(yx)x^{-1}.

Inverse property loops
(IP loops) possess the left and right inverse properties. A loop in which

(2.23)

x(y(xz))=(x(yx))z

for all
x,y,z\in Q
is called a
(left) Bol loop
. Bol loops are LIP loops
[
31
, Thm. 2.1]
. Moreover, Bol loops are power-associative, i.e., every element generates a group. In fact, by
[
31
, Lmm. 2.1]
for all
x,y\in Q
and all integers
n
,

(2.24)

y^{n}x=y^{n-1}y*x=y^{n-1}*yx.

If a Bol loop satisfies the AIP, it is called a
Bruck loop
. We will use an additive notation for Bruck loops:
(Q,+,0)
, but keep in mind that these need not be commutative. A Bruck loop in which the map
x\mapsto x+x=:2x
is invertible is called a
uniquely
2
-divisible Bruck loop
. Finite uniquely
2
-divisible Bruck loops have odd order
[
13
, Prop. 1]
. An identity these loops adhere to (cf.
[
13
, Lmm. 1]
) which we will employ in Section
4.1.1
is

(2.25)

2(x+y)=x+(2y+x).

If
(Q,*,\backslash_{*},/^{*})
is a left symmetric Latin quandle with fixed element
e\in Q,
then

(2.26)

x+y=(x/^{*}e)*(ey)

endows
Q
with the structure of a uniquely
2
-divisible Bruck loop whose identity element is
e
. Conversely, given a uniquely
2
-divisible Bruck loop
(Q,+,e),
the operation

(2.27)

x*y=2x-y

is a left-symmetric Latin quandle multiplication. These two constructions are mutually inverse
[
41
, Thm. 1]
.

Moufang loops

3.2.

The concept of symmetric space offers a powerful, unifying framework for aspects of Lie groups and Riemannian manifolds. Loos’s purely algebraic definition
[

best suits our purposes, as it extends the concept to discrete spaces.

Definition 3.5
.

A magma
(Q,*)
is a

if it is
a left symmetric quandle.
If
Q
is a manifold, we require
*:Q^{2}\to Q
to be a differentiable map, and that for all

such that if

Equivalently, a symmetric space
(Q,s)

(for

s=\{s_{x}:Q\to Q\mid x\in Q\}
such that each
s_{x}
fixes

, and for every

x
and

adhere to the distributivity condition

(3.1)

s_{s_{x}(y)}(s_{x}(z))=s_{x}(s_{y}(z)).

Moreover, if
Q
is a manifold, we require each
s_{x}
to be differentiable and that there be a neighborhood in which
x
is the sole fixed point of
s_{x}
. A
pointed

(Q,s,o)
is a symmetric space with a fixed

.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(Q,s,o)
be a pointed symmetric space. Then
(x,y)\mapsto(s_{x}(y),x)
and

Begin by letting

.

and

. Hence,
(Q,r)
is nondegenerate. Consider
(a,b,c)\in Q^{3}
. Then, by direct calculations in (
1.1
) with one application of (

), we obtain

is a braiding:

\displaystyle r^{12}r^{23}r^{12}(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=r^{12}r^{23}(s_{a}(b),a,c)=r^{12}(s_{a}(b),s_{a}(c),a)=(s_{s_{a}(b)}(s_{a}(c)),s_{a}(b),a)=

\displaystyle=(s_{a}(s_{b}(c)),s_{a}(b),a)=r^{23}r^{12}r^{23}(a,b,c).

And, finally,

is dihedral, again due to direct calculations and involutivity of symmetries. Namely, for

\displaystyle(\tau r)^{2}(a,b)=\tau r\tau(s_{a}(b),a)=\tau r(a,s_{a}(b))=\tau(s_{a}(s_{a}(b)),a)=(a,b).

Now, we let

. The second entry of

is

\displaystyle s_{s_{a}(s_{o}(b))}(s_{o}(s_{s_{o}(a)}(s_{o}(c))))

\displaystyle=s_{s_{a}(s_{o}(b))}(s_{o}(s_{o}(s_{a}(c))))

\displaystyle=s_{s_{a}(s_{o}(b))}(s_{a}(c))

\displaystyle=s_{a}(s_{s_{o}(b)}(c))

\displaystyle=s_{a}(s_{s_{o}(b)}(s_{o}(s_{o}(c))))

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

be an LBDS. Then all of the following hold:

(a)

.

(b)

(c)

Proof.

A proof of the fact that

∎

In any LBDS

(b)

(c)

(a) The fact that

. Let

so that

We claim

is an isomorphism of

onto
(Q,\boxplus,f)
. Since

and
e
are idempotent and
S
is an automorphism of
(Q,\circ,\backslash_{\circ},/^{\circ})
,

,

is idempotent, and, by Lemma

is an automorphism of

.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
(Q,+,e)
be a uniquely

. Then the operations

Proof.

We need to check that

is a quasigroup satisfying identities (

) and (

). Identities (IL), (SL), and (

) follow from the LIP and AIP. The fact that

is due to power-associativity of Bruck loops. Furthermore, (

\displaystyle(x\circ y)\circ(x^{S}\circ z)

\displaystyle=2(2x-y^{S})-(2x^{S}-z^{S})^{S}=2(2x-y^{S})-(2x-z)

\displaystyle=(2x+(-2y^{S}+2x))-(2x-z)=2x+(-2y^{S}+(2x+(-2x+z)))

\displaystyle=2x+(-2y^{S}+z)=2x-(2y-z^{S})^{S}=x\circ(y\circ z).

We used (

\displaystyle=2(y^{S}+\frac{1}{2}(-y^{S}+x))-y^{S}=(y^{S}+((-y^{S}+x)+y^{S}))-y^{S}

\displaystyle=y^{S}+((-y^{S}+x)+(y^{S}-y^{S}))=x.

The penultimate equality is by (

Lemma 4.15
.

The constructions of Lemmas

and

are mutually inverse. That is, if

is a uniquely 2-divisible Bruck loop with involutive automorphism

. Conversely, if

Proof.

Suppose
(Q,+,e)

2

4.1.2.

Suppose that

We can now also plainly see that

Next, define

(b)

Appendix A
Some proofs

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(Q,r)
be an LBDS. Then
(Q,r)
is a non-degenerate solution.

Proof.

We will show that, for each
y\in Q
, the right translation map
R^{\bullet}_{y}\colon Q\to Q
,
R^{\bullet}_{y}(x)=x\bullet y
is a bijection. Let
x\bullet y=z\bullet y
for some
x,z\in Q
. By (
2.6
) one has:
(x\bullet y)\circ(x\circ y)=(z\bullet y)\circ(z\circ y)
and, since
(Q,\circ)
is a left quasigroup,
x\circ y=z\circ y
. Now, since
(Q,\circ)
is a right quasigroup, too,
x=z
and
R^{\bullet}_{y}
is an injection. To show that it is
onto
, consider the element
a=w\bullet(w\setminus_{\circ}y)\in Q
for some
w\in Q
. Then

\displaystyle(w\bullet(w\setminus_{\circ}y))\bullet y=(w\bullet(w\setminus_{\circ}y))\bullet(w\circ(w\setminus_{\circ}y))=w.

The first equality is due to the fact that
(Q,\circ)
is a left quasigroup, so
w\circ(w\setminus_{\circ}y)=y
and the second one, by (
2.5
). This shows that for each
w\in Q
there exists
a\in Q
such that
w=R^{\bullet}_{y}(a)
and
R^{\bullet}_{y}
is a surjection.
∎

Proof.

Translating (

Lemma A.3
.

The diagonal mapping
S
is involutive. In particular,
S=T
.

Proof.

Substituting

by

in (

Lemma A.4
.

In any LBDS
(Q,r)
, all of the following hold:

(a)

x\bullet(x\bullet y)=(x\bullet y)\circ x
.

(b)

(x\bullet y)\bullet x=x\circ(x\bullet y)
.

(c)

(x\bullet y)\bullet(y\setminus_{\circ}y)=x
.

Proof.

(a) By (
2.6
) and (
2.2
) we have:

\displaystyle y=(x\bullet y)\circ(x\circ y)=((x\bullet y)\circ x)\circ(((x\bullet y)\bullet x)\circ y).

On the other hand, by (
2.6
), we have also

\displaystyle(((x\bullet y)\bullet x)\bullet y)\circ(((x\bullet y)\bullet x)\circ y)=y.

And, since
(Q,\circ)
is a right quasigroup,
(x\bullet y)\circ x=((x\bullet y)\bullet x)\bullet y
follows. Now, by (
2.4
) and (
2.5
)
((x\bullet y)\bullet x)\bullet y=x\bullet(x\bullet y)
.

Substituting in (a)
x
by
x\bullet y
and
y
by
x\circ y
we obtain (b).

First, to prove (c), substitute in (
2.2
)

x
by
x\bullet y
,
y
by
y\setminus_{\circ}y
and
z
by
y
:

\displaystyle(x\bullet y)\circ(y\setminus_{\circ}y)=((x\bullet y)\circ(y\setminus_{\circ}y))\circ(((x\bullet y)\bullet(y\setminus_{\circ}y))\circ y).

Since
(Q,\circ)
is a left quasigroup, we obtain then

\displaystyle((x\bullet y)\bullet(y\setminus_{\circ}y))\circ y=((x\bullet y)\circ(y\setminus_{\circ}y))\setminus_{\circ}((x\bullet y)\circ(y\setminus_{\circ}y))=((x\bullet y)\circ(y\setminus_{\circ}y))^{S}.

By (

Lemma A.5
.

Let
(Q,r)
be an LBDS. Then
(x\circ y)\bullet z=(x\bullet z)\circ(y\bullet(x\circ z))
.

Proof.

By (
2.5
) and (
2.6
),
y\bullet(x\circ z)=((x\bullet y)\circ(x\circ y))\bullet(((x\bullet y)\bullet(x\circ y))\circ z)
. Applying now (
2.3
) to the latter, we obtain the following

\displaystyle y\bullet(x\circ z)=\underbrace{((x\bullet y)\bullet((x\circ y)\circ z))}_{w}\circ\underbrace{((x\circ y)\bullet z)}_{d}.

Now, consider the expression

(w\bullet d)\circ(w\circ d)=d

) and (

w\bullet d=((x\bullet y)\bullet(x\circ y))\bullet z=x\bullet z

∎

Lemma A.6
.

Let
(Q,r)
be an LBDS. Then

Proof.

We substitute
