Probably Approximately Correct Maximum A Posteriori Inference
Matthew Shorvon, Frederik Mallmann-Trenn, David S. Watson

Abstract
Computing the conditional mode of a distribution, better known as the maximum a posteriori (MAP) assignment, is a fundamental task in probabilistic inference. However, MAP estimation is generally intractable, and remains hard even under many common structural constraints and approximation schemes. We introduce probably approximately correct (PAC) algorithms for MAP inference that provide provably optimal solutions under variable and fixed computational budgets. We characterize tractability conditions for PAC-MAP using information theoretic measures that can be estimated from finite samples. Our PAC-MAP solvers are efficiently implemented using probabilistic circuits with appropriate architectures. The randomization strategies we develop can be used either as standalone MAP inference techniques or to improve on popular heuristics, fortifying their solutions with rigorous guarantees. Experiments confirm the benefits of our method in a range of benchmarks.

Introduction

Given evidence
\bm{E}=\bm{e}
, what is the most likely value of one or more query variables
\bm{Q}
? Solving such questions is a fundamental task in probabilistic inference, where it is generally known as the
maximum a posteriori
(MAP) problem. It comes up naturally in prediction and planning, where we frequently need to make decisions under uncertainty on the basis of available information. Common examples include diagnosing disease status from physical symptoms or correcting corrupted portions of an image based on the content of surrounding pixels.

Supervised learning provides adaptive procedures for tackling such problems, but these methods face important limitations. In the continuous setting, regression algorithms are generally designed to estimate conditional
expectations
, which may correspond to low-density regions of the target distribution. Classifiers, on the other hand, typically compute MAP by default, if constrained to return a single label. However, supervised learners of either type are
discriminative
models that require (a) an
a priori
selection of the outcome variable
Y
and (b) conditioning on a complete feature vector
\bm{x}
.
A full
generative
model, by contrast, allows users to compute MAP for arbitrary query variables and potentially use partial evidence, conditioning on a strict subset of the available features
\bm{e}\subsetneq\bm{x}
. This latter task is sometimes referred to as marginal MAP (MMAP).
Generative models can also be used to compute the most probable explanation (MPE)—i.e., the most likely joint assignment to all variables, with an empty conditioning set—a task that cannot be solved with discriminative techniques.

These maximization problems have been widely studied in probabilistic reasoning, e.g. via Bayesian networks (BNs)
(
10
)
, probabilistic graphical models (PGMs)
(
17
)
, and probabilistic circuits (PCs)
(
30
)
.
The latter formalism is especially useful in analyzing the complexity of tasks like MAP, as structural properties of PCs can often guarantee the existence of polytime solutions.
In general, however, MAP estimation is
\text{NP}^{\text{PP}}
-complete
(
23
)
and remains intractable even under various constraints on graph structure and variable cardinality
(
11
;
12
)
.
Previous authors
(
10
;
6
)
have established sufficient conditions for exact polytime MAP inference in PCs, including a property known as
determinism
(formally defined below).
However, this constraint is highly restrictive and limits the expressive capacity of resulting circuits.

We take a different approach, striking a pragmatic balance between accuracy and efficiency to compute MAP estimates. Specifically, we propose algorithms for
probably approximately correct
(PAC)-MAP inference. These methods are not guaranteed to produce exact or even
\varepsilon
-approximate MAP solutions (although, as we demonstrate below, they can and often do).
Instead, their outputs satisfy a strictly weaker guarantee: for any user-supplied parameters
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, PAC-MAP solutions are within a factor
1-\varepsilon
of the true mode, with probability at least
1-\delta
.
Our results illustrate that introducing nonzero error tolerance and failure probability can improve practical performance in many cases.

We study the properties of these randomized algorithms in discrete and continuous settings.
We derive a uniformly optimal PAC-MAP solver and characterize tractability conditions in information theoretic terms.
When a target guarantee is unattainable within a fixed computational budget, we propose an efficient, Pareto-optimal procedure for recovering relaxed PAC certificates.
Though Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods for randomized MAP inference have been proposed in the Bayesian modeling literature
(
31
;
15
;
28
)
, these approaches generally rely on strong parametric assumptions and come with few or no theoretical guarantees.
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to propose PAC techniques for MAP.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Following a brief review of notation and formal definitions in Sect.
2
, we introduce and study oracle versions of our PAC-MAP solvers in Sect.
3
. In Sect.
4
, we examine circuit architectures that efficiently implement these procedures. Experimental results are presented in Sect.
5
. Following a brief discussion in Sect.
6
, we conclude in Sect.
7
.

Background

Let
\bm{Z}\in\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a collection of random variables with joint distribution
P
and probability mass/density function
p
.
We partition the features into three disjoint sets,
\bm{Z}=\bm{Q}\cup\bm{E}\cup\bm{V}
, representing queries, evidence, and nuisance variables, respectively.
The maximization task is to compute the assignment
\bm{q}^{*}
(not necessarily unique) satisfying:

\displaystyle\bm{q}^{*}:=\argmax_{\bm{q}}~\sum_{\bm{v}}~p(\bm{q},\bm{v}\mid\bm{e}).

The corresponding posterior mass/density is
p^{*}:=p(\bm{q}^{*}\mid\bm{e})
.
When
\bm{V}\neq\emptyset
, we have an instance of MMAP.
When
\bm{V}=\bm{E}=\emptyset
, we have an instance of MPE.
Otherwise, we have an instance of MAP.
With slight abuse of terminology, we use the term MAP to refer to this entire class of maximization problems, unless it is important to specify that we are in the MMAP or MPE setting.

Even with binary data, MAP is notoriously difficult. Let
n
be the dimensionality of the query space
\mathcal{Q}
. Because the number of possible assignments to queries grows exponentially with the size of the state space, brute force strategies for solving the problem require
\Omega\big(\exp(n)\big)
operations in the worst case, even with access to an oracle for computing marginals.
Indeed, MAP is
\text{NP}^{\text{PP}}
-complete in general, while approximating MAP to within any constant factor is NP-hard
(
23
)
.

To better understand the complexity of MAP inference and develop new methods for tackling this problem, we adopt the language of probabilist circuits. This formalism builds on and unifies various algorithmic approaches to probabilistic inference, including arithmetic circuits
(
9
)
, sum-product networks
(
25
)
, and probabilistic sentential decision diagrams
(
16
)
.
PCs are directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) with univariate probability distributions as their leaf nodes, and either sum or product operations as their inner nodes. They define joint distributions as mixtures (sums) and factorizations (products) over simpler distributions.

Formally, let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{G},\bm{\theta})
denote a PC over variables
\bm{Z}
with graphical structure
\mathcal{G}
and mixture weights
\bm{\theta}
. Let
\mathsf{N}
denote a node in
\mathcal{C}
, and
\operatorname{ch}(\mathsf{N})
the set of
\mathsf{N}
’s children. The probability distribution at
\mathsf{N}
is then recursively defined as follows:

\mathsf{N}(\bm{z})=\begin{cases}p_{\mathsf{N}}(\bm{z})\>&\text{if $\mathsf{N}$ is a leaf node}\\
\prod_{c\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathsf{N})}c(\bm{z})&\text{if $\mathsf{N}$ is a product node}\\
\sum_{c\in\operatorname{ch}(\mathsf{N})}\theta_{\mathsf{N},c}\cdot c(\bm{z})&\text{if $\mathsf{N}$ is a sum node}.\end{cases}

A major advantage of PCs is that structural properties of circuits can guarantee polytime inference for various probabilistic inference tasks, including marginalization and maximization. We consider three properties in particular: smoothness, decomposability, and determinisim.

A sum node is
smooth
if all of its children depend on all of the same variables.
A product node is
decomposable
if all of its children depend on disjoint sets of variables.
We say that a PC is smooth if all of its sum nodes are smooth, and decomposable if all of its product nodes are decomposable. A smooth, decomposable PC can compute marginal queries of the form
\sum_{\bm{v}}p(\bm{q},\bm{v})
in linear time
(
9
;
24
)
.
1
1

1

The complexity of inference tasks in PCs is measured w.r.t. circuit size
|\mathcal{C}|
(i.e., the number of edges in
\mathcal{G}
).

A sum node is
deterministic
if, for all fully instantiated inputs, the output of at most one of its children is nonzero.
Deterministic PCs impose a partition on the feature space, such that any full assignment of values to variables eliminates all but a single path through the sum nodes. It has been shown that decomposable, deterministic PCs can solve MAP in linear time
(
4
)
.
Though the problem remains intractable for non-deterministic PCs, several exact or approximate MAP solvers have been proposed for this setting
(
7
;
22
;
21
;
5
;
2
)
. We examine these methods more closely in Sect.
5
.

3
Oracle PAC-MAP

In this section, we propose novel, randomized approaches to MAP inference.
Solutions delivered by our methods are neither exactly nor approximately maximal, but
probably approximately correct
.
We prove the optimality of these procedures and characterize tractability conditions.

We assume access to a
sampler
, which generates i.i.d. draws from the conditional distribution
P(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
; and an
oracle
, which provides exact solutions to queries of the form
p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})=~?
, for any
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}
.
In Sect.
4
, we will consider practical methods for implementing these essential functions.

We say that algorithm
A
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-
PAC-MAP solver
if, for any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
,
A
returns some
\hat{\bm{q}}
such that

\displaystyle\Pr\big(p(\hat{\bm{q}}\mid\bm{e})\geq p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)\big)\geq 1-\delta,

with randomness over the sampling distribution of candidates,
\hat{\bm{q}}\sim P(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
.
If the inequality holds, we say that the true maximizer
\bm{q}^{*}
is
PAC-identified
.
Moreover,
A
is
tractable
if its runtime can be upperbounded by a polynomial function of
n

and
\log(1/\delta)
.
2
2

2

Typically, we include
1/\varepsilon
among the inputs that characterize feasibility for PAC algorithms. This is not the case for PAC-MAP. See Appx. A for a discussion.

We say that
A
is
purely random
if its search procedure relies exclusively on sampling from the target distribution, as opposed to
adaptive
PAC-MAP solvers that employ a mix of exploration and exploitation.

An important point to acknowledge upfront is that we cannot guarantee the existence of tractable PAC-MAP solutions for arbitrary distributions.
Indeed, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon,\delta
, the task is effectively indistinguishable from classical MAP, and therefore at least NP-hard.
However, sampling provides a principled strategy for navigating the state space and qualifying our best guess with appropriate precision and confidence.

3.1
Binary data

Consider the binary setting, with
\mathcal{Q}=\{0,1\}^{n}
. Generalization to arbitrary discrete queries is straightforward, while continuous extensions are handled separately below.

A simple strategy for randomized MAP estimation is to draw
m
samples from
P(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
and return the maximizing assignment
\hat{\bm{q}}:=\argmax_{i\in[m]}~p(\bm{q}_{i}\mid\bm{e})
. Call this naïve approach Alg. 0.

Lemma 1

(Discovery complexity)
.

Fix any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
.
Define the
\varepsilon
-superlevel set:

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{\bm{q}:p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})\geq p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)\big\},

with corresponding mass
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{\bm{q}\in G_{\varepsilon}}p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})
.
Then Alg. 0 PAC-identifies
\bm{q}^{*}
in
\Theta\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\log\delta^{-1}\big)
samples.

(All proofs in Appx. A.) Lemma
1
provides tight bounds on the complexity of a straightforward sampling approach.
Of course, Alg. 0 is not much use without prior information on
p^{*}
or
\mu_{\varepsilon}
.
However, the result highlights an important distinction between
discovering
and
certifying
PAC-MAP solutions.
Just as computing and verifying answers to decision problems characterize distinct complexity classes (assuming the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse), we must distinguish between the two aspects of our PAC-MAP task.
Suppose that, unbeknownst to us, the true distribution is uniform. Then
discovering
a solution is trivial, since every
\bm{q}
is in
G_{\varepsilon}
and so
\mu_{\varepsilon}=1
.
Certifying
that a candidate solution is PAC, by contrast, is maximally challenging, since we must search through an exponentially large number of candidates before we can be confident that we have not missed any more probable atoms.

In lieu of any distributional assumptions, we need some adaptive stopping criteria.
Our strategy, outlined in Alg.
1
, is to approach the maximal probability
p^{*}
from both directions: a lower bound
\hat{p}
that increases every time we find a new maximizer among the sampled atoms, and an upper bound
\check{p}
that decreases as we gradually remove assignments from consideration.
If
\hat{p}
rises above
\check{p}(1-\varepsilon)
, the algorithm halts, since this ensures an
\varepsilon
-approximate solution at any
\delta
. (Note that the solution is exact if
\hat{p}\geq\check{p}
.)
Otherwise, we continue sampling until we reach the threshold
\hat{p}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}
. If this stopping criterion is triggered, we issue a PAC certificate for the current leading candidate
\hat{\bm{q}}
.

Alg. 1 can be uncompetitive in high-entropy distributions. For example, with access to a pmf oracle, we can always solve MAP exactly in
2^{n}
queries by simply enumerating all possible assignments. Alg. 1 requires
2^{n}(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}
samples
to PAC-identify
\bm{q}^{*}
in the case of a uniform pmf, which exceeds the trivial bound whenever
(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}>1
. Conversely, PAC-MAP may be far more efficient than brute force methods when mass concentrates.
To formalize this, we use the
min-entropy
, aka the Rényi entropy of infinite order:

\displaystyle H_{\infty}(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e}):=-\log_{2}p^{*},

which represents the number of bits encoded by the MAP configuration. We henceforth abbreviate this statistic by
h:=H_{\infty}(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
.

Theorem 1

(Certification complexity)
.

Alg.
1
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC-MAP solver.
With probability at least
1-\delta
, the procedure executes in time
\mathcal{O}\big(\exp(h)\log\delta^{-1}\big)

The following corollary is immediate.

Corollary 1.1
.

With fixed PAC parameters
\varepsilon,\delta
, Alg. 1 is tractable if and only if
h=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.

In other words, a necessary and sufficient condition for tractable PAC-MAP is that the min-entropy grows at a rate at most logarithmic in
n
.

Of course, we cannot compute
h
directly without knowing the true
p^{*}
, so it is difficult to evaluate the complexity of a given PAC-MAP instance in advance. However, we can lower-bound this measure by sampling candidate queries. Finding even a single atom with mass not equal to
2^{-n}
falsifies the uniformity hypothesis.
If top atoms are substantially more probable than this lower bound, then
M
will decrease swiftly with the updates at line 14. If we are not so fortunate, then we can terminate the algorithm after some suitably large number of samples.
Even when intractable, this strategy remains optimal in the following sense.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all probability distributions on
\mathcal{Q}
. Let
(\bm{q}_{m})_{m\geq 1}\sim P\in\mathcal{P}
be an i.i.d. sample stream with induced filtration
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P}=\sigma(\bm{q}_{1},\dots,\bm{q}_{m})
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the class of purely random PAC-MAP solvers. Let
M_{A}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)
be a random variable denoting the stopping time of algorithm
A\in\mathcal{A}
, adapted to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P}
and input PAC parameters. (We assume that all algorithms also have access to a pmf oracle
p
, but suppress this dependence for notational convenience.) We write
M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)
to denote the stopping time of Alg.
1
.

Theorem 2

(Uniform optimality)
.

For any algorithm
A\in\mathcal{A}
, distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
, and PAC parameters
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq M_{A}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta).

In other words, no purely random PAC-MAP solver terminates faster than Alg.
1
on any possible data stream.

For high-dimensional, high-entropy distributions, even the uniformly optimal rate may prove too onerous. One option in such cases is to employ an adaptive sampling strategy (more on this in Sect.
4
); another is to sample up to some preset budget.
Early stopping means we cannot certify the target guarantee—but weaker ones can still be recovered.
Observe that, given any plug-in estimate
\hat{p}
, there is a deterministic relationship between the PAC parameters
\varepsilon,\delta
and the number of samples
M
required for a corresponding PAC certificate.
Inverting this function and jointly optimizing for error tolerance and confidence level, we may construct a curve describing the tradeoff between the two for a given dataset.
The solution
\hat{\bm{q}},\hat{p}
is PAC-certified at every point along this curve, providing a range of guarantees that practitioners can report or attempt to improve upon by increasing their sample budget
M
.

Let
\preceq
be a partial ordering on PAC parameter pairs.
We say that one pair
dominates
another if the former is strictly better in at least one respect (improved accuracy or confidence) and no worse in either respect.
Formally, we write:

\displaystyle(\varepsilon,\delta)\prec(\varepsilon^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})\Leftrightarrow(\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}\lor\delta<\delta^{\prime})\land(\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}\land\delta\leq\delta^{\prime}).

We say that a PAC pair is
admissible
if it is not dominated by any other pair. The set of admissible pairs forms a Pareto frontier of PAC parameters that are optimal w.r.t. the sampled data, insomuch as no valid PAC certificate can be issued with greater accuracy without reducing confidence or vice versa.

We compute Pareto-optimal PAC parameters for a fixed sample budget as follows. First, draw sample
S=\{\bm{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
from
P(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
and record the maximal observed probability
\hat{p}
, as well as the residual mass
\check{p}
. These represent anytime-valid bounds on
p^{*}
(provided they do not cross, in which case
\hat{\bm{q}}
is an exact solution). Thus we can always get a cheap PAC certificate with
\varepsilon=\min\{0,1-\hat{p}/\check{p}\},\delta=0
.

Now suppose that we want to tighten
\varepsilon
by introducing nonzero failure probability
\delta
.
Our equations imply a feasible interval of
[0,1-\hat{p})
for
\varepsilon
(see Appx. A for details).
Plugging in values in this range and rearranging our worst-case stopping criterion to solve for
\delta
, we get minimal corresponding failure probabilities via
\big(1-\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)^{M}
.
We use boldface notation for
\bm{\varepsilon}
in the pseudocode for Alg.
2
to indicate that it is a set (albeit a singleton if
\hat{p}\geq\check{p}
), while
\delta
is now a function of
\hat{p},\varepsilon

Each point along the curve is

3.2
Continuous data

Continuous PAC-MAP is more difficult than its discrete counterpart.
Since we cannot in principle enumerate all assignments over a continuous domain, extra assumptions are required to guarantee computability in this case.
To formalize this, we introduce some notation.
For continuous
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mu(B)
denote the mass of subset
B\subseteq\mathcal{Q}
w.r.t. Lebesgue measure over the query space, conditional on evidence:

\displaystyle\mu(B):=\int_{\bm{q}\in B}~p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})~d\bm{q}.

(In the continuous setting, the oracle is presumed omniscient not only w.r.t. densities evaluated at particular points but also w.r.t. integrals over arbitrary subsets.)
Recall that
G_{\varepsilon}
denotes the superlevel set of
\varepsilon
-approximate solutions.
Then we have the following identifiability conditions.

Lemma 2

(Identifiability)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define
\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)=\{\bm{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}:\lVert\bm{q}^{\prime}-\bm{q}\rVert\leq r\}
as a Euclidean ball of radius
r
with center
\bm{q}
.
Then
\bm{q}^{*}
is PAC-identifiable iff there exists some
\bm{q}\in G_{\varepsilon}
and
r>0
such that (a)
\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)\subseteq G_{\varepsilon}
; and (b)
\mu\big(\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)\big)>0
.

Condition (a) states that there exists an
r
-neighborhood of some high-density atom in which all neighboring points are also part of the superlevel set
G_{\varepsilon}
.
Without this condition, we have no way of lower-bounding the set’s mass, which is essential for computing
M
.
Condition (b) ensures that the resulting
r
-neighborhood has nonzero mass and can therefore be found via sampling. These requirements are quite mild in practice, and automatically satisfied under standard smoothness assumptions.

For example, suppose our density satisfies a Hölder condition around the mode. That is, we posit the existence of some
r,k,c>0
such that, for all
\bm{q}\in\mathcal{B}(\bm{q}^{*},r)
,

\displaystyle p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)\leq k\lVert\bm{q}-\bm{q}^{*}\rVert^{c}.

This ensures that continuous PAC-MAP solutions are identifiable, and suggests a principled strategy for discretizing the query space into hyperspheres of radius
r=(\varepsilon/k)^{1/c}
.
Alternatively, we could use data-driven techniques to partition
\mathcal{Q}
into hyperrectangles using decision trees or more general polytopes using ReLU networks.
However we instantiate the partition, the basic logic is the same: convert the pdf over continuous
\mathcal{Q}
into a pmf over a discrete representation, then follow the steps laid out by Alg.
1
for fixed PAC parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)
or Alg.
2
for fixed sample budget
M
. So long as resulting cells are small enough to reliably distinguish between low- and high-density regions, the previous results apply.

The oracle algorithms presented above are idealized in several respects, most notably in their assumption that we have access to constant-cost samplers and oracles. In practice, we must implement these functions with models learned from data, incurring potentially nontrivial errors and complexity costs. via the equality
p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})=p(\bm{q},\bm{e})/p(\bm{e})
, where the denominator is given by
\sum_{\bm{q}}p(\bm{q},\bm{e})
.
(For MMAP, we must additionally marginalize over the nuisance variables
\bm{V}
in both the numerator and denominator.)
Even if marginalization occurs just once outside the while-loop, this raises potential difficulties.
Computing exact marginals is generally #P-complete, while approximations are NP-hard
(
26
)
. However, since smooth, decomposable PCs compute marginals in linear time, they are a convenient choice for PAC-MAP solvers. Plugging a smooth, deterministic circuit
\mathcal{C}
in for our sampler and oracle in the algorithms above increases runtime by a constant factor
|\mathcal{C}|
.

Recall that PCs have their own approach to efficient maximization, with linear time MAP inference guaranteed for decomposable, deterministic circuits.
3
3

3

In fact, decomposability can be replaced with a weaker property known as
consistency

(
6
, §6.2)
. Since our target in this section is determinism, not decomposability, we will not explore this direction further.

For example, a decision tree (DT) that models joint distributions via recursive partitions and piecewise constant likelihoods can be compiled into a deterministic PC
(
8
)
. Since DTs are universally consistent under mild assumptions
(
20
)
, this would seem to resolve MAP altogether: learn a tree, compile a circuit, and maximize at will.

Unfortunately, matters are not so simple.
First, the universal consistency guarantees that motivate this move only apply in the limit of infinite tree depth. Linear dependence on circuit size is not so benign when the tree from which a PC is compiled may be indefinitely large.
Second, DTs are unstable predictors that can be highly sensitive to the inclusion/exclusion of individual datapoints. That is why they are rarely used on their own, but more often as part of ensembles such as random forests (RFs)
(
3
)
or gradient boosting machines (GBMs)
(
14
)
.

Several PC architectures are based on tree ensembles
(
13
;
8
;
18
;
27
;
32
)
, but they do not generally satisfy determinism.
Though rigorous results are lacking, empirical evidence suggests that determinism has a negative impact on expressive efficiency, which may explain why most leading PCs do not impose it by default.
These challenges highlight the need for approximate MAP solvers in non-deterministic PCs.
We test against several such methods below.

PAC-MAP Variants

The theoretical results of Sect.
3
were limited to purely random PAC-MAP solvers, which make no distributional assumptions.
However, when such assumptions are justified, alternative strategies may be preferable.
For example, Alg. 1 can be augmented by adding an exploitation step, as is standard in reinforcement learning.
We implement this in our experiments below with a method we call
smooth-PAC-MAP
, which periodically scans the Hamming neighborhood of the top candidate (pseudocoe in Appx. C). This procedure always returns some
\hat{p}
at least as high in expectation as that returned by vanilla
PAC-MAP
. However, it can find more probable solutions in some challenging cases, provided the superlevel set satisfies a modest smoothness condition.
Alternative exploitation schemes may be appropriate given further information about the data generating process.
These tricks do not violate our PAC guarantees, provided that the index
m
of Alg.
1
and the budget
M
of Alg.
2
only count random draws.
We compare the performance of purely random and adaptive strategies in our benchmarks.

Adaptive PAC-MAP algorithms can also complement approximate MAP solvers in PCs by using outputs from the latter as warm starts for randomized search. In the best case, this will improve on the heuristic solution; in the worst case, it will provide a PAC certificate for the existing result. We illustrate this collaborative approach in our experiments below.

6
Discussion

To proponents of tractable probabilistic models, it may seem vaguely heretical to use PCs for randomized inference. A central motivation for this formalism is to render probabilistic reasoning exact and coherent.
However, exact polytime MAP solutions are only possible in deterministic circuits, which show limited expressive efficiency in practice.
We argue that randomization is underexplored in PCs, and likely has other valuable applications.
Our methods are also a natural fit for MCMC samplers, where existing procedures for approximate MAP inference could be made more robust with rigorous PAC guarantees.
One possible extension of the present work is to design reinforcement learning algorithms for PAC-MAP, incorporating additional structure into the sampling procedure via methods like Monte Carlo tree search.

Figure 3
:

Estimated MAP probabilities are given at the top of the graph.

We note several limitations of our method. First, and most obviously, it does not provide exact solutions except in relatively simple cases.
If exactness is essential, then alternative solvers
are more appropriate.
Second, PAC-MAP is intractable when min-entropy grows faster than
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
.
This can lead to impractically large stopping times in some real world examples, making it hard to issue PAC certificates when optimizing over hundreds or thousands of variables.
This is an unfortunate but inevitable byproduct of the complexity of MAP itself, which would remain intractable even under a collapse of the polynomial hierarchy.
Finally, the Hamming neighborhood scan is a fairly crude exploitation strategy that scales poorly with the radius hyperparameter.
Still, we find that
smooth-PAC-MAP
fares reasonably well in our experiments with a fixed Hamming ball radius of
1
.

7
Conclusion

MAP inference is an important and challenging task in probabilistic reasoning.
Because the problem is inherently intractable, creative strategies are required to compute approximate solutions that strike a reasonable balance between error and complexity.
Our approach combines the theoretical rigor of PAC reasoning with the sampling and marginalization capabilities of PCs to do just that.
We have shown that a simple algorithm for PAC-MAP inference (Alg.
1
) is uniformly optimal over the class of purely random strategies, and derived information theoretic tractability conditions for our solver.
Though the task remains infeasible in the worst case, we mitigate against this negative result with an anytime-valid algorithm that issues Pareto-optimal PAC certificates under arbitrary computational budgets (Alg.
2
).
Experiments confirm that our method is not only competitive against state of the art solvers, but can team up with heuristic methods to improve upon their results and/or provide theoretical guarantees.
Future work will explore new directions for adaptive search and randomized inference with PCs.

Lemma 1
(Discovery complexity)
.
Fix any
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Define the
\varepsilon
-superlevel set:

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}:=\big\{\bm{q}:p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})\geq p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)\big\},

with corresponding mass
\mu_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{\bm{q}\in G_{\varepsilon}}p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})
.
Then Alg. 0 PAC-identifies
\bm{q}^{*}
in
\Theta\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\log\delta^{-1}\big)
samples.

Proof

To prove the lemma, we must show that
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\log\delta^{-1}
samples are asymptotically necessary and sufficient for PAC-identification with Alg. 0.

Take sufficiency first. By the i.i.d. assumption, the probability of missing any
\varepsilon
-approximate query in
m
random samples from
P(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
is exactly
(1-\mu_{\varepsilon})^{m}
. To satisfy the PAC guarantee, this value must be upper bounded by
\delta
. Exploiting the fundamental inequality
1-x\leq\exp(-x)
, we have

\displaystyle(1-\mu_{\varepsilon})^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-m\mu_{\varepsilon}).

We upper bound the rhs by
\delta
, take logs, and solve for
m
:

\displaystyle\exp(-m\mu_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq\delta

\displaystyle-m\mu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\log\delta

\displaystyle m\mu_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\geq\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}.

Thus
m=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\log\delta^{-1})
, establishing that this many samples are sufficient in the worst case.

Next, we derive a matching lower bound.
Suppose that we fail to connect sufficient samples for the PAC guarantee, in which case
(1-\mu_{\varepsilon})^{m}>\delta
. Taking logs and solving for
m
:

\displaystyle m\log(1-\mu_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle>\log\delta

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle<\frac{\log\delta}{\log(1-\mu_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\delta}{-\mu_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}.

The penultimate line follows from the standard inequality
\log(1-x)\leq-x
, for
x\in(0,1)
.
This establishes that
\Omega\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\log\delta^{-1}\big)
samples are necessary for Alg. 0, which completes the proof.

Theorem 1
(Certification complexity)
.

Alg.
1
is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC-MAP solver.
With probability at least
1-\delta
, the procedure executes in time
\mathcal{O}\big(\exp(h)\log\delta^{-1}\big)
.

Proof

The theorem makes two claims: that Alg. 1 is sound, and that its runtime is bounded from above with high probability. We consider both claims in turn.

Soundness

To show that Alg. 1 is sound, we must demonstrate that any
\hat{\bm{q}}
returned by the procedure is a valid PAC-MAP solution, i.e. satisfies the guarantee:

\displaystyle\Pr\big(p(\hat{\bm{q}}\mid\bm{e})\geq p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)\big)\geq 1-\delta.

(1)

This requires an analysis of two separate stopping criteria.
The first is triggered when
\hat{p}\geq\check{p}(1-\varepsilon)
. Suppose this condition is met.
Note that the largest possible mass of any unsampled atom is
\check{p}
, so either (a) we have already seen the MAP configuration, in which case
\hat{p}=p^{*}
; or (b) we have not, in which case
p^{*}\leq\check{p}
. Under scenario (a), we can obviously stop since our solution is exact. Under scenario (b), we have

\displaystyle\hat{p}

\displaystyle\geq\check{p}(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq p^{*}(1-\varepsilon),

which means we must have sampled at least one element of the superlevel set
G_{\varepsilon}
.
In either case, we can be certain that
\hat{\bm{q}}
is (at least) an
\varepsilon
-approximate solution.
That is why results under this condition are not just
probably
approximately correct—they hold with
\delta=0
. Moreover, when
\hat{p}\geq\check{p}
, the solution is exact, i.e.
\hat{p}=p^{*}
and
\varepsilon=\delta=0
.

By contrast, a solution is at best PAC when
m\geq\hat{p}^{-1}(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}
. The argument here proceeds along similar lines to that for Lemma 1.
Specifically, we fail to return an
\varepsilon
-approximate solution if
\hat{p}<p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)
. Thus the true MAP has to exceed
\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)
for Alg. 1 to err. What is the probability of such an error? Given that our sampler is i.i.d., the probability of missing some atom with mass greater than
\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)
in
m
draws is exactly
\big(1-\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)^{m}\leq\exp\big(-m\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)
.
We upper bound the rhs by
\delta
and solve for
m
:

\displaystyle\exp\big(-m\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\delta

\displaystyle-m\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\log\delta

\displaystyle m\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\geq\log(1/\delta)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}^{-1}~(1-\varepsilon)~\log\delta^{-1}.

This establishes the soundness of our second stopping criterion. Since Alg. 1 is guaranteed to deliver a valid solution, we conclude that it is an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC-MAP solver.

Complexity

We now focus on the second claim of Thm. 1, which posits upper bounds on certification complexity in terms of distributional difficulty
h
and failure probability
\delta
.
This analysis deliberately ignores the deterministic stopping condition of line 11, since only the probabilistic condition of line 14 holds uniformly for all distributions. The former, by contrast, applies exclusively in advantageous settings where mass is heavily concentrated.

Let
S_{m}=\{\bm{q}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}\sim P(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e})
denote a random sample of
m
i.i.d. draws from the query distribution, with maximum observed probability
\hat{p}_{m}=\max_{\bm{q}\in S_{m}}~p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})
.
Observe that
\hat{p}_{m}
is a random variable that converges to
p^{*}
as
m\rightarrow\infty
.
In this section, we will show that for
\hat{p}_{m}
to exceed
p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)
with high probability, it suffices to draw at most
M=\big\lceil 2^{h}\log\delta^{-1}\big\rceil
samples.

If
S_{m}
contains an element of the superlevel set
G_{\varepsilon}
, then the sample maximum satisfies
\hat{p}_{m}\geq p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)
. Thus if the stopping condition is triggered, then soundness implies:

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}}{\hat{p}_{m}}~

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{w.h.p.}}}}{{\leq}}~\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}}{p^{*}(1-\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{p^{*}}

\displaystyle=2^{h}\log\delta^{-1},

where the final line exploits the definition of the min-entropy,
h:=H_{\infty}(\bm{Q}\mid\bm{e}):=-\log_{2}p^{*}
.
This bound fails iff we do
not
sample an element of
G_{\varepsilon}
, a failure mode that occurs with probability at most
\delta
. This establishes the following stochastic upper bound, which holds uniformly for all distributions
\mathcal{P}
on
\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle\forall P\in\mathcal{P}:~\Pr_{P}\big(M\leq 2^{h}\log\delta^{-1}\big)\geq 1-\delta.

This completes the derivation.

Lower Bounds

As a concluding remark, we consider the corresponding lower complexity bound.
Define the residual mass of sample
S_{m}
as
\check{p}_{m}:=1-\sum_{\bm{q}\in S_{m}}~p(\bm{q}\mid\bm{e})
.
Assuming the deterministic stopping condition of line 11 is unmet, then
\hat{p}_{m}\leq\check{p}_{m}(1-\varepsilon)
. Therefore we can lower-bound the stopping time via:

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}}{\hat{p}_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}}{\check{p}_{m}(1-\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\check{p}_{m}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\text{w.h.p.}}}}{{\geq}}~\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\mu_{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle=\Omega\big(\mu_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\log\delta^{-1}\big).

To see why this final inequality holds, it helps to rewrite our success criterion in terms of residual mass.
Observe that failure occurs iff the superlevel set is a subset of unsampled states, i.e.
G_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}\backslash S_{m}

By the monotonicity of measures, this implies that
\mu_{\varepsilon}\leq\check{p}_{m}
.
Thus we find an
\varepsilon

\mu_{\varepsilon}>\check{p}_{m}

In the worst case,
G_{\varepsilon}
is a singleton set and
\mu_{\varepsilon}=p^{*}
.
Call this adversarial class of distributions
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{P}
.
When
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
, PAC certification requires at least

\displaystyle\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{p^{*}}

\displaystyle=2^{h}\log\delta^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Omega\big(\exp(h)\log\delta^{-1}\big)

samples.
This lower bound looks very similar to the upper bound derived above, giving tight bounds in the worst case. Formally, we have:

\displaystyle\forall P\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}:~

\displaystyle\Pr_{P}\big(M\geq 2^{h}\log\delta^{-1}\big)\geq 1-\delta,

and therefore when sampling from a distribution in this class, we have
M=\Theta\big(\exp(h)\log\delta^{-1}\big)
with high probability.

With fixed PAC parameters

\displaystyle D_{\infty}(P~||~U)

Theorem 2
(Uniform optimality)
.

For any algorithm
A\in\mathcal{A}
, distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}
, and PAC parameters
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq M_{A}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta).

Proof

The theorem makes a strong pointwise optimality claim: that no purely random PAC-MAP solver terminates faster than Alg.
1
on any conceivable data stream.

The proof proceeds by
reductio
. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists some
A_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
M_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)<M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)
.
We will show that
A_{0}
cannot be an element of
\mathcal{A}
.

Recall that this function class is defined as the set of all purely random
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-PAC-MAP solvers. Thus every element of
\mathcal{A}
is
sound
, insomuch as it satisfies the PAC guarantee of Eq.
1
.
Meanwhile, the
purely random

\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P}
.
The algorithms in this class therefore vary only with respect to their stopping criteria.
We will show that any stopping condition faster than those of
M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)
violates soundness, resulting in contradiction.

We consider deterministic and stochastic stopping conditions in turn. For fixed
P,\varepsilon,\delta
, we abbreviate
m_{1}:=M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)
and
m_{0}:=M_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)

by assumption.

Case 1: Deterministic Stopping Condition

Suppose that Alg.
1
terminates at time

because the deterministic condition
\hat{p}_{m_{1}}\geq\check{p}_{m_{1}}(1-\varepsilon)
is satisfied.
Algorithm
A_{0}
stops at time
m_{0}<m_{1}
on the same sample stream.
By definition of
m_{1}
, the deterministic stopping condition is not satisfied at any earlier time, so we have
\hat{p}_{m_{0}}<\check{p}_{m_{0}}(1-\varepsilon)
.

At time
m_{0}
,
A_{0}
has observed exactly the same information as Alg.
1

\mathcal{F}_{m_{0}}^{P}
.
After
m_{0}

p^{*}=\check{p}_{m_{0}}
.
Under such a completion, the reported maximizer
\hat{p}_{m_{0}}
is not in the

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{m_{0}}

\displaystyle<\check{p}_{m_{0}}(1-\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=p^{*}(1-\varepsilon),

so any certificate issued at time

cannot issue a valid PAC certificate with

Suppose instead that Alg.

\displaystyle m_{1}\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}}{\hat{p}_{m_{1}}},

By definition of
m_{1}

\displaystyle m_{0}<\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\log\delta^{-1}}{\hat{p}_{m_{0}}}.

Solving for

\displaystyle\exp\big(-m\hat{p}_{m_{0}}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)>\delta.

Conditioned on the observed filtration
\mathcal{F}_{m_{0}}^{P}

has total mass exactly

\displaystyle\mu_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\hat{p}_{m_{0}}}{1-\varepsilon},

and none of its elements have yet been sampled. Under this distribution, the probability of failing to observe any element of

\exp\big(-m\hat{p}_{m_{0}}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)
. Therefore, if
A_{0}
stops at time
m_{0}
, then with probability strictly larger than
\delta
its output fails to return a valid
\varepsilon
-approximation.
This violates the PAC guarantee, contradicting the assumption that
A_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
.

Thus in either case, any algorithm that terminates strictly before
m_{1}
must violate PAC soundness. Hence the existence of some
A_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
M_{0}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)<M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)
leads to contradiction.

We therefore conclude that, for all
A\in\mathcal{A},P\in\mathcal{P}
and
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle M_{1}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta)\leq M_{A}(\mathcal{F}_{m}^{P},\varepsilon,\delta),

which completes the proof.
\Box

Theorem 3
(Pareto optimality)
.
Alg. 2 is sound and complete w.r.t. PAC parameters, returning all and only the admissible
(\varepsilon,\delta)
pairs for a given sample
S
and corresponding MAP estimate
\hat{\bm{q}},\hat{p}
.
The procedure executes in time
\mathcal{O}(M)
.

Proof

The theorem makes three claims: that Alg. 2 is (1) sound; (2) complete; and (3) executes in linear time. Item (3) is immediately evident from the pseudocode, as we simply draw
M
samples from
P
and perform a handful of trivial calculations whose complexity is independent of algorithm inputs. Therefore, the following will focus on claims (1) and (2).

Alg. 2 is sound if the empirical maximum
\hat{p}
satisfies the target PAC guarantee at any
\varepsilon\in\bm{\varepsilon}
and corresponding confidence level
\delta(\hat{p},\varepsilon)
returned by the procedure. This is obvious in the exact case, when
\check{p}\leq\hat{p}
, for the same reasons given in the proof above for Thm. 1. If residual mass exceeds the maximal observed probability, however, a range of slack parameters are feasible. Specifically,
\varepsilon
can take any value in the interval
[0,1-\hat{p})
. The lower bound is fixed by the nonnegativity constraint, while the upper bound follows from rearranging the inequality
\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\leq 1
. Once again, we exploit the i.i.d. sampling procedure to bound our failure probability via
\delta\geq\big(1-\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)^{M}
.
Pairing any feasible
\varepsilon
with any
\delta
that satisfies this inequality suffices for a sound solution. Since we report the minimum
\delta
consistent with the target guarantee, we conclude that Alg. 2 is sound.

To establish completeness, we must show that the PAC parameters returned by Alg. 2 form a Pareto frontier of admissible solutions, i.e. that no possible
(\varepsilon,\delta)
pair dominates those returned by the procedure. Since our
\bm{\varepsilon}
set covers the entire feasible region of values for the tolerance parameter, this amounts to demonstrating the optimality of the confidence function:

\displaystyle\delta(\hat{p},\varepsilon)=\big(1-\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\big)^{M}.

This follows immediately from the i.i.d. sampling procedure itself, which ensures that the probability of missing the
\varepsilon
-superlevel set is exactly
\big(1-\mu_{\varepsilon}\big)^{M}
.
Since
\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)\leq\mu_{\varepsilon}
, any
\delta^{\prime}<\delta(\hat{p},\varepsilon)
would fail to satisfy the PAC guarantee in the worst case, e.g. if just a single atom’s mass exceeds
\hat{p}/(1-\varepsilon)
by an infinitesimal amount.
Thus
\delta(\hat{p},\varepsilon)
represents the infimum of all valid confidence levels, completing the proof.
\Box

Lemma 2
(Identifiability)
.
Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define
\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)=\{\bm{q}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Q}:\lVert\bm{q}^{\prime}-\bm{q}\rVert\leq r\}
as a Euclidean ball of radius
r
with center
\bm{q}
.
Then
\bm{q}^{*}
is PAC-identifiable iff there exists some
\bm{q}\in G_{\varepsilon}
and
r>0
such that (a)
\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)\subseteq G_{\varepsilon}
; and (b)
\mu\big(\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)\big)>0
.

Proof

To prove the lemma, we must show that conditions (a) and (b) are necessary and sufficient for PAC-identifiability in the continuous case. Recall that
\bm{q}^{*}
is PAC-identifiable iff there exists a finite
M
such that we draw at least one sample from the superlevel set
G_{\varepsilon}
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Take sufficiency first. If there exists some
\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)\subseteq G_{\varepsilon}
with strictly positive measure
\mu\big(\mathcal{B}(\bm{q},r)\big)=\gamma>0
, then by the monotonicity of measures,
\mu(G_{\varepsilon})\geq\gamma
. We can now calculate a concrete stopping time:

\displaystyle M\geq\frac{\log\delta^{-1}}{\log(1-\gamma)^{-1}}.

Since
\gamma>0
, the denominator is finite and positive. Thus there exists a finite
M
that satisfies the PAC criteria.

Now consider the necessity of (a) and (b). The probability of hitting
G_{\varepsilon}
in
M
trials is
1-\big(1-\mu(G_{\varepsilon})\big)^{M}
. For this value to be greater than
0
(a necessary condition for identifiability), we must have
\mu(G_{\varepsilon})>0
. This integral is strictly positive iff
G_{\varepsilon}
contains at least one subset of nonzero Lebesgue measure. Thus any PAC-identifiable atom
\bm{q}^{*}
in continuous space must satisfy criteria (a) and (b).
\Box

Pseudocode

In this section, we provide pseudocode for
smooth-PAC-MAP
, which adds an exploitation step to the pure exploration strategy of
PAC-MAP
. This can lead to stronger results and faster convergence under mild smoothness assumptions. In the pseudocode, the exploration/exploitation tradeoff is probabilistically controlled by a hyperparameter
\eta
; in our implementation, we deterministically run an exploit loop every
100
samples, effectively imposing
\eta=0.01
.
