A geometric obstruction to self-simulation for groups
Sebastián Barbieri, Kanéda Blot, Mathieu Sablik and Ville Salo

Abstract
. We introduce a new quasi-isometry invariant for finitely generated groups and show that every group with this property admits a subshift which is effectively closed by patterns and that cannot be realized as the topological factor of any subshift of finite type. We provide several examples of groups with the property, such as amenable groups, multi-ended groups, generalized Baumslag-Solitar groups, fundamental groups of surfaces, and cocompact Fuchsian groups. Keywords : Symbolic dynamics, self-simulation, quasi-isometry, effective dynamics. MSC2020 : Primary: 37B10. Secondary: 37B05, 20F65, 20F10.



1.
Introduction

Given a countable group
\mathbb{G}
and a finite set
\Sigma
, a subshift is a closed subset of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
under the prodiscrete topology that is invariant under the shift
\mathbb{G}
-action. Subshifts of finite type (SFTs) are an important class of subshifts which are defined through a finite number of local rules. Following the celebrated result of Hochman
[
20
]
that states that any computable
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-action can be obtained as a factor of a subaction of a
\mathbb{Z}^{d+2}
-SFT, a number of results in the literature
[
4
,
15
,
22
,
6
,
8
]
have explored how far are SFTs and their symbolic factors, called sofic subshifts, of encoding all computable dynamics of a given group.

In the setting of expansive actions, it is natural to ask which dynamics can be realized by sofic subshifts. One natural computability obstruction (for finitely generated and recursively presented groups) is that sofic subshifts are effective; that is, there exists a description of the subshift through a set of forbidden patterns that can be enumerated algorithmically. A series of studies have explored which dynamical properties of an effective
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
-subshift actually force it to become sofic, for instance, combinatorial constraints from
\Pi_{1}^{0}
-sets
[
31
]
, or very low pattern complexity
[
13
]
.

Conversely, it is natural to explore which effective subshifts are not sofic. To the best of our knowledge, the most well-known example of an effective
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-subshift that is not sofic is the mirror subshift described in
[
21
]
. This is the space of all
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
configurations over
\{0,1,\star\}
with the property that if one
\star
occurs, it extends vertically and imposes a mirror symmetry on the remainder of the configuration, see
Figure

1.1
. The proof of non-soficity is based on a combinatorial argument that heavily relies in the amenability of the underlying group.

Variations of the argument used to prove non-soficity of the mirror shift have been used to demonstrate the non-soficity of a subshift corresponding to the superposition of Sturmian words of the same slope
[
16
]
or minimal effective subshifts which have high entropy dimension
[
18
]
. In the case of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, other sufficient conditions for non-soficity can be expressed in terms of extender sets
[
24
]
and Kolmogorov complexity
[
14
]
.

In the context of finitely generated groups with decidable word problem, the mirror subshift argument has been used to construct effective subshifts that are not sofic when the group is amenable or multi-ended
[
2
]
. Hence a natural question is whether such a construction exists in all groups or if there are groups where all effective subshifts are sofic. In
[
8
]
the authors introduce the class of
self-simulable
groups, in which every effective subshift is sofic and show that it is non-trivial. We remark that while the definition of self-simulable is actually more general, see
Definition

2.5
, for finitely generated and recursively presented groups it is equivalent to the statement that all effective subshifts are sofic.

In
[
8
]
it was shown that the direct product of any two finitely generated nonamenable groups is self-simulable. This, along with some stability properties of the class of self-simulable groups, was used to produce a list of interesting examples such as
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
for
n\geq 5
and Thompson’s
V
. It was also shown that Thompson’s group
F
is self-simulable if and only if it is nonamenable.

Two properties of a finitely generated group with decidable word problem that provide obstructions to being self-simulable are amenability and the property of having multiple ends. However, it was also shown in
[
8
]
that there exist groups which are nonamenable and
1
-ended that are not self-simulable, such as
F_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

The main purpose of this article is to present a new quasi-isometry invariant for finitely generated groups which provides an obstruction to self-simulability. Furthermore, all examples we are aware of that are not self-simulable satisfy this property. We call groups which satisfy this property
extraterrestrial
because their Cayley graphs admit structures which roughly resemble flying saucers.

Let us be more precise. Consider an infinite graph
G
of bounded degree and non-negative integers
m,k,r
. An
(m,k,r)
-UFO is a triple of sets of vertices
(U,F,O)
of
G
which satisfies
|U|\geq m|F|
, that
U
and
O
can be matched by paths of length at most
k
, and that all paths from
U
to
O
which avoid vertices in
F
have at least length
r
. A graph is called extraterrestrial if for all
m
there is some
k
such that for all
r
then
G
admits an
(m,k,r)
-UFO. In
Figure

1.2
we represent a UFO in a Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Intuitively, a graph
G
is extraterrestrial if there are pairs of subsets of vertices
U,O
of the same size, which are close (parametrized by
k
) in
G
, but that can be made to be arbitrarily far (parametrized by
r
) by removing a set of vertices
F
which is small (parametrized by
m
) in comparison with
U
and
O
.

Our first observation is that the property of being extraterrestrial is a geometric invariant:

Theorem A
.

Being extraterrestrial is a quasi-isometry invariant for graphs of bounded degree.

In particular, given a finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
, it follows that if for some finite generating set
S
of
\mathbb{G}
the Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
is extraterrestrial, then the property is preserved by changing the set of generators. In particular, being extraterrestrial is a property of the group.

Our main result is that being extraterrestrial is an obstruction to self-simulability.

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated extraterrestrial group, there exists a
\mathbb{G}
-subshift which is effectively closed by patterns and which is not the topological factor of any
\mathbb{G}
-SFT.

Intuitively, a subshift is effectively closed by patterns if it can be described by a list of forbidden cylinders that are produced by a Turing machine. In the case where
\mathbb{G}
is recursively presented, every subshift which is effectively closed by patterns is topologically conjugate to an expansive action by computable maps on a
\Pi_{1}^{0}
subset of the Cantor space, thus in particular these groups cannot be self-simulable. In the case of groups which are non-recursively presented, this provides an obstruction to the natural relativization of self-simulability, which we call strong self-simulability, see
Section

2.2
.

The proof of
B
relies on a construction that in a very abstract way generalizes the mirror shift. More precisely, we replace the algebraic global symmetry with respect to mirror symbols by global symmetry through matchings in the group induced by a lexicographic ordering. The property of being extraterrestrial provides the precise geometrical structure in the group that makes this construction work.

The last part of the article is dedicated to producing examples of extraterrestrial groups and tools to prove that a group is extraterrestrial. The following theorem summarizes the most relevant examples.

Theorem C
.

The following classes of finitely generated groups are extraterrestrial:

•

Infinite amenable groups (
Proposition

5.1
).

•

Multi-ended groups (
Proposition

5.2
).

•

Amalgamated free products relative to an amenable subgroup with strict embeddings (
Proposition

5.7
).

•

HNN-extensions relative to an amenable subgroup (
Proposition

5.9
).

•

Generalized Baumslag-Solitar groups (
5.13
).

•

Cocompact Fuchsian groups (
5.17
).

•

Fundamental groups of surfaces (
5.18
).

In
[
8
, Question 9.3]
we asked whether every
1
-ended hyperbolic group is self-simulable. The previous result answers this question negatively: the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus
2
is a
1
-ended hyperbolic group which is extraterrestrial and thus cannot be self-simulable.

The main tool used to provide the examples in
C
is that every group that admits an amenable subgroup whose associated Schreier graph is multi-ended is extraterrestrial (
Theorem

5.3
). We do not know any example of extraterrestrial group which does not fall into this case. We also do not know any example of finitely generated group which is not (strongly) self-simulable and fails to be extraterrestrial.

Question 1.1
.

Is there any group which is neither strongly self-simulable nor extraterrestrial?

Question 1.2
.

Is there any group which is extraterrestrial but does not admit an amenable subgroup whose associated Schreier graph is multi-ended?

2.
Preliminaries

We shall use the notation
A\Subset B
to denote that
A
is a finite subset of
B
. We also use
A^{*}
to denote the set of words on alphabet
A
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a group with identity
1_{\mathbb{G}}
and
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
a symmetric set, that is, such that if
s\in S
then
s^{-1}\in S
. The
word problem
of a group
\mathbb{G}
with respect to
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
is the language

\texttt{WP}_{S}(\mathbb{G})=\{w\in S^{*}:\underline{w}=1_{\mathbb{G}}\}

where
\underline{w}
denotes the element of
\mathbb{G}
represented by the word
w
.

Now let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group and
S\Subset G
a finite generating symmetric set. We say that
\mathbb{G}
is
recursively presented
(with respect to
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
) if
\texttt{WP}_{S}(\mathbb{G})
is recursively enumerable. A group is said to have
decidable word problem
(with respect to
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
) if
\texttt{WP}_{S}(\mathbb{G})
is decidable. These notions do not depend upon the choice of generating set
S
.

A
graph
is a pair
G=(V,E)
where
V
is a set of
vertices
and
E
is a set of undirected
edges
, that is, of unordered pairs of elements of
V
. The degree
\mathrm{deg}(v)
of a vertex
v\in V
is the number of edges containing
v
. A graph has
bounded degree
if
\max_{v\in V}\mathrm{deg}(v)
is finite. A
path
of length
n\geq 0
between
u
and
v
in
G
is a finite sequence
w_{0},\dots,w_{n}
of distinct elements of
V
such that
u=w_{0}
,
v=w_{n}
and
\{w_{i},w_{i+1}\}
is an edge for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
. We say
G
is
connected
if there is path between every pair of
u,v\in V
. For a connected graph
G
we denote by
d_{G}
the metric on
V
where
d_{G}(u,v)
is the length of the shortest path from
u
to
v
. If the graph is not connected and no such path exists between
u,v
we set
d_{G}(u,v)=\infty
.

For a group
\mathbb{G}
and a symmetric
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
, the
right Cayley graph

\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
is the graph where the vertices are
\mathbb{G}
and there is an edge between
g
and
g^{\prime}
if there exists
s\in S
such that
g^{\prime}=gs
. If
\mathbb{G}
is generated by
S
then
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
is a connected graph of bounded degree and we denote the metric
d_{\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)}
on
\mathbb{G}
by
d_{S}
instead. For an element
g\in G
we denote
|g|_{S}=d_{S}(g,1_{\mathbb{G}})
and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
we write
B_{k}=\{g\in\mathbb{G}:|g|_{S}\leq k\}
for the ball of radius
k
on
\mathbb{G}
under the metric
d_{S}
.

Similarly, for a subgroup
\mathbb{H}\leqslant\mathbb{G}
and a symmetric
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
, the
right Schreier graph

\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
is the graph where the vertices are
\{\mathbb{H}g,g\in\mathbb{G}\}
and there is an edge between
\mathbb{H}g
and
\mathbb{H}g^{\prime}
if there exists
s\in S
such that
\mathbb{H}g^{\prime}=\mathbb{H}gs
. It can equivalently be defined as the quotient graph of
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
by the left action of
\mathbb{H}
by multiplication.

2.1.
Shift spaces

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet and
\mathbb{G}
be a group. The full
\mathbb{G}
-shift is the set
\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}=\{x\colon\mathbb{G}\to\Sigma\}
equipped with the left
shift
action
\mathbb{G}\curvearrowright\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
by left multiplication given by

gx(h)=x(g^{-1}h)\qquad\mbox{ for every }g,h\in\mathbb{G}\mbox{ and }x\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}.

The elements
a\in\Sigma
and
x\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
are called
symbols
and
configurations
respectively. We endow
\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
with the prodiscrete topology generated by the clopen subbase given by the
cylinders

[a]_{g}=\{x\in\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}:x(g)=a\}
where
g\in\mathbb{G}
. Given
F\Subset\mathbb{G}
, a
pattern
with support
F
is an element
p\in\Sigma^{F}
. We denote the cylinder generated by
p
by
[p]=\bigcap_{h\in F}[p(h)]_{h}
.

In the case of a finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
, it will be useful to encode patterns in a computable manner. Let
S
be a symmetric set of generators and
\Sigma
a finite alphabet. A
pattern coding
is a map
c\in\Sigma^{W}
for some
W\Subset S^{*}
. The cylinder induced by
c
is given by

[c]=\bigcap_{w\in W}[c(w)]_{\underline{w}}.

where
\underline{w}
is the element of
\mathbb{G}
represented by the word
w
. Notice that the cylinder associated to a pattern coding may be empty.

Definition 2.1
.

A subset
X\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
is a
\mathbb{G}
-subshift
if and only if it is
\mathbb{G}
-invariant and closed in the prodiscrete topology.

If the context is clear, we drop the
\mathbb{G}
from the notation and speak plainly of a subshift. It follows directly from the definition that
X
is a subshift if and only if there exists a set of patterns
\mathcal{F}
such that
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
, where

X_{\mathcal{F}}={\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}\setminus\bigcup_{p\in\mathcal{F},g\in\mathbb{G}}g[p]}.

If
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
, we say that
\mathcal{F}
is a set of
forbidden patterns
for
X
.

Let
X\subset(\Sigma_{X})^{\mathbb{G}}
and
Y\subset(\Sigma_{Y})^{\mathbb{G}}
be
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts. A
morphism
is a continuous function
\phi\colon X\to Y
which is
\mathbb{G}
-equivariant, that is,
\phi(gx)=g\phi(x)
for every
x\in X
and
g\in\mathbb{G}
. A morphism is a
factor map
if the map is onto, and a
conjugacy
if it is bijective. Given a factor map
\phi\colon X\to Y
we say that
Y
is a factor of
X
, and that
X
is an extension of
Y
.

Equivalently, by the Curtis-Lyndon-Hedlund theorem, see
[
11
, Theorem 1.8.1]
, a map
\phi\colon X\to Y
is a morphism if and only if there exists
F\Subset\mathbb{G}
and
\Phi\colon(\Sigma_{X})^{F}\to\Sigma_{Y}
such that
(\phi(x))(g)=\Phi(g^{-1}x|_{F})
for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
. We say that a morphism
\phi\colon X\to Y
is a
1
-block map
if there exists
\Phi\colon\Sigma_{X}\to\Sigma_{Y}
such that
(\phi(x))(g)=\Phi(x(g))
for every
g\in\mathbb{G}
and
x\in X
.

It well-known that a
\mathbb{G}
-action by homeomorphisms on a compact metrizable space
(X,d)
of topological dimension zero is conjugate to some
\mathbb{G}
-subshift if and only if it is
expansive
, that is, there is a constant
C>0
such that for every
x,y\in X
then
x=y
if and only if
d(gx,gy)<C
for every
g\in\mathbb{G}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a group and
S\Subset\mathbb{G}
a symmetric set of generators of
\mathbb{G}
. We say that a subshift
X\subseteq\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
is:

(1)

S
-nearest neighbor
, if
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
for a finite set of forbidden patterns
\mathcal{F}
. And the support of every pattern in
\mathcal{F}
is of the form
\{1_{\mathbb{G}},s\}
for some
s\in S
.

(2)

a
subshift of finite type (SFT)
, if there exists a finite set of forbidden patterns
\mathcal{F}
such that
X=X_{\mathcal{F}}
.

(3)

a
sofic subshift
, if it is a factor of an SFT.

We remark that all
S
-nearest neighbor subshifts are SFTs, and all SFTs are sofic subshifts. It is well known that every subshift of finite type is conjugate to an
S
-nearest neighbor subshift. Furthermore, if
X
is a sofic subshift, it is always possible to construct an SFT
Y
which is
S
-nearest neighbor and a
1
-block factor map
\phi
such that
\phi(Y)=X
. For a proof of these facts, see
[
5
]
.

2.2.
Computability of group actions

Next we will introduce notions of computability for group actions. We will assume the reader is acquainted with the basic concepts of computability, such as (oracle) Turing machines. An introduction can be found in
[
12
]
.

A subset
X\subset\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
effectively closed
or
\Pi_{1}^{0}
if there exists a recursively enumerable language
L\subset\{0,1\}^{*}
such that
X=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\setminus\bigcup_{w\in L}[w]
, where
[w]
denotes the cylinder set of all
x\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
which begin by the word
w
. A map
f\colon X\to\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
computable
if there is an oracle Turing machine which on oracle
x\in X
and input
n\in\mathbb{N}
outputs
f(x)(n)
. An action of a finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
on a zero-dimensional topological space is called
effective
if it is topologically conjugate to a
\mathbb{G}
-action on an effectively closed set by computable maps.

Definition 2.3
.

A
\mathbb{G}
-subshift is
effective
if it is conjugate to an (expansive) effective
\mathbb{G}
-action.

It is often useful to have an explicit characterization of effective subshifts without passing through conjugacies. This can be done using pattern codings up to a small subtlety which we shall explain in a moment.

Definition 2.4
.

A
\mathbb{G}
-subshift is effectively closed by patterns, if there exists a recursively enumerable set
\mathcal{C}
of pattern codings such that
X=\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}\setminus\bigcup_{c\in\mathcal{C},g\in\mathbb{G}}g[c].

In the case where
\mathbb{G}
is a recursively presented group, a
\mathbb{G}
-subshift is effectively closed by patterns if and only if it is an effective subshift
[
7
, Corollary 7.7]
and in particular all sofic
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts are effective. Furthermore, if
\mathbb{G}
has decidable word problem there is an enumeration of the group that makes the group operations computable and thus instead of considering pattern codings one can directly talk about recursively enumerable sets of forbidden patterns for the group.

Definition 2.5
.

A finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
is
self-simulable
if every effective
\mathbb{G}
-action is the topological factor of a
\mathbb{G}
-SFT.

It was shown in
[
8
, Proposition 3.7]
that if
\mathbb{G}
is a recursively presented self-simulable group, then
\mathbb{G}
is nonamenable. Furthermore, if
\mathbb{G}
is recursively presented and nonamenable, then
\mathbb{G}
is self-simulable if and only if every effective
\mathbb{G}
-subshift is a factor of a
\mathbb{G}
-SFT
[
8
, Theorem 4.6]
. Therefore for recursively presented groups being self-simulable coincides with the statement that the classes of sofic
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts and effective
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts coincide.

If
\mathbb{G}
is not recursively presented, then for
|\Sigma|\geq 2
even the full
\mathbb{G}
-shift
\Sigma^{\mathbb{G}}
is non-effective, see
[
7
, Proposition 7.9]
. It follows that the notion of self-simulation as stated above is not very meaningful beyond recursively presented groups. However, there is an easy way to “fix” the definition by relativizing the notion of an effective action.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group and
S
a finite set of generators. Let
F(S)
be the free group on basis
S
and take
\psi\colon F(S)\to\mathbb{G}
be the canonical epimorphism where for a reduced word
w\in F(S)
we let
\psi(w)
be the element obtained by multiplying the symbols of
w
as elements in
\mathbb{G}
. We say that an action
\mathbb{G}\curvearrowright X
is
relatively effective
if there exists an effective action
F(S)\curvearrowright Y
such that if we let

X^{\prime}=\{y\in Y:gy=y\mbox{ for every }g\in\ker(\psi)\},

then the actions
\mathbb{G}\curvearrowright X
and
F(S)/\ker(\psi)\curvearrowright X^{\prime}
are topologically conjugate.

In the definition of relative effective action we have “factored out” the computational complexity of the word problem of
\mathbb{G}
. In fact, it is easy to see that for a recursively presented group
\mathbb{G}
both notions coincide. It is natural then to consider self-simulation for non-recursively presented groups using this notion instead. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.6
.

A finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
is
strongly self-simulable
if every relative effective
\mathbb{G}
-action is the factor of a
\mathbb{G}
-SFT.

We remark that the main theorem of
[
8
]
which states that the direct product of any two finitely generated nonamenable groups is self-simulable, can be directly extended to show that in fact it is strongly self-simulable. In retrospective, we believe that strong self-simulability is the right notion for non-recursively presented groups, and thus in fact the right notion for all finitely generated groups.

The next proposition shows that
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts which are effectively closed by patterns are precisely the expansive relative effective
\mathbb{G}
-actions on spaces with zero topological dimension. Let
\mathbb{G}
be finitely generated by
S
and take
\psi\colon F(S)\to G
the canonical epimorphism. Note that every
x\in A^{F(S)}
such that
gx=x
for every
g\in\operatorname{ker}(\psi)
induces a configuration
\psi^{*}(x)\in A^{\mathbb{G}}
given by

\psi^{*}(x)(\phi(g))=x(g)\mbox{ for every }g\in\mathbb{G}.

Proposition 2.7
.

A
\mathbb{G}
-subshift
X
is effectively closed by patterns if and only if there exists an effective
F(S)
-subshift
Y
such that

X=\{\psi^{*}(y):y\in A^{F(S)}\mbox{ and }gy=y\mbox{ for every }g\in\operatorname{ker}(\psi)\}.

A proof of
Proposition

2.7
can be found in
[
7
, Section 7]
. We note that if
\mathbb{G}
is a finitely generated non-amenable group, then every relatively effective
\mathbb{G}
-action is the topological factor of an effectively closed by patterns
\mathbb{G}
-subshift (the proof is exactly the same as in
[
8
, Theorem 4.6]
). Therefore for a non-amenable group
\mathbb{G}
, it follows that
\mathbb{G}
is strongly self-simulable if and only if the classes of sofic
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts and effectively closed by patterns
\mathbb{G}
-subshifts coincide. Later we will show that no amenable group is strongly self-simulable (

and

) and thus the previous characterization stands for all finitely generated groups.

3.
UFOs and extraterrestrial graphs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. Given two finite subsets
A,B
of
V
, a
matching
is a relation
M\subset A\times B
such that the projection maps to each component are injective. A matching
M
is called
complete
if it induces a bijection (i.e, every
a\in A
and
b\in B
occurs in a unique element of
M
). We say that a matching
M
is by
paths of length at most
k
if for every
(u,v)\in M
we have
d_{G}(u,v)\leq k
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. An
(m,k,r)
-UFO is a triple
(U,F,O)
of disjoint finite subsets of
V
such that:

(1)

|U|\geq m|F|
;

(2)

there is a complete matching between
U
and
O
by paths of length at most
k
;

(3)

any path from
U
to
O
in
G
goes through a vertex in
F
or is of length at least
r
.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a graph
G
is
extraterrestrial
, if for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an
(m,k,r)
-UFO.

The acronym “UFO” means Under, Front and Over and is inspired by
Figure

1.2
where a UFO is drawn on the canonical Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. We remark that this is not equivalent to the notion of UFO used in
[
17
]
.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
generated by the canonical basis
S=\{v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\left\|v\right\|_{1}=1\}
. For positive integers
m,r
put

•

U=[0,r-1]^{d-1}\times[-3^{d-1}m,-1]

•

F=[-r,2r-1]^{d-1}\times\{0\}

•

O=[0,r-1]^{d-1}\times[1,3^{d-1}m]

The triple (U,F,O) forms a
(m,3^{d-1}m+1,2r+4)
-UFO, see
Figure

1.2
for the case
m=1,d=2,r=7
. We deduce that
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{Z}^{d},S)
is extraterrestrial.

Next we will show that being extraterrestrial is an invariant of quasi-isometry for graphs of bounded degree.

Definition 3.4
.

Two graphs
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
are

if there exists a function
f\colon V\to V^{\prime}
and positive real constants
A
,
B
and
C
such that

•

f
is a
quasi-isometric embedding
: for every

one has

\frac{1}{A}d_{G}(v_{1},v_{2})-B\leq d_{G^{\prime}}(f(v_{1}),f(v_{2}))\leq Ad_{G}(v_{1},v_{2})+B.

•

f(V)
is
relatively dense
: for every
v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}
there exists
v\in V
such that

d_{G^{\prime}}(v^{\prime},f(v))\leq C.

Next we give the proof of
A
, that is, that the property of being extraterrestrial is an invariant of quasi-isometry for graphs of bounded degree.

Proof of
A
.

Let
G=(V,E)
and
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
be quasi-isometric graphs of bounded degree. Suppose that
G
is extraterrestrial and let

be a map which realizes the quasi-isometry between
G
and
G^{\prime}
with associated constants
A,B
and
C
as in the definition.

Let
D
be the largest of the degrees of
G
and
G^{\prime}
. It follows that the number of elements in a ball of radius
n
centered on any vertex on any of both graphs is at most
D^{n}
. In particular,
D^{AB}
is an upper bound of the number of preimages of a vertex by

, as two vertices

with the same image must satisfy

.

Let
(U,F,O)
be an
(m,k,r)
-UFO of
G
. Define

F^{\prime}=\{v^{\prime}\in V^{\prime}:d_{G}(v^{\prime},f(F))\leq\alpha\}\textrm{ with }\alpha=A^{2}(1+B+2C)+B+C.

Define also
U^{\prime\prime}=f(U)\setminus F^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime\prime}=f(O)\setminus F^{\prime}
. Consider a complete matching
M\subset U\times O
between

and

by paths of length at most

. Take a maximal subset

with the property that for any pair

\min(d_{G}(u_{1},u_{2}),d_{G}(o_{1},o_{2}))>AB
. Define

M^{\prime}=\{(f(u),f(o)):(u,o)\in M^{\prime\prime}\mbox{ and }(f(u),f(o))\in U^{\prime\prime}\times O^{\prime\prime}\}.

Notice that by the property we required on
M^{\prime\prime}
the map
f
is injective in both of its projections and thus it follows that
M^{\prime}
is indeed a matching which satisfies
|M^{\prime}|\geq|M^{\prime\prime}|-2|F^{\prime}|
.

Finally, define
U^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime}
as the projections of
M^{\prime}
onto the first and second coordinates, respectively. With this definition,
M^{\prime}
is a complete matching between
U^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime}
. Our aim is to prove that for
m
large enough, the sets
(U^{\prime},F^{\prime},O^{\prime})
form an
(m^{\prime},k^{\prime},r^{\prime})
-UFO for some
(m^{\prime},k^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
.

(1)

An element of

has at most
D^{\alpha}
elements at distance at most than
\alpha
and so
|F^{\prime}|\leq|F|D^{\alpha}
. Moreover, as each vertex in

can have at most

preimages under

, it follows that
|M^{\prime\prime}|\geq\frac{|M|}{D^{2AB}}\geq\frac{m|F|}{D^{2AB}}
.

By definition of

and the above considerations, we have

|U^{\prime}|=|M^{\prime}|\geq|M^{\prime\prime}|-2|F^{\prime}|\geq\frac{m|F|}{D^{2AB}}-2|F^{\prime}|=\left(\frac{m}{D^{2AB+\alpha}}-2\right)|F^{\prime}|\geq m^{\prime}|F^{\prime}|.

Where
m^{\prime}=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{D^{2AB+\alpha}}-2\right\rfloor

(2)

Given two adjacent vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
, one has

d_{G^{\prime}}(f(v_{1}),f(v_{2}))\leq Ad_{G}(v_{1},v_{2})+B=A+B.

It follows that if there is a path of length less than
k
between
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, then there is path of length less than
k(A+B)
between
f(v_{1})
and
f(v_{2})
. Put
k^{\prime}=\lceil k(A+B)\rceil
, we deduce that the set
M^{\prime}
is a complete matching between
U^{\prime}
and
O^{\prime}
by paths of length at most
k^{\prime}
.

(3)

Assume that there is a path
v^{\prime}_{0},v^{\prime}_{1},\dots,v^{\prime}_{\ell}
that avoids

of length

in
G^{\prime}
between

and

. Given two consecutive vertices of this path, there exists

and

such that

Thus there is a path in

between
v_{i}
and

of size at most

. Consider a vertex

of this path, one has

\displaystyle d_{G^{\prime}}(v^{\prime}_{i},f(v))

\displaystyle\leq C+d_{G^{\prime}}(f(v_{i}),f(v))

\displaystyle\leq C+Ad_{G}(v_{i},v)+B

\displaystyle\leq C+A(A(1+B+2C))+B

\displaystyle=\alpha.

Since

, one has

so

is not in

. We deduce that there exists a path from

to

which avoids

and whose length is bounded by

between elements of

and

which avoids

.

Now we can prove the

, we choose

such that

. Consider

-UFO for any

admits a
(m^{\prime},k^{\prime},r^{\prime})

is extraterrestrial
∎

If

A group

4.
Extraterrestrial groups are not self-simulable

In this section, we will show
B
. To that end, we will require a technical lemma that shows that for every set of generators
S
of a group

there is a configuration

that distinguishes locally all elements of
\mathbb{G}
by their neighborhoods, furthermore, the size of said neighborhood is linear with respect to the distance between the elements induced by

.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a universal constant

such that for every infinite and finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
, and every finite generating set
S
, there exists a configuration
\xi\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{G}}
such that for every integer

and

with

with

such that

.

Proof.

It is shown in
[
3

that if
(s_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is an enumeration of
\mathbb{G}\setminus\{1_{G}\}
,

is sufficiently large (specifically,

suffices) and
(T_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of finite subsets of
\mathbb{G}
such that

and
T_{i}\cap s_{i}T_{i}=\varnothing
, then there is a configuration
\xi
where for each

and each

there is
t=t(g,i)\in T_{i}
such that
\xi(gt)\neq\xi(gs_{i}t)
. Take
A=3\lceil C\rceil
and fix some set of generators
S
. It suffices to show that we can choose an enumeration
(s_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{G}\setminus\{1_{G}\}
and a sequence of finite subsets

with the properties above and such that
T_{i}\subset B_{A|s_{i}|_{S}}
for all

.

Fix an enumeration
(s_{i})_{i\geq 1}
of
\mathbb{G}\setminus\{1_{G}\}
in such a way that if

then
|s_{i}|_{S}\leq|s_{j}|_{S}
. In this way we are sure that whenever
|s_{i}|_{S}=k
, then
i\leq|B_{k}|
.

For
i\geq 1
denote
k=|s_{i}|_{S}
. We take
T_{i}
as any maximal (for inclusion) subset of
B_{kA}
with the property that
T_{i}\cap s_{i}T_{i}=\varnothing
. We claim that
|T_{i}|\geq\frac{A}{3}|B_{k}|
and thus that
|T_{i}|\geq Ci
.

Indeed, suppose that
3|T_{i}|<A|B_{k}|
. As
\mathbb{G}
is infinite, we have
|B_{kA}|\geq A|B_{k}|
from which it follows that
3|T_{i}|<|B_{kA}|
and thus that there exists
g\in B_{kA}\setminus(s_{i}^{-1}T_{i}\cup T_{i}\cup s_{i}T_{i})
. Take

. By maximality of

, thus either there are

such that

. Clearly

∎

We note that the value

works as the universal constant from

.

4.1.
The generalized mirror shift

We will construct, for every finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
, a
\mathbb{G}
-subshift
X_{\texttt{GM}}
which is effectively closed by patterns but not sofic. We call
X_{\texttt{GM}}
the
generalized mirror subshift
because it is a geometric generalization of the classical mirror shift construction from
[
21
]
discussed in the introduction. Variants of the mirror shift on nonabelian groups appear also in
[
2
,
8
]
.

For the remainder of this section, fix a finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
and a finite symmetric set of generators
S
such that
1_{\mathbb{G}}\notin S
. Take
F(S)
the free group generated by
S
and let
\psi\colon F(S)\to\mathbb{G}
be the canonical epimorphism. We also fix an arbitrary order
\leq
on
S
and consider the total order
\preceq
on
S^{*}
where words are first ordered by length and then lexicographically, that is:
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{n}\prec s^{\prime}_{1}s^{\prime}_{2}\dots s^{\prime}_{m}
if and only if either
n<m
or
n=m
and for some
k<n
,
s_{1}s_{2}\dots s_{k}=s^{\prime}_{1}s^{\prime}_{2}\dots s^{\prime}_{k}
and
s_{k+1}<s^{\prime}_{k+1}
.

Fix an integer
A
as in
Lemma

4.1
, consider an arbitrary alphabet
\Sigma
and take
\Lambda=\Sigma\times\{0,1\}
. We will first define two types of patterns on alphabet
\Lambda
in
F(S)
which will be used in the definition of
X_{\texttt{GM}}
. For a reduced word
g\in F(S)
denote by
|g|
its word length. Furthermore, for
T\subset F(S)
and
p\in\Lambda^{T}
, denote by
p_{\Sigma}\in\Sigma^{T}
and
p_{\{0,1\}}\in\{0,1\}^{T}
its projections, that is, the maps such that for every

.

For a positive integer

, let

. For a pattern

and

we say that

are

-equivalent and write

if for every

p_{\{0,1\}}(gt)=p_{\{0,1\}}(ht)
.

Definition 4.2
.

A pattern

is

if for every pair of

-equivalent

we have
p_{\Sigma}(g)=p_{\Sigma}(h)
.

Intuitively, we use the

where two elements

are identified if the symbols in

and

coincide. The fundamental observation is that

ensures that some configuration must encode the equivalence relation on

given by the word problem of
\mathbb{G}
.

Now we choose
\Sigma
explicitly, namely, we take

\Sigma=\{\star,\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{o}\}\times\{-,+\}.

For
T\subset F(S)
and
p\in\Lambda^{T}
, we denote by
p_{\Sigma,A}
and

the projections of

to each component of
\Sigma
respectively. Furthermore, write
\mathcal{U}(p)
(respectively
\mathcal{O}(p)
) for the set of positions

where
p_{\Sigma,A}(g)=\mathfrak{u}
(respectively
\mathfrak{o}
).

For a coherent pattern
p\in\Lambda^{B_{(A+1)k}}
, we define a matching
M(p)\subset F(S)\times F(S)
with paths of length at most
k
inductively. Let
w_{1},\dots,w_{m}
be the sequence of elements of
B_{k}\setminus\{1_{F(S)}\}
of length at most

ordered according to
\preceq
. Take

as the initial matching and

as the initial set of available vertices.

Suppose that
M_{i-1}(p)
and

have been constructed for some

. We set
M_{i}(p)=M_{i-1}(p)
,

and modify them as follows:

•

First, we remove from

all

such that either
g\in\mathcal{U}(p)
but
gw_{i+1}\notin B_{k}
or

but

.

•

In lexicographic order: for every

such that

with

and

to

and remove from

all

or

.

Finally, we set

. The intuition behind this procedure is that elements from the quotient graph

are matched from

to
\mathcal{O}(p)
prioritizing according to the order on

. If at some point some element could have potentially been matched with an element outside of

, we remove it from the set of available vertices (as in a larger pattern it could have been matched to an element outside). With this procedure, the matching

has the following properties:

(1)

The procedure to check if a pattern

is coherent and to construct

are effective.

(2)

M(p)
is a matching by paths of length less than

between a subset of
\mathcal{O}(p)
and a subset of
\mathcal{U}(p)
.

(3)

If
k\leq k^{\prime}
and

,

are two coherent patterns such that
p^{\prime}|_{B_{(A+1)k}}=p
, then

. Thus we can define
M_{k}(y)
for

that does not contain non-coherent patterns as the union over

of

.

Definition 4.3
.

A coherent pattern
p\in\Lambda^{B_{(A+1)k}}
is

if for every pair
(g,h)\in M(p)
we have
p_{\Sigma,\pm}(g)=p_{\Sigma,\pm}(h)
.

With this, we can define
Y_{\mathtt{GM}}\subset\Lambda^{F(S)}
as the set of all configurations given by the following set of forbidden patterns:

(1)

Coherence rule:
For all
k\geq 1
, we forbid all patterns
p\in\Lambda^{B_{(A+1)k}}
which are not coherent.

(2)

Matching rule
: For all
k\geq 1
, we forbid all patterns

which are coherent but are not matched.

It is clear from the observation above that

is an effective subshift. Recall that we denote by
\psi\colon F(S)\to\mathbb{G}
the canonical epimorphism and that for

which is fixed by

we can define
\psi^{*}(y)\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{G}}
given by
\psi^{*}(x)(\psi(g))=x(g)
for every

.

Definition 4.4
.

The
generalized mirror shift
is the set of configurations

5.
Examples of extraterrestrial groups

The purpose of this section is to give examples of extraterrestrial groups and which thus satisfy the conclusion of

5.1.
Amenable and multi-ended groups

In
[
2

Recall that a discrete group
\mathbb{G}
is amenable if and only if for every
\varepsilon>0
and
K\Subset\mathbb{G}
there is

such that
|FK\setminus F|\leq\varepsilon|F|.

Proposition 5.1
.

Finitely generated infinite amenable groups are extraterrestrial.

Proof.

Let
m\geq 1
be a positive integer and fix a finite symmetric set of generators
S
for
\mathbb{G}
which contains the identity. As
\mathbb{G}
is amenable, it follows that there exists
U\Subset\mathbb{G}
such that
|US\setminus U|\leq\frac{1}{m}|U|
.

Take
F=US\setminus U
and let
O\subset\mathbb{G}\setminus US
be any set of size
|U|
(this is possible since
\mathbb{G}\setminus US
is infinite). We have that
|O|=|U|\geq m|F|
and that
U
and
O
lie on distinct components of
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
after removing

k=\max\{d_{S}(g,g^{\prime}):g\in U\textrm{ and }g^{\prime}\in O\}
, it follows that
(U,F,O)
is an
(m,k,r)
-UFO for every

and thus
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.
∎

Recall that the number of ends of a finitely generated group
\mathbb{G}
is the limit as
n\to\infty
of the number of infinite connected components that occur in
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
(for some set of generators

) after removing the ball of radius
n
. Alternatively, it is the number of equivalence classes of rays in
Cay(\mathbb{G},S)
, where two rays
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are said to be equivalent if there exists a ray that contains infinitely many vertices of both
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
. It is well known that the number of ends of a group does not depend upon the choice of generators. We say that
\mathbb{G}
is

if it has more than one end. Equivalently,
\mathbb{G}
is multi-ended if for some finite set of generators

, there exists

such that
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
has at least two infinite components after removing
F
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Multi-ended groups are extraterrestrial.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{G}
is multi-ended, there exists a finite subset
F
such that
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
has more than one infinite connected component after removing
F
. Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, take
U
in a connected component of
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)\setminus F
such that
|U|\geq m|F|
and
O
in an other infinite connected component such that
|U|=|O|
. Take
k=\max\{d_{S}(g,g^{\prime}):g\in U\textrm{ and }g^{\prime}\in O\}
. It follows that there exists a matching between
U
and
O
by paths of length less than
k
. Moreover, there is no path from
U
to
O
which avoids
F
. We deduce that for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
(U,F,O)
is a
(m,k,r)
-UFO. Thus
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.
∎

5.2.
Amenable extensions of multi-ended graphs

In
[
9
]
, we prove that the group
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
is not self-simulable. It is an example of one ended non-amenable group which is not self-simulable. Using the language of Schreier graphs, we can state a generalization of this example.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group and
\mathbb{H}\leqslant\mathbb{G}
an amenable subgroup. If for some symmetric generating set
S
of
\mathbb{G}
, the Schreier graph
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
is extraterrestrial, then so is
\mathbb{G}
.

Note that the choice of
S
does not matter, as being extraterrestrial is quasi-isometry invariant.

Proof.

Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{H}\backslash\mathbb{G}
be the canonical projection map given by
\pi(g)=\mathbb{H}g
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\kappa(m)
to be minimal such that for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an
(m,\kappa(m),r)
-UFO in
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
.

Now, fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
(U_{r},F_{r},O_{r})
be a
(2m,\kappa(2m),r)
-UFO in
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
. Choose
U^{\prime}_{r}\subset\mathbb{G}
such that
\pi|_{U^{\prime}_{r}}
is a bijection onto
U_{r}
. Now, by the definition of distance in the Schreier graph, there exists
O^{\prime}_{r}
such that
\pi|_{O^{\prime}_{r}}
is a bijection onto

and there is a complete matching with paths of length at most
\kappa(2m)
between
U^{\prime}_{r}
and
O^{\prime}_{r}
.

Also choose
F^{\prime}_{r}
arbitrarily so that
\pi|_{F^{\prime}_{r}}
is a bijection onto
F_{r}
. Let now

K_{r}=\{h\in\mathbb{H}:\mbox{ there exists }u\in U^{\prime}_{r}\mbox{ and }f\in F^{\prime}_{r}\mbox{ such that }d_{S}(u,hf)\leq r\}.

In other words,
K_{r}
is the set of heights of elements in the fibers of
F_{r}
that are at distance at most
r
from
U^{\prime}_{r}
. Note that, as both
U^{\prime}_{r}
and
F^{\prime}_{r}
are finite, we also have that
K_{r}
is finite.

As
\mathbb{H}
is amenable, there exists a finite set

such that
|TK_{r}\setminus T|\leq|T|
. We define

By our previous definition, we have
|U|=|U^{\prime}_{r}||T|=|U_{r}||T|
and
|F|=|TK_{r}||F^{\prime}_{r}|\leq 2|T||F_{r}|
. Since
(U_{r},F_{r},O_{r})
is a
(2m,\kappa(2m),r)
-UFO,
|U_{r}|\geq 2m|F_{r}|
, and so
|U|\geq m|F|
.

As by construction there is a complete matching
M^{\prime}
with paths of length at most
\kappa(2m)
between
U^{\prime}_{r}
and
O^{\prime}_{r}
, it follows that there is also a complete matching
M
with paths of length at most
\kappa(2m)
between
U
and
O
. More explicitly, we may take
M=\{(hu,ho):(u,o)\in M^{\prime},h\in T\}
.

Finally, let
u=hu_{0}\in U
with
h\in T
and
u_{0}\in U^{\prime}_{r}
and consider a path
(hu_{i})_{0\leq i\leq r}
in
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
such that

. As any path in
\mathbb{G}
projects onto a path in
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
and since
(U_{r},F_{r},O_{r})
is a
(2m,\kappa(2m),r)
-UFO, there exists
i_{0}\in\{1,\dots,r\}
for which
hu_{i_{0}}\in\mathbb{H}f
for some

. But if
hu_{i_{0}}=h^{\prime}f
with
h^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}
, then
d_{S}(u_{1},h^{-1}h^{\prime}f)=i_{0}\leq r
and thus it must be that
h^{-1}h^{\prime}\in K_{r}
. We conclude that
hu_{i_{0}}\in TK_{r}F^{\prime}_{r}=F
. This means that any path of length at most
r
between
U
and
O
must cross

, and so
(U,F,O)
is a
(m,\kappa(2m),r)
-UFO.∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
1\to\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{G}\to^{\pi}\mathbb{K}\to 1
be a short exact sequence where
\mathbb{H}
is amenable,
\mathbb{G}
is finitely generated and
\mathbb{K}
is extraterrestrial. Then
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

For any finite generating set
S
of
\mathbb{G}
, the Schreier graph
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
is isomorphic to the Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{K},\pi(S))
. In particular, it is extraterrestrial. By
Theorem

5.3
,
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group, and
\mathbb{H}\leq\mathbb{G}
an amenable subgroup. If for some generating set
S
, the Schreier graph
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H},S)
is multi-ended, then
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Theorem

5.3
and
Proposition

5.2
.
∎

We will say that a group admits an amenable cut if it satisfies the hypotheses of
5.5
. All known examples of groups that are not self-simulable fall under this corollary. However, there are cases where it is more natural to construct UFOs geometrically, as we will see in the case of hyperbolic surface groups. As a particular case of this previous corollary, the result of

which states that
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
is not self-simulable falls into the following corollary.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{K}
such that
\mathbb{H}
is amenable and
\mathbb{K}
is not one-ended. The group
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence
1\to\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{G}\to^{\pi}\mathbb{K}\to 1
where
\pi
is the natural projection to the second coordinate. If
\mathbb{K}
is zero-ended, then it is finite and so
\mathbb{G}
is amenable. By
Proposition

5.1
, it is extraterrestrial. Otherwise,
\mathbb{K}
is multi-ended and so by

5.2
it is extraterrestrial. We conclude with

.
∎

5.3.
Amalgamated free products and HNN extensions

In this section we will provide natural conditions under which amalgamated free products and HNN extensions are extraterrestrial.

Given groups
\mathbb{G}_{1},\mathbb{G}_{2},\mathbb{H}
and embeddings
\phi_{1}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{G}_{1}

the

A normal form for an amalgamated free product can be obtained as follows. For

of the cosets

which contains the identity. A normal form is a sequence
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
such that
x_{0}\in\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H})
and for
j\geq 1
each
x_{j}
is either in
T_{1}\setminus\{1\}
or
T_{2}\setminus\{1\}
in an alternating way. Every element is equal to a normal form, and distinct normal forms correspond to distinct elements, see
[
25
]
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{1},\mathbb{G}_{2},\mathbb{H}
be finitely generated groups with
\mathbb{H}
amenable. Let
\phi_{1}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{G}_{1}
and
\phi_{2}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{G}_{2}
be proper embeddings. Then the amalgamated free product
\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{2}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

Take
S_{1},S_{2}
finite symmetric set of generators of
\mathbb{G}_{1},\mathbb{G}_{2}
respectively and take
S=S_{1}\cup S_{2}
. It is clear that
S
generates
\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{2}
. For each
i\in\{1,2\}
choose a set of representatives
T_{i}
of the cosets
\phi_{i}(\mathbb{H})\backslash\mathbb{G}_{i}
which contains the identity. As the embeddings are proper, we have that
|T_{i}|\geq 2
.

By
5.5
it suffices to show that the Schreier graph
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{2},\mathbb{H},S)
is multi-ended. Take
g_{1}\in T_{1}\setminus\{1\}
and
g_{2}\in T_{2}\setminus\{1\}
. From the structure of the normal forms, it is easy to show that for any
n\in\mathbb{N},\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H})(g_{1}g_{2})^{n}
and
\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H})(g_{2}g_{1})^{n}
lie in distinct connected components of
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{,}\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H}),S)
after removing
\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H})
. Thus the rays
(\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H})(g_{1}g_{2})^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\phi_{1}(\mathbb{H})(g_{2}g_{1})^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are non equivalent in
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{2},\mathbb{H},S)
, and it is hence multi-ended.∎

We remark that
Proposition

5.7
may fail if one of the embeddings is not proper. For instance, if we take
\mathbb{G}_{1}=\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{Z}
,
\phi_{1}
the identity,
\mathbb{G}_{2}=F_{2}\times F_{2}
and
\phi_{2}\colon\mathbb{Z}\to F_{2}\times F_{2}
any embedding, then
\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{2}\cong F_{2}\times F_{2}
which is not extraterrestrial (due to
B
and the fact that it is self-simulable).

Next we consider the case of HNN-extensions. Let
\mathbb{G}
be a group,
\mathbb{H}_{1},\mathbb{H}_{2}
two subgroups of
\mathbb{G}
and
\phi\colon\mathbb{H}_{1}\to\mathbb{H}_{2}
an isomorphism. The
HNN-extension
of
\mathbb{G}
relative to
\phi
is the group

\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}=\langle\mathbb{G},t\;\mid\;t^{-1}ht=\phi(h)\mbox{ for all }h\in\mathbb{H}_{1}\rangle.

In the presentation above we call
t
the
stable generator
. We shall need the following lemma of Britton whose proof can be found for instance in
[
25
]
.

Lemma 5.8

(Britton’s lemma)
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
g=g_{0}t^{\epsilon_{1}}g_{1}\dots t^{\epsilon_{n}}g_{n}\in\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}
where each
g_{i}\in\mathbb{G}
and
\epsilon_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
. If
g=1_{\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}}
, then there exists a subword of the form
t^{-1}g_{i}t
with
g_{i}\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
or
tg_{j}t^{-1}
with
g_{j}\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
.

This can be refined to a normal form (with unique representations for elements), but this lemma suffices for our purposes.

We obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group and
\phi\colon\mathbb{H}_{1}\to\mathbb{H}_{2}
an isomorphism between amenable subgroups of
\mathbb{G}
. Then
\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

Let
t
be the stable generator of
\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}
,
F
a finite generating set of
\mathbb{G}
and let
S=F\cup\{t,t^{-1}\}
. Recall that for some word
w
on the generators we write
\underline{w}
for the corresponding element in the group.

Consider the Schreier graph
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi},\mathbb{H}_{1},S)
. Notice that if we remove the vertex
\mathbb{H}_{1}
, the rays
\{\mathbb{H}_{1}t^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and
\{\mathbb{H}_{1}t^{-n}\}_{n\geq 1}
lie in the same connected component as
\mathbb{H}_{1}t
and
\mathbb{H}_{1}t^{-1}
respectively, thus it suffices to show that
\mathbb{H}_{1}t
and
\mathbb{H}_{1}t^{-1}
lie in distinct connected components.

Let
x_{1}\dots x_{n}\in S^{*}
be such that
\mathbb{H}_{1}tx_{1}\dots x_{n}=\mathbb{H}_{1}t^{-1}
. It follows that there is
a\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
such that

atx_{1}\dots x_{n}t=1_{\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}}.

By Britton’s lemma, either there exists a subword of
w_{0}=atx_{1}\dots x_{n}t
of the form
t^{\epsilon}xt^{-\epsilon}
with
x\in F^{*}
such that either
\epsilon=-1
and
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
, or
\epsilon=1
and
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
. It follows that we may replace any of these words by some word in
F^{*}
and obtain another word which represents
1_{\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi}}
and has one less occurrence of both
t
and
t^{-1}
. Iteratively define
w_{i+1}
from
w_{i}
by doing the leftmost possible replacement as above.

As the number of pairs of
t,t^{-1}
is reduced with each iteration, it follows that there is some
k\geq 0
such that there is a prefix
u_{k}
of
w_{k}
that has the form
atxt^{-1}
with
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{H}_{2}
. In particular,
\underline{atxt^{-1}}\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
. Write
w_{k}=u_{k}v_{k}
the replacements described above and note that, as we are replacing a word on
F
, the word we are going to replace is either completely contained in
u_{k}
or
v_{k}
, thus we may write
w_{k-1}=u_{k-1}v_{k-1}
by performing the replacement in the corresponding portion. Note that
\underline{u_{k}}=\underline{u_{k-1}}
. Iterating this procedure we obtain a decomposition
w_{0}=u_{0}v_{0}
such that
\underline{u_{0}}=\underline{u_{k}}\in\mathbb{H}_{1}
. Furthermore,
u_{0}
ends with
t^{-1}
, thus it is a strict prefix of
w_{0}
. Therefore any path from
\mathbb{H}_{1}t
to
\mathbb{H}_{1}t^{-1}
must cross
\mathbb{H}_{1}
.
It follows that
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G}^{\ast\phi},\mathbb{H}_{1},S)
is multi-ended, and so by
5.5
,
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.∎

From the cases of amalgamated free products and HNN-extensions, we can deduce a general proposition about groups acting on trees. For this, we introduce the notion of graph of groups. A
graph of groups
is the data of

(1)

An oriented graph
G=(V,E)
with origin and end maps
o,t\colon E\to V
and inversion map
i\colon E\to E
.

(2)

A family of
vertex groups

(\mathbb{G}_{v})_{v\in V}
.

(3)

A family of
edge groups

(\mathbb{H}_{e})_{e\in E}
with monomorphisms
\psi_{e,o}\colon\mathbb{H}_{e}\to\mathbb{G}_{o(e)}
and
\psi_{e,t}\colon\mathbb{H}_{e}\to\mathbb{G}_{t(e)}
.

Then, if
T
is a covering tree of
G
, the
fundamental group
of the graph of groups is the group generated by the vertex groups
(\mathbb{G}_{v})_{v\in V}
and additional generators
s_{e}
for every
e\in E
subjected to the following relations.

(1)

\forall e\in E,s_{i(e)}=s_{e}^{-1}.

(2)

\forall e\in T,s_{e}=1.

(3)

\forall e\in E,\forall x\in\mathbb{G}_{e},s_{e}^{-1}\psi_{e,o}(x)s_{e}=\psi_{e,t}(x)
.

The choice of a covering tree does not change the fundamental group up to isomorphism
[
30
, §5, Proposition 20]
.

Example 5.11
.

The fundamental group of a segment of groups
\mathbb{G}_{1}
\mathbb{G}_{2}
\mathbb{H}
is the free product with amalgamation
\mathbb{G}_{1}\ast_{\mathbb{H}}\mathbb{G}_{2}
. The fundamental group of a loop of groups
\mathbb{G}
\mathbb{H}
is the HNN-extension
\mathbb{G}^{\ast\psi_{e,t}\circ\psi_{e,o}^{-1}}
.

Fundamental groups of graphs of groups hence are a generalization of amalgamated free products and HNN-extensions, moreover, they are built inductively from these two constructions.

If
\mathbb{G}
acts cocompactly by automorphisms without edge inversions on a tree
T
, then there exists a surjective graph morphism
\pi:T\to T/\mathbb{G}
, and
T/\mathbb{G}
is finite. Since
\mathbb{G}
acts without edge inversions, an element that stabilizes an edge also stabilizes its ends. Hence, for every
e\in E(T)
, there are monomorphisms
\psi_{e,o}
and
\psi_{e,t}
from
\mathrm{Stab}(e)
to the stabilizers of its ends. We can then endow
G=T/\mathbb{G}
with a graph of groups structure by setting
G_{\pi(v)}=\mathrm{Stab}(v)
for every
v\in V(T)
and
G_{\pi(e)}=\mathrm{Stab}(e)
for every
e\in E(T)
with the monomorphisms induced by the
\psi_{e,o},\psi_{e,t}
. The structure theorem of groups acting on trees
[
30
, §5, Theorem 13]
states that the graph of groups thus defined has
\mathbb{G}
as a fundamental group.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group. Suppose that
\mathbb{G}
acts cocompactly by automorphisms without edge inversions on a tree. Suppose further that no vertex is fixed by
\mathbb{G}
and that edge stabilizers are amenable. Then
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

By the structure theorem of groups acting on trees,
\mathbb{G}
may be realized as the fundamental group of a finite graph of groups with amenable edges. As no vertex is fixed by
\mathbb{G}
, we can also observe that
\mathbb{G}
is not a vertex group of this graph of groups. But since fundamental groups of graphs of groups are built inductively by amalgamated free products and HNN-extensions, it follows that either
\mathbb{G}
is an HNN-extension over an amenable subgroup, or it is an amalgamated free product over an amenable subgroup. In this case, since
\mathbb{G}
is not a vertex group of the graph of groups, this amenable subgroup must be a proper subgroup. We conclude with
Proposition

5.7
and
Proposition

5.9
.
∎

We remark that since edge stabilizers inject in vertex stabilizers, it follows from this result that if the infinite group
\mathbb{G}
acts co-compactly by automorphisms without edge inversions and with amenable
vertex
stabilizers on a tree, then
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial. Indeed, if a vertex is universally fixed, then
\mathbb{G}
is infinite and amenable and we conclude by
Proposition

5.1
, and if not, then
\mathbb{G}
verifies the condition of
Proposition

5.12
. We also note that the proof of
Proposition

5.12
also shows that fundamental groups of graphs of groups with at least one amenable edge that embeds properly, are extraterrestrial.

An immediate corollary of
Proposition

5.12
is the following.

A
generalized Baumslag-Solitar group
is a group that has a faithful cocompact action on a tree by automorphisms, without edge inversions and with infinite cyclic edge and vertex stabilizers.

Corollary 5.13
.

Generalized Baumslag-Solitar groups are extraterrestrial.

5.4.
Groups quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane

In this section we show that any finitely generated group which is quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane is extraterrestrial. We will first provide a direct proof.

Definition 5.14
.

The
pentagon model
is the graph with vertices
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and edges

\{\{(m,n),(m+1,n)\}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\{\{(m,n),(2m,n-1)\}:m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Figure 5.2
.
A finite portion of the pentagon model.

In
Figure

5.2
a representation of the pentagon model is shown. It is well known that the pentagon model is quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane
\mathcal{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}
with the usual hyperbolic metric. An explicit quasi-isometry from the vertices of the pentagon model to
\mathcal{H}
is given by

(m,n)\mapsto m2^{n}+2^{n}i.

Lemma 5.15
.

The pentagon model is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

Let
m,r\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. An
(m,6m+1,r)
-UFO is given by

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=\{(h,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:-3m\leq h<0\mbox{ and }0\leq v<r\},

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\{(0,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:-r\leq v\leq 2r-1\},

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\{(h,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:0<h\leq 3m\mbox{ and }0\leq v<r\}.

Indeed, we have
|U|=3mr
and
|F|=3r
, thus
|U|\geq m|F|
. A matching by paths of length at most
6m+1
is given by
M=\{\bigl((-h,v),(h,v)\bigr)\in U\times O:0<h\leq 3m\mbox{ and }0\leq v<r\}
, the paths are given by just increasing the first coordinate one by one. Finally, notice that any path from
U
to
O
must necessarily contain a vertex with horizontal coordinate
0
. If said path avoids
F
, then this vertex necessarily has vertical coordinate either smaller than
-r
or larger than
m+r
. As each edge in this graph modifies the vertical coordinate at most by one, said path has length at least
r
.
∎

Proposition 5.16
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a finitely generated group which is quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane
\mathcal{H}
. Then
\mathbb{G}
is extraterrestrial.

Proof.

Let
S
be a generating set for
\mathbb{G}
. As both the pentagon model and
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
are quasi-isometric to
\mathcal{H}
, it follows that the pentagon model is quasi-isometric to
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
. By
A
and
Lemma

5.15
we deduce that
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{G},S)
is extraterrestrial.
∎

A Fuchsian group is a discrete subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
. These groups act faithfully on the hyperbolic plane by Möbius transformations. In particular, when they act cocompactly, they give a natural class of groups which are finitely generated and quasi-isometric (by the Schwarz-Milnor lemma) to
\mathcal{H}
. We obtain the following corollary

Corollary 5.17
.

Every cocompact Fuchsian group is extraterrestrial.

Another interesting class of groups which we can completely classify are fundamental groups of surfaces. In the case of surfaces which are not closed (that is, either they are not compact or they have boundary), the fundamental group is free and thus is extraterrestrial by
Proposition

5.2
. For closed surfaces we may discard the case of non-orientable surfaces, as they admit an orientable 2-cover and thus their fundamental groups are commensurable to the fundamental group of an orientable surface. Therefore, the only remaining case is when the surface is closed and orientable and the only remaining parameter is the genus
g
. If
g\leq 1
the fundamental group is amenable and thus is extraterrestrial by
Proposition

5.1
. Finally, if
g\geq 2
the fundamental group is given by

\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{g})=\langle a_{1},b_{1},...,a_{g},b_{g}\;\mid\;\;[a_{1},b_{1}]\ldots[a_{g},b_{g}]=1\rangle.

See for instance
[
26
, Chapter 4]
. These groups are all commensurable for
g\geq 2
and quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane, thus by
Lemma

5.15
we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 5.18
.

The fundamental group of any 2-manifold is extraterrestrial.

5.5.
An amenable cut in the surface group

We now give an alternative proof that the fundamental group of a closed orientable surface of genus
g=2
(from now on, the surface group) is extraterrestrial. This has the advantage of showing explicitly that this group admits an amenable cut in the sense of
5.5
. Before giving a proof, let us outline how one can “see this immediately” in the previous proof. Surface groups are hyperbolic, and in a hyperbolic group
\mathbb{G}
, every element of infinite order will define a quasi-geodesic, i.e.
n\mapsto g^{n}
is a quasi-isometric embedding of
\mathbb{Z}
into
\mathbb{G}
, see
[
10
, Corollary 3.10]
.

Thus, if we pick any torsion-free element
g
, then
\langle g\rangle
induces a quasi-geodesic in the pentagon model. It is easy to convince oneself that such a quasi-geodesic must roughly cut the space into two parts. Now one just has to find elements of the group such that right translation by them moves one respectively to the left and right side of the path. It is easy to convince oneself that these exist (one may also construct this explicitly using the Cayley graph depicted in
[
1
]
and perform an analogous argument directly there).

For the precise proof, we essentially directly obtain the statement from the literature, though it takes a bit of work to translate everything to our setting. Specifically, we will use a theorem from
[
29
]
. Thus, we need to recall the definition of ends used in
[
29
]
, as it is slightly different from ours. Let
\mathbb{G}
be a group and
C
a subgroup. Let
C\backslash\mathbb{G}
denote the quotient of
\mathbb{G}
by the left action of
C
. Then
\mathbb{G}
acts on the right on
C\backslash\mathbb{G}
. Define
\overline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(C\backslash\mathbb{G})
as the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-vector space of subsets of
C\backslash\mathbb{G}
under symmetric difference, and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}(C\backslash\mathbb{G})
as the subspace of vectors with finite support (i.e. the support consists of finitely many cosets). Define now
E(C\backslash\mathbb{G})=\overline{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(C\backslash\mathbb{G})/\mathbb{Z}_{2}(C\backslash\mathbb{G})
and let
C^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))=E(C\backslash\mathbb{G})
and
C^{1}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))=\{\phi:\mathbb{G}\to E(C\backslash\mathbb{G})\}
be the usual cochain groups. Take the canonical map
\partial_{1}:C^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))\to C^{1}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))
where for
\phi\in E(\mathbb{G},C)
and
g\in\mathbb{G}
we have

\partial_{1}(\phi)(g)=g\phi\cdot\phi.

Set the
0
-th homology group as
H^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))=\operatorname{ker}(\partial_{1}).

Definition 5.19
.

e(\mathbb{G},C)
is the dimension, as a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-vector space, of the group
H^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))
.

Lemma 5.20
.

e(\mathbb{G},C)
is the number of ends of the Schreier graph of
\mathbb{G}
with respect to
C
.

Proof.

The elements
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))
are precisely the elements of
E(C\backslash\mathbb{G})
which satisfy that for all
g\in\mathbb{G}
then
g\phi\cdot\phi=\phi
. That is, it is the vector space of subsets of
C\backslash\mathbb{G}
(modulo finite differences) which are invariant under right translation (modulo finite differences).

Take some finite symmetric generating set
S
of
\mathbb{G}
and consider the Schreier graph
\Gamma=\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},C,S)
. Thus in terms of the Schreier graph,
e(\mathbb{G},C)
is the dimension of the vector space of subsets of the Schreier graph (modulo finite differences) which are invariant under right translation (modulo finite differences).

Suppose there are at least
k
ends in
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},C,S)
, then we can find a finite subset of the Schreier graph
D\subset C\backslash\mathbb{G}
such that
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},C,S)
has at least
k
infinite connected components after removing
D
. The characteristic functions of these components are elements of
H^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))
which are linearly independent, thus
e(\mathbb{G},C)\geq k
. On the other hand, suppose that the dimension is at least
k
, and let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in H^{0}(\mathbb{G},E(C\backslash\mathbb{G}))
be linearly independent. We can find a finite subset
D
of
C\backslash\mathbb{G}
outside which the
v_{i}
are closed under
S
-translations (i.e, for
i\in\{1,\dots,k
and
s\in S
take
D_{i,s}
as the finite set of elements where
v_{i}
differs from
sv_{i}
and then take
D
as the union of the
D_{i,s}
). Thus, their supports must be formed out of connected components of
\mathrm{Sch}(\mathbb{G},C,S)
after removing
D
. For the dimension to be at least
k
, we need at least
k
connected components.
∎

Proposition 5.21
.

The surface group has an amenable cut.

Proof.

[
29
]
states that if
\mathbb{G}
