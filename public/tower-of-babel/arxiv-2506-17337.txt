Can Common VLMs Rival Medical VLMs? Evaluation and Strategic Insights
Yuan Zhong 1 ∗, Ruinan Jin 2,3 ∗, Xiaoxiao Li 2,3 †, Qi Dou 1 †

Abstract
Medical vision-language models (VLMs) leverage large-scale pretraining for diverse imaging tasks but require substantial computational and data resources. Meanwhile, common or general-purpose VLMs (e.g., CLIP, LLaVA), though not trained for medical use, show promise with fine-tuning. This raises a key question: Can efficient fine-tuned common VLMs rival generalist medical VLMs for solving specific medical imaging tasks? This study systematically evaluates common and medical VLMs across disease diagnosis and visual question answering (VQA). Using CLIP-based and LLaVA-based models, we examine (1) off-the-shelf performance gaps in in-domain (ID) settings, (2) whether fine-tuning bridges these gaps, and (3) generalization to out-of-domain (OOD) tasks on unseen medical modalities. While medical-specific pre-training provides advantages in ID settings, common VLMs match or surpass medical-specific models after lightweight fine-tuning, with LoRA-based adaptation proving highly effective among different tasks. In OOD tasks, common VLMs demonstrate strong adaptability in some tasks, challenging the assumption that medical-specific pre-training is essential. These findings suggest that leveraging common VLMs with fine-tuning offers a scalable and cost-effective alternative to developing large-scale medical VLMs, providing crucial insights for future research in the medical imaging field.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare has led to the emergence of medical imaging foundation models (FMs)—large-scale AI systems trained on vast collections of medical data. These models aim to provide a generalist approach to a range of medical tasks, from clinical text processing to medical image interpretation, by leveraging extensive
pretraining

One area where this debate is particularly relevant is medical imaging, a field encompassing tasks such as disease diagnosis and image-based vision question answering (VQA). FMs have demonstrated utility in these domains by learning transferable representations from generalist medical VLMs, enabling task adaptation with limited labeled data

AI models are expected to excel at specific tasks, particularly within the data distributions in which they are deployed

While the prevailing assumption is that larger models trained on diverse medical datasets yield superior generalization, this raises another hypothesis that a domain-agnostic VLM model, with minimal fine-tuning, could rival or even outperform an expensive medical-specific model on certain medical imaging tasks. Furthermore, claims of strong generalization in medical VLMs are questionable in out-of-domain (OOD)

An open question in the “Debate on AI” session at
MICCAI 2024
was whether substantial investments in pretraining generic medical VLMs are justified or if existing common VLMs enhanced with minor fine-tuning can achieve comparable performance on medical imaging tasks. This research systematically examines the trade-offs between cost and generalization performance across various medical imaging applications by pretraining medical VLMs or leveraging the existing pre-trained common VLMs. Specifically, we aim to answer the following research questions (RQs) on pre-trained medical and common VLMs, also shown in Fig.
1
:

RQ1
: For solving specific medical imaging tasks (e.g., diagnosis, VQA), what is the performance gap between medical and common VLMs?

RQ2
: If the gap exists, can a common VLM, after simple fine-tuning, achieve comparable or superior performance?

RQ3
: Does fine-tuning on either a medical or common VLM provide strong generalization to OOD tasks?

To provide plausible answers to the above research questions, we focus on two prominent families of VLMs that have gained popularity both in the general domain and have their Siamese in the medical domain: the
CLIP
family
[
18
]
for diagnosis tasks and the
LLaVA
family
[
15

for VQA tasks. To answer RQ1, we evaluate off-the-shelf VLMs on medical VLMs’ ID datasets, revealing medical VLMs generally outperform common VLMs with certain exceptions. To answer RQ2, we explore light-weighted fine-tuning, including sparse fine-tune and parameter-efficient fine-tune (PEFT), on common VLMs using the targeted datasets. Our results show that fine-tuning significantly improves general VLM performance, often surpassing that of off-the-shelf medical VLMs. For RQ3, we fine-tune both medical and general VLMs on OOD datasets and observe that proper fine-tuning enables common VLMs to be more effective than medical counterparts with the same level of fine-tuning for diagnostic tasks in new medical imaging domains.

In summary, this work contributes in the following three aspects:

– We conduct a systematic comparison of representative common and medical VLMs, including six diagnoses and four VQA datasets, encompassing 90 scenarios for diagnosis and 48 scenarios for VQA.

– Our study addresses three critical research questions towards understanding the needs of training generalist medical VLMs.

– We delve deeper into studying how finetuning on specific target datasets can narrow the generalization gap.

CLIP

[
18
]
. Specifically, it learns to align visual and textual representations by optimizing a contrastive objective—maximizing similarity between matched image-text pairs while minimizing similarity for mismatched pairs. Its variants in medical VLM domain include MedCLIP

[

; PLIP

[

, which leverages the PMC dataset across multiple modalities.

LLaVA
family
. LLaVA
[
15
]
is a VLM model that leverages CLIP vision encoders and large language models(LLMs). This integration enables advanced image-text interactions, such as VQA and image captioning
[
15
]
. By aligning visual representations with language models and instruction-tuning on a self-curated multi-turn conversational dataset, LLaVA significantly improves contextual image understanding. In the medical domain, LLaVA-Med
[
13
]
follows a similar architecture as the original LLaVa and conducts additional pretraining alignment on medical data (MIMIC-CXR
[

]
, CheXpert
[

]
, ROCO
[
19
]
, etc.) using contrastive learning (CLIP-style) to enhance image-text alignment, followed by instruction tuning.

Setups

3.1
Tasks, Models, and Datasets

Diagnosis on
CLIP
-style models.
We adapt the CLIP family for disease diagnosis from medical images. In the off-the-shelf diagnosis setting, we follow the approach of Jin et al.
[
9
]
, computing the similarity score between the disease prompt and the image. Beyond off-the-shelf inference, we apply two efficient fine-tuning strategies: 1) Sparse FT: following the original CLIP paper
[
18
]
, we apply a linear probe (LP) on the image encoder using the training set. 2) PEFT: we perform low-rank adaptation (LoRA) on the image encoder
[
5
]
, with a rank of eight. During inference, the model outputs the logits for individual classes, representing the probability scores for the presence of the target disease.

VQA on LLaVA-style models.
In the off-the-shelf setting, we follow the approach of Li et al.
[
13
]
, where the model generates free-text answers to medical queries based on image inputs. For fine-tuning, we also apply the two efficient fine-tuning strategies to improve medical VQA performance: 1) Sparse FT: like the pre-training in the original LLaVA paper
[
15
]
, we fine-tune the multilayer perception while keeping the rest of the model frozen. 2) PEFT: we apply LoRA
[
5
]
to the image encoder, text decoder, or a combination of these components along with the MLP layer. The detailed methodology is introduced in Sec.
4
.

Datasets for diagnosis tasks.
For the evaluation of VLMs in medical diagnosis, we leverage a diverse set of datasets encompassing various medical imaging modalities
[

]
, including
CheXpert

[
7
]
, a large-scale chest radiograph dataset;
FairVLMed10k

[

PAPILA

[

, a color fundus dataset designed for diagnosing glaucoma. Additionally,
HAM10000

[

Camelyon17

GF3300

[
17

Datasets for VQA tasks.
For VQA tasks, we evaluate models’ performances on four biomedical VQA datasets, including over 15k images with 63k QA pairs. The questions are categorized into two types: open-ended questions with short phrase answers and closed-ended questions (
i.e.
, yes/no types). Among all datasets,
VQA-RAD

[

and SLAKE
[

are two radiology datasets which cover rich imaging modalities and diverse human body parts.
PathVQA

[

FairVLMed

[
16
]
dataset contains 10k SLO fundus images, each paired with clinical notes e for diagnosing glaucoma. To utilize it for VQA evaluation, we follow
[

3.2
Evaluation Methods

Diagnosis.
We use Area Under the Curve (AUC) as the sole metric due to its widespread use in evaluating classification models, particularly in medical diagnosis under class imbalance. It measures model discrimination by representing the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve.

VQA.
We employ two distinct evaluation metrics for closed-ended and open-ended questions. For closed-ended questions, accuracy is reported. For open-ended questions, traditional exact match-based metrics such as accuracy and BLEU are sensitive to language patterns, making them ineffective for evaluating synonyms.
Consequently, semantically correct answers generated by models not fine-tuned on the current dataset may receive disproportionately low scores in token similarity-based comparisons due to variations in language usage and conventions.
To this end, we assess the correctness of predictions using LLMs, enabling semantic-level comparisons that are agnostic to language patterns.
Following
[
15
]
, we utilize GPT-4 to quantitatively evaluate the quality of the generated responses. It assesses the helpfulness, relevance, accuracy, and level of detail of the responses, and provides an overall score on a scale from 1 to 100.

5
Conclusion

This study systematically evaluates the effectiveness of common and medical VLMs across key medical imaging tasks, including disease diagnosis and VQA. Our findings suggest that common VLMs can achieve comparable or superior performance with lightweight fine-tuning, saving the computational cost of training medical-specific VLMs on vast amounts data. While medical VLMs have an advantage in ID tasks, common VLMs adapt surprisingly well to achieve comparable performance, particularly when fine-tuned using LoRA. In OOD settinsg, common VLMs also present strong adaptability to OOD modalities in many tasks. These results suggest that rather than investing in costly large-scale medical-specific VLMs, adapting common VLMs through efficient fine-tuning presents a cost-effective alternative. Future work will explore more fine-tuning strategies and benchmark additional medical image datasets and tasks.
