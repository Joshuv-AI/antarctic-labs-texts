RhoA in postnatal spinal motoneuron is essential for peripheral myelination
Authors: Ye He, Shuyi Xu, Ying Zou, Mengyao Zhao, Mi Li, Jiale Cai, Xinrui Ma, Anbo Zhang, Xianghai Wang, Jiasong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral myelination requires precise axon-glia communication, yet the neuronal intrinsic machinery that governs the release of axonal signals remains incompletely understood. Here, we discover that RhoA, a classic cytoskeletal regulator, is highly expressed in postnatal spinal motoneurons and unexpectedly governs this axon-glia communication. RhoA conditional knockout in postnatal motoneurons causes profound peripheral hypomyelination without affecting neuronal survival, dendrites, or axonal caliber. Mechanistically, RhoA deficiency in postnatal spinal motoneurons attenuates ROCK2/p-Erk/SP1/BACE1 signaling and NRG1-Ⅲ secretion, then disrupts Schwann cells differentiation, lipid biosynthesis, and myelin formation. Together, this study reveals a novel, non-cell-autonomous role for RhoA and provides further insights into the complexity of neuronal control over peripheral myelination.

Introduction

The small GTPase RhoA, a master regulator of the cytoskeleton, has been extensively studied in neuroscience1,2. RhoA is widely regarded as an inhibitor of neuronal survival and axonal regeneration in the injured adult nervous system3,4. Our recent work demonstrated that RhoA conditional knockout in motoneurons alleviates dendrite degeneration and promotes dendrite regeneration after brachial plexus injury in adult mice5. In contrast to its negative roles in neuronal regeneration, RhoA plays indispensable roles during embryonic neuronal development with remarkable cell-type and context-dependent heterogeneity, influencing diverse processes from neuronal migration to axon guidance6–9. In the peripheral nervous system, significant research has defined RhoA functions in glial cells and nerve repair. These collective findings have been thoroughly reviewed, with a particular focus on Schwann cell‑intrinsic RhoA in myelination10 and on Rho GTPases in neuronal development11. Nevertheless, the expression and function of RhoA in spinal motoneurons during postnatal maturation remain completely undefined.

The postnatal maturation of spinal motoneurons is a multidimensional process involving dendritic arbor refinement, axonal elongation, and a pivotal later event: the myelination of their peripheral axons, which is essential for rapid saltatory conduction. Peripheral myelination is primarily governed by Schwann cell-intrinsic signaling programs that drive their differentiation and myelination12. However, these intrinsic pathways are instructed by extrinsic cues from the axon, establishing a critical neuron–glia dialog13. Among the axonal signals, Neuregulin1 type Ⅲ (NRG1-Ⅲ) is the most pivotal and well-characterized factor13,14. The axon NRG1-Ⅲ affects the Schwann cells ErbB receptor to initiate and control the Schwann cells differentiation and myelination15–17. While the importance of NRG1-Ⅲ is clear, the neuronal-specific molecular mechanisms governing its secretion remain elusive. Thus, a critical question is whether and how fundamental neuronal signaling molecules such as neuronal RhoA participate in this essential transcellular control mechanism.

Here, we discover that RhoA is highly expressed in spinal motoneurons during the critical postnatal period of peripheral myelination. Using a line of inducible conditional knockout mice, we unexpectedly find that depleting RhoA in postnatal spinal motoneurons does not affect neuronal survival, axonal caliber, or dendritic arborization. Instead, the deficit is specific to their axonal myelination. RhoA loss in neurons impairs ROCK2/p-Erk/SP1/BACE1 signaling and NRG1-Ⅲ secretion, which in turn disrupts Schwann cell differentiation and myelin formation via the ErbB2/3 signaling pathway. These findings provide the first view that RhoA controls myelination by modulating axon-glia communication. This reveals a novel, non-cell autonomous function for RhoA and significantly expands its known roles beyond cytoskeletal regulation, indicating an extended therapeutic avenue for treating dysmyelinating neuropathies.

Results

RhoA expression in spinal motoneurons persists at elevated levels in juvenile mice

To map the temporal expression profile of RhoA in the postnatal spinal cord, immunofluorescence co-staining was executed in the lumbar spinal cord across postnatal day 1 to day 56 (P1, P3, P7, P14, P21, P56) of wild-type mice. Following identification of motoneurons with ChAT immunostaining (Fig. 1A), sustained RhoA expression was observed in motoneurons at all time points, notably higher than in surrounding cells (Fig. 1B, C). The RhoA expression levels gradually increase, peaking around P14–P21, and slightly decrease by P56. Therefore, the Rhoafl/fl mice were crossed with Chat-CreERT2 mice to generate motoneuron-specific inducible conditional knockout (icKO) animals (Fig. 2A). Newborn mice received subcutaneous tamoxifen injections for three consecutive days. Genotyping confirmed the genotype, and Western blotting of the lumbar spinal cord validates efficient RhoA decrease in icKO mice (Fig. 2B, C). RhoA deficiency in spinal motoneurons disrupts Schwann cell differentiation

To determine whether motoneuronal RhoA deletion affects Schwann cell differentiation—a prerequisite for proper myelination12, the L5 ventral roots of icKO mice at P7 were immunostained with antibodies of Oct6 (a marker of promyelinating Schwann cells19) and Sox10 (indicating total Schwann cells in the nerve20). The number of Oct6-positive cells is increased, whereas the number of Sox10-positive cells remains unchanged in the icKO mice (Fig. 4A–C). To assess whether motoneurons influence Schwann cells' biology via secreted factors, a co-culture system was established that prevented direct contact but permitted molecular exchange between motoneurons and Schwann cells (Fig. 4G), after the primary spinal motoneurons and Schwann cells were isolated from neonatal mice and identified by immunostaining for p75NTR/NeuN and S100, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4A–D). The expression of RhoA in icKO motoneurons was significantly depressed after 4-Hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) treatment (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). After 3 days of co-culture, immunostaining revealed a significantly lower MAG (a marker of differentiated Schwann cells23) expression and higher GFAP (a marker of non-myelinating Schwann cells24) expression in Schwann cells when cultured with RhoA-knockout motoneurons (Fig. 4H–J). These results indicate that motoneuronal RhoA loss impairs Schwann cell differentiation through a juxtacrine mechanism.

Since protease BACE1 is required for NRG1-Ⅲ processing and has been reported to be regulated by ROCK (a downstream molecule of RhoA) in cortical neurons of Alzheimer’s disease models25,26, we evaluated whether RhoA regulates BACE1 in the spinal motoneurons. Western blotting indicates that both the spinal cord and sciatic nerve of icKO mice have significantly reduced BACE1 levels (Fig. 5E, G). Immunofluorescence confirms decreased BACE1 intensity in spinal motoneurons in icKO mice (Fig. 5F, H). Similarly, RhoA knockout or pharmacological inhibition (using cell-permeable Rho inhibitor CT04) in primary motoneurons also suppresses BACE1 expression (Fig. 5I–N).

RhoA expression in spinal motoneurons is required for lipid biosynthesis in peripheral nerves

As myelin is highly enriched in lipids27, and our previous transcriptomic analysis shows significant dysregulation of the PPAR pathway (Fig.5A), a central coordinator of lipid synthesis, we executed further bioinformatics analysis on our Bulk mRNA sequence data focused on genes encoding enzymes responsible for producing myelin components. Notably, pathways for the biosynthesis of major myelin lipids, including cholesterol, are strongly inhibited (Fig. 6A). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) for cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism of icKO mice shows a downward trend (Fig. 6B). Consistent with this, Western blotting shows significantly reduced levels of cholesterol-related enzymes, HMGCS1, MVK, IDI1, and LSS in the sciatic nerve lysate from icKO mice (Fig. 6C, D). Supporting the lipid biosynthesis defects, fluorescence intensity quantification of FluoroMyelin-staining in the L5 ventral roots reveals a substantial decrease in myelin lipid content in icKO mice (Fig. 6E, F). RhoA regulates BACE1 via the ROCK2/p-Erk/SP1 pathway in spinal motoneurons

While ROCK signaling has been implicated in BACE1 regulation in pathological models such as Alzheimer’s disease26,28, its role in physiological contexts, particularly in spinal motoneurons, remained unclear. This raised the fundamental question: Does RhoA signal through ROCK2 to control BACE1 expression in motoneurons? We first examined the expression of ROCK2, the predominant ROCK isoform in the nervous system29. Western blotting analysis reveals that ROCK2 expression is significantly decreased in both spinal cord and sciatic nerve tissues following motoneuron-specific RhoA knockout (Fig. 7A, B). Immunofluorescence staining further confirms the reduced ROCK2 intensity in motoneurons in the spinal cord and axons in the ventral roots (Fig. 7C–E). Previous work in cortical neurons from Alzheimer’s disease models established that a specific RhoA-GAP (ArhGAP11A) negatively modulates BACE1 expression through ROCK/p-Erk/SP1 signaling30. Intriguingly, our results demonstrate that protein levels of ROCK2, p-Erk, and SP1are all significantly decreased in RhoA-depleted motoneurons and RhoA inhibition (Fig. 7H, J–L). Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of ROCK2 using Y27632 also significantly suppressed the expression levels of p-Erk, SP1, and BACE1 in wild-type primary motoneurons (Fig. 7I, M–O). The relationship among p-Erk, SP1, and BACE1 was then further confirmed with inhibited Erk activity (Fig. 7I, M–O). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RhoA positively regulates BACE1 expression cell-autonomously in spinal motoneurons through a ROCK2-dependent signaling cascade involving p-Erk and SP1(Fig. 7P).

Discussion

The small GTPase RhoA is well established as a key inhibitor of axonal regeneration and neuronal survival following injury in the adult nervous system. In contrast, during embryonic development, RhoA is highly expressed and plays indispensable roles in neural circuit formation, including neurite outgrowth7,31 and neuronal differentiation32. A growing body of evidence from our team and others reveals RhoA is not only activated, but its expression is also significantly upregulated following neural injury33,34. Moreover, RhoA’s functions are far more complex, exhibiting remarkable cell-type and context-dependent heterogeneity. For instance, in the spinal cord injury model, conditional knockout of RhoA in neurons promotes axonal regeneration, whereas its deletion in astrocytes paradoxically suppresses repair4. Similarly, our group has reported that RhoA-specific ablation in Schwann cells enhances peripheral nerve regeneration33, while macrophage-specific RhoA deletion inhibits the regenerative process35. Our recent studies also proved RhoA’s functional repertoire extends well beyond the canonical regulation of the actin cytoskeleton36. For instance, in both neurons and Schwann cells, RhoA can regulate microtubule dynamics. In neurons, it achieves this by modulating the expression of microtubule-severing proteins spastin and p60-katanin31, while in Schwann cells, it orchestrates microtubule dynamics via a YAP1/TEAD3/CDK2/ASPM/p60-Katanin axis36. Furthermore, in microglia, RhoA governs post-injury repair by regulating glycolytic energy metabolism through the Arhgap25/HIF-1α pathway34. Importantly, these diverse functions frequently operate independently of their classic downstream effector, ROCK. In Schwann cells, RhoA influences proliferation via the AKT pathway31 and differentiation via the JNK signaling37. This collective evidence underscores that RhoA’s role is not monolithic but is critically determined by cellular context, developmental stage, and pathological state.

This cell-type-specific duality is also observed during neuronal development. For example, embryonic RhoA knockout promotes the maturation and proliferation of certain neuronal populations in the cerebral cortex9,38, midbrain39, and forebrain neural progenitors39, while disrupting the neural proliferation of spinal neuroepithelial cells40. During late stages of rat hippocampal neuron development, forced RhoA activation induces dendritic retraction41, whereas glutamate-mediated RhoA elevation promotes elongation without changing branch number18. Similarly, in spinal motoneurons, RhoA’s role varies with developmental stage: its deletion in neural epithelial cells reduces the number of motoneurons generated42,43, whereas knockout in post-mitotic, immature motoneurons impairs neurite outgrowth without affecting their survival43.

Motoneurons undergo a critical postnatal maturation phase, involving dendritic branching to receive cortical inputs and axonal elongation with myelination for efficient signal transmission to muscles44,45. However, the expression dynamics and physiological functions of RhoA during this pivotal period remain entirely unknown. Our study addresses this gap by identifying a peak in RhoA expression in spinal motoneurons during the critical postnatal period of active myelination (P14–P21), which suggests that RhoA might support developmental processes beyond its established roles in embryonic development or its role in adult nerve regeneration. Using an inducible conditional knockout strategy, we specifically deleted RhoA in postnatal motoneurons, thereby distinguishing its postnatal functions from potential embryonic requirements. Notably, this temporal and cell-type-specific approach indicates that the hypomyelination of motoneuron RhoA loss is entirely distinct from that described in previous studies focusing on the role of RhoA in Schwann cell myelination. This phenotype is specifically attributable to neuron–axon communication rather than intrinsic defects in Schwann cells or general axonal integrity.

NRG1‑Ⅲ is essential for Schwann cell myelination, influencing Schwann cell differentiation, radial sorting, and myelin thickness14,46,47. It is proteolytically processed by sheddases to generate the mature NRG1‑Ⅲ that exerts its functions. Among these sheddases, ADAM17 acts as a negative regulator, whereas BACE1 and ADAM10 cooperatively promote myelin thickening. However, how neurons influence the regulation of BACE1 in peripheral myelination remains unclear. Contrary to reports where RhoA/ROCK signaling can suppress Erk activity30,48, the present findings demonstrate that RhoA in spinal motoneuron activates a ROCK2/p-Erk/SP1 cascade to promote BACE1 expression, thereby modulating peripheral myelination via regulating Schwann cells differentiation and lipid biosynthesis (Fig. 8). This positive regulatory relationship contrasts with the mechanism described in Alzheimer’s disease models, where ArhGAP11A-mediated RhoA activation reduces BACE1 expression through ROCK/p-Erk signaling30. These findings fundamentally redefine the physiological role of RhoA in postnatal motoneurons, revealing a previously unknown, non-cell autonomous function in orchestrating peripheral nerve myelination. Furthermore, our research identifies that modulating neuronal RhoA signaling could represent a novel strategy for treating dysmyelinating neuropathies49. Interestingly, our previous studies also identified that RhoA is highly expressed in myelinating Schwann cells and that Schwann cell-specific RhoA deletion impairs Schwann cell differentiation and myelination33. These findings suggest that enhancing RhoA signaling in the postnatal stage can promote myelination by improving Schwann cells' intrinsic program and axon-glia communication. Targeting this pathway may offer benefits for the hypomyelination-related peripheral neuropathies or impaired nerve repair.

While our study establishes a novel RhoA-dependent pathway in myelination, several questions remain. First, we cannot exclude the involvement of additional RhoA effectors or secreted factors that might contribute to the phenotype. Second, the precise mechanism linking ROCK2 activation to NRG1-Ⅲ secretion in motoneurons requires further elucidation. Third, the translational potential of our findings to human disorders needs careful evaluation. Future studies should explore whether targeting RhoA activation in the postnatal stage can be applied to treat the hypomyelination-related peripheral neuropathies. Additionally, investigating whether similar mechanisms operate in central nervous system myelination would broaden the significance of our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, our study fundamentally redefines the physiological function of RhoA in postnatal motoneurons, revealing a previously unknown role in orchestrating peripheral nerve myelination through regulation of axon-glia communication. This work not only provides mechanistic insights into how neurons intrinsically control their myelination but also identifies potential therapeutic targets for disorders of myelination.

Methods

Animals

All animal procedures were carried out with the approval of the Southern Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee (approval No. SMU-L2018158) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal ethics and welfare. Mice were housed in a controlled environment under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. Wild-type C57BL/6J mice at various postnatal ages (P1, P3, P7, P14, P21, P56) were supplied by the Animal Center of Southern Medical University (license No. SCXK (Yue) 2016-0041).

The Rhoafl/fl and Chat-CreERT2 mouse lines were constructed by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China). For inducible deletion, tamoxifen-inducible motoneuron-specific RhoA conditional knockout mice (icKO) were produced by crossing Rhoafl/fl with Chat-CreERT2 mice. Intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen was used to induce RhoA conditional knockout in newborn mice for 3 consecutive days50. Genotyping of icKO offspring was performed by PCR using primers listed in the Supplementary Information. The day of birth was recorded as postnatal day 0, and the specific developmental stages examined are indicated in the corresponding figures. Both male and female mice in the same litter were randomly used for all experiments. All measures were taken to minimize pain and distress. No adverse events were observed in any experimental group. The sample size was chosen based on the minimal number of animals required to obtain statistically interpretable results while adhering to the 3R principle of reduction. Once mice exhibit body weight loss >20% of initial weight, or inability to reach food or water, or persistent hunched posture with ruffled fur for >24 h, they will be immediately euthanized by CO₂ inhalation. No animal reached a humane endpoint before the scheduled end of the study.

Primary spinal motoneuron culture

Motoneurons were isolated from the lumbar spinal cords of embryonic day 13–15 mice. Briefly, ventral horn tissues were collected in cold HBSS and digested with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA for 20 min at 37 °C. Digestion was terminated by adding fetal bovine serum (FBS), and tissues were gently dissociated by trituration and filtered to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cells were cultured in neurobasal medium with 10% FBS, L-Glutamine, B27 supplement, and 10 ng/mL BDNF for 12 h. Then, the cultures were treated with 10 μM cytosine arabinoside (Ara-C) for 48 h to eliminate fibroblasts and glial cells, and treated with 2 μM 4-Hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) for 3 days to induce the conditional knockout in the subjected cells. To investigate the effects of the RhoA signaling pathway, primary motoneurons were also treated with 2 μg/mL CT04 (RhoA inhibitor), 10 μM Y27632 (ROCK2 inhibitor), and 200nM SCH772984 (Erk1/2 inhibitor) for 24 h before evaluation.

Primary Schwann cell culture

Schwann cell primary cultures were established as previously described33,36. In brief, spinal and sciatic nerves were dissected from postnatal day 5–6 wild-type mice and digested with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA at 37 °C for 30 min to generate a single-cell suspension. Cells were plated onto poly-L-lysine-coated dishes and maintained in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS for 24 h, then 10 μM Ara-C was added for further 48 h to suppress fibroblast growth. Following this purification step, cultures were expanded in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS, 3 μM forskolin, 10 ng/mL heregulin-β1, 4 mM L-Glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin.

Motoneuron-Schwann cell coculture

Following the established protocol51, spinal motoneurons were cocultured with Schwann cells using 6.5 mm transwell chambers (8 µm pores). Before the co-culture, the motoneurons were purified and treated with 4-OHT in the upper chamber, and the purified Schwann cells were seeded in the lower chamber and treated with non-proliferative medium (DMEM/F12 containing 3% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin) for 24 h. During the co-culture, Schwann cells were cultured with differentiated medium (DMEM/F12 containing 3% FBS, 1 mM dbcAMP, 10 ng/mL heregulin-β1). Three days later, the Schwann cells were fixed for immunofluorescent staining with MAG and GFAP.

Western blotting

Protein extracts from tissues or cultured cells were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors36. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min, and the extracts were then separated on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature (RT) for 1 h, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized with the ECL substrate kit. Quantification was performed in ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), with protein levels normalized to α-tubulin or β-actin. The antibodies used are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Golgi–Cox staining and Sholl analysis

To evaluate the impact of motoneuron-specific RhoA deletion on dendritic development, we performed Golgi–Cox staining on spinal cord tissues using the Hito Golgi–Cox OptimStain™ Kit as in our previous article5. Briefly, freshly dissected lumbar spinal cords were immersed in a 1:1 mixture of Solutions #1 and #2 for 2 weeks in the dark, followed by incubation in Solution #3 at 4 °C for 72 h. Tissues were then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and 90-μm sections were prepared using a freezing microtome (Leica) at −25 °C. After sequential rinsing in Solutions #3–5, dehydration in ethanol, and clearing in xylene, sections were mounted with neutral balsam.

For analysis, images of neurons in the anterolateral horn were acquired using a ×20 objective on a light microscope (Leica). Four mice per group were assessed, with five discontinuous randomly selected sections analyzed per mouse. One to two structurally intact neurons per section were selected, and their soma and dendrites were traced after background removal using Photoshop. Sholl analysis was conducted in ImageJ to quantify dendritic complexity, including parameters such as longest dendrite length, total dendritic length, dendritic span, and numbers of primary and secondary branches.

Immunohistochemistry and FluoroMyelin staining

For Immunofluorescence staining, neural tissue was collected after 4% PFA perfusion of the mice, and 10 μm sections were prepared using a freezing microtome (Leica). Cells were seeded on cell-climbing slices pretreated with 0.1 mg/mL Poly-L-lysine hydrobromide solution before being fixed in 4% PFA. 0.5% Triton X-100 was treated for permeabilization for 30 min, followed by blocking in 5% gelatin (0.5% Triton X-100) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies were then applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or 568 for 2 h at RT. After washing three times in PBS, samples were mounted subsequently in Antifade Mounting Medium (with DAPI).

For FluoroMyelin staining, L5 ventral roots frozen sections were washed three times in PBS, and FluoroMyelin solution was applied23. After incubation for 1 h at RT in the dark, sections were mounted in Antifade Mounting Medium (with DAPI) before being washed three times in PBS.

Transmission electron microscopy

The L5 ventral roots of icKO and Rhoafl/fl mice were processed for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization. Animals were perfused with a fixative solution containing 4% PFA and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer. L5 ventral roots were isolated, fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in anhydrous acetone/embedding solution as described previously. The samples were then sectioned into 70-nm slices and dyed with uranium and plumbum. Images were randomly captured using a transmission electron microscope (H-7500; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Ten random fields in each sample were captured and analyzed. Axon area, axon diameter, myelin thickness, and g-ratio (the ratio of the inner to the outer diameter of the myelin sheath of a myelinated axon) were calculated using MyelTracer software with manual correction.

ELISA assay

An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was conducted to quantify the levels of NRG1 EGF fragment concentration. The primary motoneurons were incubated in culture medium at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 48 h, the supernatant was harvested, and the concentration of NRG1 EGF was measured using a mouse-specific NRG1 beta 1 ELISA kit.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA from the sciatic nerve was extracted using the Universal RNA Extraction CZ Kit (RNC643, ONREW). RNA quantity was analyzed using Qubit 4.0 (Invitrogen) and quality examined by electrophoresis on a denaturing agarose gel. Only samples with an RNA concentration ≥10 ng/μL, and a total amount ≥500 ng were selected for library preparation. Libraries were prepared using the VVAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NR605-0, Vazyme), followed by sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with the 150 paired-end sequencing strategy. The raw data were handled by Skewer v0.2.2 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/skewer/files/?source=navbar), and data quality was checked by FastQC v0.11.2 (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The read length was 2 × 150 bp. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse genome (GRCm39) from Ensembl using STAR, with one mismatch allowed. Differential gene expression was analyzed by DESeq2 (v1.16.1) (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html). Then DEGs (with a threshold for P < 0.05) were chosen for function and signaling pathway enrichment analysis using the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html). The significantly enriched pathways were determined when P < 0.05, FDR < 0.25, and sorted in descending order on Log10-pvalue. For Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), differentially expressed genes were analyzed using the Python-based GSEApy with the Enrich tool. Enrichment of mRNA, library construction, sequencing, and data analysis were performed by Shanghai Xu Ran Biotechnology Co., Ltd (http://www.xurangene.com).

Statistics and reproducibility

All immunofluorescence images are acquired using an upright microscope (Leica, DM40008, Germany). For tissue and cell‑level quantification, at least 3 random fields were acquired per section. For quantitative comparisons of relative fluorescence intensity, the magnification, laser power, gain, and scanning speed were kept identical across all image acquisitions. The fluorescence intensity was obtained by calculating the mean intensity according to the related threshold and subtracting background fluorescence from a nearby cell-free area. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 as described in the figure legends. No specific inclusion or exclusion criteria for animals were established a priori. All animals that survived the experimental period were included in the analysis. All data points were obtained and analyzed under double-blind conditions, and none of them were excluded. All data have been verified for equality of variance before performing parametric tests. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was used when the assumption of homogeneity of variances was violated, while the unpaired Student’s t test was applied when variances were equal. Other data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA test. Error bars in all figures represent mean ± SEM. A threshold of *P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Animal and cell plate icons used in the schematic figures are derived from the Generic Diagramming Platform52 and were composed and designed using Adobe Illustrator 2026.
