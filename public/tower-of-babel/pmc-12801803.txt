Determinants of proclivity for anaesthesia as a career among medical students in a Southwestern Nigeria tertiary institution: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Boluwatife Adefunke Bolatito, Christabel Ijeoma Uche-Orji, Abigail Olawumi Oyedokun, Save Harmony Chufor, Eniola Oreoluwa Olatunde, Adeniyi Abraham Adesola, Olusola Idowu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNigeria faces a critical shortage of anaesthetists (0.113 per 100,000) exacerbated by consistently low uptake among medical graduates. This study investigated factors influencing medical students’ interest in anaesthesia as a career choice.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted among 147 final-year and penultimate medical students at University College Hospital, Ibadan, using a structured self-administered online questionnaire. Data were collected in March 2025 and analyzed using SPSS version 27 employing Chi-square tests and binary logistic regression.ResultsAmong students considering specialization, 29.2% expressed interest in anaesthesia and 9.2% ranked it first choice. Among respondents interested in anaesthesia, 28.9% developed interest during clinical exposure, while 65.8% did so after their posting. Key motivators included perceived lucrativeness (92.1%), personal interest (76.3%), and positive rotation experiences (73.7%), while deterrents were low personal interest (90.3%), lack of role models (37.1%), and experience during clinical rotation in the department (25.4%). While most understood the anesthetist’s role, 58.5% planned to practice abroad. Logistic regression found no significant sociodemographic or academic predictors.ConclusionInterest in anaesthesia among Nigerian medical students remains low but higher than previously reported in other regions. Clinical exposure during rotations plays a pivotal role in shaping career preferences, suggesting a need for enhanced undergraduate training programs and improved mentorship to address the shortage of anaesthetists in Nigeria’s healthcare system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08408-9.Keywords: Anaesthesia, Residency training, Specialty preference, Medical students, Ibadan

Introduction

The selection of a medical specialty represents a pivotal decision in a physician’s career trajectory, with far-reaching implications for both individual practitioners and healthcare systems [1]. This choice affects not only personal career satisfaction and earning potential but also shapes the distribution of the physician workforce across specialties [2]. In Nigeria, where the physician-to-population ratio stands at 4 doctors per 10,000 people, significantly lower than developed nations like the United Kingdom with 30 doctors per 10,000, the specialty choices of medical graduates carry particular significance for healthcare delivery [3].

Among medical specialties, anaesthesia faces unique challenges in attracting new practitioners despite its crucial role in healthcare delivery [4, 5]. Research indicates consistently low selection rates for anaesthesia across various countries: 1% in Israel, 1.4% in Ghana, and 12% in the USA, with Nigeria showing particularly concerning statistics [5, 6]. A recent study at the University College Hospital (UCH), Ibadan, revealed that only 3% of final-year medical students ranked anaesthesia as their first choice [7]. This low preference for anaesthesia has resulted in a critical shortage of anaesthetists in Nigeria, with just 0.113 anaesthetists per 100,000 population [8].

The unpopularity of anaesthesia as a career choice presents a significant challenge for healthcare delivery in Nigeria. While medical students often form strong preferences for certain specialties during their undergraduate years, anaesthesia frequently serves as a second or third choice for postgraduate trainees who fail to secure positions in their preferred specialties [9]. This pattern undermines the development of a robust anaesthesia workforce, essential for surgical care and critical medicine.

While previous studies have examined medical specialty choices broadly, there remains a crucial gap in understanding the specific factors influencing anaesthesia choices within the Nigerian context, particularly in Ibadan. This study aimed to assess the percentage of final year and penultimate medical students at the University College Hospital (UCH), Ibadan considering anaesthesia as a future specialty and the factors affecting this choice investigate these factors. This was done through a cross-sectional survey. The research is particularly timely given the recent wave of healthcare professional emigration from Nigeria, which may further impact specialty workforce distribution. By examining their choice for anaesthesia in 3 levels, i.e. as first, second and third choice, and exploring students’ intentions regarding practice location, this study will provide valuable insights for addressing the anaesthesia workforce crisis and inform strategies to build a more balanced medical workforce in Nigeria.

Methods

Study design and settings

The study was conducted among the penultimate and final-year medical students of the College of Medicine, University of Ibadan. This is Nigeria’s oldest medical school, founded in 1948, and consistently ranks as a top medical institution in the country, known for training a significant portion of Nigerian doctors. It has trained a substantial number of health personnel, with one in five Nigerian doctors being graduates of the college. A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted using a structured self-administered online questionnaire.

Study population and sampling technique

Quota sampling technique was employed to ensure adequate representation of both penultimate and final-year medical students. The sample was allocated to each class based on their relative sizes, with a slightly larger quota for the final-year class, as they had completed their anaesthesia postings and had more exposure. Respondents were then recruited on a non-random basis until the required numbers were reached. Only penultimate and final year students were sampled because they were deemed to have clearer views on the scope of medicine, having gone through all rotations. The study involved 147 consenting medical students from two consecutive final year classes.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the Leslie Kish formula for cross-sectional studies, with an adjustment made for non-response. A prevalence of 3% was obtained from a similar previous study by Ezegwui et al. [7] The degree of accuracy was set at 0.05 (precision = 5%), and the standard normal deviation at a 95% confidence interval (Z = 1.96). This was computed for each quota. The sample size required was 108 participants. However, a total of 147 responses were received, yielding a response rate of approximately 136% relative to the calculated sample size.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included all consenting final year and penultimate medical students who had rotated through anaesthesia. Exclusion criteria included failure to consent to the study.

Survey development and data collection

Data were collected in March 2025 using a structured, self-administered questionnaire developed by the research team after reviewing existing literature on medical students’ specialty preferences. Some items were adapted from a previously published study7, while other questions were developed de novo. The draft instrument was reviewed for content validity by a consultant anaesthetist at the College of Medicine, University of Ibadan, who provided expert input on the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the questions. Although the survey was not pilot-tested prior to data collection, the structured design and expert scrutiny ensured face and content validity.

The questionnaire comprised three sections. Section A explored the sociodemographic characteristics of respondents and their self-reported scores from the end-of-posting tests. Section B assessed respondents’ choice of specialty, while Section C explored the different factors that informed their decision to choose certain specialties. Respondents were required to rank anaesthesia in order of preference and indicate which of the listed factors influenced their choices (Appendix 1). To minimize response bias, the questionnaire was self-administered anonymously via an online form, reducing the likelihood of socially desirable responses. The questions were structured in clear and neutral language to avoid leading respondents. To address potential non-response bias, reminders were sent to both classes through their official WhatsApp groups and class representatives during the data collection period.

Data management and analysis

Data was entered into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. The information obtained was kept confidential, and participants were not required to input their names so as to maintain confidentiality. There was also no use of personal identifiers on the questionnaires. The questionnaire was adapted from already published studies [10, 11] and was piloted before actual data was collected. Data cleaning was done, and descriptive statistics were carried out on each questionnaire item. Chi-square tests of independence were used to test for association between the choice of specialty and sociodemographic characteristics and academic performance. Multivariate analysis was also carried out. We trichotomized “interest in anaesthesia” into “Yes, No, Undecided” because it is in line with our research objective, our small sample size and it helps us to communicate to readers clearly and concisely. A statistically significant level of 0.05 was used for the analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the institution’s ethics review board (UI/EC/24/01016). The principles of ethical conduct of research employed were consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained electronically; respondents gave consent by clicking a banner at the start of the anonymous questionnaire before they proceeded.

Reasons for interest vs disinterest in anaesthesia

Among students not interested in anaesthesia (n = 62), the top 3 reasons were low personal interest (90.3%), lack of role models (37.1%) and experience during clinical rotation in the department (24.2%). 17.7% of the students also highlighted inspiration and guidance from God as well as long/unpredictable work hours as their reason for disinterest. The specialty was also perceived by some as lacking prestige or being difficult to study.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study found that 29.2% of medical students expressed interest in anaesthesia as a specialty, with only 9.2% selecting it as their first choice. Clinical exposure during the anaesthesia posting was the strongest influence on career interest, with 65.7% of decisions made after clerkship. Motivating factors included perceived lucrativeness, positive clinical experiences, and personal interest, while deterrents were mainly low personal interest, lack of role models, and negative clinical rotation experiences. Importantly, less than half of students interested in anaesthesia planned to practice in Nigeria, highlighting a concerning workforce implication. Sociodemographic and academic characteristics did not significantly predict interest in anaesthesia. However, rotation through anaesthesia posting was a significant predictor of interest.

Contextualising the study

Our findings contrast with reports from other Nigerian regions where interest in anaesthesia was markedly lower. For instance, only 3% of final-year students in Calabar [5], 2% in Enugu [12], and 0.2% in southeastern schools [13] considered anaesthesia as a future specialty. The relatively higher interest observed in this study may reflect more recent trends, as these earlier studies were conducted nearly a decade ago. Nevertheless, national studies continue to report low interest, with only 3.6%–3.55% of students choosing anaesthesia as their first-choice specialty [14, 15]. Similar low figures have been recorded in Ghana (1.4%) and Rwanda (6%) [10, 11]. The pattern of anaesthesia being selected more often as a second or third choice, also noted in our study, has been previously documented in Nigerian nationwide surveys [14].

Influence of clinical exposure, motivators and deterrents

A key finding was the strong influence of clinical rotations on students’ interest. About 94.7% made their decisions during or after the anaesthesia posting, underscoring the importance of clerkship experiences. This is consistent with findings from Ghana, where nearly half of students with paediatrics interest attributed it to enjoyable clerkship exposure [10]. At the University of Ibadan, the relatively longer anaesthesia rotation (four weeks across two years) may partly explain the higher level of interest compared with other schools. The importance of mentorship was also evident: the lack of role models emerged as a major deterrent, echoing similar observations in previous studies [5, 11]. The most common motivators were perceived financial rewards, personal interest, and positive clinical experiences, aligning with findings by Chan and Oku [5, 11]. Conversely, personal disinterest, absence of role models, and unsatisfactory clinical postings were key deterrents. Interestingly, Okonkwo et al. identified dependence on surgeons, lack of recognition, and minimal patient contact as stronger deterrents in their cohort [15], while in Ghana, students described anaesthesia as boring or difficult [10]. These differences highlight how contextual and institutional factors shape perceptions of the specialty.

Predictors of interest

Although none of the sociodemographic or academic variables were statistically significant predictors, some trends are noteworthy. Final-year students were more likely to choose anaesthesia than penultimate students, consistent with Okonkwo et al. who reported higher interest following direct specialty exposure [15]. The presence of a medical doctor in the family was associated with lower interest, echoing Okeke et al. who suggested that medical families steer students toward traditionally prestigious fields [4]. Academic performance showed an inverse trend, with higher-scoring students less likely to select anaesthesia, consistent with Alshahrani et al. who found top performers gravitated to competitive specialties such as surgery or radiology [16]. Female students were also more likely to consider anaesthesia, a trend reported by Alatassi [6]. Possible reasons include perceived work-life balance and flexible schedules. Importantly, timing of decision was the only factor that was of statistical significance, with students who made their decision during the anaesthesia posting being about 38 times more likely to choose the specialty compared to before the posting (p = 0.001). This underscores the pivotal role of clerkship exposure in shaping students’ career preferences.

Implications for anaesthesia workforce in Nigeria

The finding that over half of students interested in anaesthesia intend to practice outside Nigeria poses significant concerns for workforce sustainability. This “brain drain” trend could further exacerbate the shortage of anaesthetists, already critically low in Nigeria. Strengthening undergraduate exposure, improving mentorship, and creating incentives for local practice are therefore vital to addressing this crisis.

Strengths

A key strength of this study is that it provides up-to-date evidence on medical students’ interest in anaesthesia, a specialty that has historically been underexplored in Nigeria. Unlike most previous reports, the study specifically examined the timing of decision-making and included a detailed analysis of motivators and deterrents, offering practical insights for workforce planning. The inclusion of both penultimate and final-year students, who have had substantial clinical exposure, also strengthens the validity of the findings.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, which may not adequately represent the broader population and could limit the generalizability of our findings. This constraint also increases the risk of random error and may reduce the ability to detect meaningful patterns within the data. Other limitations are single-institution design which will limit the application of this study to other institutions, potential recall bias especially for the final year classes who had their postings long time ago before this study, social desirability bias in self-reported perceptions, and non-response bias from eligible students who participated. Nevertheless, our findings still provide valuable insights and serve as a useful starting point for future research.

Conclusion

This study has demonstrated a modest level of interest in anaesthesia among medical students, representing an improvement compared with previous studies in Nigeria. Clinical exposure during anesthesia rotations emerged as the most critical factor influencing career choice, with the majority of interested students developing their preference during clinical postings. Key motivators included perceived financial prospects, personal interest, and positive clinical experiences, while lack of role models and poor clinical experiences were primary deterrents.

Recommendations

A concerning trend is that the majority of students interested in anaesthesia have the intention to practice outside Nigeria. This poses a further threat to the current anesthesia workforce crisis. Sociodemographic and academic variables did not significantly predict specialty interest, suggesting that personal factors and clinical exposure were more influential than traditional predictors. Addressing the anaesthesia workforce crisis in Nigeria requires targeted interventions such as strengthening undergraduate clinical rotations in anaesthesia, enhancing mentorship opportunities, and developing incentives to retain graduates within the country. These findings offer practical guidance for medical educators and policymakers committed to building a more sustainable and resilient anaesthesia workforce in Nigeria.
