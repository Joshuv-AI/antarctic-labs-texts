General Duffin–Schaeffer-type counterexamples in diophantine approximation
Sam Chow and Manuel Hauke and Andrew Pollington and Felipe A. Ramírez

Abstract
. Duffin and Schaeffer provided a famous counterexample to show that Khintchine’s theorem fails without monotonicity assumption. Given any monotonically decreasing approximation function with divergent series, we construct Duffin–Schaeffer-type counterexamples by restricting the denominator. We also extend these constructions to the inhomogeneous setting. Our results resolve some natural questions arising from the works of Erdős, Vaaler, and Yu.

1.
Introduction

For
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
, the set of
\psi
-well approximable numbers in
[0,1]
is

W(\psi)=\{{\alpha}\in[0,1]:\exists^{\infty}q\in\mathbb{N}\quad\|q{\alpha}\|<\psi(q)\},

where
\|x\|=\min\{|x-a|:a\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. Khintchine’s theorem
[
3
, Theorem 2.3]
, the cornerstone of metric diophantine approximation, asserts that if
\psi
is non-increasing then

(1.1)

{\lambda}(W(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)<\infty\\
\\
1,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)=\infty.\end{cases}

Here and throughout, we write
{\lambda}
for Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Duffin and Schaeffer
[
5
]
famously constructed a counterexample to show that monotonicity cannot be relaxed in general. In the same article, they formulated the Duffin–Schaeffer conjecture on non-monotonic approximation with reduced fractions. Building upon the important steps made by Erdős
[
6
]
, Vaaler
[
13
]
and Pollington–Vaughan
[
11
]
, the conjecture was ingeniously resolved by Koukoulopoulos and Maynard
[
10
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Koukoulopoulos and Maynard)
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
and

W^{\prime}(\psi)=\{{\alpha}\in[0,1]:\exists^{\infty}(q,a)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\qquad|q{\alpha}-a|<\psi(q),\quad(a,q)=1\}.

Then

(1.2)

{\lambda}(W^{\prime}(\psi))=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(q)\psi(q)}{q}<\infty\\
\\
1,&\text{if }\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi(q)\psi(q)}{q}=\infty.\end{cases}

1.1.
Main results

Our work is principally motivated by the aforementioned results of Erdős and Vaaler. The latter asserts that

\psi(q)\ll 1/q\quad\forall q\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>\text{\eqref{DSconclusion}}.

This raises the question of whether or not

\psi(q)\ll 1/q\quad\forall q\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>\text{\eqref{KhintchineConclusion}}.

We answer this question negatively,
showing
a fortiori
that if
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
is
any
non-increasing function such that

(1.3)

\lim_{q\to\infty}f(q)=0,\qquad\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}f(q)=\infty,

then

\psi(q)\leqslant f(q)\quad\forall q\>\>\not\Longrightarrow\>\>\text{\eqref{KhintchineConclusion}}.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a non-increasing function satisfying (
1.3
). Then there exists
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{q\in S}f(q)=\infty,\qquad{\lambda}(W(f{\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}))=0.

Though it may at this stage be clear to a handful of experts, we wish to point out that our constructions can be modified to obtain Duffin–Schaeffer-type counterexamples that are monotonic on a support that has full density at infinitely many scales. Writing

\bar{\delta}(S)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|S\cap[1,N]|}{N}

for the upper density of
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we establish the following refinement of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a non-increasing function satisfying (
1.3
). Then there exists
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
, non-increasing on its support, such that

(1.4)

\psi\leqslant f,\qquad\sum_{q\in\mathbb{N}}\psi(q)=\infty,\qquad{\lambda}(W(\psi))=0,\qquad\bar{\delta}(\supp\psi)=1.

Remark 1.4
.

Note that, in Theorem
1.3
, it cannot be guaranteed that
\psi
will take the form
{\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}f
for some
S\subset\mathbb{N}
with full upper density. For example, if
f(n)\gg 1/\varphi(n)
, then Theorem
1.1
precludes the existence of such a set
S
. We explain this in §
2.2
.

Note also that “upper density” in Theorem
1.3
cannot be replaced by “lower density”, since the Duffin–Schaeffer theorem
[
5
, Theorem III]
— not to be confused with the Duffin–Schaeffer conjecture — implies that if

\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{|S\cap[1,N]|}{N}>0

then

\sum_{q\in S}\psi(q)=\infty\implies{\lambda}(W(\psi))=0.

1.2.
Inhomogeneous results

There has been considerable interest around inhomogeneous, non-monotonic approximation in recent years
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
12
,
14
]
. For
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
and
{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}
, define

W(\psi,{\gamma})=\{{\alpha}\in[0,1]:\exists^{\infty}q\in\mathbb{N}\quad\|q{\alpha}-{\gamma}\|<\psi(q)\}.

The second motivation for our work comes from the aforementioned article of Yu
[
14
]
. A
Liouville number
is
{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
such that if
w\in\mathbb{R}
then

\|q{\gamma}\|<q^{-w}

holds for infinitely many
q\in\mathbb{N}
. These form a set
\mathcal{L}
. Yu introduced the set
\mathcal{T}
of
tamely Liouville numbers
, which satisfies

\mathbb{R}\setminus(\mathbb{Q}\cup\mathcal{L})\subsetneq\mathcal{T}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q},

and showed in
[
14
, Theorem 1.3]
that

(1.5)

\psi(q)\ll\frac{1}{q(\log\log q)^{2}}\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>\left(\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)=\infty\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>{\lambda}(W(\psi,{\gamma}))=1\>\>\forall{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}\right).

This raises the question of whether the tame Liouville assumption can be replaced by the weaker assumption of irrationality. We answer this question negatively,
showing
a fortiori
that if
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
is a non-increasing function satisfying (
1.3
), then

\psi(q)\leqslant f(q)\quad\forall q\>\>\not\Longrightarrow\>\>\left(\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)=\infty\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>{\lambda}(W(\psi,{\gamma}))=1\>\>\forall{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}\right).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a non-increasing function satisfying
(
1.3
).
Then there exists a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and an uncountable set
\Gamma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
containing
\mathbb{Q}
such that if
{\gamma}\in{\Gamma}
then

(1.6)

\sum_{q\in S}f(q)=\infty,\qquad{\lambda}(W({\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}f,{\gamma}))=0.

Far from being tamely Liouville in the sense of Yu, the set
\Gamma
in Theorem
1.5
consists of real numbers that are “wildly” Liouville in a sense that depends on the function
f
. In fact, to each
f
we are able to associate a notion of wildly Liouville that is sufficient for the existence of a set
S=S_{\gamma}
such that (
1.6
) holds (see Theorem
3.4
). As a consequence, we have the following theorem for functions of the form

(1.7)

f_{k}(q)=\frac{1}{q(\log q)(\log\log q)\cdots\log^{(k)}q},

where
\log^{(k)}
denotes the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
iterate of

x\mapsto\max\{1,\log x\}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
k\geqslant 0
be an integer, and let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. If

\liminf_{q\to\infty}\lVert q\gamma\rVert\exp^{(k+3)}(q^{7000})<\infty,

where
\exp^{(k)}
denotes the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
iterate of
\exp
, then there exists
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{q\in S}f_{k}(q)=\infty,\qquad\lambda(W({\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}f_{k},\gamma))=0.

In particular, Yu’s result cannot be extended to real numbers
\gamma
for which

\liminf_{q\to\infty}\lVert q\gamma\rVert\exp^{(4)}(q^{7000})<\infty.

We remark that there is an enormous gap between this wild Liouville notion and Yu’s tame Liouville notion. While one can most probably sharpen the estimates applied in this article, it does not seem that the gap can be closed simply by optimising the proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Our constructions have the novel feature of monotonicity on the support. In previous Duffin–Schaeffer-type constructions, the approximating function
\psi
has the weaker property that
q\mapsto\psi(q)/q
is monotonic.

We conclude our introductory discussion with an open problem, to prove or disprove the following quantitative refinement of (
1.5
), that

\psi(q)\ll 1/q\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>\left(\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)=\infty\>\>\Longrightarrow\>\>{\lambda}(W(\psi,{\gamma}))=1\>\>\forall{\gamma}\in\mathcal{T}\right).

1.3.
Methods

Let us describe the proof of Theorem
1.2
in coarse terms. First of all, we require that

{\lambda}\left(\bigcup_{q\in S^{\prime}}A_{q}\right)=o\left(\sum_{q\in S^{\prime}}{\lambda}(A_{q})\right),

where
S
is a disjoint union of sets of the form
S^{\prime}
. Secondly, we require the series
\sum_{q\in S}f(q)
to diverge very slowly. To these ends, we take

S^{\prime}=X\cdot Y:=\{xy:x\in X,\>y\in Y\}.

The set
Y
is highly multiplicatively structured. We use some probability theory to show that a generic pair of elements of
Y
has a large common divisor. This enables us to deal with the first requirement. The set
X
is then chosen, consisting of much larger elements, so as to carefully secure the second requirement.

For the inhomogeneous theory, say Theorem
1.5
, our shift
{\gamma}
is very close to some rational number with denominator
b
, in fact a sequence of these. The key extra ingredient, Lemma
3.2
, asserts that if
\|b{\gamma}\|\leqslant b{\varepsilon}
then
A_{q}^{\gamma}({\varepsilon})\subseteq A_{bq}(2b{\varepsilon})
. This essentially reduces the problem to the homogeneous setting.

Notation

For
{\varepsilon}>0,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
q\in\mathbb{N}
, denote

A_{q}^{\gamma}({\varepsilon})=\left\{\alpha\in[0,1]:\lVert q\alpha-\gamma\rVert<{\varepsilon}\right\}.

For
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we denote
A_{q}^{\gamma}(f)=A_{q}^{\gamma}(f(q))
, and when it is clear from the context which function we are working with, we write
A_{q}^{\gamma}
for this set. Thus,

W(f,\gamma)=\limsup_{q\to\infty}A_{q}^{\gamma}(f).

When there is no superscript
\gamma
, the notation should be interpreted with
\gamma=0
.

We write
\mathbb{P}=\{p_{1}<p_{2}<\ldots\}
for the set of all primes. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we abbreviate
\mathbb{Z}_{m}=\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
.

2.
Homogeneous counterexamples

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

2.1.1.
Construction of counterexample blocks

The statement of the Theorem
1.2
will follow straightforwardly from the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be a non-increasing function satisfying (
1.3
).
Then, for any
\varepsilon,M>0
, there exists a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{N}\cap(M,\infty)
such that

(2.1)

\sum_{q\in S}\lambda(A_{q})\in[1,2],\qquad\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}\right)<\varepsilon.

The proof of Proposition

2.1
will be quite lengthy. Assuming it for now to be given, Theorem
1.2
follows from a standard procedure which we include for completeness.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
, assuming Proposition
2.1
.

We construct pairwise disjoint sets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots
recursively using Proposition
2.1
. First, we choose
M=1
and
{\varepsilon}=1/2
to obtain
S_{1}
satisfying (
2.1
) with
S_{1}
in place of
S
. Having constructed
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k-1}
in this way, we choose
M_{k}=\max S_{k-1}
and apply Proposition
2.1
with
{\varepsilon}=2^{-k}
. Finally we set
S=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}S_{k}
. We see from
(
2.1
) that

\sum_{q\in S}\lambda(A_{q})=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{q\in S_{k}}\lambda(A_{q})=\infty.

On the other hand,

\lambda(W{\boldsymbol{1}}_{S})\leqslant\lim_{K\to\infty}\sum_{\ell\geqslant K}\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in S_{\ell}}A_{q}\right)\leqslant\lim_{K\to\infty}\sum_{\ell\geqslant K}2^{-\ell}=0.

∎

We now proceed in stages to prove
Proposition
2.1
.
For
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, let

Y(\mathcal{I})=\left\{\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{2i-a_{i}}:a_{i}\in\{0,1\}\right\},\quad P(\mathcal{I})=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{2i}p_{2i-1},

and define the function

g_{\mathcal{I}}(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in\mathcal{I}\\
p_{2i}\mid z\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{2i}}\right).

With this notation, we make the following observation.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{N}
with
(x,P(\mathcal{I}))=1
, and let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,\infty)
be non-increasing. Then

(2.2)

\lambda\left(\bigcup_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I})}A_{xy}\right)\leqslant 2\sum_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I})}g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)f(xy).

Proof.

Clearly,

\lambda\left(\bigcup_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I})}A_{xy}\right)=\sum_{y\in Y(I)}\lambda\left(A_{xy}\setminus\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{\prime}\in Y(\mathcal{I})\\
y^{\prime}<y\end{subarray}}A_{xy^{\prime}}\right),

and we will bound the right-hand side from above term-wise. Observe that

A_{xy}=\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{xy}}\left[\frac{a}{xy}-\frac{f(xy)}{xy},\frac{a}{xy}+\frac{f(xy)}{xy}\right]\pmod{1}.

Since
f
is non-increasing, so is
f(y)/y
. Thus, if
\frac{a}{xy}=\frac{b}{xy^{\prime}}
for some
b\in\mathbb{Z}
and some
y^{\prime}<y
, then

\left[\frac{a}{xy}-\frac{f(xy)}{xy},\frac{a}{xy}+\frac{f(xy)}{xy}\right]\pmod{1}\subseteq A_{xy^{\prime}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I})}\lambda\left(A_{xy}\setminus\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y^{\prime}\in Y(\mathcal{I})\\
y^{\prime}<y\end{subarray}}A_{xy^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\frac{f(xy)}{xy}\#\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{xy}:\not\exists y^{\prime}\in Y(\mathcal{I}),y^{\prime}<y,b\in\mathbb{Z}:\frac{a}{xy}=\frac{b}{xy^{\prime}}\right\}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=2\frac{f(xy)}{y}\#\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{y}:\not\exists y^{\prime}\in Y(\mathcal{I}),y^{\prime}<y,b\in\mathbb{Z}:\frac{a}{y}=\frac{b}{y^{\prime}}\right\}.

For
y\in Y(\mathcal{I})
, we decompose
y=y_{e}y_{o}
, where

y_{e}=\gcd\left(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{2i},y\right),\quad y_{o}=\gcd\left(\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{2i-1},y\right).

We claim that if
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{y}
contributes to the count in (
2.3
), then
(a,y_{e})=1
. Indeed, suppose
(a,y_{e})>1
. Then there exists
i\in\mathcal{I}
such that
p_{2i}\mid a
and
p_{2i}\mid y
.
Defining the “conjugate”
c_{i}(y)=y\frac{p_{2i-1}}{p_{2i}}\in Y(\mathcal{I})
, we observe that
c_{i}(y)<y
. Since
y\mid p_{2i}c_{i}(y)\mid ac_{i}(y)
, there exists
b\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\frac{a}{y}=\frac{b}{c_{i}(y)}
. This confirms the claim. Therefore

\#\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}_{y}:\not\exists y^{\prime}\in Y(\mathcal{I}),y^{\prime}<y,b\in\mathbb{Z}:\frac{a}{y}=\frac{b}{y^{\prime}}\right\}\leqslant y_{o}\varphi(y_{e})=yg_{\mathcal{I}}(y).

Substituting this into (
2.3
) completes the proof.
∎

2.1.2.
Construction of well-behaving
\mathcal{I}

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\delta>0
and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, let

Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)=\{y\in Y:g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\geqslant\delta\},\quad y_{\min}=\min_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I})}y.

Then for any
\delta>0
, there exists
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

(2.4)

\frac{\#Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)}{\sum\limits_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I})}\frac{y_{\min}}{y}}<\delta.

Proof.

For each
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we define

I_{j}=\left\{i\in\mathbb{N}:p_{2i-1},p_{2i}\in\mathbb{P}\cap[e^{j},e^{j+1}),\>p_{2i}-p_{2i-1}\leqslant 40j\right\},

and for
K\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\mathcal{I}_{K}=\bigcup_{5\leqslant j\leqslant K}I_{j}.

For any fixed
\delta>0
, we will prove that if
K
is sufficiently large then
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{K}
satisfies (
2.4
).

To do so, we start by finding a lower bound for the denominator in (
2.4
). Let us abbreviate
Y=Y(\mathcal{I})
. Using the condition on the gaps, we see that for
K
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{y_{\min}}{y}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(1+\frac{p_{2i-1}}{p_{2i}}\right)=2^{\#\mathcal{I}}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\frac{p_{2i}-p_{2i-1}}{2p_{2i}}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant\#Y\prod_{p\leqslant e^{K+1}}\left(1-\frac{20\log p}{p}\right)\geqslant\#Y\exp\left(-40\sum_{p\leqslant e^{K+1}}\frac{\log p}{p}\right)

(2.5)

\displaystyle\geqslant\#Y\exp(-50K),

where we used Mertens’ first theorem in the last line.

Next, we find an upper bound for
\#Y
.
Denoting

X_{j}(y)=\#\{i\in I_{j}:p_{2i}\mid y\},

we see that for
K
sufficiently large,

g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\leqslant\prod_{j\leqslant K}\left(1-\frac{1}{e^{j+1}}\right)^{X_{j}(y)}\leqslant\exp\left(-\sum_{(\log K)^{2}\leqslant j\leqslant K}\frac{X_{j}(y)}{e^{j+1}}\right).

If
X_{j}(y)\geqslant\frac{e^{j}}{500j}
for all
(\log K)^{2}\leqslant j\leqslant K
, then this gives

g_{\mathcal{I}_{K}}(y)\leqslant K^{-1/2000}<\delta,

provided that
K
is sufficiently large. The upshot is that

(2.6)

Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)\subseteq\left\{y\in Y:\exists j\in[(\log K)^{2},K]:X_{j}(y)\leqslant\frac{e^{j}}{500j}\right\}.

We will now be bound the cardinality of this set, from above.

Choosing the uniform probability measure on
Y
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(y)=\frac{1}{\lvert Y\rvert},y\in Y
, we can interpret
(X_{j})_{j=1}^{k}
as random variables. We can see immediately that for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant K
, we have

X_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\Bin(\#I_{j},\tfrac{1}{2})
where
\Bin(n,p)
denotes the binomial distribution. An application of Hoeffding’s inequality thus shows that

\mathbb{P}\left[X_{j}(y)\leqslant\tfrac{\#I_{j}}{10}\right]=\frac{\#\left\{y\in Y:X_{j}(y)\leqslant\tfrac{\#I_{j}}{10}\right\}}{\#Y}\leqslant\exp\left(-\tfrac{\#I_{j}}{4}\right).

Next, we bound for
\#I_{j}
from below. By the prime number theorem, if
j
sufficiently large then

\#\{p\in[e^{j},e^{j+1}):p\in\mathbb{P}\}\geqslant\frac{e^{j}}{5j}.

This implies that the average prime gap in
[e^{j},e^{j+1})
is at most
10j
, and so the average gap arising between the primes
p_{2i}
in this range is at most
20j
. Thus Markov’s inequality implies that, for sufficiently large
j
,

\#I_{j}\geqslant\frac{e^{j}}{50j}.

For
K
sufficiently large, the union bound now yields

\displaystyle\frac{\#\left\{y\in Y:\exists j\in[(\log K)^{2},K]:X_{j}(y)\leqslant\frac{e^{j}}{500j}\right\}}{\#Y}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{(\log K)^{2}\leqslant j\leqslant K}\mathbb{P}\left[X_{j}(y)\leqslant\frac{\#I_{j}}{10}\right]\leqslant\sum_{(\log K)^{2}\leqslant j\leqslant K}\exp\left(-\frac{\#I_{j}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant(\log K)^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{e^{j}}{200}\right)\ll\exp(-100K).

Combining this with (
2.5
) and (
2.6
) completes the proof.
∎

2.1.3.
Completion of the proof of Proposition
2.1

We assume, as we may, that the constant
{\varepsilon}>0
is sufficiently small. By Lemma
2.3
, there exists
\mathcal{I}
such that (
2.4
) is satisfied, for some
\delta=\delta_{\varepsilon}
to be specified later. Denote

(2.7)

Y=Y(\mathcal{I}),\qquad P=P(\mathcal{I})=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}p_{2i}p_{2i-1},

as well as

y_{\min}=\min Y,\qquad y_{\max}=\max Y.

We construct the set
S
in the following way.

(1)

We choose
x_{\min}^{\prime}\geqslant M
large enough for
f(x_{\min}^{\prime}y_{\min})
to be arbitrary small, even if multiplied by whatever term we will need depending on
Y
. We denote such a quantity by
o_{Y}(1)
.

(2)

Next, we choose
B\in\mathbb{N}
, coprime to
P
, such that
BP+1\geqslant x^{\prime}_{\min}
.
Then we define

x_{\min}=BP+1,

noting that

(2.8)

x_{\min}^{\prime}\leqslant x_{\min}\leqslant 2BP.

(3)

We claim that there exists
x_{\max}\in\mathbb{N}
, depending on the quantities defined above, such that

(2.9)

\sum_{y\in Y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in[x_{\min},x_{\max}]\\
x\equiv 1\pmod{BP}\end{subarray}}f(xy)\in[1,2].

Indeed, let
h(x)=\sum_{y\in Y}f(xy)
. Then

\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}h(x)\geqslant\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}f(y_{\min}x)=\infty

and, since
h
inherits monotonicity from
f
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{N}\\
x\equiv 1\pmod{BP}\end{subarray}}h(x)=\infty.

Since
f(q)\to 0
as
q\to\infty
, we have

h(x)=o_{Y}(1)<1\qquad(x\geqslant x_{\min}).

This confirms that a positive integer
x_{\max}
satisfying (
2.9
) can be found. We may clearly take
x_{\max}\equiv 1\pmod{BP}
.

(4)

We set

X=\{x\in[x_{\min},x_{\max}]:x\equiv 1\pmod{BP}\}

and

S=X\cdot Y:=\{xy:x\in X,\>y\in Y\}.

Having constructed
S
, we apply Lemma
2.2
to obtain

\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}\right)\leqslant\sum_{x\in X}\lambda\left(\bigcup_{y\in Y}A_{xy}\right)\leqslant 2\sum_{x\in X}\sum_{y\in Y}g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)f(xy)=2\sum_{y\in Y}g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)t(y),

where
t(y)=\sum_{x\in X}f(xy)
. Since
f
is non-increasing,

\frac{1}{yBP}\sum_{0\leqslant k\leqslant yBP-1}f(xy+k)\leqslant f(xy)\leqslant\frac{1}{yBP}\sum_{0\leqslant k\leqslant yBP-1}f(xy-k).

Consequently,

\begin{split}t(y)&\leqslant f(x_{\min}y)+\frac{1}{yBP}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{\min}<x\leqslant x_{\max}\\
x\equiv 1\pmod{BP}\end{subarray}}\sum_{0\leqslant k\leqslant yBP-1}f(xy-k)\\
&=f(x_{\min}y)+\frac{1}{yBP}\sum_{z=yx_{\min}+1}^{yx_{\max}}f(z)\end{split}

and

t(y)\geqslant\frac{1}{yBP}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{\min}\leqslant x\leqslant x_{\max}\\
x\equiv 1\pmod{BP}\end{subarray}}\sum_{0\leqslant k\leqslant yBP-1}f(xy-k)=\frac{1}{yBP}\sum_{z=yx_{\min}+1}^{yx_{\max}}f(z).

Thus, writing

F(y)=\frac{1}{BP}\sum_{z=yx_{\min}+1}^{yx_{\max}}f(z),

we conclude that

t(y)=\frac{F(y)}{y}+O(f(x_{\min}y))=\frac{F(y)}{y}+o_{Y}(1).

For
x_{\min}^{\prime}
sufficiently large, it follows that

\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}\right)\leqslant 2\left(\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)}{y}F(y)\right)+\delta.

Since
f\geqslant 0
is non-increasing, so too is
F(y)/y
. Using the notation

Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)=\left\{y\in Y:g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\geqslant\delta\right\}

from Lemma
2.3
, and the fact that
g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)\leqslant 1
, we compute that

\begin{split}\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)}{y}F(y)&=\sum_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)}\frac{g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)}{y}F(y)+\sum_{y\in Y\setminus Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)}\frac{g_{\mathcal{I}}(y)}{y}F(y)\\
&\leqslant\frac{F(y_{\min})}{y_{\min}}\sum_{y\in Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)}1+\delta\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{F(y)}{y}\\
&\leqslant\#Y(\mathcal{I},\delta)\frac{F(y_{\min})}{y_{\min}}+3\delta,\end{split}

where we used (
2.9
) in the last step. This shows that

(2.10)

\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}\right)\leqslant 2\#Y(\mathcal{I},\varepsilon)\frac{F(y_{\min})}{y_{\min}}+7\delta.

Next, we define

G(y)=\frac{1}{BP}\sum_{z=y_{\min}x_{\min}+1}^{yx_{\max}}f(z),

which inherits monotonicity from
f
.
By (
2.8
), if
y\in Y
then

\begin{split}\lvert F(y)-G(y)\rvert&\leqslant\frac{1}{BP}\sum_{z=y_{\min}x_{\min}+1}^{y_{\max}x_{\min}}f(z)\leqslant\frac{1}{BP}y_{\max}x_{\min}f(x_{\min}^{\prime}y_{\min})\\
&\leqslant 2y_{\max}f(x_{\min}^{\prime}y_{\min})=o_{Y}(1).\end{split}

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in Y}\sum_{x\in X}f(xy)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{F(y)}{y}\right)+o_{Y}(1)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{G(y)+o_{Y}(1)}{y}\right)+o_{Y}(1)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{G(y)}{y}\right)+o_{Y}(1).

Combining this with (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) yields

(2.11)

\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}\right)\leqslant 4\frac{\#Y(\mathcal{I},\varepsilon)\frac{F(y_{\min})}{y_{\min}}}{\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{G(y)}{y}}+8\delta.

Observe that

\begin{split}\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{G(y)}{y}\geqslant G(y_{\min})\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{1}{y}&\geqslant F(y_{\min})\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{1}{y}\end{split}.

Hence, by Lemma
2.3
,

\frac{\#Y(\mathcal{I},\varepsilon)\frac{F(y_{\min})}{y_{\min}}}{\sum_{y\in Y}\frac{G(y)}{y}}\leqslant\frac{\#Y(\mathcal{I},\varepsilon)}{\sum\limits_{y\in Y}\frac{y_{\min}}{y}}<\delta.

Substituting this into (
2.11
) and choosing
{\delta}={\varepsilon}/12
completes the proof of Proposition
2.1
,
thereby completing the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Before proving Theorem
1.3
, we briefly justify the first part of Remark
1.4
namely that, in Theorem
1.2
, the function
\psi
cannot necessarily be taken as
{\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}f
for some
S\subset\mathbb{N}
with full upper density. Suppose the function
f
satisfies
f(q)\gg\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}
. Suppose

\overline{\delta}(S)=1
, and let
{\varepsilon}\in(0,1/2)
. Let
Q\geqslant 1
be such that

\frac{\#([1,Q]\cap\mathbb{N})\setminus S}{Q}<{\varepsilon}.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{q\in S}\frac{\varphi(q)f(q)}{q}

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{q\in S\cap[1,Q]}\frac{\varphi(q)f(q)}{q}\gg\sum_{q\in S\cap[1,Q]}\frac{1}{q}\geqslant\sum_{{\varepsilon}Q<q\leqslant Q}\frac{1}{q}\gg\log({\varepsilon}^{-1}).

Since
{\varepsilon}>0
can be arbitrarily small, this shows that

\sum_{q\in S}\frac{\varphi(q)f(q)}{q}=\infty
. By Theorem
1.1
,

\lambda(W(f{\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}))=1.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

The function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
will be supported on pairwise disjoint blocks:

(2.12)

\supp{\psi}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}(C_{j}\cup D_{j}).

The blocks
C_{j}
will be sparse, and defined in a way that contributes substantially to
\sum\psi(q)
without significantly affecting
W(\psi)
. The blocks
D_{j}
will be long strings of consecutive integers on which
\psi
contributes negligibly to
\sum\psi(q)
. These serve to ensure that
\supp{S}
has full upper density.

To start the construction, put

D_{0}=\{1\}
,
Q_{0}=1
, and
\psi(1)=f(1)
. For each
j\geqslant 1
, we will have
\psi(Q_{j-1})>0
, and we choose
M_{j}>Q_{j-1}
large enough that
f(M_{j})\leqslant\psi(Q_{j-1})
. By Proposition
2.1
, we may find a finite set

C_{j}\subset\mathbb{N}\cap(M_{j},\infty)
such that

(2.13)

\sum_{q\in C_{j}}f(q)\in[1/2,1]\quad\textrm{and}\quad\lambda\left(\bigcup_{q\in C_{j}}A_{q}\right)<2^{-j}.

Let
D_{j}=(\max C_{j},Q_{j}]\cap\mathbb{N}
, where
Q_{j}
is chosen large enough that

(2.14)

\frac{Q_{j}-\max C_{j}}{Q_{j}}\geqslant 1-\frac{1}{j}.

Define

\psi(q)=\begin{cases}f(q)&\textrm{if }q\in C_{j},\textrm{ for some }j\geqslant 1\\
\min\{f(q),q^{-2}\},&\textrm{if }q\in D_{j}\textrm{ for some }j\geqslant 1\\
0,&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly, we have
\psi(q)\leqslant f(q)
for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
, as well as (
2.12
). Notice that

\supp\psi
has upper density
1
by (
2.14
), and that
\psi
is non-increasing on
\supp\psi
by construction. Moreover, by (
2.13
),

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}\psi(q)\geqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q\in C_{j}}\psi(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q\in C_{j}}f(q)=\infty.

It remains to show that
{\lambda}(W(\psi))=0
. To see this, note from (
2.13
)
and the first Borel–Cantelli lemma that almost no
x
lie in infinitely many of the sets
\bigcup_{q\in C_{j}}A_{q}
. Furthermore, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{q\in D_{j}}\psi(q)\leqslant\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-2}<\infty,

so almost no
x
lie in infinitely many of the sets
A_{q}\,(q\in D_{j})
. Therefore

\lambda(W(\psi))=0.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

The proof above can be straightforwardly adapted to the inhomogeneous setup, giving inhomogeneous constructions with upper density

that are non-increasing on the support.
We omit a full description for the sake of conciseness; one only needs to replace the “counterexample blocks”

by the inhomogeneous counterexample blocks from §

3.
Inhomogeneous counterexamples

In this section, we prove the inhomogeneous Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
. They are based on the following inhomogeneous version of Proposition
2.1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1/2)
be a non-increasing function satisfying (
1.3
). Then, for any
{\varepsilon},M>0
and any positive integer
b
, there exists a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{N}\cap(M,\infty)
such that

\sum_{q\in S}f(q)\in[1/2,1],

and such that

(3.1)

\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}^{\gamma}(f)\right\rparen<{\varepsilon}

holds for every

\gamma\in\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\frac{j-\delta}{b},\frac{j+\delta}{b}\right]
, where
\delta=bf(\max S)
.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
relies on the following
observations.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
{\varepsilon}>0
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and
b\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\lVert b\gamma\rVert\leqslant b{\varepsilon}
, then
A_{q}^{\gamma}({\varepsilon})\subseteq A_{bq}(2b{\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in A_{q}^{\gamma}({\varepsilon})
, so that there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z}
with

\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{a+\gamma}{q}\right\rvert<\frac{{\varepsilon}}{q}.

For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{a+\gamma}{q}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{ba+b\gamma}{bq}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{ba+j+(b\gamma-j)}{bq}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{ba+j}{bq}\right\rvert-\left\lvert\frac{b\gamma-j}{bq}\right\rvert.

Combining yields

\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{ba+j}{bq}\right\rvert<\frac{{\varepsilon}}{q}+\left\lvert\frac{b\gamma-j}{bq}\right\rvert\qquad(j\in\mathbb{Z}).

Meanwhile, since
\lVert b\gamma\rVert\leqslant b{\varepsilon}
, there is some integer
j

such that
\lvert b\gamma-j\rvert\leqslant b{\varepsilon}
, so

\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{ba+j}{bq}\right\rvert<\frac{2{\varepsilon}}{q}=\frac{2b{\varepsilon}}{bq}.

This shows that
\alpha\in A_{bq}(2b{\varepsilon})
, hence

A_{q}^{\gamma}({\varepsilon})\subseteq A_{bq}(2b{\varepsilon})
as claimed.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
be intervals, and
b\geqslant 1
. Then

\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}b\bullet I_{i}\right\rparen\leqslant b\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}\right\rparen,

where
b\bullet I_{i}
denotes the concentric dilation of
I_{i}
by the
factor
b
.

Proof.

The intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{k}
may be assumed without loss of generality to be open. Write

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}J_{j},

where
J_{1},\dots,J_{\ell}
are pairwise disjoint open intervals. Notice that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}b\bullet I_{i}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}b\bullet J_{j}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}b\bullet I_{i}\right\rparen\leqslant\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}b\bullet J_{j}\right\rparen\leqslant b\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda(J_{j})=b\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell}J_{j}\right\rparen=b\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}I_{i}\right\rparen.

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

By an application of Proposition
2.1
, we may find a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{N}\cap(M,\infty)
such that

\sum_{q\in S}2f(q)\in[1,2]\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}(2f)\right\rparen<\frac{{\varepsilon}}{b}.

Thus, by Lemma
3.3
,

\sum_{q\in S}f(q)\in[1/2,1]\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}(2bf)\right\rparen<{\varepsilon}.

Put
\delta=bf(q_{\max})
, where
q_{\max}=\max S
, and let

\gamma\in\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\frac{j-\delta}{b},\frac{j+\delta}{b}\right].

Then

(3.2)

\lVert b\gamma\rVert\leqslant\delta=bf(q_{\max})\leqslant bf(q),

for every
q\in S
so, by Lemma
3.2
,

A_{q}^{\gamma}(f(q))\subseteq A_{bq}(2bf(q)).

Note that
f(q)
is a number here, not the name of a function, so
A_{bq}(2bf(q))
does not mean
A_{bq}(2bf)=A_{bq}(2bf(bq))
.

Finally, since
x\mapsto bx\pmod{1}
is a measure-preserving transformation on
[0,1)
,

\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}^{\gamma}(f)\right\rparen\leqslant\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{bq}(2bf(q))\right\rparen=\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S}A_{q}(2bf)\right\rparen<{\varepsilon}.

This establishes (
3.1
) and finishes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
1.5
. The idea is to apply Proposition
3.1
repeatedly with an increasing sequence of values for

. We will choose this sequence
(b_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
so that if

for all sufficiently large

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

For
k\geqslant 1
, let
b_{k}=(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k})^{G_{k}}
, where
(G_{k})_{k\geqslant 1}
is an

3.1
furnishes
\delta_{k}>0
and a finite set

\sum_{q\in S_{k}}f(q)\in[1/2,1]\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\lambda\left\lparen\bigcup_{q\in S_{k}}A_{q}^{\gamma}(f)\right\rparen<2^{-k}

for every

large enough that for each for each

The condition (
3.2

\gamma
in terms of

Let

\sum_{q\in S}f_{k}(q)=\infty,\qquad\lambda(W({\boldsymbol{1}}_{S}f_{k},\gamma))=0.

But

\frac{1}{f_{k}(H(q^{2}))}\leqslant\frac{1}{f_{k}(\exp^{(k+3)}(q^{6000}))}<\exp^{(k+3)}(q^{7000})

for all sufficiently large
q
, therefore

\liminf_{q\to\infty}\lVert q\gamma\rVert\exp^{(k+3)}(q^{7000})<\infty

suffices and the theorem is proved.
∎
