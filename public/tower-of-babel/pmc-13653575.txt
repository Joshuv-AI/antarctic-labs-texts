Non‐Covalent PL, CD, and CPL Activation of Linear Aromatic Oligomers within Axially Chiral Aromatic Capsules
Authors: Yoshihisa Hashimoto, Yuya Tanaka, Daiki Tauchi, Masashi Hasegawa, Michito Yoshizawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCovalent functionalization is requisite to enhancing/inducing the luminescent and chiroptical properties of oligo‐aromatic compounds, owing to their rigid framework and strong aggregation characters. Here we report that linear aromatic oligomers display intense PL, CD, and CPL properties upon encapsulation by axially‐chiral aromatic capsules under ambient aqueous conditions. The treatment of para‐phenylene oligomers with chiral BINOL‐based amphiphiles generates host‐guest composites in water, exhibiting oligomer‐based PL, CD, and CPL bands with strong intensities (up to Φ F = 36%, |g abs| = 6 × 10−3, and |g lum| = 3 × 10−3). This method is applicable to other aromatic oligomers, comprising fluorene, carbazole, and thiophene rings, and further PL and CPL enhancement (up to Φ F = 87% and |g lum| = 4 × 10−3) is demonstrated with the resultant composites. The observed chiroptical features stem from tight host‐guest CH‐π interactions in the adaptable aromatic cavity. Besides the oligomers, polymer‐based chiroptical activities are found from the corresponding polymers upon encapsulation. In the solid state, the host‐guest composites display further prominent CD and CPL bands, using fluorene trimers (|g abs| = |g lum| = ∼3 × 10−2). This study is the first example of the PL, CD, and CPL activation of simple aromatic oligomers to a large extent, via non‐covalent encapsulation. The present strategy will facilitate the supramolecular fusion between aromatic oligo/polymers and chiral functions.

Introduction

Non‐covalent encapsulation is a potent method to tune or alter the properties and reactivity of molecules with high efficiency and designability [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Unlike small to medium‐sized molecules (i.e., less than 1 nm), however, the majority of synthetic oligomers and polymers have been rarely encapsulated by (supra)molecular tubes [9, 10, 11], cages, and capsules [12, 13], owing to their large dimensions, strong self‐aggregation, and/or poor host‐guest interactions. The encapsulation of those compounds comprising rigid aromatic frameworks remains further difficult to date [14, 15, 16, 17]. There have been many reports on the covalent chiral/steric functionalization of linear aromatic oligo/polymers to induce their chiroptical properties, i.e., circular dichroism (CD) and circularly polarized luminescence (CPL), using flexible central chiral groups (Figure 1a) [18, 19, 20, 21, 22] as well as to improve their photoluminescence (PL) properties [23]. The functionalized compounds usually display strong chiroptical bands under aggregation conditions. To develop the alternative using rigid axially chiral units, here we report a non‐covalent strategy, for the first time, to enhance/induce the PL, CD, and CPL properties of simple, linear aromatic oligomers upon encapsulation (Figure 1b). The details are (i) the efficient encapsulation of non‐chiral and non‐substituted, para‐phenylene oligomers 5‐7P by axially chiral aromatic capsule s BC in water (Figure 1c,d). The resultant host‐guest composites display strong PL, CD, and CPL bands derived from the oligomers (i.e., up to Φ F = 36%, |g abs| = 5.9 × 10−3, and |g lum| = 2.9 × 10−3). (ii) This encapsulation method is applicable to linear fluorene‐, carbazole‐, and thiophene‐based oligomers (Figure 1d), exhibiting enhanced PL and CPL properties (i.e., up to Φ F = 87% and |g lum| = 3.6 × 10−3). (iii) The mechanistic studies on the observed chiroptical behavior are examined by the theoretical calculation. (iv) Polymer‐based PL, CD, and CPL bands are also generated from fluorene and carbazole polymers upon efficient encapsulation in water. In addition, (v) the solid of the host‐guest composite providing fluorene trimers displays further enhanced PL, CD, and CPL activities (i.e., Φ F = 67%, |g abs| = 2.6 × 10−2, and |g lum| = 3.3 × 10−2), unlike the mixed solid of the capsule subunits and trimers without encapsulation.

(a) Covalent functionalization (previous works) and (b) non‐covalent encapsulation (this work) of linear aromatic oligo/polymers for their PL, CD, and CPL enhancement/induction. (c) BINOL‐based bent amphiphile s BAM for chiral aromatic capsule s BC and (d) linear aromatic oligo/polymers, studied herein.

Micelle‐type aromatic capsules, composed of bent amphiphiles with non‐chiral anthracene or pentamethylbenzene panels, show broad encapsulation abilities in water, through efficient π–π/CH‐π interactions in the adaptable cavities [24, 25, 26]. We herein employed axially chiral analogue s BAM providing a mesityl‐substituted, bent BINOL framework (Figure 1c) [27], which forms chiral aromatic capsule s BC (= (s BAM) n ; n = ∼12) in water in a quantitative fashion. Unlike chiral macrocycles and cages reported previously [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34], the chiral aromatic cavity with adaptability enables the strong CD induction (up to |g abs| = ∼2 × 10−3) of planar metallodyes (e.g., metalloporphyrins and phthalocyanines) with widths of ∼2 nm upon encapsulation, without PL and CPL activities [27]. The chiroptical induction of selected molecules (e.g., 1,1,2,2‐tetraphenylethene (TPE) and BODIPYs) was observed within terpene‐based chiral capsules in water [35]. However, their efficiencies were low to moderate (up to |g abs| = ∼3 × 10−4 and |g lum| = ∼3 × 10−3), because of the weak host‐guest aliphatic‐aromatic interactions. Oligomers 6P, 3F, 3C, and 6T studied herein possess similar, rod‐like frameworks with a length of ∼3 nm and show no water solubility and quite poor solubility even in organic solvents, except for 3F and 3C.

Results and Discussion

Phenylene Hexamers within Chiral Aromatic Capsules

A grinding‐assisted encapsulation method efficiently generated host‐guest composite s BC•(6P) m , consisting of chiral amphiphiles s BAM and achiral phenylene hexamers 6P, with characteristic absorption, PL, CD, and CPL properties in water. As the optimized protocol, a clear aqueous solution of s BC•(6P) m (1.0 mM based on s BAM) was obtained from a mixture of solids s BAM (2.0 µmol) and 6P (0.4 µmol), through sequential manual grinding (2 min), H2O addition (2.0 mL), centrifugation, and filtration (Figure 2a) [36]. In the UV‐visible spectrum of the resultant colorless solution, broad and intense π–π* absorption bands derived from encapsulated (6P) m appeared at ∼300–400 nm, unlike weak bands from empty s BC (< ∼350 nm; Figure 2b). In PL studies, whereas solid 6P showed weak PL bands at 380‐550 nm (Φ F = 2%, τ = 0.34 ns, λ ex = 360 nm), the s BC•(6P) m solution exhibited strong bands at λ max = 421 nm in the same region with 13‐fold enhanced emissivity (Φ F = 25%, τ = 0.48 ns; Figures 2c and S3). This enhancement is most probably attributed to the suppression of extended (6P) m aggregation upon encapsulation, which was supported by the DLS analysis of s BC•(6P) m , due to the detection of only small particles (i.e., d av. = ∼3 nm; Figure 2h). The obtained host‐guest structure remains intact for at least 7 d under ambient aqueous conditions and high dilution conditions (up to 10 µM based on s BAM; Figure S1b), through efficient aromatic–aromatic interactions (discussed later).

Host‐guest composite s BC•(6P) m displayed strong induced CD and CPL bands, derived from achiral 6Ps via encapsulation under ambient aqueous conditions. The CD spectrum of s BC•(6P) m (1.0 mM based on s BAM) showed a clear negative Cotton effect from encapsulated (6P) m at 305–380 nm (Figure 2d). The maximum absolute absorption dissymmetry factor (|g abs|) was estimated to be 3.2 × 10−3 at 329 nm. In the CPL spectrum, moderate negative bands were found in the range of 390–490 nm with a maximum PL asymmetry factor (|g lum|) of 1.7 × 10−3 at 416 nm (Figure 2e). Nearly mirror‐image CD and CPL spectra were obtained from enantiomeric host‐guest composite r BC•(6P) m (|g abs| = 3.0 × 10−3 and |g lum| of 1.7 × 10−3) under the same conditions (Figures S4 and S5). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first instance to induce the chirality of 6P and other pristine oligophenylenes through a non‐covalent approach [37]. The chirality induction efficiency of s BC toward 6P was higher than those of other chiral hosts under the same conditions. Analogous BINOL‐based capsules (s BA) n (R = H), (s BAP) n (R = phenyl), and (s BAN) n (R = naphthyl; Figure 2g, left) showed low to similar encapsulation abilities (i.e., ∼0.1–1.2‐fold) [38] yet low to moderate |g abs| values (i.e., up to ∼0.6‐fold), relative to those of s BC (Figure 2f). No chirality induction toward 6P was observed with terpene capsule (MA) n (Figure 2g, middle), even with its comparable encapsulation ability (i.e., ∼0.8‐fold) relative to s BC. No or poor encapsulation of 6P occurred with typical yet achiral alkyl micelles (DA) n and (DS) n (Figure 2g, right and Figures S18 and S19) [36]. The observed, superior encapsulation and chirality induction abilities of s BC, featuring bent axially chiral frameworks with bulky mesityl‐substituents, are most likely derived from multiple host‐guest CH‐π interactions in the adaptable cavity (discussed later).

Length‐Dependent Properties of Phenylene Oligomers with the Capsules

Thanks to the cavity adaptability of s BC toward guest lengths and volumes, CD and CPL‐active host‐guest composites s BC•(5P) m and s BC•(7P) m (1.0 mM based on s BAM) were also obtained through the same grinding protocol using linear phenylene pentamer (5P) and heptamer (7P), respectively [36]. Their absorption spectra indicated that the encapsulation efficiencies of s BC toward 5P and 7P are roughly comparable to that of 6P (Figures 3a and 2b) [39]. The DLS‐based product sizes of composites s BC•(5‐7P) m were also comparable in aqueous solution (i.e., d av. = ∼3 nm; Figures 3f and 2h). On the other hand, the PL quantum yields of s BC•(5P) m and s BC•(7P) m were 0.6‐fold lower (Φ F = 16%, λ max = 407 nm) and 1.4‐fold higher (Φ F = 36%, λ max = 430 nm) than that of s BC•(6P) m , respectively, under the same conditions (Figures 3b and 2c). Interestingly, the highest chirality induction was demonstrated with the 5P‐based host‐guest composite under ambient conditions. The aqueous solution of s BC•(5P) m exhibited intense CD bands with |g abs| = 5.9 × 10−3 at 320 nm (Figure 3c) and CPL bands with |g lum| = 2.9 × 10−3 at 407 nm (Figure 3d) [40]. These values are 1.8 and 1.7‐fold higher than those of s BC•(6P) m (Figure 3e). In contrast, the CD and CPL‐activities of s BC•(7P) m were comparable to lower (0.5‐0.9‐fold) based on those of s BC•(6P) m . The present length‐dependent induced chirality within s BC results from effective host‐guest contacts, where the relative contact area of shorter 5P is larger than that of longer 7P, and the effective transition dipole moments of the guests.

Theoretical Studies on Chirality Induction within the Capsules

The observed, unusual chirality induction was further studied from the mechanistic viewpoint by the combination of experimental and theoretical analyses. The host‐guest structure of s BC•(6P) m was revealed by DLS, AFM, and molecular modeling analyses. Besides the solution‐state DLS data discussed above (d av. = 2.9 nm, Figure 2h), the AFM image of s BC•(6P) m on mica under dry conditions also displayed small spherical particles with heights of 2.5‐3.5 nm (Figures 4a,b, and S7). On the basis of the host‐guest sizes and ratio (i.e., s BAM:6P = 3:1) [41], molecular modeling studies suggested the formation of a spheroidal (s BAM)9•(6P)3 composite on average [36, 42], with the shorter and longer diameters of 2.2 and 3.5 nm, respectively (∼3 nm in average diameter; Figure 4c), where 3•6P are aggregated in a nearly edge‐to‐face conformation in the cavity. The presence of multiple host‐guest CH‐π interactions was supported in the optimized structure.

The chirality induction through the direct host‐guest interactions, rather than the formation of intertwined oligomers, was supported by the TD‐DFT‐based CD studies and non‐covalent interaction (NCI) plots of partial 1:1 host‐guest composite s BAM’•6P as the simplest model (R’ = CH3; Figure 4d,e, respectively). The calculated CD spectrum displayed a strong negative Cotton effect in the range of 290 to 400 nm, which is in good qualitative agreement with the experimental one of s BC•(6P) m in water (Figures 4f and S64). Theoretical s BAM’•(5P or 7P) composites (R’ = CH3; Figure S65) showed similar negative CD bands at 290‐450 nm (Figure 4g). The relative band intensities are in the order of s BAM’•5P > s BAM’•6P > s BAM’•7P, in a manner similar to that of the experimental one (i.e., s BC•(5P) m > s BC•(6P) m > s BC•(7P) m ; Figure 3e). These results suggest that larger host contacts with the smaller guest framework facilitate stronger chirality induction even toward pristine oligophenylenes upon encapsulation. The NCI plots of s BAM’•6P indicated the presence of five CH‐π interactions between the substituted BINOL and phenylene frameworks (Figures 4e and S69). In particular, two closest contacts between the phenylene‐based CH groups and mesityl‐based π rings (i.e., 2.6 Å in distance) indicated that the bulky mesityl groups play a crucial role in the host‐to‐guest chirality induction, which is supported by the experimental results (Figure 2f). The theoretical CPL spectrum of s BAM’•6P also qualitatively supported the experimental one of s BC•(6P) m (Figures 4h and S67). The calculated structures of s BAM’•6P remained almost unchanged in the ground and excited states (Figure S68a), indicating the orbital‐orbital interaction‐based chirality induction.

Other Aromatic Oligomers within the Chiral Capsules

Large PL enhancement of host‐guest composites was observed under ambient aqueous conditions, when dimethylfluorene trimer 3F and N‐methylcarbazole trimer 3C were employed as oligomer guests (Figure 1d), which are composed of the 6P framework with three ‐C(CH3)2‐ and ‐N(CH3)‐ bridges, respectively. The grinding protocol with s BAM and 3F (2.0 and 0.2 µmol, respectively) yielded host‐guest composite s BC•(3F) m in water (1.0 mM based on s BAM, Figure 5a), displaying intense absorption bands at 350–390 nm and PL bands at 380‐520 nm (λ ex = 360 nm), derived from encapsulated 3Fs (Figure 5b,c). Notably, the PL quantum yield reached 87% in water (Figures 5f), which is 1.7‐fold higher than that of solid 3F (Figure S31) and comparable (∼0.9‐fold) to that of free 3F in CH2Cl2 (0.01 mM; Figure S31), due to the suppression of tight guest‐guest aggregation in the discrete cavity. Analogous dimethylfluorene tetramer 4F showed similar absorption and PL properties (Φ F = 57% at 390–550 nm) upon encapsulation by s BC (Figure S35). Host‐guest composite s BC•(3C) m was prepared from s BAM and 3C in the same way. The resultant, aqueous solution provided similar absorption and PL bands at 350–410 and 390–560 nm, respectively (Figure 5b,c), with high PL efficiency (i.e., Φ F = 52%; Figure 5f) [43]. Accordingly, up to 3.5‐fold PL enhancement was demonstrated upon simple replacement of 6P with 3F or 3C within capsule s BC.

Fluorene oligomer‐based host‐guest composite s BC•(3F) m in hand exhibited the best CPL performance among the present studies. In the CD spectrum, the aqueous solution of s BC•(3F) m (1.0 mM based on s BAM) showed a moderate, negative Cotton effect in the region of 340–390 nm (Figure 5d). The |g abs| value was estimated to be 2.0 × 10−3 at 358 nm, which is 4.0‐fold higher and 0.6‐fold lower than those of s BC•(3C) m and s BC•(6P) m , respectively (Figure 5f). These results clarified that the order of the induced chirality is 6P > 3F > 3C, among aromatic oligomers with the same length, upon encapsulation. The order is consistent with the low to high planarity of the phenylene rings of the linear oligomers. Thanks to its high PL and moderate CD activities, the CPL spectrum of s BC•(3F) m displayed prominent 3F‐based bands with |g lum| = 3.6 × 10−3 at 399 nm (Figures 5e), which is 2.1‐fold higher than s BC•(6P) m under the same conditions (Figures 5f). In contrast, the CPL of s BC•(3C) m was slightly superior (∼1.1‐fold) in |g lum| to that of s BC•(6P) m , most probably owing to its higher PL activity (Φ F = 52%). Besides fluorene oligomer 3F, thiophene hexamer 6T (Figures 1d), as a typical heteroaromatic oligomer, also showed moderate CPL bands with |g lum| = 2.2 × 10−3 at 585 nm upon encapsulation by s BC, due to the combination of its low PL and high CD activities (i.e., Φ F = 2% and |g abs| = 5.9 × 10−3 at 416 nm, respectively; Figure 5f). To exclude the product size‐dependency on the observed chiroptical properties, the DLS analysis of the host‐guest composites was examined under the same aqueous conditions (1.0 mM based on s BAM). The average diameter of s BC•(3F) m was estimated to be 2.8 nm, which is comparable to those of s BC•(3C) m , s BC•(6T) m , and s BC•(6P) m (i.e., d av. = ∼3 nm; Figures 5g and 2h). The combination of the DLS size, NMR‐based integration ratio [44], and molecular modeling studies of s BC•(3F) m suggested the formation of a spheroidal assembly of (s BAM)8•(3F)2 with 2.2–3.2 nm in diameter on average (Figures 5h and S29) [36, 42].

Aromatic Polymers within the Chiral Capsules

Besides the aromatic oligomers, the PL, CD, and CPL properties of the corresponding polymers were also altered upon encapsulation by capsule s BC in water. For example, a mixture of poly(dimethylfluorene) PF (0.25 mg; Figure 1d) and s BAM (2.0 µmol) was ground for 2 min. After H2O addition (2.0 mL) to the mixture, the resultant suspension was sonicated (40 kHz, 150 W, ∼10 °C) for 20 min and centrifuged (14,800 rpm) for 10 min to give a clear s BC•(PF) m solution [45]. The resultant solution exhibited a PF‐based absorption band at 350‐430 nm (Figure 6a) and PL band at 410–550 nm with Φ F = 14%, which is 3.5‐fold higher than that of solid PF (Φ F = 4%; Figures 6b). The PL enhancement and band shifts (Δλ = –21 nm) of s BC•(PF) m indicated the partial disaggregation of PFs upon encapsulation. The host‐guest composite showed moderate CD bands at 325‐420 nm (Figure 6c) and CPL bands at 410‐470 nm derived from encapsulated PFs (Figure 6d). The corresponding |g abs| and |g lum| values were calculated to be 3.5 × 10−4 at 392 nm and 3.5 × 10−4 at 429 nm, respectively, which are 0.1‐0.2‐fold lower than those of s BC•(3F) m (Figures S52 and S53). In a manner similar to oligomers 5‐7P, the longer aromatic framework likely causes inefficient host‐guest contacts within the adaptable capsule. Similar PL, CD, and CPL activities were observed upon encapsulation of poly(N‐methylcarbazole) PC (Figure 1d). Those of host‐guest composite s BC•(PC) m were estimated to be Φ F = 14% (4.7‐fold enhancement), |g abs| = 1.6 × 10−4 (373 nm), and |g lum| = 8.3 × 10−4 (448 nm), respectively (Figure 6a–d) [46]. The mirror‐like images of the CD and CPL spectra were shown with enantiomers r BC•(PF or PC) m under the same conditions. Whereas the present induction efficiencies require further improvement, as compared with the previous covalent functionalization systems [18, 19, 20, 21, 22], successful examples of non‐covalent chirality induction remain quite rare for such pristine aromatic polymers.

Solid‐State Properties of the Host‐Guest Composites

Finally, with the aid of the tight encapsulation, the present host‐guest composites provided moderate to high PL, CD, and CPL features even in the solid state. When an aqueous solution (50 µL) of s BC•(3F) m (2.0 mM based on s BAM) was cast onto a quartz plate and vacuum‐dried at ambient temperature, a white thin layer of the host‐guest composite was formed on the plate. The UV‐visible spectrum showed broad bands at 350‐410 nm, derived from both s BC and 3Fs (Figure 6e). Clear 3F‐based PL bands were observed at 380–520 nm (λ max = 413 nm, λ ex = 360 nm) with high quantum yield (Φ F = 67%) from the solid sample [47], which are nearly identical to those of s BC•(3F) m in water (Figure 6f), suggesting the retainment of the host‐guest structure on the plate. In the same way, a white thin layer of s BC•(6P) m was obtained from the corresponding aqueous solution. The solid‐state absorption and PL spectra exhibited 6P‐based bands at 350–400 nm and 350–520 nm (λ max = 402 nm, λ ex = 280 nm), respectively (Figures S74 and S75) [48, 49].

Next CD and CPL studies revealed the strong chiroptical activities of the solid‐state host‐guest composites at room temperature. The CD spectrum of solid s BC•(3F) m displayed prominent, positive Cotton effects in the region of 250‐370 nm, corresponding to encapsulated 3Fs (Figures 6g and S72). The solid of enantiomer r BC•(3F) m showed nearly the same CD bands in a mirror‐image fashion. These band intensities and shapes were almost insensitive to the rotation and inversion of the sample plates [49]. The solid‐state |g abs| value of s BC•(3F) m was roughly estimated to be 0.03 at 341 nm. As key control experiments, no Cotton effects derived from 3F and 6P were detected in both solid samples obtained from (i) simple grinding of a mixture of s BAM and 3F/6P (1:0.25 ratio) as well as (ii) solvent evaporation of a methanol solution of them (Figures S79 and S80). These results indicated the importance of the present host‐guest structures for the efficient chirality induction. Furthermore, prominent 3F‐based CPL bands were also observed from solids s BC•(3F) m and r BC•(3F) m at 370‐470 nm with a mirrored image (Figure 6h) and their |g lum| values reached 0.033 at 391 nm [49], which are approximately 10‐fold higher than that in the solution state (i.e., |g lum| = 3.6 × 10−3 at 399 nm). Solid s BC•(6P) m likewise exhibited guest‐based CD and CPL bands at 250–360 and 350–450 nm, respectively, with a high |g lum| value (i.e., 0.016 at 378 nm; Figure 6g,h). It should be noted that the realization of 10−2‐ordered |g lum| values remains rare with non‐covalent host‐guest systems, particularly in the solid state [50, 51, 52], owing to requiring the suppression of guest‐guest and host‐guest aggregation caused quenching (ACQ) as well as the enhancement of effective host‐guest chiral interactions [35, 53, 54, 55, 56]. The combination of chiral capsule s BC and aromatic oligomer 3F thus generated the non‐covalent host‐guest composite with the best chiroptical properties, both in solution and in the solid state, within this study.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated the encapsulation of linear aromatic oligomers by axially‐chiral aromatic capsules to non‐covalently activate their PL, CD, and CPL properties under ambient aqueous conditions. Unlike previous host compounds, the present capsules efficiently encapsulated various aromatic oligomers, comprising benzene, fluorene, carbazole, and thiophene rings, in the adaptable cavities. Without typical covalent chiral/bulky functionalization, the resultant host‐guest composites displayed strong oligomer‐based PL, CD, and CPL bands, due to effective CH‐π interactions with the BINOL‐based aromatic frameworks. Among them, fluorene‐based trimers exhibited remarkably high PL and CPL properties (i.e., Φ F = 87% and |g lum| = 3.6 × 10−3) upon encapsulation. These chiroptical activities were observed even in the solid state, with a ∼10‐fold higher |g lum| value, and the present method was applicable to the corresponding polymers in solution. This study developed, for the first time, the non‐covalent PL, CD, and CPL activation of simple aromatic oligomers upon encapsulation, both in solution and in the solid state. The further design of new chiral capsules (e.g., with planar and mechanical/interlocked chiralities) and further expansion of oligo/polymer scopes (e.g., with nonlinear and branched frameworks) could enlarge supramolecular functions for chiral chemistry and materials.
