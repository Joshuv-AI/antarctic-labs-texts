Can synthetic data reproduce real-world findings in epidemiology? A replication study using tree-based generative AI
Jan Kapar, Kathrin Günther, Lori Ann Vallis, Klaus Berger, Nadine Binder, Hermann Brenner, Stefanie Castell, Beate Fischer, Volker Harth, Bernd Holleczek, Timm Intemann, Till Ittermann, André Karch, Thomas Keil, Lilian Krist, Berit Lange, Michael F. Leitzmann, Katharina Nimptsch, Nadia Obi, Iris Pigeot, Tobias Pischon, Tamara Schikowski, Börge Schmidt, Carsten Oliver Schmidt, Anja M. Sedlmair, Justine Tanoey, Harm Wienbergen, Andreas Wienke, Claudia Wigmann, Marvin N. Wright

Abstract
Generative artificial intelligence for synthetic data generation holds substantial potential to address practical challenges in epidemiology. However, many current methods suffer from limited quality, high computational demands, and complexity for non-experts. Furthermore, common evaluation strategies for synthetic data often fail to directly reflect statistical utility. Against this background, a critical underexplored question is whether synthetic data can reliably reproduce key findings from epidemiological research. We propose the use of adversarial random forests (ARF) as an efficient and convenient method for synthesizing tabular epidemiological data. To evaluate its performance, we replicated statistical analyses from six epidemiological publications and compared original with synthetic results. These publications cover blood pressure, anthropometry, myocardial infarction, accelerometry, loneliness, and diabetes, based on data from the German National Cohort (NAKO Gesundheitsstudie), the Bremen STEMI Registry U45 Study, and the Guelph Family Health Study. Additionally, we assessed the impact of dimensionality and variable complexity on synthesis quality by limiting datasets to variables relevant for individual analyses, including necessary derivations. Across all replicated original studies, results from multiple synthetic data replications consistently aligned with original findings. Even for datasets with relatively low sample size-to-dimensionality ratios, the replication outcomes closely matched the original results across various descriptive and inferential analyses. Reducing dimensionality and pre-deriving variables further enhanced both quality and stability of the results. In summary, ARF reliably generates high-quality synthetic data that replicate epidemiological study findings across diverse scenarios, supporting its practical use for applications such as rapid prototyping and data sharing under appropriate privacy and regulatory considerations. Introduction

Researchers in epidemiology often encounter specific challenges that must be addressed before conducting statistical analyses: health-related data are highly protected by privacy laws, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act
[
1
]
in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation
[
2
]
in the European Union, and data access request procedures can take several months from the initial application to final data access. In many cases, the data contain missing values or are imbalanced, which can significantly lower the performance of statistical models or even make their use impossible.

Generative artificial intelligence (AI)
[
3
]
, famously known for text and image data generation with applications such as ChatGPT
[
4
]
and DALL-E
[
5
]
, has great potential to address these obstacles
[
6
,
7
]
. Realistic, privacy-preserving synthetic health data could be shared more openly and used as a preview during the application process or even as a substitute for the original data where appropriate. Under-represented categories in the original data could be up-sampled and missing data imputed with realistic entries using generative modelling.

However, while the remarkable success of generative AI methods is transferable to medical image synthesis
[
8
,
9
]
, generating high-quality tabular health data–—i.e., data organized in rows and columns, with rows as individual health observations and columns as variables—–and evaluating their utility continue to be challenging. Existing synthesis approaches primarily adapt successful deep learning methods for image and text generation to the tabular setting
[
10
,
11
]
. Many of these methods struggle to consistently achieve sufficient quality, are computationally intensive, and require expert knowledge and substantial hardware resources.
Assessing the quality of generative models and synthetic data remains particularly difficult for tabular data, which cannot be evaluated as naturally as images or text. Commonly used metrics are not fully reliable and do not necessarily provide insight into the practical statistical utility of synthetic data
[
12
,
13
,
7
]
.
With only a few exceptions
[
14
,
15
]
, most generative approaches are evaluated on standard machine learning datasets without health focus, using metrics without clear implications for the statistical utility of synthetic data in applied epidemiological research.

We suggest the use of adversarial random forests (ARF)
[
16
]
, a generative AI method tailored to tabular data. It is conveniently available as an
R
package
[
17
]
, easy to use for researchers with little experience in machine learning, runs fast on standard hardware, and shows competitive performance
[
16
,
18
]
. We demonstrate its usefulness in diverse real-world epidemiological settings by replicating the statistical analyses of six publications
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
based on the German National Cohort (NAKO Gesundheitsstudie, NAKO)
[
25
]
, the Bremen STEMI Registry U45 Study (BSR-U45)
[
21
]
, and the Guelph Family Health Study (GFHS)
[
26
,
22
]
using ARF-generated data and comparing original and synthetic data results.

2
Data and methods

The selected datasets, along with the synthesis and evaluation approach, were chosen with a focus on their applicability to epidemiological research.

2.1
Data

We selected the included data sources and original studies with an emphasis on variety regarding study subject, data origin, size and dimensionality, and the complexity of statistical analyses. This way, we cover different challenging scenarios for successful data synthesis, such as small data size, high dimensionality, mixed variable types and irregular distribution shapes.

2.1.1
Data sources

The original studies chosen for this replication analysis were based on NAKO, BSR-U45, and GFHS data.
The NAKO is Germany’s largest cohort study, following over 200 000 participants from age 19 to 74 over the long term. It encompasses socioeconomic, demographic, genetic and lifestyle information as well as medical history and examination data in order to investigate the development of major diseases, such as cancer, cardiovascular conditions, diabetes, and mental health disorders
[
25
]
.
The BSR-U45 is a specialized substudy within the Bremen Heart Registry that focuses on patients aged 45 or younger who experienced myocardial infarction, combining retrospective and prospective data on demographic, lifestyle, clinical, and laboratory factors to investigate early-onset MI risk
[
21
]
.
The GFHS is a long-term cohort study that involves over 246 families in Wellington County, Ontario, Canada. It aims to identify early life risk factors for obesity and chronic diseases and help families establish healthy routines related to nutrition, physical activity, sleep and screen time
[
26
]
.

2.2
Methods

The generative AI method ARF was used for data synthesis. The statistical analyses of the included studies were replicated using synthetic data, and their results then compared to the original findings to assess the statistical utility of the synthetic data.

2.2.1
Tree-based generative artificial intelligence for tabular data: adversarial random forests

Generative AI is concerned with modelling the underlying joint distribution of given data in order to generate realistic synthetic samples
[
3
]
. Deep learning approaches dominate this discipline, especially for image and text data. Tabular data, however, present unique challenges, such as mixed variable types (e.g., continuous or categorical), a lack of natural positional ordering of variables, and irregular distributions, all of which substantially complicate the learning task
[
27
,
10
,
28
]
. Recent advances of generative deep learning methods for tabular data often come at increased computational cost and demanding hardware requirements, and using these methods is often non-trivial for non-experts
[
10
]
.

Tree-based methods
[
29
]
are a well-suited and less resource-demanding alternative to deep learning for tabular learning tasks
[
30
,
18
,
31
]
. The tree-based method ARF is competitive to state-of-the-art deep learning models for tabular data synthesis while often operating orders of magnitude faster and requiring no GPU calculations
[
16
,
18
]
. Synthetic data can be created with one line of
R
code:
synthetic_data <- rarf(original_data)
.

ARF utilises random forests (RFs)
[
32
]
as its foundation. It iteratively learns data dependencies starting with an RF classification, where the original dataset is distinguished from a constructed version built from original entries with permuted, and thus independent, variables. Hereby, a partitioning of the data manifold is determined in which original and constructed entries are indistinguishable for the RF. This is utilised to assume variable independence for the original data locally and perform variable-wise univariate density estimation within the partitioning units, which are then combined in a global mixture distribution
[
33
]
. ARF generates data by drawing samples from this estimated mixture
[
16
]
.

2.2.2
Replication and evaluation procedure

The replication and evaluation procedure is split into three main parts: original dataset preparation before synthesis, generating synthetic data with ARF, and performing the original statistical analyses both on original and synthetic data to compare the results.

Original dataset preparation

Data cleaning, missing data handling, and variable derivations followed the original authors’ protocols where available, or were inferred to match their results using original data. For Berger et al.
[
23
]
, a separate COVID survey dataset was merged with baseline NAKO data before synthesis. To show the effect of dimensionality and complexity induced by derived variables, a dataset with a task-specific variable subset was prepared in addition to the full dataset for selected analyses. We refer to the corresponding synthesis approaches as
full dataset synthesis
and
task-specific dataset synthesis
, respectively: for full dataset synthesis, all variables used in the original analyses were included without derivations; for task-specific synthesis, only the variable subset used in each analysis was included, with derived variables replacing original ones when applicable.

Synthesis

Data synthesis with ARF contains two sources of randomness: model training and data sampling. To account for that, we trained 20 ARF models for each original dataset and sampled five times from each model, resulting in a total of 100 synthetic datasets per original dataset. When the original data contained missing values, we let ARF generate corresponding missingness in the synthetic data instead of using its default on-the-fly-imputation, to prevent divergent analysis results caused by systematic missingness patterns. No model tuning was performed.

Evaluation

For each statistical result of the original studies, both median and the empirical percentile-based 95% confidence interval (CI)
[
34
]
over all 100 synthetic datasets were reported to examine the variability during synthesis. These statistics were compared with the original data results. We recalculated the original data results, as participant consent withdrawals over time in the cohort studies led to differences between the datasets received and those in the original publications.

4
Discussion

Summarising the results, ARF consistently generated synthetic datasets that closely replicated original study findings across a range of data sizes and dimensionalities without tuning. Various descriptive and inferential analyses, such as linear, logistic and Cox regressions, were repeated using synthetic ARF data with similar outcomes. These results align with existing benchmark analyses which attested ARF’s competitive performance on several non-health-related datasets using different evaluation metrics
[
16
,
18
]
. In most of the cases, median results on synthetic data were close and the percentile-based 95% CIs covered the original values. We found that a higher participants-to-dimensionality ratio generally led to closer resemblance and more stable results in terms of narrower CIs. Whenever possible, we therefore recommend restricting the data to variables used in subsequent analyses and performing any necessary variable derivation before synthesis. We acknowledge that while creating such a ”task-specific” dataset is feasible for applications such as data balancing and augmentation, this might usually not be the case for privacy-preserving data sharing scenarios, where such preprocessing would need to be carried out by the data provider. ARF successfully handled datasets with a high proportion of missing values, but care is required in such cases. If missingness is not introduced during synthesis, results may deviate from the original ones in the presence of systematic missingness patterns. To capture dependencies involving missing values across variables, a suitable missing value handling strategy should be chosen, such as enabling MIA (missing incorporated in attributes)
[
37
]
in ARF or including missingness indicators as auxiliary variables.

A key strength of this study is its epidemiology-focused validation across multiple real-world datasets and analysis types. However, we did not examine the statistical utility in the context of specific downstream applications. While missing data imputation
[
38
]
and what-if analyses
[
39
]
with ARF have already been proposed and evaluated, the specific use of synthetic ARF data for tasks such as data balancing or augmentation remains to be explored. Similarly, a comparison with other synthetic data approaches was beyond the scope of this work but would help to better contextualize ARF’s relative performance. It is important to emphasize that synthetic data does not inherently provide privacy guarantees
[
7
]
. Privacy inference attacks can target training data inclusion and disclosure of individuals’ sensitive attributes
[
40
]
. As we did not assess privacy protection in this study, future work should include dedicated evaluations—–especially since this remains a non-trivial task and an active area of research
[
18
,
7
]
.

5
Conclusion

This study demonstrated that ARF is a suitable method for synthesizing tabular epidemiological data, consistently producing synthetic datasets that closely replicate original descriptive and inferential findings across varying research topics, data sizes and dimensionalities. Restricting synthesis to variables relevant to specific analyses and applying variable derivations beforehand further improved data quality and stability; if feasible, these steps should be considered. These findings support ARF as a practical tool for diverse epidemiological workflows, including rapid prototyping, method testing, and facilitating data sharing under regulatory and privacy considerations that warrant further study.
