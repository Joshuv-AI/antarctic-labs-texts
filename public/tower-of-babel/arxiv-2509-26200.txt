Abstract
This paper introduces a novel framework for proactive cross-domain resource orchestration in 6G RAN-Edge networks, featuring large language model (LLM)-augmented agents. The system comprises specialized RAN (energy efficiency) and Edge (latency assurance) agents that engage in iterative negotiation, supported by advanced reasoning and planning capabilities. Agents dynamically interact with a digital twin (DT) to test their proposals and leverage a long-term collective memory where their joint successful and failed agreements along with the related network contexts are distilled into strategies to either follow or avoid and subsequently stored. Given that agents are subject to a plethora of cognitive distortions when retrieving those past experiences---such as primacy, recency, confirmation and availability biases---we propose in this work a novel unbiased memory design 1 1 1 A reusable mockup version of the unbiased memory source code is available for non-commercial use at https://github.com/HatimChergui/unbiased-collective-memory . featuring (i) semantic retrieval of past strategies via Jaccard similarity; (ii) learning from failures through amplified weighting of SLA violations and mandatory inclusion of failed negotiation cases to mitigate confirmation bias; (iii) diversity enforcement to minimize availability bias and (iv) recency and primacy weighting with slow decay to counteract temporal biases. Evaluation results showcase the impact of existing biases and how the unbiased memory allows to tackle them by learning from both successful and failed strategies, either present or old, resulting in × 4.5 \times 4.5 and × 3.5 \times 3.5 reductions of unresolved negotiations compared to non-memory and vanilla memory baselines, respectively, while totally mitigating SLA violations as well as improving latency and energy saving distributions.



I
Introduction

Traditional telecom automation frameworks—spanning TM Forum’s Levels 1 to 3—have delivered incremental efficiencies through scripting, analytics, and AI-based orchestration, but remain limited to task-specific, reactive behaviors and are often fragmented across vendor-specific platforms, custom pipelines, and loosely integrated toolchains, forming a siloed, non-communicative architecture. This fragmentation fundamentally constrains the system’s ability to generalize, collaborate, or adapt in real time-blocking the leap toward higher levels of autonomy. To reach Level 4 (AI-assisted automation) and beyond, 6G must embrace a new paradigm: one where intelligence is connected, semantic, and proactive. In such systems, the network must be able to adaptively achieve complex, multi-objective goals across dynamic environments—even under limited supervision. This shift requires Agentic AI, where LLM-powered autonomous agents are capable of perception by invoking (multimodal) telemetry, reasoning and capitalizing on past experiences via memory use, planning, communicating under various intents, and acting by invoking tools/APIs. Specifically, those agents can negotiate, reconcile cross-domain objectives, and explain or refine their strategies over time. This allows for interacting across traditional silos—such as RAN, edge and core domains—to optimize conflicting goals collaboratively.

I-A

Agentic AI for 6G

Recent studies have explored the use of agentic AI for autonomous network management, each advancing the field from different perspectives. The LINKs framework introduced in
[
1
]
represents one of the few efforts to integrate LLMs with digital twin-based 6G management systems. It enables agents to interpret high-level intent and perform control actions, although its reasoning capabilities are limited and its interaction with real-time RAN telemetry remains underdeveloped. In parallel,
[
2
]
proposes a foundational framework that conceptualizes generative AI models as distributed agents capable of collaborative task planning across network elements. While it emphasizes emergent communication and semantic coordination, it currently lacks implementation specifics tied to telecom protocols or operational data. A more modular perspective is provided in
[
3
]
, which introduces a two-stage architecture that decouples cloud-based intent parsing from edge-level policy generation. This approach addresses scalability and latency concerns but is only validated in high-level simulations without real telecom data. Similarly,
[
4
]
explores agentic digital twins for cyber-physical system optimization using large generative models and a Model Context Protocol (MCP), demonstrating strong orchestration potential—but in urban logistics rather than telecom settings. Efforts to improve grounding and reduce hallucinations in agentic reasoning are presented in
[
5
]
, which proposes a retrieval-augmented architecture that integrates structured network knowledge into the reasoning loop. However, this remains a conceptual design without deployment in live systems. Lastly,
[
6
]
outlines an end-to-end architectural vision for embedding LLM agents within the O-RAN framework to automate the lifecycle management of xApps and rApps. While well-aligned with O-RAN modularity and interface specifications, it remains at the proposal stage without empirical validation or tested integration with RIC platforms.

I-B

Cognitive Distortions in Agentic Systems: A Review

As agentic systems gain traction in future 6G autonomous networks, understanding their susceptibility to cognitive distortions becomes critical. Inspired by classical human biases such as confirmation bias, recency effects, and groupthink
[
7
,
8
]
, recent research investigates how such distortions emerge within and across AI agents and how they may impact collective decision-making, fairness, and safety. An exhaustive work in this space is MindScope
[
9
]
, which provides a comprehensive dataset of 72 human cognitive biases and uses it to evaluate how LLM agents exhibit these distortions during multi-agent dialogue. The study highlights that even when individual models are aligned, agent-agent interactions can surface complex, latent biases such as the gambler’s fallacy or anchoring effects. Mitigation strategies, including retrieval augmented generation (RAG), structured debate, and reinforcement learning (RL)-based adjudication, show promise in reducing these effects. Furthering this, Liu et al.
[
10
]
explore how conversational echo chambers form in multi-agent setups. They show that agents engaging in iterative discussions tend to amplify biases, subtly shifting their positions over time due to consensus seeking behavior; a dynamic absent in single agent setups. This demonstrates that cognitive distortions can emerge through interaction, not just from pretrained knowledge. In a more structural approach, the Fairness in Agentic AI framework
[
11
]
addresses how systemic cognitive distortions can arise from decentralized agent collaboration. It links ethical alignment with incentive mechanisms and dynamic negotiation constraints, offering tools to reduce emergent bias and unfair treatment of users or tasks based on implicit reward design. Lastly, the Hidden Profile Benchmark
[
12
]
adapts a well-known social psychology task to the multi-agent LLM setting, showing that agents often fail to surface critical but hidden information held by individual peers; mirroring informational bias and groupthink in human teams. This exposes key vulnerabilities in reasoning diversity and underlines the need for diversity-aware memory or communication protocols. Together, these works reveal that cognitive distortions in agentic systems are not only inherited from pretraining data but actively emerge from interactions, memory usage, and decentralized coordination.

Studying the impact of cognitive biases on the performance of agentic AI-driven 6G autonomous systems is still to be considered to the best of our knowledge.

I-C

Contributions

This paper introduces a new 6G cross-domain RAN-Edge agentic negotiation framework featuring LLM-powered agents with conflicting goals. The agents use various tools such as DT and memory and through the reasoning process, various cognitive biases surface. The work then presents a novel unbiased memory that tackles temporal, confirmation and availability biases by learning from a wide spectrum of stored strategies via,

•

Semantic retrieval using Jaccard similarity for relevant past strategies,

•

Learning from failures—via amplified weighting for SLA violations and conflicts—and diversity enforcement, mitigating confirmation and availability biases, respectively.

•

Recency and primacy weighting with a slow decay to combat temporal biases, ensuring all relevant historical data remains salient,

•

Traffic-aware bolder inference of context-specific configurations, optimizing energy savings in low/medium traffic and latency in high traffic,

In the light of the unbiased memory gains, we finally demonstrate the negative impact of the biases inherent to vanilla memory designs on 6G performance.

II
RAN-Edge Agentic Framework

II-A

System Overview

The system model in Figure
1
represents a 6G network slice that spans both the Radio Access Network (RAN) and edge domains. The edge domain is characterized by its maximum CPU frequency
f_{\max}
and the number of bits processed per CPU cycle, denoted
U
. It hosts a containerized application server (APP) and a user-plane function (UPF), both deployed as cloud-native functions (CNFs). The RAN and edge domains are managed by separate autonomous agents operating as LLM-based entities, each optimizing for distinct objectives: the RAN agent seeks to minimize energy consumption by dynamically adjusting the allocated bandwidth—thereby influencing the transmission rate and queuing delay at the radio interface—while the edge agent aims to reduce computation latency by tuning the CPU frequency allocated to the UPF.

Each agent operates within a structured interaction loop comprising three stages: i) Upon receiving messages from the opposing agent and the current system performance metrics, the agent consults a shared
Collective Memory
. This memory contains insights from past successful strategies and, critically, captures patterns associated with failures such as Service Level Agreement (SLA) violations or unresolved negotiations. ii) The agent then employs an internal
Digital Twin (DT)
to validate any proposed actions. This predictive model simulates the expected impact of a candidate decision on system performance, including latency and resource contention. A self-correction mechanism is embedded in this process: if a proposed action fails validation—e.g., due to exceeding SLA latency thresholds or triggering CPU over-allocation—the agent iteratively adjusts and re-tests its proposal until a compliant solution is identified or no viable alternative exists. iii) Finally, based on its objectives, the received information, memory-derived insights, and Digital Twin validation, the agent formulates a negotiation move. This move adheres to a strict, structured messaging protocol, enabling it to either propose a new resource configuration, accept a proposal from the other agent, or declare that no agreement is possible.
In this respect, an E2-like interface is adopted to enforce the decision into the simulated network environment, which differs from DT dynamics such as in terms of fluctuations in spectral efficiency. Consequently, decisions validated within the Digital Twin are not guaranteed to produce equivalent outcomes when executed via the E2-like interface.

During each discrete time interval
t
(
t=1,\dots,T
) of duration
\tau
, downlink packet requests arrive at the edge-located application server following a time-varying Poisson process:

\lambda_{t}=\max\{\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma),0\},

(1)

where
(\mu,\sigma)
are distribution parameters inferred from empirical traffic traces such as those presented in
[
13
]
.

Note that given the use of a remote LLM API for reasoning, the time scale in this work is slowed down. Therefore, we are just emulating what would happen if we had a local fast inference LLM suitable for near real-time scenarios, since the primary objective is to check the impact of reasoning and cognitive biases mitigation on the 6G performance.

II-B

Edge-RAN Queuing Model

The UPF is equipped with a computation queue for processing packets from the application server, which are then forwarded to the RAN central unit (CU). The CU implements a communication queue to manage transmission over the radio interface.

At each time step
t
, a number
U_{t}^{(a)}
of bits arrive at the UPF, governed by the traffic profile in (
1
). The computation queue evolves as:

Q_{t+1}^{(e)}=\max\left(0,Q_{t}^{(e)}-U_{t}^{(e)}\right)+U_{t}^{(a)},

(2)

where
U_{t}^{(e)}=\tau f_{t}U
denotes the number of bits processed during interval
t
at a CPU frequency
f_{t}
.

Subsequently, the RAN CU communication queue is updated according to:

\begin{split}Q_{t+1}^{(r)}&=\max\left(0,Q_{t}^{(r)}-U_{t}^{(r)}\right)\\
&\quad+\min\left(Q_{t}^{(r)},U_{t}^{(e)}\right),\end{split}

(3)

where
U_{t}^{(r)}
is the number of bits transmitted over the radio interface in interval
t
. This is computed as:

U_{t}^{(r)}=\tau\times C_{t},

(4)

with
C_{t}
being the instantaneous channel capacity. Assuming perfect channel state information (CSI) and full spatial multiplexing, the capacity is approximated as:

C_{t}\simeq\eta_{t}B_{t}N_{s},

(5)

where
\eta
is the spectral efficiency,
B
is the allocated bandwidth, and
N_{s}\sim\mathcal{O}(\min(N_{t},N_{r}))
denotes the spatial multiplexing gain, with
N_{t}
and
N_{r}
representing the numbers of transmit and receive antennas, respectively.

The total queuing state of the system is given by the sum of computation and communication queues:

Q_{t}=Q_{t}^{(e)}+Q_{t}^{(r)}.

(6)

Note that fronthaul and backhaul delays are neglected in this model due to their relatively constant and insignificant contributions.

II-C

End-to-End Latency

Using Little’s law
[
14
]
, the end-to-end latency is directly proportional to the average queue length. Let
\bar{U}^{(a)}=\mathbf{E}_{t^{\prime}}\left[U_{t^{\prime}}^{(a)}/\tau\right]
denote the average arrival rate. Then, the long-term end-to-end latency up to time
t
is:

L_{t}=L_{t}^{(e)}+L_{t}^{(r)},

(7)

where
L_{t}^{(e)}
and
L_{t}^{(r)}
represent the edge and RAN latencies, respectively:

L_{t}^{(e)}=\bar{U}^{(a)}\cdot\frac{1}{t}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}Q_{t^{\prime}}^{(e)},

(8a)

L_{t}^{(r)}=\bar{U}^{(a)}\cdot\frac{1}{t}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}Q_{t^{\prime}}^{(r)}.

(8b)

In this way, Little’s Law simplifies the complex interactions of queuing and processing into an interpretable metric—latency—that the autonomous agents can directly use to guide their negotiation and decision strategies.

II-D

Power Consumption

Assuming a reference power
P_{0}
per
B_{0}=20
MHz carrier, the power consumption at time
t
is simply expressed as,

P_{t}=\frac{B_{t}\times P_{0}}{B_{0}}.

(9)

III
A2A Cross-Domain Negotiation

Fig. 2
:
A2A cross-domain negotiation sequence diagram.

Figure
2
depicts the negotiation process between the RAN and Edge agents, which is an iterative, turn-based exchange as also exemplified in Listing
1
for the baseline scenario (without memory). As instigated by the different agents’ prompts (See Appendix), each round begins with an agent formulating a message, which is then transmitted to the opposing agent. This communication adheres to a strict Agent-to-Agent (A2A) interface, designed to facilitate clear and machine-readable interactions. The A2A structure requires a clear declaration of intent:
PROPOSE_ACTION
,
ACCEPT_AGREEMENT
, or
NO_AGREEMENT_POSSIBLE
. For
PROPOSE_ACTION
and
ACCEPT_AGREEMENT
intents, the A2A protocol mandates a JSON-formatted payload. This payload precisely specifies the proposed or accepted resource configurations, such as bandwidth and CPU frequency, along with a textual ’reason’ explaining the agent’s decision. The parsing of these messages is critical; a dedicated module rigorously validates the message structure against the defined protocol, extracting the intent and any associated parameters. This robust parsing mechanism is essential for maintaining the integrity and stability of the negotiation, as it identifies and flags any malformed messages, thereby preventing potential system errors or impasses.

Tool invocation is a dynamic and integral part of the agents’ reasoning process within this A2A framework. During their turn, agents, guided by their internal prompts and the current network state, dynamically decide whether to invoke external tools. For instance, an agent might invoke a collective memory tool to retrieve historical data on successful or failed strategies, or call upon the Digital Twin to predict the performance implications of a potential resource allocation. When the LLM generates a tool call, the system intercepts this call, executes the corresponding function (e.g., a query to the memory module or a simulation step on the Digital Twin), and then feeds the tool’s output directly back to the LLM. This closed-loop feedback mechanism allows the LLM to refine its understanding of the system’s state and the potential outcomes of its actions, enabling it to adjust its strategy and formulate a more informed and effective negotiation move. The Digital Twin, in particular, functions as an internal validation gate, compelling agents to self-correct and iterate on their proposals until critical performance constraints (e.g., SLA compliance, CPU allocation limits) are satisfied prior to formal presentation to the opposing agent.

If both agents propose an identical configuration or one agent accepts the other’s proposal, a consensus is reached. The agreed-upon configuration is then enforced, and the system’s performance metrics are updated accordingly. However, the negotiation can also lead to failures: either an agent explicitly declares
NO_AGREEMENT_POSSIBLE
after exhausting viable compromises, or the negotiation may remain unresolved if the maximum number of iterations is reached without consensus. Additionally, parsing failures, where an agent’s message does not conform to the expected structured format, can also lead to an immediate impasse, highlighting the importance of strict adherence to the A2A messaging protocol. The outcome of each negotiation, whether successful or failed, is then distilled and stored in the collective memory, providing valuable historical context for future interactions.

IV
A Novel Unbiased Memory Design

After recalling the definitions of the considered biases in our analysis, namely, recency/primacy, confirmation and availability biases, this section introduces the novel memory mechanisms embedded within the agentic negotiation framework to reduce those biases, which will have a direct impact on the cross-domain management performance.

IV-A

Definitions of the Considered Biases

Definition 1

(Confirmation Bias)
.

An LLM-powered agent selectively queries memory or APIs, or weights evidence favoring its prior hypothesis. Formally, if
S(\mathcal{D})
denotes a selection operator that filters data in favor of hypothesis
H
, then the posterior distribution becomes

p(H\mid S(\mathcal{D}))\propto p(H)\prod_{x\in S(\mathcal{D})}p(x\mid H),

(10)

which illustrates how selective evidence skews inference. In telecom networks, for instance, an agent that hypothesizes congestion may only retrieve logs consistent with that belief, thereby biasing its decision-making.

Definition 2

(Recency / Primacy Temporal Biases)
.

An LLM-powered agent may place disproportionate weight on either recent observations (recency) or initial observations (primacy) when interpreting memory. This can be modeled by a weighted estimator with temporal kernel
w_{t}
,

\hat{\theta}=\frac{\sum_{t}w_{t}x_{t}}{\sum_{t}w_{t}},

(11)

w_{t}\propto e^{-\nu(T-t)}\ \text{(recency)}\quad\text{or}\quad w_{t}\propto e^{-\nu t}\ \text{(primacy)},

(12)

so that either the most recent or earliest samples dominate the estimate. In telecom fault recovery, an agent may base its conclusion on only the last 10 minutes of telemetry or on the earliest samples, ignoring the longer-term trend.

Definition 3

(Availability Bias)
.

An LLM-powered agent tends to overestimate the probability of an event when examples of that event are easier to recall or retrieve from memory or tool logs. Formally, let
R\subseteq\mathcal{D}
denote the subset of data items actually retrieved, with retrieval probability
r(x)
biased toward salient items. The estimated probability of event
E
is then

\hat{p}(E)\approx\frac{|\{x\in R:x\text{ supports }E\}|}{|R|},

(13)

which deviates from the true
p(E)
whenever
R
is not a representative sample of the full dataset
\mathcal{D}
, where
|\cdot|
stands for the cardinality of a set.
In telecom scenarios, an agent may over-prioritize a recent outage alarm because it is highly visible in logs, even though chronic congestion is the more frequent problem.

\mathcal{M}

\mathcal{M}_{\text{raw}}
and a distilled memory repository
\mathcal{M}_{\text{distilled}}
. The first layer,
\mathcal{M}_{\text{raw}}

IV-C

Debiasing and Semantic Retrieval

To support context-aware decision-making, agents retrieve relevant memory entries by scoring candidate strategies using a composite semantic relevance function. The retrieval process starts by tokenizing the current context
\mathbf{c}_{\text{query}}
into a set of keywords
\mathcal{K}_{\text{query}}
, which may include terms such as
high traffic, latency,
or
SLA
. Similarly, each stored strategy
m_{t}
is associated with a keyword set
\mathcal{K}_{t}
extracted from its
\mathbf{c}_{t}
and
\mathbf{o}_{t}
. The semantic similarity score is computed using the Jaccard index,

\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{semantic}}=\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{\text{query}}\cap\mathcal{K}_{t}|}{|\mathcal{K}_{\text{query}}\cup\mathcal{K}_{t}|},

(14)

To control the temporal bias and prevent overemphasis on recent episodes, a temporal decay factor is applied. Let the age of entry
m_{t}
be defined as
\text{age}_{t}=t_{\text{query}}-t
. The temporal decay score is then given by,

\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{decay}}=e^{-\theta\cdot\text{age}_{t}},

(15)

where
\theta
is a tunable decay coefficient. In parallel, to counteract confirmation bias and the availability heuristic, a failure amplification mechanism assigns additional importance to negative outcomes. For each memory
m_{t}
, the inflection score is defined as,

\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{inflection}}=\begin{cases}1.0&\text{if }v_{t}=1,\\
0.5&\text{if the negotiation was unresolved},\\
0.0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(16)

Furthermore, to avoid repetitive selection of similar strategies and encourage behavioral diversity, a diversity penalty is introduced. Let
\mathcal{K}_{\text{selected}}
denote the union of keyword sets from all previously selected strategies. Then the diversity score is computed as,

\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{diversity}}=\gamma\cdot\frac{|\mathcal{K}_{t}\cap\mathcal{K}_{\text{selected}}|}{|\mathcal{K}_{t}\cup\mathcal{K}_{\text{selected}}|},

(17)

where
\gamma
is a diversity sensitivity parameter.
These components are combined into a weighted scoring function that balances relevance, timing, failure learning, and diversity. The base score used for memory ranking is defined as,

\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{base}}=\alpha\cdot\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{semantic}}+\beta\cdot\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{decay}}+\delta\cdot\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{inflection}},

(18)

where
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\delta
are relevance weights for each component. The final score for retrieval is computed as,

\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{final}}=\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{base}}-\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{diversity}}.

(19)

The entry with the highest score,
m^{*}=\arg\max_{t}\Phi^{(t)}_{\text{final}}
, is selected as the memory anchor for decision guidance.

IV-D

Parameters Impact

As shown above, the unbiased memory uses several key parameters to intelligently retrieve past strategies. The
\alpha
parameter weights the
semantic similarity
between the current query and past strategies, ensuring relevance.
\beta
controls the influence of time-decay, giving more importance to recent memories, while a smaller
\theta
(e.g.,
5.0
) makes the decay slower, thereby encouraging the retrieval of older, potentially still relevant, memories. The
\gamma
parameter introduces a diversity penalty, discouraging the selection of strategies that are too similar to those already chosen, thus promoting a broader range of insights. Finally,
\delta
applies an inflection bonus, giving higher scores to strategies associated with past failures (SLA violations or unresolved negotiations). This debiasing mechanism ensures that the system learns not just from successes, but crucially from its mistakes, helping to avoid repeating them.

IV-E

Inference from Retrieved Memory

The selected memory
m^{*}
is used to infer a new strategy
(\hat{B}_{t},\hat{f}_{t})
suited to the current context. For the RAN agent, bandwidth
\hat{B}_{t}
is reduced under low or medium traffic to maximize energy efficiency, while under high traffic, more conservative reductions are adopted to safeguard latency. For the edge agent, the CPU frequency
\hat{f}_{t}
is increased under high traffic to reduce processing delay, and moderately increased under lighter traffic to support the RAN agent’s energy-saving goals. Having said that, final decisions emanate from the agent-to-agent negotiation.

V-B

Unbiased Memory Validation

The empirical findings from our simulation underscore the critical role of unbiased memory in shaping agent learning and negotiation dynamics. Unlike baseline configurations-namely, standard (vanilla) memory and memory-less agents-the incorporation of unbiased memory does not primarily aim for minimum latency or maximum energy savings. Instead, its strength lies in promoting robustness and behavioral reliability by mitigating cognitive biases. As shown in Figure
4(a)
, agents using unbiased memory retrieve strategies with a significantly higher average age (mean:
10.07
trials ago, std:
12.05
), in contrast to those using standard memory (mean:
3.33
, std:
1.98
). This broader temporal reach is driven by the inclusion of the diversity penalty (
\gamma
) and the calibrated decay rate factor, both of which attenuate recency bias and encourage learning from a wider historical context rather than overemphasizing recent experience, since it contribute to the memory ranking as shown in Eq. (
18
). Furthermore, Figure

further highlights the system’s capacity to mitigate confirmation bias. Agents with standard memory retrieve approximately
5.45

1.78
, due to the inflection bonus parameter (
\delta
) amplifying the salience of failed strategies. This mechanism enables agents to internalize lessons from past mistakes, rather than disregarding them. Most notably, this improved cognitive balance translates into measurable behavioral advantages. As depicted in Figure
4(c)
, conflict occurrences during negotiation drop to zero shortly after the initial learning phase for agents using unbiased memory. This contrasts with persistent conflict patterns in the other scenarios.

Unbiased Memory Impact on Conflicts and SLA Violation Rate

The empirical results, summarized in Figure

This influence is most evident in the sharp reduction of conflicts and service-level agreement (SLA) violations, underscoring the practical utility of debiasing mechanisms in multi-agent coordination. As illustrated in Figure

This marked improvement validates the impact of cognitive debiasing—specifically, the negative outcome amplification factor (

This performance gap reveals a key behavioral distinction: agents with unbiased memory internalize not just the outcomes, but the reasons behind failures, leading to conservative, SLA-preserving decisions. In contrast, vanilla memory—shaped predominantly by recent successful but potentially unrepresentative experiences—may encourage riskier behaviors that sometimes result in SLA breaches. These findings illustrate how the unbiased memory framework fosters a safety-first orientation in agent planning, ultimately translating into more reliable system-wide behavior.

VI
Conclusion

This work introduced a framework for autonomous, cross-domain resource coordination in future radio access and edge networks, driven by specialized language model-based agents. Central to the proposed system is a novel unbiased collective memory design that addresses several cognitive distortions commonly observed in agentic systems, namely temporal, confirmation and availability biases. The results demonstrate that the unbiased memory system significantly improves negotiation outcomes, reducing unresolved negotiations by a factor of 4.5 compared to the memoryless setup and by a factor of 3.5 relative to the standard memory configuration. More importantly, agents utilizing the unbiased memory achieved a
0
% SLA violation rate. These gains are directly attributed to the core design principles of the memory architecture, which include emphasizing the importance of past failures and ensuring diversity in the recall of prior strategies in contrast to vanilla memory which tends to favor recent or successful but unrepresentative strategies.
