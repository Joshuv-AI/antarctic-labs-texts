Investigation of the estrous status in Thoroughbred mares coming to a stud farm for breeding
Authors: Yuko TOISHI, Nobuo TSUNODA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn the Thoroughbred industry, artificial insemination is prohibited. Veterinarians examine mares to determine the time to breed; however, some mares refuse breeding at the stud farm. Estrous status was investigated at breeding in 12,388 mares by measuring blood progesterone (P4) concentrations, and conception rates between mares in estrus and in the luteal phase were compared. Mares that refused breeding were examined by rectal palpation and P4 measurement to identify the causes of non-receptiveness. Mares with high P4 concentrations (≥2.0 ng/ml) showed lower conception rates, and 124 mares of the 177 mares that could not be bred had high P4. Measuring P4 concentrations is useful for evaluating mare estrous status and contributes to the safety of live cover in Thoroughbreds.Keywords: Thoroughbred, progesterone, mares, estrous status

In the Thoroughbred industry, artificial insemination is prohibited [4]. The number of breedings per day is usually limited to up to 4 mares, depending on the stallion. At each broodmare farm, owners of mares who plan to have their mares bred to popular stallions need to estimate the best day for breeding based on a veterinarian’s diagnosis and make a reservation for breeding a few days in advance. However, they cannot always choose the best breeding date, depending on the availability of the stallion. To prevent valuable Thoroughbred stallions from injury caused by aggressive behaviors of mares, all mares are exposed to teaser stallions before the breeding. Although all mares are supposedly confirmed to be at the proper time for breeding before coming to the stud farm, some mares refuse to be bred. In the luteal phase, progesterone is secreted from the corpus luteum, and mares will refuse stallions [1, 2]. This suggests that measurement of blood progesterone (P4) concentrations would be a useful for evaluating if mares are in the luteal phase. Few reports have investigated the actual estrous status in mares coming to a stud farm for breeding [13]. Therefore, the estrous statuses of mares in this study were evaluated by measuring P4 concentrations.

In the first investigation performed in this study, the P4 concentrations of 12,388 mares that came to a single Thoroughbred stud farm in Hokkaido, Japan, for breeding from 2013 to 2022 were measured by chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (CLEIA) with a PATHFAST immunoanalyzer (LSI Medience Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) to confirm their estrous status [15]. Blood samples were taken from mares with heparinized tubes for a physical checkup just before breeding. The use of these blood samples for measuring P concentrations to evaluate estrous status in mares was approved by both the stud farm and the owners of the mares. All mares were bred with no apparent rejection behavior, and P4 concentrations were measured within 12 hr of blood collection. For these measurements, 100 µl of blood samples were put into cartridges for CLEIA, and the P4 concentrations were measured automatically. The distribution of P4 concentrations in pregnant and non-pregnant mares is shown in Fig. 1. Mares with P4 concentrations ≥2.0 ng/ml were defined as being in the luteal phase, as defined in previous reports, and conception rates were compared between mares in estrus and those in the luteal phase [11, 16]. The conception rate of mares in estrus (52.24%) was significantly higher than that of those in the luteal phase (24.01%; χ2 analysis, P<0.01). These results are shown in Table 1. The limitation of this first investigation was that the estrous statuses of mares were evaluated only by P4 concentrations. Follicular size, the time of ovulation, and uterine edema were not evaluated. However, the conception rate in mares with P4≥2.0 ng/ml was significantly lower. This indicated that mares with high P4 concentrations (P≥2.0 ng/ml) were not at their proper time for breeding and that measuring P4 concentrations was useful for evaluating the estrous status in mares.

In this study, it was revealed that 5.11% of mares (633 out of 12,388 mares) were misdiagnosed as being in estrus. High P4 concentrations would have suppressed estrous behavior in these mares and made them non-receptive to stallions [1, 2]. However, those mares were able to be bred in spite of the high P4 concentrations (P4≥2.0 ng/ml). Because all mares had their noses twitched for the safety of the Thoroughbred stallions at breeding at this stud farm, the refusal behavior of the mares might have become non-visible [10]. Of the 633 mares bred in the luteal phase, 152 mares became pregnant. Fertile stallions produce spermatozoa with a longevity of 96 to 120 hr [17]. Mares that conceived while being bred in the luteal phase might have been at the end of the luteal phase. It was thought that P4 concentrations had decreased drastically at the end of the luteal phase and that ovulation had occurred within the spermatozoa lifetime [8].

Seventeen mares confirmed to have high P4 concentrations (P4≥2.0 ng/ml) were administered 7.5 mg of Prostaglandin F2α (PG; dinoprost, Zoetis Japan, Tokyo, Japan), and five of them became pregnant (data not shown). In a previous report, PG administration stimulated PG production from endometrial tissues [5]. It was suggested that both exogenous and endogenous PG induced luteolysis. In another report, PG stimulated LH secretion from the pituitary [14]. Therefore, ovulation must have occurred within the spermatozoa lifetime as a result of the PG administration. However, the effect of post-breeding PG in the luteal phase on conception rate requires further investigation because the follicular and uterine conditions were not followed up in this study.

In the second investigation performed in this study, mares that had refused to be bred were examined to confirm their estrous statuses by rectal palpation with ultrasonography, observation and palpation of the external cervical os and measurement of P4 concentrations. From 2013 to 2022, 177 mares that came to the same stud farm could not be bred because of their non-receptive attitudes toward stallions (Fig. 2). Although 53 of the 177 mares were diagnosed as being in estrus (large follicles and uterine edema were observed, external cervical os was soft and open, and P4 concentrations were below 2.0 ng/ml), they could not be bred. The possible causes were as follows: after ovulation (n=21), not at proper time to be bred (n=8), foal heat (n=5), maiden mare (first time for breeding, n=3), and unaccounted (n=16). Regarding the 21 post-ovulation mares, it was thought that ovulation had occurred within a day or two before breeding because it takes 24 to 48 hr for the corpora luteum to develop after ovulation [18]. A previous study reported that mares at their first ovulation in the breeding season had longer low P4 concentration periods than mares at their second ovulation [12]. In that study, mares showed estrous behavior for a prolonged period after ovulation while P4 concentrations were still low. The reason why the 21 mares in the current study were not receptive to stallions during the low P4 concentration period after ovulation is unknown. Ovulation had not yet occurred in 32 of the 53 mares. Regarding the 8 mares that were not at the proper time to be bred, they were not receptive to stallions but were in estrus with a large follicle and uterine edema. Four of these 8 mares were able to be bred the next day. In a previous report, the duration of sexual receptivity during estrus has been shown to vary among individual mares [6, 7]. It was thought that these 8 mares had estrous signs for only a very short period in estrus. Regarding the 5 mares that were not receptive to stallions at foal heat, the mares became receptive to stallions and breedable at the second heat. From past experience, the authors noted that some mares show fewer estrous signs in foal heat than in their second or later estrus. Regarding the 3 maiden mares, it has been reported that inexperienced maiden mares do not show estrous behavior even when they have developed dominant follicles, and this condition is called “silent heat” [9].

The other 124 of the abovementioned 177 mares that could not be bred were not in estrus (P4 concentrations ≥2.0 ng/ml). Among them, 15 of the mares were evaluated only by measuring P4 concentrations and were not examined by rectal palpation. The remaining 109 mares were examined by rectal palpation and observation of external cervical os in addition to measuring P4 concentrations. The examination findings are shown in Fig. 2. In 65 mares examined by ultrasonography, the corpus luteum was present and there was no uterine edema, although developed dominant follicles (≥3.5 cm) were present (Fig. 3). Palpation revealed that the external cervical os was closed and not soft in these mares. It was thought that these dominant follicles originated from a secondary follicular wave. The examination findings, including no estrous signs, no edema in the uterus, presence of a corpus luteum, and a tightly closed external cervical os with developed dominant follicles, were consistent with a previous report referring to large follicles in the luteal phase [3]. To prevent mares in the luteal phase being misdiagnosed as being in estrus, it is important for veterinarians to observe by ultrasonography not only dominant follicles but also uterine edema and the absence of a corpus luteum. It is also important to check the cervical conditions when diagnosing mares for the proper time for breeding.

Ultrasonographic images obtained during rectal palpation are shown for one mare that refused to be bred. In this mare, a large follicle (≥3.5 cm) was observed in the right ovary, and a corpus luteum was also observed in the left ovary. uterine edema was not observed.

In this study, P4 concentrations were measured by CLEIA. It took only 26 min to measure. This rapid measurement is very helpful for evaluating the estrous status of mares coming to a stud farm.

In conclusion, it was determined that 5.11% of the mares coming to this one Thoroughbred stud farm were in the luteal phase and had been misdiagnosed as being in estrus. The conception rate in those mares was significantly lower than that of mares in estrus. Measuring P4 concentrations is very useful for evaluating estrus status in mares. It should help in conducting safe breeding at Thoroughbred stud farms by detecting mares in the luteal phase.
