Scattering of the defocusing Calogero–Moser derivative nonlinear Schrödinger equation
Xi Chen

Abstract
We consider the defocusing Calogero–Moser derivative nonlinear Schrödinger equation (CM-DNLS) i ​ ∂ t u + ∂ x 2 u − 2 ​ Π ​ D ​ ( | u | 2 ) ​ u = 0 , ( t , x ) ∈ ℝ × ℝ . \displaystyle i\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}^{2}u-2\Pi D\left(|u|^{2}\right)u=0,\hskip 10.00002pt(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}. Using the Gérard-type explicit formula, we prove the scattering result of solutions to this equation with initial data in L + 2 , α ​ ( ℝ ) := { u ∈ L + 2 ​ ( ℝ ) : | x | α ​ u ∈ L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) } L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}):=\{u\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):|x|^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\} with some α > 0 \alpha>0 . \Pi(f)(x):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\xi x}\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

1.1
The Lax pair

In this paper, we denote by
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
the Hardy space corresponding to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
functions having a Fourier transform supported in the domain
\xi\geq 0
. Also, we denote by
L_{+}^{p}(\mathbb{R})
(
1\leq p\leq\infty
) the space corresponding to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
functions having a Fourier transform supported in the domain
\xi\geq 0
. Recall that the space
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
identifies to holomorphic functions on the upper-half plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x+iy)|^{2}dx<+\infty.

The Riesz-Szegő projector
\Pi
is the orthogonal projector from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
onto
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is given by

\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},\hskip 9.24994pt\Pi f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{iz\xi}\widehat{f}(\xi)d\xi=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(y)}{y-z}dy.

(1.4)

The Toeplitz operator
T_{b}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
associated to a function
b\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is defined by

\displaystyle T_{b}f:=\Pi(bf),\hskip 9.24994pt\forall f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

For
u\in H_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, the Lax operator
L_{u}
corresponding to (
1.1
) is defined by

\displaystyle\forall f\in\operatorname{Dom}\left(L_{u}\right)=H_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}),\hskip 9.24994ptL_{u}f:=Df+uT_{\bar{u}}f\text{ with }D:=\frac{1}{i}\frac{d}{dx}.

For
u\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the Lax operator
L_{u}
can be defined via an approach using quadratic forms with
\operatorname{Dom}(L_{u})\subset H_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
(see also
[
12
, Appendix A]
).

For
u\in H_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, another operator in the Lax pair, denoted by
B_{u}
, is defined by

\displaystyle B_{u}=-uT_{\partial_{x}\bar{u}}+\partial_{x}uT_{\bar{u}}+i\left(uT_{\bar{u}}\right)^{2}.

Also, we recall the definition of
X^{*}
in
[
15
]
,

\forall f\in\operatorname{Dom}\left(X^{*}\right):=\left\{f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\exists\lambda_{f}\in\mathbb{C}:xf+\lambda_{f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right\}=\left\{f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\widehat{f}\in H^{1}(0,\infty)\right\},

(1.5)

with

\widehat{X^{*}f}(\xi):=i\frac{d}{d\xi}[\widehat{f}(\xi)]\mathbf{1}_{\xi>0}.

Here
X^{*}
is the adjoint of the operator of multiplication by
x
on
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\left(\operatorname{Dom}\left(X^{*}\right),-iX^{*}\right)
is maximally dissipative. Notice that
-iX^{*}
is the infinitesimal generator of the adjoint semi–group of contractions
\left(S(\eta)^{*}\right)_{\eta\geq 0}
with

\displaystyle\forall\eta\geq 0,\hskip 9.24994ptS(\eta)^{*}=\mathrm{e}^{-i\eta X^{*}}.

We also notice that

\displaystyle\forall f\in\operatorname{Dom}\left(X^{*}\right),\left|\widehat{f}\left(0_{+}\right)\right|^{2}=-4\pi\operatorname{Im}\left\langle X^{*}f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 4\pi\|X^{*}f\|_{L^{2}}\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore, we can define

\forall f\in\operatorname{Dom}\left(X^{*}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptI_{+}(f):=\widehat{f}\left(0_{+}\right).

(1.6)

Moreover, we can easily deduce that

\forall f\in\operatorname{Dom}\left(X^{*}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptX^{*}f=xf+\frac{1}{2i\pi}I_{+}(f).

(1.7)

1.2
Maximally dissipative operators and the Kato-Rellich theorem

In this paper, we will also apply the Miyadera–Gustafson theorem to show that operators remain maximally dissipative after some perturbations. To present the Miyadera–Gustafson theorem for maximally dissipative operators, we first need to introduce the following definition of the dissipative and maximally dissipative operators in Banach spaces.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(D(A),A)
be an operator in a Banach space
\mathscr{B}
.

1. We say that
A
is dissipative if for all
g\in D(A)
and all
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\|(\lambda I-A)g\|\geq\lambda\|g\|.

2. We say that
A
is maximal dissipative if it is dissipative and for all
h\in\mathscr{B}
and for all
\lambda>0
, there exists
g\in D(A)
such that
(\lambda I-A)g=h
.

Remark 1.2
.

In fact, an operator
(D(A),A)
in a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
is dissipative if and only if for all
g\in D(A),\,\Re\langle Ag,g\rangle\leq 0
.

Remark 1.3
.

Let
(D(A),A)
be a maximally dissipative operator in a Banach space
\mathscr{B}
. From the definition of maximally dissipative operators, we can deduce that, for all
\lambda>0
, we have

\|(\lambda I-A)^{-1}\|_{\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{B})}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda},\hskip 18.49988pt\|A(\lambda I-A)^{-1}\|_{\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{B})}\leq 2.

(1.8)

Since the Miyadera–Gustafson theorem involves the related notion of the perturbation of operators, we give the following definition of the relative bound of an operator with respect to another operator.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\left(D(A),A\right)
and
\left(D(B),B\right)
be densely defined linear operators on a Banach space
\mathscr{B}
. Suppose that:

(i)
D(A)\subset D(B)
;

(ii) For some
a
and
b
in
\mathbb{R}
and all
\varphi\in D(A)
,

\displaystyle\|B\varphi\|\leq a\|A\varphi\|+b\|\varphi\|.

Then
B
is said to be
A
-bounded. The infimum of such
a
is called the relative bound of
B
with respect to
A
. If the relative bound is
0
, we say that
B
is infinitesimally small with respect to
A
.

Then we state the Miyadera–Gustafson theorem for maximally dissipative operators.

Theorem 1.5

(Miyadera–Gustafson theorem)
.

Let
\left(D(A),A\right)
be a maximally dissipative operator which is densely defined on a Banach space
\mathscr{B}
and assume
\left(D(B),B\right)
to be dissipative and
A
-bounded with the relative bound smaller than 1. Then
\left(D(A),A+B\right)
is also a maximally dissipative operator.

We refer to
[
25
]
for the proof of the Miyadera–Gustafson theorem.

1.5
Main result

In this paper, we used the Gérard-type explicit formula (

) to study the long-time behavior of (

1.1

\alpha>0
.

Theorem 1.9
.

Let
u_{0}\in L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}):=\{w\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):|x|^{\alpha}w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\}

. Then the solution

to the defocusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.1
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
satisfies

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\left\|u(t)-\mathrm{e}^{i\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=0.

(1.10)

Here
\widehat{v_{0}}(\xi)=\widetilde{u_{0}}(\xi)
for every
\xi>0
with
\widehat{v_{0}}(\xi)=0
for every
\xi<0
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{d}(f)=\widetilde{f},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

is the distorted Fourier transform assciated with
L_{u_{0}}
(see Section
2.1
and Section
2.2
for details).

Remark 1.10
.

As mentioned in the introduction, Kim and Kwon proved the scattering result for solutions to (
1.3
) in
H^{1,1}(\mathbb{R})

[
21
]
. More precisely, their appraoch relies on the pseudo-conformal symmetry, and thus requires

. Also, it is expected that we can follow their approach to show the scattering result for the defocusing (CM-DNLS) (

) in

. The great progress we made in this paper is to extend the chiral scattering result to more singular chiral initial data in
L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
with some
\alpha>0
. On the other hand, we can characterize the scattering term using the distorted Fourier transform associated with the Lax operator
L_{u_{0}}
of the defocusing (CM-DNLS) (

). In fact, following the method used in this paper, combined with the analogous result on the distorted Fourier transform associated with the focusing (CM-DNLS) (

)

11

, we can also establish the scattering result for solutions to the focusing (CM-DNLS) (

with some
\alpha>0
, which is an extension of the chiral scattering result for the focusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.3
) in
[
21
]
. See Remark
3.2
for details. Another highlight of this paper is that it is one of the first works that apply the Gérard-type explicit formula to study the long-time behavior of an integrable equation for a broad class of initial data, beyond the previously studied rational cases. See also
[
6
]
for the study on the long-time behavior of the Benjamin–Ono equation with rational initial data and

]

In

21
]
, Kim and Kwon also showed the soliton resolution for global solutions to the focusing (CM-DNLS) (

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we study the generalized eigenfunctions and the distorted Fourier transform associated with the Lax operator
L_{u}
of (
1.1
). For
u\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, it is known that
L_{u}
has no eigenvalue in this case. Under some additional decay assumption on
u\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we show the existence of the generalized eigenfunctions in
L_{+}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and then introduce the corresponding distorted Fourier transform. See also
[
11
, Section 4]
for the analogous result on the distorted Fourier transform associated with the focusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.3
).

2.1
Generalized eigenfunctions

First we show that if
u\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, the Lax operator
L_{u}
has no eigenvalue.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
L_{u}
has no eigenvalues.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in H_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{R})
be an eigenvector of
L_{u}
. From the proof of
[
12
, Proposition 5.1]
, we can infer

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi}|\langle\psi,u\rangle|^{2}=0.

Thus
\psi=0
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Using the Weyl’s theorem, we can easily conclude that
\sigma_{ess}(L_{u})=\sigma_{ess}(D)=[0,\infty)
. Since
L_{u}
has no eigenvalues, we can conclude that
\sigma(L_{u})=\sigma_{ac}(L_{u})\cup\sigma_{sc}(L_{u})=[0,\infty)
.

Remark 2.3
.

For the Lax operator of the focusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.3
), if
\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<2\pi
, we can also infer that this Lax operator has no eigenvalues.

We now give a fundamental result concerning the generalized eigenfunctions of
L_{u}
, which constitute the foundation of the distorted Fourier transform.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
u\in L^{2,\alpha}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
with some
\alpha>0
. For every
\lambda>0
, the space

\mathscr{M}_{u}(\lambda)=\bigl\{\,m\in L^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):\;(L_{u}-\lambda)m=0\ \text{in the distributional sense}\bigr\}

is one–dimensional. Every element
m\in\mathcal{M}_{u}(\lambda)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}m(x).

has a limit
\ell_{m}(\pm\infty)\text{ as }x\rightarrow\pm\infty
. Furthermore,
\ell_{m}(-\infty)
characterizes
m
and

\displaystyle\left|\ell_{m}(+\infty)\right|=\left|\ell_{m}(-\infty)\right|.

Proof.

Notice that, if
m\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\Pi(\bar{u}m)\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

so that
uT_{\bar{u}}m\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Equation
(L_{u}-\lambda)m=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle D\left(\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}m(x)\right)=-\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}u(x)\Pi(\bar{u}m)(x),

which implies that

\displaystyle D\left(\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}m(x)\right)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}),

hence
\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}m(x)
has a limit
\ell_{m}(\pm\infty)
as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle i\left(\left|\ell_{m}(+\infty)\right|^{2}-\left|\ell_{m}(-\infty)\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}[m(x)\overline{Dm(x)}-Dm(x)\overline{m(x)}]dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[-m(x)\overline{uT_{\bar{u}}m(x)}+uT_{\bar{u}}m(x)\overline{m(x)}\right]dx

\displaystyle=-\left\|T_{\bar{u}}m\right\|^{2}+\left\|T_{\bar{u}}m\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

This proves
\left|\ell_{m}(+\infty)\right|=\left|\ell_{m}(-\infty)\right|
. It remains to prove that the space of such functions
m
is one dimensional.

Given
\ell\in\mathbb{C}
, the problem
\left(L_{u}-\lambda\right)m=0,\ell_{m}(-\infty)=\ell
is equivalent to

m=\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}a,\hskip 9.24994pta-K_{\lambda}(a)=\ell,

(2.1)

where
K_{\lambda}:L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is defined as

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(a)(x):=-i\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda\cdot}a\right)(y)dy.

We claim that
1-K_{\lambda}:L^{\infty}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
is bijective. We shall use the Fredholm alternative by showing that
K_{\lambda}
is compact and
1-K_{\lambda}
is injective.

Lemma 2.5
.

The operator
K_{\lambda}:L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is compact.

Proof.

If
\|a\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
,
\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}a\right)
belongs to a bounded subset of
L^{1}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\left|K_{\lambda}(a)(x)-K_{\lambda}(a)\left(x^{\prime}\right)\right|\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}}\left(\int_{x}^{x^{\prime}}|u(y)|^{2}dy\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\hskip 9.24994ptx\leq x^{\prime}

\displaystyle\left|K_{\lambda}(a)(x)\right|\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{x}|u(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\left|K_{\lambda}(a)(x)-K_{\lambda}(a)(+\infty)\right|\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}}\left(\int_{x}^{+\infty}|u(x)|^{2}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

For all these reasons, the set

\displaystyle\left\{K_{\lambda}(a),\|a\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\right\}

is relatively compact in
L^{\infty}
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

The operator
\operatorname{Id}-K_{\lambda}:L^{\infty}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
is injective.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}
such that
f=K_{\lambda}(f)
. Let us prove that
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. First, we observe that

\displaystyle m(x):=\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}f(x)

satisfies
\left(L_{u}-\lambda\right)m=0
and
\ell_{m}(-\infty)=0
, therefore
\ell_{m}(+\infty)=0
, or equivalently

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}f\right)(y)dy=0,

so that

\displaystyle f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}f\right)dy=-\int_{x}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}f\right)(y)dy.

Let
\alpha>0
such that
|x|^{\alpha}u\in L^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle g(x):=\bar{u}(x)\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}f(x)

satisfies
\left(1+|x|^{\alpha}\right)g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, which is equivalent to
\widehat{g}\in H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
. By Hardy’s inequality,
\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\widehat{g}\in H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
, or
\left(1+|x|^{\alpha}\right)\Pi g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. This implies

\displaystyle|y|^{2\alpha}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}f\right)(y)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}),

and consequently,

\displaystyle|x|^{2\alpha}|f(x)|\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}).

If
\alpha>\frac{1}{4}
, this implies
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Otherwise, we plug this information in the above argument. More generally, if
|x|^{\beta}f\in L^{\infty}
for some
\beta\geq 0
, we have
\left(1+|x|^{\alpha+\beta}\right)f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, hence, again by Hardy’s inequality,

\displaystyle\left(1+|x|^{\gamma}\right)g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

with
\gamma=\alpha+\beta
if
\alpha+\beta<\frac{1}{2}
, and
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon>0
if
\alpha+\beta\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle|x|^{\alpha+\gamma}f\in L^{\infty}

which leads to
f\in L^{2}
after a finite number of iterations. Since

\displaystyle m(x)=\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}f(x),

we infer that
m\in L_{+}^{2}\text{, and finally }m\in H_{+}^{\frac{1}{2}}
is an eigenvector of
L_{u_{0}}
. Since the kernel of
L_{u}-\lambda
is trivial, we conclude
m=0
.
∎

Now we can conclude the proof of Proposition
2.4
. By the Fredholm alternative, the operator
\operatorname{Id}-K_{\lambda}:L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is bejective. Coming back to (
2.1
), the linear form

\displaystyle m\in\mathscr{M}_{u}(\lambda)\mapsto\ell_{m}(-\infty)\in\mathbb{C}

is bijective. Therefore
\mathscr{M}_{u}(\lambda)
is one dimensional.
∎

If
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we can also conclude the limit of
m(x,\lambda)
as
\lambda\to 0_{+}
, which is useful in the latter proof.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
\ell_{m}(-\infty)=1
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0_{+}}m(x,\lambda)=1.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=0
in (
2.1
), then following the previous operations, we can also prove that the solution to (
2.1
) with
\lambda=0
exists and is unique. It is not hard to see that
m=a=1
is the solution to (
2.1
) with
\lambda=0
and
\ell=1
. Now we define
K_{0}:=K_{\lambda}|_{\lambda=0}
, and we can easily infer that

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),\hskip 9.24994pt\lim_{\lambda\to 0_{+}}\|(K_{\lambda}-K_{0})f\|_{L^{\infty}}=0.

Then from the second resolvent identity, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|a(\cdot,\lambda)-1\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\left\|(\operatorname{Id}-K_{\lambda})^{-1}1-(\operatorname{Id}-K_{0})^{-1}1)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\rightarrow 0\text{ as }\lambda\to 0_{+}.

Since for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}\to 1
as
\lambda\to 0_{+}
, we can conclude the lemma.
∎

2.2
The distorted Fourier transform

From Proposition
2.4
, for
u\in L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
with some
\alpha>0
, there exists a
unique
m_{-}\in\mathscr{M}_{u}(\lambda)
such that
\ell_{m_{-}}(-\infty)=1
. Given
f\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we can define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{d}f(\lambda):=\widetilde{f}(\lambda):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\overline{m_{-}(x,\lambda)}dx,\hskip 9.24994pt\lambda>0.

If moreover
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}((0,+\infty))
. Denote by
\mathcal{H}_{c}\left(L_{u}\right)
the continuous space of
L_{u}
, namely the space of those
f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
having a spectral measure with no pure point part. Denote by
P_{c}(L_{u})
the orthogonal projector from
L_{+}^{2}
to
\mathcal{H}_{c}\left(L_{u}\right)
.

Proposition 2.8
.

The operator
f\mapsto\widetilde{f}
extends to
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with the identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f(x)\right|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|P_{c}(L_{u})f(x)\right|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}|\widetilde{f}(\lambda)|^{2}d\lambda.

(2.2)

Proof.

From the spectral theorem and the fact that
L_{u}
has no eigenvalue, we have, for every
f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

\|f\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\left\|P_{c}(L_{u})f\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\left(L_{u}-\lambda-i\varepsilon\right)^{-1}f-\left(L_{u}-\lambda+i\varepsilon\right)^{-1}f,f\right\rangle d\lambda.

(2.3)

Set
g_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}(.,\lambda):=\left(L_{u}-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon\right)^{-1}f
. We have

\displaystyle g_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}=(D-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}\left(Id+uT_{\bar{u}}(D-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}\right)^{-1}f.

The following lemma can be proved along the same lines as the properties of
operator
K_{\lambda}
in the previous section.

Lemma 2.9
.

The following properties hold for every
\lambda\geq 0
, uniformly if
\lambda
belongs to a compact set of
[0,\infty)
.

1.
(D-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}:L^{1}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
is uniformly bounded and strongly convergent to
(D-\lambda\mp i0)^{-1}:L^{1}\rightarrow L^{\infty}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}
.

2.
uT_{\bar{u}}(D-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}:L^{1}\rightarrow L^{1}
is uniformly compact as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}
, and is convergent to
uT_{\bar{u}}(D-\lambda\mp i0)^{-1}:L^{1}\rightarrow L^{1}
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}
.

3.
\left(Id+uT_{\bar{u}}(D-\lambda\mp i\varepsilon)^{-1}\right)^{-1}:L^{1}\rightarrow L^{1}
is strongly convergent to

\displaystyle\left(Id+uT_{\bar{u}}(D-\lambda\mp i0)^{-1}\right)^{-1}:L^{1}\rightarrow L^{1}

as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0_{+}
.

Assume that
f\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. From this lemma, we infer that
g_{\pm}^{\varepsilon}
tends to
g_{\pm}
in
L^{\infty}
with

\displaystyle g_{+}(x,\lambda)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda(x-y)}f(y)dy-\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda(x-y)}u(y)\Pi_{+}\left(\bar{u}g_{+}(.,\lambda)\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle g_{-}(x,\lambda)=-\int_{x}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda(x-y)}f(y)dy+\int_{x}^{+\infty}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda(x-y)}u(y)\Pi_{+}\left(\bar{u}g_{-}(.,\lambda)\right)(y)dy

In particular, from Proposition
2.4
,
g_{+}-g_{-}=-\ell_{g_{-}}(-\infty)m_{-}
, with

\displaystyle-i\ell_{g_{-}}(-\infty)=\lim_{R\rightarrow+\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}\left(g_{-}\overline{Dm_{-}}-Dg_{-}\bar{m}_{-}\right)dx=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\bar{m}_{-}dx=-\widetilde{f}(\lambda)

and finally

\displaystyle g_{+}(x,\lambda)-g_{-}(x,\lambda)=i\widetilde{f}(\lambda)m_{-}(x,\lambda)

and, passing to the limit in the right hand side of (
2.3
) leads to (
2.2
) for
f\in L_{+}^{1}\cap L_{+}^{2}
. By density, the operator
f\mapsto\widetilde{f}
extends to
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with identity (
2.2
).
∎

Remark 2.10
.

By the polarization identity, the isometry property implies preservation of the inner product, i.e., for any
f,g\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we have

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\langle\widetilde{f},\widetilde{g}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}.

(2.4)

Remark 2.11
.

Notice that, if
f\in H_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\widetilde{L_{u}f}(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}L_{u}f(x)\overline{m_{-}(x,\lambda)}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\overline{L_{u}m_{-}(x,\lambda)}dx=\lambda\widetilde{f}(\lambda).

Applying the spectral theorem to the self-adjoint operator
L_{u}
restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{c}(L_{u})
, combined with Proposition
2.4
, we can infer the surjectivity of the distorted Fourier transform.

Corollary 2.12
.

The map
f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\mapsto\widetilde{f}\in L^{2}(0,\infty)
is onto.

Remark 2.13
.

From the the spectral theorem and Proposition
2.8
, we can also conclude that
\sigma(L_{u})=\sigma_{ac}(L_{u})=[0,\infty)
.

Using the interpolation between
\mathcal{F}_{d}:L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{\infty}(0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{F}_{d}:L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(0,\infty)
, we can also extend the distorted Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{d}
to
\mathcal{F}_{d}:L_{+}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow L^{p^{\prime}}\left(0,\infty\right)
for
1<p<2
.

Corollary 2.14
.

The distorted Fourier transform can be extended uniquely and continuously to

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{d}:L_{+}^{p}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\rightarrow L^{p^{\prime}}\left(0,\infty\right),\hskip 9.24994pt1\leq p\leq 2,

where

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1.

As is well known, the classical Fourier transform is a continuous bijection from
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
onto itself. Similarly, for the distorted Fourier transform defined above, if we restrict to
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, we obtain an analogous property.

Proposition 2.15
.

For
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, the distorted Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{d}:\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{*}:=\{f=\left.g\right|_{(0,\infty)}:g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and }\operatorname{supp}(g)\subset[0,\infty)\}
is well-defined and a continuous bijection.

Proof.

Let us recall (
2.1
) with
\ell=1
,

\displaystyle m_{-}=\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda x}a,\hskip 9.24994pta-K_{\lambda}(a)=1\text{ with }K_{\lambda}(a)(x):=-i\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda\cdot}a\right)(y)dy.

so we have

\displaystyle a=(Id-K_{\lambda})^{-1}(1).

Then

\displaystyle\partial_{x}a(x,\lambda)=-i\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}u(x)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda\cdot}a\right)(x)\in L_{x,\lambda}^{\infty}.

From the above expression, it is not hard to see that

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{+},\hskip 9.24994pt\partial_{x}^{n}a(x,\lambda)\in L_{x,\lambda}^{\infty}.

Also,

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}a(x,\lambda)

\displaystyle=(Id-K_{\lambda})^{-1}\partial_{\lambda}K_{\lambda}(Id-K_{\lambda})^{-1}(1)

\displaystyle=(Id-K_{\lambda})^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u[T_{\bar{u}},y]\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}a(y,\lambda)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i\pi}(Id-K_{\lambda})^{-1}\left(\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)\int_{-\infty}^{x}u(y)\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}dy\right),

here the last identity comes from

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{\infty},\hskip 9.24994ptu[T_{\bar{u}},y]f=\frac{u}{2i\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\bar{u}dx.

Since

\displaystyle\left|\lambda\widetilde{u}(\lambda)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\mathrm{e}^{-ix\lambda}\bar{a}(x,\lambda)dx\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\partial_{x}(\mathrm{e}^{-ix\lambda})\bar{a}(x,\lambda)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-ix\lambda}\bar{a}(x,\lambda)dx\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\mathrm{e}^{-ix\lambda}\partial_{x}\bar{a}(x,\lambda)dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq C,

we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\lambda}a\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{-1}.

Furthermore, it is not hard to conclude

\displaystyle\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{+},\hskip 9.24994pt\|\partial_{\lambda}a\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq C_{k}(1+|\lambda|)^{-k}.

Moreover, since

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\partial_{\lambda}a=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\widetilde{u}(\lambda)u(x)\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}-i\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}u(x)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda\cdot}\partial_{\lambda}a\right)(x),

we have

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}\partial_{\lambda}a\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\|\widetilde{u}(\lambda)u(x)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}+\|\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}u(x)\Pi\left(\bar{u}\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda\cdot}\partial_{\lambda}a\right)(x)\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{-1}.

It is not hard to conclude

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{+},\hskip 9.24994pt\|\partial_{x}^{n}\partial_{\lambda}a\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{-1}.

For higher
\lambda
–derivatives, differentiating repeatedly in
\lambda
produces sums of terms where derivatives fall on
\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y},\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}
and on
a
. Each time we gain a primitive like

\int_{-\infty}^{x}y^{k}u(y)e^{-i\lambda y}dy
(which decays faster than any power of
|\lambda|
since
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
),
\int_{\mathbb{R}}y^{k}\bar{u}(y)e^{i\lambda y}\partial_{\lambda}^{\ell}a(y,\lambda)dy
or
\partial_{\lambda}^{k}\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)
. Then we can follow the previous argument and use the recurrence to show that

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\text{ and }\forall m\in\mathbb{N}_{+},\hskip 9.24994pt\|\partial_{x}^{n}\partial_{\lambda}^{m}a\|_{L_{x}^{\infty}}\leq C(1+|\lambda|)^{-m}.

So
a
is a symbol of order 0, by the classical pseudo-differential operators theory, we can conclude that the distorted Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{d}:\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R}):=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\cap L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathcal{S}_{*}:=\{f=\left.g\right|_{(0,\infty)}:g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})\text{ and }\operatorname{supp}(g)\subset[0,\infty)\}
is well-defined and a continuous map. Proposition
2.8
implies the injection of
\mathcal{F}_{d}
. Since the distorted Fourier transform is a unitary operator, we can easily infer the surjection of
\mathcal{F}_{d}
from
\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
to
\mathcal{S}_{*}
.
∎

Corollary 2.16
.

For
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, the distorted Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{d}
extends uniquely and continuously
to the tempered distributions:

\mathcal{F}_{d}:\mathcal{S}_{+}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{S}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, via

\langle\mathcal{F}_{d}F,\ \phi\rangle:=2\pi\langle F,\ \mathcal{F}_{d}^{-1}\phi\rangle,\hskip 18.49988pt\forall F\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{+}(\mathbb{R}),\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{R}).

Proof.

By Proposition
2.15
we know that the operator

\mathcal{F}_{d}f(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\lambda x}\overline{a(x,\lambda)}f(x)\,dx

maps
\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
continuously onto
\mathcal{S}_{*}
.
Its inverse on
\mathcal{S}_{*}
is given by

\mathcal{F}_{d}^{-1}\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i\lambda x}a(x,\lambda)\,\varphi(\lambda)\,d\lambda,

which is continuous
\mathcal{S}_{*}\to\mathcal{S}_{+}
and satisfies

\mathcal{F}_{d}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{F}_{d}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{S}_{+}}
and
\mathcal{F}_{d}\circ\mathcal{F}_{d}^{-1}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathcal{S}_{*}}
.

For
F\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, we can then define the distorted Fourier transform of this distribution by

\langle\mathcal{F}_{d}F,\ \phi\rangle:=2\pi\langle F,\ \mathcal{F}_{d}^{-1}\phi\rangle,\hskip 18.49988pt\forall F\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{+}(\mathbb{R}),\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{R}).

Here
\mathcal{F}_{d}F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.
∎

2.3
Computation of the scattering term

We have already completed the distorted Fourier transform theory in the previous part. In this section, using the distorted Fourier transform, we try to find the relation between
X^{*}f
and
i\partial_{\lambda}\widetilde{f}
, and then we can compute the scattering term. First we go to analyze
\partial_{\lambda}a
. We know that, the problem
(L_{u}-\lambda)m_{-}=0,\ell_{m_{-}}(-\infty)=1
is equivalent to

\displaystyle m(\lambda,x)=e^{i\lambda x}a(x,\lambda),\hskip 9.24994pta(x,\lambda)=1+K_{\lambda}(a)(x),

where

\displaystyle K_{\lambda}(a)(x):=-i\int_{-\infty}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda y}u(y)\Pi\left(\bar{u}(y)\mathrm{e}^{i\lambda y}a(\lambda,y)\right)dy.

Again, we assume that
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
. We have
\partial_{\lambda}a
is well defined in
L_{+}^{\infty}
and

\displaystyle\partial_{\lambda}a=\frac{1}{2i\pi}\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-i\lambda y}u(y)dy+K_{\lambda}(\partial_{\lambda}a).

Denote
n(x,\lambda):=e^{i\lambda x}\partial_{\lambda}a
, we infer

\displaystyle L_{u}n-\lambda n=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)u(x).

Applying the distorted Fourier transform and Proposition
2.4
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\widetilde{n}(\mu,\lambda)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}p.v.\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}+c\delta(\mu-\lambda),

i.e.,

n(x,\lambda)=n_{1}(x,\lambda)+c\,m_{-}(x,\lambda),

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{n}_{1}(\mu,\lambda):=-\frac{1}{2\pi}p.v.\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}

and
c
to be determined.

To obtain
c
, we need to give the aysmptotic behavior of
\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}n_{1}(x,\lambda)
as
x\to-\infty
. For this purpose, we establish the following lemma.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}_{+}(\mathbb{R})
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-i\lambda x}n_{1}(x,\lambda)\longrightarrow\frac{i}{4\pi}|\widetilde{u}(\lambda)|^{2}\text{ as }x\rightarrow-\infty.

Proof.

By the classical Fourier transform theory, we know that

\displaystyle p.v.\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)e^{ix\mu}}{\mu-\lambda}d\mu

\displaystyle=p.v.\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{|\widetilde{u}(\lambda)|^{2}e^{ix\mu}}{\mu-\lambda}d\mu+p.v.\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)-\widetilde{u}(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}e^{ix\mu}d\mu

\displaystyle=e^{i\lambda x}|\widetilde{u}(\lambda)|^{2}\left[-\mathrm{Ci}(\lambda|x|)+i\operatorname{sgn}(x)\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\mathrm{Si}(\lambda|x|)\right)\right]+p.v.\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)-\widetilde{u}(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}e^{ix\mu}d\mu,

where

\displaystyle\operatorname{Si}(w)=\int_{0}^{w}\frac{\sin t}{t}dt,\hskip 9.24994pt\operatorname{Ci}(w)=-\int_{w}^{\infty}\frac{\cos t}{t}dt.

Since
\widetilde{u}\in\mathcal{S}_{*}
, we know that

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)-\widetilde{u}(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}\in L^{1}([0,\infty)),

so we infer that

\displaystyle p.v.\int_{0}^{\infty}\bar{\widetilde{u}}(\lambda)\frac{\widetilde{u}(\mu)-\widetilde{u}(\lambda)}{\mu-\lambda}e^{ix\mu}d\mu\longrightarrow 0\text{ as }x\rightarrow-\infty.

Thus we have

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u_{0}\in L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}):=\{w\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R}):|x|^{\alpha}w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\}
with some
\alpha>0
. Then the solution

to the defocusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.1
) with

Here
\widehat{v_{0}}(\xi)=\widetilde{u_{0}}(\xi)
for every
\xi>0
with
\widehat{v_{0}}(\xi)=0
for every
\xi<0
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{d}(f)=\widetilde{f},\hskip 9.24994pt\forall f\in L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

is the distorted Fourier transform assciated with
L_{u_{0}}

Proof.

First of all, the mass conservation law
\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
implies that
{u(t)}
is uniformly bounded in
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, hence it admits weakly convergent subsequences in
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as
t\to\infty
. We shall prove that the weak limit is unique.

Let us recall the formula (
2.19
) for
u_{0}\in L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
with some
\alpha>0
,

u(t,z)-\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{2i\pi}J_{+}\left[\left(i\partial_{\lambda}+B(\widetilde{u}_{0})+2t\lambda-z\right)^{-1}B(\widetilde{u}_{0})\left(i\partial_{\lambda}+2t\lambda-z\right)^{-1}\widetilde{u}_{0}\right].

(3.2)

We recall (
2.13
) for
u=u_{0}
and we rewrite it as

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}_{0}(t,\lambda):=(i\partial_{\lambda}+2t\lambda-z)^{-1}\widetilde{u}_{0}=i\,\mathrm{e}^{it\lambda^{2}-iz\lambda}\int_{\lambda}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-it\eta^{2}+iz\eta}\widetilde{u}_{0}(\eta)d\eta.

Then from (
2.20
) and (
3.2
), we infer

\displaystyle\left|u(t,z)-\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\left|J_{+}\left[\left(i\partial_{\lambda}+B(\widetilde{u}_{0})+2t\lambda-z\right)^{-1}B(\widetilde{u}_{0})\left(i\partial_{\lambda}+2t\lambda-z\right)^{-1}\widetilde{u}_{0}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\left\|\widetilde{u}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}\left\|\left(i\partial_{\lambda}+2t\lambda-z\right)^{-1}\widetilde{u}_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle C\left\|\widetilde{u}_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(0,\infty)}^{2}\|\widetilde{h}_{0}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\infty)}.

Since
z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
, Now we apply the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma and we can conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|\widetilde{h}_{0}(t)\|_{L_{\lambda}^{\infty}(0,\infty)}=0

Thus we deduce

\displaystyle\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},\hskip 9.24994ptu(t,z)-\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}(z)\to 0\text{ as }t\to\infty.

If
u(t_{n})\rightharpoonup w
in
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
as

, then for every

, we have

\displaystyle u(t_{n},z)-w(z)\to 0\text{ as }t_{n}\to\infty.

Therefore we can deduce

and the weak limit is unique, i.e.,

\displaystyle u(t)\rightharpoonup\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}\text{ in }L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\text{ as }t\to\infty.

Since
\|u(t)\|_{L^{2}}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}=\|\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}

L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The proof is complete.

z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
,

u(t,z)=\frac{1}{2i\pi}I_{+}\left[\left(X^{*}+2tL_{u_{0}}-z\mathrm{Id}\right)^{-1}\varphi\right]+\frac{1}{2i\pi}I_{+}\left[\left(X^{*}+2tL_{u_{0}}-z\mathrm{Id}\right)^{-1}P_{ac}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}\right].

(3.5)

For the second term above, following the approach in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we can show that

\displaystyle\forall z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},\hskip 9.24994pt\frac{1}{2i\pi}I_{+}\left[\left(X^{*}+2tL_{u_{0}}-z\mathrm{Id}\right)^{-1}P_{ac}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}\right]\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}(z),

where
\widehat{v_{0}}(\xi)=\widetilde{u_{0}}(\xi)
for every
\xi>0
with
\widehat{v_{0}}(\xi)=0
for every
\xi<0
. From this we can deduce the sacttering term of the focusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.3
). Notice that if
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<2\pi
,
L_{u_{0}}
has no eigenvalues. In this case
P_{p}(L_{u_{0}})=0
and
P_{ac}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}=u_{0}
, so from the above argument we can directly deduce the scattering of the focusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.3
) for
u_{0}\in L_{+}^{2,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})\cap\{\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}<2\pi\}
with some
\alpha>0
.

Now we analyse the first term on the right hand side in (
3.5
). Following the method used in the proof of
[
9
, Lemma 3.1]
, we can also deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2i\pi}I_{+}\left[\left(X^{*}+2tL_{u_{0}}-z\mathrm{Id}\right)^{-1}P_{p}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}\right]=U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}P_{p}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}(z).

and

\displaystyle u(t)=U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0}.

Since
\left\{\varphi_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
are the normalized eigenfunctions of
L_{u_{0}}
, we know that

\displaystyle U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}P_{p}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}\varphi_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-it\lambda_{j}^{2}}U(t)\varphi_{j}.

Here the unitary operator
U(t)
is defined by the linear initial value problem in
\mathscr{L}\left(L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\right)
,

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(t)=B_{u(t)}U(t),\hskip 9.24994ptU(0)=\operatorname{Id}\text{ with }B_{u}:=uT_{\partial_{x}\bar{u}}-\partial_{x}uT_{\bar{u}}+i(uT_{\bar{u}})^{2}.

From the Lax identity
L_{u(t)}U(t)=U(t)L_{u_{0}}
, we can infer that
\phi_{j}(t):=\mathrm{e}^{-it\lambda_{j}^{2}}U(t)\varphi_{j}
is the normalized eigenfunctions of
L_{u(t)}
. Thus we have

\displaystyle U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}P_{p}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\phi_{j}(t).

So we have

u(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\phi_{j}(t)+\mathrm{e}^{it\partial_{x}^{2}}v_{0}+o(1)

(3.6)

in
L_{+}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Notice that (
3.6
) is exactly the asymptotic bound-state/radiation decomposition for the global solution
u(t)
to the focusing (CM-DNLS) (
1.3
). In fact, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}u_{0},U(t)\mathrm{e}^{-itL_{u_{0}}^{2}}\varphi_{j}\right\rangle=\left\langle u(t),\phi_{j}(t)\right\rangle,

therefore
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle\phi_{j}(t)=P_{p}(L_{u(t)})(u(t))
. Moreover, from
[
11
, Lemma 3.7]
, we know that for every
1\leq j\leq N
,

\displaystyle|\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle|^{2}=2\pi.

Then we can deduce that

\displaystyle\|v_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|P_{ac}(L_{u_{0}})u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\left\langle u_{0},\varphi_{j}\right\rangle|^{2}=\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2\pi N,

which coincides with one of the statements in
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Remark 3.3
.

From the soliton resolution result in
[
21
, Theorem 1.1]
and Remark
3.2
, we are naturally led to the following conjecture: as
t\to\infty
,
\sqrt{2\pi}\phi_{j}(t)
should asymptotically converge to a traveling soliton profile.
