Complex variational autoencoders admit Kähler structure
Andrew Gracyk

Abstract
It has been discovered that latent-Euclidean variational autoencoders (VAEs) admit, in various capacities, Riemannian structure. We adapt these arguments but for complex VAEs with a complex latent stage. We show that complex VAEs reveal to some level Kähler geometric structure. Our methods will be tailored for decoder geometry. We derive the Fisher information metric in the complex case under a latent complex Gaussian regularization with trivial relation matrix. It is well known from statistical information theory that the Fisher information coincides with the Hessian of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. Thus, the metric Kähler potential relation is exactly achieved under relative entropy. We propose a Kähler potential derivative of complex Gaussian mixtures that has rough equivalence to the Fisher information metric while still being faithful to the underlying Kähler geometry. Computation of the metric via this potential is efficient, and through our potential, valid as a plurisubharmonic (PSH) function, large scale computational burden of automatic differentiation is displaced to small scale. We show that we can regularize the latent space with decoder geometry, and that we can sample in accordance with a weighted complex volume element. We demonstrate these strategies, at the exchange of sample variation, yield consistently smoother representations and fewer semantic outliers.



Key words.
Variational autoencoder, VAE, geometric VAE, geometric AI, complex geometry, Kähler geometry, Kähler potential, statistics on manifolds, Fisher information, Fisher metric, complex Gaussian

5.2
The Fisher information metric and the Hessian of the KL divergence as a Kähler potential

It is well known in statistical theory that the Fisher information is the Hessian of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. Let the Fisher information with respect to the paramater be defined as

\displaystyle I=\mathbb{E}\Big[(\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x))(\nabla_{\theta}\log p_{\theta}(x))^{T}\Big].

(58)

As Riemann integral and Lebesgue integrals respectively, the KL divergence is

\displaystyle\text{KL}(p_{\theta}(x)\parallel p_{\theta^{\prime}}(x))=\int p_{\theta}(x)\log\frac{p_{\theta}(x)}{p_{\theta^{\prime}}(x)}dx=\int\log\frac{dP_{\theta}}{dP_{\theta^{\prime}}}dP_{\theta}.

(59)

In particular, it is known
44

\displaystyle I=\text{Hess}_{\theta^{\prime}}\text{KL}(p_{\theta}(x)\parallel p_{\theta^{\prime}}(x))\Bigg|_{\theta^{\prime}=\theta}.

(60)

However, a Kähler potential necessitates

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}K(z,\overline{z}),

(61)

i.e. the partial derivative is with respect to
z
coordinate, whereas the Fisher information derivatives are taken with respect to the neural network parameters in the above. Thus, if we take
z
as the parameter in the above, we have

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}=\text{Hess}_{z^{\prime},\alpha,\overline{\beta}}\text{KL}(p(x|z)\parallel p(x|z^{\prime}))\Bigg|_{z^{\prime}=z}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\text{KL}(p(x|z)\parallel p(x|z^{\prime}))\Bigg|_{z^{\prime}=z}=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}K(z,\overline{z}),

(62)

and so we take our Kähler potential as

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0.5469,0,0.5469}\boxed{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rule{0.0pt}{14.22636pt}\;\ \ \ \ \ K(z,\overline{z})=\text{KL}(p(x|z)\parallel p(x|z^{\prime}))\ \ \ \ \ \;\rule[-8.53581pt]{0.0pt}{0.0pt}}

(63)

subsequently evaluated at

once the Hessian is taken.

Moreover,

is complex Gaussian, and the KL divergence between complex Gaussians has a closed form. It is known

\displaystyle\text{KL}\big(p(x|z)\parallel p(x|z^{\prime})\big)=\mathrm{Tr}\!\big(\Sigma(z^{\prime})^{-1}\Sigma(z)\big)+(\mu(z^{\prime})-\mu(z))^{\dagger}\Sigma(z^{\prime})^{-1}(\mu(z^{\prime})-\mu(z))-n+\ln\!\frac{|\Sigma(z^{\prime})|}{|\Sigma(z)|}.

v_{i}:=x-\mu_{i}
. First, notice, in Einstein notation and since
\mu
is pluriharmonic,

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}=(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}v_{i}^{\alpha}v_{i}^{\overline{\beta}}

(71)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}\Psi_{i}=(\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}^{\delta})(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\delta\overline{\beta}}v_{i}^{\overline{\beta}},\ \ \ \ \ \partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}=(\partial_{\overline{\beta}}v_{i}^{\overline{\delta}})(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\alpha\overline{\delta}}v_{i}^{\alpha}

(72)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}=(\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}^{\delta})(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\delta\overline{\gamma}}(\partial_{\overline{\beta}}v_{i}^{\overline{\gamma}})=(\partial_{\alpha}v_{i})^{\dagger}(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})(\partial_{\overline{\beta}}v_{i}).

(73)

Now, using the exponential derivative and the product rule,

\displaystyle\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Big(e^{\Psi_{i}/\rho^{2}}\Big)=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i})\Omega_{i}

(74)

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Big[\partial_{\alpha}\Omega_{i}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}+\Omega_{i}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}\Big]=\frac{\Omega_{i}}{\rho^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\Psi_{i}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}+\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}\Big]

(75)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{i}}{\rho^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}(\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}^{\delta})(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\delta\overline{\gamma}}v_{i}^{\overline{\gamma}}(\partial_{\overline{\beta}}v_{i}^{\overline{\delta}})(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\gamma\overline{\delta}}v_{i}^{\gamma}+(\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}^{\delta})(\Sigma_{i}^{-1})_{\delta\overline{\gamma}}(\partial_{\overline{\beta}}v_{i}^{\overline{\gamma}})\Big]:=\Xi_{i}.

(76)

Thus, we will endow
z
to be on a compact subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, then by uniform convergence by the Weierstrass M-test, we can exchange differentiation and the infinite sum

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Big(\rho^{2}\log\Big(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big)\Big)

(77)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\partial_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{1}{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)}\sum_{i}a_{i}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big)

(78)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\Bigg(-\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}\partial_{\alpha}\Omega_{i}(z)}{(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z))^{2}}\sum_{i}a_{i}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Omega_{i}(z)+\frac{1}{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)}\sum_{i}a_{i}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Omega_{i}(z)\Bigg)

(79)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}\Bigg(\frac{-1}{(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z))^{2}}(\sum_{i}a_{i}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Omega_{i}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i})(\sum_{i}a_{i}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Omega_{i}\partial_{\alpha}\Psi_{i})+\frac{1}{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)}\sum_{i}a_{i}\Xi_{i}\Bigg).

(80)

This is the Kähler metric of the complex VAE. Let us justify this more. Now, notice the following limits, which we will justify shortly:

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\partial_{\alpha}\Psi_{i}}{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\Psi]

(81)

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}}{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]

(82)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)}\sum_{i}a_{i}\Xi_{i}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{w}[\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\Psi\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi+\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi].

(83)

Thus,

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}=\rho^{2}\Bigg(\frac{-1}{\rho^{4}}\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\Psi]\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{w}[\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\partial_{\alpha}\Psi\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi+\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]\Bigg)

(84)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ =\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\Psi\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]-\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\Psi]\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]\Big)

(85)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0.5469,0,0.5469}\boxed{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}=\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\text{Cov}(\partial_{\alpha}\Psi,\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi)}

(86)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}w_{i}(z)\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}(z)+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\Bigg(\sum_{i}w_{i}(\partial_{\alpha}\Psi_{i}(z)\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}(z))-\Big[\sum_{i}w_{i}\partial_{\alpha}\Psi_{i}(z)\Big]\Big[\sum_{i}w_{i}\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi_{i}(z)\Big]\Bigg).

(87)

We have

\displaystyle w_{i}(z)=\frac{a_{i}e^{-\Psi_{i}(z)/\rho^{2}}}{\sum_{j}a_{j}e^{-\Psi_{j}(z)/\rho^{2}}},\ \ \ \ \ \mathbb{E}_{w}[X]=\sum_{i}w_{i}(z)X(z).

(88)

It is known that
\text{Hess}=J^{\dagger}J
is reasonable. Thus, we can take

\displaystyle h_{\alpha\overline{\beta}}=(1+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}})\mathbb{E}_{w}[\Phi]-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\alpha}\Psi]\mathbb{E}_{w}[\partial_{\overline{\beta}}\Psi]

(89)

if desired for suitable Hessian surrogate
\Phi
.

The argument for our expectations come from weighted ergodic theory. Consider a sample
\{\Gamma_{i}(z)\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
with weights
\mathbb{E}[w]<\infty,\mathbb{E}[w\Gamma]<\infty
. Taking the limit, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\Gamma_{i}(z)}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\Gamma(z)]=\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(z,\theta)d\mu(\theta).

(90)

We borrow from Theorem 11 in
39
. Since our domain is compact,
\Gamma_{i}(z)=\Psi_{i}(z)
is

and take

to be

Let us also justify more our infinite series converges so that the Weierstrass M-test applies. In particular, let us examine

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\log\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big)=\rho^{2}\log\Big(\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big)

(91)

on a compact set. The interchange of the log and limit is justified since

Our metric can also be computed using

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\partial\overline{\partial}\log\Big(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big),

(92)

or alternatively, using a fast approximation,

\displaystyle\rho^{2}\Big(J_{(z,\overline{z})}\log\Big(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big)\Big)^{\dagger}\Big(J_{(z,\overline{z})}\log\Big(\sum_{i}a_{i}\Omega_{i}(z)\Big)\Big).

(93)

However, the above is currently an ill-defined computation, since it is a Jacobian of a scalar, hence the result is rank one. Thus, we will work with directional derivatives to ensure nontriviality. We can remedy this by taking
F(z)=\Sigma^{-1/2}(x(z)-m(z)),m(z)=\sum_{i}w_{i}\mu_{i}
, and take directional derivatives
g_{k}(z)=J_{F}^{\dagger}(z)v^{(k)}
, and then

\displaystyle\widehat{h}(z)=\frac{\rho^{2}}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}g_{k}(z)g_{k}^{\dagger}(z)

(94)

is our high-rank proxy. To reiterate, this lists the true Hessian, an ill-defined Jacobian proxy (since it is scalar), and a Hessian proxy that is high-rank.

We make some other remarks. Suppose we wanted to attempt to approximate the Fisher metric of equation
35
by using discrete substitutions
\mu_{i},\Sigma_{i}^{-1}
. This is nontrivial because attempting to find the closest point among a collection requires a nearest neighbor algorithm. This is especially nontrivial in large-scale settings, which is the focus of this work. This is already baked into the expectation calculation via the softmax function, so our proposed metric of

Our metric indeed discards the
\partial_{\gamma}\Sigma

\partial_{\gamma}\Sigma

Conclusions

We have developed two Kähler potentials whose complex Hessian
\partial\overline{\partial}
is the decoded Fisher information metric, one exact and one approximate. Our approximate metric remains a valid Kähler potential
K
, and it is efficient because the computational burden is displaced from automatic differentiation to an expectation. This expectation is also more efficient than using a nearest neighbor algorithm. The efficiency matters because constructing a metric of the latent space and sampling from this metric is nontrivial. Our work is primarily interesting because the intersection of complex geometry and ML is a largely unexplored area.
