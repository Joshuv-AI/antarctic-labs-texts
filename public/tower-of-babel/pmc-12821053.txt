Diselenide-bridged hybrid mesoporous silica for radiosensitization and radioprotection in the radioimmunotherapy of colorectal cancer
Authors: Fan Zhang, Xiaochun Xie, Junna Lu, Hui Fang, Quanxing Ning, Jiayi Wang, Yige Shan, Shaowen Wang, Xin Zhao, Fangman Chen, Wenfei Dong, Zhimin Chang, Li Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radioimmunotherapy has been recognized as a promising strategy for the treatment of colorectal cancer; however, it faces several challenges, including limited efficacy, weak immune activation, and severe radiation-induced colitis. To address these issues, we developed hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SeH-MONs) that integrate dynamic diselenide bonds, exhibiting high drug loading ability as well as X-ray-responsive degradation and release properties. The degradation products derived from diselenide bonds differentially modulate selenometabolism in colorectal cancer cells and intestinal epithelial cells, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in tumor cells while mitigating radiation-induced damage in normal cells. After optimizing the content of diselenide bonds, SeH-MONs, combined with radiotherapy, not only resulted in enhanced anticancer activity and elicited a sequence of immunogenic cell death but also simultaneously achieved alleviation of radiation-induced intestinal injuries. When loaded with immunomodulators, indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase inhibitors, SeH-MONs enabled the notable suppression of aggressive peritoneal metastatic colon cancer along with low systemic toxicity. This study reveals the therapeutic potential and mechanism of degradable SeH-MONs beyond their drug delivery ability, thus providing a promising nanoplatform for the highly efficient radioimmunotherapy- and radiation-induced ablation of colitis.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03910-0.

Introduction

Advanced colorectal cancer (CRC) is a common and highly aggressive malignancy with poor survival outcomes worldwide [1–3]. Patients with locally advanced rectal cancer or metastatic CRC face unfavorable long-term prognosis, underscoring the urgent need for more effective treatment strategies. Radiation therapy (RT) plays a crucial role in the definitive management of patients with inoperable CRC [4–6]. Irradiation induces profound DNA damage and immunogenic cell death (ICD), leading to the recruitment and activation of immune cells [7–9]. Despite the reported advantages of the combination of radiotherapy and immunotherapy, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal, largely due to multiple resistance mechanisms and overlapping toxicities [10–12]. Therefore, novel strategies are urgently required to enhance radio-sensitization and mitigate adverse effects in CRC radioimmunotherapy.

Cellular redox balance is maintained by a powerful antioxidant system that scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) in normal cells [13–15]. Since excessive ROS production in irradiated tissues is a key driver of inflammation, antioxidant therapies have shown utility in reducing radiotherapy-induced toxicity and ameliorating radiation-associated inflammatory conditions [15, 16]. In contrast, cancer cells typically exhibit elevated ROS levels and dysregulated redox homeostasis, which not only limit the efficacy of ROS-dependent radiotherapy but also promote tumor metastasis [17–19]. Consequently, antioxidant therapy has emerged as a “double-edged sword” in cancer treatment, thereby leading to tumor cells being more aggressive and invasive [20]. Recent research has shifted from broad antioxidant use toward targeting ROS as a therapeutic vulnerability. Strategies that disrupt redox homeostasis offer promising avenues to overcome resistance in cancer radiotherapy and immunotherapy [21–23]. Given that irradiation generates abundant ROS in both malignant and normal cells of the tumor microenvironment (TME), we hypothesize that selectively elevating ROS could sensitize CRC cells to radiotherapy and amplify ICD, while scavenging ROS in normal tissues could mitigate radiation damage and reduce colitis incidence. Such a dual approach could enable safer and more effective radioimmunotherapy for advanced CRC. However, achieving cell-type-specific redox modulation remains a major challenge.

Selenium (Se), which is an essential trace element known for its antioxidant properties, plays a crucial role in maintaining the cellular redox balance and promoting the activity of selenoproteins [24, 25]. Due to the discrepancy in cellular selenium metabolism between normal and tumor cells, supplementation with exogenous Se may exert different biological effects on different cell lines [26–28]. Therefore, recent studies have highlighted the potential application of Se-containing materials in modulating the cellular response to oxidative stress induced by radiotherapy [29, 30]. However, the development of multifunctional selenium-based nanoplatforms to simultaneously achieve protection from radiosensitization-induced radiation damage and a high synergy with radioimmunotherapy is urgently needed.

Therefore, in this study, we tailored an intelligent nanoplatform by introducing diselenide bonds as “ROS sensors” to selectively modulate the redox homeostasis of different cells in the CRC microenvironment. We evaluated biodegradable diselenide-bridged hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SeH-MONs) with varying selenium densities and observed that high-diselenide SeH-MONs enhanced ROS-mediated radio-sensitization in CRC cells, overcoming radio-resistance and promoting ICD to stimulate antitumor immunity. Moreover, SeH-MONs that were administered at the same dose were able to exert protection against irradiation-induced damage to normal cells through the scavenging of ROS and inflammation ablation. Further loaded with an indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase inhibitor (IDOi) and coated with a homologous CRC cell membrane, this specific biomimetic nanomedicine (CM@SeH-MON@IDOi) preferentially accumulated in tumor tissues and released IDOi in response to irradiation, thereby reversing immunosuppression and enabling effective CRC radioimmunotherapy with minimized colitis. This study proposes a novel redox-modulating strategy using diselenide-bridged nanoplatforms to overcome key barriers in CRC radioimmunotherapy (Scheme 1).

Materials and methods

Fabrication of SeH-MONs

A modified sol-gel method was employed to synthesize biodegradable diselenide bond-bridged hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (SeH-MONs), utilizing CTAT as a template and TEOS and bis[3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl]diselenide (BTESePD) as silica sources, along with silane PEG. First, BTESePD was prepared by the reaction of γ-chloropropyl trimethoxysilane with freshly prepared sodium diselenide. The sodium diselenide stock solution was prepared according to the previous reports.Then 10 g of the γ-chloropropyl trimethoxysilane was dropped to 30 mL of sodium diselenide stock solution and stirred at room temperature overnight. Then ice water was added to cease the reaction. The mixture was extracted with dichloromethane; the organic layer was dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate. The crude product was purified by silica-gel column chromatography (PE: DCM, 10:1–1:1) to obtain the.

product as dark yellow liquid (1.8 g, Yeild = 18%). Next, 0.6 g of CTAT, 0.15 g of TEAH3, and 40 mL of deionized water were stirred at 80 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, 1.0 g of TEOS was added dropwise to the abovementioned surfactant mixture, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h. A further mixture of 3.0 g of TEOS, 1.0 g of BTESePD, and 1 mL of EtOH was added dropwise, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 3 h. After the solution was cooled to room temperature, 0.2 g of silane PEG was added, and the mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature. The mixture was then heated to 80 °C for 24 h and allowed to cool to room temperature. The resulting product was collected by centrifugation and washed twice with ethanol. The product was dispersed in water at a concentration of 2 mg/mL, and 0.6% (w/v) NH3OH was added. The mixture was subsequently heated to 110 °C for 3 h. The final product was collected by centrifugation, washed three times with ethanol, and refluxed in a 1% (w/v) ethanol solution of NH4NO3 for 12 h. Finally, the synthesized mesoporous nanoparticles were collected, washed, and dried for subsequent experiments.

Characterizations of SeH-MONs

The morphologies of the SeH-MONs were characterized using a transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Thermo Fisher Talos F200x) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Merlin compact). The SeH-MONs were dispersed in ethanol and added dropwise onto a copper grid for TEM analysis and onto a monocrystalline silicon wafer for SEM imaging. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the nanoparticles were measured using a Nano-ZS 90 Nanosizer (Malvern). The surface area and porosity were determined by Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis using an automatic specific surface and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2460) with a pretreatment temperature of 200 °C. The specific surface area and pore size distributions were calculated using the BET and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) methods.

Cell viability analysis

Human intestinal epithelial cells (HCoEpiCs), murine colon carcinoma cells (CT26 and MC38 cells), and human colon carcinoma cells (HCT-116 cells) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To evaluate the impact of SeH-MONs on cell viability, cells in the optimal growth phase were seeded into 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well). Following overnight incubation (37 °C, 5% CO₂), adherent cell monolayers were confirmed by phase-contrast microscopy. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing test materials at the concentrations specified in the main text. At designated time points (24 h posttreatment), the plates were removed from the incubator and processed for the CCK-8 assay. For this assay, the blank control was comprised of medium + CCK-8 without cells. The negative control involved untreated cells. Viability was calculated by using the following formula:

viability (%) = [(ODsample- ODblank)/(ODnegative- ODblank)] ×100%.

Clonogenic assays and calculation of the radiosensitivity coefficient

The clonogenic assay was performed as previously described. Known numbers were then replated to allow cells colony (> 50 cells) development. After the cells had adhered to the wall, they were treated with SeH-MONs for 6 h and then subjected to X-ray treatment (0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 Gy). The processed cells were cultivated without any special treatment conditions. Culturing was stopped when visible colony appeared in the culture dish within 2-3weeks. The cells were stained and counted with crystal violet. The plating efficiency (PE) and surviving fraction (SF) for given treatments were calculated:

PE = (grown colonies/plated cells) ×100%;

SF= (grown colonies after treatment/plated cells) ×PE.

The mean lethal dose (D0), SF, quasi-threshold dose (Dq) and SF2 sensitized ratio of CT26 cells in different treatment groups were calculated by fitting the cell survival curve with multi target single hit equation: SF = 1 − (1 − exp(D/D0))^N applied by Origin software.

Intracellular ROS detection assays

We used a Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai) to detect the total intracellular reactive oxygen species content. The basic principle of this method involves the hydrolysis of DCFH-DA (2,7-dichlorodi-hydrofluorescein diacetate) by intracellular esterases to produce DCFH. DCFH aggregates in living cells and reacts with ROS to generate fluorescent DCF (excitation wavelength of 480 nm, emission wavelength of 525 nm). We quantitatively analyzed the intensity of the generated fluorescence signal using flow cytometry.

In vitro ROS consumption kinetics of SeH-MONs

We conducted in vitro ROS consumption simulations using 0.5% hydrogen peroxide and three types of SeH-MONs. Equal masses (20 mg) of the SeH-MONs were added to 20 mL of PBS containing 0.5% hydrogen peroxide, and the mixture was slowly shaken at 37 °C. The reaction mixture was collected timely (as described in the main text), and Amplex Red (Beyotime) was used to determine the concentration of hydrogen peroxide. The reaction kinetics curves were fit by Origin software based on the hydrogen peroxide concentration (C) and time (t).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assay for mRNA expression of selenoproteins

The mRNA expression levels of SEPHS, SCLY, and SELENOP in HCoEpiCs, CT26, MC38, and HCT116 cells were quantified using quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Cell samples (approximately 1 × 10⁵ cells per sample) were digested, washed, and centrifuged, after which the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR amplification was performed using SYBR Green on a real-time PCR detection system.

Analysis of intracellular selenocysteine

The content of selenic acid, selenious acid, selenomethionine(SeMet), and selenocysteine(SeCys) in the collected cells was quantified using an SCIEX 7500 system coupled with high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HPLC–MS). Briefly, cells from each treatment group were digested and harvested as described in the main text. The harvested cells were evenly divided for parallel quantification of total protein and the specified selenium species. Total protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. For HPLC–MS analysis, cell samples were resuspended in digestion buffer containing 4 mg/mL trypsin and 4 mg/mL Proteinase K, followed by sonication on ice. The lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before HPLC–MS analysis.

Animal ethics statement

The animal experimental protocols implemented in this study receives approval from the Ethics Committee for the Use of Experimental Animals at South China University of Technology (the assigned approval/accreditation number: ACE2023061).

Establishment of a subcutaneous tumor model and experimental analysis

The CT26 subcutaneous tumor mouse model was established in female BALB/c mice by inoculating 5 × 10⁵ cells into the lateral right leg. The mice were randomized into five groups (n = 3) and then administered saline, radiotherapy (RT) alone, and three types of CM@SeH-MON at a dosage of 5 mg/kg every one week 7 days after tumor implantation, the tumor-bearing site and the ipsilateral abdomen of the mice were irradiated with 4 Gy radiation. For mice in the material-treated groups (as detailed in the main text), the prepared formulations were administered intraperitoneally 12 h prior to irradiation.

Tumor volume and body weight were measured every two days, and general activity along with bowel habits were monitored at designated time points. After 21 days of treatment, all mice were anesthetized and humanely euthanized via cardiac exsanguination. Tumor and intestinal tissues were then harvested for analysis. Tumors were weighed and photographed for documentation. Small intestinal samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and histological sections were prepared to measure villus length in each group.

Immunogenic death indicator assays

Cells pretreated with SeH-MONs (50 µg/mL, 12 h) were subjected to 4 Gy X-ray irradiation. Prior to irradiation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium. The conditioned medium was collected 12 h post-irradiation, and extracellular levels of HMGB1 and ATP were quantified using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT) was quantitatively analyzed by flow cytometry for each treatment group.

Establishment of abdominal metastasis model and evaluation of radiotherapy immunotherapy efficiency

A peritoneal metastasis model of colon cancer was established by intraperitoneal injection of CT26 cell suspension (1 × 10⁵ cells/mouse) into female BALB/c mice. Five days after inoculation, the mice were positioned supinely and subjected to 4 Gy abdominal irradiation. In the material-treated groups (as described in the main text), the respective formulations were administered via intraperitoneal injection 12 h prior to irradiation.

Body weight was recorded every other day, while coat condition and bowel function were monitored at predetermined time points. After 21 days of treatment, all mice were anesthetized and euthanized by cardiac exsanguination. Peritoneal lavage fluid was collected for immune cell profiling by flow cytometry to determine immune cell populations and their proportions. The abdominal cavity was fully exposed, and metastatic nodules were documented by photography. All visible tumors were carefully dissected, collected, and weighed for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Origin 2021 software (OriginLab Corporation, USA). All the data is presented as mean ± SD with at least three replicates. For comparison of two groups, a Student’s t-test was used. For more than two dependent variable groups, One-way ANOVA was performed with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For comparison of more than two dependent variable groups with two independent variables, Two-way ANOVA was performed. ns = not significant and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p < 0.0001 (****), respectively.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of three types of SeH-MONs

Diselenide-bridged MSNs (Se-MONs) were synthesized using a modified sol-gel method [31], in which tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and BTESePD were used as co-precursors, and ethyl-trimethylammonium tosylate was mixed as a structure-directing agent. Afterwards, hollow mesoporous diselenide-silica nanospheres were fabricated by a one-step growth-induced etching method by using monodisperse Se-MONs as templates. When considering that the Se content and degradation of SeH-MONs are vital to their radiosensitive and radioprotective functions, three types of SeH-MONs with different Se contents were prepared by tuning the mass ratio of TEOS to BTESePD during the fabrication of the MSN templates. As shown in Fig. 1a, the three types of SeH-MONs exhibited uniformly spherical morphologies with comparable diameters of ≈ 50 nm. The EDS analysis in Fig. 1b revealed that the three types of SeH-MONs contained different contents of Se (SeH-MON1: 0.15% Se; SeH-MON2: 2.18% Se; and SeH-MON3: 3.65% Se). Furthermore, all of the SeH-MONs exhibited X-ray-responsive degradation, as evidenced by the irregular aggregation observed after irradiation treatment (Fig. 1c). Elemental release kinetics were systematically investigated under both oxidative and irradiation conditions by time-dependent elemental analysis. As shown in Fig. 1d-e, the three types of SeH-MONs were able to release silicon and selenium species in the presence of H2O2, and X-ray irradiation significantly accelerated the release rates of free-state silicon and selenium species. Notably, SeH-MON3 exhibited the highest degradation efficiency under both oxidative and irradiation conditions, which indicated that the degradation of SeH-MON increased with increasing Se content.

High-density diselenide in SeH-MONs potentiates antioxidant efficacy

The impact of nanomaterials on cell viability and proliferative capacity constitutes a fundamental aspect of their biological applicability. As an initial evaluation, we assessed the cytotoxicity of SeH-MONs toward HCoEpiCs. As shown in Fig. 2a, all SeH-MON variants exhibited negligible effects on HCoEpiC viability at concentrations below 100 µg/mL, with half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values exceeding 500 µg/mL (detailed parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1). The low cytotoxicity of SeH‑MONs can be attributed to their surface charge neutrality, structural stability, and favorable aqueous dispersibility. We next performed colony formation assays to evaluate the influence of SeH‑MONs on the radiosensitivity of HCoEpiCs. Following X‑ray irradiation, SeH‑MON‑pretreated HCoEpiCs showed significantly higher proliferative capacity compared to untreated controls, indicating a clear radioprotective effect (Fig. 2b). Among the three variants, SeH‑MON3 demonstrated the strongest protection, correlating with its highest selenium content. Given that radiation induces DNA damage primarily through reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction, we quantitatively analyzed intracellular ROS levels across treatment groups (Fig. 2c). A 4 Gy X‑ray irradiation markedly increased ROS in HCoEpiCs, while pretreatment with SeH‑MONs effectively mitigated this oxidative stress. SeH‑MON3 showed the highest ROS‑scavenging efficacy, reducing ROS levels by 38.31%, compared to 9.33% for SeH‑MON1 and 28.70% for SeH‑MON2. These differences were positively correlated with selenium content, confirming a dose‑dependent protective effect. In addition, γ‑H2AX staining experiments revealed that treatment with higher‑selenium SeH‑MONs significantly reduced radiation‑induced DNA damage, further supporting their protective role (Fig. 2e, f).

These findings were further supported by ex vivo simulation experiments (Fig. 2d). In a 0.5% H₂O₂ oxidative model, SeH‑MON3 displayed the fastest reaction kinetics (t₁/₂ = 12.3 min) and the highest radical scavenging efficiency (91.8%), significantly outperforming SeH‑MON1 (t₁/₂ = 28.7 min, 67.5%) and SeH‑MON2 (t₁/₂ = 18.5 min, 85.4%). These results indicate that diselenide bridge modification effectively confers ROS‑scavenging capability to mesoporous silica.

Notably, the radioprotective mechanism of SeH‑MONs extends beyond the chemical reactivity of diselenide bonds. To investigate whether SeH‑MONs could serve as a selenium source and modulate endogenous antioxidant systems, we measured selenoprotein oxidoreductases, including GPx4 and MSRB1. Results showed that SeH‑MONs with higher selenium contents (2.18% and 3.65%) significantly enhanced oxidoreductase activity, thereby potentiating cellular ROS neutralization capacity (Fig. 2g-i).

SeH-MONs affect selenometabolites to potentiate radiosensitivity

We investigated the radiosensitizing effects and underlying mechanisms of three SeH-MON variants in multiple gastrointestinal tumor cell lines (CT26, MC38, and HCT116). As shown in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 2, the three SeH-MONs exhibited distinct cytotoxicity profiles. Among them, SeH‑MON3 demonstrated the most potent antitumor activity across all tested cell lines, suggesting a positive correlation between selenium content and antitumor efficacy.

To evaluate radiosensitization, cell survival curves were generated and fitted using the single-hit multitarget model (Fig. 3b, d). Compared to X-ray irradiation alone, the combination of SeH‑MON3 (50 µg/mL, a concentration with no observed adverse effects) and irradiation significantly reduced key radiobiological parameters in CT26 cells: the mean lethal dose (D₀) decreased from 2.39 Gy to 1.15 Gy, and the surviving fraction at 2 Gy (SF2) dropped from 0.89 to 0.70. The sensitization enhancement ratio (SER) reached 2.08, accompanied by marked narrowing of the shoulder region of the survival curve (Supplementary Table 2), indicating impaired sublethal damage repair capacity. Consistent radiosensitization effect of SeH‑MON3were observed in MC38 or HCT116 (Figure S10).

Intracellular ROS analysis revealed that SeH‑MON pretreatment significantly elevated baseline ROS levels in CT26 cells (Fig. 3c), thereby sensitizing them to subsequent irradiation. This effect may be attributed to the degradation behavior of SeH‑MONs, which releases exogenous particles during decomposition. Upon degradation, SeH‑MONs lose their regular morphology and electrically neutral surface, transforming into a stimulus source capable of triggering cellular stress responses.

Given the elemental composition of SeH‑MONs, we further explored the potential mechanisms by analyzing the dynamics of several intracellular selenium compounds. These compounds follow two distinct metabolic routes: an indirect assimilation pathway, which requires conversion into bioavailable forms like selenic acid, and a direct utilization pathway, enabling immediate incorporation into selenoproteins such as selenomethionine and selenocysteine. Comparative analysis of CT26 cells (Fig. 3e) and HCoEpiC cells (Figure S1) showed elevated intracellular levels of various selenium-containing compounds after SeH‑MONs treatment. However, under radiotherapy, their metabolic patterns diverged markedly (Fig. 3f, Figure S1b): tumor cells accumulated significantly more selenic acid without a corresponding increase in selenoamino acids, whereas normal cells exhibited moderate rises in both selenic acid and selenocysteine. This metabolic divergence likely stems from fundamental differences in selenium assimilation between CT26 and HCoEpiC cells (Figure S2), potentially affecting the metabolic conversion efficiency of inorganic selenium released during radiotherapy-induced SeH‑MON degradation.

Two physiologically relevant findings emerged. First, in normal cells, increased selenoamino acid biosynthesis facilitated the upregulation of antioxidant selenoenzymes. Second, tumor cells displayed impaired metabolic processing of accumulated selenic acid, leading to incomplete conversion into functional selenoproteins. This metabolic bottleneck likely contributes to radiotherapy sensitization by interfering with proliferative pathways in tumor cells (Figure S1c). The observed metabolic-preference-based divergent transformation of SeH‑MONs underlies the enhancement of radiosensitivity, revealing that SeH‑MONs can induce synthetic lethality in irradiated cancer cells. These findings redefine silica-based nanomaterials not as drug carriers, but as active metabolic disruptors, offering a novel strategy to exploit selenium processing vulnerabilities in CRC (Fig. 3g).

In vivo radiosensitization and ablation of colitis by SeH-MON

To further validate the dual radiosensitizing and radioprotective effects of SeH‑MONs, we established CT26 subcutaneous tumor models to evaluate their combined efficacy with X-ray irradiation (Fig. 4a). To enhance blood circulation and tumor targeting, we cloaked SeH‑MONs with a homologous CT26 cell membrane (CM), forming CM@SeH‑MONs. The resulting nanoparticles showed a uniform spherical morphology with a lipid shell coating. An increase in hydrodynamic size and a more negative zeta potential confirmed successful CM coating (Figure S3a). Compared with uncoated SeH‑MONs, CM@SeH‑MONs exhibited higher tumor accumulation, achieving a tumor-to-intestine uptake ratio of 4.7:1 (Figure S4). We subsequently detected the therapeutic effects of X-ray irradiation combined with three SeH-MONs. Tumor growth kinetics (Figure. 4c) revealed that, compared with RT alone, CM@SeH-MONs + RT exhibited superior tumor suppression effects. Moreover, by day 21, the tumor growth inhibition rate of SeH-MON3 plus RT reached 85%, which was greater than that of SeH-MON1 plus RT, SeH-MON2 plus RT, and RT alone. Consistent results were detected in the terminal tumor weight analysis (Figures. 4b, d), with tumor weight inhibition rates of 51%, 77%, and 90% being observed for SeH-MON1 plus RT, SeH-MON2 plus RT, and SeH-MON3 plus RT, respectively, which significantly surpassed the inhibition rate of the RT group. These results demonstrate that SeH‑MON3, with the highest selenium content, exerts the strongest radiosensitizing effect. We also assessed whether SeH‑MONs could mitigate radiation-induced intestinal injury—a common clinical complication in abdominal or pelvic radiotherapy for colorectal cancer. Body weight, food intake, and intestinal histomorphology were monitored in all groups. As shown in Fig. 4e, irradiation alone significantly reduced body weight and average chow intake compared to control groups (Figure S5a). However, these declines were markedly alleviated in SeH‑MON–pretreated mice, with SeH‑MON3 showing the most pronounced protective effect. Histomorphometric analysis (Figs. 4f) further supported these findings: mice receiving SeH‑MON3 + RT exhibited intestinal villi 42% longer than those in the RT-only group (325 ± 18 μm vs. 229 ± 15 μm), and also longer than those in the SeH‑MON1 and SeH‑MON2 combination groups. To further verify intestinal mucosal integrity, we measured serum levels of diamine oxidase (DAO) and intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (iFABP), biomarkers released upon gut barrier injury. As shown in Figures S5b, c, RT alone significantly elevated both DAO and iFABP levels, whereas SeH‑MON cotreatment maintained them at low levels, with the SeH‑MON3 group showing the smallest increase.

SeH-MONs for combined radioimmunotherapy

RT is well established to induce immunogenic cell death and remodel the tumor microenvironment (TME), providing a strong rationale for combining immunomodulatory treatment with RT to enhance therapeutic outcomes and elicit systemic antitumor immunity. Capitalizing on their hollow structure and X-ray-responsive degradation, SeH-MONs demonstrated high loading capacity for the indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase inhibitor (IDOi) 1-methyltryptophan (7.1%), along with irradiation-triggered drug release, enabling spatially and temporally coordinated radioimmunotherapy within tumors (Figure S6). Treatment with SeH-MON3 + RT induced the most pronounced exposure of calreticulin (CRT) (Figure S7a) and the highest release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including HMGB1 (Figure S7b) and ATP (Figure S7c), attributable to the enhanced radiosensitization effect of the combination. When bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were cocultured with CT26 cells pretreated with SeH-MON3 + RT, the strongest maturation of dendritic cells (Figure S7d, e) and upregulation of MHC II surface expression were observed (Figure S7f), indicating that SeH-MON3 synergizes with RT to amplify immunogenic cell death and potentiate immunotherapy.

Given these properties, SeH-MONs present a promising platform for the delivery of immune modulators to maximize radioimmunotherapy efficacy. We constructed SeH-MON3 loaded with IDOi and coated with a CT26-derived cell membrane, forming CM@SeH-MON@IDOi, and evaluated its performance in an aggressive peritoneal metastatic colon cancer model (Fig. 5a). Visual inspection of peritoneal tumors (Fig. 5b), tumor weight measurement (Fig. 5c), and inhibition rate calculations (Fig. 5d) demonstrated that CM@SeH-MON@IDOi combined with RT nearly completely suppressed metastatic nodule formation with minimal systemic toxicity. By reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment during radiotherapy, CM@SeH-MON@IDOi-mediated delivery of IDOi enhanced antitumor immunity at both the cellular (CD8⁺ T cell infiltration) and cytokine (IFN-γ production) levels, culminating in a potent radio-immunotherapeutic synergy (Fig. 5e).

Discussion

We systematically investigated the metabolism-oriented radiobiological behavior of diselenide-bridged SeH–MONs. SeH–MONs undergo radiation-accelerated degradation in cells, serving as an alternative selenium source. Since selenium—whether in its inorganic or organic forms—can modulate cellular processes such as proliferation and antioxidant capacity, disruption of the balance between these forms exerts distinct effects across cell types. Tumor cells exhibit heightened sensitivity to such homeostatic imbalance, resulting in a radiosensitizing effect. In normal cells, however, selenium released from SeH–MONs under irradiation is effectively incorporated into antioxidant selenoproteins, thereby mitigating radiation-induced damage.

A key enzyme in the conversion of inorganic to organic selenium is SEPHS. We observed significantly higher SEPHS transcript levels in tumor compared to epithelial cells, reflecting aberrant metabolic activity in tumor. We hypothesize that degradation products of SeH–MONs—namely, excess selenium and silicon–oxygen fragments—interfere with normal SEPHS function. This disruption impairs the conversion of inorganic selenium into selenoamino acids essential for tumor cell survival, ultimately enhancing radiotherapeutic efficacy.

The diselenide bridge serves not only as a stimuli-responsive motif for controlled drug release, but more importantly, its metabolic fate is highly context-dependent. In complex physiological environments, the degradation pathway of diselenide bonds diverges according to the metabolic demands and functional characteristics of different cells. While epithelial cells utilize selenium metabolites to activate antioxidant defenses against radiation-induced damage, tumor cells accumulate degradation products that exacerbate oxidative stress. This metabolic divergence explains the dual bioeffects observed: radiosensitization in malignant cells versus radioprotection in healthy tissues.

Notably, the differential accumulation of ROS in tumor promotes damage-associated molecular pattern release, thereby stimulating antitumor immunity. To leverage this immunometabolic interplay, we loaded SeH–MONs with 1-methyltryptophan, enabling simultaneous modulation of selenium metabolism and immune responses.

Although this study focuses on diselenide-bridged silica nanomaterials, the findings illuminate a broader metabolic principle: stimulus-responsive nanomaterials generate active fragments whose biological effects are determined by the metabolic landscape of their microenvironment. This insight highlights the importance of understanding cell-type-specific metabolic processing in the design of precision nanomedicines.

Conclusion

In this research, we fabricated SeH-MONs with different contents of dynamic diselenide bonds to explore their potential and biological mechanisms in radiosensitization and protection from radiation injury. The SeH-MONs exhibited a uniform spherical morphology and X-ray-responsive degradation behavior. Additionally, the SeH-MONs mitigated radiation-induced oxidative damage in normal HCoEpiCs while increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in colorectal cancer cells; moreover, the radiosensitization and radioprotective effects of the SeH-MONs increased with increasing Se content, which was likely since the degradation of SeH-MON fragments changed seleno-metabolic homeostasis in different manners between cancer cells and normal cells. Therefore, compared with singlet RT, SeH-MONs combined with RT demonstrated enhanced antitumor effects and effectively relieved radiation-induced intestinal injury during the treatment of colorectal cancer. Importantly, SeH-MONs exhibited high loading efficiency and X-ray-responsive release properties. When serving as drug carriers to deliver IDOi, SeH-MONs enabled superior tumor suppression with low systematic toxicity. Our study revealed the therapeutic applications and mechanistic basis of SeH-MONs in oxidative damage mitigation and radiotherapy potentiation, thereby providing a promising nanoplatform for the efficient and safe radioimmunotherapy of colorectal cancer.
