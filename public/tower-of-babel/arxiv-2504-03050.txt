Abstract
. If G G is a finite group, the structure of the modular representation theory depends on the cochains C ∗ ​ ( B ​ G , k ) C^{*}(BG;k) , viewed as a commutative ring spectrum. We consider here its singularity category (in the sense of the author and Stevenson [ 20 ] ) and show that the singularity category is the bounded derived category of the Ω \Omega -Tate spectrum (the k k -nullification of the Koszul dual connective ring spectrum C ∗ ​ ( Ω ​ B ​ G p ) C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p}) ). 1.
Introduction

This paper is a contribution to the overarching aim of describing a hierarchy of behaviour for finite
groups
G
from the point of view of commutative algebra (we also
comment on compact Lie groups
G
).
We recall the context in the rest of the introduction, but
highlight here that the novelty here is based around
the
\Omega
-Tate homology. We show that it
satisfies a form of Tate duality and that it
captures the singularity category of
C^{*}(BG)
. Finally, if the group
satisfies a complete intersection condition (for example Chevalley groups at
good primes), we give methods for calculating the
\Omega
-Tate homology.

1.A.
The enhanced group cohomology ring

Many structural features of the representation theory of a finite group
G

over a field
k
of characteristic
p
are reflected in the cohomology ring

H^{*}(BG;k)=\mathrm{Ext}_{kG}^{*}(k,k)
, starting with Quillen’s theorem that the
Krull dimension is the
p
-rank of
G
. This is a Noetherian ring (Venkov) and
very special structurally: for example if it is Cohen-Macaulay, it is
automatically Gorenstein (Benson-Carlson
[
3
]
). However the structural
features are more clearly reflected if we consider an enrichment: we
consider the cochains
C^{*}(BG)=C^{*}(BG;k)
rather than the cohomology
ring
H^{*}(BG)=\pi_{*}(C^{*}(BG))
. For many purposes it is
enough to consider it as an
A_{\infty}
-ring, which is familiar in
algebraic contexts, but in fact we may take
C^{*}(BG)=\mathrm{map}(BG,Hk)
to
be the spectrum of maps from
BG
into the Eilenberg-Maclane spectrum

Hk
. As such it is an
E_{\infty}
-ring, and we may capture relevant
structures by working in a symmetric monoidal category of spectra in
which it is a commutative ring.

1.B.
The spectrum of behaviour

A massive benefit of working with cochains is that
C^{*}(BG)
is
Gorenstein
[
13
, Subsection 10.3]
for all finite groups
G
without exception. At
the other extreme, following Auslander, Buchsbaum and Serre in
classical commutative algebra, one may define regular
local rings in a homotopy invariant way, and it turns out that
C^{*}(BG)
is regular if
and only if
G
is
p
-nilpotent.

It is then natural to consider the
spread of behaviour on the spectrum between
Gorenstein and regular, and to use the singularity category to place
groups along the range.

Results of
[
20
]
(explained below) allow the apparatus of the
singularity category to be applied for
C^{*}(BG)
. Some specific
calculations have been made in
[
5
,
1
]
.
In these cases it was possible to calculate all coefficient rings and
to give small and explicit algebraic models, but we cannot expect to be explicit in
general. In the present paper we develop some structural and
homotopy invariant methods we can apply more generally.

In particular, we prove that the singularity category is
the bounded derived category of the
\Omega
-Tate ring
spectrum (Definition
4.2
). When
C^{*}(BG)
is a complete intersection
in a suitable sense, we give methods of calculation.

1.C.
Koszul duality

Morita theory allows us to take a

kG
-module
M
and obtain the module
C^{*}(BG;M):=\mathrm{Hom}_{kG}(k,M)

over the Koszul dual ring

(kG)^{!}:=\mathrm{Hom}_{kG}(k,k)\simeq C^{*}(BG)
. One might hope this is one direction
of a Morita equivalence, but the situation is a little more complicated: if we attempt to return to

kG
-modules we obtain an action of the ring
\mathcal{E}
of

C^{*}(BG)
-endomorphisms of
k
.
The Eilenberg-Moore spectral sequence arises from an equivalence

\mathrm{Hom}_{C^{*}X}(k,k)\simeq C_{*}(\Omega X),

provided
X
is connected,
p
-complete and
\pi_{1}(X)
is a finite

p
-group
[
11
]
. Since
the Bousfield-Kan
p
-completion

BG\longrightarrow(BG)_{p}^{\wedge}
induces an isomorphism in
H^{*}(\cdot;k)
,
we see

\mathcal{E}=C^{*}(BG)^{!}=\mathrm{Hom}_{C^{*}(BG)}(k,k)\simeq C_{*}(\Omega(BG_{p}^{\wedge})).

For brevity we write
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})=C_{*}(\Omega(BG_{p}^{\wedge}))
from now on.
The point is that
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})=((kG)^{!})^{!}
is the double Koszul dual of
kG
, so we
have a double-centralizer completion map
kG\longrightarrow C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
. This is an
equivalence if
G
is a
p
-group, but generally very far from it. For
example if
G
is not
p
-nilpotent,
the homology ring
H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is not finite dimensional.

1.D.
Morita equivalence

The advantage of working with
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is that we do get a precise
Morita equivalence between appropriate categories of

C^{*}(BG)
-modules and
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
-modules. To establish the context, we must
establish a good theory of ‘finitely generated’ modules. We explain
this in more detail below, but there is a quick definition sufficient
to let us to state our main results. We may say that a
C^{*}(BG)
-module
M
is

finitely generated
if
\pi_{*}(M)
is finitely generated over

\pi_{*}(C^{*}(BG))=H^{*}(BG)
, and define the bounded derived
category to be the homotopy category of modules with homotopy finitely
generated over
H^{*}(BG)
,

\mathsf{D}^{b}(C^{*}(BG))=\mathrm{Ho}(\{M\;|\;\pi_{*}(M)\mbox{ is finitely generated over }H^{*}(BG)\})

It is shown in
[
20
]
that this has good formal properties, and that there is a Morita equivalence

\mathsf{D}^{b}(C^{*}(BG))\simeq\mathsf{D}^{b}(C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})).

Accordingly, we can move back and forth between
C^{*}(BG)
-modules and

C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
-modules, which makes precise what one learns about
kG
-modules by
considering
C^{*}(BG)
-modules.

1.E.
Gorenstein duality

We have already highlighted the Gorenstein duality property for

C^{*}(BG)
. This is closely related to the fact that there are two
approaches to Tate duality: one which splices together homology and
cohomology and one which kills finite free spectra. The fact that
these two give the same answer is important: it is the Anderson
self-duality of the Tate spectrum. In group cohomology, this is often
called
Tate duality
.

It is known that the noncommutative ring
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is Gorenstein, and
in Theorem
5.1
we formulate and prove the corresponding Gorenstein
duality
statement.

This in turn is sufficient to allow us to define the
\Omega
-Tate ring
spectrum, and give a description of the singularity category for

C^{*}(BG)
as the bounded derived category of the
\Omega
-Tate
spectrum (Corollary
9.6
).

1.F.
Complete intersections

Finally, under a complete intersection hypotheses, the duality properties for

C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
can be formulated as a local cohomology theorem,
showing that
H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is a very special ring (Corollary

10.16
and Section
11
).

To explain the assumption, recall that in the commutative
algebra of Noetherian rings Gulliksen has
shown that ci rings are precisely those for which the Ext algebra

\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{*}(k,k)
has polynomial growth. Without an
assumption of that type, the ring
H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})\simeq\pi_{*}(\mathrm{Hom}_{C^{*}(BG)}(k,k))
has no hope of good Noetherian
behaviour. Accordingly it is entirely reasonable to make a ci assumption. It
was shown in
[
8
]
that there is a range of different ways to
transpose the ci assumption to ring spectra like
C^{*}(BG)
, and we will
assume a form of the ci condition. Section
10

shows that under this assumption,
the localization can be constructed using a stable Koszul complex
inverting central elements, which gives a convenient
method of calculation.

1.G.
Organization

In Section
2
we describe the general context of homotopy
invariant commutative algebra, and in Section
3
we
focus in on the symmetric Gorenstein
context obtained from a faithful representation of the group in a
connected compact Lie group.
In Section
4
we introduce the
\Omega
-Tate spectrum as a
Bousfield localization, and in Section
5
we show that it
enjoys Anderson duality for
k
-orientable groups. In Section

6
we consider the norm map and determine it for all
finite groups. In Section
7
we give an alternative
approach through Benson’s squeezed resolutions, hence determining

\Omega
-Tate homology in purely algebraic terms.
In Section
8
we recall the definition of the singularity category of

C^{*}(BG)
, and in Section
9
we study modules over the

\Omega
-Tate spectrum and show that the singularity category of

C^{*}(BG)
is the bounded derived category of the
\Omega
-Tate spectrum.
In Section
10
we show that under complete
intersection hypotheses we may calculate the
\Omega
-Tate homology by
a Cech complex. Finally, in Section

we mention a

2.
Homotopy invariant commutative algebra and Morita
equivalence

The motivation for our methods comes from classical commutative algebra with the study
of a Noetherian local ring with residue field
k
. In this section we recall some
basic homotopy invariant definitions and how they apply to our examples.

We note the recurrent theme that it is sometimes best to look at modules over
the commutative algebra
C^{*}(BG)
and sometimes best to look at modules
over
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
.

2.A.
Building

We recall some useful language from
[
13
]
.

Given an object
M
in a triangulated category, we say
M

finitely
builds

N
if
N
can be constructed in finitely many steps from

M
by taking mapping cones, suspensions and retracts (in other
words, if
N
is in the thick subcategory generated by
M
). We say
that
M

builds

N
if arbitrary coproducts are permitted (in
other words, if
N
is in the localizing subcategory generated by
M
).

2.B.
Regularity

By results of Auslander, Buchsbaum and Serre, a Noetherian commutative local ring
is regular if and only if
k
is small as an
R
-module, or
equivalently that
\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{*}(k,k)
is finite dimensional. For a ring
spectrum
R
with a map
R\longrightarrow k
we
define

R
to be regular if

k
is small over
R
. This is again equivalent to

\pi_{*}\mathcal{E}=\pi_{*}(\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,k))
being finite dimensional.

Thus
C^{*}(BG)
is regular if and only if
\pi_{*}C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})=H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is finite
dimensional, and this happens if and only if
G
is
p
-nilpotent

[
7
, Theorem 7.3]
.

2.C.
Proxy regularity

Of course regularity is a very restrictive condition, and we need a more
inclusive finiteness condition to play the role of the Noetherian
condition. In classical commutative algebra, we may choose generators of
the maximal ideal and consider the Koszul complex
K
. The existence
of such a complex is the finiteness condition we need.

For ring spectra with a map
R\longrightarrow k
with
k
a field, and we will require the
finiteness hypothesis that
R
is
proxy-regular
in the sense
[
13
, 4.14]
that
there is a small
R
-module
K
finitely built by
k
and so that
k

is built by
K
.

It is shown in
[
13
, Subsection 5.7]
that
C^{*}(BG)
is proxy regular
if
G
is any finite or compact Lie group, and by

[
13
, 4.17]
this implies
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is proxy regular. The ring

C_{*}(G)
(the group ring if
G
is finite) is proxy regular by
[
13
, 5.9]
.

2.D.
The Gorenstein condition

We first consider the Gorenstein condition. In classical commutative
algebra there are a number of different characterisations of
Gorenstein local rings, one of which is the condition that
\mathrm{Ext}_{R}^{*}(k,R)
is
1-dimensional.

Definition 2.1
.

[
13
, 8.1]
A proxy-regular augmented ring spectrum
R\longrightarrow k
is said to be
Gorenstein
of shift
a
if there is an equivalence of

R
-modules
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,R)\simeq\Sigma^{a}k
.

It is shown in
[
13
, Subsection 10.3]
that for a finite group

G
, the ring
C^{*}(BG)
is Gorenstein of shift 0, and it follows
from the Morita invariance statement
[
13
, 8.5]
that
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is
also Gorenstein of shift 0.

If
G
is a compact Lie group with
k
-orientable adjoint
representation (i.e., if the conjugation action of
G
on the tangent
space at the identity preserves orientation), then the rings
C^{*}(BG)

are again Gorenstein of shift equal to the dimension of
G

[
13
, Subsection 10.3]
.

2.E.
Effective constructibility

The power of Koszul duality is that it gives very organized
constructions of
k
-cellularization. This is based on the observation
that for any
R
-module
M
,
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,M)
is a
\mathcal{E}
-module and hence built from
\mathcal{E}
and
hence
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,M)\otimes_{\mathcal{E}}k
is built from
k
.

Definition 2.2
.

[
13
, 4.3]

We say
k
-cellularization is
effectively constructible
if the
evaluation map

\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,M)\otimes_{\mathcal{E}}k\longrightarrow M

is the
k
-cellularization for all
R
-modules
M
.

It is shown in
[
13
, 4.10]
that if
R
is proxy-regular then the
cellularization is effectively constructible.

2.F.
Gorenstein duality

If
R
is a
k
-algebra we may form the Brown-Comenetz dual

R^{\vee}=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,R)
, which obviously has the Matlis lifting property

\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(T,R^{\vee})=\mathrm{Hom}_{k}(T,k).

Thus if
R\longrightarrow k
is a
k
-algebra which is Gorenstein of shift
a
,
we have equivalences

\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,R)\simeq\Sigma^{a}k\simeq\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(k,\Sigma^{a}R^{\vee}).

A priori this is only an equivalence of
R
-modules, but if
\mathcal{E}
has
a unique action on
k
, it is an equivalence of
\mathcal{E}
-modules and we
may apply
\otimes_{\mathcal{E}}k
to deduce

\mathrm{Cell}_{k}R\simeq\Sigma^{a}\mathrm{Cell}_{k}(R^{\vee}).

The trivial action condition for
C^{*}(BG)
and for
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is automatic
for finite groups
G
(since

\pi_{1}(BG_{p})
is a finite
p
-group that can only act trivially on

k
). For a compact Lie group, the action is given by the action of

\pi_{1}(BG_{p})
on
H^{d}(S^{ad(G)};k)
; if this is trivial, we say that the adjoint
representation is
k
-orientable (this is automatic if
G
is finite
or connected or if
k
is of characteristic 2).

Finally, if
R
is connective or coconnective with
R_{0}=k
a field, we
see that
R^{\vee}
is already
k
-cellular, so that we have the
Gorenstein duality statement

\mathrm{Cell}_{k}R\simeq\Sigma^{a}R^{\vee}.

Under complete intersection hypotheses we will also give an algebraic description of

\mathrm{Cell}_{k}R
putting the Gorenstein duality into the form of a local
cohomology spectral sequence
[
17
]
.

3.A.
Normalization

In commutative algebra, a Noether normalization of

convenient counterpart to this in our context.

For any finite group

Since

The Tate localization

4.A.

When

One of the powerful features of these localizations is invariance

repeated use of it, both here and in the non-commutative case.

Anderson-Tate duality for

We show that the same condition that gave

5.A.
Classical Tate duality for finite groups

We restate the classical situation in our language as a template. For a finite group

The only degree in which

Since homology and cohomology are dual, we obtain the Tate duality

that the positive codegrees are dual to
negative degrees with a shift

\hat{H}^{n}(BG)=H^{n}(BG)=\mathrm{Hom}(H_{-n}(BG),k)=\mathrm{Hom}(\hat{H}_{-1-n}(BG),k),

\hat{H}^{*}(BG)\simeq\Sigma^{1}(\hat{H}^{*}(BG))^{\vee}.

Similarly for a compact Lie group

\hat{H}^{*}(BG)\simeq\Sigma^{d+1}(\hat{H}^{*}(BG))^{\vee}.

6.E.

Finally, we make a statement covering both the case of

An ordinary projective resolution of a module

This gives a

is bounded above the
process of killing homotopy groups shows

is the homotopy direct limit of bounded below
complexes with
k
-cellular homology.
∎

8.
The singularity category

Now that we have all the ingredients, we recall the definition of the
singularity category of
C^{*}(BG)
.

8.A.
The definition

For a commutative local ring
R
the singularity category is the
Verdier quotient

\mathsf{D}_{sg}(R):=\frac{\mathsf{D}^{b}(R)}{\mathsf{D}^{c}(R)}

where the numerator (the bounded derived category) may be defined as
the homotopy category of finite complexes of finitely generated
modules, and the denominator (the derived category of compact objects)
may be seen to be the homotopy category of finite complexes of
finitely generated projectives. The point of the definition is that
(by the Auslander-Buchsbaum-Serre Theorem)

\mathsf{D}_{sg}(R)
is trivial if and only if the local ring
R
is regular. Its
nontriviality therefore measures the deviation of
R
from being
regular. It has especially good formal properties if
R
is
Gorenstein.

We need to extend the definition of the singularity category to

R=C^{*}(BG)
in a way that respects Koszul duality. We described the
symmetric Gorenstein context of
[
20
]
in Subsection
3.C
.

8.B.
BGG correspondence

We explained the definition of the bounded derived category in Section

3
we
may define the singularity category

\mathsf{D}_{sg}(R):=\frac{\mathsf{D}^{b}(R)}{\mathsf{D}^{c}(R)}

when
R=C^{*}(BG)
or
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
.

Alongside this, we have the
definition of the dual
cosingularity
category

\mathsf{D}_{csg}(R):=\frac{\mathsf{D}^{b}(R)}{\langle k\rangle},

where the denominator is the thick subcategory generated by
k
.
Serre’s Theorem establishes the importance of the invariant: for a graded
connected
k
-algebra
R
, it describes
quasicoherent sheaves over

\mathrm{Proj}(R)
:
\mathsf{D}_{csg}(R)\simeq\mathsf{D}^{b}(\mathrm{Proj}(R))
.
We may thus think of the cosingularity category as very geometric in
flavour.

The Morita equivalence of bounded derived categories exchanges compact
objects (finitely built from the ring) and objects finitely built from
k
.

Theorem 8.1
.

[
20
, 9.10]

Koszul duality induces the equivalences

\mathsf{D}_{sg}(C^{*}(BG))\simeq\mathsf{D}_{csg}(C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p}))\mbox{ and }\mathsf{D}_{sg}(C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p}))\simeq\mathsf{D}_{csg}(C^{*}(BG)).

Note that it follows in particular that
\mathsf{D}_{sg}(C^{*}(BG))
is trivial if and only if

C^{*}(BG)
is regular.

To see the relationship to the BGG correspondence consider the
second when
G
is an elementary abelian
2
-group of rank
r
: in
the light of Serre’s Theorem it shows the singularity category of an exterior algebra on

r
-generators is the derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves on
the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{r-1}
in the familiar way.

9.B.
The singularity category as a bounded derived category

We are now equipped to outline the strategy for understanding

.

\mathsf{D}_{csg}(C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p}))\simeq\mathsf{D}^{b}(L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})).

10.
Central reduction

In the commutative setting (for example for modules over
C^{*}(BG)
)
there is a well known construction of the
cellularization
\Gamma_{k}
and the nullification
L_{k}
. The purpose of
this section is to give conditions under which these same
constructions work for the noncommutative ring
C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
. The flavour
of the conditions is that
C^{*}(BG)
behaves like a complete
intersection. The first requirement is that
H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
has polynomial
growth. For commutative local rings, a growth condition is enough to
characterize complete intersections, but for
C^{*}(BG)
we need a
little more. The actual condition is about Hochschild cohomology, but
it suffices to assume that
G
has a normalization in which the
cohomology of the fibre is a complete intersection. This applies for example to
Chevalley groups at good primes.

10.A.
Polynomial growth

To make this useful we need to understand the ring spectrum

L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
. At the crudest level we want to understand its coefficient
ring, but we are working towards an understanding of its module
category.

When
H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is periodic with periodicity element
\tau
(as in the
case of cyclic Sylow subgroup
[
5
]
), then
we need only check that
\tau
may be taken central, and then as a
module, the Tate localization is just a mapping telescope, so that

L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})=C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})[1/\tau]
. Furthermore we understand the terms in the
telescope, and the homotopy groups are clear
\pi_{*}(C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})[1/\tau])=(H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p}))[1/\tau]
.

The general situation is more complicated. It is familiar
from commutative algebra that the singularity category behaves much
better for complete intersections. In fact we can
make good progress here under a finiteness assumption directly analagous to
the growth condition that characterises complete intersections in
commutative algebra.

We will use some results from the study of complete intersections

[
8
]
, starting with the growth condition.

Definition 10.1
.

[
8
]

A
p
-complete space
X
is said to be
gci
if
H_{*}(\Omega X)
has
polynomial growth (the letter
g
refers to the fact that this is a
growth condition).

It is essentially due to the work of Felix-Halperin-Thomas
[
14
]
that this
finiteness condition gives good control over the structure of the homology.

Lemma 10.2
.

[
8
, 9.10]

Let
X
be a Gorenstein gci space, then
H_{*}(\Omega X)
is left and
right Noetherian and it is finitely generated over a central
polynomial subalgebra.

Since
X=BG_{p}
is automatically Gorenstein, the first assumption is
absolutely harmless. We will proceed on the assumption that
X=BG_{p}

is a gci space. If
G
is
p
-perfect, by Levi’s
Dichotomy Theorem
[
22
]
,
H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
otherwise has at least
semi-exponential growth, and in that case it is hard to even
understand what it means for a module to be finitely generated.

We will assume the growth is polynomial and
name the generators of the polynomial subring as follows.

Assumption 10.3
.

H_{*}(\Omega X)
is finite over a central polynomial subalgebra
k[\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{s}]
.

10.B.
The commutative case

To set up notation we recall the construction in the
commutative case.

Given a commutative Noetherian ring
R
, and an element
\tau
we
define the unstable Koszul complex
K_{n}(R)=(R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau^{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}R)

and their direct limit,
the stable Koszul complex
K_{\infty}(R)=(R\longrightarrow R[1/\tau])
.

Now, given an ideal
I=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{s})
we may construct the stable Koszul complex

K_{\infty}(I)=K_{\infty}(\tau_{1})\otimes_{R}\cdots\otimes_{R}K_{\infty}(\tau_{s}).

One may check that up to homology isomorphism this only depends on the
radical of
I
and for an
R
-module
N
we may define the local
cohomology by

H^{*}_{I}(R;N):=H^{*}(K_{\infty}(I)\otimes_{R}N).

Grothendieck showed that this calculates the right derived functors
of the
I
-power torsion functor. Similarly we may omit the degree 0
copy of
R
and regrade to get the Cech complex, which fits into a fibre sequence

K_{\infty}(R)\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow CH_{I}(R).

The Cech cohomology is defined by

CH^{*}_{I}(R;N)=CH_{I}(R)\otimes_{R}N,

and it is easy to see that it calculates the
right derived functors of global sections of
N
, viewed as a sheaf
over
\mathrm{Spec}(R)\setminus V(I)
.

These constructions immediately adapt to the case that
R
is a
commutative ring spectrum and
I
is an ideal in the Noetherian ring

R_{*}
. The stable Koszul complex is replaced
by the
R
-module
\mathbf{fib}(R\longrightarrow R[1/x])
. We write
\Gamma_{I}N:=K_{\infty}(R)\otimes_{R}N
and
L_{I}N=CH_{I}(R)\otimes_{R}N
. One may see that
\Gamma_{I}N
is the
cellularization with respect to
K:=K_{1}(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{s})
and
L_{I}N

is the
K
-nullification.

The construction gives a natural
filtration giving rise to spectral sequences

H^{*}_{I}(R_{*};N_{*})\Rightarrow\pi_{*}(\Gamma_{I}N)\mbox{ and }CH^{*}_{I}(R_{*};N_{*})\Rightarrow\pi_{*}(CH_{I}N).

When
K
and
k
generate the same localizing subcategory
we write
\Gamma_{k}N=\Gamma_{I}N
and
L_{k}N=L_{I}N
. This
holds for instance if there is a proxy small map
R\longrightarrow k

and
(R_{*},I,k)
is a local ring.

10.C.
Adapting to bimodules

If
C_{*}(\Omega X)
is finite as a module over a commutative ring

with a map

by Lemma

We now observe that we may adapt this to

may be realized by maps of

Lemma 10.4
.

Suppose

is proxy small and

is finite over a central polynomial subalgebra

k[\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{s}]
. Provided
\tau_{i}\in H_{*}(\Omega X)
is realized by a bimodule
self-map of
C_{*}(\Omega X)
, there are spectral sequences for calculating
the homotopy of the
k
-cellularization
\Gamma_{k}M
and

k
-nullification

of a
C_{*}(\Omega X)
-module

taking a familiar

the spectral sequences are

H^{*}_{I}(\pi_{*}(M))\Rightarrow\pi_{*}(\Gamma_{k}M)

and

Remark 10.5
.

If

this collapses to the familiar
calculation

\pi_{*}(L_{k}M)=M_{*}[1/\tau].

Proof :
We repeat the construction with
R=C_{*}(\Omega X)
and realize
\Gamma_{I}R
as an

-bimodule. It is built from

and

is finite dimensional over

is

-cellular,
and the map

is a

∎

In the light of this, it is natural to make a definition.

Definition 10.6
.

We say that

has a
central system of parameters
if
there are central elements
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{s}\in H_{*}(\Omega X)
over which

is finite and these are realized by bimodule maps

.

Thus if

may be represented by homotopically central
elements of

, we have an equivalence of modules

L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega X)\simeq CH_{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{s}}(C_{*}(\Omega X)).

10.D.
Hochschild cohomology

In order to show that polynomial generators
\tau_{i}
may be chosen
homotopically central, we need control over the Hochschild cohomology

HH^{*}(C_{*}(\Omega X))
. Since

HH^{*}(C_{*}(\Omega X))\cong HH^{*}(C^{*}X)

[
5
, 5.3]
or
[

, 1.10.1]
, there are two obvious spectral sequences for this:

HH^{*}(H^{*}X)\Rightarrow HH^{*}(C^{*}X)\mbox{ and }HH^{*}(H_{*}(\Omega X))\Rightarrow HH^{*}(C_{*}(\Omega X)).

In the second case the edge homomorphism is a map

HH^{*}(C^{*}(X))=\pi_{*}(\mathrm{Hom}_{C^{*}X^{e}}(C^{*}X,C^{*}X))\longrightarrow\pi_{*}(\mathrm{Hom}_{C^{*}(X)}(k,k))\cong H_{*}(\Omega X).

Example 10.7
.

In the case that

has a cyclic Sylow subgroup of order

[
5
]

describes both spectral sequences. The differential in the first occurs later (at the

(p^{n}-1)
-page, whilst the second occurs precisely

earlier, where

In that case the

-pages of the two spectral sequences are
isomorphic (though graded quite differently), and the differential is
on ‘the same’ element (that corresponding to the exterior generator of

H_{*}(\Omega X)
).

Definition 10.8
.

The ring
C^{*}(X)
is said to be

intersection (HHci)
if the spectral sequence

HH^{*}(H^{*}X)\Rightarrow HH^{*}(C^{*}X)
collapses at a finite stage.

Proposition 10.9

If the spectral sequence
HH^{*}(H^{*}X)\rightarrow HH^{*}(C^{*}X)

collapses at the

page, then all

infinite cycles.

Proof :
In characteristic
p
every differential vanishes on
p
th powers by
the Leibniz rule, so that if the spectral sequence collapses at the

E_{r-2}
-page,
x^{p^{r}}
survives for every element
x
.
∎

A sufficient condition for the collapse of the Hochschild cohomology
spectral sequence for cochains comes
from the coefficient level.

Lemma 10.10
.

If
H^{*}(F)
is a complete intersection then the spectral sequence

HH^{*}(H^{*}(F))\Rightarrow HH^{*}(C^{*}F)

collapses at a finite stage: cci implies HHci.

Proof :
If
H^{*}F=k[x_{1},\ldots,x_{c}]/(f_{1},\ldots,f_{c})
then the Hochschild
cohomology
HH^{*}(H^{*}(F))
is described explicitly by Buchweitz and
Roberts
[
10
]
. We only need to know that there are generators in
bidegrees
(-1,-|x_{i}|)
and
(-2,-|f_{i}|)
. Since
F
is finite this
shows that
HH^{*}(H^{*}F)
is concentrated in
a strip of vertical length equal to the dimension of
F
and below a
line of slope
1
. It follows that the spectral sequence

HH^{*}(H^{*}(F))\Rightarrow HH^{*}(C^{*}F)

collapses at a finite stage.
∎

10.E.
Retreat to normalizations

We now suppose given a normalization of
C^{*}X
, which is a
p
-adic
fibration
F\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow Y
with
Y
regular and
F
finite. We will
show that we may obtain the required behaviour by imposing conditions
on
F
rather than on
X
.

Definition 10.11
.

We say that
X

has a normalization with Property P

if there is a normalization
F\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow Y
so that
F
has
Property P.

The four properties P of interest are

•

having a central system of
parameters (
C_{*}(\Omega F)
has a central system of parameters),

•

being Hochschild cohomology ci (HHci) (i.e., the spectral
sequence
HH^{*}(H^{*}(F))\Rightarrow HH^{*}(C^{*}(F))

collapses at a finite stage,

•

being coefficient ci (cci)
(i.e.,
H^{*}(F)
is ci), and

•

being strongly spherical ci (ssci)
(i.e.,
H^{*}(F)
is an exterior algebra).

It is clear that ssci implies cci, and Lemma
10.10
shows cci
implies HHci.

Lemma 10.12
.

If
X
has a normalization with a central system of parameters then as a module
over
C_{*}(\Omega F)
,
L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega X)
has a Cech construction and there is
a spectral sequence

CH^{*}_{k}(H_{*}(\Omega X))\Rightarrow\pi_{*}(L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega X)).

Proof :
We have a cofibre sequence

C_{*}(\Omega F)\longrightarrow C_{*}(\Omega X)\longrightarrow C_{*}\Omega Y,

and since
Y
is regular,
\Omega Y
is finite. In particular,

C_{*}\Omega Y\simeq C_{*}(\Omega X)\otimes_{C_{*}(\Omega F)}k

is finite dimensional, and hence
C_{*}(\Omega X)
is finitely built by

C_{*}(\Omega F)
. This shows that some power of each
\tau_{i}\in H_{*}(\Omega X)

lifts to
H_{*}(\Omega F)
.

By the hypothesis on
F
,
L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega X)
can be constructed as a
C_{*}(\Omega F)
-module using a Cech construction and apply Lemma
10.4
.
∎

10.F.
Conditions on cochains

Finally, we show that a complete-intersection condition on
C^{*}X
(cochains),
is sufficient to guarantee the existence of a central system of
parameters in
C_{*}(\Omega X)
(chains); this then gives
the required spectral sequence for
L_{k}C_{*}(\Omega X)
.

Proposition 10.13
.

If
C^{*}(X)
has a normalization which is HHci then the normalization
has a central system of parameters.

Proof :
If
F\longrightarrow X\longrightarrow Y
is the HHci normalization, we saw in Proposition

10.9
that some powers of the classes
\tau_{i}\in H_{*}(\Omega X)

lift to
H_{*}(\Omega F)
, and we must show that some power of these
survive to
HH^{*}(C^{*}(\Omega F))
.

By Proposition
10.9
, it suffices to show
the elements
\tau_{i}
are represented in the Hochschild cohomology ring

HH^{*}(H^{*}F)
.

In fact we have a spectral sequence
\mathrm{Ext}_{H^{*}(F)}^{*,*}(k,k)\Rightarrow H_{*}(\Omega F)
, and we may choose representatives of
\tau_{i}
in
the
E_{2}
-term. Using the map

\mathrm{Ext}_{H^{*}(F)}^{*,*}(k,k)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ext}_{H^{*}(F\times F)}^{*,*}(H^{*}F,H^{*}F)=HH^{*}(H^{*}(F))

we obtain elements
\tau_{i}
in the
E_{2}
-term of the spectral
sequence for
HH^{*}(C^{*}(F))
.
∎

Remark 10.14
.

It is natural to ask if the condition that
X
is gci already implies
HHci, or indeed if gci is sufficient for the two spectral sequences for Hochschild
cohomology to collapse.

One may also ask if the collapse of one of the spectral
sequences at a finite stage implies the other collapses at a finite
stage.

10.G.
Returning to groups

We return to the special case
X=BG
. In this case it is natural to
focus on normalizations coming from group homomorphisms.

Definition 10.15
.

We say that
G
has a normalization with property P
if there is a group homomorphism
\rho:G\longrightarrow U
with
U

regular, so that
C^{*}(U/G)
has property P.

As before, the three properties P of interest are (in order of increasing
strength) HHci, coefficient ci (cci), and strongly spherical ci (ssci).

Corollary 10.16
.

If
G
has an HHci normalization then

C_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})
is finite over a weakly central polynomial subalgebra, and
hence the coefficients may be calculated by the spectral sequence of
Lemma
10.4
.

Remark 10.17
.

The group
U
need not be a compact connected Lie group: we only need
it to be regular. For example
if
k
is of characteristic
p
and
q|p-1
, we may consider a
non-trivial split extension
G=C_{p^{n}}\rtimes C_{q}
(up to homotopy
equivalence this is the general case of a group with cyclic Sylow

p
-subgroup as in
[
5
]
). In this case we may let
U=S^{1}\rtimes C_{q}
, a 1-dimensional compact Lie group which behaves like a

(2q-1)
-dimensional compact connected Lie group.

11.
Examples

Methods in previous work have only applied to hypersurfaces. The
present paper
shows that the class of groups they are
effective for is closed under products. Accordingly we can immediately generate
many examples not covered previously by using products of
s-hypersurfaces.

Example 11.1
.

Let
G=C\rtimes D
with cyclic Sylow

p
-subgroup
C=C_{p^{n}}
and
D=C_{q}
acting non-trivially. We can
express this as a strongly spherical
hypersurface via the fibration

T/C\longrightarrow BG\longrightarrow BU

where
BG=BC^{hD}
and
BU=BT^{hD}
. This has

H^{*}(BG)=k[X]\otimes\Lambda(T)\mbox{ and }H_{*}(\Omega BG_{p})=\Lambda[\xi]\otimes k[\tau]

with

|X|=-2q,|T|=-2q+1,|\xi|=2q-1,|\tau|=2q-2.

Example 11.2
.

We can take
G=A_{4}
with
p=2
and
U=SO(3)
and use the 2-adic
fibration

S^{3}\longrightarrow BA_{4}\longrightarrow BSO(3).

Any product of of these examples give another ssci group, by virtue
of the fibration

S^{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times S^{n_{c}}\longrightarrow B(G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{c})\longrightarrow B(U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{c}).

One may then hope to construct indecomposable examples from
these. For example, if
D
is a group of order prime to
p
and it acts on

U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{c}
preserving
G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{c}

in such a way that the action on
U_{1}/G_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{c}/G_{c}
is
trivial on homology, then taking semidirect products gives another example

G=(G_{1}\times\cdots\times G_{c})\rtimes D,U=(U_{1}\times\cdots\times U_{c})\rtimes D.

The following class of examples may seem more compelling.

Example 11.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a simply connected compact Lie group for which
p
is not a
torsion prime (for example any group
SU(n)
qualifies, and the
situation is summarised in
[
6
, Section 5]
). The
classifying space of the finite
Chevalley groups
G=\Gamma(q)
for
q
prime to
p

fits into a
p
-adic homotopy pullback square

\textstyle{BG\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\Gamma\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{B\Gamma\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\{1,\Psi^{q}\}}
\textstyle{B\Gamma\times B\Gamma}

Accordingly, there is a fibration
\Gamma\longrightarrow BG\longrightarrow B\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is connected and the fibre is finite, this
is a normalization, and since
p
is not a torsion prime
H^{*}(\Gamma;\mathbb{F}_{p})
is exterior. This shows that
G
is ssci and hence HHci.
