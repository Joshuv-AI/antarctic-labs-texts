Development and validation of disulfidptosis-related genes signature for patients with glioma
Authors: Jia Wang, Junchi Luo, Sha Yang, Yongbing Deng, Peng Chen, Ying Tan, Yang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisulfidptosis has recently emerged as a novel form of regulated cell death (RCD). Evasion of cell death is a hallmark of cancer, and the resistance of many tumors to apoptosis-inducing therapies has heightened interest in exploring alternative RCD mechanisms.MethodsTranscriptomic and clinical data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA). Glioma samples were classified using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF). A predictive model was constructed using Lasso regression analysis, and its performance was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses. The relationship between the model and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) as well as treatment sensitivity was also assessed. Finally, we validated the expression of key signature genes in glioma.ResultsGlioma samples were categorized into two distinct subtypes based on disulfidptosis-related genes, showing significant differences in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) between the subtypes. A genetic risk score model was then developed using these genes. A nomogram predicting OS was constructed using the risk score and clinical variables. Patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups based on the median risk score from the TCGA cohort. Low-risk patients had significantly better outcomes compared to high-risk patients (TCGA cohort, OS: p < 0.001; PFS: p < 0.001; CGGA cohort, OS: p < 0.001). The risk score was associated with HLA expression, immune checkpoint genes, immune cell infiltration, immune function, tumor mutation burden, tumor stemness score, and drug sensitivity. Lastly, the expression of 11 signature genes was confirmed in glioma tissues.ConclusionsThe disulfidptosis-related gene-based risk score model effectively predicted glioma outcomes and highlighted the role of disulfidptosis-related genes in tumor immunity. This study offers potential new avenues for glioma treatment by targeting disulfidptosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01664-z.Keywords: Disulfidptosis, Prognosis, Glioma, Tumor immune microenvironment, Drug sensitivity

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common primary malignant brain tumors in adults, including low-grade gliomas (LGG) and high-grade gliomas (HGG) [1]. Glioblastoma (GBM) is the deadliest type of glioma, with a median overall survival (OS) of only 15 months [2]. The current standard of treatment includes surgical excision as complete as possible and combined chemoradiotherapy.[3, 4] Gliomas are considered to be very heterogeneous, and the origin of tumors may come from a variety of cells [5]. Therefore, in 2021, WHO CNS5 has added multiple molecular biomarkers based on Grade I to Grade IV histological grading, which is conducive to the evaluation of good prognosis for the guidance of patient treatment [6]. According to the current consensus, adult gliomas should be divided into two categories: the mutation status of isocitrate dehydrogenase gene IDH1 or IDH2 [7]. IDH-mutant gliomas usually have a lower histological grade and a better prognosis, with a survival time of decades, but can change to a higher grade in later stages of the disease [8, 9]. In contrast, IDH wild-type gliomas are mostly GBM, which are clinically aggressive grade IV gliomas and have a poor prognosis despite aggressive multimodal treatment, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 5% [8, 10]. Temozolomide (TMZ) is an alkylating agent that induces tumor cell death and is currently the standard treatment for glioblastoma (GBM) [11]. O6-methylguanine DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) can reverse this alkylation process by depleting itself [12]. Consequently, MGMT promoter methylation is considered an accurate prognostic biomarker. However, clinical trials have shown that TMZ treatment in GBM patients with high MGMT expression may lead to tumor recurrence [13], and mutations involving MGMT gene fusion or rearrangement can induce resistance to TMZ [14]. This highlights the urgent need for more effective targeted therapies. Targeting EGFR represents another therapeutic strategy, but most EGFR-targeted therapies have not demonstrated significant clinical benefits in trials [15–19]. Additionally, the PI3K pathway has proven ineffective as a therapeutic target for GBM, partly due to low patient tolerance [22, 23] and possibly due to the complex molecular regulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [24]. While cancer immunotherapy has benefited patients with various cancers, its role in glioma patients remains limited. Classical markers for predicting the response to immunotherapy, such as tumor mutational burden (TMB), tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL), and microsatellite instability (MSI), are not always reliable in the context of gliomas [25–27]. In summary, the management of glioma is still suboptimal, and significant breakthroughs have yet to occur.

The advent of high-throughput sequencing and machine learning has revolutionized oncology, enabling the identification of key mutations such as HER2 amplification in breast cancer, BRAF mutation in melanoma, IDH mutation in glioma, and EGFR, ROS, and ALK mutations in lung cancer [28–32]. These discoveries have enhanced our understanding of tumor heterogeneity, which partly explains the lack of a universally applicable cancer treatment strategy. However, intertumor heterogeneity alone does not fully account for the varied prognoses and therapeutic sensitivities observed in glioma patients. Recently, a foundational publication from the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center introduced a novel cell death mechanism called disulfidptosis, triggered by the vulnerability of the actin cytoskeleton to disulfide stress [33]. This mechanism occurs when SLC7A11-high cancer cells experience glucose starvation, leading to the accumulation of disulfide molecules that disrupt actin organization and result in cell death. Notably, disulfidptosis differs from other cell death pathways, such as apoptosis and ferroptosis, as it specifically impacts the actin cytoskeleton, crucial for cell shape and survival [34–37]. Furthermore, disulfidptosis can release tumor-associated antigens, enhancing the effects of immunotherapy, and its relationship with the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is vital for understanding tumor development, metastasis, and drug resistance [38–40]. This study leverages glioma data from public databases and machine learning algorithms to explore the role of disulfidptosis-related genes in glioma, assess the correlation between disulfidptosis and TIME, and evaluate its implications for immunotherapy. We also constructed a predictive model to forecast patient outcomes.

Material and method

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The gene expression profiles and clinical information of glioma in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the gene expression profiles of brain in Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) from the UCSC XENA (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). We obtained all transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) data in the UCSC database which was set as training datasets [40]. And then Wilcox test was used to identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were defined as the absolute value of logFC (fold change) > 0.5 and adjusted p value < 0.05, between the glioma samples and the normal brain samples. Moreover, the validation datasets with detailed clinical annotations used in our present study were obtained from Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA, http://www.cgga.org.cn/), including CGGA‐693 and CGGA‐325. The raw data from CGGA were transformed from fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM) to TPM.

Identification of disulfidptosis-related genes

Liu et al. published studies on disulfidptosis in 2020 and 2023, detailing the mechanism of novel cell death induced by disulfide stress [33, 34]. Therefore, based on their report, we obtained 177 disulfidptosis-related genes for subsequent analysis. We subsequently obtained the expression matrix of these genes in the validation datasets.

Non-negative matrix factorization clustering

In order to assess the differences of disulfidptosis regulation between the different glioma clusters, Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) was used in training datasets to classify glioma samples by using the “NMF” R package [42], which was is an effective technique better than consensus clustering to decompose a non-negative matrix into the product of two non-negative matrices. According to the NMF rank survey and consensus heatmap, the optimal k value was determined [43]. In addition, we analyzed the differences of recurrence-free and overall survival of patients in different clusters.

Construction and validation of the prognostic signature

First, univariate Cox regression analyses was performed to assess the prognostic value of disulfidptosis-related genes in training dataset. Genes with p < 0.01 were selected for further analysis. Next, the disulfidptosis-related genes with prognostic value were intercrossed with DEGs to obtain the differentially expressed disulfidptosis-related genes.(DE-disGs) Meanwhile, the expression matrix of these DE-disGs was obtained in the validation set and merged with survival information. Subsequently, a Cox regression analysis of LASSO penalties was utilized to develope a prognostic model which can be used to lower the likelihood of the model overfitting so as to build an optimal model [21, 22]. The “glmnet” R package [44] was utilized to employe the LASSO technique, the model's penalty parameters (λ) were established using 10-foldcross-validation and basing our decisions on the minimal criteria. And by this way prognostic gene signatures were identified in training datasets and the risk score of each patient both in training and validation datasets were calculated, which were determined by the regression coefficients and the normalized expression levels of each gene signature. All patients were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the medium cut-off value of riskscore in the validation set. For survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves and log‐rank tests were performed to explore the prognostic significance of the signature using “survminer” R package. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis was performed utilizing the "timeROC" R package [46] to examine the predictive efficacy of signature.

In order to assessing the ability of the gene signatures to cluster, principal component analysis (PCA) [47] and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [48] analyses were employed by using “prcomp” and “Rtsne” R packages respectively [49].

Estimation of independent prognostic value of the disulfidptosis-associated signature

It is well known that there are already some clinical indicators to evaluate the prognosis of glioma patients. Therefore, we further evaluated the independent prognostic value of our disulfidptosis-associated signature using univariate as well as multivariate Cox regression analyses. In addition, we constructed nomograms utilizing clinical characteristics and our risk score by using the “rms” R package to analyze the 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates of glioma patients. At the same time, to illustrate the agreement between the predicted and actual survival rates of the nomogram, calibration curves were also established.

Functional RICHNESS ANALYSIS

The GO and KEGG Pathway enrichment analysis were carried based on the DEGs between the high- and low-risk subgroups (|log 2FC|≥ 0.5, adjusted p-values < 0.05) using the “ClusterProfiler” R package. Additionally, for single sample gene-set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), we conducted infiltrating score of 16 immune cells and the activity of 13 immune-related pathways by using “gsva” and “GSEAbase” R package.

Immune landscape analysis

Using “estimate” R package, ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to calculate the ESTIMATE, immune, and stromal scores which can be used to predict the level of infiltrating immune and stromal cells and these forms the basis to infer tumor purity [50]. We also evaluate the abundance of 22 distinct leukocyte subsets by using “CIBERSORT” R package [51]. To illustrate the robustness of the results. other algorithms, including MCPcounter, QUANTISEQ, XCELL, CIBERSORT-ABS, TIMER and EPIC, were also used to analyze the immunological characteristics by quantifying the relative proportions of infiltrating immune cells of the high-risk groups and low-risk groups.

Moreover, Potential immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) response was predicted by calculating Tumour Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score through the TIDE database [52]. The higher the TIDE score, the poorer immune checkpoint blocking treatment and worse prognosis. Besides, differentially expressed immune checkpoints and HLA (human leukocyte antigen) between two risk groups were visualized.

Gene mutation analysis

TMB of TCGA cohort were calculated with mutation data and “maftools” R package [53], which has been proved to be important biomarkers of the tumor microenvironment (TME). We also performed a survival analysis to compare the outcomes of the high and low TMB score groups based on the median value of the TMB score. Moreover, RNA-based stemness scores (RNAss) were obtained to analyze the correlations of risk score and RNAss [54].

Drug sensitivity and interactions

The “pRRophetic” R package [55] was used to calculate half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), an evaluation index of drug sensitivity, to evaluate sensitive the patient to the drug. We obtained the drug sensitivity information file from the CellMiner (http://discover.nci.nih.gov/cellminer). Furthermore, we explored potential targeted drugs for gene signature in our model using the Drug Gene Interaction Database [56] (DGIdb, https://dgidb.genome.wustl.edu/search_interactions). Cytoscape v3.7.2 was finally used to visualize the Durg-gene network.

Pan-cancer analysis of CENPH and FNDC3B

We investigated the difference in CENPH and FNDC3B expression between tumor and normal tissues from datasets in Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) and TCGA databases. The RNAss tumor stemness score we obtained from the mRNA signature of each tumor obtained from a previous study [57]. Then, the relationship between gene expression and RNAss, 44 marker genes of third class RNA modification (m1A(10), m5C(13), m6A(21)) genes was explored. We then divided the patients into high-expression and low-expression groups according to the median value of gene expression, and we used the chi-square test to assess the difference in gene mutation frequency in samples from each group.

Patients and tissue preparation

Tissue samples of 10 glioblastomas (GBM) and 10 brain tissues were obtained in the Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital. They were approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital. All patients have written informed consents.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA from tissues or cells was extracted using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Beijing, China). The concentrations of these RNA samples were then measured using a spectrophotometer and cDNA were synthesized from the RNA specimens using the Primescript RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa). The primer sequence and product size for CENPH was: forward 5′- GCGTTTGCCTGTTGAGTG-3′ and reverse 5′- TCCCAAGATTCCTGCTGT-3′; FNDC3B: forward 5′- CTCTTGTTTCTGTCCTCC-3′ and reverse 5′-TACCCTGATTCACCTTCT-3′; UBE2D2: forward 5′-GAGGCGTGGGCTCATAGA-3′ and reverse 5′-TCAGCAGGTCCTGTTGGA-3′; SLC25A23: forward 5′-GGTCATTGTGCCGTCTCG-3′ and reverse 5′-CGCCCAACAGGGTATCTC-3′; MGAT2: forward 5′-ATGCCCACAGCCACAGAG-3′ and reverse 5′- GGCGACAAAGGAAGAACG-3′; FAM126A: forward 5′-TACAGTCGCTGCGGTCTT-3′ and reverse 5′-TTCATTGCCTCATGGTCC-3′; EV12B: forward 5′-ACAGTCGCTGCGGTCTTA-3′ and reverse 5′-TTCATTGCCTCATGGTCC-3′; CPM: forward 5′-TGGGTTCAGGCGATTTCT-3′ and reverse 5′-TGGGCTGGTGGTTATGCT-3′; SNX10: forward 5′-GTGCTGGCGATTGTTCAT-3′ and reverse 5′-TTTGTAAGTGTCTGGGTTCG-3′; ARC: forward 5′- CACTAATAAACAACCCAACATC-3′ and reverse 5′-GATAGCAGCCTTCACCTCT-3′; SGCD: forward 5′-GATGGATTGTGGGTTTGG-3′ and reverse 5′-GGTTGACCTCGGAGTTGG-3′; GAPDH forward 5′-TTCCAGCCTTCC TTCCTGGG-3′ and reverse 5′-TTGCGCTCAGGAGGAGCAAT-3′. Amplification conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 45 s. The relative fold changes in mRNA levels were calculated according to the relative quantification method (2 − ΔΔCt).

Identification of glioma subtypes with disulfidptosis-related genes by the NMF algorithm

To further illustrate the feasibility of using disulfidptosis-related genes to construct prognostic models, we identified 2 distinct glioma clusters based on disulfidptosis-related genes, which had a significant correlation with OS (p < 0.01), using the NMF method with 100 iterations by the “brunet” selection criterion.(Fig. 1C,D) These 2 clusters were significantly different in OS (Fig. 1E, log rank p < 0.001) and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 1F, log rank p < 0.001) which illustrated that patients in C2 had worse prognosis than those in C1.

Establishment and validation of a prediction model

The cox-LASSO regression analysis chosen 21 DE-disGs to develop prediction model with minimize over-fitting risk (Supplementary Table 1). Sample risk scores were calculated as the sum of the gene expression multiply by respective coefficient. Based on median risk score, training datasets samples were first divided into high-risk (N = 328) and low-risk (N = 328) subgroups (Fig. 2A). The prognostic relationship between two risk subgroups with OS (Fig. 2B, log rank p < 0.001) and PFS (Fig. 2C, log rank p < 0.001) showed that patients in low-risk subgroups exhibited better prognosis than those in high-risk subgroup. The risk plot showed that patients with high risk scores tended to have poor survival outcomes and shorter survival times (Fig. 2D). Besides, PCA and t-SNE analyses indicated that the two subgroups could be well distinguished (Fig. 2E, F). The ROC curve analysis showed the AUC values reached 0.882, 0.915, and 0.918 for 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS, respectively (Fig. 2G).

Using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis, we proved that the risk score was an independent risk factor compare to other clinical characteristics (Fig. 4A, B). Based on the C-index values, we constructed a nomogram (Fig. 4C). Total points were calculated by adding the points of the risk score and other clinical characteristics. Calibration plots showed that the 1-, 2- and 3-year survival times nomogram-predicted survival were highly consistent with actual survival outcomes, which indicated that our model had a high accuracy (Fig. 4D). Biological function analysis

To understand the biological function between the high-risk and low-risk subgroups, 5266 DEGs were identified between two risk subgroups. GO and KEGG pathway analysis were performed to investigate the biological function of these DEGs. The GO results indicated that DEGs were involved in regulation of trans-synaptic signaling, cognition, regulated exocytosis, actin binding, calmodulin binding, Glutamatergic synapse, ion channel activity, and so on (Fig. 5A). The KEGG results indicated that DEGs were enriched in MAPK signaling pathway, Cell adhesion molecules, Glutamatergic synapse, Proteoglycans in cancer, Glioma, Notch signaling pathway, and so on (Fig. 5B). The all immune-related functions obtained from ssGSEA method were activated in the high-risk subgroup (Fig. 5C). GSVA results showed that many differentially enriched signaling pathways between the two risk subgroups mainly involved in N-glycan biosynthesis, mismatch repair, p53 signaling pathway, Starch and sucrose metabolism, glutathione metabolism, glutathione metabolism, complement and coagulation cascades, antigen processing and presentation, intestinal immune network for IgA production, which suggested that disturbances of these signaling pathways may make prognosis of patients in the high-risk subgroup worse (Fig. 5D).

Moreover, we observed that TIDE did not show significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 6D), but Risk score was significantly negatively correlated with TIDE (Fig. 6E, p = 0.004), and the patients in low-risk subgroup obtained a lower exclusion score than the patients in high-risk subgroup (Fig. 6F, p < 0.001). Drug sensitivity and interactions

The pRRophetic algorithm was performed to explored the relationship between riskscore and chemoresistance/ targeted therapeutic agents by predicting the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of drugs (Supplementary Fig. 2). We observed that patients in the high-risk subgroup were more sensitive to 85 drugs, while patients in the low-risk subgroup were more sensitive to 28 drugs. Only two drugs were found to target SLCO1C1 and one drug targeted HNRNPA1 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Importance of CENPH and FNDC3B in pan-cancer

Our pan-cancer analysis found that FNDC3B was significantly upregulated in 21 tumors, and its high expression was significantly associated with shorter OS in 12 tumors and shorter tumor progression-free interval (PFI) in 10 tumors. Moreover, we found that the IDH1 mutation rate was significantly lower and the ERFR mutation rate was significantly higher in glioma samples with high FNDC3B expression (Supplementary Fig. 4) Our pan-cancer analysis based on the UCSC database showed that significant upregulation of CENPH was observed in 32 tumors, and its high expression was significantly associated with shorter OS in 11 tumors and shorter tumor progression-free interval (PFI) in 12 tumors. In addition, we were surprised to find that CENPH had significant positive correlation with almost all marker genes of three types of RNA modification (m1A(10), m5C(13), m6A(21)) genes. Moreover, we found that the IDH1 mutation rate was significantly lower and the ERFR mutation rate was significantly higher in glioma samples with high CENPH expression. At the same time, CENPH was significantly positively associated with RNAss in 30 tumors (Supplementary Fig. 5).

FNDC3B, UBE2D2, MGAT2, FAM126A, EVI2B, SNX10, CPM, and ARC expression was higher in GBM compared to normal, while SLC25A23 and SGCD expression was lower in GBM compared to normal, which were consistent with the results of our bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The management of glioma continues to pose significant challenges. Although various methods exist to evaluate the prognosis of glioma patients, accurate predictions remain elusive due to the tumor's inherent heterogeneity [5, 6]. While many cancer patients have benefited from immunotherapy and targeted therapies, glioma patients have not seen substantial improvements in their prognoses from these treatments [13, 15–19]. This underscores the urgent need for the development of a new predictive model that can more accurately assess the prognosis of glioma patients. Concurrently, identifying key genes involved in the onset and progression of glioma is essential for the creation of new therapeutic options. Cell death, like cell proliferation and differentiation, is a critical aspect of metabolism and plays a vital role in maintaining biological life [58]. Programmed cell death, regulated by specific genes, explains many biological phenomena, including cancer [59]. The latest classification of cell death encompasses various pathways such as apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy-dependent cell death, and immunogenic cell death, among others. Recently, a novel cell death pathway known as disulfidptosis has been identified [33, 34]. This pathway is notable because tumor cells undergoing programmed death can die at rates up to 20 times faster than they proliferate, suggesting that inducing disulfidptosis in tumor cells may represent a promising new strategy for cancer treatment. Currently, there are no studies investigating disulfidptosis-related genes in glioma. To address this gap, our study focuses on disulfidptosis-related genes using data from glioma patients in the TCGA and CGGA databases.

Using disulfidptosis-related genes, we successfully classified glioma patients into two distinct clusters through the NMF algorithm, revealing significant prognostic differences between them. This finding suggests that disulfidptosis-related genes play a crucial role in glioma development, supporting the rationale for constructing a prognostic model based on these genes. Through LASSO-Cox regression analysis, we identified 21 differentially expressed disulfidptosis-related genes (DE-disGs). Survival analysis demonstrated significant differences in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) between high-risk and low-risk subgroups, allowing for clear distinction between the two.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that the risk score serves as an independent risk factor, while the nomogram indicated that the risk score is the most important prognostic predictor. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that multiple cancer-related pathways were abnormally activated in the high-risk group, suggesting that targeting these pathways could provide new treatment strategies for these patients.

Numerous studies have highlighted the critical role of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in tumor occurrence, progression, metastasis, and drug resistance [60, 61]. Our correlation analysis showed a strong relationship between the risk score and tumor dryness score. Differential analysis indicated significant variations in immune cell infiltration between the high-risk groups, with all immune-related functions activated in the high-risk subgroup. Consistent with our expectations, tumor mutational burden (TMB), RNA signature (RNAss), HLA expression, and immune checkpoints all exhibited significant differences between high-risk and low-risk subgroups. This implies that patients in these groups may have markedly different sensitivities to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy. Additionally, we observed varying sensitivities to chemoresistance and targeted therapeutic agents between the two subgroups, indicating that tailored treatment strategies could be developed based on risk scores of patients.

Additionally, we analyzed the expression of 11 signature genes in glioblastoma (GBM) tissues and normal brain tissues using q-PCR. Among the genes utilized in our prognostic model, several, including FNDC3B, DDIT4, and HNRNPA1, have been previously studied in the context of glioma. FNDC3B (Fibronectin type III domain containing 3B) is an endoplasmic reticulum transmembrane protein involved in cell adhesion, proliferation, and growth signaling [63]. Abnormal FNDC3B expression has been observed in various cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma [64], colorectal cancer [65], acute promyelocytic leukemia [66], cervical cancer [67], and glioblastoma [68]. Targeting FNDC3B has been shown to inhibit malignant glioblastoma cells [69, 70], and high FNDC3B expression correlates with poor prognosis in both cervical cancer and glioblastoma, though its carcinogenic mechanism remains unclear. Rajasagi et al. identified tumor mutation-derived peptides in FNDC3B that elicited durable cytotoxic T-cell responses in chronic lymphocytic leukemia cells [71]. Furthermore, Wang et al. reported that FNDC3B may serve as a prognostic biomarker and an immune-related therapeutic target for glioma, aligning with our findings that high FNDC3B expression is linked to poor prognosis. DNA damage-inducible transcript 4 (DDIT4), a downstream target of HIF-1α and the PI3K signaling pathways, is widely expressed in adult tissues [73, 74], although typically at low levels. DDIT4 expression can be induced by cellular stressors such as hypoxia and DNA damage [75–79]. It has also been identified as a blocker of mTOR complex 1 [80–82]. HNRNPA1, an RNA-binding protein and part of the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein (hnRNP) family [83], has been shown by Luan et al. to interact with let-7a and c-Myc in a feedback loop that regulates PKM2 expression, thereby influencing glucose metabolism and glioma cell growth [84]. HNRNPA1 expression is modulated by GSK3α and β isoforms, and the imbalance between these isoforms may contribute to tumorigenesis through its downstream effects on hnRNPA1 [85]. Studies have demonstrated that overexpression of the HNRNPA1 gene in glioma cells promotes cell survival, highlighting a HNRNPA1-dependent survival signaling pathway [86].

Several other genes have also been implicated in tumors, including CENPH, SDE2, EVI2B, LMO7, FAM126A, XPOT, UBE2D2, CPM, SNX10, and SLCO1C1. Centromere protein H (CENPH) was initially identified for its role in connecting centromeres to spindle microtubules, likely in association with the cell cycle-related protein cyclin-dependent kinase 1 [87, 88]. CENPH is known to be overexpressed in various cancers, such as colorectal cancer [89], gastric cancer [90, 91], oral squamous cell carcinoma [92], tongue cancer [93], renal cell carcinoma [94], and hepatocellular carcinoma [95]. Cong et al. demonstrated the oncogenic role of PVT1 in endometrial cancer, identifying a PVT1/miR-612/CENPH/CDK1 axis where over-activated CENPH promotes cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and inhibits apoptosis [96]. This finding aligns with our study, which also associates high CENPH expression with poor prognosis in glioma. Silencing defective 2 (SDE2), another RNA-binding protein (RBP), is involved in multiple cellular processes, including DNA replication, telomere silencing, heterochromatin formation, and pre-mRNA processing. Deficiencies in SDE2 lead to ribosome biogenesis defects and extensive alternative splicing changes that can completely inactivate the cell [98]. SDE2 has been linked to DNA damage responses in prostate cancer cells [99]. Ecotropic viral integration site 2B (EVI2B) has been identified as a lymphocyte-specific attractor gene in various tumors, including colorectal adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, melanoma, and ovarian cancer [100], and may predict tumor progression. LIM domain only 7 (LMO7), a fibrous actin-binding protein [100], has increased expression reported in cancers such as colorectal, thyroid, lung, gastric, liver, breast, pancreatic, and prostate cancers [102–105]. LMO7 plays a crucial role in cytoskeletal reorganization and cancer metastasis during tumorigenesis [106]. Hall et al. used N-glycan mutant cell lines from rats to explore the relationship between N-glycosylation changes and neuroblastoma (NB), finding that NB_1(-Mgat2) had the least invasive properties and impaired cell proliferation, indicating the role of MGAT2 in NB progression. Family sequence similarity 126, member A (FAM126A) is critical for epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and has been associated with several cancers, including pancreatic, prostate, breast, kidney, lung, and colorectal cancers [105–109]. Exportin-T (XPOT) is essential for nuclear tRNA processing, transport, and protein translation, playing a key role in the progression of various tumors, such as neuroblastoma [117], hepatocellular carcinoma [118], breast cancer [119], pleural mesothelioma [120], and promyelocytic leukemia [121]. UBE2D2 is a circular RNA (circRNA) linked to tamoxifen resistance in breast cancer, where exosome-mediated transfer enhances resistance by interacting with miR-200a-3p and regulating cell viability and metastasis [122]. Tong et al. also reported that circ-UBE2D2 is overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis [123]. Carboxypeptidase M (CPM) is recognized as a target of antibodies against cell surface antigens in mature macrophages and human B-lineage acute lymphoblastic leukemia cell lines [124, 125]. Evidence suggests that CPM may be involved in adipogenesis, apoptosis, and cancer development [126]. Lu et al. found that miR-146a-5p regulates Src and FAK expression, activating the Src-FAK pathway and promoting colorectal cancer cell migration and invasion. Recently, Fang et al. demonstrated that a circular CPM (circCPM)-miR-21-3p-PRKAA2 axis is critical for regulating autophagy and 5-FU resistance in gastric cancer [128]. Finally, sorting nexin 10 (SNX10) regulates protein sorting, trafficking, and endosomal homeostasis; studies indicate that SNX10 knockout promotes colorectal cancer development [130]. SNX10 controls SRC levels, a key mediator of cellular signaling, by facilitating autophagosome-lysosome fusion for SRC degradation [131]. SLCO1C1, identified as a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), is significantly overexpressed in gastric cancer and associated with shorter overall survival; it promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and resistance to oxaliplatin chemotherapy [132].

The exploration of these genes in tumors, particularly glioma, remains incomplete. While recent studies have linked these genes to disulfidptosis, their specific roles in this process during tumor development have not been reported. Our study addresses this gap, providing new insights that could lead to potential therapeutic targets for tumor treatment.

However, our study has some limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective analysis based on public database data, it relies on large sample sizes and relatively complete clinical information. Nonetheless, large-scale, meticulously designed prospective studies are required to further validate the accuracy and generalizability of our risk score model. Secondly, although we identified several key genes with potential biological significance in both glioma and pan-cancer, further in vivo and in vitro studies are necessary to explore and confirm their roles. Thirdly, while our analysis revealed correlations between the risk model and immune functions, immune cells, immune checkpoints, HLA molecules, and TIDE, these findings only suggest potential associations with the tumor immune microenvironment and warrant further investigation to confirm these relationships.

Future research should focus on several key areas. First, prospective studies should investigate the applicability of our risk score model in clinical settings, particularly in predicting patient responses to immunotherapy, targeted therapy, and chemotherapy. Additionally, deeper mechanistic studies are needed to elucidate the biological functions of the disulfidptosis-related genes identified in our model, both in glioma and other cancers. Finally, further research should explore the unresolved questions regarding disulfidptosis, including its mechanisms of action, its immunogenicity in the host, and its interactions with other forms of cell death. By addressing these aspects, future studies can provide crucial insights into the therapeutic potential of targeting disulfidptosis in cancer treatment.

Conclusion

Our study is the first to explore the key role of disulfidptosis-related genes in glioma, and we identified 21 key genes, most of which have been poorly studied in cancer, especially in glioma, which will be valuable for future research. Finally, we constructed a risk score model and found that the risk score was significantly correlated with the TIME and the sensitivity of immunotherapy, targeted therapy and chemotherapy, and drew a clinically valuable nomogram.
