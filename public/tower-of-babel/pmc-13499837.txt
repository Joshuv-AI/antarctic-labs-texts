Real-World Economic Burden Associated with Progressive Pulmonary Fibrosis in the US: An Administrative Claims Database Analysis
Authors: Joseph Yang, Bonnie H. Bui, Tim Bancroft, Andrea Steffens, Rebecca Fee, Bijan J. Borah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsAmong patients with non-idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (non-IPF) interstitial lung disease (ILD), developing progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) is associated with increased economic burden, inpatient (IP) admissions, and mortality. However, little is known about the burden within specific payer types. This retrospective study assessed the economic burden of PPF and associated time to first IP admission among distinct cohorts of Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug (MAPD) beneficiaries and commercial enrollees in the United States (US).MethodsMedical and pharmacy claims were analyzed among patients diagnosed with non-IPF ILD between 1/1/2017 and 11/30/2023. Patients who developed PPF (cases) were matched with those who had not yet progressed (comparators) using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with replacement. The index date was the earliest PPF date for cases; the respective comparator was assigned an index date to create identical pre-index durations. Variable follow-up began on the index date and lasted for ≥ 3 months. Primary outcomes were all-cause and ILD-related healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and costs (US dollars [USD] 2023) during follow-up. Outcomes were stratified by insurance type and weighted per patient per month (wPPPM). Multivariable models assessed all-cause costs and time to first IP admission, adjusting for covariates that remained imbalanced after PSM.ResultsA total of 60,171 matched pairs were analyzed: 50,683 MAPD and 9488 commercial. PSM generated well-balanced cohorts, except for race/ethnicity, ILD diagnosis year, unique pre-index medications filled (MAPD), and age group (commercial). For both insurance types, the PPF group was associated with significantly higher economic burden than the comparator group. All-cause costs averaged $3640 versus $1897 wPPPM (p < 0.001) in MAPD and $6164 versus $2741 (p < 0.001) in commercial. Differences were primarily driven by IP burden (MAPD: $1412 vs $507; commercial: $2094 vs $559; both p < 0.001). Mean length of IP stay was significantly longer among patients that developed PPF (MAPD: 0.9 vs 0.4 days; commercial: 0.5 vs 0.2 days; both p < 0.001). A generalized linear model showed that PPF was associated with higher adjusted all-cause costs (MAPD cost ratio [95% confidence interval {CI}]: 1.861 [1.807–1.916]; commercial: 2.096 [1.897–2.316]). Cox proportional hazards ratios (95% CI) showed a significantly higher hazard of first IP admission with PPF (MAPD: 2.060 [2.005–2.116]; commercial: 2.324 [2.133–2.532]). Notably, follow-up use of antifibrotics was low among patients with PPF (0.8% MAPD; 1.0% commercial).ConclusionFor MAPD and commercial cohorts, PPF was associated with significantly higher HCRU, costs, and hazard of IP admission. Differences in economic burden were primarily driven by IP utilization. These findings support the prioritization of treatments or strategies that attenuate the risk and rate of progression to mitigate downstream healthcare burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40273-026-01623-2.

Key Points for Decision Makers

Among patients with interstitial lung diseases, this study demonstrated that PPF was associated with roughly double the healthcare costs for both Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug and commercial enrollees, underscoring the substantial economic impact of disease progression.Hospital stays were the main drivers of the costs associated with PPF, highlighting the importance of early identification and timely intervention for at-risk patients.Use of approved antifibrotic treatments was very low for both insurance types, suggesting an important gap in real-world treatment uptake and opportunities to optimize current disease management strategies.

Introduction

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) encompass a range of over 200 pulmonary disorders characterized by chronic inflammation, lung-related comorbidities, and increased mortality [1, 2]. The prevalence of ILDs in the United States (US) has increased from 168 to 200 cases per 100,000 from 2010 to 2019, while mortality increased from 5.5 to 6.6 per 100,000 over the same time period [1]. The origins of ILDs are broadly categorized as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and non-idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (non-IPF). While the underlying etiology of IPF remains unknown, common non-IPF ILDs include hypersensitivity pneumonitis and autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic sclerosis. Overall, IPF has been more thoroughly characterized in terms of prevalence and the associated burden of disease [3–5].

Between 15% and 38% of all ILDs may develop progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF), in which ongoing disease progression is marked by advancing respiratory symptoms, declining lung function, and worsening of thoracic imaging [6–11]. Suggestive of a shared pathophysiologic pathway, progressive cases of both IPF and non-IPF ILDs have been associated with significant economic burden and increased mortality [3–7]. For example, among patients with non-IPF fibrosing ILD enrolled in Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug (MAPD) and commercial programs, patients with a progressive phenotype incurred $4382 in per patient per month (PPPM) all-cause costs, compared with $2243 among those who had not yet progressed (US dollars [USD] 2019) [4].

Importantly, earlier studies [3, 4, 6] in patients in the US did not stratify economic outcomes by insurance type, which can vary meaningfully between MAPD and commercial programs. A stratified assessment would provide US payer-specific insights into the economic burden associated with PPF. Accordingly, this study sought to quantify the healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and costs associated with progression from non-IPF ILDs to PPF within distinct MAPD and commercial cohorts in the US.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective analysis used the Optum Research Database (ORD), which contains administrative claims data from MAPD and commercial insurance enrollees. The study period spanned from January 1, 2016, through February 29, 2024 (Online resource 1 in the electronic supplementary material). Patients in the ORD were first identified by a diagnosis of non-IPF fibrosing ILD between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2023 (patient identification period). The earliest available non-IPF fibrosing ILD diagnosis date was defined as the fibrosing ILD date; the 12 months prior comprised the pre-ILD baseline period. Patients were required to have a second non-IPF ILD diagnosis during the study period and within 365 days of the fibrosing ILD date. Diagnosis could include either (1) two International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 fibrosis codes or (2) one fibrosis code and one underlying ILD type code (see Online resource 2 for the complete code list).

Evidence of ILD progression (PPF) was defined based on the methods described by Singer et al. and Nili et al. [3, 8]. Briefly, the criteria for progression included any of the following: (1) at least two ICD-10 diagnosis codes for PPF (J84.170) on separate dates; (2) a medical or pharmacy claim for antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) occurring after the fibrosing ILD date; or (3) evidence of progression via claims-based progressive proxy criteria after the fibrosing ILD date (Online resource 3 has the complete list of proxy criteria).

Patients who developed PPF (cases) were matched with patients who had not yet progressed (comparator group) using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with replacement. To maximize sample size and to allow the best matches to be re-used, a not-yet-progressed comparator could be matched with as many as ten PPF cases, provided the comparator had not yet progressed and maintained continuous enrollment (CE).

For each case, the index date was the date with earliest evidence of progression. The pre-index period was defined as the time between the fibrosing ILD date and the index date. Each comparator was assigned an index date such that the pre-index duration was equal to that of its matched case. If the same comparator was matched with multiple cases, then the comparator’s index date was adjusted to align with each case’s index date.

Section 2.3 (“Statistical Methods”) describes the variables used for matching. Each patient’s variable follow-up period began on their index date and lasted for at least 3 months until the earliest of death, disenrollment, the end of the study period, or evidence of PPF among a comparator. If a comparator progressed after at least 3 months of progression-free follow-up, that patient was subsequently re-assigned to the PPF cohort and matched with a new comparator. A dynamic cohort design was employed to reflect that progression to PPF is a time-varying clinical state rather than a static characteristic. Allowing comparators to transition into the PPF cohort ensured that cohort membership aligned with each patient’s evolving disease status following their initial ILD diagnosis. Matching and follow-up analyses were conducted within insurance type.

Additional inclusion criteria were age at least 18 years as of the fibrosing ILD date and CE in the same insurance program during the pre-ILD baseline, pre-index, and follow-up periods (depicted in Online resource 1). Exclusion criteria were any evidence of IPF (ICD-10 code J84.112) in any position during the study period, a diagnosis of fibrosing ILD during the pre-ILD baseline period, or missing demographic information.

Measurement of Variables and Outcomes

Demographic and clinical characteristics were assessed during the pre-ILD baseline period. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance type, geographic region (in accordance with the US Census Bureau’s regional designations), and year of non-IPF ILD diagnosis. Clinical characteristics included Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score [9] and evidence of comorbidities and other respiratory diseases.

Primary outcomes included all-cause and ILD-related HCRU and costs. These were assessed during the pre-ILD baseline, pre-index, and follow-up periods. Use of ILD-related medications and nonpharmacologic treatments was also assessed during these same time periods. All outcomes were assessed for the overall study population and by insurance type (MAPD and commercial).

The count of all-cause HCRU included ambulatory visits (office and hospital outpatient), emergency room (ER) visits, inpatient (IP) admissions, length of IP stay (in days), and pharmacy fills. Evidence for and time to first all-cause IP admission were assessed during follow-up. The patient- and plan-paid total healthcare costs were reported as the sum of total medical and pharmacy costs. Medical costs were subdivided into ambulatory, ER, IP, and other medical costs. Other medical costs are related to items such as medical devices and at-home care.

Claims were considered ILD-related if they included any of the following: (1) a medical claim with fibrosing ILD in the primary position; (2) a pharmacy claim for antifibrotic medication; or (3) a medical claim with a Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code for a lung-related imaging test (e.g., radiography).

Statistical Methods

To control for potential confounding, PSM was conducted to match an eligible patient with PPF to a comparator within each insurance type. Matching was based on the following pre-ILD baseline variables: age group (18–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80+ years); sex; geographic region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West, other, according to the US Census Bureau’s regional designations); non-IPF ILD diagnosis year; CCI score category (0, 1–2, 3–4, 5+); comorbidities (Table 1, except for acute coronary syndrome, due to low prevalence); medication class use (Table 1, except for antifibrotic use, which was selected as a proxy criterion for PPF); oxygen therapy use (commercial cohort only because the magnitude of the standardized mean difference [SMD] in the MAPD cohort [65.7%] adversely affected the available matching space); smoking status; indicator variables for non-IP visits with provider specialties of interest (primary care physician, rheumatology, pulmonology); counts of all-cause office visits, outpatient visits, and pharmacy HCRU (0 and quartiles 1, 2, 3, and 4 of nonzero values); number of ER visits (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5+); number of IP admissions (0, 1, 2+); and quartiles of all-cause medical and pharmacy costs. Following the recommendation of prior literature [10], an absolute SMD of less than 10.0% was considered balanced for a given covariate. Due to the variable follow-up length, economic outcomes were analyzed as weighted per patient per month (wPPPM). Rather than average each patient’s HCRU or cost, wPPPM combined all HCRU or cost within each cohort and divided it by the cohort’s total combined months of follow-up. This approach aimed to reduce volatility and attenuate the effect of outliers. Cost data were inflation-adjusted to USD 2023 using the medical component of the Consumer Price Index [11].

Multivariable analysis was conducted to compare outcomes of interest for the matched pairs by progression status (PPF vs comparator) within each insurance type during follow-up. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis estimated time to first all-cause IP admission during follow-up. The corresponding hazard was modeled using Cox proportional hazards regression. To avoid conflating the progression-defining event with subsequent progression-related burden (potentially introducing immortal time bias), the first hospitalization was not counted as an outcome if it was respiratory related. Time to first all-cause IP admission therefore reflects the hazard of post-progression hospitalizations, rather than counting the admission as both a progression-defining event and outcome. All-cause costs were compared using a generalized linear model with gamma distribution and log link. Robust errors were used to account for correlation due to matching with replacement, as well as PPF patients serving as comparators prior to progression. Each model included covariates that remained imbalanced (SMD ≥ 10.0%) after PSM. Regression diagnostics assessed goodness-of-fit and violations of model assumptions, including normality of residuals, gamma distribution, and log link function.

Variables are reported as mean (SD), count (proportion), or ratio (95% confidence interval [CI]). Significance was set at the 0.05 level (two-tailed). All analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC). To protect patient privacy, cell counts of less than five are not reported.

Data Source and Ethics Approval

The ORD is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Within the ORD, medical claims information included diagnosis and procedure codes from the ICD-10, Clinical Modification; CPT or Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes; laboratory test codes; service site codes; and other information. Pharmacy claims information included drug name, National Drug Code, dosage, dosage form, and fill date.

Institutional review board approval or waiver of approval was not required for this study because the study data were secondary and de-identified in accordance with the US Department of Health and Human Services Privacy Rule’s requirements for de-identification codified at 45 C.F.R. § 164.514(b).

Results

Study Population

A total of 60,171 matched pairs met the inclusion criteria (Online resource 4 depicts the patient selection and attrition process). The MAPD cohort included 50,683 matched pairs (84.2%), while the remaining 9488 matched pairs (15.8%) were enrolled in commercial insurance plans (Table 1). Matched pairs generated by PSM were well-balanced (SMD < 10.0%) across most baseline characteristics, such as sex, geographic region, year of non-IPF ILD diagnosis, CCI score, smoking status, comorbidities, and use of selected ILD-related medications and nonpharmacologic treatments. Exceptions included race/ethnicity, year of ILD diagnosis, baseline count of unique medications filled (MAPD cohort only), and age group (commercial cohort only). Online resource 5 details the characteristics of the pre-match cohorts by insurance type.

Although PSM was conducted by insurance type, we also assessed pre-match and post-match characteristics of the combined study population (Online resource 6). After PSM, the PPF and comparator groups (i.e., patients without evidence of PPF) were generally well-balanced across age group, geographic region, CCI score, comorbidities, and the use of most ILD-related treatments. Exceptions included race/ethnicity, where patients with PPF were less likely to be Hispanic (11.0% vs 15.5% among the comparator group, SMD = 13.3%). Patients with PPF were also more likely to have their ILD diagnosis in 2023 (11.5% vs 7.4%, SMD = 14.2%).

Follow-up was longer among patients with PPF than the comparator group for both insurance types (Online resource 7). In the MAPD cohort, follow-up lasted for a mean (SD) of 23.5 (18.2) months and 19.3 (16.7) months for the PPF and comparator groups, respectively (SMD = 23.8%); median (interquartile range [IQR]) follow-up was 18.0 (9.4, 33.1) months for PPF and 13.4 (6.7, 26.6) months for comparators. In the commercial cohort, the corresponding follow-up durations averaged 23.4 (18.3) months and 17.8 (15.6) months, respectively (SMD = 33.0%); median (IQR) follow-up was 17.9 (9.2, 32.5) months for PPF and 12.3 (6.2, 23.9) months for comparators.

The unadjusted and adjusted hazard of first IP admission was significantly higher with PPF than the comparator group for both insurance types (Fig. 3). After adjusting for baseline covariates, the hazard ratio (HR) with 95% CI for any IP admission was 2.060 (2.005–2.116) within MAPD. The corresponding HR was 2.324 (2.133–2.532) among commercial enrollees. This assessment excluded any initial respiratory IP admission during which progression to PPF was first recorded. Baseline covariates included variables with a residual imbalance between patients with PPF and comparator group following PSM (MAPD: race/ethnicity, fibrosing ILD diagnosis year, and count of unique medications filled; commercial: age group). Discussion

This retrospective claims analysis determined that patients with non-idiopathic PPF incurred significantly higher all-cause and ILD-related economic burden, compared with a matched comparator group (patients who had not yet progressed). The adjusted all-cause cost ratios associated with PPF were 1.861 and 2.096 for MAPD and commercial insurance, respectively. Among MAPD beneficiaries, wPPPM all-cause total costs averaged $3640 and $1897 for the PPF and comparator groups, respectively. The corresponding costs averaged $6164 and $2741 among commercial enrollees. Treatments and economic burden specific to ILDs were also significantly higher with PPF, regardless of insurance type. Importantly, antifibrotics were used by only 1.0% or less among patients with PPF, despite the availability of efficacious treatment options. These findings underscore PPF as a high-priority target that warrants immediate clinical and policy intervention.

Inpatient HCRU and costs primarily drove the overall burden associated with PPF for both insurance types. In the PPF group, IP costs were 2.8-fold higher than the comparator group in MAPD ($1412 vs $507), and 3.7-fold higher among commercial enrollees ($2094 vs $559). Furthermore, the PPF group had more than double the adjusted HR of first IP admission: 2.060 for MAPD and 2.324 for commercial. Finally, the average length of stay was significantly longer with PPF. From a population health perspective, the significantly elevated HR of IP admission and higher costs associated with PPF highlight a critical area where novel therapies may deliver maximal value, both clinically and economically. These findings support prioritizing new treatment and management approaches that delay or reduce progression of the disease, thereby decreasing the overall healthcare burden associated with PPF.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature regarding PPF burden, including the importance of IP stays [3, 4]. Nili et al. assessed the costs of progression among MAPD and commercial patients in the US with three different types of non-idiopathic ILDs (autoimmune, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, and sarcoidosis) [3]. Overall, the PPF group incurred significantly higher all-cause costs versus their comparator group. Within the autoimmune ILD group, costs averaged $4518 with PPF versus $2303 for the comparator group (wPPPM in USD 2021). Consistent with our results, costs associated with IP stays were primarily responsible for the observed differences: $1657 versus $526 for PPF and comparator groups, respectively. Singer et al. also reported that among US patients enrolled in MAPD or commercial insurance, PPF was associated with significantly higher all-cause costs: $4382 for PPF versus $2243 for their comparator group (mean wPPPM costs in USD 2019) [4]. They determined that IP costs were higher with PPF ($1729 vs $523) and drove much of the overall cost differences. Importantly, our study may inform stakeholders by reporting payer-specific assessments not found in prior research.

Despite the availability of efficacious treatments for PPF, antifibrotic utilization was remarkably low (0.8% MAPD; 1.0% commercial). Such underutilization is particularly noteworthy given that nintedanib has been available to these patients since 2020, based on its demonstrated efficacy in slowing disease progression [12]. Current real-world data on antifibrotic utilization in PPF populations remain limited. Our study addresses that gap by providing the first payer-segment-specific characterization of antifibrotic use in a large PPF cohort. The substantial burden of PPF documented here, coupled with unexpectedly low antifibrotic utilization, suggests potential treatment adoption barriers that challenge optimizing PPF outcomes. Future research should investigate the specific factors driving this treatment gap, including clinician knowledge gaps, patient tolerability concerns, access barriers, or diagnostic and treatment initiation delays.

This study had several strengths, including PSM to derive matched pairs that were well-balanced across most demographic and clinical characteristics. Second, the comparator cohort’s index dates were assigned to equate pre-index duration within each matched pair, thereby aligning the time between fibrosis ILD diagnosis and cohort entry. Comparators were required to be progression-free at index but could later progress and be reassigned to the PPF cohort. This dynamic assignment ensured that follow-up time was attributed to the appropriate disease state. Therefore, the cohort design prevented comparators who eventually progressed from contributing immortal or misclassified person-time to the non-progressed comparator cohort. Finally, the ORD is national in scope and representative of the insured US population at large, which improves generalizability [13].

Likewise, important limitations should be considered. First, administrative claims data are not gathered for research purposes, so the presence of a claim does not confirm a diagnosis. Second, the proxies for progression used in this study (and prior studies) have yet to be validated against medical records. Therefore, a patient classified with PPF may not actually have PPF, while those in the comparator group may have had signs of progression that were not identified in claims data. To mitigate potential overestimation of PPF burden due to misclassification, we applied inclusive proxy criteria and classified patients meeting any criterion in the PPF group. As a result, estimates of relative burden may be conservative. In addition, claims data do not capture some clinical indicators of disease severity (e.g., pulmonary function or imaging findings), which may affect the study outcomes. Third, follow-up duration was variable. Although this study used time to event methods and standardized cost outcomes, variable follow-up length and censoring may affect outcome estimates, particularly if costs are concentrated around specific periods of diagnosis or progression. Fourth, the dynamic cohort design allowed comparators who progressed to eventually be counted in the PPF cohort. Therefore, economic burden and hospitalization risk may reflect evolving disease status while patients were still classified as not yet progressed. As a result, observed outcome differences may be conservative, since early progression in comparators could dilute between‑group contrasts. Fifth, the claims data may not capture all patient care events, such as clinical trial participation, medication samples, or providers encountered outside the patient’s participating network. Finally, we required CE for at least 12 months before and 3 months after index. This limits generalizability to patients who survived the first 3 months of follow-up and had sufficient CE, potentially underrepresenting patients with gaps in coverage or less stable access to care.

Conclusions

Among patients with non-IPF ILD, those who progressed to PPF had significantly greater economic burden and hazard of first IP admission compared with patients who had not yet progressed. Costs associated with IP utilization were primarily responsible for the overall cost differences. The use of antifibrotic treatment among patients with PPF remained low despite its potential to slow disease progression. All observed results were consistent for both MAPD and commercial insurance. Our findings underscore the potential value of early identification of at-risk patients and early PPF interventions that may reduce disease progression and the hazard of IP admission.
