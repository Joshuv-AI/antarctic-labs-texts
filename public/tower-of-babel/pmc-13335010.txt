Synergistic in silico models uncover endocrine and reproductive toxicity of organophosphates
Authors: Aurélien Stab, Guillaume Ollitrault, Florence Severac, Olivier Taboureau, Lucie Oziol, Maya Bimbot, Grégoire Hervé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOrganophosphates (OPs) are ubiquitous environmental contaminants used in applications ranging from agriculture to home electronics. Their endocrine-disrupting properties have primarily been investigated for a limited number of legacy compounds. Here, we developed an in silico model integrating spherical harmonics, QSAR, and molecular docking, with 97% sensitivity for estrogen receptor (ER) disruptors, validated against in vitro assays targeting ERα and androgen receptor (AR), to assess endocrine activity across 42 OPs and commercial mixtures. A large proportion (88%) disrupted ERα and/or AR at sub-toxic levels, with aryl and triaryl phosphates showing the strongest effects and frequent dual receptor activity. Docking analyses indicate that many OPs interact within the ERα ligand-binding domain through predominantly hydrophobic contacts, showing ∼60% overlap with estradiol-binding residues, while reduced polar interactions may explain their antagonistic behavior. Endocrine activity was consistent with reproductive toxicity evidence. A distinct OP sub-cluster exhibited inactivity driven by steric constraints and limited flexibility.Subject areas: Environmental science, Environmental toxicology, Toxicology

Introduction

Organophosphorus compounds or organophosphates (OPs) are widely used as flame retardants and plasticizers, and are used in consumer products such as home electronics, cellphone screens, textiles, plastics, rubber, paints, adhesives, food packaging, furniture, and vehicles, among others.1,2,3,4,5,6 Furthermore, OPs have been prevalent in the environment due to extensive use in agriculture (e.g., pesticides),7,8,9,10,11 industry, and transportation (e.g., hydraulic fluids and lubricants). Public data from 2006 to 2020 estimated that approximately 1 million tons of OPs, excluding pesticides, were produced annually worldwide.2,3,10,12,13 Due to their environmentally persistent and bioaccumulative effects,2,3,10,12,13 OP pollutants pose a risk to ecosystems, the environment, and human health. Water contamination is a major concern,14 as for example, in Lake Victoria, East Africa, where a total level of 18 μg/L of several OPs was recorded, including tricresylphosphate (TCP), tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate (TCEP), and triphenyl phosphate (TPP).15 In urban areas in China, OP air concentrations were found to be in the range of 0.056–6.38 ng/m3, primarily due to the presence of tris(1-chloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TCIPP), TCEP, and tri-n-butylphosphate (TnBP).16 Soils have been contaminated by numerous OPs, such as those detected in farmlands in different regions of China, with concentrations of 62–394 ng/g dry weight.17 OPs have also contaminated and bioaccumulated in aquatic organisms.18 The ubiquitous presence of OPs in the water, air, soil, and food supply implies that humans are exposed to multiple OPs through all routes of uptake (absorption, inhalation, and ingestion). Food can be contaminated by OPs10,14,19 as well. Aviation lubricants are another source of human exposure to certain OPs; specifically, TnBP, TCP, and other triaryl phosphates have been detected in aircraft cabin air on otherwise-routine flights, attributed to the contamination of the ventilation supply air with engine and hydraulic oils20,21,22 with documented health and flight safety impacts, risks for pilots, crew, and passengers.21,23,24 Humans have been exposed, directly and/or indirectly, to OPs, and subsequently OP compounds have been detected in the human body.25,26 A recent study carried out in rural areas in the Middle East identified OP exposure of school children with a mean OP pesticide concentration of 13.6 ng/g in children’s hair.27 Another study of Chinese mothers’ breast milk samples gave a total OP median value of 10.6 ng/mL,28 including tris(2-butoxy ethyl) phosphate (96% detected), TPP, and TnBP.29 Similarly, TnBP and TPP have also been detected in at least 90% of blood samples from study participants at concentrations as high as 1.21 ng/mL.30 Alarming trends have shown urinary OP metabolite concentrations increasing up to 15-fold in the US population from 2002 to 2015.31

The increasing prevalence of OPs in the environment and in biological samples has raised serious concerns about their health and environmental impacts, as OPs are known to exhibit a wide range of toxicological effects. The transition from polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs) to OPs has even been criticized as a regrettable trade-off due to OPs’ toxic and bioaccumulative properties.32 Indeed, OPs are recognized for their extensive toxicity spectrum. For example, neurotoxicity caused by the inhibition of various serine hydrolases by OPs that are essential in regulating neurotransmitter activity33,34,35 has been known for a long time. (AChE) inhibition by OPs leads to cholinergic overstimulation, cholinergic syndrome, and crises that are life-threatening.8,35,36,37,38 Neurodevelopmental effects may also result from gene expression dysregulation caused by even chronic low levels of OP exposure.39,40,41,42 TnBP has been specifically reported to induce both neural growth inhibition and teratogenicity on the zebrafish model.43 In turn, humans experience sex-specific developmental effects of prenatal exposure to OPs on neurobehavioral outcomes observed in 36-month-old children.44 OPs were also associated with decreased gross-motor scores at the age of 12 months.45,46 The risk of developing neurodegenerative disease later in life may also be increased by early exposure to certain neurotoxicants.47,48

From a different perspective, cancer risks are also a major concern, with evidence of hepatotoxic potential and activation of cancer pathways in TCP-treated human hepatocellular cells.49 A study on flame retardants concluded that OPs and their metabolites could promote the progression of bladder cancer by affecting the expression of immune genes.50,51 Additionally, thyroid cancers have been reported52,53 and correlated with thyroid hormone disruption.54,55,56,57

Importantly, the serious reproductive risks of exposure to certain OPs with endocrine-disrupting properties demand attention.55,58,59,60 Certain OPs, such as TCP, are officially classified as reprotoxic by international regulatory agencies.61 This class of chemicals, acting as endocrine disruptors such as bisphenol A62 and phthalate esters,63 has been increasingly implicated in reproductive toxicity.13,55,58,59,64,65,66 Low-level exposure to household dust that contains various EDCs, including OPs, can seriously interfere with human nuclear hormone receptors (NHRs).67 In dust samples containing as little as 4 μg of EDCs, 87% exhibited antagonistic activity on androgen receptors (ARs), and 96% showed agonist activity on estrogen receptors (ERs).67 TCP and TPP are among the most prominent endocrine-disrupting OPs in the environment.57,58,68,69,70 The AR binding affinity of TPP was first reported in 2003,71 but it is still in widespread use. Subsequent studies have shown that TPP, TnBP, and TCP have AR antagonistic activity.72,73 Additionally, TPP has demonstrated dual estrogen receptor α (ERα) activity, acting as an agonist in some cell-based transactivation assays and an antagonist in breast cancer cell lines.59,74 Various estrogenic activities have been reported, with differences depending on the test conditions and the specific in vitro or in vivo models used. These variations are attributed to cell-, tissue-, or animal-specific cofactors, as well as co-regulatory proteins and effector molecules, all of which can either activate or inhibit allosteric enzymes.55

The estrogenic and antiestrogenic behavior observed in TCP72,75 further highlights its extreme susceptibility to interfering with NHRs' computational analyses. Cheminformatics, encompassing quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models and molecular docking, provides powerful tools to explore toxicological pathways in complex biological systems and to support the development of advanced statistically-based predictive models, such as those using deep learning algorithms.76,77 QSARs, by far the most widely used in silico models,77 benefit from international efforts like the CERAPP and COMPARA consortia.78,79 Interestingly, molecular docking has revealed that aryl-phosphates, such as TCP, showed strong binding affinities for both agonistic and antagonistic interactions with human ERα nuclear receptors.80 For ARs, OPs generally exhibit antagonistic binding confirmed through cell assays.72 Computational analyses found that aryl-OPs exhibit stronger estrogenic and androgenic interference than alkyl-OPs,80 with symmetric TCP, such as tri-meta isomer (TmCP), demonstrating the highest activity on NHRs.80 Finally, simulations have shown that AR and ER are among the most vulnerable NHRs prone to OP interference,80 emphasizing the importance of prioritizing in vitro ER and AR testing to effectively assess the endocrine-disrupting potential of such compounds.

However, limitations of in silico models have been noted, particularly in managing unbalanced datasets, which can lead to potentially uncertain results, especially when predicting new or structurally different molecules.76,77,81 Docking approaches are also impeded by low-energy structural complexities, such as protein flexibility, conformational changes, solvation, and allosteric effects, which may alter the accurate prediction of binding affinity scores.76,77,82 Finally, no specific in silico models and comprehensive patterns have been genuinely reported on OPs. To the best of our knowledge, most studies have only focused on the most widespread OPs found in the environment.83

To address this gap, we developed a synergistic framework combining in silico modeling and targeted in vitro validation to evaluate the endocrine-disrupting potential of OPs acting on ERα and AR (Figure 1, Video S1). The approach integrates 3D structural clustering,84 QSAR modeling, and molecular docking to support mechanism-informed hazard identification across a representative and chemically diverse set of 42 OPs and commercial mixtures (see Table S1; Figure S1 in the supplemental information). Together, these analyses provide one of the most comprehensive structure–activity evaluations of endocrine disruption within this chemical family.

Overview of the workflow. 42 OPs were selected and encoded using spherical harmonics (SH) to capture their 3D structures

(A) 3D SH-based similarity matrix was computed and visualized as a dendrogram to define structural clusters. Their potential endocrine-disrupting properties were first assessed using in silico prediction tools (QSAR and docking), followed by experimental validation using reporter gene assays targeting ERα and AR. Integration of these data within the structural similarity clusters provided mechanistic insights into receptor interactions, particularly within the ERα ligand-binding domain, and their relationship to endocrine-disrupting activity and associated reproductive toxicity.

Results

Global and cluster analysis

While the scientific literature includes a substantial body of work on the estrogenic and androgenic interferences of certain OPs, often limited to a few emblematic substances like industrial flame retardants, it is particularly striking to observe, in a broader dataset of 42 substances, that 87% of the studied OPs exhibit adverse effects on AR or ERα at concentrations below 100 mg/L (Figure 2). This figure rises to 88% when including tris(para-cresyl) phosphate (TpCP) and two representative commercial mixtures evaluated in this study (Tables 1, 2, and Table S2). These figures align with recent studies demonstrating that both traditional and emerging OPs disrupt hormonal receptors such as ERα and others at similar concentration levels.85

This initial statistical approach underscores the endocrine-disrupting potential of OPs, particularly toward sensitive NHRs such as ERα and AR. These data also highlight the need for extreme caution when selecting or substituting OPs, as inappropriate substitutions may lead to regrettable and potentially counterproductive outcomes32 and the ongoing challenge of identifying truly safer alternatives. Among the four structural clusters defined in our previous work,84 marked disparities emerged in both the potency of effects and the proportion of active substances across clusters and sub-clusters, as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3. Cluster 4, composed of aryl and triaryl phosphates, distinguished itself by its exceptionally potent endocrine-disrupting activity: 100% of the 15 substances are active at concentrations <100 mg/L. This proportion remains very high at concentrations below 31 mg/L (93%) and even 10 mg/L (67%). Alarmingly, one-third of these molecules exhibit activity at concentrations below 1 mg/L, a critical threshold similar to that of TPP, a well-documented and representative compound in this cluster55,86,87,88 recently classified by the ECHA as an endocrine disruptor under the CLP regulation.61 Other known substances, such as bisphenol A, stand at comparable levels illustrating the potential hazards of such cluster.89 Beyond well-known substances like TPP and TCP, other compounds in cluster 4, such as O,O,O-triphenyl phosphorothioate (cluster4_8), recently classified as reprotoxic,61 interfered at comparable concentrations on ERα. Less-studied compounds, like Cluster4_1 or 4_4, also raise concern, flagging the need to investigate the potential hazards of the other compounds in this critical cluster. Furthermore, nearly two-thirds of the active substances in this cluster display multiple modes of action: 27% have both agonistic and antagonistic activity on ERα, and one-third affect both ERα and AR, a dual effect noted D in Figure 2 that complicates toxicological assessment. This receptor interaction plasticity, combined with effects observed at low concentrations, elevates the risk profile of these substances for both ecosystems and human health. While several previous studies have investigated the endocrine-disrupting potential of OPs on NHR activity, such as ER and AR, the results have often been contradictory or inconclusive, both regarding their overall biological effects80,87 and their specific modes of action as agonists or antagonists.90 This lack of uniformity is now explained by a more refined understanding of the underlying mechanisms involved: the response of an OP is not intrinsic, but highly dependent on the biological model, the experimental conditions (e.g., concentration), and the cellular context. In other words, the same compounds may elicit nuanced, even opposite effects, depending on the biological environment. Also, OPs are known to possess significant conformational flexibility,84 allowing them to adapt to LBD and function as selective modulators, capable of producing either agonistic or antagonistic effects depending on the context. Among them, TPP accumulates all warning signals: low effect concentrations, modulation effects, and dual effects, making this substance of particular concern, and justifying its recent classification by the ECHA.61

Cluster 1 primarily comprises aromatic OPs with higher molar weights and larger hydrodynamic volumes than those in Cluster 4, with a predominance of diphosphate structures. This group displays marked heterogeneity in biological activity, with two contrasting patterns: on the one hand, little to no interference with ERα and AR receptor activity for about 40% of the compounds, primarily grouped within sub-cluster 1C. On the other hand, more than half of the remaining substances, mainly assigned to sub-clusters 1A, 1B, and 1D, exhibit significant endocrine activity at low concentrations (<10 mg/L) (Figure 2).

Cluster 2, composed exclusively of alkylated OP compounds with varying degrees of branching and heterogeneous alkyl chain lengths, including halogenated structures, showed a relatively high proportion of active substances. 62% of OPs displayed adverse antagonist effects on ERα and/or AR activity at concentrations below 10 mg/L. Interestingly, this is the only cluster with no observed modulatory activity on ERα. Sub-cluster 2A is particularly representative of this pattern (Figure 2), comprising short-chain alkyl phosphates with 2–4 carbon atoms per alkyl chain. Among them, TnBP (cluster2_4) and tri-isobutyl phosphate (TiBP, cluster2_3) warrant particular attention. These compounds, commonly used in certain non-flammable hydraulic fluids, showed dual endocrine-disrupting activity affecting both ERα and AR, raising significant concerns about their continued use. In contrast, Cluster2_9 is the only longer-chain alkyl phosphate, each composed of 8 carbons and highly branched, not disrupting ERα and AR. It is worth noting that halogenated-alkyl phosphates do not interfere with this class of NHRs, as recently reported by Kim et al.91 These differences will be further examined in STAR Methods, where mechanistic aspects are explored through molecular docking analyses.

Given the limited biological data available for Cluster 3 (only 1 of the 3 molecules was experimentally tested), no definitive conclusion can be drawn. However, the in silico analyses consistently point out a high toxicity risk, with convergent evidence from both QSAR predictions and docking results for both ER and AR targets.

Analysis of commercial OP-based mixtures

Several OP compounds are employed and marketed as mixtures, particularly in the lubricant industry. Table 1 compiles both in silico and in vitro data for common symmetric TCP isomers (TmCP, TpCP) compared with data from commercially available TCP mixtures. ToCP (cluster4_11), although not present in current blends, was included as a reference substance. All tested isomers showed marked antagonist activity on AR, with maximal activity observed for TpCP (<10 mg/L). In contrast, ERα in vitro assays generally display greater sensitivity across the tested isomers, a trend confirmed as well by in silico models. Notably, even the yeast assay, typically less sensitive than human cell-based tests, revealed significant adverse effects with TpCP.

The other commercial mixture (TBuPP blend) studied comprised tert-butylated TPP derivatives containing a relatively significant amount of TPP (20–30%). The results are compiled in Table 2. TPP largely drives the observed endocrine-disrupting activity of the commercial mixture, inducing effects at concentrations and with activity profiles comparable to those of pure TPP. In contrast, the other individual constituents generally exhibited lower potency and more limited modes of action. However, symmetrical tri-tert-butyl TPP (cluster4_6) exhibited notable interference with the activity of both receptors at low concentrations <10 mg/L supporting the concerns raised by Kim et al. regarding its use in automotive applications.91

In contrast to neurotoxic effects reported by Karaboga84 in the same OP series, commercial blends did not exhibit stronger interference with studied NHR activity than their individual pure compounds, suggesting an absence of synergistic effects at this level. However, given the multiple modes of action of OPs, in vivo studies remain essential to validate these findings within more complex biological systems. Notably, several European national agencies have already classified OP mixtures as endocrine disruptors like TCP.92

Performance and comparison of in silico with in vitro methods

Given the structural diversity and heterogeneous activity profiles of OPs, reliable in silico tools are essential for risk assessment, prioritizing substances, and guiding substitution strategies. In this context, such tools could enable high-throughput in silico screening of numerous structurally diverse compounds, for example, by expanding cluster 1, which our study identified as the most statistically favorable group, to enhance success rates and pinpoint optimal candidates. Subsequent experimental validation would confirm the final selections and help converge on ideal substances. Performance of the in silico predictive model for ERα activity disruption by OPs

Correlation with in vitro reporter gene assay results is shown in terms of accuracy (left) and sensitivity (right). The merged approach combines the 3D-SH QSAR model with docking predictions.

The ER QSAR model developed in this OP context, based on 2D and 3D spherical harmonics descriptors,84 exhibited notably high predictive performance, achieving 77% accuracy and substantially surpassing the widely used CERAPP consensus model (62%) developed by the international consortium.78 Strikingly, integration with molecular docking further elevated the model’s performance, reaching approximately 90% accuracy and an exceptional 97% sensitivity. These results underscore the remarkable predictive capability of this combined in silico model and its potential as a state-of-the-art tool for endocrine disruptor screening. In contrast to ERα, the biological interference with AR activity is relatively more difficult to predict. QSAR models generally performed poorly on independent validation sets,78,79 and for the 42 OPs examined in this study, predictive performance for AR remained limited, with accuracy fluctuating around 50%, comparable to that of COMPARA models. One likely explanation lies in the overall weaker and less distinct activity profile of OPs toward AR than ER. Among the 26 OP compounds active on AR, half displayed moderate activity (between 10 and 100 mg/L), while only 7 of the 20 active compounds in the OECD 458 assay exhibited maximal inhibition above 50%. These weak or ambiguous effects are particularly challenging to model and represent a major source of error for in silico approaches.79 Fortunately, AR activity is almost systematically accompanied by a similar disruption of ERα: 94% of compounds active on AR (<31.6 mg/L) also induce significant disruption of ERα activity at comparable concentrations. More broadly, ERα consistently exhibits markedly higher sensitivity to OPs. Overall, 56% of the tested compounds interfere with ERα at low concentrations (<10 mg/L), compared to only 31% for AR. These findings support the hypothesis that ERα may serve as an operational standalone biomarker for identifying endocrine-disrupting OPs. By prioritizing ERα as a sensitive and mechanistically informative indicator of OP endocrine activity, this approach provides a practical strategy for high-throughput chemical screening and may significantly improve the early identification of potentially hazardous OPs in predictive toxicology applications.

Discussion

Mechanistic pathways of OP interactions with the ERα LBD

General interaction patterns and structural analogy with 17β-estradiol

Interference with the receptor ERα is particularly prevalent among OPs, positioning it as a key factor in endocrine disruption risk. This prompted a detailed investigation into the specific mechanisms of action of representative compounds from each cluster and sub-cluster, aiming to elucidate their distinct interaction patterns with ERα. A total of 27 OPs were successfully docked into the ERα LBD, predominantly exhibiting hydrophobic interactions with amino acid residues largely overlapping those involved in the estradiol–ERα LBD complex, whereas polar interactions were seldom shared with estradiol (Figures 5 and 6). According to our docking model, the other OPs do not appear to bind within the ERα LBD. At least half of the OPs in each cluster consistently interact with a specific subset of residues unique to that cluster, underscoring slightly distinct interaction patterns (Figure 6). OPs typically engage in hydrophobic interactions analogous to those of estradiol, targeting common key amino acid residues such as PHE404, LEU525, LEU391, LEU387, ILE424, ALA350, LEU346, and LEU428. Notably, within Cluster 4, all OPs (100%) interact with ILE424, mirroring the interaction observed with estradiol (Figure 6). As for TPP, the most described representative of the group, it interacts with most of the listed residues, consistent with previous findings by Ji et al., who identified similar amino acid residues.87

Other regions of the ERα ligand-binding pocket, not engaged by estradiol, are frequently targeted by OPs. The OPs in Cluster 4 also bind to all three of these residues, indicating some overlap of ligand-binding activity across clusters. Differential interaction mechanisms of OPs from cluster 1 and 4 with ERα

The residue PHE404 appears to play a central role, as it does with estradiol, interacting with 100% of the molecules bound within the active site, particularly exemplified by the aromatic OPs present in Clusters 1 and 4, which deliver π-π interactions. In contrast to Cluster 4, where substances establish a single energetically significant π-π interaction with PHE404, cluster 1, which is mainly composed of molecules containing two phosphate groups flanked by aromatic rings, adopts a conformation similar to TPP (typical example of cluster 4), but with an additional π-π interaction occurring between the second phosphate group and TRP383 located at the pocket entrance (Figure 7). It is also important to highlight the presence of polar interaction with LYS529 as for substances 1_1, 1_9, 1_10, and 1_13, interactions that appear unique to this cluster. In addition, cluster 1-specific hydrophobic contacts were identified, particularly with LEU354 and GLU380 for most of the docked compounds (Figure 7). The greater number of interactions in Cluster 1 may induce relatively strong binding energies, which could explain the relatively high in vitro activities observed for approximately 60% of the OPs in this cluster, comparable to that of Cluster 4.

Fortunately, although in a lower proportion than observed for neurotoxicity,84 40% of the remaining OPs in Cluster 1 exhibit a markedly different interaction pattern. The most plausible hypothesis is that this more favorable profile possesses high steric constraints, particularly molecular volume and structural rigidity, that exceed a critical energetic threshold, effectively prohibiting access to the LBD. To evaluate this hypothesis, two molecules from Cluster 1 were studied more extensively to evaluate their degrees of conformational freedom by comparing rotational energy differences between cluster1_1 (particularly active) and cluster1_8 (inactive) (Figure 8). These compounds feature two structurally distinct central cores: the former contains a biphenyl moiety, while the latter has a biphenyl separated by a gem-dimethyl group between the two aromatic rings, imposing greater conformational constraints.

Degrees of conformational freedom assessed by comparing rotational energy profiles

Energy values (in kcal/mol) are plotted as a function of the torsional angle around the central bond of the spacer connecting the two aromatic rings. Bonds involved in the rotation are highlighted in pink in the right-hand frame.

The comparison reveals that cluster1_8 (inactive) exhibits a rotational energy barrier approximately 25–30 kcal/mol higher along its central axis, limiting its ability to move within the ERα LBD, compared to 1_1, which is free to rotate around the same axis. In summary, steric and geometric constraints that reduce molecular flexibility render certain OPs incapable of entering the LBD, and thus, inactive toward ERα, whereas more flexible counterparts exhibit a greater potential for interaction, as supported by docking analyses showing significant contacts within the binding pocket.

Distinctive interaction profiles of OPs in cluster 2 with ERα

Cluster 2 exhibits only weak hydrophobic interactions with no unique or specific interactions as shared with other clusters (Figure 7), which can be attributed to the absence of stronger π-π interactions that typically surpass van der Waals forces in binding energy. These binding energy values are reported in the supplemental information. Half of the docked molecules in Cluster 2 interact with LEU428, a residue also engaged by estradiol. More specifically, the two phosphonates in sub-cluster 2_B (2_5, 2_8) display a distinct and unique mode of action by forming a hydrogen bond with THR347 (Figure 7), a likely significant interaction involving the hydroxyl group of threonine, correlated with activity observed at low concentrations comparable to that of TnBP and TiBP (cluster 2_3 and 2_4, respectively). Also, Cluster2_1 exhibits a unique ionic interaction between ARG394 and its carboxylic acid moiety. Within cluster 2, docking analysis of halogenated OPs (2_2, 2_6, 2_7) show substantially weaker interactions with ERα, consistent with the in vitro results reported here and earlier by Kim et al.91 The endocrine-disrupting effects of halogenated OPs are therefore likely mediated by alternative pathways other than direct interaction with hormone receptors, such as disruption of sex hormone biosynthesis, as previously documented via steroidogenesis assays using H295R cell models or other models.87,93,94

Antagonistic vs. agonistic modes of actions

In contrast to estradiol, which forms hydrogen bonds with Glu353, Arg394, and His524, hydrogen bonding specific to OPs is somewhat less frequent and involves almost no overlapping polar contact sites with estradiol (Figure 6). This markedly reduced polar interaction profile, combined with a predominance of hydrophobic contacts, may underlie the antagonistic potential of OPs and their overall limited agonistic activity as observed by Ji et al.90 These OPs may thus be perceived as exogenous compounds capable of obstructing hormone access to the LBD, through the mechanisms previously described. This is particularly true when dual anchoring occurs both inside and outside the LBD, as observed for certain active diphosphates from cluster 1. OPs with estrogen-modulating activity (noted M in Figure 2) did not display distinctive binding modes in docking simulations with ERα, suggesting their agonistic effects may involve alternative pathways, notably via the membrane-bound G protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER), as proposed in recent studies by Ji et al.70,87 While other mechanisms could contribute to their agonist activity, the GPER hypothesis is further supported here by the absence of agonistic responses in yeast assays, which do not express this receptor.

Potential correlation between endocrine disruption and reproductive toxicity of OPs

According to the World Health Organization’s 2023 report, infertility now affects approximately 17.5% of the global population, ranking as the third most pressing public health issue worldwide.95 This alarming prevalence underscores the need for early identification of environmental pollutants that may contribute to reproductive harm. Endocrine disruption established as one of the “ten key characteristics” of reproductive toxicants59,65,96 is hypothesized here to play a central role in the reproductive toxicity of OPs.

It is important to note that reproductive toxicity classifications used in this study are derived from internationally harmonized OECD test guidelines and evaluated through standardized weight-of-evidence approaches across regulatory agencies, ensuring a high level of consistency and comparability despite originating from multiple sources. Among the 42 OPs analyzed in this study, 13 substances are already officially classified by regulatory agencies worldwide either as reproductive toxicants or endocrine disruptors or have demonstrated reproductive toxicity in standardized OECD or EPA studies (OECD TG 414, 415, 416, 421, 422, 443, EPA 870.3700, EPA OTS 798.4900) at doses below 300 mg/kg bw/day (Figure 9). Of these 13 high-risk compounds, 12 OPs exhibit significant in vitro endocrine disruption on the activity of at least one of the two tested hormone receptors (ERα or AR), with critical effect concentrations below 10 mg/L. Only TCEP, a halogenated OP, shows no meaningful activity on ERα or AR in our assays. This absence of interference with receptor activity suggests an alternative mode of action, likely involving interference with steroidogenesis, as previously discussed and reported for several halogenated OPs such as TCEP, tris(2-chloroisopropyl) phosphate (TCPP), and tris(1,3-dichloro-2-propyl) phosphate (TDCPP).93,94 Notably, most OPs in cluster 4 are already recognized as reproductive toxicants according to established regulatory standards. Based on our findings on endocrine disruption and the observed correlation, we anticipate that this classification will eventually extend to all compounds within this cluster, implying, indeed, a vast majority of triarylphosphates. This trend continues to be supported by emerging evidence,55,86,90,97,98 such as a recent study establishing a clear link between long-term endocrine disruption induced by TmCP and pronounced reproductive impairments in zebrafish.98

Endocrine axis interference, particularly via ERα, assessed in vitro or predicted in silico with comparable accuracy, appears to be a robust indicator of OP-induced reproductive toxicity. Combined testing on ER and AR achieves a sensitivity of 92% in detecting recognized reproductive toxicants, underscoring their pivotal role in key mechanisms of reproductive toxicity, one of the key characteristics of reproductive toxicants as reported earlier.59,65,96 Estrogenic and androgenic hormones regulate oogenesis and spermatogenesis and exert negative feedback on the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis via their receptors (ER and AR), with proper hormonal balance and NHR activation being essential for reproductive function and fertility.99 For instance, some OPs may interfere with normal ovarian function and development, leading to abnormal reproduction outcomes such as ovarian disease and miscarriage.100 OPs can also impair sperm fertilizing ability by disrupting maturation processes in the efferent ducts and epididymis, potentially causing male reproductive toxicity.101 TCP is particularly critical, as exposure in male rats resulted in 100% infertility and significant reductions in testis and epididymis weights.60 By analogy, epidemiological studies report decreased sperm concentration and quality in men exposed to OPs, with correlations observed between indoor dust levels of TPP and reduced semen quality in men attending infertility clinics.99

Conversely, the absence of effect on ER and AR activity, pending confirmation through finalized standard tests such as OECD TG 443, shows a notable correlation with the lack of in vivo reproductive toxicity. Among the five OPs exhibiting weak endocrine activity in vitro (>100 mg/L), three have already been evaluated in OECD-compliant reproductive toxicity studies (OECD443, OECD 421, OECD 414, EPA 870.3700), with no adverse effects observed up to 1000 mg/kg bw/day. More specifically, OPs such as cluster1_8 and 2_9, which did not disrupt ERα or AR activity, also failed to elicit developmental or reproductive toxicity in comprehensive studies following OECD 443, a demanding multi-generational assay considered a benchmark for reproductive hazard assessment. Taken together, using in vitro ERα and AR-dependent transcriptional assays, or equally effective combined in silico ER models, as early biomarker tools offers a rapid, cost-efficient strategy for large-scale OP screening, enabling the identification of low-risk OPs and early elimination of those with high endocrine and reproductive toxicity potential.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the endocrine-disrupting potential of various OPs by combining structural clustering, QSAR modeling, molecular docking, and in vitro receptor-based assays. Across a structurally diverse dataset of 42 OPs and commercial mixtures, the vast majority of compounds (87–88%) disrupted ERα or AR-dependent signaling at concentrations below 100 mg L−1, highlighting the widespread endocrine activity within this chemical family and its potential implications for reproductive toxicity. Among the four structural clusters previously identified, aryl and triaryl phosphates in Cluster 4 emerged as the most hazardous group, with all compounds displaying endocrine activity and one-third exhibiting effects at sub-milligram concentrations. In contrast, several compounds in Cluster 1 displayed more favorable profiles, where inactivity toward ERα could be mechanistically explained by steric hindrance and reduced conformational flexibility, preventing access to the LBDs.

The integrated in silico framework combining 3D clustering, QSAR modeling, and molecular docking demonstrated strong predictive performance, reaching up to 97% sensitivity for endocrine disruptors and 92% sensitivity for reproductive toxicants. Beyond predictive accuracy, molecular docking analyses provided mechanistic insights into OP–receptor interactions, revealing how very subtle structural variations influence binding modes and endocrine-disrupting activity.

Taken together, these findings establish an efficient strategy for early hazard identification and chemical prioritization within the organophosphate family. By enabling rapid screening and mechanistic interpretation of endocrine activity, this framework supports safer-by-design chemical development, informed substitution strategies, and proactive regulatory decision-making. More broadly, the approach presented here is readily transferable to other chemical families and may contribute to improving predictive toxicology frameworks for the identification and management of numerous endocrine-disrupting and reprotoxic chemicals.

Limitations of the study

This study presents some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, although the in silico models demonstrated very high predictive performance, their applicability domain remains constrained by the chemical space of organophosphate structures used for training. Extension to other chemical families could be beneficial to the study, but it would require additional training, calibration, and validation. Second, endocrine activity was primarily assessed using in vitro reporter-gene assays targeting ERα and AR, which do not capture the full complexity of endocrine pathways, including metabolism, bioavailability, and systemic feedback mechanisms. In addition, the concentrations used in vitro may exceed typical environmental or human exposure levels, which could lead to an overestimation of biological effects. However, molecular docking provides complementary insights into the intrinsic ability of OPs to interact with receptor binding sites, supporting the identification of compounds with inherent endocrine-disrupting potential. While the combination of multiple in silico approaches, including 3D clustering, QSAR modeling, and molecular docking, further supported by in vitro validation, helps to reduce uncertainty and the risk of misclassification, it does not completely eliminate prediction errors or model-dependent biases. Nevertheless, this combined framework provides a robust approach to identify and prioritize safer candidates within a given chemical family.

The present methodology extends our previous 3D clustering of 42 OPs using Spherical Harmonics (SH), which identified four main clusters originally applied to neurotoxicity.84 This method structures the chemical space based on molecular shape similarity, facilitating the identification and interpretation of toxicity patterns. The in vitro endocrine-disrupting potential of selected OPs, focusing on their interaction with ERα and AR activity, was then assessed in accordance with Tier 2 of the OECD Conceptual Framework for Testing and Assessment of Endocrine Disrupting Chemicals.102 Two types of in vitro ERα- or AR-mediated transcriptional assays were used, one involving recombinant yeasts74 and the other involving human cell lines in accordance with OECD test guidelines, N°455103 and N°458 assays.89 These reporter-gene assays assess transactivation, i.e. the increase in gene transcription mediated by a transcription factor, a receptor-hormonal ligand complex, binding to DNA hormonal response element. These tests screen xenobiotics for endocrine-disrupting potential, with endocrine hormones generating the majority of their effects via the genomic pathway. The assays provide information on the agonist activity of NHRs, as well as on hormone-competitive effects during hormone-dependent transcriptional activity, with particular sensitivity to antagonistic interactions. The 42 tested substances, along with the commercial mixtures, are listed in the supplemental information and in the key resources table.

Experimental model and study participant details

Yeast strains reporter-gene assays

Yeast Estrogen Screen (YES)74 and Yeast Androgen Screen (YAS)104 were used to assess the endocrine-disrupting potential of OPs on ERα and AR activity, respectively. Agonist and antagonist activities were evaluated by exposing yeast cells to the test compounds alone or in combination with the corresponding endogenous hormone.105 YES in vitro testing procedure has been developed in the Genetics Department at Glaxo to identify compounds that can interact with the human ERα-mediated transcriptional activity employing yeast (S. cerevisiae) cells in which the human ER (hER) DNA sequence was stably integrated into the main chromosome. The yeast cells also contain expression plasmids carrying the reporter gene lac-Z, under the transcriptional control of estrogen receptor or androgen receptor elements, and encoding the enzyme β-galactosidase, whose measured activity is considered to be proportional to the transcriptional activity induced after cellular exposure to OPs. This protocol was also developed for screening AR-disrupting activities (YAS in vitro testing), in replacing the hER sequence in the yeast cells with the human AR (hAR). Yeast cells were exposed for 48 hours at 30°C in a growth medium containing the β-galactosidase substrate chlorophenol red-β-D-galactopyranoside (CPRG), in the presence of either control solutions or a series of seven concentrations of test compounds dissolved in DMSO. Concentrations ranged from the maximum solubility down to three orders of magnitude lower (0.01 mg/L to 1000 mg/L). All concentrations were tested in triplicate. To assess potential antagonist effects, yeast cells were co-exposed to the test compounds and reference hormones (17β-estradiol at 2.6.10-4 mg/L for ERα assays and 5α-dihydrotestosterone at 4.6.10-4 mg/L for AR assays). Following the 48-hour incubation, β-galactosidase expression, reflecting ERα- or AR-dependent transcriptional activation or inhibition, was quantified via the production of CPRG, measured spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. As a cell viability control, turbidity linked to yeast growth was assessed at 690 nm. Tests showing >50% yeast mortality were excluded from analysis. Each assay plate included two negative controls (H2O and DMSO), with DMSO serving as the baseline, and one hormone positive control (17-ß-estradiol or 5α-dihydrotestosterone). Activity was quantified as the percentage change relative to this agonist positive control. 4-hydroxytamoxifen (ERα antagonist, concentration tested 3.9x10-3 mg/L to 3.9 mg/L) and flutamide (AR antagonist, concentration tested 2.8x10-2 mg/L to 28 mg/L) were used as antagonist positive controls in co-exposure experiments. For each experiment, a dose-response curve of hormonal controls has been carried out. For “antagonist” experiments, a dose-response curve of antagonist controls was also made.

PC10% was used as the positive control value threshold for agonist tests – Concentration at which the tested compound exhibits 10 % of the activity of the agonist positive control (17-ß-estradiol or 5α-dihydrotestosterone). PC50% was used as the positive control value threshold for antagonist tests, concentration at which the tested compound exhibits 50 % of the inhibition of the antagonist positive control (4-hydroxytamoxifen or flutamide).

Human cell reporter-gene assays

Two transactivation assays based on human cells lines were used according to OECD guidelines, OECD 455 and OECD 458 in vitro assays, measuring ERα and AR-mediated transcriptional activity, respectively. The protocols are detailed in Fouyet et al.106 Agonist and antagonist activities were evaluated by exposing cells to the test OPs alone or in combination with the corresponding endogenous hormone. An OECD 455-like test was adapted for HELN-ERα cell line, and an OECD 458-like was adapted for UALH-hAR cell line Toxem (under license RT00586).107 These two cell lines stably express the firefly luciferase gene, under the transcriptional control of estrogen receptor or androgen receptor elements, whose measured activity is proportional to the transcriptional activity induced after cellular exposure to OPs. HELN-hERα cell lines107 and UALH-hAR cell lines were used to screen the disrupting potency on ERα and AR activities, respectively. These two transactivation assays were developed in accordance with OECD Test Guidelines 455 and 458.89,103 They enable the measurement of hormone-dependent transcriptional activity perturbations by employing cells transfected with a luciferase reporter gene, the expression of which is driven by a hormone response element.

Upon ligand binding (hormone or xenobiotic ligand), the nuclear receptor-ligand complex translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to specific DNA response elements and activates the transcription of a firefly luciferase gene. This leads to increased cellular production of the luciferase enzyme, whose activity is considered to be proportional to nuclear receptor-dependent transcriptional activity. This activity is measured using a luminometer, by enzymatic conversion of its substrate, luciferin, into oxyluciferin, a bioluminescent product. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 100,000 cells per well in test medium (DMEM/F-12 without phenol red, supplemented with 5% DCC-FBS), and incubated for 24 hours at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Following this incubation period, cells were exposed in test medium for 24 h to controls or a range of concentrations of the test samples diluted in DMSO in order to measure their agonist potency. Due to the high sensitivity of these cellular transaction bioassays, the tested concentrations were tenfold lower than tested concentrations in YES/YAS assays, with the highest concentration being 100 mg/L. A total of seven concentrations spanning three orders of magnitude were evaluated, each in quadruplicate. For the antagonist potency measurements, cells were co-exposed to 1.36.10-5 mg/L 17-ß-estradiol (as ERα agonist) or 8.7.10-5 mg/L 5α-dihydrotestosterone (as AR agonist). At the end of cell exposure to different conditions, luciferase activity was measured by adding 0.3 mM luciferin and quantifying the resulting luminescence using a Synergy HT microplate reader (Biotek). Cell viability was assessed using the resazurin assay, which relies on the conversion of resazurin (40 μM) into the fluorescent compound resorufin by metabolically active cells. Fluorescence was measured at 530 nm (excitation) and 600 nm (emission), as described in.108 This step ensures that concentrations selected for the transactivation assays do not induce more than 20% cytotoxicity. Results were excluded if cytotoxicity exceeded 20%. Each assay plate included one negative control (0.1% DMSO), one hormone positive control (both dosed at 80% of their maximum effect, 17-ß-estradiol, 10 nM, or 5α-dihydrotestosterone, 100 nM). Activity was quantified as the percentage change relative to this agonist positive control. 4-hydroxytamoxifen (ERα antagonist, 10 μM) and hydroxyflutamide (AR antagonist, 1 μM) were used as antagonist positive controls in co-exposure experiments. For each experiment, a dose-response curve of hormonal controls has been carried out. For “antagonist” experiments, a dose-response curve of antagonist controls was also made.

PC10% was used as the positive control value threshold for agonist tests, concentration at which the tested compound exhibits 10 % of the activity of the agonist positive control (17-ß-estradiol or 5α-dihydrotestosterone). PC30% was used as the positive control value threshold for antagonist tests, concentration at which the tested compound exhibits 30 % of the inhibition of the antagonist positive control (4-hydroxytamoxifen or hydroxyflutamide).

Method details

QSAR statistical methods

In QSAR, the structure and physicochemical properties of a molecule is generally encoded into diverse sets of parameters i.e., descriptors and embedded into a mathematical function that allow to propose a possible correlation between the descriptors and a biological endpoint and so to develop a predictive model for new molecules. The incorporation of 3D descriptors in QSAR modeling presents a complex trade-off between complexity and reliability.76 Advanced 3D methodologies like Comparative Molecular Field Analysis (CoMFA) and Comparative Molecular Similarity Indices Analysis (CoMSIA) require precise molecular alignment and extensive preparatory work, which can limit their applicability. Simpler 3D methods often struggle to show significant improvements over traditional 2D QSAR models.110,111,112 In contrast, 3D spherical harmonics (3D-SH) descriptors have demonstrated considerable value in effectively capturing the three-dimensional shape of molecules in applications such as pharmacology113 and toxicology.84 A key advantage of 3D-SH descriptors relies on their rotation independence, allowing for consistent molecular feature representation. CERAPP and COMPARA,78,79 have paved the way for the generation of QSAR models for predicting endocrine disruption. These projects have compiled extensive datasets and developed comprehensive consensus models, incorporating up to 40 individual QSARs, which are now accessible through the publicly available software OPERA. Using these large datasets, two new QSARs including 3D spherical harmonics descriptors were built and compared to the consensus models. This section provides a concise overview of the QSAR methodology utilized in this study. Further details are provided in the QSAR Model Reporting Format (QMRF) sections (S3 and S4) in the supplemental information.

The standardization and conformer generation for all molecular structures were conducted using RDKit.84 From these structures, SH pharmacophores and quantitative property 3D descriptors were generated.84,113 RDKit was also employed for the calculation of two-dimensional (2D) molecular descriptors.114 A filtering process was used on the descriptors on each dataset to remove descriptors with near-zero variance and to eliminate those with a pairwise correlation coefficient greater than 0.95. The QSAR modeling process was implemented using the R programming language, leveraging Random Forest (RF) algorithm with maxTrees = 200 and mtry = 3 implemented in the Random Forest package V4.7-1.2.115,116

ER QSAR performance and benchmark

The newly developed 3D-SH-based QSAR model demonstrates reliable and balanced predictive performance on an external validation dataset, with results comparable to the modified CERAPP consensus model, which integrates outputs from 17 individual models contributed by different scientific groups within the CERAPP initiative.78 While both models achieve identical balanced accuracy (91%), the 3D-SH model exhibits a slight improvement in sensitivity (92% vs. 87%), though with a slight trade-off in specificity (89% vs. 94%). The new one is also somewhat simpler than a consensus of 17 models. Notably, 3D-SH descriptors are among the most influential features, representing 17 of the top 30 ranked by mean decrease in Gini impurity. All the other descriptors and details are provided in in the QSAR Model Reporting Format (QMRF) sections (S3 and S4) in the supplemental information.

AR QSAR

Modeling the androgen receptor activity remains more challenging, achieving a balanced accuracy (BA) of 78% compared to 92% for ER binding on COMPARA external validation dataset. Compared to the estrogenic activity endpoint (CERAPP project), COMPARA presented significantly fewer active compounds (17% less), necessitating the addition of 15 drug-like compounds to enhance the active count for AR. Moreover, androgenic activity assessments suffer from inconsistent experimental data in literature. For instance, COMPARA characterized TPP as non-androgenic,79 whereas both Fang et al.71 and our in vitro tests identified it as having androgenic activity. The new resulting model achieved comparable performance to that of the COMPARA consensus model on the validation dataset. Thus, a comparable model for AR disruption could not be developed with the same level of performance.

Docking

Docking analyses of the 42 OPs were performed using co-crystallized structures of AR in its agonist-bound conformation (PDB: 3L3X117) and ERα in its agonist-bound conformation (PDB117), as implemented in the Endocrine Disruptome tool.81 Docking was carried out using the Smina software a variant of AutoDock Vina118,119 tailored to accommodate custom scoring functions.120 The agonistic conformation was considered for both receptors. Only compounds that successfully docked within the ligand-binding pocket and exhibited favorable binding energies, defined as docking scores below –5 kcal/mol, were retained. This threshold reflects the minimum interaction strength likely required to elicit biological activity, as supported previously.81 Partial charges and PDBQT files were obtained using mk_prepare_receptor4.py within Meeko (https://github.com/forlilab/meeko), water molecules were removed. Ligands were prepared as follow: 3D structures were computed using RDKit114 with the ETKDGv3 force field,80 the molecules were set to pH 7 using openbabel121 atom types, partial charges and PDBQT files were obtained using mk_prepare_ligand.py within Meeko (https://github.com/forlilab/meeko). Docking was carried out using Smina,122 a variant of AutoDock Vina118,119 tailored to accommodate custom scoring functions. The ligand-protein interactions were evaluated using the Vinardo scoring function.120 The bounding box for the docking was set to be 40 Å around the bound ligand and the exhaustiveness was set to 128. The docking protocol was evaluated by redocking each co-crystallized ligand-nuclear receptors complex with its known ligand from the co-crystal. We validated the robustness of our approach by successfully recovering the co-crystallized ligand’s position within the binding pocket for the two nuclear receptors in the first docking pose, achieving an RMSD below 2 Å (AR=0.202 and ERα= 0.214). The first docking pose, corresponding to the lowest energy score, was selected for the docking results. Hydrogens positions were optimized using Protoss,123 and the protein-ligand interactions were analysed using PLIP124 and the RMSD calculated with pymol.125

Rotational energy barrier calculation

Rotational energy barriers were used to describe an activity cliff between two molecules from Cluster 1 using the RDKit library. Calculations were performed with the MMFF force field by applying torsional constraints to the relevant bond and subsequently minimizing the molecular energy.114

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and model performance evaluations were conducted using R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and associated packages as detailed in the key resources table, including the Random Forest implementation (randomForest package v4.7-1.2). QSAR model performance was assessed using standard classification metrics including balanced accuracy (BA), sensitivity, specificity, and mean decrease in Gini impurity for feature importance ranking. Docking results were evaluated based on binding energy scores (kcal/mol) and root-mean-square deviation (RMSD, Å) for redocking validation.

No formal hypothesis testing (e.g., t-tests or ANOVA) was performed, as the study relies on predictive modeling, comparative benchmarking, and dose–response classification rather than inferential statistical testing.

The value of n corresponds to the number of chemical compounds analyzed in each dataset (42 organophosphates), or the number of independent experimental replicates in in vitro assays (each concentration tested in quadruplicate across at least three independent experiments, as described in the 4.2 method details section). For docking validation, n corresponds to the number of ligand–receptor complexes analyzed.

Measures of central tendency are reported as mean values. Dispersion and variability in in vitro assays are expressed as standard deviation (SD) across replicate wells unless otherwise specified. No confidence intervals were calculated, as analyses were primarily comparative and classification-based rather than inferential.

Statistical and predictive results are reported in the results section, figure legends, and Table S2, which compiles all QSAR predictions, docking scores, and in vitro assay outcomes.
