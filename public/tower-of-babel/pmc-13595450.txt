Associations Between Preexisting Depression and Anxiety and Postoperative Outcomes Among Medicare Beneficiaries Undergoing Head and Neck Cancer Surgery
Authors: Xiaodan Hu, Daniel Gilmore, Ericka L. Erickson, Catherine T. Haring, Kyle VanKoevering, Nolan B. Seim, Stephen Y. Kang, James W. Rocco, Ann Scheck McAlearney, Lauren E. Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDepression and anxiety are common in patients with head and neck cancer (HNC), but their impacts on postoperative outcomes after major HNC surgery are not well defined. We evaluated whether preexisting, clinically diagnosed depression or anxiety independently predicted short‐term postoperative outcomes among adults undergoing HNC resection.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study using 100% Medicare Standard Analytic Files from 2016 to 2023. Beneficiaries with HNC who underwent oncologic head and neck resection were identified using ICD‐10‐CM/PCS codes. Preexisting depression and anxiety were defined using Chronic Conditions Warehouse algorithms (≥ 1 inpatient or ≥ 2 outpatient claims in the year before surgery). Outcomes included length of stay (LOS), extended LOS (> 75th percentile), in‐hospital complications, and 30‐ and 90‐day readmission and mortality. LOS was modeled using truncated negative binomial regression; binary outcomes were modeled with multivariable logistic regression adjusting for demographics, comorbidities, tumor site, tobacco use, hospital teaching status, procedure complexity, and co‐occurring depression or anxiety.ResultsAmong 45 293 patients (median age 72 years), 6.6% had preexisting depression and 7.6% had preexisting anxiety. Depression was associated with a longer LOS (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.06, 95% CI 1.07–1.13), higher 30‐day mortality (odds ratio [OR] 1.28, 95% CI 1.06–1.54), 90‐day mortality (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.07–1.39), and higher 30‐ and 90‐day readmissions (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.07–1.34; OR 1.27, 95% CI 1.15–1.39), but not with in‐hospital complications. Anxiety was similarly associated with longer LOS (IRR 1.10, 95% CI 1.07–1.13), extended LOS (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.20–1.48), in‐hospital complications (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.06–1.30), and higher 30‐ and 90‐day mortality (OR 1.38, 95% CI 1.16–1.65; OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.18–1.50), but not readmissions.ConclusionsAmong Medicare beneficiaries undergoing HNC surgery, preexisting depression or anxiety are independent markers of increased postoperative risk. Routine mental health screening and targeted perioperative support may be important components of high‐quality head and neck oncologic care.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are common in people with cancer, with meta‐analyses estimating the prevalence of major depression between 8% and 24% across tumor types [1, 2, 3]. These psychiatric conditions are associated with reduced treatment adherence, greater symptom burden, poorer quality of life, and increased risks of disease progression and mortality [4, 5, 6]. Patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) appear to be particularly vulnerable, with prior literature showing that roughly 20%–40% of patients with HNC ultimately develop depression, a proportion that exceeds that observed in many other cancer populations. Further, HNC survivors have a suicide rate of about 63 suicides per 100 000 person‐years, nearly twice that of survivors of other cancers [7, 8, 9, 10].

Despite the prevalence and potential impacts of these conditions, important gaps remain in understanding how preexisting mental health conditions may influence perioperative outcomes among patients undergoing major HNC surgery. Notably, depression in HNC is shaped by a range of factors and has behavioral and biologic pathways through which it could influence oncologic outcomes [11, 12, 13, 14]. Prior work found that patients with HNC were nearly three times as likely as patients with other cancers to screen positive for depression on the Patient Health Questionnaire‐8, yet reported rates of diagnosed depression and antidepressant use similar to those patients with other cancers, suggesting substantial under‐recognition and undertreatment of depression among HNC patients [15]. To our knowledge, no prior studies have evaluated whether preexisting clinically diagnosed depression or anxiety are predictors of short‐term postoperative outcomes after HNC resection in a Medicare population.

To address these gaps, we evaluated patients with HNC to determine whether preexisting depression or anxiety independently predicted postoperative outcomes. We hypothesized that preexisting depression and anxiety would each be independently associated with longer hospitalization and higher risks of early mortality and readmission following HNC surgery, even after accounting for clinical and demographic factors.

Methods

Data Source

Data used in this study was obtained from 100% Standard Analytic Files (SAF) for the years between 2016 and 2023. The SAF are comprehensive claims datasets maintained by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) that contain detailed patient‐level information on healthcare services provided to Medicare fee‐for‐service beneficiaries. Using codes from the Tenth Revision of the International Classification of Disease (ICD‐10), we identified Medicare beneficiaries with a HNC diagnosis (ICD‐10‐CM) who underwent a relevant HNC specific surgical resection (ICD‐10‐PCS) (Tables S1 and S2). Patients were excluded if not continuously enrolled in Medicare or enrolled in a health maintenance organization (HMO) during the analytic period. The analytic period was anchored to each patient's index procedure date and corresponding discharge date and extended from 12 months before the index procedure through 90 days after discharge. This study was not considered human subjects research and therefore did not require exemption or approval from the Institutional Review Board at The Ohio State University.

Variable Construction

ICD‐10‐CM codes (Table S3) derived from the CMS Chronic Conditions Warehouse [16] were used to identify patients with a diagnosis of depression or anxiety in the index patient population. Consistent with definitions used in prior research on mental illness and surgical outcomes [17, 18, 19], we defined a preexisting diagnosis of depression or anxiety as present if, in the 12 months preceding the index surgery, a patient had at least one inpatient or two outpatient Medicare claims in which the respective condition (i.e., depression or anxiety) was coded as the principal diagnosis. Postoperative outcomes included length of stay (LOS), extended LOS, in‐hospital complications, 30‐ and 90‐day readmissions, and 30‐ and 90‐day mortality. Extended LOS was defined as a hospital stay exceeding the 75th percentile for the cohort's LOS distribution. Postoperative complications were identified using ICD‐10‐CM codes for conditions commonly occurring in general surgical populations, including pulmonary failure, pneumonia, myocardial infarction, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, acute renal failure, surgical site infection, gastrointestinal bleeding, and postoperative hemorrhage (Table S4) [17, 20, 21]. Thirty‐ and 90‐day readmissions were defined as any inpatient admission within 30 or 90 days of discharge from the index hospitalization, respectively. Thirty‐ and 90‐day mortality were defined as all‐cause death occurring within 30 or 90 days of the index surgery, respectively, ascertained using the date of death in the Master Beneficiary Summary File.

Control variables included HNC site, preoperative comorbidities, age, sex, race, tobacco use, teaching hospital status, and procedural complexity. HNC site was categorized as the following: oral cavity, oropharynx, larynx, hypopharynx, salivary, and other. Procedure complexity was captured by counting all ICD‐10‐PCS procedure codes on the index surgery claim across the 25 procedure fields and including this count as a continuous marker of case complexity. In preliminary models we also included an indicator for microvascular free flap reconstruction, but this variable was highly correlated with procedure complexity and teaching hospital status and showed evidence of negative confounding, so it was excluded from the final models. Preoperative comorbidities were operationalized using the Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) [22] and calculated using the Comorbidity SAS Macros [23]. Teaching hospital status was determined using the Indirect Medical Education (IME) field. Tobacco use was identified through ICD‐10‐CM codes of F17.2x, Z72.0, Z87.891, and Z71.6.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized using median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage [%]) for categorical variables. Data management was performed using SAS 9.4; analysis was performed using RStudio (Version 2025.05.0 + 496). To determine the relationship between depression, anxiety, and LOS, a truncated negative binominal regression with a trimming of the 1% most extreme LOS values was utilized. Binary outcomes (i.e., extended LOS, in‐hospital complications, 30‐ and 90‐day readmissions, and 30‐ and 90‐day mortality) were each analyzed using multivariable logistic regression models using a logit link. Model diagnostics included assessment of collinearity and overall fit statistics. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two‐sided).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Across 45 293 patients who met the criteria for the final analytic HNC cohort (median age = 72 years, IQR: 67–78), 31.9% (n = 14 461) were female and 88.5% (n = 40 094) were White. Overall, most (42.1%; n = 19 072) had oral cavity cancers. Most patients (n = 35 127, 77.6%) were treated at teaching hospitals, and the median LOS was 5 days (IQR: 2–9). Thirty‐ and 90‐day mortality occurred in 3.7% (n = 1692) and 8.7% (n = 3923) of patients, respectively, while 14.1% (n = 6401) and 24.0% (n = 10 890) were readmitted within 30 and 90 days. Any in‐hospital complication at the index admission occurred in 17.9% (n = 8095) of the cohort, and 53.3% (n = 24 159) had documented tobacco use (Table 1).

Preexisting depression was present in 6.6% of patients (n = 2981). Compared with patients without depression, those with depression were younger (69 vs. 72, p < 0.001). Female patients had higher odds of a depression diagnosis prior to the cancer procedure than their male counterparts (Odds Ratio [OR] = 1.63, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: [1.51–1.76]). Odds of depression did not differ significantly between race categories. Compared with patients who underwent resection for oral cavity cancer, those with hypopharyngeal and laryngeal cancers had a higher odds of a prior depression diagnosis (hypopharynx: OR = 1.63, 95% CI [1.33, 1.98]; larynx: OR = 1.51, 95% CI [1.36, 1.67]), whereas patients with salivary gland cancer had lower odds (OR = 0.80, 95% CI [0.69, 0.92]). Patients with a diagnosis of depression had a higher CCI than those without these conditions (depression: 2, IQR: 1–4 vs. 1, IQR: 0–2, p < 0.001), as well as higher odds of tobacco use (OR = 1.23, 95% CI [1.14, 1.33]).

Anxiety was present in 7.6% (n = 3443) of patients before surgery. Similar to patients with depression, patients with anxiety were younger (69 vs. 72, p < 0.001) and more likely to be female (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: [1.58–1.82]). Compared with oral cavity subsite, patients with hypopharyngeal, laryngeal, and oropharyngeal cancers had higher odds of anxiety (hypopharynx: OR = 1.64, 95% CI [1.35, 1.98]; larynx: OR = 1.87, 95% CI [1.71, 2.06]; oropharynx: OR = 1.23, 95% CI [1.08, 1.39]), whereas patients with salivary gland cancer had lower odds (OR = 0.74, 95% CI [0.64, 0.85]). Patients with a diagnosis of anxiety had a higher CCI index (2, IQR: 1–3 vs. 1, IQR: 0–2, p < 0.001) and higher odds of documented tobacco use (OR = 1.23, 95% CI [1.14, 1.32]).

Postoperative Outcomes: Impact of Depression

In the multivariable truncated negative binomial model, a preoperative diagnosis of depression was associated with longer hospital LOS. After controlling for anxiety and clinical covariates, those with depression had a 1.06 times higher expected LOS (IRR, 95% CI [1.07, 1.13]) relative to patients without depression. In the corresponding logistic models, depression was not associated with odds of extended LOS but consistently associated with higher mortality and readmission risks. Relative to patients without depression, those with depression had higher odds of 30‐day mortality (OR = 1.28, 95% CI [1.06, 1.54]) and 90‐day mortality (OR = 1.22, 95% CI [1.07, 1.39]). Patients with depression also had higher odds of 30‐day readmission (OR = 1.20, 95% CI [1.07, 1.34]) and 90‐day readmission (OR = 1.27, 95% CI [1.15, 1.39]) compared to those without depression (all p < 0.05). In contrast, depression was not independently associated with postoperative complications at the index admission after controlling for anxiety and clinical covariates (OR = 1.05, 95% CI [0.94, 1.17]) (Table 2).

Postoperative Outcomes: Impact of Anxiety

Results for preoperative anxiety followed a broadly similar pattern but showed stronger associations for immediate postoperative outcomes such as LOS, readmission and complications at index. In the multivariable truncated negative binomial, those with anxiety had a 1.10 times higher expected LOS (IRR, 95% CI [1.07, 1.13]) compared with patients without anxiety. In the corresponding logistic models, anxiety was also associated with higher odds of extended LOS (OR = 1.33, 95% CI [1.20, 1.48]), 30‐day mortality (OR = 1.38, 95% CI [1.16, 1.65]), 90‐day mortality (OR = 1.33, 95% CI [1.18, 1.50]), and any complication during the index hospitalization (OR = 1.18, 95% CI [1.06, 1.30]; all p < 0.05) compared to patients without anxiety. Anxiety was not independently associated with 30‐ or 90‐day readmission after adjustment for depression and other control variables. (Table 2).

Discussion

In this national cohort of Medicare beneficiaries undergoing HNC surgery, preexisting depression or anxiety were independently associated with worse short‐term outcomes. Patients with either depression or anxiety had longer hospital stays and higher risks of adverse events after surgery. After adjustment for demographic, clinical, and hospital factors, depression was associated with higher odds of 30‐ and 90‐day readmission and early mortality, whereas anxiety was more strongly associated with prolonged length of stay, in‐hospital complications, and increased 30‐ and 90‐day mortality. These findings suggest that mental health comorbidity may represent a perioperative risk not fully explained by traditional clinical variables.

Our results build on prior work demonstrating a high burden of psychological morbidity in HNC and its association with poorer survival and quality of life [7, 24, 25]. In our cohort of over 45 000 patients undergoing HNC surgery, we found that preexisting depression or anxiety were associated with longer length of stay and higher rates of inpatient complications, readmissions, and mortality. Earlier registry and cohort studies have linked depression to worse survival in HNC [25, 26], while more recent work has identified pretreatment anxiety as an independent predictor of worse survival [27]. Outside of HNC, analyses of Medicare beneficiaries undergoing gastrointestinal cancer surgery have shown that preexisting depression is associated with longer length of stay, more complications, higher readmissions, greater 90‐day mortality, and increased post‐discharge expenditures, but these studies combined heterogeneous cancer types and did not evaluate anxiety [17]. Taken together, the existing literature and our findings indicate that mental health disorders are both common and clinically meaningful risk factors in complex cancer surgery.

Several mechanisms may explain the associations we observed. Depression and anxiety can impair self‐care, reduce adherence to perioperative instructions and rehabilitation, and contribute to ongoing tobacco and alcohol use, which are common in HNC and are themselves linked to worse surgical outcomes. Physiologic pathways found in patients with depression and anxiety, including chronic activation of stress systems, systemic inflammation, and impaired immune function, may also increase susceptibility to infection, thromboembolic events, and poor wound healing [12, 25]. System‐level factors such as fragmented care coordination, limited psychosocial resources, and inadequate response to positive distress screens likely compound these individual‐level risks [14, 28].

Our findings have direct clinical implications. First, they support the use of routine, structured screening for depression and anxiety in patients with HNC being evaluated for major surgery, rather than relying on existing problem lists in the medical record. Brief tools such as the PHQ‐9 and GAD‐7 could be incorporated into preoperative assessment to identify high‐risk patients. Second, recognition of depression or anxiety should prompt anticipatory planning: closer perioperative monitoring, more aggressive discharge planning and follow‐up, and early involvement of psychiatry, psychology, or social work. Randomized trials in HNC have shown that prophylactic use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, such as escitalopram, can reduce the incidence of major depression during treatment by more than half, underscoring the feasibility of targeted preventive interventions in this population [29]. Finally, our results provide a rationale for future trials testing whether integrated psychosocial and “prehabilitation” interventions can improve not only symptom burden but also reduce complications, readmissions, and early mortality after HNC surgery.

This study has limitations. Claims‐based algorithms for depression and anxiety are highly specific but insensitive, so many patients with clinically relevant symptoms are likely misclassified as unaffected, biasing associations toward the null. We lacked detailed information on tumor stage, HPV status, margin status, and adjuvant therapy, which may confound the relationship between mental health and outcomes. Nasopharyngeal and sinonasal cancer cases were excluded from all models due to small sample sizes and heterogeneity in treatment patterns. Symptom severity could not be assessed, nor could we capture patients enrolled in Medicare Advantage or younger populations. In addition, because claims may not reliably capture the true diagnosis date of HNC, our definitions cannot distinguish long‐standing depression or anxiety from conditions that emerged in response to the cancer diagnosis; the exposure therefore reflects any clinically recognized disorder in the year before surgery, regardless of etiology. This likely introduces causal heterogeneity by combining baseline mental health burden with cancer‐related distress, which may obscure the specific drivers of perioperative anxiety or depression—an important opportunity for future longitudinal studies.

Future work should examine whether these associations vary among patients with HPV‐positive oropharyngeal cancer, who differ in prognosis, treatment patterns, and survivorship needs from those with HPV‐negative disease. Interventional studies are also needed to determine whether systematic preoperative screening, integrated psycho‐oncology or collaborative care models, and targeted prehabilitation can reduce complications, readmissions, and early mortality among high‐risk patients. Finally, extending this work to study Medicare Advantage and younger, commercially insured populations and incorporating mental health comorbidity into perioperative risk prediction tools would strengthen the generalizability and clinical utility of our findings. Together, these efforts could move the field from recognizing depression and anxiety as prognostic markers to testing and implementing strategies that meaningfully improve outcomes for patients undergoing head and neck cancer surgery.
