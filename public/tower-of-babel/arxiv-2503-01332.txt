Answer, Refuse, or Guess? Investigating Risk-Aware Decision Making in Language Models
Cheng-Kuang Wu, Zhi Rui Tam, Chieh-Yen Lin, Yun-Nung Chen, Hung-yi Lee

Abstract
Knowing when to answer or refuse is crucial for safe and reliable decision-making language agents. Although prior work has introduced refusal strategies to boost LMs’ reliability, how these models adapt their decisions to different risk levels remains underexplored. We formalize the task of risk-aware decision-making, expose critical weaknesses in existing LMs, and propose skill-decomposition solutions to mitigate them. Our findings show that even cutting-edge LMs–both regular and reasoning models–still require explicit prompt chaining to handle the task effectively, revealing the challenges that must be overcome to achieve truly autonomous decision-making agents.



1
Introduction

Autonomous agents capable of decision making in a variety of scenarios have been of central interest in the field of artificial intelligence
Wooldridge and Jennings 1995
.
Recent advancements in language models (LMs)
Brown et al. 2020
;
Wei et al. 2021
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
have given rise to the emergence of
language agents

Yao et al. 2022
;
Zhou et al. 2023
;
Shinn et al. 2024
, which can interpret goals and achieve them through natural language interaction.

A critical challenge arises when an LM lacks sufficient knowledge to make confident decisions, thereby compromising their reliability.
To mitigate this issue, prior research has proposed mechanisms that enable LMs to “refuse to answer”
Feng et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
, deferring the language agent’s decisions in situations of high uncertainty.
This refusal strategy allows for human intervention, reducing the risk of making errors.

While prior research has studied refusal mechanisms to mitigate errors, an important yet underexplored aspect is that the decision to answer or refuse should be context-dependent and guided by the
risk level
of the scenario, as shown in Figure
1
.
If the risk level is high, errors can have severe consequences, making it essential for LMs to refuse unless they are highly confident in their choice.
Conversely, when the situation is less risky, LMs can afford to provide answers with moderate confidence.
However, how LMs adapt their decision-making processes when they are informed of varying risk levels remains underexplored in the existing literature.
To address this gap, we aim to investigate LMs’ decision-making abilities when informed of different risk levels.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

)

2
Evaluation Framework

2.1
General Setup

In this work, we focus on multiple-choice decision-making scenarios due to their unambiguity and ease for evaluation.
In these scenarios, LMs face questions with a set of possible choices.
Specifically, the LM must either:

1.

Answer with one of the choices if it considers answering more rewarding than refusing, or

2.

Refuse to answer if it judges that deferring the decision is more optimal instead.

Given the LM’s decisions, the outcomes are quantified as follows:

•

s_{\mathrm{cor}}
: the reward if the LM answers
cor
rectly,

•

s_{\mathrm{inc}}
: the penalty if the answer is
inc
orrect,

•

s_{\mathrm{ref}}
: the score if the LM
ref
uses to answer.

With this formulation, one can alter the risk level of a scenario by setting the values of
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}},s_{\mathrm{ref}})
, which we elaborate in section
2.3
.
For a dataset of
N
total instances, suppose the LM answers
n_{\mathrm{ans}}=n_{\mathrm{cor}}+n_{\mathrm{inc}}
instances, and refuses
n_{\mathrm{ref}}
instances.
Then, its raw total score can then be calculated as:

S_{\mathrm{raw}}=n_{\mathrm{cor}}\times s_{\mathrm{cor}}+n_{\mathrm{inc}}\times s_{\mathrm{inc}}+n_{\mathrm{ref}}\times s_{\mathrm{ref}}

To facilitate comparisons with an “ideal” scenario where every question is answered correctly, we normalize
S_{\mathrm{raw}}
by
Ns_{\mathrm{cor}}
:

S=\frac{S_{\mathrm{raw}}}{N\times s_{\mathrm{cor}}}

Thus,
S=1
indicates the maximum performance, whereas negative
S
implies that the penalties on incorrect instances outweigh the rewards gained.
We use
S
as the performance metric in this work.

2.2
Datasets

We use the following three multiple-choice benchmark datasets in our experiments:

MedQA

Jin et al. 2020
;
Saab et al. 2024

The questions are collected from medical licensing exams, which evaluate medical doctors’ professional knowledge in clinical practice.
Since errors in medical treatment or diagnosis have serious consequences, MedQA naturally reflects the risk-aware setting where deferring an uncertain answer to a medical doctor may be preferable.

MMLU

Hendrycks et al. 2020

This benchmark includes 57 subjects in humanities, social science, STEM fields, and more.
The broad coverage ensures that LMs are tested on diverse topics.

GPQA

Rein et al. 2023

The dataset consists of graduate-level questions in physics, chemistry, and biology.
Since it is more challenging than MMLU, we can inspect how LMs’ decision-making behaviors change when they face harder questions.

2.3
Risk Level Settings

To model different risk conditions, we vary the tuple
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})
while keeping
s_{\mathrm{ref}}=0
. In other words, refusing to answer yields neither reward nor penalty.
This design aligns with the intuition that deferring a risky decision often avoids both potential gains and losses.

Expected Reward Threshold

All the questions in MedQA, MMLU, and GPQA feature four answer choices with exactly one correct option.
Consider an LM guessing randomly among these four choices, its expected reward per question is:

\mathbb{E}[\text{reward from guess}]\;=\;\tfrac{1}{4}\,s_{\mathrm{cor}}\;+\;\tfrac{3}{4}\,s_{\mathrm{inc}}.

If this expected value is
positive
, then guessing confers a net benefit, whereas a
negative
expected value implies that random attempts are detrimental.
At the specific tuple
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})=(3,-1)
, this expected reward is exactly zero.

High-Risk vs. Low-Risk Settings

We interpret an expected value of zero (like the setting of
(3,-1)
) as a threshold, partitioning other score configurations into
high-risk
(negative expected reward) and
low-risk
(positive expected reward).
For example, high-risk configurations of
(1,-8)
and
(1,-4)
make the expected reward negative, so LMs should attempt answering only when they are highly confident.
Conversely, in low-risk settings such as
(4,-1)
or
(8,-1)
, the expected reward from guessing is positive, suggesting that the LM should generally attempt an answer.

In the following experiments, we evaluate the LM’s behavior under four representative risk settings:
(1,-8)
,
(1,-4)
,
(4,-1)
, and
(8,-1)
, unless stated otherwise.
Our analysis explores whether an LM’s decision-making behavior aligns with a rational policy under each risk level.

3
Preliminary Findings

3.1
LMs Fail to Behave Rationally When Informed of the Risk

Ideally, once a decision-making LM is explicitly informed of the risk specified by
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}},s_{\mathrm{ref}})
, it should adopt a strategy that outperforms the baseline where the LM is unaware of the risk.
To investigate whether this holds in practice, we compare the metric
S
(see Section
2.1
) under two prompts:

No risk informed prompt
(Figure
2
):
The instructions simply ask the LM to solve the given task without mentioning the associated risk.

Risk-informing prompt
(Figure
3
):
The instructions explicitly inform the LM of the risk and highlight that it may refuse to answer.

We tested six LMs from three different model families, and the results are shown in the first two rows (“no risk informed” and “risk informed”) of each model’s performance in Table
2
.
From these results, we draw two main findings.
First,
performance improves in high-risk settings
, and this pattern holds consistently across different models and datasets.
Second,
performance fails to improve in low-risk settings
.
To further analyze the failure, we examine the proportion of refused answers (i.e., the fraction of questions for which a model chose to refuse rather than answer) under four representative risk levels.
We also add two risk levels
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})=(0,-1)
and
(1,0)
in the refusal analysis to investigate whether LMs are able to make rational decisions in extreme conditions.

Figure
4(a)
shows these refusal proportions on GPQA
Rein et al. 2023
.
Notably, at the high-risk setting
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})=(0,-1)
, where refusing is always the best decision since answering yields no scores, LMs only refuse 6-42% of instances.
Additionally, at low-risk settings where
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})
is
(4,-1)
,
(8,-1)
, or even
(1,0)
, LMs still refuse to answer some instances–despite the fact that random guessing would yield a positive expected reward.
To conclude,
LMs fail to act rationally in both high-risk and low-risk situations
.

We hypothesize that this suboptimal behavior arises from issues related to
compositional generalization

Hupkes et al. 2020
;
Yin et al. 2024
;
Hosseini et al. 2024
, which refers to the ability of a model to solve tasks that require novel combinations of its learned skills.
Take our risk-aware decision making task as an example, the model must combine at least the following three skills:

Downstream task:

In our work, the downstream task is multi-choice question answering.

Confidence estimation:

Assessing how likely it is that the chosen answer is correct.
This ability is often referred to as calibration
Guo et al. 2017
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Kadavath et al. 2022
.

Reasoning with expected values:

Weighing rewards and penalties based on the estimated confidence to decide between answering or refusing.

Specifically, we hypothesize that the observed failure in Figure
4(a)
arises from LMs’ inability to
reason with expected values
when the task is compositional, and not only requires LMs to perform
this reasoning skill
.
To test whether this hypothesis underlies the observed failure, we investigate a “pure gambling” subtask to isolate the skill of
expected-value reasoning
in the next subsection.

Figure 6:
The stepwise prompt (left) versus prompt chaining (right). Note that the prompt for “Skill 1: Question Answering” in prompt chaining is exactly the same as the “No risk informed prompt” in Figure
2
. We adopt the naming of “stepwise prompt” and “prompt chaining” from
Sun et al. 2024
, where they study text summarization.

3.2
LMs Behave More Rationally in the Pure Gambling Subtask

Figure
5
displays the prompt for the pure gambling subtask.
Since we cannot assess refusal rates using a single prompt instance, we manually paraphrased the prompt into 100 variations with equivalent semantics (e.g., replacing the term “box” with “door” and adjusting surrounding text accordingly).
Examples of the paraphrased prompts are shown in Appendix
A.1
.
Unlike the risk-informing prompt in Figure
3
, this pure gambling setup removes the need for
question answering
or
confidence estimation
, thereby isolating the LM’s ability to
reason about expected values
.
Because the subtask still provides four choices, our previously defined high-risk (negative expected reward) and low-risk (positive expected reward) settings apply directly.

Figure
4(b)
reports the refusal proportions for each LM under this pure gambling scenario.
Compared to results in Figure
4(a)
, we observe more rational behaviors in both high-risk and low-risk scenarios.
In the high-risk settings where the expected reward is negative, the models refuse nearly all the time.
Notably, when
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})=(0,-1)
, the 6-42% refusal rate in Figure
4(a)
improves to 93-99% in Figure
4(b)
.
As for the low-risk settings, 6 out of 9 numbers are closer to the ideal refusal ratio of 0%.

Overall, the results suggest that LMs behave more rationally when expected-value reasoning is the only skill required.
In contrast, the same LMs fail to achieve rational behavior when
downstream task
,
confidence estimation
, and
expected-value reasoning
are required, aligning with our hypothesis about compositional generalization failures.
Building on this insight, the next section demonstrates how careful decomposition of these skills can improve risk-aware decision making.

4
Improving Risk-Aware Decision Making through Skill Decomposition

4.1
Methods

We propose two straightforward solutions based on decomposition of the three core skills:

Stepwise prompt
:
As illustrated in Figure
6
(left), the stepwise prompt explicitly guides the model through each skill in a
single
inference pass.
The prompt is partitioned into structured steps, ensuring the LM focuses on each skill sequentially.

Prompt chaining
:
In contrast, prompt chaining (Figure
6
, right) divides the prompting process into
multiple
inference steps—one step per skill.
The output of each step is passed as input to the next, so the LM uses each skill independently.

Including the previously mentioned “no risk informed” and “risk-informing” settings (§
3
), comparisons between methods are shown in Table
1
.

4.2
Consideration of Prompt Sensitivity

A critical aspect of LM evaluation is
prompt sensitivity
: ensuring that differences in performance across methods do not merely reflect how prompts are constructed.
Following the recommendations of
Mizrahi et al. 2024
, we conduct a
multi-prompt
evaluation to enhance the robustness of our findings.
Specifically, for each of our four methods–no risk informed prompt (Figure
2
), risk-informing prompt (Figure
3
), stepwise prompt (Figure
6
, left), and prompt chaining (Figure
6
, right)–we create three distinct prompt variations (See Appendix
A.2
), all semantically identical but phrased differently.

Implementation Details

For better reproducibility, we elaborate the hyperparameters and implementation in Appendix

.

4.4
Main Results

We report scores for six LMs across three datasets (MedQA, MMLU, and GPQA) under four representative risk levels in Table
2
.
The reported scores are calculated by averaging the performance across three prompt variations.
Here are the key findings:

4.4.1
Prompt Chaining Consistently Boost Performance in High-Risk Settings

In the high-risk columns
(1,-8)
and
(1,-4)
, every LM shows a notable performance gain over other three methods when using the prompt chaining strategy.
Nearly all LMs reach their best scores, with the only one exception of
gemini-1.5-pro-002
on MMLU.
This indicates that decomposing the task into separate inference steps for each skill is especially beneficial in high-risk scenarios.

4.4.2
Stepwise Prompts Do NOT Offer Consistent Improvements

In high-risk settings, although the stepwise prompt (Figure
6
, left) explicitly outlines the three skills, it seldom outperforms the simpler risk-informing prompt (Figure
3
).
By contrast, prompt chaining consistently yields better results.
This comparison highlights that merely decomposing skills does not always guarantee a performance boost; the method to implement skill decomposition is also crucial.
We discuss why prompt chaining is more effective than stepwise prompts in section
5.1
.

4.4.3
Risk-Informing Methods Degrade Performance in Low-Risk Settings

Under low-risk settings
(4,-1)
and
(8,-1)
, risk-informing methods—including risk-informed prompts, stepwise prompts, and prompt chaining—actually
reduce
the average performance compared to the baseline (no risk informed) approach.
We discuss the implications of this finding in section
5.2
.

5
Discussion

5.1
LMs’ Ability to Reason with Expected Values Is Elicited by Prompt Chaining

As suggested in Section
3
, our core hypothesis is that
LMs’ expected-value reasoning ability remains hidden when the compositional task also requires other skills
.
To test whether skill decomposition elicits this ability, we examined how often LMs explicitly calculate expected values to answer or refuse.
Figure
7
illustrates examples of using (left) versus not using (right) expected-value reasoning.

To quantify the results, we provided
gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18
with a 6-shot prompt containing three positive (using expected-value reasoning) and three negative (lacking such reasoning) examples, and instruct it to classify LMs’ output.
As shown in Figure
8
, prompt chaining dramatically increases the proportions of explicit expected-value calculations, whereas risk-informing or stepwise prompts do not.
Notably, simply scaling the models does not consistently enhance proportions of expected-value reasoning for risk-informing or stepwise prompts.
We further discuss why prompt chaining is more effective than stepwise prompts in Appendix
D
.

5.2
Why Does Prompt Chaining Perform Worse in Low-Risk Settings?

Prompt chaining underperforms the “no risk informed” baseline in low-risk settings.
We assume it is because
LMs still refuse some questions
, thereby losing the positive expected reward from random guessing.
To test this, we plot each experiment run as a dot on Figure
9
.
The three dots on the lower right, for instance, represent running three prompt variations with
gpt-4o-mini
on GPQA.
The figure shows that higher refusal rates correlate with greater degradation.
This finding gives a straightforward solution:
In low-risk scenarios with positive expected rewards, do
not
give LMs the option to refuse–let them answer every question instead.

Do Reasoning Models Eliminate the Need for Skill Decomposition?

Recent progress in reasoning LMs–such as
o1
,
o1-mini
,
o3-mini
, and
DeepSeek-R1
–has substantially improved performance on complex reasoning tasks
Guo et al. 2025
.
This naturally raises the question of whether these models can already tackle risk-aware decision making
without
skill decomposition.
Due to the high cost of reasoning LMs and our budget constraints, we run
o3-mini-2025-01-31
and
DeepSeek-R1
on GPQA in high-risk settings as representative experiments, and show the results in Table
3
.
Results indicate that reasoning LMs still require skill decomposition through prompt chaining to handle the task more effectively.

6
Related Work

Enabling Language Models to Refuse

LMs tend to hallucinate when they lack sufficient knowledge to fulfill instructions
Ji et al. 2023
, so increasing efforts have been made to enable them to refuse.

Zhang et al. 2024
identify queries that exceed LMs’ parametric knowledge and construct refusal-aware instruction-tuning data to fine-tune them.

Feng et al. 2024
propose a collaborative approach where multiple LMs probe each other to detect knowledge gaps, thereby improving refusal accuracy.

Xu et al. 2024
use reinforcement learning with a reward model to incentivize refusal for out-of-knowledge questions.
Our work extends this line of research by examining not just whether an LM should refuse, but also when it should do so in the context of varying risk levels.

Biases of LMs in Decision Making

Prior work discusses decision-making biases of LMs from various perspectives.

Horton 2023
replicates classic experiments in behavioral economics
Kahneman et al. 1986
;
Samuelson and Zeckhauser 1988
;
Charness and Rabin 2002
on GPT-3
Brown et al. 2020
, showing that it often reproduces human-like biases.

Ross et al. 2024
further study LMs’ behavioral tendencies in economic games via utility theory.

Jia et al. 2024
propose an evaluation framework to estimate an LM’s risk preference, probability weighting, and loss aversion.
However, these studies primarily rely on toy datasets or game-based scenarios, focusing on measuring biases rather than identifying their causes or offering solutions.
In contrast, we identify LMs’ issues from a compositional generalization perspective, and propose skill-decomposition methods to address the identified shortcomings.

Compositional Generalization

Lake and Baroni 2018
introduce the SCAN dataset to study compositional generalization (CG) in neural networks, revealing that they may lack compositional abilities.
Since then, this topic has received increasing attention.
For instance,
Li et al. 2021
study CG in encoder-decoder Transformers for machine translation.

Press et al. 2023
investigate LMs’ performance gap between compositional and individual questions on multi-hop question answering.

Hosseini et al. 2024
examine LMs’ instance-level CG in arithmetic questions (
Q
), where each instance is composed in a way that a variable of
Q_{2}
depends on the solution to
Q_{1}
.
This work extends the study of CG to a new task of
risk-aware decision making
, revealing new compositional gaps in current LMs.

7
Conclusion and Future Work

In this work, we formalize the task of risk-aware decision making, and reveal current LMs’ suboptimal behaviors across different risk levels.
We draw two main conclusions from the experiments:
(1) In high-risk settings, skill decomposition via prompt chaining substantially improves performance for both regular and reasoning LMs.
(2) In low-risk settings, we should simply omit the refusal option and allow LMs to attempt every question, given the positive expected reward of guessing.

By framing risk numerically, we can systematically evaluate LMs’ performance and identify their irrational decision-making behaviors.
However, real-world risk often appears through more subtle natural-language cues, like the patients’ health conditions in Figure
1
.
How LMs’ biases manifest in these less structured scenarios remains an open question, which we leave for future work.

Limitations

Risk Representation

In our evaluation framework, we presents risk levels to LMs as numeric values, which enables us to conduct controlled experiments to study LMs’ behaviors systematically.
However, risk might be described in natural language in practical scenarios.
For example, in medical practice, health conditions are typically documented in textual descriptions.
For patients with cormobidities, the consequences of making the wrong decision are severe, which corresponds to the high-risk setting;
for patients with healthy underlying conditions, the consequences would be more benign, which corresponds to the low-risk setting.
In these scenarios, either humans must formulate risk factors into numeric scores, or LMs themselves must learn to integrate textual descriptions of risk into the decision-making process.
How to quantify LMs’ performance and biases in these scenarios remains an open question.

Improvements on Individual Skills

In our skill-decomposition solution, we decompose the task of risk-aware decision making into three skills:
downstream task
,
confidence estimation
, and
expected-value reasoning
.
Therefore, one obvious approach to boost the final performance is to improve these individual skills.
For example, there are a lot of techniques to improve LMs’ confidence estimation from existing calibration studies
Guo et al. 2017
;
Lin et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2023
;
Xiong et al. 2024
.
Applying these techniques to the confidence estimation step has the potential to enhance the overall performance in risk-aware decision making, as evidenced by the discussion in Appendix
E
, where we show that better confidence estimation indeed contributes to higher scores.
As a result, although we do not elaborate how to improve performance on these individual skills in detail, we consider this as a promising direction.

Integrating Existing Refusal Mechanisms

Our work complements existing refusal strategies by extending a new research direction on how LMs should adapt their decisions according to varying risk levels.
In principle, existing methods
Feng et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
could be integrated in our framework to further refine refusal performance.
However, we view these two lines of research (i.e., refusal mechanisms
per se
vs. risk-aware decision making) as orthogonal, so we do not pursue a detailed integration of their methods in this paper.
Future research might explore a unified system that leverages both existing refusal techniques and risk-aware decision making to develop safer, more reliable LMs.

Appendix D
Why Stepwise Prompts Perform Worse than Prompt Chaining at High-Risk?

From the main results in Table
2
, we find that stepwise prompts generally perform worse than prompt chaining in the high-risk settings.
Since both methods (see Figure
6
) use skill decomposition to solve the task, the final score
S
(see section
2.1
) depends on the performance of each individual skill.
Therefore, we can inspect how well do LMs perform the individual skills by the following metrics:

1.

Downstream Task:
In this work, the downstream task is multi-choice question answering, so we measure it by
accuracy (ACC)
.

2.

Confidence Estimation:
We use
expected calibration error (ECE)

Guo et al. 2017
to evaluate this skill.

3.

Expected-Value Reasoning:
As shown by Figure
7
in section
5.1
, LMs sometimes fail to explicitly calculate expected values. We thus measure this ability by the
proportion of using
e
xpected-
v
alue
r
easoning (EVR)
.

We list metrics for these individual skills and the final performance
S
in Table
9
, which uses
(s_{\mathrm{cor}},s_{\mathrm{inc}})=(1,-8)
as the representative risk level.
From the results of “averaged across all models”, we can see that prompt chaining generally performs better at each individual skill, except for ECE in MedQA.
Accordingly, it is probable that the superior performance of prompt chaining arises from the fact that it performs better at each skill.

Appendix E
Better Confidence Estimation (Calibration) Improves Final Scores

As mentioned in the limitations (§
Limitations
), improving the individual skills has the potential to increase the overall performance of prompt chaining on risk-aware decision making.
Here, we show evidence that
improving confidence estimation (i.e., calibration) increases the final score in most cases
.

From the prompt shown in Figure
6
, we know that there are
two
obvious choices to provide
{predicted_answer}
for the “Skill 2: Confidence Estimation” prompt:

1.

Predicted answer
= the answer letter only:
We only include the answer letter by extracting the letter from “ANSWER: <letter>” in the LMs’ output from “Skill 1: Question Answering” (see the upper right prompt in Figure
6
).

2.

Predicted answer
= rationale + answer:
Otherwise, we may also include both the reasoning process (rationale) and the answer letter as the
{predicted_answer}
.

The comparison between these two choices is illustrated in Figure
10
.
We show the calibration curves / metrics of the two choices in Figure
11
, which reveals that including rationales in the predicted answer increases the expected calibration error (ECE).
On the other hand, including only the answer letters leads to better calibration.
We then compare the final scores of the two different choices of confidence estimation, and show the results in Table
4
.
Overall, the results indicate that the choice with better calibration performance indeed leads to better final performance in 10 out of 12 cases, so we use choice 1 in our main experiments (§
4.4
).
