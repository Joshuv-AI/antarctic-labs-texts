Clinicopathological Features of Epstein-Barr Virus-Positive Neuroendocrine Carcinoma: Analysis of Twenty-Two Cases
Authors: Xiao-Ying Zhang, Jia Fu, Mei-Ling Chen, Xin-Chun Chen, Shi-Min Zhang, Yi-Ling Luo, Mao Fang, Han-Wen Jiang, Fang Chen, Hao Wang, Jin-Hua He, Yan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)–positive neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) is a rare neoplasm with limited histopathological and therapeutic data. This report presents 22 cases of EBV-positive NEC, analyzing age distribution, morphology, and immunophenotype. The median patient age was 47 years (range: 27–67 years), with a male-to-female ratio of 17:5. Most cases (86%, 19/22) were localized to the nasal cavity or nasopharynx, while the remaining three (14%, 3/22) involved the lung, eyelid, and chest wall. Tumors were identified as small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) or large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC) based on cellular morphology. Immunohistochemical analysis showed positivity for at least one, but generally, two neuroendocrine markers and INI1, while negativity for NUT and squamous cell carcinoma markers, such as p63, p40, and CK5/6. In situ hybridization consistently revealed EBV early RNAs (EBERs) in all cases. Notably, eight patients benefited from chemoradiotherapy. Recognizing this rare tumor is essential for optimizing treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12022-024-09837-w.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are defined as neoplasms with proven neuroendocrine morphology and expression of general neuroendocrine markers [1], encompassing a diverse group of proliferations found in various body regions. NENs are distinguished into well differentiated neoplasms, called neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and poorly differentiated neoplasms, called neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs). Overall, the most common anatomical sites in which NENs may arise are the gastroenteropancreatic system and the lungs, with NETs being most common in the former, whereas NECs are more frequently found in the latter location [2]. However, NENs may arise in virtually any anatomical location and NECs are the most common form of NENs in non-thoracic and non-digestive sites [3]. Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) includes small cell (SCNEC) and large cell (LCNEC) subtypes, both of which are clinically aggressive and have limited treatment options [1].

Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) infects over 90% of the global population and contributes to approximately 200,000 new cancer cases annually, including B cell-derived malignancies such as Burkitt lymphoma and Hodgkin lymphoma, as well as epithelial tumors such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) and EBV-positive gastric carcinoma [3]. However, EBV-positive NEC is extremely rare, with only a few reported cases originating in the nasopharynx [4–11]. The rarity of EBV-positive NEC leaves its clinicopathological characteristics insufficiently described, highlighting the need for additional case studies.

This report presents 22 cases of EBV-positive NEC, offering a detailed summary of their clinicopathological features and an analysis of treatment processes and outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Case Selection

Twenty-two cases of EBV-positive NEC were retrospectively reviewed from the Department of Pathology at Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center, Guangzhou, China, between January 2015 and May 2024. Nineteen diagnostic samples were from the nasopharynx and nasal regions, and were biopsies, while three extra-nasopharynx/nasal samples were surgical specimens. Clinical data, including age, gender, clinical history, primary tumor location, initial presentation, imaging data, treatment regimens, dissemination or relapse, and survival outcomes, were analyzed. Written informed consent for the collection and publication of medical information was secured from all patients during their initial hospital visit. The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board at our center. Validation of the article's authenticity was achieved by uploading the key raw data to the Research Data Deposit public platform (www.researchdata.org.cn).

Morphological Evaluation

Specimens from 22 cases of EBV-positive NEC were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4.0-micron thick slices. The sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The diagnosis of NEC relied on the finding of the typical morphology of SCNEC and LCNEC, as described by the latest WHO classification of neuroendocrine tumors [1], as well as on the expression of neuroendocrine markers (usually two among synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and INSM1) and on the negativity of specific markers for other histotypes (e.g.: lack of p63, CK5, and NUT, and maintained expression of INI1). All cases were reviewed microscopically by two pathologists, who assessed architectural patterns, cytoplasmic characteristics, nuclear morphology, mitotic activity, necrosis, and lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates.

Immunohistochemical Analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed with the Leica Autostainer (BOND-MAX, M495644), incorporating both negative and positive controls. Details of the primary antibodies and their working dilutions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

In situ Hybridization (ISH)

In situ hybridization for EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER) was performed using the EBV Probe ISH Kit (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The protocols used were detailed in a previous study [12].

DNA Isolation, Targeted Sequencing and Data Analysis

Tumor tissue DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and purified with the Agencourt AMPure XP Kit (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). Hybridization with capture probes and amplification followed the manufacturer’s protocol. Target capture was performed with the OncoScreen Plus panel, encompassing 520 genes over 1.64 megabases of the human genome. Sequencing was conducted on a Nextseq 500 (Illumina, Inc., CA, USA), utilizing paired-end reads with an average depth of 1000 × . Sequencing data were aligned to the hg19 human genome reference using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner version 0.7.10 [13]. Local alignment optimization, duplication marking, and variant calling were executed with Genome Analysis Toolkit version 3.2 and VarScan version 2.4. 3 [14, 15]. Variants were filtered through the VarScan fpfilter pipeline, excluding loci with a depth below 100. For base calling, at least 8 supporting reads were required for single nucleotide variations and 2 to 5 supporting reads for insertion-deletion variations. Remaining variants were annotated using ANNOVAR (2016–02-01 release) and SnpEff version 3.6 [16, 17]. Structural variation analysis was performed using Factera version 1.4.3 [18].

Results

A significant male predominance was observed, with 17 male and 5 female patients (male-to-female ratio: 17:5). Most cases (86%, 19/22) involved the nasal cavity or nasopharynx, while three cases (14%, 3/22) were located in the lung, eyelid, or chest wall. Tumors were classified by cellular morphology as EBV-positive SCNEC in 32% (7/22) of cases and LCNEC in 68% (15/22). Neoplastic cells predominantly formed solid sheets and nests, often within desmoplastic stroma. Necrosis and extensive apoptosis were common. Moderate to prominent lymphoplasmacytic infiltrations were present in 82% (18/22) of cases, scarce lymphoplasmacytic infiltrations were present in 18% (4/22) of cases.

EBV-Positive SCNEC

All EBV-positive SCNEC cases originated from the nasal cavity or nasopharynx (Fig. 1A–F). These tumors were distinguished by poorly differentiated, small, round to oval cells with minimal cytoplasm and subtle nucleoli. Mitotic activity was notably high, exceeding 20 mitoses per 2 mm2. Most cases showed nuclear molding and crush artifacts. Tumor cells, generally smaller than three resting lymphocytes in diameter, were organized in solid sheets, nests, organoid formations, or trabecular patterns within the submucosa. Lymphoplasmacytic infiltrations were present in some cases. Notably, ovarian metastasis from the nasopharynx in case 10 exhibited tumor cells arranged in sheets, nests, and rosettes (Fig. 1G–I). EBV-Positive LCNEC

EBV-positive LCNEC cases were observed in diverse locations: the nasal cavity/nasopharynx (Fig. 2A–B), chest wall (Fig. 2C–D), eyelid (Fig. 2E–F), and lung (Fig. 2G–I). These tumors featured medium to large cells, typically larger than three small resting lymphocytes. The neoplastic cells had moderate to abundant pale eosinophilic or amphophilic cytoplasm, indistinct cell membranes, irregular nuclear shapes, vesicular chromatin, and small to medium-sized nucleoli. Mitotic activity was high, with more than 20 mitoses per 2 mm2. Tumor cells were arranged in nests and sheets. In some cases, alongside lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, neutrophils and eosinophils were also noted. Fig. 2Histological features of EBV-positive LCNEC. Immunophenotypic Characteristics

The immunohistochemistry and ISH results for EBER are detailed in Table 2. All cases tested positive for pan-keratin (AE1) (CK, Fig. 3A) and/or CK8/18. Each case exhibited expression of at least one, generally two neuroendocrine markers, including chromogranin A (CgA), synaptophysin (Syn), and INSM1 (Fig. 3B–D). The Ki-67 index varied from 40 to 95% (Fig. 3E). Thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) was positive in 20% of cases (2/10, Fig. 3F). The neoplastic cells were negative for P63 (Fig. 3G), CK5/6 (Fig. 3H), P40, NUT, and exhibited no null expression of INI1. EBER ISH revealed strong reactivity in all cases (Fig. 3I). Molecular Analysis

Three cases (case 9, 18 and 21) underwent targeted DNA sequencing. Case 9 exhibited mutation of TP53, PTEN and KIT genes, as well as amplification of CCND1 gene. Case 18 revealed mutations in TP53 and SLX4, amplification of KAT6A and POLB, an insertion/deletion mutation in KMT2A, and rearrangement of GTF2I::WDR41. Case 21 exhibited mutations in TP53, TET2, and PIK3CA, deletions of RB1, PTEN, and FAS, and amplifications of FGF3, FGF4, FGF19, and CCND1. Germline mutations were absent in case 18 and 21, while germline mutation of BRCA2 gene was detected in case 9. All cases had a low tumor mutation burden (TMB) and were microsatellite stable (MSS).

Treatment and Outcomes

Treatment data were available for 19 patients. At diagnosis, 18 patients (95%) had metastatic disease, with metastases found in lymph nodes (74%, 14/19), bone (5%, 1/19), adrenal gland (5%, 1/19), or a combination of lymph nodes, liver, and bone (11%, 2/19) (Fig. 4A). All 18 patients (95%) received combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy; two also underwent immunotherapy, and two received targeted therapy. One patient (5%) with a lung tumor had surgical intervention. Follow-up data were available for 16 patients, with follow-up periods ranging from 4 to 106 months. Of these, 8 patients (50%) were alive and free of detectable tumor, three (19%) were alive with residual tumor, and five (31%) died from tumor progression (Fig. 4B–C). Among 13 patients with nasal or nasopharyngeal tumors, 11 (85%) were alive, while two (15%) died from the disease. Four of the 11 survivors (36%) experienced tumor recurrence or metastasis to the lung or ovaries after combined treatment. Discussion

EBV-positive NEC is an exceptionally rare malignancy, with only a limited number of cases involving the nasopharynx reported to date [4–6, 8–11]. This study examines 22 cases of EBV-positive NEC. Of these, 17 occurred in the nasopharynx, two in the nasal cavity, and three in other sites, including the lung, eyelid, and chest wall. These findings indicate that EBV-positive NEC can manifest in various anatomical locations and is not restricted to the nasopharynx. Patient ages ranged from 27 to 67 years, with the majority falling between 40 and 60 years. The male-to-female ratio was 3.4:1, reflecting a male predominance. Common symptoms included epistaxis, hearing loss, and neck mass. Notably, 95% (18/19) of patients presented with metastases at diagnosis, highlighting the tumor’s aggressive and metastatic nature.

The histological features of EBV-positive NEC closely resembled those of EBV-negative NEC. The neoplastic cells primarily formed solid sheets and nests with high mitotic activity. Some SCNEC cases also exhibited organoid, trabecular, or rosette architectures. Necrosis and apoptotic cells were frequently observed. Immunohistochemically, EBV-positive NEC demonstrated expression of neuroendocrine markers and was negative for squamous cell carcinoma markers such as p63, CK5/6, and p40. These histological and immunohistochemical features differentiate EBV-positive NEC from NPC.

The molecular profile of NEC is commonly characterized by TP53 and RB1 gene alterations across most anatomical sites, accompanied by other key driver genes, including, RAS family, APC, CDKN2A, and MYC, et al. [19]. However, site-specific heterogeneity of NEC has been highlighted in recent studies. For instance, a study on digestive NECs identified TP53, APC, KRAS, BRAF, and RB1 as the most frequently mutated genes [20]. Common genetic alterations in pulmonary LCNEC included TP53 (78%), RB1 (38%), STK11 (33%), KEAP1 (31%), KRAS (22%), and PTEN (13%) [3, 21]. Bi-allelic inactivation of TP53 and RB1 was observed in most small cell lung cancers (SCLC), with NOTCH family gene mutations in approximately 25% of SCLC cases [22]. Abnormal p53 expression and Rb loss are also prevalent in NEC of the head and neck [23, 24]. However, Rb protein loss was not found in nasal and laryngeal NEC [23]. The genetic landscape of EBV-positive NEC remains poorly characterized. In this study, targeted DNA sequencing was performed on three LCNEC cases, with results summarized in Supplementary Table S2. TP53 mutations but not RB1 alterations, were observed in case 9 and 18 with nasopharyngeal tumors, consistent with findings in nasal and laryngeal NEC [23]. Case 21 with a chest tumor, exhibited TP53 mutation, and deletions of RB1 and PTEN, mirroring the molecular changes seen in pulmonary LCNEC [3, 21]. However, a recent study on three nasopharyngeal EBV-positive SCNEC cases reported no p53 or Rb alterations [10], suggesting distinct genomic profiles in EBV-positive SCNEC and EBV-positive LCNEC. It also may suggest other key cancer genes mutations playing the important role of driving the nasopharyngeal EBV-positive SCNEC.

Several tumor-causing viruses, including Merkel cell polyomavirus (MCPyV), Human papillomavirus (HPV), Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8), hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), human adult T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1), human enteroviruses (hEV), tick-borne encephalitis virus (TBEV) and EBV, have been identified as key viruses in association with NENs [25]. Among these, several studies have revealed the tumorigenic mechanism of MCPyV, which disrupts cell cycle regulation and promotes uncontrolled cell growth, contributing to Merkel Cell Carcinoma (MCC) development [26–28]. However, the tumorigenic mechanisms of the other viruses associated with NENs remain largely unclear. EBV is known to be critical in the development of NPC in head and neck and in pulmonary lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (LELC), which genetically resembles NPC [29]. High rates of somatic mutations in TP53, enrichment of mutations in the classical NF-κB, PI3K/Akt/mTOR, and MAPK signaling pathways (such as PTEN, PIK3CA and FGFR3), high-frequency amplification of CCND1 and loss of CDKN2A have been detected in NPC, driving tumor progression [30]. The overactivation of NF-κB signaling induced by the EBV-encoded latent membrane protein 1 (LMP1) also contributes to NPC pathogenesis. In this study, two out of three patients showed TP53 and PTEN inactivation and amplification of CCND1, similar to NPC, leading us to speculate that EBV may also play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of EBV-positive NEC. This is particularly relevant in the EBV-positive SCNEC without p53 and Rb alterations, suggesting EBV may play key roles in tumorigenesis. Further research is required to better understand the pathogenesis of EBV-positive NEC.

NEC patients generally face a poor prognosis. In this study, all three patients with EBV-positive LCNEC of the lung, eyelid, or chest wall died of the disease within months of diagnosis, highlighting the highly aggressive nature of EBV-positive NEC, similar to NEC. In contrast, four of six patients with EBV-positive SCNEC and five of seven with EBV-positive LCNEC of the nasal cavity or nasopharynx responded favorably to chemoradiotherapy, suggesting the similarities between EBV-positive NEC and NPC, both of which are sensitive to chemoradiotherapy. This supports our speculation that EBV-positive NEC possesses characteristics of both NPC and NEC according to its clinical and molecular features. The prognostic characteristics of EBV-positive NEC remain largely unknown, complicating treatment decisions due to the variability in patient outcomes. For patients showing poor response to chemoradiotherapy, a better understanding of molecular alterations could guide the use of molecular targeted therapy or immunotherapy. In this study, case 21 exhibited PIK3CA mutation, PTEN deletion, amplifications of FGF3, FGF4, FGF19 and CCND1. Therefore, she may benefit from treatment with inhibitors targeting PI3K/AKT, PI3K/mTOR, FGF/FGFR or CDK4/6 [31–35]. Unfortunately, she died three months after being diagnosed with EBV-positive NEC, without receiving targeted therapy.

This study presents a retrospective analysis of 22 cases of EBV-positive NEC, highlighting the importance of recognizing this rare entity to ensure appropriate patient treatment.
