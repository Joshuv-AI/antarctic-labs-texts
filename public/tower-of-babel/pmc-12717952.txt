Cross-Cultural Adaptability and Psychometric Validation of the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire (TSRQ) for Application on the Persian-Speaking Type 2 Diabetes Patients
Authors: Habibeh Matin, Parvin Sarbakhsh, Abdolreza Shaghaghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAutonomous self-regulation is a proxy for adapting to long-term healthy behaviors and has a key impact on the prevention of chronic illnesses. The psychometric properties of the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire-Persian Version (TSRQ-P) were assessed in this study to examine different types of self-regulation among Iranian type 2 diabetes patients.Patients and MethodsA preliminary draft of the TSRQ-P was prepared based on a standard translation/back-translation procedure and appraisal of the face and content validities by 15 allied health specialists. The internal consistency measure of Cronbach’s alpha and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) were estimated to verify the content validity and reliability of the TSRQ-P. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were performed to check the scale’s structural components.ResultsThe calculated Cronbach’s alpha= 0.893 and ICC= 0.982 deemed the scale to be appropriate, in terms of internal consistency and measurement invariance. The EFA and CFA outputs yielded two distinct factor structures for TSRQ-P [(N=189) (RMSEA=0.078, NFI=0.849, RFI=0.806, IFI=0.913, TLI=0.886, and CFI= 0.911)].ConclusionThe TSRQ-P has robust psychometric features for application to Persian-speaking patients with type 2 diabetes. Owing to the cross-border cultural diversity of the subgroups of Persian-speaking populations, further psychometric scrutiny is recommended to prevent bias and sociolinguistic inconsistencies.Keywords: type 2 diabetes, self-regulation

Introduction

Empowering people to regulate their lifestyles, adopt healthy behaviors, and abstain from or change high-risk behaviors may have a significant impact on the prevention of serious chronic illnesses, their induced complications, disabilities, and mortality.1,2 However, in reality, many more individuals have unhealthy lifestyles and high-risk behaviors that put them in danger of contracting serious or fatal illnesses. This is partly due to a lack of self-motivation and a failure in the autonomous regulation of behavioral patterns to sustain a healthy and vivid life.3,4

Self-determination theory (SDT), developed by Deci and Ryan5,6 introduces autonomous regulation as a proxy to achieve a long-term change in behaviors.1 Autonomous regulation with the gradual substitution of internal controls for external controls helps individuals to stay on track in a goal-setting process, in comparing their achievements with the goals, and eventually being able to overcome challenges of behavior change.7

According to SDT, intrinsic and extrinsic motives can drive people to act or not when action is needed on a daily basis. Amotivation, the absence of both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation in a person, may invoke a state of unwillingness to perform a task or practice.

Distinct variations are observable in people’s general sources of motivation; hence, they can be categorized on a spectrum of low and high self-motivation. Highly self-determined individuals are classified as having the highest levels of intrinsic motivation, and consequently, autonomous regulation. People with low levels of self-determination generally depend on extrinsic motivation; therefore, they are categorized as having low levels of autonomous self-regulation. The four pathways through which extrinsic/intrinsic motives influence our behaviors include the following.

The first two regulatory paths are within the controlled regulation area, but the identified and integrated regulation paths are within the autonomous regulation area (Figure 1), which generally leads to a change in behavior based on an internal motive.2,4,8–12

Self-determination, motivation and self-regulation continuum.

The philosophical assumptions of SDT posit that individuals naturally act proactively and have an inherent tendency to grow; therefore, to manage their internal and external motives instead of being a product of social learning.13 It is based on an autonomous regulating pathway in which people emotionally feel commitment to a behavior and consider it as a duty and adjust their behavior in order to receive positive feedback.2,14

Uncontrolled type 2 diabetes can cause many life-threatening complications, is very prevalent and it is expected that by 2030, diabetes management costs and its complications will account for 12% of the world’s health budget. Different complications of type 2 diabetes occur because of the absence or low level motivation of patients to adhere to the prescribed therapeutic regimen. The successful management of type 2 diabetes is based on a complex set of effective, evolving, and nonstop self-management processes as prerequisite elements of interventional strategies to prevent both long-term and immediate risks of disease-induced complications, including hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy.8,9,15

Based on the unquestionable importance of self-management competencies in type 2 diabetes patients to control the illness and prevent its debilitating complications, the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire (TSRQ) was introduced by Ryan & Cannell16, as a generic tool to assess the independence of patients in autonomous self-regulation of healthy behaviors, changing high-risk behaviors, and identifying and adhering to therapeutic recommendations.2,17,18

Different versions of the TSRQ have been adapted in various studies19,20 to examine the effect of autonomous regulation on distinct behaviors, such as dietary habits,1,15,19 physical activity,1,21 drinking behavior,22 smoking1,23,24 and weight loss practices.12 Consistent with these studies’ importance of autonomous regulation on diabetes control, care and self-management, positive lifestyle changes, therapeutic adherence and regular physical activity, diet change, blood glucose control and stress control were studied.3,4,8,12,13,25–30

The TSRQ has been tested psychometrically in various studies, in different cultural contexts, and on individuals with different demographic characteristics, for its use in the self-regulation appraisal of patients or healthy subjects1,6,7,10,31,32 but has not been validated for application in Persian (national language in Iran)-speaking populations. The 15-item TSRQ was recently validated for use among patients with type 2 diabetes in Iran33 but the 19-item TSRQ measures motivation to make therapeutic recommendations, including controlled motivation that is not retained in the 15-item version. As the main objective of the TSRQ application in health studies is to collect data on distinguished motivation types across multiple samples and health behaviors,1 the 19-item version of the scale was used in this validation study.

Materials and Methods

Study Objective

The main aim of this study was to examine the cultural adaptability and psychometric characteristics of the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire (TSRQ-P) for possible application in studies (or in practice settings) on autonomous behavioral regulation competencies of Iranian patients with type 2 diabetes.

Study Sample

The study sample included 189 registered patients with type 2 diabetes at the diabetes clinic of Shahid Madani Hospital in the city of Khoy, northwest Iran, who were recruited through a random sampling method by using random number table from the list of all registered patients in recent five years. The inclusion criteria were having at least 30 years of age (age at risk for type 2 diabetes) and being a native Iranian (to accurately assess cultural adaptation) and the study exclusion criteria included having type 1 diabetes, gestational diabetes, mental disorders such as Alzheimer, mental retardation, severe psychomotor dysfunction such as limb disability and other underlying illnesses such as disabling cardiovascular disease.

Measurement Scale

The TSRQ with 19 questions in two subscales, autonomous regulation (AR) and controlled regulation (CR),16 (Appendix A), was used to collect the study data. Two headline sentences in the subscales were “I take my medications for diabetes and/or check my glucose because …” “ that correspond to 8 questions and” “The reason I follow my diet and exercise regularly is that …” that coincide with 11 questions. To calculate the respondents’ AR scores their answers to questions 2, 3, 7, 10, 13, 16, 18, and 19, were considered, and the answers to questions 1, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14, 15, and 17 were used to estimate the CR score relative to the overall target behavior. The response options for each scale item ranged from 1 (not at all true) to 7 (very true). The TSRQ-P overall score (Relative Autonomy Index [RAI]) was also calculated based on the average scores of subscales, as recommended in the original TSRQ.

Data Collection

Three trained interviewers employed the TSRQ-P, which was drafted according to a standard translation/back-translation procedure34 by two fluent English translators. Interviewers received specialized training to ensure consistency and impartiality, and used neutral language throughout the data collection process. The interview sessions lasted 20–40 minutes in average and the entire data collection procedure was completed in three months. Other incorporated baseline sociodemographic variables measured in this study were age, sex, marital status, occupation, level of education, place of residence, income, body mass index, glycosylated hemoglobin level, types of medications use, and presence or absence of type 2 diabetes-related complications.

Content Validity

Qualitative and quantitative validity appraisals were performed based on expert panel members’ feedback about the necessity, relevance, and appropriateness of the items wordings. Minor revisions were made based on the comments, and the estimated Content Validity Index (CVI) (0.79) and Content Validity Ratio (CVR) (0.49) were within acceptable ranges.35

Reliability

The internal consistency measure of Cronbach’s alpha and the test-retest Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) as an index of TSRQ-P reliability were calculated based on the responses provided by the 20 patients at 20 days interval.

Ethical Approval Procedure

The validation study was approved by the institutional Medical Ethics Board of Trustees (MEBoT) affiliated with the Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR.TBZMED.REC.1396.192). All ethical codes and acts were observed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.36 Written informed consent was obtained from the recruited patients before formal initiation of the interviews to complete the data collection tool. All necessary precautions were taken to keep the participants information confidential.

Statistical Analysis

The study data were tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The Data skewness and kurtosis were checked; the ceiling and floor effects were also evaluated and decided to be considered abnormal when the highest/lowest scores observed in more than 15% of the participants. The mean scores and standard deviations for the AR and CR subscales were calculated, and the RAI scores were derived by subtracting the mean CR from the mean AR.16

To examine the convergent validity, the obtained TSRQ-P scores were correlated with the scores of respondents from application of the Perceived Competence Scale for Diabetes (PCSD) in a simultaneous study on the interviewees.37 The discriminant validity of the TSRQ-P was investigated by inspecting the observed correlation in the scores of the different study participants according to their age, sex, marital status, employment, income, education level, and place of residence.

The structural validity appraisal of the TSRQ-P was performed through EFA and CFA. The Kaiser Meyer Olkin test (to demonstrate the adequacy of the sample size), Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity (to confirm the existence of a correlation between items and the possibility of performing factor analysis), Varimax Rotation, Principle Axis Factoring were performed and Eigenvalues>1 and factor loadings greater than 0.3 were considered acceptable to assign questions to possible factors. A modification Index (MI) above 7 was used to merge the extracted factors that were akin to the rival version of the scale (four factors) into the factors corresponding to the version used in the study (two factors). All descriptive statistical and exploratory factor analyses were performed using SPSS version 20 software and confirmatory factor analysis with AMOS version 22 software.

Based on the analysis outputs the calculated mean score of the controlled regulation (CR) was higher than (45.762) the mean score of the autonomous regulation (AR) (43.318) [ARI= −2.444]. The respondents’ AR scores indicated a statistically significant association with place of residence (P= 0.043) and level of education (P= 0.000). The estimated values of the CVI and CVR for the TSRQ-P items (CVI= 0.7–1, CVR= 0.5–1) and whole scale (CVI=0.91, CVR=0.83) represented its acceptable content and face validities.

The value of the Kaiser Meyer Olkin (KMO) statistic (0.843) proved the suitability of the study data for the EFA and CFA. Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was performed with a chi-square test value of 1817.984, 171 degrees of freedom, and P-value < 0.001, all of which were indicative of the fitness of the factor analysis for the data set. The EFA outputs obtained a 4-factors model solution, and the factor loadings were in the range 0.432–0.790 (Table 3). The total variance explained by the identified 4 factors model was 63.24%. Regarding the multiple versions of the TSRQ that exist and for making comparison possible and facilitating broader application of the results, also based on SDT continuum from autonomous forms to controlled regulation, the four identified factors in this study were merged into two factors of AR and CR (MI>7) (Figure 2). The analysis results of the CFA in the 2-factor model were even more favorable than the 4-factor model (Chi Square= 285.859, df= 133, P-value < 0.001, RMSEA= 0.078, NFI= 0.849, RFI= 0.806, IFI= 0.913, TLI= 0.886, CFI= 0.911).

The floor effect was not observed in AR, and the ceiling effect was very low. The lowest score for AR was 8, and only 1.1% of the respondents had AR score below 8; the highest score was 56, with 15.9% of people having AR scores above this level. The floor and ceiling effects were also not observed in CR scores because the lowest score of CR was 16, with 1.1% of people having CR values below 16, the highest score being 77, and only 0.5% of the respondents having CR score above 77. The internal consistency and reliability of the TSRQ-P were confirmed as α=0.893 and ICC= 0.982, P <0.001.35

A significant correlation was observed between the estimated scores of the AR and PCSD (r= 0.511, P <0.05), indicating convergent validity of the AR. Place of residence (0.043) and level of education (P < 0.001) also indicated a significant association with the participants’ AR scores.

Discussion

The main purpose of this study was to culturally adapt and psychometrically test the Persian version of the TSRQ for application in studies on motivations and self-regulation paths that potentially arbitrate self-management of type-2 diabetes among Persian-speaking patients. The study findings revealed the appropriateness of the TSRQ-P for research application, probably in practice settings, and support its applicability in appraising the motivational climate that influences therapeutic regimen adherence among Iranian patients with type 2 diabetes.

The original scale was validated for use in other languages ie English,1 Polish,7 Portuguese,10 French,31 Spanish,6 and Hungarian32 and validity corroboration of the TSRQ-P adds to the overall functionality of the TSRQ. The psychometric results of the instrument in our study were satisfactory and aligned with the findings reported in previous research.

The tested 19-items TSRQ-P in this study indicated robust psychometric properties in both the emerged 4-factor and 2-factor models and the CFA outputs replicated the underlying factor structure of the TSRQ demonstrated in previous studies.1,6,7 The four-factor extracted model in this study is consistent with the findings of the 15-item-validation study33 but the difference is in the floor and ceiling effect, and the value of internal consistency, chi-square test (in EFA and CFA), and RMSEA.

The findings revealed the pathway of motivation that inspired the recruited Iranian patients with type 2 diabetes in their attempts to better control type 2 diabetes.

An increased understanding of how self-regulation could influence therapeutic regimen adherence sheds light on best-practice intervention architectures as indispensable antecedents in the selection of more stringent algorithms to optimize lifestyle change achievements in patients with type 2 diabetes.38 Thus, tailor-made intervention strategies could be adopted based on the patients’ motivational nomenclature, as suggested in other studies.6

Consistent with the findings of this study, SDT posits that autonomous regulation is a sine qua non element for the maintenance of favorable health practices.6 Due to inter-cultural and cross-cultural diversities among population subgroups, further scrutiny is required to determine which interventions (or combinations of interventions) are the most feasible and cost-effective choices in persuading patients to follow therapeutic recommendations. Despite the uncertainties that still prevail about the motivational aspects of behavioral regulation, it is recommended that autonomous motivation be integrated into theoretical models of health behavior,6 most of which currently rely on expectancy value theory as an interpretive lens to construe the behavior acquisition process.39

Limitations

Caution is warranted in generalizing the findings of this study due to several limitations. Recruitment of patients from a diabetes clinic in a midsize city (Khoy), which may interfere with the sociocultural representativeness of the participants in a cross-sectional study, might pose a bias to the observations and findings of this research. Self-report bias and tendencies to provide favorable answers to the study questions could be another source of concern regarding the findings. The study team was also unable to check for long-term behavioral profiles of the study participants and their performance in relation to disease management due to inherent limitations of the chosen study design. However, the affinity of the study results with other psychometric assessments of the TSRQ implies the applicability of the TSRQ-P as a vivid and feasible instrument in studies on the motivational maintenance of behavioral decision-making.

Conclusion

Psychometric analysis of the TSRQ-P, that for making comparison possible and facilitating application, and based on SDT motivation continuum, the four identified factors in this study were merged into two factors of AR and CR, provided evidence for the usability of the instrument in the Iranian context and for the determination of motivational paths for adherence to therapeutic recommendations among persian-speaking patients with type 2 diabetes. Due to cultural diversity, further research is suggested to endorse cross-border adoptability of the scale in different sub-samples of Persian-speaking adults with type 2 diabetes worldwide and to determine the possible roles of other precedent variables in motivating patients to fully adhere to or refuse/renounce the therapeutic recommendations.
