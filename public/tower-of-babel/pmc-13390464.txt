Comparison of surgical safety among different comprehensive treatment models for adenocarcinoma of esophagogastric junction: a single-center cohort study
Authors: Hongmei Dai, Yinkui Wang, Fei Shan, Shuangxi Li, Yongning Jia, Kan Xue, Rulin Miao, Zhemin Li, Xiaolei Xiu, Ziyu Li, Jiafu Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) are increasingly used to treat adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG). However, there is insufficient clinical evidence to compare surgical safety among the three comprehensive treatment models, including the surgery‑first model, NAC model, and nCRT model. Therefore, this study was designed to compare surgical safety of the three comprehensive treatment models.MethodsThis is a retrospective cohort study. From January 2012 to December 2018, 276 consecutive patients with Siewert II and III AEG (cT2-4NxM0) in the Gastrointestinal Cancer Center, Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute were enrolled in this study. All these patients underwent surgery and were categorized into three groups: surgery‑first group, NAC group and nCRT group.Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to balance baseline characteristics between the three groups.ResultsThe overall postoperative complication rate in nCRT groups was 59.6%, which was significantly higher than that in the surgery-first group (42.2%, P = 0.040) and the NAC group (39.3%, P = 0.027). Moreover, the rate of major postoperative complications differed significantly among the three groups (P = 0.011), with the nCRT group showing a notably higher incidence than the surgery-first group (P = 0.008) and the NAC group (P = 0.043). No significant differences in postoperative complication rates were found between the surgery-first group and the NAC group. After IPTW, nCRT was significantly associated with increased postoperative complication (weighed OR = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.13–4.50, P = 0.022), and major postoperative complications (weighed OR:3.27, 95% CI: 1.45–7.39, P = 0.005). Thoracoabdominal approach (weighed OR = 6.47, 95% CI: 1.44–29.13, P = 0.015), combined organ resection (weighed OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 1.66–15.51, P = 0.004), and operative time exceeding 240 min (weighed OR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.20–4.12, P = 0.011) were also identified as independent risk factors for postoperative complications.ConclusionsNeoadjuvant chemotherapy did not increase the risk of postoperative complications. In contrast, nCRT was associated with worse postoperative complications. We should pay more attention to AEG patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.Keywords: Adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, Surgical safety, Postoperative complication

Background

The incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG) has shown an increasing trend both in Western and Asian countries in recent years [1–3], and the GLOBOCAN 2018 database showed that most of the global estimated AEG cases occurred in East Asia in 2018 [4]. In China, almost half of gastric cancers are diagnosed in the advanced or metastatic stage [5]. Thus, AEG is frequently associated with poor prognosis.

Surgery is the mainstay for treating patients with locally advanced AEG and multimodal treatment strategies have been developed to improve survival including perioperative chemotherapy and chemoradiotherapy [6]. According to the MAGIC [7] and FNCLCC/FFCD [8] trials, perioperative chemotherapy significantly increased the overall survival of patients with AEG compared with surgery alone. Despite the small number of AEG patients included in the trials, the CROSS [9] and CALGB 9781 [10] trials reflected a survival benefit from the use of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.

As an increasing number of patients with AEG receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy, more attention should be given to postoperative complications after neoadjuvant therapy. Schuhmacher C et al. [11] reported that the postoperative complication rate in patients receiving preoperative chemotherapy was 27.1%, much larger than that in patients receiving only surgery (16.2%). In addition, while some studies have addressed postoperative complications in patients with AEG receiving neoadjuvant therapy [12, 13], most studies have been more focused on the adverse effects of the chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy rather than surgical safety.

As few studies have focused on comparing postoperative complications among the three groups including the surgery-first group, neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) group and chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) group, we intended to compare the surgical safety of the three treatment models.

Methods

Study patients

This study was a single-center retrospective cohort study, which was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research. The clinical records of 326 patients who underwent surgery for Siewert II and III [14] carcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (cT2-4NxM0) between January 2012 and December 2018 were identified. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 276 patients were included in our study (shown in Fig. 1). The Peking University Cancer Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 2018YJZ56) approved this study, and all patients provided written informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) pathologically diagnosed adenocarcinoma; 2) 18–80 years old; 3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score 0 or 1 before the start of treatment; 4) tumor staging of cT2-4Nx with no distant metastasis; and 5) Siewert type II/III esophagogastric junction cancer. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) multiple gastric cancer; 2) presence of other malignant tumors; 3) neuroendocrine carcinoma; 4) adenosquamous carcinoma; and 5) patients in the NAC group receiving less than two cycles of chemotherapy.

Comprehensive treatment models

The multidisciplinary team (MDT) determined the patients’ treatment methods. According to different perioperative treatment methods, the patients were categorized into three groups: surgery-first group, NAC group, and nCRT group. In the surgery-first group, patients received surgery followed by adjuvant chemotherapy. Common adjuvant chemotherapy regimens included capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (CapeOX), S-1 plus oxaliplatin (SOX), capecitabine, cisplatin, and S-1. In the NAC group, preoperative chemotherapy was performed with 2–4 cycles, and the regimes included CapeOX, SOX, paclitaxel liposome plus S-1 and paclitaxel plus S-1. Some HER2-positive patients were treated with trastuzumab in combination with chemotherapy. Patients were revaluated every two cycles using abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT). If the disease progressed after 2 cycles, chemotherapy was stopped, and patients received surgery. Otherwise, NAC or gastrectomy was chosen after the approval from patients. Patients underwent surgery within 3–8 weeks after the last chemotherapy dose and received adjuvant chemotherapy based on the primary neoadjuvant regimen after gastrectomy. In the nCRT group, patients received radiotherapy and concurrent chemotherapy. The gross tumor volume (GTV) included the primary tumor and metastatic lymph nodes. GTV and the high-risk lymphatic drainage area were included in the clinical tumor volume (CTV). A total dose of 45 Gy was applied to the CTV, using 25 fractions of 1.8 Gy and external beam radiotherapy was applied to the GTV at a total dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions of 2.0 Gy, 5 times a week. The chemotherapy regimen included SOX, S-1, CapeOX and docetaxel, oxaliplatin and S-1 (DOS).

All patients underwent standard gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy, and the resection range was chosen according to the Japanese gastric cancer treatment guidelines [15, 16]. Reconstruction methods included esophagojejunostomy, esophagogastrostomy, double-tract reconstruction and Roux-en-Y reconstruction. Some patients received combined organ resection and thoracoabdominal surgery to achieve R0 resection.

Outcome definition

Surgical safety of the three treatment models mainly indicated morbidity and mortality after surgery, which were main outcomes in this study. Postoperative complications occurred during the hospital stay after surgery and were graded according to the Clavien-Dindo (CD) classification; major complications were defined as those of grade III-V [17]. The postoperative mortality indicated death that occurred within 30 days after surgery.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD) or the median [interquartile range (IQR)], and categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages (%). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, the Kruskal‒Wallis test and chi-square test were used to compare the differences between the three groups’ variables. Bonferroni-corrected p values were used when we performed multiple comparisons.

The inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) [18–20] was used to reduce imbalance in measured confounders between the three treatment groups. Propensity scores were estimated using a multiple logistic regression model in which the three treatment modalities served as the dependent variable and the following baseline covariates served as independent variables: age, gender, comorbidity, histologic type, body mass index (BMI), clinical T stage (cT), clinical N stage (cN), Siewert type, and Lauren classification. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to assess covariate balance and a value > 0.2 was considered a sign of imbalance.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with overall complications and major complications in the unadjusted and IPTW-adjusted cohort. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using weighted generalized linear models with a quasibinomial distribution. Variables with a P-value ≤ 0.10 in the univariable analysis, were entered into the multivariable model. In the weighted cohort, counts and sample sizes may not be integers as they represent the sum of stabilized weights in the pseudo-population.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

2General information

Among the 276 patients included in the study, 135 underwent surgery without preoperative treatment, 84 received preoperative chemotherapy, and the remaining 57 accepted preoperative chemoradiotherapy (Fig. 2). The baseline and clinicopathological characteristics of all patients before and after IPTW are summarized in Table 1. Prior to weighting, significant imbalances were identified in clinical T stage (P = 0.001) and clinical N stage (P < 0.001). Notably, the surgery-first group harbored a substantially higher proportion of cT2 tumors (11.9%) relative to the the NAC (1.2%) and nCRT (0.0%) group, whereas the nCRT group was predominantly composed of cT4 disease (73.7%), exceeding that of the surgery-first (50.4%) and NAC (60.7%) groups.The other clinicopathological characteristics, including age, gender, baseline body mass index (BMI), Siewert type, and comorbidities, were comparable among the three groups. After application of IPTW, all baseline characteristics achieved satisfactory balance across the three groups.

In contrast, postoperative length of stay differed significantly among the three groups, both before (P < 0.001) and after IPTW (P < 0.001). The nCRT group had the longest median postoperative stay at 14 days [IQR: 13–21], compared to 13 days in both the surgery-first [IQR: 10–15] and NAC [IQR: 10–16] groups. This difference persisted after weighting (15.00 days vs. 13.00 days vs. 12.45 days), suggesting that neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy may be associated with a prolonged postoperative recovery period.

The surgical approach, resection range, combined organ resection, surgical radicality (R0/R1 status), and esophageal infiltration length were comparable among the three groups before and after IPTW (all P > 0.05). Staging laparoscopy was not mandatory prior to neoadjuvant therapy initiation; however, 56.8% of patients in the NAC group and 61.4% in the nCRT group underwent staging laparoscopy before commencing neoadjuvant treatment. All patients underwent intraoperative abdominal exploration to confirm staging. Open surgery remained the predominant approach across all three groups, accounting for 83.0%, 85.7%, and 87.7% of cases in the surgery-first, NAC, and nCRT groups, respectively. Sixteen patients required thoracoabdominal resection (7 in the surgery-first group, 5 in the NAC group, and 4 in the nCRT group), of whom 5 underwent a left thoracoabdominal approach and 11 a right thoracoabdominal approach.

Significant differences were observed in the number of retrieved lymph nodes among the three groups (P < 0.001). The nCRT group had fewer retrieved lymph nodes than the surgery-first group (25.00 vs. 31.00 before weighting; 26.00 vs. 31.00 after weighting; both P < 0.001) and the NAC group (25.00 vs. 34.50 before weighting; 26.00 vs. 34.00 after weighting; both P < 0.001). The number of metastatic lymph nodes also differed significantly among the three groups (P < 0.001). Fewer metastatic lymph nodes were identified in the nCRT group compared with the surgery-first group (0.00 vs. 3.00; P < 0.001, before and after weighting) and the NAC group (0.00 vs. 1.00; P < 0.001, before and after weighting). Moreover, the surgery-first group harbored more metastatic lymph nodes than the NAC group (3.00 vs. 1.00; P < 0.001, before and after weighting).

Regarding tumor long diameter, the surgery-first group exhibited a larger tumor size than the NAC group (P < 0.001, before and after weighting) and the nCRT group (P < 0.001, before and after weighting). Pathological T stage and N stage differed significantly among the three groups (both P < 0.001, before and after weighting). The proportion of pathological T3–4 stage was higher in the surgery-first group than in the NAC group (87.4% vs. 70.2%, P = 0.003 before weighting; 89.3% vs. 70.5%, P = 0.001 after weighting) and the nCRT group (87.4% vs. 56.1%, P < 0.001 before weighting; 89.3% vs. 51.1%, P < 0.001 after weighting). Similarly, the rate of pathological N-positive stage was higher in the surgery-first group than in the NAC group (79.3% vs. 56.0%, P < 0.001) and the nCRT group (79.3% vs. 29.8%, P < 0.001). The pathological complete response rate was numerically higher in the nCRT group than in the NAC group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (15.8% vs. 8.3%, P = 0.272 before weighting; 18.0% vs. 7.4%, P = 0.066 after weighting).

Completion of adjuvant chemotherapy

A significant difference in the completion rate of adjuvant chemotherapy was observed among the three groups before and after weighting (Table 2). In the surgery-first group, 87.4% of patients received adjuvant chemotherapy following resection. In the NAC group, 92.9% of patients completed adjuvant chemotherapy. In the nCRT group, 70.2% of patients received adjuvant chemotherapy, 17.5% did not, and information regarding adjuvant chemotherapy was unavailable for the remaining 12.3% of patients.

Postoperative complication

The overall postoperative complication rates were 42.2%, 39.3%, and 59.6% in the surgery-first, NAC, and nCRT groups, respectively, with a statistically significant difference observed among the three groups (P = 0.031). Pairwise comparisons further demonstrated that the incidence of postoperative complications in the nCRT group was significantly higher than that in both the surgery-first group (P = 0.040) and the NAC group (P = 0.027). The results remained consistent after adjustment using IPTW.

The major postoperative complication rate also significantly differed among the three groups (P = 0.011), and the nCRT group had the largest severe complication rate (29.8%) compared with the surgery-first group (12.6%, P = 0.008) and NAC group (14.3%, P = 0.043). There was no significant difference in the severe postoperative complication rate between the surgery-first and NAC groups (P = 0.877). One patient in the nCRT group died from intra-abdominal bleeding secondary to anastomotic fistula, which did not occur in the surgery-first and NAC groups. Eleven patients in the nCRT group had an anastomotic leak, a higher number than the 9 such patients in the surgery-first group (P = 0.018) and the 5 such patients in the NAC group (P = 0.029). In contrast, neoadjuvant chemotherapy did not significantly increase the anastomotic leak rate compared with the surgery-first group (P = 1.000). One patient in the nCRT group developed a gastroesophageal anastomotic leakage, while the remaining 24 patients had esophagojejunal anastomotic leakage. In the nCRT group, one patient underwent reoperation due to pyothorax secondary to anastomotic leakage. The findings after IPTW adjustment remained consistent with the pre-weighting results. After IPTW adjustment, univariable logistic regression analysis identified treatment group, surgical approach, combined organ resection, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss as potential risk factors for postoperative complications (P < 0.05). Discussion

In the present study, statistically significant differences were identified in the incidence of postoperative complications and mortality among AEG patients receiving surgery-first, neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery, or neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery. Furthermore, patients who underwent resection following nCRT demonstrated a significantly higher severity of postoperative complications, with a notable trend toward an increased incidence of anastomotic leakage compared with the surgery-first and NAC groups.

Few studies have directly compared the postoperative short-term safety of the three treatment models, and this was attributed to the fact that many studies mainly included esophageal carcinoma and gastric cancer rather than adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction. According to the CROSS study [9], there was no significant difference in postoperative complications between the surgery-first and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy groups. For neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the MAGIC study [7] indicated that the rate of postoperative complications was 45.3%, which was not significantly different from 45.7% in the surgery-first group. Nevertheless, only 88 out of 357 patients in the CROSS study [9] and 58 out of 503 patients in the MAGIC study [7] were diagnosed with AEG. The strengths of this study were that only adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction was included, and postoperative complications were assessed as the primary outcome, providing extensive details about the different postoperative complications.

In the present study, nCRT was associated with an increased risk of both overall and major postoperative complications in AEG patients. Klevebro F et al. reported [21] that the rate of severe postoperative complications in the nCRT group was much higher than that in the NAC group, and Markar SR et al. [22] also found that a higher anastomotic leak rate was shown in patients receiving nCRT compared with patients undergoing surgery directly. In another study, there was no significant difference in postoperative complications between NAC and nCRT, despite the rate of postoperative major complications being higher in the nCRT group than in the NAC group [23]. The short-term results of the CROSS study indicated that there was no difference in postoperative complications between the nCRT group and the surgery-alone group [24]; however, this trial predominantly enrolled patients with esophageal and Siewert type I tumors who underwent esophagectomy, which differs from the Siewert type II/III population in our cohort.

Despite the discrepancies across studies regarding the relationship between nCRT and postoperative complications, the findings from the present study, combined with the existing literature, suggest that nCRT may adversely affect postoperative recovery in a proportion of AEG patients. Notably, the incidence of anastomotic leakage was significantly higher in the nCRT group (19.3%) compared with the surgery-first (6.7%) and NAC (6.0%) groups. This may be related to the detrimental effects of radiotherapy on tissues surrounding the anastomotic site. Preclinical and clinical studies have shown that radiotherapy can induce submucosal fibrosis, microvascular sclerosis, and reduced microvessel density at the surgical margins, thereby compromising local tissue perfusion and impairing wound healing [25, 26]. Jiang W et al. [27] further demonstrated that patients receiving nCRT exhibited significant alterations in collagen structure and reduced microvessel density at the anastomotic margins compared with those receiving chemotherapy alone, providing histopathological evidence for the elevated anastomotic leakage rate. Additionally, radiation-induced tissue edema and fibrosis may distort normal anatomical planes, increasing the technical difficulty of anastomotic construction and further contributing to leakage risk [26]. These findings underscore the importance of meticulous radiation field planning and surgical technique optimization when nCRT is employed in AEG patients. Considering the risk of postoperative complications in patients receiving nCRT, surgeons should cautiously choose patients with adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction to receive nCRT and more clinical trials are needed to clarify this question.

nCRT resulted in significant downstaging compared with NAC, but tumor downstaging did not improve survival or decrease recurrence compared with NAC [23, 28–30]. Another study indicated that patients could receive survival benefits from nCRT, but it closed because of low accrual [31]. The TOPGEAR trial confirmed that the addition of preoperative chemoradiotherapy to perioperative chemotherapy failed to improve overall survival in resectable gastric and GEJ adenocarcinoma [32]. The rate of adjuvant chemotherapy completion was significantly lower in the nCRT group (70.2%) compared with the surgery-first (87.4%) and NAC (92.9%) groups. This observation is likely attributable to the higher incidence and greater severity of postoperative complications in the nCRT group. Sisic L et al. [33] demonstrated that prolonged hospital stay due to postoperative morbidity was an independent predictor of adjuvant chemotherapy omission in patients with esophagogastric adenocarcinoma. Mokdad AA et al. [34] similarly reported that postoperative complications were a major barrier to the delivery of planned adjuvant therapy following gastrectomy. Thus, the potential oncological benefit of the enhanced pathological response achieved by nCRT may be partially offset by the reduced capacity for adjuvant therapy completion resulting from increased postoperative morbidity, which might influence prognosis [35]. Future prospective studies should systematically evaluate the impact of nCRT-related postoperative morbidity on adjuvant therapy delivery and long-term survival.

The surgical complications in AEG patients receiving NAC showed no increasing trend compared with the surgery-first group in the current study, consistent with the previous research [7, 8]. Both the MAGIC study [7] and the FNCLCC/FFCD study [8] indicated that NAC could improve gastric cancer patient survival without increasing the postoperative complications. Therefore, based on the previous trials, this study provided more evidence to support NAC in locally advanced AEG.

AEG is located in the zone between the esophagus and the stomach, and tumors in this special location need different surgical approaches to achieve curative treatment. Some patients needed thoracoabdominal esophagectomy with intrathoracic anastomosis, especially for Siewert type II tumors, to achieve R0 resection. This study found that the thoracoabdominal approach was an independent risk factor for severe postoperative complications. Among the 8 patients receiving thoracoabdominal esophagectomy, severe complications occurred in 5 patients, including 2 patients with anastomotic leakage, 1 with pneumonia, 1 with pleural effusion and 1 with pulmonary embolism. For gastric cancer of the cardia or subcardia, the left thoracoabdominal approach was associated with worse postoperative complications than the abdominal-transhiatal approach, especially pulmonary complications [36]. The thoracoabdominal approach aggravated patients’ respiratory function, and vital capacity after the thoracoabdominal approach was much less than that after the abdominal-transhiatal approach [37]. In this situation, the thoracoabdominal approach was related to worse quality of life postoperatively [38]. In addition, a meta-analysis also indicated that a higher rate of postoperative complications was identified in patients treated with the thoracoabdominal approach [39]. Although the thoracic route provides enough space to achieve excision of the thoracic lymph node and perform esophagectomy [40], surgeons should pay more attention to the complications after the thoracoabdominal approach.

Combined organ resection with gastrectomy was related to more severe complications than gastrectomy alone [41]. Although gastric cancer from all locations was included in the study and its result could not be directly applied to AEG [41], more cautions should be taken regarding combined organ resection in AEG because we found that combined organ resection was an independent risk factor for severe postoperative complications in AEG patients. For pT4b gastric cancer, curative-intent multivisceral resection could achieve complete resection, which was an independent prognostic factor [42, 43]. Nevertheless, postoperative complications caused a delay in adjuvant chemotherapy, and the start of adjuvant chemotherapy after eight weeks postoperatively was associated with worse survival in gastric cancer [44]. Considering the postoperative complications and survival benefits [45] brought by combined organ resection, combined organ resection should be cautiously chosen for AEG patients in the clinic.

Prolonged operative time was identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative complications in our study, consistent with previous reports [46]. Prolonged operative time increases tissue exposure and dehydration and amplifies the systemic inflammatory response. These factors may increase the risk of infectious complications and anastomotic edema [47]. In addition, Extended surgical duration is often associated with more intraoperative blood loss and more frequent transfusion [48]. From a practical standpoint, efforts to reduce operative time without sacrificing oncological adequacy should be encouraged.

There were some limitations in our study. First, this was a single-center retrospective analysis, which inherently limits the generalizability of our findings to other institutions with different patient populations and perioperative protocols. Second, the sample size, while moderate, may have been insufficient to detect meaningful differences in certain subgroup analyses. Third, the three treatment groups were unbalanced in both size and baseline characteristics. Although IPTW was applied to adjust for measured confounders and multivariable analyses were performed for primary outcomes, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, adjuvant chemotherapy data were unavailable for 12.3% of patients in the nCRT group, and esophageal infiltration length was not recorded in 10 of 276 cases. These missing data may have affected the precision of treatment-related analyses, though the overall proportion of missingness was modest.

Conclusions

This study showed that the postoperative complications was significantly different among the surgery-first group, NAC group and nCRT group. nCRT was an independent risk factor for surgical complications and was associated with severe postoperative complications.
