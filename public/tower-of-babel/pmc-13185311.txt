Real-world treatment courses after anti-TNF de-escalation in older adults with rheumatoid arthritis: a medicare cohort study
Authors: Jiha Lee, Jonathan Martindale, Una E. Makris, Julie P. W. Bynum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOlder adults with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) treated with biologic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs), including anti-TNFs, are at an increased risk of adverse effects. Current guidelines recommend de-escalating DMARDs for patients with low disease activity or remission to optimize the benefit-harm ratio of treatment. Our prior study found that two-thirds of older adults with RA de-escalated anti-TNFs yet real-world treatment courses after de-escalation in older adults are poorly understood. We aimed to describe real-world post-de-escalation treatment courses and identify factors associated with re-escalation.MethodsWe used 20% Medicare data from 2009 to 2022 to identify RA patients ≥ 66 years of age on anti-TNF therapy with de-escalation by cessation (> 90-day gap) or by taper (> 50% effective dose reduction), and at least one rheumatologist visits pre- and post-de-escalation (i.e., index date). Re-escalation was defined as restarting the same anti-TNF or another bDMARD or increasing the effective dose by > 50% after de-escalation. Those without re-escalation were considered to have sustained de-escalation. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify factors associated with re-escalation, relative to sustained de-escalation.ResultsAmong 949 beneficiaries who met inclusion criteria, the mean age was 75.1 years, 82.5% were female, and 41.9% had low-income subsidies (LIS). 38.4% sustained de-escalation, while 61.6% re-escalated treatment with a median time to re-escalation of 164 (IQR 113–301) days. In Cox analyses, re-escalation, compared to sustained de-escalation, was more likely among individuals who were Black, Hispanic/other, and with a lower comorbidity burden.ConclusionsNearly four in ten older adults with RA sustained de-escalation of anti-TNF, supporting the feasibility for selected individuals in routine practice. Most re-escalations occurred within six months, suggesting a need for closer follow-up early after de-escalation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-026-00642-y.

Introduction

Individuals 65 years and older now represent one of the fastest-growing segments of the rheumatoid arthritis (RA) population [1, 2]. These older adults have high rates of multimorbidity, polypharmacy, and functional limitations, which can increase the risks associated with long-term biologic disease modifying antirheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) such as anti tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (anti TNFs) [3–7].

In recent years, treatment strategies for RA have evolved, with an increasing emphasis on treat-to-target (T2T) approaches aimed at achieving remission or low disease activity [8, 9]. As a result, many patients attain stable disease control, raising important questions about the long-term need for continued high-intensity biologic therapy. Current guidelines, including the 2021 American College of Rheumatology guideline, recommend considering de-escalation of bDMARDs, either by tapering the dose or discontinuing therapy altogether, in patients who have maintained stable disease for at least six months [9, 10]. This approach aligns with the broader movement toward deprescribing in geriatric medicine, where reducing unnecessary or potentially harmful medications is a key component of optimizing care for older adults [11, 12].

Randomized trials and systematic reviews have evaluated tapering or discontinuation strategies under controlled conditions [13, 14]. However, little is known about how de-escalation occurs and is sustained in routine practice, particularly among older adults who are underrepresented in RA trials [15] and may differ in disease course, comorbidity burden, treatment priorities, and tolerance for changes in biologic therapy. In our prior Medicare claims based study, we found that de escalation of anti TNF therapy was common among older adults with RA, with complete cessation occurring more frequently than dose tapering [16]. However, that study did not evaluate subsequent treatment courses following de-escalation, including whether patients re-escalate therapy or sustain reduced treatment intensity.

Understanding what happens after de-escalation in routine care including whether older adults sustain reduced treatment or re-escalate therapy is essential to clarifying real-world feasibility of de-escalation and may highlight which patient characteristics are associated with earlier return to therapy or prolonged treatment reduction. Sustained de-escalation, defined as the continued absence or reduction of anti-TNF use without resuming or intensifying therapy, has potential benefits and risks for patient safety and healthcare utilization [17, 18] Successful sustained de-escalation may reduce the risk of medication-related harms and lower treatment costs, whereas unsuccessful attempts may lead to disease flare(s), functional decline, and increased healthcare utilization [19–21]. Identifying patient and treatment characteristics associated with re escalation versus sustained de escalation can inform future efforts to tailor de escalation strategies for older adults.

In this follow up study, we evaluated patterns of subsequent treatment after anti TNF de escalation in older adults with RA using national Medicare data. We evaluated the frequency of sustained de-escalation versus re-escalation of therapy and examined patient characteristics associated with re-escalation.

Methods

We analyzed a 20% sample of Medicare claims data, drawing from the Master Beneficiary Summary File (MBSF), Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MedPAR), Outpatient, Carrier, and Part D Event files spanning 2008 to 2022. Medicare claims were used because older adults are underrepresented in RA clinical trials [15], and longitudinal, national data are needed to evaluate real world treatment patterns. To enhance both the cohort size and the length of follow-up needed to identify de-escalation events, we incorporated the most comprehensive and up-to-date Medicare data available at the start of the study. This study received Institutional Review Board approval from the University of Michigan (HUM00168022). We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [22].

Study cohort

We adopted a previously validated claims-based algorithm to identify a cohort of older adults in fee-for-service (FFS) Medicare with a diagnosis of RA and anti-TNF use [23–25]. Anti TNFs were selected because they remain the most frequently used bDMARD and have the most complete longitudinal capture in Medicare claims, allowing consistent assessment of use patterns over time [26].

First, we identified Medicare beneficiaries who met the criteria for RA. Eligible individuals had at least two outpatient visits with an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code for RA diagnosis at least seven days apart but within 365 days. We restricted RA case identification to outpatient encounters to improve diagnostic specificity, given the higher positive predictive value of outpatient compared with inpatient claims (92% vs. 47–86%) [27]. The date of RA diagnosis was assigned as the earliest date on which these criteria were satisfied. Next, from this group, we identified patients who initiated one of four self-administered anti-TNF therapies covered under Medicare Part D - adalimumab, etanercept, golimumab, or certolizumab - within three months following their RA diagnosis. These medications were ascertained using National Drug Code (NDC) identifiers in the Part D Event files. Anti-TNF biosimilars were not included in this analysis because biosimilars for the originator products evaluated in this study were introduced to the U.S. market only in 2023, after the study period [28, 29]. The first anti-TNF agent dispensed within three months of the RA diagnosis was designated as the individual’s primary anti-TNF therapy. We required at least six months of continuous use of the primary anti-TNF medication as a proxy for stable use. Patients who switched to another bDMARD (including a different anti-TNF or non-TNF therapy) within this initial six-month period were excluded from the analytic sample.

We then identified older adults with de-escalation, either by cessation or by taper, using an algorithm adapted from our prior study [16]. Cessation was defined as a gap of more than 90 days in anti-TNF supply without evidence of prior dose reduction or an increase in the dosing interval [13, 16, 30]. Tapering was defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in effective dose relative to the individual’s initial anti-TNF dose, a measure calculated using strength and days’ supply information from Part D claims [13, 16, 31]. The initial dose was defined as the average monthly dose of the primary anti-TNF during the first 180 days following treatment initiation. Effective dose reductions could occur through changes in dose strength, extension of dosing interval, or both. For example, reducing etanercept from 50 mg weekly to 25 mg weekly or spacing weekly injections to every 2 weeks. This approach aligns with the de-escalation protocols used in clinical trials [14, 21, 32–34]. The index date was defined as the final day of anti-TNF therapy (i.e., the last prescription fill date plus the days’ supply) before de-escalation occurred.

The cohort was then restricted to beneficiaries with uninterrupted fee-for-service (FFS) Medicare enrollment, including Parts A, B, and D, during the 6 months preceding the RA diagnosis through the index date, and for at least 6 months following the index date. To ensure engagement with rheumatology care, beneficiaries were required to have at least one outpatient visit with a rheumatologist (Medicare provider specialty code 66) within the 12 months preceding and following the index date. Follow-up commenced at the index date and continued until the earliest occurrence of death, disenrollment from FFS Medicare and/or Part D, or the end of the study period in December 2022.

In summary, the final study cohort consisted of Medicare beneficiaries aged 66 years and older who met RA identification criteria, with de-escalation of primary anti-TNF therapy, had at least one rheumatology visit before and after the index date, and maintained continuous enrollment in FFS Medicare and Part D throughout the study period between 2008 and 2021. Outcome measure

The primary outcome was re-escalation, defined as either restarting the same anti-TNF or any bDMARD (including another anti-TNF or non-TNF therapy) after a gap of at least 90 days, or increasing the effective dose of anti-TNF by at least 50% following the index date. Patients who did not meet these criteria were classified as having sustained de-escalation.

Patient measures

Patient characteristics, including age at index date, sex, race/ethnicity, comorbidity, and low-income subsidy (LIS) status at baseline, were obtained. The baseline was defined as the 12 months preceding the index date. Comorbidity burden was assessed using a modified version of the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI), based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, and included conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, and chronic renal or liver disease, but excluded autoimmune conditions [35, 36]. To account for differences in coding across ICD-9 and ICD-10 eras, a crosswalk was developed a priori to ensure consistent identification of conditions. We identified 25 non-autoimmune disease conditions for inclusion in the comorbidity assessment. Each condition was ascertained using one of two criteria: [1] at least two outpatient claims with relevant ICD codes separated by at least 7 days but occurring within a 365-day window, or [2] at least one inpatient claim with a corresponding ICD code. The ECI score was calculated as the unweighted sum of these identified conditions. Information on the number of rheumatology visits at baseline was obtained.

We also collected information on concomitant use of medications. Specifically, Part D Event files were used to identify baseline use of five conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs), methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, leflunomide, and azathioprine. Long-term glucocorticoid (GC) use at baseline was defined as having a near-daily prescription for oral GCs for more than 90 days and a cumulative dose of > 450 mg prednisone equivalent in the year preceding the index date.

Statistical analyses

We used descriptive statistics to characterize the cohort and to compare older adults who re-escalated treatment with those who sustained de-escalation. To evaluate factors associated with re-escalation, we conducted a time-to-event analysis using Cox proportional hazards regression, in which sustained de-escalation was treated as the censored outcome. In this framework, a lower hazard ratio (HR) for re-escalation indicates a greater likelihood of sustained de-escalation over time. Patients were followed from the index date until (1) the development of the re-escalation event (outcome) or (2) the end of the study period (2022) or death or loss to follow-up (censored). The main independent variables were the mode of de-escalation, patients’ age at the index date, sex, race/ethnicity, LIS status, comorbidity by ECI, concomitant csDMARD use, long-term GC use, and index year of diagnosis.

Data were analyzed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using supremum tests and graphical inspection of residual plots.

Results

We identified 949 Medicare beneficiaries with RA who de-escalated anti-TNF and met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). During the follow-up period, 38.4% sustained anti-TNF de-escalation. Treatment was re-escalated in 61.6%, with a median time to re-escalation was 164 days (IQR 113, 301) from the index date (Fig. 3). Among individuals who re-escalated treatment, the rate was higher in those who had de-escalation of anti-TNF by cessation (61.9%) compared to those who tapered (55.8%). Most re-escalation events (82%) involved restarting the same primary anti-TNF agent. Discussion

In this national Medicare cohort of older adults with RA, nearly four in ten individuals sustained de-escalation of anti-TNF therapy over time, indicating that durable treatment reduction is achievable for a substantial subset of patients. Among those who re-escalated, bDMARD therapy was typically resumed within six months of de-escalation. In adjusted models, re-escalation, compared to sustained de-escalation, was associated with African American or Hispanic/Other race/ethnicity and with having a lower comorbidity burden, indicating that patients with higher comorbidity burden were more likely to sustain de-escalation.

These findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that while sustained de-escalation of anti-TNF therapy is achievable for some patients, it remains challenging in broader clinical practice. In a systematic review, Ward et al. reported that approximately 40–58% of patients with RA remained in low disease activity or remission 37–52 weeks after discontinuing anti-TNF therapy in induction-withdrawal trials, with similar proportions (47–48%) observed in maintenance treatment discontinuation studies [37]. The proportion of older adults who sustained de escalation in our study was somewhat lower than these trial based estimates.

Several factors unique to older adults may help explain differences from trial-based findings. The average age of participants in RA clinical trials is typically in the mid-50s [15], whereas the mean age in our study population was in the mid-70s. Trials also often exclude individuals with multimorbidity and frailty which is common in older adults. Older age may correspond to longer-standing RA in some individuals, a factor associated with reduced likelihood of successful discontinuation in prior trials [37]. Conversely, approximately one-third of older adults with RA are diagnosed later in life, known as late-onset RA (LORA), which is characterized by an acute onset of systemic symptoms and more limited exposure to anti-TNF therapy before de-escalation [38]. These differing clinical contexts may each contribute to lower rates of sustained de-escalation, either due to more refractory disease or insufficient treatment duration to achieve durable remission.

Treatment strategy and study design differences may also contribute to the discrepancies between our results and trial-based estimates. The 43 studies included in Ward et al.’s review focused exclusively on discontinuation, limiting their generalizability to broader de-escalation strategies that include tapering. The STRASS trial (Spacing of TNF-blocker injections in Rheumatoid ArthritiS Study), which followed 96 patients for up to three years, demonstrated that sustained remission was less frequent in the discontinuation arm, whereas tapering was associated with superior long-term disease control [18]. Other studies also report that tapering is associated with more favorable clinical outcomes and higher rates of sustained remission in RA compared to abrupt withdrawal [13, 34, 39]. Although tapering appeared more successful than cessation in crude comparisons in our cohort, this difference was not statistically significant after adjustment, which may reflect unmeasured differences in disease activity, clinician rationale, or patient preference that are not captured in claims. These unmeasured factors underscore the challenge of separating disease driven from preference or safety driven decisions in administrative data.

Trial design differences, in addition to de-escalation strategies, may help explain the discrepancies in sustained de-escalation rates between our real-world cohort and prior clinical trials. Sustained de-escalation rates in clinical trials, particularly those using induction–withdrawal designs, tend to be modestly higher than those seen in maintenance treatment discontinuation studies [37]. Induction–withdrawal designs selectively enroll participants who have already achieved disease control, which may inflate success rates compared to more heterogeneous real-world populations. In parallel, the definition and measurement of RA remission vary across clinical settings and studies, potentially influencing who is considered eligible for de-escalation and how sustained remission is assessed [13, 33, 37, 40]. Together, these differences in study design, patient selection, and outcome measurement may contribute to the higher rates of sustained de-escalation reported in prior trials compared to our findings.

An unexpected finding was that older adults with lower comorbidity burden were more likely to re-escalate anti-TNF therapy (i.e., less likely to sustain de-escalation) after initial de-escalation. This differs from some trial based evidence suggesting that higher comorbidity is associated with poorer durability of remission after de escalation [37]. In our cohort, patients with higher comorbidity burden were less likely to re-escalate, despite being more likely to de-escalate in the first place, as shown in our prior study [16]. One plausible explanation is that individuals with higher comorbidity may have been less likely to resume biologics due to concerns about infection risk, polypharmacy, or competing health priorities, even if disease activity increased. Conversely, individuals with fewer comorbidities may have been viewed as better candidates for re-escalation due to perceived treatment tolerance. These interpretations should be viewed cautiously, as claims data do not capture clinical disease activity or the rationale behind treatment changes.

The observed racial and ethnic differences in re escalation also merit careful interpretation. While all individuals in our cohort had initiated anti TNF therapy under rheumatologic care, prior research demonstrates that Black and Hispanic patients may experience higher cumulative disease burden, more functional limitations, and episodic care, which could increase the likelihood of flare or treatment intensification after a treatment gap [41]. These differences likely reflect social and structural factors, including access, monitoring frequency, and contextual clinical factors, rather than biological differences in treatment response. In addition to clinical factors, patient preferences may also influence de-escalation and re-escalation patterns. One study using adaptive conjoint analysis found that Black patients with RA were significantly less likely than White patients to prefer aggressive treatment, even after adjusting for disease severity and education level [42]. This preference for less intensive therapy may initially support de-escalation, but may also lead to re-escalation if treatment goals are not met.

By leveraging a large, national claims dataset, this study provides a longitudinal view of treatment patterns after de escalation in older adults, a population rarely represented in de-escalation trials. These findings complement, rather than replace, trial evidence by highlighting how de escalation unfolds in routine practice among individuals with multimorbidity, polypharmacy, and diverse treatment histories. Clinically, anti-TNF de-escalation may be feasible for selected older adults but requires close monitoring given the high rate of subsequent treatment resumption. From a health system perspective, understanding real-world patterns of treatment reduction and re-escalation may inform clinical and shared decision-making and guide planning for follow-up care after de-escalation.

This study has several limitations. First, the observational nature of the study limits causal inference and confounding by indication is likely, as disease activity, flare severity, patient preference, and clinician rationale are not captured. Second, the absence of clinical measures limits the ability to distinguish remission from low disease activity or treatment intolerance at the time of de escalation. Although we required 6 months of continuous anti TNF use on cohort entry as a proxy for stability, this approach cannot confirm clinical remission. Third, cohort selection required continuous FFS Medicare enrollment and rheumatology visits before and after de escalation, which may select for patients with more stable access to care and limit generalizability to those in Medicare Advantage or with inconsistent follow up Fourth, a minority of index dates occurred during the COVID 19 pandemic and although we adjusted for index year, some disruptions in care may not be fully captured. Finally, claims cannot distinguish between preference-, safety-, or disease-driven treatment decisions, which limits interpretation of the reasons underlying re escalation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, nearly four in ten older adults with RA sustained anti TNF de escalation in routine practice, supporting that treatment reduction is feasible for selected individuals. However, most re-escalated therapy within six months, underscoring the need for careful follow up after dose reduction. Further studies with claims data linked to clinically enriched data sources or prospective cohorts are needed to inform tailored approaches to de-escalation and monitoring strategies and to better understand factors influencing treatment decisions in older adults.
