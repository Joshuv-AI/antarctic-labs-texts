Visualization and quantitation of spine deformity in zebrafish models of scoliosis by micro-computed tomography
Authors: Elizabeth A Bearce, Bryson Tyler B Ricamona, Katherine H Fisher, Johnathan R O’Hara-Smith, Daniel T Grimes
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryZebrafish (Danio rerio) are increasingly used to investigate spine development, growth, and for studying the etiology of spinal deformity, such as scoliosis. Here, we present a micro-computed tomography-based pipeline for visualizing the zebrafish skeleton. We describe steps for sample preparation, imaging, data management, and processing. We then detail analysis of vertebral and spine morphology using open-source software. This protocol will be useful for scientists using zebrafish to understand spine development and disease.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Bearce et al. (2022).1Subject areas: Developmental biology, Health Sciences, Model Organisms

Before you begin

Zebrafish have emerged as prominent models to study the skeleton and skeletal deformity.1,2,3,4 The protocol below describes the specific steps to prepare zebrafish samples for whole body micro-computed tomography (μCT) scanning followed by three-dimensional reconstitution, spine and vertebral isolation then spinal curve quantitation.

CRITICAL: Ensure zebrafish are fully submerged in fixative. Shorter fixation times with poor penetration can lead to the survival of gut microbes that will cause gas expansion in the digestive tract and distortion of the sample. Samples can also be scanned freshly culled without fixation, but these will need to be fixed or discarded immediately after scanning to prevent degradation.

Pause point: For long-term storage at 4°C, after 2 days to 1–2 weeks of fixation, transfer zebrafish to 1 x PBS and change as needed to maintain freshness. Long term storage in buffered fixative solutions is not uncommon for curated historical samples, and adequate buffering will reduce decalcification of mineralized tissues. If you intend to use bone mineral density measures from your samples, using consistent preps with short fixation times will give the best results. If you are chiefly interested in morphological data, you may find that your results are not sensitive to fixation length, but these should be kept consistent.

Timing: 15–30 min

This section describes mounting zebrafish in agarose for μCT scanning.

Note: Agarose works well as a mounting medium for non-contrast imaging of bone, as outlined here. Water-soluble contrast agents such as PTA, PMA, or Iodine will diffuse into agarose. CRITICAL: If you do not see your fish when you attempt your Scout View, and instead see a repeating artifact, your sample may have loaded too far forward in the scanner. There must be a small region of tube-free space at the beginning of the scan for it to calibrate. If the sample needs to be moved, stop the scan, wait for the panel to say that the machine is safe to open, move the sample holder one slot towards the user, and repeat the Scout-view.

CRITICAL: Higher resolution scans will capture a larger number of data slices and improve distinguishable detail. However, a smaller voxel will increase scan time, and may increase the costs of acquisition. We have performed scans at a range of resolutions (typically 10–32 μm), and all are sufficient to visualize general spine morphology. Perform a few rounds of optimization and analysis with 2–3 scans of the same zebrafish at different resolutions to determine the level of detail that you would like to capture. Once established, then begin scanning your bulk datasets with consistent parameters.

Note: All users should receive radiation awareness training through institutional bodies prior to implementing μCT-based protocols.

Note: Ensure sufficient data storage is available prior to data collection; raw scan files may exceed 5–8 GB. (If there is not enough room available, the program will abort the scan shortly after it is initialized.) The next processing step will save the scan volume in a new data format, which will double the storage space required. In general, good data management is critical for scanner workstations!

Note: We often digitally isolate the spine by drawing a fixed diameter tube through the length of the zebrafish that follows the trajectory of the center of the vertebral column. As this function trims away the bulk of the zebrafish, performing these spine isolations is a convenient way to pare down the amount of memory used by downstream analysis and reconstruction processes. It also removes fin and scale elements that would obscure the spinal column from dorsal and ventral views.

Note: The Cobb angle is a metric used to quantify irregular spine curves in human patients5; an angle greater than ten degrees is required for the diagnosis of scoliosis. A Cobb angle is drawn between the most displaced vertebral elements at the rostral and caudal ends of a curve, and the angle is taken over the most displaced (apex) vertebra in the curved region (Figure 7). As the Cobb angle was designed for analysis of X-rays of human patients, this method of curve analysis is best used with a maximum projection (or 2-dimensional) image taken from the dorsal aspect, where the spine will generally appear linear. We have adapted this method to quantify spinal curves from a lateral view, assessing primarily dorsal-ventral curvature in the zebrafish, which is more akin to a sagittal Cobb angle in humans.

Expected outcomes

It is expected that users who are following this protocol will be able to successfully fix, mount, and scan juvenile or adult zebrafish using micro-computed tomography, extract elements from the data stack to create spine or vertebral isolations, and then perform morphological quantitation on these structures. This is ideal for comparing wild-type and mutant zebrafish exhibiting spinal dysmorphologies.

Limitations

This protocol as outlined does not cover means to crop or compress scan volumes (of course, there are quite a few ways to handle this), and it hinges on a computer that is able to apply various edits and translocations to several thousand high-resolution images at once. As such, the analysis will work best for smaller data volumes (2–3 zebrafish in total) at medium-to-low resolution, and will still benefit from a workstation with good processing power. (One of our workstations is a 2.3 GHz 8-Core Intel Core i9 MacBook Pro with 64 GB 2667 MHz DDR4 memory, and AMD Radeon Pro 5500M 8 GB and Intel UHD Graphics cards; this computer handles Painting and Grow From Seeds segmentation and subsequent 3D visualization on up to 4–5 uncompressed fish volumes at once, but accomplishes many of the steps very slowly.) Preprocessing to reduce downstream computation times can be performed with the Crop Volumes module in 3D Slicer. If the analysis station can only handle smaller data volumes, individual zebrafish should be “digitally dissected” as smaller substacks within the Evaluation software on the Scanco Vivascan workstation.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

At step 18, your computer may fail to open the full scan volumes in 3D Slicer.

Potential solution

One can also perform fish segmentation from within the Scanco Evaluation software rather than 3D Slicer, so that only individual fish files will be opened on the analysis workstation rather than the full scan. To accomplish this within the Scanco Evaluation software, use the free-form ROI tool to lasso around the entire area of the first zebrafish in the tube intermittently every 20–30 axial slices (as opposed to using a rigid-sized circle to capture the entire tube volume). Make sure to include a few slices in front of the snout, generously enclose its widest points, take care not to remove fins, and to enclose the last bits of the tail, if a whole zebrafish reconstruction is desired. Then, use the Range feature to highlight all slices that contain this zebrafish, and then apply the Morph function to smooth the ROI shape across the range of data and encapsulate the zebrafish in an irregular 3D lasso. At this point, additional ROIs are placed to capture the remaining zebrafish in the tube, and each of the lassos are exported as individual DICOM files. This method creates DICOM files that contain small regions of neighboring zebrafish if not done very rigorously, so further processing in Segment Editor will likely still be warranted.

Problem 2

At step 22, Grow from Seeds may fail, or there may be errors in result (usually poorly-assigned fins or missing ones).

Potential solution

If it fails altogether: At least 2 segments must be created for Grow from Seeds to work; if you are only segmenting one fish, you will still need to create a “background” segment that contains some undesired material, like scales or gut contents in order to use Grow from Seeds. Similarly, failure may arise from painting segments into multiple views (i.e., axial and saggital slices simultaneously), or from painting segments in neighboring slices. Ensure at least one unpainted slice separates all seeded slices, and that seeds are only placed in one view window.

If there are errors in the segmentation: Once initialized, Grow From Seeds will continue to update its results when it is given new paint inputs. Results will be updated when you add new painted regions (or erase bad ones that were causing errors); you can re-initialize or cancel it to start over, or hit Apply again to save the new changes to the 3D Viewer. However, for large files like this, correcting a bad “paint job” seems to require more memory than a fresh initialization. To avoid lagging or freezing, one can also close the program, re-open and revert to their saved painted segments, and then correct the slices that caused errors in the first pass before hitting Initialize again. Once you are happy with the corrections, hit Apply to save them to your segments.
