S-Nitrosothiols modulate Porphyromonas gingivalis virulence
Authors: Tsian D. Ramrattan, Evan R. Stair, Shreya Dandu, Robert L. Morgan, Rajnish Kumar, Quincy K. Snyder, Leslie M. Hicks, Ronit Freeman, Mark H. Schoenfisch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Porphyromonas gingivalis is the key etiological pathogen of periodontal disease, a chronic oral inflammatory condition that affects over 14% of the global population. P. gingivalis possesses several potent virulence factors—including gingipains, hemagglutinins, and fimbriae—that drive microbial dysbiosis. To restore homeostasis, nitric oxide (NO) is endogenously produced by immune cells to eliminate the infection, but disease persistence necessitates the use of therapeutic agents. The multiple antibacterial mechanisms of NO minimize the potential for antimicrobial resistance compared to traditional antibiotics, making exogenous NO delivery a potential attractive strategy for combating P. gingivalis. The objective of this study was to evaluate the effect of exogenous NO on the virulence of P. gingivalis, including NO's capacity to influence biofilm formation and dispersion, gingipain activity and production, invasion of host cells, and mitigation of immune responses. Nitric oxide effectively modulated the virulent behavior of anaerobic P. gingivalis via transnitrosylation and nitrosative stresses, reducing the pathogen's ability to form biofilms and gingipain activity. Within the anaerobic niche, NO exposure alters the pathogen's ability to export gingipains, thereby significantly reducing its potential to invade host cells and evade the immune system. This work highlights the therapeutic potential of exogenous NO as a promising therapeutic to alter periodontal disease through its multimodal effects on the virulence of P. gingivalis.

Introduction

Periodontal disease, a chronic oral inflammatory condition, affects over 1 billion people worldwide, with severe forms of this disease (i.e., periodontitis) characterized by tissue destruction, alveolar bone loss, and tooth destabilization [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Moreover, periodontal disease is a risk factor for the development of comorbidities such as diabetes [5], Alzheimer's disease [6], and cardiovascular disorders [2,[7], [8], [9]]. Microbial dysbiosis initiates periodontal disease, whereby commensal bacteria populations become dysregulated due to microenvironmental changes caused by lifestyle, diet, or health conditions (i.e., pH, medications, environmental toxins) [[10], [11], [12]]. Although the immune system can often restore homeostasis by regulating this dysbiosis, persistent microbial imbalance due to poor oral hygiene leads to chronic inflammation that drives disease progression [13]. The onset of periodontal disease shifts the oral microbial community from predominantly Gram-positive aerobes to one dominated by Gram-negative anaerobes [14]. The most notable of these Gram-negative species is Porphyromonas gingivalis, which is considered to be the key etiological pathogen in periodontal disease [15]. P. gingivalis maintains microbial dysbiosis even at low population densities by altering redox potential, oxygen gradients, and nutrient availability to favor pathogenic species via potent virulence factors [14,16,17].

P. gingivalis possesses long and short fimbriae, gingipains (i.e., cysteine proteases), hemagglutinins, and lipopolysaccharides (LPS), all of which contribute to its ability to form and maintain biofilms, invade host cells, evade immune cells, and dysregulate the host's immune response [15,18,19]. Fimbriae are filamentous appendages attached to the outer membrane of the bacteria that mediate adhesion and invasion by binding to tissues, promoting coaggregation (i.e., multispecies biofilm formation), and enticing endocytic uptake into cells [20,21]. Gingipains are arginine-specific (RgpA and RgpB) or lysine-specific (Kgp) cysteine proteases that encompass 85% of P. gingivalis' extracellular proteolytic activity [18,22,23]. Gingipains contain adhesive regions that adhere to host cell membranes and catalytic active sites that hydrolyze proteins at arginine or lysine residues, thereby cleaving cell-surface receptors and proteins reducing cell membrane integrity [18].

Treatment of P. gingivalis is challenging as these bacteria reside deep below the gingiva, often embedded in a dense biofilm matrix composed of exopolysaccharides and proteins [24]. Traditional antibiotics (e.g., penicillin, tetracyclines) are often prescribed to treat oral infections, with limited favorable outcomes as they are unable to penetrate the biofilm matrix [17,[24], [25], [26]]. Overuse of traditional antibiotics with limited success promotes antimicrobial resistance, further exacerbating dysbiosis [27,28]. In the clinical setting, attenuation of pathogen virulence parallels eradication with respect to outcomes as modulating virulence disarms the pathogen to facilitate improved immune clearance [29]. It is thus imperative to investigate therapeutic alternatives capable of modulating pathogen virulence to reduce reliance on traditional antibiotics.

Nitric oxide (NO) is an endogenously produced free radical generated by numerous cells involved in the innate immune system (e.g., macrophages, neutrophils, fibroblasts, epithelial cells) [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Indeed, NO is produced at high concentrations (μM) during infection to elicit inflammation, reduce bacterial load, and restore tissue homeostasis [33]. The NO reacts with other mediators to facilitate nitrosative (i.e., dinitrogen trioxide, S-nitrosothiols) and oxidative (i.e., nitrogen dioxide, peroxynitrite) stresses on bacteria that result in protein nitrosation, DNA damage, and degradation of the protective membranes [35,36]. The small size (i.e., ∼1.15 Å) and neutral (i.e., uncharged) nature of NO enables its diffusion through, disruption of, and eradication of biofilms [37,38]. The multiple mechanisms by which NO elicits antibacterial activity reduces the risk of antimicrobial resistance commonly observed for traditional antibiotics, making the use of NO prodrugs attractive for treating a wide array of challenging health conditions, including periodontal disease [39,40].

We have previously reported the antibacterial efficacy of NO-releasing materials against planktonic P. gingivalis, the effect of NO against P. gingivalis in vivo, and the ability of NO to reduce the metabolic activity of ex vivo periodontal biofilms [[41], [42], [43]]. However, the effects of NO on the virulence of P. gingivalis are not clear. Herein, we set out to determine the potential of NO to suppress the virulence of two P. gingivalis strains (i.e., 33277 and 381) using small molecule NO donors [[44], [45], [46]]. The effects of NO on biofilm prevention and dispersion were examined in vitro using standard assays. Furthermore, NO's ability to alter the production of virulent proteins produced by P. gingivalis. Lastly, the role of exogenous NO treatment on the invasion of P. gingivalis and its effect on host recognition of the pathogen was assessed. In total, these results provide evidence for NO's capacity to modulate the virulence of P. gingivalis, suggesting exogenous NO treatments via NO-releasing agents may be an attractive alternative therapeutic for the treatment of periodontal disease.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of NO-releasing test agents

Syntheses of the NO-releasing test agents S-nitroso-N-acetylpenicillamine (SNAP) and (N-Acetyl-S-nitrosopenicillaminyl)-S-nitrosopenicillamine (SNAP-SNAP) and their respective nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra are included in Supplementary Information.

Measurement of NO-release kinetics

A Sievers 280i chemiluminescence nitric oxide analyzer (NOA; Boulder, CO) was used to monitor NO release in real time. The NO-release kinetics of SNAP, SNAP-SNAP, and MD3 were determined in deoxygenated pH 7.4 PBS at 37 °C to mimic physiological conditions. To assess differences in the NO-release kinetics in the presence of physiologically relevant concentrations of glutathione (i.e., 2-10 mM), 10 mM solutions of glutathione in pH 7.4 PBS were also utilized for measurement. As RSNOs are photolabile, samples were protected from light to limit any photochemical liberation of NO ensuring only the native release was measured. Nitrogen was utilized as a carrier gas (200 mL min−1) for NO to be measured by the instrument. All measurements were terminated once NO levels fell below the limit of quantification of the NOA (i.e., < 10 ppb mg−1).

Culturing of bacterial strains

Strains of P. gingivalis (33277 and 381) were either grown in WCB or on TSA plates supplemented with 5% v/v defibrinated sheep's blood, 5 μg mL−1 hemin, and 1 μg mL−1 menadione under anaerobic conditions (5% v/v CO2, 10% v/v H2, and 85% v/v N2) in a Coy Laboratories Vinyl Anaerobic Chamber (Grass Lake, MI). In broth, the bacterial cultures were grown until late-log phase (OD600 of 0.6-0.7) prior to use in assays. All assays performed with light sensitive NO-releasing test agents (i.e., SNAP and SNAP-SNAP) were protected from light for the duration of the experiment.

Minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) assays

The bactericidal potential of the NO-releasing test agents and the relevant non-NO-releasing controls were evaluated using MBC assays. Phosphate buffered saline supplemented with 10% v/v WCB (100 μL) was added to a 96-well plate. Solutions of NO-releasing test agent in 10% v/v WCB were added to the first column of a 96-well plate and 2-fold serially diluted across the plate to achieve a range of treatment concentrations (i.e., 4-2000 μg mL−1 for SNAP and SNAP-SNAP, and 0.25 - 125 μg mL−1 of MD3). Suspensions containing 5 × 105 CFU mL−1 (10 μL) of bacteria were then added to wells containing treatment. The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C. After 4 and 24 h of exposure, aliquots from each well (10 μL) were plated on agar and incubated until colonies were visible on the plates. The lowest concentration necessary for ≥ 3 log or 99.9% reduction in bacteria was recorded as the MBC.

Cell Culture

The HOECs were cultured in human oral epithelium complete growth media and grown until 80% confluency prior to utilization in assays. Human THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% v/v FBS, 1 wt % PS, 1% v/v GlutaMAX, 1 mM MEM NEAA solution, 10 mM HEPES buffer, and 0.55 mM β-mercaptoethanol and were utilized in assays once culture density was greater than 7.5 × 105 cells mL−1. Human embryonic kidney (HEK)-Blue-hTLR2 and HEK-Blue-hTLR4 cells were grown in high glucose, phenol red and sodium pyruvate free DMEM supplemented with 10% v/v FBS, 1 wt% PS, and 100 μg mL−1 normocin antimicrobial reagent and 1X HEK-Blue selection. The cells were incubated in 5% v/v CO2 at 37 °C until 80% confluence was reached. All cell types were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% v/v CO2. Assays conducted with light sensitive NO-releasing test agents (i.e., SNAP and SNAP-SNAP) were protected from light for the duration of the experiment.

Cytotoxicity of NO-releasing test agents

For the HOECs, suspensions of 2.5 × 105 cells mL−1 were prepared with 100 μL aliquots added to tissue culture-treated 96-well plates followed by incubation overnight. After the incubation period, media was aspirated from the wells and replaced with fresh media containing a range of treatment concentrations (i.e., test material). The plates were incubated for 24 h after treatment and then exposed to a 0.1 mg mL−1 solution of resazurin (20 μL per well) and incubated for an additional 4 h. The fluorescence (Fl) was measured at excitation and emission wavelengths of 544 nm and 590 nm, respectively, on a Molecular Devices SpectraMax M2 spectrophotometer. Biofilm prevention

Biofilm prevention assays were adapted from previously published protocols to assess the ability for NO to prevent P. gingivalis biofilm formation [47]. Briefly, cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.2 in supplemented TSB (5% v/v hemin, 5 mg mL−1 yeast extract, 1 mg mL−1 menadione, and 200 mg mL−1 l-cysteine). Bacterial culture (200 μL) was added to the relevant inner wells of tissue culture-treated 96-well plates containing 3X the desired final concentration of NO-releasing test agent dissolved in supplemented TSB (100 μL). Outer wells were filled with PBS (200 μL) to prevent evaporation from the inner wells. The plates were anaerobically incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the broth was gently aspirated from the wells to remove residual planktonic bacteria, and the adhered biofilms were washed three times with Milli-Q water. The biofilms were heat fixed at 70 °C for 1 h and stained with 0.06% w/v solution of CV in water (50 μL) for 5 min. Residual CV was aspirated from the wells and the wells washed with Milli-Q water to remove excess stain. Acetic acid (30% v/v; 150 μL) was added to the wells to extract CV back into solution. After a 10 min incubation at RT, the absorbance of the solution in each well was measured at 600 nm using a Molecular Devices SpectraMax M2 spectrophotometer (San Jose, CA). Untreated wells with biofilm and wells containing only broth were stained with CV and utilized as biofilm controls and blanks, respectively. Biofilm dispersion

Biofilms were grown and treated with a range of concentrations of NO-releasing test agents to determine the ability for NO to disperse P. gingivalis biofilms. Briefly, cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.2 in PBS supplemented with 10% v/v TSB and added to the inner wells of tissue culture-treated 96-well plates (200 μL). Outer wells were filled with PBS (200 μL) to limit evaporation of the inner wells. The plates were anaerobically incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Post incubation, broth was gently aspirated from the wells and replaced with 10% v/v TSB in PBS to create static growth conditions. To the wells, aliquots of 3X more concentrated solutions of NO-releasing test agent were added (100 μL) to ensure desired final concentrations within the wells. The plates were incubated for an additional 24 h, after which the supplemented PBS was aspirated from the wells, and the biofilms were washed three times with Milli-Q water. The wells were stained with CV as described above (Section 2.8) with the normalized biofilm mass calculated using Equation 3.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging

Bacterial cultures were diluted to an OD600 of 0.2 in supplemented TSB and added to the wells of a 24-well plate (1 mL) containing sterile paper discs. Solutions containing 3X the desired final concentration of NO-releasing test agent (500 μL) were added to relevant wells. The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. After the incubation period, the paper discs were gently rinsed with PBS to remove any planktonic bacteria, and the discs were processed for imaging following previously published protocols [39]. In brief, discs were immersed overnight at 4 °C in a fixative solution composed of 4% w/v glutaraldehyde, 2% w/v paraformaldehyde, 0.15% w/v alcian blue, and 1% w/v tannic acid in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4). The samples were then serially dehydrated by immersing the discs in 30, 50, 70, 90, 95, and 100% v/v ethanol for 10-min intervals. Subsequently, samples were dried in solutions of 50:50 v/v ethanol:HMDS and 100% v/v HMDS for 15-min immersion periods and left to air dry in a vented biosafety cabinet for at least 48 h. Samples were sputter coated with 15 nm of Au/Pd alloy and imaged with an S-4700 cold cathode field emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Gingipain activity assessment

The effect of NO on gingipain activity was studied based on previously published protocols [23,48,49]. Cultures of P. gingivalis were grown and bacteria pelleted, then washed once with PBS and resuspended in 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer containing 5 mM CaCl2, 0.2 M NaCl, 10 mM NaOH, and 9 mM l-cysteine. Suspensions were diluted to an OD600 of 1.0 for assays with aliquots of cultures added to 96-well plates (100 μL) in either the presence or absence of NO-releasing test agent (50 μL). For Rgp assessment, plates were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C in the dark for 3 h and aliquots then diluted 1:10 in buffer prior to the addition of BAPNA (50 μL). After 10 min, 30% v/v acetic acid (50 μL) was added to the wells to quench the reaction and the absorbances of the wells read at 405 nm. Due to the slower production of Kgp, to monitor substrate specific binding, aliquots of 1 mM of ALNA (50 μL) were added to relevant wells prior to a 3 h incubation. After incubation, 30% acetic acid (50 μL) was added to the wells to quench the reaction and the absorbances of the wells were read at 405 nm. The gingipain-specific substrates were hydrolyzed by gingipains to produce chromogenic substrates which were correlated to protease activity. Wells containing no treatment and wells containing only buffer were utilized as controls, with gingipain activity calculated using Equation (4).(Equation 4)Activity(%)=Abssample−AbsblankAbscontrol−Absblank×100

Determining non-inhibitory concentrations of NO-releasing test agents

Cultures of P. gingivalis at OD600 ≥ 0.7 were confirmed using LC-MS/MS to produce sufficient protein concentrations for spectroscopic analyses. Non-inhibitory concentrations of NO-releasing test agents were identified for assessing the effect of NO on the virulence of P. gingivalis via proteomics. Proteomic response of P. gingivalis

Sample collection

Cultures were grown to OD600 > 1.0 and diluted to an OD600 of 1.0. To 50 mL centrifuge tubes, 40 mL of bacteria cultures (n = 5) and 10 mL of either broth, MD3, SNAP-SNAP or NAP-NAP, were added for final concentrations of 0, 16, 500 and 500 μg mL−1, respectively, of each treatment. The tubes were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacteria were pelleted at 10,000 rpm for 15 min and the supernatant collected and syringe filtered (0.2 μm pores) to remove any residual bacteria present in the supernatant. The cell pellets were immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until assessment. The supernatant proteins were precipitated in 10% w/v of TCA in acetone at −20 °C and collected via centrifugation at 3220 RCF for 20 min. The supernatant was decanted, and the proteins were flash frozen and stored at −80 °C until use.

Global proteomics

Proteins collected from the supernatant were resuspended in 4 M urea in PBS and quantified using a CB-X protein assay (G-Biosciences, MO). Samples were normalized to 1 mg mL−1, and 50 μg were taken for analysis. Proteins were reduced with 10 mM DTT, alkylated with 30 mM IAM, and then precipitated with 10x volume of 100 mM ammonium acetate in methanol for 1 h at −20 °C. Proteins were resuspended in 2 M urea in PBS and digested overnight at 37 °C with a 1:40 Trypsin Gold to protein ratio. Samples were then desalted using C18 ZipTips, vacuum centrifuged to dryness and resuspended in 0.1% formic acid prior to LC-MS/MS analysis.

Proteins from the bacterial cell pellets were extracted with 0.1% v/v Triton X-100, 0.25% w/v sodium dodecyl sulfate, and protease inhibitors in PBS via end-over-end rotation for 1 h at RT protected from light. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 3220 RCF for 10 min at 4 °C. Proteins were precipitated from the supernatant with 5X volume of 100 mM ammonium acetate in methanol at −20 °C for 1 h and pelleted by centrifugation at 3220 RCF for 10 min at 4 °C. Samples were resuspended in 4 M urea in PBS and protein was quantified using a CB-X protein assay (normalized to 1 mg mL−1). For analysis of protein from the cell pellet, 100 μg of protein was reduced, alkylated, and digested as described above. Samples were desalted via solid phase extraction and vacuum centrifuged to dryness prior to resuspension in 0.1% v/v formic acid for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Redox proteomics

Oxidized cysteine resin assisted capture (OxRAC) was performed from both the cell pellet extract and the supernatant, as previously described [50]. Following protein collection from the supernatant and extraction from the cell pellet, proteins were incubated for 1 h with 100 mM IAM at RT to irreversibly block thiols reduced in vivo. Proteins were precipitated with 100 mM ammonium acetate in methanol, washed once with methanol to remove excess IAM, and then resuspended in 4 M urea in PBS. From the cell pellet, 1 mg of protein from each sample was used while 100 μg of protein were used from the supernatant. Reversibly oxidized cysteines were reduced for 1 h with 10 mM DTT at RT by mixing end-over-end. Proteins were precipitated with 100 mM ammonium acetate in methanol at −20 °C for 1 h. Samples were centrifuged at 20000 RCF for 15 min to pellet proteins before being resuspended in 0.25% SDS and PBS prior to enrichment.

Thiopropyl resin (100 μL per sample; G-Biosciences, MO) was used to enrich for reversibly oxidized cysteines. The resin was washed three times with PBS before being resuspended in 100 μL of PBS. Proteins were added to the resin and mixed for 2 h at RT. Samples were transferred to a MobiSpin column (Boca Scientific) and were washed with 0.25% SDS and PBS, 50% acetonitrile, and 1 M urea in PBS, centrifuging at 750 RCF for 5 s between each wash step to remove unbound protein. The spin columns were plugged, and the resin was resuspended in 1 M urea in PBS prior to the addition of 5 μg of Trypsin Gold (Promega, WI) to the cell pellet samples and 0.5 μg of Trypsin Gold to the supernatant samples for overnight digestion.

Following digestion, unbound peptides were removed by centrifuging at 1000 RCF for 5 s. The resin was washed once with 50% acetonitrile and once with water, centrifuging in between each wash step. The spin columns were re-plugged before the resin was incubated with 50 mM DTT for 30 min to elute bound cysteine containing peptides. The resin was washed twice with 50% acetonitrile and combined with the eluate. Samples were desalted via C18 ZipTip prior to analysis by LC-MS/MS.

LC-MS/MS analysis of peptides

Samples were analyzed using a Waters Acquity M-class UPLC coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% v/v formic acid in water while mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% v/v formic acid in 80% v/v acetonitrile. For global supernatant samples, ∼200 ng (4 μL) injections were made, while 200 ng injections (1 μL) were made for cell pellet proteomic samples. For OxRAC samples, 4 μL injections were made from both the supernatant and cell pellet. Samples were injected onto an Aurora Elite C18 column (15 cm × 75 μm ID, 1.7 μm; IonOpticks, Australia) at a flow rate of 300 nL min−1. After injection, mobile phase B was ramped from 5 to 40% over 75 min before increasing it to 60% over 10 min. Mobile phase B was then ramped to 99% over 1 min and held for 5 min. The column was then ramped to 5% mobile phase B for 5 min and allowed to equilibrate for 24 min. Survey scans were collected across a 375-1475 m/z range at a 300% AGC target and a maximum injection time set to Auto at 60,000 resolving power. Fragmentation scans were collected in a data dependent manner for monoisotopic precursor ions having a minimum intensity >5000 and a +2 to +6 charge state for a total cycle time equivalent to 1.5 s. Fragmentation scans were collected using a 30% NCE, a 200% AGC target, and a maximum injection time set to Auto at 15,000 resolving power. A 20-s dynamic exclusion was applied after 1 occurrence.

Proteomics bioinformatics analysis

Raw data files were imported into Progenesis QI (Nonlinear Dynamics, v4.2) for peak picking, alignment, quantification, and normalization. A combined peak list (.mgf) containing all fragmentation spectra for each feature m/z was exported for peptide sequence identification using Mascot (Matrix Science, v2.5.1). Database searching was performed against the P. gingivalis strain 33277 UniProt proteome (2025 entries, accessed 20250328) and sequences for common laboratory contaminants (https://www.thegpm.org/crap/, 116 sequences). Precursor/fragment ion tolerances of 15 ppm/0.02 Da were used with a fixed cysteine carbamidomethylation modification for global samples and a variable cysteine carbamidomethylation for OxRAC samples, variable methionine oxidation and protein N-terminus acetylation modifications, and two possible missed proteolytic cleavages. Significant identifications above the homology threshold were adjusted to an FDR <1% using the embedded Percolator algorithm. Peptide identifications were imported back into Progenesis for protein quantification [51]. Protein and peptide measurements were imported into R and analyzed using a custom script (https://github.com/hickslab/QuantifyR), as previously described [50]. Fold changes were calculated as the difference in mean protein abundance across conditions. Statistical analysis was performed using a Benjamini-Hochberg corrected two-sided student's t-test [52]. Observations with a q-value <0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1 were considered statistically significantly different.

Epithelial cell invasion assays

To delineate the effect of treatment on the invasiveness of P. gingivalis, intracellular invasion assays were performed with bacteria pre-treated with SNAP-SNAP, NAP-NAP and MD3 as previously described in Section 2.13.1. Invasion assays were modified and conducted based on previously published protocols [53]. Briefly, cell suspensions of 1.2 × 105 cells mL−1 of HOECs in antibiotic-free oral keratinocyte media supplemented with oral keratinocyte growth serum were added to 24-well plates (1 mL) and incubated overnight to allow for the cells to adhere. After incubation, the media was aspirated from the wells and replaced with fresh media. Bacterial suspensions of pre-treated bacteria exposed over 24 h to NAP-NAP, SNAP-SNAP and MD3, were created by centrifuging the bacteria, washing the cultures with PBS and resuspending in antibiotic-free media. Aliquots of the bacteria were immediately added to the HOECs at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 100. The plates were incubated for 90 min, after which the media was aspirated from the wells, the wells were washed with PBS, and fresh media containing 300 μg mL−1 of gentamycin, and 200 μg mL−1 of metronidazole (1 mL) was added to the wells and the plates further incubated for an additional 1 h to eradicate any residual extracellular bacteria. The antibiotic containing media was then aspirated from the wells, the wells washed once with PBS, followed by the addition of sterile Milli-Q water to the wells (1 mL) for 20 min to lyse the cells and liberate intracellular bacteria. Aliquots (100 μL) were taken from the wells and serially diluted in PBS prior to plating on agar for colony enumeration. Untreated bacteria were utilized as controls.

TLR2 and TLR4 reporter cell assay

Cells were cultured as previously described prior to assays. Toll-like Receptor 2 and TLR4 cell suspensions of 2.8 × 105 and 1.4 × 105 cells mL−1, respectively, were made in antibiotic-free media. The suspensions were aliquoted (180 μL) into the relevant wells of 96-well plates and the cells incubated overnight. After incubation, the media was gently aspirated from the wells and replaced with fresh media (180 μL) prior to exposure 20 μL aliquots of 1 × 108 CFU mL−1 of pre-treated bacteria that was washed with PBS and resuspended in antibiotic-free cell media. The cells were further incubated for an additional 24 h prior to determining cell receptor induction. This was performed via monitoring secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase production, for which these reporter cell lines are engineered to express as a response to receptor stimuli, utilizing Quanti-Blue solution as per manufacturers instruction.

Endogenous ROS quantification

Cells were seeded in chambered cover glass wells and incubated for 24 h to allow them to adhere. After incubation, media was aspirated from the wells and the cells were either left untreated or exposed to SNAP-SNAP at 500 μg mL−1 for 1.5 h. During the final 30 min of treatment, cells were all exposed to Hoechst 33342 and either DHE or DHR. All dyes were added such that the final concentration of each in solution was 10 μM. After the incubation period, media was aspirated from the wells and apical surface of the cells washed. Fresh media was then added, and the cells immediately imaged using an Andor Drangonfly spinning disk confocal microscope (Oxford Instruments, CA) equipped with an iXon Life 888 EMCCD camera. Images were taken via sequential acquisition using an HC PL APO 20x/0.75 LWD objective through a 40 μm pinhole at excitation wavelengths of 405, 488, and 561 nm. Exposure time for measuring DHE, DHR and Hoechst 33422 was 200, 100, and 100 ms, respectively, and laser powers were set at 1,1, and 0.5%, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data for in vitro assays are reported as the average ± standard error of the mean for n ≥ 3 biological replicates. Significance was evaluated with two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's tests as corrections for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was the significance cutoff.

Results and discussion

NO-release characterization

Conventional, chemical NO donors were employed to exogenously deliver NO in a controlled manner. Two S-nitrosothiol (RSNO) NO donors, SNAP and SNAP-SNAP (Scheme S1), and the C-diazeniumdiolate NO donor, MD3, were selected and synthesized for study to provide variance in NO-release mechanisms and diverse byproducts. The NMR spectra of SNAP and SNAP-SNAP are provided in Fig. S1 and S2, respectively and the byproducts of NO release highlighted in Scheme S2. The RSNO agents decompose under physiological conditions (i.e., 37 °C, pH 7.4) to liberate NO molecules and either form thiols or disulfides [54]. The C-diazeniumdiolate donor, MD3, decomposes via a proton-initiated mechanism at pH 7.4 to liberate NO and simple, bioinert byproducts (i.e., nitrite, formic acid and other low molecular weight metabolites) [46].

The real time and cumulative NO-release kinetics of SNAP, SNAP-SNAP, and MD3 were determined over 24 h at 37 °C in pH 7.4 PBS (Fig. 1A and B). In addition, SNAP and SNAP-SNAP were assessed in the presence of physiologically relevant concentrations of GSH (i.e., 10 mM; Fig. 1C and D). For all measurements, PBS was purged with nitrogen to ensure the solution was anaerobic prior to measurements. In the absence of GSH, the C-diazeniumdiolate, MD3, liberated its NO in a burst-like manner while SNAP and SNAP-SNAP had profiles with minimal initial flux (Fig. 1A). Despite differences in NO-release mechanisms, all three NO-releasing agents had sustained profiles following pseudo first-order release kinetics (Fig. 1B). The 24-h NO payloads for the NO-releasing agents in the absence of GSH were determined to be 1.17 ± 0.15, 1.18 ± 0.16 and 6.07 ± 0.19 μmol mg−1 for SNAP, SNAP-SNAP, and MD3, respectively. Of the three NO-releasing agents, MD3 had the greatest NO-payload, delivering approximately 6X greater concentrations of NO compared to SNAP and SNAP-SNAP. The NO payloads delivered during subsequent assays at relevant timepoints (i.e., 1.5, 3, 4, and 24 h) are detailed in Table S1.Fig. 1Representative (A and C) NO-release profiles and (B and D) cumulative NO release in the (A and B) absence and (C and D) presence of 10 mM GSH for SNAP (black), SNAP-SNAP (gray) and MD3 (blue) normalized to 1 mg of test agent.

Representative (A and C) NO-release profiles and (B and D) cumulative NO release in the (A and B) absence and (C and D) presence of 10 mM GSH for SNAP (black), SNAP-SNAP (gray) and MD3 (blue) normalized to 1 mg of test agent.

In the presence of physiologically relevant concentrations of GSH, the initial NO flux of SNAP and SNAP-SNAP increased by approximately 5% and 30%, respectively, compared to release kinetics measured in the absence of GSH (Fig. 1C and Table S2). Further, the NO payloads delivered for SNAP and SNAP-SNAP were 1.80 ± 0.48 μmol mg−1 and 1.28 ± 0.21 μmol mg−1, respectively, with accelerated NO release durations of 12.57 ± 1.86 and 7.59 ± 0.50 h as compared to > 24 h in the absence of GSH. Free thiols in solution promote rapid disulfide bond formation, thereby enhancing NO liberation [54]. Accordingly, the NO-release profiles of SNAP and SNAP-SNAP demonstrate that thiol-rich environments significantly alter both the kinetics and chemistry of NO release. Under more complex conditions, these donors may induce oxidative (i.e., disulfide formation), nitrosative (i.e., NO release), and transnitrosylation-mediated stress.

Therapeutic indices of NO-releasing test agents

To further evaluate the efficacy of NO-releasing agents as therapeutics for periodontal disease, cytotoxicity assessments were performed using two cell types predominant within the periodontium: HOECs, and THP-1s. Both cell types are involved in the innate immune response, contributing to pathogen clearance and maintaining homeostasis [32,57,58]. Further, HOECs play an important role in preserving tissue integrity and stabilization of teeth [57,59]. The cell viabilities of HOECs, and THP-1s after exposure to a range of concentrations of the NO-releasing agents were utilized to generate dose-response curves (Fig. S3) from which IC50 values were determined (Fig. 2A). Of the three NO-releasing systems, SNAP-SNAP was best tolerated by the cells despite delivering comparable concentrations of NO versus SNAP. Further, MD3 was the most cytotoxic, anticipated due to the significantly larger NO-payload compared to SNAP and SNAP-SNAP. This data corroborates trends observed with the MBCs (Section 3.2) whereby the smaller molecular weight of SNAP and the NO payloads delivered by MD3 enhances toxicity toward cells compared to SNAP-SNAP. While macrophages have protective systems that should enable a greater tolerance to NO levels, the HOECs were more robust against SNAP and SNAP-SNAP as compared to THP-1 cells [60]. Effect of NO on biofilm prevention and dispersion

P. gingivalis is heavily involved in plaque formation, utilizing both long and short fimbriae to mediate its adherence to other bacterial species (e.g., Fusobacterium nucleatum, Streptococcus gordonii), periodontal tissues and teeth [61]. The formed biofilm matrix ensures greater resistance to therapeutics, evasion of the host immune system, and a decrease in oxygen concentration within the periodontal pocket, creating ideal growth conditions for anaerobic pathogens involved in disease progression [62,63]. Prevention and dispersion of biofilms are both necessary for the treatment of P. gingivalis. Previous studies have demonstrated the robust expression of fimbriae for both tested strains (i.e., 33277 and 381), validating their use as model strains to evaluate the efficacy of NO release on the formation of biofilms [64].

For the assessment of biofilm prevention, large numbers of planktonic culture (i.e., 1.2 × 109 CFU mL−1) in supplemented media were exposed to concentrations of SNAP (63-1000 μg mL−1), SNAP-SNAP (63-1000 μg mL−1), MD3 (4-63 μg mL−1), or their respective non-NO-releasing controls prior to overnight incubation. Concentrations of the NO donors were selected based on the 24 h MBC values and are lesser or equivalent to concentrations of antibiotics utilized in FDA approved therapies (e.g., Atridox® and Arestin® that administer ≥ 1000 μg of antibiotic agents, such as doxycycline HCl and minocycline HCl, to each site of infection) [65,66]. Further, similar concentration ranges of NO-releasing materials have been evaluated by our lab in a murine model for periodontal disease with no reported adverse side effects [41]. Residual biofilm mass was quantified to determine if any of the tested agents reduced biofilm formation (Fig. 3A–C and Figure S4 A and B). As expected, the non-NO-releasing controls did not provoke any differences in biofilm formation compared to the untreated controls. At comparable concentrations to the non-NO-releasing controls, NO-releasing agents significantly reduced biofilm formation, demonstrating that biofilm prevention by these molecules is NO-dependent. Strain 33277 consistently required 2-4 times greater concentrations of the NO test agents to prevent biofilm formation compared to strain 381, suggesting that 33277 can better tolerate the effects of the NO. Despite reports of strain 381 forming thicker and more structured biofilm matrices, strain 33277 has greater fimbriae expression versus strain 381, which may contribute to biofilm formation regardless of NO treatment [64,67]. To corroborate the biofilm prevention capabilities of NO, SEM images were taken of the biofilms formed over 24 h (Fig. S5). For the untreated control, clusters of bacteria held together in a fibrous matrix are present whereas a significant reduction in both bacteria and biofilm matrix on the substrate was observed for all NO-treated samples. Upon establishing biofilm prevention, the impact of the NO-releasing test articles on P. gingivalis biofilm dispersion was evaluated (Fig. 3D–F). For biofilm dispersion assays, large concentrations of bacteria culture in supplemented media (i.e., 1.2 × 109 CFU mL−1) were incubated for 48 h to promote the formation of dense biofilm matrices. Following incubation, residual planktonic bacteria were removed, and the biofilms treated with the three NO-releasing systems and their respective non-NO-releasing controls. After a 24 h treatment period, residual biofilm mass was measured to assess the effects of NO on dispersion.

Similarly to the trends observed in the biofilm prevention assays, the non-NO-releasing controls had no effect on dispersing biofilms (Figure S4 C and D). Strain 33277 required greater concentrations of NO-releasing test agents to disperse biofilms relative to strain 381, further implicating fimbriae expression to be important in biofilm stability. Lower concentrations of SNAP and SNAP-SNAP were required to significantly disperse biofilms for both strains relative to the concentrations needed for biofilm prevention (i.e., 32 versus 125 μg mL−1). Low concentrations of NO have previously been reported to disperse biofilms by lowering the production of bacterial cyclic di-GMP [37,38]. However, the opposite trend was observed for MD3 as biofilm dispersal required greater concentrations of MD3 versus biofilm prevention. While MD3 portrayed great potency against planktonic species, the large net-negative charge of MD3 may result in repulsion at the negatively charged biofilm interface, thereby limiting its penetration into the biofilm and reducing its ability to disperse the matrix. Of note, approximately 6X higher concentrations of NO are required for MD3 (i.e., 0.19 μmol mg−1) compared to SNAP and SNAP-SNAP (i.e., 0.04 μmol mg−1) to disperse matured biofilm matrices. These results clearly reveal the importance of both precursor structure and charge in modulating P. gingivalis virulence. Together, the biofilm prevention and dispersion data reveal the ability of NO-releasing agents to deter P. gingivalis from forming biofilms. The anti-biofilm activity of NO highlights its capacity to penetrate and disperse matured biofilm matrices, thus demonstrating potential as a therapeutic against P. gingivalis.

Gingipain activity

P. gingivalis produces gingipains that hydrolyze membrane proteins and receptors to allow for intracellular epithelial invasion and evasion of immune cells [19,53]. Gingipains are exported to the cell exterior where they can exist membrane bound, free in suspension, or packaged for transport on the surface or within outer membrane vesicles that bud from the bacterial membrane [68]. Despite strain 33277 having greater proteolytic activity compared to 381, both strains produce gingipains, contributing to the virulence of P. gingivalis [55]. The activities of both the arginine-specific (RgpA and RgpB) and the lysine-specific (Kgp) gingipains were assessed following treatment with concentrations of NO-releasing test agents that are cytocompatible with relevant cells types (i.e., ∼ IC20; ≥80% cell viability) to decipher if there were effects on proteolytic activity. Chromogenic substrates BAPNA and ALNA, specific to Rgp and Kgp, respectively, were utilized to determine enzymatic activity colorimetrically. As expected, given reports by Coats et al. which demonstrated that Rgp is produced at higher basal levels and differ in secretion processing as compared to Kgp, lower concentrations of NO imparted less pronounced effects on reducing Rgp activity versus Kgp [55]. The observed effect of NO agents on Rgp activity may be a result of greater overall Rgp production compared to Kgp. In assessments of both Rgp and Kgp activity, strain 381 had a significant reduction in its activity across a wider range of NO donor concentrations compared to 33277. The observed disparity between the strains may be attributed to the pronounced difference in baseline levels of proteolytic action between strains 33277 and 381 [55]. Despite the variation between strains, Rgp and Kgp activities were reduced in a concentration-dependent manner for both strains with larger concentrations inducing more significant effects compared to controls. Exposure to SNAP significantly affected Rgp and Kgp activity for 33277 at the largest concentration tested (i.e., 140 μg mL−1) while MD3 only impacted Kgp activity at the greatest concentration tested (i.e., 16 μg mL−1). However, exposure to SNAP-SNAP enticed significant differences in Rgp activity at ≥165 μg mL−1 with Kgp activity reduced for all tested concentrations (41-660 μg mL−1). For strain 381, SNAP demonstrated reduced Kgp activity at all tested concentrations (9-140 μg mL−1), while all other trends were maintained for the two tested strains.

Concentrations of MD3 were less effective at modulating gingipain activity relative to SNAP and SNAP-SNAP despite MD3 having the greatest effect on reducing P. gingivalis invasion into epithelial cells (Section 3.5). Together, these data suggest that the mechanism by which MD3 modulates P. gingivalis virulence may differ relative to SNAP and SNAP-SNAP. The SNAP and SNAP-SNAP NO donors can decompose to liberate NO radicals and form disulfides or perform transnitrosylation reactions capable of oxidizing amino acid residues (i.e., cysteine) [54,[69], [70], [71]]. We hypothesize that in microenvironments of lower redox potential (i.e., anaerobic), SNAP and SNAP-SNAP likely entice oxidative and nitrosative stress relative to MD3 that may rely solely on nitrosative stresses to modulate P. gingivalis virulence [72].

Due to the high protein concentrations necessary to quantify relative gingipain abundance, strain 33277 was selected for assessment as it exhibits greater proteolytic activity relative to strain 381 [55]. In addition, only SNAP-SNAP was evaluated due to its ability to reduce Kgp activity at all tested concentrations for both strains (Fig. 4D). To compare the effects across NO donors (i.e., S-nitrosothiol versus C-diazeniumdiolate), MD3 was also evaluated in these studies. Gingipains have no metal centers available for nitrosylation but rather rely heavily on cysteine residues for appropriate protein folding and proteolytic abilities [73]. To confer if the reduced activity of Kgp, RgpA and RgpB was attributed to nitrosylation of cysteine residues, cysteine sites of gingipains present in both the supernatant and the cell pellet were evaluated via redox proteomics utilizing OxRAC. For the gingipains exported into the supernatant and in the cell pellet, no significant oxidation occurred at the active sites (i.e, Kgp: C477, RgpA: C468 and RgpB: C473) for any tested material (Tables S3 and S4). However, nitrosylation of any cysteine residue can have major implications on protein folding, activity, and export.

In Gram-negative bacteria, like P. gingivalis, unfolded proteins are first transported to the periplasm where disulfide bonds form, ensuring correct protein folding prior to export across the outer membrane via the Type IX Secretion System (T9SS) to the microenvironment [68]. While the T9SS has a high proportion of aromatic amino acid residues (i.e., tyrosine and tryptophan) these residues will not be readily nitrated or carbonylated in the absence of peroxynitrite formation, making it less likely that the tested NO donors elicited direct inhibitory effects to this transport protein. Within the cell pellet, there was only significant oxidation at the C428 residue for Kgp after exposure to SNAP-SNAP (Log2FC: 1.78). While it is unclear how oxidation of C428 on Kgp would affect protein folding, activity, and export, our results indicate a clear difference in abundance of exported gingipains, with further work needed to gain mechanistic insight into the collateral effects of SNAP-SNAP treatment on both protein folding in the periplasm and T9SS export.

Hemagglutinins, like gingipains, are virulence factors that rely on T9SS for export and contain no metal centers but have cysteine sites for available transnitrosylation or disulfide formation. To probe if export was also implicated, the relative abundances of hemagglutinins (HagA; B2RLK7) in the supernatant and the cell pellet were determined. There were no significant changes to the relative abundance of HagA after exposure to NAP-NAP or MD3 in either the supernatant (NAP-NAP Log2FC: −0.58; MD3 Log2FC: −0.30) or cell pellet (NAP-NAP Log2FC: −0.16; MD3 Log2FC: −0.02) while SNAP-SNAP exposure resulted in a significant decrease of HagA in the supernatant (Log2FC: −1.77) and a significant increase of this protein in the cell pellet (Log2FC: 1.02). These results further indicate an effect on T9SS export following SNAP-SNAP treatment as this effect is not only isolated to gingipain export. Regardless of the mechanism of which SNAP-SNAP elicits a decrease in protein export, we observe a distinct modulation of exported virulence factors by NO treatment.

Although gingipain and hemagglutinin production remained unaffected by MD3, exposure elicited substantial nitrosative stress. This is indicated by the production of hybrid cluster protein (Hcp; B2RJM1), a redox enzyme produced by P. gingivalis with key involvement in NO detoxification pathways. The relative abundance of Hcp, as such, can be utilized as a general indicator for nitrosative stress in this assay as it has no direct association to the attenuation of virulence [49,74]. There was a significant increase in the relative abundance of Hcp in MD3-treated samples in both the supernatant (Log2FC: 4.59) and the bacterial pellet (Log2FC: 5.35). This effect, however, was not elicited by SNAP-SNAP as the difference in relative abundances of Hcp were insignificant in both the supernatant (Log2FC: 1.07) and the cell pellet (Log2FC: 0.98). This difference in effect on Hcp production may be a result of differences in the NO-release kinetics of the NO donors. The concentration of free radical NO delivered by SNAP-SNAP is minimal comparative to MD3 and may be below the threshold level for activating Hcp. Further, at the low concentrations delivered by SNAP-SNAP, NO can behave as a signaling molecule to influence the pathway by which Hcp is produced through a combinatory effect of both oxidative (e.g., disulfide formation) and nitrosative stresses (e.g., transnitrosylation) [70,71]. As the formation of reactive nitrogen species is dependent on the oxygen gradient, within an anaerobic environment, the generation of reaction nitrogen species beyond NO (e.g., peroxynitrite) should not occur. Thus, NO liberated by MD3 under anaerobic conditions has a significantly extended half-life which is likely the root cause of the elevated Hcp production after exposure to MD3.

Overall, these results suggest that SNAP-SNAP reduces the virulence of P. gingivalis by decreasing gingipain and hemagglutinin export into the microenvironment while minimizing Hcp production. As gingipains and hemagglutinins are heavily implicated in the virulence of P. gingivalis, reducing export may substantially disarm the pathogen, diminishing its pathogenicity. S-nitrosothiol NO donors have greater capacity to influence virulence as they can induce stress via transnitrosylation despite the low-redox potential (i.e., anaerobic) environment. This data strongly supports that S-nitrosothiol NO agents modulate P. gingivalis virulence despite differences in the oxygen gradient as compared to agents such as MD3 that liberate free radical NO molecules.

Host response to NO treatment

P. gingivalis predominantly relies on protein degradation rather than fermentation as a source of energy [20]. Therefore, P. gingivalis invades host cells to access nutrient-rich environments and adequately support proliferation [53]. Fimbriae mediate binding to host cell membranes while gingipains hydrolyze membrane proteins to decrease the structural integrity of the host membrane affording for intracellular invasion [18,20,53]. Reducing P. gingivalis’ ability to invade mammalian cells would preserve the structural integrity of periodontal tissues and minimize tooth loss [76]. Gingipains are also potent immune modulators as they cleave cytokines and immune receptors (i.e., TLRs, cluster of differentiation (CD) receptors) and alter signaling pathways, thereby reducing immune cell recognition and manipulating cell function [55,77,78]. While exposure to SNAP-SNAP reduced gingipain export, it also increased fimbriae expression. We hypothesize that phenotypic changes will reduce the invasive nature of P. gingivalis while shifting the pathogen from a less immune suppressive state toward a more immunostimulatory phenotype. These changes increase the potential for improved visibility of the pathogen to immune cells to facilitate enhanced clearance. To determine if the observed phenotypic changes had lasting effects on invasion and host-detectable signaling, P. gingivalis was exposed to NO-releasing treatments for 24 h, after which intracellular invasion of epithelial cells was assessed (Fig. 7A). The invasion assays were performed with primary HOECs, as this cell type is nontransformed and has neither alteration to its surface receptors nor internal physiology, making it an ideal model cell type for the study of P. gingivalis invasion into epithelial cells. Further, HEK-Blue reporter cells with upregulated expression of human TLR2 and TLR4 receptors were exposed to the bacteria. Both TLR2 and 4 receptors are important pattern recognition receptors in innate immunity and inflammation, and receptor activation is indicative of improved host recognition of the pathogen. Receptor activation was monitored via NF-κB induction and the data represented in Fig. 7B and C. For the invasion assays, following incubation and the eradication of extracellular bacteria with antibiotics, cells were lysed to liberate intracellular bacteria. Aliquots of the lysates were plated for colony enumeration and the effect of pre-treatment of P. gingivalis with NO agents was then determined relative to untreated controls (Fig. 7A). The data revealed that exposure to NAP-NAP and MD3 had no effect on P. gingivalis’ ability to invade HOECs compared to the control group. Demonstrating that any nitrosative stress imparted by MD3 was not sufficient to dampen this specific virulence phenotype. However, exposure to SNAP-SNAP had lasting effects on the invasiveness of P. gingivalis, significantly reducing invasion by > 3-logs (i.e., 99.9% reduced invasion), limiting a potential route whereby P. gingivalis can acquire nutrients and evade the immune system, decreasing the persistence of infection. While reduced invasion leaves P. gingivalis more exposed to clearance, improved TLR signaling can leader to faster immune recognition and clearance. With respect to TLR2 and 4 signaling (Fig. 7B and C), exposure to NAP-NAP did not result in any significant differences from the untreated controls while MD3 exposure only encouraged significant activation of TLR4. This data is consistent with the proteomics assessment as TLR4 primarily recognizes LPS while TLR2 are receptive to lipoproteins, lipopeptides and fimbriae [79]. Treatment with MD3 may have effects on the pathogen such that it entices TLR4 but, as demonstrated in Fig. 6, had no effect on fimbriae expression relative to the untreated control. Exposure to SNAP-SNAP, however, resulted in enhanced activation of both TLR2 and TLR4, confirming that the reduced gingipain export and increased fimbriae expression enhance immune recognition consistent with reduced immune evasion.

To delineate the impact treatment with SNAP-SNAP may have on endogenous redox signaling, HOECs and THP-1 macrophages were exposed to this NO agent and changes to reactive species were assessed via use of the fluorescent probes DHE and DHR, commonly used indicators for superoxide and broader oxidative species, respectively. For all cell types, exposure to SNAP-SNAP resulted in the concurrent suppression of intracellular oxidative species relative to the untreated cells. This dampening effect can be indicative of a more reducing environment which may facilitate decreased oxidative stress and reduced inflammation. That is, the mammalian cells may experience a protective effect from exposure to SNAP-SNAP concentrations that simultaneously diminish the virulence of P. gingivalis. Together, these data demonstrate the potential for the exogenous delivery of RSNO moieties to reduce virulence, limit immune evasion, and improve immune clearance while protecting cells from excessive oxidative stress during exposure.

Study limitations

Although the present study demonstrates that RSNO agents both modulate P. gingivalis' virulence and reduce transport of virulence-associated proteins, it does not directly establish that protein transnitrosylation is the mechanism underlying this observed effect. Transnitrosylation of cysteine residues on gingipains and HagA proteins represents a plausible means by which protein folding is altered, thereby reducing transport via the T9SS. However, studying RSNO modifications in vivo is inherently challenging as these reactions are thermodynamically and kinetically driven, and influenced by factors including concentration, local redox potentials, and enzymatic presence. Further, undesirable decomposition of the S–NO bond may occur during protein collection and sample preparation, reducing the significance of such data. As T9SS is a membrane-bound complex, direct assessment of its activity may also prove difficult. Additional mechanistic assessments are thus required to directly demonstrate transnitrosylation as the primary contributing factor to the observed effect of RSNOs on P. gingivalis’ protein export.

To further evaluate this hypothesis, site-directed mutagenesis of virulence-associated proteins could be employed to mutate available cysteine residues to serine residues, thereby preventing potential for S-nitrosylation. These modifications would allow for determination of whether the presence of unmodified cysteine residues influences the export of gingipains and hemagglutinins. In parallel, virulence-associated proteins could be tagged with a fluorescent protein or evaluated with immunofluorescence using protein specific antibodies. Such data would prove useful for visualizing intracellular localization with confocal microscopy after RSNO treatment. Evaluating if the proteins are accumulating within the cell membrane, cytoplasm or other cell components may help validate the stage in transport at which protein export is being halted. To support observations on protein accumulation, structural effects on the T9SS system after exposure to NO treatments could be assessed microscopically or through use of X-ray crystallography to ensure that activity of T9SS is not compromised and confirm that effects on export are associated with changes in protein folding. Lastly, the reversibility of the observed reduction in protein export should be further evaluated by removing RSNO stimulation and performing proteomic assessments at predetermined timepoints. Such experiments would help determine if the effect on protein export rate is reversible and modulated by transnitrosylation, as anticipated, versus irreversible protein damage.

Conclusions

Nitric oxide delivery via RSNO NO donors were shown to clearly modulate P. gingivalis virulence. Such effects on virulence provides a unique pathway for potential therapeutic intervention. Although SNAP-SNAP was less antibacterial against planktonic P. gingivalis cultures compared to MD3, alternate chemistries beyond the mere generation of NO, including disulfide bond formation and transnitrosylation, also proved effective in altering the virulence. For example, the solution-phase delivery of SNAP-SNAP demonstrated anti-biofilm activity, decreased gingipain activity, and reduced intracellular invasion while increasing the degree of TLR recognition. These combined effects promoted an immunostimulatory phenotype for improved immune recognition and clearance at cytocompatible concentrations that reduced ROS production from relevant cell types. These findings support the use of NO therapeutic agents as potential alternatives to traditional treatments for periodontal disease, as they not only aid in bacterial clearance but also protect host cells from stressors. Studies determining both potential therapeutic utility in advanced models and the molecular mechanisms by which SNAP-SNAP reduces transport of virulence factors across the membrane represent important future research directions.
