Transient Cytoskeletal Anisotropy Encodes Short‐Term Mechanical Memory in Glioblastoma Cells
Authors: Clara Gomez‐Cruz, Matthieu Gelin, Lucas Pradeau‐Phélut, Arrate Muñoz‐Barrutia, Sandrine Etienne‐Manneville, Daniel Garcia‐Gonzalez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCells experience time‐varying mechanical cues when navigating complex microenvironments, yet whether and how they retain a short‐term memory of recent deformations remains unclear. Here, we show that glioblastoma cells encode such memory through transient cytoskeletal anisotropy. Combining magneto‐mechanical actuation, nanoindentation, and selective cytoskeletal perturbations, we find that actin architectures drive opposite mechanical responses: stress fibers stiffen cells under stretch, whereas the actin cortex governs softening under compression. Vimentin intermediate filaments stabilize actin organization under load, preserving these deformation‐specific responses. Mechanical actuation aligns both networks, more strongly for actin than vimentin, and this anisotropy persists after unloading. Using a two‐step actuation protocol, we show that residual alignment biases the response to a second deformation: cells retain information about prior loading, and this bias decays as the cytoskeleton relaxes, defining a memory window of minutes to tens of minutes. To integrate these observations, we develop a multi‐network constitutive model that links cytoskeletal architecture and loading history to cell mechanics, reproducing asymmetric mechanical responses, cytoskeletal reorganization dynamics, and memory effect. These findings show how invasive cancer cells could exploit residual cytoskeletal order to adapt to fluctuating solid stresses and confinement, and identify vimentin–actin coupling and remodeling kinetics as levers to limit that adaptability.

Introduction

Cells experience mechanical cues that vary over time. This variation can arise as tissue mechanics evolve during development, repair, and fibrosis [1, 2, 3], but also, on much shorter timescales, as a migrating cell moves through a spatially heterogeneous environment, encountering changing mechanical conditions. Cancer progression represents an extreme manifestation of this variability. As tumors grow and invade, cancer cells encounter spatially and temporally heterogeneous environments shaped by solid stress, confinement, altered matrix architecture, and interactions with neighboring cells [4, 5]. In highly invasive cancers, such as glioblastoma, tumor cells migrate through mechanically distinct regions of the brain, including perivascular niches, white matter tracts, and densely packed tumor cores, experiencing alternating phases of compression, relaxation, and tensile deformation [6, 7, 8].

The cellular response to such stimuli depends not only on the instantaneous magnitude or mode of loading, but also on its temporal characteristics, including duration, rate, and the order in which deformations are applied [9, 10, 11]. In this context, the concept of mechanical memory has emerged, describing situations in which past mechanical environments influence future cellular behavior even after the original stimulus has been removed [12]. Most studies of mechanical memory have focused on long‐term effects mediated by transcriptional reprogramming, epigenetic modifications, or stable changes in cell state, particularly in cancer and fibrosis [3, 5, 13]. However, migrating cells often operate on much shorter timescales (minutes to hours), raising the possibility that memory could also be encoded through faster, purely structural mechanisms [14].

The cytoskeleton is a prime candidate for such short‐term mechanical memory. As the primary load‐bearing architecture of the cell, cytoskeletal networks continuously assemble, disassemble, and reorganize in response to mechanical cues, thereby introducing intrinsic timescales into cellular mechanics [15, 16, 17]. Because different cytoskeletal networks reorganize in distinct ways, they offer multiple structural routes through which a mechanical history could be stored. Actin stress fibers align and reinforce under tensile load, the actin cortex sets cell shape and resistance to deformation, and vimentin intermediate filaments stabilize these structures and support invasive behavior in cancer cells [6, 13, 18, 19, 20, 21]. Mechanical loading can induce cytoskeletal alignment and anisotropy, which are closely linked to cell polarity, persistent migration, and directional force generation [17, 22, 23, 24]. Whether such mechanically induced organization can persist after unloading and bias subsequent mechanical responses remains largely unexplored.

Addressing this question requires resolving how individual cytoskeletal networks contribute to cell mechanics under different deformation modes, and how their interactions change as loading evolves [25]. Among these interactions, the coupling between actin and vimentin is particularly important: the two are structurally interdependent, with vimentin stabilizing actin organization and enhancing resistance to mechanical stress [26]. Yet this coupling has been studied mainly under static or simplified loading [25, 27], leaving open how the networks respond to sequential deformations and whether the resulting transient cytoskeletal anisotropy can store a memory of prior mechanical states. Computational models have begun to capture key features of cytoskeletal mechanics, including nonlinear stiffening, anisotropy, and active remodeling [28, 29, 30], but most treat steady‐state or isolated loading and do not address how deformation history shapes transient organization [31]. Closing this gap calls for combining controlled mechanical perturbation, targeted cytoskeletal manipulation, and mechanistically grounded modeling that links cytoskeletal architecture to time‐dependent mechanical responses.

Here, we take this combined approach to investigate how glioblastoma cells encode and retain short‐term mechanical information. Using contactless magneto‐mechanical actuation to apply controlled tensile and compressive deformations, we show that transient cytoskeletal anisotropy, arising from deformation‐dependent reorganization of actin and its stabilization by vimentin, underlies short‐term mechanical memory in living cells. Glioblastoma cells subjected to the same mechanical stimulus produce markedly different cytoskeletal outcomes depending on their prior deformation history. This history‐dependence decays as relaxation time increases or the second stimulus is prolonged, defining a short‐term memory window on the timescale of minutes. The behavior resembles the hysteretic response of dissipative materials, suggesting that transient cytoskeletal remodeling acts as a structural mechanism, through which recent mechanical history influences subsequent cellular responses. Looking ahead, this framework may help explain how invasive cancer cells exploit residual cytoskeletal anisotropy to adapt to changing mechanical conditions.

Results

Actin‐Driven Deformation‐Dependent Stiffening Requires Vimentin‐Mediated Structural Stabilization

The cytoskeleton is not a static scaffold but a dynamic, reconfigurable structure that adapts its mechanics to the forces a cell encounters. We hypothesized that this adaptation emerges from the mechanically coupled organization of cytoskeletal networks under load, shaped by three factors (Figure 1a): the direction of actuation, which governs whether stress fibers align or de‐align with the loading axis; actin–vimentin interactions that preserve fiber integrity; and the cell's prior loading history, such that identical final deformation states can leave the cytoskeleton in distinct mechanical configurations. To test this, we combined selective cytoskeletal perturbations with mechanical actuation controlling tensile and compressive deformation modes. We focused on four major cytoskeletal components: branched actin forming the actin cortex, actin stress fibers, vimentin intermediate filaments, and microtubules, and assessed their respective contributions to basal and deformation‐induced cell stiffness. We first quantified the effect of selectively disrupting individual cytoskeletal networks on basal stiffness, measured in the relaxed, non‐actuated state by nanoindentation (Figures 1b and S1). Disruption of the actin cytoskeleton using cytochalasin D, which inhibits actin polymerization, resulted in a pronounced reduction of basal stiffness by 50%, establishing actin as the primary determinant of baseline mechanical resistance. Inhibition of ROCK‐dependent actomyosin contractility using Y‐27632 led to a much smaller decrease in basal stiffness (5%), indicating that stress fiber‐associated contractility contributes only modestly to the relaxed mechanical state. In contrast, inhibition of Arp2/3‐dependent branched actin polymerization using CK666 caused a substantial softening comparable to cytochalasin D (50%), consistent with a dominant contribution of branched cortical actin networks to basal stiffness. To limit the increased actomyosin contractility associated with nocodazole, a low dose of Y27632 was added to the microtubule disruption condition. Neither this combination nor nocodazole alone produced a measurable change in basal stiffness, following microtubule depolymerization (Figures 1b and S2). Vimentin knockout likewise produced no measurable change in basal stiffness, indicating that this network does not significantly contribute to the cell's relaxed cortical mechanical response, although vimentin depletion has been reported to reduce global whole‐cell stiffness [32].

We next probed how these cytoskeletal components regulate stiffness under active mechanical loading. Cells were subjected to controlled uniaxial magneto‐mechanical actuation using an upgraded version of the NeoMag device (60Nd S.L.) [33], compatible with inverted microscopy and nanoindentation (Figure 1c). Two loading configurations were applied: actuation parallel to the cell's long axis, resulting in uniaxial stretching, and actuation perpendicular to this axis, resulting in compression along the long axis. Cells were not pre‐oriented. Because the actuation axis is fixed by the device, individual cells were classified as parallel or perpendicular according to the angle between their morphological long axis and the actuation direction (see Methods). As previously demonstrated, using the same platform and 30% actuation amplitude, perpendicular substrate stretch generates compressive strain along the cell major axis at the cellular scale [33]. These two configurations therefore separate tensile from compressive loading along the cell axis.

In control cells, uniaxial stretch induced a robust stiffening response, with stiffness increasing by 37% relative to the relaxed state, whereas uniaxial compression led to a significant softening (38% decrease) (Figure 1d). This asymmetric mechanical response shows that cell stiffness is not only deformation‐dependent but also mode‐specific, with cells actively reinforcing their mechanics under tensile loading while yielding under cp, consistent with our prior findings in primary astrocytes [33]. Disrupting the actin cytoskeleton abolished this deformation‐dependent behavior. Following cytochalasin D treatment, cells failed to stiffen under stretch and did not soften under cp, remaining mechanically indistinguishable from the relaxed state in both loading modes (Figure 1e). This complete loss of mechanical adaptability establishes actin as a major driver of deformation‐dependent stiffening and softening. Pharmacological perturbation of actin sub‐networks revealed distinct functional roles under different deformation modes. Inhibition of ROCK‐dependent actomyosin contractility using Y‐27632 suppressed stretch‐induced stiffening, while compression still produced a marked softening response (Figure 1f). In contrast, inhibition of Arp2/3‐dependent branched actin polymerization using CK666 preserved stretch‐induced stiffening but abolished cp‐induced softening (Figure 1g). These differential responses show that distinct actin architectures drive the two asymmetric behaviors. Stretch‐induced stiffening relies on stress fiber‐associated contractile actomyosin structures that bear and reinforce tensile loads, consistent with their aligned, tension‐dominated organization, and known strain‐stiffening behavior [33, 34]. By contrast, cp‐induced softening depends on the branched cortical actin network, a thin shell‐like layer at the cell periphery. We attribute this softening to a loss of cortical load‐bearing capacity under in‐plane cp, plausibly through buckling or wrinkling instabilities [35, 36, 37].

In contrast to actin‐specific perturbations, vimentin‐deficient cells exhibited pronounced mechanical softening under both uniaxial stretch and cp, with stiffness decreasing by 22% in both conditions (Figure 1h). In these cells, fluorescence imaging revealed a marked disruption of the actin cytoskeleton under deformation, indicating that the observed softening arises from a failure to preserve actin network organization rather than due to the lack of vimentin directly. Thus, although actin‐driven mechanisms are responsible for deformation‐dependent stiffening, vimentin is required to mechanically stabilize the actin cytoskeleton under load. Because this knockout disrupts all organized intermediate filaments [32], this stabilizing role likely reflects the intermediate filament network as a whole rather than vimentin specifically. By contrast, microtubule‐depleted cells retained the full deformation‐dependent mechanical response observed in controls, stiffening under stretch and softening under compressio(Figure 1i), further confirming that microtubules do not play a dominant role in regulating cell stiffness under either basal or actuated conditions in this study. Additional experiments on cells at intermediate actuation angles are shown in Figure S3.

Together, these results establish a hierarchical organization of cytoskeletal mechanics: actin networks actively drive deformation‐dependent responses, with stress fibers and branched cortical actin governing tensile stiffening and compressive softening, respectively, while vimentin ensures the structural integrity of these actin architectures under load. In its absence, actin‐driven mechanics collapse, producing global softening irrespective of deformation mode. These findings indicate that deformation‐dependent cell mechanics arise not from the presence of specific cytoskeletal components but from their mechanically coupled organization, motivating a structural analysis of cytoskeletal reorganization and anisotropy in the following section.

Cytoskeletal Reorganization and Anisotropy Provide the Structural Origin of Deformation Mode–Specific Mechanics

To identify the structural mechanisms underlying deformation mode‐dependent mechanical responses, we quantified the organization, orientation, and anisotropy of actin and vimentin networks under relaxed and traction (tr)‐actuated conditions. Cytoskeletal organization was extracted from fluorescence microscopy images by resolving individual filament orientations and color‐coding fibers according to their angular direction (Figure 2a). This approach enabled a quantitative comparison of fiber alignment relative to the imposed uniaxial deformation axis, as well as the computation of network anisotropy and principal orientation. Because microtubule disruption did not measurably affect cell mechanics under the loading conditions considered here (Figures 1i and S2), we restricted the structural analysis to actin and vimentin.

We first verified that the applied actuation effectively deforms cells across conditions by quantifying global cell shape changes. Cell aspect ratios were measured under relaxed and actuated states for all perturbations (Figure 2b). In control cells, as well as in Y27632‐treated and vimentin knockout cells, tensile actuation induced a significant increase in cell aspect ratio (40% increase), confirming elongation along the loading direction. CK666‐treated cells exhibited a high aspect ratio already in the relaxed state, which remained largely unchanged upon actuation, indicating that these cells were already elongated prior to loading. Importantly, these measurements confirm that actuation induces comparable global deformation across conditions. The differences in cytoskeletal organization described below, therefore, reflect network‐specific remodeling rather than differences in cell elongation. We next analyzed the spatial organization of actin and vimentin networks in cells under non‐actuated conditions (Figures 2c and S4). In control cells, both actin and vimentin fibers displayed on average largely isotropic orientation distributions, with no dominant preferential direction. This reflects the population‐level averaging of individual cells that each carry a modest intrinsic anisotropy along their own long axis, as evidenced by the nonzero relaxed fractional anisotropy (FA) values in Figure 2f. Across a randomly oriented population, these preferred directions cancel, yielding an isotropic mean distribution. Upon uniaxial stretch, actin fibers reorganized into a highly anisotropic architecture aligned with the loading direction, while vimentin also aligned but to a lesser extent. Quantitatively, actin FA increased by 0.24, compared to 0.11 for vimentin (Figure 2f), and the principal direction of both networks rotated toward the actuation axis, with a stronger directional bias for actin. These results establish that deformation induces coordinated, but nonidentical reorganization of actin and vimentin, consistent with their distinct mechanical roles identified in Figure 1. Selective cytoskeletal perturbations revealed how this reorganization depends on network integrity (Figures 2d and S5). In Y27632‐treated cells, actin stress fibers were absent, resulting in a weak and poorly organized actin signal. Despite this, the vimentin network developed a pronounced anisotropy and aligned along the direction of actuation, closely resembling the behavior observed in control cells. In CK666‐treated cells, both actin and vimentin networks exhibited partial anisotropy, but to a lesser degree than in controls. Notably, in vimentin knockout cells, vimentin was absent, and the actin network appeared severely disrupted under actuation, with few discernible fibers, and no coherent alignment.

To establish in which conditions cytoskeletal alignment can be meaningfully quantified, we first assessed the relative abundance of actin and vimentin networks across perturbations in relaxed non‐actuated conditions (Figure 2e). CK666 treatment did not significantly alter the overall abundance of either network. Y27632‐treated cells exhibited a strong reduction in actin content, while maintaining vimentin levels comparable to control, whereas vimentin knockout cells retained actin levels similar to control in relaxed conditions and lacked vimentin, as expected. Because some perturbations partially deplete or compromise one or both networks, anisotropy was interpreted only where a sufficiently organized filament network was present. Within this framework, quantitative analysis of anisotropy and principal direction revealed distinct, network‐specific reorganization responses to actuation (Figure 2f–i). In control cells, both actin and vimentin exhibited a significant increase in anisotropy upon actuation, accompanied by alignment along the loading axis, with a more pronounced response for actin (Figure 2f). In Y27632‐treated cells, actin anisotropy remained negligible due to the absence of organized actin fibers, whereas the vimentin network developed anisotropy and aligned with the actuation direction similarly to control cells (Figure 2g). In CK666‐treated cells, both actin and vimentin networks displayed elevated anisotropy already in the relaxed state, which did not increase further upon actuation. Nevertheless, both networks reoriented their principal direction toward the loading axis (Figure 2h), indicating reorientation without an additional increase in anisotropy. This arises because CK666‐treated cells already display elevated, randomly oriented baseline FA, consistent with their markedly elongated morphology in the relaxed state (Figure S1f). Upon actuation, reinforcement of parallel‐oriented networks (increasing FA) and reorganization of perpendicular ones (reducing FA) balance out, leaving mean FA unchanged while the principal direction still shifts toward the loading axis. Finally, in vimentin knockout cells, actin organization under relaxed conditions was comparable to control, but actuation failed to induce increased anisotropy or stable alignment due to disruption of the actin network under loading (Figure 2i). Quantitative FA analysis was not performed for this condition because the disruption of the actin network under load precludes reliable fiber orientation measurement. Qualitative comparison of network architectures can be found in Figure S5.

Together, our mechanical and structural measurements show that distinct actin architectures are causally required for specific deformation‐mode responses. Selective removal of stress fibers abolishes both stretch‐induced stiffening and stretch‐induced actin alignment, while leaving cp‐softening intact; conversely, selective removal of branched cortical actin abolishes cp‐softening while preserving stretch‐stiffening and its associated alignment. This double dissociation, observed consistently at the mechanical and structural levels, indicates that cytoskeletal anisotropy is not merely correlated with, but structurally underlies, deformation mode‐specific mechanics. Consistent with this, actin shows pronounced alignment and anisotropy under stretch, tracking deformation‐induced stiffening, whereas vimentin aligns more weakly but is critical for stabilizing actomyosin and stress‐fiber organization under tensile load, complementing previous reports that vimentin also supports cortical actin [38]. The foundation established here is therefore mechano‐structural, linking cytoskeletal architecture directly to the mechanical response rather than to a molecular‐signaling mechanism, and motivates the constitutive modeling framework introduced next.

A Multi‐Network Constitutive Model Reproduces Actin‐Driven Stiffening and Vimentin‐Stabilized Anisotropy

The experiments above identify clear functional roles for actin stress fibers, the actin cortex, and vimentin, but they also raise mechanistic questions that observation alone cannot resolve: how each network contributes individually, and how their interactions produce the observed responses. To address these, we developed a continuum constitutive model that explicitly links cytoskeletal organization to deformation‐dependent cell mechanics. The model is grounded on the experimentally observed hierarchy of cytoskeletal contributions and serves as a quantitative testbed for the hypotheses, summarized in Table 1.

Within this framework, the cell is described as a continuum whose mechanical response emerges from the interaction between an isotropic background contribution, representing the cytosolic matrix and unresolved components, and anisotropic contributions associated with actin stress fibers, actin cortex, and vimentin intermediate filaments (Figure 3a). All cytoskeletal networks are assumed to experience the same macroscopic deformation, while contributing through distinct constitutive responses and evolving internal microstructural states. A central feature of the model is the explicit incorporation of cytoskeletal anisotropy and reorganization as state variables. For each network, the model tracks the degree of alignment, quantified by a FA parameter; the preferred orientation; and the effective load‐bearing fraction of polymerized filaments (fibrosity). Together, these control the magnitude and directionality of cytoskeletal reinforcement (Figure 3b) and provide a direct structural link between the organization quantified in Figure 2, and the mechanics measured in Figure 1. Motivated by the experimental observations, actin stress fibers and vimentin intermediate filaments are treated as tension‐dominated networks that progressively reinforce under stretch, whereas the actin cortex is modeled as an effective isotropic shell‐like contribution whose mechanical role is strongly reduced under compressive deformation. Coupling between actin and vimentin is incorporated to reflect the experimentally observed stabilizing role of vimentin on actin organization under load. A detailed description of the constitutive equations, internal variables, and evolution laws is provided in Methods and Supporting Information.

Model simulations were performed under loading protocols that mirror the experimental actuation conditions, including a rapid stretch ramp followed by sustained deformation (Figure 3c). Model parameters were drawn from the literature where available (Table S1). The only calibration to data from this study was the relative weighting of cytoskeletal contributions to basal stiffness, set to match the fold‐change data in Figure 1b (Figure 3d); all other parameters were fixed before any comparison with deformation‐dependent stiffness, cytoskeletal dynamics, or memory experiments. With this single calibration, the model captures the asymmetric mechanical response under active loading, predicting actin‐driven stiffening under traction and softening under compression, as well as the collapse of deformation‐dependent mechanics upon vimentin removal (Figure 3e; further analyses in Figure S6). Because the calibration used only basal‐stiffness data, this agreement with the deformation‐dependent response constitutes a parameter‐free prediction rather than a fit.

Beyond reproducing these observations, the model provides a predictive framework for how cytoskeletal perturbations affect cell mechanics across different mechanical environments. This is particularly relevant in glioblastoma progression, where cytoskeletal remodeling and mechanical adaptability are tightly linked to invasive and migratory phenotypes [39, 40]. The formulation naturally extends to time‐dependent remodeling processes, allowing the evolution of cytoskeletal organization to be explicitly coupled to mechanical loading history. Next, we use the model to predict the dynamics of cytoskeletal reorganization under sustained deformation.

Model‐Guided Analysis Predicts Cytoskeletal Alignment Dynamics and Responses to Biological Perturbations

Having shown that the constitutive framework quantitatively reproduces deformation‐dependent stiffness without recalibration, we next asked whether it can also predict the structural dynamics of cytoskeletal reorganization. This mirrors the experimental progression above, where mechanical responses were first identified at the level of cell stiffness and then traced to network‐specific anisotropy.

Model simulations were performed under stretch protocols matching the experimental conditions, consisting of a rapid loading ramp followed by sustained deformation (Figure 4a). Starting from different initial network configurations, the model predicts a progressive, time‐dependent evolution of both actin, and vimentin organization, characterized by changes in network orientation and anisotropy (Figure 4b). In agreement with experimental observations, actin networks exhibit a rapid increase in anisotropy and align efficiently with the principal stretch direction, whereas vimentin reorganizes more gradually, displaying slower alignment kinetics and more moderate anisotropy development. These distinct temporal responses emerge naturally from the network‐specific remodeling mechanisms encoded in the model and reflect the different structural roles of actin and vimentin identified experimentally. Quantitative comparison with experimental measurements confirms that the model accurately predicts the evolution of cytoskeletal orientation and anisotropy. Both vimentin (Figure 4c) and actin (Figure 4d) networks progressively rotate toward the direction of applied stretch, with alignment dynamics and anisotropy growth that depend on the initial network orientation and degree of organization. The model captures not only the steady‐state aligned configurations but also the transient pathways by which these states are reached, demonstrating that the observed cytoskeletal reorganization arises from continuous remodeling rather than instantaneous structural realignment. The predictive capability of the framework further extends to biologically perturbed conditions. In simulations mimicking vimentin depletion, the model predicts a destabilization of actin organization under sustained deformation, manifested as a progressive loss of actin fibrosity and reduced capacity to maintain aligned, load‐bearing structures (Figure 4e). This behavior is consistent with experimental observations showing that vimentin is required to preserve actin integrity under mechanical load, and highlights the role of vimentin as a stabilizing scaffold rather than a primary driver of force generation. Further parametric analyses on vimentin and actin reorganization are shown in Figure S7. Together, these results demonstrate that the proposed constitutive model not only reproduces deformation‐dependent cell mechanics, but also predicts the time‐dependent structural reorganization of the cytoskeleton across networks and perturbations. The model reveals that cytoskeletal remodeling is inherently transient, with characteristic timescales governing the evolution and relaxation of anisotropy and alignment under sustained loading. This transience implies that, after unloading, cells do not instantly reset: a fraction of the deformation‐induced organization persists, providing a structural basis for short‐term mechanical memory. In the following section, we build on this insight to investigate how residual cytoskeletal order gives rise to short‐term mechanical memory and to identify the characteristic relaxation processes that govern the transition between adaptive and memory‐driven mechanical responses.

Residual Cytoskeletal Order Enables Short‐Term Mechanical Memory

We next asked whether glioblastoma cells exhibit a microstructure‐encoded short‐term memory, and whether our constitutive model can predict such history effects without additional variations or calibration. This question is motivated by soft active materials, where history‐dependent responses are notoriously hard to interpret: inelastic (plastic) microstructural deformations and slow viscous relaxation can produce similar macroscopic signatures [41, 42]. In ultra‐soft magneto‐responsive elastomers, for instance, apparent memory effects can arise from field‐driven viscous mechanisms coupled to slow microstructural rearrangements, persisting over long times yet partially resetting after sufficient relaxation [43]. Such systems show that mechanical history can be encoded transiently in an evolving internal architecture, through an interplay of instantaneous deformation, rate‐dependent relaxation, and microstructural adaptation. To probe this in cells, we designed a two‐step actuation protocol (Figure 5a) consisting of a first stretching actuation (1 h), followed by a relaxation interval (3 min, 15 min, or 1 h), and a second actuation applying either traction or compression during 3 min or 1 h. The central hypothesis is that cytoskeletal remodeling is inherently transient, and that a fraction of the deformation‐induced anisotropy may persist after unloading, thereby biasing subsequent reorganization and enabling memory‐like behavior.

(g) Heatmaps of the memory ratio, defined as the final anisotropic fraction after the full protocol normalized by the anisotropic fraction obtained under the corresponding single‐actuation control (no‐memory) for parallel and perpendicular re‐actuation. A two‐tailed unpaired t‐test with Welch's correction was used for statistical analysis, with ns p > 0.05 (not shown), * p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01, *** p ≤0.001.

We first quantified, experimentally, the mechanically induced anisotropy remaining after unloading. After the relaxation period, cells retained a substantial residual anisotropy, which decayed progressively as relaxation time increased (Figure 5b). This result supports the idea that unloading does not instantly erase the structural state: instead, cytoskeletal organization relaxes on characteristic timescales, leaving a measurable “state variable” that can carry information forward into a subsequent loading event. We next tested whether this residual anisotropy translates into directional memory during reloading. To isolate the contribution of prior history, we compared conditions sharing an identical final stimulus (a 3 min actuation) but differing in their preconditioning: the direction of the first actuation (parallel or perpendicular) and the duration of the relaxation interval (3 min, 15 min, or 1 h). When relaxation was short, each preconditioning history produced a distinct final anisotropy, and these outcomes differed from cells subjected to the 3 min actuation with no prior history (Figure 5c,e). A second actuation parallel to the first re‐established a highly anisotropic state, consistent with a pre‐aligned network that can be reinforced with minimal additional remodeling. In contrast, perpendicular reloading drove cells toward markedly lower anisotropy, as the residual alignment competed with the new loading direction. As relaxation time increased, these history‐dependent differences progressively diminished, with the 1 h relaxation conditions converging toward the no‐history baseline, demonstrating that the memory is encoded in the transient cytoskeletal state and erases as that state relaxes. Representative orientation maps (Figures 5d and S8) illustrate these distinct outcomes.

These experiments served as a predictive test of the model. Using the parameters already fixed in the previous sections, with no recalibration, we simulated the two‐step protocol and compared the predictions against the measurements only afterward. The model reproduced the experimental anisotropy trends across loading direction and short relaxation times (Figures 5c,e and S9), showing that encoding cytoskeletal history through internal microstructural variables is sufficient to capture short‐term mechanical memory. Agreement held at short waits but deteriorated at the longest relaxation time (1 h). We interpret this not as a failure of the cytoskeletal framework, but as a sign of its scope: the model encodes history through cytoskeletal internal variables, whereas hour‐scale relaxation allows slower, cell‐level processes to take over, such as focal adhesion maturation and disassembly, changes in adhesion turnover and force transmission, and other reconfigurations that reshape the boundary conditions for the second actuation. These processes lie deliberately outside the present description. The loss of predictivity at hour‐scale relaxation therefore marks the boundary of cytoskeletal memory rather than a shortcoming of the model, and points toward future multi‐timescale extensions.

Finally, we asked whether memory persists when the second actuation is prolonged (1 h). In this case, the memory signature was largely diminished (Figures 5f, S8, and S10), and outcomes approached those obtained under a single actuation of equivalent duration and direction. Long reloading therefore allows the system to “overwrite” prior history and converge toward a state dominated by the current loading conditions. Consistent with this interpretation, the ratio map comparing the anisotropic fraction achieved with and without prior mechanical history (Figures 5g and S11) showed the largest memory amplification for short timescales (short second actuation), while the ratio approached unity when the second actuation was long, for both parallel and perpendicular configurations; that is, the response became indistinguishable from a one‐step loading. Together, these results delineate a short‐term memory window governed by transient cytoskeletal order: mechanical memory is strongest when the protocol interrogates the system before slow relaxation and broader cell‐level remodeling erase the state encoded by the first actuation. This window is bounded by the characteristic relaxation time of the cytoskeleton, on the scale of minutes to tens of minutes.

Discussion

Our results establish a direct link between cytoskeletal microstructural organization and cell‐scale mechanical behavior. Deformation‐induced cytoskeletal anisotropy is a key microstructural variable controlling both the magnitude and the sign of mechanical adaptation under load. Rather than behaving as a homogeneous material, the cytoskeleton reorganizes into architecture‐specific states whose mechanical roles depend on the imposed deformation: actomyosin stress fibers selectively drive stiffening under tension, whereas branched cortical actin governs softening under cp. This anisotropy persists transiently after unloading, leaving a residual structural state that relaxes over characteristic timescales and gives rise to a short‐term mechanical memory linking deformation history to subsequent responses. A multi‐network constitutive model that couples deformation, cytoskeletal anisotropy and remodeling dynamics unifies these findings. By quantitatively reproducing both mechanical responses and cytoskeletal dynamics across deformation modes and perturbations, it was instrumental in establishing transient anisotropy as the structural mechanism encoding this memory.

Some of these results sit in apparent tension with earlier work. The cortical softening we observe under in‐plane compression appears to contrast with the literature, but this likely reflects the loading geometry: the actin cortex has mostly been probed by AFM indentation, which loads it through out‐of‐plane bending and in‐plane stretching of the membrane–cortex [44, 45], whereas our approach induces the in‐plane compressive stresses previously shown to buckle cortical actin networks [35]. Our finding that vimentin does not set basal stiffness but stabilizes actin under load complements previous reports that vimentin stabilizes cells under compressive stress and promotes compression stiffening [46]. Microtubule depolymerization, by contrast, had no measurable effect on cell mechanics under basal or compressive conditions here. However, given their intimate crosstalk with actin and intermediate filaments, more subtle or indirect contributions cannot be excluded [26, 47], and their known resistance to compression and acetylation‐enhanced stability may operate on longer timescales [48, 49].

The present study focuses on short timescales, capturing transient cytoskeletal remodeling and short‐term mechanical memory. Deformation‐induced cytoskeletal alignment does not translate into stable cell polarization, as supplementary analyses reveal minimal reorganization of the nucleus–centrosome axis despite clear anisotropic deformation of the cell body (Figure S12). Thus, transient cytoskeletal memory can bias filament organization and global morphology but is insufficient, on these timescales, to establish a fully polarized migratory phenotype. Nuclear morphology, by contrast, was more sensitive to deformation. Sustained actuation aligned and deformed the nucleus along the principal stretch direction, with enhanced nuclear strain in vimentin‐deficient cells (Figure S13). This indicates that cytoskeletal anisotropy can be mechanically transmitted to the nucleus, in a manner dependent on intermediate filament integrity [32, 50, 51]. Given recent work linking nuclear deformation in vimentin knockout cells to altered migration and transcription [51], this coupling may provide a route by which short‐term cytoskeletal reorganization influences longer‐term regulatory processes. Moreover, the pronounced increase in actin stress fiber alignment upon stretching suggests that focal adhesion size and spatial distribution are likely remodeled on similar timescales. Slower processes, including focal adhesion maturation [52], nucleus‐cytoskeleton coupling [53], transcriptional regulation [54] and long‐term phenotypic adaptation [55, 56, 57], are not addressed here and may contribute to longer‐lived mechanical memory. Extending this approach to migration and motility assays and to 3D or confined environments would clarify how the transient mechanisms identified here shape persistent migration, invasion, and long‐time adaptation in vivo.

From a modeling perspective, the constitutive framework adopts an isostrain assumption at the macroscopic scale, whereby all cytoskeletal networks experience the same deformation gradient. At the same time, each network is endowed with its own internal variables and branch‐specific kinematics, including distinct internal deformation measures and remodeling laws. Thus, although the formulation operates within a continuum mixture description, the individual cytoskeletal contributions are not mechanically independent, as they are physically interconnected through cytolinker proteins such as plectin that bridge intermediate filaments, microtubules, and F‐actin [58]. These interactions are incorporated through explicit dependencies in the evolution equations governing anisotropy, phase fractions and internal reference configurations. In this sense, the framework captures inter‐network coupling at the level of remodeling kinetics and load‐bearing capacity while remaining computationally tractable at the cell scale. This cell‐scale description extends naturally to spatially resolved, finite‐element implementations [28, 30], which would represent heterogeneous distributions of networks, anisotropy and state variables within the cell, enabling explicit local decoupling between networks and the incorporation of focal adhesions and nucleus–cytoskeleton interactions. The framework could also be coupled to molecular clutch models [59, 60], providing a route to account for the transmission of substrate‐derived mechanical cues to the cytoskeleton and to explore how cytoskeletal memory interacts with adhesion dynamics.

Placed in a physiological context, the mechanisms identified here are particularly relevant to the dynamic environments glioblastoma cells face in vivo, where tumor growth, solid stress, edema, inflammation, and tissue heterogeneity generate evolving sequences of cp, relaxation and tensile loading, rather than static mechanical conditions [61, 62, 63]. Such deformation histories can transiently bias cytoskeletal organization through residual anisotropy, modulating subsequent mechanical responses and endowing cells with a short‐term, history‐dependent mechanical state [57, 64]. When the interval between mechanical events approaches cytoskeletal relaxation times, this residual anisotropy can bias force generation, polarization and reorganization during invasion, providing a structural mechanism by which cells may sustain migratory efficiency under fluctuating constraints. These short timescales (minutes to hours) are those of cells encountering repeated mechanical events while migrating through heterogeneous tissue, rather than of tissue remodeling, which unfolds over much longer times and is better described by transcriptional and epigenetic memory [11, 13, 65].

Our results open several avenues for future investigation. Whether this short‐term, structurally encoded memory couples with these longer‐term phenomena remains to be tested. Moreover, while mechanical adaptability and mechanical memory are sometimes presented as alternatives, we speculate that they may instead be complementary, with memory providing a directional bias inherited from recent deformation that shapes how the cell responds to the next event. Because these transient structural changes also modulate cell stiffness, they may influence migration in 3D environments, where vimentin‐mediated stabilization of actin would promote robustness not by raising stiffness per se but by preserving load‐bearing structures across successive deformations. Beyond migration, deformation‐history‐dependent states may modulate signaling pathways tied to actomyosin contractility, cortical organization [66] and mechanosensitive channels [67], extending the reach of short‐term memory to proliferation and mechanotransduction pathways implicated in tumor progression [56, 68]. In this sense, cytoskeletal anisotropy emerges not only as a mechanical descriptor but as a dynamic internal variable linking mechanical history to downstream cellular responses.

These findings complement recent work showing that the actin cortex encodes mechanical memory of past confinement events in migrating cells, with cortex remodeling timescales governing the persistence of memory [69]. Whereas that work identifies memory at the level of cell morphology and migration efficiency over hour‐long confinement events, the present study resolves memory at the level of cytoskeletal anisotropy on minute timescales, suggesting that structurally encoded mechanical memory operates across multiple scales of cellular organization. More broadly, our results are consistent with the emerging view that mechanical memory in cells spans a hierarchy of timescales [11, 65]: the short‐term, purely structural memory identified here at the cytoskeletal level, and longer‐term forms mediated by transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms [13]. While these findings provide a mechanistic framework for deformation‐history‐dependent cytoskeletal remodeling in glioblastoma cells, several limitations should be noted. Experiments were performed primarily in U251‐MG cells, and a vimentin knockout derivative cultured on 2D substrates; generalization to other GBM lines or in 3D and confined microenvironments remains to be established. Furthermore, as the vimentin knockout used here disrupts all intermediate filament networks [32], the effects attributed to vimentin perturbation reflect the loss of IFs rather than vimentin‐specific contributions. Moreover, measurements were obtained at a defined substrate stiffness, and given the well‐known dependence of cytoskeletal organization and force transmission on matrix rigidity and adhesion, the magnitude of the observed responses may vary across different mechanical environments.

From a translational and conceptual perspective, the combined experimental‐theoretical framework introduced here provides a platform to interrogate how cytoskeletal architecture, deformation mode, and mechanical history jointly regulate cell behavior. The constitutive model enables in silico testing of mechanisms and helps interpret complex, history‐dependent responses that are otherwise difficult to disentangle experimentally. More broadly, by positioning glioblastoma cells as active materials with microstructure‐dependent memory, this work establishes a framework for mechanically informed therapeutic strategies, including ways to disrupt mechanical adaptability by targeting the structural and dynamical rules governing cytoskeletal anisotropy.

Methods

Cell Culture

U251 MG cells (ECACC 09063001) were cultured growth medium containing minimum essential medium (MEM) with Glutamax (Gibco, 41090036), supplemented with 10% FBS (Eurobio Scientific, CVFSVF00‐01), 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), and 1% non‐essential amino‐acids (10 mM of each amino acid, Gibco, 11140050). Cells were cultured under standard conditions of 37 and 5% CO2. Cell cultures were passaged every 3‐4 days at 1:10 cell concentration.

U251 vimentin knockout cells were obtained as described in [32]. Briefly, U251 GBM cells were transfected with guide RNAs‐containing plasmids targeting vimentin exon 2. Transfection was done by electroporation in Ingenio Electroporation Solution (Mirus, MIR 50114) with a Nucleofector device (Lonza). Vimentin knockout was determined using western blot analysis, and complete disruption of vimentin networks was confirmed by immunofluorescence. Importantly, this knockout also results in the downregulation of other intermediate filament proteins and the disruption of the intermediate filament network in these cells [32].

Generation of a U251‐MG Vimentin–mCherry Knock‐In Cell Line (Vim‐mCherry KI)

Endogenous tagging of vimentin with mCherry was performed using CRISPR–Cas9–mediated homology‐directed repair. A single guide RNA targeting exon 10 (sgRNA: UCAGGAGCGCAAGAUAGAUU) was cloned into the pSpCas9(BB)‐2A‐GFP (PX458) vector (#48138, Addgene) using BbsI restriction sites, according to Ran et al. method [70]. A donor repair template was designed to introduce mCherry in frame with the endogenous coding sequence. This template contained a 600 bp 5' homology arm, a flexible Gly‐Ser linker, the mCherry coding sequence, and a 600 bp 3' homology arm. Homology arms and linker–mCherry were, respectively, amplified from genomic and plasmid DNA (Table S2). Fragments were assembled using Gibson assembly, cloned into the PCRBlunt II‐TOPO vector and amplified. The donor template was linearized by EcoRI digestion prior to delivery. U251‐MG cells were co‐transfected with the PX458‐sgRNA plasmid and linearized donor template using Nucleofector 2b Basic Kit for Glial Cells (Lonza) and the Nucleofector II (Amaxa), according to manufacturer's instructions (Prog: T20[71]). Cas9‐GFP‐positive cells were enriched by FACS 48 h after nucleofection. A subsequent round of FACS was performed to isolate mCherry‐positive cells. Correct insertion at the endogenous locus was confirmed by genomic sequencing, western blotting, and immunofluorescence analysis (Figure S14).

Western Blot of Vim‐mCherry KI

Cells were lysed in LDS sample buffer (Merck) supplemented with 100 mM DTT, heated at 95 for 5 min, and sonicated. Proteins were separated on 4% –12% Bis–Tris gels (Merck) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Merck). Membranes were blocked in 5% milk in TBS‐Tween or in EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (Bio‐Rad). They were incubated either with primary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, followed by HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, or directly with fluorescently labeled primary antibodies. Antibodies were diluted in 5% milk in TBS‐Tween or EveryBlot Blocking Buffer (Bio‐Rad). Signal detection was performed using enhanced chemiluminescence (Bio‐Rad Clarity) and imaged using a ChemiDoc MP system (Bio‐Rad). Quantification was performed using Fiji, with normalization to GAPDH. Western blots were quantified using Fiji/ImageJ. The fraction of tagged protein was calculated as the ratio of tagged Vimentin to total Vimentin (tagged and untagged). Statistical analyses were performed in Prism (Wilcoxon test for relative expression; Mann–Whitney test for fraction of tagged protein). Antibodies used are detailed in Table S3.

Drug Treatments

To study the effect of cytoskeletal disruption on cell mechanics in relaxed and actuated conditions, cultured cells were subjected to different drug treatments. Cells were incubated with 1 μM cytochalasin D (22144‐77‐0, Sigma‐Aldrich), 10 μM nocodazole (31430‐18‐9, Sigma‐Aldrich) + 1 μM Y‐27632 (331752‐47‐7, Sigma‐Aldrich), 10 μM Y‐27632, or 50 μM CK666 (442633‐00‐3, Sigma‐Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 and 5% CO2 prior to analysis.

Magneto‐Mechanical Actuation System

Mechanical actuation experiments were performed using the magneto‐mechanical stimulation platform NeoMag (60Nd S.L., Madrid), which is directly derived from the magnetically actuated system originally developed and validated in our previous work [33]. The device enables controlled, contactless application of mechanical deformation to adherent cells through externally generated magnetic fields, while remaining fully compatible with standard cell culture and microscopy workflows. Mechanical actuation is achieved by generating a controlled magnetic field that induces deformation of magneto‐active substrates coupled to the cellular microenvironment. The applied magnetic field is generated by permanent magnets whose relative position is precisely controlled, allowing for reproducible deformation modes. To enable high‐resolution imaging and seamless integration with inverted fluorescence microscopy, the magneto‐active substrates were adapted for this study. Specifically, cells were cultured on custom‐fabricated magneto‐active elastomeric substrates mounted in P35 plastic culture dishes with enlarged inner apertures, allowing optical access with high–numerical aperture objectives. These modifications preserve the mechanical actuation capabilities of the system while enabling simultaneous mechanical stimulation and fluorescence imaging. The effective transmission of substrate deformation to adherent cells was verified by direct observation of immediate cell elongation upon actuation. Cells were not subjected to any pre‐orientation prior to actuation; they naturally adopted a range of morphological orientations on the substrate. For experiments in which the same cell was measured before and after deformation (stiffness measurements), individual cells were selected based on the angle between their morphological long axis (determined from cell aspect ratio) and the fixed actuation direction: cells with a long‐axis angle below 22.5 were assigned to the parallel (traction) condition, and cells with an angle above 67.5 to the perpendicular (compression) condition. For population‐level cytoskeletal imaging and orientation analyses, randomly oriented cell populations were studied without pre‐selection, which accounts for the isotropic fiber orientation distributions observed in the relaxed state. This relaxed isotropy reflects genuinely low individual‐cell FA together with random cell orientations, rather than the cancellation of strongly aligned parallel and perpendicular subpopulations. The low absolute FA values in the relaxed state in Figure 2f provide direct evidence for this. Cell‐induced pre‐stress on the substrate is expected to be negligible relative to the imposed deformation, since cell‐generated strains in soft elastomeric substrates are typically on the order of 1% –5% [72, 73], compared to the 30% actuation amplitude applied by the device.

Dynamic Indentation Setup and Measurement Protocol

Indentation measurements were performed 48–72 h after seeding the U251 cells. All measurements were done with cells in complete medium supplemented with 10 mM HEPES and without phenolred to avoid changes in medium color that may affect the measurements. Indentation probes of 0.015–0.033 N/m stiffness and 3–3.5 μm spherical tip radius (P250172M, Optics11 Life) were used with a Chiaro nanoindentation system (Optics11 Life, Netherlands). Displacement‐controlled monotonous indentation was performed with a final indentation of 4 μm, corresponding to approximately ∼2/3 of the bead diameter, with an indentation rate of 1 μm/s. The raw data were analyzed using Piuma Viewer software. Young's modulus was obtained from the slope at the start of the indentation following the Hertzian model, assuming a Poisson ratio of 0.5.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells were fixed for 10 min at room temperature in cytoskeleton buffer (MES 10 mM, KCl 138 mM, MgCl2 3 mM, EGTA 2 mM, pH 6.8) supplemented with 10% sucrose, 0.1% Triton‐X100, 0.05% glutaraldehyde, and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), as described in [74]. Fixation was followed by incubation in 1 mg/ml NaBH4 in PBS solution for 10 min to reduce the aldehyde functions. Samples were then incubated with 0.1% Triton‐X100 for 10 min and blocked for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were washed 3 times with PBS between each step. Primary antibody incubation was performed in 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Secondary antibody, Hoetsch, and labeled phalloidin incubation was performed in 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Samples were washed three times with 2% BSA in PBS for 5 min each after each antibody incubation. Samples were mounted in Prolong Diamond (Invitrogen, P36961). Images were acquired with a Leica DM6B epifluorescent microscope equipped with a x63 1.4 NA oil objective and recorded on a CCD Leica DFC3000G camera with Leica Application Suite X Software or with a Nikon Ti Eclipse 2 with Yokogawa Spinning unit CSU W1, equipped with a Plan Apo x60 1.4 NA oil Nikon objective and recorded on a Prime BSI Express sCMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics, Tucson, AZ, USA) with Micromanager Software (purchased through Gataca systems).

Image Processing and Data Analysis

Most image processing and analysis were performed using the Fiji software [75]. Visualization of cytoskeletal orientation was performed using the OrientationJ plugin [76]. For cytoskeletal analysis, a mask of the cell contour and fluorescence images of cytoskeletal markers were used for single‐cell quantification of cytoskeleton structures using the CSKMorphometrics algorithm in MATLAB [77]. Additional processing, graph design, and statistical analysis were performed using custom Python codes. Cytoskeletal anisotropy was quantified using FA, which measures the degree of network alignment independently of its direction; the latter is tracked separately through the principal network orientation n. This separation makes FA more suitable than the orientation order parameter S, which conflates alignment degree and orientation relative to a fixed reference axis: a perfectly aligned network at 45 to the traction axis and a fully isotropic network both yield S = 0, making them indistinguishable. The importance of tracking these two descriptors independently is well established in the constitutive modeling of fiber‐reinforced biological tissues, where the degree of anisotropy and the preferred fiber direction are treated as distinct structural state variables [78, 79]. The variables FA and n play the analogous roles in the cytoskeletal framework presented here. FA also maps directly onto the FA state variable used in the constitutive model, ensuring a consistent link between the imaging measurements and the model predictions.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using custom Python codes, except for the validation of the Vim‐mCherry knock‐in line, for which statistical analyses were performed in Prism (Wilcoxon test for relative expression; Mann–Whitney test for fraction of tagged protein). Unless otherwise indicated, statistical comparisons were two‐tailed unpaired t‐tests with Welch's correction, or two‐way ANOVA for comparisons with multiple conditions. Sample sizes and the number of independent experiments for each assay are reported in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical significance is indicated as ns p≥0.05, * p≤0.05, ** p≤0.01, *** p≤0.001, **** p≤0.0001.

Constitutive Model

The mechanical behavior of glioblastoma cells was described using a continuum constitutive model explicitly grounded in cytoskeletal structure and remodeling. Motivated by the experimental evidence presented above, the cell was modeled as an incompressible continuum whose macroscopic mechanical response emerges from the combined contributions of multiple cytoskeletal networks and an isotropic background. This framework enables a direct link between cytoskeletal organization, deformation history, and cell‐scale mechanical response. The total Helmholtz free‐energy density Ψ was assumed to decompose additively into contributions associated with an isotropic background, the vimentin intermediate filament network, and the actin cytoskeleton, (1) Ψ=Ψiso(F)+Ψvim(F,nvim,FAvim)+Ψact(F,ζact), where F denotes the deformation gradient, and ζact are state variables associated with the actin network. The isotropic contribution Ψiso represents the effective mechanical response of the cytosolic matrix and other cellular components that are not explicitly modeled as filamentous networks and depends solely on the deformation gradient. The vimentin contribution Ψvim accounts for the anisotropic mechanical response of the vimentin intermediate filament network and depends on the deformation gradient, the preferred vimentin orientation nvim, and the FAvim describing the degree of network alignment.

The actin contribution is written as a weighted sum of three actin phases, (2) Ψact=ϕSFΨSF+ϕcΨc+ϕGΨG, where ϕSF, ϕc, and ϕG denote the fractions of actin incorporated into stress fibers, actin cortex, and monomeric (G‐)actin, respectively, and satisfy ϕSF+ϕc+ϕG=1. The stress‐fiber contribution is defined as (3) ΨSF=ΨSF(F,nSF,FASF,FSF), where nSF is the preferred stress‐fiber orientation, FASF is the stress‐fiber FA, and FSF is an internal variable representing the evolving elastic reference configuration of the stress‐fiber network. The actin cortex contribution is defined as (4) Ψc=Ψc(F,mbuck), where mbuck is a scalar buckling reduction factor that modulates the effective mechanical contribution of the cortex under compressive in‐plane deformation. The G‐actin contribution ΨG does not introduce an effective mechanical response.

The first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor is obtained from the free‐energy density through (5) P=∂Ψ∂F−pF−T, where p is a Lagrange multiplier enforcing incompressibility.

All constituents are assumed to experience the same macroscopic deformation gradient at the continuum scale, consistent with an isostrain assumption. Nevertheless, the networks are not mechanically independent, as inter‐network coupling is incorporated through their respective internal variables and evolution equations. The proposed framework describes the constitutive behavior of a representative volume of cytoplasm and cytoskeletal networks rather than the mechanics of the entire cell geometry. Consequently, it does not explicitly account for cell morphology, membrane curvature, protrusions, focal adhesion distributions, or nuclear mechanics. These geometric and boundary‐dependent effects are beyond the scope of the present constitutive description and would only enter through a subsequent cell‐scale mechanical balance problem. The model is instead calibrated against cell‐scale observables (nanoindentation stiffness and cell‐averaged cytoskeletal anisotropy) that represent effective quantities averaged over local geometric heterogeneities. Time‐dependent cytoskeletal remodeling is captured through evolution equations for selected internal variables. In the present model, the preferred orientations nvim and nSF, the fractional anisotropies FAvim and FASF, the stress‐fiber internal reference configuration FSF, the actin phase fractions ϕSF, ϕc, and ϕG, and the cortical buckling variable mbuck evolve in time according to deformation‐dependent flow rules. These evolution laws account for deformation‐driven reorientation, anisotropy development, actin turnover between phases, stress‐fiber remodeling, and cortical buckling and recovery. The specific functional forms of the free‐energy contributions, the evolution equations, and all model parameters are provided in the Supporting Information.
