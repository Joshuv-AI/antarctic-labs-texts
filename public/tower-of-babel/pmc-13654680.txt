Beyond the oscillometric cuff: the utility of point-of-care ultrasound–derived blood pressure in shock
Authors: Komatineni Himaja Ravikumar, Rajat Naik, Sarbari Swaika, Mohammed Arif Abdul Salam
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Accurate blood pressure measurement is fundamental to clinical decision-making in emergency medicine. In the emergency department (ED), systolic blood pressure frequently serves as a primary trigger for critical interventions, including fluid resuscitation, vasopressor initiation, and escalation of monitoring [1]. Despite its central importance, clinicians commonly encounter oscillometric failure, in which automated devices are unable to generate a reading in patients with profound shock, severe arrhythmias, severe obesity, or after return of spontaneous circulation [2]. In these high-acuity situations, physicians, particularly trainees, are often left with limited and unreliable alternatives for objective hemodynamic assessment.

Although ultrasound-based approaches to hemodynamic evaluation are well established, direct point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS)-based blood pressure measurement remains an emerging and sparsely studied field [3]. Earlier Doppler ultrasound studies evaluated arterial flow and blood pressure estimation primarily in specialized vascular and pediatric settings; however, bedside POCUS applications for quantitative blood pressure measurement in emergency and critical care environments have gained meaningful attention only more recently [4]. Clinically practical portable techniques emerged predominantly after 2017, with formal validation studies appearing after 2024 [3,5]. Although the available evidence is promising, it remains limited in scope, and larger prospective studies are required before this approach can be considered a validated standard of care.

When automated blood pressure measurements fail, the traditional fallback approach is manual auscultation or palpation. However, even modern stethoscopes may be ineffective in noisy ED environments [6]. POCUS serves as a visual extension of the physical examination by providing additional hemodynamic information that does not depend on auditory assessment and may therefore be particularly valuable when auscultation fails [7]. Modern resuscitation bays present substantial challenges for auditory-based assessment. High ambient noise generated by alarms, suction devices, mechanical ventilation, and continuous team communication interferes with the identification of Korotkoff sounds. Manual palpation of a peripheral pulse is similarly limited because it is subjective, operator-dependent, and susceptible to interobserver variability. Previous studies have suggested that palpation-based techniques may underestimate systolic blood pressure by clinically meaningful margins of 15–20 mmHg or more, particularly in the presence of peripheral vasoconstriction [8–10]. Nevertheless, it should be recognized that oscillometry, auscultation, and POCUS-augmented sphygmomanometry are all subject to distinct sources of error, and no single noninvasive technique is entirely reliable in critically ill patients.

The clinical value of POCUS-augmented sphygmomanometry lies in its potential role as a rapid hemodynamic bridge rather than a definitive monitoring solution. Although invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring remains the reference standard in critically ill patients, arterial line placement is time-consuming and may be delayed in unstable patients or high-volume public EDs [11]. During this interval, clinicians are often required to initiate or titrate therapy without objective confirmation of blood pressure. POCUS-derived systolic blood pressure may therefore provide timely bedside information with minimal interruption to ongoing resuscitation. However, clinicians should recognize that this technique has not yet been adequately validated across diverse shock phenotypes, and its accuracy under extreme hemodynamic conditions remains uncertain.

The proposed technique, ultrasound-augmented sphygmomanometry, replaces subjective palpation with direct visualization of arterial flow. A high-frequency linear transducer is positioned over the brachial or radial artery during manual cuff deflation, and the reappearance of pulsatile flow on color or power Doppler corresponds to systolic blood pressure [3,5].

In hypotensive states, particular attention should be paid to probe pressure because excessive compression of a collapsible artery may lead to an underestimation of systolic blood pressure [12]. To minimize this risk, we recommend a float-the-probe technique involving generous ultrasound gel application, light placement of the transducer on the skin without downward pressure, and confirmation that a circular arterial lumen is present on B-mode imaging before Doppler activation [12]. This method provides a clear visual endpoint based on the presence or absence of arterial flow, which may reduce interoperator variability compared with palpation, although further validation is required. Nevertheless, operator proficiency and familiarity with Doppler imaging remain essential prerequisites, and the technique should not be attempted by clinicians without adequate POCUS training [3].

Several important limitations of this approach should be acknowledged. First, Doppler-assisted ultrasound techniques are generally limited to estimating systolic blood pressure and do not reliably provide diastolic pressure or mean arterial pressure, both of which are essential for comprehensive hemodynamic management [13]. Second, the current evidence base derives predominantly from small observational studies and single-center validation studies; therefore, multicenter high-acuity trials are required to establish robust performance benchmarks. Third, the technique may be less reliable in patients with severe peripheral vasoconstriction, substantial arterial calcification, or upper limb vascular abnormalities. Fourth, Doppler-capable equipment and adequately trained operators may not be consistently available across all emergency settings. Collectively, these limitations reinforce that POCUS-augmented sphygmomanometry should be regarded as a complementary adjunct for selected clinical scenarios rather than a universal replacement for established monitoring methods [5,9,14].

Although individual reports have demonstrated the feasibility of POCUS in patients with absent peripheral pulses or unrecordable blood pressure, and other studies have examined POCUS for hemodynamic assessment, standardized guidance on Doppler-augmented blood pressure measurement as a rescue technique when oscillometric devices fail in the ED remains lacking. Unlike disease-specific reports or single-patient descriptions, this letter synthesizes the available validation data into a practical and low-cost framework that addresses environmental noise, technological limitations, and training variability inherent to high-volume emergency care. Nevertheless, we recognize that this synthesis is based on a limited body of evidence and that the proposed protocol requires prospective clinical validation.

From a resource stewardship perspective, ultrasound-augmented blood pressure measurement makes use of equipment already available in most EDs and avoids the immediate costs and procedural risks associated with invasive monitoring [3]. These practical advantages may be particularly meaningful in resource-limited settings. However, implementation of this technique requires investment in operator training, protocol standardization, and quality assurance measures to ensure reproducibility. Incorporating this approach into residency training programs may help standardize responses to “unmeasurable” blood pressure readings, although formal competency frameworks and simulation-based training programs will be necessary to ensure consistent performance [14,15].

Importantly, it remains unclear whether POCUS-derived systolic blood pressure measurements translate into improved clinical decision-making or patient outcomes in critically ill populations. Further prospective studies are required to evaluate not only measurement accuracy but also the impact of this approach on patient-centered outcomes. In conclusion, POCUS-augmented sphygmomanometry may serve as a useful adjunct for systolic blood pressure estimation when oscillometric devices fail. Although the technique offers practical advantages, including rapid bedside use and integration into existing ultrasound workflows, limitations such as restriction to systolic measurements, operator dependence, and limited validation must be carefully considered [16]. Further research is required to clarify its role within standard emergency and critical care practice.
