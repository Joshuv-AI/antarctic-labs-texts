Highly sensitive microphones based on large freestanding reduced graphene oxide membranes
Authors: Guanzhong Zhao, Yuebin Zheng, Chang Liu, Qin Zhou, Yuhua Ma, Zhaoyi Wan, Mingyan Dai, Haoru Zhu, Enze Tian, Kangwen Zhao, Xu Zhou, Hao Hong, Enge Wang, Kehai Liu, Kaihui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHighly sensitive acoustic sensing devices are essential for human‒machine interfaces and modern advanced artificial intelligence (AI) technology. Two-dimensional (2D) materials exhibiting atomically thin thickness and superior mechanical properties are, in principle, ideal membranes for ultimate acoustic sensing. In this work, we present an ultrasensitive microphone using large freestanding reduced graphene oxide (rGO) membranes. The membranes were suspended by a designed pressure-assisted double transfer strategy, resulting in a diameter-to-thickness ratio of ~ 106 (diameter of 8 cm, thin thickness of 80 nm). They exhibit a static pressure responsivity of ~ 500 μm/Pa, and a dynamic signal-to-noise ratio up to ~ 115 dB at 1 kHz. Notably, a rGO-based broadband microphone (100 Hz‒50 kHz) demonstrates a superior language recognition accuracy (90% vs. 70%) compared to that of commercial micro-electromechanical system (MEMS) microphones at a distance of 9 m. Our work provides a reliable route for fabricating large freestanding ultrathin membranes and will promote the development of advanced acoustic devices.Subject terms: Mechanical and structural properties and devices, Two-dimensional materials, Sensors

Introduction

Highly sensitive and sophisticated sensing capabilities are essential for developing advanced artificial intelligence (AI) systems1,2. For acoustic perception, highly sensitive acoustic devices can detect and analyse sound waves with exceptional precision, far surpassing the perception limits of humans3–7. The mechanical responsivity of an acoustic device refers to its ability to convert mechanical vibrations or pressure changes (sound waves) into digital signals (i.e., electrical signals)8. This responsivity is influenced by the mechanical properties of the material9,10 and is theoretically determined by the freestanding geometric parameters (including the diameter (D) and thickness (h))11,12. Increasing the geometric characteristic ratio (i.e., the diameter-to-thickness (D/h) ratio) would greatly improve the responsivity, accuracy and response bandwidth13,14. However, for conventional bulk materials (such as metals, polymers and silicon), the achievable freestanding geometric ratio is still limited because of the small mechanical modulus and low tensile fracture strength when the thickness is reduced to the nanometre scale, which greatly limits the development of high-performance acoustic devices15–17.

Two-dimensional (2D) materials are ideal candidates for highly sensitive acoustic detection because of their atomically-thin thickness and superior mechanical properties18–22. For example, at just one atom thick ( ~ 0.34 nm), a perfect graphene monolayer retains an extraordinary tensile fracture strength of 130 GPa, making it ~200 times stronger than steel by weight. Over the past decades, a few works have demonstrated that the performance of acoustic sensors can be further enhanced by increasing the D/h of graphene membranes8,23, including both the ultrasonic communication graphene microphone with up to 500 kHz response frequency17 and the capacitive graphene microphone exhibiting over twice higher responsivity than conventional silicon-based micro-electromechanical system (MEMS) microphones24. Nevertheless, to date, the reachable mechanical responsivity of 2D membranes is still only on the order of 1 μm/Pa (marginally superior to that of devices made from bulk materials). The main constraint is the limited freestanding area and corresponding geometric ratio. Although large-area graphene membranes for acoustic devices can be achieved via chemical vapour deposition or spin-coating from slurries25–27, the poor transfer method employing sacrificial polymer layers usually results in cracks or voids in ultrathin membranes28. The random local stresses induced during the suspension process also result in excessive wrinkling and folding29,30. Therefore, a reliable strategy for fabricating large freestanding 2D material membranes is highly required.

Here, we designed a pressure-assisted double transfer strategy for the fabrication of large freestanding reduced graphene oxide (rGO) membranes. Without the use of sacrificial polymer layers, the membranes can be released from the substrate with the assistance of water surface tension. In the following suspension process, external pressure modulation is employed to stretch the membranes and tune the internal stress. Centimetre-scale (largest of 8 cm) tensioned freestanding rGO membranes with a large D/h ratio ( ~ 106) have been reliably fabricated. The membranes demonstrate a high mechanical responsivity of 500 μm/Pa and a high dynamic signal-to-noise ratio (115 dB at 1 kHz) of microphones.

Results

Design of large freestanding membranes

We first revisit the key characteristics of a freestanding 2D membrane responding to acoustic vibration (Fig. 1a). The central deflection of the membrane (Z) under external pressure (P) quantifies its mechanical responsivity, which is defined as ∆Z/∆P. The responsivity depends primarily on the diameter (D), thickness (h) and internal stress (σ) of the membrane under the assumption of small deformation dZ (Fig. 1b). According to the formula for membrane deflection under external pressure, the responsivity can be written as dZdP~D216σh (Supplementary Note 1)31,32. The responsivity of the 2D membrane is proportional to the D2/h value and inversely proportional to σ. Therefore, increasing D, reducing h, or lowering σ are effective ways to improve the responsivity of 2D membranes (Fig. 1c). Specifically, increasing D by two orders of magnitude increases the responsivity of a membrane by four orders of magnitude (Fig. 1d). Reducing h from 1000 nm to 10 nm further enhances the responsivity by another two orders of magnitude, reaching 103 μm/Pa (Fig. 1e). In addition, reducing σ can also increase the responsivity (Fig. 1f). However, excessively low stress can inevitably lead to membrane wrinkling, whereas excessively high stress can lead to membrane rupture30,33. Therefore, enhancing the D/h ratio (specifically the D2/h value), and optimizing σ are the requisite ways to realize highly sensitive freestanding 2D membranes.

Careful optimization of the D/h ratio (specifically the D2/h value) and modulation of σ are indicated to be necessary to realize highly responsive freestanding 2D material membranes.

Fabrication of large freestanding membranes

In our experiment, a uniform graphene oxide (GO) film was first deposited onto a silicon wafer by spin-coating (Fig. 2a). The GO films were reduced by soaking the coated wafers in 55% concentrated hydroiodic acid (HI) at 50 °C for 10 min (details in the Method section). The reduction reaction removed a significant number of oxygen-containing surface groups, forming rGO, thus increasing the interlayer adhesion between nanosheets and reducing the interfacial energy between the film and substrate. The duration time was optimized to achieve effective conversion of GO to rGO while maintaining structural integrity, ensuring sufficient removal of oxygen-containing groups while avoiding defect generation25,34,35. The reduction result was evidenced by an increase in the water contact angle (Supplementary Fig. 1) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 2). As a result, the rGO film could be easily peeled off from the substrate onto a water surface.

The subsequent fabrication involved a designed pressure-assisted double transfer process (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3). The floating membrane on the water surface was first transferred onto a metallic ring, where it remained loose and wrinkled. Then, external pressure was gradually applied to the membrane in a chamber, displacing it from the equilibrium position and stretching it. By fine-tuning the pressure difference to control the release gap, the σ in membrane could be effectively controlled at this stage (Supplementary Note 3). The stretched membrane was then adhered to a smaller metallic ring, producing a large and prestressed freestanding rGO membrane. The σ of the membranes could be extracted from the following pressure test, with stress levels in the range of tens of MPa (as indicated in Supplementary Note 1; the slope of the pressure‒displacement curve near zero is inversely proportional to the stress σ). A series of large-scale freestanding rGO membranes with different D and h values were then fabricated (as shown in Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 5) by using this method; these membranes presented large D/h ratios of ~106 (D2/h values of 104 m), with the largest freestanding D value of ~8 cm. Notably, owing to their thin h, the freestanding rGO membranes exhibited good optical transparency in the visible range (as shown in Fig. 2b, c).

The membrane properties directly influence the total fabrication yield of large membranes. Thin rGO membranes with sufficiently higher tensile strength could sustain larger suspension without rupture. In our experiments, the membranes with a flake size of ~1.4 μm presented the highest tensile fracture strength (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 7). As a result, the use of the optimal size led to an overall higher fabrication success rate of large membranes (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 1). Specifically, membranes with a D2/h value of 8×104 m (D/h ratio of 106) could have a relatively high yield of ~50% by using the optimal size, which is comparable with other work (Supplementary Table 2). In contrast, both smaller and larger flakes reduced the success rate. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the nanosheets with the optimal flake size exhibited high-quality stacking (with an interlayer spacing of ~0.38 nm, very close to the interlayer spacing of multilayer graphene of ~0.34 nm) (Fig. 2e). The closely packed structures reduced possible voids and defects, leading to increased tensile strength and thus increased fabrication yield for the suspension.

What’s more, the coated GO film can be immersed in a cobalt chloride solution (0.1 mol/L) before reduction reaction, allowing cobalt ions (Co2+) to crosslink with the GO nanosheets to further enhance the strength of the films. The addition of Co2+ ions prior to reduction preserves edge coordination bonds after removal of basal-plane oxygens, reduces wrinkling, enhances mechanical robustness, and improves the peel-off success of large freestanding rGO membranes36,37. The optimized tensile strength of the Co2+-doped membrane can be increased by ~20% (exceeding 1.4 GPa, shown in Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 3). Therefore, we choose to use the Co2+-doped membranes with ~1.4 μm flake size in the following experiments.

High mechanical responsivity of large freestanding membranes

To evaluate the mechanical responsivity of the freestanding Co2+-doped rGO membranes, a static differential pressure sensor was developed (Fig. 3a). An external differential pressure was applied to the membrane in a chamber, and its deflection was measured using a laser ranger. The mechanical responsivity is not restricted by the specifics of the electric readout circuit and thus reflects the ultimate sensing capability. With optimized σ, the responsivity of the membrane (D/h ratio of ~106) increased under the same differential pressure, reaching 500 μm/Pa (Fig. 3b). In our experiment, the rGO device could accurately respond to the periodic pressure variations of ~0.1 Pa, whereas the commercial pressure sensor (TSI 5825) exhibited limited resolution and failed to detect such weak pressure differences (Fig. 3c). The deflection was monitored using a laser displacement sensor (CL-P015), which has a resolution of ~3 nm, thus with a theoretical sensitivity (minimum detectable pressure variation) as low as ~6×10-6 Pa (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 8).

The rGO devices exhibit a monotonic, nonlinear response with increasing pressure. The typical linear operating range was ~0.6 Pa (Supplementary Fig. 8), which could be further extended via force balance control (Supplementary Fig. 9). In this method, a voltage-controlled electrostatic force generated between electrode plates and the membrane counteracts the external pressure load, keeping the membrane in a balanced position38,39. In experiments, the linear operating range of the pressure sensor could be extended to ~4 Pa by applying a voltage of ~600 V between the membrane and electrode. The experimental and simulation results demonstrated a quadratic relationship between the external pressure and applied voltage (Supplementary Fig. 9).

In addition to static pressure detection, the rGO membrane demonstrates potential superiority in detecting low-frequency dynamic pressure variations. The fundamental resonance frequency of the membrane in air ( ~ 100‒200 Hz) remains far higher than the frequencies in the low-frequency dynamic pressure (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 10 and Note 4). To evaluate the feasibility, weak low-frequency acoustic waves were generated using a custom-built generator composed of a motor, an eccentric shaft, a telescopic rod, and an acrylic disc. Rotation of the reciprocating motor drives the eccentric shaft and rod, imparting a controlled periodic reciprocating motion to the acrylic disc (details in the Method section). The deflection performances of freestanding rGO membranes with D/h ratio of ~106 (specifically with D2/h values of 2×104 m‒4×104 m) were analysed. The rGO device could remarkably represent clear and measurable responses even at frequencies as low as 3 Hz (Fig. 3e).

High signal-to-noise ratios of large freestanding membranes

Large freestanding rGO membranes are also ideal for highly sensitive audio-frequency range electrostatic capacitive microphones. One obvious advantage is the significant increase in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). According to the Johnson‒Nyquist thermal noise, the noise would decrease with increasing D. Hence a larger membrane will result in a better SNR (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Note 2). The overall SNR is a combination of the membrane mechanical responsivity and electrical responsivity restricted by the readout circuit and amplification.

The freestanding rGO microphone demonstrated great accuracy at long distances.

Here, a prototype microphone incorporating a freestanding rGO membrane was fabricated, with the basic capacitive structure including the Co2+-doped rGO membrane (with a D of ~8 cm and an h of ~100 nm) and two electrodes (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Note 3)17. A bias voltage applied to the rGO membrane charges the electrodes and forms a variable capacitor. Incoming acoustic waves pass through the electrodes and drive the membrane vibration. This changes the capacitance between the electrodes and the membrane, induces charge redistribution. It generates an electrical current signal corresponding to the acoustic waves, which is read out by a current detection circuit connected to the electrode. Standard acoustic measurements were conducted using a Bruel & Kjaer (B&K) acoustic measurement system in a semi-anechoic room (details in the Methods). The rGO microphone exhibited a flat response from 100 Hz to 50 kHz, with variations within 10 dB, exceeding the human audible range (Fig. 4d). The rGO microphone featured an acoustic responsivity of −7.6 dBV (at 1 kHz), which was significantly greater than that of B&K type 4190 (Ref. mic 1#) and 4939 (Ref. mic 2#) microphones with responsivities of −26.0 dBV and −48.0 dBV, respectively, with the same measurement setup. Furthermore, the SNR of the rGO microphone reached ~115 dB at 1 kHz, exceeding the highest level among other state-of-the-art commercial microphones (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Table 5).

Owing to the high SNR, the rGO microphone exhibited excellent far-field sound recognition performance. For long-distance detection scenarios, speech recordings were played at various test distances. The output signals from the rGO microphone and a reference MEMS microphone were simultaneously recorded and compared (details in the Methods). The rGO microphone maintained an accuracy of over 90% at a distance of 9 m and over 60% at a distance of 15 m, demonstrating superior recognition accuracy compared to commercial MEMS microphones (Fig. 4f). The higher speech recognition accuracy demonstrates strong potential for future automatic speech recognition systems for human‒machine interactions.

Discussion

Compared with other 2D material acoustic sensors, the rGO devices have exhibited excellent performance due to its large D/h ratio and high responsivity (Supplementary Table 4 and 5). The rGO membranes have lower Young’s modulus, and brings higher compliance and larger deflection than graphene. What’s more, the well-designed fabrication process and precise parameter control also demonstrate reliable reproducibility, performance uniformity, and the long-term stability (Supplementary Note 3). On the other hand, although the solution-based method is suited for scalable preparation, the fabrication process becomes more complex due to the combination of pressure-assisted transfer steps. The larger device size also limits the overall integration and compactness.

In summary, our large freestanding rGO membranes demonstrate some of the highest intrinsic mechanical responsivity and SNR among 2D pressure sensors and commercial microphones reported in the literature (Fig. 3f and Fig. 4e). With a further increase in the D/h ratio and optimized internal stress, the mechanical responsivity of the freestanding rGO membrane could reach a high level of 500 μm/Pa. The application of high-responsivity membranes can be directly extended to diverse scenarios in both daily life and industrial applications in the emerging AI-empowered era, including in high-end audio equipment, intelligent communication interfaces, and early warning systems.

Methods

Sample preparation

Fabrication of the freestanding rGO membranes involved a designed pressure-assisted double transfer process following the reduction of graphene oxide (GO). Initially, a 2 mg/L GO (flake size of ~1.4 μm) dispersion was prepared by diluting a 10 mg/g GO slurry (Hangzhou Gaoxi Technology) in a methanol-water solvent mixture (volume ratio 4:1). This dispersion was spin-coated on an air plasma-treated silicon wafer to achieve the desired film thickness. The coated wafer was then immersed in a 0.1 mol/L cobalt chloride (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology) solution for 30 minutes (this step was omitted for samples without metal ion doping used for comparing fracture strength), allowing cobalt ions to crosslink with the GO nanosheets. Subsequently, the wafer was soaked in 55% concentrated hydroiodic acid (HI, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology) at 50 °C for 10 minutes, reducing the GO to rGO film on the silicon substrate. This duration time was optimized to achieve effective conversion of GO to rGO while maintaining structural integrity, ensuring sufficient removal of oxygen-containing groups while avoiding defect generation. Excessively long reduction times can lead to excessive penetration of iodine species into the interlayer galleries, which introduces defects and degrades the mechanical strength. The rGO film was released onto a water surface, with ~0.01 wt% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology) added to the water to reduce surface tension. A smooth 304 stainless steel ring was used to gently lift the rGO film from the water. After drying, the rGO film adhered to the steel ring, forming a relaxed, freestanding rGO membrane. In the second stage, a dry transfer method was employed to stretch and secure the rGO membrane. The relaxed membrane was mounted onto a custom-built gas chamber, where gradual evacuation of the chamber at a controlled rate stretched the rGO taut. A smaller, stainless-steel ring was carefully positioned beneath the stretched membrane. Upon contact, the membrane adhered tightly to the smaller ring under controlled differential pressure, resulting in the formation of a freestanding rGO membrane.

Sample characterization

Optical images were taken with a Canon EOS R7 Mirrorless Camera. The cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy samples were prepared using the focused ion beam technique (Thermo Fisher Helios 5UX). Transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-F200) was conducted to characterize the structure. AFM measurements were performed using Asylum Research Cypher in ambient atmosphere. The transmittance measurements were conducted using a UV-VIS-NIR Spectrophotometer (Hitachi UH4150). The elemental composition and valence state analysis of the samples were performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher ESCALAB XI + ) and excited by monochromatic Al Kα radiation. The displacement measurement of the suspended samples was conducted using two laser displacement sensors (Keyence CL-3000 and LK-G5000). The contact angle measurement of the samples was conducted using contact angle meter (Sindin SDC-200SE). The measurement of GO flake size was conducted using field emission scanning electron microscopy (Thermo Fisher Verios 5UC) and analysed with ImageJ software.

Mechanical properties measurement

The mechanical properties of the freestanding rGO were evaluated using the bulging test. The freestanding rGO membrane was securely mounted on a custom-built chamber, into which high-purity air was introduced via a flow controller (Supplementary Fig. 21a). A micro-differential pressure gauge was employed to measure the pressure difference across the membrane. As the chamber was pressurized, the freestanding rGO membrane deformed outward, forming a bulge. The deformation was quantified using a confocal laser displacement sensor, confirming the pressure‒displacement curve. The initial prestress and Young’s modulus of the freestanding rGO membrane could be extracted by fitting the experimental data with MATLAB (Supplementary Eq. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 8a). Additionally, the critical pressure and corresponding displacement at the rupture point were recorded as the pressure was gradually increased until the membrane failed. Subsequently, finite element simulations were performed to determine the fracture strength of the freestanding rGO membrane (Supplementary Note 4).

Infrasound measurement

The custom infrasound generator is assembled from a 12 V DC motor, an eccentric shaft, a telescopic rod, and an acrylic disc with diameter of 10 cm (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Rotation of the reciprocating motor drives the eccentric shaft and rod, imparting a controlled periodic reciprocating motion to the acrylic disc. The relationship between the motor’s supply voltage and the oscillation frequency was calibrated using a confocal laser displacement sensor (Keyence CL-3000). The displacement of the acrylic disc during reciprocating motion was recorded, yielding a time-displacement curve for oscillation frequency extraction. By repeating the measurements, the calibration was established between supply voltage and reciprocation frequency (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c).

Sound wave measurement

The frequency response measurement was performed in a semi-anechoic room within the free-field condition, with B&K pulse system conducted outside the room (Supplementary Fig. 12a). A sine wave sweeping signal, generated by signal generator module (B&K 3161) and transmitted to a loudspeaker. During testing, the reference microphone and the freestanding rGO microphone were positioned side by side on the loudspeaker’s axis to ensure that both received identical acoustic signals. The sound pressure detected by the reference microphone and the freestanding rGO microphone was recorded using multi-channel input module (B&K 3052), and the input-output signals were analysed using B&K pulse system. The frequency scan was performed in two segments: the first segment covered the range of 100 Hz to 20 kHz with a commercial loudspeaker (Yamaha HS8), while the second segment spanned from 10 kHz to 50 kHz with an ultrasound speaker (Avisoft-UltraSoundGate 116Hme). The microphones used for measurement and comparison in the experiment include: B&K 4190 (Ref. mic 1#), B&K 4939 (Ref. mic 2#), Lewitt LCT540 (Ref. mic 3#, data source: https://www.lewitt-audio.com/microphones/lct-recording/lct-540s), Sony C-100 (Ref. mic 4#) and Infineon IM73A135 (Ref. MEMS mic)40.

Far-field sound recognition accuracy measurement

Speech recordings were played with a laptop (Asus Tianxuan Air 2024 14-inch) at various test distances to evaluate far-field sound recognition (Supplementary Fig. 12b). The output signals from the freestanding rGO microphone and a reference MEMS microphone were connected to a sound card (Topping E2x2 OTG) and simultaneously recorded using the software (Topping Professional Control Center) on the second laptop (ThinkPad T480). All recordings were normalized and then imported into a speech-to-text software (CapCut), and the recognition accuracy was then compared. The recognition algorithm of CapCut might be found in technical papers released by the company’s research team (https://bytedancespeech.github.io/seedasr_tech_report/).
