Clinical outcomes and molecular features of CEBPA-mutated MPAL: a multi-center case series
Authors: Yirong Wu, Weijie Luo, Na An, Xiaohan Zhang, Jianyun Li, Yixuan Cao, Yunxin Zeng, Peng Ke, Lu Liu, Linlin Yang, Changru Luo, Shiyu Chen, Sitian Yang, Haoyu Peng, Xiaoqing Li, Jingchao Fan, Rujiao Dong, Bingbing Wen, Xin Du, Lixin Wang, Guangyang Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL) is rare and aggressive, with no standard therapy. CEBPA mutations define favorable-risk AML, but their significance in MPAL is unknown. We reviewed eight CEBPA-mutated MPAL patients from five Chinese centers. Four received allo-HSCT in first complete remission and all remained alive and relapse-free (median follow-up of 24.0 months). Among four non-transplanted patients, three died (one refractory, two relapsed). Molecularly, four had biallelic inactivation and six had bZIP mutations. The high mortality among non-transplanted patients may suggest an aggressive nature of CEBPA-mutated MPAL, even in those with bZIP mutations, unlike its favorable-risk status in AML. Given the small sample and biases, no conclusions regarding optimal post-remission therapy can be drawn, though intensive strategies including transplantation may be considered based on this aggressiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40164-026-00823-y.

To the Editor:

Mixed phenotype acute leukemia (MPAL) is a rare, aggressive malignancy with poor outcomes and no standardized therapy [1]. CEBPA mutations define a favorable-risk subgroup in AML [2, 3], but their prognostic significance and optimal post-remission strategy in MPAL remain undefined. Small studies have identified CEBPA mutations in MPAL [4, 5], yet their clinical implications are unclear. Here we report a multi-center case series of eight patients with CEBPA-mutated MPAL, describing their outcomes and molecular characteristics.

We retrospectively reviewed eight consecutive patients with CEBPA-mutated MPAL diagnosed per WHO 2022 [6] or ICC criteria [7] at five Chinese centers (2020–2025). All consecutive patients diagnosed with MPAL at the five participating centers during the study period were screened. Diagnosis integrated morphology, flow cytometry, cytogenetics and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Bi-phenotypic MPAL was defined as blasts co-expressing myeloid and lymphoid markers; bi-lineal as two distinct blast populations. Mutations were classified by type, zygosity (biallelic: two distinct mutations in trans, or single VAF > 70% suggesting CN-LOH), and bZIP involvement. Minimal residual disease (MRD) was assessed by flow cytometry (sensitivity 10⁻⁴; negativity < 0.01%). The study received institutional approval and written informed consent.

Clinical, molecular, and outcome data are summarized in Table 1; Fig. 1. Of 45 MPAL patients diagnosed across the five centers, eight harbored CEBPA mutations. The median age was 36 years (range 15–49). Four patients had B/myeloid and four had T/myeloid immunophenotypes (Table S1). Of the eight patients (Figure S1), four had biallelic CEBPA inactivation: two with classical double mutations (P1, P2), and two with a single high-VAF mutation suggestive of possible CN-LOH (P6, P8); the remaining four carried monoallelic mutations. bZIP domain mutations were present in six patients. The most common co-mutations were SETD2 (n = 3), FLT3-ITD (n = 2), WT1 (n = 2), and NPM1 (n = 1). Treatment strategies varied: four patients received allo-HSCT in first complete remission (CR), while the other four did not (reasons: patient refusal [n = 2], physician decision [n = 1], and primary refractory disease [n = 1]). CR was achieved in seven patients (87.5%). Among the four transplanted patients, all were alive and in continuous CR (CCR) at last follow-up (median 24.0 months; range 17.7–72.9), and none relapsed. Among the four non-transplanted patients, three experienced refractory disease or relapse, and three died. One non-transplanted patient (P5) had primary refractory disease and was never eligible for transplant in CR1. The remaining three non-transplanted patients achieved CR; of these, two relapsed and died, while one (P6) remains alive and in CCR at 22.5 months. Table S2 summarizes the descriptive outcomes of the two groups.

This case series provides several insights. First, CEBPA mutations were equally distributed between B/myeloid and T/myeloid subtypes, contrasting with earlier reports of T/myeloid enrichment [5]. Second, bZIP mutations were frequent, suggesting the favorable AML signature may also occur in this MPAL. Third, allo-HSCT in first CR was associated with no relapses and 100% survival, versus 75% relapse/refractory and 75% mortality with chemotherapy alone. These observations may suggest the disease aggressive nature, which may warrant intensive strategies, including allo-HSCT. A EBMT study of nearly 200 MPAL patients [8] demonstrated improved outcomes with allo-HSCT overall; our data provide molecularly detailed evidence for this specific subgroup.

Limitations include small sample size (n = 8), retrospective design, and treatment heterogeneity. Outcome differences should not be interpreted causally, as transplant recipients represent a selected population that survived to transplantation. One non-transplanted patient (P6) remains in CCR at 22.5 months, indicating transplantation is not the sole path to remission. These limitations underscore the descriptive nature of our findings; causal benefit of transplantation cannot be established.

Despite these limitations, our findings have clinical implications. Although bZIP-mutated AML is ELN-classified as favorable-risk [2], our results suggest this should not be extrapolated to MPAL. Non-transplanted patients experienced high relapse and death, may suggesting an aggressive nature of this disease in the MPAL context, supporting the consideration of intensive strategies including transplantation, though this suggestion is based on disease biology rather than on comparative data from this series.

In conclusion, the high mortality among non-transplanted patients may suggest an aggressive nature of CEBPA-mutated MPAL, even in bZIP, unlike its favorable-risk status in AML. Given the small sample and biases, no conclusions regarding optimal therapy can be drawn, though intensive strategies including transplantation may be considered based on this aggressiveness.
