Retinal ALKBH5 inhibition induces myopia protection through selective regulation of ERK1/2 signaling
Authors: Jiru Zhu, Yutong Xia, Xiang Li, Yingying Wen, Xuhong Zhang, Le Jin, Liyue Zhang, Ziyu Wang, Yanqing Li, Xin Yu, Zhitong Chen, Jiayun Ge, Ye Shen, Jianping Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyopia has reached epidemic proportions globally with limited therapeutic targets. This study elucidated the pivotal role of retinal epitranscriptomic regulation in myopia pathogenesis, aiming to identify novel therapeutic targets for myopia control.MethodsThe study enrolled 38 myopic patients scheduled for refractive surgery to analyze peripheral blood N6-methyladenosine (m6A) levels. In parallel, we established a murine form-deprivation myopia (FDM) model to examine retinal m6A dynamics and Alkylation repair homolog protein 5 (ALKBH5) expression. Pharmacological interventions using the ALKBH5 inhibitor DDO-2728 and Extracellular signal-related kinases 1 and 2 (ERK1/2) activator Ro67-7476 were administered intravitreally. To genetically validate the role of ALKBH5, ALKBH5-knockout (ALKBH5-KO) mice were subjected to the FDM model. Comprehensive assessments included refractive measurements, retinal structure analysis, and molecular profiling of inflammatory and extracellular matrix (ECM) markers. Statistical analyses employed Pearson’s correlation for clinical data and ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc tests for experimental comparisons.ResultsClinical data revealed significant inverse correlations between blood m6A levels and myopia severity (refraction: r = 0.435, p < 0.01; axial length: r = -0.338, p < 0.05). FDM retinas exhibited progressive m6A reduction and specific upregulation of ALKBH5 in the ganglion cell layer (GCL), with no changes observed in other m6A regulators. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that intravitreal injection of the selective ALKBH5 inhibitor DDO-2728 (2.5 mM) effectively attenuated myopia progression, mitigated retinal thinning, suppressed inflammatory mediators, and modulated early ECM dysregulation through specific suppression of ERK1/2 hyperphosphorylation. Crucially, genetic ablation of ALKBH5 recapitulated the protective phenotype of the inhibitor, conferring significant resistance to myopia development and associated retinal pathology. Importantly, co-administration of the ERK1/2 activator Ro 67-7476 completely abolished DDO-2728’s protective effects.ConclusionsPeripheral blood m6A reduction correlates with human myopia severity. Retinal ALKBH5 upregulation drives myopia progression through ERK1/2 hyperactivation. Both pharmacological inhibition and genetic deletion of ALKBH5 effectively protect against myopia, attenuate inflammatory responses, and mitigate ECM dysregulation, mechanistically linked to the suppression of ERK1/2 signaling. These findings collectively identify ALKBH5 as a novel therapeutic target and DDO-2728 as a promising strategy for myopia control.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07332-2.Keywords: Myopia, M6A, ALKBH5, DDO-2728, ERK1/2 signaling

Myopia has emerged as a global epidemic, affecting up to 90% of young adults in East Asia, with projections suggesting it will impact half the world’s population by 2050 [1, 2]. Beyond refractive error, high myopia significantly increases the risk of blinding complications including retinal detachment and macular degeneration [3]. Current interventions like low-dose atropine [4] and orthokeratology [5, 6], though partially effective, are largely empirical approaches with limited foundation in the mechanistic understanding of myopia pathogenesis. While optical interventions like orthokeratology lenses and specialized spectacles can effectively slow myopia progression, their efficacy is highly dependent on consistent patient adherence, which is particularly challenging to maintain in children and adolescents over the long term. Furthermore, these approaches primarily manage the visual consequences of myopia without directly targeting the underlying biological drivers of ocular elongation. Pharmacological agents like low-dose atropine have emerged as a promising alternative; however, their mechanisms of action are not fully understood, and side effects such as photophobia and blurred vision can affect tolerability. Therefore, there remains a pressing and unmet clinical need for novel pharmacotherapies that are highly effective, well-tolerated, and derived from a clear understanding of myopia’s molecular pathogenesis.

The retina has long been hypothesized as the initiator of myopiagenic signals through light-dependent modulation of neuromodulators like dopamine and glucagon [7]. However, the molecular mechanisms translating visual input into growth regulation remain incompletely understood. Furthermore, recent advances highlight epigenetic regulation [8, 9] as a key interface between environmental stimuli and gene expression changes in myopia. Among these mechanisms, m6A RNA modification, the most prevalent internal mRNA modification in eukaryotes [10–12] - represents a particularly promising yet unexplored pathway. M6A dynamically regulates mRNA stability, splicing, and translation efficiency through writer (METTL3/14), eraser (FTO/ALKBH5), and reader (YTHDF) proteins [11]. Notably, m6A-mediated regulation of scleral remodeling has been reported in myopia [13], whereas independent work identified the role of the tRF-22-METTL3-Axin1/Arid1b axis for ameliorating choroidal vasculopathy and attenuating myopia progression in vivo [14]. Despite these clues, the retinal m6A landscape in myopia remains completely uncharted, which represents a critical knowledge gap given the retina’s role as the primary phototransducer and signal generator.

ALKBH5, an α-ketoglutarate-dependent m6A demethylase, exhibits three characteristics that make it uniquely compelling for myopia research: neural specificity through its high expression in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) [15], environmental responsiveness as its activity is modulated by hypoxia and oxidative stress [16, 17], and pathway regulation via control of ECM organization and growth factor signaling [18–20]. Although ALKBH5 has been characterized in retinal diseases like diabetic retinopathy (regulating microglia polarization [21] and ferroptosis [22]) and age-related macular degeneration (modulating retinal pigment epithelium anomalies and choroidal neovascularization [17]), its function in refractive development remains unknown.

In the present study, we investigated whether retinal m6A methylation modifications are involved in myopia pathogenesis, with the aim of identifying novel therapeutic targets for myopia control. Herein, we reveal that peripheral blood m6A levels inversely correlate with myopia severity in humans, while murine FDM model exhibits retinal m6A reduction and ALKBH5 upregulation. Mechanistically, both pharmacological inhibition (via DDO-2728) and genetic ablation of ALKBH5 protect against myopia progression, attenuate inflammatory responses, and mitigate early-stage ECM dysregulation. The protective effects of ALKBH5 inhibition are mediated through selective modulation of ERK1/2 signaling, as ERK1/2 activation (via Ro67-7476) largely abolishes the benefits of DDO-2728. The concordant protective phenotype observed in ALKBH5-knockout mice further validates ALKBH5 as a central regulator in myopia pathogenesis. Guided by this mechanism, we demonstrate that DDO-2728—a specific ALKBH5 inhibitor—effectively suppresses ocular elongation, positioning it as a prospective therapeutic strategy for myopia control.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 38 patients (29 women and 9 men; mean age 25.289 ± 0.516 years) who were undergoing implantable collamer lens (ICL) implantation at the Eye Center of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine were enrolled. Preoperative peripheral blood samples and comprehensive ocular parameters (including refractive error, axial length, etc.) were collected from all participants. Participants with systemic comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) or recent medication use were excluded. All peripheral blood samples (800–1000 µL/sample) used in this study were residual waste specimens from routine complete blood count (CBC) testing. The collection process imposed no additional discomfort or risks to patients, adhering to the ethical principles for secondary use of biological specimens as stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2023 − 0490), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice at postnatal day 21 (P21) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). After 7 days of acclimation, postnatal day 28 (P28) was designated as experimental Day 0 (D0), marking the start of all procedures. ALKBH5-KO mice on a C57BL/6JGpt background were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China). To eliminate potential sex-based variability in ocular parameters [23, 24], only male mice were included in this study. Animals were housed under standardized conditions: 5 mice/cage, 12/12-hour light-dark cycle (lights on 07:00–19:00) with controlled illumination (200 ± 10 lx), ambient temperature (22 ± 1 °C), and relative humidity (50 ± 5%), with free access to standard chow and autoclaved water. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University and conducted in compliance with NIH guidelines for laboratory animal care and use(Approval No. 2025 − 0123).

Form-deprivation myopia

For the induction of FDM, hemispherical translucent diffusers were affixed to the right eyes using ophthalmic-grade cyanoacrylate adhesive (Vetbond™, 3 M, USA) applied to the periocular fur. These diffusers effectively degraded visual acuity while completely blocking unfiltered light transmission. To prevent device removal, all animals were fitted with customized polyethylene collars. The untreated contralateral left eyes served as within-animal controls (self-control, SC group), with age-matched naive mice were included as normal controls (NC group).

Intravitreal injection in mice

Mice were anesthetized with tribromoethanol (M2960, Aibei Biotechnology, Nanjing, China), and pupils were dilated with topical 1% tropicamide(Bausch & Lomb Freda, Shandong, China). A 26-gauge needle (0.26-mm inner diameter) was first used as a trocar to create a vitreous entry port approximately situated 1-mm posterior to the limbus. Through this entry port, a 33-gauge Hamilton microsyringe delivered 1 µL of pharmacological agents (0.1, 0.5, 2.5, or 12.5 mM DDO-2728 [HY-155489, MCE, USA]; 2.5 mM DDO-2728 plus 1 mM Ro 67-7476 [HY-100403, MCE, USA]; or 1 mM Ro 67-7476 alone) or vehicle control (5% DMSO [HY-Y0320C, MCE, USA] + 40% PEG300[25322-68-3, SCR, Shanghai, China] + 5% Tween80 [9005-65-6, SCR, Shanghai, China] + 50% saline) into the vitreous cavity. The injection angle was maintained at approximately 30° to avoid damage to the lens and retina. Successful injection was confirmed by transient visualization of a fluid meniscus at the injection site.

Beginning at D0, intravitreal injections were administered every 3–4 days for a total of 4 doses per eye. After the procedure, tobramycin and dexamethasone eye ointment (Novartis, Switzerland) was applied post-procedure to prevent infection. Animals were monitored daily for complications such as ocular inflammation or opacity.

Determination of intravitreal dosing concentrations and frequency for DDO-2728 and Ro 67-7476

No prior pharmacokinetic data or foundational studies exist for the intravitreal administration of DDO-2728 or Ro 67-7476. Previous research demonstrated that intraperitoneal injection of DDO-2728 at 10 mg/kg significantly inhibited tumor growth in nude mice without altering body weight or major organ pathology [25]. Based on the baseline body weight of mice in our study (mean 14.721 ± 0.143 g), the molecular weight of DDO-2728 (578.45 Da), and a standard intravitreal injection volume of 1 µL, we calculated that direct translation of the systemic dose to intravitreal delivery would yield a concentration of 254.512mM. However, given the confined intraocular space and the imperative to minimize local toxicity, intravitreal doses must be substantially lower than systemic equivalents. Consequently, we established a concentration gradient of 0.1, 0.5, 2.5, and 12.5 mM for DDO-2728, initiating testing at lower concentrations. For Ro 67-7476, its intravitreal concentration (1 mM) was determined by combining: (i) the validated safe-efficacy dilution factor derived from DDO-2728 gradient screening (relative to systemic exposure), (ii) its molecular weight (319.39 Da), and (iii) the reported effective intraperitoneal dose (4 mg/kg) in prior tumor models [26]. This concentration was functionally confirmed through Western blot analysis of retinal ERK1/2 and phospho-ERK1/2 expression, as shown in Fig. 8B. Owing to the absence of vitreous half-life data for both compounds, the dosing frequency (every 3–4 days) was defined based on established protocols for intravitreal therapeutics [27, 28] and the need to mitigate risks associated with repeated invasive procedures.

Measurement of refraction, axial length, and retinal thickness

Refractive measurements were performed at D0, D7, and D14 in conscious mice without anesthesia. Following pupil dilation with 1% tropicamide, assessments were conducted under dark-adapted conditions using an infrared eccentric photorefractor (ST-PR.01, STRIA.TECH, Germany). Both axial length (AL) and vitreous chamber depth (VCD) measurements were obtained at all three time points (D0, D7, and D14) using spectral-domain anterior segment optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT, CASIA2, TOMEY, Japan) in mice anesthetized with tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg, i.p.), as shown in Fig. 1E. The retinal thickness was assessed at D14 using Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA, BM-400 K, TowardPi, Beijing, China) as shown in Fig. 4B, in anesthetized mice (tribromoethanol, 250 mg/kg, i.p.) with dilated pupils (1% tropicamide). For each image, five circumferentially equidistant points at a consistent distance from the optic disc were selected for retinal thickness measurement, and the mean value was derived.

Western blot

Equal protein quantities were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.2 μm; ISEQ00010, Millipore). Membranes were blocked with Protein Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (PS108; EpiZyme, China) for 0.5 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies in Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) at 4 °C overnight. Following three 10-min TBST washes, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (BL520B; Biosharp, China) on a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.50i; NIH, USA) after normalization to HSP90. All antibodies are listed in Additional file 1.

When two target proteins have similar molecular weights, we perform sequential western blot analyses on the same membrane. After initial detection, the used membrane is incubated in membrane regeneration buffer (P1650, APPLYGEN, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 30 min. This treatment selectively removes both primary and secondary antibodies bound to the membrane antigens while preserving the electrotransferred proteins. To verify complete antibody removal, the regenerated membrane is incubated with ECL working solution for approximately 1 min, followed by X-ray film exposure and development. Only after confirming the absence of residual antibody signals is the membrane re-blocked and reprobed with a different antibody for subsequent rounds of western blot analysis.

RNA isolation

For human sample-related experiments, total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples using the MolPure® Blood RNA Kit (19241ES50; YEASEN, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For murine samples, total RNA was isolated from retina using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (400 − 100; GOONIE, Guangzhou, China) according to the standardized protocol. RNA concentration and purity were verified spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop™ One; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with all samples demonstrating A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0.

M6A Dot blot

Global m6A levels in peripheral blood of myopia patients and retinal tissues from experimental mice at D3/7/14 post-induction were quantitatively assessed using dot blot analysis. RNA samples were serially diluted in nuclease-free tubes, heat-denatured (95 °C, 3 min), and spotted (2 µL) onto Hybond-N + membranes (RPN303B; Cytiva, USA). Membranes were UV-crosslinked (30–60 min) and blocked with 5% skim milk (D8340; Solarbio, China) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Immunodetection was performed sequentially with: (1) anti-m6A rabbit monoclonal antibody (HA721152; HUABIO, China; 1:1000 in TBST, 4 °C overnight), and (2) HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (7074 S; Cell Signaling Technology, USA; 1:5000 in TBST, 1 h at room temperature). After three 5-min TBST washes, signals were developed with ECL substrate (G2014; Servicebio, China) using a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). For loading control, membranes were stained with 0.2% methylene blue (M917264; Mackun, China) in 0.4 M sodium acetate (N917741; Mackun, China)/0.4 M acetic acid (A801295; Mackun, China) buffer (30 min, room temperature), destained with distilled water, and re-imaged. Dot intensities were quantified using ImageJ.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (RR036A; Takara Bio, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative PCR was performed in 10µL reactions containing: 5µL ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711; Vazyme, China), 0.2µL each of forward and reverse primers (10µM), 2µL template cDNA (diluted 1:2 − 1:3 from stock), and 2.6µL nuclease-free water (AM9915G; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Reactions were run in triplicate on a CFX96 Deep Well Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA) using the following program: 95 °C for 30 s (initial denaturation); 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s (amplification); followed by melting curve analysis (65–95 °C, increment 0.5 °C/5 sec) to confirm reaction specificity. Gene expression was quantified using the 2-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the reference gene. The primer sequences are listed in Additional file 2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Retinal tissues were fixed in eyeball fixative (HK2011, HaoKebio, China), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Sections were mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides and processed as follows: (1) deparaffinization in xylene (2 × 10 min); (2) rehydration through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 80%, for 5 min each); (3) antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) using microwave heating (95–100 °C, 30 min) followed by 60-min cooling; (4) endogenous peroxidase blocking (3% H₂O₂ [H792072; Mackun, China], 20 min); (5) nonspecific binding blocking (QuickBlock™, P0260; Beyotime, 30 min). Primary antibodies diluted in QuickBlock™ Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer for Immunol Staining (P0262; Beyotime) were applied overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After three 5-min washes with phosphate buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST), sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (AS014; ABclonal; 1:200 dilution in PBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (G1212-200T; Servicebio) for 2–5 min with microscopic monitoring, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining (G1004; Servicebio, 30 s) and dehydration through graded alcohols. Negative controls were processed without primary antibody. Stained sections were examined under an OLYMPUS BX53 light microscope, and immunostaining results were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software by two independent observers.

TdT-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)

Apoptotic cells in retinal paraffin sections were identified using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (FITC; Yeasen Biotechnology, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with proteinase K (20 µg/mL) for 20 min at room temperature to enhance permeability. For positive controls, selected slides were treated with DNase I (1 U/µL) for 10 min at room temperature prior to the TUNEL reaction. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture (containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase and FITC-labeled dUTP) for 1 h at 37 °C in a dark humidified chamber. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium (BL739B; Biosharp, China). Fluorescent images were acquired using an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope under consistent exposure parameters. TUNEL-positive cells were quantified by analyzing FITC fluorescence intensity in three randomly selected fields per section using ImageJ software.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining (HE)

Paraffin-embedded retinal Sect. (4-µm thickness) were stained using the Hematoxylin-Eosin Staining Kit (G1120, Solarbio, China) in accordance with standard protocols. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 5 min) and rehydrated through graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, and 80%, for 5 min each). After rinsing in distilled water, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol for 30 s, and blued in 0.5% ammonia water. Eosin counterstaining was performed for 1 min. Finally, sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Stained sections were examined under an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope. Images were captured at 200× magnification under consistent lighting conditions. Retinal layer thickness measurements (e.g., ganglion cell layer, inner nuclear layer, outer nuclear layer) were performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). For each eye, three non-consecutive sections were analyzed, with five measurements taken per layer per section.

Masson trichrome staining

Paraffin-embedded retinal Sect. (4 μm) were stained using the Masson Trichrome Stain Kit (G1340, Solarbio, China) in adherence to the manufacturer’s instructions. After deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through graded ethanol, the sections were stained with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (10 min) for nuclear visualization, followed by differentiation in acid alcohol. Tissues were then sequentially treated with Lichun Red fuchsin (5 min), phosphomolybdic-phosphotungstic acid (2 min), and aniline blue (2 min) to differentially stain muscle fibers (red) and collagen (blue). RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Retinal tissues from FDM and SC groups (n = 3 pooled samples per group, with each sample containing 10 retinas) were snap-frozen and shipped on dry ice to LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) for transcriptome profiling. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® Reagent, with RNA integrity numbers (RIN) > 7.0 confirmed by Bioanalyzer analysis. Strand-specific cDNA libraries were prepared and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating ≥ 40 million 150-bp paired-end reads per sample with Q30 scores > 85%. Bioinformatics analysis included quality control using fastp, alignment to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) using HISAT2, and transcript quantification via StringTie. Differential expression analysis identified 1,637 significant genes (edgeR; |log2FC|>0.25, p < 0.05), which were subsequently analyzed for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment using the STRING database (FDR < 0.05). For cross-sample comparison, FPKM values were standardized to Z-scores (Z = [X-µ]/σ) after excluding genes with zero standard deviation (constant expression).

Hydroxyproline content quantification

As hydroxyproline (HYP) constitutes a major component of collagen in organisms, its content serves as a key quantitative biomarker reflecting collagen metabolism and fibrotic processes. Retinal HYP levels were quantified using a commercial assay kit (BC0255, Solarbio, China). Freshly isolated retinal tissues were finely minced and hydrolyzed in concentrated hydrochloric acid at 95 °C for 2–6 h. After cooling to room temperature, the hydrolysates were neutralized to pH 6–8 using 10 mol/L NaOH (S111507, Aladdin, China), brought to final volume with distilled water, and centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 20 min at 25 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. Based on the manufacturer’s protocol, the samples were oxidized with chloramine-T reagent for 20 min at room temperature (20–25 °C). The reaction was then developed with p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde at 60 °C for 25 min, followed by 20 min of stabilization at room temperature. Absorbance measurements were performed at 550 nm using a BioTek Synergy Neo2 multimode microplate reader. Quantification was achieved using a hydroxyproline standard curve (0.234–30 µg/mL) run in parallel with experimental samples. All measurements were performed in duplicate. Final HYP concentrations were normalized to tissue weight (µg/mg) and expressed as fold changes relative to the mean value of control group samples for statistical comparison.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of cytokines and chemokines, interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-10, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), in retinal homogenates were determined using commercial sandwich ELISA kits purchased from Youke Life Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) with the following catalog numbers: IL-6 (EM0004), IL-10 (EM005), MCP-1 (EM0025), TNF-α (EM0010), and TGF-β1 (EH002).

Briefly, freshly dissected mouse retinas were rinsed with ice-cold PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.4) to remove residual blood. After weighing, the tissues were minced into small pieces. The minced tissues were then homogenized in an appropriate volume of ice-cold PBS (generally at a 1:9 weight-to-volume ratio) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail using a glass homogenizer on ice. To ensure complete cell lysis, the homogenates were further disrupted by sonication. The homogenates were subsequently centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 24 °C.

The ELISA procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In summary, standards and diluted samples were added to the pre-coated 96-well plates and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, a biotin-conjugated detection antibody was added to each well, followed by incubation with a streptavidin-HRP conjugate. The colorimetric reaction was developed using a tetramethylbenzidine substrate and stopped with the stop solution. The optical density of each well was measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader.

The concentration of each cytokine was calculated by interpolating from a standard curve generated using a four-parameter logistic curve fit. Final cytokine concentrations are expressed as picograms per milligram of total protein (pg/ml protein).

Statistical analysis and data visualization

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of data distribution was verified using both D’Agostino-Pearson omnibus, Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests (significance threshold set at p > 0.05 for normality assumption). Based on the confirmed normal distribution, we employed the following parametric statistical tests: (1) Pearson correlation coefficient for evaluating linear relationships between continuous variables; (2) one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s honestly significant difference post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons; and (3) the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests for comparisons between two independent groups. All statistical tests were two-tailed with the significance level (α) set at 0.05.

For the analysis of clinical correlation, multiple linear regression was employed to assess the independent association of refraction or AL with peripheral blood m6A levels while controlling for potential confounders. Given the sample size constraint (n = 38), we adhered to the principle of parsimony to avoid model overfitting. The primary models were adjusted for age and sex. A sensitivity analysis was performed by additionally including average corneal curvature (K) in the model to test the robustness of the primary findings.

Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism, with final figure composition completed in Adobe Illustrator CC 2023 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA). The sample sizes (n) indicated in the figure captions represent the biological replicates.

Peripheral blood m6A levels correlated with myopia severity in clinical patients

Quantitative dot blot analysis of peripheral blood RNA (60 ng input, methylene blue-validated) from 38 ICL surgery candidates, whose clinical parameters are summarized in Table 1 (25.289 ± 0.516 years; -3D to -18D) demonstrated progressive m6A reduction with worsening myopia. The observed correlations revealed that higher m6A levels were associated with less negative refractive error values (r = 0.435, p < 0.01), while lower m6A levels correlated with greater axial elongation (r = -0.338, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1B, C). These findings were robust to further adjustment for average corneal curvature (K) in a sensitivity analysis (refraction: β = 0.078, p < 0.05; AL: β = -0.174, p < 0.05). Temporal progression of myopia and retinal m6A reduction in the FDM model

The FDM model developed characteristic myopic changes over 15 days (Fig. 1F). Initial D0 measurements showed no interocular differences in refractive error, AL, or VCD between groups (all p > 0.05). At D7, FDM eyes exhibited significant myopic refraction (-4.997 ± 0.427 D vs. NC: 0.119 ± 0.462 D; p < 0.0001). By D14, FDM eyes demonstrated severe myopia (-5.513 ± 0.398 D vs. NC: 0.702 ± 0.555 D; p < 0.0001) with significant AL elongation (0.012 ± 0.004 mm vs. NC: -0.001 ± 0.002 mm, p < 0.01) and VCD increase (0.003 ± 0.001 mm vs. NC: 0.000 ± 0.001 mm, p < 0.05).

The analysis of retinal m6A levels (Fig. 1G) revealed significant reductions in FDM eyes versus controls at both D7 (p < 0.05 for 50/100 ng inputs) and D14 (50 ng: p < 0.01; 100 ng: p < 0.0001), with stronger effects at higher RNA inputs. No differences were observed at D3.

Western blot analysis confirmed these findings at the protein level, with ALKBH5 significantly elevated in FDM retinas (1.435 ± 0.030 fold) versus NC and SC (both p < 0.01), without alterations in other enzymes (Fig. 2B and C).

TUNEL staining revealed concentration-dependent retinal toxicity of DDO-2728 (Fig. 3C). Although no significant apoptosis was observed in any retinal layers at concentrations ≤ 2.5 mM, the 12.5-mM treatment group showed marked TUNEL-positive signals specifically in the outer nuclear layer (ONL). Ocular parameter analysis demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship for DDO-2728 treatment (Fig. 3D). The 2.5-mM concentration emerged as the most effective therapeutic dose, with form-deprived eyes showing significantly attenuated myopic refraction (-3.592 ± 0.337 D vs. vehicle controls − 5.753 ± 0.365 D, ##p < 0.01) and reduced AL (0.007 ± 0.001 mm vs. 0.014 ± 0.001 mm, #p < 0.05) at D14. The 0.5-mM group showed modest refractive improvement (-4.823 ± 0.277 D) and AL reduction (0.008 ± 0.002 mm); however, these changes remained non-significant. The 0.1-mM concentration showed no detectable protective effects.

Structural preservation of the retina by ALKBH5 inhibition (via DDO-2728)

OCTA imaging revealed significant alterations in retinal thickness among the experimental groups (Fig. 4C). The SC group showed normal retinal thickness (227.152 ± 2.774 μm), whereas both of the form-deprived groups exhibited substantial thinning (FDM: 199.805 ± 2.126 μm, p < 0.0001; DMSO: 200.595 ± 1.868 μm, p < 0.0001 vs. SC). Notably, DDO-2728 treatment at 2.5 mM partially preserved retinal thickness (213.650 ± 1.820 μm), and demonstrated significant improvement compared to vehicle control (p < 0.01) despite remaining thinner than the retina in SC eyes (p < 0.01).

HE staining confirmed preserved cytoarchitectural integrity with distinct laminar organization across all cohorts, while histomorphometric quantification provided precise layer-specific thickness measurements (Fig. 4D and E). The SC group showed characteristic retinal architecture with total thickness of 208.257 ± 3.336 μm, whereas form-deprived eyes displayed marked thinning (FDM: 178.763 ± 2.076 μm; DMSO: 179.152 ± 2.832 μm; both p < 0.0001 vs. SC). DDO-2728 treatment significantly attenuated this thinning (192.055 ± 4.240 μm), with intermediate values between SC and vehicle groups (p < 0.01 vs. both). Layer-specific analysis revealed distinct thickness alteration patterns across retinal layers. In the GCL, the SC group maintained normal thickness (23.371 ± 0.862 μm), whereas both FDM (19.563 ± 0.891 μm, p < 0.05) and DMSO groups (18.673 ± 1.254 μm, p < 0.01) showed significant thinning. Notably, DDO-2728 treatment maintained GCL thickness (23.475 ± 0.461 μm) comparable to SC group levels, which demonstrated significant protective effects compared to the vehicle control (p < 0.01). In the INL, all experimental groups demonstrated mild but significant thinning as compared to SC controls (38.192 ± 0.730 μm): the FDM group (34.228 ± 0.751 μm, p < 0.01), the DMSO group (35.353 ± 0.527 μm, p < 0.05), and the DDO group (35.109 ± 0.563 μm, p < 0.05). The most pronounced thickness reductions occurred in the ONL, where all experimental groups showed marked decreases relative to the SC group (51.776 ± 1.030 μm): FDM (45.054 ± 0.954 μm, p < 0.001), DMSO (45.447 ± 0.846 μm, p < 0.001), and DDO (43.741 ± 0.977 μm, p < 0.0001).

Inflammatory modulation in the retina by ALKBH5 inhibition (via DDO-2728)

The qPCR and ELISA analyses demonstrated marked inflammatory activation in myopic retinas (Fig. 4F, G). Form-deprived groups showed significant upregulation of all measured cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, MMP2, MCP-1, TNF-α, and TGF-β1) versus SC controls (all p < 0.05). DDO-2728 treatment effectively normalized most inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, MMP2, MCP-1, and TGF-β1) to SC levels while showing no significant effect on TNF-α expression, which remained elevated as compared to SC (p < 0.05) and was unchanged versus the vehicle control.

ECM dysregulation in myopic retinas and therapeutic modulation by ALKBH5 inhibition (via DDO-2728)

Western blot analysis revealed distinct alterations in ECM regulators (Fig. 5A and B). Compared to SC retinas, form-deprived groups exhibited significant upregulation of both collagen type I (FDM: 3.630 ± 0.138-fold, p < 0.01; DMSO: 4.046 ± 0.661-fold, p < 0.01) and MMP2 (FDM: 2.703 ± 0.014-fold, p < 0.01; DMSO: 2.810 ± 0.131-fold, p < 0.001). DDO-2728 treatment partially reversed these changes, with collagen type I showing a non-significant increasing trend versus SC (2.200 ± 0.327-fold) although it was significantly lower than that with DMSO (p < 0.05). MMP2 expression remained elevated relative to SC (DDO: 1.917 ± 0.186-fold, p < 0.05) despite reduction versus DMSO (p < 0.05). α-SMA expression showed no significant intergroup differences (p > 0.05). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated progressive collagen accumulation in myopic retinas (Fig. 5C and D). Full retinal cross-sections showed increased collagen deposition in FDM (5.813 ± 0.486% area coverage, p < 0.0001) and DMSO groups (5.895 ± 0.343%, p < 0.0001) versus SC (2.273 ± 0.239%). The DDO-2728 treatment significantly reduced collagen content (3.650 ± 0.164%, p < 0.001 vs. DMSO), but did not fully normalize to the content to SC levels (p < 0.05). Specific analysis of photoreceptor cell layer(PRC) revealed similar trends, with FDM (9.245 ± 0.463%, p < 0.0001) and DMSO (10.230 ± 1.160%, p < 0.0001) groups exceeding SC values (3.080 ± 0.358%), whereas DDO-treated retinas showed intermediate levels (5.624 ± 0.843%, p < 0.001 vs. DMSO). Consistent with histological findings, HYP assays confirmed elevated collagen content in form-deprived retinas (FDM: 1.939 ± 0.066-fold, p < 0.01; DMSO: 1.792 ± 0.119-fold, p < 0.01 vs. SC), which was attenuated by DDO-2728 treatment (1.202 ± 0.133-fold, p < 0.05 vs. DMSO) without reaching statistical significance versus SC (p > 0.05; Fig. 5E). RNA-seq analysis identified 28 differentially expressed genes that were involved in ECM organization (Fig. 5F), including 9 collagen-related genes (e.g., COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1) and 19 ECM regulators (e.g., ADAMTS19, MMP14), which were all significantly upregulated in FDM versus SC retinas.

Systemic monitoring confirmed the safety of all treatments, with body weights remaining comparable across groups (DDO: 20.333 ± 0.604 g; DDO + Ro: 20.433 ± 0.281 g; Ro: 20.222 ± 0.307 g) and no signs of retinal apoptosis were detected by TUNEL staining in any experimental group (Fig. 7B and D).

The therapeutic efficacy of ALKBH5 inhibition was significantly modulated by the ERK1/2 activation status. At study endpoint, DDO-2728 monotherapy demonstrated protection against myopia progression, with significantly less refractive error (-3.767 ± 0.339 D) compared to both the combination group (-4.968 ± 0.369 D, p < 0.05) and Ro 67-7476 alone group (-5.305 ± 0.230 D, p < 0.01). This protective effect extended to axial length measurements, where the DDO group exhibited minimal elongation (0.005 ± 0.001 mm) versus the DDO + Ro (0.014 ± 0.001 mm, p < 0.05) and Ro-only (0.014 ± 0.001 mm, p < 0.05) groups. While vitreous chamber depth changes followed similar trends (DDO: 0.004 ± 0.001 mm; DDO + Ro: 0.009 ± 0.001 mm; Ro: 0.010 ± 0.001 mm), these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 7C).

Histological analyses revealed striking layer-specific protection patterns. DDO-2728 treatment preserved total retinal thickness (203.000 ± 1.170 μm) significantly better than both combination (194.321 ± 1.228 μm, p < 0.05) and Ro-only (193.571 ± 1.054 μm, p < 0.01) treatments. This protection was particularly evident in the GCL, where DDO-treated retinas maintained near-normal thickness (22.940 ± 0.682 μm) compared to the attenuated measurements in DDO + Ro (19.988 ± 0.875 μm, p < 0.05) and Ro-only (19.048 ± 0.962 μm, p < 0.05) groups. OCTA confirmed these findings, and DDO-treated retinas demonstrated significantly greater overall thickness (218.473 ± 0.905 μm) than either combination (211.740 ± 1.619 μm, p < 0.05) or Ro-only (210.180 ± 1.173 μm, p < 0.05) treatments (Fig. 7E-G).

QPCR and ELISA quantifications revealed that ERK1/2 activation by Ro 67-7476 significantly upregulated inflammatory mediators in myopic retinas at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 8A, B). At the mRNA level, compared to DDO-2728 monotherapy, the DDO + Ro combination group showed increased mRNA expression of IL-6 (1.300 ± 0.092-fold, p < 0.05), IL-8 (2.016 ± 0.130-fold, p < 0.001), IL-10 (1.283 ± 0.031-fold, p < 0.0001), MMP2 (2.684 ± 0.283-fold, p < 0.05), MCP-1 (1.190 ± 0.039-fold, p < 0.01), and TGF-β1 (1.376 ± 0.042-fold, p < 0.01). Similarly, the Ro-only group exhibited elevated levels of IL-6 (1.343 ± 0.036-fold, p < 0.01), IL-8 (1.863 ± 0.139-fold, p < 0.01), IL-10 (1.267 ± 0.031-fold, p < 0.0001), MMP2 (2.644 ± 0.483-fold, p < 0.05), MCP-1 (1.192 ± 0.032-fold, p < 0.01), and TGF-β1 (1.460 ± 0.108-fold, p < 0.01). Consistently, at the protein level, ELISA results demonstrated a significant increase in the secretion of key inflammatory cytokines in the DDO + Ro group compared to the DDO-2728 monotherapy group: IL-6 (10.080 ± 0.957 vs. 6.563 ± 0.410 pg/ml, p < 0.05), IL-10 (4.909 ± 0.276 vs. 2.285 ± 0.290 pg/ml, p < 0.0001), MCP-1 (12.050 ± 1.092 vs. 7.688 ± 0.438 pg/ml, p < 0.05), and TGF-β1 (2165.464 ± 203.171 vs. 976.442 ± 118.257 pg/ml, p < 0.001). The Ro-only group also showed a similar significant upregulation compared to the monotherapy control (all p < 0.05).

Western blot analysis of extracellular matrix components and signaling molecules provided complementary mechanistic insights (Fig. 8C and D). The DDO + Ro group showed significant increases in collagen type I (1.800 ± 0.051-fold, p < 0.01) and MMP2 (2.606 ± 0.265-fold, p < 0.05) protein expression compared to DDO monotherapy. Similarly, the Ro-only group exhibited elevated levels of collagen type I (1.801 ± 0.086-fold, p < 0.01) and MMP2 (2.874 ± 0.372-fold, p < 0.01) protein expression. The effectiveness of Ro 67-7476 as an ERK1/2 activator was confirmed by elevated phosphorylated ERK1/2 levels (1.761 ± 0.028-fold in DDO + Ro, p < 0.001; 1.689 ± 0.086-fold in Ro, p < 0.01) and p-ERK1/2/ERK1/2 ratios (3.146 ± 0.172-fold in DDO + Ro, p < 0.0001; 3.108 ± 0.158-fold in Ro, p < 0.0001).

Histological and biochemical analyses demonstrated corresponding changes in ECM organization (Fig. 8E-G). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed increased collagen deposition in DDO + Ro (5.045 ± 0.381% in total retina, p < 0.05; 11.480 ± 1.799% in PRC, p < 0.05) and Ro-only (6.069 ± 0.380% in total retina, p < 0.001; 11.691 ± 1.971% in PRC, p < 0.05) retinas versus DDO group (3.574 ± 0.254% in total retina; 5.400 ± 0.504% in PRC). Furthermore, hydroxyproline assays confirmed these findings (DDO + Ro: 2.859 ± 0.228-fold; Ro: 2.867 ± 0.178-fold; both p < 0.0001 vs. DDO).

Phenotypic assessments confirmed that ALKBH5 deficiency conferred significant resistance to myopia development (Fig. 9B). The protective effect was most pronounced in refractive error, where the interocular difference in ALKBH5-KO mice was significantly attenuated (-4.151 ± 0.239 D) compared to wild-type (WT) controls (-5.812 ± 0.432 D; p < 0.01). A significant protective effect was also observed AL. The change in AL was minimal in ALKBH5-KO mice (0.002 ± 0.003 mm), which was substantially smaller than that in WT mice (0.013 ± 0.003 mm; p < 0.05). Although VCD changes followed a similar trend (WT: 0.003 ± 0.001 mm; KO: 0.001 ± 0.001 mm), the inter-group difference did not reach statistical significance.

Histological analysis of retinal architecture revealed that FDM induced significant pathological thinning in WT mice (Fig. 9C and D). The total retinal thickness in the FDM group (193.523 ± 2.821 μm) was substantially reduced compared to the SC group (210.831 ± 2.703 μm; p < 0.001). This FDM-induced thinning was significantly mitigated in ALKBH5-KO mice. The retinal thickness in the KO-FDM group (203.304 ± 1.039 μm) was significantly greater than that in the WT FDM group (p < 0.05) and was comparable to the KO-SC group (209.663 ± 2.788 μm), indicating a protective role of ALKBH5 deficiency against retinal structural impairment.

At the molecular level, ALKBH5 knockout significantly modulated the retinal inflammatory milieu (Fig. 9E). ELISA analysis of retinal homogenates demonstrated that the FDM-induced upregulation of key cytokines, including IL-6, IL-10, MCP-1, and TGF-β1, was substantially blunted in KO-FDM retinas (IL-6: 7.589 ± 0.372 vs. FDM group : 10.042 ± 0.917 pg/ml, p < 0.05; IL-10: 2.900 ± 0.343 vs. FDM group : 5.831 ± 0.329 pg/ml, p < 0.0001; MCP-1: 8.079 ± 0.548 vs. FDM group : 12.170 ± 1.482 pg/ml, p < 0.05; TGF-β1: 1225.670 ± 134.748 vs. WT-FDM: 2085.364 ± 130.131 pg/ml, p < 0.001). Notably, TNF-α levels, which were unaffected by ALKBH5 ablation, remained elevated in the KO-FDM group, suggesting a distinct regulatory mechanism (Fig. 9E).

Western blot analysis further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying the protective effect of ALKBH5 knockout (Fig. 9F, G). The increases in the levels of p-ERK1/2 and the ratio of p-ERK1/2 to total ERK1/2 were substantially attenuated in the KO-FDM group (p-ERK1/2: FDM: 1.879 ± 0.047-fold ; KO-FDM: 1.411 ± 0.144-fold, p < 0.05;p-ERK1/2 / ERK 1/2: FDM: 4.423 ± 0.754-fold ; KO-FDM: 1.418 ± 0.210-fold, p < 0.01). Concurrently, ALKBH5 knockout effectively counteracted the early-stage ECM dysregulation induced by FDM. The increases in the levels of collagen type I and MMP2 were substantially attenuated in the KO-FDM group (Collagen type I : FDM: 2.187 ± 0.055-fold ; KO-FDM: 0.769 ± 0.345-fold, p < 0.01;MMP2: FDM: 2.187 ± 0.194-fold ; KO-FDM: 1.407 ± 0.243-fold, p < 0.05).

Masson’s trichrome staining revealed the FDM-induced increases of collagen deposition were substantially attenuated in the KO-FDM group (Fig. 9H, I), particularly in the photoreceptor layer (Total retina: FDM: 5.818 ± 0.494% vs. KO-FDM: 3.738 ± 0.328%, p < 0.001; PRC layer: FDM: 8.909 ± 0.303% vs. KO-FDM: 5.296 ± 0.438%, p < 0.0001).

Discussion

The global rise in myopia prevalence underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic to target its underlying molecular mechanisms. In this study, we identified ALKBH5, an m6A RNA demethylase, as a critical regulator of myopia progression through selective modulation of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway. Critically, retinal ALKBH5 inhibition conferred multifaceted protection during FDM progression, which manifested through four coordinated therapeutic effects: reduced refractive error and axial elongation; mitigated retinal thinning; suppressed inflammatory activation; and attenuated early-stage ECM dysregulation characterized by diminished collagen deposition. These results not only expand our understanding of epigenetic regulation in refractive development but also highlight DDO-2728, a selective ALKBH5 inhibitor, as a promising candidate for myopia control.

The clinical data establish peripheral blood m6A reduction as a novel epigenetic signature of myopia progression, as evidenced by significant correlations with refractive error (r = 0.435, p < 0.01) and axial elongation (r = -0.338, p < 0.05). This finding converges with emerging evidence that implicates m6A modifications in retinal signaling pathways [29–31] and scleral remodeling [13], as well as paralleling precedents from ischemic stroke [32] and cancer biology [33, 34] where circulating m6A levels serve as non-invasive biomarkers of systemic pathophysiology. By extending this paradigm to refractive disorders, our study reveals dual clinical-translational and mechanistic implications: blood m6A quantification represents a potential monitoring tool analogous to HbA1c in diabetes, while simultaneously implicating RNA methylation dysregulation in ocular growth control—a relationship that is experimentally substantiated through our identification of retinal ALKBH5-ERK1/2 axis dysregulation in animal models.

This study provides compelling evidence that ALKBH5-mediated retinal m6A hypomethylation drives FDM progression. The temporal dynamics of this epigenetic modification—characterized by no significant changes at D3 but progressive decline at D7 and D14—reveal its specific role in regulating the active phase of axial elongation, rather than initial pathogenesis. This time-dependent alteration in m6A epigenetic regulation may exhibit progressive accumulation and cascade amplification effects during myopia pathogenesis. This mechanistic pattern suggests the existence of a potential therapeutic window for early-phase intervention.

The selective upregulation of ALKBH5—absent changes in other m6A regulators (METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, and FTO)—defines a form deprivation-specific epitranscriptomic response. This specificity may be attributed to several factors. First, the spatial confinement of ALKBH5 induction to the GCL suggests a cell-type-specific mechanism, as other regulators may be more active in retinal layers unaffected by early myopic signaling. Second, ALKBH5 and FTO, despite both being demethylases, exhibit non-redundant roles and target distinct mRNA substrates; ALKBH5 is particularly associated with inflammation and fibrosis-related pathways, which are central to our observed phenotype. Finally, the upstream regulatory cues triggered by form deprivation may specifically engage ALKBH5’s transcriptional or post-transcriptional control mechanisms while leaving FTO unchanged. This mirrors ALKBH5-predominant induction in diseases such as peripheral arterial disease and cancer [35, 36], where it drives pathology through targeted mRNA demethylation. Strikingly, such context-specific ALKBH5 dominance occurs despite vastly divergent pathological milieus, which suggest that conserved principles of epitranscriptomic operate across diseases. Numerous studies indicate that RGCs are critical for ocular refractive development and FDM progression that likely initiate key myopia-related signals [37–39]. Our finding of specific ALKBH5 upregulation in the GCL not only supports this role but also suggests that ALKBH5-mediated m6A modification probably participates in the retinoscleral signaling cascade that underlies myopia. Concurrently, ALKBH5 has been well-established as a critical regulator of both inflammatory responses and fibrotic processes across multiple tissue types, including the pulmonary [40, 41], hepatic [42, 43], cardiac [44, 45], and renal systems [46, 47]. Notably, emerging evidence further implicates chronic low-grade inflammation [48, 49] in the pathogenesis of myopia, particularly in retinal inflammation and fibrosis [50]. Clinically, high myopia is a recognized risk factor for idiopathic preretinal macular fibrillopathy (IPMF), which is a condition that is characterized by abnormal fibrotic tissue deposition in the macular region [51]. This clinical association aligns with experimental findings from Bo Bao et al., who demonstrated significant retinal fibrosis in lens-induced myopia models [52]. Together, these clinical and preclinical lines of evidence strongly suggest that inflammatory and fibrotic mechanisms (potentially modulated by ALKBH5) play a key role in myopia-associated retinal pathology. Given these connections, our subsequent pharmacological investigations specifically focused on retinal inflammatory markers and fibrosis-related phenotypes.

To specifically inhibit ALKBH5 activity, we employed DDO-2728—a potent and selective ALKBH5 inhibitor with established efficacy in pharmacological and experimental studies [25, 53]. Systemic safety was confirmed by daily body-weight monitoring across all cohorts (NC, SC, DMSO, and DDO-2728 groups: 0.1, 0.5, 2.5, and 12.5 mM), but revealed no significant intergroup differences throughout the study. This indicates intravitreal DDO-2728 administration induces no detectable systemic toxicity or metabolic disturbances. Parallel retinal safety assessments via terminal TUNEL staining established a dose-dependent safety profile: concentrations ≤ 2.5 mM exhibited negligible apoptotic activity, whereas the 12.5-mM group showed marked TUNEL-positive signals that were localized specifically to the ONL, and thereby indicated significant cytotoxicity at this supratherapeutic concentration. Building upon this safety foundation, functional evaluations demonstrated progressive concentration-dependent efficacy against myopia progression. Quantitative analyses revealed the complete absence of a therapeutic effect at 0.1 mM, a non-significant protective trend at 0.5 mM, and statistically significant attenuation of myopia development at 2.5 mM, relative to DMSO controls (p < 0.01 for refraction and p < 0.05 for AL). The convergent evidence of retinal safety and robust anti-myopic efficacy at 2.5 mM justified its selection for subsequent mechanistic interrogation of ALKBH5 inhibition pathways.

Consistent with prior studies using identical myopia models [27, 54], HE staining and OCTA anterior segment imaging confirmed significant retinal thinning in form-deprived eyes versus the SC group. Notably, the DDO group (2.5-mM DDO-2728 treatment) effectively mitigated this thickness reduction, and thereby demonstrated statistically significant preservation of retinal architecture versus the DMSO group. The concordance between histological (HE) and in vivo imaging (OCTA) modalities provides multimodal validation of ALKBH5 inhibition’s structural protective capacity. Laminar analysis further revealed that this protective effect was predominantly localized to the GCL. This layer-specific efficacy aligns with IHC findings showing selective ALKBH5 upregulation in the GCL.

Our investigation into the anti-inflammatory potential of ALKBH5 inhibition within the context of myopia progression revealed compelling evidence that supports its protective role in the retina. Consistent with established literature that implicates inflammation [49, 55, 56] in myopia pathogenesis, we observed a significant upregulation of seven key inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, MMP2, MCP-1, TNF-α, and TGF-β1) in the retinas of form-deprived eyes. Crucially, pharmacological inhibition of ALKBH5 demonstrated a significant therapeutic effect that effectively ameliorated the elevated expression levels of six of these factors: IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, MMP2, MCP-1, and TGF-β1. This robust attenuation of multiple pro-inflammatory and remodeling-associated signals strongly indicates that ALKBH5 inhibition confers a potent anti-inflammatory action on the retina during myopic progression. The notable exception was TNF-α, whose levels remained largely unaltered by the treatment. This selective modulation, which influenced the majority of the investigated pathway-associated factors whereas sparing TNF-α, strongly suggests that the anti-inflammatory protection afforded by ALKBH5 inhibition operates through a specific and potentially targeted molecular pathway, rather than a generalized suppressive effect on retinal inflammation.

Our study uncovers an interesting early-stage ECM dysregulation phenotype in the retinas of form-deprived eyes, as characterized by a “dual-acceleration” of collagen synthesis and degradation that differs from classical fibrotic transformation. Specifically, we observed significant upregulation of collagen type I and MMP2 alongside elevated HYP content and histologically confirmed collagen deposition—predominantly localized to the PRC layer—without an accompanying α-SMA elevation or scar-like cross-linking. RNA-seq analysis further corroborated this coordinated ECM imbalance, with GO term enrichment for “collagen fibril organization” and “extracellular matrix organization” revealing synchronized activation of both anabolic (e.g., COL1A1, COL3A1, ADAMTS2) and catabolic (e.g., MMP14, ADAMTS7) genes. Although this profile aligns with prior lens-induced myopia models [53] reporting both collagen type I/MMP2 co-upregulation and α-SMA elevation (indicative of classical fibrosis) with PRC-specific accumulation during 2–6 weeks post-induction, our model uniquely lacks α-SMA induction despite similar collagen/MMP2 dynamics. This discrepancy may stem from interspecies variation (mice vs. guinea pigs), differential induction methods (form-deprivation vs. lens-defocus), or delayed fibrotic commitment (reminiscent of pulmonary fibrosis models [57, 58] where the upregulation of α-SMA expression emerges after 2–4 weeks of sustained insult). The confinement of collagen deposits to the PRC layer implies photoreceptor-specific ECM vulnerability, which potentially disrupts retinal pigment epithelium-photoreceptor metabolic coupling. Although this dual-accelerated ECM turnover remains reversible in its pre-fibrotic state, unchecked inflammatory signaling may create a self-perpetuating cascade: TNF-α and IL-6 can stimulate further MMP2 release, while MMP2-processed TGF-β1 feeds back to amplify collagen synthesis, establishing a precarious equilibrium prone to collapse into either fibrosis (if TGF-β1 dominates) or structural degradation (if MMPs prevail) [59–62]. Within this critical window, ALKBH5 inhibition —consistent with its selective suppression of IL-6, MMP2, and TGF-β1 but not TNF-α— partially attenuated the dysregulated ECM signature. This implies that TNF-α-dependent inflammatory drivers (unaffected by ALKBH5 inhibition) may sustain residual ECM imbalance.

Our findings demonstrate significant alterations in the ERK1/2 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways in form-deprived retinas, as revealed by GO enrichment analysis of RNA-seq data and further validated at the protein level. The observed phosphorylation-mediated activation of ERK1/2 and suppression of Wnt signaling align with previous reports in models of FDM [52, 63–65]. Notably, our study provides novel evidence that ALKBH5 inhibition partially rescues the aberrant phosphorylation of ERK1/2 although it fails to modulate Wnt pathway suppression, and this indicates the pathway-specific therapeutic potential of ALKBH5 targeting. This differential effect suggests that although both pathways are implicated in form-deprivation myopia, their regulatory mechanisms may involve distinct upstream mediators, with ALKBH5 preferentially influencing ERK1/2 activation. Furthermore, the results of IHC further demonstrate the cell-type-specific localization of these molecular alterations as follows: consistent with the specific upregulation of ALKBH5 expression in the GCL, the downregulation of total ERK1/2 protein also occurred predominantly within the GCL. This spatial correlation implies a potential functional link between ALKBH5 and ERK1/2 signaling in RGCs, which suggests that this layer may represent a critical site for the pathogenesis of retinal changes induced by form deprivation.

Building upon the established critical role of ERK1/2 signaling in myopia pathogenesis and its well-documented regulation of both inflammation [66–70] and ECM dysregulation [71] in ocular and other tissues, we mechanistically investigated its interaction with ALKBH5 inhibition using a FDM model. To specifically interrogate ERK1/2’s role, we employed Ro 67-7476 – a selective ERK1/2 agonist [72–74] that directly induces phosphorylation independent of glutamate signaling – in combination with the ALKBH5 inhibitor DDO-2728. Through intravitreal injections at defined time points that were consistent with the preceding experiments, three experimental groups were compared: (1) DDO-2728 alone (DDO group), (2) DDO-2728 + Ro 67-7476 (DDO + Ro group), and (3) Ro 67-7476 alone (Ro group). Rigorous safety assessments, including daily weight monitoring and TUNEL staining of retinal sections, confirmed the absence of significant systemic or local retinal toxicity associated with any of the treatment regimens. Crucially, co-administration of the ERK1/2 activator Ro 67-7476 completely abrogated the multifaceted therapeutic benefits conferred by DDO-2728. This reversal was strikingly comprehensive, and encompassed not only the primary biometric hallmarks of myopia progression (refractive error and AL) but also pathological alterations in retinal thickness. Furthermore, the significant reduction in inflammatory cytokines (on the specific cytokines identified previously) and the mitigation of early-stage ECM dysregulation, specifically the favorable modulation of MMP2 levels, collagen type I expression, and the proportional area of retinal collagen fibers, achieved by ALKBH5 inhibition were entirely negated upon ERK1/2 pathway activation. These results demonstrate that ALKBH5 inhibition concurrently attenuates myopia progression, preserves retinal architecture, suppresses inflammation, and prevents early-stage ECM dysregulation, predominantly through selective suppression of ERK1/2 signaling. This positions ERK1/2 as a critical downstream effector mechanism for ALKBH5-targeted therapy in myopia.

To strengthen our mechanistic conclusions, we employed a genetic approach using ALKBH5-KO mice. The knockout consistently reproduced the protective effects of DDO-2728, demonstrating that ALKBH5 knockout confers a robust protective phenotype against myopia development, an effect that was associated with the concurrent suppression of retinal inflammation, preservation of ECM homeostasis, and inhibition of ERK1/2 pathway activation.

Although our findings delineate a novel ALKBH5/ERK1/2 signaling axis in myopia pathogenesis and demonstrate the therapeutic potential of its inhibition, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, the clinical correlation that establishes peripheral blood m6A reduction as a potential biomarker of myopia severity was derived from a cohort that primarily composed of young adults (age 25.289 ± 0.516 years) who were undergoing ICL surgery. This specific demographic, focused on surgical candidates within a relatively narrow age range, may not fully represent the broader spectrum of myopic populations, particularly children and adolescents in the critical phase of active myopia progression. Consequently, extrapolating the biomarker potential of blood m6A levels for widespread myopia screening or monitoring, especially in prevention strategies targeting younger individuals, necessitates validation in larger, more diverse cohorts that encompass the crucial developmental stages of myopia onset and progression. Secondly, the mechanistic insights and therapeutic efficacy of ALKBH5 inhibition were predominantly established using a murine FDM model. Although FDM is a well-established and valuable experimental paradigm, the complex etiology of human myopia, involving intricate interactions between genetic predisposition, prolonged near-work visual demands, and other environmental factors [75, 76], may not be fully recapitulated in this acute, monocular deprivation model. Therefore, the generalizability of the identified ALKBH5-ERK1/2 pathway and the translational promise of DDO-2728 require further substantiation across more phylogenetically diverse animal models (such as tree shrews or primates) and more complex experimental paradigms—such as lens-induced myopia models or genetic susceptibility models—that better recapitulate the multifaceted (optical, genetic, and environmental) etiology of human myopia, as well as through additional preclinical studies. Thirdly, although we establish the functional linkage between ALKBH5 inhibition and ERK1/2 signaling modulation leading to myopia protection, the precise molecular cascade connecting ALKBH5-mediated m6A demethylation to ERK1/2 hyperphosphorylation remains incompletely defined. Future investigations employing techniques such as m6A methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) are crucial to identify the direct mRNA targets of ALKBH5 in the myopic retina that may orchestrate ERK1/2 pathway activation. Furthermore, this study was conducted exclusively in male mice to control for potential sex-based variability. While this approach strengthened the internal validity of our mechanistic findings, it limits the generalizability of our results. Future studies are essential to confirm whether the ALKBH5-ERK1/2 pathway operates similarly in females and to explore potential sex-specific effects in myopia pathogenesis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study illuminates a previously unrecognized epitranscriptomic pathway that regulates myopia progression, wherein ALKBH5 upregulation in RGCs initiates a pathogenic cascade through selective hyperactivation of ERK1/2 signaling. The inverse correlation between peripheral blood m6A methylation levels and human myopia severity establishes RNA methylation as a promising biomarker candidate. Furthermore, both pharmacological inhibition (DDO-2728) and genetic ablation of ALKBH5 effectively suppresses ocular elongation, mitigates retinal thinning, attenuates retinal inflammation, and modulates early-stage ECM dysregulation, thereby providing complementary pharmacological and genetic preclinical evidence for its therapeutic potential. Crucially, the complete abolition of DDO-2728’s protective effects upon pharmacological ERK1/2 activation mechanistically establishes this signaling pathway as the essential downstream effector of ALKBH5-driven myopiagenesis. The concordance between the protective phenotypes observed in ALKBH5-KO mice and DDO-2728-treated mice strongly supports ALKBH5 as a highly promising therapeutic target. These findings not only shed light on longstanding questions concerning retinal signaling in refractive development but also propose an innovative mechanistic strategy for myopia intervention—of administering the ALKBH5 inhibitor DDO-2728 during myopia progression. Future investigations to validate this pathway in higher-order organisms or models and define direct ALKBH5 mRNA targets will accelerate the clinical translation of m6A-focused interventions, and thereby offer new avenues for managing the global myopia epidemic.
