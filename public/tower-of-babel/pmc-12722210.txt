Metal-organic framework modified carbon nanotubes for hydrogen production from formic acid with carbon dioxide capture and conversion
Authors: Dina Thole, Kwena D Modibane, Reineck Mhlaba, Sheriff A Balogun, Ebrahiem Botha, Nicholas M Musyoka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing demand for sustainable energy and the need to reduce carbon emissions have driven research into advanced materials for hydrogen production and CO₂ capture. This study presents a novel copper-based metal-organic framework (Cu-MOF) integrated with Multiwalled﻿ carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) as a photocatalyst for hydrogen (H₂) generation and carbon dioxide (CO₂) capture. The composite’s structural, thermal, and morphological properties were analyzed using FTIR, XRD, TGA, and SEM/EDS. Photocatalytic performance was evaluated using formic acid, yielding 11 mmol/g of gas with 75% H₂ purity, confirmed by gas chromatography. Post-reaction analysis revealed CO₂ capture and reversible photoelectrocatalytic behavior. Introduction

The interest in alternative energy resources to replace fossil fuels has become increasingly significant due to critical concerns such as air pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, and energy scarcity amidst a growing global population1–5. Consequently, to acquire an energy source that is completely renewable and environmentally friendly, numerous scientists and researchers have been diligently examining ways to alleviate this issue in recent times6,7. The prospect of H2 energy is encouraging as it shows potential as a dense, environmentally friendly, and sustainable energy source8. The utilization of H2 energy has emerged as an energy strategy aimed at easing the constraints on energy supply, ensuring energy security, and enhancing environmental sustainability9.

Formic acid is a secure and practical liquid storage molecule that can efficiently generate H2 in moderate conditions. It can be generated through the direct conversion of CO2 with H210. Researchers recognize the significance of formic substance due to its notable characteristics, including its ability to remain stable without catalysts, its liquid form at room temperature, its high hydrogen content (4.4 wt%), its environmentally friendly nature, its non-toxic properties, and its reversibility, meaning it can be decomposed to produce H2 and CO2, and be converted to formic acid again. These qualities have contributed to making it a highly researched and promising option as a liquid H2 carrier11.

Metal-Organic Frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as promising catalysts for H2 production from formic acid due to their tunable pore structures, high surface areas, and versatile active sites12–15. Specifically, MOFs functionalized with metal nanoparticles, such as Pd or Cu, exhibit remarkable catalytic activity for the decomposition of formic acid into hydrogen16. Wen and his co-workers reported on the use of Pd-MOF and produced a remarkably high H2 production rate of 42,000 mL h–1g–112, while Alamgholiloo and his team reported that Cu-MOF exhibited excellent catalytic activity to produce 147 mL of gas using formic acid. However, these studies report that without support, MOF is not stable17.

Multiwalled ﻿carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), graphene oxide, and zeolite are examples of support materials18. The dispersion could be enhanced by the extensive specific surface areas of the aforementioned nanomaterials. MWCNTs are exceptional candidates for MOF support materials in this context due to their exceptional mechanical strength and thermal stability.

The combination of MOFs and MWCNTs leverages the unique properties of both materials to create hybrid catalysts with enhanced catalytic activity and stability19. MOFs provide a scaffold for anchoring catalytic metal centers and facilitating the adsorption and activation of formic acid molecules20, while MWCNTs facilitate electron transfer kinetics and promote efficient mass transport21. The intimate integration of MOF nanoparticles with MWCNTs results in synergistic effects that amplify the catalytic performance, thereby enabling efficient H2 production from formic acid under mild reaction conditions.

This study aims to provide the latest insights into the development of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF catalysts for H2 production from formic acid. It highlights the pivotal role of MOF in photocatalytic H2 production, demonstrating their ability to function as active catalysts and as components in heterojunctions with MWCNTs serving as support materials. Furthermore, the research explores the dual capability of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF: capturing CO₂ generated during formic acid decomposition and facilitating the conversion of CO₂ back into formic acid. This approach provides a sustainable and recyclable pathway for H2 production from formic acid, offering significant potential for advancing clean energy technologies.

Experimental

Chemicals

Multiwalled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), with purity greater than 99.9 wt%. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 97–98 wt%), Nitric acid (HNO3, 65%), formic acid (HCOOH, 98–100 wt%), Nafion resin (5 wt% solution in aliphatic alcohols and water), copper nitrate trihydrate (Cu (NO3)2.3H2O, 99.9 wt%), ethylene glycol (EG), and N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich®. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) bottles were collected from the University of Limpopo waste bins, Polokwane, South Africa. Labels, rings, and caps were removed immediately. Double-distilled water was used throughout the experiments.

Characterization techniques

The Varian Cary 300 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer was used to obtain the UV-vis spectra at room temperature in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm. To determine the crystallinity of the synthesized catalyst, X-ray diffraction (XRD Phillips PW 1830, CuKα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å) was employed. The Spectrum II FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer) was used to confirm the desired functional groups on the catalyst. With a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ and a minimum of 32 scans, the spectra were captured at ambient temperature in the wavenumber range of 400 to 4000 cm⁻¹. Scanning electron microscope images of the samples were recorded with a Zeiss-DSM 960 microscope equipped with an EDS detector. The thermal degradation was studied using a PerkinElmer TGA thermogravimetric analyzer. The electrocatalysis for all the catalysts was done using the biological potentiostat instrument in a temperature-controlled lab at 25 °C. Gases being produced from the photoreactor with a UV lamp of 450 watts were identified and quantified by gas chromatography equipped with flame ionization detection (GC-FID) using a Shimadzu 2010 model instrument.

Catalyst Preparation

Oxidation of carbon nanotubes

Despite their excellent qualities, pure MWCNTs have limitations in a variety of applications because they are not soluble in most popular solvents, and they have a property to aggregate22. To resolve that, it is important to start by functionalizing them under acidic conditions to introduce carboxylic groups on their walls to increase their solubility and catalytic efficiency23. Functionalization of MWCNTs was done by chemical oxidation using sulfuric acid and nitric acid as reported by Singh et al.24 with adjustments. Commercial MWCNTs (0.3 g) were introduced to 5 M 98% sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and 5 M 65% nitric acid (HNO3) solutions in a 3:1 ratio, respectively, and vigorously stirred for 6 h to control the amount of oxygen functional groups. The solutions were then rinsed and filtered numerous times with distilled water through a vacuum filtration system until the solution pH reached 7–8. Subsequently, O-MWCNTs were produced as a precipitate. They were dried in an oven at 80 °C for 24 h.

Preparation of copper metal organic framework

The 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate (H2BDC) was synthesized from polyethylene terephthalate (PET), which is found in waste bottles. The synthetic procedures (Scheme 1, Reaction 1) are carried out in a 100 mL Teflon-lined hydrothermal reactor. Briefly, the reactor was filled with 60.00 mL of water (H2O), 3.0 g of waste bottle flakes, and 6.50 mL of ethylene glycol (EG). They were heated for 8 h at 210 °C. A centrifuge was used to separate the resulting solution, which was washed with ethanol and water in (1:1) ratios, respectively, and then dried for 24 h in an oven overnight. After drying, the white powder was ground with a pestle and mortar and regarded as H2BDC25. The process for synthesizing copper-based MOF (Scheme 1, Reaction 2) followed: approximately 8.00 g of Cu (NO3)2.3H2O salt, 1 mL of DMF, and 3.32 g of H2BDC were dissolved in deionized water, agitated for 15 min, and then ultrasonically mixed for 1 h. The reaction was transferred to an autoclave reactor and heated to 220 °C for 8 h. The final product (a copper-based MOF) was filtered, washed with distilled water, and dried in an oven at a temperature of 100 °C; the steps are outlined in Scheme 126.

Preparation of H2BDC, Cu-MOF and O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF.

Preparation of copper metal-organic framework-Carbon nanotubes composite

A composite of copper metal-organic framework nanotubes (Scheme 1, Reaction 3) was synthesized following established literature with some modifications27. In a standard procedure, 15 mmol Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and H2BDC were each dissolved in 30 mL ethanol through stirring. Subsequently, 0.1 g of O-MWCNTs were thoroughly dispersed in an ethanol solution through sonication for a duration of 30 min. Subsequently, the two uniform mixtures were rapidly combined with the introduction of 5 mL of DMF while maintaining continuous agitation until a gel was produced. Following a 12 h exposure to air, the gel underwent drying at 80 °C in an oven. Following drying, it was classified as O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF.

Results and discussion

Structural characterization

Figure 1a displays the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra of O-MWCNTs, Cu-BDC, and O-MWCNTs-Cu-BDC composite. The spectrum of oxidized MWCNTs displayed a characteristic band at 3300 cm⁻¹ due to the hydroxyl functional group, a less intense peak at 1600 cm⁻¹ caused by the C = O stretching vibration, 1345 cm⁻¹ (associated with O-H bending deformation in COOH), and 1300–1000 cm⁻¹ (may be assigned to C-H stretches). The presence of a hydroxyl group and C = O proves the successful oxidation of the carbon nanotubes by nitric and sulfuric acid28–31. FTIR spectra of Cu-BDC, which is also known as Cu-MOF, displayed absorption bands with one sharp peak at 3500 cm⁻¹, which is due to the hydroxyl group in the free region32. Then the spectra indicated sharp peaks with high intensity 1600 cm⁻¹, which related to asymmetric and symmetric stretching modes of coordinated carboxylic acid. Narrow bands at 1025 and 731 cm⁻¹ are attributed to δ(C–H) and γ(C–H) vibration of aromatic rings, respectively33–35. The Cu-O vibration is observed at lower wavelength 620 cm⁻¹36,37. In the FTIR spectrum of O-MWCNTs-Cu-BDC, there was a disappearance of the hydroxyl group, which appeared on both O-MWCNTs and Cu-BDC. This confirms that the metal (copper) centre of the Cu-BDC used that to link with the O-MWCNTs and the O-H bonds on the O-MWCNTs were engaged in the bondage38. A similar finding was reported by Radhika et al.39.

While metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) such as Cu-MOF exhibit high porosity, it is crucial to analyze dislocation density and conduct Williamson-Hall (W-H) analysis to gain more understanding about their structural integrity and performance. The dislocation density offers valuable information regarding the quantity of defects or irregularities present in the crystal lattice. This factor can significantly affect mechanical stability and the accessibility of active sites for catalytic reactions, including hydrogen production. A regulated number of defects can improve catalytic performance, whereas an overabundance of defects might undermine the stability of the framework. In the meantime, W-H analysis serves to distinguish between the contributions of crystallite size and lattice strain to the broadening observed in XRD peaks. This analysis is essential for assessing the crystallinity, internal strain, and microstructural characteristics of the Cu-MOF, all of which influence its surface area, porosity, and catalytic performance. Both parameters are crucial for evaluating the quality and potential performance of the MOF in applications such as hydrogen generation, where structural stability and efficient charge transfer play a significant role.

Optical characterizations

In addition to measuring the dispersion of nanomaterials in solvents, UV-vis absorption spectroscopy can be utilized to investigate the nanomaterials’ photocatalytic characteristics27. All UV results were measured before experiments to confirm wavelength consistency. According to the UV results in Fig. 3a, O-MWCNTs showed characteristic absorption in the region of 450–300 nm, which is broad. This peak is attributed to the π → π* transitions of aromatic rings (C-C bonds) and the n→ π* transitions of carbonyl groups (C = O bonds), respectively, which confirms the oxidation of the carbon nanotubes46. A Tauc plot (Fig. 3b) was also used to estimate the band gap energy of the nanoparticles. According to the Tauc plot, the band gap energy of the carbon nanotubes is estimated to be 5.24 eV. This band gap energy is notably high; this is due to the energy difference between the bonding and antibonding orbitals47,48. It is again caused by the strong C-C bonds, which are reported to have a very large band gap49. UV absorbance for the Cu-MOF shows an absorption spectrum of 320–380 nm, which agrees with the literature50. According to the Tauc plot, the energy band gap of Cu-MOF is 3 eV. It is interesting to note the absorption peak for the nanocomposite of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF, its band gap is 2.76 eV, which has drastically decreased due to the synergy of the carbon nanotubes and Cu-MOF. A reduction in the band gap energy indicates an increase in the catalyst efficiency for hydrogen production. Hence, the integration of O-MWCNTs as a support for Cu-MOF has led to a notable enhancement in its photocatalytic activity. The band gap energies (Eg) were estimated from Tauc plots derived from UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectra. The conduction band (CB) and valence band (VB) edge potentials were estimated using electronegativity-based empirical equations. The CB edge was calculated using the formula: ECB = χ – Ee – (Eg/2), and the VB was derived using EVB = ECB + Eg, where χ is the absolute electronegativity of the material (estimated from the geometric mean of the constituent atoms), Ee is the energy of free electrons on the hydrogen scale (taken as 4.5 eV), and Eg is the band gap in electron volts. All calculated band edge potentials are reported versus the Normal Hydrogen Electrode (NHE) at pH 0. These values were used to assess the redox capabilities of the photocatalysts and to verify their suitability for photocatalytic hydrogen evolution under UV-light irradiation. Nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms

The BET surface area technique was used to assess the nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms of Cu-MOF, individual CNTs, and their composites. According to Table 3, the specific surface areas of the O-MWCNTs, Cu-MOF, and O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF were 4.289 m²/g, 15.06 m²/g, and 6.064 m²/g, respectively. The N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms of the catalysts fall under the typical IV isotherm category, which is associated with mesoporous materials (Fig. 4a)51,52. The discrepancy between the expected high surface area of Cu-MOFs and its corresponding composites based on their structure and the lower surface area measured by the BET method, particularly concerning nitrogen adsorption, can indeed raise concerns about sample purity. This suggests that the Cu-MOF structure might not be fully accessible or that impurities are blocking pores, hindering adsorption. The pore-size distribution is determined using the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) approach, and the findings are shown in Fig. 4b. The pore-size distribution of all the materials was between 10 and 20 nm, placing them in the mesopore area. The decrease in the total surface area of Cu-MOF may be a result of the decrease in the pore width and pore blockage by the O-MWCNTs in the pores of Cu-MOF. These results can be confirmed by SEM, where the MWCNTs can be seen tangled around the MOF structure.

Raman analysis

Raman spectroscopy was employed to investigate the structural features and confirm the successful integration of O-MWCNTs with Cu-MOF. The Raman spectra of O-MWCNTs, Cu-MOF, and their composites were analyzed and compared as shown in Fig. 5a. The Raman spectrum of pristine MWCNTs displayed two prominent peaks: the D band around ~ 1344 cm⁻¹, and the G band around ~ 1584 cm⁻¹53. The D band is attributed to the breathing modes of sp³ carbon atoms, indicating structural defects or disorder in the carbon lattice54. The G band corresponds to the in-plane vibration of sp²-hybridized carbon atoms in a graphitic structure55. The intensity ratio I-D/I-G gives the degree of disorder; a lower ratio suggests a higher degree of graphitization56. In this study, the I-D/I-G ratio of O-MWCNTs was found to be 0.56, indicating a relatively low structural integrity of the nanotubes. The Raman spectrum of Cu-MOF exhibited several distinct peaks corresponding to its organic linker and metal coordination environment. Characteristic bands were observed at approximately 1600 cm⁻¹ and 1450 cm⁻¹, which are assigned to C = C stretching vibrations of the aromatic linkers. Peaks in the region of 500 cm⁻¹ and around 300–400 cm−1were attributed to Cu–O vibrational modes, confirming the coordination between copper ions and the organic ligands. The Raman spectrum of the composite material displayed features from both O-MWCNTs and Cu-MOF, indicating successful hybridization. The D and G bands of O-MWCNTs were preserved at D at ~ 1344 cm⁻¹, G at ~ 1584 cm⁻¹) and a change in intensity ratio (I-D/I-G) decreased to 0.50. This shift suggests an interaction between MWCNTs and Cu-MOF, possibly due to π–π stacking or coordination with the Cu centers, which may influence the electronic structure of the carbon nanotubes. The adsorption of CO2 on the surface of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF was also confirmed by Raman spectra (Fig. 5b) where the C = O peak was more intense after hydrogen production which agrees with the EDS, and the FTIR.

Photocatalysis results

Typically, 1 g of the catalyst was placed in a 200 mL volumetric flask. The UV light was placed 1 cm apart from the reaction vessel. Followed by adding formic acid of the desired concentration into the flask, then stirred at 750 rpm to aid in mass transfer. The volume of gas released during the reaction was measured using the burette water displacement technique. To validate the gases produced during the photocatalytic decomposition of formic acid, the photoreactor was coupled with gas chromatography (GC) equipped with a flame ionization detector. The GC column was cleaned with helium, followed by running standard 99,9% of pure H2 and CO2 to confirm the retention time of the produced gasses during photocatalysis. See appendix section for standard hydrogen and carbon dioxide. To establish the photocatalytic activity of the catalysts, experiments were carried out first in the absence of light with the catalyst and then in the presence of light without the catalyst. In both circumstances, there was no hydrogen evolution. Only when both light and a catalyst were present during the reaction was hydrogen produced.

As exhibited in Fig. 6a, all the samples exhibit photocatalytic H2 production. However, O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF shows improved H2 output as compared to individual components. The corresponding gas produced is 7.6 mmolg⁻¹, 7.8 mmolg⁻¹, and 11.2 mmolg⁻¹ for the O-MWCNTs, Cu-MOF, and O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF samples, respectively. This indicates that the synergy between the O-MWCNTs and the Cu-MOF facilitates the charge transfer and thus accelerates the production kinetics. This is again proven by the graph of the rate of gas production against time as shown in Fig. 6b, which shows that the composite reaches a production rate of 181 µmol.g.min⁻¹, which is greater than that of the Cu-MOF and O-MWCNTs. In order to confirm the real production of H2, the photoreactor was coupled with a GC equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). The results are shown in Fig. 6c. The column was cleaned with helium gas to remove any impurities, followed by the injection of gases formed from the photocatalytic formic acid from the photoreactor by different catalysts, which were O-MWCNTs, Cu-MOF, and O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF. The decomposition of formic acid produces two gases, namely H2 and CO2. The peak of H2 is shown at a retention time of 0.95 min, while that of carbon dioxide appears at 1.8 min. The two peaks responsible for those gases are present in the GC data in Fig. 6c; however, H2 peaks are more dominant than the CO2 peaks for all the catalysts. Table 4 shows the calculated purity of the hydrogen produced with the composite, giving 75%, while 71% and 63.8% are that of Cu-MOF and O-MWCNTs, respectively; this indicates that the material has the capability to absorb CO2.

The mechanism of H2 production from formic acid using the composite. Upon illumination with UV light, the Cu-MOF absorbs photons, exciting electrons from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), creating electron-hole pairs. The excited electrons in the conduction band of the Cu-MOF are transferred to the carbon nanotubes. Copper acts as a catalyst or co-catalyst, enhancing electron transfer and selectivity during hydrogen evolution reactions. Functionalized MWCNTs provide an excellent conductivity, and active sites for improved catalyst dispersion. Both Cu and O-MWCNTs create synergistic effects, lowering overpotential and boosting H₂ production efficiency. The holes remain in the valence band of the Cu-MOF, ready to participate in oxidation reactions. Formic acid (HCOOH) acts as the substrate. The holes in the Cu-MOF valence band oxidize formic acid to produce carbon dioxide (CO₂) and protons (H⁺). The protons (H⁺) generated from formic acid reduction at the surface of the O-MWCNTs are reduced by the electrons (transferred from Cu-MOF) to produce hydrogen gas (H₂). The overall reaction for the photocatalytic decomposition of formic acid to produce hydrogen and carbon dioxide is displayed in Fig. 6d and Eq. 2. Additionally, its recyclable pathway where carbon dioxide reacts with hydrogen to regenerate formic acid is represented by Eq. (3).

First, CO2 preferentially forms bonds with M2+ ions (Cu2+) with the strong electrostatic interaction between the quadrupole moments of CO2 with M2+ ions. Next, CO2 is adsorbed by the materials capillaries by Van der Waals interactions until adsorption equilibrium is reached as shown in in Scheme 2b57. Due to low surface area of the catalyst, the adsorption equilibrium of CO2 is quickly reached on to the surface then the CO2 leak to the atmosphere, as evidenced by the GC results where only 25% of CO2 was detected by the GC during hydrogen production. For the uncaptured CO2 to recycle back to formic acid. Closed electrochemical cell, as evidenced by the CV results, was used where small reduction peak was shown, which indicated the reverse of the uncaptured CO2 back to formic acid. The photoelectrochemical (PEC) conversion of CO₂ back to formic acid (HCOOH) involves a photoanode-cathode system that drives the reduction of CO₂ using photogenerated electrons and protons. Under light illumination, a semiconductor photoelectrode photons, generating electron-hole pairs. The photogenerated electrons migrate to the cathode, where they participate in the CO₂ reduction reaction, which subsequently convert into formic acid (HCOOH). Meanwhile, photogenerated holes at the anode drive the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) by oxidizing water (from 1 M formic acid solution), maintaining charge balance in the system. The high affinity of Cu-based MOFs and O-MWCNT-based composite catalysts for CO₂ adsorption and efficient proton transfer of conversion enhance the selectivity of capture and hydrogen production. Cu-based catalysts are generally considered highly selective for formic acid production, especially when combined with supporting materials like O-MWCNTs, which help enhance the electronic properties of the catalyst, promoting more efficient CO₂ activation and suppression of competing reactions like CO production, as evidenced by the GC results. Additionally, CO2 is easily converted to formic acid unlike CO.

The FTIR of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF before and after photocatalysis is shown in Fig. 6e. From the results of FTIR after photocatalysis and the emergence of new peaks, the carbonyl stretches at around 1650 cm−1, this is due to the absorbed CO2 on the catalyst. The products of formic acid decomposition are CO2 and H2. The results show that some of the CO2 is adsorbed on the surface of the catalyst. These results agree with what was observed in TGA analysis (Fig. 6f). TGA shows that the nanocomposite degrades at lower temperatures as compared to their degradation temperature before catalysis due to the desorbed CO2 from the surface. Comparing composites before and after photocatalysis using XRD (Fig. 6g), a major decrease in a peak at 2θ = 9.1° on the composite after use is observed; this indicates that the structure loses its crystallinity. The SEM morphology of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF after photocatalysis (Fig. 6h) showed that the nanotubes were thinner and shorter length as compared to before photocatalysis. The calculated diameter of the tubes was 78 nm before hydrogen production and 46 nm after H2 production. Thus, the treatment of the composite with formic acid causes severe itching of the graphitic surface of the material, leading to tubes of shorter length with a large population of disordered sites. This display of surface disruption is due to the thermal heating of the solution during photocatalysis caused by the light radiation of the sample and the harsh environment of formic acid. From the EDS spectrum (Fig. 6i), there is a clear increase in the carbon content of the material after photocatalysis. These results agree with a more intense carbonyl peak on the FTIR; together, they show that the composite can capture CO2 during a photocatalytic reaction.

Reusability

The reusability of the Cu-MOF, O-MWCNTs and composite for hydrogen production using formic acid has been evaluated (Fig. 7), revealing that the Cu-MOF and the composite do not exhibit stable catalytic performance while reused. The continuous reaction and interaction with formic acid and might weaken the metal-ligand bonds, leading to collapse of the MOF framework, this is supported by the XRD results where the disappearance of a highly intense peak on the used Cu-MOF results which indicates the crystallinity of the MOF structure. has collapsed. Additionally, the decomposition of the Cu-MOF structure weakens the interaction between Cu species and the O-MWCNTs support, reducing electron transfer efficiency and overall catalytic activity for the composite. Furthermore, Cu-MOFs are sensitive to acidic environments, leading to dissolution. as reported by Li et al. and Liu and Gao58,59. As a result, without effective regeneration strategies, such as post-reaction treatment or structural modifications to enhance stability, the long-term applicability of O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF composites in hydrogen production from formic acid remains limited.

Electrocatalysis

To investigate the conversion of CO2 to formic acid, electrochemical methods were employed in the presence and absence of light. The cyclic voltammetry curves of formic acid oxidation and reduction are presented. Figure 8a shows a cyclic voltammogram of formic acid in the dark; the results show only an oxidation peak at −0.2 V. However, upon light radiation, as seen by the results in Fig. 8b, there is a clear oxidation peak at ∼0.5 V, which corresponds to direct oxidation of HCOOH to CO2 + H2 as compared with the inset of the bare electrode analysis as a reference. On the reverse scan, there is also the presence of a peak that shows a direct reduction of CO2 + H2 to formic acid. The presence of light did not only assist in high current response on the CV results, but it has also improved the reversibility of CO2 + H2 back to formic acid. Figure 8c displays the LSV curve of the materials, which is in line with the oxidation process. To thoroughly investigate the electron transport characteristics of these electrodes, EIS research was carried out in 1 M formic acid solution via an EC-lab workstation. Figure 8d displays the Nyquist plots of the fitted data of the electrodes, while Fig. 8e represents equivalent circuits employed to fit the obtained data. The EIS results obtained were fitted using the circuit’s components, such as solution resistance (Rs), capacitance (C), and charge transfer resistance (Rct). The generated EIS-fitted data and individual chi-square (x2) values are shown in Table 5. The negative X2 values and the minimal percentage errors in the parentheses showed that the EIS data were successfully fitted. The Rct values recorded for the electrodes, as presented in Table 5, are 31.6, 23.6, 11.7, and 3.59 Ω for the O-MWCNTs, Cu-MOF, and O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF, respectively. O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF possess the lowest Rct, demonstrating their capacity to transfer electrons quickly over other electrodes. The high conductivity of the MWCNTs, which act as a superior electron conductor on the Cu-MOF surface, is what gives O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF their quick electron transfer capability. The bare electrode was effectively modified with the catalysts, as evidenced by the decrease in the semicircle and Rct values of the modified electrodes60. The CV results agree with the EIS data, where observed increased current output in CV gives a small Rct or small semicircle in EIS. The resistance that charge carriers meet during their transfer between the electrocatalyst surface and the electrolyte solution is denoted by Rct. Faster kinetics, which suggest more effective electron transfer during the hydrogen evolution process, are shown by lower charge transfer resistance61,62. Furthermore, chronoamperometry (CA) was used to test stability of the cell at voltage 0.3 V. Stability refers to the capacity to retain performance under constant current and voltage conditions63. All the catalysts showed high stability as shown by the CA in Fig. 8f.

Conclusion

In summary, high-yield H2 production was successfully achieved through the photocatalytic decomposition of formic acid using O-MWCNTs-Cu-MOF photocatalysts under UV light irradiation. The process produced an optimal gas yield of 11 mmol/g at a production rate of 180 µmol/g·min⁻¹, with 75% of the output being pure H2, as confirmed by gas chromatography (GC) analysis. Post-reaction characterization of the catalyst revealed two key findings: firstly, a slight structural degradation of the Cu-MOF occurred due to the harsh conditions of formic acid and UV light exposure; secondly, the catalyst effectively captured CO2 during formic acid decomposition. Electrochemical studies further demonstrated that the uncaptured CO2 could be recycled back into formic acid, establishing a sustainable CO2 recycling mechanism. This work introduces a novel strategy for achieving high-yield hydrogen production under ambient conditions with solar irradiation, providing a promising platform for advancing solar-to-fuel energy conversion technologies.
