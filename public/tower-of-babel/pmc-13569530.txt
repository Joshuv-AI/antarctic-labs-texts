Species-specific patterns in fine-root traits and their coordination in the multidimensional root economics space at the Mongolian forest-steppe ecotone
Authors: Naoki Makita, Taiga Masumoto, Davaajav Dalkhsuren, Gerelbaatar Sukhbaatar, Baatarbileg Nachin, Tetsuoh Shirota, Koh Yasue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the bioclimatically sensitive forest-steppe ecotone of northern Mongolia, belowground strategies can be elucidated using an integrated approach that considers root functional traits and symbiotic associations. We elucidated how three dominant tree species, namely Larix sibirica, Pinus sylvestris, and Betula platyphylla, coordinate their morphological, chemical, and symbiotic (ectomycorrhizal [EM]) traits within a multidimensional root economics space (RES) to adapt to this harsh ecotone. Although the RES framework generally associates thicker roots with greater reliance on fungi, EM colonization was highest in the thinnest-rooted species, B. platyphylla, with the highest specific root length (SRL), and lowest in the thickest-rooted species, L. sibirica, with the lowest SRL. Principal component analysis revealed that the primary axis of variation represents a synergistic acquisition gradient, rather than a traditional trade-off between morphological exploration and symbiotic associations. Along this axis, SRL and EM colonization were positively coordinated, indicating that B. platyphylla maximized its resource acquisition capacity by integrating high soil exploration with intensive fungal collaboration. This synergy is likely driven by higher root branching intensity in thinner roots, which provides more infection sites for EM fungi. In contrast, the conifers exhibit different root-trait combinations: P. sylvestris optimizes structural and chemical conservation, whereas L. sibirica employs a decoupled strategy to minimize symbiotic investment and maintain high metabolic potential. Our study highlights that tree species coexist by employing divergent resource-acquisition pathways, ranging from integrated combinations to resource-conservative trait syndromes, under intensifying climatic stress in Central Asia.Keywords: Boreal ecosystem, Belowground, Collaboration gradient, Ectomycorrhizal symbiosis, Resource acquisition

Introduction

The forest-steppe ecotone in northern Mongolia is a bioclimatically sensitive region in Central Asia, where extreme temperature fluctuations and chronic water scarcity severely limit tree survival (Dulamsuren et al. 2010, 2023). In these semi-arid landscapes, ecosystem functions, particularly C sequestration and water cycling, are tightly coupled with hydroclimatic variability, making them vulnerable to the shifting precipitation patterns associated with global climate change (Poulter et al. 2014). Recent eddy covariance studies at the Mongolian Forest Research Station underscore ecosystem vulnerability at the local scale, showing that C and water vapor fluxes are highly sensitive to episodic drought and soil moisture depletion (Kotani et al. 2025). Furthermore, anthropogenic and natural disturbances such as large-scale forest fires and illegal logging, which significantly inhibit natural regeneration, compromise the resilience of these forests (Sakamoto et al. 2021). In this high-stress environment, the survival of dominant woody species depends on their ability to optimize resource acquisition through specialized belowground strategies.

Fine-root functional traits are indicators of a plant’s investment strategy between resource acquisition and conservation. Variations in root diameter, a foundational trait, influence soil exploration efficiency, such as specific root length (SRL) and the plant’s reliance on symbiotic fungi (Ma et al. 2018; Ding et al. 2023). SRL represents the volume of soil explored per unit of C invested, whereas root tissue density (RTD) is associated with physical persistence and exhibits a trade-off with acquisition speed (Freschet et al. 2018; Kou et al. 2026). Furthermore, the root N content serves as a proxy for metabolic activity and nutrient uptake capacity (Makita et al. 2015). These traits do not vary independently, but are coordinated through structural trade-offs, often linked to internal anatomy, such as stele-cortex allometry, leading to nonlinear trait relationships (Kong et al. 2019; Li et al. 2023). Such trait syndromes are shaped by species phylogeny and mycorrhizal affiliations (Yahara et al. 2019). Importantly, in ectomycorrhizal (EM) associations, the coordination between root morphology and fungal colonization may differ from that in global arbuscular mycorrhizal models because EM fungi primarily colonize root tips, making root branching architecture a key determinant of symbiotic intensity (Ding et al. 2020; Kou et al. 2026).

The fine-root functional capacity is closely linked to their symbiotic microbiomes, which mediate plant responses to global environmental change (Trivedi et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2025). In particular, EM fungi are essential for nutrient acquisition in high-stress ecosystems (Cudlin et al. 2007; Ding et al. 2023; Moora et al. 2025). These interactions have been integrated into the multidimensional root economics space (RES) framework (Bergmann et al. 2020; Weigelt et al. 2021), which unifies multifaceted functional traits into a comprehensive conceptual model. The RES proposes that belowground strategies vary along two independent axes: a conservation gradient (resource turnover) and a collaboration gradient representing the trade-off between self-driven morphological investment and fungal outsourcing (Matthus et al. 2025). Although the collaboration gradient is often described as a trade-off between morphological investment and fungal outsourcing, for certain tree types, these pathways can be synergistic rather than mutually exclusive (Ding et al. 2020). This multidimensional approach is essential for capturing interspecific variation and niche differentiation across environmental gradients (Masumoto et al. 2026; Mrak et al. 2026; Tawa et al. 2026). However, the cost-benefit balance of symbiosis, which is highly context-dependent under extreme abiotic stress, influences the success of these strategies (Bennett and Groten 2022; Moora et al. 2025). Therefore, understanding how trees coordinate their root-microbe associations is crucial for predicting forest resilience in drought-prone and temperature-limited environments.

Despite these theoretical advances, the empirical applications of multidimensional root frameworks in extreme semi-arid environments remain limited. This study characterizes the fine-root trait syndromes and symbiotic patterns of three dominant tree species, Larix sibirica, Pinus sylvestris, and Betula platyphylla, in a Mongolian forest-steppe ecotone. Specifically, we evaluate how these species coordinate their morphological (SRL, RTD, and diameter), chemical (N content), and EM symbiotic traits within the RES. By simultaneously quantifying root traits and mycorrhizal infection in a cold, semi-arid climate, we aim to elucidate the belowground mechanisms and resource acquisition strategies that facilitate species coexistence and survival under escalating climatic stress.

Materials and methods

Study sites and target species

We conducted field research at the Udleg Forest Research and Training Station of the National University of Mongolia (48°15′N, 106°50′E; 1338 m a.s.l.) in the Batsumber district, Tov province, approximately 50 km north of Ulaanbaatar. The study area featured a forest-steppe ecosystem, with grasslands and forests dominating the south- and north-facing slopes, respectively (Sakamoto et al. 2021). The soils in this region are predominantly classified as Dark Kastanozems or Phaeozems with thin organic layers. In northern Mongolian forest-steppe ecotones, topsoils under Larix sibirica and Betula platyphylla forests generally exhibit total C and N concentrations of 3.5–5.0% and 0.2–0.4%, respectively, with characteristically high C/N ratios of 13–16 (Dulamsuren et al. 2010). Due to low mean annual temperatures and limited moisture, nitrogen mineralization proceeds slowly in these soils, leading to an inorganic nitrogen pool dominated by ammonium with extremely low nitrate concentrations. Consequently, plant-available N is strictly constrained, forming a major nutrient bottleneck in this ecosystem. The mean annual air temperature and precipitation were − 0.50 °C and approximately 385 mm (2010–2012), respectively, with 80% falling during the growing season from June to August (Miyazaki et al. 2014). The primary forest types included the deciduous L. sibirica Ledeb. stands, B. platyphylla Suk. stands, and mixed boreal forests containing evergreen conifers, such as P. sylvestris L. (Otoda et al. 2013; Miyazaki et al. 2014; Sakamoto et al. 2021). We selected three dominant tree species for this study: B. platyphylla (an early successional deciduous broad-leaved species), L. sibirica (an early to mid-successional deciduous conifer), and P. sylvestris (a late-successional evergreen conifer). All species formed symbiotic associations with EM fungi.

Root sampling

In late August 2023, we established a 50 m × 50 m plot for each species and selected 10 mature target trees per plot. The diameter at breast height was 18.7 ± 5.1 cm (mean ± SD) for B. platyphylla, 40.7 ± 11.7 cm for L. sibirica, and 36.5 ± 8.9 cm for P. sylvestris. To collect fine-root systems, two sampling points were selected within 0.5–1.5 m of the stem base of each tree. The root systems were gently exposed from the top 15 cm of the soil horizon, including the organic layer. Species identity was confirmed by tracing lateral roots directly back to the main root collars of the target trees, alongside species-specific visual characteristics based on the diameter, branching pattern, and the color and texture of the root bark and epidermis. From each sampling point, intact fine-root branches containing 1st - to 3rd -order roots (approximately 5–10 cm in length, all with root diameters < 2.0 mm, and predominantly < 1.0 mm) were carefully excised using sharp shears. Each sampling point served as an independent replicate (n = 20 per species; 10 trees × 2 sampling points). The root samples were immediately placed in a cooler to maintain moisture and transported to the laboratory within 1 h.

In the laboratory, living roots were distinguished from dead roots by their color and elasticity (Ohashi et al. 2025). We selected intact fine-root segments (typically 1st- to 3rd-order roots), all with root diameters < 2.0 mm, and predominantly < 1.0 mm). These segments were carefully cleaned with tap and distilled water to remove soil and organic matter. Each segment was divided into one sample for morphological and chemical analyses, and one for mycorrhizal colonization assessment.

Root morphological and chemical analyses

We scanned sample roots for morphological analysis in color at 600 dpi (GT-S650, Epson, Nagano, Japan). We determined total root length (m), volume (cm³), and average root diameter (AD; mm) using WinRHIZO Pro 2013a (Regent Instruments, Quebec, Canada). After scanning, we oven-dried the samples at 50 °C for at least 48 h to determine their dry masses. SRL (m g⁻¹) was calculated as total root length divided by dry mass, and RTD (g cm⁻³) was calculated as dry mass divided by total root volume. Then, we ground the dried segments into a fine powder and measured root N content (mg g⁻¹) using an elemental analyzer (Flash EA 1112, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Mycorrhizal colonization analysis

We randomly selected approximately 100 root tips from one root segment per sampling point and examined them under an optical microscope to determine EM colonization rates. We classified root tips as colonized if they exhibited characteristic swelling, fungal mantle, and the presence of extraradical hyphae (Freschet et al. 2021). We calculated the EM colonization rate as the percentage of colonized root tips relative to the total number of observed root tips.

Statistical analysis

We calculated the mean values of root morphological traits (AD, SRL, and RTD), chemical traits (N content), and mycorrhizal colonization for each sampling point (n = 20 per species). We tested differences between species for each trait using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s HSD test (P < 0.05). We performed principal component analysis (PCA) based on a correlation matrix using standardized variables across all measured traits to characterize the overall fine-root trait syndromes and evaluate species positions within the multidimensional RES. R version 4.1.2 was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Root length distribution across diameter classes

Root length distribution across the diameter classes varied markedly among the three species (Fig. 1). B. platyphylla exhibited a distinct strategy, with most of its total root length being concentrated in the finest diameter class (< 0.2 mm), which accounted for approximately 63% of the total root length. The dominance of extremely fine roots indicated a high capacity for self-driven soil exploration. In contrast to B. platyphylla, the two conifers allocated a higher proportion of root length to the thicker classes (> 0.2 mm). Specifically, L. sibirica showed the highest length proportion in the 0.3–0.5 mm class among the three species. P. sylvestris gradually allocated a proportion of root length across the 0.2–0.7 mm class.

Interspecific variation in morphological and chemical traits

We observed significant differences among the three species for all measured fine-root traits (P < 0.05; Fig. 2). Regarding morphological traits, B. platyphylla was characterized by the thinnest roots, the highest spatial acquisition efficiency, the lowest diameter (Fig. 2a), and the highest SRL (Fig. 2b). L. sibirica showed the opposite trend, with the highest AD and lowest SRL values. P. sylvestris displayed intermediate values for AD and SRL, although its morphology was closer to L. sibirica than to B. platyphylla.

Species differed significantly in their resource conservation strategies. B. platyphylla exhibited a unique combination of having the highest RTD (Fig. 2c) and N content (Fig. 2d). The two conifers also exhibited different strategies. L. sibirica had higher N content than P. sylvestris, with values comparable to those of B. platyphylla. In contrast, P. sylvestris had a higher RTD than L. sibirica, with RTD values comparable to those of B. platyphylla.

Ectomycorrhizal colonization

The EM colonization rates showed significant interspecific variation (P < 0.05; Fig. 3). B. platyphylla exhibited the highest colonization rate (approximately 62%), followed by P. sylvestris. L. sibirica had a significantly lower colonization rate than B. platyphylla. In this study, species with the thinnest roots, such as B. platyphylla, showed the highest level of fungal collaboration.

Multidimensional coordination of root traits

The PCA revealed a clear separation among the three species, with the first two axes explaining 66.7% of the total variance (Fig. 4). The first principal component (PC1) accounted for 39.0% of the variance and was defined by positive coordination between SRL and EM colonization, both of which were negatively associated with root diameter. B. platyphylla was positioned at the positive end of the axis and was associated with thinner roots, higher SRL, and higher EM colonization, representing a synergistic acquisition strategy. L. sibirica was positioned at the opposite end and was characterized by thicker roots, lower SRL, and lower EM colonization.

The second principal component (PC2) explained 27.7% of the variance and was mainly driven by the relationship between the RTD and root N content. Along this axis, the species were differentiated by their conservation-metabolism trade-offs; L. sibirica was associated with a higher N content but lower RTD, whereas P. sylvestris showed a higher RTD but lower N content. B. platyphylla displayed high values for both traits, indicating a high metabolic activity paired with high tissue persistence.

Discussion

Phylogenetic constraints and interspecific variation in trait syndromes

Our findings reveal distinct interspecific variations in fine-root morphological and chemical traits among the three dominant species in the Mongolian forest-steppe ecotone (Figs. 1 and 2). These differences align with the global patterns reported by Hou et al. (2024), in which plant functional types (angiosperms vs. gymnosperms) are the primary drivers of root trait variation. Specifically, the angiosperm B. platyphylla exhibits significantly higher SRL and thinner root diameters than the gymnosperms P. sylvestris and L. sibirica.

Notably, B. platyphylla exhibits a unique syndrome characterized by high N content and RTD. Although extreme environments often filter traits toward resource conservation (low N, high RTD; Freschet et al. 2018), the combination observed in B. platyphylla suggests a specialized adaptation to the short, severe Mongolian growing season. This high-N, high-RTD syndrome likely enables the species to maximize resource acquisition during a brief window of favorable conditions, while ensuring tissue persistence during severe winter freezing (Kong et al. 2019; Ding et al. 2020). In contrast, the two conifers exhibited more conservative morphological traits, with L. sibirica representing the most conservative, low-investment end of the morphological spectrum, characterized by the largest average diameter and lowest SRL.

Integration into the multidimensional RES

The coordination of root functional traits and symbiotic patterns provides strong empirical support for a multidimensional RES framework (Weigelt et al. 2021; Matthus et al. 2025). In this study, integrating symbiotic patterns into the RES reveals unique EM-specific trait coordination, representing a significant divergence from the global collaboration gradient traditionally defined by arbuscular mycorrhizal models. According to the framework of Bergmann et al. (2020), thicker-rooted species are expected to outsource resource uptake by fungi, whereas thinner-rooted species follow a do-it-yourself strategy. However, our results show the opposite; the thinnest-rooted species, B. platyphylla, exhibits the highest EM colonization, whereas the thickest-rooted species, L. sibirica, shows the lowest (Fig. 3).

This reverse collaboration pattern is a key feature of the EM associations. Unlike arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, which colonize the root cortex, EM fungi primarily infect root tips. Ding et al. (2020) and Kou et al. (2026) propose that species with thinner roots and higher branching intensities have a greater number of infection points per unit of root mass. Consequently, for these EM species, root fineness and fungal symbiosis are positively coordinated rather than exhibiting a trade-off. This synergistic coordination aligns with global patterns described by the RES framework, which demonstrates that fine-root morphological traits and mycorrhizal collaboration function as complementary dimensions rather than mutually exclusive trade-offs (Bergmann et al. 2020). This synergistic coordination appears to be an effective ecological strategy for coping with the hydroclimatic constraints of semi-arid ecosystems, where resource supply is both pulse-driven and spatially heterogeneous (de la Riva et al. 2021). In such water-limited environments, plants often face trade-offs between rapid resource acquisition and structural protection (Brunner et al. 2015). Furthermore, because cold and dry climatic conditions severely retard organic matter decomposition and nitrogen mineralization in these forest-steppe soils, efficient foraging for both water and inorganic/organic N is essential for plant survival. The overlap of high SRL and high EM colonization observed in B. platyphylla demonstrates a complementary belowground strategy: high SRL enables rapid soil exploration to capture pulse-driven precipitation in shallow soil layers, whereas EM hyphae extend the effective absorptive surface into soil micropores that remain inaccessible to plant root hairs, while simultaneously facilitating enzymatic N mobilization from organic pools (Bergmann et al. 2020). Thus, B. platyphylla exhibits covariation between morphology and symbiosis, maximizing nutrient and water uptake by combining high soil exploration with intensive fungal collaboration.

In this trait space, the two conifers are segregated along the PC2 axis (Fig. 4). P. sylvestris roots represent the most conserved end of the structural and chemical spectra, exhibiting high tissue density coupled with low N content. This investment reflects a classic self-protective strategy that focuses on maximizing root tissue longevity (Freschet et al. 2018; Hou et al. 2024). Despite this high structural conservation, P. sylvestris maintains an intermediate level of EM colonization along the primary PC1 axis (Fig. 4). This apparent decoupling suggests that fungal symbiosis may prioritize baseline ecological defense, protecting long-lived tissues from pathogens or severe moisture deficit, rather than intensive nutrient uptake (Ma et al. 2018; Trivedi et al. 2022). Although speculative, this functional hypothesis requires further community-level investigations of mycorrhizal fungi.

In contrast, L. sibirica roots display a unique two-faceted response across the two dimensions. Along the dominant PC1 axis, L. sibirica behaves conservatively by maintaining thick roots and minimizing fungal colonization (Fig. 4), thereby reducing external C costs. However, its internal profile shifts toward a high metabolic potential, characterized by high root N content and lower tissue density. This indicates that, although L. sibirica avoids morphological exploration and mycorrhizal costs, it simultaneously retains high physiological activity within its thick tissues. This strategy could be an effective evolutionary adaptation for surviving the highly unpredictable resource supply of the forest-steppe, allowing for rapid internal activation during small precipitation pulses (Dulamsuren et al. 2010; Kotani et al. 2025) without the continuous consumption of extensive fungal networks.

The divergent root strategies observed in this study, ranging from synergistic acquisition to low conservative investment, have important implications for forest resilience to intensifying drought in Mongolia (Kotani et al. 2025). This divergence into contrasting functional syndromes suggests that the harsh environmental filters of the forest-steppe ecotone drive species toward divergent adaptation strategies to ensure survival. The synergistic strategy of B. platyphylla facilitates rapid growth during the short growing season; however, its high metabolic activity and reliance on fungal networks may result in high C demand, potentially increasing its vulnerability during prolonged droughts under restricted C assimilation (Bennett and Groten 2022).

Conversely, the conservative strategy of L. sibirica and intermediate strategy of P. sylvestris may provide greater stability. L. sibirica avoids heavy reliance on high-cost symbiosis, which could be critical for survival in the most water-limited sites in the forest-steppe (Dulamsuren et al. 2023). It is also worth noting that the Mongolian forest-steppe ecotone sits near the southern limit of sporadic permafrost distribution (Miyazaki et al. 2014). In this unique hydroclimatic setting, the seasonal thawing of the active layer can provide a crucial deep-soil water supply, buffering trees against extreme atmospheric drought despite low annual precipitation (385 mm). The fine-root trait synergies in shallow soil layers in our study (e.g., high SRL and EM collaboration in B. platyphylla) are primarily optimized for capturing topsoil pulse precipitation and localized organic nutrients. However, interactions between shallow absorptive roots and deep permafrost-derived moisture reserves warrant further investigation. Determining whether these root trait syndromes are uniquely shaped by permafrost-affected hydrology or reflect general patterns applicable across broader arid and semi-arid ecosystems represents an important direction for future research. Furthermore, testing how these simple functional traits (e.g., SRL, RTD, root diameter, and root N content) vary intraspecific compared to the same species growing in less stress-prone European or Siberian regions (e.g., Hou et al. 2024; Kou et al. 2026) remains a crucial future challenge. Such cross-regional intraspecific comparisons will help disentangle whether the observed synergistic and conservative strategies reflect fixed phylogenetic traits or adaptive phenotypic plasticity driven by extreme climate stress. Our research highlights that incorporating EM-specific coordination, in which branching-driven colonization refines the collaboration framework, is crucial for accurately forecasting tree coexistence and how semi-arid forest ecosystems respond to global environmental changes.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that fine-root trait coordination in the Mongolian forest-steppe ecotone is structured in a multidimensional space, with the collaboration axis reflecting the synergy between morphology and symbiosis. The thinnest roots, such as B. platyphylla, exhibit the highest EM colonization, challenging the universal applicability of the traditional do-it-yourself vs. outsourcing trade-off. Instead, we propose various strategies ranging from synergistic acquisition to conservative, low-investment approaches, and decoupled internal allocation dynamics observed in conifers. These results highlight the need to consider the root architecture and taxon-specific symbiotic mechanisms to elucidate the resilience of semi-arid forest ecosystems.
