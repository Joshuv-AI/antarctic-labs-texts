Evaluation of a flexible thyroid shield in dental panoramic radiography utilizing bismuth oxide silicone rubber composite and Rando phantom
Authors: Leila Shahbazi, Dariush Sardari, Shahryar Malekie, Mohsen Kheradmand Saadi, Sedigheh Kashian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Initially, micro and nano bismuth oxide/ silicone rubber (Bi2O3/SR) composites at concentration of 70 wt% were fabricated. Analyses including XRD, SEM, EDS-Map, and TGA were performed on the samples, which indicated the dispersion of bismuth oxide micro and nano particles in the polymer matrix. Subsequently, the initial irradiation tests of the samples were performed by X-ray tube 60 kV at the SSDL of Iran. Results showed that there was no significant difference between the shielding effectiveness of micro and nano composites at 70 wt%. Therefore, in the final phase, considering economic considerations, a thyroid shield with a strap length of 58 cm, width of 23 cm, strap width of 6.4 cm, and length of 14 cm with thickness of 4 mm was manufactured using micro particles of Bi2O3 in the SR matrix. Then, radiation tests of the composite thyroid shield were conducted in an Orthopantomogram (OPG) radiology using the TLD-GR200 dosimeters and head-neck part of an adult male Rando phantom at 75 kV, 13 mA, with exposure time of 14.1 s. Results reveal that the thyroid gland received an absorbed dose of 386.6 ± 219.8 µSv, 19.0 ± 12.4 µSv, and 37.9 ± 18.7 µSv for unshielded, composite shield, and commercial thyroid collar (equivalent to 0.5 mm Pb), respectively. This shows a 95.1% reduction in dose with composite shielding, which is 5.4% more effective than commercial thyroid collar.

Introduction

The report issued by the United Nations Scientific Committee on the effects of atomic radiation reveals that the highest levels of artificial radiation exposure in individuals are predominantly linked to medical uses1. Dental radiology typically necessitates imaging of the teeth, which is an essential practical tool for detecting abnormalities and injuries within the oral cavity2. The objective of dental radiography is to assess and analyze the condition of the oral cavity. With an increasing emphasis on improving health standards and community well-being, the significance of oral and dental health has become increasingly evident, leading to a rise in the number of patients seeking care at dental facilities. Among the three dental radiography techniques including Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), panoramic using Orthopantomogram (OPG), and periapical, the CBCT method is designed for diagnostic and specialized purposes, whereas panoramic and periapical radiographs are the most frequently employed3,4.

Numerous studies have established a correlation between dental radiography and an increased incidence of thyroid cancer, and genotoxic consequences in children as well as the development of salivary gland cancer, brain cancer and the development of meningioma cranial tumors and parotid gland tumors1,5–7. Regrettably, this issue has not received adequate attention. The butterfly-shaped thyroid gland, weighing between 15 and 20 g, is among the largest endocrine glands and is located at the front of the neck8. Metabolism, growth, and development are regulated by the thyroid gland through the action of several specific hormones9. In the context of radiation therapy for tumors located in the head, neck, and chest, it is essential to recognize the thyroid gland as a high-risk organ. Reports indicate that the effective dose associated with panoramic imaging varies between 19 and 75 µSv, influenced by the type of equipment employed10.

A thyroid shield or collar serves to shield the thyroid gland and other surrounding organs11. Evidence suggests that employing thyroid protection in radiographic methodologies can result in a significant decrease in absorbed dose, with reductions reported up to 75%1. Lead thyroid shields are commonly used in dental radiology centers, but some disadvantages, including toxic properties of lead and its harmful absorption effects in humans, which may result in anemia, neurological issues, and brain injury, coupled with its non-recyclable characteristics and negative ecological consequences, have led researchers to explore alternative lead-free materials. The model of the OPG device, the type of shielding material, and the design of the shield significantly influence the reduction of radiation dose10.

In the previous studies by the authors12–14, polymer-heavy metal oxide nanocomposites containing nanoparticles with high surface-to-volume ratio were introduced as the novel radiation shields applied in the nuclear medicine and diagnostic imaging centers. Recently, polymer composites have been considered as radiation shielding materials at different energies for diagnostic purposes15–17.

The objective of this research is to assess and quantify the radiation shielding effectiveness of an innovative thyroid collar, which is lead-free and fabricated using a bismuth oxide/silicon rubber (SR-Bi2O3) composite, against the X-rays in an OPG device at 75 kV, 13 mA, and exposure time of 14.1 s for dental radiology. To achieve this, a set of TLD-GR200 dosimeters and a head-neck part of an adult male Rando phantom were employed.

Materials and methods

Preparation the materials

In this experimental work, silicone rubber grade RTV2 with density of 1.02 g/cm3 from Iran Composite Kavian Company was prepared. Bismuth oxide nanopowder with particle sizes under 80 nm and a density of 8.9 g/cm³ was obtained from US Nanomaterial Inc. Also, bismuth oxide micropowder, with sizes less than 5 μm, and density of 8.9 g/cm3 was obtained from a commercial Chinese company. It should be noted that RTV2 is a condensation-type silicone rubber that consists of two components and cures at room temperature. It is commonly referred to as two-part silicone rubber, where Part A is a liquid silicone that flows easily, and Part B serves as the curing agent (Hardener with weight ratio of 4%), which is a tin catalyst. This material is primarily utilized for mold making. An overview of the specifications pertaining to RTV2 is presented here. Color: transparent, curing time 3–5 h under 25 °C, tensile strength (MPA) > 4, tear strength > 23, shrinkage rate < 0.25%, viscosity (mPa.s) 20,000 ± 4000 at 25 °C, hardness (shore) 20 ± 2 at 25 °C, mix ration (per) 4% by weight, elongation > 500%.

Fabrication of the nano, and microcomposites

This investigation involves two phases of manufacturing. In the initial phase, to assess the shielding capabilities of micro and nano-sized Bi2O3 particles embedded in a SR substrate, pure silicone rubber was fabricated alongside micro, and nanocomposites of Bi2O3/SR at concentration of 70 w% through a direct mixing approach. Initially, 31.55 g of Bi2O3 powder was added to 13 g of RTV2 silicone rubber solution and mixed using a mechanical stirrer for 30 min. The composite was then subjected to ultrasonication in an Elmasonic ultrasonic bath system, operating at a frequency of 20 kHz and a power of 60 W for 2 h at a temperature of 80 °C to achieve an optimal dispersion. Subsequently, the amount of 520 mg hardener was added into the composite, representing a weight ratio of 4% to the SR matrix, and the mixture was immediately poured into a steel mold with dimensions of 8 cm × 8 cm and thickness of 2 mm. The samples were fully dried after being stored at room temperature for 48 h. To evaluate the shielding properties of these samples, each fabricated sample was divided into four equal sections measuring 4 cm × 4 cm. In the subsequent phase, a thyroid shield was designed based on the dimensions of a standard thyroid collar commonly used in dental radiography centers for an adult person18, featuring a strap length of 58 cm, a width of 23 cm, a strap width of 6.4 cm, 14 cm in length and 4 mm in thickness. Fabrication of the final composite thyroid shield

The construction process of the thyroid shield is detailed as follows: 273.5 g of RTV2 silicone rubber was poured into a 600 ml glass beaker. Subsequently, 664 g of Bi2O3 micropowder was gradually incorporated and mixed using a mechanical stirrer for 30 min. The resulting composite was then subjected to ultrasonication for 2 h at 80 °C utilizing an Elmasonic ultrasonic bath system to enhance sample dispersion. Finally, amount of 10.9 g hardener was introduced to the composite, constituting 4% of the silicone rubber, and the mixture was promptly poured into a steel mold with dimensions prescribed at Fig. 1. The samples were allowed to cure at room temperature for 72 h before being placed at 120 °C oven for 4 h to ensure complete drying. A schematic of the fabrication process of the samples is exhibited in Fig. 2. Density of the composite is evaluated as 2.68 g/cm3. Effective shielding area of this thyroid shield is ~ 500 cm2. Instruments for material characterization

In order to distribute the Bi2O3 particles homogeneously in the SR matrix, an ultrasonic bath (model Elmasonic, 20 kHz, 60 W) was used. An oven (Behdad Co., Tehran, Iran) was employed to dry the final composite shield at a temperature of 120 °C. The crystalline structure of the composites was evaluated by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Philips diffractometer, PW1730, USA). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)-based energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX or EDS-Map) was evaluated using VEGA3 TESCAN, Czech Republic. Finally, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was used to analyze the thermal behaviors of the nano, and micro composites using the Perkin Elmer Pyris instrument. All the material characterizations were carried out at Beam Gostar Taban laboratory in Tehran-Iran.

Instruments for X-ray irradiation and measurement

To evaluate the initial shielding features of nano, and micro Bi2O3/SR composites against the X-rays at level suitable for dental radiology, the COMET MXR 320/26 irradiation system has been utilized at the Secondary Standard Dosimetry Laboratory (SSDL) of Iran, featuring a maximum current of 26 mA and a peak voltage of 320 kV. Also, a NOMEX Multimeter (PTW, Germany), as a non-invasive measuring device for reference dosimetry and quality control measurements in diagnostics was used to measure the radiation shielding characteristics of the composites at the SSDL. NOMEX Multimeter is capable to measure air kerma rate ranging from 5 µGy/s to 500 mGy/s, with uncertainty as ± 3.5%. In addition to the irradiation and initial measurements in the SSDL, final tests measuring the radiation shielding properties of the thyroid collar made of Bi2O3/SR composite were conducted using an OPG device, model Orthopantomograph OP200 D. The dose rate of the tube head is 13.4 R/min (equivalent to 134 mSv/min) at 85 kV, 10 mA, 42 cm19. A set of calibrated TLD-GR200 dosimeters (LiF: Mg, Cu, P), with 0.9 mm thickness, and 4 mm diameter using a Harshaw 4500 TLD Reader were applied. All the TLD reading and annealing were done at the NSTRI of Tehran-Iran.

Rando Phantom

In this research, to assess the radiation shielding effectiveness of the fabricated thyroid collar using the Bi2O3/SR composites at concentration of 70 wt%, a head-neck part of an adult male Rando phantom (Serial number 759) was employed (Fig. 3), in which the shield is wrapped around the phantom’s neck. In Fig. 3, the details of this phantom along with the corresponding slices for the head and neck from 1 to 10, and distribution of slices as well as the TLDs locations are exhibited.

It should be noted that, in Rando phantom, the thyroid gland is located within slices numbered 9 and 1010. This anatomical positioning allows for a distinct evaluation of both the superior and inferior segments of the thyroid during the experimental procedure10. To measure the dose in the thyroid gland, the TLD dosimeters were placed at eight points on the slices #9, and #10 in the center, right, left, and front of the thyroid (four TLDs in each slice). Finally, the average of dose in different locations was considered as the thyroid gland dose. Thus, by utilizing the TLD dosimeters within the phantom, researchers can effectively measure the radiation doses from both the primary beam and the scattered rays resulting from interactions with the head and neck tissues.

TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA), is a key indicator for thermal stability of a particular material. Figure 6 illustrates the TGA analysis of pure SR, micro-, and nanocomposites at air atmosphere and heating rate of 10 °C/min, at the range of 35–600 °C. For pure SR, the initial decomposition phase was observed at 390 °C, reaching a peak weight loss at 470 °C. The subsequent decomposition phase occurred between 501 and 578 °C, with the highest weight loss occurring at 600 °C. The initial decomposition temperature of the microcomposite (at 470 °C) was found higher than nanocomposite (at 452 °C). The maximum weight changes between the temperatures of 35–600 °C were measured at 77.2%, 45.8%, and 41.5% for pure SR, nano, and microcomposites, respectively. It can be mentioned that for nano, and microcomposites compared to pure SR, the rate of weight loss decreases remarkably. Additionally, the TGA analysis revealed that the weight loss % of the nanocomposite exceeded that of the microcomposite by an amount of 10.3%.

SEM analysis

To investigate the morphology of three samples namely pure SR, micro, and nanocomposites, and also to verify the dispersion state of the Bi2O3 particles into the composites, SEM test was carried out. Figure 8a demonstrates the morphology of the pure SR at 2 μm magnification. As shown in Fig. 8b, the dispersion state of the Bi2O3 nano-fillers into the SR matrix confirms a relatively homogeneous dispersion of the inclusions. However, there are some agglomerations in the nano, and microcomposites (Fig. 8c). This issue may arise from the elevated viscosity of SR, which complicates achieving a completely homogeneous dispersion at high weight fractions of the inclusions or reinforcement phase loading. Based on the observations in TGA, and SEM analyses, it is concluded that Bi2O3 nanoparticles are prone to agglomeration due to attractive van der Waals forces, particularly at higher weight fractions within the polymer matrices.

Results of irradiation at the SSDL

As can be seen from Fig. 9, and Table 3, comparison of the linear attenuation coefficients of three samples namely pure SR, Bi2O3/SR micro and nanocomposites, against the X-rays of 60 kV, 4 mA, and 30 s exposure time at the SSDL is depicted.

To determine the linear attenuation coefficient of each sample, the dose rate was measured for the samples with various thicknesses (up to four layers combined) using a Nomex Multimeter at the SSDL. Measurements were conducted with 60 kV X-rays at a fixed distance of 100 cm from the focal spot, with each measurement repeated seven times to obtain the transmitted dose rate (I). The dose rate without any sample (I₀) was also measured under identical conditions. The natural logarithm of the ratio I₀/I was plotted against sample thickness (x), and the linear attenuation coefficient was derived from the slope of the resulting plot. Using these data, key protective quantities, including the HVL, TVL, µ, and µ/ρ (mass attenuation coefficient) were calculated for each sample, as presented in Table 3.

Generally, to evaluate standard deviation or uncertainty, both Type-A and Type-B error components should be considered36. Type-A uncertainty arises from random errors and is evaluated by statistical analysis of repeated measurements (repeatability). Type-B uncertainty is associated with systematic errors, such as the accuracy of measurement devices. The combined standard uncertainty, UC, is calculated by taking the positive square root of the sum of all individual standard uncertainties squared36:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{C}=\sqrt{\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{{u}_{i}}^{2}}$$\end{document}

In which ui represents the individual standard uncertainties from both Type-A and Type-B sources. In this experimental work, Type-A uncertainty for the quantities of µ, µ/ρ, HVL, and TVL was assessed by repeating dose rate measurements seven times using the NOMEX Multimeter at the SSDL. Thus, the mean and standard deviation of these measurements were calculated, resulting in a Type-A uncertainty of 0.1%. Type-B uncertainty was primarily influenced by the accuracy of the NOMEX Multimeter’s calibration certificate (± 3.5%) and the precision of the micrometer used for thickness measurements (0.1%). Applying Eq. (7), the combined standard uncertainty was determined to be approximately 3.5% (1σ). This value is included with the data presented in Table 3.

Results of this study show that the absorbed dose to the thyroid gland during OPG radiology at 75 kV, 13 mA, with an exposure time of 14.1 s using a Rondo phantom and TLD-GR200 dosimeters for unshielded position is 386.6 ± 219.8 µSv (56.9% standard deviation, 1σ), in the composite shielded is 19.0 ± 12.4 µSv (65% standard deviation, 1σ), and in a commercial thyroid collar (equivalent to 0.5 mm lead) is 37.9 ± 18.7 µSv (49.5% standard deviation, 1σ).

The elevated dose received by the superior section of the thyroid gland is a result of its closeness to the focal spot of the X-rays. This issue was observed in the experimental work of Hafezi, et al., in which investigated the radiation dose in the thyroid gland using different protective collars in panoramic imaging10.

Therefore, the dose reduction in the composite shielded is 95.1% and in the conventional shield collar is 90.2%, which indicates that the shielding effectiveness of the composite shield is 5.4% higher than that of the commercial thyroid collar. Also, according to Table 4, results of this study show that the composite shield in the present work provides better shielding effectiveness than other commercial samples, Thus, it can be concluded that using the lead-free material based on Bi2O3/SR composite as a thyroid shield with optimal thickness and size provides better protective effectiveness than conventional lead or lead equivalent shields. The optimal thickness of a composite radiation shield is determined by two primary criteria. First, the thickness should be on the order of the TVL, which is the thickness required to reduce the radiation intensity to one-tenth of its initial value. This ensures significant attenuation of radiation to safe levels. Second, the shield’s thickness should be at least equivalent to 0.5 mm Pb, as specified by standard shielding protocols42, to provide a baseline level of protection consistent with established safety standards. The composite material’s composition, and density should be considered to optimize its effectiveness while maintaining structural integrity and minimizing weight. In fact, a composite shield designed with optimal thickness should demonstrate radiation shielding effectiveness equivalent to a minimum of 0.5 mm of lead, while also preserving flexibility and minimizing overall weight.

this research involved designing and fabricating a composite shield made of 70 wt% bismuth oxide/silicone rubber with thickness of 4 mm. A primary challenge in this study was achieving a balance or compromise between the shield’s optimal thickness and its flexibility. The optimal thickness should ensure that the shielding effectiveness is at least equivalent to 0.5 mm Pb, in accordance with standard shielding requirements42. The findings demonstrate that the composite shield’s effectiveness is 5.4% higher than that of a commercial lead shield, suggesting that its thickness likely exceeds the equivalent of 0.5 mm Pb. Future research should focus on developing a shield that further reduces weight, enhances flexibility, and improves imaging quality by minimizing artifacts, while maintaining or improvement the protective efficacy of the established standard.

Artifact test

Investigations into commonly thyroid shields composed of lead, employed in dental radiography and notably in CBCT, indicate the occurrence of artifact that could lead to 1–7% rise in image noise, and also decrease in contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), thereby affecting the overall quality of dental images43–45.

Figure 11 illustrates the artifact test on Rando phantom in an OPG mode at 75 kV, 13 mA, and 14.1 s using the composite shield (made of 70 wt% Bi2O3/SR microcomposite). Figure 11a depicts the artifact test conducted in an unshielded position, yielding satisfactory image quality. As illustrated in Fig. 11a, a shadow is evident in the lower jaw region of the image. This phenomenon can be attributed to the OPG imaging process, where the X-ray source is positioned behind the neck, projecting radiation from the right ear to the left ear, with the detector placed in front of the face. Given the cervical spine’s alignment within the X-ray beam’s path, it is likely responsible for the observed shadow in the lower jaw area. Additionally, dose reduction in OPG imaging increases image noise, which can decline overall image quality45–48. In clinical practice, patients are instructed to keep their mouth open and press their tongue against the palate to optimize image clarity. However, this procedure could not be replicated with the Rando phantom, potentially contributing to the observed effect.

Figs. 11b & 11c present the artifact test on a Rando phantom, in which the microcomposite shield is wrapped back, and in front of the neck, respectively. The irradiation angle for OPG imaging was established at 8° below the horizontal plane of the jaw, placing the thyroid shield directly in the path of the X-rays, which resulted in an artifact effect that compromised the image quality for the composite shield. Nevertheless, as evidenced in Fig. 11c, when the composite shield is covered in front of the neck, the image quality is notably suitable compared to the back position (Fig. 11b).

Dental OPG artifact assessment at 75 kV: (a) unshielded, (b) shielded from rear, and (c) shielded from front using Rando phantom and 70 wt% Bi2O3/SR microcomposite.

To justify this phenomenon, it can be mentioned that the OPG device is rotated around the head of the patient and radiation is delivered from the rear49,, thus, the application of a thyroid shield during OPG leads to the appearance of heavy metal (or heavy metal oxide) artifacts in the resulting images. This is primarily due to the interaction between the radiation and the lead material, which disrupts the clarity of the diagnostic image49. The X-ray shields employed in OPG should be designed to achieve a balance between minimizing the dose of X-ray exposure and the generation of metallic artifacts. This optimization is crucial for enhancing the quality of imaging while ensuring patient safety during dental radiographic procedures. In OPG, since the image quality of the mandible may be affected by the artifact caused by the presence of a thyroid shield, and radiation is also delivered from the back of the neck, it is recommended that the patient elevate their chin to significantly reduce the dose to the thyroid gland and improve image quality by reducing artifact50. This issue was reviewed by Qu et al.51, who recommended that patients might be asked to elevate their chin so that the inferior border of the mandible is parallel to the horizontal plane during the scanning. Also, altering the irradiation angle could serve to mitigate this issue, taking into account that exposure of the thyroid during panoramic dental imaging can result from primary radiation, as well as internal and external scatter. Also, the model of the OPG device, along with the type of shielding material and shield design, plays a vital role in minimizing radiation exposure10. These factors should be carefully considered. Their influence on dose reduction cannot be overlooked. So, it is reasonable to anticipate that comparable improvements could be achieved in periapical radiography, where irradiation is conducted from above the face at a particular angle to the jaw. Consequently, additional research is warranted to assess image quality when using the composite thyroid shield in future investigations.

Conclusion

In this paper, radiation shielding properties of the bismuth oxide/silicone rubber composite at concentration of 70 wt% against the X-rays at 75 kV were investigated using head-neck part of an adult male Rando phantom. The dispersion state of the inclusions into the polymer matrix was investigated by the SEM, and EDS-Map tests, in which exhibited a suitable dispersion state. Other characterization methods including, XRD, and TGA were carried out. Based on the observations in TGA, and SEM analyses, it is concluded that Bi2O3 nanoparticles are prone to agglomeration due to attractive van der Waals forces, particularly at elevated weight fractions within the polymer matrices due to high viscosity of the silicone rubber solution.

Results of the artifact test using an OPG dental X-ray system at 75 kV during the imaging of the jaw in Rando phantom showed that quality of the image affected by the composite shield. Modifying the angle of irradiation may partially address this problem. Additionally, the implementation of this composite shield during periapical radiography could potentially enhance the quality of dental radiographic images.

The findings of this experimental work indicate that the thyroid gland was exposed to absorbed doses of 386.6 ± 219.8 µSv, 19.0 ± 12.4 µSv, and 37.9 ± 18.7 µSv for unshielded, composite shield, and commercial thyroid collar (equivalent to 0.5 mm Pb) configurations, respectively. This demonstrates a 95.1% decrease in dose with the use of composite shielding, which is 5.4% more effective than the standard lead shielding.

In conclusion, this experimental research suggests that utilizing a flexible thyroid shield made of lead-free Bi2O3/Silicone rubber composite, with optimized thickness and size, yields enhanced protective performance relative to standard lead or lead-equivalent shields for reducing the radiation dose during the dental radiology. However, more research is essential to analyze the quality of images affected by artifacts when utilizing the composite thyroid shield in forthcoming examinations.
