Dual clinical utility of cholesterol, high density lipoprotein, and glucose index: from advanced cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome pathogenesis to inflammatory-mediated mortality across early-transitional stages
Authors: Benjie Li, Qixian Wang, Zhuoxuan Yu, Yujun Zhang, Jingjing Song, Chunfeng Xie, Jingyang Zhao, Xinxin Liu, Zhigang Jie, Guoyang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome represents an emerging notion focused on the interconnection among cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic disorders. The predictive value of the cholesterol-high-density lipoprotein-glucose (CHG) index for advanced CKM syndrome and its stage-stratified mortality risk across stages 0 to 3 remains uncertain. This research aims to examine the clinical importance of the CHG and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) indices in characterizing the development of advanced CKM syndrome and predicting mortality within the 0–3 stage.MethodsWe processed the data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2003–2018. Associations were evaluated using multivariate logistic regression for disease progression and Cox models with restricted cubic splines (RCS) for mortality outcomes, accompanied by threshold effect and mediation analyses.ResultsUsing multivariable logistic regression models, we found that both the CHG index and the TyG index are positively related with advanced CKM syndrome. Multivariable-adjusted modeling showed a 44% increase in the threat of all-cause mortality for each 1-unit rise in the CHG index, while no association with cardiovascular mortality was found. RCS analysis revealed a J-curve pattern in the association of the CHG index with all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, with thresholds of 5.16 and 5.27. Mediation analyses identified that alkaline phosphatase, neutrophils, and the systemic immune-inflammatory index mediated between 2.1% and 17.2% of the effect in the relationship between the CHG index and all-cause mortality.ConclusionsThe CHG index demonstrates dual clinical utility: stratifying patients with advanced CKM syndrome and predicting nonlinear mortality across the early progressive continuum (stages 0–3). Its J-shaped association with mortality suggests complex pathophysiological relationships requiring further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-02055-2.

Introduction

Over the past few years, Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome has been proposed as a new concept. It is defined as a syndrome resulting from the interplay between diseases of the cardiovascular system, kidney, and metabolic system, imposing severe burdens on families and public health systems [1]. CKM syndrome is systematically categorized into stages from 0 (no risk factors) to 4 as confirmed cardiovascular disease (CVD) to adequately describe its evolving nature, with stages 3 (subclinical CVD) and 4 (confirmed CVD) referred to as advanced CKM syndrome. Previous epidemiological studies have found during 2011–2020, approximately 89.5% of American adults suffered from CKM syndrome to varying degrees, and 14.6% of the U.S. population is in the advanced stages (stages 3–4) [2]. Furthermore, an epidemiological study in China revealed that only 9.9% of middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals were at no risk of CKM syndrome as stage 0 [3]. As a progressive disease, CKM syndrome has been classified into five stages by the American Heart Association (AHA) to guide individualized prevention and treatment, with stages 0–3 considered crucial for the prevention of CVD [4]. Relative to stage 0, CKM syndrome stages 3–4 demonstrate a correlation with a higher vulnerability to premature mortality [5]. Without effective preventive measures, the global disease burden caused by CKM syndrome-related conditions, particularly its key components such as high BMI and diabetes, will continue to escalate [6]. Globally, the major components of CKM syndrome, including renal dysfunction, metabolic disorders, and CVD, have emerged as principal causes of death in the global population [7].

In the cardiovascular system, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) participates in reverse cholesterol transport and exerts vasculoprotective effects through anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antithrombotic mechanisms [8, 9]. A notable inverse connection has been observed between HDL-C levels and CVD [10, 11]. Hyperglycemia is generally acknowledged as a contributor leading to cardiovascular system damage, and diabetes results in various vascular complications, elevating the risk of diabetic panvascular disease (DPD) [12]. Prediabetic conditions contribute to higher atrial fibrillation prevalence [13]. Metabolic disorders of glucose and cholesterol often lead to the onset and worsening of kidney diseases [14, 15]. The Cholesterol, High-Density Lipoprotein, and Glucose (CHG) index, a cost-effective novel diagnostic indicator comprised of total cholesterol (TC), HDL-C, and glucose, demonstrates better performance than the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index in predicting diabetes [16], and the CHG index possesses comparable predictive ability for CVD risk as the TyG index [17]. Nevertheless, previous investigations of the TyG and CHG indices primarily focused on isolated metabolic diseases such as CVD and diabetes. The influence of dietary and nutritional factors on metabolic indices is well-established in the scientific literature. For instance, Arslan et al. provide valuable insights into short-term metabolic responses and the insulin-glucose dynamics following the intake of different protein sources [18]. Their work also elucidates the roles of nutritional biomarkers, such as vitamin D and the prognostic nutritional index, in relation to inflammatory status [19]. However, these studies did not evaluate their utility in the context of CKM syndrome—a complex multiorgan condition requiring integrated risk assessment. Crucially, advanced CKM stages encompass subclinical CVD (Stage 3) and clinical CVD (Stage 4), enabling novel exploration of these biomarkers across the full spectrum of disease severity. Meanwhile, no study has comprehensively examined the CHG index’s relationship to advanced CKM syndrome risk across the full spectrum and subsequent death.

Therefore, we assessed the relationships of the CHG index and the traditional TyG index regarding the risk of advanced CKM syndrome, and cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome (stages 0 to 3), utilizing the NHANES database. Our objective was to identify an effective biomarker for predicting advanced CKM syndrome, subsequently enhancing clinical prognostic management in patients with early CKM syndrome.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES, managed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and funded by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), is a comprehensive and ongoing research initiative every two years. A cohort study was performed on the publicly accessible NHANES database covering the years 2003 to 2018, comprising 80,312 participants in total. Criteria for exclusion included the following: (1) participants under the age of 20 (n = 35,522); (2) participants with incomplete or missing data regarding CKM syndrome (n = 24,223) and CHG (n = 1166); (3) participants without complete information on covariates, sample weights, and survival status (n = 5401). After applying these exclusion criteria, the study ultimately included 14,000 participants aged 20 years and above (Figure S1).

Definition and staging of CKM syndrome

CKM syndrome represents a systemic dysfunction characterized by the interplay of metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular disease processes, leading to adverse outcomes such as multi-organ dysfunction. The staging of patients is as follows: Stage 0 comprises patients without metabolic, cardiac, or renal risk factors. Stage 1 includes those with increased BMI, waist circumference, or early manifestations of diabetes. Stage 2 encompasses patients with metabolic risk factors like diabetes, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, hypertriglyceridemia, and those with CKD. Stage 3 involves patients with subclinical CVD syndrome (i.e., those anticipated to have very high-risk chronic kidney disease or high-risk CVD as per the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification) [1]. Table S1 provides more detailed information on the stage definitions, in which stages 3 and 4 were defined as advanced CKM syndrome.

Definition of CHG index and TyG index

To calculate the TyG index, the formula below was applied: TyG index = Ln (TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2). The CHG index was defined as follows [16, 17]: CHG index = Ln [FBG (mg/dL) × TC (mg/dL)/2 × HDL (mg/dL)]. As indicated in the laboratory method documentation, TG, TC, and HDL were measured using a fully automated, random-access, software-controlled Cobas 6000 Chemistry Analyzer, while FBG was quantified using the Roche/Hitachi Cobas C311 Chemistry Analyzer. The study population was split into Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4 based on the quartiles of the CHG and TyG indices, with Q1 designated as the standard group.

Definition of the outcomes

Data on mortality followed up to 2019 was sourced from the NHANES database, with mortality for all causes and cardiovascular issues being considered as outcomes in this study. Deaths from any cause were considered for the calculation of all-cause mortality. Cardiovascular deaths, specifically, were identified in our study using codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69 from the 10th edition of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10).

Assessment of covariates

We carefully examined a variety of possible covariates that could affect the research results. Through structured interviews and comprehensive baseline data collection, we obtained multidimensional information including sociodemographic characteristics, health-related indicators, and laboratory test results. These covariates include: age (years), sex (male or female), race (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, Other races), education level (high school or less, more than high school), poverty income ratio (low, middle, high), smoking status (never, now, former), body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), hypertension (no, yes), diabetes mellitus (DM, no, yes), fasting blood glucose (FBG, mg/dL), marital status (married or living with partner, living alone), insulin level (µU/ml), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, %), drinking status (never, mild, moderate, heavy, former), TC (mg/dL), triglycerides (TG, mg/dL), serum creatinine (mg/dL), serum uric acid (SUA, mg/dL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, mg/dL), HDL-C (mg/dL), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mg/dL). The comprehensive collection of these covariates aims to control for potential confounding effects, ensuring the validity and credibility of the findings. Detailed descriptions of covariates are shown in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Considering the intricate sampling design of the NHANES survey, the research team applied weight adjustments specifically fasting 2-year sample weights to the data to ensure statistical accuracy and the nationwide representativeness of non-institutionalized Americans in the study findings. Continuous variables are shown as means and SD, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages in the baseline data. To assess intergroup differences, the research utilized weighted t-tests for continuous variables and weighted chi-square tests for categorical ones. Associations between the CHG index, TyG index and advanced CKM syndrome were evaluated through weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. In addition, we established weighted univariate and multivariate Cox regression models to analyze the relationships of the CHG index and TyG index with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risks in CKM syndrome patients at stage 0–3, and hazard ratios (HR) were correspondingly calculated. Both indices were analyzed as continuous and categorical variables for sensitivity analyses. The model specifications are as follows: Model 1 represents the unadjusted model, whereas Model 2 comprises the adjusted model, which accounts for factors including age, sex, race, education level, and marital status. Model 3 further adjusted for BMI, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, LDL-C, hypertension, creatinine, SUA, and BUN based on model 2. Furthermore, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to assess dose-response links between the CHG/TyG indices and advanced CKM syndrome, as well as all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. If non-linearity was identified, we estimated the threshold by maximizing the likelihood across all possible values. To investigate the relationship of mortality risk above and below the threshold, two-segment Cox proportional hazards models were employed. Furthermore, subgroup analyses and interaction assessments were conducted for the exploration of these relationships in subpopulations to test the robustness of the results. Additionally, mediation analyses were performed to investigate the mediating role of inflammatory markers, such as alkaline phosphatase, neutrophils, and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), in the association between CHG and all-cause mortality.

The most advanced machine learning (ML) techniques were utilized to develop predictive models for the CHG index and all-cause mortality. The dataset was allocated at random two independent subsets with 70% as the training set (N = 8,656) and 30% as the validation set (N = 3,709). We improved the ML models by performing feature selection, pinpointing the most significant variables for predicting outcomes. We employed two feature selection methods: the Boruta algorithm and the Adaptive Best Subset Selection (ABESS) algorithm, to discern crucial predictive factors in the analysis. By synthesizing the results of these two methods, we selected consistent variables when building the initial 2 model, as race, age, marital status, drinking status, education level, BMI, PIR, creatinine, LDL-C, and CHG.

After selecting variables, we evaluated the multicollinearity with the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) to improve the selection process. A VIF value over 5 suggested significant multi-collinearity, leading to the removal of the related variable. Subsequently, we applied the following ML algorithms to the dataset for model development: Naive Bayes (NB), Decision Tree (DT), Logistic Regression (LR), Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). To ensure the robustness and reliability of each ML model, hyperparameter tuning and 10-fold cross-validation were employed. The evaluation of model performance was conducted with the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. A comprehensive set of evaluation metrics was utilized, including Area Under the Curve (AUC), Average Precision Score (APS), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), false negative rate (FNR), specificity, false positive rate (FPR), and F1 score. Simultaneously, we performed Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) to evaluate the models’ practical value. The primary measure for determining the best ML model was the AUC of the ROC curve. R software (version 4.4.2) was utilized to conduct statistical analyses, in which nhanesR package was adopted for survival analysis, nhanesR and rms package was used for RCS and mlr3verse, mlr3, FSelectorRcpp, absee and Boruta packages were utilized for ML. P < 0.05 indicated significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study enrolled 14,000 participants, averaging in age at 47.11 years, with 49.20% being male. The baseline characteristics of the participants were stratified by CHG index quartiles, as illustrated in Table 1. In comparison to individuals in the Q1, those in the Q4 of CHG were typically older, more likely to be male, often had a high school education or less, identified as Mexican American, and tended to be married or living together. Moreover, participants with elevated CHG levels typically exhibited higher FBG, insulin, HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, creatinine, SUA, BUN, and TyG. Furthermore, the findings indicated that participants falling into higher CHG quartiles were more likely to engage in unhealthy lifestyle habits. This includes a significant elevated prevalence of individuals with conditions such as obesity, smoking habits, high alcohol consumption, hypertension, and diabetes. These results suggest a noteworthy association between unhealthy lifestyle choices and elevated levels of CHG, indicating that individuals with such risk factors may experience increased CHG levels.

Associations of TyG index and CHG index with advanced CKM syndrome

Table 2 presents multivariate logistic regression results examining associations of the CHG and TyG indices with the risk of advanced CKM syndrome. Model 1 showed that each 1-unit CHG index increment resulted in a 103% increase of advanced CKM syndrome (odds ratio [OR] = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.71–2.40), while the corresponding increase in the TyG index was 108% (OR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.88–2.30). These associations persisted in Model 2 even after adjustments for sociodemographic factors (age, sex, race, education, marital status) and were further robust in Model 3, which incorporated adjustments for BMI, PIR, lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol use), LDL-C, hypertension, and renal markers (creatinine, SUA, BUN). Sensitivity analyses treating CHG and TyG as categorical variables (quartiles) revealed consistent trends. Compared to the lowest quartile, the highest quartile of the CHG index in Model 3 was associated with a 106% elevated risk of advanced CKM syndrome (OR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.59–2.66), whereas the TyG index exhibited a 59% risk increase (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.24–2.03). RCS (Fig. 1) indicates that CHG is positively correlated with advanced CKM syndrome risk (P for overall < 0.01), and no non-linear trend was detected (P for nonlinear = 0.060). Meanwhile, the analysis revealed a significant nonlinear correlation of the TyG index with advanced CKM syndrome risk (P for nonlinear = 0.027).

Non-linear correlation between CHG index and TyG index and mortality

As depicted in Fig. 2, we conducted a thorough evaluation of the dose-response relationships involving the CHG index and the TyG index regarding all-cause and cardiovascular mortality through RCS analyses. Our analysis indicated a J-shaped relationship of CHG levels with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (Fig. 2A, B). The inflection points observed at 5.16 for all-cause mortality and 5.27 for cardiovascular mortality were statistically significant. In contrast to the findings associated with CHG, the association between the TyG index and mortality was not statistically significant, as indicated by a P-value greater than 0.05 for the overall assessment (Fig. 2C, D). The detailed nonlinear relationship between the CHG index and mortality, specifically regarding cardiovascular diseases and all-cause mortality, is further elaborated in Table 4. It was noted that when CHG values fell below 5.16, the correlation was not significant (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.46–1.06). Once CHG reached or exceeded 5.16, a significant positive correlation with all-cause mortality was identified (HR: 1.90, 95% CI:1.51–2.38). Similarly, when CHG levels surpassed 5.27, the risk of death due to cardiovascular causes increased by an alarming 103% for each additional unit increase in CHG (HR: 2.03, 95% CI:1.59–2.60). Conversely, no significant association with the risk of cardiovascular mortality was identified for CHG levels below 5.27 (HR: 0.80, 95% CI:0.56–1.14).

Feature engineering

The ABESS and Boruta algorithms were employed to pinpoint the key biomarkers and clinical features linked to all-cause mortality. To improvie both the interpretability and forecasting effectiveness, this feature selection process was carried out. Utilizing the ABESS (Fig. 4A), our model reached the minimum generalized information criterion value with only 10 variables in order to predict the probability of mortality for all causes in the validation set. The filtering results from the Boruta algorithm indicated that the CHG variable was the second most heavily weighted, following age (Fig. 4B). Together, these two algorithms identified the following 10 variables: age, race, marital status, education level, PIR, drinking Status, BMI, creatinine, LDL-C, and CHG. The lack of multicollinearity among the predictor variables was verified by analyzing the VIF (Table S4). As a result, a machine learning model was developed incorporating above variables.

Assessment of ML models and determination of the best-performing model

These 10 variables were used in various ML algorithms. While constructing the model, grid search alongside ten-fold cross-validation was carried out to determine the optimal hyperparameters, thereby guaranteeing robust generalization capability. The validation set is used to be the primary dataset to determine the best-performing model, demonstrating its value and relative superiority in applications beyond the training set. Figure S2 displays the confusion matrices for the nine models on the basis of the validation set. Figure 5 presents the performance of several algorithms, including LR, LDA, QDA, NB, KNN, DT, RF, XGBoost, and LightGBM on the validation set. The AUC for the nine ML models varied between 0.719 and 0.880. Among them, the XGBoost model outperformed the others with an AUC of 0.880, APS of 0.983, and achieving an F1 Score as high as 0.956 (Table S5). The model was further assessed using DCA (Fig. 6). The XGBoost model demonstrated greater net gains across most threshold ranges compared to the strategies of both the “no intervention” and” all intervention”, indicating potential superior clinical utility.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, there has not been any previous extensive cohort study aimed at discovering the relationship connecting the High-Density Lipoprotein, and Glucose (CHG) index to the progression of advanced cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome and predicting mortality within the 0–3 stage of CKM syndrome. Our results demonstrated a marked association of increased CHG and Triglyceride-glucose (TyG) indices with advanced CKM syndrome. After comprehensive adjustment for covariates, the CHG index demonstrated a non-linear correlation with mortality outcomes, with risk escalation becoming pronounced when CHG levels exceeded critical thresholds. Mediation analyses further identified systemic inflammation as a potential plausible mechanism underlying this association, with the proportions ranging from 2.1% to 17.2%. Complementing these findings, ML models incorporating CHG achieved robust predictive accuracy, evidenced by AUC values exceeding 0.85 in 7 out of 9 evaluated algorithms. Collectively, these findings support the notion that routine CHG monitoring may provide a clinically feasible strategy for identifying high-risk populations and patients in epidemiological surveillance, which could enable timely risk stratification and precision prevention initiatives to mitigate adverse clinical outcomes.

Given the severity of adverse outcomes in advanced CKM syndrome, some studies have focused on stages 3–4 of CKM syndrome as research subjects, which are also identified as advanced CKM syndrome. Research targeting Americans aged 70–79 revealed that compared with participants in CKM syndrome stages 0–2, those in stages 3–4 had a 50% higher risk of dementia [20]. Advanced CKM syndrome stages correlate with heightened risks of mortality from all causes and cardiovascular issues [21]. Thus, it is crucial to establish a new index for evaluating the risk of late-stage CKM syndrome. New study unveiled that escalating TyG index levels are connected to a greater prevalence of advanced CKM syndrome [22]. In our study, we further assessed the CHG index as a potential novel indicator that may serve as a tool for evaluating advanced CKM syndrome risk. In addition, the individuals with CKM syndrome with stages ranged from 0 to 3 have become a critical focus of contemporary cardiovascular research. Extensive investigations are exploring the utility of indices assessing insulin resistance and lipid dysregulation for CVD risk stratification in this population. These indices include the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), the TyG index, TyG-derived indices, and the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) [23–27]. Accumulating evidence indicates that both the TyG index and its modified variants such as TyG-BMI independently predict incident CVD in individuals with early-stage CKM syndrome, with modified indices demonstrating superior predictive performance compared to the conventional TyG metric [28]. Research conducted by Hu et al. has revealed a strong positive link between AIP and CVD within this specific population. This relationship appears to be primarily influenced by metabolic dysregulation, indicating that as AIP levels increase, so does the risk of developing CVD due to various metabolic disturbances [27]. Similarly, analysis of patients diagnosed with CKM syndrome in stages 0–3 has demonstrated that the eGDR has a notable nonlinear inverse association with the risk of CVD. This finding suggests that eGDR may function as a reliable indicator for assessing the likelihood of cardiovascular events in these patients [26]. However, prognostic indicators for mortality outcomes in early-stage CKM syndrome populations remain underexplored. Emerging evidence from recent studies by Zhang et al. indicated a significant positive link between TyG-related indices, such as TyG-BMI and TyG-waist circumference (WC), rather than the conventional TyG index, and all-cause/cardiovascular mortality [29]. Their study also underscored the need for clinical vigilance regarding abnormal TyG profiles in the subgroups of stage 1 and stage 3 CKM syndrome. This evolving landscape underscores the imperative need to identify novel, mortality-oriented biomarkers that address the pathophysiological complexity of early CKM syndrome progression.

The CHG index, a novel composite biomarker integrating lipid-glucose homeostasis, was first introduced as a diagnostic tool for diabetes and insulin resistance (IR) [16]. In contrast to the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) and systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII) [30–32], which are primarily reflective of the immune and inflammatory status, the CHG index incorporates direct measurements of plasma glucose and lipid metabolism. This fundamental difference is critical because glycemic and lipid parameters are established components in the pathogenesis of IR, thereby granting the CHG index a more direct and stronger association with IR pathophysiology compared to immune-derived surrogates. Meanwhile, CHG index demonstrated superior specificity (83.4% vs. 68.6%) compared to the TyG index in diagnosing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), establishing it as a cost-effective screening tool especially valuable in resource-limited settings where advanced assays such as the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) are unavailable. The CHG index functions as a mechanistic representation of the complex relationship between diabetic dyslipidemia and IR-induced metabolic disturbances. Diabetic dyslipidemia is characterized by elevated levels of very low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C), TC, and LDL-C, along with a reduction in HDL-C. This abnormal lipid profile reflects underlying metabolic dysfunctions arising from insulin resistance. Additionally, these disturbances may be influenced by factors such as dysfunctional adipose tissue and changes in lipase activity, both of which are crucial to lipid metabolism. Consequently, the CHG index captures these complex interactions, providing valuable insights into the metabolic alterations associated with diabetes [33, 34]. Recently, a study reported that both the CHG and TyG indices independently predict CVD risk, with CHG exhibiting higher HRs despite similar predictive accuracy [17]. This enhanced risk discrimination may arise from CHG’s unique incorporation of HDL-C and TC—both established predictors of atherogenesis [11, 35]—in conjunction with glycemic parameters. Our findings further contribute to this paradigm by revealing a J-shaped nonlinear relationship between CHG and mortality in the early stages of CKM syndrome. This contrasts with the TyG index, which wasn’t related to mortality in our cohort, thereby challenging its universal applicability in CKM syndrome populations.

The CHG index is associated with multiple domains of cardiovascular risk, namely nutritional metabolism, dietary protein sources, and vitamin-inflammation interactions. Specifically, it acts as a composite marker that integrates key metabolic parameters—including lipid metabolism, cardiovascular function, and insulin sensitivity—thereby offering significant clinical utility. Additionally, evidence suggests that varying protein sources modulate short-term metabolic and insulin-glucose responses, which reinforces the interpretive value of the CHG index [18]. Furthermore, vitamin D was inversely correlated with inflammation and was positively associated with PNI [19]. Taken together, these connections help delineate a coherent path from dietary intake and nutritional status through inflammation to the development of metabolic disorders like CKM syndrome. A growing body of literature has established connections between components of the CHG index and inflammatory status, while the conceptual foundation of the index itself inherently links dietary intake and nutritional status to such pathways [36–38]. Collectively, these findings position the CHG index as a potential bridge, connecting dietary factors and nutritional status through inflammation to the development of metabolic disorders such as CKM syndrome. To validate this integrative pathway, future studies should prioritize in-depth mechanistic investigations and randomized controlled trials.

Nevertheless, the biological foundation of the correlations between the CHG index and the advanced CKM syndrome and mortality risk remain inadequately understood. The observed relationship likely arises from synergistic interactions between metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation. Similar to the TyG index, the CHG index reflects integrated lipid-glucose homeostasis and IR [39, 40], promoting endothelial injury and vascular remodeling through impaired nitric oxide (NO) synthesis via inhibition of the PI3K/Akt pathway [41, 42] and the activation of pro-atherogenic cascades such as angiotensin II signaling [43]. However, our mediation analysis revealed a novel aspect as inflammatory mediators, including alkaline phosphatase, neutrophils, and SII. This finding aligns with evidence that IR-driven chronic inflammation accelerates leukocyte infiltration (e.g., macrophages and neutrophils) into atherosclerotic plaques [44, 45], where neutrophils amplify oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by NADPH oxidase [46] and destabilize plaques through extracellular trap formation [44]. This series of reactions culminates in atherosclerotic plaque rupture, acute thrombosis, and cardiovascular events, representing a significant pathological basis for the mortality associated with the CHG index [47]. Notably, the inclusion of HDL-C in the CHG formula may further modulate inflammatory responses. Reduced HDL-C levels impair its anti-inflammatory capacity to inhibit monocyte chemotaxis and neutralize oxidized lipids [48], while dysfunctional HDL-C in metabolic disorders loses its ability to suppress NF-κB signaling in macrophages [49], thereby amplifying pro-inflammatory cytokine release [50]. This dual role of HDL-C—linking lipid metabolism to immune regulation—explains why the CHG index, unlike the TyG index [51], offers a more complete assessment of inflammation-mediated organ damage. For instance, while the TyG index primarily reflects triglyceride-glucose interplay and correlates with generalized oxidative stress [52], the CHG index captures HDL-C’s critical regulatory effects on both lipid efflux and leukocyte activation. Future studies should investigate longitudinal changes in CHG-driven inflammatory signatures (e.g., neutrophil extracellular traps, HDL-C proteome) and validate therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways in CKM syndrome population.

In the initial stages of CKM syndrome, the link of the CHG index to mortality exhibits a “J”-shaped pattern, illustrating a complex interplay of dual pathophysiological factors. On one hand, elevated CHG levels are strongly correlated with increased IR and metabolic disorders [53]. IR is associated with metabolic disturbances like hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, and these disturbances have a close link to adverse cardiovascular events [54]. IR results in chronic hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, which may promote oxidative stress, facilitate the development of foam cells, intensify inflammatory reactions, hinder endothelial function, and speed up the proliferation of smooth muscle cells [55]. In addition, prolonged IR may intensify sympathetic nervous system activity, leading to the retention of sodium and increased hypertension. These conditions may subsequently increase cardiac burden and impair blood vessels and kidneys [56]. Conversely, low CHG levels might reflect poor nutrition and physical weakness, factors that can undermine the body’s overall recovery ability and immune response. As a result, patients become more susceptible to infections, cachexia, and other complications, which can elevate the risk of all-cause death [57–59]. Moreover, extremely low TG or FPG levels correlate with poor health results and might speed up disease worsening [60, 61]. Reduced triglyceride levels in women are linked to a higher danger of hemorrhagic stroke [62], while low TG levels predict cardiac mortality in heart failure patients [63]. Moreover, research indicates that hypoglycemia may trigger an increase in adrenaline levels, leading to vascular constriction and thrombocyte aggregation, resulting in a higher likelihood of cardiovascular events [64]. To sum up, the J-shaped correlation between CHG and mortality risk underscores the intricate interactions among metabolic, cardiovascular risk factors and nutritional conditions. Elevated CHG levels intensify cardiovascular risk by triggering metabolic disorders and inflammatory responses, whereas decrease CHG indicates suboptimal nutrition and physical frailty, both of which contribute to higher mortality in individuals with CKM syndrome. Although the J-shape trend was identified, no significantly elevated risks for all-cause or cardiovascular mortality were observed in the lower range of the J-curve. Therefore, high-quality longitudinal studies are warranted to validate this J-shaped association, particularly the clinical significance of its left portion.

This study demonstrates various notable strengths, foremost among them its pioneering investigation into the prognostic utility of the CHG index across stages 0–3 of CKM syndrome, while also evaluating its performance relative to the established TyG index. Additionally, this research suggests a link of the CHG index to the late stages of CKM syndrome, supporting its potential role in the comprehensive assessment of CKM progression. By utilizing nationally representative data from the NHANES cohort, the analysis minimizes selection bias and enhances generalizability through complex sampling design. Methodologically, the integration of Cox proportional hazards models, RCS, and threshold effect analyses confirms the non-linear relationship between the CHG index and mortality, while identifying critical inflection points that provide actionable thresholds for clinical risk stratification. Simultaneously, the combination of multivariate logistic regression analyses and RCS further demonstrates the positive correlation between the CHG index and advanced CKM syndrome. Furthermore, the mediation analysis suggested that inflammatory pathways may partly explain the association between CHG and mortality. Collectively, these results point to the possible clinical relevance of CHG index in the early management of CKM syndrome, advocating for the monitoring of inflammation in high-risk patients in routine outpatient or primary care settings, thus bridging epidemiological findings with clinical practice.

Despite its contributions, this research has several limitations that merit consideration. The predominant reliance on U.S.-based NHANES data, while methodologically rigorous, raises concerns regarding its generalizability to other diverse ethnic and geographic populations. This necessitates validation in multinational cohorts to validate its universal applicability. Moreover, the utilization of a single baseline measurement of the CHG index precludes the assessment of temporal variability and its dynamic impact on mortality risk, underscoring the need for longitudinal studies to capture evolving metabolic profiles. Additionally, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables may persist despite comprehensive covariate adjustments. The observational design inherently restricts causal inference, and the lack of genomic data limits the ability to perform Mendelian randomization analyses to substantiate causality. By undertaking cross-cultural validations, conducting repeated biomarker assessments, and implementing mechanistic studies to address these limitations, the practical clinical significance of the CHG index in CKM syndrome will be improved.

Conclusions

To sum up, the CHG and TyG indices are positively linked to advanced CKM syndrome. We also identified a J-shaped pattern linking the CHG index to all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with thresholds of 5.16 and 5.27 in participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–3. When CHG exceeded these thresholds, both the mortality risk remarkably elevated. In the early stage of CKM syndrome patients, the CHG index demonstrated a robust association with the risk of mortality.
