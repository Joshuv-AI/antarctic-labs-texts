Optimizing a novel laser enrichment device for broilers: Evaluating an 8-minute exposure four times daily on broiler performance, walking distance behavior, and leg health
Authors: KM Elmore, AK Johnson, EA Bobeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIncreasing environmental complexity using a laser device (4 minutes duration, 4 times/day) has previously been found to improve voluntary broiler activity, locomotion, and bone quality without compromising growth performance or welfare indicators. Understanding the optimal duration for the laser device would maximize benefit for both the bird and producer. Therefore, the study objectives were to assess an increased laser duration of 8-min period occurring 4 times daily on Ross 708 growth performance, bird walking distance behavior, and tibia quality. Ross 708 broilers (n = 1,360) were randomly assigned to laser enrichment (8-min laser periods 4 times daily) or control (no laser enrichment) for 7-wk. On d 0, 70 focal birds were randomly selected for weekly gait and contact dermatitis scoring, and behavioral analysis. Focal bird walking distance was analyzed d 0-8 and 1 d/wk through wk 7. The right tibia was collected from all focal birds for dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry and bone breaking strength analyses on d 49. Data were analyzed using mixed linear models with pen nested within enrichment as a random effect (PROC MIXED), while walking distance data were analyzed using negative binomial generalized linear mixed models to account for overdispersion (PROC GLIMMIX, SAS 9.4). Enrichment did not impact feed intake, weight gain, or body weight across production phases (P > 0.05). Feed conversion ratio was increased 3 points in laser-enriched birds during the starter period (P = 0.01) but did not differ thereafter. Laser-enriched birds walked significantly further than controls during d 0–8 (40.3 cm, P = 0.03). No differences were observed in gait score, contact dermatitis, or air and litter quality. Laser-enriched birds had significantly greater tibia bone mineral density by 11.1% at d 49 compared with control birds (P = 0.04). These findings suggest that the laser enrichment continued to support favorable effects on broiler walking distance activity and bone mineral density, without adversely affecting growth performance or welfare outcomes.Keywords: Bone quality, Environmental enrichment, Gait, Walking distance, Welfare

Introduction

Broiler welfare has become a central focus of poultry production policy in the U.S. and internationally. In the European Union, regulations mandate lower stocking densities, welfare outcome-based monitoring, and environmental and management conditions (Council of the European Union, 2007; Sandøe et al., 2022). In the past, U.S. broiler production has mainly centered on maximizing growth rate and feed efficiency, with fewer welfare improvements in poultry driven by formal legislation, apart from the 1957 Poultry Products Inspection Act and the 1994 Twenty-Eight-Hour Law (USDA, 1994; USDA FSIS, 2022). Integrators are now looking towards consumer-driven management changes, including introducing more environmental enrichment into the broiler’s environment. Third-party auditing programs, such as Global Animal Partnership and Human Farm Animal Care, require environmental enrichment to meet broiler welfare standards (Duncan et al., 2012; Certified Humane, 2014). These standards emphasize environmental enrichment to promote natural behaviors and improve overall welfare and allow integrators to market certified and welfare-focused labeled products (Duncan et al., 2012; Bessei, 2018).

Previous data has shown that increasing broiler bird locomotion can reduce lameness and improve tibia quality, without negatively impacting growth (Reiter and Bessei, 2009; Wang et al., 2021; van der Sluis et, al., 2022). Lameness and leg disorder development can lead to limited mobility, restricted access to feed and water, and increased mortality (Weeks et al., 2000; Bessei, 2006; Granquist et al., 2019). Therefore, approaches that increase environmental complexity can improve both broiler bird productivity and welfare.

Environmental enrichments are a practical approach to improve broiler welfare by stimulating voluntary movement and natural behaviors, which may support skeletal development and reduce leg lameness incidence (Newberry, 1995). Research on physical enrichments has shown that perch use in broiler houses is low, whereas elevated platform use has been associated with improved gait scores (Kaukonen et al., 2017; Mocz et al., 2022). In addition, Baxter and others (2018) reported increased broiler bird foraging and dustbathing behaviors when provided oat hulls compared to birds offered straw bales.

Previous work by our group has clearly demonstrated that a laser environmental enrichment device can stimulate broiler locomotion without compromising broiler performance. In Ross 308 broilers, a 4-min laser enrichment exposure four times daily over a 6-wk grow-out increased feed intake and body weight gain and improved feed conversion ratio (Meyer et al., 2019). Laser enrichment also significantly increased broiler activity (157% at wk 3) and walking distance (367.2 cm at wk 2) relative to non-enriched controls (Meyer et al., 2020). Similar responses were observed in Ross 708 broilers using the same enrichment schedule, including improved starter-phase growth performance and greater live body weight, with laser-enriched birds weighing approximately 0.14 to 0.15 kg more than controls at d 42 and 49 of age (Meyer et al., 2021a). Laser-enriched birds also walked farther on wk 3 and 4 than controls (Meyer et al., 2021b). When laser duration exposure was extended to 6-min four times daily in Ross 708 broilers, laser enrichment continued to increase locomotor activity at several early time points. However, effects on growth performance were not detected (Meyer et al., 2023a, b). Laser enrichment did not negatively impact bone quality, gait, or contact dermatitis scores across studies (Meyer et al., 2019, 2021a, 2021b, 2023a, 2023b).

These outcomes highlight the need to refine the laser enrichment device further to identify a duration that optimizes both broiler performance and activity. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to determine whether extending the duration of laser enrichment enhanced its benefits for broiler production and key welfare measures. Specifically, this work evaluated the effects of increased laser duration (8-min four times daily) on Ross 708 broiler growth performance, gait and contact dermatitis scoring, and tibia bone quality.

Materials and methods

Animals, housing, and feeding

The Iowa State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee #22-219 approved all live bird procedures. A total of 1,360 D-old straight-run Ross 708 broiler chicks were sourced from a commercial hatchery (International Poultry Breeders Hatchery, Bancroft, IA) and transported to the Robert T. Hamilton Poultry Teaching and Research Facility at Iowa State University (Ames, IA). Upon arrival, chicks were randomly allocated to one of two rooms within the barn to reduce potential cross-exposure to laser enrichment. Each room housed 20 floor pens (1.22 × 2.44 m; 34 birds/pen), for a total of 40 pens. Seventy chicks randomly selected upon arrival (n = 5 chicks/pen across 14 designated camera pens) were marked as focal birds using wing bands and livestock-safe colored spray paint (red, pink, orange, green, or blue; All-Weather® Quik Shot®, LA-CO Industries, Elk Grove Village, IL). Half of these focal birds were placed in laser-enriched pens (n = 7 pens), while the remaining half were placed in control pens (n = 7 pens). Paint markings were applied to the dorsal side of each chick and reapplied as needed during the 7-wk study.

All pens received approximately 10 cm of fresh pine shavings and were equipped with two hanging feeders (BRHF151, Brower Equipment, Houghton, IA) and a single nipple water line offering 8 nipples per pen. During the first wk, each pen received supplemental heating via two 125-W bulbs mounted in 22.9 cm reflectors (Sylvania, Wilmington, MA). Chicks were provided continuous access to water and mash-formulated diets meeting Ross 708 nutritional guidelines for starter (d 0-14), grower (d 14-28), finisher 1 (d 28-42), and finisher 2 (d 42-49; NRC, 1994; Table 1). Lighting was gradually reduced from 24 h/d (30-40 lux) on d 0-7 to 22 h (20-30 lux) from d 8 to 49. Environmental monitoring included daily assessments of room temperature and humidity (Table 2), and weekly air-quality measurements of ammonia levels at bird height in the front, middle, and back of each room (GasAlert Extreme, BW Technologies, Schaumburg, IL). Additionally, litter moisture was evaluated weekly in three randomly selected pens per room, representing front, middle, and back sections, following National Chicken Council audit protocols (NCC, 2017; Table 2).

Laser enrichment device & camera set-up

Second-generation laser enrichment devices, as described by Meyer et al. (2023a), were mounted to a custom-built wooden framework suspended 2.4 m above two adjoining pens. Each laser device contained five synchronized red lasers, with the center laser centered over the two adjoining pens. All laser devices were programmed to emit slow-moving, randomized circular projections across the litter of both pens simultaneously. These enrichment sessions were scheduled for four 8-min intervals daily to stimulate bird activity (05:30, 11:30, 17:30, and 23:30). To reduce potential disturbances, personnel’s access to the barn was restricted for one hour during each enrichment window (e.g., 05:00–06:00, 11:00–12:00, etc.).

A total of 14 pens were randomly chosen for behavioral observation (n = 7 per treatment). Each selected pen was outfitted with a Sony HDR-CX440 Handycam (Sony Corp. of America, New York, NY), securely mounted above the pen using adjustable brackets to ensure a full view of the pen floor space. Continuous video footage was recorded at 30 frames per second from 04:00 to 24:00 daily during the first 9 d of life (d 0-9), and then once per week on d 16, 23, 30, 37, and 44. Adobe Premiere Pro (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA) was used to extract footage specifically covering the four daily 8-min laser enrichment periods: 05:30-05:38, 11:30-11:38, 17:30-17:38, and 23:30-23:38.

Performance

Broiler bird body weight was recorded at placement (d 0) and at the end of each performance period for all pens. Feed intake (FI), body weight, weight gain, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated on a per-bird basis, with FCR values adjusted to account for mortality.

Observer training and behavior analysis

Using protocols adapted from Meyer et al. (2020); Meyer et al. (2021), observers assigned to video analysis were trained by an experienced researcher with over two years of expertise in broiler behavioral assessment. Each observer participated in individual training sessions, during which they reviewed randomly selected 8-min clips of laser enrichment periods across all video time points. Training continued until observers reached at least 90% agreement with the trainer. Each observer was assigned a specific pen(s) to evaluate independently. To maintain data integrity throughout the study, the lead trainer conducted periodic quality checks by independently analyzing two or more randomly chosen clips per enrichment period at each recorded timepoint. Due to the visibility of laser devices and their structural supports in the footage, observers could not be masked from treatment.

Walking Distance. To assess broiler bird walking activity during laser enrichment, the blue-marked focal birds (n = 7 per treatment) were observed using protocols adapted from Meyer et al. (2020). Walking distance was quantified with a custom measurement tool in Adobe Photoshop (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA). The measured space between two segments of the nipple water line within the barn (58.4 cm) was measured and used to calibrate the pixel-to-distance ratio for each video. Focal bird movement was tracked minute by minute during the 8-min laser period, and the distance covered each minute was summed to determine the total walking distance per enrichment session.

Gait score and contact dermatitis presence

All focal birds (n = 70) were removed from their home pens weekly on d 3, 10, 17, 24, 31, 38, and 45 for gait scoring, breast blister, and footpad dermatitis assessments. Evaluations were conducted by the same two trained researchers at the same time each day, scheduled to avoid behavioral recordings. After each assessment session, the livestock-safe paint was reapplied to all focal birds to maintain focal bird identification.

Gait score procedures were based on methods outlined by Meyer et al. (2020) and the National Chicken Council guidelines (2017). Birds were individually placed at one end of a 1.5 m custom-built plywood runway and allowed to walk the full length unassisted or with gentle prompting. Gait was evaluated using a three-point scale, with a score of 0 indicating the bird walked the whole distance without any signs of lameness, a score of 1 indicating the bird completed the distance but showed uneven steps or sat down at least once, and a score of 2 indicating the bird was unable to walk the entire 1.5 m without stopping or showed obvious signs of lameness.

Following the gait assessment, each focal bird was examined for breast blisters and footpad dermatitis. The breast skin was inspected using criteria adapted from Greene et al. (1985), with lesions classified as “present” if one or more blisters, burns, or scabs ≥ 1.27 cm2 were observed. Birds without any visible lesions were scored as “absent.” Footpads were evaluated using a binary pass/fail system following the American Association of Avian Pathologists’ Paw Scoring Guidelines (2015). A “pass” was assigned to birds with normal yellow pads or mild discoloration with thickened skin covering less than 50% of the pad. A “fail” was recorded when signs of severe irritation, such as scabbing, erosion, bleeding, swelling, or ulceration, affected more than half the footpad.

Bone quality

On d 49, all focal birds were euthanized using carbon dioxide for tibia bone quality measures (n = 70; 35/trt group). The right tibia was collected from each focal bird. After removing the cartilage caps, all bones were immediately frozen at −20°C for later analysis. Prior to scanning, tibias were thawed at room temperature, blotted dry, weighed, and scanned in groups of 7 or fewer using the "infant whole-body scan" procedure for bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mineral density (BMD) using dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Hologic, Marlborough, MA, USA). Following DXA, the same tibias were utilized to assess bone-breaking strength (BBS) using an Instron 3367 Universal Testing Machine (Instron, Norwood, MA), following procedures adapted from Meyer et al. (2021). Briefly, each tibia was positioned horizontally between the upper and lower platens of the machine, with the natural curvature of the tibia facing downward. A vertical load was applied to each tibia at a rate of 10 mm/min and a 15% load application rate until fracture occurred. The test was automatically stopped when the system detected a 40% drop in applied force or manually upon observing a concurrent audible break and a decrease in load on the display. The peak force (kgf) at fracture was recorded and divided by the tibia’s cross-sectional area (cm2; determined by DXA) to calculate BBS, following the manufacturer’s guidelines (Instron, Norwood, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using pen as a random effect nested within enrichment treatment, reflecting the random assignment of birds to pens. Due to confounding between barn room and enrichment treatment, room was excluded from the model. Prior to analysis, data distributions were evaluated using PROC UNIVARIATE (SAS software version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 2016). Performance and tibia data were analyzed using a mixed linear model (PROC MIXED) with enrichment treatment as a main effect. Pearson correlation coefficients (PROC CORR) were calculated to evaluate the relationships among tibia characteristics. Walking distance data exhibited non-normal distributions and were evaluated using Akaike’s Information Criterion and dispersion diagnostics. Poisson models exhibited substantial overdispersion (Pearson χ2/DF = 86.02 and 132.38), whereas negative binomial models improved model fit and accounted for dispersion (Pearson χ2/DF = 0.36 and 0.39). Accordingly, walking distance data were analyzed using negative binomial generalized linear mixed models (PROC GLIMMIX) that included fixed effects of enrichment treatment (control vs. laser), day or week, and the interaction between enrichment and day/week. Least-square means and SEM were reported for all analyzed variables. Statistical significance was denoted at P ≤ 0.05, with tendencies between 0.05 and 0.10. Gait scores, breast blister incidence, and footpad dermatitis data were not subjected to statistical testing, as gait abnormalities were rare (scores above 0 were infrequent), and no cases of breast blisters or footpad dermatitis were observed.

Results

Performance

Feed intake was unaffected by enrichment treatment during the starter, finisher 1, and overall periods (P > 0.05, Table 3). Control birds tended to consume 0.04 kg more feed during the grower phase versus laser-enriched birds (P = 0.08). Inversely, laser-enriched birds tended to consume 0.04 kg more than control birds during finisher 2 (P = 0.08). Gain tended to be 0.01 kg greater in control birds than laser-enriched birds during the starter phase (P = 0.06), but remained similar across treatment groups for the grower, finisher 1, and finisher 2 phases, and overall (P > 0.05). FCR was 3 points higher in laser-enriched birds than control birds during the starter phase (P = 0.01), but remained similar across treatment groups for the grower, finisher 1, and finisher 2 phases, and overall (P > 0.05). Body weight was unaffected by enrichment treatment on d 0, 42, and 49 (P > 0.05). Control birds tended to be heavier on d 14 by 0.01 kg and d 28 by 0.05 kg compared to laser-enriched birds (P = 0.07 and P = 0.09, respectively).

Walking distance

Enrichment treatment main effect impacted total distance walked by focal birds, with laser-enriched birds walking 40.3 cm further than control birds over d 0-8 (P = 0.03; Supplemental Figure 1). Day of life did not impact total distance walked (P = 0.19). However, walking distance was numerically increased in laser-enriched birds compared to control birds (73.4 vs. 67.0 cm, respectively) and greatest in wk 1 and 3 (89.3-94.8 cm vs. 48.7-70.6 cm).

Gait score and contact dermatitis

A gait score of 0 was awarded to all focal birds (n = 70) throughout wk 1-7. All focal birds scored “absent” on breast blisters and “pass” on footpad dermatitis throughout the study. Ammonia levels were similar between barn rooms throughout the study, with the highest ammonia level reaching 3 ppm in the control room on wk 7, and 2 ppm in the laser-enriched room on wk 4 and 7 (Table 2). Both barn rooms received passing litter scores for all 7 wk of the study.

Bone quality

Tibia BMD increased by 11.1% in laser-enriched birds at d 49 (P = 0.04, Table 4). Tibia weight and BMC was not affected by enrichment on d 49 (P = 0.72 and P = 0.80, respectively). There was a numerical increase in BMC by 0.02 g in laser-enriched birds compared to their control counterparts. No differences were observed in tibia BBS due to enrichment treatment (P = 0.88). There was a strong positive correlation between bird BW and tibia weight (r = 0.72, P < 0.0001). A moderate, positive correlation existed between BMD and BMC (r = 0.53, P < 0.0001). There was a weak negative correlation between BMD and BBS (r = −0.19, P = 0.09) and a strong negative correlation between BMC and BBS (r = −0.70, P < 0.0001). The remaining correlation coefficients were nonsignificant.

Discussion

Previous work evaluating a laser environmental enrichment setting of 4-min, 4 times daily, has demonstrated consistent improvements in broiler growth performance across two genetic broiler lines. In Ross 308 broilers, laser enrichment increased FI and weight gain across all performance phases compared to control (Meyer et al., 2019). In addition, laser-enriched birds were more feed efficient at the grower and finisher phases, as well as overall (Meyer et al., 2019). In Ross 708 broilers, laser enrichment increased FI in grower and finisher 1 phases, weight gain in the starter and grower phases, and improved starter FCR (Meyer et al., 2021a). When laser exposure duration was extended to 6-min four times daily over a 7-wk grow-out, laser enrichment did not affect Ross 708 growth performance (Meyer et al., 2023a).

In the present study, increasing the laser setting further to 8-min four times daily tended to increase FI during the grower and finisher 2 phases. The increased FI observed in laser-enriched birds may be due to direct laser stimulation or inter-bird disturbance that encourages movement towards feeders, as previously hypothesized by Meyer and others (2019). Although feed conversion and gain (trend) were reduced during the starter period due to laser enrichment in the current study, this effect did not persist later in production, as feed efficiency and body weight were comparable between treatments by the end of grow-out (d 42 and 49). Further, growth performance observed in the current study was within expected ranges across performance periods outlined in Aviagen Ross 708 performance objectives (2022, Table 3). This suggests that laser enrichment did not negatively affect overall productivity and was not below industry standards.

Laser enrichment increased locomotion during the first week of life (d 0-8) in the current study. This finding is consistent with previous work demonstrating that laser enrichment is effective at stimulating movement during the early life stage (Meyer et al., 2021b; 2023b). Walking distance was not consistently impacted by enrichment across wk 1-7 in the current study, as previously observed by Meyer and others (wk 4, 2021b; wk 2, 2023b). This could be a result of potential habituation with increased exposure time compared to previous research trials, as well as known age-dependent decline in broiler activity (Sherlock et al., 2010; Norring et al., 2016). Although laser impact on locomotion was modest in the current study, it is important to note that increased locomotion during early life stages may be biologically relevant and influential in bone quality outcomes on d 49. These findings suggest that while laser enrichment increased activity early in life, it is unlikely that changes in activity level altered energy expenditure, as feed intake, growth performance, and feed efficiency were not negatively affected by laser enrichment over time.

Increased activity has been shown to be critical for bone development and increased tibia strength in broilers (Reiter and Bessei, 2009; Rath et al., 2000). Following 8-min 4 times daily laser exposure, enrichment increased tibia BMD at market age. Previous results have numerically but not statistically improved BMD or BMC using this research model (Meyer et al., 2021b; 2023b). BMD and/or BMC may be difficult to increase statistically over a 49-d grow-out period, given the duration and propensity for resting increases as broilers reach market weight. Therefore, early and continued increases in movement may be required to statistically improve bone quality in this model system. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to further investigate the relationship between tibia traits. The observed positive relationship between BMD and BMC was expected as both variables are derived from DXA measurements, and BMD represents mineral content normalized to scanned bone area. Additionally, previous studies have consistently reported strong associations between bone densitometric traits in broilers and other species (Rath et al., 2000; Hester et al., 2004). However, BMD was weakly correlated with BBS, suggesting improvements in BMD did not equate to increased BBS, which was an interesting outcome given that statistical significance was reached for BMD. As in all biological systems, statistical difference may not equate to a physiological difference (difference of 0.01 g/cm2 in BMD). These results contrast with previous work which reported positive relationships between bone mineralization and mechanical strength (Rath et al., 2000; Blake and Fogelman, 2002).

These findings indicate that increases in mineral content are often associated with improved bone breaking strength. However, other bone traits, such as bone geometry, mineral distribution, collagen crosslink content, and organic matrix composition, have been shown to impact bone strength (Rath et al., 1999; Rath et al., 2000; Blake and Fogelman, 2002). This suggests that BMD alone may not fully explain variation in bone breaking strength. Therefore, mineral deposition may have occurred in regions that contributed minimally to bone breaking strength in the current study. Further, tibia breaking strength does not capture alterations in epiphyseal or growth plate regions, which are commonly observed in the development of tibial dyschondroplasia and may be directly associated with pain and lameness in broilers (McGeown et al., 1999; Garner et al., 2002). Future work investigating these regions using histological or inflammatory markers could be used to evaluate the impact of laser-stimulated exercise on bone health or inflammatory status.

Laser enrichment did not affect gait score, breast blister prevalence, or footpad dermatitis incidence. The low prevalence of gait abnormalities and absence of breast blister and footpad dermatitis in this study may have been influenced by experimental conditions and the low environmental challenge of a research setting. For example, fresh wood shavings were used in the current study rather than the common practice of reusing litter in commercial production systems. Under these conditions, improvements in welfare outcomes due to laser enrichment may be difficult to detect. Future research evaluating laser enrichment under more challenging environmental conditions, where potential benefits may be more pronounced, is of interest. However, bone quality and welfare outcomes in the current study suggest that extended laser duration did not compromise these physical welfare measurements or skeletal integrity and are consistent with previous findings from shorter enrichment durations (Meyer et al., 2021b; 2023b).

In summary, the longer 8-min laser exposure duration in this research setting did not further enhance broiler growth performance, locomotion, welfare indicators, or bone quality compared to shorter durations at the same frequency of 4 times daily. Future research investigating additional behavioral measurements, including broiler time budget, bird interaction with laser enrichment, and fearfulness tests to laser enrichment are required to determine an optimal laser enrichment setting for use in commercial broiler production.
