Factors influencing early mobilisation for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery from multiple perspectives: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Yun-Xia Ni, Zhi Li, Li-Li Zhou, Shu Gong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesDespite early mobilisation as a key component of enhanced recovery after surgery pathways for pancreatic surgery, the implementation of early mobilisation remains unsatisfactory. What factors influence the implementation of early mobilisation from the lens of all stakeholders is unclear. The aim of this study was to identify the influencing factors of early mobilisation in pancreatic surgery from the perspective of patients, family members and health professionals.DesignA qualitative descriptive design using individual interviews and focus groups.Setting and participantsTwenty-two patients undergoing pancreatic surgery, 10 family members and 10 healthcare professionals from a large university teaching hospital in China.MethodsWe collected data on participants’ views on factors influencing early mobilisation after pancreatic surgery. Two researchers independently reviewed the transcripts and emergent coding. The data were analysed using qualitative content analysis.ResultsThree main categories that influenced the implementation of early mobilisation in pancreatic surgery were identified: (1) attitude towards early postoperative mobilisation (eg, perceived advantages or disadvantages of early mobilisation), (2) subjective norm (eg, impact from health professionals, family members and fellow patients) and (3) perceived behavioural control (eg, knowledge, abilities, resources and environment).ConclusionFactors influencing early mobilisation are diverse and multidimensional. The successful implementation of early mobilisation requires the dedication of both patients and healthcare professionals.Keywords: Pancreatic surgery, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Behavior

Introduction

The number of patients undergoing pancreatic surgery has grown dramatically in recent decades,1 and the incidence rate of pancreatic cancer was 11.0 per 100 000 person-years in 2016, which increased by 32.5% from 1970 to 2016.2 Patients undergoing pancreatic surgery experience significant postoperative morbidity (eg, pneumonia and thromboses), ranging from 35% to 64%,3–5 since pancreas resection remains a difficult and major surgery. In recent decades, enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways have been well accepted and implemented in different types of surgery worldwide and have significantly reduced postoperative morbidity, length of hospital stay and costs without increasing the risk of complications after surgery.6 7 Recently, ERAS protocols have been applied in pancreatic surgery patients,8 and their feasibility and effectiveness have also been confirmed.9

Early mobilisation, as a key component of ERAS, is strongly recommended by perioperative care guidelines despite a relatively low level of evidence supporting its use.8 Evidence has documented various benefits of early mobilisation.10–12 However, the implementation of early mobilisation has been repeatedly reported to be unsatisfactory.13–15 Among patients undergoing pancreaticoduodenectomy, adherence to early mobilisation targets was reported to be only 47%, and 71% of patients with low compliance with ERAS pathways suffered complications.14 In contrast, patients who comply with early mobilisation experience a shorter length of hospitalisation12 and faster recovery of physical function.16

The barriers to the implementation of early postoperative mobilisation are complex and multifaceted, varying across different institutions, contexts and cultures,12 17 18 such as institutional-related barriers (eg, nurse-to-patient ratio) and patient-related barriers (eg, complications). Although a small number of studies have explored factors influencing the implementation of and adherence to early mobilisation,13 19 20 these studies largely focused on the whole procedure of the ERAS pathway instead of the specific component (ie, early mobilisation) or explored influencing factors solely from either patients’ or health professionals’ perspectives rather than multiple perspectives.21

Successful implementation of early mobilisation requires multidisciplinary coordination of care and involves patients and their family members.17 As such, understanding barriers and enabling early mobilisation from all stakeholders, including both healthcare providers (ie, health professionals) and customers (ie, patients, family members), is essential in building an effective implementation strategy.22 Particularly, patients, as behaviour subjects, have experiences and attitudes towards being out of bed early, which is extremely important to the successful implementation of early mobilisation,22 in keeping with the theory of planned behaviour (TPB) developed by Ajzen.23 Ajzen proposed this theory as a means of better understanding why people perform or do not perform a behaviour.23 Application of this theory has been useful in better understanding and identifying influencing factors in a multitude of behavioural domains.23 Few studies have used a theoretical framework to explain the behaviour of early mobilisation. Therefore, the TPB provides a potentially useful lens through which to understand the experience of early mobilisation and what factors affect its implementation. Moreover, available studies identifying factors impacting early mobilisation mainly used quantitative approaches,13 24 such as retrospective designs and cross-sectional surveys. Evidence suggests that barriers and facilitators to implementation of a programme, especially when multidisciplinary, are multifaceted, complex and difficult to measure using a quantitative approach.21 Qualitative research provides an effective means to grasp diverse, multidimensional and complete information.25 However, to date, few studies have used a qualitative design to identify the influencing factors of early mobilisation among patients undergoing pancreatic surgery from multiple perspectives.18 21

Therefore, this study used a qualitative design to identify the influencing factors of early postoperative mobilisation in pancreatic surgery from the perspective of patients, family members and health professionals and to further provide guidance to improve early mobilisation strategies.

Methods

Design

This study is part of a large research programme aiming to promote early postoperative mobilisation in pancreatic surgery patients. A qualitative descriptive design was used for this study involving individual semistructured interviews and focus group discussions to answer the following research question: What factors influence the implementation of early mobilisation after pancreatic surgery? A qualitative descriptive design, drawing from the naturalistic paradigm and staying closer to the data obtained, is considered particularly appropriate when the goal of the study is to inform practice. Given the purpose of the current study, we considered this approach appropriate to allow us to identify facilitators and barriers to the implementation of postoperative early mobilisation and to further guide future intervention. Individual interviews were conducted with patients after pancreatic surgery and their family members in an effort to understand their unique experiences in postoperative early mobilisation. Focus group discussions were conducted with health professionals to gain a broad and diverse understanding of these issues. The COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative studies26 was used in this study.

Theoretical frameworks

The TPB is one of the most widely used psychological theories to explain and predict behaviour, which is underpinned by the assumption that human behaviour is essentially rational.27 The TPB28 suggests that the best determinant of behaviour is intention, which can be predicted by three constructs, namely, attitude towards the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control. Attitudes refer to a personal evaluation of the behaviour (eg, performing early mobilisation is good); subjective norms refer to perceptions of pressure from important others to a given behaviour (eg, important others would want me to mobilise); and perceived behavioural control refers to the extent to which people believe that the decision to perform the given behaviour is entirely up to them (eg, it would be difficult for me to perform early mobilisation). The use of TPB helps us gain a deeper understanding of the comprehensive (eg, motivating, internal and external factors) factors that influence patients’ early mobilisation.

Participants and settings

This study was conducted in a pancreatic surgery ward with 72 beds of a large university teaching hospital that is a leading medical centre and the largest hospital in Southwest China, equipped with 4300 beds. Convenience sampling and purposive sampling with maximum variation were used to recruit a heterogeneous sample of information-rich participants. This study sought a sample that varied in terms of age, sex, education level, type of operation, postoperative days and the occupation of healthcare professionals. Eligibility criteria for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery included age above 18 years, ability to communicate and voluntary participation in the study. Patients who had mental disorders or simultaneously participated in other research studies were excluded. Family members were eligible to participate if they were involved in caring for a patient undergoing pancreatic surgery (not necessarily caring for the patients who participated in our study) in an informal way (not paid). Healthcare professionals were eligible to participate if they provided direct care for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery and had at least 1 year of work experience in the study setting.

Data collection

The data were collected between April 2022 and May 2022 using face-to-face individual semistructured interviews and one focus group discussion. Researchers contacted the potential participants to arrange interviews and focus groups at their convenience. Prior to the data collection, we explained the purpose of the study and obtained written informed consent from the participants. The researchers did not previously know the participants before conducting the interviews.

The interview guide for the semistructured individual and focus group interviews (see box 1) was developed based on the TPB27 that was drafted by the first author and refined via discussion with the research team. Two pilot-tested individual interviews were conducted. The pilot data were included in the analysis, as the interview questions did not need further changes.

All individual interviews were conducted by the first author, who has a doctor’s degree in nursing science and rich experience in qualitative research. The focus group was led by two researchers, one of whom was an experienced moderator and an observer who assisted and took field notes to ensure the identification of speakers. The individual interviews with patients after pancreatic surgery were carried out in a single bedroom of the pancreatic surgery ward, and they remained in bed to ensure safety and comfort, whereas interviews with family members were conducted in a quiet meeting room. The focus group was held in a discussion room, and no other people were present besides the moderator, observer and participants. Demographic data were collected at the start of the interviews and focus group discussions. All the individual interviews and the focus group were carried out in person, audiorecorded and transcribed verbatim. Individual interviews lasted an average of 32 min, and the focus group lasted 79 min. Data collection took place until data saturation was reached. Memos were constructed to clarify the researcher’s thoughts about the interview and data. Repeat interviews were not conducted.

Data analysis

Data analysis was carried out simultaneously with data collection. Interviews and focus group data were pooled and analysed collectively. The transcripts were analysed using deductive content analysis with the process guided by the approach taken by Elo and Kyngäs29 and Assarroudi et al.30 A categorisation matrix of the main categories and related subcategories was deductively developed based on the TPB (see table 1), and then these categories were defined accurately. Two researchers independently read and reviewed all the transcripts several times to become immersed in the data. The same two researchers extracted meaning units and coded the transcripts using the predetermined categorisation matrix where possible. Coding disagreements were resolved by discussion. Next, codes were grouped and categorised according to their similarities, differences and meanings. Text that could not be coded into one of the predetermined categories was inductively analysed to ensure that important information was not lost. Finally, the research team carefully reviewed the categories and subcategories to determine if these categories were coherent and distinct from each other and eventually refined and named the categories. To gain an integrated understanding of factors influencing early mobilisation, data from patients, family members and healthcare professionals were integrally analysed.31 Data were stored and managed using NVivo V.9.2 software.

Rigour

The trustworthiness of this qualitative study was ensured by achieving credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability.32 Credibility was achieved by selecting a heterogeneous sample and taking field notes. Additional credibility was established by using data (individual interviews and focus groups) and researcher triangulation. To increase dependability, the interview questions were developed based on the theory, the transcripts were independently coded by two researchers and a consensus was reached. Confirmability was ensured by a substantial use of quotes from different participants. To ensure transferability, a detailed description of the sample information and data collection process and adequate raw data were given.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Findings

Forty-two participants were recruited for the study. The sample comprised 22 patients after pancreatic surgery, 10 family members and 10 health professionals. Patients undergoing pancreatic surgery were aged between 26 and 69 years (mean age of 56.6 years), and family members were aged between 32 and 65 years (mean age of 46.0 years). The group of health professionals was composed of three surgeons, four nurses, two physiotherapists and one psychologist, with an average age of 33.4 years old and an average of 9 years of experience caring for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery. Category 1: attitude towards early postoperative mobilisation

This category relates to the participants’ feelings and evaluations of early postoperative mobilisation along a dimension of favour or disfavour, good or bad. Almost all health professionals believed that early mobilisation after pancreatic operation has many benefits for patients, whereas patients and family members held a breadth of attitudes ranging from very positive to very negative dispositions. This category included the subcategories of perceived advantages and disadvantages of early mobilisation.

Perceived advantages of early mobilisation

The patients’ or family members’ positive attitudes towards early mobilisation derive from their perceived advantages and benefits of mobilisation. They believed that early postoperative mobilisation can promote faster recovery (eg, intestinal peristalsis and early oral feeding) and reduce the risk for postoperative complications (eg, thrombosis, abdominal adhesion and pneumonia). The perceptions of advantages acted as facilitators of patients’ early mobilisation.

It (early mobilization) is good for my recovery, such as benefits for lung function and intestinal peristalsis. In fact, recovery is a systematic project; if you get out of bed early, your intestinal peristalsis will recover early, and then you can eat early, ultimately you recover early from operation. (Patient 7)

Patients were motivated to mobilise and move their body since they considered early mobilisation as an effective way of reducing the most frequent postoperative symptoms, such as pain, dizziness and nausea, and even improving their psychological status.

I benefited from getting up. My mind becomes clearer, and I can breathe easier. The dizziness disappeared. (Patient 7)

I am truly happy when I get up to walk, and it is a good sign of recovery. (Patient 7)

Perceived disadvantages of early mobilisation

Not all patients made the decision to get out of bed early. Several patients and family members had negative attitudes towards early postoperative mobilisation. These participants articulated that pancreatic surgery was a ‘serious injury’ and that they had suspicious thoughts about whether early mobilisation was safe, which has become a barrier to patients’ engagement in early mobilisation. Patients worried about whether early mobilisation would cause problems such as increasing pain, becoming too weak, dislodging lines or tubes, putting a strain on their wound or unexpected postoperative complications.

I dare not to mobilize, you know. This may cause problems. I am afraid of tearing my stitches out or whatever they’ve fixed on me. (Patient 10)

I am concerned about the safety of getting out of bed. After all, I had such a major operation. (Patient 18)

A recurrent concern was patients’ fear of falls when participating in mobilisation. Several patients felt that walking would precipitate a fall, and they were at risk for falls, followed by the healthcare professionals, who also pointed out that mobilisation may increase the risk of patients falling.

I am afraid of falling, and I would fall when I get up to walk. (Patient 4)

Patient safety was our responsibility. Mobilization may lead to falls. (Nurse 3)”

Uncertainty about the risk of early postoperative mobilisation hindered the implementation of early mobilisation. One caregiver described his confusion about the uncertainty:

What I'm worried about is… I do not know what’s going to happen after getting out of bed. There is a risk of doing it every time. (Caregiver 3)

The psychologist also mentioned uncertain risk, which may prevent patients from early mobilisation:

Patients fear many unknown events, and these fears are obstacles to implementing early mobilization. (Psychologist)

Category 2: subjective norm

Patients reported that a variety of the most important people around them affected their intention to conduct early mobilisation. Family members, health professionals, fellow patients and care workers were identified as the main important people by patients. Family members and healthcare professionals also mentioned the importance of social support to promote successful early mobilisation.

Impact of health professionals

Almost all patients sensed that healthcare professionals provided much support, encouragement and direction in their early mobilisation. Patients addressed professionals as important motivators of early mobilisation. They usually followed healthcare professionals’ suggestions of being out of bed due to their knowledge, expertise, skills and abilities, which was known as ‘expert power’.

Almost every medical staff member says, ‘You are to move as much as possible’. I have to get out of bed to walk. I did as much as I was told to do. (Patient 1)

Some patients described nurses as ‘angels’, who usually encouraged them to get out of bed to walk during nurse–patient interactions, resulting in their participation in mobilisation.

Nurses motivated me…They usually said, “Move slowly and slowly, you need try your best to move. Believe yourself, and you can do it.” (Patient 12)

Trust is an important issue in regard to ‘expert power’. Patients and family members were more inclined to trust surgeons, and healthcare professionals also believed that patients tended to trust surgeons. Hence, if the encouragement comes from a surgeon, it would influence patients’ thinking slightly more than if it was to come from other professionals. Health professionals further discussed that if trust was compromised, it had a negative impact on patients’ engagement in early mobilisation.

For patients, the most trusted person is the surgeon, so the guidance and encouragement of the surgeon is the most important. (Surgeon 1)

Once a patient receives inconsistent information from different medical staff, his trust in professionals is compromised, which affects his intention to participate in early mobilization. (Psychologist)

All healthcare professionals perceived that conducting successful early mobilisation was a team effort and needed multidisciplinary team involvement encompassing medical, physiotherapy and nursing components. Communication among multidisciplinary team members was identified by all professions as an important factor that promoted patient mobilisation. However, insufficient communication was raised by some professionals, acting as a barrier to early mobilisation.

Different professionals were not together during the ward round. We could not discuss the changeable barriers for patients’ early mobilization with other members of the team in time, such as intraoperative hemorrhage, pain management, and planned extubation time. (Physiotherapist 2)

Impact of family members

Both patients and health professionals stated that family members were an essential source for instrumental and psychological support. Companionship and encouragement from family can increase patients’ motivation to engage in early mobilisation.

What helped me is my husband. He always said, ‘You should get out of bed and walk.’ I am motivated by him. (Patient 16)

I couldn’t get up and move my body on my own. My family caregiver helps me get out of bed. (Patient 21)

While family members’ instrumental and emotional support motivated patients to leave bed, sometimes, family was seen as a barrier to patients’ engagement in mobilisation. One caregiver felt that his sick family member was too ‘ill’ to participate in early mobilisation and would not let the patient do anything.

I think she needs to wait for her body to heal more before getting out of bed, since I don’t want her too tired. (Caregiver 3)

Impact of fellow patients

Some patients stated how fellow patients impelled them to get out of bed. They were motivated by observing what their fellow patients were doing.

I saw him (the patient in the next bed) leave bed and walk outside the room; therefore, I think I should also get out of the bed early. (Patient 4)

The impact of fellow patients on early mobilisation was also mentioned by health professionals.

If one patient conducts a successful early mobilization, he (or she) sets an example among other patients. Others in the same room will imitate him to get out of bed early. However, if no patient got out of bed first, then all patients in the same room were less likely to engage in early mobilization. (Nurse 2)

Category 3: perceived behavioural control

Patients, family members and health professionals identified several factors that can facilitate or impede early mobilisation, which were divided into two subcategories: capacity and autonomy.

Perceptions of capacity

This subcategory relates to the perceived ability to perform early mobilisation, as determined by knowledge, skills and resources. Some patients reported that inadequate knowledge and skills hindered their participation in mobilisation. They do not know when and how often they should walk and what matters require attention during mobilisation.

It’s difficult for me to mobilize, and I do not even know how to stand and take steps. (Patient 17)

Although all patients received a leaflet about the information and knowledge of early mobilisation the day before surgery, they would have liked in-depth verbal explanations in addition to this written information. Several patients indicated that they needed sufficient time to digest the information and adequately prepare for their impending postoperative mobilisation. Two patients and some professionals suggested that the ideal timing of handing out this leaflet was considered to be as soon as patients were admitted to the hospital.

Actually, I didn’t truly have time to read the leaflet. If professionals sent it to me earlier, I might have acquired more knowledge related to mobilization. It should be sent to me when I was admitted to the hospital. (Patient 13)

At the same time, patients, caregivers and health professionals proposed several strategies to provide more effective and standardised information and help patients obtain a better understanding of early mobilisation. These strategies included providing more detailed knowledge (eg, body reactions during mobilisation, points for attention when getting in and out of bed, preparation for leaving bed) and using vivid health materials (eg, videos, pictures).

We need a playbook for guiding every step, telling us what should do and what should not do. The current information is too rough. (Caregiver 9)

Audio visual materials may be more conducive to our understanding of early mobilization. (Patient 6)

We can develop an app or a small video to guide patients in conducting successful early mobilization. (Surgeon 1)

Several participants reported that prior surgery and hospitalisation experiences made them acquire knowledge and skills related to mobilisation as drivers for early mobilisation during this surgery.

I had a gallstone surgery a few years ago, so I know some knowledge about being out of bed early. (Patient 13)

In terms of mobilisation ability, all patients stated that bodily sensations, such as dizziness, fatigue, pain and nausea, were the most important barriers affecting their active engagement in early mobilisation. Some patients who suffered extensively from postoperative symptoms, such as pain, were less likely to leave bed early.

I am reluctant to get out of bed to walk since sometimes I truly feel intensive pain. (Patient 20)

I feel dizzy when I change from a seated position to a standing position. I have to stop moving until the dizzy sensation subsides. (Patient 14)

Health professionals mentioned that patients’ medical characteristics (eg, high comorbidity, presentation with advanced disease, intraoperative bleeding volume) and burdensome symptoms, particularly inadequate perioperative pain control, made patients feel too weak and incapable of performing recommended mobilisation.

In fact, regardless of whether the surgery is large or small, every patient can successfully get out of bed early. Pain control is the number one issue that affects their ability to participate in early mobilization. (Physiotherapist 1)

Some patients believed that willpower is a crucial motivator for being out of bed, considered an inner resource. Rather than being stopped by various barriers (eg, uncomfortable symptoms), some patients still insisted on leaving bed early to walk. These patients attributed their successful early mobilisation to willpower.

Despite the pain, I am not discouraged from getting out of bed… Once you feel a pain you go back to bed, and how can you get out of bed early. (Patient 4)

Perceptions of autonomy

This subcategory refers to the extent to which individuals believe that the decision to perform early postoperative mobilisation is entirely up to them. Most patients and family members described that the machines (eg, ECG monitor, syringe pump) or tubes (eg, intravenous lines, Foley catheters and abdominal drain) restricted their movement and kept them in bed or in their hospital room.

I want to get out of bed, but how can I move with this thing? There are too many tubes with me, and we spend much time handling tethers when I get up. (Patient 8)

Several health professionals advised that removing unnecessary lines could be a way to encourage patients to get out of bed and walk.

The number of tubes affected patients’ intention to engage in early mobilization. We should eliminate unnecessary tubes. (Surgeon 3)

The hospital environment, such as insufficient staffing and limited space of patient rooms, was reported by several patients as a barrier to early postoperative mobilisation.

The professionals are too busy. They only spend a few minutes with me at a time; otherwise, I may get out of bed earlier. (Patient 7)

Narrow space results in patients lying on the bed instead of engaging in mobilisation.

It’s inconvenient for me to get up to walk inside of my room, you see, there are six patients in this room. There are too many people in one room. (Patient 17)

Discussion

This study explores the influencing factors of early mobilisation in pancreatic surgery from the perspective of patients, family members and health professionals with a broad range of occupations. Drawing on the TPB,23 three categories were identified: attitude towards early postoperative mobilisation, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control. This study provides detailed information about how patients experience early mobilisation after pancreatic surgery and identifies diverse, multidimensional barriers that prevent them from initiating mobilisation by different stakeholders, which will inform future intervention studies to improve early mobilisation for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery.

An important finding of this study is that patients’ attitudes towards early postoperative mobilisation, reflected in perceived advantages and disadvantages, affected the implementation of getting out of bed, confirming the TPB.23 Patients who have a positive and favourable attitude towards early mobilisation are more likely to move their body. This finding has seldom been described previously in early mobilisation among patients undergoing abdominal surgery, including pancreatic surgery. Similar results were found in other behavioural domains, such as self-care behaviour33 and technology acceptance behaviour,34 where findings indicated that attitude is an essential factor for explaining and predicting a specific behaviour, and the motivation for a behaviour may stem from awareness of the benefits of the behaviour.35 Security issues are a significant determining factor as to whether patients engage in mobilisation. Patients’ and family members’ concern about risk (eg, lines or tubes dislodging, pain increasing, falls) was a barrier to early mobilisation, which has been reported in other studies that patients’ fear of falls hindered their engagement in mobilisation.36 According to the TPB,23 to motivate people to engage in early mobilisation, one of the most effective strategies is to form a favourable attitude towards mobilisation, that is, making individuals believe that mobilisation can contribute to positive outcomes instead of negative outcomes.

Our results highlighted that health professionals, as one of the most important persons around patients during the perioperative period, provide much encouragement for patients and stimulate them to engage in early mobilisation. This result can be explained by the TPB, which indicates that people can be motivated to behave in accordance with what they believe others think they should do, known as ‘expert power’.28 In particular, patients tend to trust those who have adequate knowledge, rich experiences and strong abilities,28 such as surgeons. Hence, improving training and education for health professionals is necessary to enhance ‘expert power’ and to facilitate patient mobilisation participation. Our findings are in agreement with those of Pearsall et al,21 who found that healthcare providers with insufficient knowledge could be a barrier to patient mobilisation, suggesting that education and reeducation of the multidisciplinary team was a strategy to increase compliance with ERAS. Multidisciplinary cooperation is recommended in implementing early mobilisation for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery; however, the cooperation between health professionals was poor in our study (eg, limited communication), which hindered early mobilisation. To enhance communication between team members, early mobilisation can be discussed in patient ward rounds when everyone is present,37 and regular conferences are scheduled to share ideas with each other.

Consistent with previous studies,38 family members are significant sources of both instrumental (eg, assisting patients to get out of bed) and emotional support (eg, encouragement) for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery. Both patients and health professionals noted that a competent caregiver can promote successful early mobilisation. An interesting finding was that, sometimes, family members could be an obstacle to patients’ mobilisation that was influenced by traditional Chinese culture. In Chinese culture, family is a very important concept for people.39 When the family member is sick, other family members take the responsibility to take good care of the sick ones and even help them do anything even if these things can be done by the sick ones themselves. Similar findings were reported in the studies by Li et al,40 who found the family’s obstruction of patients’ exercise. Our results showed the impact of fellow patients in motivating or discouraging patients from early mobilisation and supported the TPB, which demonstrated that peer pressure is an important determinant of behaviour.28 A similar finding has been reported in Mathiasen et al,18 who described how fellowship between patients impelled them to leave bed after colorectal surgery.

Patients overwhelmingly perceived that they acquired inadequate knowledge and information related to early mobilisation as a barrier. Patients further described their desire to obtain information in advance about early mobilisation and how their body could react to mobilisation. Studies41 42 have demonstrated that increasing patients’ level of knowledge about early mobilisation prior to surgery is crucial to successful ERAS programmes, emphasising the importance of sufficient and timely preoperative and postoperative information provided to patients. Our study confirmed these findings and further found that written information given preoperatively was insufficient to help patients actively engage in early mobilisation, and patients desired more detailed and vivid patient education materials. These findings suggested that healthcare professionals should provide systematic information and ensure that patients understand it to inspire patients to play an active role in early mobilisation.43 In addition, we found that willpower is a facilitator of patient active engagement in early mobilisation, consistent with the TPB. Willpower, namely, self-control, is considered a personal inner resource that is positively associated with advancing goals. Patients who have strong willpower are likely to overcome difficulties (eg, pain) and make efforts to engage in a desired behaviour, including early mobilisation, in this study.

Optimal symptom management is a cornerstone of the implementation of early mobilisation. We found that patients who experienced postoperative symptoms (eg, pain, nausea and fatigue) may postpone getting out of bed, as also reported in previous studies.18 44 Therefore, burdensome symptoms that prevent patients from moving should be controlled or improved as much as possible. Studies have indicated that the hospital environment (eg, raised beds) and treatment factors (eg, machines and tubes) keep patients in bed and discourage movement.36 45 Our results confirmed these findings and showed that patients undergoing pancreatic surgery do indeed consider hospitals as an unwelcoming space for mobilisation and early recovery. Early removal of drains, as one component of ERAS, is important to promote optimal postoperative recovery.46 Hence, health professionals may remove unnecessary drains early according to the patients’ clinical condition. It is not surprising that a lack of staffing is a barrier to patients’ successful participation in mobilisation, and this barrier is not easily changeable.37 Involving family members early and improving their capability to support the patient in mobilisation is a feasible strategy.38

The study has several limitations. While a broad range of stakeholders for early mobilisation were selected to participate in this study, it is possible that the perspectives of the participants may not be applicable to other hospital wards, countries or cultures since the participants were recruited from only one site. Another limitation was that to minimise the load of the study for participants, transcripts were not returned to participants, and there was no opportunity to gain feedback validation. Furthermore, the potential selection bias towards motivated and enthusiastic participants could not be avoided because recruitment was done by invitation.

Conclusions

This study offers insights into what factors affect early mobilisation for patients undergoing pancreatic surgery from the lens of patients, family members and health professionals. Barriers and facilitators to the implementation of early mobilisation are diverse and multidimensional. The findings can be applied to inform the development of interventions to improve surgical patients’ engagement in early mobilisation.
