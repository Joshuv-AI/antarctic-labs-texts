Additive outcome of platelet rich fibrin neourethral coverage of tubularized incised plate in primary distal hypospadias repair
Authors: Abdullah M Mansour, Ezzat A Ismail, Mohamed O Abdalla, Ahmed M El Nashar, Iman Y Ismail, Khaled M Abdelhalim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealing complications are a significant concern after hypospadias repair, often attributed to deficient growth factors and reduced healing potential of hypospadiac tissue. Background

Tubularized Incised Plate (TIP) urethroplasty is the most widely used technique for hypospadias repair, particularly for distal cases [1]. The most common complication is urethro-cutaneous fistula (UCF), which occurs in 4–8% of primary distal cases [2].

Many factors affect the outcome of TIP repair, such as hypospadias type, suturing method and post operative care [3]. The most crucial aspect is to minimize fistula formation by avoiding overlap of suture-lines with soft tissue interposition over the neourethra [2].

Hypospadiac skin is found to be lacking growth factors [4]. Additionlly, hypospadiac tissues exhibit local ischemic changes attributed to deficient angiogenesis or aggressive dissection, contributing to poor healing after repair [5]. Consequently, providing a mechanical barrier and a healing promotor by the covering layer will be an ideal option.

Platelet concentrates have been successfully used in modern medicine, such as oral and maxillofacial surgery [6], dentistry [7], orthopedics, plastic and general surgery [8]. Their widespread use can be accredited to their influence on tissue regeneration by promoting angiogenesis and successive stages of wound healing [9].

PRF is the latest evolution of platelet concentrates that is more efficient than its predecessors in biological efficacy and more simple [10]. PRF is a rich source of growth factors (e.g. transforming growth factor β (TGFβ), platelet derived growth factor (PDGF), insulin like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), etc.) which are responsible for the accelerated healing events that PRF mediates [11]. Additionally, PRF has a substantial proportion of host immune cells, including leukocytes, which promote local wound healing and resist infection [10].

The current study assessed the role of the PRF membrane coverage for the neourethra combined with Dartos flap in improving TIP repair results in primary distal hypospadias cases.

Methods

This is a prospective randomized study, conducted on children with primary hypospadias between September 2019 and March 2022. This study is not a clinical trial (Clinical trial number: not applicable). Approval was obtained from our institutional ethics committee under number 3993/2019. The parents provided informed consent. Only patients older than 6 months with primary distal hypospadias (coronal, subcoronal and distal penile types) who were candidates for TIP repair were recruited for the study. Patients with previous penile surgery (e.g. circumcision) or received hormonal treatment were excluded.

Sample size (n) was calculated using the following formula: n= (Zα/2 + Zβ)2*2*σ2/d2, where Zα/2 is the critical value of the normal distribution at α/2 (for a confidence level of 95%, α is 0.05 and the critical value is 1.96), Zβ is the critical value of the Normal distribution at β (for a power of 80%, β is 0.2 and the critical value is 0.84), σ2 is the population variance (660), and d is the difference (26) according to Soyer et al. 2011). With addition of 20% dropout, final sample size was 19 cases per group [12, 13].

Forty-five patients randomized into two equal groups using random generated numbers by the Random Allocation software (Sealed Envelope Allocation Software (Version 2.3). Smith, J., Johnson, K., & Doe, J. (2022). Cases were randomly divided into two groups using a 1:1 ratio. Twenty-three patients were allocated to Group A (with one case omitted due to lost follow-up), and the rest were allocated to group B. The two groups were characterized based on the neourethra’s covering layer(s). The first group (control) underwent hypospadias repair with the classical single preputial Dartos flap layer (group A). In the second group (test group), an autologous platelet-rich fibrin membrane and a single Dartos flap were employed (group B). The study was single-blinded. However, to further limit bias throughout follow-up period and post operative care, caregivers were blinded from each case allocation to either group.

Surgical technique

To ensure standardization, All surgeries were performed by the same operator using the same technique (TIP repair [1]), and using the same suture materials. The procedure was done under general anaesthesia, followed by a caudal nerve block to reduce postoperative pain.

A U-shaped incision was made outside the apparent edges of the urethral plate, two millimeters proximal to the original meatus. Complete degloving of the penile skin downwards to the penoscrotal junction. A deep relaxing midline incision was performed to broaden the urethral plate, then tubularized over a silicon catheter 6–10 fr (according to meatus size) starting proximally and moving distally. First layer closure was done with interrupted 6/0 polygalactin subcuticular sutures, terminating before glans tip; rendering an oval neomeatus with appropriate diameter. A highly vascularized pedicled Dartos flap was retrieved from dorsal preputial and penile shaft skin.

In group A, the pedicled Dartos flap was flipped ventrally to cover the neourethra using 6/0 polygalactan stitches. The mucosal collars of the glans were closed above Dartos flap, The superfluous skin was excised and closed with 5/0 polygalactin interrupted sutures.

Preparation of the PRF membrane

The PRF protocol is simple and quick. Ten millimeters blood sample was aspirated during surgery from the femoral or a peripheral vein, prior to finishing the urethroplasty. The sample was housed in a sterile glass tube (BD Vacutainer Z, reference 368430) without anticoagulant and was immediately centrifuged in the operating room. Centrifugation was performed at 2700 rpm (400 g) for twelve minutes using a table centrifuge (Thermo Scientific Medifuge™ 2017, USA) [14] (Fig. 1a). Consequently, a fibrin clot developed in the center of the pipe (with massively trapped platelets), between the platelet poor plasma (top) and red blood corpuscles (RBCs) (bottom) (Fig. 1b).

(a) The centrifuge (Thermo Scientific Medifuge™). (b) formation of PRF clot

The fibrin clot (Fig. 2a) was retrieved with forceps and carefully pounded between two surgical pads to create a soft and resistant PRF membrane, about 10–15 mm in length and 1 mm in thickness (Fig. 2b). The process was carried out under strict aseptic settings [15]. Prompt handling is crucial to obtain a substantial and resilient membrane. Delays in blood collection or the start of centrifugation can result in failure to produce a suitable membrane.

(a) PRF clot extracted from tube. (b) PRF membrane

In group B, the PRF membrane was secured upon urethroplasty and secured with 6/0 polyglactin few interrupted sutures (Fig. 3), after that Dartos flap was rotated to be applied over. The glans and skin were closed as in group A, then non-compressive dressing (using Vaseline paper and gauze) was used.

Application of PRF membrane over the neourethra

All patients received parenteral ampicillin/sulbactam, starting intraoperatively for two days, followed by oral cefixime until the urethral catheter was removed. Pain was controlled by simple analgesics. Initial dressing was left in place for an average of 3 days, after which it was replaced on postoperative day 3. Urethral catheter remained in place for seven days.

After discharge, patients were assessed weekly during the first month and monthly for at least 6 months postoperatively. Outcomes and any complications were documented. Successful cases were those that healed without any complications;, a slit-like meatus at the apex of the glans and a normal voiding force and caliber. Complications were compared between the groups using Chi-square test, with significance set at a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

The study assessed 71 children with hypospadias, with 45 meeting inclusion criteria and undergoing surgery. One patient from Group A was lost to follow-up, resulting in 44 patients, with 22 in each group (Fig. 4).

The mean ages were 38 ± 28 and 30 ± 21 months for groups A and B respectively. None of the patients had associated congenital anomalies or chronic illnesses and all had normal coagulation profiles.

There were no complications related to venous puncture sites and no blood transfusions were required. The additional time required for applying the PRF membrane was minimal. The mean operative time for group A was 90 ± 9 min and 105 ± 5 min for group B. Post operative edema was mild and transient. During the follow-up period, no patients in either group had urethral strictures. Complete wound dehiscence or skin necrosis did not occur in any case. No penile torsion was observed.

The overall rate of UCF was 5 cases: 4 in group A and 1 in group B. Although the application of PRF in group B obviously reduced the rate of fistula formation (4.5% compared to 18.2% in groups B and A respectively), the difference was not statistically significant. UCF was associated with wound infection in 3 cases, with meatal stenosis in one case and a single complication in the last case. All instances of fistulae were identified following catheter removal. All of them were single, small (≤ 2 mm) and coronal (n = 2) or sub-coronal (n = 3). They were re-operated after 6 months with simple multi-layer closure. Their outcome is satisfactory.

Wound infection rates differed significantly (P < 0.05). In group B, wound infection was associated with UCF in 3 cases (which were reoperated) and superficial in the other 2 cases which resolved with conservative management (antibiotic and regular dressing). Meatal stenosis occurred in a total of 4 cases (two in each group). However, all of them responded well to regular dilatation.

Discussion

Several studies investigated the reduced healing potential in hypospadias tissues, and recorded decreased expression of EGF and its receptor [4], reduced microvssel density and decreased expression of VEGF and VEGFR [16, 17]. These growth factors are crucial for angiogenesis, epithelial proliferation, and wound healing. Therefore, their deficiency might attribute to impaired wound healing and recurring complications following hypospadias surgery [17].

In hypospadias repair, the use of an interposition layer covering the neourethra has been adopted as a key strategy to reinforce the urethroplasty and serve as a waterproof layer to lower UCF rates. Various options for this interposition layer include Dartos fascia, corpus spongiosum, tunica vaginalis, dorsal preputial skin, and scrotal Dartos flaps [18].

Regardless of these techniques, complications such as compromised blood supply to nearby tissues and flap necrosis can still occur. Additionally, lack of healthy tissues and fibrosis in recurrent repairs limits the effectiveness of local flaps necessitating the use of supportive tissues that can promote better healing [5, 19].

Tissue sealants (e.g. fibrin glue) have been proposed to provide a good sealing effect following urethroplasty [20]. However, many drawbacks of fibrin sealant were reported. Allergic reactions and transmission of infection are possible risks as it is prepared from multiple plasma donors and is processed with solvent and detergent suspension. Moreover, the high cost and low commercial availability of fibrin glue reduce its practical application [7].

Research on platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has explored its effectiveness in hypospadias repair. Eryilmaz et al. utilized PRP gel to get additional coverage in mid-penile hypospadias repair, which resulted in 10% UCF, 5% urethral stenosis, and 5% wound infection rates in the PRP group. In contrast, the group without PRP experienced 25% UCF, 25% urethral stenosis, and 35% wound infection rates [21].

Nevertheless, PRP gel also has limitations, such as relatively poor durability, lengthy preparation time (including two centrifugation cycles and an incubation period), and the risk of contamination due to external additives like bovine thrombin and calcium [22].

PRF is a polymerized fibrin matrix containing large quantities of cytokines, platelets, WBCs and growth factors that are slowly released over time. Compared to older platelet concentrates like PRP and fibrin glue, PRF offers enhanced biological properties, including higher platelet concentrations and greater growth factor content. Additionally, its fibrin content gives a more durable membrane. The PRF technique is also simple, time-efficient (requiring only 10–12 min of centrifugation of the patient’s own blood), and does not involve external additives, thereby minimizing the risk of contamination [11].

Since its introduction by Choukroun et al. in 2001 [14], PRF has been commonly utilized in oral and maxillofacial surgery [7]. Its applications have expanded to include promoting soft tissue regeneration and wound healing in various surgical contexts, such as facial plastic surgery [6], recalcitrant wounds [8], chronic leg and diabetic foot ulcers [23], burn wounds and skin graft donor wounds [24]. In the field of Urology, PRF has also been tested in varied applications (e.g. urethral surgery [20], endovesical treatment of interstitial cystitis [25] and repair of vaginal prolapse [26].

PRF functions as a biological binder between skin and deep tissues, acting like glue, and preventing overlap between opposing suture lines. The substance is believed to expedite soft tissue healing by promoting angiogenesis and the connective tissue proliferation [9]. Also, it has antimicrobial properties that help in local disinfection and contamination control of wounds [10]. The fibrin matrix can regulate immune reactions, directly through its leukocyte content and growth factors, and indirectly through its angiogenic properties, aiding in the drainage of edema and inflammation [22].

In urethral surgery, a study investigated the impact of PRF on urethral defect repair. The authors suggested that PRF can enhance TGFβ and VEGF expression in urethral tissue, potentially enhancing the success of urethral repair [27]. Another case study highlighted the effective use of autologous PRF in managing a UCF following hypospadias surgery, introducing PRF as a novel option compared to traditional fistula-prevention techniques [12].

Few studies have examined PRF as a covering layer for the neourethra in hypospadias repair (Table 3). Guinot et al. were the first to apply PRF in primary hypospadias repair [15]. Similar to our study, they compared 2 groups of patients with distal hypospadias. However, they used a PRF patch only as a second layer in the first group (n = 33) and used a single dartos flap for the second (control) group (n = 72).

Elsayed et al. [28] retrospectively evaluated 20 patients (including distal and midpenile types) who received a single PRF patch between the neourethra and skin but did not include a control group. Al-awadi et al. [29] conducted a single-arm study where they applied a single layer of PRF to 30 patients undergoing TIP repair. This study included only recurrent or circumcised cases whereas a healthy preputial Dartos is lacking. Although the risk of complications is anticipated to be higher with recurrent cases (reported in up to 30%) [2], their UCF rate was only 6.7% which is comparable to our results (4.5%) as well as to the other studies: 6.1% with Guinot et al. and 10% with Elsayed et al.

While other studies used PRF membranes alone, our study combined PRF membrane with a Dartos flap, a unique approach designed to harness the benefits of both layers. This combination may explain the slightly better results observed in our study.

In our study, the addition of PRF to Dartos flap obviously reduced the rate of wound infection (0% compared to 22.7% with single Dartos flap) with a statistically significant difference (p₌0.04). Similarily, Guinot et al. did not report any case with wound infection in their study. Also, Elsayed et al. and Al-awadi et al. each reported only one case with wound infection in their studies. This can be correlated to the antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties of PRF which seals the wound against infecting agents and reducing the risk of inflammation [10]. No cases of glans dehiscence were reported in our study nor the other studies that used PRF. So, PRF application did not prevent tension-free closure of glans wings.

The strengths of our study include its prospective randomized design with a control group. We exclusively included cases of distal primary hypospadias, all of which were operated on by the same surgeon using a standardized procedure. This design helps mitigate biases that were present in previous studies.

Study limitations

Despite its strengths, our study has certain limitations. First, regardless of being statistically justified, our relatively small sample may increase risk of type II errors, reduced statistical power, vulnerability to outliers and generalizability issues. To overcome these limitations in further studies, and to improve reliability and generalizability of study findings; we recommend increasing sample size, employment of meta-analyses and Bayesian statistics and facilitating replication studies. Second, the follow-up duration was brief. Thus, additional research with long-term follow-up is required to uncover any late complications. Third, the study did not evaluate the efficacy of using the PRF membrane alone without a Dartos flap for urethroplasty, and further research is recommended. Fourth, the study was conducted in a single center, therefore, multicenter studies are planned to gather more information and provide more accurate results.

Conclusions

Combined neourethral coverage with a PRF membrane and a Dartos flap leads to a trend toward a significant decrease in rate of UCF and leads to a significant reduction in wound infection after TIP repair compared to a single Dartos flap. This reflects the additive antimicrobial and sealing effect of PRF. The technique is technically simple, inexpensive, and not time-consuming. Future randomized comparative studies in larger number of patients are needed to confirm the beneficial role of PRF in hypospadias surgery.

We recommend using this technique as an efficient alternative to other conventional neourethral covers in TIP urethroplasty. The protective effect of PRF alone should be evaluated in larger prospective studies especially in situations where there is deficient healthy soft tissue (e.g. redo cases without suitable foreskin or Dartos, circumcised hypospadias). In the future, basic research on PRF’s impact on urethral wound healing would be required.
