Mosaic Learning: A Framework for Decentralized Learning with Model Fragmentation
Sayan Biswas, Davide Frey, Romaric Gaudel, Nirupam Gupta, Anne-Marie Kermarrec, Dimitri Lerévérend, Rafael Pires, Rishi Sharma, François Taïani, Martijn de Vos

Abstract
DL ( DL ) enables collaborative ML ( ML ) without a central server, making it suitable for settings where training data cannot be centrally hosted. We introduce Mosaic Learning, a DL framework that decomposes models into fragments and disseminates them independently across the network. Fragmentation reduces redundant communication across correlated parameters and enables more diverse information propagation without increasing communication cost. We theoretically show that Mosaic Learning (i) shows state-of-the-art worst-case convergence rate, and (ii) leverages parameter correlation in an ML model, improving contraction by reducing the highest eigenvalue of a simplified system. We empirically evaluate Mosaic Learning on four learning tasks and observe up to 12 12 percentage points higher node-level test accuracy compared to EL ( EL ), a state-of-the-art baseline. In summary, Mosaic Learning improves DL performance without sacrificing its utility or efficiency, and positions itself as a new DL standard.

3
The Mosaic Learning framework

Here, we present Mosaic Learning, a unified
DL
framework that leverages model fragmentation to facilitate more rapid and diverse information dissemination in the network.
We focus on model fragmentation as a first-class design choice because it exposes a fundamental degree of freedom in
DL
algorithms that is largely orthogonal to existing algorithmic improvements and remains theoretically unexplored.
Prior works have shown that splitting models into fragments can be beneficial for enhancing privacy and handling asynchrony, however, these benefits have been studied in isolation and from a systems perspective
(
3
;
4
)
. The main idea is that fragmentation directly affects how information propagates and mixes across the network during training.

To analyze the effects of model fragmentation, we present the Mosaic Learning algorithm in
Algorithm

Figure

On a high level, it conserves the usual structure of
DL

The algorithm starts by each node
i
initializing its local model
x_{0}^{(i)}
(line 2).
At the start of each iteration

\{W_{t}^{(k)}\}_{t,k}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
(line 4).
For our theoretical analysis (
Section

4

W_{t}
is row stochastic but not necessarily column stochastic.
A node then trains its current local model

for

\tilde{x}_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}^{(i,H)}

Model Fragmentation.

In contrast to

and

Formally, we view fragmentation as a mapping

Equivalently and more compactly, we work with orthogonal projectors

Formally, we have
(\Pi^{(k)}x)[i]=(x)[i]\iff\mathcal{C}(i)=k

We consider

\Pi^{(k)}\Pi^{(q)}=0\ (k\neq q),\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Pi^{(k)}=I_{d}.

For simplicity, we assume each fragment has the same number of parameters (

Furthermore, the fragmentation algorithm is fixed across iterations.
The parameters in fragment

Discussion.

Mosaic Learning induces the same communication footprint of classical
DL

Mosaic Learning therefore alters the structure of information flow without increasing communication cost or introducing additional synchronization requirements.
Importantly, Mosaic Learning does not rely on asynchrony, sparsification, or partial participation; it modifies only the gossip step while leaving the local optimization procedure unchanged.

4
Theoretical Analysis

In this section, we present the formal analysis to capture the benefits of model fragmentation in the learning aspects of
DL
.
First, we show that Mosaic Learning is guaranteed to converge as fast as existing literature, regardless of the number of fragments
K
. Further, we show that a higher
K
results in faster consensus in simpler convex landscapes.
All results in this section hold independently of the fragmentation
\mathcal{C}
;
i
.
e
., the specific heuristic used to generate the
K
model fragments does not affect the convergence guarantees of Mosaic Learning.

Our theoretical contributions are twofold:

(i)

We derive state-of-the-art worst-case convergence rates under multiple communication settings (
Section

4.1
).

(ii)

Using a simplified formalism, we show that increasing the number of fragments improves consensus in the convex case (
Section

4.2
).

We impose no specific constraints on the communication matrix, except for the general convergence analysis (
Section

4.1
), which requires
W_{t}
to be undirected and regular. This aligns with standard assumptions in the decentralized optimization literature
(
6
;
13
)
. Notably, our analysis of Mosaic Learning in the convex landscape (
Section

4.2
) remains agnostic to the underlying network topology. We emphasize that all the results presented below hold regardless of whether the topology is dynamic (
W_{t}
varies each round) or static (
W_{t}=W,\forall t
).

4.1
General convergence

First, we show the worst-case convergence rate of Mosaic Learning matches prior work.
Intuitively, the number of fragments

4.2
Impact of the number of fragments

The worst-case convergence derived by in
Theorem

1
does not capture how fragmentation itself reduces redundant communication across correlated parameters or how it changes the contraction properties of the consensus step. To make this effect explicit, we analyze a simplified quadratic setting where the correlation between model parameters is captured by a positive-definite matrix
A
:

Assumption 1
.

The local losses are quadratic and identical across nodes:
\forall i,\;f_{i}(x)=f(x)=\|x-x^{*}\|_{A}^{2}
with
A\succ 0
,
A=A^{\top}
.

We concatenate local models into a vectorized form
X_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{t}^{(1)}&\cdots&x_{t}^{(n)}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}
and write
X^{*}=\mathbf{1}_{n}\otimes x^{*}
. The gradient step is

\displaystyle X_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=X_{t}-2\eta\,(I_{n}\otimes A)\,(X_{t}-X^{*}).

(2)

These notations allow us to capture the effect of fragmentation on gossip without tensor operations. Consider
K
gossip matrices
W_{t}^{(k)}
, each associated to a chunk. We define:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{t}:=\mathrm{diag}(W_{t}^{(\mathcal{C}\left(1\right))},\dots,W_{t}^{(\mathcal{C}\left(d\right))})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Pi^{(k)}\otimes W_{t}^{(k)}.

(3)

This matrix is block-diagonal, with each block associated with the gossip matrix for a parameter.
For
K=1
(no fragmentation),
\mathbf{W}_{t}=I_{d}\otimes W_{t}^{(1)}
. The number of fragments
K
controls the number of distinct blocks in
\mathbf{W}_{t}
.

To match the node-wise ordering of
X_{t}
we use the
commuting
(or vec-permutation) matrix
\mathbf{K}^{(n,d)}

(
22
;
9
;
18
)
.

\mathbf{K}^{(n,d)}
permutes Kronecker-product orderings so that blocks correspond to nodes rather than parameter groups; this makes the block-diagonal structure in
Equation

3
compatible with the stacked vector
X_{t}
.
The gossip step thus becomes:

\displaystyle X_{t+1}=\mathbf{K}^{(n,d)}\mathbf{W}_{t}\mathbf{K}^{(d,n)}X_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(4)

Combining
Equations

4
and
2
, we can view the evolution of
X_{t}
as a linear system.
This allows us to analyze the impact of fragmentation on consensus:

Lemma 2

(Consensus error evolution)
.

Consider
e_{t}=X_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}
to be the consensus error at iteration
t
. Then, the consensus error evolves according to:

\displaystyle e_{t+1}=P\mathbf{K}^{(n,d)}\mathbf{W}_{t}\mathbf{K}^{(d,n)}\left(I_{n}\otimes\left(I_{d}-2\eta A\right)\right)e_{t},

where
P=\left(I_{n}-\tfrac{1}{n}\mathds{1}_{n}\mathds{1}_{n}^{\top}\right)\otimes I_{d}
is the projector onto the disagreement subspace.

Using
Lemma

2
, we show that the consensus distance is driven by the matrix:

\displaystyle M_{t}:=P\mathbf{K}^{(n,d)}\mathbf{W}_{t}\mathbf{K}^{(d,n)}\left(I_{n}\otimes\left(I_{d}-2\eta A\right)\right).

In particular, the norm of the consensus distance is governed by
\rho(M_{t}^{\top}M_{t})
, the largest eigenvalue of
M_{t}^{\top}M_{t}
, as we consider the norm of the consensus error
e_{t}^{\top}e_{t}
.
We now consider two examples, with two types of correlation between model parameters (represented by matrix
A
). Our numerical simulations using
2

Figure

decreases when the number of fragments

7
Conclusion

This work presents Mosaic Learning, a novel
DL
framework that studies the latent potential of model fragmentation. Mosaic Learning provides a unifying paradigm-shifting approach to
DL
that leverages model fragmentation to drive faster, more diverse information flow. By deriving the theoretical convergence guarantees of Mosaic Learning, we are the first to capture the evolution of the entire global model within a model-fragmented network architecture, showing that Mosaic Learning maintains a competitive edge over the state-of-the-art
EL
in terms of utility. We demonstrate that, for convex loss landscapes, Mosaic Learning gains its edge by maximizing information diversity, effectively curbing redundant parameter sharing and ensuring that nodes receive uncorrelated updates and, therefore, optimizes the utility gain under the same communication budget. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Mosaic Learning outperforms
EL
in the presence of highly heterogenous node data, while remaining as good as
EL
in more uniform settings.
In summary, Mosaic Learning establishes a new, theoretically-grounded standard for efficient and scalable
DL
.
