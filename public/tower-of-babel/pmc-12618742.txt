Intracranial undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma of right frontal lobe: a case report
Authors: Yulong Deng, Yueqiao Deng, Lurong Li, Xiaozhi Cheng, Qiwen Chen, Er Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntracranial undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS) is extremely rare and mostly seen among the middle-aged people. The patients are asymptomatic or have atypical manifestations such as headaches and seizures. The imaging findings can be easily misdiagnosed as glioma. Surgical resection is the primary treatment, followed by adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy; however, the average overall survival remains relatively short.Case presentationIn this article, we report the case of an 11-year-old girl who presented with headache, dizziness, and focal seizures. Imaging found an irregular lesion in the right frontal lobe, with heterogenous contents and mass effect. The patient underwent resection and postoperative pathological examination confirmed UPS. Adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy were then administered. At the 35-month follow-up, the patient was in good condition with no evidence of recurrence.ConclusionsThis case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of intracranial UPS, particularly in pediatric patients, given its rarity and nonspecific clinical and radiological features. Although UPS is typically aggressive with a poor prognosis, early and complete surgical resection combined with adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy may improve outcome.

Background

Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), once referred to as malignant fibrous histiocytoma (MFH), is the most common among all soft tissue sarcomas. It usually affects the extremities and occurs in middle-aged and elderly men. In 3–10% of cases, UPS affects the head, with the neck and nasal cavity affected in most such cases [1, 2]. Primary intracranial UPS is extremely rare, with fewer than 50 cases reported since its first description in 1964 [3–6]. Radiation, chronic inflammation, and mutations in oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes increase the risk of UPS.

On computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), UPS can resemble gliomas, with blurry boundaries, central necrosis, irregular contrast enhancement, and edema of the surrounding tissues. Therefore, the diagnosis of UPS highly depends on histopathology, which is tipically defined by highly cellular, pleomorphic, round, and spindle-shaped cells growing in a whorled or storiform pattern, with a high mitotic rate. On immunohistology, UPS lacks a specific differentiation marker and is negative for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) or oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (Olig2), which helps differentiate it from gliomas. UPS is usually positive for markers of vessels like vimentin and CD34 and has a high Ki-67 index. Complete surgical removal of the tumor is the first-line treatment for UPS. Due to its inherent aggressiveness and high rates of metastasis and recurrence, adjuvant radiation and chemotherapy are advised for most patients [1, 7].

Herein, we reported a very rare case of an 11-year-old girl with primary intracranial UPS in the right frontal lobe. She received surgical resection, adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Three years of follow-up showed no relapse.

Case presentation

An 11-year-old girl was admitted to our unit on 12th March, 2022 for headache, dizziness, and focal seizures experienced over the past 5 days. She was afebrile and had no other abnormal symptoms. She was previously healthy, and her development had been normal. She had no history of relevant infections, no contact with toxic substances, and no exposure to radiation. Her family history was unremarkable.

Physical examination showed normal vitals and no neurological signs were observed. All laboratory test parameters were within the normal range, except for slightly elevated carbohydrate antigen 72 − 4 (CA72-4) of 8.88 U/mL (normal range 0-6.9U/mL). CT of the patient’s brain showed a mass of mixed density with fluid levels and edema of the surrounding tissue on the right frontal lobe (Fig. 1A). MRI of her brain showed that the mass had a low signal on T1WI (Fig. 1B) and high signal on T2WI (Fig. 1C). Significant nonhomogeneous enhancement was observed in post-contrast T1WI (Fig. 1D). The images were suggestive of high-grade glioma.

After thorough evaluation, extracranial metastasis and contraindications to surgery were ruled out. Surgical resection of the tumor and cranioplasty were performed. Figure 2A showed the intraoperative view of the tumor, and Fig. 2B-C showed the site after the tumor was removed. The tumor measured 42 mm × 32 mm × 38 mm and was well vascularized and clearly bounded. It was macroscopically friable, containing necrotic tissue inside. Microscopically, the tumor comprised crowded short spindle cells scattered with multinucleated pleomorphic cells, and mitotic figures were increased (Fig. 2D). Immunohistology indicated GFAP (-), Olig-2 (-), SyN (-), CK (-), p53 (-), Ki-67 (80%), CD34 (vessels+), Vimentin (+), SMA (-), Desmin (-), S-100 (-), and EMA (-). The final pathological diagnosis of the tumor was UPS.

Etoposide (100 mg) and cisplatin (20 mg) were started on 27th May, 2022 and administered for 3 consecutive days. Two weeks later, on 15th June, 2022, the patient underwent radiotherapy at a dose of gross tumor volume (GTV) 60 Gy/30f and clinical tumor volume (CTV) 50 Gy/30 f. Then from 17th September, 2022 to 1st February, 2023, she received six rounds of IA chemotherapy (Adriamycin 40 mg D1 + ifosfamide 2 g D1-6). The patient underwent follow-up and MRI examination every 3 months; she did not use anti-seizure medications and was asymptomatic and showed no evidence of recurrence on MRI. On the eighth day after the surgery, CT, MRI and post-contrast imaging showed no tumor residual and edema of the surgical site (Fig. 3A-D). The latest head MRI was taken 35 months after surgery, which showed a postoperative change of the right frontal lobe, with residual cavity filled with fluid. (Fig. 3E, F).

Discussion and conclusions

UPS, previously called MFH, is a diagnosis of exclusion because its histopathological appearance and histogenesis are indeterminate. Immunohistology analysis is also crucial for differential diagnosis. UPS usually has a high Ki-67 index, indicating its rapid growth. It could be positive for CD34 and vimentin, and in a few cases, it could be focal positive for S-100 [1, 8]. Intracranial UPS is an extremely rare entity and predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly men. Head trauma, intracranial surgery, and radiation have been found to increase the risk of UPS. The prognosis of primary intracranial sarcoma is poor, with a 5-year survival rate of 58%–77% [4]. The scarcity of this disease hindered the development of its treatment. Currently, surgical excision is the first-line therapy, and adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy are highly recommended, which help lower the risk of recurrence and metastasis.

There is currently no guideline targeting primary intracranial UPS because of its rarity. In the treatment for general UPS, anthracyclines combined with gemcitabine and/or docetaxel are widely used [2]. In recent years, emerging targeted therapy and immunotherapy have been shown to be successful in the management of UPS. Tyrosine kinase receptors (TKR) have been identified to be dysregulated in UPS, and drugs targeting TKR, including pazopanib and anlotinib, have shown efficacy in some clinical trials [1, 4, 7]. Some preliminary trials have shown that immunotherapy, represented by PD-1 suppressors (e.g., nivolumab and pembrolizumab), is a potential treatment for complicated UPS cases. More studies now focus on the molecular mechanisms underlying UPS. Studies have indicated that in some UPS cases, TP53 mutation causes p53 overexpression, and pathways like PI3K/Akt/mTOR are affected, which are associated with poorer prognosis [7, 8].

We reported the case of a 11-year-old girl with right frontal lobe UPS which radiologically resembled a high-grade glioma. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy were given after gross total resection of the tumor. No signs of recurrence after 35 months. According to previously published cases, patients who are younger and have no history of radiation exposure seem to have longer event-free survival [4, 5, 9, 10]. Besides, at the time of writing this report, our patient was also negative for p53, which is the most common oncogenic marker in sarcomas. These could contribute to her relatively better outcomes. Furthermore, few studies have explored the efficacy of combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The treatment of this patient suggested the possible superiority of the combination. Nevertheless, much still remains unexplored about the pathogenesis of UPS. This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of intracranial UPS, particularly in pediatric patients. Early and complete surgical resection combined with adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy may improve outcomes.
