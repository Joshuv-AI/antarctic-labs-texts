RNA modifications: roles in immune cell biology and tumor regulation
Authors: Hongyan Liu, Zihan Yang, Ziyi Xu, Xiaochen Ding, Xue Chen, Penghui Li, Jiachun Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, RNA modifications have been shown to play a key role in regulating immune cell functions, reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), mediating immune escape, and influencing the efficacy of immunotherapy. These processes are central to the field of epitranscriptomics. Researchers have discovered various RNA modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A), 5-methylcytosine (m5C), N1-methyladenosine (m¹A), N7-methylguanosine (m7G), and N4-acetylcytosine (ac4C), that dynamically regulate the development, differentiation, activation, and functional state of immune cells through the " writers-readers-erasers " system—a set of enzymes that add, recognize, and remove these modifications—thus contributing to the formation and evolution of the TIME. Furthermore, RNA modification enzymes can serve as predictive markers of general immune responses and are also closely linked to responses to immunotherapy. Accordingly, they have become potential targets for combination therapies. As RNA modification detection technologies advance, researchers are uncovering the spatial heterogeneity and cell-specific regulatory mechanisms of RNA modifications in tumor immunity, which provides new strategies for targeted immunotherapy. However, the regulatory mechanisms of certain RNA modifications on specific immune cells remain unclear, and how to translate research findings into clinical applications also requires further exploration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04096-z.Keywords: RNA modifications, Immune cells, Cancer, Tumor immunity, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Epigenetics is the study of reversible and heritable phenotypes involving DNA and RNA methylation, histone modifications, non-coding RNA modifications, and chromatin rearrangement [1]. In recent years, RNA modifications have become a key player in epigenetic regulation, with over 170 different RNA chemical modifications reported, including acetylation, phosphorylation, adenylylation, and methylation [2]. Numerous RNA modifications, including m6A, m5C, m¹A, m7G, and ac4C, play a crucial role in influencing various pathological mechanisms, particularly in the context of cancer, via modulating post-transcriptional processes related to RNA metabolism. These encompass the translation, degradation, splicing, export, and folding of RNA molecules [3]. The intricate control of RNA modifications is dependent on three categories of functional proteins: writers, which are responsible for the addition of specific modifications to RNA sites; readers (also referred to as binders), which selectively identify and associate with these modified sites; and erasers, which facilitate the elimination of these modifications [4] (Table 1).

Immune cells activate cell-mediated immune responses by recognizing tumor-associated antigens or tumor-specific antigens, thereby exerting an anti-tumor effect. However, tumor cells can avoid detection by immune cells by downregulating or mutating these antigens. Additionally, they establish immune escape mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME) by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), interleukin-10 (IL-10), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), or by recruiting immunosuppressive cells like regulatory T cell (Treg), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), M2 macrophages, thus escaping immune surveillance and evading the immune system [5, 6]. In recent years, the important roles of RNA modifications in the biological processes of immune cells has gained widespread attention in academia. A study conducted by Katalin Karikó’s team has demonstrated that modifications in RNA, specifically m⁶A and m⁵C, can profoundly influence the activation mechanisms of dendritic cells (DCs) as well as cells that express Toll-like receptors (TLRs) [7]. These results initially demonstrated the impact of RNA modifications on the functionalities of immune cells. Meanwhile, many studies have shown that RNA changes are linked to the development of cancers.

Therefore, the role of RNA modifications in tumor immune regulation has also received growing attention. Research indicates that irregularities in RNA modifications are significantly associated with the immune profiling of a range of malignant tumors, such as colorectal cancer (CRC) [8], breast cancer (BC) [9], lung cancer (LC) [10], hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [11], gastric cancer (GC) [12], prostate cancer (PCa) [13], and bladder cancer [14], among others. In particular, modifications to RNA can influence the extent of infiltration by immune cells, the expression levels of immune checkpoints, and the strategies employed by tumors to evade immune responses. Consequently, these alterations play a significant role in modulating the effectiveness of immunotherapeutic approaches for cancer treatment [15, 16]. Notably, RNA modifications can also serve as potential biomarkers for predicting tumor prognosis and responses to immunotherapy, providing new molecular bases for the clinical implementation of personalized cancer treatment strategies [17, 18]. However, the field still faces many challenges. On one hand, the functional annotation of various RNA modifications is extremely scarce, and their roles in immune regulation are almost blank. On the other hand, clinical translation faces the “biomarker paradox”—higher pan-cancer RNA modification score (RMS) usually indicate more significant immune escape, but coexist with high immune infiltration in some cancer types. This phenomenon suggests that there is still a lack of dynamic models that link “modification profile - immunophenotype - therapeutic response” as a causal chain. Therefore, it is urgent to utilize technologies such as single-cell multi-omics to elucidate how RNA modifications reprogram the TIME.

Overall, this review aims to systematically discuss and summarize the latest research progress on RNA modifications in the biology of immune cells and cancer. While future research can further reveal the interactions between RNA modifications and immunity-tumor dynamics, this paper not only presents novel insights into the mechanisms underlying cancer development but also proposes innovative concepts for the formulation of clinical therapeutic strategies.

RNA modifications

N6-Methyladenosine

M⁶A is a type of methylation that occurs at the N6 position of adenosine. As the most common internal modification in eukaryotic messenger RNA (mRNA), it is widely present in mammalian mRNA and various non-coding RNAs [19]. The m⁶A modification predominantly takes place in the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) and in proximity to the stop codon of mRNA [20, 21]. This modification process is jointly regulated by methyltransferases (writers) and demethylases (erasers), forming a dynamic and reversible biological process [22]. RNA that has undergone m⁶A modification can be specifically identified by and attached to certain reader proteins, which in turn influence crucial biological functions, including RNA processing, translation, and stability [23, 24]. The core members of the m⁶A methyltransferase complex (MTC) include methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), METTL14, Wilms’ tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP), RNA-binding motif protein 15/15B (RBM15/15B), zinc finger CCCH-type containing 13 (ZC3H13) and vir-like m⁶A methyltransferase-associated (VIRMA, also known as KIAA1429), which play important roles in catalyzing m⁶A modifications of different RNA types [25, 26]. Both METTL3 and METTL14 possess a methyltransferase domain; however, the primary role of METTL14 is to facilitate the interaction of METTL3 with RNA substrates. This function is attributed to the absence of a S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)-binding motif at its catalytic site [27, 28]. WTAP promotes the m⁶A modification by stabilizing the METTL3-METTL14 complex [29]. In recent years, new m⁶A writers have also been discovered, such as METTL16 [30], Zinc finger CCCH-type containing 4 (ZCCHC4) [31], and Cbl Proto-Oncogene Like 1 (CBLL1) [32]. The removal of methyl groups from m⁶A is facilitated by eraser proteins, including fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) and AlkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5). FTO achieves demethylation by oxidizing m⁶A to intermediate products and then hydrolyzing them to adenosine, while ALKBH5 can directly remove m⁶A modifications [33]. M⁶A methylation reader proteins are a class of key molecules that can specifically recognize m⁶A modifications in RNA, influencing gene expression and cellular functions by regulating RNA metabolism processes [34]. The primary proteins in this category are from the insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein (IGF2BP) family (comprising IGF2BP1, IGF2BP2, and IGF2BP3) and the YT521-B homology (YTH) domain family of proteins (including YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDF3, YTHDC1, and YTHDC2). Other proteins include ELAV like RNA binding protein 1 (ELAVL1), heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (HNRNP) (such as HNRNPC, HNRNPG, and HNRNPA2B1), eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs) (such as eIF3, eIF4E, eIF4G), and poly(A) binding proteins (PABPs) [35]. These reader proteins initiate downstream effects by recognizing m⁶A modification sites, including translation regulation, splicing, nuclear export, and degradation, thereby finely tuning the gene expression network [36]. Research has indicated that m⁶A modifications play a significant role in the regulation of diverse malignant biological activities [37, 38]. Consequently, targeting m⁶A and its associated regulatory proteins may present a novel and promising approach for impeding tumor progression [39, 40].

M⁶A detection technologies are mainly divided into two categories: sequencing and non-sequencing methods. The latter include techniques such as Thin-Layer Chromatography (TLC) [41], Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS)/MS, and N⁶-methyladenosine Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (m⁶A-ELISA), which provide the advantage of accurately determining the overall content level of m⁶A, while they have limitations in precisely locating modification sites [42]. As for sequencing methods, N⁶-methyladenosine sequencing (m⁶A-seq) and methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq), as the earliest-developed second-generation sequencing technologies, enrich m⁶A-modified fragments using specific antibodies before sequencing analysis. However, these methods require a large amount of starting RNA and have low spatial resolution [43–45]. To address these technological bottlenecks, subsequent improved methods such as m⁶A individual-nucleotide-resolution cross-linking and immunoprecipitation sequencing (miCLIP-seq) have significantly increased the detection resolution and greatly reduced RNA sample requirements [46]. It is important to highlight that the advancement of third-generation sequencing technologies (TGS) [47], including Nanopore and Pacific Biosciences’ Single Molecule Real-Time Sequencing (PacBio SMRT), has led to a significant breakthrough in the detection of m⁶A modifications [42]. These novel technologies enable the direct identification of m⁶A modification sites on RNA molecules, thereby eliminating the need for complex sample preprocessing procedures. They facilitate accurate detection at a single-base resolution and maintain the integrity of the original modification data, paving the way for new research opportunities to explore the dynamic distribution characteristics of m⁶A across various transcript isoforms in greater depth [48, 49]. In summary, the development of m⁶A detection technologies has significantly advanced cancer immunology research by providing accurate information about modification sites and preserving original epigenetic information. These technologies have helped identify key m⁶A targets involved in immune cell differentiation, TME regulation, and anti-tumor immunity. With the advancement of TGS, m⁶A detection is expected to reveal dynamic modification networks in cancer immunity, thereby facilitating the discovery of novel biomarkers and the development of new immunotherapeutic strategies.

Methylcytosine

M⁵C represents a crucial modification of RNA, characterized by the addition of a methyl group to the fifth carbon position of cytosine. This modification is extensively present across a range of RNA species, encompassing mRNA, transfer RNA (tRNA), ribosomal RNA (rRNA), and long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), and exhibits significant enrichment near the stop codons of mRNA, in coding sequence regions, and in the circular structures of tRNA [50–52]. It is crucial in the regulation of RNA metabolism, primarily influencing aspects including RNA splicing, stability, translation efficacy, maturation processes, and pathways of degradation [51, 53, 54]. Its modification process exhibits dynamic equilibrium characteristics: the methylation process is catalyzed by writers, mainly including NOP2/Sun domain (NSUN) family proteins and DNA methyltransferase 2 (DNMT2) [55, 56]; demethylation is mediated by erasers such as Ten-eleven translocation (TET) family proteins [57, 58] and α-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase ABH1 (ALKBH1). This bidirectional regulatory mechanism ensures the dynamic plasticity of m⁵C modifications. The modified m⁵C can be recognized by specific readers: the Aly/REF export factor (ALYREF) protein promotes nuclear-cytoplasmic transport by recognizing m⁵C modifications on mRNA; the Y-box-binding protein 1 (YBX1) [59] maintains mRNA stability by binding to m⁵C; and fragile X messenger ribonucleoprotein 1 (FMRP), as a multifunctional reader, can coordinate the activities of writers and erasers and participate in transcription-coupled homologous recombination processes [60–62].

Regarding detection technologies for m5C modifications, the analytical approaches can be broadly categorized into three distinct groups: sequencing-based techniques, which encompass bisulfite RNA sequencing, MeRIP-Seq, 5-azacytidine-mediated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (5-azaIP-Seq), and miCLIP-Seq; MS; total base composition analysis, and nearest neighbor analysis techniques [63–65]. With its high-resolution advantages, provides important technical support for the precise localization of m⁵C modifications. Clinical studies have shown that the abnormal regulation of m⁵C modifications is closely related to the occurrence and development of various malignant tumors, indicating its potential application value in tumor diagnosis and treatment [66, 67]. Although m⁵C detection technology does not directly explain how m⁵C modifications affect immune cells, it facilitates a better understanding of tumor immune mechanisms. This is achieved by revealing the role of m⁵C in regulating the expression of tumor-related genes and the signaling pathways these genes control.

N1-Methyladenosine

The m¹A methylation represents a prevalent and evolutionarily conserved post-transcriptional alteration, which involves the addition of a methyl group to the first nitrogen atom of the adenosine nucleotide. This modification is widely present in tRNA, rRNA, mRNA, lncRNA, and mitochondrial RNA, and is typically enriched in the 5’ UTR region of mRNA, especially near the transcription start site, suggesting its potential role in translation regulation [68–70]. The dynamic regulation of m¹A modifications relies on three types of key molecules: writers, readers and erasers. Writers include tRNA methyltransferase 6 (TRMT6), TRMT61A, TRMT61B, TRMT10B, TRMT10C, base methyltransferase of 25 S RNA (BMT2), methyltransferase ribozyme1 (MTR1), and nucleolar factor nucleomethylin (NML), which are responsible for catalyzing the formation of m¹A. Readers include YTH domain family proteins 1/2/3 (YTHDF1/2/3), which influence RNA metabolism processes by binding to m¹A modification sites. Erasers are represented by members of the AlkB family (ALKBH1/3/7) and FTO, with ALKBH3 being the only known mRNA m¹A demethylase. These enzymes rely on Fe²⁺ and α-ketoglutarate to catalyze dealkylation reactions, dynamically regulating m¹A modification levels [2, 71]. The biological functions of m¹A modifications are closely related to their unique chemical properties. Due to the positive charge carried by the methyl group, m¹A may alter the charge distribution of RNA and interfere with base pairing (such as pairing with uracil), thereby affecting RNA secondary structure and RNA-protein interactions [72]. This modification is involved in both pathological and physiological processes, including the proliferation, invasion and metabolism of tumor cells, via modulating the mechanisms of RNA splicing, transport, degradation, and translation [70].

In recent years, m¹A detection technologies have evolved from traditional methods (such as Two-Dimensional Thin-Layer Chromatography (2D-TLC), to High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) [73]) to high-throughput sequencing (AlkB-facilitated RNA methylation sequencing (ARM-seq), MeRIP-seq), significantly improving the precision of analysis [74]. Notably, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/dead-Cas13b–Chemical-Induced Proximity (dCas13b-CIP) system developed by Xie et al. (AI-dm¹A and AI-m¹A tools) has achieved the precise and reversible editing of m¹A modifications, providing revolutionary means to reveal its dynamic regulatory mechanisms [75]. These advances not only deepen the understanding of m¹A functions but also open up new avenues for targeted interventions in related diseases. Although m¹A RNA modification detection technology has enhanced our ability to study RNA modifications in tumor development, the specific effects of m¹A modifications on tumor immune mechanisms remain unclear; further research is needed to determine their potential applications in immunotherapy.

N7-Methylguanosine

M⁷G is a key RNA modification formed by adding a methyl group at the N7 position of guanosine [76, 77]. This modification is primarily found in the 5’ cap structure of eukaryotic mRNA and is also present in tRNA, rRNA and microRNA (miRNA) [78–80]. A key characteristic of m⁷G is its role in the assembly of the 5’ cap structure (m⁷GPPPN) found in mature mRNA, small nuclear RNA (snRNA), and small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) [81]. Studies have also found that m⁷G is enriched in the 5’ UTR, coding sequence (CDS), and 3’ UTR regions of mRNA, as well as in pre-mRNA [82–84].

In mRNA, the m⁷G modification is mainly catalyzed by the methyltransferase METTL1, forming a complex with WD repeat domain protein 4 (WDR4) [85]. In eukaryotes and some Archaea, the RNA guanine-7 methyltransferase (RNMT) enzyme and its activating protein RNMT-activating miniprotein (RAM) mediate the m⁷G methylation of the mRNA 5’ cap, which is crucial for maintaining mRNA stability and resisting degradation by nucleases [86, 87]. The m⁷G modification has wide involvement in various aspects of mRNA processing metabolism, including transcription stability, nuclear transport, pre-mRNA splicing, translation regulation, and polyadenylation [88, 89]. In human 18 S rRNA, the m⁷G modification at G1639 is catalyzed by the WBSCR22-TRMT112 complex, which plays an important regulatory role in the biogenesis of 18 S rRNA precursors [90]. Trimethylguanosine synthase 1 (TGS1) and H29K (NUDT16) act as modification and demodification enzymes, respectively, specifically participating in the regulation of m⁷G modifications in snRNA and snoRNA [91, 92]. To date, no research has found these two enzymes to have any effects on m⁷G modifications in mRNA, tRNA, or rRNA. In addition, the eukaryotic translation initiation factors 4E (eIF4E) protein possesses the ability to selectively identify the m⁷G cap structure present on mRNA, which is crucial for the initiation of translation [93]. It operates alongside the cap-binding complex (CBC), which comprises CBP80 and CBP20, to collaboratively manage both the nuclear export of mRNA and the efficiency of translation [94–96]. A multitude of research investigations have demonstrated that m⁷G methylation is significantly associated with various tumor biological processes, including IME regulation, tumor occurrence and development, and prognosis assessment [97–99].

RNA modification detection is fundamental to studying the regulation of gene expression by m⁷G modifications, which is mainly divided into quantitative detection and high-throughput sequencing categories. Quantitative detection includes LC-MS/MS and northern blot, which are used to assess the overall modification level of m⁷G in RNA [82, 85, 100]. However, these techniques have limited resolution and are unable to precisely locate modification sites at the single nucleotide level. High-throughput sequencing methods can accurately locate m⁷G modification sites, mainly including antibody-based m⁷G MERIP-Seq and m⁷G-miCLIP-seq, as well as chemical detection-based m⁷G Methylated RNA Analysis and Profiling sequencing (m⁷G-MAP-seq), m⁷G-Seq, AlkAniline-Seq, and tRNA-Track-Seq [101]. However, antibody methods are prone to false positives, and while chemical methods can achieve single nucleotide resolution detection, their accuracy still needs improvement due to partial reduction, deamination, or cleavage [102]. To address this problem, Zhang et al. developed m⁷G Quantitative Sequencing (m⁷G-Quant-Seq), further enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of m⁷G site detection [103]. High-throughput sequencing and computational methods have enabled precise localization of m⁷G modification sites. This technological advancement may help researchers reveal the association between m⁷G-related genes and the tumor immune landscape in various cancers, deepen the understanding of immune evasion mechanisms, and provide new research directions for developing targeted immunotherapy strategies.

N4-Acetylcytosine

Ac⁴C is a well-conserved RNA acetylation modification widely present in the tRNA, rRNA and mRNA of eukaryotes and prokaryotes [104, 105]. This modification was first discovered in tRNA and rRNA, and its presence has also been detected in mRNA since [106]. In terms of structural distribution, ac⁴C exhibits significant regional specificity. In rRNA, the modification ac⁴C is predominantly localized adjacent to the decoding regions of mammalian 18S rRNA, specifically within helices 34 and 45 [107]. Within tRNA, this particular modification predominantly manifests on the d-stem of eukaryotic tRNASer/Leu [104, 108]. Meanwhile, in mRNA, ac⁴C sites are mainly enriched in the CDS region, with a small distribution in the 5’ UTR region [105]. NAT10 is currently the only known ac⁴C writer, belonging to the G protein subunit alpha transducin (GNAT) superfamily of N-acetyltransferases. This protein possesses two essential functional domains: an acetyltransferase domain and an RNA-binding domain. These domains facilitate its ability to catalyze the synthesis of ac⁴C in a range of transcripts, including 18 S rRNA, tRNA and mRNA [109–111]. It is important to highlight that the synthesis of ac⁴C in human rRNA and tRNA necessitates the involvement of additional auxiliary proteins. Specifically, the box C/D snoRNA U13 serves as a crucial factor for the acetylation of 18 S rRNA, whereas THUMP domain-containing 1 (THUMPD1) functions as a specialized RNA adapter protein involved in the tRNA acetylation mechanism [107, 108]. The ac⁴C modification plays an important role in regulating gene expression. Studies have shown that this modification participates in various cellular processes, including apoptosis and autophagy, via affecting mRNA stability and translation efficiency [112]. However, there are still many unknown areas regarding the regulatory factors and molecular functions of ac⁴C, with only its writers identified, so far——protein NAT10—and no erasers or reader proteins discovered yet. It is important to highlight that numerous investigations have substantiated the critical involvement of ac⁴C in the onset and progression of tumors [113]. Moreover, elevated levels of NAT10 expression have been significantly associated with the aggressiveness and invasiveness of a variety of cancer types [112, 114, 115].

The presence of ac⁴C modifications does not alter the complementary CG base pairs, rendering them undetectable through standard RNA sequencing techniques. In response to this bottleneck, researchers have established a range of methodologies for the identification of ac⁴C RNA, including HPLC and LC-MS, which facilitate both qualitative and quantitative analysis. Additionally, a high-throughput technique known as acetylated RNA immunoprecipitation and sequencing (acRIP-seq) has been introduced, which employs specific antibodies targeting ac⁴C RNA to acquire accurate sequence data. Another innovative approach is ac⁴C-seq, a chemically aided sequencing technique that utilizes borohydride to convert ac⁴C into N4-acetyl-3,4,5,6-tetrahydrocytidine, enabling the identification of ac⁴C RNA sites via the detection of mutations occurring during subsequent reverse transcription processes. Furthermore, there are several predictive tools for ac⁴C sites in mRNA, such as PACES and the machine learning model XG-ac⁴C [112, 116]. Nonetheless, the investigation of ac⁴C-associated proteins and their underlying mechanisms remains incomplete, highlighting the necessity for the further advancement of detection techniques to uncover previously unexplored domains. However, our understanding of proteins related to ac⁴C modification, such as NAT10, and their functional mechanisms remains incomplete. Further development of more precise detection methods, such as combining single-cell multi-omics or spatial transcriptomics, is required to explore unexplored aspects—especially how ac⁴C regulates immune cell infiltration and function in the TIME. This research will provide a basis for developing novel immunotherapy strategies targeting ac⁴C.

RNA modification is a core regulatory mechanism of epitranscriptomics that dynamically influences RNA stability, splicing, translation efficiency, and subcellular localization at the post-transcriptional level. It serves as a crucial link between the intrinsic state of cells and their external functions. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have revealed that RNA modification not only regulates the proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis of tumor cells but also plays an important role in the immune response and particularly has a profound impact on shaping the fate and function of immune cells. By regulating the expression of immune-related genes, RNA modification participates in key processes such as T cell activation and exhaustion, macrophage polarization, and DC antigen presentation. These actions directly or indirectly affect the composition and functional state of the TIME. Therefore, before exploring the characteristics of tumor-associated immune cells, understanding the basic functions and mechanisms of RNA modification is essential. This knowledge not only helps to reveal the molecular basis of immune cell dysfunction but also lays a theoretical foundation for subsequent analysis of its role in tumor immune evasion and therapeutic resistance.

RNA modifications regulate tumor innate immunity

The carcinogenic process can activate the host’s innate immune system, triggering a series of anti-tumor responses. This process involves the synergistic action of various immune cells, mainly including tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), MDSCs, DCs, neutrophils, and natural killer (NK) cells. It is noteworthy that the behavior and function of these immune cells are profoundly influenced by various factors in the TME, such as hypoxia, acidosis, nutrient competition and metabolic reprogramming. At the same time, the plasticity, spatial heterogeneity, and cellular mixed phenotypes of the TME can further affect the metabolic state and phenotypic polarization of immune cells, thereby promoting tumor immune escape [117, 118]. Considering that RNA modifications play a widespread role in regulating gene expression at the post-transcriptional level, they have been found in recent years to be critically involved in the activation, differentiation, and function of innate immune cells. It is thus necessary to systematically explore several key types of immune cells, focusing on the role of RNA modifications in their functional regulation and their potential connection with tumor innate immunity, providing new perspectives for understanding the mechanisms of tumor immune regulation [119] (Fig. 1).

RNA modification enzymes such as the METTL family, FTO, ALKBH family, NSUN family, and NAT10 regulate key proteins in immune cells through five modifications: m⁶A, m⁵C, m⁷G, m¹A, and ac⁴C, controlling myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) chemotaxis, polarization of M1 and M2 macrophages, maturation and cross-presentation of dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cell balance, infiltration of neutrophils, T cell proliferation and differentiation, and B cell activation, creating an IME that can either promote or inhibit tumors

RNA modifications and Tumor-Associated macrophages

Macrophages are a crucial part of the innate immune system, which differentiate from monocytes and play key regulatory roles in biological processes such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and immune suppression [120–122]. On the basis of functional characteristics, TAMs can be divided into two subtypes: classically activated pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and alternatively activated anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. Studies have shown that TAMs participate in the entire process of tumor occurrence and development through various mechanisms, including tumor progression, invasion and metastasis, angiogenesis, as well as the development of drug resistance [123, 124]. In the dynamically changing TME, macrophage phenotypes exhibit temporal conversion characteristics: early on, M1-type pro-inflammatory responses dominate, while later, they shift to M2-type anti-inflammatory responses [125]. Notably, M1-type macrophages typically have tumor-suppressive properties, while M2-type macrophages exhibit pro-tumor characteristics [126]. However, it should be emphasized that macrophage phenotypes are plastic and M1/M2 markers can co-express or convert into each other [127]. Recent studies have indicated that inducing TAMs to polarize from the pro-tumor M2 type to the anti-tumor M1 type can effectively inhibit tumor progression [128]. The extensive utilization of monocytic/macrophage cell lines, including THP-1 and RAW, has led to a multitude of studies demonstrating that the functional adaptability of TAMs is considerably affected by regulatory proteins associated with RNA modifications [129–131].

RNA modification-related writers regulate the functions of TAMs and their polarization states by adding specific chemical modifications. Based on melanoma (MEL) and LC animal models and in vitro experiments, preliminary evidence suggests that the deletion of METTL3 in macrophages leads to a reduction of m⁶A modification on SPRED2 mRNA. This reduction decreases the recognition and binding affinity of YTHDF1 for SPRED2 mRNA, resulting in lower SPRED2 protein expression and weakening the inhibitory effect on the ERK signaling pathway. Simultaneously, the activation of NF-κB/STAT3 is enhanced, driving M1/M2 macrophage polarization, remodeling the TME [132]. Besides, writers such as ZC3H13 [133]and members of the NSUN family [134–136]can also influence immune responses by regulating TAMs, making them potential targets for macrophage polarization regulation. RNA modification regulatory protein readers modulate RNA fate by recognizing specific RNA modifications, thereby determining the functional states of TAMs [40]. By constructing various mouse tumor models and applying single-cell transcriptome and epitranscriptomics technologies, the study systematically revealed a key role of the m⁶A-reading protein YTHDF2 in immune evasion within tumor cells. The lack of YTHDF2 in tumor tissues can lead to the upregulation of CX3CL1 expression, thereby recruiting macrophages, inhibiting glycolytic metabolism, and enhancing mitochondrial respiratory function in CD8⁺ T cells. In the presence of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), the absence of YTHDF2 promotes the polarization of inflammatory macrophages and enhances their antigen presentation capabilities [137]. Furthermore, high expression of the m5C reader ALYREF in bladder cancer has been positively correlated with macrophage infiltration and can serve as an independent prognostic marker predicting poor survival outcomes for patients [138]. Erasers achieve dynamic regulatory balance by reversing the modifications made by writing enzymes. Through clinical sample analysis, in vitro experiments, and mouse model studies, we found that ALKBH5 recruits programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-positive TAMs in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). These TAMs secrete the chemokines CCL2 and CXCL10, which promote M2 macrophage polarization. Additionally, the TAMs secrete interleukin-6 (IL-6), which activates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in cancer cells synergistically [139]. By integrating multi-omics data, in vitro functional experiments, and immune-competent mouse models, the ALKBH5/MAP3K8 axis was found to drive tumor growth, metastasis, and macrophage recruitment [140]. In addition, through an in vitro macrophage model system, it was found that knocking out FTO inhibits the NF-κB pathway by reducing STAT1/PPAR-γ mRNA stability through YTHDF2, thereby blocking macrophage activation [141, 142]. Therefore, targeting key RNA regulatory factors such as METTL3, YTHDF2 and ALKBH5 can effectively modulate the polarization state of TAMs, achieve functional reprogramming, reshape the TME, and enhance anti-tumor immunity, providing new strategies for combination immunotherapy [143–145].

RNA modifications and Myeloid-Derived suppressor cells

Under normal circumstances, bone marrow cells undergo an orderly differentiation process from multipotent hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) to mature subpopulations, ultimately forming fully functional terminally differentiated cells such as macrophages, DCs and granulocytes [146]. In the pathological state of cancer, the TME can mediate the differentiation process of normal myeloid cells via the release of various soluble factors, transforming them into cells with immune suppressive functions. Among them, MDSCs are a mixed group of immature cells that exhibit significant pathological activation characteristics. These cells have been found to be extensively present in the spleen, cancerous tissues of mice with tumors, as well as in the peripheral blood and tumor locations of individuals with cancer, forming a crucial part of the immune-suppressive microenvironment associated with tumors [147, 148]. At the same time, these immature MDSCs can effectively suppress T cell activity, thereby creating favorable conditions for the immune evasion of malignant tumors [149].

Recent studies have found that MDSCs play a key role in tumor radioresistance and immune suppression. For example, Wang et al. revealed that radiation therapy triggers the m⁶A-dependent degradation mechanism of BAMBI mRNA in MDSCs through the NF-κB/RELA-YTHDF2 axis by constructing a bone marrow chimeric mouse model and an adeno-associated virus-mediated delivery of BMP and activin membrane-bound inhibitor (AAV-BAMBI) gene delivery system. The absence of BAMBI (TGF-β pseudo-receptor) leads to the excessive activation of TGF-β signaling, driving the infiltration of MDSCs into the TME, promoting IL-10 secretion, and suppressing CD8⁺ T cell function [150]. Subsequently, by constructing a conditional knockout mouse model of YTHDF2, studies confirmed that the absence of YTHDF2 can reshape the biological characteristics of MDSCs through the IR-YTHDF2-NF-κB pathway, including blocking their differentiation, infiltration and immune suppressive functions, thereby effectively enhancing anti-tumor immunity and overcoming radioresistance [151]. Furthermore, in the field of CRC research, Chen et al. systematically elucidated that METTL3 enhances the activity of the BHLHE41-CXCL1 signaling axis through m⁶A modification, driving MDSCs chemotaxis and suppressing T cell function through multi-model in vivo experiments combined with clinical sample analysis. Importantly, targeted intervention of METTL3 or the CXCL1 receptor CXCR2 can effectively eliminate MDSCs [152]. Clinical evidence shows that the abnormal accumulation of MDSCs is significantly associated with poor prognosis in various malignant tumors (including BC, LC, and hematological malignancies). Multiple studies have consistently indicated that high levels of MDSCs not only promote tumor progression but also significantly reduce patients’ response rates to immunotherapy, further highlighting the clinical value of targeting MDSCs in cancer treatment [147, 153, 154].

RNA modifications and dendritic cells

DCs act as messengers between the innate and adaptive immune systems, processing antigenic material from pathogens and presenting it on their surfaces to activate T cells. Nonetheless, within the TME, the roles and functions of DCs are modulated by immunosuppressive agents as well as by their interactions with a variety of immune cell types. This regulation can facilitate immune evasion and potentially intensify tumor development. Recent research indicates that modifications to RNA are essential in governing the activation and functionalities of DCs via the modulation of proteins [155, 156].

RNA modifications modulate the anti-tumor immune activities of DCs via two distinct mechanisms. In terms of DCs maturation regulation, by constructing a DC-specific METTL3 knockout mouse model, it has been shown that METTL3 increases the translational efficiency of CD40, CD80, as well as the TLR4 adaptor protein Tirap, through a mechanism that is dependent on m⁶A modification. This process subsequently activates the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, facilitating DCs maturation and promoting T cell activation that is mediated by DCs [157]. Additionally, Bai et al. analyzed 754 GC samples and found that the expression of m⁶A regulatory genes, with YTHDF1 being highly expressed in GC and predicting poor prognosis, can inhibit tumor proliferation and induce DCs infiltration, IL-12 secretion, and CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell activation through the IFN-γR1-JAK/STAT1 pathway [158]. In terms of antigen presentation regulation, Han et al. demonstrated that the m⁶A-YTHDF1 signaling pathway limits the ability of DCs to perform cross-presentation by constructing YTHDF1 gene knockout mouse models. Conversely, the lack of YTHDF1 leads to an increased efficiency in the cross-presentation of tumor antigens and subsequently enhances the activation of CD8⁺ T cells in vivo [159]. Conversely, Shi et al. demonstrated through multiple animal models that FTO weakens the antigen presentation function of DCs, comprising a potentially important mechanism of immune evasion [160]. These findings systematically elucidate how RNA modifications influence anti-tumor immune responses by regulating DCs maturation and antigen presentation, providing new targets for DCs-based immunotherapy.

Other cells

NK cells and neutrophils are integral components of the innate immune system, serving crucial functions in the immune responses against tumors [161–163]. NK cells, as cytotoxic lymphocytes of the innate immune system, exert anti-cancer defense functions through the direct killing of target cells and the secretion of cytokines [164]. Regarding the regulation of m⁶A methylation, a study using NK cell-specific METTL3 knockout mouse models found that METTL3-mediated methylation activity enhances the sensitivity of AKT and MAPK signaling pathways to IL-15 by upregulating SHP-2 expression. This mechanism plays a crucial role in preserving the homeostasis of NK cells and fostering anti-tumor immune responses [165]. Furthermore, an analysis of clinical data indicated that NSUN6 exhibits elevated expression levels in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), correlating with reduced survival durations for affected patients. Notably, the expression of NSUN6 demonstrated an inverse relationship with the activity of B cells, NK cells and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes [166, 167]. Additionally, in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) [168], glioma [169], and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [170], the m5C modification status of resting and activated NK cells was significantly correlated with poor prognosis.

Under inflammatory conditions, neutrophils play a key role as a primary defense mechanism, combating pathogens through effector cell functions [171]. Studies have shown that neutrophils exhibit dual roles in various cancer types: on the one hand, they inhibit tumor progression via cytotoxic activity; on the other hand, they may promote immune suppression in cancer [172]. Regarding the impact of RNA modifications on neutrophil functions, multiple studies have revealed the regulatory mechanisms of different modification types. Through cell experiments combined with nude mice subcutaneous tumor and lung metastasis models, it was found that m⁶A modifications inhibit NF-κB signaling by inactivating c-Rel and RELA mRNA, thereby reducing IL-8 secretion. This process suppresses neutrophil infiltration in a METTL3- and YTHDF2-dependent manner, subsequently affecting the progression of thyroid papillary carcinoma [173]. By integrating sample data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases, the study found that NSUN4 is integral to the modulation of m⁵C RNA modifications within neutrophils [174]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), based on RNA-seq data from databases such as TCGA, algorithms like CIBERSORT found that the expression levels of m⁷G regulatory factors were positively correlated with neutrophil infiltration. Patients with high m⁷G scores accompanied by increased neutrophils exhibited higher malignancy and poorer prognosis [175, 176]. Notably, the opposite phenomena have been observed in LUAD and skin MEL, where patients with high m7G levels showed reduced neutrophil infiltration [177, 178].

RNA modification regulates adaptive immunity

RNA modification, as an important post-transcriptional modification, has been widely confirmed to be a key regulatory factor in adaptive immunity, profoundly affecting the process and outcome of host anti-tumor immune responses. Adaptive immunity, frequently referred to as specific immunity, represents the body’s tailored defensive mechanism activated by the presence of antigens [179]. This intricate process primarily involves the recognition and capture of tumor-associated antigens by antigen-presenting cells. Subsequently, these cells initiate the activation of effector cells, such as T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes, via the process of antigen presentation. This cascade ultimately leads to the initiation of immune responses aimed at combating tumors [180]. Recent investigations have revealed that RNA modifications significantly contribute to adaptive immunity via the regulation of both the stability and translational efficiency of crucial immune-related mRNAs. These modifications influence immune cell activation, modulate the intensity of immune responses, and improve the recognition of tumor antigens [181]. To clarify the molecular basis of the system, the following will focus on analyzing the specific regulatory mechanisms of RNA modification in adaptive immune cells such as T cells and B cells (Fig. 1).

RNA modifications and T lymphocytes

T lymphocytes have crucial functions in the adaptive immune system, and their maturation can be categorized into three distinct phases: initially, they differentiate from hematopoietic stem cells into progenitor cells within the bone marrow; next, they migrate to the thymus for further maturation; and ultimately, they are disseminated to peripheral tissues to execute their immune functions [182]. Mature T cells mainly include two major subgroups: CD4⁺ helper T cells and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, which play crucial roles in anti-tumor immunity via recognizing antigens and activating immune responses [183]. CD4⁺ T cells primarily act as helper T (Th) cells, regulating immune responses by secreting cytokines, including Th1, Th2, Th17, and Tfh subtypes. Some CD4⁺ T cells can also differentiate into Treg cells, exerting immunosuppressive effects. CD8⁺ T cells primarily act as cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), directly killing infected or tumor cells by releasing perforin, granzymes, or inducing apoptosis [4, 184, 185]. Cancer immunotherapy strategies based on T cells take advantage of this characteristic by ex vivo expanding tumor-specific T cells or engineering T cell receptors (TCRs) to accurately recognize and kill tumor cells. Additionally, adjusting immunosuppressive factors in the TME can greatly boost T cells’ anti-tumor activity, providing new breakthroughs for tumor treatment [186].

Recent research has demonstrated that RNA modifications are essential in governing the differentiation, development and infiltration of T cells. Numerous investigations have underscored the distinct regulatory impacts of various RNA modification processes on T cells function. In terms of RNA methylation modifications, METTL3 plays a dual role in T cell regulation. Through multi-model in vivo experiments combined with clinical sample analysis, it is found that inhibiting METTL3 maintains the activation and proliferation of CD4+/CD8+ T cells, reverses immune suppression, and inhibits CRC progression [152]. Simultaneously, studies showed that METTL3 expression promotes Th1/Th17 cell differentiation, a process closely related to the IL-7/STAT5/SOCS signaling pathway [187]. In terms of RNA acetylation modifications, Wang’s team, through combining in vitro cell experiments with nude mouse tumor transplantation models, found that kynurenine inhibits T cell activation through NAT10-mediated PD-L1 ac⁴C acetylation, promoting immune evasion in CRC [188]. Indicated by the latest analysis of clinical data utilized by the m⁶A/m⁵C-related lncRNA prognostic model based on TCGA and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) databases, high-risk Low-Grade Glioma (LGG) patients have a shorter survival period, with their TIME showing significant suppressive characteristics, encompassing the atypical invasion of immune cell populations, including B lymphocytes and CD4⁺ T cells, and the upregulation of immune checkpoint expression [189]. Additionally, through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) combined with non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) clustering analysis, it was demonstrated that the m⁷G score of CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells in the nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) microenvironment was decreased and was associated with an immunosuppressive phenotype [190]. These findings not only enhance our understanding of T cell development and function but also offer new therapeutic strategies for targeting T cell immune responses.

RNA modifications and B lymphocytes

B lymphocytes are crucial effector cells in the adaptive immune response. Their development can be divided into several critical stages: they originate from multipotent HSCs in the bone marrow, differentiating into pro-B cells and pre-B cells via common lymphoid progenitors (CLPs), and then differentiating into immature B cells expressing surface IgM. Immature B cells travel to secondary lymphoid tissues, such as the spleen or lymph nodes, where they undergo differentiation into transitional B cells. Following this initial stage, they may evolve into either marginal zone B cells or follicular B cells. The concluding phases of their development lead to the generation of memory B cells or enduring plasma cells (PCs) [191]. B lymphocytes perform a variety of immune roles, encompassing the synthesis of specific antibodies, functioning as specialized antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that participate in the process of antigen presentation, releasing a range of cytokines (including IL-10, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), IL-6, among others), and facilitating T cell activation via co-stimulatory molecules [192].

Many studies have demonstrated that RNA modifications affect TIME by regulating B cells functions, thereby influencing tumor occurrence and development. Yu et al. through the construction of a gene prognosis model and molecular typing study that m⁵C modification regulates B cells functions and TIME via the TET3/NSUN2/YBX1 axis, which can serve as a prognostic marker for PCa. Combining the targeting of m⁵C pathway (such as TET3 inhibitors) with immune checkpoint blockade (Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte-Associated Protein 4 (CTLA4)/programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)) may reverse immunosuppression and enhance B cell-mediated anti-tumor responses [13]. Furthermore, Cui et al. conducted an analysis of public databases and experimental verification, finding that elevated levels of NSUN6 expression in colon cancer are associated with a reduced infiltration of activated B cells and various immune cell types. This observation implies that NSUN6 may facilitate tumor progression by establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment [166]. In the latest research, Li et al. constructed an m⁶A/m⁵C-related lncRNA prognostic model and found that patients in the high-risk group exhibited significantly increased abundances of B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, macrophages and other immune cells within the TME, indicating a tumor-promoting IME [189]. Zhai et al. conducted clustering analysis of m⁷G RNA methylation regulators in PCa and constructed a risk model. Their study found that m⁷G modification influences IME by reshaping immune cell infiltration (B/T cells, macrophages) and driving PCa progression [193]. These findings offer new theoretical insights and targets for immunotherapy in tumors of the digestive system.

In summary, RNA modifications influence immune responses by regulating the differentiation and functions of immune cells, a process closely related to tumor occurrence and development. Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms are contingent upon the specific type of RNA modification and the various subgroups of immune cells. Future investigations are essential to discover the interaction networks that exist between RNA modifications and immune cell populations, and on this basis, explore individualized immunotherapy strategies.

RNA modification regulates tumor immunotherapy

With the rapid development of tumor immunotherapy, RNA modification, as an important component of epigenetic regulation, has been proven to profoundly influence immune responses and treatment tolerance in various systemic tumors. Studies have shown that RNA modifications through regulating immune cell differentiation, cytokine expression, and the stability of immune checkpoint molecules, reshape the TIME and thereby affect the efficacy of immunotherapy. Regardless of the type of tumor, RNA modifications exhibit systematic, heterogeneous, and highly targetable regulatory characteristics, providing new intervention strategies and potential biomarkers for breaking through the bottleneck of immunotherapy [194–197] (Fig. 2) (Table 2).

Digestive system tumors

In CRC research, RNA modification significantly affects the efficacy of immunotherapy by reshaping the TIME. YTHDF1, as an m⁶A-specific binding protein, was discovered through clinical data, genetic engineering mice, single-cell sequencing and multi-omics integration studies to promote the upregulation of p65 translation and the expression of CXCL1, thereby activating the CXCL1-CXCR2 axis, promoting the migration of MDSC, and ultimately inhibiting functional CD8+ T cells in TIME. This m⁶A-p65-CXCL1/CXCR2 axis-related mechanism reveals how YTHDF1 impairs anti-tumor immunity. It is worth noting that targeting YTHDF1 through CRISPR or vesicle-like nanoparticles-encapsulated YTHDF1-siRNA (VNPs-siYTHDF1) can significantly enhance the anti-PD-1 efficacy of microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) CRC and overcome the anti-PD-1 resistance of microsatellite-stable (MSS) CRC [8, 198]. Importantly, the reduction of YTHDF1 has the capacity to reinstate the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and improve the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade therapy. Furthermore, the expression level of YTHDF1 exhibits a significant correlation with patient prognosis as well as the outcomes of immunotherapy, indicating the promising potential of YTHDF1 as a therapeutic target for CRC treatment [199]. In mouse orthotopic liver cancer models, subcutaneous tumor models, and lung metastasis models, METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification upregulates the lncRNA taurine-upregulated gene 1 (TUG1), which, through the miR-141/340/YBX1 axis, synergistically elevates PD-L1 and CD47, inhibits CD8⁺ T cells and weakens macrophage phagocytosis, driving HCC immune evasion. The study confirmed that the combination of siTUG1 and PD-L1 antibody can significantly enhance the anti-tumor immune response, providing a new combined strategy and target for HCC immunotherapy [200]. Additionally, RNA modification proteins from the ALKBH family [201] and NSUN family [202] regulate the functions of immune cells and the microenvironment, thereby influencing the efficacy of immunotherapy. Notably, in pancreatic cancer [203], GC [12] and other digestive system tumors, RNA modification also affects the immune treatment response by regulating the functions of immune cells or the IME. These important findings provide new theoretical foundations and potential therapeutic targets for immunotherapy of digestive system tumors.

Respiratory system tumors

NSCLC, as a highly prevalent malignant tumor in the respiratory system, is closely related to RNA modification-mediated post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms in its occurrence and development. Studies have shown that various RNA modifications participate in the tumor immune evasion process via regulating PD-L1 expression, which can be novel targets for NSCLC immunotherapy. In terms of m⁶A modifications, Liu’s team discovered through constructing an immune-healthy mouse LC model that the METTL3-m⁶A-YTHDC1 axis stabilizes circIGF2BP3, alleviating the inhibitory effect of miR-328-3p/miR-3173-5p on plakophilin 3 (PKP3). PKP3 maintains OTUB1 mRNA stability by recruiting FXR1, reducing PD-L1 deubiquitination levels and inhibiting the killing action of CD8⁺ T cells, ultimately forming a circIGF2BP3/PKP3/PD-L1 immune evasion pathway. This study confirmed that inhibiting this pathway can significantly enhance the effects of anti-PD-1 therapy [10]. In the field of m⁵C modifications, Yang et al. revealed through a mouse LC model that NSUN2 enhances the stability of PD-L1 mRNA by catalyzing its m⁵C modification, which is recognized by ALYREF, thereby upregulating PD-L1 expression and inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell functions. The study has verified that targeting NSUN2 can enhance the efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors, providing a new target and combination strategy to overcome immune therapy resistance. Subsequent studies further established that NSUN3 also stabilizes PD-L1 mRNA via the m⁵C modification, confirming the important role of NSUN family members in immunotherapy [204]. Besides, through the establishment of a LC xenograft nude mouse model, it was found that the ac⁴C modification mediated by NAT10 stabilized growth arrest-specific5 (GAS5), activated the MYBBP1A-p53/IRF1 axis, enhanced the type I IFN signal, promoted the secretion of CXCL10/CCL5, and thereby facilitated immune cell infiltration, enhancing the immune treatment response of NSCLC. This study proposed a new strategy of using GAS5 as a predictive biomarker and sensitizing target for immunotherapy [205]. It is worth noting that in LUAD, the analysis based on the TCGA and GEO datasets revealed that the high expression of NPM1 enhances stability through m⁶A-YTHDF2 modification, inhibiting B cell/NK cell infiltration by promoting glycolysis, and promotes immune escape and progression of LUAD. It is a potential immunotherapy target and prognostic marker [206].

Urogenital system tumors

Urogenital system tumors (bladder cancer, PCa, renal clear cell carcinoma [207], etc.) reshape the IME via a triple axis of “RNA modification-metabolism-immunity.” Bladder urothelial carcinoma stands out as the most prevalent malignant condition within the urinary system, marked by a notably high rate of recurrence. In bladder cancer, RNA modification regulatory mechanisms significantly impact anti-tumor immunotherapy [208]. Recent studies have indicated that in the mouse model, METTL3 upregulates CXCL5 in an m⁶A manner, downregulates CCL5, recruits MDSCs, and excludes CD8⁺ T cells, forming an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Inhibiting METTL3 can reverse this imbalance, considerably enhancing the effects of anti-PD-1 therapy [14]. Similarly, Ni et al. found in subcutaneous tumor models and in orthotopic bladder cancer mouse models that the JNK-c-Jun axis upregulates METTL3, stabilizing PD-L1 mRNA in an m⁶A-IGF2BP1 manner, blocking the killing action of CD8⁺ T cells, thus revealing a new mechanism of immune evasion in bladder cancer, and providing new targets and combination strategies for immunotherapy [209]. In addition, high methionine dual-inhibits immune responses via the m⁶A-YTHDF1 axis. On the one hand, YTHDF1 recognizes m⁶A modifications and degrades RIG-I mRNA, blocking the RIG-I/MAVS/IFN-I signal and weakening CD8⁺ T cell recruitment and activation; on the other hand, YTHDF1 binds to eIF5B to enhance PD-L1 translation, forming an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Local methionine deprivation or targeted intervention by YTHDF1 can significantly enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), providing a new approach for bladder cancer immunotherapy [210].

Considerable progress has been made in studying RNA modification regulatory mechanisms in PCa. The m⁵C prognostic model constructed by Yu et al. revealed that the m⁵C regulatory gene NSUN2-TET3-YBX1 axis reshapes the TME through both epigenetic and immune pathways. High expression of TET3/NSUN2 increased m⁵C levels, forming an immunosuppressive TME. Such tumors may exhibit “immune cold” characteristics, indicating that they have a poor response to ICIs such as PD-1/PD-L1 or CTLA4 antibodies [13]. Furthermore, by establishing PCa mouse model and combining with CRISPR-Cas9 technology, METTL1-mediated m⁷G modification influences the IFN-STAT1 signaling pathway by regulating tRNA fragment generation, and determining macrophage polarization and T cell infiltration status. At the same time, it was found that in the mouse model with METTL1 gene knockout, the combined treatment of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 exhibited significantly enhanced anti-tumor efficacy [211].

Regarding the female reproductive system, cervical cancer (CC) ranks as one of the most common and least favorable malignant tumors in terms of prognosis [212]. Recent studies indicated that the IME of CC is regulated by various RNA modifications. Chen et al. conducted in vitro cell experiments and mouse subcutaneous tumor models to demonstrate that NAT10 promotes glycolysis and lactate secretion via the ac⁴C-FOXP1 axis, inducing Treg expansion and inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell functions, thus suppressing responses to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy [213].

Other system tumors

In BC research, through in vitro experiments and studies using mouse transplanted tumor models, it was found that the RNA modification mechanism plays a significant role in tumor immune regulation. Clinical tissue chip and public dataset analysis revealed that among patients receiving anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy, those with high expression had a higher response rate, suggesting that METTL3/IGF2BP3 can serve as a potential predictive marker for immune therapy response [9]. In triple-negative BC, the NAT10-ac⁴C-JunB-LDHA pathway promotes the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment by enhancing glycolytic levels. The study also found that high expression of NAT10 is associated with resistance to PD-1/CTLA-4 therapy. Targeting NAT10 can transform “cold tumors” into “hot tumors”, thereby enhancing the immune checkpoint blockade response [214]. In NPC, through in vitro experiments and animal model studies, it was found that the NAT10/DDX5/HMGB1 axis promotes immune suppression by inducing T cell dysfunction. Knockout of NAT10 can increase the sensitivity of anti-PD-1 therapy as a combined treatment for NPC. Therefore, targeting NAT10 is expected to become an important combined strategy for enhancing the immunotherapy sensitivity of NPC [215]. Furthermore, in aggressive neoplasms including thyroid carcinoma [216], HNSCC [217], and osteosarcoma [218], RNA modification significantly influences the TIME by regulating the states of immune cells (such as T cells, NK cells, and macrophages) and immune checkpoint molecules (such as PD-1/PD-L1), thus providing a new perspective for immunotherapy strategies [219].

Conclusion and outlook

This review systematically elaborates on the core roles of RNA modifications such as m6A, m5C, m¹A, m7G, and ac4C in dynamically regulating the metabolic processes of coding RNA and non-coding RNA, and in the regulation of immune cell infiltration and functions. These modifications, through their " writers-readers-erasers " system, are widely involved in the development, differentiation, activation, and apoptosis of immune cells, thereby influencing the TIME and immune escape mechanisms. Thus, these modifications have established their important position as potential new targets for immunotherapy, providing new research directions for the development of immunotherapy [220].

Although studies have revealed the crucial role of RNA modifications in tumor immunity, the existing evidence still has limitations and clinical translation urgently needs to be advanced. Therefore, this field still faces several important challenges and future opportunities. Firstly, it is necessary to further clarify the specific targets and downstream signaling networks of different RNA modifications, especially in non-m6A modifications (such as m1A, m7G) and non-T cell immune subsets (such as macrophages, NK cells), where the regulatory mechanisms are still unexplored. Secondly, developing cell and site-specific RNA modification editing tools is crucial for advancing clinical translation, aiming to achieve precise intervention without systemic toxicity. Finally, future research should focus on integrating basic discoveries with clinical applications, such as constructing tumor vaccines based on RNA modification regulatory factors or individualized combined immunotherapy regimens, so as to reverse tumor suppression and treatment tolerance through remodeling the TIME [220]. To address these challenges, in the future, priority should be given to adopting integrated multi-omics strategies, such as combining single-cell epigenetic transcriptional profiling technology based on long-read sequencing, systematically mapping the distribution patterns of non-m⁶A modifications in various immune cells and verifying their functions [221, 222]. At the same time, developing site-specific editors based on CRISPR-dCas13 to precisely regulate immune cell functions [223, 224], and developing small molecule inhibitors or engineered vaccines for key writers and readers (such as pseudouridine synthase 7 (PUS7)) to enhance anti-tumor responses in combination with ICIs [225–227]. These strategies will deepen the understanding of RNA modification regulatory mechanisms and provide feasible paths for developing precise immunotherapies targeting specific immune cell subsets.
