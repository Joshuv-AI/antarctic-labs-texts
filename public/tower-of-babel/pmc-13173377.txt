HIV Preexposure Prophylaxis Prescriptions Among Black and Hispanic or Latino Populations
Authors: Douglas Krakower, Ya-Lin A. Huang, Elizabeth Crull, Jesse G. O’Shea, Thomas W. Carton, Karen W. Hoover
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study evaluates preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) prescribing among Black, Hispanic or Latino, and other priority populations.

Introduction

In 2023, there were an estimated 39 000 HIV diagnoses in the US, with disproportionately higher rates among Black and Hispanic or Latino (hereafter, Hispanic/Latino) populations.1 HIV preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is highly efficacious2 and central to national HIV prevention efforts. While the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) monitor PrEP prescribing using claims databases,3 these sources lack complete race and ethnicity data. Electronic health record (EHR) systems may offer more comprehensive sociodemographic data. We used data from National Patient-Centered Clinical Research Network (PCORnet), an EHR-based clinical research network representing over 45 million patients annually,4 to evaluate PrEP prescribing among Black, Hispanic/Latino, and other priority populations.

Methods

We analyzed data from PCORnet to identify individuals aged 16 years or older with at least 1 clinical encounter and 1 prescription for PrEP between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2023. PrEP prescriptions were identified using National Drug Codes and RxNorm identifiers for all Food and Drug Administration–approved PrEP medications (tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine, tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine, and cabotegravir). To minimize misclassification of prescriptions for HIV postexposure prophylaxis or treatment for HIV or hepatitis B, we excluded individuals who had additional antiretroviral medications prescribed within 30 days of their index PrEP prescription, as well as those with HIV or hepatitis B diagnoses in the prior 3 years. Results reporting adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. This study was determined by the CDC to be exempt from institutional review board review and the need for informed consent due to use of deidentified data.

We calculated the number of unique individuals prescribed PrEP each year and summarized the proportion of the population receiving PrEP over the study period by race, ethnicity, age, sex, urbanicity, Area Deprivation Index (ADI),5 and primary payer. Race and ethnicity data were collected from EHRs, which are generally self-reported. Proportions excluded individuals with missing data, assuming missingness was nondifferential across categories.

Results

Among 53 597 784 persons in the general PCORnet population across 34 health care organizations in 21 states, 127 159 persons (0.24%) were prescribed PrEP (mean [SD] age, 35.5 [12.0] years; 113 511 male [89.3%]; 18 497 [14.5%] Black or African American; 22 169 [17.4%] Hispanic; 75 340 [59.0%] White; and 14 063 [11.1%] other race groups [American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, or multiple races]). Counts of patients receiving PrEP increased from 16 279 persons in 2017 to 27 047 in 2023 (Figure). As compared with their representation in total PCORnet population, a greater proportion of those receiving PrEP were Black (18 497 persons [14.5%] vs 7 407 606 [13.8%]), belonged to other race groups (14 053 persons [11.1%] vs 4 916 368 [9.2%]), or were Hispanic/Latino (22 169 persons [17.4%] vs 6 936 741 [12.9%]). A greater proportion were also male (113 511 persons [89.3%] vs 23 439 089 [43.7%)], lived in urban areas (117 480 persons [92.4%] vs 45 792 862 [85.4%]) or jurisdictions in the greatest quartile of socioeconomic disadvantage (50 408 persons [39.6%] vs 13 448 125 [25.1%]), or had commercial insurance (54 910 persons [43.2%] vs 19 269 433 [36.0%]) or Medicaid (16 637 persons [13.1%] vs 5 293 374 [9.9%]) (Table).

Discussion

In one of the largest EHR data networks in the US, the proportion of persons receiving PrEP prescriptions who identified as Black or Hispanic/Latino was slightly greater than their proportions in the overall population. Prescribing was also greater among those who were younger, male, and residing in urban areas and communities with greater socioeconomic disadvantage—patterns that align with public health goals to reach populations with higher HIV incidence. Nonetheless, prescribing in Black and Hispanic/Latino populations remains disproportionately low when considering national inequities in HIV incidence.1

Despite increases over time, PrEP prescribing in the evaluated clinical population was low: 127 159 of 53 597 784 patients (about 0.24%) had a first PrEP prescription during 2017 to 2023. For context, a 2023 national estimate6 suggests about 0.67% of the US population had PrEP indications (2 254 489 of 334 914 895), highlighting substantial unmet need. Persistent gaps could reflect limited access, stigma, structural inequities, and low practitioner engagement.2,3

Limitations include the absence of data from 29 states and non-PCORnet health care organizations, which may affect generalizability. Race and ethnicity data in EHRs cannot be independently verified, and missingness and representativeness may differ by these variables, which could bias results. Prescriptions obtained through pharmaceutical company or government-sponsored PrEP access programs may also be incompletely captured. Integrating EHR data with existing public health monitoring of PrEP could improve tracking of equitable access by race and ethnicity and inform strategies to reduce HIV disparities in the US.
