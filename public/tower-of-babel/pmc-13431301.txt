Pituicytoma in the third ventricle suggesting a third ventricular floor origin: illustrative case

Pituicytoma is a rare glial neoplasm arising from the posterior pituitary or infundibulum, characterized immunohistochemically by positivity for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and S100, negativity for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and strong nuclear expression of thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1).1–3 In the 2022 WHO classification, pituicytoma is categorized as a posterior pituitary tumor (PPT), together with spindle cell oncocytoma and granular cell tumor (GCT). Subsequent WHO classification commentaries and neuropathological reviews have further clarified that these tumors are TTF-1–positive neoplasms derived from pituicytes of the posterior pituitary–­infundibular region and can be regarded as a tumor spectrum.1–3

Comprehensive analyses of the literature have documented 35 pathologically confirmed cases in 20112 and more than 100 pooled cases in a subsequent large-scale review published in 2021.4 Most pituicytomas arise in the sellar or suprasellar region, although rare intraventricular occurrences have been described. A recent systematic review identified 5 previously reported intraventricular pituicytomas and included 1 additional illustrative case, bringing the total number to 6.5 Here, we report a case of pituicytoma originating in the third ventricle, and we discuss its potential origin from cells in the floor of the third ventricle, as well as the utility of extended endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery (eTSS).

Illustrative Case

Patient

A 72-year-old man was referred to our institution for evaluation of an incidentally detected suprasellar mass. MRI at the initial evaluation demonstrated a well-circumscribed lesion in the suprasellar region with marked contrast enhancement (Fig. 1A–C). Coronal T2-weighted imaging revealed intratumoral flow voids, suggesting a hypervascular lesion (Fig. 1C). Initial CT revealed no evidence of intratumoral calcification (Fig. 1D). As the patient was asymptomatic, conservative management with radiological follow-up was selected.

During the 18 months following the initial evaluation, the tumor gradually increased in size (Fig. 1E–H). T2-weighted MR imaging demonstrated the development of a cystic component with a fluid-fluid level (Fig. 1F), suggesting intratumoral hemorrhage. No enlargement of the ventricular system or radiological evidence of obstructive hydrocephalus was observed. Baseline endocrine parameters remained within normal limits throughout this period. Ophthalmological examination revealed a focal temporal paracentral visual field defect in the left eye.

Based on the clinical course and imaging findings, the differential diagnosis included craniopharyngioma and meningioma. However, the absence of calcification, the presence of intratumoral flow voids suggestive of hypervascularity, and the preservation of pituitary function throughout the preoperative course indicated that the lesion had features atypical of a typical craniopharyngioma. Given progressive tumor growth and the development of visual field disturbance, the patient underwent surgery.

Surgery

The patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia in the supine position using an extended endoscopic transsphenoidal approach with a transtuberculum extension. Optical neuronavigation and visual evoked potential (VEP) monitoring were used throughout the procedure, whereas lumbar drainage was not used. A right transseptal approach and wide sphenoidotomy were performed.

After opening the dura, the tumor appeared reddish (Fig. 2A). The pituitary stalk was identified running anterosuperiorly along the right side of the lesion, with the superior hypophyseal artery coursing immediately anterior to it. After confirming these anatomical relationships, a safe operative corridor was established without requiring pituitary transposition.

The tumor capsule was incised, and stepwise internal decompression was performed using suction (Fig. 2B). The tumor was soft and bled easily. No continuity was observed between the tumor and the pituitary gland or pituitary stalk. With further internal debulking and circumferential dissection, the third ventricle was opened and the mammillary bodies came into view, revealing that the tumor was firmly adherent to the tuber cinereum. We decided to preserve the right tumor margin because adequate access would have required excessive traction on the pituitary stalk and the superior hypophyseal artery, with a risk of injury to perforating branches arising from the posterior communicating artery (Fig. 2C). Intraoperative frozen-section analysis showed a cellular proliferation composed of spindle to small cells arranged in a solid pattern, raising suspicion for meningioma (Fig. 3A).

C: H&E staining demonstrating mild nuclear atypia with scarce mitotic figures and no evidence of necrosis. D: Immunohistochemical stain for S100 demonstrating diffuse positivity. Sellar reconstruction was performed based on the concept of the shoelace dural closure technique described in our previous studies.6–9 The tuber cinereum defect was covered with a collagen matrix (DuraGen, Integra LifeSciences). The dural edges were then approximated in a shoelace fashion with an autologous fat graft harvested from the umbilical region. Additional onlay fat grafting was performed, followed by rigid reconstruction using a bioabsorbable plate. The reconstruction was subsequently covered with preserved sphenoid sinus mucosa, and fibrin sealant was applied. No intraoperative complications occurred. Although the VEP amplitude transiently decreased during tumor removal, no permanent deterioration was observed by the end of surgery.

Diagnosis

Permanent sections revealed spindle-shaped tumor cells arranged in interlacing fascicles, with mild nuclear atypia, scarce mitotic activity, and no necrosis (Fig. 3B and C).

Immunohistochemically, the tumor demonstrated strong nuclear positivity for TTF-1, diffuse positivity for S100, and positivity for GFAP. EMA showed a focal dot-like and canalicular staining pattern. The findings for Pit-1, progesterone receptor, SF-1, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and INSM-1 were all negative. The Ki-67 (MIB-1) labeling index was low, at approximately 1%–2% (Fig. 3D–G). Based on these histopathological and immunohistochemical findings, the lesion was diagnosed as a pituicytoma, a member of the PPT group.

Additionally, through DNA methylation profiling using the Heidelberg CNS tumor classifier (version 12.8) the tumor was assigned to the tumors of the sellar region superfamily (calibrated score 0.97), with a high-confidence match to the pituicytoma/GCT/spindle cell oncocytoma methylation subclass (calibrated score 0.97), providing strong molecular support for the diagnosis.

Postoperative Course

Postoperatively, the patient developed transient diabetes insipidus followed by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, both of which resolved with conservative management. No new neurological deficits were observed, and visual function was preserved at the preoperative level.

MRI performed 3 days after surgery demonstrated a nodular residual lesion beneath the right optic tract (Fig. 4A and B). At 6 months after surgery, MRI revealed enlargement of the residual tumor (Fig. 4C and D). Given the risk of further tumor growth, Gamma Knife radiosurgery was performed at a collaborating institution. The postoperative course after radiosurgery has been favorable, without subsequent neurological deficits to date.

Discussion

TTF-1 is physiologically expressed not only in thyroid and lung development but also in the ventral neuroepithelium of the developing brain, including the infundibulum and regions adjacent to the floor of the third ventricle during embryogenesis. Experimental studies in rodents have demonstrated TTF-1 expression in these forebrain structures, supporting the concept of a common developmental lineage for TTF-1–positive sellar and third ventricular tumors.10–12 Consistent with this developmental pattern, TTF-1 expression is observed not only in PPTs but also in other tumors of the suprasellar or third ventricular region, including chordoid glioma and neurohypophyseal GCT.13–15 Aberrant TTF-1 expression has additionally been reported in schwannomas.16 Thus, TTF-1 positivity likely reflects the regional identity of tumors derived from TTF-1–expressing cell populations located along the third ventricular floor and infundibulum, rather than representing a lineage-specific immunohistochemical marker.

To date, 6 cases of intraventricular pituicytoma have been reported in the literature (Table 1).4,5,17–20 All reported patients were male. In most previously described cases, the tumor demonstrated involvement of the infundibular recess, and surgical management was predominantly performed via transcranial approaches. In all reported cases, gross-total resection (GTR) was not achieved. In the present case, no clear involvement of the infundibular recess was identified on preoperative imaging or intraoperative inspection, and no anatomical continuity between the tumor and the pituitary stalk was observed. Instead, the lesion was firmly adherent to the floor of the third ventricle, particularly the tuber cinereum. These findings support the possibility that the tumor originated from TTF-1–expressing glial lineage cells intrinsic to the third ventricular floor rather than from the infundibulum itself.

Tumors arising in the third ventricle have traditionally been treated using transventricular or transcallosal craniotomy approaches. However, the anterior third ventricle is surrounded by critical structures such as the optic chiasm and the hypothalamic floor, and these approaches inevitably require traversing brain parenchyma, thereby carrying a risk of neurological morbidity.21 With advances in endoscopic surgical techniques, extended eTSS has recently gained attention as a minimally invasive alternative approach for third ventricular tumors.

Originally developed for suprasellar lesions such as craniopharyngiomas, extended eTSS provides a direct corridor to the third ventricle while allowing preservation of critical neurovascular structures, including the optic apparatus, superior hypophyseal artery, and pituitary stalk.22

This approach has been increasingly applied to tumors with a primary location within the third ventricle through the use of adjunctive techniques such as pituitary transposition and posterior clinoidectomy.23,24 Lopez et al. reported a GCT centered within the third ventricle that was successfully treated using a fully endoscopic transsphenoidal transplanum/transtuberculum approach, achieving GTR without major neurological complications.14 However, this approach may involve manipulation of the pituitary gland and stalk, and postoperative hypopituitarism and diabetes insipidus have been reported.25,26 The present case suggests that an extended eTSS may represent a viable and minimally invasive surgical strategy for third ventricular pituicytoma. This approach provided direct access to the third ventricle and allowed maximal safe resection while preserving critical neurovascular structures.

Although pituicytoma is generally regarded as a low-grade neoplasm, both recurrence and tumor progression are not uncommon. Large pooled analyses have reported recurrence rates of 4.9%–16.7% even after GTR, whereas tumor progression has been observed in 35.2%–60% of patients who underwent subtotal resection or non-GTR.4,27 These findings suggest that pituicytoma may exhibit slow but progressive growth and underscore the importance of long-term radiological surveillance. Although the role of adjuvant radiotherapy for pituicytoma has not been firmly established, several reports have suggested that radiotherapy may be effective for residual or recurrent disease. Recent case reports have indicated that radiotherapy should be considered when GTR is not feasible, particularly in tumors with residual suprasellar extension.28 In a comprehensive pooled analysis of 115 patients with pituicytoma, Wei et al. demonstrated that tumor progression occurred in 35.2% of patients after non-GTR, compared with only 4.9% after GTR, highlighting the prognostic importance of complete removal.4 Importantly, among patients who underwent non-GTR followed by adjuvant radiotherapy, no tumor progression was observed during a median follow-up of 36 months (range 12–96 months).4 Although the overall evidence remains limited, these data suggest that adjuvant radiotherapy may contribute to disease control in cases with residual or progressive pituicytoma, particularly when further resection is not feasible. On the basis of these findings, adjuvant radiotherapy was considered a reasonable treatment option for our patient.

Observations

This case demonstrates a rare third ventricular pituicytoma that appears to originate from the floor of the third ventricle. Unlike previously reported intraventricular cases, no anatomical continuity with the pituitary stalk or infundibular recess was identified. Instead, the tumor was firmly adherent to the tuber cinereum, supporting a local origin from TTF-1–expressing cells within the third ventricular floor. These findings provide new insight into the developmental origin of pituicytoma and suggest that extended eTSS may represent a feasible and minimally invasive approach.

Lessons

This case suggests that pituicytoma may rarely originate from the floor of the third ventricle, providing additional insight into its possible developmental origin and surgical anatomy. Extended eTSS may represent a minimally invasive and feasible approach for carefully selected lesions in this region. However, this report is limited by its single-case design, and further studies are required to determine the generalizability of these findings. Careful long-term follow-up remains important given the potential for tumor progression.
