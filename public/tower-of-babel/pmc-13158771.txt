Paraneoplastic pemphigus in primary peritoneal clear cell carcinoma: a multidisciplinary management approach
Authors: Emma Barr, Allison Brodsky, Mika Tabata, Amir Jazaeri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First case of Paraneoplastic pemphigus (PNP) in peritoneal clear cell carcinoma.•PNP/PAMS is a rare, high-mortality paraneoplastic syndrome.•Manifestations include ocular, oral, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, and pulmonary.•Management requires multidisciplinary and goal-concordant care.Keywords: Paraneoplastic pemphigus; Paraneoplastic autoimmune multiorgan syndrome; Primary peritoneal clear cell carcinoma; Clear cell carcinoma; Multidisciplinary management; Platinum-resistant disease; Ocular, oral, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, pulmonary lesions

Introduction

Paraneoplastic pemphigus (PNP), also known as paraneoplastic autoimmune multiorgan syndrome (PAMS), is a rare, highly morbid, mucocutaneous blistering condition associated with an underlying malignancy. PNP/PAMS has been reported in a variety of malignancies, with the vast majority (84%) reported in lymphoproliferative disorders (Anderson et al. 2024). Only approximately 8.4% of cases occur in carcinomas, and there has only been one reported case in ovarian cancer (Kaplan et al., 2004, Rubin et al., 2022). Classically, patients present with intractable stomatitis with polymorphic cutaneous lesions. Other organ systems may also be involved and can result in severe morbidity and mortality, including pulmonary disease with bronchiolitis obliterans, ocular disease with corneal melting, and gastrointestinal involvement with erosive esophagitis (Anderson et al. 2024). Genital lesions are also common (Anderson et al. 2024). Diagnosis can be difficult given the broad differential, including various autoimmune blistering conditions, medication reactions, and infectious eruptions. Management decisions are then difficult and complex, given the lack of established evidence-based guidelines or randomized clinical trials, once the diagnosis of PNP/PAMS has been established. We present the case of a 47-year-old with recurrent, metastatic primary peritoneal clear cell carcinoma who developed severe paraneoplastic mucocutaneous blistering requiring a multidisciplinary approach to management.

Case Description

A 47-year-old female with recurrent, platinum-resistant primary peritoneal clear cell carcinoma presented with a one-month history of worsening painful oral lesions with intermittent epistaxis and significant oral and nasal secretions. She also noted a more recent, four-day history of pruritic rash on her lower legs, groin, and trunk. She endorsed blurry vision, ocular pruritus, and crusting in both eyes.

Regarding the patient’s cancer history, she was initially diagnosed with stage IIIC primary peritoneal clear cell carcinoma two years before the development of PNP/PAMS. She had undergone optimal primary cytoreductive tumor surgery, followed by six cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel (Fig. 1 for treatment timeline). One month after completing her chemotherapy, the patient developed biopsy-proven recurrence of external iliac and inguinal lymph nodes. She underwent external beam radiation with nodal boost. Five months later, she had a recurrence in the same region. She received three cycles of fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan-nxki with disease progression. She then enrolled in a clinical trial and received five cycles of dostarlimab and LB-100 (NCT06065462) with disease progression. The patient then underwent four cycles of gemcitabine, cisplatin, and bevacizumab, with her last cycle administered approximately four weeks before she first noticed the oral lesions. Her subsequent imaging revealed disease progression with significant adenopathy above and below the diaphragm. The decision was made to go on a drug holiday while monitoring the oral lesions.

On examination of the oral cavity, there were widespread erosions and ulcerations on the tongue, soft palate, and buccal mucosa with hemorrhagic crusting on the lips. Ulcers sloughed easily, and there was bleeding with minimal manipulation. Flexible laryngoscopy by otolaryngology showed severe dry blood crusting in the bilateral nasal cavity; however, a normal appearing oropharynx and larynx without lesions. Skin exam revealed scattered eroded vesicles on an erythematous base at different stages of healing on the distal lower extremities, thighs, buttocks, and mons pubis (Fig. 2). Erythematous plaques with weeping, clear fluid covered the abdomen. Eye examination by ophthalmology revealed bilateral palpebral conjunctival injection with mucus stranding and diffuse punctate epithelial erosions (Fig. 3).

Over the subsequent four months, the patient had multiple hospitalizations due to worsening of her symptoms, including several prolonged admissions, lasting up to 50 days. Given the multisystem involvement, a multidisciplinary team (including dermatology, rheumatology, otolaryngology, ophthalmology, infectious disease, dental oncology, hematology, and palliative care) was engaged to guide treatment. For management of suspected PNP/PAMS, the patient was initially started on intravenous high-dose corticosteroids, followed by an oral steroid taper. Her symptoms overall worsened. She then received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), rituximab, cyclophosphamide, and cyclosporine, which temporarily prevented the formation of new blisters. Unfortunately, with every attempt to taper steroids, even while on cyclophosphamide and later cyclosporine, the patient’s disease flared with worsening cutaneous, oral, and ocular desquamation.

Multiorgan involvement was managed symptomatically. Her oral lesions were managed with prednisolone swish-and-spit and oral mucositis mouthwash, although pain limited use. Lidocaine mucosal solution and a salt-and-soda mouthwash provided some relief. Tranexamic acid swish-and-spit helped minimize oral ulcer bleeding. Secondary oral candidiasis was treated with clotrimazole troches and nystatin swishes. New cutaneous ulcers were treated with clobetasol ointment, and all open areas were covered with petroleum ointment. Ophthalmic involvement was managed with multiple medications, including trials of various antibiotics (tobramycin-dexamethasone, gatifloxacin, moxifloxacin, erythromycin), artificial and autologous tears, topical spironolactone, and cyclosporin ophthalmic solution. She ultimately developed significant corneal abrasions and ulcers, requiring temporary bilateral tarsorrhaphy (suturing of eyelids closed) for two weeks. The patient later developed herpes simplex virus lesions on the conjunctiva and thighs, which were treated with valacyclovir. Guaifenesin was initiated for increased oronasal secretions. Sodium chloride nasal spray and petroleum ointment were used for nasal crusting.

During her multiple hospitalizations, the patient suffered additional complications. She was never restarted on any systemic cancer therapy. Anticoagulation was held due to anemia requiring multiple transfusions and continued bleeding from oral lesions, which resulted in worsening of a chronic deep venous thrombosis and new pulmonary embolism. An inferior vena cava filter was placed because her bleeding limited anticoagulation. Due to oral bleeding, she required a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube placement and enteral feeding. Palliative care was also consulted for pain control and supportive counseling given her prolonged, recalcitrant course. On her final admission, she experienced shortness of breath, and worsening pulmonary metastases were found on imaging. The patient and her family decided to pursue comfort-focused care, and the patient ultimately died from her malignancy approximately four months after initial presentation of PNP/PAMS.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of PNP/PAMS associated with a primary peritoneal carcinoma. There have been two other gynecologic-associated PNP/PAMS cases reported. Rubin et al. (2022) report a case of PNP in a patient with high-grade ovarian serous carcinoma; however, their patient presented with cutaneous lesions without mucosal involvement months before the discovery of metastatic carcinoma. Treatment details were not reported. Kennedy and Dawe (2009) report a case of PNP without mucosal involvement associated with recurrent endometrial cancer. Her cutaneous eruption was initially treated with a variety of topical agents, including clobetasone butyrate, oxytetracycline, nystatin, acitretin, and oral corticosteroids. She had flares of this cutaneous eruption until she was discovered to have a recurrence of her endometrial carcinoma. After resection of the recurrent carcinoma, her PNP/PAMS completely resolved, and she remained symptom-free 18 months post-resection.

Establishing a precise diagnosis for our patient was challenging, given the diagnostic overlap among various possible blistering diseases and her biopsy findings. No universally accepted diagnostic criteria exist for PNP/PAMS; however, most proposed criteria rely on a combination of clinical features (mucocutaneous eruption with an associated neoplasm), histopathology, direct and indirect immunofluorescence microscopy (DIF and IIF), and immunoserology (Huang et al., 2024, Kappius et al., 2025). Histopathology findings generally include intraepidermal acantholysis, dyskeratosis with vacuolar or lichenoid interface changes, and dyskeratotic keratinocytes (Huang et al., 2024, Kappius et al., 2025). DIF shows IgG with or without complement deposition on intercellular/epithelial cell surfaces and/or basement membrane zone; while IIF shows autoantibodies targeting intercellular proteins found in transitional or stratified squamous epithelia (Huang et al., 2024, Kappius et al., 2025). Serologic detection of envoplakin autoantibodies with commercially available assays can also confirm the diagnosis (Huang et al. 2024). Desmoglein-3 antibodies may also be detected, as in our patient.

Given that many mucocutaneous blistering eruptions have similar appearances, correlation with objective histopathologic, immunofluorescence, and serologic markers is imperative. Subepithelial immunobullous diseases, such as bullous pemphigoid and epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, were considered, given positive IgG and IgA basement membrane zone antibody reactivity with dermal localization on a human split-skin substrate. However, our patient had normal IgG BP180 and IgG BP230 antibody levels and no epidermal pattern antibody reactivity on split-skin substrate by IIF, thus ruling out bullous pemphigoid. Furthermore, her normal IgG-type VII collagen antibodies by ELISA are inconsistent with the diagnoses of epidermolysis bullosa acquisita and bullous lupus erythematosus. Pemphigus vulgaris was also considered, given that IgG desmoglein-3 antibody levels were increased by ELISA; however, IIF was negative for IgG and IgG4 cell-surface antibody staining, which is expected to be positive in pemphigus vulgaris.

Of note, bullous pemphigoid-like blistering disease has been reported in patients who have received immunotherapy, presenting with dermatologic reactions occurring 3 weeks to 2 years after therapy (Jour et al., 2016, Sadik et al., 2021). Our patient had been treated with dostarlimab approximately eight months before developing her oral and skin lesions, suggesting a possible delayed drug-related eruption. Ultimately, immunotherapy-related pemphigoid was excluded, given that such reactions are generally milder, respond quickly to discontinuation of the offending agent (i.e., within one month), and improve with straightforward, first-line treatment with topical or systemic corticosteroids (Jour et al., 2016, Sadik et al., 2021). LB100 (an investigational agent) and cisplatin/gemcitabine/bevacizumab can also cause cutaneous side effects, but they are not associated with blistering presentations, and our patient’s DIF and serologies were consistent with a paraneoplastic presentation rather than a drug-induced etiology. Altogether, the diagnosis of PNP/PAMS was the leading diagnosis and was used to guide treatment.

As with the lack of universal diagnostic criteria for PNP/PAMS, standardized, evidence-based management guidelines are also lacking. Management strategies are largely derived from case reports and small case series. Surgical resection of the primary malignancy is recommended when feasible. Although some cases report resolution of PNP/PAMS after treatment of the underlying malignancy, other cases report progression even after successful treatment of the underlying malignancy (Huang et al., 2024, Anhalt, 2004). High-dose systemic corticosteroids are generally considered first-line; however, additional immunosuppressants, such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclosporine, are often required as the blistering disease progresses or is refractory to corticosteroids alone (Kappius et al. 2025). Reducing B-cell-related autoantibodies with rituximab or alemtuzumab has been effective for some patients and was thus tried in our patient with limited impact (Huang et al. 2024). IVIG and plasmapheresis have also been used to sequester or eliminate autoantibodies, with some success (Nanda et al., 2007, Rossum et al., 2004, Schoen et al., 1998).

Although the above treatments may temporarily reduce cutaneous lesions, mucosal involvement is often poorly responsive, and the overall course is recalcitrant. As in other conditions with loss of epidermal integrity, patients with PNP/PAMS are at increased risk of infection, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalances (Kappius et al. 2025). Sepsis is a common cause of death (Kaplan et al., 2004, Huang et al., 2024, Zou et al., 2025). In a systematic review including 290 articles (504 total patients with PNP/PAMS), median survival was 24 months, and respiratory failure and infections were the most common causes of death (Zou et al. 2025). A multidisciplinary team is often required to manage the spectrum of manifestations associated with this condition, and care should be taken to protect the skin and mucosal surfaces, as exemplified by the case described here. Our patient’s PNP/PAMS required input from a variety of specialties, including dermatology, pathology, rheumatology, otolaryngology, ophthalmology, infectious disease, dental oncology, hematology, interventional radiology, and palliative care, in addition to her primary gynecologic oncology team. Frequent multidisciplinary goals of care discussions were held with the patient and her family, given her poor prognosis from a cancer perspective (recurrent, platinum-resistant disease having already progressed through multiple lines of therapy), and her recalcitrant PNP/PAMS.

In conclusion, this exceedingly rare case of PNP/PAMS associated with primary peritoneal clear cell carcinoma underscores the importance of considering this diagnosis in patients with any type of solid or hematologic malignancy, not only lymphoproliferative malignancies as previously thought. This diagnosis should be considered in any patient with severe, treatment-resistant stomatitis and polymorphous cutaneous eruptions, particularly in the setting of a known neoplasm, although it can also be occult. Optimal management requires a multidisciplinary approach, due to the lack of treatment guidelines, with early and ongoing goals-of-care discussions throughout the disease course.
