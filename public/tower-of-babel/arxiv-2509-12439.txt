Exploring the entropic region
Laszlo Csirmaz

Abstract
The paper explores three known methods, their variants and limitations, that can be used to obtain new entropy inequalities. The Copy Lemma was distilled from the original Zhang–Yeung construction which produced the first non-Shannon inequality. Its iterated version, effects of symmetrizations, and connections with polyhedral vertex enumeration are discussed. Another method, derived from the principle of maximum entropy, has the Copy Lemma as a special case. Nevertheless, none of the two presented variants is known to generate more inequalities than the iterated Copy Lemma. Finally, the Ahlswede-Körner method is shown to employ a hidden application of the Copy Lemma – the underlying lemma alone cannot generate new inequalities –, which makes this method strictly weaker than the Copy Lemma. The paper is written in a tutorial style and concludes with a list of open questions and research problems. Keywords entropy; information inequality; polymatroid; representability; copy lemma; maximum entropy principle. AMS Numbers 06B35, 26A12, 52B12, 90C29

Introduction

Questions about probabilistic representation of structures and structure
classes can often be reduced to the following one: Does there exist a
collection of (finite) random variables with specified entropies? Examples
include, but are not limited to, channel coding
[
17
]
and
network coding in particular
[
52
]
, estimating the efficiency
of secret sharing schemes
[
3
,
4
,
15
,
20
,
26
]
, questions about matroid representability
[
2
]
, guessing
games
[
47
]
, and finding
conditional independence inference rules
[
49
]
. The
N
-variable

entropy region
is the collection of the entropies of the non-empty
subsets (i.e., the marginals) of every possible jointly distributed
N
discrete
random variables indexed by the non-empty subsets of
N
. Shannon’s basic
information inequalities
[
51
]
provide linear inequalities among
these entropies, thus yielding a polyhedral bound of the entropy region. The
long-standing open question of whether this Shannon bound is tight has been
settled by Zhang and Yeung
[
53
,
54
]
by exhibiting a linear inequality
that holds for entropies but is not a consequence of the basic inequalities.
Their discovery initiated intensive research to further investigate the
shape of the entropy region. The phrase
Copy Lemma
was coined by
Dougherty et al.
[
19
]
to describe the general method distilled from
the original Zhang–Yeung construction. It has been applied successfully to
generate several hundred sporadic and a couple infinite families of
non-Shannon entropy inequalities
[
16
,
19
,
38
]
. A
different method using an information-theoretic lemma attributed to Ahlswede
and Körner
[
1
]
was proposed in
[
33
]
; later it
was shown to be equivalent to a special case of the Copy Lemma
[
30
]
.
Structures satisfying all instances of the Copy Lemma were named

self-adhesive
by F. Matúš who characterized self-adhesive
structures for the case when
N
has at most four elements
[
39
]
.
Self-adhesivity was an important ingredient in investigating different
representations of conditional independence models

[
6
,
7
]
.

The Copy Lemma, and its variants, are especially amenable to numerical
computations
[
2
,
4
,
12
,
26
]
. Although the Copy
Lemma can utilize previously generated non-Shannon inequalities, the same
effect can be achieved by applying the Lemma
iteratively
. The influential
paper of Dougherty et al.
[
19
]
also introduces such iterative
variants. Additional tricks, such as exploiting the inherent symmetry and
special structure of the entropy region, can significantly reduce the
otherwise prohibitively huge computational complexity
[
12
]
.

The Zhang-Yeung method can be recovered by an ingenious application of

principle of maximum entropy
. The principle, which was conceived in
its modern form by E. T. Jaynes
[
29
]
, is easy to formulate: “If a
probability distribution is specified only partially, take the one with the
largest entropy,” see
[
25
]
. In this case the partial
specification requires certain marginals (those at the “copied instance”
positions) to be isomorphic to a given, fixed distribution. Since this
requirement imposes linear constraints on the distribution probabilities,
the maximum entropy distribution always exists
[
8
]
, is
unique, and satisfies numerous independence statements, including those
provided by the Copy Lemma.

While it is clear that the Maximum Entropy Method is more general than the
Copy Lemma, it is not known whether it is actually stronger and could
provide additional entropy inequalities. Finally, it is also unknown whether
any of these methods captures the complete entropy region, but there are
indications that this is not the case
[
6
]
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section

2
recalls some basic notions used later, such as probability
distribution, Shannon entropy, entropy region, and polymatroids. Basic facts
about the entropy region are proved in Section
3
, while
Section
4
is a quick introduction to polymatroids and
operations on them. Linear polymatroids and the Ingleton expression are
treated in Section
5
. The Copy Lemma and its refinements are
discussed in Section
6
. Section
7
describes
the Maximum Entropy Method and its limitations. The method based on the
Ahlswede-Körner lemma is discussed briefly in Section
8
.
Section
9
summarizes the results and collects some open
questions and research problems. Proofs of two information-theoretic
reductions, thinning and compressing a distribution, are provided in the

Appendix
.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper all sets are finite. Capital letters, such as
A
,
J
,
K
,

N
, denote (finite) sets; elements of these sets are denoted by lowercase
letters such as
a
,
i
,
j
, etc. The union sign between sets is
frequently omitted, as well as the curly brackets around singletons. Thus,

Nij
denotes the set
N\cup\{i,j\}
. The difference of two sets is
written as
A\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muB
, or
A\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0mub
if the second set is a singleton. The
star in
A\mathrel{\cup\mkern-1.0mu^{*}\mkern-3.0mu}B
emphasizes that the arguments
A
and
B

are disjoint sets. A
partition
of
N
is a collection of non-empty
disjoint subsets of
N
whose union equals
N
.

A
discrete random variable
\xi
, taking values from the finite set

\mathcal{X}
called the
alphabet
, is determined by the probability
mass function
\Prob_{\xi}=\{\Prob_{\xi}(x):x\in\mathcal{X}\}
specifying that

\xi
takes the value
x\in\mathcal{X}
with probability
\Prob_{\xi}(x)
.
Being probabilities, we must have
0\leq\Prob_{\xi}(x)\leq 1
, and

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\Prob_{\xi}(x)=1
. When the random variable
\xi
is
clear from the context, the index
\xi
in
\Prob_{\xi}
is omitted. The

support
of
\xi
is the set of elements of
\mathcal{X}
which
\xi

can take with positive probability.
\xi
is
uniform
if it takes
every element of its alphabet with equal probability.

Suppose
\xi
is defined on the direct product
\mathcal{X}=\prod\{\mathcal{X}_{i}:i\in N\}
for some finite index set
N
, also called
base
.
For every non-empty subset
A
of the base the
marginal distribution
\xi_{A}
is
defined on the partial product
\mathcal{X}_{A}=\prod\{\mathcal{X}_{i}:i\in A\}

so that the probability of each
y\in\mathcal{X}_{A}
is the sum of the
probabilities of those
x\in\mathcal{X}
whose projection to
\mathcal{X}_{A}

is just
y
:

\Prob_{\xi_{A}}(y)=\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\sum$}\mkern 1.5mu\{\Prob_{\xi}(x):x\mathord{\upharpoonright}A=y\}.

In particular, for each
i\in N

\xi_{i}
is a random variable defined on

\mathcal{X}_{i}
, and
\xi_{N}
is the same as
\xi
. To emphasize that they are
marginals, we write
\xi=\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
, and say that the variables

\{\xi_{i}\}
are
jointly distributed
. Such a distribution is

quasi-uniform
if each
\xi_{i}
is uniform, and the marginals
\xi_{A}

are uniform on their own support for all non-singleton subsets
A
(so
there might be elements of
\mathcal{X}_{A}
that are taken with zero
probability, but all positive probabilities are the same).

Typical examples of quasi-uniform distributions come from groups

[
10
,
51
]
. Let
G
be a finite group and
G_{i}\trianglelefteq G
be subgroups
of
G
for
i\in N
. The domain of
\xi_{i}
is the collection of the left
cosets
\{gG_{i}:g\in G\}
, and the joint distribution
\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
is
defined as follows. Choose
g\in G
uniformly and set
\xi_{i}
to the left
coset
gG_{i}
. The probability of a sequence of such left cosets is the
number of group elements that produce these cosets, divided by the size of
the group
G
. For
A\subseteq N
let
G_{A}=\bigcap\{G_{i}:i\in A\}
. As the left
cosets of
G_{i}
and
G_{j}
are either disjoint or have exactly
|G_{ij}|=|G_{i}\cap G_{j}|
common elements, the intersections of the elements of the
coset families
\{gG_{i}:g\in G\}
and
\{gG_{j}:g\in G\}
form a partition of

G
where each part has size
|G_{ij}|
. Similarly, the support of

\xi_{A}
has exactly
|G|/|G_{A}|
elements, each obtained by the same number
of group elements, thus having the same probability.

The
Shannon entropy
of
\xi
is

\mathbf{H}(\xi)=\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\textstyle\sum$}\mkern 1.5mu\big\{\,{-}\Prob_{\xi}(x)\log_{2}\Prob_{\xi}(x):~x\in\mathcal{X}\big\},

with the convention that
0\cdot\log_{2}0=0
. If
\xi=\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
is
jointly distributed on the base
N
and
A\subseteq N
, then we write

\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)
for
\mathbf{H}(\xi_{A})
; and even the index
\xi
is dropped from the
notation and we write
\mathbf{H}(A)
when
\xi
is clear from the context. By
convention,
\mathbf{H}(\emptyset)=0
. Other information measures, called
conditional entropy, mutual information, and conditional mutual information,
are defined as follows; see
[
51
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muB)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{H}(A\cup B)-\mathbf{H}(B),

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}(A,B)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{H}(A)+\mathbf{H}(B)-\mathbf{H}(A\cup B),

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}(A,B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muC)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muC)+\mathbf{H}(B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muC)-\mathbf{H}(A\cup B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muC).

Here
A
,
B
,
C
are arbitrary subsets of
N
.
Shannon
information inequalities
(S1) and (S2) below express, in an equivalent
form, that these information measures are non-negative (which, incidentally,
follows from the concavity of the
\log
function, see
[
51
]
).

(S1)

If
A\subseteq B
then
0\leq\mathbf{H}(A)\leq\mathbf{H}(B)
(monotonicity),

(S2)

\mathbf{H}(A)+\mathbf{H}(B)\geq\mathbf{H}(A\cap B)+\mathbf{H}(A\cup B)
(submodularity).

Monotonicity in (S1) is equivalent to the non-negativity of the conditional
entropy
\mathbf{H}(B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)\geq 0
, while submodularity in (S2) is the non-negativity
of the conditional mutual information
\mathbf{I}(A,B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA\cap B)\geq 0
in a different
form. Non-negativity of mutual information is the special case of (S2)
when
A\cap B=\emptyset
. Non-negativity of
\mathbf{I}(A,B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muC)
for arbitrary
subsets
A
,
B
,
C
also follows from (S1) and (S2).

Any joint distribution
\xi
on base
N
gives rise to the marginal
entropies
\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)
arranged into a vector indexed by the non-empty
subsets of
N
. The collection of these
(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
-dimensional real
vectors forms the
entropy region
, which is denoted by
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}

[
51
]
. Elements of
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
are considered interchangeably as
vectors, as points in this high-dimensional Euclidean space, and as
functions assigning non-negative real numbers to non-empty subsets of the
base set
N
. A
(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
-dimensional point (or vector, or a real
function on the non-empty subsets of
N
) is
entropic
if it is in

\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, and it is
almost entropic
, or
aent
, if it is in
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, the closure of
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
in the usual Euclidean topology. In other words,

\mathbf{x}
is aent, if for each positive
\varepsilon
there is an entropic

\mathbf{y}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
such that
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|<\varepsilon
in the
usual Euclidean norm. The
entropy profile
of the distribution
\xi

is the vector formed from the marginal entropies of
\xi
, and the vector

\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
(or point, or function) is
represented by
the
distribution
\xi
if
\mathbf{x}
is the entropy profile of
\xi
.

Notions of conditional entropy, mutual information and conditional mutual
information are formally extended to the functional form of entropic points.
If
f
is a function on subsets of
N
then the following notations are used
as abbreviations for arbitrary subsets
A,B,C
of
N
:

\displaystyle f(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muB)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(AB)-f(B),

\displaystyle f(A,B)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(A)+f(B)-f(AB),\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle f(A,B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muC)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}f(AC)+f(BC)-f(C)-f(ABC).

Although the entropy function
f
is not defined on the empty set (since there
is no coordinate in
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
indexed by the empty set), when convenient,

f(\emptyset)=0
will be assumed. In particular,
f(A,B\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mu\emptyset)
is
considered the same as
f(A,B)
. If
f\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, then each of the above
expressions is non-negative as guaranteed by the Shannon inequalities (S1)
and (S2).

Shannon inequalities (S1) and (S2) impose linear constraints on the
entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
. The collection of vectors satisfying all Shannon
inequalities is denoted by
\Gamma_{\!N}
, clearly
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}\subseteq\Gamma_{\!N}
.
\Gamma_{\!N}
is a

pointed polyhedral cone

[
55
]
as it is bounded by finitely
many hyperplanes and each bounding hyperplane contains the origin. The
collection of the facets of
\Gamma_{\!N}
is the (unique) minimal subset of
Shannon-inequalities – the so-called
basic inequalities
, see

[
51
]
– which imply all other Shannon inequalities. This
minimal set, written in functional form, consists of the following
inequalities:

(B1)

f(N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0mui)\leq f(N)
for all
i\in N
, and

(B2)

f(aK)+f(bK)\geq f(K)+f(abK)
for all
K\subseteq N
and different

a,b\in N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK
(including
K=\emptyset
with
f(\emptyset)=0
).

It is worth pointing out that the non-negativity of
f
follows from
(
B1
) and (
B2
) and needs not be specified separately.

Polymatroids
are the elements of
\Gamma_{\!N}
, written as functions that
assign
non-negative real numbers to non-empty subsets of
N
. In this sense
polymatroids are axiomatized by the (basic) Shannon inequalities (
B1
)
and (
B2
). Polymatroids are discussed in more detail in Section

4
.

A
non-Shannon
(linear)
entropy inequality
is a half-space in
the
(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
-dimensional Euclidean space (the space of the entropy
region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
), which goes through the origin (so this half-space can be
specified by the coefficients of its normal), contains the complete entropy
region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
on its non-negative side, and cuts into the polymatroidal
region
\Gamma_{\!N}
(implying that it is not a consequence of the Shannon
inequalities). The intersection of all valid linear entropy inequalities is
the closure of the conic hull of
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
.

A natural strategy to separate
\Gamma_{\!N}
and
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
by such an inequality is the
following. Find some linear operation on the real vectors that
maps entropic vectors into entropic vectors but maps some polymatroid
outside the polymatroid region. If such an operation is found, the entropic
region sits inside the pullback of the polymatroid region along the inverse
of the operation. Since the operation is linear, the pullback of one of
the bounding hyperplanes provides a linear inequality that separates
\Gamma_{\!N}
and

\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
. In fact, this strategy works with the additional twist that
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
is
mapped to
\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
with a larger base set
M
.

3
The entropy region

This section explores various properties of the entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
. Many
of the results and proofs in this section are from the paper
[
37
]

by F. Matúš. For additional material on Information Theory and on the
entropy region, consult one of the books
[
11
]
or

[
17
]
or
[
51
]
.

For each real number
\alpha\geq 0
, there is a random variable
\xi
such
that
\mathbf{H}(\xi)=\alpha
. In fact, the range the entropy of a random variable
defined on the alphabet
\mathcal{X}
can take is the closed interval

[0,\log_{2}|\mathcal{X}|]
, and
\xi
takes the upper bound only when it is
uniform on
\mathcal{X}
. From this it follows immediately that
\Gamma^{*}_{\!1}
, the
1-dimensional entropy region for a single variable, is the non-negative real
half-line.

3.1
Independence

Two random variables
\xi
and
\eta
jointly distributed on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
are
independent
if for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
and every
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\Prob(\xi=x,\eta=y)=\Prob(\xi=x)\cdot\Prob(\eta=y);

(1)

this occurs if and only if
\mathbf{H}(\xi\eta)=\mathbf{H}(\xi)+\mathbf{H}(\eta)
. If
\xi
is
defined on
\mathcal{X}
and
\eta
is defined on
\mathcal{Y}
, then

joining them independently
yields the distribution
\xi\times\eta
on

\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
that has independent marginals identical to

\xi
and
\eta
. The probability mass of this distribution is determined
uniquely by the formula (
1
). If both
\xi
and
\eta
are
jointly distributed on the same base set
N
, then
\xi\times\eta
can also be
considered jointly distributed on the same base set. In this
case the marginal of
\xi\times\eta
on
A\subseteq N
is isomorphic to the
independent join of the marginals
\xi_{A}
and
\eta_{A}
, therefore,

\mathbf{H}_{\xi\times\eta}(A)=\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)+\mathbf{H}_{\eta}(A)~~~\mbox{ for all }A\subseteq N.

This observation proves the first part of the following claim.

Claim 1
.

Both the entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
and the almost entropy region
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
are
closed under addition.

Using Minkowski sum notation
[
55
]
and the fact that the all-zero vector
is clearly in
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, this claim can be expressed equivalently as
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}+\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}=\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, and
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}+\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}=\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
.

Proof.

The claim for
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
was proved above. The other part follows from this part
and the continuity of vector addition.
∎

If
\xi
is multiplied by itself
n
times, the resulting distribution is
denoted by
\xi^{n}
, and is referred to as
n
i.i.d.
(independent and
identically distributed) copies of
\xi
. Sometimes it is also called the

tensorization
of
\xi
. If
\xi=\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
is jointly
distributed on
\mathcal{X}=\prod_{i\in N}\mathcal{X}_{i}
, then
\xi^{n}
is a
distribution on
\mathcal{X}^{n}
, which is isomorphic to (and frequently
identified with)
\prod_{i\in N}\mathcal{X}^{n}_{i}
.
Therefore,
\xi^{n}=\{\xi^{n}_{i}:i\in N\}
is also jointly distributed on
N
; moreover the entropy profile
of
\xi^{n}
is the entropy profile of
\xi
multiplied by
n
.

If
\xi
and
\eta
are quasi-uniform, then
\xi\times\eta
is
quasi-uniform as well. Additionally, if both
\xi
and
\eta
are
group-generated by the subgroups
G_{i}\trianglelefteq G
and
G^{\prime}_{i}\trianglelefteq G^{\prime}
,
respectively, then
\xi\times\eta
is group-generated by the subgroups

G_{i}\times G^{\prime}_{i}\trianglelefteq G\times G^{\prime}
, where
G_{i}\times G^{\prime}_{i}
is the usual
group direct product. Claim
2
below summarizes these facts.

Claim 2
.

Suppose
\xi
is jointly distributed on
N
, and
\xi^{n}
is
n

i.i.d. copies of
\xi
.

(i)

For all
A\subseteq N
,
\mathbf{H}_{\xi^{n}}(A)=n\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)
.

(ii)

If
\xi
is quasi-uniform (or if it is group-generated),
then so is
\xi^{n}
.

In Claim
3
, functional notation is used:
f(A)

denotes the coordinate of the vector
f
indexed by
A
.

Claim 3
.

Suppose
N
and
M
are disjoint base sets,
f\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
and
g\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
.
Then the
direct sum

f\oplus g
is also entropic, where
f\oplus g

is defined on non-empty subsets of the disjoint union
N\cup M
as

f\oplus g:A\mapsto f(A\cap N)+g(A\cap M)~~~A\subseteq N\cup M.

Additionally, if
f,g\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, then
f\oplus g\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N\cup M}
.

Proof.

Choose the distributions
\xi
and
\eta
with the entropy profiles
f
and

g
, respectively. The independent join
\xi\times\eta
can be considered to
produce jointly distributed variables on the base set
N\cup M
. The
function
f\oplus g
expresses that the marginals of
\xi\times\eta
are
just
\xi
and
\eta
, and that they are independent.
The claim about closure follows from the fact that the operation

f\oplus g
is continuous in
f
and
g
.

3.2
Restricting, factoring, duplicating

Restricting
a distribution to a subset of variables means
discarding the rest and replacing the probabilities with their marginal values.

Factoring
is the process when a group of variables is taken as (or
grouped into) a single variable. Finally
duplicating
means adding an
identical copy of one of the variables so that the duplicate always takes
the same value as the original.

These operations on distributions have their corresponding operations on the
entropy region since the entropy of the result depends only on the entropy
of the operands and not on the particular distribution. If
f
is the
entropy profile of a distribution
\xi
on
N
and the distribution is
restricted to the smaller base
M\subseteq N
, then the entropy profile of
the restricted distribution will be denoted by
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}M
. Clearly,

(f\mathord{\upharpoonright}M)(A)=f(A)
for all
A\subseteq M
, and this value does not depend
on the particular distribution
\xi
. Using
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}M
is a slight abuse of
notation as
f
is restricted not to
M
but to the (non-empty) subsets of

M
. The restriction operation is also known as
deleting
the
complement of
M
, and the corresponding notation is
f\mathbin{\setminus}(N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muM)
.

Factoring
requires an equivalence relation
\sim
on
N
; this
relation defines the groups of variables that are to be merged. When lifted
to the entropy profile
f
, the factored profile, denoted
f/{\sim}
, is
defined on subsets of the equivalence classes, and on a subset of
equivalence classes it takes the value that
f
would do on their union.
Again, the result clearly depends on the profile only and not on the
representing distribution.

Duplicating at index
a\in N
is the result of adding a “twin” of
a

to
N
, named
a^{\prime}
, and extending the base set to
N\cup\{a^{\prime}\}
. If the
original entropy profile is
f
and the extended entropy profile is

f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}
, then, as
a
and
a^{\prime}
always take the same value,

\begin{array}[]{l@{\,=\,}l}f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}(A)&f(A),\\[2.0pt]
f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}(a^{\prime}A)&f(aA)\end{array}~~~~\mbox{ for all subsets $A\subseteq N$,}

independently of the representing distribution.
In particular,
f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}(a)=f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}(a^{\prime})=f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}(aa^{\prime})=f(a)
.

Claim 4
.

(i)
Suppose
M
is a subset of
N
. If
f\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, then
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}M\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
. Similarly, if
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, then
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}M\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!M}
.

(ii)

Suppose
\sim
is an equivalence relation on
N
; let
M=N/{\sim}
be
the set of equivalence classes. If
f\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, then
f/{\sim}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
.
Similarly, if
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, then
f/{\sim}\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!M}
.

(iii)

Let
f\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
,
a\in N
, and
f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}
be the function on
M=N\cup\{a^{\prime}\}

which duplicates
f
at
a
. In this case
f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
. If
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, then

f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!M}
.

Proof.

Claims regarding the entropy region follow from the discussion above. The claims
are also true for the aent region as each operation is continuous.
∎

3.3
The aent region is a closed cone

For
J\subseteq N
let
\mathsfsl r_{J}
be the indicator vector of the
relation
J\cap A\neq\emptyset
, that is,

\mathsfsl r_{J}(A)=\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}1&\mbox{if $J\cap A\neq\emptyset$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Notice that
\mathsfsl r_{\emptyset}
is the all-zero vector.

Lemma 5
.

(i)

\lambda\,\mathsfsl r_{J}
is entropic for each
\lambda\geq 0
.

(ii)
The vectors
\{\mathsfsl r_{J}:\emptyset\neq J\subseteq N\}
are linearly
independent, thus span the complete
(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
-dimensional Euclidean
space.

Proof.

(i) Take a random variable
\xi
on
\mathcal{X}
with
\mathbf{H}(\xi)=\lambda
, and
fix some
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
. Define the jointly distributed variables
\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
as follows:

\xi_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}\xi&\mbox{if $i\in J$,}\\
x_{0}&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Then
\xi_{A}
is identical to
\xi
if
A\cap J\neq\emptyset
, thus has
entropy
\lambda
. If
A\cap J=\emptyset
, then
\xi_{A}
is identically

x_{0}
, thus has entropy
0
.

(ii)
This statement can be proved, for example, by induction on the size of the
base set
N
. Another possibility is to observe that

\mathsfsl r_{N}-\mathsfsl r_{N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muJ}=\sum_{A\subseteq J}e_{A}

where
e_{A}
is the unit vector with 1 at coordinate
A
and zero
everywhere else. The Möbius inversion formula
[
5
]
applied to
the Boolean lattice gives

e_{J}=\sum_{A\subseteq J}(-1)^{|J\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muA|}\,(\mathsfsl r_{N}-\mathsfsl r_{N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muA})

for each
J\subseteq N
,
showing that each unit vector is a linear combination of the vectors
\mathsfsl r_{J}
.
Since their number equals the dimension of the space, they must also be
linearly independent.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Suppose
\lambda\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
. If
\mathbf{x}
is entropic, then so is

\lambda\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mkern 1.0mu\mathsfsl r_{N}
. That is,
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
implies

\lambda\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\,\mathsfsl r_{N}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
\xi=\{\xi_{i}\}
is jointly distributed on the alphabet

\prod\{\mathcal{X}_{i}:i\in N\}
,
\mathcal{Y}_{i}=\mathcal{X}_{i}\cup\{z\}
where

z
is a new element shared by all
\mathcal{Y}_{i}
, and
0\leq p<1
. Define
the variables
\eta=\{\eta_{i}\}
jointly distributed on
\prod\{\mathcal{Y}_{i}:i\in N\}
as follows. Let
\eta=\xi
with probability
p
, and let all

\eta_{i}=z
with probability
(1-p)
. For each
A\subseteq N
we have

\mathbf{H}_{\eta}(A)=\big(p\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)-p\log_{2}p\big)-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p),

where the first term comes from the cases when
\eta=\xi
as every
probability there is multiplied by
p
, and the second term is the case when
all
\eta_{i}
are equal to
z
. This construction proves that if
\mathbf{x}

is entropic, then so is
p\cdot\mathbf{x}+h(p)\cdot\mathsfsl r_{N}
where

h(p)=-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)

is the binary entropy function. Since
h(p)
is non-negative, continuous
and
h(0)=0
,
there is an integer
n>\lambda
such that
h(\lambda/n)<\varepsilon
. Let

p=\lambda/n
, then

\lambda\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\mkern 1.0mu\mathsfsl r_{N}=\big(p\cdot(n\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{x})+h(p)\cdot\mathsfsl r_{N}\big)+(\varepsilon-h(p))\mathsfsl r_{N}.

Since
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
is closed for addition by Claim
1
,

n\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, thus, by the remark above, the first term on the
right hand side is entropic as well. Lemma
5
(i) and

\varepsilon-h(p)>0
implies that the second term is also in
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, proving the
lemma.
∎

Thus the entropy profile of this distribution is

\mathbf{u}(J)=\min\{|J|,2\}~~~\mbox{ for $J\subseteq N$}.

Claim 7
.

If
\lambda
is not of the form
\log_{2}n
for some integer
n\geq 2
, then

\lambda\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{u}
is not entropic.

Proof.

Take a distribution on
N=\{a,b,c\}
with entropy profile
\lambda\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{u}
. Then
any two of the variables
a
,
b
,
c
are independent and any two determine
the third one. Denote the alphabets of the variables by
\mathcal{A}
,

\mathcal{B}
, and
\mathcal{C}
, respectively, keeping only those elements
that occur with positive probability. Create a matrix
\mathbb{M}
with rows
indexed by
\mathcal{A}\cup\{{*}\}
and columns indexed by
\mathcal{B}\cup\{{*}\}
. The matrix entry
\mathbb{M}[a,b]
in the row marked by
a
and
in the column marked by
b
is the list

\langle\Prob(a,b,c):c\in\mathcal{C}\rangle,

where
*
is interpreted as taking the marginal. For example,
\mathbb{M}[{*},{*}]
is the marginal distribution of
c
. Since
b
and
c
are
independent,
\mathbb{M}[{*},b]
equals
\mathbb{M}[{*},{*}]
multiplied by

\Prob(b)
, and
\mathbb{M}[a,{*}]
is the multiple of
\mathbb{M}[{*},{*}]

by
\Prob(a)
. In addition, the sum of the probabilities in the list
\mathbb{M}[a,b]
is

\Prob(a,b)
, which is not zero since
a
and
b
are independent.

By assumption,
a
and
b
together determine the value of
c
. This means
that the list at
\mathbb{M}[a,b]
contains exactly one non-zero probability.
Furthermore, since
a
and
c
together determine the value of
b
, the same
non-zero position
c
cannot occur twice in the row indexed by
a
. The sum
of lists in row
a
is the list at
\mathbb{M}[a,{*}]
with all non-zero
probabilities, thus in row
a
all non-zero positions occur exactly once. By
symmetry, the same holds for each column
b
.

Suppose that the only non-zero entry in
\mathbb{M}[a,b]
is labeled by
c
. It is
the same value that appears in
\mathbb{M}[a,{*}]
at position
c
; thus it
is
\Prob(a)\Prob(c)
. Similarly, the same number occurs at position
c
in

\mathbb{M}[{*},b]
, thus it also equals
\Prob(b)\Prob(c)
. Therefore

\Prob(a)=\Prob(b)
for any
a
and
b
, which means that all these marginal
probabilities are the same, that is,
a
takes every value with the same
probability. Thus
\mathbf{H}(a)=\log_{2}|\mathcal{A}|
, which is a logarithm of an
integer number.
∎

Theorem 8
.

\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, the closure of
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, is a full-dimensional convex cone.
Furthermore, internal points of
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
are entropic.

Proof.

Since non-negative multiples of the vector
\mathsfsl r_{A}
are entropic,
their conic (that is, non-negative linear) combination is also entropic.
The vectors
\mathsfsl r_{A}
span the entire space by Lemma

5
, thus
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, and then
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
is full-dimensional. To show
that
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
is closed for conic combinations, choose
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
from
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
as well as the non-negative real numbers
\lambda
,
\mu
. There are
entropic points
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
arbitrarily close to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, respectively. By Lemma
6
there are entropic
points arbitrarily close to
\lambda\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\mu\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
, therefore

\lambda\mathbf{x}+\mu\mathbf{y}
is indeed in the closure of the entropic
points.

For the last statement let
\mathbf{y}
be an internal point of
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
,
which means that an open neighborhood of
\mathbf{y}
is also in
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
. In
particular, there is a positive
\varepsilon
such that the point

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\mathbf{y}-\sum_{A\subseteq N}\varepsilon\mkern 1.0mu\mathsfsl r_{A}

is also in
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
, which means that there are entropic points arbitrarily
close to
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}
. Since the vectors
\{\mathsfsl r_{A}:A\subseteq N\}
span the
whole space, any vector can be written as their linear combination. So there
is an entropic point
\mathbf{x}\in\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
such that

\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}^{\prime}=\sum_{A\subseteq N}\mu_{A}\mathsfsl r_{A}

where all the scalars
\mu_{A}
have an absolute value less than
\varepsilon/2
. Then

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}+\sum_{A\subseteq N}(\varepsilon-\mu_{A})\mathsfsl r_{A}

is a sum of entropic points by (i) of Lemma
5
(as
\varepsilon-\mu_{A}

is positive), therefore
\mathbf{y}
is also entropic.
∎

3.4
The case of two, three and four variables

The entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!1}
of a single variable is one-dimensional; it is
the non-negative half-line; and it coincides with its closure
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!1}

For the two-element base set
N=\{a,b\}
the region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!2}
sits in the
three-dimensional space with coordinates labeled
a
,
b

written in this order. The vectors
\mathsfsl r_{J}

\displaystyle\mathsfsl r_{a}

\displaystyle=\langle 1,0,1\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathsfsl r_{b}

\displaystyle=\langle 0,1,1\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathsfsl r_{ab}

\displaystyle=\langle 1,1,1\rangle.

By Lemma
5
any non-negative linear combination of these vectors
is in
\Gamma^{*}_{\!2}
, therefore
\Gamma^{*}_{\!2}
contains the (infinite) cone
\mathcal{C}_{2}
,

\mathsfsl r_{a}

\mathsfsl r_{b}

\mathsfsl r_{ab}

On the other hand, the entropy region is bounded by the basic Shannon

\displaystyle x+y\geq z

\displaystyle~~\mbox{ from (\hyperlink{B2}{B2})}.

Each of these inequalities specifies a half-space bounded by the plane

\Gamma^{*}_{\!2}
is on the non-negative side of
that plane. Each plane contains the origin, the first plane contains

\mathsfsl r_{a}
and
\mathsfsl r_{ab}

\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Similarly, the second plane contains
\mathsfsl r_{b}
and
\mathsfsl r_{ab}
,
the third one contains
\mathsfsl r_{a}
and
\mathsfsl r_{b}

\Gamma^{*}_{\!2}
is bounded by the

\mathcal{C}_{2}
, which means that the two-variable

\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{C}_{2}
is closed,

\Gamma^{*}_{\!2}

,
\mathsfsl r_{b}
and

The entropy region

of three variables

also contains the vector

3.5
Conditioning

Conditioning is an important operation in Information Theory. Given a subset

K\subseteq N
, the conditional information measures the average information
content of
A\subseteq N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK
as variables in
K
run over the alphabet
\mathcal{X}_{K}
. More
precisely, the conditional entropy of
A
is the weighted average of the
entropies of the conditional distributions
\xi_{A}\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mu\xi_{K}=z
as
z
runs
over the elements of
\mathcal{X}_{K}
:

\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muK)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{X}_{K}}\Prob(\xi_{K}=z)\mkern 1.0mu\mathbf{H}_{\xi_{A}\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mu\xi_{K}=z}(A).

(4)

A simple calculation shows that this value equals
\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(AK)-\mathbf{H}_{\xi}(A)
,
in accordance with how conditional entropy was defined earlier. This
conditioning gives rise to an operation on entropy functions.
Suppose
f
is defined on subsets of
N
,
K\subseteq N
and
M=N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK
is
the complement of
K
. The operation of
conditioning
f
on
K
results
in the function
f^{\mathsf{c}}_{M}
defined on subsets of
M
as

f^{\mathsf{c}}_{M}:A\mapsto f(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muK)~~~\mbox{ for }A\subseteq M.

Claim 9
.

Suppose
N
is partitioned into the non-empty sets
K\mathrel{\cup\mkern-1.0mu^{*}\mkern-3.0mu}M
, and
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
is almost entropic. Conditioning
f
on
K
gives the almost entropic

f^{\mathsf{c}}_{M}\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!M}
.

Proof.

Since conditioning is a continuous operation, it suffices to show
that conditioning an entropic function results in an almost entropic
function. First, remark that the result of conditioning by (
4
)
does not depend on the distribution
\xi
, only on its entropy profile. So
assume that
f
is the entropic function of the distribution
\xi
.
Then for each
z\in\mathcal{X}_{K}

f_{z}:A\mapsto\mathbf{H}_{\xi_{A}\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mu\xi_{K}=z}(A),~~~~A\subseteq M

is the entropy profile of the conditional distribution
\xi_{M}\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mu\xi_{K}{=}z
; thus it is in
\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
. Since
f^{\mathsf{c}}_{M}
is a convex linear
combination of the functions
f_{z}
by (
4
) and
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!M}\supseteq\Gamma^{*}_{\!M}
is convex by Theorem
8
, we are done.
∎

Conditioning an entropic function can result in a non-entropic function
(but will be almost entropic by Claim
9
). For an
example, take the
following distribution on
N=\{a,b,c,d\}
. The variable
d
takes values

0
and
1
with equal probability. If
d=0
then
\{a,b,c\}
is
distributed so that its entropy profile is
\mathbf{u}
as defined
in (
2
), and if
d=1
then its entropy profile is
2\cdot\mathbf{u}
.
When conditioned on
d
, the function
f^{\mathsf{c}}_{abc}
is the average
(3/2)\mathbf{u}
, which is not entropic according to Claim
7
.

3.6
Group-based distributions

The celebrated theorem of Chan and Yeung
[
10
]
can be
interpreted by saying that entropy functions arising from group-based
distributions are “typical”. In many cases if a property holds for those
“typical” functions, then it also holds for all entropy (and aent)
functions, thus one can rely on properties of distributions that provide
typical functions. We will see such an application in Section

3.7
.

Theorem 10

(Chan–Yeung)
.

Non-negative multiples of entropy functions arising from group-based distributions
(and, consequently, from quasi-uniform distributions)
form a dense subset of
\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi=\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
be a joint distribution on the alphabet

\mathcal{X}=\prod\{\mathcal{X}_{i}:i\in N\}
. Approximate the probabilities by
rational numbers and write every probability mass as a rational number with
the same denominator. Take a set
\Omega
with as many elements as this
denominator, and take the uniform distribution on it. Consider
\xi
as a
map from
\Omega
to
\mathcal{X}
which, for any particular
x\in\mathcal{X}
, maps so many elements of
\Omega
to
x
that their total mass adds up
to
\Prob(x)
. For each
i\in N
the variable
\xi_{i}
determines a partition
of
\Omega
that group the elements assigned by
\xi_{i}
to the same
value in
\mathcal{X}_{i}
. The set
\Omega
with these
N
partitions is the

partition representation
of the distribution
\xi
, see

[
34
]
.

Replace each point in
\Omega
by the same number of points to get a set of

T
points, and let
G_{i}
be the set of all permutations on these
T

points that respect the partition induced by the
random variable
\xi_{i}
. Clearly,
G_{i}
is a subgroup of the permutation
group
G
on these
T
points. If the set sizes in the partition are
p_{1}
,

\dots
,
p_{k}
such that
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{k}=T
, then

\displaystyle\frac{|G_{i}|}{|G|}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}!p_{2}!\cdots p_{k}!}{T!}=(1+o(1))\Big(\frac{p_{1}}{T}\Big)^{p_{1}}\cdots\Big(\frac{p_{k}}{T}\Big)^{p_{k}}={}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\,2^{-T\mathbf{H}(\xi_{i})}.

As permutations in
G
that respect the partition induced by
\xi_{A}
are
just the permutations that fix the partitions induced by
\xi_{i}
for all
i\in A
, we have similarly

\frac{|G_{A}|}{|G|}=(1+o(1))\,2^{-T\mathbf{H}(\xi_{A})}.

Let the distribution
\eta
be based on
G
and the subgroups
G_{i}
.
The support of
\eta_{A}
has exactly
|G|/|G_{A}|
elements, each having the same
probability mass, therefore
\mathbf{H}(\eta_{A})=\log_{2}(|G|/|G_{A}|)
. Thus

\frac{1}{T}\mathbf{H}(\eta_{A})=\frac{1}{T}\log_{2}\frac{|G|}{|G_{A}|}=\mathbf{H}(\xi_{A})-\frac{\log_{2}(1+o(1))}{T}

for all
A\subseteq N
, which proves that the entropy profile of
\xi
can
be arbitrarily approximated by multiples of entropy functions originating
from group-based distributions.
∎

3.7
Thinning and compressing

Let
\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
be a quasi-uniform distribution where each alphabet

\mathcal{X}_{i}
is “large enough”. Fix the set
Z\subset N
of the
variables; the subset
A\subseteq N
, however, will vary. Let
\mathcal{Z}

be the set of elements from
\mathcal{X}_{Z}
that
Z
can take with positive
probability, and define
\mathcal{A}
similarly. Since the distribution is
quasi-uniform, each element of
\mathcal{Z}
has probability
1/|\mathcal{Z}|
, and each element of
\mathcal{A}
has probability
1/|\mathcal{A}|
.
Consequently,
\mathbf{H}(Z)=\log_{2}|\mathcal{Z}|
, and
\mathbf{H}(A)=\log_{2}|\mathcal{A}|
, or
after exponentiation,
|\mathcal{Z}|=2^{\mathbf{H}(Z)}
and
|\mathcal{A}|=2^{\mathbf{H}(A)}
.

Consider the matrix
\mathbb{M}
with columns labeled by
\mathcal{Z}
and
rows labeled by
\mathcal{A}
, where the entry at
\mathbb{M}[a,z]
is the
probability
\Prob(\xi_{A}=a
,
\xi_{Z}=z)
. Quasi-uniformity implies that
each matrix entry is either zero or
2^{-\mathbf{H}(AZ)}
(to account for the
total entropy of
AZ
). The probabilities in a row add up to
1/|\mathcal{A}|=2^{-\mathbf{H}(A)}
(the
probability of that row), and in a column they add up to
2^{-\mathbf{H}(Z)}
.
Therefore, of the
|\mathcal{Z}|=2^{\mathbf{H}(Z)}
many elements in a row exactly

\frac{2^{-\mathbf{H}(A)}}{~2^{-\mathbf{H}(AZ)}~}=2^{\mathbf{H}(Z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)}

are non-zero, and, similarly, of the
|\mathcal{A}|=2^{\mathbf{H}(A)}
many
elements in a column exactly
2^{\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)}
are non-zero.

The operation
thinning
creates a new distribution by randomly choosing

2^{\alpha}
many columns from
\mathbb{M}
and then renormalizing the
probabilities. Consider the row labeled with
a\in\mathcal{A}
. When choosing a
column randomly, the probability that this row contains a non-zero value in
that column is

\frac{\mbox{ non-zero positions }}{\mbox{total length}}=\frac{\;2^{\mathbf{H}(Z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)}\;}{2^{\mathbf{H}(Z)}}=2^{-\mathbf{I}(A,Z)}.

After choosing
2^{\alpha}
columns randomly, we expect
2^{\alpha}\cdot 2^{-\mathbf{I}(A,Z)}
non-zero entries in this row. If
\alpha>\mathbf{I}(A,Z)
, then we
expect this many non-zero entries in each row; therefore, after
normalization, every row will have equal probability, and then the new
entropy of the rows will remain
\log_{2}|\mathcal{A}|=\mathbf{H}(A)=\mathbf{I}(A,Z)+\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)
.

If, however,
\alpha<\mathbf{I}(A,Z)
, then we expect that each row contains at most
one non-zero entry in these
2^{\alpha}
columns. Each column contains

2^{\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)}
non-zero entries, for different columns these are in
different rows. Therefore
2^{\alpha}\cdot 2^{\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)}
rows have (equal)
non-zero probabilities (other rows have zero probability), and then the new
entropy of the rows is the
\log_{2}
of this value, that is,
\alpha+\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)
.

Combining the two cases, the new entropy of the distribution in the
rows after thinning will be
\min\{\mathbf{H}(A),\alpha+\mathbf{H}(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)\}
. Since thinning
is performed on the alphabet
\mathcal{Z}
and the process itself is oblivious to
what the subset
A\subseteq N
is, thinning gives a new distribution where
the entropy of
A
is (approximately) the above value for every
A\subseteq N
. This heuristic can be made precise (see
Appendix
). Since
quasi-uniform distributions are “typical” by Theorem
10
,
it proves that the
Generalized Ahlswede-Körner
operation,
abbreviated as
\GAK
and defined below, preserves the aent functions.

Theorem 11
.

Suppose
Z\subseteq N
,
\alpha\geq 0
, and
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
is aent. Then the
function
f^{*}=\GAK(f)
, defined as

f^{*}:A\mapsto\min\{f(A),\alpha+f(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muZ)\}~~~\mbox{ for }A\subseteq N,

is also aent.∎

A special case of Theorem
11
is known as the

Ahlswede-Körner lemma
. This lemma is implicit in

[
1
]
or in the book
[
17
]
, and explicitly
stated in
[
30
]
and
[
33
]
. Recall that
\mathsfsl r_{z}
is the
(entropic) indicator function of the relation
z\in A
from Section

3.3
.

Lemma 12

(Ahlswede-Körner)
.

If
f
is aent, then so is
f-f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)\mkern 1.0mu\mathsfsl r_{z}
whenever

z\in N
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
11
with a single element
Z=\{z\}
, and
alpha
as
the mutual information

\alpha=f(z,N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)=f(z)-f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)=f(z)+f(N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)-f(N).

The aent function
f^{*}=\GAK(f)
can be written as

f^{*}:A\mapsto\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}f(A)&\mbox{ if $z\notin A$},\\[3.0pt]
f(A)-f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)&\mbox{ if $z\in A$},\end{array}\right.

using that
f(Az)-f(A)\geq f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)
when
z\notin A
. Since
\mathsfsl r_{z}
is
the indicator function of
z\in A
, the function claimed to be aent is just

f^{*}
.
∎

Returning to the matrix
\mathbb{M}
, the dual operation of thinning is

compressing
. While keeping all variables in
N
, a new variable
z^{\prime}

is created by making
2^{\alpha}
many buckets and placing each of the

|\mathcal{Z}|
columns into one of the buckets randomly. This process
compresses the columns into
2^{\alpha}
many buckets. The probability mass of
a bucket is the probability mass of the columns in that bucket. The entropy
of
Az^{\prime}
is the entropy of the distribution in which columns in the same bucket
are added. Similarly as before, consider the row labeled
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Since there are
2^{\alpha}
many buckets, each of the
2^{\mathbf{H}(Z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)}

non-zero elements in this row ends up in one of the buckets, thus each bucket
is expected to contain
2^{\alpha-\mathbf{H}(Z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)}
non-zero entries from this row.
If
\alpha>\mathbf{H}(Z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)
, then it means that in each row each bucket contains
the same number of non-zero probabilities, which add up to the same
probability mass. Consequently
A
and
z^{\prime}
are independent, and then

\mathbf{H}(Az^{\prime})=\mathbf{H}(A)+\alpha
. If
\alpha<\mathbf{H}(Z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muA)
, then each bucket is expected
to contain at most one non-zero element from each row. This means that all
non-zero elements in the matrix
\mathbb{M}
are in different (bucket, row)
pairs; thus
\mathbf{H}(Az^{\prime})
is the same as the total entropy of the distribution
in
\mathbb{M}
, that is,
\mathbf{H}(AZ)
. Combining the two cases into a single formula
yields

\mathbf{H}(Az^{\prime})=\min\{\mathbf{H}(A)+\alpha,\mathbf{H}(AZ)\}.

Similarly to the thinning case, the bucketing is oblivious to what the subset

A
is, thus the same formula works for every
A\subseteq N
. This
heuristic leads to the following theorem of F. Matúš
[
37
]
.
A formal proof is provided in the
Appendix
.

Theorem 13

(F. Matúš)
.

Suppose
Z\subseteq N
,
\alpha\geq 0
, and
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
is aent. Then the
extension
f^{\prime}
of
f
to subsets of
Nz^{\prime}
is also aent, where

\begin{array}[]{l@{\,=\,}l}f^{\prime}(A)&f(A),\\[3.0pt]
f^{\prime}(Az^{\prime})&\min\{f(A)+\alpha,f(AZ)\}\end{array}~~~\mbox{ for }A\subseteq N.

In the special case when
Z=\{z\}
has a single element and
\alpha=f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)
, the aent function
f^{\prime}
on subsets containing
z^{\prime}
can be computed as

\begin{array}[]{l@{\,=\,}l}f^{\prime}(Az^{\prime})&f(A)+\alpha,\\[3.0pt]
f^{\prime}(Azz^{\prime})&f(Az)\end{array}~~~\mbox{ for }A\subseteq N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz,

in particular,
f^{\prime}(z^{\prime})=\alpha
. Conditioning (see Section
3.5
)

f^{\prime}
on
z^{\prime}
gives the following aent function defined on subsets of
N
:

f^{\prime\mathsf{c}}_{N}:A\mapsto\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}f(A)&\mbox{ if $z\notin A$},\\[3.0pt]
f(A)-f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)&\mbox{ if $z\in A$},\end{array}\right.

which recovers the Ahlswede-Körner lemma
12
.

Operations defined in Theorems
11
and
13
can
move entropic points into non-entropic points; however, the result is
always almost entropic.

4
Polymatroids

Polymatroids
are non-negative real functions defined on the
(non-empty) subsets of some finite base
N
that satisfy the Shannon
inequalities (S1) and (S2). As the basic inequalities imply all Shannon
inequalities, polymatroids are
axiomatized
by the inequalities in
(
B1
) and (
B2
).

Polymatroids, as their name suggests, emerged as generalizations of

matroids
, which, in turn, were designed to capture fundamental
properties of dependencies and independencies in matrices and linear spaces

[
44
]
. Many polymatroid terms originate from matroids. The
intimate connection between polymatroids and Shannon information was
recognized by Fujishige
[
21
]
. This connection was used
extensively, either implicitly or explicitly, to investigate numerous
optimization problems related to entropies. For a comprehensive overview,
see
[
43
]
. This section recalls some terminology and results for
polymatroids and investigates how these are related to the notions discussed in
Section
3
.

A polymatroid is usually defined by specifying both the function, called the

rank function
, and the base set
N
, as the tuple
(f,N)
. When convenient, the
rank of the empty set is assumed to be zero. When it is clear from the
context, the base of the polymatroid is omitted and the rank function
f
alone
defines the polymatroid. A polymatroid is identified with the

(2^{|N|}\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
-dimensional vector (or point) consisting of the rank values.

f
is a polymatroid if and only if this vector, also written as
f
, is an
element of the cone
\Gamma_{\!N}
. The polymatroid is
entropic
if its rank
function determines an entropic point in
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, and it is
almost
entropic
, or
aent
, if
f\in\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\hbox{$\mkern-1.0mu\Gamma\mkern 4.0mu$}}\mkern-4.0mu^{*}_{\!N}
.

A
matroid
is an integer valued polymatroid with the additional
requirement that the rank of each singleton is either zero or one, implying
that the rank of
A\subseteq N
is at most
|A|
. In addition to
defining matroids by their rank functions, matroids have several
combinatorial axiomatizations as well, for details consult
[
44
]
.
Polymatroids can be considered “fractional” matroids, and in contrast to
matroids, they are geometrical objects rather than combinatorial ones.

Many operations on entropic points discussed in Section
3

apply to, or generalize to, polymatroids, and many have the corresponding
operation on matroids. However, the terminology sometimes differs.
Operations such as restricting, factoring, and duplicating in Section

3.2
generalize to polymatroids trivially, so we
discuss them only to introduce the terminology.

The polymatroid
(g,M)
is a
restriction
of
(f,N)
, or
(f,N)
is an

extension
of
(g,M)
, if
M\subseteq N
and
g(A)=f(A)
for all

A\subseteq M
. If
N
has exactly one more element than
M
, then
(f,N)

is a
one-element
, or
one-point
extension. The restriction of

(f,N)
to
M\subseteq N
is determined uniquely by the base set
M
and is
written as
f\mathord{\upharpoonright}M
.
Deleting

K\subseteq N
, written as
f\mathbin{\setminus}K
, is the same as restricting
f
to the complement of
K
:
f\mathbin{\setminus}K=f\mathord{\upharpoonright}(N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK)
.

Factoring
requires an equivalence relation
\sim
on
N
; the base of
the factor
f/{\sim}
is the set of equivalence classes, and the rank is the

f
-rank of the union of the corresponding equivalence classes. The factor
is clearly polymatroid.

The operation of duplicating the element
a\in N
in the polymatroid
(f,N)

is called
parallel extension along
a
; the resulting one-element
extension is denoted by
f^{\resizebox{}{327680}{\raisebox{2.0pt}{$\parallel$}}}_{a}
, and the added element is denoted by
a^{\prime}
.

Contracting
a polymatroid is the analogue of conditioning: the
contract of
(f,N)
to
M=N\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK
, is the polymatroid
(f^{\mathsf{c}}_{M},M)
where

f^{\mathsf{c}}_{M}:A\mapsto f(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muK),~~~A\subseteq M,

and
f(A\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muK)=f(AK)-f(K)
. The contract is also written as
f\mathbin{\mkern-7.0mu\not\mkern 7.0mu}K
,
and it is easy to see that it is indeed a polymatroid.
Minors

of a polymatroid are obtained by repeated applications of deletions and
contracts. Actually, at most one application of one operator followed by an
optional application of the other suffices to generate all minors.

In matroid parlance, independence corresponds to
modularity
. The subsets

A
and
B
are
modular
if
f(A)+f(B)=f(AB)+f(A\cap B)
. Disjoint
modular sets are sometimes called
independent
. Setting
K=A\cap B
,
A
and
B

are modular if and only if
aK
and
bK
are modular for all
a\in A\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK

and
b\in B\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK
, similarly to the entropy case.

The
direct sum

(f_{0},N_{0})\oplus(f_{1},N_{1})
of polymatroids mimics
the direct sum of distributions. First, the base sets
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
are
made (or assumed to
be) disjoint. Then the base of the direct sum is
N_{0}\cup N_{1}
, and the rank
function is

g:A\mapsto f_{0}(A\cap N_{0})+f_{1}(A\cap N_{1}),~~~A\subseteq N_{0}\cup N_{1}.

Thus
(f_{i},N_{i})
is isomorphic to the restriction of the direct sum to

N_{i}
, while
N_{0}
and
N_{1}
are modular; these properties uniquely
determine the direct sum. A polymatroid is
connected

if it is not the direct sum of two of its restrictions. In other words,

(f,N)
is connected if
N
cannot be partitioned into two (non-empty,
disjoint) modular parts.

The subset
A\subseteq N
is a
flat
, if every subset larger than
A
has a
strictly larger rank. In particular, the complete set
N
is always a flat.
The intersection of flats is a flat, and the
closure
of a subset is the
smallest flat containing it. Flats of a polymatroid form a lattice with
N

as the largest flat, and the collection of all elements with zero rank
(which can be the empty set) as the minimal flat. Elements of zero rank
are called
loops
.

4.1
Principal extension

The
principal extension
is analogue of Matúš’ Compression Theorem

13
. In the context of matroids, a special case of this extension
was treated in
[
32
]
and
[
44
, page 270]
, while a more general case
appears in
[
24
]
and
[
41
]
.

Let
(f,N)
be a polymatroid,
Z\subseteq N
, and
\alpha\geq 0
. The

principal extension of
(f,N)
along
Z
by
\alpha
is the one-point
extension
(f^{\prime},Nz^{\prime})
of
(f,N)
such that for all
A\subseteq N
,

\begin{array}[]{l@{\,=\,}l}f^{\prime}(A)&f(A),\\[3.0pt]
f^{\prime}(Az^{\prime})&\min\{\,f(A)+\alpha,f(AZ)\}.\end{array}

Claim 14
.

The principal extension is a polymatroid.

Proof.

It is clear that
f^{\prime}
is non-negative, monotone, and is an extension of

f
. For submodularity it suffices to check the basic inequalities

f^{\prime}(aK)+f^{\prime}(bK)\geq f^{\prime}(K)+f^{\prime}(abK)

(5)

listed in (
B2
). If
z^{\prime}
is not an
element of
abK
, then (
5
) holds as
(f,N)
is a
polymatroid. If one of
a
or
b
is
z^{\prime}
, say
b=z^{\prime}
, then
f^{\prime}(aK)=f(aK)
,

f^{\prime}(K)=f(K)
. Using submodularity for
f
we have

\begin{array}[]{r@{\;}c@{\;}l@{\;\ge\;}l}f(aK)+f(K)+\alpha&=&f(K)+\big(f(aK)+\alpha\big)\hfil\;\geq&f^{\prime}(K)+f^{\prime}(az^{\prime}K),\\[3.0pt]
f(aK)+f(KZ)&\geq&f(K)+f(aKZ)\hfil\;\geq&f^{\prime}(K)+f^{\prime}(az^{\prime}K),\end{array}

which shows that
f(aK)+f^{\prime}(z^{\prime}K)\geq f(K)+f^{\prime}(az^{\prime}K)
, as required. Finally,
if
z^{\prime}
is in
K
, say

, then there are four possibilities where the

\begin{array}[]{r@{\;\ge\;}l}(f(aL)+\alpha)+(f(bL)+\alpha)\;\geq&(f(L)+\alpha)+(f(abL)+\alpha),\\[3.0pt]

as was claimed.
∎

An interesting application of principal extension is

: any
base element can be split into two with specified ranks so that
the factor that merges the new elements will be isomorphic to the original.

Claim 15
.

Let
(f,N)
be a polymatroid, and

. Write the rank of

as
f(a)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}
with
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
. There is a

on

such that

, and
the factor of

merging
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
is isomorphic to
(f,N)
.

Proof.

Take the principal extension of
f
along
a
by
\alpha_{0}
to get the
polymatroid
(f_{0},N\cup a_{0})
, then take the principal extension of
f_{0}

along
a
by
\alpha_{1}
to get
(f_{1},N\cup a_{0}a_{1})
. An easy calculation
shows that for each

\begin{array}[]{l@{\;=\;}l}f_{1}(A)&f(A),\\[3.0pt]
f_{1}(Aa_{i})&\min\{f(A)+\alpha_{i},f(Aa)\},\mbox{ and }\\[3.0pt]

where the last equality holds since

The restriction of

to

∎

Observe that each rank in the splitting polymatroid

is either equal to
some rank in the original polymatroid, or it exceeds some old rank by
\alpha_{i}
.
Consequently if all ranks in

are integers and one of the singletons
has rank greater than one, then this singleton can be split into two with
smaller integer ranks, while keeping the polymatroid integer. Continuing the

where all singletons have rank zero or one so that the original polymatroid
is its factor. As an integer polymatroid with singleton ranks zero or one is
a matroid, we proved the following result of T. Helgason
[

]
.

Theorem 16

(Helgason)
.

Every integer polymatroid is a factor of a matroid.

The generalized Ahlswede-Körner operation

Claim 17

Suppose

4.2
Tightening

Let

be a polymatroid and

Claim 18
.

If
0\leq t
, then
(f{\uparrow}^{z}_{t},N)

(f{\downarrow}^{z}_{t},N)

Proof.

For operation

) along

to

, and then the contract
of

on

The

This function can be written equivalently as

f{\downarrow}z=f-f(z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muN\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muz)\mkern 1.0mu\mathsfsl r_{z}

(6)

is the

of

[

Tightening is one of the operations that preserves almost entropic

.

Proof.

If

5
Linear polymatroids

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, and consider the
d
-dimensional vector space
over
\mathbb{F}
, denoted as
\mathbb{F}^{d}
. Suppose that for each
i\in N

we have a set
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{F}^{d}
of linearly independent vectors. For

A\subseteq N
, let
f(A)
be the rank (over the field
\mathbb{F}
) of the
matrix formed from the vectors in
V_{A}=\bigcup\{V_{i}:i\in A\}
as rows; it
is the same value as the
dimension
of the linear subspace spanned by
the vectors in
V_{A}
. Since the rank equals the maximal number of
independent vectors, we have
f(i)=|V_{i}|
. Clearly,
f
is an integer
polymatroid.
Linearly representable polymatroids
are the ones that
can be obtained in this way. Occasionally these polymatroids are called

multi-linear
or
folded-linear
, see
[
2
]
, to
emphasize that the vector set
V_{i}
, assigned to the base element
i
, may
contain several vectors. The polymatroid is
p
-representable
if it
has a vector representation where the underlying field
\mathbb{F}
has
characteristics
p
.

As an example, the uniform polymatroid
\mathbf{u}:J\mapsto\min\{|J|,2\}

for all
J\subseteq N
from (
2
) is linear over every large enough
field
\mathbb{F}
independently of its characteristics. To show this, choose

|N|
two-dimensional vectors
\mathbf{v}_{i}
from
\mathbb{F}^{2}
so that none
of them is a multiple of another. One can do it when
\mathbb{F}
has
|N|-1

or more elements. Since any two of these vectors span
\mathbb{F}^{2}
, setting

V_{i}=\{\mathbf{v}_{i}\}
gives

\dim(V_{i})=1,~~~\dim(V_{i}V_{j})=2,~~~\dim(V_{J})=2\mbox{ for $|J|\geq 3$},

as required by
\mathbf{u}
.

The fact that a collection of vectors forms a linear representation of a
polymatroid can be expressed as an algebraic condition on the vector
coordinates. Actually, this condition says which subdeterminants of the
matrix, formed from the vectors in
V_{N}=\bigcup\{V_{i}:i\in N\}
, are zero and
which are non-zero. Consequently, the compactness theorem holds: if a
polymatroid is linearly representable over any field, then it is also
representable over a
finite
field. In particular,
f
is

0
-representable if and only if it is
p
-representable for every large
enough prime
p
. Furthermore, since the value of a determinant does not
change when switching from a field to an extension, if a polymatroid is
representable over
\mathbb{F}
, then the same representation works over
every extension of
\mathbb{F}
.

When polymatroids
(f,N)
and
(g,N)
are linearly representable over the

same
field
\mathbb{F}
, then
f+g
is also representable over the
same
\mathbb{F}
(using the direct product of the representing vector
spaces). As a consequence, integer conic combinations of
p
-representable
polymatroids are also
p
-representable. It is so because the underlying fields
can be assumed to be finite, say
|\mathbb{F}_{i}|=p^{n_{i}}
, and then the
finite field on
p^{n_{1}n_{2}\cdots}
elements is a common extension of all

\mathbb{F}_{i}
. The sum of polymatroids representable over fields of
different characteristic is not necessarily linearly representable over any
field
[
45
]
.

Definition 20

(Linear polymatroids)
.

A polymatroid is
linear
if it is in the closure of the multiplies of
linearly representable polymatroids.

Linear polymatroids do not necessarily form a cone, but contain the
closure of the conic hull of the
p
-representable polymatroids for each

p
.

For
|N|\leq 3
every polymatroid on
N
is linear. It is so as in these
cases every extremal ray of
\Gamma_{\!N}
contains a polymatroid that has a linear
representation over every field. A representation of the
exceptional extremal ray
\mathbf{u}\in\Gamma_{\!3}
from Section

3.4
was discussed earlier. Non-negative integer
combinations of these polymatroids are also representable over every field,
and multiples of these combinations form a dense subset of
\Gamma_{\!N}
.

Linear polymatroids allow for the extraction of
common information
. An
element
z\in N
represents the common information of
A
and
B
if both

A
and
B
determine
z
(meaning
f(Az)=f(A)
,
f(Bz)=f(B)
), and
f(z)

takes the maximum possible value under this condition, which is
f(A,B)=f(A)+f(B)-f(AB)
. The common information of
A
and
B
can be extracted if
the polymatroid
f
has an extension with such an element
z
. Linear
polymatroids even have such a linear extension. Clearly it suffices to show
that this is the case when
f
is represented by the vector sets
\{V_{i}:i\in N\}
over the field
\mathbb{F}
. The new element
z
is assigned a
maximal independent set of vectors (a base) from the intersection of the linear
span of
V_{A}
and the linear span of
V_{B}
. Using this extractability
property, linear polymatroids for
|N|=4
and
|N|=5
can be characterized

[
35
,
18
]
. In both cases, linear polymatroids form a closed
polyhedral cone whose extremal rays contain polymatroids that are representable over
every field. For
|N|=4
this cone is the conic hull of the
35
non-Vámos
extremal rays of
\Gamma_{\!4}
(see Section
3.4
). The
cone has
34
bounding hyperplanes. Among these hyperplanes
28
come from
the basic Shannon inequalities (
B1
) and (
B2
), the remaining six are
determined by the non-negativity of the
Ingleton expression

f[a,b,c,d\,]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-f(a,b)+f(a,b|c)+f(a,b|d)+f(c,d),

(8)

and its permuted versions. They determine six hyperplanes as (
8
)
is symmetric for swapping
a
and
b
as well as swapping
c
and
d
.

Claim 21
.

Let
N=\{abcd\}
. The polymatroid
f\in\Gamma_{\!N}
is linear if and only if all six
instances of the Ingleton expression
(

are non-negative.

Proof.

As noted above, all non-Vámos extremal rays of
\Gamma_{\!N}
have points
linearly representable over all fields, thus all polymatroids with
non-negative Ingleton values are linear indeed. To finish the proof, it
suffices to show that a linearly representable polymatroid

satisfies the
Ingleton inequality
f[a,b,c,d\,]\geq 0
; non-negativity of the other
expressions follows by symmetry. Extend
f
by the element
z
to
a (still linear) polymatroid so that
z
represents the common information of

a
and
b
. This means
f(az)=f(a)
,
f(bz)=f(b)
, and
f(z)=f(a,b)
.
A simple calculation shows that in this case we also have

f(a,b\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muz)=f(a,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mub)=f(b,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mua)=f(cd,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muab)=0.

Next, we quote a five-variable Shannon inequality from
[

]
which we
will call (

[a,b,c,d\,]+(a,b\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muz)+(a,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mub)+(b,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mua)+3\mkern 1.0mu(cd,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muab)\geq 0.

(MMRV)

It can be checked by an automated tool, such as
[

]
, that it is
indeed a consequence of the Shannon inequalities. Actually, (

)
can be rearranged to be the sum of

basic inequalities from (

Plugging in the one-point extension

∎

The magical Shannon inequality in (

) was apparently pulled out
of a magic hat. Another possibility is to use an LP solver to check that the
basic Shannon inequalities (
B1
) and (
B2
) for the base set

together with the constraints

The value of the Ingleton expression

The archetypal example of a distribution with a negative Ingleton value is
the

and

,
pulled independently. If both are pulled, bell
a
rings, and if any of them

Since

Claim 22
.

Linearly representable polymatroids have group-based entropic multiples.
As a consequence, every linear polymatroid is aent.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is represented by the vectors
V_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{d}
where

\mathbb{F}
is a finite field. Define the joint distribution

\xi=\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
as follows. Choose the vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
randomly with uniform distribution (this can be done because there are only
finitely many elements in
\mathbb{F}^{d}
). The value of
\xi_{i}
is the
sequence of the scalar products
\langle\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}:\mathbf{y}\in V_{i}\rangle
. Since
V_{A}=\bigcup\{V_{i}:i\in A\}
has
f(A)
many linearly
independent elements (this is the dimension of the linear span of the
vector set

),
\xi_{A}
can take

different values, each
with the same probability. Thus the entropy profile of

is
(\log_{2}|\mathbb{F}|)\mkern 1.0muf
, which is a multiple of

. This distribution is clearly
quasi-uniform, but it is also group-based. The set

G_{i}=\{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}:\mathbf{z}\cdot\mathbf{y}=0~~\mbox{ for all }\mathbf{y}\in V_{i}\}

is a subgroup of the additive group
G
of the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{d}
.
For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{d}
the left coset
\mathbf{x}+G_{i}
is uniquely
represented by the scalar product sequence
\langle\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}:\mathbf{y}\in V_{i}\rangle
. Indeed,
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z}

give the same sequence if and only if
\mathbf{z}\in G_{i}
, proving that this
distribution is group-based.
∎

\mathbb{F}^{d}

6.4
Iterating

Starting from an entropic (or aent) polymatroid, the Copy Lemma guarantees
an entropic (or aent) extension. Thus the Lemma can be applied iteratively
to this new polymatroid, obtaining a larger extension of the original one, and
so on. Such an
iterated copy
is usually specified by the sequence of subset
pairs, where the pairs are separated by semicolons, and a pair is separated by a
colon, such as

\langle\,A_{1}:D_{1};~~A_{2}:D_{2};~~\cdots;~~A_{k}:D_{k}\,\rangle.

Here each copied set
A_{j}
and the over set
D_{j}
is a subset of the base of
the previous copy.
Since the copy of a restriction is a restriction of a copy by Proposition

25
, this iterated copy is a restriction of the
iterated
full copy
determined solely by the sequence
\langle D_{1};D_{2};\cdots\rangle

of the over sets.

As an example, let the base of the initial polymatroid be
\{{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1}{\tt c}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}\}
.
Take a
{\tt d}_{1}
-copy over
{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1}
, and mark the “mirror image”
of
{\tt d}_{1}
by
{\tt d}_{2}
. That is, the canonical map
\pi_{1}
of this copy step
gives
\pi_{1}({\tt d}_{1})={\tt d}_{2}
. Thus
\pi_{1}:{\tt d}_{1}{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1}\leftrightarrow{\tt d}_{2}{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1}

describes the copy isomorphism, while the conditional independence is

({\tt d}_{2},{\tt c}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mu{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1})=0
. The next iteration is an
{\tt a}_{1}
-copy over

{\tt b}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}
naming the image as
\pi_{2}({\tt a}_{1})={\tt a}_{2}
. The final iteration is a

{\tt b}_{1}
-copy over
{\tt a}_{1}{\tt a}_{2}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}
with
\pi_{3}({\tt b}_{1})={\tt b}_{2}
. This copy
sequence can be written succinctly as

{\tt d}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt d}_{1}\mkern 1.5mu{:}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1};~~{\tt a}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt a}_{1}\mkern 1.5mu{:}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt b}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2};~~{\tt b}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt b}_{1}\mkern 1.5mu{:}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt a}_{1}{\tt a}_{2}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}.

The third copy in this sequence is a polymatroid on seven points, as
{\tt c}_{1}

is carried over. When
taking full copies the first iteration results in a six-point
polymatroid, the second iteration in a nine-point polymatroid, and the
third iteration in a 14-point polymatroid. The full-copy iteration can be
specified by the over sets
{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1}
,
{\tt b}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}
, and

{\tt a}_{1}{\tt a}_{2}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}
only; the complete specification with explicit naming of
the new elements is

\begin{array}[]{r@{\m=}l}{\tt c}_{2}{\tt d}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu&{\tt c}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}\mkern 1.5mu{:}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt a}_{1}{\tt b}_{1};\\[2.0pt]
{\tt a}_{2}{\tt c}_{3}{\tt c}_{4}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu&{\tt a}_{1}{\tt c}_{1}{\tt c}_{2}\mkern 1.5mu{:}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt b}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2};\\[2.0pt]
{\tt b}_{2}{\tt c}_{5}{\tt c}_{6}{\tt c}_{7}{\tt c}_{8}\mkern 1.5mu{=}\mkern 1.5mu&{\tt b}_{1}{\tt c}_{1}{\tt c}_{2}{\tt c}_{3}{\tt c}_{4}\mkern 1.5mu{:}\mkern 1.5mu{\tt a}_{1}{\tt a}_{2}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}.\end{array}

(14)

Restricting this iterated full copy
f^{*}
to the seven element subset

{\tt a}_{1}{\tt a}_{2}{\tt b}_{1}{\tt b}_{2}{\tt c}_{1}{\tt d}_{1}{\tt d}_{2}
gives the result of the previous iteration sequence.
Observe that
f^{*}
contains eight isomorphic instances of the original
polymatroid; these are at
{\tt a}_{i}{\tt b}_{j}{\tt c}_{k}{\tt d}_{\ell}
where
i
,
j
, and

\ell
are in
\{1,2\}
, and
k
goes from
1
to
8
.

Consequences of the iterated Copy Lemma can be determined similarly to that
of a single application which was discussed in Section

6.1
. First, the iterated copy is translated to an LP
instance, and then to a vertex enumeration problem. The
main
LP variables
are indexed by the non-empty subsets of the initial polymatroid base, while

auxiliary
LP variables are indexed by the additional subsets of the
base of the last copy. Equality constraints expressing isomorphism induced
by the canonical maps as well as the consequences of the conditional
independences are written separately for each iteration. Finally, inequality
constraints are added, these are the basic Shannon inequalities (from
(
B2
) if only balanced inequalities are required) written for the base
elements of the last iteration. (Note that these include the same
inequalities for smaller base sets.) From this LP instance the translation
to a vertex enumeration problem of a polyhedral cone
\mathcal{Q}
analogous
to the one defined in (
11
) is straightforward. The dimension of

\mathcal{Q}
is determined by the number of main variables, which remains
the same as for a single copy-step. However, the number of auxiliary variables
and constraints can grow significantly. While the complexity of vertex
enumeration algorithms grows exponentially with the dimension of
\mathcal{Q}
, it grows quite modestly with the number of constraints. These vertex
enumeration problems are, therefore, typically tractable for a couple of
iterations, and have been used successfully to generate several hundreds
of new four-variable Shannon inequalities
[
19
]
.

A
k
-iterated copy has the same consequences as the very first copy
instance when the matrices
P
and
Q
of that copy step are supplemented by
those linear inequalities on
A^{\prime}N
that guarantee that the subsequent

(k\mkern 1.5mu{-}\mkern 1.5mu1)
iterations can be applied successfully. The converse is also true:
adding a single linear inequality stated for
A^{\prime}N
provided by some
(possibly iterated) application of the Copy Lemma, can be replaced by adding
an instance (or iterated instances) of the Copy Lemma on top of
A^{\prime}N
which
forces that inequality to hold. If more than one such an inequality is
required, further iterations can be added to the chain. While the
two methods:

a)

applying a single copy step augmented with additional (non-Shannon)

start exploring these symmetries with some definitions.

Maximum Entropy method

The Copy Lemma
27
can be recovered by an ingenious application
of the
principle of maximum entropy
conceived in its modern form by

[
29
]
. The principle can be formulated as “if a probability
distribution is specified only partially, take the one with the largest
entropy”, see
[
25
]
. In this case “partial specification” means that some of the
marginal distributions agree with those of a fixed distribution. So let
N

be the base of some fixed distribution
\xi=\{\xi_{i}:i\in N\}
defined on the
alphabet
\mathcal{X}=\prod\{\mathcal{X}_{i}:i\in N\}
. The family of subsets
of
N
where the marginal distributions will be required to be the same will
be denoted by
\mathcal{F}
. Clearly, if for some distribution
\eta
, the
marginal
\eta_{F}
on some
F\subseteq N
agrees with the marginal
\xi_{F}
,
then the marginals on the subsets of
F
will be the same. Thus
\mathcal{F}

can be extended by subsets of its elements, or, equivalently, can be
restricted to its maximal elements. So let
\Upsilon_{\!\mathcal{F}}
denote
the family of those distributions
\eta=\{\eta_{i}:i\in N\}
that are on the
same alphabet
\mathcal{X}
as
\xi
, and, for ever
F\in\mathcal{F}
, have
the same marginal on
F
that
\xi
does:

\Upsilon_{\!\mathcal{F}}=\{\eta:\eta_{F}=\xi_{F}~\mbox{ for all }F\in\mathcal{F}\}.

Since the probability weights in a marginal are sums of the probability
weights of the original distribution, the condition on the marginals is
actually a large number of linear constraints on the probability weights of

\eta
. The total Shannon entropy
\mathbf{H}(\eta)
is a strictly concave
function of these weights, thus finding a distribution in

\Upsilon_{\!\mathcal{F}}
with maximum entropy is a concave optimization
problem on a compact space (the space of all distributions on

\mathcal{X}
) with linear constraints. Consequently, see

[
8
]
, there is always a unique optimal

\hat{\eta}\in\Upsilon_{\!\mathcal{F}}
where the entropy is maximum. While
structural properties of such maximum entropy distributions are mainly
unknown, they certainly satisfy some independence statements.

To describe these statements, let us introduce some notions. A

3-partition
is a partition of the base set into three pieces as

N=X\mathrel{\cup\mkern-1.0mu^{*}\mkern-3.0mu}Y\mathrel{\cup\mkern-1.0mu^{*}\mkern-3.0mu}D
. Occasionally such a 3-partition is written as

\langle X,Y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muD\rangle
to emphasize the distinct role of the
D
component. When it is
clear from the context, the simpler form
N=XY\!D
is used. The
3-partition
XY\!D

separates
the collection
\mathcal{F}
of
subsets of
N
if no
F\in\mathcal{F}
intersects both
X
and
Y
. Thus

\mathcal{F}
is separated into two (not necessarily disjoint) parts: those

F\in\mathcal{F}
which are subsets of
X\cup D
, and those
F\in\mathcal{F}
, which are subsets of
Y\cup D
.

Claim 39

\mathcal{F}

of

to the base set

such that

is parallel to

. Apply Lemma

to
f^{\prime}
, the family

and the single

. The provided polymatroid

While clearly more general than the Copy Lemma, and can encompass some

7.1
Consequences

This Section discusses how all consequences of a
MAXE
instance can be
extracted. There are two interrelated notions in a
MAXE
setting: the
family
\mathcal{F}
of marginals to be preserved, and the family
\mathcal{P}
of 3-partitions of
N
which separate
\mathcal{F}
. For any family

\mathcal{F}
of subsets, and any family
\mathcal{P}
of 3-partitions
define
\mathcal{F}^{\bot}
as a family of 3-partitions, and
\mathcal{P}^{\bot}
as a family of subsets as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\bot}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{XY\mkern-3.0muD:\mbox{ for all $F\in\mathcal{F}$, either $X\cap F$ or $Y\cap F$ is
empty}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\bot}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{F:\mbox{ for all $XY\!D\in\mathcal{P}$, either $X\cap F$ or $Y\cap F$ is
empty}\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{F}^{\bot}
is just the set of 3-partitions that separate

\mathcal{F}
. These operations provide a Galois connection between these
families. In particular, both
\mathcal{F}
and its closure,
\mathcal{F}^{\bot\bot}\supseteq\mathcal{F}
, have the same set of 3-partitions that
separates them. Thus one can take
\mathcal{F}
to be closed, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\bot\bot}
; in this case
\mathcal{F}
is also closed downwards.
Similarly, the
MAXE
instance can be specified equally by a family

\mathcal{P}
of 3-partitions providing the conditional independence
statements. This family
\mathcal{P}
can also be assumed to be closed. If

\mathcal{P}
consists of a single element
XY\!D
, then it is closed, and

\mathcal{P}^{\bot}
contains all non-empty subsets of
XD
and
Y\!D
. This
latter set is the same as all subsets of
N
excluding those that
intersect both
X
and
Y
.

Assume that the
MAXE
instance is specified by the families
\mathcal{F}

and
\mathcal{P}
such that
\mathcal{F}^{\bot}=\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\top}=\mathcal{F}
; this implies that both
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{P}
are
closed. The balanced inequality
\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq 0
is a
consequence of this
MAXE
instance if
\mathbf{e}
contains non-zero
coefficients only at positions whose labels are elements of
\mathcal{F}
(as
only these polymatroid values are guaranteed to be preserved by
f^{*}
), and

\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq 0
holds in every polymatroid on
N
that
satisfies all conditional independence statements in
\mathcal{P}
.

The value of the conditional independence statement
(X,Y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muD)
, generated by
the 3-partition
N=XY\!D
, is zero if and only if
each of the following set of basic Shannon inequalities, denoted by
\mathcal{S}(XY\!D)
, evaluates to zero:

\mathcal{S}(XY\!D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\big\{(a,b\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muK):~K\supseteq D,~a\in X\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK,~b\in Y\mkern 1.0mu{-}\mkern 1.0muK\big\}.

Referring to the Farkas’ lemma
[
55
]
again, the balanced inequality

\mathbf{e}\cdot\mathbf{x}\geq 0
holds in polymatroids which satisfy the
conditional independence statements in
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
\mathbf{e}
is a linear combination of the basic Shannon inequalities in (
B2
)
such that inequalities
not
in

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}=\bigcup\big\{\mathcal{S}(XY\!D):XY\!D\in\mathcal{P}\big\}

have non-negative coefficients, while those in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
can
have arbitrary coefficients. Since we want a minimal generating set, it
suffices to consider cases when combining coefficients of inequalities in

\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
are non-positive, and coefficients of other
inequalities are non-negative.

As was done in Section
6.1
, the complete algorithm works
as follows. Create LP variables indexed by the non-empty subsets of
N
. The

main variables
, collected into the vector
\mathbf{x}
, are indexed by
subsets in
\mathcal{F}
. The remaining
auxiliary variables
, with
indices not in
\mathcal{F}
, are collected into
\mathbf{y}
. Create a matrix
with columns indexed by the LP variables, and rows indexed by the basic
Shannon inequalities in (
B2
). A matrix row contains the coefficients
of the corresponding basic inequality, it is negated if the inequality
is in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Write the matrix as
(P,Q)
separating the
main and auxiliary variables. The minimal set of inequalities, of which
everything else follows, can be
recovered from the extremal rays of the polyhedral cone

\mathcal{Q}=\{\mathbf{h}P:\mathbf{h}Q=\mathbf{0},~\mathbf{h}\geq 0\}.

Similarly to the Copy Lemma case, rows, where the
Q
-part is all zero, can
be deleted, and rows with the same
Q
-part can be merged. Symmetries of the
problem, as in Section
6.5
for the iterated Copy Lemma,
can also be applied to reduce the computational complexity. The average of
polymatroids
\sigma(f^{*})
where the permutation
\sigma
of
N

preserves
\mathcal{F}
(elementwise, not pointwise) provides a polymatroid

\textsf{MAXE}_{\mathcal{F}}(f)
which is symmetrical for all of these
permutations. Thus, without loss of generality, the polymatroid
f^{*}

provided by Lemma
40
can be assumed to be symmetrical for all
of these symmetries. In spite of this reduction, the polyhedral computation
is significantly more demanding, and only a few cases have been settled, see

[
14
,
39
]
. Each of them handles a single-element
3-partition set
\mathcal{P}
. Claim
44
, which is presented here
without proof, summarizes these results.

Claim 44
.

(i)
No new inequality is generated when
|D|=1
.

(ii)

No new inequality is generated for the 3-partition
\langle x,y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muab\rangle
.

(iii)

The 3-partition
\langle x,y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muabc\rangle
generates the non-Shannon inequalities

\displaystyle(a,x\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muc)+(a,b\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0mux)+(a,b\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muy)+(c,y)+{}

\displaystyle{}+\left\{{(b,x\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muac)\atop(b,y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muac)}\right\}+\left\{{(c,x\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muab)\atop(c,y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muab)}\right\}\geq(a,b),

(choosing either the top or the bottom line in the curly brackets), plus

xyabc
keeping the partition.

(iv)

The 3-partition
\langle cd,z\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muab\rangle
generates the non-Shannon inequalities

Part (iv) recovers the inequalities stated in Claim

.

Any automated inequality prover, such as
[

]
, can be used to quickly
check that these inequalities are indeed consequences of the stated
arrangements. However, proving that there are no more, is more challenging.
A partial reason stems from polyhedral geometry. Given all extremal rays and
all facets of a polyhedral cone (such as
\mathcal{Q}
above), it is not
known whether the maximality of the specified extremal rays can be checked
faster than recreating them from scratch.

7.2
Many instances

The iterated Copy Lemma, discussed in Section
6.4
,
creates many instances of the initial polymatroid embedded into the final
polymatroid. A variant of
MAXE
, called
GMAXE
, does the same embedding in a
single step. To describe the method we need some preparations. Let

(f,N)
be the polymatroid to be embedded, and let
\varphi:M\to\mkern-14.0mu\to N
be a
many-to-one map with range
N
. The subset
T\subseteq M
is a

transversal
if
\varphi
, restricted to
T
, is a one-to-one map
between
T
and
N
. Finally, let
\mathcal{T}
be a collection of
transversals that mark the embedded instances in
M
.

Lemma 45

(Generalized Maximum Entropy Method)
.

Suppose
(f,N)
is entropic (or aent), and let
\varphi:M\to\mkern-14.0mu\to N
and the
collection
\mathcal{T}
of transversals
as above. There is an entropic (or aent, respectively) polymatroid
(g,M)
, such that

(i)

for all transversals

\varphi\mathord{\upharpoonright}T

(ii)

of

separates

\mathcal{T}
, then
g(X,Y\mkern 1.0mu|\mkern 1.0muD)=0
.

Proof.

Take
|M|
many parallel extensions of
(f,N)
such that
i\in M
is parallel
to
\varphi(i)
, and then restrict the extension to
M
. The resulting
polymatroid
(f^{\prime},M)
is clearly entropic (or aent) and satisfies (i).
Apply the
MAXE
Lemma
40
to

and
\mathcal{T}
to get the
required polymatroid
g
.
∎

Keeping track of instances of the original polymatroid during a sequence of

\varphi

The Ahlswede-Körner method

This section discusses a third technique to create non-Shannon type
information inequalities. It was used by Makarychev et al. in
[

]
to
derive an infinite family of non-Shannon inequalities. In
[

the
method was called MMRV from the initials of the authors of

, but it is also
known as the Ahlswede-Körner method since it is based on Lemma

attributed to them. As proved in Claim

operation, thus direct applications of Ahlswede-Körner Lemma

f^{*}(A)=\left\{\mkern-6.0mu\begin{array}[]{l@{~~~}l}f(A)&\mbox{ if $z\notin A$},\\[3.0pt]

This function
f^{*}
is the same as the tightening
f{\downarrow}z
, as seen in the

[

]

and in
[

(17)

9
Conclusions and open problems

Only a few methods and techniques are known to generate non-Shannon
information inequalities. This paper explored these methods and their
relationship, and investigated some of their variants.

9.1
Foundation

The foundation was established in Sections
3
and

4
.
Basic facts about the entropy region
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
, determined by
N
jointly
distributed discrete random variables, were recalled and proved in Section

3
. Natural manipulations on probability distributions give
rise to natural operations on the entropy region. These operations include
restricting, deleting, factoring, and independent drawing. Two sophisticated
operations on distributions, called
thinning
and
compressing
,
were defined in Section
3.7
. They arise as limits of a certain
type of random coding. Similar constructions can be found in the literature
as auxiliary tools in converse theorems of coding problems, but Theorems

11
and
13
, expressing properties of the thinning and
compressing operations, have not been stated in this general form. The
corresponding proofs in the
Appendix
separate typicality
considerations from random coding, thus achieving an error term of order

(\log n)/n
instead of the best possible error term
(\log n)/\sqrt{n}
when
typical sequences are used.

Polymatroids
, the second pillar of the foundation, are the abstract
structures satisfying all basic Shannon inequalities. Section

4
provided basic facts and specific terminology
concerning polymatroids. Many natural operations on the entropy region

\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
extend to the set
\Gamma_{\!N}
of polymatroids. However, the terminology
sometimes differs. For example, the operation corresponding to
compressing (Section
3.7
) is called
principal extension

(Section
4.1
).

A rich source of probability distributions is linear codes over finite
fields. The corresponding polymatroid class, discussed in Section

5
, originates from linear subspace arrangements of finite
dimensional vector spaces. The definition of
linear polymatroid

is more subtle, because representations over fields with different
characteristics do not mix well.

9.2
Copy Lemma

Finding a non-Shannon information inequality is equivalent to separating
regions
\Gamma^{*}_{\!N}
and
\Gamma_{\!N}
. A natural strategy is to look for an operation

case of the analogous problem of separating linear and general polymatroids,

9.3
Maximum Entropy Method

The Maximum Entropy Method uses the fact that the probability masses of a
distribution can be modified so that some fixed marginals have the same
distribution, while the total entropy, within this constraint, is maximum.
Such a maximum entropy distribution satisfies independence statements
that depend on which marginals are fixed, see Claim
39
. Two
flavors of this method were presented. The first one, dubbed
MAXE

requires an aent polymatroid
f

f
on a
collection of subsets of the base set, and additionally satisfies
conditional independence statements, see Lemma
40
. Claim

43
proves that the Copy Lemma is equivalent to the special
case of
MAXE
with a single independence statement. Section

7.1
describes an algorithm that extracts all
consequences of a
MAXE

symmetries of the problem. Results of the algorithm are presented for a
couple of cases in Claim
44

The second flavor of the Maximum Entropy Method, called

Lemma
45
. It encompasses many instances of the same base
polymatroid marked by a collection of

, mimicking the
work of the iterated Copy Lemma, while letting the maximum entropy principle
stretch the distribution to satisfy many conditional independence
statements. Defying intuition, high transversal density destroys the
strength of

, see Claim
46
.

9.4
Ahlswede-Körner method

The Ahlswede-Körner method was used in
[

to derive an infinite

[

machinery. In Section

consequence of the Copy Lemma when copying a singleton and to be strictly
weaker (as it cannot prove that the Vámos vector is not aent).
