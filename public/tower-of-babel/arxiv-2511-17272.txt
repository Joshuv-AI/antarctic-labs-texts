Lower Bounds for CSP Hierarchies Through Ideal Reduction Thanks: This is the publically available version of a paper with the same title to appear in SODA ’26 .
Jonas Conneryd Yassine Ghananne Shuo Pang

Abstract
We present a generic way to obtain level lower bounds for (promise) CSP hierarchies from degree lower bounds for algebraic proof systems. More specifically, we show that pseudo-reduction operators in the sense of Alekhnovich and Razborov [Proc. Steklov Inst. Math. 2003] can be used to fool the cohomological k k -consistency algorithm. As applications, we prove optimal level lower bounds for c c vs. ℓ \ell -coloring for all ℓ ≥ c ≥ 3 \ell\geq c\geq 3 , and give a simplified proof of the lower bounds for lax and null-constraining CSPs of Chan and Ng [STOC 2025].

Introduction

The class of
constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs)
is a cornerstone of research in logic, algorithms, and complexity theory, encompassing a wide range of natural computational tasks such as Boolean satisfiability, graph
k
-colorability, and solving systems of linear equations. Despite their generality, CSPs possess enough structure to enable a detailed understanding of which variants are tractable, which are not, and what fundamentally distinguishes the two.

Every constraint satisfaction problem can be formulated as the task of determining, given two relational structures
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{T}
, whether there is a homomorphism
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}

[
50
,
38
]
. A widely studied variant is when the structure
\mathbf{T}
is kept fixed;
\mathbf{T}
is then called the
template
of the CSP. A significant amount of research on CSPs has been motivated by the
dichotomy conjecture
of Feder and Vardi
[
38
]
. Broadly generalizing classical results of Schaefer
[
75
]
and Hell and Nešetřil
[
47
]
, the conjecture states that every fixed-template CSP is either in
{\mathsf{P}}
or
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete.

The dichotomy conjecture was resolved independently by Bulatov
[
22
]
and Zhuk
[
79
]
, as the culmination of two decades of efforts into the so-called
algebraic approach
to CSPs; for an overview of this area, see
[
12
]
. Informally, the results of Bulatov and Zhuk show that a CSP is tractable if and only if its template admits a certain type of non-trivial symmetry, or
polymorphism
. Any CSP lacking such symmetries was previously known to be
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard
[
63
]
, and Bulatov and Zhuk exploit such a symmetry to construct a polynomial-time algorithm for the CSP.

Following the resolution of the dichotomy conjecture, a new research direction has emerged around a broad generalization of CSPs, known as
promise CSPs
or
PCSPs
. An overview of this area can be found in
[
58
]
. Roughly speaking, the task in a PCSP is to decide whether an instance is satisfiable in a strong sense, or not even satisfiable in a weaker sense. More formally, given a pair of relational structures
(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T})
such that
\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{T}
and an instance structure
\mathbf{A}
, the
decision version
of the PCSP with template
(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T})
is the task of determining whether
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{S}
or
\mathbf{A}\not\to\mathbf{T}
, promised one of the two holds. In the
search version
, the task is to find a homomorphism
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}
promised that
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{S}
.

The formal study of PCSPs was initiated by Austrin, Guruswami, and Håstad
[
7
]
, but special cases have been investigated for much longer. For instance, perhaps the most well-known PCSP is
approximate graph coloring

[
45
]
, also called
c
vs.
\ell
-coloring
, where
\ell\geq c\geq 3
and the task is to decide whether an input graph
G
is
c
-colorable or not even
\ell
-colorable, promised one of the two holds. There are large gaps in our understanding of even this very natural PCSP: approximate graph coloring is widely believed to be
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hard for all constants
\ell\geq c\geq 3
, but
{\mathsf{NP}}
-hardness is known only for
c
vs.
(2c-1)
-coloring for
c\geq 3

[
11
]
, and for
c
vs.
\big(\binom{c}{\lfloor c/2\rfloor}-1\big)
-coloring when
c\geq 4

[
57
]
.
By contrast, the best known polynomial-time algorithm for coloring
3
-colorable graphs guarantees only an
\smash{O(n^{0.19747})}
-coloring for an
n
-vertex graph
[
52
]
.

In general, the existing algorithms for solving (P)CSPs, including those of Bulatov and Zhuk, can be said to consist of two main building blocks:
local consistency
, and solving systems of
linear equations
. Local consistency algorithms check whether small subproblems of the original CSP are satisfiable, and then enforce that the local solutions are consistent with each other. These checks take various forms: for instance, the well-known
k
-consistency
algorithm accepts an instance if and only if a consistent collection of partial homomorphisms with domain size at most
k
exists. The more stringent
Sherali–Adams hierarchy

[
77
]

of linear programming relaxations asks for a probability distribution on partial solutions that is consistent with the marginals of a distribution over satisfying assignments. The
sum-of-squares
or
Lasserre
hierarchy
[
71
,
59
]
of semidefinite programming relaxations further demands that the probabilities assigned to the local solutions satisfy certain orthogonality conditions when viewed as vectors.

Algorithms that use linear equations to solve (P)CSPs are in turn based on the observation that any fixed-template CSP can be formulated as a system of linear equations over Boolean variables. These algorithms then relax the variable domain to one where, e.g., Gaussian elimination can be used to efficiently search for a
global
solution over the relaxed domain. A prominent example is the family of
affine relaxations
, which solve (variations of) the linear system formulation of CSPs over the integers. This can be done efficiently using, e.g., the classical Smith normal form, see
[
61
]
. Examples include the
affine integer programming (AIP)

[
21
,
11
]
and
linear Diophantine equation

[
18
]
relaxations.

It is well known that neither local consistency checking nor the linear equation paradigm alone solves all tractable CSPs, and both Bulatov’s and Zhuk’s algorithms use elaborate combinations of the two. In recent years, quite some work has gone into designing and analyzing simpler CSP algorithms than those of Bulatov and Zhuk. Besides providing a more fine-grained understanding of CSP families, the ultimate, if distant, goal of this line of work is to find a
uniform
CSP algorithm—that is, one where the template
\mathbf{T}
is part of the input—which runs in polynomial time whenever
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
is tractable. In addition to its intrinsic importance, such an algorithm might also help establish the border of tractability for more general problems, such as PCSPs. The most well-studied algorithms in this line of work are so-called
hierarchies
of relaxations, each defined with respect to a parameter
k
called its
level
. Larger values of
k
yield increasingly tighter relaxations, while the
k
-th level of a hierarchy runs in time
m^{O(k)}
on an instance of size
m
(with some caveats in the case of semidefinite programming relaxations, see
[
69
]
).

1.1
CSP Hierarchies

1.2
Lower Bounds for CSP Hierarchies

An integral part of the design and analysis of (P)CSP algorithms is to understand their limitations; this is especially relevant for PCSPs, where the border of tractability is not known. For hierarchies, such results take the form of lower bounds on the level required to solve a given (P)CSP. Besides disqualifying candidates for uniform CSP algorithms through showing they fail on tractable CSPs, level lower bounds may help identify recurring features of hard instances for different families of hierarchies, which is instructive both in algorithm design and for proving stronger hardness results.

1.3
CSP Hierarchies and Proof Complexity

All hierarchies for
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
mentioned in
Section

1.1
always accept instances
\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}
. Therefore, to prove a lower bound on the level required to solve
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
, we should exhibit a
fooling instance
: a structure
\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{A}\not\to\mathbf{T}
, but which is nevertheless accepted by the
k
-th hierarchy level. For a PCSP with template
(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T})
, a fooling instance is a structure
\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{A}\not\to\mathbf{T}
, but such that the
k
-th level of the hierarchy for
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{S})
accepts
\mathbf{A}
.

This setting closely resembles that in

\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}

[
78
]

[
28
]
, who showed that, with high probability, random
k
-SAT requires exponential size to refute in the
resolution

CSP instances are often hard, and by now, lower bounds for multiple families of random CSPs are known for resolution
[
66
,
65
,
13
,
15
,
67
,
25
]
, as well as for other proof systems. As two examples relevant to this work, we mention
polynomial calculus

[

,
3
,

,
35

and

[

Multiple results and techniques from proof complexity have informed the study of lower bounds for CSP algorithms and vice versa; some examples include

1.5
Technical Overview

We now review the connection between polynomial calculus degree lower bounds and level lower bounds for cohomological
k
-consistency, formally established in Lemmas
3.1
and
4.4
. To this end, we first provide a brief overview of the necessary technical background.

Proof Complexity Background:

The starting point of our connection is a technique for proving lower bounds for the
polynomial calculus
proof system
[
34
,
1
]
.
Given a field
\mathbb{F}
and a set
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, polynomial calculus proves that the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
have no common root through deriving new polynomials in the ideal
\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
generated by
\mathcal{P}
until reaching the constant polynomial
1
.

The most common complexity measures of a polynomial calculus proof are
size
, defined as the number of monomials in it, and
degree
, defined as the maximum degree among all polynomials appearing in the proof. It is known that a strong enough lower bound on degree implies lower bounds on size in a black-box fashion
[
48
]
; indeed, almost all known polynomial calculus size lower bounds are proved in this way. Barring some early results
[
24
,
46
,
16
,
23
]
, almost all polynomial calculus degree lower bounds are in turn proved by constructing a so-called
pseudo-reduction operator
or
R
-operator

[
73
]
, which is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear operator that sends all low-degree polynomials derived from
\mathcal{P}
to
0
but sends
1
to
1
; thus, there can be no low-degree polynomial calculus proof that
\mathcal{P}
is unsatisfiable.

Pseudo-reduction operators are often based on the notion of
reduction modulo ideals
from Gröbner basis theory. Given a
so-called
admissible
total order
\prec
on the monomials in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and an ideal
I\subseteq\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the
reduction operator
R_{I}(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
acts on a polynomial
p
by iteratively identifying a term
t
in
p
and a polynomial
q\in I
whose largest term with respect to
\prec
is
t
, subtracting
q
from
p
, and repeating until no such
q
exists. It then returns the resulting polynomial.
As an analogy (and indeed a special case), one can think of a system of linear equations being reduced to row-echelon form.

If the polynomials in a set
\mathcal{P}
have a common root, then
R_{\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle}
is a pseudo-reduction operator: all polynomials derived from
\mathcal{P}
, of any degree, belong to
\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
and are thus reduced to
0
, and since the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
have a common root, we have that
1\not\in\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
and hence
R_{\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle}(1)=1
. A pseudo-reduction operator
R
should therefore behave as
R_{\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle}
would on low-degree monomials if the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
had a common root.
In line with this heuristic, Alekhnovich and Razborov
[
3
]
pioneered an approach for constructing pseudo-reduction operators based on
local
reductions: to each low-degree monomial
m
, associate a satisfiable subset
S(m)
of
\mathcal{P}
, define
R(m)=R_{\langle S(m)\rangle}(m)
, and define
R
on arbitrary polynomials by linear extension.
We refer to operators constructed in this way as
local
pseudo-reduction operators.

Cohomological
k
-Consistency:

Given an instance
\mathbf{A}
of
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
, the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm maintains a collection
\mathcal{H}=\{\mathcal{H}(X):X\in\smash{\binom{A}{\leq k}}\}
of partial homomorphisms
\mathbf{A}[X]\to\mathbf{T}
, which is iteratively refined in two steps, outlined below. When
\mathcal{H}
stabilizes, the algorithm rejects
\mathbf{A}
if
\mathcal{H}(X)=\varnothing
for some
X\in\smash{\binom{A}{\leq k}}
and accepts otherwise.

A
k
-consistent
collection
\kappa
of partial homomorphisms is one where (1) for each
\varphi\in\kappa
with domain size smaller than
k
,
there is some
\psi_{S}\in\kappa
extending
\varphi
to any larger domain
S
of size
k
, and (2) all restrictions of all
\varphi\in\kappa
to smaller domains are also in
\kappa
. In the first step of each iteration, the algorithm finds the maximal
k
-consistent collection
\kappa
in
\mathcal{H}
and assigns
\mathcal{H}=\kappa
.

In the second step, the algorithm seeks a family of solutions over

\mathbb{Z}
to a linear system formulation

L_{k}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{T})
of
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
. The system
L_{k}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{T})
has variables
x_{\varphi}
for all partial homomorphisms
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}
with domain size at most
k
. The solutions to
L_{k}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{T})
must all be supported on
\kappa
, and for each
\varphi\in\kappa
there must be a solution that sets
x_{\varphi}
to
1
and
x_{\psi}
to
0
for all
\psi\in\kappa
with the same domain as
\varphi
. If no such solution exists, then
\varphi
is removed from
\mathcal{H}
in the next iteration.

A Link Between the Two:

Homomorphisms
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}
can be encoded as the common roots of a set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}}
of polynomials over
\mathbb{F}
in Boolean variables
\{x_{a,i}\mid a\in A,i\in T\}
. Any (partial) function
\varphi:A\to T
can then be encoded as a monomial
m_{\varphi}=\prod_{(a,i)\in\varphi}x_{a,i}
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{A}\not\to\mathbf{T}
, that
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic

, and that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}}
admits a degree-

to show that

is then a fooling instance for the cohomological

-consistency algorithm for all

. More specifically, we show that:

1.

the functions

form a
D
-consistent collection of partial homomorphisms;

2.

the assignment

is a solution to

, except over the polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
instead of
\mathbb{Z}
;

3.

for each monomial

of degree at most
D
, the polynomial

has integer coefficients.

Consequently, composing the map

with
any evaluation on a point in
\mathbb{Z}^{\left\lvert A\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert T\right\rvert}
, or more generally with any linear map

such that

, yields a solution to
L_{k}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{T})
over
\mathbb{Z}
. As we show in

4.4
, the specific properties of local pseudo-reduction operators lets us judiciously choose these maps to produce the family of solutions to

sought by the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm.

Items
1
and
2
are established in
Lemma

3.1
and follow rather naturally from the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}}
and general properties of pseudo-reduction operators.
The integrality of
R(m)
in
Item

3
is proved in
Lemma

5.3
, and the key tool we use is the

[
39
]
. The lex game over a polynomial ring

with a lexicographic monomial order
\prec
has two players named Lea and Stan, and is defined with respect to a set

and monomial
m
.

It is shown in
[
39
]
that Lea has a winning strategy in the lex game if and only if the ideal

of polynomials that vanish on

contains a polynomial with largest term
m
. The key insight for showing
Item

3
is that this winning strategy can itself be encoded as a polynomial in

, which has largest term

and has integer coefficients when

.
Therefore, each reduction step performed by
R_{I(V)}
can be done using a polynomial with integer coefficients, and hence the output is integral. Since the ideals
\langle\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}(m)\to\mathbf{T}}\rangle
are over Boolean variables, our result applies to them, and so
Item

3
follows.

Given the generality of our connection and the flexibility it offers in constructing solutions to the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm, we expect it to be useful for proving lower bounds for other (P)CSPs, and potentially also for other hierarchies.

Applications:

To prove

, we apply our connection to the polynomial calculus degree lower bound in

For

Relational Structures and Constraint Satisfaction Problems

A
signature

\sigma

\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{\ell}
, each with respective arity
\operatorname{ar}(\Gamma_{i})\in\mathbb{N}
. A
\sigma
-structure

\mathbf{A}
consists of a domain
A
and, for each
\Gamma\in\sigma
, a relation
\Gamma^{\mathbf{A}}\subseteq A^{\operatorname{ar}(\Gamma)}
. A
relational structure
is a
\sigma
-structure, for some signature
\sigma
. For a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbf{A}
and a set
X\subseteq A
, the
substructure of
\mathbf{A}
on
X
, denoted
\mathbf{A}[X]
, is the
\sigma
-structure with domain
X
where, for
\Gamma\in\sigma
, the relation
\Gamma^{\mathbf{A}[X]}
consists of the tuples in
\Gamma^{\mathbf{A}}
that mention only variables in
X
. A relational structure
\mathbf{A}
is
finite
if
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
is finite. All relational structures in this paper are finite unless otherwise stated.

For two
\sigma
-structures
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
, a
homomorphism
from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
is a map
\varphi\colon A\to B
such that for each symbol
\Gamma\in\sigma
and each tuple
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\operatorname{ar}(\Gamma)})
, it holds that if
\mathbf{a}\in\Gamma^{\mathbf{A}}
then
\varphi(\mathbf{a})=(\varphi(a_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(a_{\operatorname{ar}(\Gamma)}))\in\Gamma^{\mathbf{B}}
. If there exists a homomorphism from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
, we write
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{B}
; if not, we write
\mathbf{A}\not\to\mathbf{B}
. The restriction of a homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}
to a set
X\subseteq A
is denoted
{{\varphi}\upharpoonright_{X}}
. Given sets
Y\subseteq X\subseteq A
and a homomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{A}[Y]\to\mathbf{T}
, if there exists a homomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbf{A}[X]\to\mathbf{T}
such that
{{\psi}\upharpoonright_{Y}}=\varphi
, we say that
\psi

extends

\varphi
. We denote the set of homomorphisms from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{B}
by
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})
.

For a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbf{T}
, an
instance of the constraint satisfaction problem with template
\mathbf{T}
is a
\sigma
-structure
\mathbf{A}
such that, for all
\Gamma\in\sigma
, there are no tuples in
\Gamma^{\mathbf{A}}
with repeated elements.
3
3

3

This technical condition corresponds to having no repeated variables in the scope of any constraint in the instance.
We define
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
as the computational problem where the input is an instance
\mathbf{A}
and the task is to determine whether
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}
.

For a pair
(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T})
of fixed
\sigma
-structures such that
\mathbf{S}\to\mathbf{T}
, the
promise constraint satisfaction problem (PCSP) with template
(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T})
, denoted by
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T})
, is the computational task of distinguishing whether
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{S}
or
\mathbf{A}\not\to\mathbf{T}
for a given instance
\mathbf{A}
, promised that one of the two holds.
4
4

4

This is the
decision
variant of PCSP. In the
search
variant, the input is an instance
\mathbf{A}
such that
\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{S}
, and the task is to find a homomorphism from
\mathbf{A}
to
\mathbf{T}
. For CSPs, it is a standard fact that the search version reduces to the decision version, but no generic search-to-decision reduction is known for PCSPs. Since an efficient algorithm for the search version can be used to also solve the decision version, a lower bound for the latter also holds for the former.
Note that
\operatorname{PCSP}(\mathbf{T},\mathbf{T})=\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
.

Graphs and Hypergraphs

A
hypergraph
consists of a finite vertex set
V
together with a collection
\mathcal{E}
of subsets of
V
. The elements of
\mathcal{E}
are called
hyperedges
. A hyperedge set
\mathcal{E}
is
t
-uniform
if all hyperedges in
\mathcal{E}
contain exactly
t
vertices, and a hypergraph
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
is
t
-uniform if its hyperedge set is. For a hyperedge set
\mathcal{E}
, we denote the set
\bigcup_{e\in\mathcal{E}}e
of vertices in
\mathcal{E}
by
V(\mathcal{E})
. For a vertex
v\in V
, the
degree

\deg_{\mathcal{E}}(v)
of
v
in
\mathcal{E}
is the number of hyperedges in
\mathcal{E}
containing
v
. The subscript
\mathcal{E}
is usually omitted if the hyperedge set is clear from context. A hypergraph is
d
-regular
if all its vertices have degree exactly
d
. For a set
U\subseteq V
and a hyperedge set
\mathcal{E}
, the
neighborhood
of
U
in
\mathcal{E}
is
N_{\mathcal{E}}(U)=\{v\in V(\mathcal{E})\setminus U:\exists u\in U,\exists e\in\mathcal{E}\mid\{u,v\}\subseteq e\}
. For a hypergraph
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
and
U\subseteq V
, the subhypergraph of
H
induced by
U
is
H[U]=(U,\mathcal{E}[U])
, where
\mathcal{E}[U]=\{e\in\mathcal{E}:e\subseteq U\}
.

A
walk of length
\ell
in a hyperedge set
\mathcal{E}
is a sequence of vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{\ell}
and a sequence of hyperedges
e_{0},\ldots,e_{\ell-1}
such that
\{v_{i},v_{i+1}\}\subseteq e_{i}
for all
0\leq i<\ell
. A walk is a
(Berge) path
if
v_{0},\ldots,v_{\ell}
are distinct and
e_{0},\ldots,e_{\ell}
are also distinct; the vertices
v_{0},\ldots,v_{\ell}
are then the
connecting vertices
of the path, and
v_{0}
and
v_{\ell}
are its
endpoints
. A walk is a
(Berge) cycle
if (1)
\ell\geq 2
, (2)
v_{0},\ldots,v_{\ell-1}
are distinct, (3)
v_{\ell}=v_{0}
, and (4)
e_{0},\ldots,e_{\ell-1}
are distinct.
The
girth
of a hyperedge set
\mathcal{E}
, denoted
\operatorname{girth}(\mathcal{E})
, is the minimum length of a cycle in
\mathcal{E}
, or
\infty
if there are no cycles in
\mathcal{E}
.
A hyperedge
e_{i}
in a path is
simple
if every vertex in
e_{i}\setminus\{v_{i},v_{i}+1\}
has degree
1
in
\{e_{j}:0\leq j<\ell\}
, and a connecting vertex
v_{i}
in a path is simple if it has degree
2
in
\{e_{j}:0\leq j<\ell\}
. A path is simple if all its hyperedges and non-endpoint connecting vertices are simple. A cycle is simple if all its hyperedges and connecting vertices are.
A path is
pendant
in a hyperedge set
\mathcal{E}
if none of its non-endpoints belong to any hyperedge in
\mathcal{E}\setminus\{e_{0},\ldots,e_{\ell-1}\}
.

A
proper
c
-coloring
of a hypergraph
H=(V,\mathcal{E})
is a map
\chi\colon V\to[c]
such that
\left\lvert\chi(e)\right\rvert\geq 2
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
. If there exists a proper
c
-coloring of
G
, we say that
G
is
c
-colorable
. The
chromatic number
of a hypergraph
G
is the smallest
c
such that
G
is
c
-colorable.

A
graph

G=(V,E)
is a
2
-uniform hypergraph; in particular, all hypergraph notions in this section specialize to their usual graph-theoretic meaning.

2.3
Polynomials and Reduction Modulo Ideals

For a ring
K
, we denote the polynomial ring over
K
in
n
variables by
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, and the set of variables appearing in a polynomial
p
by
\operatorname{Vars}(p)
. A
monomial
is a product of variables. For a vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we write
x^{\alpha}
to denote the monomial
\prod_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Given a monomial
m
, its
multilinearization

m_{\mathrm{mult}}
is the product of the variables appearing in
m
. For a set of polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
in
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the set of polynomials of the form
\sum_{i\in[m]}q_{i}p_{i}
for
q_{i}\in K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is called the
ideal generated by
\mathcal{P}
and is denoted by
\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
. For a set
S\subseteq K^{n}
, we denote the ideal of polynomials vanishing on all of
S
by
I(S)
. If
S
is empty, we define
I(S)
to be
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. For a polynomial
p
and a (possibly partial) mapping
\rho\colon\operatorname{Vars}(p)\to K
, we denote the mapping
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\to K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that evaluates
p
on
\rho
by
\operatorname{eval}_{\rho}
, and the polynomial obtained by evaluating
p
according to
\rho
by
{{p}\upharpoonright_{\rho}}
.

For a field
\mathbb{F}
, a total well-order
\prec
on the monomials in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is
admissible
if (1) for any monomial
m
with at least one variable, it holds that
1\prec m
, and (2) for any monomials
m_{1},m_{2}
and
m
such that
m_{1}\prec m_{2}
, it holds that
m\cdot m_{1}\prec m\cdot m_{2}
. A prominent example which will be central to this work is the
lexicographic
order, where
x_{1}\succ\ldots\succ x_{n}
and for any two monomials
x^{\alpha_{1}},x^{\alpha_{2}}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, it holds that
x^{\alpha_{1}}\succ x^{\alpha_{2}}
if and only if the leftmost entry of the vector difference
\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is positive.

Fixing an admissible order
\prec
on the monomials in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, the
leading term
of a polynomial is its term with largest monomial with respect to
\prec
. A polynomial
p
is
reducible modulo
an ideal
I
if there exists
q\in I
with the same leading term as
p
; otherwise,
p
is
irreducible
modulo
I
. It is straightforward to show that for any polynomial
p
and any ideal
I
, there exists a unique representation
p=q+r
where
q\in I
and where
r
is a linear combination of irreducible monomials modulo
I
. The polynomial
r
is called the
reduction
of
p
modulo
I
, and the operator
R_{I}
on
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that takes a polynomial
p
to its reduction modulo
I
is called the
reduction operator modulo
I
. A straightforward argument shows that the reduction operator is
\mathbb{F}
-linear.

Finally, we recall the Boolean Nullstellensatz, omitting its standard proof (see, e.g.,
[
35
, Appendix A]
).

Lemma 2.1

(Boolean Nullstellensatz)
.

For any finite set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
that contains the set of Boolean axioms
\{x_{1}^{2}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}-x_{n}\}
, and any polynomial
q\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
,
q
vanishes on all common roots of the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
if and only if
q\in\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
. In particular,
1\in\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
if and only if the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
have no common root.

2.4
Algebraic Proof Complexity

Nullstellensatz

[
14
]
is a proof system
that uses algebraic reasoning to prove that a set
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
of
polynomials in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
over a field
\mathbb{F}
is unsatisfiable, that is, that the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
have no common root in
\mathbb{F}
. We often refer to
\mathcal{P}
as the set of
axioms
,
and we say that a subset of axioms
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq\mathcal{P}

is
satisfiable
if the polynomials in
\mathcal{Q}
have a
common root. A
Nullstellensatz refutation
of
\mathcal{P}
is a certificate that
1
is in the ideal generated by
\mathcal{P}
, in the form of a polynomial identity

\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}q_{i}=1

(1)

where
q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}
are arbitrary polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Nullstellensatz is sound: no certificate (
1
) can exist if the polynomials in
\mathcal{P}
have a common root, since assigning it to both sides of (
1
) results in
0=1
. It is well known that Nullstellensatz is complete over any field
\mathbb{F}
when the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
can only take values in
\{0,1
}, meaning that the
Boolean axioms

\{x_{i}^{2}-x_{i}:i\in[n]\}
are present in
\mathcal{P}
.
5
5

5

More generally, Nullstellensatz is complete over any field whenever the domains of
x_{1},\ldots x_{n}
are all finite.
The Boolean axioms are present in all sets
\mathcal{P}
considered in this paper. The primary complexity measure of a Nullstellensatz refutation as in (
1
) is
degree
, defined as the maximum degree among the polynomials in it.

The proof system considered in this paper is
polynomial calculus

[
34
]
, which can be seen as a dynamic version of Nullstellensatz. Starting from
\mathcal{P}
, polynomial calculus derives new polynomials in the ideal
\langle\mathcal{P}\rangle
through two
derivation rules:

Linear combination:

\displaystyle\frac{p\quad q}{ap+bq},\;a,b\in\mathbb{F}\,;

(2a)

Multiplication:

\displaystyle\frac{p}{x_{i}p},\;x_{i}\text{ any variable.}

(2b)

A
polynomial calculus derivation
of a
polynomial
p
from the set
\mathcal{P}

is a sequence
of polynomials

(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\tau})
,
where

p_{\tau}=p
and each
p_{i}

is either
in
\mathcal{P}
or
obtained by applying one of
(
2a
)–(
2b
)
to polynomials
p_{j}
with
j<i
. A
polynomial calculus
refutation of
\mathcal{P}
is a derivation of

1
from
\mathcal{P}
.

The most common complexity measures
of polynomial calculus refutations are
size
and
degree
.
The
size
of a polynomial
p
is its number of monomials when
expanded into a linear combination of distinct monomials, and the

degree
of
p
is the maximum degree among all
of its monomials. The size of a polynomial calculus refutation

\pi
is the sum of the sizes of the polynomials in
\pi
,
and the degree of
\pi
is the maximum degree among all
polynomials in
\pi
.

This paper only concerns degree lower bounds for polynomial calculus, but nevertheless we should mention that a strong enough polynomial calculus degree lower bound immediately implies a size lower bound through the
size-degree relation

[
48
]
: if all polynomials in a set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
have degree at most
D_{0}
and any polynomial calculus refutation of
\mathcal{P}
requires degree at least
D
, then any polynomial calculus refutation of
\mathcal{P}
requires size
\exp(\Omega((D-D_{0})^{2}/n))
.

For technical reasons, most papers about polynomial calculus in fact consider the better-behaved proof system
polynomial calculus resolution (PCR)

[
1
]
, where
additionally each variable
x_{i}
appearing in
\mathcal{P}
has a formal
negation
\overline{x}_{i}
enforced by adding the set of polynomials

\{x_{i}+\overline{x}_{i}-1:i\in[n]\}
to
\mathcal{P}
. Polynomial calculus and PCR are
equivalent with respect to degree; hence, we will be lax in differentiating between them in this paper.

The most common technique for proving polynomial calculus degree lower bounds is by constructing a
pseudo-reduction operator
, as mentioned in
Section

1.5
.

Definition 2.2

(Pseudo-reduction operator)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, let
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
be unsatisfiable. An
\mathbb{F}
-linear
operator
R
on
\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

is a
degree-
D
pseudo-reduction operator for
\mathcal{P}
if

1.

R(1)=1
,

2.

R(p)=0
for every polynomial

p\in\mathcal{P}
,

3.

R(x_{i}m)=R\bigl(x_{i}R(m)\bigr)
for every monomial
m
of degree at most

D-1
and every variable
x_{i}
.

The kernel of a pseudo-reduction operator provides an overapproximation of what can be derived from
\mathcal{P}
in degree at most
D
which is fine-grained enough to not contain
1
. The existence of a degree-
D
pseudo-reduction operator for a set
\mathcal{P}
of polynomials implies a polynomial calculus degree lower bound of
D+1
for refuting
\mathcal{P}
, as stated in
Lemma

2.3
.
6
6

6

It is folklore that the converse direction also holds, and hence that degree-
D
pseudo-reduction operators for
\mathcal{P}
characterize the existence of degree-
D
polynomial calculus refutations of
\mathcal{P}
. However, to the best of our knowledge, this result has never been published.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
73
]
, Lemma 3.2)
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of polynomials over

\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. If there exists a degree-
D

pseudo-reduction operator for
\mathcal{P}
, then any polynomial calculus
refutation of
\mathcal{P}
over
\mathbb{F}
requires degree strictly greater
than
D
.

Proof sketch.

Apply

R
to all polynomials in a purported polynomial
calculus refutation of
\mathcal{P}
of degree at most
D
. Conclude by induction on refutation length that it is
impossible to reach the constant polynomial
1
.
∎

The treatment of the Alekhnovich–Razborov method in this section partly follows that of

Recall from

For a set of polynomials

and again refer the reader to

\langle\operatorname{cl}(m^{\prime})\rangle
if and only if
m^{\prime}
is reducible modulo
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle
.

Then, the
\mathbb{F}
-linear extension
R
of the map

is a degree-

pseudo-reduction operator for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}}
.

The proof of

4.2
can be found in

, and is by a now-standard argument first introduced by Alekhnovich and Razborov
[

]
and subsequently clarified by Galesi and Lauria

as well as Mikša and Nordström
[

]
.

In

defined as in

with respect to a lexicographic order restrict to operators

.

Lemma 4.3

(Integrality assumption)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{T},\operatorname{cl}(\cdot)
, and

be defined as in

4.2
. Let
\mathbb{F}
be any field of characteristic

and let

be any lexicographic order on the monomials in

.

Given

4.3
, we can establish our main technical lemma needed to prove lower bounds for the cohomological

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that the assumptions of
Lemma

4.2
are satisfied for all monomials
m\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
of degree at most
D
, where
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic zero and monomials are ordered by a lexicographic order

. Then, the cohomological

-consistency algorithm for
\operatorname{CSP}(\mathbf{T})
accepts
\mathbf{A}
for all
k\leq s
, where
s
is the largest integer such that
\left\lvert\operatorname{cl}(m)\right\rvert\leq D
for all monomials

of degree at most
s
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.3
, the operator
R\colon\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]\to\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
in the statement of
Lemma

4.2
restricts to an operator
\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]\to\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
, and by
Lemma

4.2
, we have that
R
is a degree-
D
pseudo-reduction operator for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}}
.

By
Lemma

3.1
, the collection
\kappa_{R}=\{\kappa_{R}(X):{X\in\binom{A}{\leq k}}\}
where
\kappa_{R}(X)=\{\varphi\in\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{A}[X],\mathbf{T})\mid R(m_{\varphi})\neq 0\}
is
k
-consistent for all
k\leq D
. Moreover, also by
Lemma

3.1
the assignment to the variables
x_{X,\varphi}
of (
4a
)–(
4b
) which sets

to
\operatorname{eval}_{y}\circ R(m_{\varphi})
for

and

is a solution to (
4a
)–(
4b
) over
\mathbb{Z}
for any point

. Therefore, it suffices to show that for all sets

and every homomorphism

, there exists a point

such that

Denote the polynomial

by

, and recall that

-consistency algorithm stabilizes on

-consistency algorithm for

Remark 4.6

(On requiring a lexicographic order)
.

Insisting on a lexicographic order in Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
might seem like a technical artifact of our proof techniques, but there is in fact reason to believe that the same result does not hold for other orders. For instance, the pseudo-reduction operators in

can be put on the same form as in

5
Integrality of Reductions and the Lex Game

In this section we establish an integrality property of polynomial reductions (
Lemma

5.3
), from which
Lemma

4.3
follows as an easy corollary. This property says that for any finite set
V\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
and any field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic 0, the reduction of a monomial
m\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
modulo the ideal
I(V)
under the lexicographic order is always a polynomial with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
.

The proof of
Lemma

5.3
uses a game characterization of reducibility under a lexicographic order, namely the
lex game
of Felszeghy, Ráth, and Rónyai
[
39
]
; we use an equivalent formulation from
[
40
]
.

Definition 5.1

(Lex game;
[
40
]
, Section 3)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field,
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
a
finite set, and
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
an
n
-dimensional vector of natural numbers. The
lex game

\operatorname{Lex}(V;\alpha)
is the following deterministic game played between two players named Lea and Stan. Lea and Stan both know
V
and
\alpha
. If
V=\varnothing
, Lea wins immediately. For a nonempty
V
, the lex game proceeds in rounds.
In round
i
, Lea picks
\alpha_{n-i+1}
elements of
\mathbb{F}
, and Stan picks a value
y_{n-i+1}
which is different from all of Lea’s choices in that round;
if he cannot do so, Lea wins immediately.
After
n
rounds, if Stan’s answers
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is a vector in
V
, he wins; otherwise, Lea wins.

The lex game is finite, deterministic, and cannot end in a draw. Therefore, for any
V
and
\alpha
, one of the players has a winning strategy for
\operatorname{Lex}(V;\alpha)
.
The main result of
[
39
]
is that the existence of a winning strategy for Lea characterizes whether the
monomial
x^{\alpha}
is reducible modulo
I(V)
under the lexicographic order.

Theorem 5.2

(
[
39
]
, Theorem 2)
.

For any field
\mathbb{F}
,
finite set
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the monomial
x^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
is reducible modulo
I(V)
in the lexicographic order induced by
x_{1}\succ\ldots\succ x_{n}
if and only if Lea has a winning strategy in the game
\operatorname{Lex}(V;\alpha)
.

Following
[
39
]
,
we show the following corollary of the game characterization.
We note that similar results were also shown in
[
4
, Section 4]
,
[
41
, Corollary 5.1.2]
, and
[
40
, Corollary 3]
by arguments different from ours.

Lemma 5.3

(Integrality lemma)
.

If
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic zero and
V
is a subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
, then for any monomial
m=x^{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
it holds that
R^{\mathrm{lex}}_{I(V)}(m)\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

Intuitively, given a reducible monomial
m
, we can express Lea’s guesses in her winning strategy as a
set
of polynomials

f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{n}(x)
, where
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
represents Stan’s answers and
each
f_{i}
is a Boolean-valued function that depends only on
\{x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
.
We will use these polynomials to rewrite
m
into smaller terms.

Proof.

We
use induction over the lexicographic order of
m=x^{\alpha}
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume
m
is multilinear, as
m=m_{\mathrm{mult}}
modulo
\langle x_{1}^{2}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}^{2}-x_{n}\rangle
where the latter ideal is
I(\{0,1\}^{n})\subseteq I(V)
.

The base case
m=1
is immediate: 1 either reduces to 0 or itself, both of which are integers.

For the induction step, we denote
I=I(V)
.
If
m
is irreducible modulo
I
then
R_{I}(m)=m
, which trivially has integer coefficients. Hence, we may suppose that
m
is reducible modulo
I
.
By
Theorem

5.2
, in this case Lea has a winning strategy in the game
\operatorname{Lex}(V;\alpha)
. Recall that Lea makes a guess for the value of
x_{i}
for each
i
where
\alpha_{i}=1
, her guess for
x_{i}
depends only on Stan’s previous answers, and her guesses are
\{0,1\}
-valued since
V\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Therefore, we can write her guess for
x_{i}
as a polynomial

f_{i}(x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^{n-i}}c_{a}m_{a}

where
m_{a}=\prod_{j:a_{j}=1}x_{j}\prod_{j:a_{j}=0}(1-x_{j})
is the indicator polynomial of “
x=a
” and
c_{a}\in\{0,1\}
is her guess when Stan answered
(x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})=a
. Clearly,
f_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.
As in
[
39
]
, we now consider the polynomial

p(x)=\prod_{i:\ \alpha_{i}=1}(x_{i}-f_{i})

(12)

which has integer coefficients. The leading term of each factor
x_{i}-f_{i}
is
x_{i}
as
f_{i}
depends only on
x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}
, so the leading term of
p
is
x^{\alpha}=m
. Moreover, we claim that
p
is in the ideal
I
. To see this, notice that for any
a\in V
, we have that
p(a)=0
. That is, since Stan loses the lex game instance corresponding to
\alpha
, Lea can always stop him from answering
a
. This means that there is some
i\in[n]
such that
f_{i}(a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{n})=a_{i}
.
It follows that
p
vanishes on
V
and so is contained in
I
.

We now complete the induction by considering the reduction of
m-p
. As
p\in I
, we have
R_{I}(p)=0
so
R_{I}(m)=R_{I}(m-p)
.
We have shown that
p\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
and that
p
has leading term
m
, so
m-p=\sum_{i}d_{i}m_{i}
where
m_{i}\prec m
for all
i
and all
d_{i}
are integers. Therefore,
R_{I}(m-p)=\sum_{i}d_{i}R_{I}(m_{i})
where each
R_{I}(m_{i})
is in
\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
by the induction hypothesis. It follows that
R_{I}(m)\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma

4.3
follows as a straightforward corollary of
Lemma

5.3
.

Lemma
4.3

(Restated)
.

Let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{T},\operatorname{cl}(\cdot)
, and
D
be defined as in
Lemma

4.2
. Let
\mathbb{F}
be any field of characteristic
0
and let
\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}
be any lexicographic order on the monomials in
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
. For any monomial
m
of degree at most
D
, it holds that
R^{\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
.

Proof.

Note that
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle=I(V_{\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{A}[\operatorname{cl}(m)],\mathbf{T})})
as ideals in
\mathbb{F}[x_{\mathbf{A}[\operatorname{cl}(m)],\mathbf{T}}]
by the Boolean Nullstellensatz (
Lemma

2.1
), where
V_{\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{A}[\operatorname{cl}(m)],\mathbf{T})}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\left\lvert\operatorname{cl}(m)\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert T\right\rvert}
denotes the set of (indicator vectors of) homomorphisms
\mathbf{A}[\operatorname{cl}(m)]\to\mathbf{T}
.
Then, by
Lemma

5.3
,
R^{\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)\in\mathbb{Z}[\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{cl}(m),T}]
for every monomial
m
over
\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{cl}(m),T}
that has degree at most
D
. The same remains true when
m
is over
\mathbf{x}_{A,T}
and reductions are done within the larger polynomial ring
\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
. To see this, set all variables outside of
\mathbf{x}_{\operatorname{cl}(m),T}
to 0; this assignment does not touch any variable of any generator of
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle
, nor of
m
, so the reductions in the two polynomial rings coincide by uniqueness of the representation
m=R^{\prec_{\mathrm{lex}}}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)+q
for
q\in{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}
. ∎

.

]

Lower Bounds for Approximate Graph Coloring

In this section, we use the machinery developed in Sections
3
-
5
to prove lower bounds for the level needed for the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm to solve approximate graph coloring, also called
c

\ell
-coloring. In this problem, the task is to decide whether the input graph

is either
c
-colorable or not even
\ell
-colorable, for some
\ell\geq c
. Phrased as a PCSP, approximate graph coloring is
\operatorname{PCSP}(K_{c},K_{\ell})
, where
K_{a}
denotes the complete graph on
a
vertices.

The fooling instances are sampled from the
random regular graph model

\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
, which is the uniform distribution over
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices.
8
8

8

Note that a
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices can only exist if

is even. We ignore this technical issue in what follows.
We write

to denote that

is a graph sampled from
\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
. More quantitatively, the main result in this section is
Theorem

6.1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

There is an absolute constant
C
such that the following holds for natural numbers

and

such that

and
6d^{3}\leq\log{n}
. With high probability as

, a graph

has chromatic number strictly larger than

, but the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm for

-colorability accepts

for all

. Consequently, the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm does not solve
3
vs.
{d/4\log{d}}
-coloring on
n
-vertex graphs, for any

.

The same result holds for sparse Erdös-Rényi random graphs (where each edge in the graph appears independently with some fixed probability
p
), with a slighly worse dependence on

in the parameters. The proof is essentially the same as that of
Theorem

6.1
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of
Theorem

6.1
. Fortunately, all the technical work has already been done in
[
35
, Sections 5-6]
(partially building on ideas of
[
74
]
) for the purpose of proving degree lower bounds for the polynomial calculus proof system in

is, more or less, an immediate consequence of their degree lower bound combined with

, and all that is needed in this section is to reiterate and rephrase their proof for our setting.

We first recall the well-known fact that the chromatic number of a graph
G\sim\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
is concentrated around

.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
53
,
36
]
)
.

With probability
1-o_{n}(1)
, a graph
G
sampled from
\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
where

has chromatic number strictly larger than

and at most

.

9

9

The cited references establish much stronger concentration bounds than those stated in

, but these bounds do not meaningfully improve the parameters in our main results.

The crucial property of random graphs that is used to establish both the satisfiability and reducibility conditions is

.

Definition 6.3

(Sparsity)
.

A graph
G=(V,E)
is

(s,\varepsilon)
-sparse
if every vertex
set
U\subseteq V
of size at most
s
satisfies

\left\lvert E(U)\right\rvert\leq(1+\varepsilon)\left\lvert U\right\rvert
.

As is well known, random regular graphs are sparse with high probability and with strong parameters.

Lemma 6.4

(See, e.g.,
[
35
]
, Lemma 2.7)
.

For
n,d\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying
{\varepsilon\delta=\omega(1/\log{n})}
and
d^{2}\leq\varepsilon\delta\log{n}
, it holds with high probability that a graph
G
sampled from
\mathbb{G}_{n,d}
is
(s,\varepsilon)
-sparse for
s=(8d)^{-(1+\delta)(1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}n
.

All not-too-large subgraphs in a sparse graph are
3
-colorable, as shown in the next lemma. Since the proof of this folklore result is short and instructive, we include it with no claim of originality.

Lemma 6.5

(Folklore, see, e.g.,
[
35
]
, Lemma 2.6)
.

If a graph
G=(V,E)
is
(s,\varepsilon)
-sparse for
some
\varepsilon<1/2
, then it holds for every subset
U\subseteq V

of size at most
s
that
G[U]
is
3
-colorable.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\left\lvert U\right\rvert
. The base case
\left\lvert U\right\rvert=1
is
immediate. For the induction step, assume that the claim holds
for all sets of size at most
s-1
. Consider a set
U\subseteq V
of
size
a\leq s
. The average degree in
G[U]

is
2\left\lvert E(U)\right\rvert/a
, which is at most
2(1+\varepsilon)<3
by sparsity. Hence,
there exists a vertex
v\in U
with degree at most 2 in
G[U]
. The
graph
G{[U\setminus\{v\}]}
is
3
-colorable by the induction
hypothesis, and every
3
-coloring witnessing this will leave at
least one color available for
v
. Hence, every

3
-coloring of
{G[U\setminus\{v\}]}
can be extended to

G[U]
. The lemma follows.
∎

The main technical insight needed to establish the satisfiability and reducibility conditions is an appropriate notion of
closed
set (cf.
Section

4
), for which we need the following definitions from
[
35
, Section 5]
. Given a linear order
\prec
on
V
, an

increasing (decreasing) path
in
G
is a simple path

(v_{1},\ldots,v_{\tau})
where
v_{i}\prec v_{i+1}

(
v_{i}\succ v_{i+1}
) for all
i\in[\tau-1]
. For vertices

u,v\in V
we say that
v
is a
descendant of
u
if there
exists a decreasing path from
u
to
v
, and for a set

U\subseteq V
, a vertex
v
is a
descendant of
U
if it is
a descendant of some vertex in
U
. We denote the
set of all descendants of
U
by
\operatorname{Desc}(U)
and define every vertex to be a
descendant of itself so that
U\subseteq\operatorname{Desc}(U)
.

A
\tau

U\subseteq V
is a
simple path or a simple cycle of length
\tau
with the property
that its two endpoints are both contained in
U
(in the case of

in
U
. A
lasso with respect to
U
is a
walk
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5})
where
v_{2}=v_{5}
and all other
vertices are distinct, and where
v_{1}\in U
.

Definition 6.6

(Closure)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let
U\subseteq V
. We say that
U
is

closed
if
U=\operatorname{Desc}(U)
and there are no 2-,
3-, 4-hops or lassos with respect to
U
. A

of

is any minimal closed set that contains
U
.

It is shown in
[

Proof of

Let

of characteristic

and

By

7.10

7.14
.
Moreover, the set

Consider the set of edges in

that nontrivially intersect

Moreover, since

8
Concluding Remarks

We show that pseudo-reduction operators from proof complexity that are constructed via the Alekhnovich-Razborov method can be used to prove level lower bounds for (P)CSP hierarchies.
Using this connection, we prove optimal level lower bounds for the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm for approximate graph coloring, and give a new proof of the level lower bounds in
[
27
]
for lax and null-constraining CSPs.

Perhaps the most pressing open problem is whether the cohomological
k
-consistency algorithm solves all tractable CSPs.
A closely related question is whether technical connections from proof complexity, such as the one presented in this work, can be useful to prove hierarchy lower bounds for tractable CSPs, and
whether they can be used to extend our lower bounds to other hierarchies such as the C(BLP+AIP) hierarchy
[
29
,
27
]
.

It is also natural to ask whether our results for approximate graph coloring can be generalized to
approximate graph homomorphism
, which is null-constraining but not lax. Lower bounds for this problem have been established for multiple hierarchies, the strongest of which is SDA
[
30
]
, but bounds with parameters comparable to those in
Theorem

6.1
are only known for the
k
-consistency algorithm
[
31
]
(see also
[
27
, Theorem 6.24]
).

Unlike our results for lax and null-constraining CSPs, it seems unclear how to prove our results for approximate graph coloring without using the connection to polynomial calculus degree lower bounds. In particular, the definition of closure there depends on the choice of a vertex order, which in turn seems motivated by the reduction operator. It would be interesting to see a proof of
Theorem

6.1
that does not use pseudo-reduction operators; such a proof would likely also yield insights applicable to polynomial calculus degree lower bounds.

More technically, the use of the lex game in
Section

5
limits the applicability of our results to lexicographic orders on the monomials. Can we show similar results for, say, the graded lexicographic order? This would likely require different techniques from those in this paper, or might simply be impossible; see
Remark

4.6
.

Before commencing the proof of

Fact A.1
.

Let

Write

is irreducible modulo
\langle X\rangle
if
m
is irreducible modulo
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle
. Then, for all monomials
m^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{cl}(m^{\prime})\subseteq X
, it holds that
R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m^{\prime})\rangle}(m^{\prime})=R^{\prec}_{\langle X\rangle}(m^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
m
be a monomial such that
\operatorname{cl}(m)\subseteq X
, and write
R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)=\sum_{j}a_{j}m_{j}
. Recall that
m_{j}
is irreducible modulo
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle
for all
j
by definition. Moreover, we have by
A.2
that
\operatorname{cl}(m_{j})\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(m)
, and each
m_{j}
is also irreducible modulo
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m_{j})\rangle
. Therefore, it follows by assumption that each
m_{j}
is irreducible modulo
\langle X\rangle
. By definition, the polynomial
R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)
is the unique sum of irreducible terms modulo
\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle
such that
m=R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)+q
, for some
q\in\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle
. Note that
\operatorname{cl}(m)\subseteq\langle X\rangle
and hence
q\in\langle X\rangle
. Since all terms in
R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)
are also irreducible modulo
\langle X\rangle
, it follows by uniqueness that
R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m)=R^{\prec}_{\langle X\rangle}(m)
.
∎

With Claims
A.2
and
A.3
established, we are ready to prove
Lemma

4.2
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

Let
R
be the operator in the lemma statement. We need to show that
R
satisfies Properties
1
-
3
of a pseudo-reduction operator, that is, that (1)
R(1)=1
, (2)
R(p)=0
for every polynomial

p\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{T}}
, and (3)
R(x_{a,i}\cdot m)=R\bigl(x_{a,i}\cdot R(m)\bigr)
for every monomial
m\in\mathbb{F}[\mathbf{x}_{A,T}]
of degree at most

D-1
and every variable
x_{a,i}
.

To see that
R(1)=1
, note that
\mathbf{A}[\operatorname{cl}(1)]\to\mathbf{T}
by the satisfiability condition, and hence
1\not\in\langle\operatorname{cl}(1)\rangle
by the Boolean Nullstellensatz (
Lemma

2.1
). Since
1
is minimal in
\prec
, it follows that
R(1)=1
.

To establish Property
2
, observe that
\operatorname{cl}(m)
contains all vertices in
A
mentioned by
m
, which implies that
R
maps all axioms (
3b
)–(
3e
) to
0
. To see that
R
also maps the axioms (
3a
) to
0
, further note that
\operatorname{cl}(1)\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(\{a\})
for all
a\in A
, and hence

\displaystyle R\Bigl(\sum_{i\in T}x_{a,i}-1\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in T}R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i})\rangle}(x_{a,i})-R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(1)\rangle}(1)

\displaystyle\quad[\text{definition of $R$}]

(14a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in T}R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i})\rangle}(x_{a,i})-R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i})\rangle}(1)

\displaystyle\quad[\text{reducibility and \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{claim:reductions-the-same}}]

(14b)

\displaystyle=0\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle\quad[\text{definition of $R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i})\rangle}$}]

(14c)

as desired.

Let us now establish Property
3
, which is subtler than the first two. Let
m
be a monomial of degree at most
D-1
, and write the polynomial
R(m)
as
\sum_{j}a_{j}m_{j}
where
a_{j}\in\mathbb{F}
. We have by definition that

R(x_{a,i}R(m))=\sum_{j}a_{j}R(x_{a,i}m_{j})=\sum_{j}a_{j}R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m_{j})\rangle}(x_{a,i}m_{j})\kern 5.0pt.

(15)

The next step is to show that
R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m_{j})\rangle}(x_{a,i}m_{j})=R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m)\rangle}(x_{a,i}m_{j})
for all

\displaystyle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m_{j})

\displaystyle\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(\{a\}\cup\operatorname{cl}(m_{j}))

\displaystyle\subseteq\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m))

(16c)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m)\kern 5.0pt.

\displaystyle\quad[\text{idempotence}]

(16d)

By reducibility and
A.3
, we obtain that

for all

(17)

From (

\displaystyle\sum_{j}a_{j}R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m)\rangle}(x_{a,i}m_{j})

\displaystyle=R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m)\rangle}\Bigl(x_{a,i}\sum_{j}a_{j}m_{j}\Bigr)

(18a)

\displaystyle=R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m)\rangle}(x_{a,i}R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(m)\rangle}(m))

\displaystyle\quad[\text{definition of $\textstyle{\sum}_{j}a_{j}m_{j}$}]

(18b)

\displaystyle=R^{\prec}_{\langle\operatorname{cl}(x_{a,i}m)\rangle}(x_{a,i}m)

\displaystyle\quad[\text{monotonicity and \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{fact:reduction-operator}}]

\displaystyle\quad[\text{definition of $R$}]

(18d)

as desired. With Properties
