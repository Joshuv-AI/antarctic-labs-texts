Exacerbation of neuropsychiatric symptoms in an adult male with influenza A virus treated with oseltamivir
Authors: Masa Scott, Emily Faltin, Kathleen Londick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) package labeling has a warning for neuropsychiatric adverse events (NPAE), most commonly in children and adolescents, especially males. There are several case reports of NPAE in adults treated with oseltamivir, but few document patients with preexisting neuropsychiatric conditions without additional contributing factors. This case report describes a 22-year-old male with a history of bipolar disorder, depression, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder who had been stable on his medication regimen before experiencing sudden worsening of symptoms after the initiation of oseltamivir. The case adds to previous literature by strengthening the correlation between oseltamivir and a sudden increase in neuropsychiatric symptoms. Providers should be aware that oseltamivir may exacerbate symptoms of previously stable patients. Depending on the severity of neuropsychiatric effects, discontinuation of oseltamivir and symptom treatment with pharmacotherapy may be warranted.

Introduction

Influenza is a respiratory infection arising seasonally in the winter and spring that kills hundreds to thousands of people every year.1 A common antiviral medication, oseltamivir, is prescribed to prevent infection and treat individuals with influenza and is the only oral neuraminidase inhibitor available on the market. Although rare, oseltamivir has been reported to predispose or bring about neuropsychiatric symptoms, specifically in children and adolescents, with higher rates in males. The natural disease course of influenza may also be associated with neuropsychiatric behavioral changes; however, 1 study found an increased risk for neuropsychiatric adverse events (NPAEs) with oseltamivir after adjusting for a patient’s diagnosis of influenza.2 Direct causation has not been established between oseltamivir and neuropsychiatric effects, and the exact mechanism is unknown. It is hypothesized that the stimulation of D2 receptors, action at N-methyl-D-asparate and gamma-aminobutyric acid receptors, and inhibition of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and monoamine oxidase-A receptors are linked to causing abnormal behaviors.3 The mechanisms can cause abnormal excitatory behaviors, including confusion, psychosis, hallucinations, and self-injury.3 Most documented literature is geographically located in Asian countries and typically involves children and adolescents. Here, we report a case of a 22-year-old White male who reports to the emergency department exhibiting symptoms of acute psychosis, paranoia, and erratic behaviors, which began and worsened after initiation of oseltamivir for treatment of influenza.

Literature Review

In 2006, postmarketing surveillance of oseltamivir revealed concerns for NPAEs that prompted the FDA to add a warning to the oseltamivir label. Most of these reports were made in Japan, with the highest rates occurring in children, adolescents, and young adults in particular, and more frequently in males. A recent case-control study analyzing a National Sample Cohort from the National Health Insurance Service in South Korea found an increased risk in NPAEs regardless of age, sex, or medical conditions.2 Of note, this study did not separate out patients with psychiatric history. Additionally, several case reports have been published worldwide of oseltamivir-induced NPAEs occurring in adults.4-11 The Table outlines the 9 current published case reports on NPAEs in adults. For the majority of patients (67%), symptoms began within 48 hours of treatment with oseltamivir.4,5,7,10,11 Six of 9 patients were male. Documented NPAEs included symptoms of mania, aggression, and confusion. Only 4 male patients had a previous history of neuropsychiatric conditions.4,5,8,9,11 Two patients with underlying schizophrenia had their previously stable antipsychotic regimens reduced at the same time that oseltamivir was initiated.9 This makes it difficult to definitively conclude that oseltamivir was the cause of the patient’s acute decompensation. The remaining 2 patients had neurocognitive disorders, 1 with mild cognitive disorder and the other with vascular dementia, and received reduced oseltamivir doses.4,11 It is important to note that 1 of 2 patients also had a 9-day admission for acute encephalopathy with cellulitis and acute kidney injury 5 days before treatment with oseltamivir. However, this was the only case that rechallenged oseltamivir treatment, which did result in NPAE symptoms recurring.4 The 2 patients with neurocognitive disorders at baseline are the only cases reported oseltamivir-induced NPAE in the United States.4,11 Treatment for all 9 cases except 1 included antipsychotic therapy, with the most common choices being olanzapine and haloperidol. For the majority of cases, oseltamivir was discontinued before finishing the entire course of the medication. Some patients did not present for evaluation for NPAE until after completing their oseltamivir course.

Case Presentation

An otherwise healthy 22-year-old White male was seen in an urgent care center complaining of a sore throat, cough, and body aches that started 1 day prior. A point-of-care test resulted positive for Influenza A. At this time, the patient was prescribed a course of oseltamivir 75 mg 1 tablet by mouth twice daily for 5 days. His past medical history includes bipolar disorder, depression, and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). The patient, his mother, and pharmacy fill history confirmed medication adherence with bupropion XL 300 mg daily and sertraline 125 mg daily. Admitting documentation showed he has been stable for approximately 1 year.

After initiation of oseltamivir, the patient’s mother noted symptoms of psychosis, paranoia, and erratic behavior, which worsened during the antiviral course. One day after completing the 5-day oseltamivir course, the patient reported to the emergency department with a chief complaint of hand injury and swelling after punching a brick wall secondary to paranoia and psychosis. During his emergency department visit, the patient remained physically aggressive with staff and displayed acute psychosis and uncontrolled behaviors, which led to the requirement of chemical sedation and physical restraints. Chemical sedation consisted of 4 mg intramuscular midazolam and 5 mg intramuscular haloperidol, which was later followed by another 2 mg of intramuscular midazolam. At this time, a consultation with psychiatry was placed, and the patient was accepted into a psychiatric intensive care unit of a community hospital for further evaluation.

A urine drug screen (UDS), complete metabolic panel (CMP), and a urinalysis were completed in the emergency department. The UDS flagged positive for benzodiazepines and cannabinoids. UDS was likely positive for benzodiazepines secondary to receiving multiple rounds of midazolam in the emergency department. The patient self-reported chronic illicit cannabis use. The CMP indicated hypokalemia at 3.0 mmol/L, which was corrected with 40 mEq of oral potassium and hypochloremia at 95 mmol/L. His urinalysis was negative.

At admission, the 5-day course of oseltamivir therapy was complete, and respiratory symptoms were alleviated. The patient was started on risperidone 1 mg twice daily and continued sertraline 125 mg daily, but bupropion XL was discontinued. After 1 day of therapy, the patient began to complain of dystonic reactions. Benztropine 1 mg by mouth twice daily was started, and risperidone was changed to quetiapine XR 200 mg nightly, which was increased to 400 mg nightly over the course of 1 week. His neuropsychiatric symptoms began to improve with the new medication regimen, and he had complete recovery after 8 days in the psychiatric intensive care unit. The patient had complete recovery within 9 days of oseltamivir cessation. The patient was discharged on quetiapine XR 400 mg nightly and continued sertraline 125 mg daily, but bupropion XL remained discontinued.

The Naranjo Algorithm was used to assess the probability of adverse drug reactions, and a score of 3 resulted in a “possible” causality.12 This score was obtained by having previous conclusive reports for the reaction, adverse events appearing after the drug was administered, improving after the drug was discontinued, and having a possible alternative cause for the reaction. The patient’s chronic cannabis use was considered a possible alternative cause for the reaction.

Discussion

In the case presented, symptom onset was similar to previously identified adult cases of oseltamivir-induced NPAEs. The patient’s mother reported that symptoms worsened while on the antiviral course, and, in previous cases, the onset was around 48 hours after starting oseltamivir. The patient presented to the emergency department 1 day after completion of the oseltamivir 5-day course. The patient did have a previous psychiatric history (bipolar disorder, depression, and ADHD), but the patient reports stability before oseltamivir initiation and no medication dose changes within the last year along with adherence to medications. The patient is a 22-year-old male, which fits the demographic of young adult males in which NPAEs are more frequently observed. In this literature review of adult cases, the average patient age was 46.2 years (range 18-83 years). This case occurred in the United States, adding to the limited number of case reports outside of Asian countries. However, the patient’s urine drug screen was positive for cannabinoids, which may also contribute to acute psychosis.

In this case, the patient was initiated on quetiapine XR. Among the case reports reviewed, 6 of 9 patients were discharged on an antipsychotic4,5,8-11; 4 were new starts, and 2 involved titrating the antipsychotic dose back up to the previously stable dose.6 There was 1 patient who was started on quetiapine 50 mg, but this was discontinued once symptoms resolved.5 In the case presented, the patient’s symptoms resolved within 9 days of oseltamivir completion and 8 days after starting quetiapine. In previously identified cases, symptoms resolution ranged from 1 to 30 days after oseltamivir discontinuation or completion.

Conclusion

The findings of this case contribute to the literature on oseltamivir-induced neuropsychiatric adverse effects. This case described a 22-year-old White male with a prior psychiatric history who presented with symptoms of psychosis after oseltamivir initiation. The resolution of symptoms occurred upon completion of the oseltamivir course and the initiation of quetiapine XR. Given the available literature and this case report, providers should be aware that oseltamivir may exacerbate symptoms of psychiatrically stable patients, and careful monitoring may be warranted. Depending on the severity of neuropsychiatric effects, discontinuation of oseltamivir and treatment with an antipsychotic may be warranted.
