EF(X) Orientations: A Parameterized Complexity Perspective
Sotiris Kanellopoulos, Edouard Nemery, Christos Pergaminelis, Minas Marios Sotiriou, Manolis Vasilakis

Abstract
The concept of fair orientations in graphs was introduced by 16 , naturally modeling fair division scenarios in which resources are only contested by neighbors. In this model, vertices represent agents and undirected edges represent goods; edges have to be oriented towards one of their endpoints, i.e., allocated to one of their adjacent agents. Although EFX orientations ( envy-free up to any good ) have been extensively studied in this setting, EF orientations ( envy-free ) remain unexplored. In this work, we initiate their study, mostly under the lens of parameterized complexity, presenting various tractable cases, hardness results, and parameterizations. Our results concern both simple graphs and multigraphs. Interestingly, many of our results transfer to EFX orientations, thus complementing and improving upon previous work; notably, we answer an open question regarding the structural parameterized complexity of the latter problem on graphs of polynomially-bounded valuations. We also show that EF orientations are tractable in cases in which EFX orientations are not, particularly for binary valuations. Lastly, we consider charity 12 in the orientation setting, establishing algorithms for finding the minimum amount of edges that have to be removed from a graph in order for EF(X) orientations to exist.

3.3
Parameterization by Number of Heavy Edges

Here we consider
EF Orientation
on simple graphs.
Observe that most of the hardness results of

3.2

Our main result is an algorithm with running time

where
k

by

We remark that our algorithm works even for binary-encoded weights on the edges.

The rough idea of our algorithm is the following.
As a first step we fix an orientation of all “heavy” edges, that is, edges of value at least
2
for some endpoint.
This leaves a residual instance in which every unoriented edge has value
1
for both endpoints.
To appropriately orient these remaining edges, we reduce to an equivalent instance of

This allows us, for each fixed orientation of the heavy edges, to decide in polynomial time
whether it can be extended to an envy-free orientation.
Finally, iterating over all possible orientations of the heavy edges (of which there are at most
2^{k}
)
suffices to determine whether the instance admits an envy-free orientation.

.

There is an algorithm
for
EF Orientation
on simple graphs running in time
2^{k}n^{{\cal O}(1)}
, where
k
is the number of edges with value at least
2
for at least one endpoint.

3.4
Parameterization by Treewidth plus Maximum Shared Weight

At this point, we have shown that
EF Orientation
remains computationally hard even when restricted to simple graphs of small vertex cover and symmetric valuations:

Theorem

11
shows weak NP-hardness for graphs of vertex cover
2
,
while for polynomially-bounded weights
Theorem

12
gives a
n^{o(\text{\rm vc}/\log\text{\rm vc})}
lower bound under the ETH.
A natural question would be to consider whether we can match the latter lower bound with an algorithm.
We answer this positively: using standard DP techniques, one can get an algorithm for
EF Orientation
running in time
W^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}n^{{\cal O}(1)}
,
where
W
denotes the maximum weight of the graph and
tw
its treewidth.
For polynomially-bounded weights, this algorithm matches up to a log-factor the aforementioned lower bound (see
Remark

16
).

Pushing this idea further, we observe that in fact similar techniques apply even for multigraphs with non-symmetric valuations,
under the caveat that in this setting
W
is a bound on the
sum of the weights of a set of parallel edges for one of its endpoints; observe that this is a straightforward generalization of the maximum weight considered in the previous setting.
We define this to be the
maximum shared weight
of an instance.
As a matter of fact, our main result in this section is to show that we can solve the (more general)
Min-Charity EF Orientation
problem in essentially the same running time.

Theorem
14

(
\bigstar
)
.

Given an instance
\mathcal{I}
of
Min-Charity EF Orientation
along with a nice tree decomposition of the input multigraph of width
tw
,
one can decide
\mathcal{I}
in time
W^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}(n+m)^{{\cal O}(1)}
,
where
W
denotes the maximum shared weight of
\mathcal{I}
and
n
the number of vertices.

Proof sketch.

Our algorithm is a standard DP over a nice tree decomposition of the input graph.
For a node
t
of the given nice tree decomposition, let
G_{t}
denote the corresponding subgraph,
while
B_{t}
denotes the set of vertices appearing in the bag of node
t
.
For every node
t
of the tree decomposition, we keep track of whether there exists a partial orientation of
G_{t}

(excluding the edges among vertices of
B_{t}
)
that guarantees that the vertices of
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
do not envy any vertex of
V(G_{t})
,
while also keeping track of the number of unoriented edges of the partial orientation
as well as the following information for each of the at most
\text{\rm tw}+1
vertices in
B_{t}
:

•

its
revenue
due to edges shared with vertices in
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
,

•

its
demand
so that it does not envy any vertex of
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
.

Observe that both the revenue and the demand of a vertex in
B_{t}
can be upper-bounded by
W
,
as a vertex of revenue
W
cannot envy any other vertex, while a vertex may envy another only up to a value of at most
W
.
Lastly, by
3
we can assume that there are no edges of valuation
0
for both endpoints,
thus there are at most
2W
edges shared between any two vertices, implying that
the number of unoriented edges is at most
2W\cdot\binom{n}{2}
.
Consequently, the total running time is
W^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}(n+m)^{{\cal O}(1)}
.
∎

We obtain the following corollary for polynomially bounded weights, in which case
W=(n+m)^{{\cal O}(1)}
.

Corollary 15
.

There is an algorithm running in time
(n+m)^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}
for
Min-Charity EF Orientation
with polynomially-bounded weights.

Remark 16
.

The algorithm of
Corollary

15
nearly matches the lower bound of
n^{o(\text{\rm vc}/\log\text{\rm vc})}
in
Theorem

12
, up to a log-factor.
Recall that the treewidth of a graph is upper-bounded by its vertex cover number and that for simple graphs,
m={\cal O}(n^{2})
holds.

The following observations will be useful throughout this section. Recall the definition of
strong
envy from
Definition

.

Observation 17
.

An agent that has only received one good is not strongly envied by any agent.

Observation 18
.

For agents
i,j
, if
i
does not strongly envy
j
and
j
has received a good
g
s.t.
v_{i}(g)=0
, then
i
does not envy
j
.

4.1
From EF to EFX Orientations

It was first proven in
16
that
EFX Orientation
is NP-complete even with symmetric valuations;
this hardness persists even for simple graphs of vertex cover number
8
or for multigraphs consisting of
10
vertices,
where the problem was proven to be weakly NP-complete
17
.
In a follow-up work,
1
show NP-completeness even for symmetric instances on bipartite multigraphs with
8
vertices.
Here we present a reduction from
EF Orientation
to
EFX Orientation
that, along with
Section

3.2
,
allows us to complement and improve upon the aforementioned results. See
Figure

4
for an overview of our reduction.

EF Orientation
to
EFX Orientation
in

19
. If
e^{*}

, then all other edges incident to

d_{1}

is oriented towards

Theorem
19

(
\bigstar
)
.

There is a polynomial-time, logarithmic-space reduction from
EF Orientation
to
EFX Orientation
that only adds 2 vertices
and edges of symmetric binary valuation.
The reduction preserves simplicity.

Combining
Theorem

19
with the hardness results in
Section

3.2
leads to
Corollaries

20
and
21
.

Corollary 20
.

EFX Orientation
is weakly NP-complete even restricted to

•

simple symmetric instances of vertex cover number
4
,

•

symmetric instances with
4
vertices.

Corollary 21
.

EFX Orientation
is W[1]-hard parameterized by vertex cover number,
XNLP-hard parameterized by pathwidth,
and XALP-hard parameterized by treewidth,
even restricted to simple symmetric instances with polynomially-bounded weights.
Furthermore, for such instances,
EFX Orientation
does not admit a
n^{o(\text{\rm vc}/\log\text{\rm vc})}
algorithm under the ETH,
where

We now compare
Corollaries

20
and
21
with existing literature.

Corollary

20
improves upon the constants 8 and 10 from
17
that we mention at the start of the subsection.
As for
Corollary

, it presents the first hardness results for instances with polynomially-bounded weights and small vertex cover,
partially answering an open question of
17
regarding the hardness of
EFX Orientation
in this setting.
In the following subsection, we complement the lower bound from
Corollary

21
with an algorithm.

4.2
Parameterization by Treewidth plus Maximum Shared Weight

Moving on, we consider the
EFX Orientation
problem on graphs of small treewidth.
As previously mentioned, if one allows binary-encoded weights, then the problem remains
NP-hard even for simple graphs of constant vertex cover, or multigraphs of constant number
of vertices. Consequently, in the following we consider the case where all weights are polynomially-bounded;
this is exactly the regime for which
17
have explicitly asked whether
the structure of the input graph may prove algorithmically useful.
For this case,
Corollary

21
already gives a
n^{o(\text{\rm vc}/\log\text{\rm vc})}
lower bound under the ETH even on
simple graphs. Our main contribution in this section is to complement this lower bound with a DP algorithm parameterized by treewidth
plus maximum shared weight, in a similar fashion as in
Theorem

14
;
for polynomially-bounded weights where
W=(n+m)^{{\cal O}(1)}
this results into an algorithm of running time
(n+m)^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}
.
Again, our algorithm works even for the more general
Min-Charity EFX Orientation
problem, and for multigraphs with non-identical valuations.

We first state the following observation.

Observation 22
.

Let an instance of
EFX Orientation
where vertices
i,j
share more than one edges of weight
0
for both endpoints.
Then, removing all but one of those edges results into an equivalent instance.

To see why
22
holds, notice that any EFX orientation that does not orient all such edges to a
single
endpoint,
can be modified to an EFX orientation that does.

Theorem
23

(
\bigstar
)
.

Given an instance
\mathcal{I}
of
Min-Charity EFX Orientation
along with a nice tree decomposition of the input multigraph of width
tw
,
one can decide
\mathcal{I}
in time
W^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}(n+m)^{{\cal O}(1)}
,
where
W
denotes the maximum shared weight of
\mathcal{I}
and
n
the number of agents.

Proof sketch.

The algorithm closely follows the one of
Theorem

14
.
For a node
t
of the given nice tree decomposition, let
G_{t}
denote the corresponding subgraph,
while
B_{t}
denotes the set of vertices appearing in the bag of node
t
.
Once again, for every node
t
of the tree decomposition, we keep track of whether there exists a partial orientation of
G_{t}

(excluding the edges among vertices of
B_{t}
)
that guarantees that the vertices of
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
do not strongly envy the vertices of
V(G_{t})
,
while also keeping track of the number of unoriented edges of the partial orientation
as well as the following information for each of the at most
\text{\rm tw}+1
vertices in
B_{t}
:

•

its
revenue
due to edges shared with vertices in
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
,
separately handling the case where a vertex has no received goods,

•

its
demand
so that it does not strongly envy any vertex of
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
,
separately handling the case where a vertex is non-strongly envied by a vertex of
V(G_{t})\setminus B_{t}
.

Notice that both the revenue and the demand of a vertex in
B_{t}
can be upper-bounded by
W
,
as a vertex with revenue
W
cannot envy any other vertex, while a vertex may envy another up to a value of at most
W
.
Lastly, by
22
we can assume that for every pair of vertices there is at most one edge of valuation
0

for both endpoints,
thus there are at most
2W+1
edges shared between them, implying that
the number of unoriented edges is at most
(2W+1)\cdot\binom{n}{2}
.
Consequently, the total running time is
W^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}(n+m)^{{\cal O}(1)}
.
∎

Just like in
Section

3.4
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 24
.

There is an algorithm running in time
(n+m)^{{\cal O}(\text{\rm tw})}
for
Min-Charity EFX Orientation
with polynomially-bounded weights.

Remark 25
.

The algorithm of
Corollary

24
nearly matches the lower bound of
n^{o(\text{\rm vc}/\log\text{\rm vc})}
in
Corollary

21
, up to a log-factor.

5
Conclusion

In this work we initiated the study of EF orientations and presented a variety of both positive and negative results regarding
their computational complexity. We additionally studied them under the perspective of parameterized complexity,
and showed how some of the results can even be transferred to the related
EFX Orientation
problem, improving upon previous work on the latter in the process.

As a direction for future work, we comment that
Theorem

13
generalizes
Theorem

6
, albeit only for simple graphs.
A natural question is thus whether we can develop such an algorithm that works for multigraphs as well, that is, whether
EF Orientation

admits an FPT algorithm parameterized by the number
k
of heavy edges running in time
2^{k}n^{{\cal O}(1)}
.

Another interesting direction would be to ask for regimes for which the complexity of EF and EFX
Orientation
differs.
We showed that one such case regards instances with binary valuations, while the two problems have similar behavior in case of
polynomially-bounded weights and small vertex cover/treewidth.
In which other (valuation or structural) regime does the complexity of the two problems differ?
