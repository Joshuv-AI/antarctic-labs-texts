Toward rational understanding of the hydrogen evolution polarization curves through multiscale simulations
Authors: Weiqiang Shou, Wanghui Zhao, Yonghua Liu, Tao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To properly understand complex heterogeneous electrocatalytic processes, it is essential to consider not only the microscopic electrochemical reaction mechanisms at electrodes but also the macroscopic mass transport effects, which are usually overlooked in current computational electrochemistry. Here, we develop a multiscale simulation framework that integrates grand canonical density functional theory calculations, microkinetic modeling, and a continuum transport model to successfully reproduce the experimental polarization curves of hydrogen evolution reaction on Au(111) across the entire pH range. Our simulations reveal how the potential-dependent evolution of local environments governs reaction mechanisms and behavior, identifying a dynamic balance between local pH increases and decreases in reaction barriers that controls characteristic current plateaus. By classifying polarization curves into five distinct regimes based on their governing factors, we provide comprehensive mechanistic insights into how mass transport influences polarization behavior. Our multiscale simulation framework not only adequately reproduces existing experimental results but also provides a basis for rational optimization of the microenvironment to achieve efficient electrochemical processes.

Introduction

Electrocatalysis has played a vital role in harnessing sustainable energy into solar fuels, where designing high-performance and low-cost electrocatalysts has long been a challenging yet appealing task1–3. Achieving rational catalyst design requires fundamental understanding of electrocatalytic mechanisms, which are typically governed by both intrinsic properties of catalysts and local reaction environments4–7. Currently, the state-of-the-art characterization techniques and spectroscopic measurements greatly benefit the mechanistic studies in electrocatalysis8–16, while the theoretical simulations could further push the understanding to a more microscopic boundary17–26. To understand better or even predict new catalysts for electrocatalysis, several advanced computational methods and models have been developed to accurately calculate the reaction energy profiles for different heterogeneous electrochemical processes, which incorporate influences of electrode potential, pH, solvation, and reaction kinetics27. Based on their fundamental importance, failing to adequately integrate mass transport effects into reaction mechanism investigation may create a significant gap between computational models and realistic electrocatalytic systems.

Specifically, a practical electrochemical reaction will not only involve the microscopic process at the electrode interface, i.e., the adsorption/desorption of reactants/products, bond breaking/formation, and proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET), but also the macroscopic mass transport phenomenon emerging from the collective behavior of numerous atoms and molecules, i.e., diffusion, migration, and convection. In principle, incorporating both microscopic and macroscopic factors into computational electrocatalysis is necessary to achieve a comprehensive understanding of experimental phenomena, but it is also significantly challenging. Currently, although continuum transport models have been employed to simulate mass transport phenomena and reaction environments in macroscopic electrochemical cells28–30, the unquantifiable uncertainties of reaction kinetics associated with traditional Tafel equation-based models still need to be properly addressed31,32. Thus, multiscale simulation is still one of the most promising methods to integrate microscale reaction mechanisms with macroscale transport effects and eventually achieve a complete modeling of the electrochemical cell4,7,33–36.

Indeed, Bell and coworkers reported a pioneering multiscale model that elegantly integrates microscopic reaction mechanisms with macroscopic mass transport phenomena in a device for CO2 electroreduction, thereby explaining how mass transport influences the atomistic reaction mechanisms37. Thereafter, Chan and coworkers achieved qualitative agreement with experimental results because only the diffusion effect was included in their mass transport model38. Then, Liu and coworkers incorporated both diffusion and convection effects into their transient kinetics simulation, enabling an improved reproduction of the experimental polarization behavior of hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) on the Pt(111) surface39. Wang and coworkers also reasonably predicted polarization curves of HER on the Pt(111) surface by building a bilayer explicit water model to mimic the proton transfer from bulk water to the interfacial water40. Close inspections revealed that each work has diverse implementations and varying understandings of mass transport factors in modeling the electrochemical process, making it challenging to conduct a fair comparison and extend the methods conveniently to other new systems. Thus, a simulation workflow with the merits of facile implementation, extensibility, and strict considerations of diffusion, migration, and convection in mass transport is still missing but highly desirable.

In this work, using HER on Au catalysts as the model reaction, we establish a multiscale simulation framework that couples grand canonical density functional theory (GC-DFT), microkinetic modeling (MKM), and continuum transport models, thereby illustrating the relationship between reaction mechanisms and mass transport processes at both microscopic and macroscopic scales. In this framework, the potential-dependent reaction free energies and activation energy barriers for each elementary step were calculated using the GC-DFT method. The reaction rates obtained from microkinetic modeling were then implemented as boundary conditions in the continuum transport model to simulate electrolyte mass transport and determine pH and H2 concentration profiles. Our theoretically simulated results on Au(111) and Pt(111) surfaces demonstrate good agreement with available experimental polarization curves of HER41, enabling us to unravel the origin of the characteristic current plateau. By classifying the polarization curve into five distinct regimes, we elucidate the intricate interplay between reaction kinetics and mass transport.

Results

Multiscale simulation framework

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, the HER on electrodes proceeds through different mechanisms depending on the pH, where the proton sources are typically hydronium ions (H3O+) in acidic HER but water in the alkaline medium. The continuous electrochemical reactions lead to reactant depletion and product accumulation at the electrode, resulting in deviations between the electrode’s local environment and the bulk solution. Figure 1b and Supplementary Figs. 1-2 illustrate the specific workflow and key components of our multiscale modeling approach. First, the potential-dependent reaction free energies and energy barriers for all elementary steps are calculated using the GC-DFT method, which are then incorporated into the Microkinetic Model (MKM) to obtain the equilibrium constants, the rate constants, surface coverages, and reaction rates (with the bulk pH and H₂ concentration as inputs). At this stage, the current density, excluding mass transport effects, can be explicitly calculated. Then, the obtained current density will serve as input for the continuum transport model that resolves the local pH and H₂ concentration profiles at the cathode interface. The specific definitions of bulk / local pH and the concentrations of other relevant species are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. Finally, the updated local concentrations are iteratively fed back into the MKM until the variations in both pH and H₂ concentration between successive iterations fall below 10−3, ensuring full convergence of the simulation. Notably, the well-established mechanisms of HER allow us to elucidate the intricate interplay between kinetics and mass transport. Although extending our established framework to electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (eCO2RR) and electrochemical N2 reduction reaction (eNRR) is possible, it will pose additional challenges due to the complex reaction mechanisms and microenvironments at the electrodes42 as well as the complex compositions in the electrolyte.

Reaction mechanisms and free energy diagram of HER on the Au(111) surface

In this work, we utilized the Au(111) surface as a model system for our multiscale simulations of the HER mechanism due to the availability of comprehensive experimental data41 for direct comparison. We initiated our mechanistic analysis with Au(111) for two key reasons. First, the high stability and uniform surface characteristics of Au closely align with idealized DFT computational models43–45, thereby significantly enhancing the reliability of direct comparisons between theoretical simulations and experimental measurements on the Au(111) surface. Second, Au has a relatively weaker binding strength with hydrogen and exhibits no detectable underpotential hydrogen deposition (HUPD) before reaching the hydrogen reversible potential46–55, which would provide additional mechanistic insights for HER.

As shown in Fig. 2a, both the Volmer and Heyrovsky steps exhibit higher energy barriers in alkaline conditions than in acidic conditions. This energetic difference rationalizes the sluggish HER kinetics observed experimentally under alkaline conditions56–58. When the effect of surface coverage is taken into account, we find that higher hydrogen coverage leads to increased energy barriers for both steps, primarily due to the weakened binding strength of hydrogen. This facilitates the reverse Volmer reaction while promoting the forward Heyrovsky step. As governed by both pH conditions and surface coverage, a similar trend was also found for the Volmer-Tafel pathway in Fig. 2b. Meanwhile, the dynamic processes of the Volmer, Heyrovsky, and Tafel pathways under both alkaline and acidic conditions are shown in Fig. 2c, d. Under alkaline conditions, the explicit solvation structures are represented by a four-water cluster (H8O4) with H2O serving as the proton source, where the adjacent H2O molecules would transfer a proton to the H2O molecule that directly serves as the proton source in the Volmer and Heyrovsky steps. Under the acidic conditions, an Eigen cation (H7O3+) with a hydronium ion works as the proton source.

Understanding the effects of pH on polarization curves

Although our multiscale simulations can appropriately reproduce the experimental polarization curves, they only reflect the macroscopic outcome of the interplay between elementary steps and local environmental effects. Thus, the specific contribution of each factor remains unclear. Taking pH effect as an example, the HER activity in Fig. 3b exhibits strong pH dependence at acidic conditions (pH = 1–4), where varying pH significantly alters the reaction kinetics. In contrast, the polarization behavior remains largely unaffected under alkaline conditions (pH = 5–13).

To elucidate this pH-dependent behavior, we simulated the relationship between local pH at the cathode surface and reaction rates across the whole pH range. As shown in Fig. 3c, there are three distinct regimes, i.e., a plateau region where pH(local) ≈ pH(bulk), a pH-jump regime with proton depletion, and a linear regime exhibiting gradual pH increase with current density. Meanwhile, Fig. 3c can provide mechanistic explanations of the distinct trends observed in the polarization curves in Fig. 3a.

More specifically, at pH(bulk) = 3 in Fig. 3c, the reaction proceeds slowly at low current densities (10−6 to 100 mA cm−2), allowing sufficient time for proton transfer from the bulk solution to the electrode surface. Consequently, the local environment maintains equilibrium with the bulk environment (pH(local) ≈ pH(bulk)). However, when the current density reaches 3.1 mA cm−², the reaction rate exceeds the proton diffusion rate, resulting in progressive proton depletion near the electrode surface. This triggers a rapid transition of the pH(local) from acidic (pH = 3) to alkaline (pH ≈ 11). Notably, this pH jump is accompanied by the appearance of a distinct current plateau in the polarization curve (where current density remains constant despite potential changes). This has been widely observed experimentally in mild acidic media, which were dominated by mass transport limitations41. Then, at higher current densities in Fig. 3c, pH(local) exhibits a linear dependence on the applied current density, ultimately converging to identical trajectories regardless of pH(bulk). The coincident behavior of the polarization curves across a broad bulk pH range (5–13) can be attributed to the combined influence of two key conditions established in this regime: the convergence of the local pH to a universally alkaline regime once currents exceed 10−1 mA cm−2, and the remarkable insensitivity of the HER kinetics to its precise value within that range59. Consequently, the corresponding HER polarization curves exhibit a high degree of consistency across the investigated potential window. Our systematic analysis demonstrates that the local environment becomes dramatically different from bulk conditions (from pH = 3 to pH = 14) as current density increases, which will significantly impact the microkinetic modeling results if the mass transport phenomenon hasn’t been considered.

Understanding mechanisms behind the variation trend of the polarization curves under acidic conditions

The polarization curves in Fig. 3b under acidic conditions consistently exhibit a characteristic current plateau where the current density remains constant despite increasing negative potential. Notably, Nagel and Wendler60 first reported this phenomenon, while subsequent experimental and theoretical studies have consistently observed this behavior and attributed it to proton transfer limitations38–41. However, the underlying reaction mechanisms behind this phenomenon remain poorly understood. Indeed, our multiscale simulations indicate that the current plateau can be attributed to a dynamic equilibrium between the potential-dependent decrease in the energy barrier of the Volmer step and the increase in local pH at increasingly negative potentials.

As shown in Fig. 4a, we calculated the potential-dependent energy barriers (Ga) of Volmer steps in both acidic (Volmer(acid)) and alkaline (Volmer(alkaline)) conditions at high H* coverage and pH(bulk) = 3. As a proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) process, the Volmer step exhibits a remarkable decrease in the energy barrier with the potential shifting negatively, and the local pH progresses through three distinct stages (Fig. 4a). More specifically, the local pH remains constant from 0 to −0.7 V vs SHE in Fig. 4a. In this respect, the continuous decrease in the energy barrier of the Volmer step with increasing potentials results in a substantial enhancement in current density. Starting from −0.7 V vs SHE, further negatively shifting the potential induces a rapid increase in local pH. However, the acidic Volmer step still serves as the sole source of H* (Fig. 4b). Although the decreasing proton concentration (reflected in an increased local pH) impedes reaction kinetics, this effect is precisely counterbalanced by the ongoing reduction in energy barrier for the acidic Volmer step, thereby resulting in a dynamic steady state that maintains a constant current density despite increasing potential. At potentials above −1.4 V vs SHE in Fig. 4b, the increase in local pH becomes gentle after the sharp jump from acid to alkaline. Then, the contribution of the acidic Volmer step decreases rapidly under such severely proton-depleted conditions. When the potential is higher than −1.8 V vs SHE, the alkaline Volmer step emerges as the exclusive mechanism for H* formation.

Furthermore, we investigated the variation of the rate-determining step across different potential ranges by examining the dependence of Tafel slopes on local pH and applied potential at bulk pH = 3. As shown in Fig. 4c, the reaction kinetics are governed by three distinct factors during this process. At the low potential range, our simulated Tafel slope of 69.4 mV dec−1 is very close to the experimental value of 68 mV dec−1 as measured by Kahyarian and coworkers using Au RDE in 0.1 M NaClO4 electrolyte49, thereby confirming the Tafel step as the rate-determining step under these conditions46. In the current plateau region from −0.7 V to −1.4 V vs SHE, the Tafel slope approaches ∞ mV dec−1, indicating that the reaction is no longer limited by any PCET step but by proton transfer limitations as previously analyzed. At the high potential regime, the simulated Tafel slope of 141.5 mV dec−1 also agrees well with the experimental value of 141 mV dec−1 obtained in 0.1 M H2SO4 solution, demonstrating that the reaction becomes limited by the alkaline Volmer step52. In this respect, the water dissociation process under alkaline conditions becomes the predominant pathway for H* formation in this potential regime, as shown in Fig. 4b. Notably, between the low-potential region and the vertical plateau, a transition zone exists that exhibits intermediate Tafel slopes (ranging from 69.4 to ∞ mV dec−1), which can be attributed to mixed control by both electrochemical reaction kinetics and proton transfer. This mixed control behavior is similarly observed in the region between the vertical plateau and the high-potential Tafel line.

Building upon our systematic investigation of the potential-dependent behavior at a bulk pH of 3, which is a representative acidic condition, we have elucidated the underlying mechanistic origins of the distinct polarization curve trends. Through detailed Tafel slope analysis in Fig. 4c, we identify five characteristic potential regimes in the acidic polarization curves, each governed by different rate-limiting factors, as comprehensively mapped in Fig. 4d.

More specifically, in the low-potential regime with pH(local) ≈ pH(bulk), the reaction kinetics are exclusively limited by the sluggish Tafel step and yield negligible current density. As the potential shifts negatively, a measurable current density emerges under the mixed control of both PCET kinetics and mass transport effects, as the electrode surface environment begins to diverge from the bulk solution composition. As the potential shifts further negatively, the polarization curve exhibits a characteristic plateau, limited only by mass transport, where increasing the potential fails to enhance the current density. Upon entering the high-potential regime, the local environment stabilizes in an alkaline state distinct from the acidic bulk solution, and the local pH gradually increases with potential. As illustrated in Fig. 4b, this regime features competitive interplay between alkaline and acidic Volmer pathways, exhibiting mixed control by both PCET kinetics and mass transport, analogous to previous transitional regions. Ultimately, with a further increase in negative potential and local pH, the alkaline Volmer step emerges as the exclusive pathway for H* formation, marking the transition to a final regime governed purely by PCET kinetics. Apparently, except when the electrocatalytic process is solely controlled by the PCET process (as indicated by the red and blue dashed lines in Fig. 4d), a comprehensive understanding of catalytic processes necessitates explicit consideration of mass transport effects in theoretical modeling.

Notably, the choice of pH = 3 for our detailed analysis of polarization behavior under acidic conditions was guided by fundamental electrochemical considerations across the pH range. In strongly acidic media, the high bulk concentration of H3O⁺ leads to rapid mass transport, resulting in polarization curves dominated by proton-coupled electron transfer kinetics without a well-defined diffusion-limited current plateau. In alkaline and neutral environments, the limited availability of H3O⁺ shifts the proton source to water molecules, maintaining the reaction under kinetic control throughout the potential range. In contrast, dilute acid conditions at pH = 3 provide a unique window where different controlling regimes are prominently exhibited within experimentally accessible current densities. This behavior is well-documented in systematic studies of HER polarization curves61. Our multiscale framework builds upon these experimental observations by providing atomistic-scale insights into the mechanisms governing these transitions, making pH = 3 an ideal case for demonstrating how our model deconvolutes the origins of different polarization curve segments.

Fine-tuning and optimizations of mass transport

Till now, we have systematically examined and illustrated the physical origins of the pH-dependent polarization behaviors of HER on the Au(111) surface. However, experimental results in Fig. 5a reveal that varying catalysts significantly alter the polarization behavior in both PCET and mixed-control regions, but do not influence the diffusion-limited current density. Therefore, we further investigated how to alleviate the diffusion limitations via tuning mass transport. As clearly shown in Fig. 5b, the increase in rotation rate will profoundly improve the diffusion-limited current density. To further elucidate how rotation influences mass transport and consequently affects reaction kinetics, we analyzed the relationship between rotation rate and both local pH and convective flux in the boundary layer (pH(bulk) = 3, j = 3.5 mA cm−2). As shown in Fig. 5d, our simulated results indicate that the boundary layer maintains an alkaline local pH environment at lower rotation rates (ω = 400-1600 rpm), which are insufficient to adequately alleviate the diffusion limitations. However, when the rotation rate exceeds 1600 rpm, the local pH progressively decreases to 3–4, approaching the bulk pH. Notably, a further increase in the rotation rate from 2500 to 6400 rpm does not produce significant additional pH variation. Thus, our simulations indicate that 2500 rpm essentially represents the limit for rotation-enhanced mass transport. Further analysis of the convective flux (Supplementary Fig. 10) reveals that while the convective flux increases with distance from the electrode surface at a constant rotation rate, enhanced rotation does not directly affect mass transport at the electrode interface. Instead, it improves mass transport at the boundary layer, thereby indirectly modifying the interfacial reaction environment. All these findings collectively demonstrate that rotation rates within a specific range (ω = 1600–2500 rpm) can optimize local reaction conditions to promote the reaction. However, since rotation only affects the convective component of mass transport, its ideal performance is ultimately limited to achieving pH(local) = pH(bulk), with no capacity for further enhancement of local pH.

Discussion

In summary, we developed a multiscale simulation framework that integrates grand canonical density functional theory (GC-DFT) calculations, microkinetic modeling (MKM), and a continuum transport model to simulate the complex electrochemical reactions. Our simulations successfully reproduced the experimentally measured HER performances on Au electrocatalysts across the entire pH range in a rotating disk electrode (RDE) system, while elucidating the detailed influence of pH and mass transport on the polarization curves. Mechanism-wise, our analysis demonstrated that the accelerated proton consumption rate, induced by increasing cathodic current density, resulted in a significantly higher local pH at the cathode than the bulk pH under acidic conditions. This substantially influenced the MKM simulation results. In contrast, the coincident polarization behaviors across neutral to alkaline conditions arise from the corresponding local pH convergence to a universal alkaline regime where HER kinetics exhibit minimal pH dependence. We also identify a dynamic balance between the local pH increase and the decrease in reaction energy barrier near electrodes, which elegantly explains the physical origin of the inevitable formation of characteristic current plateaus in experimental polarization curves. By classifying polarization curves into five distinct regimes based on their governing factors, we provide comprehensive mechanistic insights into how mass transport influences polarization behavior and tunes the catalytic performances of electrocatalysts. Finally, we discussed the role of rotation in alleviating diffusion limitations, where a rotation rate of 1600–2500 rpm is sufficient to promote the reaction by tuning mass transport. While our framework can, in principle, be extended to more complex reactions such as eCO₂RR and eNRR, such extensions pose additional challenges due to their multistep reaction networks, more complex interfacial environments, and varying electrolyte compositions. More generally, we anticipate that our multiscale simulation framework provides a systemmatic approach for analyzing other electrocatalytic reactions involving mass transport phenomena, enabling a deeper understanding of local environment variations and potential-dependent mechanistic evolution.

Methods

Details of potential-dependent DFT calculations

All DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)63 as implemented in the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE)64. The Bayesian error estimation functional was selected with van der Waals correction (BEEF-vdW)65 for exchange-correlation effect. The projector augmented wave (PAW)66 method was employed to treat electron-ion interactions, with a plane-wave basis set and an energy cutoff of 400 eV for geometry optimization. A 3-layer p(3×3) supercell was employed to model the Au(111) surface, and the bottom two atomic layers of the model were fixed to mimic the bulk catalyst. The Au(111) unit cell with an area of 6.90 × 10−19 m2 contains 9 active sites, yielding an active site concentration of 2.16 × 10−5 mol m−2.

For the electrolyte, a continuum water model was applied to the bulk phase, with the system neutralized using the linear Poisson-Boltzmann implicit solvation model in VASPsol67. The electrolyte was modeled with a relative permittivity of ϵb = 78.4 and a Debye length of λD = 3.0 Å (benchmarked as in Supplementary Fig. 11). A 15 Å vacuum space was introduced along the z-axis to eliminate periodic image interactions. To better describe the effect of hydrogen bonds on the adsorbates, our model combines explicit and implicit water molecules. The hydronium ion is explicitly solvated by three water molecules to form an eigen cation (H7O3+), thereby modeling acidic conditions through H3O+ as the proton source, while alkaline conditions are modeled using a four-water cluster (H8O4) with H2O as the proton source. The hybrid explicit/implicit solvation model builds upon established computational electrochemistry approaches67–69, combining implicit electrolyte screening with explicit water molecules to capture essential hydrogen bonding effects. The methodology is supported by previous benchmarks confirming its reliability for interfacial simulations70,71, providing an ideal balance between accuracy and efficiency for investigating multiscale electrochemical phenomena. While these static calculations cannot fully capture the dynamic hydrogen bond fluctuations and presolvation effects highlighted in recent studies72, the modest energetic variations suggest that our primary conclusions regarding multiscale framework validation and key polarization behaviors analysis remain valid. We emphasize that incorporating such dynamic solvation effects represents an important direction for future methodological development in electrochemical interface modeling. Structural optimizations converged at forces below 0.05 eV Å−1 and energy changes below 10−4 eV, with Brillouin zone sampling using a 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh. Transition states were located using the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method73 with 8 images. Vibrational frequency analysis was employed to calculate zero-point energies (ZPE) and entropic contributions to the free energy at 298.15 K, considering exclusively the 3 N degrees of freedom associated with the directly reacting adsorbates.

For the constant electrode potential (CEP) modeling, we used the fully converged constant-potential (FCP) algorithm to perform grand canonical ensemble modeling with density functional theory (DFT) calculations as developed by Xiao74. The system’s electron count was modulated to control the Fermi energy, maintaining both a fixed work function and electrode potential to achieve the target state. To bridge constant-charge and constant-potential free energies, we performed quadratic potential-dependent fitting for all reaction species, which will be shown in Supplementary Tables 5 and 6. This approach quantitatively relates the applied potential to both the reaction free energy (ΔG(U)) and the activation energy barrier (Ga(U)).

Multiscale simulations

Supplementary Fig. 2 presents our multiscale simulation framework, which integrates the continuum transport model, microkinetic model, and GC-DFT. The simulation protocol initiates with the applied cathode potential (vs. SHE) as the primary input. GC-DFT calculations yield key kinetic and thermodynamic parameters, including activation barriers and reaction free energies for elementary steps. These parameters directly inform the rate constants (k), equilibrium constants (K), coverage (θ), and reaction rate (r) for all considered surface processes. The concentration of H2 (cH2) and pH are initialized using their bulk values. The MKM parameterized with GC-DFT-derived energetics, then computes the potential-dependent partial current densities for all products. These current densities are finally fed into the continuum transport model, which solves the coupled mass transport effects to obtain the local concentrations of H₂ and pH at the cathode-electrolyte interface. This MKM-transport coupling typically converges in fewer than four iterations in strongly acidic/alkaline conditions, but requires at least 20 iterations in dilute acid conditions (pH 3-5) due to the sharp local pH changes near the diffusion-limited region. Convergence was achieved when the relative change in local pH and H₂ concentration fell below 10−3.
