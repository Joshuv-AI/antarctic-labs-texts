Prevalence and attributable risk of depressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students: the role of maternal mental health and other risk factors
Authors: Lin Yang, Minggang Jiang, Jing Zhang, Li Xu, Xiaobin Zhang, Yan Zhang, Haidong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students have become significant public health concerns; however, comprehensive studies examining their prevalences and associated factors (particularly regarding the influence of maternal mental health) are limited.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 7850 secondary school students. The Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, and Perceived Social Support Scale questionnaires were used to assess depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and social support, respectively.ResultsThe self-reported prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms were observed to be 11.6% and 18.1%, respectively, among secondary school students. Multivariable analysis revealed significant risk factors, including female sex, suburban region, and maternal depressive and anxiety symptoms (all P < 0.001), along with parental discord, academic stress, insufficient physical activity, excessive screen time, and moderate household income (all P < 0.05). Population attributable risk (PAR) analysis revealed that academic pressure accounted for the greatest contribution to both depressive (PAR%=34.5%, 95% CI: 23.6–47.8%) and anxiety symptoms (PAR%=45.8%, 95% CI: 36.3–58.1%). Suburban residence (33.2% for depression, 45.0% for anxiety), insufficient physical activity (26.0% for depression), and excessive screen time (25.4% for depression, 21.0% for anxiety) were substantial contributors. Maternal anxiety symptoms explained 20.4% (95% CI: 15.7–25.7%) of the prevalence of depressive symptoms and 23.4% (95% CI: 18.8–28.6%) of the prevalence of anxiety, whereas maternal depressive symptoms contributed 12.9% (95% CI: 8.7–18.8%) to student depression despite demonstrating the strongest individual-level association (aOR = 5.497). Social support demonstrated a protective effect against both conditions (P < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings highlight the considerable prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students and underscore the significant role of maternal mental health and other modifiable risk factors.Clinical trialNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08214-7.Keywords: Adolescent mental health, Depressive symptoms, Anxiety symptoms, Maternal mental health, Risk factors, Social support

Introduction

Depressive and anxiety symptoms in adolescents have emerged as significant public health challenges worldwide [1]. Evidence has indicated that mental health issues are prevalent among adolescents, with depressive and anxiety symptoms being the most common manifestations [2]. This scenario is particularly evident among secondary school students, with studies across different countries, regions, and time periods reporting prevalence rates of depressive or anxiety symptoms ranging from 3.1% to 34% [3–6]. These mental health concerns not only impair academic performance and social functioning but may also lead to more severe consequences, including self-injury behaviors and suicidal ideation [7, 8]. The prevalence of mental health issues among adolescents has demonstrated an increasing trend in recent years, which is attributed to multiple factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic, social transformations, and academic pressure; moreover, this phenomenon has attracted substantial attention from researchers and practitioners alike [9–11].

The development of depressive and anxiety symptoms in adolescents is influenced by multiple risk factors, including individual, familial, and environmental dimensions [12–14]. Among these factors, family-related factors play a crucial role in adolescent mental health development [13], with maternal mental health status being particularly significant in relation to the psychological well-being of children [15]. Studies have demonstrated that maternal depressive and anxiety symptoms may influence the mental health development of children through various pathways, including genetic predispositions, parenting practices, and family environments [16, 17]. Additionally, factors such as urban-rural disparities, academic pressure, insufficient physical activity, and excessive screen time have been identified as potential risk factors for mental health problems among adolescents [9, 18]. Although existing research has examined the relationship between maternal and adolescent mental health, more comprehensive studies that consider the interactive effects of multiple risk factors are needed.

Social support serves as a crucial protective factor in preventing and alleviating depressive and anxiety symptoms among adolescents [19]. This protective effect operates through multiple channels [20–22], including the following family, peer, and school contexts: emotional support and understanding from family members, which enhance adolescents’ psychological resilience; positive peer relationships, which contribute to the development of social competence and self-identity; and teacher guidance, which facilitates better management of academic challenges. These supportive resources not only directly improve adolescents’ mental health status but also buffer against the adverse effects of various risk factors.

Based on the abovementioned analysis, the following hypotheses were proposed for this study: (1) high levels of self-reported prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms are observed among middle school students; (2) maternal mental health status, family environment, academic stress, and lifestyle factors are associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms in middle school students; and (3) adequate social support is associated with lower risks of depressive and anxiety symptoms. Accordingly, the aim of this study was to investigate the prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among middle school students and analyze their associated factors. Specifically, this study (1) assessed the prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among middle school students and (2) examined the influences of different risk factors, including maternal mental health status, the family environment, academic stress, and lifestyle factors, as well as the role of social support on depressive and anxiety symptoms among middle school students. To guide evidence-based intervention planning, we employed population attributable risk percentage (PAR%) analysis. PAR% estimates the proportion of cases that are potentially preventable if a risk factor is eliminated, thus accounting for both effect size and population prevalence and thereby enabling prioritization of intervention targets based on the population-level impact. In cross-sectional designs, the PAR% indicates associations and intervention priorities but cannot establish causal relationships.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted from October to December 2024 in Lianyungang City, Jiangsu Province, China. The study area encompassed three districts and one county of the city. Using random sampling, we recruited students from grades 7–9 (junior high school) and grades 10–12 (senior high school), along with their mothers. Data were collected through online questionnaires by using the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/app/survey.aspx) and WeChat (Tencent Inc., China). Prior to the survey, all of the participants (including students, teachers, and parents) were provided with detailed information about the study objectives, purposes, and procedures. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. The collected data were anonymized to protect the privacy of the participants. To ensure objectivity, neither the teachers nor the parents had access to the students’ responses. The inclusion criteria required that both students and their primary caregivers (mothers) fully completed the questionnaires. During data screening, 164 participants (2.05%) were excluded because of incomplete questionnaire responses. The final sample consisted of 7,850 student-mother pairs (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Lianyungang. Online informed consent was obtained from all of the parents before the commencement of the survey.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics included students’ age, sex, grade level, school location (urban/suburban), family structure (single parent: yes/no), and self-perceived parental relationship (harmonious/moderate/troubled). Additional collected information included self-perceived academic stress (high/moderate/low), weekly physical activity duration (< 2 h/2–4 h/>4 h), and weekly screen time (< 2 h/2–4 h/>4 h). Annual household income was categorized based on the Statistical Bulletin of National Economic and Social Development of Lianyungang City (2023); specifically, less than 100,000 RMB was defined as “moderate”, 100,000–200,000 RMB was defined as “good”, and above 200,000 RMB was defined as “excellent”. Academic stress was assessed by asking the following question: “How do you perceive your current level of academic stress?” Academic stress levels were defined as (1) low (minimal academic pressure with effective workload management), (2) moderate (manageable academic demands with intermittent strain), and (3) high (excessive academic burden with persistent difficulty with coping). The parental relationship was measured by asking the following question: “How would you describe the relationship between your parents?” The parental relationship categories were defined as (1) harmonious (parents interact positively with rare disagreements), (2) moderate (parents have stable interactions with intermittent tensions), and (3) conflicted (parents demonstrate frequent discord and ongoing relational strain). Physical activity was measured by asking the following question: “In the past week, how much total time did you spend engaging in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity?” Screen time was measured by asking the following question: “In the past week, how much total time did you spend using electronic screen devices for leisure purposes (excluding educational use)?”

Depression symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed by using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), which is a nine-item scale measuring reported depression-related symptoms experienced over the previous 2 weeks [23]. The scale evaluates symptoms such as depressed mood, anhedonia, sleep disturbance, fatigue, appetite changes, feelings of worthlessness, concentration difficulties, psychomotor agitation or retardation, and suicidal ideation. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 (0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, and 3 = nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 0 to 27. Based on validation studies performed in Chinese populations, a cutoff score of 10 was used to identify clinically significant depressive symptoms [24]. The severity of depression was categorized as minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderately severe (15–19), and severe (20–27). The PHQ-9 has demonstrated psychometric properties in both Chinese students and the general population, with good reliability and validity being observed [25, 26].

Anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were assessed by using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, which comprises seven items measuring anxiety-related symptoms experienced over the previous 2 weeks [27]. The scale assesses symptoms such as feeling nervous or anxious, difficulty controlling worry, worrying too much about different things, experiencing difficulty with relaxing, restlessness, irritability, and feeling afraid that something awful might happen. Each item is scored from 0 to 3 (0 = not at all, 1 = several days, 2 = more than half the days, and 3 = nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Anxiety severity was categorized as minimal (0–4), mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), and severe (15–21). Based on validation studies performed in Chinese populations, a cutoff score of 7 was adopted to identify clinically significant anxiety symptoms [28]. The GAD-7 has demonstrated psychometric properties in both Chinese students and the general population, with good reliability and validity being reported [25, 29].

Perceived social support

Social support was measured by using the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS), which is a 12-item instrument assessing perceived support from three domains, including family, friends, and significant others [30]. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = very strongly disagree to 7 = very strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 12 to 84. Higher scores indicate greater levels of perceived social support. In this study, we used the total score as a comprehensive measure of perceived social support. The PSSS has demonstrated good psychometric properties in Chinese populations, with strong internal consistency and validity being observed [31].

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated by using G*Power 3.1 software. Based on binary logistic regression analysis with a two-tailed test, an α error probability of 0.05, a statistical power (1-β) of 0.80, an odds ratio (OR) of 1.5, and a baseline event rate of 0.30, the minimum required sample size was 721 participants. A total of 8,014 participants were recruited; after incomplete or invalid questionnaires were excluded, 7,850 valid responses were included for analysis, thus yielding a response rate of 97.95%. Statistical analyses were performed by using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were compared by using chi-square tests. Maternal PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were analyzed as continuous variables in the correlation and regression analyses and as dichotomized variables (using the cutoff values described in the Measures section) for calculating exposure rates in the PAR% analyses. The prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among students were compared by using chi-square tests. Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate potential factors associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms (present = 1, absent = 0), with the results being expressed as ORs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To account for multiple testing of eight primary risk factor categories, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction separately for each outcome (involving depressive and anxiety symptoms), with a significance threshold of 0.05 being utilized. FDR-adjusted q values were calculated for all of the primary risk factors. We also calculated the PAR% for major risk factors. PAR% was computed by using the following formula: PAR% = [P(OR-1)]/[P(OR-1) + 1] × 100%, where P represents the exposure rate of the risk factor in the population. ORs and 95% CIs were estimated by using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations, percentile method) to account for sampling variability. The 95% CIs for the PAR% were derived from the bootstrap CIs of the adjusted ORs. The 95% CIs were calculated by using the coefficients of the logistic regression model and their standard errors. Model validation included discrimination assessment (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, or AUC), goodness-of-fit (Hosmer-Lemeshow test), multicollinearity assessment (variance inflation factors, VIF < 5 indicating an acceptable level), and influence diagnostics (Cook’s distance and standardized residuals). All of the statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 7,850 secondary school students aged 12–19 years (mean age: 14.43 ± 1.69 years) were enrolled in the study. The sample included 4,153 males (52.9%) and 3,697 females (47.1%), with 5,639 (71.8%) junior high school students and 2,211 (28.2%) senior high school students. Additionally, 4,793 (61.1%) participants originated from urban areas, and 3,057 (38.9%) were from suburban areas.

As shown in Table 1, there was no significant difference observed in age or educational level between students with and without depressive symptoms (all P > 0.05). However, significant differences were observed in maternal PHQ-9 scores (t = 39.695, P < 0.001), maternal GAD-7 scores (t = 39.224, P < 0.001), and PSSS scores (t=-35.895, P < 0.001) between the two groups. Significant differences were also observed in self-perceived parental relationships (χ2 = 539.572, P < 0.001), self-perceived academic stress (χ2 = 519.494, P < 0.001), weekly physical activity duration (χ2 = 65.483, P < 0.001), weekly screen time (χ2 = 167.259, P < 0.001), and annual household income (χ2 = 163.976, P < 0.001) between the two groups (all P < 0.001). Among students with depressive symptoms, the proportion of single-parent families was significantly greater than that among students without depressive symptoms (16.2% vs. 9.3%, χ2 = 41.779, P < 0.001). Furthermore, in the depressive symptoms group, females were more prevalent than males (59.9% vs. 40.1%, χ2 = 68.117, P < 0.001), and suburban students outnumbered the urban students (54.9% vs. 45.1%, χ2 = 110.284, P < 0.001).

Significant differences were observed between students with and without anxiety symptoms across multiple variables, including age (t = 4.623, P < 0.001), maternal PHQ-9 score (t = 30.793, P < 0.001), maternal GAD-7 score (t = 42.507, P < 0.001), PSSS score (t=-36.373, P < 0.001), self-perceived parental relationship (χ2 = 517.635, P < 0.001), single-parent status (χ2 = 47.902, P < 0.001), self-perceived academic stress (χ2 = 569.376, P < 0.001), weekly physical activity duration (χ2 = 68.055, P < 0.001), weekly screen time (χ2 = 173.044, P < 0.001), and annual household income (χ2 = 103.688, P < 0.001). Prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among students

The self-reported prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students were 11.6% and 18.1%, respectively. In terms of symptom severity, the proportions of students with mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depressive symptoms were 23.5%, 7.0%, 3.1%, and 1.6%, respectively. For anxiety symptoms, the proportions of students with mild, moderate, and severe symptoms were 18.7%, 4.7%, and 3.0%, respectively. The mean scores for each severity level are presented in Table 2.

Association analyses of the prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among students

Univariate analysis revealed that both depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms were significantly associated with sex, region, maternal PHQ-9 scores, GAD-7 scores, PSSS scores, self-perceived parental relationships, single-parent status, self-perceived academic stress, weekly physical activity duration, weekly screen time, and annual household income (all P < 0.05). Additionally, age and educational level were significantly associated with anxiety symptoms (P < 0.05) but not with depressive symptoms (P > 0.05).

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed on the variables exhibiting statistical significance. Age was included as a covariate in all of the models to adjust for potential confounding effects (regardless of its univariate significance). The results demonstrated that female sex (compared with male sex) and living in suburban areas (compared with urban areas) were risk factors for depressive symptoms (B = 0.476, P < 0.001, OR = 1.609, 95% CI: 1.342–1.929; B = 0.599, P < 0.001, OR = 1.820, 95% CI: 1.511–2.193, respectively). With respect to family factors, higher maternal PHQ-9 scores (B = 0.191, P < 0.001, OR = 1.210, 95% CI: 1.175–1.247) and maternal GAD-7 scores (B = 0.126, P < 0.001, OR = 1.134, 95% CI: 1.101–1.167) were identified as risk factors. Compared with harmonious parental relationships, both moderate (B = 0.546, P < 0.001, OR = 1.726, 95% CI: 1.422–2.095) and troubled (B = 1.171, P < 0.001, OR = 3.225, 95% CI: 2.193–4.741) relationships increased the risk of depressive symptoms, whereas single-parent status demonstrated no significant association (P = 0.127). The PSSS score emerged as a protective factor against depressive symptoms (B=-0.061, P < 0.001, OR = 0.941, 95% CI: 0.935–0.947). Moreover, compared with low and moderate levels, high levels of self-perceived academic stress (B = 1.223, P < 0.001, OR = 3.399, 95% CI: 2.288–5.048) posed a greater risk. Physical activity of less than 4 h per week (B = 0.586, P < 0.001, OR = 1.796, 95% CI: 1.378–2.341) and screen time exceeding 2 h per week (B = 0.415, P < 0.001, OR = 1.514, 95% CI: 1.216–1.885) were identified as risk factors. In terms of economic factors, compared with good and excellent income levels, a moderate annual household income level (B = 0.674, P = 0.004, OR = 1.963, 95% CI: 1.240–3.107) increased the risk of depressive symptoms. Age was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms (B= -0.044, OR = 0.957, 95% CI: 0.906–1.011, P = 0.118).

In terms of anxiety symptoms, binary logistic regression revealed that female sex (compared with male sex, B = 0.289, P < 0.001, OR = 1.335, 95% CI: 1.152–1.547), living in suburban areas (compared with urban areas, B = 0.966, P < 0.001, OR = 2.627, 95% CI: 2.246–3.072), and maternal GAD-7 scores (B = 0.261, P < 0.001, OR = 1.298, 95% CI: 1.261–1.336) were risk factors for anxiety symptoms among students, whereas the PSSS score emerged as a protective factor (B=-0.056, P < 0.001, OR = 0.946, 95% CI: 0.941–0.951). Compared with harmonious parental relationships, both moderate (B = 0.615, P < 0.001, OR = 1.850, 95% CI: 1.574–2.174) and troubled (B = 0.890, P < 0.001, OR = 2.436, 95% CI: 1.708–3.472) relationships increased the risk of anxiety symptoms. Regarding academic stress, high levels of self-perceived academic stress (B = 1.611, P < 0.001, OR = 5.006, 95% CI: 3.525–7.109) posed a greater risk compared with low and moderate levels. Physical activity of less than 2 h per week (compared with more than 4 h, B = 0.359, P = 0.001, OR = 1.432, 95% CI: 1.162–1.765) and screen time exceeding 2 h per week (B = 0.360, P < 0.001, OR = 1.433, 95% CI: 1.203–1.708) were identified as risk factors. The analysis also demonstrated that age, educational level, maternal PHQ-9 scores, single-parent status, and annual household income were not significantly associated with anxiety symptoms (all P > 0.05; Table 3).

Population attributable risk analysis

To quantify the relative contributions of various risk factors to depressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students, we calculated the PAR%. With respect to depressive symptoms, high academic pressure demonstrated the highest PAR% at 34.5%, followed by suburban region (33.2%, 95% CI: 26.6–40.4%) and physical activity < 2 h per week (26.0%, 95% CI: 14.6–37.1%). Results for maternal depressive symptoms (PAR% = 12.9%, 95% CI: 8.7–18.8%) and anxiety symptoms (PAR% = 20.4%, 95% CI: 15.7–25.7%) were also reported. Females demonstrated a PAR% of 22.8%, whereas disharmonious parental relationships demonstrated a PAR% of 20.0%. With respect to anxiety symptoms, high academic pressure similarly exhibited the highest PAR% (45.8%, 95% CI: 36.3–58.1%), followed by suburban residence (45.0%, 95% CI: 39.5–50.9%) and maternal anxiety symptoms (23.4%, 95% CI: 18.8–28.6%) (as shown in Table 4).

Discussion

The main findings of this study were that the self-reported prevalences of depressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students were 11.6% and 18.1%, respectively, thus indicating that mental health problems were relatively common in this population. Regarding depressive symptoms, female sex, residing in suburban areas, poor maternal mental health status, troubled parental relationships, high levels of academic pressure, insufficient physical activity, excessive screen time, and average household income levels were identified as factors associated with depressive symptoms. For anxiety symptoms, female sex, residing in suburban areas, maternal anxiety symptoms, troubled parental relationships, high levels of academic pressure, insufficient physical activity, and excessive screen time were each linked to anxiety symptoms. Social support was observed to be negatively associated with both depressive and anxiety symptoms. Importantly, this study is among the first to examine the association between maternal mental health status and adolescent depressive and anxiety symptoms in a coastal city population of secondary school students in China.

In this study, the prevalence of depressive symptoms was observed to be 11.6%. This rate was lower than the 17.2% reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis of Chinese adolescents conducted before the COVID-19 pandemic [32]; moreover, the rate was significantly lower than the 23.0% reported in cities in the western regions of China during the COVID-19 remission phase [33], and it was also lower than the rate of 25.6% previously observed by our research team in the same region [34]. Several factors may contribute to these differences. First, the relatively strict screening criteria used in this study may have led to a conservative estimation of the prevalence. Second, the high-quality educational resources and comprehensive social support systems in the study area may have been linked to more favorable adolescent mental health outcomes. Additionally, the timing of data collection during the winter months may have influenced the psychological state of the study population [35, 36]. Although the observed prevalence was relatively lower than that in previous reports, the 11.6% prevalence rate still indicated that depressive symptoms in this population remained a significant concern warranting continuous attention and further investigation into effective responses.

Anxiety symptoms, which represent another common mental health concern among secondary school students, demonstrated a higher prevalence in this study. The findings revealed that the prevalence of anxiety symptoms among secondary school students was observed at 18.1% in this eastern coastal city, which was comparable to the average prevalence of 20.5% reported in international studies and notably higher than the rate of 13.9% reported in the western region of China [33, 37]. Previous research has demonstrated considerable variation in anxiety prevalence rates; specifically, Karande et al. [38] reported that anxiety symptoms ranged from 24% to 40% among students aged 8–18 years, whereas Hu et al. [39] reported prevalence rates of 16.75% and 14.53% among junior and senior high school students, respectively. These variations in the findings can be attributed to differences in assessment timing, measurement instruments, and survey methodologies. Despite these variations across studies, the data consistently indicated that anxiety symptoms represented a significant mental health challenge among the secondary school student population.

The family environment (as the primary context for adolescent development) was closely linked to the mental health of secondary school students. In this study, multiple family-related factors closely associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms among adolescents were identified. Notably, maternal mental health status demonstrated a distinctive pattern of co-occurrence with adolescent mental health outcomes. Specifically, anxiety symptoms in mothers significantly increase the risk of anxiety symptoms in offspring [40]; similarly, maternal depressive status is significantly correlated with depressive symptoms in offspring [41]. This intergenerational transmission effect may operate through direct emotional influences and indirect parenting practices [42, 43]. Parental relationship quality emerged as another crucial factor, with discordant parental relationships being observed more frequently among adolescents with both depressive and anxiety symptoms [34, 44], thus highlighting the significance of the family environment. Furthermore, household income level was significantly associated with depressive symptoms but not with anxiety symptoms, thereby suggesting that family economic status may influence adolescent mental health through distinct mechanisms [3, 45, 46]. Students residing in suburban areas showed higher odds of mental health symptoms, which is potentially related to family-community interaction patterns and access to educational resources [47, 48]. These findings underscore the potential role of family factors in adolescent mental health and indicate that the complexity of family dimensions should be thoroughly considered in the design of mental health programs targeting this population.

Lifestyle factors may link to role in the mental health of secondary school students [49, 50]. In this study, insufficient physical activity and excessive screen time may be the factors commonly co-occurring with both depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms. Inadequate physical activity significantly increases the risks of these mental health problems among adolescents [51], which is potentially due to the positive emotional experiences and neuroendocrine regulation associated with exercise [52]. Additionally, excessive screen time (including the use of mobile phones and tablets) was significantly associated with both types of mental health concerns [53]. A potential interaction effect was observed between these factors; specifically, excessive screen time often leads to reduced physical activity, thereby creating a negative cycle. Prolonged screen exposure not only diminishes opportunities for face-to-face social interactions but may also compromise sleep quality, thus subsequently affecting mental health [53, 54]. These findings indicate that programs addressing adolescent mental health may benefit from considering both individual behaviors and the balance among multiple health behaviors. This balance between physical activity and screen time is particularly important in the current digital era.

Demographic characteristics and academic pressure emerged as significant factors associated with the mental health of secondary school students. Analysis of sex differences revealed that female students exhibited higher odds of both depressive and anxiety symptoms [55, 56], which is potentially attributed to their heightened emotional sensitivity and increased interpersonal pressure during adolescence. Academic pressure is notable as being a prominent risk factor that simultaneously increases the likelihood of both depressive and anxiety symptoms [57]. In the current competitive educational environment, excessive academic workload, examination stress, and anxiety related to college admission may lead to persistent psychological distress among students [58]. These findings suggest that sex-specific characteristics should be considered in the planning of mental health programs, along with attention to the potential burden associated with academic pressure.

In this study, we identified social support as a factor negatively correlated with depressive and anxiety symptoms among secondary school students [59, 60]. Adequate social support significantly reduces the risks of mental health problems [61], which is primarily achieved by providing emotional support, enhancing social connectedness, and strengthening psychological resilience. Moreover, social support co-variation with other potentially protective factors; specifically, higher levels of social support were associated with better capacity to cope with academic pressure and more healthy lifestyle behaviors [62].

The population attributable risk analysis provides quantitative support for prioritizing public health attention. High academic pressure demonstrated the highest PAR% for both depressive and anxiety symptoms, thus confirming its central role in adolescent mental health. The substantial PAR% values observed in suburban residents point to potential geographic disparities in educational resources and their relationship with mental health outcomes. Similarly, insufficient physical activity and excessive screen time demonstrate the importance of lifestyle factors. Maternal mental health (particularly anxiety symptoms) demonstrated a modest but noteworthy contribution. Notably, maternal depressive symptoms exhibited a high individual-level OR but relatively modest PAR% due to a low prevalence, thus illustrating the distinction between clinical and public health significance. However, the PAR% estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by various biases and assumptions. Specifically, due to the fact that the PAR% is calculated from ORs rather than relative risks, as well as the fact that our outcome prevalences exceed the 10% rarity threshold (anxiety: 18.1%; depression: 11.6%), the PAR% values may be inflated by 5–15% relative to risk ratio-based estimates; furthermore, when risk factors are correlated, the summation of individual PAR% values overestimates their joint population impact because of nonadditivity. These findings suggest that public health attention targeting academic pressure, geographic resource inequalities, and healthy lifestyle promotion may be associated with population-level benefits for adolescent mental well-being, though causal conclusions cannot be drawn from cross-sectional data, and the feasibility and effectiveness of specific programs would require longitudinal evaluation.

This study yielded five key practical implications. First, schools should optimize curriculum arrangements to ensure sufficient physical activities and implement targeted education programs for electronic device usage management. Second, given the observed sex differences, gender-specific mental health services should be developed, with particular attention being focused on providing targeted psychological support for female students. Third, education authorities may benefit from deepening the reform of educational evaluation by adopting diverse assessment methods, with the potential to effectively reduce the academic pressure on students. Fourth, schools and families should jointly establish comprehensive social support networks and strengthen home-school collaboration to provide timely emotional support and practical assistance. Finally, it is recommended that systematic mental health screening be established, in conjunction with early warning mechanisms for the timely identification of students’ mental health issues and appropriate interventions. The successful implementation of these programs requires coordinated efforts among families, schools, and society.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the research design prevented the establishment of causality between the variables. Second, although academic stress and parental relationships were assessed as single-item self-perceived measures and lacked standardized multi-item validation data, they demonstrated strong criterion-related validity through substantial associations with depression and anxiety outcomes, as well as considerable population attributable risk contributions. However, single-item measures are inherently more susceptible to measurement errors; additionally, they cannot fully capture the multidimensional nature of these constructs and do not permit the assessment of internal consistency or test-retest reliability. Furthermore, several considerations apply to PAR% estimates. For example, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference; in addition, PAR% should be interpreted as associations rather than causal attributions. PAR% calculations assume independence of risk factors and may overestimate population impacts if factors are correlated. PAR% may also be sensitive to misclassification of exposures and outcomes, although validated instruments were used. Future research should explore the joint PAR% of correlated risk factors by using sequential population attributable fractions and validate findings in longitudinal studies.

In conclusion, this study revealed prevalence rates of 11.6% and 18.1% for depressive and anxiety symptoms, respectively, among secondary school students, thus indicating a substantial presence of mental health symptoms in this population. Maternal mental health status showed a significant relationship with depressive and anxiety symptoms in adolescents, along with other co-occurring factors, including sex, residential location, troubled parental relationships, academic pressure, insufficient physical activity, and excessive screen time. Furthermore, social support was linked to lower odds of these mental health problems. These findings contribute to the evidence base that may inform the development of targeted mental health programs, though longitudinal research is needed to establish directionality and evaluate the effectiveness of specific strategies.
