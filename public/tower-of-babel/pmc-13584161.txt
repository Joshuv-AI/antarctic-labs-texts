Carbamate-Bearing Carbohydrates: Natural Occurrence, Pharmacological Relevance, and Chemical Synthesis
Authors: Vincent Maltais-Bourgeois, André Pichette, Joanne Plourde, Laéla Lephore, Jean Legault, Jérôme Alsarraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbamoylation is a relatively rare but relevant modification in natural carbohydrates, promoting structural diversity. Numerous families of natural glycosides, including aminocoumarins, glycopeptides, or macrolides, contain these carbamoylated carbohydrates. These natural products display a broad spectrum of pharmacological properties. In several cases, the carbamate-bearing carbohydrate contributes to these bioactivities. The synthesis of carbamate-bearing carbohydrates presents important challenges, and has been conventionally achieved through multistep sequences and the use of toxic and unstable reagents. Recent advances in catalytic site-selective carbamoylation enabled more efficient and safer syntheses of these unique carbohydrates. These advancements facilitate the study of carbamate-bearing carbohydrates, potentially accelerating the discovery of novel therapeutic agents.

Introduction

Natural glycosides exhibit remarkable structural diversity, combining various aglycons and carbohydrates. Their numerous structural features impart them relevant bioactivities, and several natural glycosides are used clinically to treat a broad spectrum of malignancies. Primary and secondary metabolisms involve carbamoylations. Carbamate-bearing carbohydrates are found in several natural products and some carbamoyltransferases, responsible for their biosynthesis, have been identified. For instance, carbamoylation of the antibiotic tobramycin (1a) has been well characterized (Scheme ). The carbamoyltransferase TobZ catalyzes the conversion of carbamoylphosphate (2) into carbamoyladenosine monophosphate (carbamoyl-AMP, 3). Carbamoylation of the carbohydrate then occurs in a distinct active site of the enzyme. Carbohydrate carbamoylation contributes to the diversity of naturally occurring glycosides. Numerous bacteria and marine organisms produce carbamate-bearing glycosides that display a wide range of attractive pharmacological properties (Figure ).

For instance, ansacarbamitocins (4) are maytansinoids isolated from Gram-positive bacteria of the genus Amycolatopsis. Ansacarbamitocin B features a 4-O-carbamoyl-β-d- N-glucopyranoside and displayed moderate antifungal activity. Warkmycin (5) is an angucycline glycoside isolated from the Streptomyces strain Acta 2930. It displays a 4-O-carbamoyl-3-O-methyl-α-D-5-epi-olivose. This compound showed antiproliferative properties against human hepatocellular carcinoma cells (HepG2), and colon adenocarcinoma cells (HT29) with half maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) in the low micromolar range. Citronamides A (6a) and B (6b) were isolated from the Australian sponge Citronia astra. They consist of a tetrapeptide aglycon linked to a carbamoylated α-iduronic acid residue. Citronamide A displayed moderate antifungal activity against Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Tetronitrose (or kijanose) is another peculiar carbamoylated mososaccharide. It was found in several antibiotics, including tetrocarcins (7) from Micromonospora chalcea , and kijanimicin from Actinomadura kijaniata SCC 1256. Concanamycins are a group of 18-membered macrolactones first isolated from Streptomyces diastatochromogenes. These macrolides are potent inhibitors of vacuolar ATPase. Concanamycins A (8a) and C (8b) only differ by the presence of a carbamate on their β-d-olivose moiety. In this specific case, the sugar is not involved in the interaction between the inhibitor and its target enzyme, and concanamycins A and C showed similar pharmacological properties.

These examples highlight the structural diversity of aglycons and carbamoylated carbohydrates found in natural carbamate-bearing glycosides. Although some of these compounds displayed attractive pharmacological properties, the specific contribution of the carbamate-bearing carbohydrate on these bioactivities has not been proven. Conversely, carbamoylated carbohydrates were shown to influence the bioactivity of several other glycosides. In this review, we detail the cases where the biological properties of glycosides are linked with their carbamate-bearing carbohydrates. We also provide an overview of the reported synthetic approaches toward carbamate-bearing carbohydrates and discuss how recent site-selective reactions could open new perspectives regarding the study of these distinctive carbohydrates.

Biological Relevance of Carbamate-Bearing Sugars

The 3-O-Carbamoylnovobiose Moiety of Novobiocin, and Related Aminocoumarins

Novobiocin (9a) is an aminocoumarin glycoside isolated from Streptomyces niveus. It features a characteristic 3-O-carbamoyl-noviose moiety (Figure ). This antibacterial compound was used clinically for the treatment of infections caused by various Staphylococci. Novobiocin binds to the ATP-binding pocket of bacterial DNA gyrase, which is an essential enzyme for the replication, elongation and transcription of DNA. Intriguingly, the 3-O-carbamoylnovobiose moiety of novobiocin is involved in the inhibitory interaction with DNA gyrase. A hydrogen bond between the carbamate function of novobiocin and an aspartate residue (ASP73) was observed in the cocrystal structure of novobiocin complexed to the DNA gyrase B protein of Escherichia coli. , A similar interaction was shown with ASP80 in the 43 kDa domain of Thermus thermophilus gyrase B (PDB code: 1KIJ) and with ASP81 in the 24 kDa N-terminal domain of Staphylococcus aureus gyrase B (PDB code: 4URO). As a result 3′-O-decarbamoylnovobiocin (9b) was shown to be much less active than novobiocin against S. aureus (MIC = 17.6 and 0.25 μM, respectively). Loss of the carbamate group also translated into loss of activity against Bacillus subtilis (ATCC 14893). Docking studies also suggest that the carbamate interacts with the ATP binding site of DNA gyrase subunit B of Micobacterium tuberculosis. On the other hand, some strains of S. niveus converted novobiocin into 2′-O-carbamoylnovobiocyn (9c) that was devoid of the antibacterial potency of novobiocin. In this case, carbamoylation could be a mechanism of bacterial resistance against novobiocin. Other related aminocoumarins were also isolated from actinomycetes including coumabiocins. The carbamate-bearing noviose of coumabiocin E (10a) was essential for antibacterial activity against Streptomyces 85E as the corresponding aglycon coumabiocin F (10b) was inactive.

Structure of novobiocin (9a) and analogues (9b–c and 10a–b).

Aminocoumarin glycosides could also inhibit 90 kDa heat shock proteins (Hsp90) − which are potential targets for the treatment of cancers and neurodegenerative diseases. Unlike DNA gyrase, the carbamate group of dehydronovobiocin was detrimental to Hsp90 inhibition. Nevertheless, novobiocin inhibited hepatocellular tumor growth in vivo through Hsp90 inhibition.

Bleomycins

Bleomycins are a class of anticancer antibiotics first isolated from Streptomyces verticillus. These glycopeptides feature a disaccharide consisting in an l-gulose linked to a characteristic 3-O-carbamoyl-d-mannose (Figure ). They are used clinically for the treatment of testicular cancers and lymphomas. Their antitumoral properties result from the cleavage of the DNA double-strand. In 2009, Hecht’s group conjugated bleomycin A5 (11) to microbubbles and showed that the resulting objects adhered selectively to cancer cells. The disaccharide moiety of bleomycin was necessary for this interaction to occur. Later, the same group showed that the disaccharide moiety of bleomycin was internalized selectively within cancer cells. , These binding/uptake properties were attributed to the 3-O-carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside moiety of bleomycins.

Structure of bleomycin A5 (11).

The membrane receptor involved in this selective uptake phenomenon remains unidentified to date. Initial assays suggested that an ATP-dependent process could be involved. Conversely, it has then been proposed that the selective uptake of carbamoylmannose could rely on facilitative glucose transporters.

The selective internalization of conjugates in malignant cells is a relevant drug delivery approach in anticancer chemotherapy that has found clinical applications with antibody-drug conjugates. Small molecules, based on receptor- or antigen-targeting ligands have also been approved in medical imagery and in fluorescence assisted surgery. Within this framework, the tumor-cell binding properties of 3-O-carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside could have significant translational potential in oncology. Recent work on this topic includes the design of camptothecin glycoconjugates. Combining camptothecin with the disaccharide moiety of bleomycins resulted in potent and selective cytotoxicity against hepatocellular, colorectal, and pancreatic carcinoma cells compared to a normal kidney cell line. Cisplatin-containing nanoparticles, decorated with carbamoylmannose, were also selectively taken up by various malignant cells and delayed hepatic tumor growth in vivo. Furthermore, drug-delivery systems based on carbamoylmannose-decorated spherical nucleic acid were designed that were effectively taken up by prostate cancer cells. Although preclinical, these studies could lead to the development of novel targeting chemotherapeutics, based on the properties of carbamoylmannose.

Moenomycin A

Moenomycins (also names flavomycins, bambermycins, or flavophospholipols) constitute a family of phosphoglycolipids. Moenomycin A (Figure ), the major constituent of these mixtures, has been produced by various Streptomyces strains including S. ghanaensis (ATCC14672), S. bambergiensis (ATCC13879), S. ederensis (ATCC15304), and S. geysiriensis (ATCC15303). Moenomycin A (12a) features a carbamate-bearing carbohydrate, named moenuronamide, which is a 3-O-carbamoyl-4-C-methyl-α-d-glucuronamide. Moenomycins are potent antibiotics, especially against Gram-positive bacteria, and to a lesser extent against Gram-negative bacteria and mycobacteria. Their activity is based on peptidoglycan glycosyltransferases (PGTs) inhibition. PGTs are enzymes involved in bacterial cell wall biosynthesis. Crystal structures of moenomycins bound to PGTs have been resolved. Strikingly, these studies highlighted hydrogen bonds between the carbamate group of moenomycins and amino acid residues of PGTs. , As the carbamate is involved in the direct inhibition of PGTs by moenomycins, the presence of this functional group affects their antibacterial potency. Decarbamoylmoenomycin A was obtained by refluxing moenomycin A in a butylamine/methanol mixture. The loss of the carbamate group led to a 1000-fold drop in activity against various strains of S. aureus.

Structure of moenomycin A (12a).

Despite potent antibacterial activity, which compared positively to vancomycin, the clinical development of moenomycin A has been hampered by low oral bioavailability, long half-life, and hemolytic activity. On the other hand, moenomycin has been used as animal feed additive since the 1960s. Interestingly, it has not resulted in the emergence of resistance to moenomycin. This could be explained by the distinctive mechanism of action of moenomycins. For these reasons, moenomycins, and more broadly PGT inhibition, is currently the subject of renewed interest. Recent advances include the potent activity of moenomycin A against multiresistant Helicobacter pylori, , a Gram-negative gastric pathogen and major risk factor of gastric cancer. Moenomycin has also demonstrated strong antigonococcal properties in vitro and in vivo, including against multidrug-resistant strains and clinical isolates of Neisseria gonorrheae.

Other recent progresses comprise the study of fluorescent probes based on the pharmacophore of moenomycin A that include carbamate-bearing glucuronamide moieties (Figure ). Among others, the probe 12b has been designed to facilitate the discovery of new antibiotics based on PGT inhibition. Moenomycin-derived probes have also enabled the labeling of transglycosydases in living bacteria.

Structure of the fluorescent probe 12b based on the carbamate-bearing pharmacophore of moenomycin A (12a).

Venturicidins and Irumamycins

Venturicidins (also known as aabomycins) and irumamycins are structurally related 20-membered macrolide glycosides isolated from various Streptomyces species. , These compounds feature a 3-O-carbamoyl-β-d-olivose moiety (Figure ) and display antibacterial and antifungal properties. Venturicidin A potentiates gentamicin against various bacteria, including methicillin resistant S. aureus (MRSA). The antifungal properties of Irumamycin (13a) and 3′-O-decarbamoylirumamycin (13b) has been evaluated and the carbamate-bearing glycoside was slightly more active against phytopathogenic fungi. ,

Structure of irumamycin (13a) and 3′-O-decarbamoylirumamycin (13b).

Streptothricins

Streptothricins have been found in actinomycetes. Their peptidyl nucleoside scaffold combines a streptolidine heterocycle, a carbamoylated gulosamine, and an l-β-lysine oligopeptide. Since the discovery of streptothricin F in 1942 from Streptomyces lavendulae, several analogues (Figure ) have been isolated from various strains. Streptothricins D (16a) and E (15a) share the same carbamoylation pattern as streptothricin F (14a), with the carbamate function on the O-4 position of gulosamine. On the other hand, they differ in the number of repeating units of the oligopeptide. Counterparts were also isolated where the carbamate function is on the O-6 position of the gulosamine motif (14b), (15b), (16b). Streptothricins demonstrated broad spectrum antimicrobial properties against pathogenic bacteria and fungi by inhibition of protein synthesis. , Strikingly, the carbamoylation pattern was shown to influence the antimicrobial properties of streptothricins considerably. 10-de-O-carbamoyl-12-O-carbamoylstreptothricin E (15b) was more active than streptothricin E against S. aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, B. subtilis, Mycobacterium smegmatis, M. tuberculosis, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, M. morganii, E. coli, and Candida albicans. Conversely, 10-de-O-carbamoyl-12-O-carbamoylstreptothricins D (16b) and F (14b) were less active than streptothricins D and F against S. aureus, B. subtilis, Bacillus cereus, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli. This result was consistent with recent cryo-electron microscopy characterization of streptothricin F bound to the 70S of Acinetobacter baumannii, showing electrostatic interactions between the carbamate moiety of streptothricin F and an adenine nucleobase (A1196).

Structure of streptothricins.

Streptothricins have been used in crop protection, but the apparition of resistance compromised this application. From a clinical perspective, the promising antibacterial properties of natural streptothricin mixtures, including against resistant strains, were mitigated by their toxicity. However, streptothricin F proved to be less toxic than its congeners, which stimulated the development of pure streptothricin F production methods. Recent work in this field includes a simplified total synthesis of streptothricin F and the biological production of streptothricin F from the bacterial fermentation of food waste. These advances could stimulate the study of streptothricin F, whose activity against resistant Gram-negative pathogens sparked a renewed interest. Furthermore, the convergent nature of the total synthesis scheme could enable the pharmacomodulation of this carbamate-bearing glycoside.

Kibdelomycin and Amycolamicin

Kibdelomycin (17a), isolated from the Gram-positive soil bacterium Kibdelosporangium sp. (MA7385), displayed attractive antibacterial properties. As novobiocin, kibdelomycin was shown to inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, but featured a structure (Figure ) and binding mode that distinguished it from other topoisomerase inhibitors. This mechanism of action gives kibdelomycin potent activity against resistant Gram-positive bacteria, with no cross-resistance. Amycolamicin was isolated from the soil actinomycete Amycolatopsis sp. MK575-fF5. Kibdelomycin and amycolamicin were first thought to be different but a recent total synthesis showed that the two molecules were identical. Several analogues of kibdelomycin have also been discovered.

Structure of kibdelomycin (17a) and deglycokibdelomycin (17b).

The 4-O-carbamoyl-6-desoxy-l-talopyranose, named amykitanose, is part of the upper binding arm of kibdelomycin. The carbamate moiety of kibdelomycin was close to a histidine residue in a cocrystal structure with S. aureus gyrase B (PDB: 4URM). Three total syntheses of kibdelomycin were reported in 2021 and 2022 which allowed to evaluate the antibacterial properties of several analogues. Intriguingly, deglycokibdelomycin (17b) was less active than kibdelomycin against E. coli (MG1655 and W0153 strains), which suggested that the carbamoylated carbohydrate could contribute to the activity of kibdelomycin. Further structure–activity relationship studies, reported in 2025, were enabled by convergent total synthesis. They included substitution of the carbamate with alkyl and aryl groups. These substitutions all resulted in a decrease of antibacterial activity, suggesting that the carbamate had to remain intact to ensure the pharmacological properties of kibdelomycin. Pharmacomodulation also enabled the discovery of an analogue, still featuring a carbamate-bearing carbohydrate. This lead compound displayed improved antibacterial potency and safety compared to kibdelomycin. It also prevented bacterial resistance mutations. Within this framework, total synthesis and structure–activity relationship studies could lead to translational applications for kibdelomycin. The biosynthesis of kibdelomycin has also been studied to eventually improve its production or the development of analogues. ,

Auriside B

Aurisides A (17a) and B (17b) are cytotoxic macrolide glycosides isolated from the Japanese sea hare Dolabella Auricularia. They differ in the nature of their carbohydrate moieties (Figure ). While auriside A features a 2-4-di-O-methyl-α-l-rhamnopyranoside disaccharide, auriside B displays a 2,4-di-O-methyl-3-O-carbamoyl-α-l-rhamnopyranoside monosaccharide. Although few studies reported the cytotoxic properties of aurisides, the nature of the sugars was shown to influence their activity. Auriside A indeed displayed a 0.17 μg/mL IC50 and auriside B displayed a 1.2 μg/mL IC50 against HeLa S3 cervix cancer cells.

Structure of aurisides (17a–b), and moorenaside (17c).

Furthermore, the decarbamoyl counterpart of auriside B, named moorenaside (17c), was isolated recently from a benthic marine cyanobacterium Moorena sp. Interestingly, this macrolide showed low cytotoxicity against HCT116 colon cancer cells as 55% viability was recorded at the highest tested concentration of 32 μM. Even though auriside B (17b) and moorenaside (17c) have not been tested against the same cell line, this result could suggest that the carbamate moiety of auriside B (17b) could contribute to its cytotoxicity.

Pharmacomodulation of Carbohydrates by Carbamoylation

Pharmacomodulation of Kaempferol 3-O-(3″,4″-O-Diacetyl-α-l-rhamnopyranoside) (SL0101)

The natural kaempferol rhamnoside SL0101 (18a) is a potent p90 ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK) inhibitor first isolated from the tropical plant Forsteronia refracta. As RSK is expressed, among others, in breast, prostate, and lung cancers, , SL0101 could find useful applications in oncology. , Acylation of the carbohydrate in SL0101 is key to the selective inhibition of RSK. , However, acetylated carbohydrates could be unstable in biological contexts due to the presence of esterases which would in turn restrict the application prospects of SL0101. Within this framework, carbamate surrogates (Table ) were synthesized to improve the stability of SL0101. The carbamate-bearing derivative 18b showed similar inhibition potency toward RSK2 compared to SL0101, along with improved stability in biological conditions. Further pharmacomodulations around the structure of SL0101 included C-5″ n-Pr substitution, and cyclitol analogues. , These modifications, combined with carbohydrate carbamoylation, led to the discovery of novel analogues (18c–f) displaying improved bioactivity.

From the hit molecule SL0101 (18a), displaying appealing bioactivity but unfavorable pharmacokinetics, carbohydrate carbamoylation has been a key modification toward lead compounds with improved pharmacologic profile. Pharmacomodulation of Sordarin

Sordarin (19a), a diterpene glycoside, was first isolated from the fungus Sordaria araneosa. It displayed interesting antifungal properties, especially against the human pathogen C. albicans. Sordarin inhibits elongation factor 2 (EF2). This mode of action distinguished sordarin from other antimycotics. 3′-O-Acylated derivatives were also isolated, exhibiting improved antiproliferative activity against various fungi. As a result, hemisynthetic 3′-O-substituted derivatives (Figure ) have been synthesized to establish structure–activity relationships. A 3′-O-carbamoylated surrogate (19c) surpassed sordarin in terms of antifungal properties but did not match the acylated derivative GR 135402 (19b).

Pharmacomodulation of 3-O-Carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranose

The promising cancer cell targeting properties of the 3-O-carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside moiety of bleomycins also prompted preliminary structure–activity relationship studies. Analogues were designed that featured substituted carbamates, as well as isomers of the characteristic 3-O-carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside found in bleomycins. Interestingly, the fluorescent glycoconjugate 20b (Figure ), featuring a 2-O-(N-methyl)­carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside showed improved selective uptake in several cancer cell lines compared to its counterpart 20a, bearing the native 3-O-carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside of bleomycins.

Chemical Synthesis of Carbamate-Bearing Carbohydrates

Stepwise Differentiation of Hydroxyl Groups Using Stoichiometric Protective Groups or Promotors

A classical challenge in glycochemistry relies on the differentiation of hydroxyl positions of carbohydrates. This is a prerequisite in the synthesis of carbamate-bearing sugars. Until recently, it was mainly achieved following classical schemes that involved protection–deprotection sequences. For instance, the synthesis of the carbamoylmannose moiety of bleomycins was performed through the protection of the O-4 and O-6 positions of mannose as a benzylidene acetal. The diol 21 was then converted into a stannylene acetal using a stoichiometric amount of dibutyltin oxide. The more nucleophilic equatorial position of the intermediate then reacted with benzyl bromide in a regioselective fashion to provide the benzyl ether 22. After a peracetylation step, the benzyl protective-group was orthogonally deprotected by catalytic hydrogenation using Pearlman’s catalyst (Pd­(OH)2/C). It is worthy of note that the use of Pd/C yielded a mixture of products resulting from acetate migration. The alcohol 23 could then be converted into the expected carbamate 24. This was achieved in 2 steps through the formation of a 4-nitrophenyl carbonate intermediate that reacted with ammonia. Altogether, the preparation of a mannoside that featured the characteristic functional group found in bleomycins required 7 steps from the commercially available 1-methyl-α-d-mannopyranoside 25 (Scheme ).

The differentiation of hydroxyls leading to the alcohol 27 relied on a multistep sequence. Briefly, 1-methyl-α-l-rhamnopyranoside was protected as an isopropylidene acetal to enable the methylation of its O-4 position. The isopropylidene protective-group was then removed under acidic hydrolysis conditions. The resulting diol was then converted into a stannylene acetal under the conditions described above to allow the benzylation of the O-3 position using benzyl bromide. The methylation of the O-2 position was then performed prior to the catalytic hydrogenation of the benzyl ether. Reaction of alcohol 27 with trichloroacetylisocyanate was followed by methanolysis of the trichloroacetyl group using K2CO3. Two , of the three total syntheses of kibdelomycin published between 2021 and 2022 also relied on the use of trichloroacetylisocyanate.

Regioselective Carbamoylation of Polyols

The regioselective functionalization of unprotected, or partially protected carbohydrates is an appealing approach in both terms of step and atom economy. It is however difficult to differentiate carbohydrate secondary hydroxyls solely based on the relative reactivity of these alcohols. The reaction of the diol-displaying mannoside 36 with benzylisocyanate clearly illustrates the challenge represented by the site-selective carbamoylation of carbohydrates. This transformation (Scheme ) provided the desired 3-O-carbamoylation pattern of bleomycin mannoside (37) in a poor 23% yield. The unsubstituted carbamate 38 was subsequently obtained by catalytic hydrogenation of the benzyl group.

Catalytic, Site-Selective Modification of Minimally Protected Carbohydrates

The synthetic approaches described above have two main drawbacks. On the one hand, the differentiation of the hydroxy group to be carbamoylated required multistep protection–deprotection sequences. On the other hand, the introduction of the nitrogen-bearing function was performed either in two steps through cyclic or activated carbonates or by using harmful and unstable isocyanates. However, significant advances have been made during the 2010s in the catalytic site-selective modification of unprotected carbohydrates. Furthermore, more user-friendly alternatives to isocyanates have been explored for the synthesis of carbamates. Based on these premises, Alsarraf et al. reported the first site-selective carbamoylation of minimally protected pyranosides (Scheme ). In this process, the borinic-acid catalyst selectively activated the equatorial position of cis-1,2-diol motifs in carbohydrates. The reaction involved 1-carbamoylimidazoles as the carbamoylation reagents. ,

Catalytic Site-Selective Carbamoylation of a cis-1,2-Diol Motif with a 1-Carbamoylimidazole in the Presence of a Borinic Acid Catalyst

These ureas were readily prepared from the reaction between amines and the benign carbonyldiimidazole in water. They circumvented the two-step carbamoylation protocol through carbonates as well as harmful and unstable isocyanates. The catalytic reaction enabled the modification of the O-3 position of various pyranosides from the manno- and galacto- series and allowed to modulate the substitution of the carbamate. It gave a straightforward access to the 3-O-carbamoyl-α-d-mannopyranoside moiety of bleomycins and the 3-O-carbamoyl-α-l-rhamnopyranoside motif found in auriside B, as well as several new derivatives. As carbamates are found on the equatorial position of cis-1,2-diols in other natural glycosides such as novobiocin or kibdelomycin, this catalytic approach could be extended to the synthesis of other bioactive carbamate-bearing natural products. Therefore, this synthetic method could facilitate the study of attractive bioactive scaffolds.

During the development of this reaction, we observed that isocyanates exhibited lower performance compared to carbamoylimidazoles. Reactions involving isocyanates were less selective as they provided mixtures of carbamoylated products. The same behavior was published by Lannigan and O’Doherty’s group, who conducted the borinic acid catalyzed site-selective carbamoylation of cyclitols using isocyanates (Scheme ). Carbamoylation of the cyclitol 39 indeed afforded carbamates 40a and 40b in 45% overall yield with a ratio of 3:1. This work would probably have benefited from the catalytic methods using 1-carbamoylimidazoles and highlights the potential interest of such reactions from a pharmacomodulation perspective. Nevertheless, this study of carbamate-bearing cyclitols enabled the discovery of optimized analogues of the naturally occurring RSK inhibitor SL0101.

Carbamoyl Migration in Carbohydrates

The migration of functional groups between hydroxyls in carbohydrates can occur under various conditions and represent a challenge in the synthesis of glycosides and oligosaccharides. Acyl group migration is well documented and is prone to take place under alkaline conditions. − Acetyl group migration has also been reported under palladium catalyzed hydrogenation conditions. In contrast, carbamoyl group migration has received less attention. Early studies reported the isomerization of novobiocin (9a) into isonovobiocin (9d) under alkaline conditions (Scheme ) which translated into a loss of antibiotic activity. As synthetic sequences leading to carbamate-bearing carbohydrates often involve the deprotection of acyl groups under basic transesterification or hydrolysis conditions, care should be taken to ensure that carbamoyl group migration does not occur.

Current Relevance and Future Outlook of Carbamate-Bearing Carbohydrates

As discussed above, several families of carbamate-bearing carbohydrates display appealing bioactivities that depend upon the presence and position of the carbamate. Although many of these compounds were discovered decades ago, they remain a relevant area of research. Several factors could explain this trend. The increasing threat and global burden of bacterial antimicrobial resistance urges the development of novel classes of antibiotics. In this context, long-known molecules such as moenomycins or streptothricins have been regaining attention. Recent breakthroughs concerning these carbamate-bearing carbohydrates include the total synthesis of streptothricin F, granting access to pure material, which constituted a significant challenge in the microbial production of this compound. The development of innovative glycoconjugates derived from the carbamate-bearing carbohydrate of moenomycin have also improved the study of its distinctive mode of action and could trigger the discovery of novel PGT inhibitors. More recently discovered structures, such as kibdelomycin, could also become new classes of antibiotics. Its recent total syntheses, reported independently by three groups in 2021 and 2022, have been a milestone in the development of kibdelomycin. Convergent strategies have also enabled pharmacomodulation, and the discovery of analogues could lead to new drug candidates.

On a different note, bleomycins have been known and used clinically since the 1960s, but the tumor-cell targeting properties of its carbamate-bearing carbohydrate has been highlighted in the 2010s. This key discovery recently prompted the study of novel glycosides and glycoconjugates dedicated to targeted chemotherapy.

From a synthetic methodology perspective, the appeal of these glycosides motivated the development of site-selective carbamoylations of carbohydrates. By saving protection–deprotection sequences and replacing harmful and unstable carbamoylation reagents, these new methods will facilitate the study of biologically relevant carbamate-bearing carbohydrates.

Conclusions

Carbamoylation is a rare modification in natural carbohydrates. However, various families of natural products contain carbamate-bearing glycosides. This unique structural feature confers intriguing properties to some carbamoylated glycosides. Among others, the carbamate-bearing moieties of novobiocin and moenomycin A contribute to their pharmacological properties. Until recently, the preparation of such carbohydrates was relatively tedious as multistep sequences and harmful reagents were involved. Nevertheless, recently reported catalytic site-selective carbamoylations paved the way for easier syntheses of carbamate-bearing carbohydrates and could accelerate valuable discoveries in this appealing class of carbohydrates.
