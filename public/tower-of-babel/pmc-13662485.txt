Assessing Analytical Performance and Standardizing Lipid Measurements–Sometimes a Conundrum
Authors: Hubert W Vesper, Uliana Danilenko, Otoe Sugahara, Sollip Kim, Yeo-Min Yun, Alicia N Lyle
Published: 2026

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are a major public health concern globally. Monitoring the levels of blood lipids (total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides [TGs], HDL-cholesterol [HDL-C], and LDL-cholesterol [LDL-C]) is important for CVD prevention and management. Because risk prediction models and treatment goals rely on specific analyte concentrations, accurate and reproducible measurement results are critical. Most risk algorithms and treatment goals focus on the assessment and reduction of LDL-C concentrations, where LDL-C can be measured directly or calculated using TC, TGs, and HDL-C measurement results [1].

Standardization involves accurate calibration and its verification, as well as improvement and control of other factors, such as analytical selectivity. When combined with accuracy-based external quality assessment (EQA) and monitoring programs, such as the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency Lipids Standardization Programs (LSPs), standardization creates a robust framework for ensuring the accuracy and reliability of clinical laboratory measurements. To achieve true standardization, the tools used in these programs, such as reference measurement procedures (RMPs) and reference materials (RMs), must have appropriate performance specifications such as high level of trueness for RMPs and commutability for RM.

The commutability study conducted by Seo et al. [2] validated the appropriateness of the RMs used in EQA programs. The authors pointed out several challenges in interpreting their results and determining commutability, including the impacts of TG concentrations and LDL particle sizes, and highlighted a major conundrum in evaluating lipid RM commutability: determining whether an observed bias arises from true non-commutability of the material or from assay-specific interfering compounds, such as TGs.

According to the CLSI EP7, an interference is a component or property of the sample that causes a medically significant difference in the result of a measurand. Interference results from non-selectivity of the detection system, suppression of an indicator reaction, inhibition of the measurand (in case the measurand is an enzyme), or other specimen-dependent effects [3]. According to the International Organization for Standardization 17511:2020, “commutability” is a property of an RM demonstrated by the closeness of agreement between the relationships among the measurement results for a stated quantity in the RM obtained using two measurement procedures (MPs) and the relationships among the measurement results for other clinical samples. In other words, commutability defines how closely pooled or otherwise modified materials align with typical patient samples, whereas interference can occur in both patient samples and RMs [4].

To appropriately assess commutability, both the materials and the patient samples must be free of interference, as outlined in recent guidance documents [5, 6]. The most recent version of the CLSI C37 protocol [7] states, “Additional acceptance criteria can be added depending on the intended use. For example, the units may be selected to meet desired concentration values for single donor or pooled serum; or additional specifications for potential interfering substances (such as bilirubin <25 μmol/L [1.5 mg/dL] or triglycerides <1.7 mmol/L [150 mg/dL]) can be stated. However, the final decision on the donor unit selection should be based on the intended use of the prepared material. For example, concentrations of triglycerides can be specified as required if the study intends to evaluate the influence of hypertriglyceridemia on MPs for another analyte or to investigate measurement procedure performance for high concentrations of triglycerides.”

Seo et al. [2] attributed the non-commutability in their study to high TG levels exceeding 2.26 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) and small LDL-C particle sizes (<265 Å) for two RMs. These findings are consistent with observations made by other groups [8–10]. Given the above-mentioned definitions, TG concentration and LDL particle size are interferences and, thus, are limitations of the MPs; they are not related to the commutability of the materials. Furthermore, this study included clinical samples with TG concentrations of up to 1.45 mmol/L (128.3 mg/dL). Including patient samples with high TG concentrations and different LDL-C particle sizes similar to those in the RMs could have provided more detailed information about the commutability of the RMs. However, we recognize that such studies are challenging. Nonetheless, the findings described by the authors suggest that HDL-C and LDL-C assays require further improvements to be less affected by dyslipidemia.

The term “commutability” was used in the early 1980s to characterize calibrators and control materials [11]. The significance of commutability in materials used for lipid testing was recognized early in the CDC-National Institutes of Health standardization initiative and emphasized during the establishment of the CDC Cholesterol Reference Method Laboratory Network (CRMLN) in the 1990s. The significant impacts of non-commutable EQA materials on EQA result interpretation were documented during that period [12]. These historical findings initiated a range of activities resulting in the development of the CLSI standards and, more recently, the introduction of International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine protocols to address some of the limitations of earlier CLSI standards [13–15]. Meanwhile, the quality of RMs and EQA materials has notably improved, and RM providers no longer use serum or other animal-origin blood products and minimize modifications of these materials by following the updated C37 protocol [7].

Despite the increased understanding of commutability as a material-quality parameter, the factors causing non-commutability remain to be comprehensively understood, making it often unpredictable. The underlying factors appear to be specific to analytical systems and technologies, particularly those that measure the analyte directly in the matrix, without prior isolation.

Freeze-thaw cycles represent another factor that affects the commutability of RMs for LDL-C accuracy testing. One study reported frozen LDL-C to be non-commutable for all methods tested, whereas most frozen HDL-C materials were commutable [16]. The mechanisms underlying the commutability loss are not fully understood but may involve structural modifications, degradation, or aggregation of lipoproteins, or changes to particle density. The potential impact of freeze-thaw cycles may vary across testing systems. To overcome these challenges, Cardiovascular Disease Biomarker Standardization Programs of CDC and CDC CRMLN members recommend using fresh, non-frozen individual donor sera for calibration, calibration verification, and trueness assessment of LDL-C measurements. Additionally, LDL-C measurements are not included in CDC LSPs for accuracy-based monitoring of assays operated in clinical and research laboratories because samples are shipped frozen. While the CDC provides reference measurements for all lipids in accuracy-based EQA programs, the interpretation of trueness of LDL-C measurements in these programs is limited by the freeze-thaw cycles these materials undergo. Nonetheless, accuracy-based EQA programs might detect major accuracy differences or changes over time, which can then be further investigated using fresh, individual donor materials.

Finally, one must note that some of the limitations associated with LDL-C measurements can be circumvented by measuring apolipoprotein B (apoB) or using newly developed calculations for LDL-C and non-HDL-C [17]. ApoB is the main structural protein component of non-HDL-C atherogenic lipoproteins, including LDL-C, and is present as a single copy on each lipoprotein. ApoB measurements are recommended in clinical practice guidelines [1] to assess CVD risk in patients because they are not affected by LDL particle size or elevated TG levels, which can impact direct LDL-C measurement accuracy. In light of these recommendations, the lipid composition of RMs and EQA materials can be customized to TG concentrations that minimize assay-specific interferences and are more suitable for direct LDL-C measurements, thereby allowing accurate assessments of participating EQA laboratories.
