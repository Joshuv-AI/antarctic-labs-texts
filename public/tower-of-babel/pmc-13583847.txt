From alphabets to action: considering next steps in ‘circulation-first’ management
Authors: Alexandra MP Brito, James E Kenny
Published: 2026

In this subanalysis of the SWAT (Shock, Whole Blood, and Assessment of TBI) trial,1 Metheny et al have added valuable prehospital context to the study of circulation-first (CAB) versus airway-first (ABC) approaches to trauma resuscitation. As with prior observational studies,2 3 patients who received both transfusion and intubation were included in the study, with CAB and ABC defined according to which intervention occurred first. This cohort was severely injured, and although the high Injury Severity Score (ISS) was listed as a limitation, their median ISS (CAB 27, ABC 34) falls between those reported in prior studies. Despite being underpowered to provide discrete recommendations, this study and prior negative studies3 remain important to understanding real-life practice patterns. The authors discuss their significant exploratory findings in the context of provider heuristics which is essential for effectively teaching high-acuity trauma care, especially for providers who manage trauma infrequently.4

Although valuable, algorithms and acronyms can oversimplify pathophysiology and its management. It is important that those designing future prospective clinical or simulation studies on this topic understand the relevant physiological mechanisms and supporting data. The data on which recommendations for the CAB approach are based support replacing lost blood, preventing further blood loss,5 and, in the absence of airway obstruction,6 delaying intubation. This is done to avoid vasodilation and reduced venous return to the heart from positive-pressure ventilation, which can lead to hypotension and precipitate cardiac arrest.7 Pragmatically, using the CAB approach to accomplish those goals differs between settings and is more complex than simply choosing transfusion versus intubation first. In the trauma bay, applying CAB might involve convincing the airway provider to delay definitive airway management in favor of non-invasive adjuncts. In a division meeting, it may mean discussing the removal of Glasgow Coma Scale score ≤8 as an absolute indication for intubating trauma patients.5 For an urban emergency medical services provider, it may involve learning when to pull a firefighter into the rig to manage the bag-valve-mask or control an extremity bleeder while the provider administers blood.

This study follows its predecessors but adds meaningful prehospital insight. Future work should evaluate all patients with prehospital recognition of massive or exsanguinating hemorrhage, as existing studies have intrinsically excluded those with well-managed severe bleeding that did not require intubation before the operating room. Limitations of prehospital data and its integration into large data sets requires refinement to effectively accomplish ongoing improvements in early trauma care. As practice continues to shift from ABC to CAB, studying and quantifying specific changes to our interventions and education will be critical.
