Excimer laser angioplasty for acute coronary occlusion: a stratified meta-analysis of efficacy against aspiration thrombectomy and conventional PCI
Authors: Fouad Hanna, Ali Dway, Sara T Zaki, Mazen Negmeldin Yassin, Abdelrahman El-Helbawy, Luiz F Ybarra, Habib Rehman Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) achieves epicardial reperfusion in most STEMI patients, yet microvascular obstruction persists in up to 60% of patients. Excimer laser coronary angioplasty (ELCA) vaporizes thrombus in situ and may reduce distal embolization, but the evidence base has not been systematically synthesized. This systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD420261422463) included comparative studies of adjunctive ELCA versus aspiration thrombectomy (Stratum A) or PCI alone (Stratum B) in acute coronary occlusion. Primary outcomes were final TIMI-3 flow and myocardial blush grade (MBG) 3; secondary outcomes were short-term mortality, MACCE, and slow-flow/no-reflow. A random-effects model with Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman confidence intervals was applied to all outcomes. Certainty was assessed with GRADE. Ten studies (1 RCT, 9 observational) were included, from a total enrolled population exceeding 3,500. In Stratum A, no outcome reached significance: MBG-3 (OR 3.57, 95% CI 0.07–185.10), mortality (OR 0.31, 0.02–4.04), MACCE (OR 0.22, 0.04–1.26), TIMI-3 flow (OR 1.58, 0.67–3.75) and slow-flow/no-reflow (OR 0.78, 0.22–2.78). In Stratum B, using each study’s propensity-matched data, no outcome differed significantly (TIMI-3 OR 0.88, 0.38–2.03; MBG-3 OR 1.06, 0.13–8.43; slow-flow/no-reflow OR 0.93, 0.29–3.02; mortality OR 0.44, 0.05–3.80). Composite endpoints were not pooled across incompatible follow-up horizons, and all outcomes were of very low certainty. Adjunctive ELCA-containing strategies during primary PCI were not associated with improved angiographic or short-term clinical outcomes against either comparator. Introduction

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the standard of care for emergent reperfusion in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI); more than 600,000 PCI procedures, including primary PCI for STEMI, are performed annually in the United States [1]. Normalized epicardial flow is achieved in approximately 90% of patients [2]. Despite a high rate of epicardial success, up to 60% experience suboptimal myocardial reperfusion, distal atherothrombotic embolization, and ischemia–reperfusion injury, which lead to microvascular obstruction (MVO) and, in turn, to adverse left ventricular remodeling, heart failure, and death [3–5]. This gap between epicardial patency and tissue reperfusion is the unmet need in STEMI management.

Manual aspiration thrombectomy improved myocardial blush grade (MBG) and ST-segment resolution in the TAPAS trial but did not reduce mortality or reinfarction in larger randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [6]. Consequently, the 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI revascularization guideline, the 2023 ESC ACS guidelines, and the 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI ACS guideline assign it a Class 3 (No Benefit) recommendation with Level A evidence [7–9]. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors are recommended mainly as bailout rather than routine therapy [9].

Excimer laser coronary angioplasty (ELCA) uses pulsed ultraviolet energy at 308 nm through fiber-optic catheters, producing photochemical ablation, photothermal vaporization of intracellular water, and photomechanical disruption by expanding vapor bubbles [10, 11]. Unlike aspiration thrombectomy, ELCA is proposed to vaporize thrombus in situ, which would reduce distal microembolization and the stroke risk associated with catheter withdrawal through the aorta; these are mechanistic rationales shown experimentally rather than established clinical effects [10, 12]. The clinical evidence is mainly from small, retrospective studies, with no systematic review available.

This systematic review and meta-analysis asked whether adjunctive ELCA during primary PCI for acute coronary occlusion improves angiographic outcomes and reduces short-term events against two pre-specified comparators: PCI with manual aspiration thrombectomy (Stratum A) and PCI alone without thrombus management (Stratum B), since combining different comparators would produce an uninterpretable summary. Because ELCA is almost always deployed within a broader procedural strategy, the comparisons are between ELCA-containing and comparator strategies.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook framework [13, 14] and was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD420261422463).

Eligibility criteria

Population, intervention, comparator, and outcomes (PICO)

Eligible studies enrolled adults (≥ 18 years) with acute coronary occlusion, including STEMI, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) or unstable angina, undergoing primary or urgent PCI. The intervention was ELCA as adjunctive therapy during PCI with any catheter size, fluence or repetition rate. The comparator was pre-specified in two independent strata: Stratum A, mechanical aspiration thrombectomy; and Stratum B, conventional or balloon-based PCI alone without dedicated thrombus management.

Primary outcomes were final Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) 3 flow and MBG 3. Secondary outcomes were slow-flow/no-reflow (intraprocedural or on final angiogram), short-term all-cause or cardiovascular mortality (in-hospital or ≤ 30 days), and MACE/MACCE as defined by each study.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they were RCTs or cohort studies with at least one ELCA arm and one non-ELCA control arm, reported at least one pre-specified outcome, and were available in English, with no restriction on publication date. Single-arm registries, animal or in vitro experiments, case reports or series, case–control studies, and secondary publications were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A pre-specified search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception to 8 June 2026, using the complete database-specific Boolean strings reported in Supplementary Table S1. Google Scholar was searched as a supplementary source, and backward and forward citation searching were performed. No language or date restrictions were applied beyond the availability of an English full text Supplementary Table S2.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted standardized data using a pre-piloted form; disagreements were resolved by consensus. Extracted study data comprised author, year, country, design, enrollment period, sample size per arm, ELCA catheter specifications, comparator stratum, endpoint definitions, outcome ascertainment, follow-up duration, statistical adjustment, and funding source. Patient-level characteristics and procedural co-interventions were extracted per arm for every study.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was independently assessed by two reviewers using study design-specific tools: the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for RCTs[15] and the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool for observational studies [16]. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Choice of statistical model

Statistical model choice was based on the expected distribution of true effects rather than by the number of contributing studies: a random-effects model with Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman confidence intervals is reported for every outcome, including those with fewer than four studies. τ2 was estimated by restricted maximum likelihood, and the ad hoc variance correction of Röver, Knapp and Friede was applied so that the Hartung–Knapp interval is never narrower than the classical random-effects interval [17]. All outcomes were binary; study-level log odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed from each 2 × 2 table and pooled by inverse variance under the random-effects model. When a study contributed a zero-event cell, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to all four of its cells before pooling; studies with zero events in both arms were non-estimable and excluded from the pooled estimate while remaining listed in the tables.

To mitigate rare event bias, a pre-specified sensitivity analysis used a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM; binomial family, random intercept logistic link) without continuity correction. All analyses were performed in R using the metafor package [18, 19].

Adjusted estimate and influence analyses

When a study reported adjusted analyses (propensity matched or multivariable), we used those estimates rather than crude event counts to reduce confounding. Accordingly, the Stratum B analyses use the propensity matched datasets of Masuda 2022 (71 pairs), Matsuda 2025/STELA (368 pairs), and Iiya 2025 (180 pairs). Composite endpoints were not pooled when the contributing studies ascertained events over incompatible horizons (in-hospital versus approximately two years); the individual study estimates are reported instead.

Leave-one-out influence analysis was performed by refitting the pooled model after omitting each contributing study in turn, using the same random effects Hartung–Knapp specification as the primary analysis [20]. Because several Stratum B estimates received more than 80% of their weight from the STELA registry, it is reported in full for every outcome to which that registry contributed Fig. S1.

Further pre-specified sensitivity analyses excluded studies at serious risk of bias (ROBINS-I), historical control studies, and studies defining slow flow intraprocedurally rather than on final angiography, and restricted the analysis to STEMI only and to non-overlapping cohorts Supplementary Table S3.

Heterogeneity assessment

Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q test (P < 0.10) and the I2 statistic (< 25%, low; 25–50%, moderate; 50–75%, substantial; > 75%, considerable) derived from the random effects fit, and τ2 was also reported.

Certainty of evidence

Certainty of evidence for each pooled outcome was rated using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach [21] and summarized in GRADEpro GDT evidence profiles for each comparator stratum, by two reviewers independently. Because the evidence was predominantly non randomized, all outcomes began at low certainty. Certainty was then rated down across the five domains: risk of bias when studies received serious overall ratings; inconsistency when I2 exceeded 50%, when τ2 indicated meaningful between study variance, or when point estimates were widely dispersed, with confidence interval overlap alone not used as a criterion; indirectness when populations, comparators or outcome definitions differed from the review question; and imprecision on the optimal information size, the number of events and whether the 95% CI crossed the no effect line or an appreciable benefit or harm threshold, with sparse event outcomes and those dominated by a single registry downgraded accordingly. Publication bias could not be tested because fewer than ten studies contributed to every outcome. The rationale for each downgrade is recorded in the evidence profile footnotes.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,411 records were identified; after removal of 279 duplicates, 1,132 were screened by title and abstract, and 20 full texts were retrieved. Ten comparative studies met all pre-specified eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis [12, 22–30] (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and methodological quality

The evidence base comprises ten comparative studies enrolling patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), predominantly STEMI. Only one RCT was identified (LaserAMI, n = 27), the remaining nine being retrospective. Three applied propensity score matching (PSM) (Masuda 2022; the multicenter STELA registry, Matsuda 2025; and Iiya 2025), two relied on era-based group assignment (Kujiraoka 2023; Shibata 2019), and one used age- and sex matched controls (Shishikura 2013). The pooled estimates were dominated by STELA (2593 patients; 368 matched pairs), and eight of ten studies originated from Japanese single centers.

The comparator was pre-specified into two strata: Stratum A compared ELCA with manual aspiration thrombectomy (Shishikura 2013; Shibata 2019; Arai 2023; Shimojo 2023), whereas Stratum B compared ELCA with conventional or balloon-based PCI without dedicated thrombus management (Ilkay 2005; Dörr 2007; Masuda 2022; Kujiraoka 2023; Matsuda 2025/STELA; Iiya 2025). Six studies enrolled STEMI exclusively, three admitted non-STEMI presentations (Shishikura 2013; Arai 2023; Iiya 2025), and Masuda 2022 enrolled both. Follow-up was predominantly confined to the index hospitalization; only STELA (median 815 days) and Iiya 2025 (approximately two years) extended beyond discharge Table 1. Baseline demographic, clinical, and angiographic characteristics

The ELCA and comparator groups were comparable in age, sex, and cardiovascular risk factors. However, ELCA was preferentially used in high thrombus burden lesions, and several studies restricted enrolment to lesions with initial TIMI flow grade 0/1 or angiographic high grade thrombus. In STELA’s multivariable model, TIMI thrombus grade 4/5 independently predicted ELCA use (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.05–2.08), as did larger reference vessel diameter and younger age. Risk of bias within studies

The single RCT was rated as having some concerns: it lacked allocation concealment, was open-label, had a small sample with baseline imbalances, no pre-registered protocol, and was partly funded by the device manufacturer. On ROBINS-I, the nine non-randomized studies were rated moderate (three) or serious (six) risk of bias, primarily due to confounding: studies with robust adjustment received a moderate rating, whereas those using minimal matching or historical comparators were rated serious. Detection bias was moderate for subjective outcomes assessed by unblinded investigators, and the absence of pre-registered protocols increased reporting bias risk (Fig. 2).

Certainty of evidence (GRADE assessment)

Certainty was very low for all five outcomes in both strata Tables 5 and 6, driven by the observational design, small samples with wide CI, and few contributing studies. In the aspiration stratum the apparent mortality and MACCE differences rest on very few events and cannot be separated from confounding, because every contributing study is at serious risk of bias.

Stratum A: ELCA vs. mechanical aspiration thrombectomy

Final TIMI 3 flow

Four studies reported final TIMI 3 flow, which was numerically more frequent with ELCA (165/193 [85.5%] vs. 173/218 [79.4%]; OR 1.58, 95% CI 0.67–3.75; P = 0.19; I2 = 0%), though the difference was not significant Fig. 3A.

MBG 3

Two studies reported MBG 3, which was numerically higher with ELCA (68/98 [69.4%] vs. 39/98 [39.8%]; OR 3.57, 95% CI 0.07–185.10; P = 0.15; I2 = 0%). With only two contributing studies, the Hartung–Knapp interval is very wide, and the difference did not reach statistical significance Fig. 3B.

Slow-flow/No-reflow

With four contributing studies, slow-flow or no-reflow was lower with ELCA (28/193 [14.5%] vs. 41/218 [18.8%]; OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.22–2.78; I2 = 31%; τ2 = 0.20) Fig. 3C.

Short-term mortality

Three studies reported mortality, which was lower with ELCA (3/130 [2.3%] vs. 12/138 [8.7%]; OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.02–4.04; P = 0.19; I2 = 0%) [GLMM: OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.07–0.92]. The estimate did not reach significance under small-sample inference, and the events are sparse; the GLMM was directionally concordant but nominally significant, a discrepancy reflecting the sparse-data setting rather than a robust effect Fig. 3D and 4A.

Short-term MACCE

Four studies reported MACCE, which was lower with ELCA (3/193 [1.6%] vs. 19/218 [8.7%]; OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.04–1.26; P = 0.07; I2 = 0%) [GLMM: OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.05–0.56]. As for mortality, the difference was not significant once small-sample inference was applied Figs. 3E and 4B.

Stratum B: ELCA vs. PCI alone

Final TIMI 3 Flow

Four studies contributed after propensity matching. Final TIMI 3 flow was achieved in 560/642 (87.2%) versus 542/605 (89.6%) (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.38–2.03; I2 = 29%; τ2 = 0.081). The analysis was dominated by Matsuda 2025/STELA, whose matched cohort showed a lower rate with ELCA (310/368 vs. 328/367; OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.41–0.98) Fig. 5A.

MBG 3

Three studies reported MBG 3, with no significant difference (410/546 [75.1%] vs. 370/498 [74.3%]; OR 1.06, 95% CI 0.13–8.43; P = 0.92). Heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 80%; τ2 = 0.391; Q P = 0.009), reflecting opposite directions of effect in Kujiraoka 2023 and the STELA matched cohort Fig. 5B.

Slow-flow/No-reflow

Four studies contributed; Dörr 2007 was not estimable (zero events in both arms). Across the three estimable studies the event rates were 147/715 (20.6%) versus 135/671 (20.1%) (OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.29–3.02) [GLMM: OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.69–1.36]. Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 74%; τ2 = 0.167; Q P = 0.018), because Kujiraoka 2023 favored ELCA while the STELA matched cohort favored PCI alone Figs. 5C and 4C.

Short-term mortality

Three studies reported mortality, which was numerically lower with ELCA (18/619 [2.9%] vs. 33/619 [5.3%]; OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.05–3.80; I2 = 46%). In the unadjusted cohorts the counts were 22/678 (3.2%) versus 185/2,417 (7.7%) (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.12–1.53), so propensity adjustment does not change the direction of effect, and neither analysis is precise enough to establish a mortality benefit Supplementary Table S3. Figure 5D.

Short-term MACE

Two studies reported a composite endpoint over incompatible ascertainment horizons (in-hospital MACCE in STELA versus approximately two-year MACE in Iiya 2025), with considerable heterogeneity (I2 = 89%), and were therefore not pooled: 46/368 (12.5%) versus 45/368 (12.2%) in the STELA matched cohort (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.66–1.59) and 3/180 (1.7%) versus 20/180 (11.1%) in Iiya 2025 (OR 0.14, 95% CI 0.04–0.46). The discordance reflects the difference in follow-up horizon and endpoint definition as much as any device effect, and the two estimates should be considered independently Fig. 5E.

Leave-one-out influence analysis

Because the STELA registry contributed most of the weight to every Stratum B outcome, the leave-one-out analysis is reported in full for that stratum Fig. S1. Omitting STELA moved every estimate away from the null and eliminated the between-study heterogeneity: TIMI 3 flow OR 1.22 (95% CI 0.32–4.71; I2 = 0%), MBG 3 OR 0.92, slow-flow/no-reflow OR 0.71 (0.07–6.76; I2 = 0%), and mortality OR 0.24. No estimate became significant, and no omission reversed a conclusion. The registry therefore attenuates the pooled effect and is the source of the observed inconsistency rather than the driver of any apparent benefit. In Stratum A, leave-one-out omission produced no material change. Excluding the two Stratum B studies at serious risk of bias, or the era-based study, moved the angiographic estimates towards PCI alone without producing a significant result Supplementary Table S3.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of ten comparative studies and over 3,500 patients with ACS finds that adjunctive ELCA-containing strategies show no statistically significant advantage over either comparator once small-sample inference and adjusted estimates are used. The largest point estimate, MBG 3 against manual aspiration thrombectomy, remains directionally favorable but rests on two studies with a very wide confidence interval. All pooled outcomes are of very low certainty.

The dissociation between equivalent epicardial patency and tissue-level perfusion aligns with the proposed mechanism of ELCA: unlike aspiration thrombectomy, it vaporizes thrombus in situ, generating inert debris too small to obstruct the microcirculation [10, 12]. In vitro, ELCA also reduced platelet reactivity (the “stunned platelet” phenomenon), an experimental observation of unproven clinical relevance [31].

In this analysis, short-term mortality did not favor ELCA. Against PCI alone, the earlier association rested on unadjusted counts from a single registry contributing more than 80% of the weight, which itself reported a propensity-matched analysis showing no association (adjusted HR 0.951, 95% CI 0.529–1.712); the matched estimates yield an OR of 0.44 (0.05–3.80). Against aspiration thrombectomy, mortality rested on only 15 events. Biological plausibility is not absent, since each 1% increase in microvascular obstruction raises mortality by 14% before infarct-size adjustment (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.09–1.19) [5], but CMR data indicating that microvascular obstruction without intramyocardial hemorrhage may not independently predict mortality weaken the causal chain from improved blush to survival [32].

As noted, routine aspiration thrombectomy is no longer recommended (Class 3, No Benefit) due to lack of mortality benefit and a potential stroke risk [6, 7, 9]. Contemporary guidance for high thrombus burden and no-reflow favors selective bailout rather than routine mechanical thrombus removal, and this is the context in which adjunctive ELCA is positioned [33]. ELCA, with its different photoablation mechanism, may represent an alternative, but the guidelines do not mention it, reflecting the paucity of randomized evidence. The CARMEL trial (n = 151) reported high device and angiographic success with greater benefit in thrombus-rich lesions [34], and the Cardiovascular Laser Society endorsed ELCA for thrombotic lesions [11]; that endorsement predates the largest registry analysis, which found no reduction in adverse cardiac events. No stroke signal has been reported, likely because of in-situ vaporization or possibly because of small samples and underreporting.

The MBG-3 result against aspiration thrombectomy (OR 3.57, 95% CI 0.07–185.10) is the most mechanistically coherent finding. The 30 percentage-point absolute difference would be clinically meaningful: MBG 3 is associated with 3% mortality versus 23% for MBG 0/1, and MBG 2/3 independently predicts lower 3-year mortality (adjusted HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.39–0.82) [35, 36]. However, this finding rests on 196 patients from two studies, both at serious risk of bias, neither of which enrolled STEMI exclusively, and both with unblinded endpoint ascertainment; the interval spans more than three orders of magnitude. Estimates should therefore be read in light of their GRADE ratings and their behavior under sensitivity analyses Supplementary Table S3, not point estimates alone. Against PCI alone, MBG 3 showed no difference (OR 1.06, 0.13–8.43).

Final TIMI-3 flow did not differ in either stratum (Stratum A: OR 1.58, 95% CI 0.67–3.75; Stratum B: OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.38–2.03), with near-ceiling rates (> 80% in both arms) limiting discriminative capacity; TIMI-3 flow is necessary but insufficient for myocardial reperfusion [37]. Slow-flow/no-reflow was non-significant in both strata, and its inconsistency across studies likely reflects differing definitions and era-related antiplatelet imbalances rather than a device effect.

Clinical heterogeneity

Beyond comparator stratification, the evidence base is heterogeneous in patient selection, thrombus burden, procedural protocol, follow-up (index hospitalization to approximately two years), and endpoint definition (in-hospital MACCE to multi-year target-vessel MACE, with two incompatible slow-flow definitions). These sources do not all bias in the same direction: enrichment of the ELCA arms for high thrombus burden would bias against ELCA on angiographic endpoints, whereas preferential selection of younger and more stable patients would bias toward apparent mortality benefit. A STEMI-restricted analysis could not be meaningfully performed in the aspiration stratum, so those results apply to predominantly STEMI ACS populations, not to STEMI specifically.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths: PRISMA 2020/Cochrane methodology with prospective PROSPERO registration; pre-specified comparator stratification; dual risk-of-bias and GRADE assessment; independent reproduction of every pooled estimate from primary event counts; use of adjusted in preference to crude estimates; and a pre-specified sensitivity-analysis programme comprising leave-one-out, restricted and non-overlapping-cohort analyses, and a GLMM sparse-event analysis.

Several limitations constrain interpretation. The evidence is overwhelmingly observational (9/10 studies), with the only included RCT underpowered and part-funded by the device manufacturer [27]. ELCA was preferentially used in lesions with higher thrombus burden Table 2, and unmeasured confounders such as operator expertise and patient frailty may still influence results [38]. All four Stratum A studies are at serious risk of bias, so no risk-of-bias-restricted sensitivity analysis was possible in that stratum and its estimates cannot be separated from confounding by indication.

Because ELCA was almost always part of a broader procedural strategy, pooled estimates compare ELCA-containing strategies versus comparators, not isolated laser photoablation. Co-interventions were inconsistently reported and unevenly distributed Table 3, a primary threat to validity. Two era-based studies had marked antiplatelet imbalances (prasugrel loading 82.1% vs 0% in Kujiraoka 2023; 100% vs 40% in Shibata 2019) alongside concurrent changes in stent technology, conflating device, and era effects, which the authors of both studies acknowledge likely favored the ELCA arm [26, 28].

Four studies came from institutions that also contributed to the multicenter STELA registry (Kujiraoka 2023, Masuda 2022, Arai 2023, and Iiya 2025), so some patients may have been counted more than once, violating the independence assumption; excluding those single-center studies did not change any Stratum B conclusion Supplementary Table S3.

Endpoint ascertainment was unblinded and non-core-laboratory in eight of ten studies, introducing detection bias for subjective angiographic endpoints [39], and geographic concentration limits generalizability. Few studies per outcome precluded funnel-plot analysis; the expected direction of publication bias would inflate the benefit. ELCA also has a steep learning curve, with complication rates falling with operator experience [40, 41], and operator volume was unreported. Lack of individual patient data prevented subgroup analyses by catheter size, fluence, thrombus burden grade or onset-to-balloon time [42].

Clinical implications

Current evidence does not support the routine use of ELCA during primary PCI. Although ELCA is used mainly as bailout after conventional measures have failed in large-thrombus-burden lesions, any mortality benefit remains unproven: in the largest registry, the association disappeared after propensity matching, and the same matched cohort showed worse corrected TIMI frame count and higher peak CK-MB with ELCA. Rather than adoption, these data support a randomized trial in high-thrombus-burden lesions at experienced, high-volume centers.

Future directions

Future multicenter RCTs should include blinded core-lab adjudication, CMR quantification of MVO and infarct size, standardized MACE definitions with ≥ 12-month follow-up, stratification by TIMI thrombus grade and ELCA catheter size, pre-specified recording of all co-interventions, and geographically diverse enrolment [32, 39, 40]. The forthcoming trial UMIN000032954 may provide the first adequately controlled data.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that adjunctive ELCA-containing strategies were not associated with improved epicardial flow, myocardial blush, or short-term mortality once small-sample inference and adjusted estimates from the dominant contributing registry were used, and all pooled outcomes were graded as very low certainty. Given the scarcity of randomized data, the predominance of observational designs, substantial clinical and definitional heterogeneity, and loss of the mortality signal on adjusted analysis, these estimates are hypothesis-generating and should not guide routine practice. Their main value is in establishing effect-size priors for multicenter RCTs with blinded core-laboratory adjudication in patients with high thrombus burden.
