Perceived organizational support as a mediator between role clarity and work engagement among neurology specialist nurses: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xueping Ma, Yinping Li, Enzhi Li, Yingpu Feng, Rong Yang, Dong Zhou, Yan Liang, Jinmei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpecialist nursing has become an integral part of the healthcare system, with neurology specialist nurses playing vital roles in providing high-quality specialized care to patients with neurological disorders. Role clarity and work engagement (WE) are associated with various aspects of nursing practice and patient outcomes. Our aim was to investigate the levels of role clarity and WE among neurology specialist nurses in China and to explore how role clarity relates to WE.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2025 and July 2025 in China. Through convenience sampling, a total of 343 neurology specialist nurses from 11 provinces were initially invited, of whom 279 completed an online survey. The survey included a questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, as well as the role clarity scale, the Perceived Organizational Support Scale (POSS), and the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES). A mediation model was employed to test direct, indirect, and total effects using the bootstrap method. The mediating effect of perceived organizational support (POS) was analyzed using the SPSS process macro (Model 4).ResultsA total of 266 valid questionnaires were included in the analysis. Among neurology specialist nurses in China, role clarity and WE were at moderate and high levels, respectively. There were significantly positive correlations among role clarity, POS, and WE (p < 0.001). Furthermore, mediation analyses revealed that the positive relationship between role clarity and WE was mediated by POS (B = 0.758, 95% CI [0.510, 1.042]), whereas role clarity did not have a significant direct effect on WE (B = 0.061, 95% CI [-0.330, 0.424]).ConclusionPOS appeared to serve as a mediator in the positive relationship between role clarity and WE among neurology specialist nurses. Our findings highlight the value of clarifying the roles of these nurses and fostering a supportive climate to enhance perceived organizational support, strategies that are associated with improved work engagement. However, the observational nature of this study limits causal inferences, and further longitudinal studies are needed.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

The growing burden of neurological disorders in China’s aging population, including dementia, Parkinson’s disease, and ischemic stroke [1], has resulted in a substantial need for neurology-specialized nursing care. Neurological nurses have to manage a range of complex symptoms and care needs associated with these diseases, such as hypertension, consciousness disorders, movement deficiencies, as well as the care associated with feeding tubes, urinary catheters, and rehabilitation [2]. Most of these nurses experience high levels of burnout [2]. These challenges mean that neurological nurses require more organizational support, including increased training opportunities, enhanced autonomy, and greater empowerment [2]. Specialist nurses are registered nurses with substantial clinical experience in their field, systematic specialty training, and specific certifications, all of which contribute to their high level of expertise [3]. Compared with general nurses, specialist nurses possess deeper theoretical knowledge and advanced clinical expertise in their field, enabling them to play vital roles in providing advice and support, teaching, engaging in research, managing complex cases, and providing specialized consultations [4–6]. Additional, they face higher levels of role stress than general nurses in China, which is reflected in role overload and role conflict [5]. Neurology specialist nurses play vital roles in reducing patients’ waiting times and length of hospitalization, avoiding unnecessary hospital admissions, reducing medical costs, and promoting continuity of care for individuals with long-term neurological conditions [7, 8]. The training and certification program for neurology specialist nurses in China was first initiated in 2012 and has promoted the specialized development of neurology nursing in China. Notably, China lacks a standardized and perfected training and certification system for specialist nurses. In developed countries, the role of neuroscience nurse specialists as providing high-quality, specialty care to patients has long been well-established [9]. However, the role of neurology specialist nurses in China is not clearly defined, with no formal guidelines outlining their specialized responsibilities nor dedicated full-time positions established. This gap means they are likely to perform both their core nursing duties and additional tasks from their specialist role, potentially leading to role overload and conflict. Research has consistently shown that clear role definition is associated with nurses’ greater autonomy, decision-making, and overall performance [10]. Understanding the role clarity of neurology specialist nurses might facilitate their optimal performance.

Role clarity refers to the degree to which employees understand their role expectations and the methods for achieving them [11]. It involves information about the tasks and performance levels expected in the specific role, as well as the behaviors required to meet these goals [12]. In clinical practice, the roles and priority tasks of specialist nurses vary according to patients’ needs and health conditions, leading to potential role clarity issues and role ambiguity [13]. Role ambiguity and role conflict among nurses may be linked to role stress and diminished work engagement [14]. Role clarity has been shown to decrease stress levels among newly registered nurses [15], reduce job-related uncertainty, and increase job satisfaction and job embeddedness [16, 17]. However, for neurology specialist nurses facing complex demands, role clarity and its correlation with key outcomes have not yet been fully elucidated, which has affected managers’ corresponding efforts to clarify the roles of their specialist nurses.

Work engagement (WE) is defined as a positive, fulfilling state of mind about work, characterized by vigor, dedication and absorption [18]. The development of specialist nursing teams is fundamental to the progress and quality enhancement of nursing, while a high level of work engagement among nurses serves as a key source of competitive strength for healthcare institutions [6]. Previous studies have shown that sufficient WE among nurses is associated with positive individual and organizational outcomes, including greater initiative, higher job satisfaction, and stronger retention intentions, as well as improved care quality and reduced hospital mortality rates [18, 19]. However, specialist nurses differ from general nurses in terms of nursing workload, psychological stress, and practice environment [5]. Thus, investigating the WE of specialist nurses and its associated factors is essential to help nurse managers identify individuals with low engagement, offer targeted support, and ultimately ensure the quality of specialized care. Although the relationship between WE and positive outcomes for both nurses and patients has been extensively explored, there is limited research focusing specifically on the specialist nurses population. Work engagement has been studied among nurses in specialties such as ophthalmology [20] and dentistry [21]. However, a gap remains regarding neurology specialist nurses. Given that they manage long-term, complex patient needs, investigating their work engagement is essential.

Among the factors associated with WE, perceived organizational support (POS) has received increasing attention. The concept of POS, first proposed by Eisenberger [22], denotes employees’ perception of the extent to which their organization values their contributions and is concerned with their well-being [23]. In nursing settings, POS refers to nurses’ perception of the degree to which the hospital values their achievement and is concerned about their physical and mental health [24]. Previous studies have indicated that enhanced POS is associated with reduced nurse burnout and turnover intentions, as well as improved job satisfaction among nurses and a lower incidence of adverse patient events [25, 26]. The positive relationships between POS and WE among nurses have also been confirmed, and these relationships are mediated by several psychological features and emotional intelligence [24, 27–30]. This general finding highlights the need to explore the relationship between POS and WE among specialist nurses, as their unique roles may shape their experiences of support and clinical practice.

As previously noted, the unique challenges in the work of neurology specialist nurses generate a distinct need for role clarity and organizational support. The positive relationship between POS and WE has been widely explored among nurses. However, evidence on the relationship between role clarity and WE is inconsistent, with reported findings ranging from positive correlation to no significant association [31, 32]. These inconsistencies likely arise from variations in sample populations and work settings, which warrant further investigation. Current evidence regarding the interrelationships and specific mechanisms among role clarity, POS, and WE in specialist nurse populations remains scarce. Exploring these complex interactions in neurology specialist nurses could shed valuable light on strategies for enhancing care within this specialty.

WE has frequently been studied within the job demands-resources (JD-R) framework [33], which reveals the motivational processes that enhance WE and proposes that enhanced WE occurs under conditions of high levels of job resources and personal resources. According to the JD-R model (Fig. 1), abundant job and personal resources can facilitate the motivational process (e.g., work engagement), ultimately enhancing job performance [33]. Organizational support (job resources) and self-efficacy (personal resources) have been confirmed to be positively correlated with WE [23]. Based on the presented evidence and the JD-R model, this study explores the relationships among role clarity, POS and WE in neurology specialist nurses, aiming to address gaps in existing research.

Methods

Purpose of this study

The aim of the study was to investigate the levels of role clarity and WE among neurology specialist nurses and explore the mechanisms through which role clarity is linked to WE. We propose a hypothetical model that elaborates the interactions among these three variables (Fig. 2). The hypotheses we propose are as follows:

Hypothesis 1

Role clarity is positively related to work engagement among neurology specialist nurses.

Hypothesis 2

Role clarity is positively related to perceived organizational support.

Hypothesis 3

Perceived organizational support is positively related to work engagement.

Hypothesis 4

Perceived organizational support mediates the relationship between role clarity and work engagement. Specifically, we predict a significant positive indirect effect of role clarity on work engagement through perceived organizational support.

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted between April 2025 and July 2025. Convenience sampling was employed to enroll eligible participants. Due to limitations in personnel and resources, we used this non-probability sampling approach to obtain preliminary exploratory data. Neurology specialist nurses working in public hospitals throughout China were recruited. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being a nurse holding a nursing license working in a department of neurology for at least one year; (2) having completed systematic training, passed the examination, and possessing a neurology-specialized nurse certificate; and (3) providing informed consent and participating voluntarily in this research. The exclusion criteria included (1) individuals who were absent from their position on sick or maternity leave during the investigation and (2) standardized training nurses or visiting nurses. This study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist.

Samples

PASS 15 was utilized to estimate the appropriate sample size for the study. For multiple linear regression, we set α at 0.05, the power at 0.9, and the moderate effect size (Cohen’s f²) at 0.15. The number of predictors was 21, resulting in a calculated sample size of 198. The predictors consisted of sociodemographic and occupational variables identified in univariate analyses (including dummy variables for categorical variables), as well as the role clarity and POS scales. Considering the possibility of 20% invalid questionnaires, a final sample of at least 248 cases was needed. Ultimately, 266 neurology specialist nurses were included in the study analysis, which provided adequate power for the primary analyses.

Measurements

General information questionnaire

This questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part focused on the demographic characteristics of the participants, including age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, single-child status, number of children, and educational level. The second part was the occupational characteristics, including professional title, working position, years of work experience as a nurse, years of working in a department of neurology, weekly working hours in the ward, type of work, and level of hospital.

Role clarity scale

The role clarity scale was developed by Rizzo [34] and adapted by Zhang [35]. The Chinese version [35] of the scale was used to measure the participants’ level of role clarity. The scale consists of six items related to individuals’ work role clarity. Each item is scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with the total score ranging from 6 to 42. The last item used reverse scoring and was reversed prior to the analyses. Example items from the role clarity scale include “I have a clear understanding of my rights at work” and “I have clearly defined goals and requirements for my job.” This scale has shown good reliability among Chinese nurses (Cronbach’s α coefficient = 0.86) [17]. Higher scores indicate a higher level of role clarity in the workplace. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.79.

Perceived organizational support scale

The simplified version of the Perceived Organizational Support Scale (POSS) was developed by Eisenberger [22]. The Chinese version of the POSS [36] was used to evaluate the participants’ perceived organizational support and care. This scale consists of 9 items and each item is scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with the total score ranging from 9 to 63. The fifth and seventh items used reverse scoring and were reversed prior to the analyses. This scale has good reliability (Cronbach’s α coefficient = 0.88) and validity (RMSEA = 0.05, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99) among Chinese doctors [37]. The higher the score is, the stronger the individual’s sense of POS. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the POSS was 0.88 in our study.

Utrecht work engagement scale

The shortened version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) was developed by Schaufeli [38].The Chinese version of the UWES [39] was used to estimate the participants’ levels of WE. This 9-item scale measures three dimensions of WE: vigor (three items), dedication (three items), and absorption (three items). Each item is scored from 0 (never) to 6(every day), with the total score ranging from 0 to 54. This scale has shown good reliability (Cronbach’s α coefficient = 0.88) and validity (RMSEA = 0.08, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0 0.90) among Chinese population [40]. Higher scores on the vigor dimension indicate a stronger and more positive emotional state, with nurses feeling energized and motivated about their work. Higher scores on dedication reflect a stronger sense of commitment, loyalty, and pride in one’s work. Higher scores on absorption suggest that nurses are highly absorbed and focus deeply on their tasks. In total, higher total scores are associated with a higher level of WE. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for work engagement was 0.91 in our study.

Data collection and rigor

The Department of Neurology at West China Hospital, accredited as a training base for neurology specialist nurses by the Sichuan Nursing Association, recruits and trains neurology nurses from across China. The person in charge of the training base has set up WeChat groups for each batch of trainees for further communication. We established a link to the electronic questionnaire via the “Wenjuanxing” website (https://www.wjx.cn/), and informed consent declarations and uniform guidelines were embedded in this questionnaire. Using convenience sampling, we recruited qualified participants through these WeChat groups and via cascade dissemination through professional networks. Specifically, after obtaining the participants’ informed consent, we distributed the link to these WeChat groups and invited eligible neurology specialist nurses to complete the online survey. These group members were also encouraged to share the survey link with other eligible neurology specialist nurses who have been trained at other qualified training bases in China. To ensure unique responses and protect participant anonymity, the survey was designed to collect only non-identifiable data and to restrict access to one submission per verified WeChat account. Data integrity was ensured via the “required question” feature of the survey platform. This setting made it technically impossible for participants to submit the survey without answering every question, thereby guaranteeing a dataset with no missing values across all variables. During the investigation, each questionnaire was reviewed, and any submissions that had numerous identical responses across all the scales were excluded to maintain data reliability. Moreover, we took systematic measures during all phases of the study to ensure the accuracy of reverse-scored items, including (1) clearly marking all reverse-scored items in the questionnaire, (2) performing uniform recoding prior to data analysis (transformed score = 8-original score), and (3) assessing the internal consistency of the scales. The questionnaire was accessible exclusively to the researcher who created the survey link to ensure the confidentiality of the data. Detailed study procedures and descriptions of participant characteristics were provided to enhance the study’s transferability.

Data analysis

SPSS 25.0 and Hayes’ PROCESS 3.5 were used for data analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and quantitative variables were presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD). We performed the Mann‒Whitney test or Kruskal‒Wallis H test to evaluate the differences in WE scores between the groups. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships among role clarity, POS, WE, and the quantitative general information variables. Hayes’ PROCESS macro (model 4) in SPSS with a bootstrapping procedure (with 5000 resamples) was used to explore the mediating role of POS in the relationship between role clarity and WE. The mediation effect is considered significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) does not include zero. The process macro was performed using one independent variable (role clarity), one mediator (POS), and one dependent variable (WE). The total effect of role clarity on WE consists of direct and indirect effects. Sociodemographic characteristics (i.e., age, marital status, number of children, professional title, position at work, years of nursing experience, years in a department of neurology, and type of work) were included as covariates on the basis of the results of the univariate analysis. These variables have previously been reported to be associated with nurses’ WE [41]. Additionally, key paths were re-estimated using heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (HC3), confirming the robustness of the mediation findings. Considering the data were self-reported and the repeated use of common scale anchors (e.g., strongly disagree, strongly agree), common method variance bias was examined [42]. On the basis of the recommendation of Podsaokff [42], Harman’s single-factor test was performed. The results revealed that five factors had eigenvalues greater than one, with the first factor explaining 38.18% of the total variance. Then, a confirmatory factor analysis was conducted to compare the hypothesized three-factor model with a single-factor model. The fit indices of the three-factor model were better than those of the single-factor model (Δχ²=1004.54, p < 0.001). Thus, common method bias was not a serious problem in our study.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Approval number: 2025 − 1049). The participants were informed about the purpose and process of the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to questionnaire completion, and they were free to withdraw from the study at any time. All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee, and all data were collected anonymously.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 343 neurology specialist nurses from 11 provinces (e.g., Sichuan, Henan, Fujian) in China were initially invited to participate in this survey. Of these, 279 agreed and completed the questionnaire (response rate: 81.3%). After 13 invalid questionnaires were excluded, 266 valid questionnaires were included in the study (valid rate: 95.3%). Participants details are shown in Table 1. The average age of the participants was 34.88 (SD = 4.72, range 22–52) years, and 54.1% were older than 35 years. The participants were predominantly female (97.4%) and of Han ethnicity (95.9%). Among the participants, 86.5% were married, and 83.4% had at least one child. Additionally, 96.3% of the participants held a bachelor’s degree or higher. The participants had an average of 12.93 (SD = 5.12, range 1–34) years of nursing practice experience, with 65.8% having more than ten years of experience. The average duration of working in a neurology department was 10.92 (SD = 4.84, range 1–34) years, with 53.4% having worked in this department for more than ten years. Approximately three quarters of the participants (75.9%) were on night shift rotation. Several factors were associated with higher levels of WE (Table 1), including being older than 35 years (p = 0.02), being married (p = 0.012), having more children (p = 0.004), holding higher-level professional titles (p < 0.001), being a nurse leader (p = 0.005), having more years of experience in nursing (p < 0.001) or neurology (p = 0.012), and working on a fixed day shift (p = 0.01).

Descriptive statistics and correlations among the main variables

The descriptive statistics for the three key variables and the corresponding dimensions are shown in Table 2. Role clarity was moderate (mean = 35.42, SD = 4.79) among the participants. The item score of the UWES (mean = 4.27, SD = 1.36) indicated a relatively high level of WE, with a slightly higher score in the vigor dimension (mean = 4.52, SD = 1.26). The results of Spearman’s correlation analysis for the main variables are shown in Table 3. Role clarity was positively associated with POS (r = 0.650, p < 0.001) and WE (r = 0.395, p < 0.001). POS was positively correlated with WE (r = 0.551, p < 0.001). The three dimensions of WE were also positively related to role clarity and POS (p < 0.001).

Test of mediation effects

The results of bootstrap analysis are shown in Table 4. The total effect of role clarity on WE was found to be significant after adjusting for demographic and occupational characteristics (B = 0.819, 95% CI [0.532, 1.118]). Role clarity explained 23.3% of the variance in WE (Table 5). The indirect effect of the mediator (POS) was statistically significant (B = 0.758, 95% CI [0.510, 1.042]), whereas the direct effect of role clarity on WE was not significant (B = 0.061, 95% CI [-0.330, 0.424]). Thus, POS showed a significant indirect effect in the association between role clarity and WE. That is, role clarity was linked to WE indirectly through POS in our model. The standardized regression coefficient among the three variables in this mediation model ranged from 0.024 to 0.619 (Table 5). The standardized path coefficients are shown in Fig. 3, and the standardized indirect effect was 0.297. The analysis of the key mediation pathways using heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors (HC3) showed that the significance of all paths remained consistent with the original results (Path a: B = 1.116, SE = 0.116, p < 0.001; Path b: B = 0.680, SE = 0.100, p < 0.001; Path c’: B = 0.061, SE = 0.207, p = 0.768), confirming the robustness of the mediation findings.

Discussion

This study explored the levels of role clarity, POS and WE among neurology specialist nurses and investigated how role clarity links to WE. Among neurology specialist nurses, role clarity was positively associated with WE, and POS accounted for a significant portion of this association, consistent with a mediating role. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine role clarity among specialist nurses in China and to test the mediating role of POS in the relationship between role clarity and WE. Specialist nurses exist in many different fields and in many countries, and findings about their role clarity and work enthusiasm are vital for delivering high-quality specialist nursing care. The mediation analysis elucidated the psychological mechanism underlying the relationship between role clarity and WE, thereby broadening our understanding of WE among specialist nurses.

There have been only a small number of studies on role clarity among nurses. In our study, the role clarity score was moderate (mean = 35.42, SD = 4.79) among neurology specialist nurses, an outcome similar to Fang’s [17] findings among Chinese nurses (mean = 32.67, SD = 5.65). Since inconsistencies and conflicts between actual tasks performed and expected tasks might exist, role ambiguity and role conflict are likely to emerge if job roles are not clearly defined [43]. Given that neurology specialist nursing in China is still in its early stages of development and that unclear job responsibilities are associated with role confusion and conflict [13], medical managers should consider enhancing role clarity for specialist nurses. Organizational support is regarded as a strong social resource that helps relieve negative work outcomes, such as job stress and burnout [44]. The mean POS item score among the participants in our study was 5.48 (SD = 0.96), which is similar to that reported in another study in China (mean = 5.36, SD = 1.8) [28]. In addition to specialist nurses, nurse managers also need adequate POS, as POS has been linked to decreased levels of burnout [45]. These findings suggest that nursing team members require a supportive work environment, particularly in the context of China’s high demand for medical services. The item score of the UWES-9 (mean=4.27, SD=1.36) indicated a high level of WE among our participants, according to Wang’s [46] criteria. However, the level of WE was moderate among nurses in Jordan (mean = 3.46, SD=1.19) [23]. This discrepancy is likely due to variations in participant samples and social cultures. Moreover, the score of the vigor dimension was the highest, while that of the absorption dimension was the lowest; these findings are consistent with those of a previous study [27]. This consistency suggests that the relative pattern of work engagement dimensions may be stable across different nurse groups. Although specialist nurses have shown higher levels of WE and job satisfaction compared with general nurses [47], there remains potential for further improvement in their loyalty and pride in their work, which is ultimately reflected in their work absorption.

We identified a positive relationship between role clarity and WE in neurology specialist nurses, thus confirming Hypothesis 1. Similarly, greater role clarity in nurses was related to higher levels of job embeddedness [17]. Nursing policymakers should note that clarifying the role responsibilities and functions of a new professional role is essential for providing high-quality specialized nursing care [16]. Strategies such as responsibility definition, job analysis, and role negotiation are recommended to help reduce the sources of job uncertainty and enhance role clarity in nursing practice [16]. In addition, Boström et al. [48] reported that role clarity correlates with health promotion experience, peer support, and an empowering leadership style, and also relies on employees’ perception of their work performance, a finding that warrants attention. Moreover, role clarity was shown to be positively related to POS in this study, thus confirming Hypothesis 2. In fact, role clarity involves role conflict and role ambiguity [17]. Role conflict is negatively related to POS and is positively correlated with job stress, and low job stress and adequate POS are positively correlated with the intent to stay among specialist trauma nurses [49]. Our study demonstrated that POS was positively associated with WE among neurology specialist nurses, thus confirming Hypothesis 3 and supporting prior studies [28–30]. This consistency may reflect the universal role of perceived organizational support in the link to professional engagement among nurses. The three dimensions of WE, namely, vigor, dedication, and absorption, are also positively related to POS, which is consistent with Wang’s [50] findings. According to social exchange theory [51], employees are willing to exchange their time, effort, and emotional support required to work effectively when they perceive the relationship with their organization to be positive. This theory supports that a higher perception of organizational support among nurses is associated with a higher level of WE. Our findings are consistent with the JD-R model, which conceptualizes role clarity and POS as personal and job resources that are positively associated with WE. Given the associations among role clarity, POS and WE identified in our study and the previous evidence mentioned above, there may be a deeper association among these variables.

One important finding of this research was that the positive association between role clarity and WE was statistically mediated by POS within our model, although causality cannot be inferred from these cross-sectional data. As shown in the results, role clarity explained 23.3% of the variance in WE among neurology specialist nurses, and the indirect effect through POS was significant. The standardized indirect effect of 0.297 indicates that a one-SD increase in role clarity is associated with a 0.297-SD increase in WE via the mediator, representing an effect of practical significance. In support of Hypothesis 4, the results were consistent with a mediation model. This suggests that in the complex environment of neurological care, the benefits of role clarity may only translate into higher work vigor, dedication, and absorption when specialist nurses feel that the organization values their contribution and provides adequate support. Other unmeasured pathways linking role clarity to WE may exist, meriting further investigation. For instance, role clarity has been shown to affect job embeddedness directly and indirectly through decent work perception [17]. Based on our findings, it is suggested that managers should consider providing adequate emotional and material support for specialist nurses. In China’s increasingly stressful healthcare context, providing a supportive work environment helps to establish a strong psychological bonding between nurses and organizations and to achieve positive work outcomes [52]. Measures such as improving the hardware and software environment, establishing a reasonable professional title advancement system, and improving career development plans are recommended [17].

In addition to its mediating role between role clarity and WE, POS has been shown to play various direct and indirect roles in nurses’ work outcomes. For example, POS reduced nurses’ burden of anxiety and workload, and enhanced their WE through self-efficacy and positive coping strategies [53, 54]. Zhu et al. [55] reported that POS positively moderated the relationship between career growth and affective commitment, thereby enhancing employee engagement. POS was associated with lower insomnia among psychiatric nurses, and this relationship was partially mediated by psychological capital [56]. In a work environment with a high prevalence of physical and emotional abuse, enhanced organizational support mitigated the negative impact of work violence on nurses’ perceived professional identity [57]. Given that patients with encephalitis often present with psychiatric symptoms, and may exhibit violent and aggressive behaviors [58], neurology specialist nurses similarly face a high risk of violence and require adequate organizational support to manage the related stress. These findings indicate that the relationship between POS and nurse outcomes is complex and that the potential significance of role clarity for the critical work outcomes of specialist nurses deserves further exploration.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, it investigates the levels of role clarity and WE among specialist nurses and the relationships between these variables, topics that have rarely been addressed in previous studies. Second, this is the first study to demonstrate the significant contributions of POS to the relationship between role clarity and WE. These findings enhance our understanding of role clarity and WE among neurology specialist nurses, who play crucial roles in neurology specialist nursing care and the development of the nursing team. Role clarification and sufficient organizational support for specialist nurses are associated with their work motivation and job performance. Our findings provide a primarily theoretical basis for healthcare managers to develop strategies aimed at enhancing the work performance of specialist nurses.

However, several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, our conclusions are based on a hypothesized conceptual model derived from a literature review and the JD-R model. However, the possible effects of other variables on WE may be complex. Therefore, further exploration of additional unmeasured pathways through which role clarity is associated with nurses’ critical outcomes is warranted. Second, the participants were recruited using convenience sampling, and the number of participants varied significantly across different provinces, which may have resulted in selection bias and limited representativeness for our target population. Therefore, random or stratified sampling should be considered in future studies. However, the participants were recruited from different economic regions, including western, central, and eastern China, which may increase the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, the participants were recruited through WeChat groups of training base and professional networks, which may have skewed the sample toward nurses with potentially stronger organizational attachment and exacerbated selection bias. Future research should adopt broader, probability-based recruitment strategies to minimize this bias. The use of WeChat for survey distribution may have introduced selection bias by overrepresenting nurses with greater familiarity with digital tools. Future studies should employ mixed-mode data collection combining online and offline channels. Third, given that 97.4% of the participants we enrolled were female, the findings may have limited generalizability to male nurses. Future research should validate these findings in a sample that more closely reflects the gender composition of the nursing workforce in China. Fourth, causal relationships could not be established among the variables in our model because there are no temporal relationships among the variables in a cross-sectional study. Therefore, prospective longitudinal or interventional studies are warranted to determine definite causal relationships among the variables in our hypothetical model. Moreover, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may have introduced the potential for social desirability bias. We suggest that future studies adopt more objective measures or multi-source data to minimize the impact of such bias.

Conclusion

This study provided insights into how role clarity and POS are correlated with WE among neurology specialist nurses in China. We identified a moderate level of role clarity and a high level of WE. Role clarity was positively associated with WE, and this association appeared to be mediated by POS, with a significant indirect effect. These findings suggest possible mechanisms for fostering positive work performance among specialist nurses and highlight the value of role clarification and organizational support.

Implications for nursing and health policy

Healthcare managers should realize the importance of defining the role expectations and tasks of specialist nurses and empower them to achieve the best specialized nursing practice. They can consider identifying work situations that are negatively related to WE and creating a supportive work environment. Our findings suggest the value of developing management strategies aimed at clarifying new professional roles and bolstering organizational support for specialist nurses, measures that are associated with improved job performance.
