Permutations with a fixed number of 321 321 patterns
Michael Waite

Abstract
. We bound the number of permutations with a fixed number r r of 321 321 patterns by a constant times the number of permutations which avoid 321 321 . We use this to show that the ordinary generating function for permutations with r r copies of 321 321 patterns is not rational.



1.
Introduction

We say that a permutation
p
contains a pattern
q=q_{1}...q_{k}
if there is a subsequence
n_{1},...,n_{k}
so that
p_{n_{i}}<p_{n_{j}}
if and only if
q_{i}<q_{j}
. We say that
p
contains
r
copies of
q
if there are
r
different such subsequences
n_{1},...,n_{k}
.

We denote the set of permutations of length
n
that avoid
q
as
S_{n}(q)
, and we denote the set of permutations with
r
copies of
q
as
S_{n,r}(q)
.

It is well-known that
|S_{n}(321)|=\frac{1}{n+1}{2n\choose n}
. A formula for
|S_{n,1}(321)|
was first found by Noonan
[
2
]
, for
|S_{n,2}(321)|
by Fulmek
[
3
]
, and for
|S_{n,3}(321)|
and
|S_{n,4}(321)|
by Callan
[
4
]
.

Most recently, Bóna
[
1
]
showed that the generating function for
|S_{n,1}(k(k-1)...1)|
is not rational for all
k\geq 3
by constructing an injection from
S_{n,1}(k(k-1)...1)
to
S_{n+2,1}((k-1)k...1)
and using the upper bound given by this injection to make an analytic argument.

In this paper, we show that the ordinary generating function for
|S_{n,r}(321)|
is not rational for all
r\geq 1
. To do this, for each possible arrangement of
r
copies of
321
patterns we will construct an injection from the set of permutations which contain
r
copies of
321
patterns in exactly that arrangement to a set of pairs of permutations each with fewer than
r
copies of
321
. In the case where
r=1
, the injection we get is related to but different from the one in
[
1
]
. Our injections are actually more similar to the bijections in
[
4
]
.

The bounds given by these injections will let us bound
|S_{n,r}(321)|
from above by a constant times
|S_{n}(321)|
. Once we have done this, an analytic argument will show that the ordinary generating function for
|S_{n,r}(321)|
cannot be rational.

2.
The injections

We will need the following definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
w
be a permutation of length
m\leq n
on some subset of
\{1,...,n\}
. Define
\text{reduce}(w)
to be the permutation on
\{1,...,m\}
whose entries are in the same relative order as
w
, and say that
w
reduces to
\text{reduce}(w)
.

Example 2.2
.

If
w=8452
, then
\text{reduce}(w)=4231
.

Definition 2.3
.

Say that
p
is of type
q
if the subsequence of all entries of
p
which participate in
321
patterns reduces to
q
. Denote
S_{n,r}^{q}(321)
to be the set of permutations on
n
of type
q
with
r
copies of
321
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
p=125643
. Then
p
has
321
patterns
543
and and
643
, so the subsequence of all entries that participate in
321
patterns is
5643
. This reduces to
3421
, so
p
is of type
3421
, and
p\in S_{6,2}^{3421}(321)
.

Now we define the map we need and prove that it is an injection. This will be immediately followed by an example of applying the map and demonstrating its injectivity.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
n,r\geq 0
are such that
S_{n,r}^{q}(321)
is nonempty. Let
b_{1}
be the first entry of
q
which takes the role of
2
in a
321
pattern. Suppose there are
j
entries in
q
to the left of
b_{1}
which are greater than
b_{1}
, and
i
entries in
q
to the right of
b_{1}
which are smaller than
b_{1}
. Then there exist
0\leq s,t<r
and an injection

\phi:S_{n,r}^{q}(321)\hookrightarrow\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}S_{k-1+i,s}(321)\times S_{n-k+j,t}(321).

Proof.

First we will define
\phi
. Let
p\in S_{n,r}^{q}(321)
. Let
\beta_{1}
be the first entry of
p
which takes the role of a
2
in a
321
pattern of
p
, and write

Let
k
be the position of
\beta_{1}
in
p
. Let
\gamma_{1},...\gamma_{j}
be the entries in
p
which appear before
\beta_{1}
and are greater than
\beta_{1}
(in the order in which they occur in
p
), and let
\alpha_{1},...\alpha_{i}
be the entries in
p
which appear after
\beta_{1}
and are less than
\beta_{1}
(in the order in which they occur in
p
). To see that there are exactly
j

\gamma
’s and
i

\alpha
’s, note that all of these entries are in
321
patterns with
\beta_{1}
, and
\beta_{1}
in
p
corresponds with
b_{1}
in
q
.

We construct two subsequences of
p
as follows:

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=w_{1}\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle\tau

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}...\gamma_{j}w_{2}.

Note that
\sigma
is of size
k-1+i
and
\tau
is of size
n-k+j
. Also note that
\sigma
and
\tau
do not contain
\beta_{1}
, so each must have at least one fewer
321
pattern than
p
. Furthermore, the number of
321
patterns that
\sigma
has is exactly the number of
321
patterns that
q
has which only include entries before
b_{1}
, and entries after
b_{1}
and smaller than
b_{1}
; let
s
be this number. Similarly the number of
321
patterns that
\tau
has is exactly the number of
321
patterns that
q
has which only include entries before
b_{1}
and larger than
b_{1}
, and entries after
b_{1}
; let
t
be this number.

Now, set

\phi(p)=(\text{reduce}(\sigma),\text{reduce}(\tau)).

Note that
\text{reduce}(\sigma)\in S_{k-1+i,s}(321)
and that
\text{reduce}(\tau)\in S_{n-k+j,t}(321)
, as we expect.

We show that
\phi
is injective in two steps:

1. The subsequences
\sigma
and
\tau
can be recovered from
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
,
\text{reduce}(\tau)
, and
q
:

First, note that from
q
, we can obtain
i
and
j
. The length of
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
plus the length of
\text{reduce}(\tau)
will always be
n+i+j-1
, so we can obtain what
n
(the length of the original permutation) is.

Now, note that
\sigma
contains all of the values
1,...,\beta_{1}-1
(and also possibly some values greater than
\beta_{1}
). Thus when we reduce
\sigma
, these values remain unchanged. In particular, all of the values for the entries given by
\alpha

\sigma
are unchanged in
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
.

Likewise,
\tau
contains all of the values
\beta_{1}+1,...,n
(and also possibly some values smaller than
\beta_{1}
), so when we reduce
\tau
, all of these values will be decreased by exactly the same amount, say
C
. Then the largest entry of
\text{reduce}(\tau)
is
n-C
, and since we have determined
n
, we can determine
C
. Now take
(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
to be the permutation given by adding
C
to each entry in
\text{reduce}(\tau)
. Then every entry in
\tau
with value greater than
\beta_{1}
will have the same value in the same position in
(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
. In particular, all of the values for the entries given by
\gamma
’s in
\tau
are unchanged in
(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
.

Now we can identify the final
i
values in
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
as the values of
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{i}
in
\sigma
, and the first
j
values in
(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
as the values of
\gamma_{1},...\gamma_{j}
in
\tau
.

In
\sigma
, the only values which differ from the values in
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
are greater than
\beta_{1}
, thus must occur within
w_{1}
, and thus must be the entries
\gamma_{1},...\gamma_{j}
. To reconstruct
\sigma
from
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
, all we need to do is replace the
j
largest entries in
\text{reduce}(\sigma)
by the corresponding

In
\tau
, the only values which differ from the values in
(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
are less than
\beta_{1}
, thus must occur within
w_{2}
, and thus must be the entries

. To reconstruct
\tau
from
(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
, all we need to do is replace the

(\text{reduce}(\tau))^{\prime}
with the corresponding
\alpha

2. The permutation

can be recovered from the subsequences
\sigma
and
\tau
:

Since we know

and

, we can obtain

and
w_{2}
by removing the final

\sigma
and removing the first

\tau
. Now

and
w_{2}
contain

and

, so it must be the case that
\beta_{1}
is the remaining value. Now we can recover

since we know
w_{1},w_{2}
and
\beta_{1}
.
∎

Corollary 2.7
.

There is a constant
K_{r}
so that

|S_{n,r}(321)|\leq K_{r}|S_{n}(321)|

for all
n
.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
r
. For
q
an arbitrary permutation of size at most
3r
(with every entry participating in a
321
pattern) we have

|S_{n,r}^{q}(321)|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}|S_{k-1+i,s}(321)||S_{n-k+j,t}(321)|

for some
0\leq s,t<r
. By induction we get

|S_{n,r}^{q}(321)|\leq K_{s}K_{t}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|S_{k-1+i,0}(321)||S_{n-k+j,0}(321)|

for some constants
K_{s},K_{t}
. Since the numbers
S_{m,0}
are the Catalan numbers then we know
S_{m+1,0}\leq 4S_{m,0}
for all
m\geq 0
, so

|S_{n,r}^{q}(321)|\leq 4^{i+j}K_{s}K_{t}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|S_{k-1,0}(321)||S_{n-k,0}(321)|=4^{i+j}K_{s}K_{t}|S_{n,0}(321)|

by the recurrence for the Catalan numbers. Letting
K=\max_{1\leq i\leq r-1}{K_{i}}
and using that
i+j\leq 3r
, we get

|S_{n,r}^{q}(321)|\leq 4^{3r}K^{2}|S_{n,0}(321)|.

Finally, we get

\displaystyle|S_{n,r}(321)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3r}\sum_{q\in S_{i}}|S_{n,r}^{q}(321)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{3r}\sum_{q\in S_{i}}4^{3r}K^{2}|S_{n,0}(321)|

\displaystyle=(1!+2!+...+(3r)!)4^{3r}K^{2}|S_{n,0}(321)|.

Setting
K_{r}=(1!+2!+...+(3r)!)4^{3r}K^{2}
, we get

|S_{n,r}(321)|\leq K_{r}|S_{n}(321)|.

∎

3.
Nonrationality

Let
s_{r}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}|S_{n,r}(321)|z^{n}
. First we need the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

The radius of convergence of
s_{r}(z)
is
1/4
.

Proof.

First note that
|S_{n,r}(321)|\geq|S_{n-3,r-1}(321)|
since if
p\in S_{n-3,r-1}(321)
, then
p\ \oplus\ 321\in S_{n,r}(321)
. Thus by induction we get
|S_{n,r}(321)|\geq|S_{n-3r}(321)|
. Since
|S_{n-3r}(321)|\leq|S_{n,r}(321)|\leq K_{r}|S_{n}(321)|
and the numbers
|S_{n}(321)|
have exponential order
4
, then the numbers
|S_{n,r}(321)|
must also have exponential order
4
. Thus
s_{r}(z)
has radius of convergence
1/4
.

∎

Now we can prove the main result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
s_{r}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}|S_{n,r}(321)|z^{n}
. Then
s_{r}(z)
is not rational.

Proof.

Let
s(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}|S_{n}(321)|z^{n}
. Since
|S_{n}(321)|
are the Catalan numbers, we know that
s(z)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4z}}{2z}
and that
s(1/4)=2
.

Since
0\leq|S_{n,r}(321)|\leq K_{r}|S_{n}(321)|
for all
n
, we get that

0\leq s_{r}(1/4)\leq K_{r}\cdot s(1/4)=2K_{r}<\infty.

Since
s_{r}(z)
has radius of convergence
1/4
, then
1/4
is the dominant singularity of
s_{r}(z)
. But since
s_{r}(1/4)
is finite, then
1/4
cannot be a pole for
s_{r}(z)
. Since
s_{r}(z)
has a singularity at
1/4
that is not a pole, then
s_{r}(z)
cannot be rational.
∎
