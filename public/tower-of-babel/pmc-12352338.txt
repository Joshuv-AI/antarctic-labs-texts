The role of vector population variation and climate in Zika virus transmission patterns in Africa: a modelling study
Authors: Jamie M Caldwell, Louis Lambrechts, Noah H Rose
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundZika virus (ZIKV) outbreaks have raised major global health concerns recently, yet reported outbreaks are rare in Africa, where ZIKV was first discovered. Recent studies on Aedes aegypti, the mosquito that transmits ZIKV, might explain this phenomenon. The Ae aegypti subspecies present in Africa shows lower preference for biting humans and reduced susceptibility to ZIKV infection compared with the subspecies distributed outside Africa. Alternatively, climate might strongly limit transmission as it affects multiple traits of ZIKV and the mosquito vector.MethodsWe used a modelling approach informed by empirical data to assess ZIKV transmission risk across Africa. We fitted the model using data from laboratory experiments, and validated the model by comparing predicted transmission suitability with seroprevalence surveys conducted across Africa. Additionally, we used mosquito genetic and climate-based projections to map future ZIKV outbreak risk at 59 urban centres in Africa.FindingsThe model predictions partially and significantly explain historical patterns of ZIKV circulation in Africa (accounting for 46% of the variation in seroprevalence surveys). Mosquito population genetics influence transmission more strongly than climate through two mechanisms: vector–host contact and vector competence. If climate and mosquito genetic population projections are accurate and there are no other changes to drivers of Zika virus transmission in Africa, we predict that approximately three-quarters of the most populous African cities will be suitable for ZIKV outbreaks by the end of the century.InterpretationBoth population-level genomic variation in mosquitoes and climate contribute to the lack of ZIKV outbreaks in Africa. Given the importance of mosquito genetics in driving this pattern, local genomic surveillance of mosquito populations would help predict outbreaks in vulnerable communities. This will become increasingly important with population growth, urbanisation, and climate change.FundingPrinceton University, French Government’s Investissement d’Avenir and France 2030 programmes, MSDAVENIR, and US National Institutes of Health.

Introduction

Zika virus (ZIKV) is an emerging mosquito-borne flavivirus of global concern that has paradoxically caused very few human outbreaks in Africa, despite the presence of highly transmissible viral strains circulating in a sylvatic cycle.1 Since ZIKV was initially discovered in 1947 in Uganda, it has been associated with sporadic human outbreaks in various parts of the world, most notably the 2016–17 epidemic in Latin America.2 However, in Africa, where the virus was initially identified, few ZIKV outbreaks have been reported. The low level of occasional ZIKV spillover to humans in Africa is surprising given active circulation of the virus in a sylvatic cycle, widespread presence of the main mosquito vector, Aedes aegypti, and a climate presumably suitable for transmission throughout much of the continent and year. One explanation is misdiagnosis and under-reporting, which are common issues with closely related diseases.3 However, it is unlikely that insufficient surveillance across Africa alone can explain the unique epidemiology of Zika in Africa because seroprevalence surveys indicate heterogeneous levels of population immunity.4 Thus, a complementary hypothesis to explain the mismatch between apparent transmission suitability and low and heterogeneous ZIKV transmission is that some overlooked aspect(s) of the interplay among the virus, vector, or climate might instead impede viral transmission or disease emergence in Africa. Given the substantial public health implications associated with ZIKV transmission, particularly in relation to congenital complications, gaining deeper insight into natural constraints on transmission would be highly valuable.

Recent studies on mosquito genetic variation have revealed important insights into the reduced risk of ZIKV outbreaks in Africa. Aedes aegypti are native to Africa but now inhabit most tropical and subtropical regions around the world.5 They exist in two different forms, sometimes referred to as subspecies, that differ morphologically,6 genetically,7 and ecologically.8 The native, dark coloured African form, Ae aegypti formosus (Aaf), are generalists found in a variety of habitats including forests, breed in natural sources of standing water such as treeholes and rock pools, and feed on human and non-human mammals. The globally invasive, light coloured form found primarily outside of Africa, Ae aegypti aegypti (Aaa), are dependent on human habitats, primarily breed in artificial water containers, and preferentially feed on humans as a blood source.9 The genetic variation found among Ae aegypti populations is associated with corresponding variation in host preference, whereby mosquito lineages that are predominantly assigned to Aaf display a lower affinity for biting human hosts,10,11 resulting in decreased vector–human contact. However, host availability across urban, peri-urban, and rural contexts likely plays an important role in feeding habits beyond relative host preference alone. Mosquito genetic make-up has also been associated with differential vector competence for ZIKV,12 where Aaf mosquitoes have lower intrinsic ability to acquire and transmit ZIKV, suggesting a potential natural barrier to viral transmission, although the precise genetic mechanisms or selective pressures underlying these differences are not yet known. Thus, mosquito genetic variation might fundamentally shape at least two important traits for transmission.

The same studies that investigated genetic variation between the two forms of Ae aegypti also detected high heterogeneity among mosquito populations within Africa with important implications for outbreak risk. Across the ancestral range of Ae aegypti in sub-Saharan Africa, the geographical structure of Ae aegypti populations are complex, with admixed genetic signatures of both mosquito forms present in many locations.7 This gradient in genetic variation is largely driven by dry season intensity and human population density.10 These findings suggest that transmission risk should be higher in locations with higher levels of shared genetic ancestry with human-specialist mosquitoes (ie, larger Aaa genetic ancestry component; hereafter, Aaa ancestry), because of increased human–vector contact rates and higher vector competence. This hypothesis is supported by findings in Cabo Verde, where mosquitoes have approximately 23% Aaa ancestry and where a large ZIKV outbreak occurred in 2015–16.13 Additionally, in other locations of the Sahel with high levels of Aaa ancestry such as Ouagadougou, Burkina Faso,7,10 there is indirect evidence of ZIKV transmission from serosurveys.14 Together, these independent lines of evidence strongly suggest the importance of local mosquito genetic ancestry on ZIKV transmission risk.

Temperature is another widely acknowledged factor influencing ZIKV transmission risk. Temperature influences mosquito and viral metabolic traits important for vectorial capacity such as survival, biting rate, and the duration of the viral extrinsic incubation period.15,16 An important feature of the relationships between temperature and trait performance is that they vary non-linearly with temperature. As a result, there are temperature ranges highly permissible to viral transmission and ranges that are restrictive because they are either too hot or too cold. Previous research indicates ZIKV transmission risk peaks at 29°C but is possible between approximately 23°C and 34°C.16 There is substantial climate variability in Africa; thus, temperature may limit viral transmission in some parts of the continent where temperatures remain unsuitable throughout the year or become unsuitable during certain times of the year (ie, winter). Importantly, average temperatures in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa are currently highly suitable for ZIKV, and year-round transmission risk is projected to expand throughout the region in the future.17

In this study, we assess the risk of ZIKV transmission across Africa using a modelling approach informed by empirical data on mosquito genetic population variation and temperature-dependent metabolic traits. We verify whether locations with evidence of previous ZIKV transmission have higher levels of human-specialist (Aaa) genetic ancestry and temperatures within the previously identified range of suitability (ie, between 23°C and 34°C). Specifically, we confront this model with data from published seroprevalence surveys from 11 countries to determine the extent to which mosquito ancestry and climate drive ZIKV transmission patterns across Africa. We then use the model to explore ZIKV transmission suitability in the most populous African cities in the present and future. This research aims to provide insights into ZIKV transmission risk in Africa and understand where future control efforts should focus. These questions are particularly pertinent to long-term planning as Africa has the fastest rates of projected population growth and urbanisation compared with all other continents18 and future warming is likely to expand outbreak risk to locations at higher latitudes and elevations.17

Methods

Model derivation

We derived an equation for the basic reproductive ratio (R0) that mathematically codifies critical entomological parameters of transmission associated with the mosquito vector and ZIKV and how they vary with mosquito Aaa ancestry and climate. R0 represents the average number of secondary infections expected from each primary infection in a fully susceptible human population and is a widely used metric to characterise epidemic potential (ie, R0 >1). Using a previously described SEI-SEIR compartmental model for dengue, chikungunya, and Zika,19 we derived an R0 equation using the next generation method (described in detail in appendix pp 1–3): R0=√ω⋅α⋅b⋅EIRμmμm+EIRα⋅pMI⋅NmNh⋅δδ+μhγ+μh The SEI-SEIR model, and derived R0, are parameterised with mosquito and virus traits that can vary with mosquito ancestry and climate (table). Natural human mortality is another parameter in the equation; although it occurs over a much longer time scale than the transmission parameters, we retained it in the model to produce conservative estimates of R0. Removing this parameter, which appears twice in the denominator, would artificially inflate R0 predictions. To determine whether ZIKV outbreaks in Africa are limited by mosquito ancestry, climate, or both, we compared outputs from three versions of this model: (1) an ancestry-only model where climate-dependent parameters are held constant; (2) a climate-only model where ancestry-dependent parameters are held constant; and (3) a full model where parameters vary with both ancestry and climate.

Data

For the ancestry-dependent model parameters, we used data from the only two studies that characterise mosquito biting preference and infection probability across a range of Ae aegypti lineages. Probability of biting a human host (given a mosquito bite) was quantified using host choice experiments with humans and guinea pigs in two-port olfactometers.10 While host availability could influence blood-feeding behaviour in the real world, previous studies demonstrate consistent support that predominantly Aaa populations feed more frequently on humans than predominantly Aaf populations.20,21 Probability of mosquito infection (pMI) was calculated from the oral infectious dose infecting 50% of blood-fed mosquitoes (OID50) as measured through dose–response curves.12 We used OID50 values because real-world viral inoculum is unknown and varies dynamically during the human viraemic period. Experimental OID50 estimates encapsulate variation in ZIKV susceptibility between mosquito populations across a range of infectious doses.12 Therefore, we assumed pMI for the most susceptible mosquito population (ie, mosquitoes from Guadeloupe with 100% Aaa ancestry) was equivalent to the optimal temperature-dependent pMI, and scaled all other mosquito populations’ OID50 values as a fraction of the OID50 for Guadeloupe. In other words, we calculated pMI by dividing the OID50 for Guadeloupe by the OID50 for each population, and then subtracted 0·7 to ensure the Guadeloupe strain pMI equalled 0·3 (ie, the optimal temperature-dependent pMI).

For the temperature-dependent model parameters, we used data from laboratory experiments that measured Ae aegypti life history traits and ZIKV trait responses at different constant temperatures. We used data collated by the most comprehensive synthesis study to date on Ae aegypti for temperature-dependent biting rate, extrinsic incubation period, adult mosquito mortality, and intrinsic incubation period.15 We used similar laboratory experimental data from Tesla and colleagues16 for the probability of human ZIKV infection per bite by an infectious mosquito and the probability of mosquito infection per bite on an infectious host. Presumably the laboratory mosquitoes had 100% Aaa ancestry, but genetic lineages were not reported in the original studies. To our knowledge, there are no studies investigating whether mosquito ancestry can influence thermal performance in Ae aegypti, although recent work suggests adult thermal performance is invariant across populations in a closely related mosquito species.22

For validation, we identified available data from a recent global systematic review of ZIKV seroprevalence.4 We then selected seroprevalence studies from those identified in the review based on the following criteria: (1) study location was in continental Africa; (2) Aaa ancestry and temperature values were within the range of the majority of data used to create the model (Aaa <20%, temperatures between 15°C and 30°C; and (3) if multiple serosurveys occurred in the same location, we selected the most recent sampling time. The resulting dataset included 15 studies from 45 unique locations in 11 countries across rural, peri-urban, and urban areas (appendix pp 3–6). One limitation with using ZIKV seroprevalence data is the potential for cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses, which would conflate ZIKV transmission with transmission of other viruses such as dengue. We complement the main results for all serosurveys with analyses on the subset of serosurveys that tested for neutralising antibodies against ZIKV. This subset of surveys provides more reliable estimates of past ZIKV transmission, but substantially reduces the sample size and geographical breadth of validation data to seven studies from 22 unique locations across six countries. For each serosurvey, we recorded ZIKV seroprevalence data from the original study at the finest spatial resolution available and extracted geographical coordinates for those locations to collate associated ancestry and temperature data. For three studies, seroprevalence was reported in aggregate for multiple locations that were geographically distinct. In these instances, we used the same seroprevalence value across sites but location-specific ancestry and temperature data to predict R0. To avoid pseudoreplication, we correlated country-level seroprevalence with model predictions (ie, median predicted R0 across all sites within a country) and show within-country variation as error bars.

To project ZIKV suitability in near-current and future timepoints, we selected 59 urban centres in continental Africa with current populations of more than 1 million. We extracted geographical coordinates for those locations to collate ancestry and temperature data.

To predict R0 at sites with seroprevalence surveys (validation) and for urban centres at different timepoints (projections), we first collected Aaa ancestry and temperature data as model inputs. We estimated Aaa ancestry through a two-step process. First, we modelled preference for human hosts as a function of human population density, precipitation seasonality, and temperature in the warmest quarter using a previously developed model.10 Second, we used the output of this model (proportion of mosquitoes preferring to bite a human host) to predict the proportion of Aaa ancestry in the population (logit[proportion Aaa + 0·001]; we used the 0·001 additive offset to deal with overlap between true zeros and very low values, but there are other ways one could structure a model to address this issue). Additionally, this approach assumes evolutionary processes track urbanisation and environmental change, which aligns with previous work,11 but in reality could have a time lag. We used mean temperature during the transmission season (ie, during the wettest quarter) as the temperature metric. For validation and projections at the near-current timepoint, we used human population density data (used to estimate Aaa ancestry) from the NASA Socioeconomic Data and Applications Center Gridded Population of the World (version 4) and bioclimatic variables from WorldClim23 for estimating Aaa ancestry (using precipitation seasonality and precipitation in the wettest quarter) and temperature (mean temperature during the wettest quarter). We used the historical WorldClim dataset for validation and projections at the near-current timepoint, which aggregate data over a long time horizon (1970–2020); this is sensible for corresponding with seroprevalence surveys that measure lifetime exposure and to serve as a baseline measurement of ZIKV suitability across the urban centres for the recent past. For projecting future timepoints, we used WorldClim future climate data (metrics averaged over 2081–2100) with both population and climate data projections from CMIP6 SSP 585 from five models: INM-CM4–8, GISS-E2–1-G, NorESM2-MM, MIROC6, and MPI-ESM1.2-HR.24 Using the predicted Aaa ancestry and temperature values as input in the R0 model(s), we generated estimated R0 values for each serosurvey (validation) and urban centre and timepoint (projections).

Model parameters and functional forms

In the ancestry-only model (ie, where traits vary with mosquito ancestry), both probability of biting a human host and probability of mosquito infection increase monotonically, saturating as Aaa ancestry approaches 100% (ie, human specialists). Thus, we fitted both traits with a generalised Michaelis–Menten function. In the ancestry-only model, we set all temperature-dependent traits (with the exception of pMI) to static values based on their performance at the optimal temperature for transmission (29°C based on previous studies15,16). The probability of biting a human host ranges from 0 (exclusive biting on non-human hosts) to 1 (exclusive biting on human hosts).

In the climate-only model (ie, where traits only vary with climate), we fitted the temperature-dependent data described above using unimodal functions because physiologically, metabolic trait performances are limited at high and low temperatures. We fitted biting rate, extrinsic incubation rate, and mosquito mortality rate with Brière functions Constant×temperature×(temperature−minimumtemperature)×√(maximumtemperature−temperature) and probability of mosquito infection with a Gaussian function. In the climate-only model, we set the probability of biting a human host to 1 (ie, exclusively biting humans).

For the full model that includes ancestry-dependent and climate-dependent traits, we used the ancestry-dependent pMI as there are fewer traits that vary with ancestry and the model is not particularly sensitive to climate-dependent pMI based on a previous sensitivity analysis.15

Model fitting

We fitted model parameters to data and estimated hyperparameters using R version 4.1.0 and Stan version 2.33. We simultaneously fitted all model parameters listed in the table to their referenced experimental datasets. For the hyperparameters, we used weakly informative normally distributed priors with mean values based on previous studies and standard deviations of varying values based on the relationship between a standard deviation change in X and its corresponding change in Y.25 We ran the model using three chains and 10 000 iterations and assessed convergence based on visual assessment of trace plots as well as R-hat and effective sample size values.

Validation

We validated the model internally and externally. Internally, we validated trait fits by comparing estimated parameter values with data from laboratory experiments used to create the model. We visually assessed these posterior predictive checks with scatterplots, comparing observed experimental data with model predicted values (median and 95% CI) for each data point. For external validation, we compared model predictions with published ZIKV seroprevalence surveys using linear regression and assessed the contribution of each competing model to explain variation in ZIKV suitability based on the R2 value.

Projections

We predicted R0 values from the best fit model for 59 urban centres across Africa for two timepoints (a near-current baseline [1970–2020] and the future [2081–2100]) to assess relative suitability for ZIKV transmission. We calculated the number of cities suitable for ZIKV transmission at both timepoints based on sites with predicted R0 values above 1. While the absolute R0 values predicted in this study are inherently relative to our calibration, they should provide useful intuition into the relative suitability of different sites for ZIKV outbreaks. As such, it is possible to reinterpret the suitability of different cities during the two time ranges using different R0 thresholds.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

The predictions from the full model encompassing both mosquito ancestry and climatic factors were positively correlated with the heterogeneous distribution of human ZIKV infections across sub-Saharan Africa, with the predominantly low levels of Aaa ancestry mainly responsible for limiting transmission in most sites (the relationship for subset of seroprevalence surveys with ZIKV neutralising antibodies is displayed in appendix p 6, showing a stronger relationship for the ancestry-only model). This analytical framework estimated the basic reproductive ratio (R0) values derived from three distinct models, each representing competing hypotheses about the role of mosquito ancestry and climate in driving ZIKV transmission risk across Africa. We show and quantify prior versus posterior distributions (appendix pp 7–9) and R-hat and effective sample sizes (appendix p 10) for all model hyperparameters, parameter convergence (appendix p 11), ancestry-dependent and climate-dependent trait fits (median and 95% CI) compared with data (appendix p 12) and posterior predictive checks (appendix p 13). The model validation indicated that climate alone accounted for 30% of the variation in seroprevalence, mosquito ancestry alone accounted for 36% of the variation in seroprevalence, and together, they explained 46% of the variation (figure 1). Importantly, the ancestry-driven traits were predominantly responsible for limiting R0 below one in the full model, reflecting expectations of realised transmission in the field. Given the analysis positively correlated ZIKV suitability across a wide geographical range, it is likely that the model captures important characteristics of this epidemiological system in explaining factors that impede human ZIKV transmission under seemingly favourable conditions. Importantly, this model shows that there are critical ranges of ancestry and temperature, where small incremental increases in those conditions will lead to disproportionately large changes in transmission potential, and, the geographically widespread seroprevalence survey sites sit close to those critical zones, suggesting many locations in Africa could benefit from increased ZIKV and vector monitoring efforts (figure 2). Previous research supports these results: for instance, when Aaa levels exceed 30%, populations start to show a strong preference for biting humans.10

Given the full model is consistent with known patterns of ZIKV transmission across Africa, we used the model to predict transmission suitability across the most populous cities on the continent to explore recent and future transmission patterns. The model predicted that across 59 cities with current populations of more than 1 million, 23 (39%) are already suitable for ZIKV emergence (figure 3). There is some indirect evidence in the literature of human ZIKV infections in these regions.26,27 If current climate and population growth projections and their predicted effects on Ae aegypti are accurate, another 22 (37%) will become suitable by the end of the century (figure 3). Notably, the majority of uncertainty in these projections arose from uncertainty in temperature and Aaa estimates (appendix pp 14–15). One reason for this uncertainty is that the adaptive capacity of mosquitoes and the realistic doses or strains of the virus in the field might not be fully accounted for in the current model. Importantly, the model of Ae aegypti evolution in response to urbanisation made several assumptions that urgently need careful testing and likely refinement—it is highly unlikely that Ae aegypti in African cities will gradually rise from 0% to 100% Aaa ancestry. Instead, regions of their genome involved in urban adaptation and specialisation on humans will become more Aaa-like, while other regions of the genome might be relatively unaffected. Therefore, the precise effects of urbanisation will depend closely on the genomic architecture of host preference and ZIKV susceptibility, patterns of linkage between them, and their responses to natural selection in rapidly urbanising environments.28 Importantly, many factors such as human behaviour, greenhouse gas emissions, population growth and associated unplanned urbanisation, and vector control and other interventions could also modify this risk.

Discussion

This study highlights the importance of mosquito ancestry in explaining why reports of ZIKV outbreaks have been rare in Africa until now. Early research documented morphological variation in Ae aegypti subspecies (ie, Aaf and Aaa) within and outside of Africa,6 with more recent work linking such morphological variation to relevant phenotypic, genetic, and behavioural variation.29–31 Such recent studies also uncovered signatures of genetic admixture between both subspecies in several African locations.7,29,30 Despite being recognised as important, variation within and among mosquito populations has not always been incorporated into models of arbovirus transmission. The proportion of Aaa ancestry found in African Ae aegypti populations is primarily driven by dry season intensity and human population density.10 In this study, we find support that low levels of Aaa ancestry in most African Ae aegypti populations limits ZIKV transmission by simultaneously reducing human–vector contact (ie, distributing mosquito bites across a variety of hosts) and limiting vector competence. Mosquito populations predominantly assigned to Aaf are less likely to become infected given an infectious blood meal compared with Aaa populations.12 Increased susceptibility to ZIKV infection in Aaa is thought to be an incidental byproduct of selection on other traits (via either linkage or pleiotropy) during domestication or a consequence of genetic drift due to the demographic bottleneck before global expansion.12 The intricate mechanisms underlying the observed level of Aaa ancestry, host preference, and vector competence in Africa suggest multiple potential contributors. The process of introgression from external populations outside Africa, as well as introgression from Aaa-like populations already present within Africa, might have played substantial roles. Additionally, adaptation from existing standing genetic variation in the mosquito populations could contribute to these observed traits. However, the specific interplay and relative importance of each of these mechanisms remain elusive, presenting a complex and promising avenue for future research. Understanding how these factors interact and shape the observed patterns of Aaa ancestry, host preference, and vector competence in Africa holds substantial potential for advancing our knowledge of ZIKV transmission dynamics and guiding targeted control strategies.

While these results indicate that mosquito ancestry is more influential than climate on ZIKV epidemiology today, climate still remains an important factor in defining both historical and current mosquito distributions. Historically, climate was considered the initial driver of genetic differentiation of Ae aegypti within Africa, followed by recent and rapid urbanisation.11 Today, climate plays a crucial role in ZIKV transmission in that it is a necessary factor for the development and activity of both the mosquito vector and the virus. Temperature influences many aspects of the mosquito’s lifecycle, such as fecundity, survival, biting rate, and development time, thereby shaping its ability to transmit the virus. However, while climate is critical for ZIKV transmission, it is not sufficient to determine effective transmission on its own. Other factors, such as mosquito population density, human behaviour, and immunity, also play essential roles in determining the likelihood and intensity of ZIKV outbreaks following introduction of the virus into the human population. Understanding the interplay between climate and other factors is crucial for predicting and managing ZIKV transmission and for implementing effective control strategies to mitigate its impact on public health.

While under-reporting could contribute to the lack of observed ZIKV outbreaks in Africa, seroprevalence surveys indicate heterogeneous transmission across the continent. In addition to limitations in surveillance capacity, there are a few possible mechanisms by which human immunity could provide protection against severe disease, ultimately limiting health seeking behaviour and reporting. One mechanism is cross protection, whereby previous exposure to one or more flaviviruses could induce partial immunity to ZIKV. While cross protection is a common phenomenon, experimental evidence suggests the opposite for ZIKV, where disease severity is increased subsequent to exposure to related viruses such as dengue and West Nile viruses.32 Thus, we would expect higher Zika disease incidence rather than lower in regions such as Africa where there is circulation of related viruses. Another mechanism is through human genetics. There are several studies linking specific genes to protection against severe disease caused by dengue virus, a closely related flavivirus, with different levels of gene expression between people with and without African genetic ancestry.33 However, it is unknown whether genetic variation among people of African ancestry is likely to be an important driver of variation in emergence risk. Additionally, it is unclear if other attributes of those populations such as socioeconomic status and comorbidities are conflated with genetic protection.3 In either case, reduced disease severity is likely to result in lower levels of health seeking behaviour, which would reduce reported cases, but would not affect seroprevalence estimates. Therefore, these factors could play a role in the limited ZIKV incidence reported across the continent but would be unlikely to explain heterogeneous seroprevalence estimates within Africa.

The model presented in this study comes with several limitations and caveats that warrant consideration. First, the model relies on certain assumptions, which are based on experiments that themselves have inherent limitations and uncertainties. For instance, using OID50 estimates from laboratory experiments to scale the probability of mosquito infection draws on the strong sigmoid relationship that is typically observed between the proportion of infected mosquitoes and the virus concentration in an artificial infectious blood meal.12,13 However, it oversimplifies the complex process of arbovirus acquisition by a mosquito when it feeds on a naturally infected human, which depends not only on the magnitude of viraemia but also on other host and viral factors.34,35 Additionally, the study did not explicitly include other important factors such as hydrology, climate seasonality and variability, ongoing transmission dynamics, and human behaviour, which can strongly influence ZIKV transmission. Such factors, which might be important for explaining the remaining more than 50% of variation in seroprevalence data not explained by the model, could also change in the future and affect the future projections presented in this study. Despite these limitations, the model provides valuable insights into ZIKV transmission risk in Africa, and future research efforts should strive to address and refine these limitations for a more comprehensive understanding of the virus’s epidemiology in the region.

An important consideration for future ZIKV emergence in Africa is the potential role of Aedes albopictus in sustaining viral transmission. Ae albopictus is an invasive mosquito species in Africa that has spread throughout much of the continent in the past two decades.36 Compared with Ae aegypti, the primary vector of ZIKV, Ae albopictus are generally considered less efficient vectors of arboviruses,37 in part because they are less anthropophilic and more ecologically flexible. Moreover, laboratory experiments report a generally lower vector competence of Ae albopictus for ZIKV.38 However, there are studies both within and outside of Africa that demonstrate high anthropophily among Ae albopictus populations in urban and suburban settings.39,40 Furthermore, there is direct evidence of ZIKV infection in field-caught Ae albopictus mosquitoes concomitant with human infections detected in Gabon.41 Beyond differential vector competence, Ae albopictus thermal breadth and optimal temperature for ZIKV transmission are shifted towards lower temperatures compared with Ae aegypti;15 as a result, climate change should continue to favour transmission by Ae aegypti as average temperatures increase towards 29°C. At a minimum however, Ae albopictus might serve as a bridge vector, and therefore should be considered in any future monitoring efforts.

The importance of mosquito ancestry in shaping ZIKV epidemiological patterns underscores the importance of monitoring genetic variation among African mosquito populations over space and time. Coupled with variation in climate and urbanisation, albeit to a lesser extent, these factors contribute to the heterogeneities in the risk of ZIKV emergence across the continent. Importantly, other flaviviruses of public health concern such as dengue show similar heterogeneities across Africa. However, recent research suggests such heterogeneity might be a function of both genetic variation in mosquito populations and strain-specific dengue virus effects.3,42 Vector genomic surveillance would allow for a comprehensive assessment of local risk and enable targeted prevention efforts to be focused on areas most susceptible to ZIKV or other flaviviruses. For instance, areas where populations exhibit high levels of human specialist (Aaa) genetic ancestry might necessitate more focused and intensive vector control measures. Such surveillance could plausibly be based on anything from cheap morphological proxies6 to amplicon panels (like those recently developed for malaria surveillance43) to whole-genome sequencing. This is particularly pertinent for identifying local populations that are more likely to spur large outbreaks like in Cabo Verde, due to evolution in response to climate, urbanisation, or introgression from more human specialist populations (ie, Aaa populations outside of Africa or Aaa-like populations within Africa). By closely monitoring the genetic dynamics of mosquito populations and their interactions with environmental factors, we can gain valuable insights into the potential emergence of ZIKV in different regions of Africa, facilitating more efficient and effective strategies to mitigate the impact of this global health threat.
