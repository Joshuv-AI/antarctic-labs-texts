Molecular and clinical characteristics of glioma patients with germline pathogenic mutations of Lynch syndrome genes: a large-scale multicenter study
Authors: Jiao Feng, Tonghui Ma, Xiang Zhang, Yi Zhang, Zhenyuan Qian, Yuxiang Dai, Zhixian Gao, Rui Zhao, Chengjun Yao, Guhong Du, Naiyuan Shao, Zhen Wu, Nan Ji, Jia Gu, Zhengchuan Liu, Qiong Yang, Xiaochen Sun, Wei Li, Xiaomo Li, Yanxiang Zhang, Zaiyuan Ye, Anchao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLynch syndrome (LS) is an inherited disorder caused by germline mutations in mismatch repair (MMR) genes or EPCAM deletions and is primarily associated with an increased risk of gastrointestinal and endometrial cancers. However, LS-associated gliomas are rare, and their clinical and molecular characteristics remain poorly defined. The objective of this study was to systematically characterize the prevalence, genetic landscape, molecular features, and clinical outcomes of LS-associated gliomas.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed a multicenter cohort of 5,594 glioma patients who underwent targeted next-generation sequencing to identify pathogenic germline mutations in LS-associated genes. Clinical characteristics, molecular features, treatment histories, and outcomes of LS-associated glioma patients were systematically evaluated and compared with those of LS-associated colorectal cancer (CRC) patients.ResultsAmong 5,594 glioma patients, 54 individuals (0.97%) were identified as carrying pathogenic germline LS-associated mutations, with a median age of 37.9 years. High-grade gliomas accounted for 68.5% (37/54) of cases, while 9.3% (5/54) were low-grade gliomas. Mutations in MSH2 and MSH6 were predominant, showing a distribution distinct from that observed in LS-associated colorectal cancer (CRC). Compared with LS CRC patients, LS-associated glioma patients exhibited significantly lower frequencies of high tumor mutational burden (TMB-H), microsatellite instability–high (MSI-H), and PD-L1 positivity, indicating unique molecular characteristics. Treatment history analysis showed that LS glioma patients generally responded well to temozolomide treatment, although those harboring MSH2 mutations had a worse prognosis. A family history of cancer was reported in 16.7% (9/54) of LS glioma patients.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive characterization of the prevalence, molecular landscape, and clinical outcomes of LS-associated gliomas. The distinct molecular features and prognostic implications identified herein may facilitate improved recognition and inform more personalized management strategies for this rare patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08119-9.

Introduction

The mismatch repair (MMR) system plays a critical role in maintaining genomic integrity by correcting base–base mismatches and insertion–deletion loops that arise during DNA replication. Germline mutations in key MMR genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or EPCAM deletions lead to MMR deficiency, which promotes tumorigenesis and cancer progression [1–3]. Lynch syndrome (LS), also known as hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), is a hereditary cancer predisposition syndrome caused by such defects and is characterized by a markedly increased risk of multiple malignancies, most commonly gastrointestinal and endometrial cancers (EC) [4, 5]. In contrast, LS-associated gliomas are rare and remain poorly characterized. Recent large-scale analyses of unselected adult gliomas have begun to quantify the prevalence of MMR deficiency and Lynch syndrome in glioma populations, underscoring implications for treatment selection and familial screening. Mechanistically, hypermutation in gliomas can emerge through distinct de novo and therapy-driven routes, with temozolomide (TMZ) selecting for acquired MMR defects and characteristic mutational signatures. TMZ-induced hypermutation has been associated with distant recurrence and reduced survival after high-grade transformation of low-grade IDH-mutant gliomas [6]. Although mismatch repair deficiency predicts benefit from PD-1 blockade across tumor types [7], responses in glioma appear context dependent; durable responses have been reported in hypermutant glioblastoma arising from germline biallelic MMR deficiency [8], and recent consortium-level data suggest improved outcomes with combined immunotherapy in replication-repair–deficient high-grade glioma after anti–PD-1 failure [9]. De novo replication repair–deficient glioblastoma has also been proposed as a distinct adult subtype with potential sensitivity to immune checkpoint blockade [10]. Ngm L. et al. systematically characterizes the biological features and clinical relevance of primary dMMR in gliomas affecting children, adolescents, and young adults (0–40 years), demonstrating that dMMR-high-grade gliomas are hypermutated, therapy-resistant tumors with distinct genomic alterations, an immunologically active microenvironment predictive of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors, and important implications for immunotherapy and temozolomide-associated resistance [11]. Notably, LS is not included among the 19 recognized brain tumor predisposition syndromes (BTPSs) in the fifth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System (2021) [12]. This omission reflects the limited understanding of the prevalence, clinicopathological features, genetic landscape, and optimal management strategies for gliomas arising in LS patients. Owing to their rarity, evidence-based guidance for clinical decision-making in LS-associated gliomas is currently lacking. Microsatellite instability (MSI) is a hypermutated tumor phenotype that arises predominantly as a consequence of MMR deficiency. In colorectal cancer (CRC) and EC patients, deficient MMR (dMMR) is strongly associated with MSI and elevated tumor mutational burden (TMB), making MSI testing a widely used screening tool for LS in these malignancies [13]. Moreover, TMB has emerged as a potential biomarker for predicting response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy. However, accumulating evidence suggests that among non-CRC and non-EC tumors, many LS-associated cancers exhibit microsatellite stability (MSS) despite underlying MMR deficiency [13]. In gliomas, Touat et al. reported that all tested MMR deficient gliomas were MSS [14], indicating that the relationship between MSI status and LS may differ substantially in the context of glioma. Consequently, the molecular features and immunogenic potential of LS-associated gliomas remain insufficiently defined. Another unresolved issue concerns the response of LS-associated gliomas to alkylating agents such as temozolomide (TMZ), a cornerstone of glioma chemotherapy. Theoretically, MMR deficiency may confer resistance to alkylating agents by failing to recognize O6 methylguanine-containing mismatches. It is reported that MSH6 mutations rose during TMZ treatment mediated TMZ resistance [15, 16], and several in vitro studies showed that MMR-deficient glioma cells were resistant to TMZ [14, 17]. In contrast, clinical data remain inconclusive, as Maxwell et al. found that MMR deficiency did not mediate clinical resistance to TMZ in patients with malignant glioma [18]. Thus, whether MMR deficiency—and specifically germline LS-associated mutations—confers intrinsic or acquired resistance to TMZ in glioma patients remains controversial.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the largest cohort ever reported with 5594 glioma patients, and identified 54 individuals with pathogenic germline mutations in LS-associated genes, herein referred to as “LS-associated glioma patients”. Using next-generation sequencing (NGS) and comparative analyses with glioma patients lacking germline LS gene mutations, we systematically characterized the clinical and molecular features of LS-associated gliomas. In addition, we compared these features with those observed in LS-associated CRC patients. Furthermore, through collecting the treatment history of ‘Lynch glioma patients’, we found that they were not initially resistant to TMZ treatment and those with MSH2 mutations were correlated with worse prognosis. Finally, we report a novel pathogenic germline variant in MLH1. Collectively, our findings provide a comprehensive molecular and clinical characterization of LS-associated gliomas and may inform personalized management strategies for this rare patient population.

Methods

Patient enrollment and sample collection

A total of 5,594 glioma samples and 2,827 CRC patient samples were reviewed and analyzed using targeted NGS (Brain Focus, Onco Focus-GI, or Onco Panscan, Genetronhealth, Beijing, China). Among these, 173 glioma patients and 181 CRC patients were ultimately included in the study. From the 5,594 glioma patients, 54 individuals harboring pathogenic germline mutations in LS-associated genes were identified and classified as LS-associated glioma patients. In addition, 119 glioma patients without germline pathogenic LS mutations but with detectable MSI or PD-L1 expression were selected from the remaining 5,540 glioma samples as controls. Among the 2,827 CRC patients, 38 patients with pathogenic germline LS gene mutations were enrolled and defined as LS-associated CRC patients. From the remaining 2,789 CRC patients without pathogenic germline LS gene mutations (termed as ‘none-Lynch CRC patients’), 143 patients with detectable MSI or PD-L1 expression were included as non-LS CRC controls (Fig. 1). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the IRB of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. KY 2018-021-02), and that informed consent was waived due to the retrospective use of de-identified data.

Genomic DNA extraction and quantification

Genomic DNA extraction and quantification were performed as previously described. Briefly, total genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood lymphocytes using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay kit (TIANGEN; Beijing, China), from formalin-fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue using a GeneRead DNA FFPE Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and from tissues using a QIAamp DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), respectively. DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA).

Library construction and sequencing

Extracted genomic DNA was sheared into 150–200 base pairs (bp) fragments with Covaris S220 Focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris, Massachusetts, USA). DNA libraries were constructed using the KAPA HTP Library Preparation Kit (Illumina platforms; KAPA Biosystems; Wilmington, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was assessed using the Qubit 2.0 system (Invitrogen) and the Agilent 2200 TapeStation System.

Prepared libraries were subjected to whole-exome capture with SureSelect Human All Exon V5 (Agilent) or targeted sequencing with Onco Panscan™ (Genetron Health). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovoSeq platform at Genetron Health (Beijing, China). Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled using FastQC (version 0.11.2) and Trimmomatic (version 0.33) [19].

Data filtering and variant calling

Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg19 with the BWA-MEM algorithm (version 0.7.10-r789) [20] for both tumor and matched normal samples. PCR duplications were marked and removed using Picard (version 1.103). Local realignment and base quality score recalibration were performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version v3.1-0-g72492bb) [21]. Somatic single nucleotide variations (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (Indels) were identified using MuTect (version 3.1-0-g72492bb) [22] and Strelka (version 1.0.14) [23], while germline variants were identified using GATK. Variant annotation was performed using Variant Effect Predictor (VEP, version 83) [24] and Oncotator (version 1.5.1.0) [25]. All coding-region mutations were manually checked using Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV, version 2.3.34) [26]. Somatic copy number variants (CNVs) were identified using ADTEx [27], and structural variations (SVs) were detected using CREST [28].

Tumor mutation burden (TMB) calculation

TMB was defined as the number of somatic, coding, base substitution, and indel mutations per megabase (Mb) of genome examined. TMB was calculated by dividing the total number of nonsynonymous somatic mutations by the size of the coding region covered by the sequencing panel [29, 30].

MSI detection

MSI status was determined as previously described [31]. Briefly, multiplex PCR was performed using fluorescently labelled primers targeting five quasi-monomorphic mononucleotide repeat markers (BAT-25, BAT-26, NR-21, NR-24, and MONO-27). Tumors showing instability in two or more markers were classified as MSI-high (MSI-H), those with instability in one marker were classified as MSI-low (MSI-L), and those without instability were classified as microsatellite stable (MSS).

PD-L1 detection

The PD-L1 expression in tumor samples was evaluated using the PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay (Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Fisher’s exact test. Not significant, p > 0.05; * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; and **** p < 0.0001.

Data availability

The data that support the findings of this study will be openly available in the China National GeneBank DataBase (https://db.cngb.org/).

Results

Characteristics of LS-associated glioma patients

The overall prevalence of LS-associated gliomas in this cohort was 54 of 5,594 (0.97%), with no sex bias (27 males and 27 females) (Fig. 2; Table 1, Fig.S1). Although a previous study reported that the peak incidence of brain cancer in patients with Lynch syndrome occurs between 50 and 69 years of age [32], the median age of LS-associated glioma patients in our cohort was notably younger (37.9 years). Among these patients, 48.1% (26/54) were classified as WHO grade 4 gliomas, which was consistent with the relatively low frequencies of IDH1 mutation (29.6%, 16/54) and 1p/19q co-deletion (3.7%, 2/54). Of note, MSH6 (38.9%, 21/54) or MSH2 (37.0%, 20/54) were the predominant germline mutations in these patients. Only 24.1% (13/54) of LS-associated glioma patients exhibited MGMT promoter methylation. In addition, fewer than 10% of patients were MSI-high (9.3%) or PD-L1 positive (3.7%), suggesting a potentially limited benefit from immunotherapy and raising concerns regarding responsiveness to temozolomide (TMZ) treatment (Fig. 2; Table 1).

Summary of clinicopathologic features of Lynch glioma patients

Comparison of clinical and molecular characteristics between LS-associated and non-LS glioma patients

To further characterize LS-associated gliomas, we compared their clinical and molecular features with those of 119 non-LS glioma patients who had undergone MSI and/or PD-L1 testing. The median age of LS-associated glioma patients was significantly younger than that of non-LS glioma patients (37.9 vs. 46, p = 0.0313), suggesting earlier tumor onset in the LS-associated group. Besides, LS-associated glioma patients had significantly fewer grade 3 and grade 4 tumors compared with non-LS glioma patients (26/54 vs. 79/119). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the proportions of IDH1-mutant gliomas with 1p/19q co-deletion or IDH1-mutant gliomas with TERT promoter mutations (2/54 vs. 15/119, p = 0.0587), indicating comparable prognostic molecular subtypes. However, the frequency of MGMT promoter methylation was significantly lower in LS-associated glioma patients than in non-LS glioma patients (13/54 vs. 48/119, p = 0.0241), suggesting a reduced likelihood of benefit from TMZ therapy. Although the proportion of TMB-high tumors was significantly higher in LS-associated glioma patients than in non-LS glioma patients (26/54 vs. 36/119, p = 0.0269), the proportions of MSI-high tumors and PD-L1–positive tumors were comparable between the two groups (MSI-H: 5/54 vs. 4/119, p = 0.2724; PD-L1: 2/54 vs. 18/119, p = 0.0893), indicating no clear immunotherapeutic advantage for LS-associated gliomas (Fig. 2; Table 2). Pathway-level analysis revealed that mutations in the MAPK signaling pathway were significantly more frequent in LS-associated glioma patients than in non-LS glioma patients, whereas no significant differences were observed in the RTK, PI3K, P53, or RB1 signaling pathways between the two groups (Fig. 2, Fig. S2, Fig. S3, Table S1).

Comparison between LS-associated glioma and LS-associated CRC patients

Given that LS-associated CRC patients are known to derive substantial benefit from immunotherapy, we next compared immunotherapy-related biomarkers between LS-associated glioma and LS-associated CRC patients. Compared with LS-associated CRC patients, LS-associated glioma patients exhibited significantly lower proportions of TMB-high, MSI-high, and PD-L1–positive tumors (TMB-H: 26/54 vs. 19/20, p = 0.0001; MSI-H: 5/35 vs. 20/20, p < 0.0001; PD-L1 positive: 2/23 vs. 12/18, p = 0.0002) (Tables 3 and 4). These findings indicate that, unlike CRC, LS-associated gliomas exhibit a weak association between LS gene mutations and immunotherapy-related biomarkers.

Comparison of immunotherapy markers between ‘Lynch’ glioma and CRC patients

As previous studies have shown that individual LS genes contribute differently to MSI status [13], we further compared the distribution of mutated LS genes between glioma and CRC patients. In LS-associated glioma patients, MSH6 (38.9%) and MSH2 (37.0%) were the most frequently mutated genes, whereas MLH1 mutations predominated in LS-associated CRC patients (57.9%). Among LS-associated glioma patients, MSI-high status was observed in 20% (1/5) of MLH1-mutated cases, 20% (3/15) of MSH2-mutated cases, and 50% (1/2) of PMS2-mutated cases, while all MSH6- and EPCAM-mutated cases were MSI-low or microsatellite stable. In contrast, all MLH1-, MSH2-, and MSH6-mutated LS-associated CRC patients were MSI-high. Comparison of specific germline alterations revealed only four identical variants shared between LS-associated glioma and CRC patients (MLH1 A681T, MSH2 Q409Rfs*7, and MSH6 F1008Sfs*2, K580*), with no evident hotspot regions in either tumor type. These findings highlight both conserved and distinct features of LS-associated germline mutations across tumor types. (Fig. 3)

Treatment history of ‘Lynch glioma patients’

Given previous reports suggesting that MMR deficiency may confer resistance to TMZ [16, 17, 33], we hypothesized that LS-associated glioma patients might exhibit primary resistance to TMZ treatment. However, among 16 LS-associated glioma patients who received TMZ therapy, only three patients (23.1%) developed TMZ resistance. The median progression-free survival (PFS) of these patients was 15 months, and the median PFS of LS-associated glioblastoma patients exceeded 12 months, which is longer than that typically observed in TMZ-resistant glioblastoma [34]. These results suggest that LS-associated glioma patients are not intrinsically resistant to TMZ. Furthermore, 76.9% (10/13) patients remained disease stable till the last follow-up, indicating a favorable therapeutic response. Notably, all three deceased patients harbored MSH2 mutations, whereas none of the patients with MSH6 mutations experienced disease progression. Consistently, patients with MSH2 mutations had significantly shorter PFS than those with other LS gene mutations, suggesting that germline MSH2 mutations may be associated with poorer prognosis in LS-associated glioma patients. Family history of ‘Lynch glioma patients’

Family history and pedigree analyses are presented in Fig. 5 and Table S2. Overall, 16.7% (9/54) of LS-associated glioma patients reported a family history of cancer, a proportion significantly higher than that observed in non-LS glioma patients (9/54 vs. 4/119, p = 0.0039) and higher than the reported population average [35]. These findings suggest that glioma patients with a positive family history of cancer are more likely to harbor LS-associated germline mutations and may benefit from germline genetic testing. Among the reported family histories of LS-associated glioma patients, 62.5% (15/24) of affected relatives had gastrointestinal (including colorectal, gastric, liver, esophageal, and pancreatic) or endometrial cancers, which are the tumor types most strongly associated with LS [4, 36]. Notably, no family members were reported to have lung cancer, the most prevalent cancer type in China, further supporting the LS-specific cancer spectrum observed in these families.

Interestingly, patient 46 was identified as carrying a germline MLH1 c.1731 + 1del variant that had not been previously reported in ClinVar or other public databases (Table S4). In silico splice-site prediction using MaxEntScan [37], NetGene2 [38], and Alternative Splice Site Predictor (ASSP) [39] indicated that this variant is likely to result in aberrant splicing. Besides, this variant was absent in the gnomAD database, which should be classified as pathogenic according to Sherloc guidelines [40]. Germline sequencing of ten family members revealed that six individuals (60%) carried the same MLH1 variant as the proband (including his grandmother, mother, and four siblings of his mother). Two of these six carriers had a documented cancer history. Additionally, four siblings of the proband’s grandmother had a history of cancer, three of whom were diagnosed with CRC. Although germline testing was not performed in these individuals, they fulfilled the Amsterdam II criteria and Bethesda guidelines for LS [4]. Based on these cumulative findings and ACMG/Sherloc criteria, the MLH1 c.1731 + 1del variant was classified as a novel pathogenic LS-associated mutation.

Discussion

LS-associated glioma is rare and remains poorly characterized. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the largest cohorts to date investigating the molecular and clinical characteristics of LS-associated gliomas. By analyzing 5,594 glioma samples using targeted NGS, we systematically characterized the clinical features, molecular landscape, and treatment outcomes of LS-associated glioma patients and compared them with those of LS-associated CRC, the most common malignancy linked to Lynch syndrome. Furthermore, treatment histories of 16 LS-associated glioma patients were reviewed, and the relationship between prognosis and specific MMR gene mutations was explored. Collectively, our findings provide a comprehensive overview of LS-associated gliomas and may facilitate personalized disease management for this rare patient population.

Brain carcinomas represent an uncommon manifestation of Lynch syndrome. Data from the Prospective Lynch Syndrome Database (PLSD) indicate that the cumulative incidence of brain tumors in LS patients ranges from 0% to 7.7% by age 75 [41]. In a recent study of 1,225 adult gliomas, only nine cases (0.73%) were MMR-deficient, and five (0.41%) were confirmed to be LS-associated [42]. In our cohort of 5,594 glioma patients, 54 cases (0.97%) were identified as LS-associated gliomas, with no apparent sex bias. Notably, this is one of the first large-scale studies to comprehensively report both molecular and clinical features of glioma patients harboring germline pathogenic variants in LS genes. Distinct from LS-associated CRC, in which MLH1 and MSH2 mutations predominate, MSH6 and MSH2 were the most frequently mutated genes in LS-associated glioma patients. In addition, the median age of LS-associated glioma patients was significantly younger than that of non-LS glioma patients, suggesting earlier disease onset.

MSI, a hallmark of mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), is strongly associated with LS-associated CRC and serves as both a diagnostic marker and a predictor of response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [13, 43]. MSI and/or dMMR testing has traditionally been performed in patients with CRC and EC to screen for LS–associated cancer predisposition [44]. Additionally, MSI-H/MMR-D was identified as a biomarker for response to immune checkpoint blockade responsiveness in patients with advanced solid malignancies [45, 46]. One study has reported that MMR-deficient gliomas are often MSS, potentially due to subtle or region-specific MSI patterns that was not identified by bulk sequencing or clinical MSI assays [14]. Consistent with these findings, only 9.3% of LS-associated glioma patients in our cohort were MSI-high, a rate significantly lower than that observed in LS-associated CRC patients. Gene-specific effects on MSI status may partially explain this discrepancy. Prior studies have shown that MLH1, MSH2, and EPCAM mutations are more frequently associated with MSI-high tumors, whereas MSH6 and PMS2 mutations tend to result in MSS phenotypes [13]. In our study, MSH6 was the most commonly mutated gene in LS-associated gliomas, whereas MLH1 predominated in LS-associated CRC. Therefore, the higher prevalence of MSH6 mutations likely contributes to the lower MSI-high rate observed in LS-associated glioma patients. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the discordance between MSI status and MMR deficiency in gliomas. In addition to MSI, PD-L1 expression and TMB are important biomarkers for predicting immunotherapy responsiveness. Although most LS-associated glioma patients in our cohort were MSI-low/MSS and PD-L1 negative, the proportion of TMB-high tumors was significantly higher in LS-associated gliomas than in non-LS gliomas. Previous studies have suggested that gliomas with acquired somatic MMR deficiency and subclonal hypermutation do not benefit from PD-1 blockade [14]. However, LS-associated glioma patients harbor germline MMR mutations present in all tumor cells, which may result in a distinct biological and therapeutic context. Supporting this notion, patient 46 with TMB-H in our cohort remained disease stable till the last follow-up following anti-PD-1 therapy after his first recurrence. Although limited by sample size, these observations suggest that a subset of TMB-high LS-associated glioma patients may benefit from immunotherapy, warranting further investigation.

Therapeutic options for gliomas remain limited, and no standard treatment guidelines exist for LS-associated gliomas. TMZ, an alkylating agent capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier, remains the cornerstone of glioma chemotherapy. However, approximately half of treated patients exhibit intrinsic resistance to TMZ, while the remaining initially responsive patients eventually develop acquired resistance after prolonged treatment [47, 48]. MGMT promoter methylation is present in approximately 50% of patients with GBM and has been established as a predictive biomarker of favorable outcomes in GBM patients treated with alkylating chemotherapy [43]. In our cohort, only 24% of LS-associated glioma patients and 16% of LS-associated GBM patients exhibited MGMT promoter methylation, a frequency lower than previously reported. Moreover, although dMMR has been implicated in resistance to alkylating agents [14, 16, 17], the role of MMR deficiency in mediating TMZ resistance remains controversial. Notably, the majority of MGMT-unmethylated LS-associated GBM patients in our cohort responded to TMZ treatment and remained progression-free, indicating that LS-associated gliomas are not intrinsically resistant to TMZ. These findings suggest that germline LS gene mutations present prior to TMZ exposure may not confer primary TMZ resistance. Therefore, TMZ should not be prematurely excluded from treatment regimens for LS-associated glioma patients, even in the absence of MGMT promoter methylation.

Finally, although LS is classically associated with gastrointestinal and endometrial cancers, family history analysis in our cohort identified a large LS pedigree carrying a previously unreported MLH1 c.1731 + 1del variant. Although this variant has not been documented in the ClinVar database, a closely related variant, MLH1 c.1731 + 1G > A, has been reported to cause aberrant splicing and is classified as pathogenic. Given that both variants affect the same canonical splice donor site, MLH1 c.1731 + 1del is likely to induce a similar splicing defect, potentially resulting in loss of MLH1 protein function. Integrating this molecular evidence with the clinical characteristics observed in the large LS family, we classified MLH1 c.1731 + 1del as a novel pathogenic variant. Accordingly, MLH1-specific genetic counseling was provided to patient 46 and his family. Germline MLH1 testing was recommended for at-risk relatives, and carriers of the pathogenic variant were advised to undergo colonoscopic surveillance every five years, as well as endometrial and ovarian cancer screening. Collectively, our findings provide valuable insights into LS-associated gliomas and may benefit families carrying the same MLH1 mutation.

From an epidemiologic perspective, Lynch syndrome (LS) accounts for approximately ~ 2–3% of colorectal cancer and endometrial cancer cases in unselected cohorts worldwide and is estimated to affect on the order of ~ 1 in 300 individuals. Within mismatch repair–deficient (dMMR) or MSI-high colorectal cancers, LS is enriched (commonly ~ 10–15%), with the remainder largely sporadic (e.g., MLH1 promoter hypermethylation). Reported spectra of pathogenic variants in MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM can also vary across populations and testing strategies (e.g., East Asian versus predominantly European-ancestry cohorts), which should be considered when interpreting gene distributions in LS-associated glioma cohorts.

In conclusion, we delineated the molecular and clinical characteristics of Lynch syndrome–associated gliomas, which may contribute to improved risk stratification and personalized management strategies for this unique patient population. Although the rarity of LS-associated gliomas inherently limits cohort size and the statistical power of subgroup analyses, our study represents one of the largest systematic investigations of this entity to date. In addition, while biomarker profiling and molecular analyses were comprehensive, some data were not uniformly available across all cases, and the use of targeted NGS may have constrained the detection of certain genomic alterations. Furthermore, mechanistic validation of the observed associations was beyond the scope of the present study. Treatment-response analyses were limited to patients with complete longitudinal records (16/54), and incomplete capture of downstream therapies across institutions may introduce selection and information bias; therefore, findings related to treatment susceptibility/resistance should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, our findings provide an important framework for understanding LS-associated gliomas and establish a foundation for future multicenter and prospective studies aimed at refining biological insights and therapeutic strategies for this rare patient population.

Conclusion

In this study, we report one of the largest cohorts to date to systematically characterize the clinical, molecular, and therapeutic features of Lynch syndrome (LS)–associated gliomas. LS-associated gliomas are rare, often occur at younger ages, and are enriched for germline MSH2/MSH6 alterations, with molecular patterns that differ from LS-associated colorectal cancer. Despite underlying mismatch repair defects, LS-associated gliomas showed low frequencies of MSI-high status and PD-L1 positivity, suggesting that commonly used immunotherapy biomarkers may behave differently in the central nervous system context. Clinically, most patients experienced benefit from temozolomide, including those without MGMT promoter methylation, indicating that germline LS status alone does not necessarily imply intrinsic resistance to alkylating therapy. A subset of patients with high tumor mutational burden may represent candidates for immune checkpoint blockade, warranting further investigation to refine predictive markers. Finally, identification of a novel pathogenic MLH1 splice-site variant highlights the value of germline testing and cascade surveillance for affected families. Collectively, these findings refine the current clinical–molecular portrait of LS-associated gliomas and support future multicenter, prospective studies to optimize risk assessment and treatment strategies.
