Seeing beyond positivity: computer vision approach to decoding BinaxNOW COVID-19 tests and forecasting negative results
Authors: Morgan N. Greenleaf, Richard Parsons, Colin Shea, Phuong-Vi Nguyen, Jesse J. Waggoner, Anuradha Rao, Leda Bassit, Heather Bowers, Courtney Sabino, Raymond F. Schinazi, Santiago Arconada, Robert G. Mannino, Wilbur Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe emergence of COVID-19 facilitated the widespread use, regulatory awareness, and societal familiarity of lateral flow assays (LFA) for self-diagnosis. The increased frequency in the use of LFA’s as well as their increased use by the general population amplified some of the major drawbacks of these tests, namely, the reliance on user interpretation of the test result, the qualitative nature of the result itself, and the lower sensitivity and specificity compared to traditional polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based diagnostic testing. Furthermore, the results of these tests and the interpretation of them by lay users were increasingly used as a public health tool by authorities, where recommendations were made on how members of the public should act in response to self-interpretation of test results. Our aims were to develop an objective image analysis algorithm that can (1) interpret the result of a popular COVID-19 LFA Rapid Antigen Test (RAT) and accurately estimate viral load, and (2) predict how long after a positive test a user will test negative on the RAT, indicating loss of infectiousness and the ability to be around others without fear of spreading infection.ResultsIndeed, we found that we could estimate viral load to within a RMSE of 1.44 Cycle Threshold (CT) of gold standard PCR testing, and that we can predict the result status of a user upon a follow up visit at least 5 days post-symptom onset with a sensitivity of 99%, a specificity of 73% and an accuracy of 94%. The analysis algorithm was finally embedded in a mobile application for ease of use.ConclusionThe development of an objective image analysis algorithm for estimating viral load combined with smartphone apps represents a significant improvement upon LFA RAT technologies being used by millions worldwide to diagnose a variety of epidemiologically significant diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44247-026-00287-4.

Introduction

Throughout the COVID-19 pandemic and the evolution of the global response, rapid antigen tests (RATs) have been widely used as a fast and convenient way to detect the presence of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, which causes COVID-19. These tests work by detecting specific proteins, or antigens, on the surface of the virus.

RATs were widely adopted as the “go to” diagnostic tool for COVID-19. The primary advantages include the short time to results, the point-of-care nature of devices incorporating this technology, and RAT’s low cost. Patients can get results in a matter of minutes, compared to Real-Time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) tests which can take several hours or even days to return results. Furthermore, patients can get these results from the comfort of their own homes. This makes RATs particularly useful in situations where rapid screening is essential, such as screening large groups of people prior to attending school or a public event, or where clinic-based screening is inconvenient or inaccessible [1].

The majority of RATs are visually-read lateral flow assays (LFAs) which despite their popularity do have disadvantages. These include the possibility of user interpretation error, lower sensitivity and specificity compared to gold standard qRT-PCR based tests, and lack of quantitative results which limits the ability to determine the viral load or infectivity of a user. Combining the rapid screening capability of RATs with the viral load quantification of qRT-PCR would present public health officials and patients alike with a significant tool to determine patient infectivity and make appropriate quarantine decisions. To that end, we developed an image analysis technique to quantify viral load based on the Abbott BinaxNow COVID-19 Ag [2] Card, a widely used RAT as a part of the United States COVID-19 pandemic response. Our goals were (A) reducing user interpretation error [3], specifically at the margins of interpretation (e.g. when the LFA test line is faint) (B) Estimating viral load and disease course by correlating the LFA result with PCR CT values to provide a semi-quantitative proxy for viral load, and (C) prediction date of negative test result on the same RAT device to guide user risk decisions e.g. when they could expect to stop isolation precautions. Finally, we incorporated this image analysis algorithm and “time-to-negative” prediction model into a smartphone app, demonstrating the accessibility and scalability of this technology (Fig. 1, Supplemental Video 1). This work represents a significant improvement over previous work [4–6], which demonstrated the feasibility of using smartphone cameras to accurately image an LFA to determine the presence of absence of lines [7].

Results

Correlation between LFA quantification metric, PCR CT, and antigen quantification

Here we report an exponential correlation between our LFA quantification metric accumulated line intensity (ALI) and PCR CT along with a sigmoidal correlation between ALI and antigen quantification (Fig. 2A-B). These data were used to develop a model for predicting CT and antigen quantification based on ALI. Validation of this model shows that the predicted values for CT and antigen concentration correlated mildly well with the actual corresponding values determined via laboratory testing with the calculated RMSE for the CT model of ± 1.44 CT and ± 21,634.87 pg/mL for the antigen model, (Fig. 3A-B). These results suggest that the CT model is a better predictive model for this test data set than the antigen model.

Time to negative model

Next, we evaluated the accuracy of our time-to-negative-test models by utilizing the ALI and the amount of time since the first test to predict when the subject will start testing negative. Accuracy can be determined by comparing the predicted result and the real result for users on subsequent follow ups to see if the predicted time-to-negative result was correct. Our model resulted in an overall accuracy of 90% in predicting a patient’s status upon follow up after a second test, with a sensitivity of 96% and a specificity of 61% (Table 1). Furthermore Table 1 below shows the results of using Harrell’s bias correction method to internally validate these models using the limited available clinical data, of which images were taken in ambient light. These results represented an improvement over those images taken using a commercially available lightbox with controlled lighting conditions, highlighting the robustness of this method (Table S1). It is important to note that the bias correction method resulted in slight decreases in specificity, sensitivity, and predictive accuracy, indicating the potential of model overfitting, which is possible given that there were 90 data points used in the model.

Discussion

In this work, we have developed an automated computer vision model that can detect the sample and control lines on an LFA and analyze the result. This technique has many benefits over traditional visual analysis, particularly (1) the removal of user error by eliminating the role of visual judgement in the interpretation of results and (2) the ability to use the size of the sample and control lines to quantitively analyze the assay. Furthermore, this technique is readily accessible to users as it is capable of being incorporated into a smartphone app. The ability to quantitatively analyze the LFA not only removes the ambiguity and variability of user-determined visual diagnosis, but it also enables broader conclusions to be drawn from each test. With visual interpretation, a LFA can only do one thing: provide a diagnosis. However, with automated analysis (I.e. analyzing the thickness ratios of the sample and control lines), we can now quantify the amount of antigen present in a sample. This has implications for the viral load of an individual at the time of use, which can determine how severe symptoms will be and how long the illness will last. Furthermore, this analysis is done quickly and automatically, arming patients with additional information about their health status and empowering them to make more informed decisions regarding their approach to recovery.

Understanding the viral load of the patient also enables us to predict the length of viral positivity. As shown in multiple examples throughout the COVID-19 pandemic, the key factor in reducing the spread of an illness is reducing contact between infected individuals and healthy individuals while the individual is contagious [8]. While more is still to be learned on the exact mechanisms and relationships between viral load and disease severity and progression, a good public health rule of thumb is the viral load required to trigger a positive test on an LFA is can be considered the contagiousness threshold [1]. This is in comparison to polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based tests in which patients can test positive for many weeks, when they are well beyond the contagious phase of the illness. Therefore, our “time-to-negative” model has implications for patient quarantine. As COVID-19 enters its 5th year, we have become generally comfortable living alongside it. A chief concern among the health-educated public concerns the length of quarantine time necessary after a positive test to ensure the safety of others. Rather than relying on waiting for a negative test (purchasing multiple expensive and somewhat invasive tests in the process), this tool enables users to more effective “plan” their lives and schedules concerning their recovery. As soon as symptoms arrive and a positive test is returned, our technology can estimate the viral load and, from that, predict approximately when it will be safe for that person to end their quarantine.

Incorporation of this technology into a smartphone app has the potential adds a tool to the nation’s public health toolbelt, as accessibility is key for widespread adoption, especially when considering that LFA tests in this context are primarily home-based tests. Furthermore, LFA tests are used to test multiple diseases, not just COVID-19. Given the similarity of readout between LFAs for different diseases (e.g. the BinaxNow tested in this study was originally developed to test for influenza), our data suggests that this technology can be adaptable for multiple diseases. To that end, we have designed the computer vision and disease modeling algorithms with smartphone integration in mind. These models were trained using smartphone images, and they employ established statistical principals, facilitating their incorporation into a smartphone app. As smartphone adoption is ubiquitous globally the development of this app is key for this technology to unlock its unique public health capabilities.

While this automated LFA reader technology has the potential to impact how people manage contagious illnesses, there are some drawbacks that must be considered. There is the potential for user error that has not been tested thus far. Technology users in the real world have differing degrees of technological proficiency, and the likelihood of lay users taking images of similar quality to those in this study taken by trained clinicians is low. Further study in home use settings is necessary to quantify this impact. Furthermore, LFAs are made by multiple manufacturers for many different diseases. These tests have different designs that may not align with the features our computer vision model focuses on to determine the region of interest for test evaluation. We are confident, however, that key design and branding elements from a variety of tests can serve the same purpose and that the algorithms can be updated and customized with minimal additional effort. Additionally, the comparatively lower specificity in relation to sensitivity of this technology could lead to false positives, where users are told they are still positive and are forced to an unnecessarily long quarantine period. While there are convenience and quality-of-life impacts to this scenario, the clinical and public health impact is minimal, compared to the alternative, where a user is incorrectly told they are negative and are allowed to rejoin society, potentially infecting many more individuals. To that end, we prioritized sensitivity over specificity when developing this “time-to-negative” model. Finally, we must acknowledge the possibility that small sample sizes in this study could have led to overfitting of our models. A large-scale, home-based study addresses all the above concerns and will be critical to our development and rollout strategy. Overall, this technology represents the first COVID-19 disease progression tool, and its development and rollout as a smartphone has significant implications for public health and the daily lives of millions worldwide.

Methods

SARS-CoV-2 sample collection

SARS-CoV-2” samples were collected from patients via nasal swab. The swab was then placed in 3mL of a saline solution in a sealed sample tube. The sample was then frozen at − 20°C and was stored frozen until it was ready to be analyzed, at which point it was thawed at room temperature.

Laboratory image collection

A first set of laboratory image-based training data was derived from well characterized SARS-CoV-2 samples. Live remnant clinical samples were pooled and diluted using negative nasal wash into a 2 fold dilution series. N2 CT was determined using the CDC assay [9]. Antigen quantification was determined by the Quanterix HD-X. 50ul of sample was spiked onto the BinaxNow swab and the test was completed per the IFU with N = 3 replicates for odd numbered dilutions (Table 2).

Second set of training images collected

Image capture was standardized using by placing all cards inside a commercially available lightbox [10], blocking all outside light and utilizing the internal light source with brightness set to 100% to maintain a consistent lighting environment for all images. The BinaxNow card was placed in the center of the lightbox so the edges of the card were fully visible within the camera frame. Images were captured with an iPhone 13 Pro using 26 mm f1.5 lens and by placing the phone on top of the lightbox and setting a two second shutter timer. Automatic exposure settings were used. Final images were exported to JPEG with 3024 × 4032 pixels and image data sizes ~2 MB (Fig. 4).

Clinical image data collection

A substudy was conducted to collect clinical samples with the aim to predict what day a person will test negative on a BinaxNOW device. Participants for this substudy were participants who have already enrolled in a large parent cross-sectional study in the Rapid Acceleration of Diagnostics (RADx) infrastructure. Clinical data was stored in a password-protected REDCap database [11, 12], and data was downloaded onto a password-protected university-monitored laptop.

Inclusion criteria for this substudy included agreeing to participate in the substudy after being briefly informed, presenting symptoms within 0–3 days of the date of enrollment, and testing positive on a BinaxNOW device at the day of enrollment. Any participant who did not satisfy one of the three inclusion criteria above was unable to participate in the study (there is one participant who was enrolled and presented symptoms 4 days prior to their enrollment date). Subjects were requested to return 3–5 days later.

Images of BinaxNow tests were taken within a controlled lighting lightbox and outside the lightbox freehand. Each test resulted in two images.

Image processing

Image processing (Fig. 5) was performed on a laptop using a computer vision algorithm developed in Python using the openCV [13] package which executes in O(n) time. Key landmarks printed in magenta ink were used to segment the test strip from a digital photo of each BinaxNOW card (Fig. 3a-e). A color difference image (red + blue - green) was calculated as a robust means to distinguish the card header from the rest of the photo. An Otsu binarization threshold was applied to extract a bounding rectangle of the card header. The dimensions and position of the header bounding rectangle were then used to crop and rotate the card into a portrait orientation. The test result region was cropped based on calculated offsets from the card boundaries. As with the card header, three magenta lines were extracted using the color difference image. The test strip was cropped from the region between and above these lines. The control and sample regions of the test strip were found by plotting the intensity of the color difference image. Peaks indicated the presence of gold nanoparticle-tagged antibody and were used to locate regions of interest (ROIs) on the LFA strip that must be analyzed to provide a diagnosis.

Calculation of accumulated line intensity

A novel measure was developed to account for differing lighting conditions over the test strip (Fig. 6a-d), as well as the variation in intensity and distribution of gold-tagged antibody indicator present within control and sample lines (Fig. 4e-f). First, the mean grayscale intensity across each pixel row of the test strip was calculated. A baseline intensity (Fig. 4g) was interpolated using a linear fit from the mean of the top and bottom 20% of each ROI to estimate the test strip intensity without bound indicator. Then, the difference between the estimated baseline and the observed intensity was summed to produce an accumulated line intensity (ALI) for both the control and sample lines. The ratio of the sample ALI to the control ALI was then used as a normalized metric for each LFA.

Prediction model construction on laboratory data

Two simple regression models were fitted on a set of 18 laboratory BinaxNOW samples to assess the strength of the relationship between ALI ratios and both cycle threshold (CT) and antigen concentration (measured in pg/mL). These two models were an exponential model using CT as the outcome, and a sigmoidal dose-response curve using antigen concentration (measured in pg/mL) as the outcome. In both models, the calculated ALI ratio was the lone predictor. The dose-response curve model fitting was performed using the drc package in R [14].

To assess these models’ predictive performance, these models were tested on a separate set of 30 blinded laboratory samples, and scatterplots were created of each model’s predicted value for either CT or antigen concentration versus the actual recorded value to visually assess performance. Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) was defined as the metric to assess testing performance. There were 6 samples that had an associated CT value of 0.0; these samples were excluded from the calculation of the RMSE concerning the difference between predicted CT and actual CT and concerning the difference between predicted antigen concentration and actual antigen concentration.

Time to negative predictions

Once the model had been trained and validated, plots of an individual’s predicted probability of testing negative given their baseline ALI ratio calculation for a given image were created. To do this, the prediction model defined in the previous section was fit at their baseline visit (so days since baseline = 0) and was extrapolated further by refitting the model, incrementing the days since baseline variable by 1 until 14 days since baseline was reached (Figure S1). 80% prediction intervals were plotted to assess estimated prediction error. The 80% prediction intervals were calculated using the merTools package, and these intervals and their corresponding predicted values were plotted using the ggplot2 package in R [17, 18].

App development

The native android app was developed in java using Android Studio. Multiple Android devices were used for testing and development of the app.
