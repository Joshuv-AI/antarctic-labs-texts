RedBench: A Universal Dataset for Comprehensive Red Teaming of Large Language Models
Quy-Anh Dang, Chris Ngo, Truong-Son Hy

Abstract
As large language models (LLMs) become integral to safety-critical applications, ensuring their robustness against adversarial prompts is paramount. However, existing red teaming datasets suffer from inconsistent risk categorizations, limited domain coverage, and outdated evaluations, hindering systematic vulnerability assessments. To address these challenges, we introduce RedBench, a universal dataset aggregating 37 benchmark datasets from leading conferences and repositories, comprising 29,362 samples across attack and refusal prompts. RedBench employs a standardized taxonomy with 22 risk categories and 19 domains, enabling consistent and comprehensive evaluations of LLM vulnerabilities. We provide a detailed analysis of existing datasets, establish baselines for modern LLMs, and open-source the dataset and evaluation code 1 1 1 Source: https://github.com/knoveleng/redeval . Our contributions facilitate robust comparisons, foster future research, and promote the development of secure and reliable LLMs for real-world deployment. Why is there no universal dataset that provides consistent risk categorization and comprehensive evaluation in diverse domains?

To ensure accessibility and reproducibility, we open-source both the RedBench dataset and the associated evaluation code.

Methodology

2.1
Data Collection

To construct RedBench, a high-quality and comprehensive dataset for red teaming large language models (LLMs), we aggregated 37 benchmark datasets sourced from leading peer-reviewed conferences, journals, and repositories. These sources include prominent venues such as the Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS), the Annual Meeting of the Association for Computational Linguistics (ACL), the International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), and the preprint repository arXiv, among others. The selection criteria prioritized peer-reviewed status, relevance to red teaming objectives, and coverage of diverse risk scenarios, ensuring a robust and representative foundation for a universal red-teaming dataset.

The resulting corpus comprises 29,362 samples, covering a wide range of prompt types designed to probe LLM vulnerabilities. These samples are categorized into two primary red teaming directions:

•

Attack
: This direction evaluates the susceptibility of the model to harmful or adversarial prompts that aim to elicit unsafe, biased, or erroneous responses
(
Dang et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
. Of the 37 datasets, 33 focus on this direction, including well-known benchmarks such as HarmBench
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
)
, AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, and DAN
(
Shen et al. 2024a
)
. These datasets contain instructions designed to exploit vulnerabilities in areas such as toxicity, misinformation, and jailbreaking.

•

Refusal
: This direction assesses the model’s tendency to over-defend by refusing benign or legitimate prompts, which can hinder usability
(
Brahman et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2024
)
. Four datasets address this direction: CoCoNot
(
Brahman et al. 2024
)
, ORBench
(
Cui et al. 2024
)
, SGXTest
(
Gupta et al. 2024
)
and XSTest
(
Röttger et al. 2024
)
. These datasets include prompts designed to test the boundaries of appropriate refusal behavior, ensuring that models do not reject harmless requests unnecessarily.

This dual-focus approach ensures that RedBench captures both offensive vulnerabilities (via attack prompts) and defensive overreach (via refusal prompts), providing a holistic framework for evaluating LLM robustness.

To ensure the quality and relevance of RedBench, each dataset was meticulously curated based on several criteria: (1)
Task Relevance
: Datasets were selected for their alignment with red teaming objectives, focusing on prompts that test model safety, robustness, or refusal behavior. (2)
Risk Scenario Coverage
: The datasets collectively cover a wide spectrum of risk categories, including toxicity, bias, misinformation, jailbreaking, and over-refusal, addressing both offensive and defensive failure modes. (3)
Data Integrity
: Only datasets with clear documentation, reproducible prompts, and verified sources were included to ensure reliability and usability. This rigorous curation process guarantees that RedBench serves as a high-quality, standardized resource for evaluating LLMs in diverse red-teaming scenarios.

By aggregating and harmonizing these 37 datasets, RedBench provides a unified and comprehensive platform for red teaming research. The dataset’s extensive coverage of attack and refusal prompts, combined with its diverse and credible sources, positions RedBench as a valuable tool for benchmarking modern LLMs and advancing the development of robust and secure language models.

2.2
Taxonomy for Datasets

A critical limitation of existing red teaming datasets is the lack of consistency in risk definitions and categorizations, which often results in labels that are overlapped, ambiguous, or poorly defined
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2024
)
. This fragmentation hinders cross-dataset comparisons and complicates systematic evaluations of LLM vulnerabilities. To address this challenge, we developed a standardized taxonomy for adversarial prompts in RedBench, assigning to each of the 29,362 samples two labels:
Risk Category
and
Domain
. This taxonomy unifies disparate datasets, ensures clarity in risk and context classification, and facilitates comprehensive red teaming evaluations across diverse scenarios.

The taxonomy is structured around two dimensions:
Risk Category
, which identifies the type of harm or misuse a prompt may elicit, and
Domain
, which specifies the contextual area in which the prompt is situated. By applying this dual-labeling approach, RedBench enables researchers to analyze LLM vulnerabilities with granularity, supporting both broad risk assessments and domain-specific investigations. The following subsections detail the definitions and annotation process for these labels.

2.2.1
Risk Categories

We define 22 distinct risk categories, each corresponding to a specific type of harm or misuse that LLMs might enable or exacerbate. These categories, presented in Table
8
, were developed through a systematic review of existing red teaming frameworks
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Xie et al. 2024
)
, safety guidelines from organizations such as NIST
(
Autio et al. 2024
)
and OWASP
(
OWASP Foundation 2025
)
. Each category is precisely defined to avoid overlap, is grounded in real-world implications, and is applicable to a wide range of testing scenarios. For prompts designed to evaluate refusal behavior (i.e., benign prompts that should not be rejected), we assign the
Risk Category
as “No Risk” to distinguish them from adversarial prompts.

2.2.2
Domains

To capture the contextual diversity of adversarial prompts, we define 19 domains, each representing a specific area of application or use case for LLMs. These domains, listed in Table
9
, were informed by a comprehensive analysis of LLM deployment scenarios
(
Singhal et al. 2022
;
Maddela et al. 2023
)
, stakeholder consultations, and application-specific red-teaming studies
(
Bhatt et al. 2023
;
Han et al. 2024
)
. The domains range from specialized fields such as healthcare and military to broader areas such as general knowledge, ensuring that RedBench reflects the multifaceted contexts in which LLMs operate.

2.2.3
Annotation Process

To assign
Risk Category
and
Domain
labels to all 29,362 samples in RedBench, we implemented a semi-automated annotation pipeline that combines the efficiency of state-of-the-art LLMs with the reliability of human oversight. The process utilized Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct
(
Team 2024c
)
, selected for its strong instruction-following capabilities and high performance in classification tasks, further validated by high agreement with human annotators on a random sample of 300 prompts (see Appendix
C
for details). The annotation pipeline proceeded as follows:

1.

Prompt Design
: We developed detailed prompts to guide the LLM in classifying each sample based on the definitions in Tables
8
and
9
. These prompts included clear instructions, category/domain definitions, and example annotations to reduce ambiguity and ensure consistent labeling. The prompts were iteratively refined based on pilot annotations to optimize model performance.

2.

Automated Labeling
: The Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct model processed the entire dataset sequentially, assigning a
Risk Category
and a
Domain
label to each sample. To prevent model fatigue or context overload, the dataset was processed in batches, with periodic checks to ensure label consistency.

3.

Human Validation
: To enhance accuracy, especially for datasets targeting specific domains (e.g., cybersecurity
(
Bhatt et al. 2023
)
or healthcare
(
Han et al. 2024
)
), we reviewed a subset of the LLM-generated labels. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and domain-specific samples were re-labeled as needed to align with expert knowledge.

This hybrid annotation approach balanced scalability with precision, leveraging the efficiency of LLMs while incorporating human expertise to maintain high-quality labels. The resulting RedBench dataset includes samples, each tagged with a unique
Risk Category
and
Domain
, ready for use in systematic red teaming evaluations.

Key Findings and Implications

The analysis reveals significant imbalances in the distribution of risk categories and domains within RedBench. Attack prompts are heavily concentrated in a few risk categories (e.g., Abusive Content, Cybersecurity Threats) and domains (e.g., General Knowledge, Technology), while categories like Confabulation and domains like Religion are underrepresented. Similarly, refusal prompts are skewed toward General Knowledge, with limited coverage in specialized domains. These imbalances reflect the focus of existing datasets and highlight gaps in red-teaming resources for certain risks and contexts. Addressing these gaps through targeted dataset expansion will be critical to ensuring comprehensive evaluations of LLM robustness across various scenarios.

3
Experiments

To evaluate the effectiveness of RedBench as a universal dataset for red-teaming large language models (LLMs), we conducted a series of experiments to assess the robustness of state-of-the-art LLMs against adversarial and refusal prompts. This section outlines the evaluation metrics, target LLMs, experimental setup, and implementation details. Our experiments aim to establish baselines for modern LLMs, quantify their vulnerabilities across risk categories and domains, and provide insights to guide future red-teaming research. All code and evaluation scripts are open-sourced to ensure reproducibility and foster community-driven advancements.

3.1
Metrics

For the 4 refusal-focused datasets in RedBench, which test the model’s tendency to over-defend by rejecting benign prompts, we use the
Rejection Rate (RR)
as the primary metric. The Rejection Rate measures the proportion of legitimate prompts that are incorrectly refused by the model, calculated as:

\displaystyle\text{RR}=\frac{\text{Number of Refused Legitimate Prompts}}{\text{Total Number of Prompts}}\times 100\%.

(1)

A high Rejection Rate indicates over-defensive behavior, which can compromise the model’s usability in real-world applications. To determine whether a prompt is refused, we employ GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2025
)
as a LLM judge, selected for its robust instruction-following and classification capabilities. The judge LLM classifies a response as a refusal if it explicitly declines to answer or provides a generic deflection (e.g., “I’m sorry, I can’t assist with that”).

For the 33 attack-focused datasets, which aim to elicit unsafe, biased, or erroneous responses, we use the
Attack Success Rate (ASR)
as the primary metric. The ASR quantifies the proportion of prompts that successfully bypass the target LLM’s safety mechanisms, defined as:

\displaystyle\text{ASR}=\frac{\text{Number of Successful Attacks}}{\text{Total Number of Attempted Attacks}}\times 100\%,

(2)

where a successful attack is recorded when a prompt elicits an unsafe or inappropriate response, as determined by Llama-Guard-3-8B
(
Llama Team 2024
)
, a specialized LLM judge designed for a robust safety assessment. The ASR provides a direct measure of the effectiveness of adversarial prompts in exploiting LLM vulnerabilities.

4
Conclusion

Our paper presents RedBench, a unified dataset for large language model red teaming that integrates 37 benchmarks into a standardized framework comprising 29,362 samples. By introducing a taxonomy of 22 risk categories and 19 domains, RedBench enables consistent evaluation of LLM vulnerabilities and reveals substantial imbalances in existing benchmarks, such as the overrepresentation of Abusive Content and General Knowledge. Empirical results show that open-source models are particularly vulnerable to adversarial prompts, especially under RainbowPlus, while closed-source models demonstrate stronger resistance. High Rejection Rates in refusal benchmarks further highlight usability challenges.

Our contributions include
(1) a standardized red teaming dataset
,
(2) an analysis identifying gaps in risk and domain coverage
, and
(3) baseline evaluations of contemporary LLMs released alongside the dataset
. Future work will expand underrepresented categories and domains, evaluate a broader range of models, and incorporate more advanced red teaming methods. Overall, RedBench provides a foundation for systematic and reproducible red teaming, supporting the development of safer and more robust LLMs.

Limitation

RedBench offers a robust and standardized resource for red teaming large language models (LLMs), aggregating a diverse set of existing datasets into a unified framework. Nonetheless, as with any large-scale dataset, certain aspects reflect the current state of the field and present opportunities for further refinement.

The sample distribution in RedBench mirrors the emphasis found in prior red teaming efforts, with higher representation of well-studied risk categories such as Abusive Content and Cybersecurity Threats, and domains like General Knowledge and Technology (Section
2.3
). Categories like Confabulation and Election Interference, as well as domains such as Religion and Travel, have comparatively fewer samples. Future expansions could incorporate additional targeted collections to achieve even broader coverage across emerging risk scenarios.

The annotation process employs a scalable semi-automated pipeline with state-of-the-art LLM assistance and human validation (Section
2.2.3
). While effective, ongoing advancements in annotation techniques could further enhance consistency, particularly for complex or ambiguous cases.

Our experimental evaluation focuses on a selection of representative LLMs available as of late 2025 (Section
3.2
). Continuous benchmarking with newly released models would provide valuable insights into evolving safety alignments. Additionally, RedBench primarily comprises English-language prompts, consistent with the majority of sourced datasets; extending to multilingual content would broaden its applicability.

These considerations highlight pathways to strengthen RedBench as the landscape of LLM safety research progresses, ensuring it continues to serve as a valuable community resource.

Ethics Statement

The development and evaluation of RedBench, a comprehensive dataset for red teaming large language models (LLM), aims to improve model safety and robustness by systematically identifying vulnerabilities through adversarial and refusal prompts. We acknowledge the ethical implications of red teaming, as the 29,362 samples in the dataset, including prompts targeting sensitive risk categories (e.g., Extremism and Radicalization, Child Safety; see Table
8
), could potentially be misused to exploit LLMs in harmful ways. To mitigate this risk, we adhere to responsible research practices and prioritize transparency in our methodology and findings.

We advocate for ethical red teaming practices, including collaboration with model developers to mitigate vulnerabilities and adherence to legal and ethical standards in AI safety research. The goal of RedBench is to support the trustworthy deployment of LLMs in safety-critical domains such as healthcare and finance, balancing rigorous evaluation with the prevention of harm. We remain committed to transparency and community oversight to ensure that RedBench contributes positively to AI safety.

, reveal both the strengths and vulnerabilities of modern LLMs, highlighting key challenges and opportunities to advance the robustness of the model.

The evaluation of refusal benchmarks (CoCoNut, ORBench, SGXSTest, and XSTest) demonstrates significant variability in Rejection Rates across models. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct’s high Rejection Rate (28.53% average) indicates an over-defensive stance, particularly in domains like General Knowledge, which may limit its usability in real-world applications where legitimate prompts are common
(

The high Rejection Rate of GPT-4.1-Nano on ORBench (35.18%) underscores the influence of dataset-specific prompt design, suggesting that refusal benchmarks require further standardization to ensure consistent evaluation across models.

For attack benchmarks, the Attack Success Rates (ASRs) on HarmBench reveal stark differences between open-source and closed-source models. Open-source models, particularly Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410 (97.81% ASR with RainbowPlus) and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (96.25% ASR with RainbowPlus), exhibit significant vulnerabilities to adversarial prompts, especially in risk categories such as Economic Harm and Extremism and Radicalization, and domains such as Environment and Nutrition
(
Dang et al. 2025
)
. In contrast, closed-source models such as GPT-4.1-Nano (6.88% ASR with RainbowPlus) and GPT-4o-Mini (28.75% ASR with RainbowPlus) demonstrate robust resistance, probably due to advanced safety alignment techniques. The effectiveness of the RainbowPlus method across both types of models highlights its potency as a red teaming strategy, which warrants further investigation of its mechanisms and potential countermeasures.

The analysis in Section
2.3
Overview of Analysis
reveals significant imbalances in RedBench’s composition, with risk categories like Abusive Content (3,523 samples) and Cybersecurity Threats (2,906 samples) dominating attack prompts, while categories like Confabulation (71 samples) and Election Interference (158 samples) are underrepresented. Similarly, domains like General Knowledge (8,581 attack samples) and Technology (4,041 attack samples) are overrepresented, while domains like Religion (122 attack samples) and Travel (102 attack samples) are sparse. These imbalances reflect the focus of existing datasets and highlight gaps in coverage for certain risks and contexts. For example, the limited representation of Election Interference prompts may hinder LLM evaluations in political contexts, a critical area given their potential to influence democratic processes
(
Autio et al. 2024
)
. Addressing these gaps through targeted dataset augmentation is essential for ensuring comprehensive red-teaming evaluations.

Despite its contributions, RedBench has limitations that merit discussion. First, the reliance on existing datasets introduces biases inherent in their design, such as the skew toward General Knowledge and Technology domains. Second, the semi-automated annotation pipeline, while efficient, may introduce inconsistencies in edge cases, despite human validation efforts
(Section
2.2.3
Annotation Process
).
Third, the evaluation of only six LLMs, constrained by API access limitations for models like Google Gemini and Anthropic Claude, limits the generalizability of our findings. Finally, the focus on HarmBench for attack evaluations, while representative, may not fully capture the diversity of adversarial prompts in RedBench, suggesting the need for broader testing across all 33 attack datasets.

The findings have broader implications for LLM development and deployment. The high ASRs of open-source models underscore the need for improved safety alignment techniques, particularly for domains like Environment and Nutrition, where vulnerabilities are pronounced. The trade-off between safety and usability, evident in high Rejection Rates, suggests that developers must balance defensive mechanisms to avoid compromising user experience. In addition, the open source of RedBench and its evaluation code promotes transparency and reproducibility, allowing the research community to build on our work and develop stronger LLMs for safety-critical applications such as healthcare
(
Singhal et al. 2022

and legal advisory systems

Appendix B
Related Work

This section reviews prior work on red-teaming large language models (LLMs), focusing on automated red-teaming techniques and datasets designed for red-teaming. We situate RedBench within this landscape, highlighting its contributions to addressing the limitations of existing approaches, particularly in dataset standardization and comprehensive coverage of risk scenarios.

B.1
Automated Red Teaming

Red teaming is critical for evaluating and aligning LLMs by identifying vulnerabilities that lead to unsafe, biased, or incorrect output
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
. Traditional manual red teaming, while effective in controlled settings, is labor-intensive and scales poorly with the complexity of modern LLMs. Automated red teaming has emerged as a scalable alternative, using computational methods to generate adversarial prompts that elicit undesirable responses. The early work of
Perez et al. 2022a
pioneered this field, demonstrating that LLMs could autonomously generate test cases to uncover harmful outputs, reducing the dependence on human oversight. Subsequent research has advanced this paradigm through fully automated and hybrid strategies. For example,
Deng et al. 2023
combined manual curation with in-context learning to improve prompt efficacy, while
Paulus et al. 2024
introduced an optimization algorithm that prioritizes efficiency and speed in prompt generation.
Liu et al. 2023
developed goal-oriented adversarial attack frameworks, tailoring prompts to specific failure modes such as toxicity or misinformation. Furthermore, quality-diversity search techniques, such as Rainbow
(
Samvelyan et al. 2024
)
and its enhanced version RainbowPlus
(
Dang et al. 2025
)
, have improved the diversity and effectiveness of adversarial prompts, allowing for more comprehensive vulnerability assessments. These advancements underscore the need for robust datasets to support automated red teaming, which RedBench aims to address.

B.2
Datasets for Red Teaming

The development of red-teaming datasets has been pivotal in enabling systematic evaluation of LLM vulnerabilities. Existing datasets, such as HarmBench
(
Mazeika et al. 2024
)
, AdvBench
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
, and DAN
(
Shen et al. 2024a
)
, focus on attack prompts designed to elicit unsafe responses, covering risks such as toxicity, misinformation, and jailbreaking. However, these datasets often lack standardized risk categorizations, leading to overlapping or ambiguous labels that hinder cross-dataset comparisons. For refusal testing, datasets like CoCoNut
(
Brahman et al. 2024
)
, ORBench
(
Cui et al. 2024
)
, and StrongREJECT
(
Souly et al. 2024
)
assess over-defensive behavior by evaluating responses to benign prompts, but their limited domain coverage and inconsistent prompt design limit generalizability. Specialized datasets, such as PurpleLlamaCyberSecEval
(
Bhatt et al. 2023
)
for cybersecurity and MedSafetyBench
(
Han et al. 2024
)
for healthcare, address domain-specific risks, but lack integration into a unified framework.

RedBench addresses these limitations by aggregating 37 benchmark datasets, comprising 29,362 samples, into a standardized framework with a comprehensive taxonomy of 22 risk categories and 19 domains (see Section
2.2
). Unlike previous datasets, RedBench unifies attack and refusal prompts, ensuring broad coverage of risk scenarios and facilitating systematic evaluations.

Appendix G
Red-Teaming Methods

This section contains details of the Red-Teaming methods used in

3.3.2
Results on Attack Benchmark Datasets
.

•

Zero-Shot

(
Perez et al. 2022c
)
: Generate test cases using an attacker LLM in a zero-shot setting, prioritizing scalability, but often lacking specificity for targeted behaviors.

•

Human Jailbreaks (Human)

(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
: Applies in-the-wild jailbreak templates (e.g. Do Anything Now, DAN) with substituted behavior strings, testing template-driven attacks but lacking adaptability.

•

Direct
: Uses raw behavior strings as prompts to test LLMs’ refusal capabilities, serving as a lower-bound baseline for safety alignment.

•

RainbowPlus

(
Dang et al. 2025
)
: A adaptive Quality-Diversity search, that achieves superior attack efficacy and prompt diversity, outperforming both QD-based methods and state-of-the-art red-teaming approaches.

– Content that promotes or facilitates economic crimes or disruptions, such as market manipulation, insider trading, or large-scale fraud.
