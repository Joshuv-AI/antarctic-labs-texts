The relationship between food components and constipation: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuhan Xu, Zengfu Xue
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe worldwide incidence of constipation is exhibiting an increasing trend. The objective of this study was to assess the relationship between dietary components and the occurrence of constipation.MethodsSociodemographic characteristics data of participants experiencing constipation and those without were sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; 2000–2010; n = 1903; ≥20 years). Sociodemographic characteristics of participants experiencing constipation and those without were sourced from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). Data from two 24-hour dietary recalls (n = 1696; participants aged 20 years and older) were utilized to compare the dietary intake of various food components between constipated and non-constipated individuals, as well as to investigate the relationship between food component consumption and constipation. Odds ratios (OR) were calculated using an adjusted model. The distribution of the data was illustrated through a histogram. Based on this distribution, vegetable intake was categorized into five groups (0-0.733, 0.733–1.47, 1.47–2.2, 2.2–2.93, 2.93-8) for the purpose of subgroup analysis.ResultsThe median age of individuals experiencing constipation is 41.5 years, with women accounting for 65.3% and men accounting for 34.7%. The consumption of fruits (constipated people = 0.79 cups/d; non-constipated people = 0.93 cups/d), vegetables (1.21 cups/d; 1.59 cups/d), dairy (1.68 cups/d; 1.55 cups/d), and oils (15.31 g/d; 19.61 g/d) is below the recommended dietary intake for both groups. After multivariable adjustment, vegetable consumption (OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.59–0.92, p = 0.008) and female (OR: 2.12, 95% CI: 1.38–3.24, p < 0.001) were identified as significant predictors of constipation. The subgroup analysis concerning vegetable consumption indicated a statistically significant result within Group (2.93,8 ] [OR: 0.2, 95% CI: 0.069–0.57].ConclusionsThe findings of the research suggest that it is advisable for individuals to consume a minimum of 2.93 cups of vegetables daily. Furthermore, females exhibit a significantly elevated risk of experiencing constipation. However, no association has been identified between constipation and either age or educational level.

Introduction

Constipation is a symptomatic disease, mainly manifested by poor stool and/or difficulty in defecation. Defecation difficulties include laborious defecation, difficulty in defecation, the feeling of incomplete defecation, time-consuming defecation, and the necessity for manual assistance in defecation, which has existed for at least 3 months within the past 12 months [1]. The global prevalence of functional constipation is 10.1% (Rome IV criteria). Regardless of the criteria employed, including the Rome criteria, the prevalence of functional constipation among women is higher [2]. Furthermore, the incidence of constipation tends to rise with advancing age, particularly after the age of 65 [3]. In the United States, constipation is responsible for approximately 2.5 million medical consultations annually [4], with an average cost of $2,752 per patient [5]. However, about half of the patients with constipation are not satisfied with the treatment effect [6].

Constipation is associated with factors such as female gender, low socioeconomic status, negative emotional states, and dietary habits. At present, opinions differ on the relationship between dietary and constipation. In general, eating less cellulose food, following a low-residue diet, consuming less food, reducing intake, and drinking more coffee will increase the incidence of constipation [7–9]. A cross-sectional study points out that less fluid intake will increase the risk of constipation, but constipation is not related to fiber intake and exercise [10]. Additionally, clinical trials have demonstrated that the efficacy of dietary fiber in the management of constipation is limited [11]. However, in a study of university students, the occurrence of functional constipation characterized by a “complex” dietary pattern was found to be significantly greater than that of students adhering to “vegetable, fruit, egg, and milk-based” and “livestock and aquatic product-based” dietary patterns (9.9% vs. 3.1% vs. 2.8%, p < 0.001) [12]. Research indicates that higher consumption of alpha carotene may be linked to a decreased likelihood of chronic constipation among women [13]. A high consumption of live microorganisms in the diet may lead to a decrease in the incidence of constipation. Additionally, a study found a negative correlation between the intake of dietary copper and the occurrence of chronic constipation in adults [14].

Epidemiological and clinical studies support the correlation between constipation and dietary, but there are few studies on the relationship between food components and constipation. The primary aim of this study is to investigate the association of individual food components with the occurrence of constipation. Additionally, the secondary objective is to characterize the prevalence of constipation, as defined by the Bristol Stool Form Scale, within a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, and to evaluate potential factors that may be associated with constipation.

Methods

Study population and data collection

The data from individuals who participated in NHANES from 2009 to 2010 were used for a cross-sectional secondary data analysis. NHANES is an ongoing, nationally representative study on the nutrition and health status of the civilian population in noninstitutionalized institutions in the United States. Detailed design and data collection procedures for NHANES can be available in other sources. In short, NHANES adopts a complex, multistage, probability cluster design. The Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) approved the protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent. The NHANES 2009–2010 cycle provides a comprehensive overview and detailed characterization of the study participants. These individuals underwent both physical and laboratory assessments at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). Inquiries regarding gastrointestinal symptoms were conducted in the MEC interview room utilizing the Computer-Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) system. Dietary intake data were gathered in collaboration with the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the United States Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) through two 24-hour dietary recall interviews. The first dietary recall interview was collected in person at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), and the second interview was collected by telephone after 3 to 10 days.

Study variables

The Bristol Stool Form Scale was employed to assess stool consistency, categorizing stool morphology into seven distinct types. These types include: type 1 (separate hard lumps, like nuts), type 2 (sausage-like, but lumpy), type 3 (like a sausage but with cracks in the surface), type 4 (like a sausage or snake, smooth and soft), type 5 (soft blobs with clear-cut edges), type 6 (fluffy pieces with ragged edges, a mushy stool), and type 7 (watery, no solid pieces). Participants were asked to look at the color card (picture and text description of the 7 stool types) and tell the researchers the number corresponding to the type of stool they usually or most often see. Constipation is defined as type 1 or type 2, and non-constipation is defined as types other than type 1 and type 2 [10].

As part of the What We Eat in the United States (WWEIA) survey, trained interviewers gathered self-reported dietary intake data from adults through two 24-hour dietary recalls. WWEIA is a collaborative project between the USDA and the DHHS. According to this partnership, DHHS is responsible for survey sample design and data collection, while the USDA is responsible for data encoding, processing, and review of the dietary database. The food and beverage consumption recorded in the two 24-hour dietary recalls will be converted into the 2-day food components as defined by the USDA, utilizing the Food Patterns Equivalents Database (FPED). Dietary variables were established by calculating the average of the food pattern equivalents derived from the 2-day food components. The analysis encompassed nine dietary variables, namely fruits, vegetables, grains, protein foods, dairy, oils, solid fats, added sugars, and alcoholic beverages. The distributions of these dietary variables were categorized into quartiles.

The additional variables incorporated in this study are presented in Table 1. These variables were included in the analysis for the purpose of providing descriptive information and serving as potential covariates. Demographic information, including age (< 65, ≥ 65 years), gender, marital status (non-married, married), race and ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, other race), education (less than high school, high school/some college, or college graduate or above), and BMI (< 18.5, 18.5–24.9, > 24.9 kg/m²), was obtained through self-reporting. The power-to-income ratio (1.3, 1.3–3.5, > 3.5) is classified based on SNAP eligibility. Activities were defined by questions about “How much time do you spend doing moderate-intensity sports, fitness, or recreational activities on a typical day?” After the data analysis of the answers to this question, the quartile was used to divide the activity time into less than 30 min, between 30 and 60 min, and more than 60 min.

Analyses and statistical methods

The dataset utilized in this study is derived from four distinct NHANES documentation sources, namely demographics data, bowel health data, body measures data, and physical activity data, as well as the FPED database [15]. A total of 10,537 respondents participated in the 2009–2010 cycle of NHANES. Individuals with missing covariate data and those under the age of 20 (n = 8,634) were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, a sample of 1,903 participants was employed to examine the sociodemographic characteristics of individuals with constipation compared to those without. Following this, an additional exclusion of participants with missing food components data (n = 207) was performed, resulting in a final sample of 1,696 individuals for the analysis of the relationship between food components and constipation. The dataset comprises both categorical and continuous variables. Categorical variables are characterized using percentages, while continuous variables are described through the mean or interquartile range. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test is employed to assess the normality of continuous variables. In terms of statistical methods for p-value calculation, the t-test is applied when the data meet the normality assumption, whereas the Mann-Whitney test is utilized when this assumption is not satisfied. A p-value of less than 0.05 is deemed statistically significant for all tests, with the exception of the logistic regression model, where a p-value of less than 0.1 is considered statistically significant. The logistic regression model was employed to examine the association between food components and constipation, yielding an estimate of the odds ratio (OR) along with the associated 95% confidence interval (CI). In the analytical framework of this study, variables identified as significant through univariate analysis were incorporated into the multivariate analysis. Subsequently, those variables deemed significant in the multivariate context were included in the final analysis. The final analysis employed the stepwise backward elimination technique. Furthermore, the data distribution was illustrated using a histogram. All statistical analyses were conducted utilizing R version 4.2.1.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

The sociodemographic characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. The constipation cohort comprised 118 adults with a median age of 40 years. Among these participants, approximately 68.6% were female, and 69.7% identified as non-Hispanic White. Additionally, 7.9% identified as Mexican-American, 8.5% as non-Hispanic Black, 5.5% as other Hispanic, and 8.3% as belonging to other racial categories. Furthermore, 10.6% of the adults had an educational attainment of less than a high school diploma, while over half of the participants (60.3%) resided in households with partnered caregivers. Nearly 20% (19.7%) of the sample reported a low poverty-to-income ratio. The median BMI for the adults was 26.16 kg/m², with 58.7% classified as overweight. Additionally, 63.2% of the participants engaged in daily activities for a duration of 30 to 60 min.

Intake of food components in constipated and non-constipated individuals

Table 2 illustrates the daily intake equivalents for each food component. According to the dietary guidelines established by the USDA for residents of the United States, and based on a caloric requirement of 2000 kcal per day per individual, the median daily intake of all food components among individuals experiencing constipation is below the recommended levels. Notably, the consumption of oils is significantly lower than the suggested amount. In contrast, for individuals without constipation, only the intake of grains and protein foods marginally exceeds the recommended values. Figure 2 provides a visual representation of the disparities in intake and recommended values for various food components between constipated and non-constipated individuals. Overall, when employing a significance level of p < 0.05, there are statistically significant differences in the intake of vegetables, protein foods, and oils between the two groups.

Regression analysis of risk factors related to constipation

In order to investigate the association between dietary components and constipation, we employed logistic regression analysis, designating constipation status (either as constipated or non-constipated) as the dependent variable and various food components as independent variables. The findings from the analysis indicate that an increase of one unit in vegetable consumption is inversely associated with the likelihood of experiencing constipation, as evidenced by both the univariate analysis (Odds Ratio [OR]: 0.71, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.57–0.89, P value: 0.003) and the final model (OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.59–0.92, P value: 0.022). Although the analysis revealed a statistically significant p-value (p < 0.05) for added sugars, the corresponding OR value was 1.01, suggesting ambiguity regarding the impact of added sugar on constipation incidence. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the risk of constipation among women is 2.12 times greater than that of men (OR: 2.12, 95% CI: 1.38–3.24, p < 0.001). Further details are available in Table 3.

Recommended intake of vegetables

Our analysis indicates that while the vegetable consumption of both constipated and non-constipated individuals predominantly falls within the range of 0.8 to 1.07 cups per day, the variability in vegetable intake is greater among non-constipated individuals. Furthermore, it is observed that when vegetable intake exceeds 2.93 cups per day, there is a significant reduction in the incidence of constipation (Fig. 3). A segmented analysis of vegetable consumption reveals that individuals consuming between 2.93 and 8 cups per day [OR: 0.2, 95% CI: 0.069–0.57] are less likely to experience constipation compared to those in other intake categories (Table 4).

Discussion

The objective of this research was to examine the association between the intake of various food components and the incidence of constipation (defined by the Bristol Stool Form Scale) among American adults, utilizing data from the NHANES 2009–2010. The food components analyzed encompassed fruits, vegetables, grains, protein foods, dairy products, oil, solid fat, and added sugars. The findings indicated that the overall prevalence of constipation within the sample population was 6.2% [95% CI 5.1, 7.3]. After adjusting for other established factors contributing to adult constipation, the research identified an increased intake of vegetables as a modifiable factor that may contribute to a reduction in constipation incidence.

Compared with the recent systematic review and meta-analysis of the global prevalence of constipation of 10.1% [95% CI 8.7, 11.6], our overall prevalence estimate using the definition based on the Bristol Stool Form Scale is 6.2% [95% CI 5.1, 7.3] [2]. The prevalence of constipation among adults worldwide has been documented to range from 2.5 to 79%, highlighting significant variability in published estimates [16]. This discrepancy can be attributed primarily to the differing diagnostic criteria for constipation employed by various research entities, as well as the lack of standardization in survey methodologies. Notably, when considering the definition of constipation, the prevalence of self-reported symptoms tends to be higher than that derived from symptom-based questionnaires or assessments of stool frequency [10].

Similar to other cross-sectional studies of constipation, we found two related factors that were consistently reported in the literature. Overall, we identified common risk factors for constipation, including female [17–19] and low economic level [20, 21]. Notably, our study did not establish age as a significant risk factor for constipation, and the age of 65 years did not emerge as a critical threshold for increased susceptibility to constipation [1]. Conversely, some other cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have indicated that age is a significant risk factor for constipation [18, 22]. Furthermore, our investigation did not reveal a correlation between low education level and the incidence of constipation [23, 24].

At present, there are many studies on the relationship between dietary intake and the incidence of constipation [25–27]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials showed that fiber supplementation was effective in improving constipation. Notably, fiber intake is associated with an increase in the frequency of bowel movements, particularly at higher doses. Specifically, a dosage of psyllium exceeding 10 g per day, administered over a treatment period of more than four weeks, appears to yield the best therapeutic benefits [28]. Nevertheless, analogous literature has indicated that, in comparison to a placebo, high-dose fiber supplements (exceeding 15 g per day) markedly enhance the frequency of defecation [29]. From a sociocultural standpoint, individuals appear to possess a greater awareness of food components than of dietary fiber. However, there exists a paucity of research examining the correlation between the intake of food components and the incidence of constipation. In the present study, we conducted an analysis of various food components, including fruits, vegetables, grains, protein foods, dairy, oils, solid fats, and added sugars. We first conducted a univariate analysis, and the results show that, except for grains, dairy, and solid fats, all other food components are associated with constipation. After removing the confounding factors, the final optimal model shows that only vegetables, added sugar, and female are related to constipation. Strangely, the p-value of added sugar is less than 0.05, but the OR value is very close to 1. Nevertheless, an odds ratio (OR) value that approaches 1 should not be regarded as entirely devoid of significance. Firstly, the width of the confidence interval remains a critical factor, even when the OR is near 1. In the case of added sugars, the confidence interval is notably narrow and closely centered around 1, suggesting that the estimated values are highly precise and that the effect size is, in fact, near zero. Secondly, this investigation is based on a substantial sample size, which enables the detection of minimal effects. Although these effects may lack clinical relevance, they can still achieve statistical significance. In summary, the association between added sugars and constipation warrants additional investigation. Furthermore, while our research did not identify a correlation between constipation and fruit consumption, existing literature suggests that certain fruits, such as apples and mangoes, may have a beneficial effect on alleviating constipation [30, 31].

The previous studies showed that there was a correlation between vegetable intake and the incidence of constipation. In our analysis, we performed a subgroup analysis regarding the consumption of vegetables. The findings suggest that increased vegetable consumption is associated with a reduced risk of constipation. However, the odds ratio (OR) confidence interval for the group consuming 2.2–2.93 cups per day includes the value of 1, which may imply that vegetable intake within this range does not exhibit a statistically significant relationship with constipation (Table 4). This observation suggests that the risk of constipation does not diminish with an increase in vegetable intake until a certain threshold is surpassed. This conclusion aligns with the findings of prior studies [28, 29]. It is important to note the limitations of our study, which include the exclusion of the timing of vegetable intake as a variable and the lack of analysis regarding the relationship between constipation and specific types of vegetables.

Conclusion

In summary, the findings of our research indicate a notable association between vegetable consumption and the incidence of constipation among American adults. To mitigate the risk of constipation, it is advisable for individuals to incorporate at least 2.93 cups of vegetables into their daily diet. Nonetheless, it is crucial to emphasize that additional prospective studies are necessary to corroborate and strengthen these conclusions. Moreover, research indicates that females demonstrate a markedly higher susceptibility to constipation. Nevertheless, no correlation has been established between constipation and either age or educational attainment.
