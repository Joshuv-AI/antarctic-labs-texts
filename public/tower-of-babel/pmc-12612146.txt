Sleep quality and body composition in active and inactive young adults
Authors: Krystian Maruszczak, Wojciech Kasperek, Konrad Kustra, Grzegorz Przysada, Maciej Kochman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Body composition is influenced by a variety of internal and external factors, with early adulthood being a particularly sensitive period for its fluctuations. This observational study aimed to assess the differences in body composition, anthropometric indices, and sleep quality between active and inactive young adults as well as to explore the relationship between these parameters. A group of one hundred young adults were recruited for the study and assigned to two groups: physically active and physically inactive. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF), sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and body composition was measured using the validated InBody 770 analyzer. Participants were classified as physically active if they achieved a “moderate” or “high” activity level according to IPAQ-SF scoring criteria, which includes individuals engaging in at least 600 MET-minutes of physical activity per week. Those categorized as physically inactive scored at the “low” level, not meeting the thresholds for moderate or high activity. Physically active individuals showed significantly more favorable body composition, including higher total body water, muscle mass, and bone mineral content, as well as lower fat mass, BMI, WHR, WHtR, and ABSI values (all p < 0.001). They also had significantly better sleep quality, as indicated by lower Global Sleep Score (GSS) scores (p < 0.001). Significant associations were observed between sleep quality and body composition parameters, such as a positive correlation between the GSS and fat mass, as well as negative correlations between GSS and total body water, fat-free mass, and bone mineral content. Additionally, GSS showed positive correlations with both WHR and WHtR (p < 0.05). No significant associations were found between the GSS and muscle mass, nor between GSS and ABSI (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that regular physical activity is related to more favorable body composition and better sleep quality. Moreover, specific components of body composition, particularly fat mass and indicators of central adiposity, appear to be significantly associated with sleep quality, highlighting a potential link between metabolic health and sleep patterns. However, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, causality cannot be inferred.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23324-0.

Introduction

Physical activity is widely recognized as a cornerstone of health, exerting profound effects on both physical and mental health1,2. In recent years, the scientific community has increasingly focused on elucidating the multifaceted benefits of regular physical activity, ranging from cardiovascular3 and metabolic improvements to enhanced cognitive function, mental health and even hormonal regulation4,5. In accordance with scientific findings, regularly engaging in physical activity positively affects a wide range of physiological processes as well, including sleep. It may contribute to better sleep quality and increased duration6,7, as well as serve as an effective tool in supporting the treatment of its disorders, such as insomnia8. Furthermore, the current scientific literature abounds with evidence confirming that regular physical activity contributes to reducing the risk of chronic diseases9,10. Therefore, its importance in daily functioning is amplified, particularly in light of the increasing prevalence of screen time and sedentary lifestyles, which have extensive adverse effects on the human body and are linked to numerous negative health outcomes11.

Sleep is a fundamental biological necessity for humans and crucial for overall health and well-being12. It is a metabolically active state during which various internal processes occur, including hormone release and brain responses to numerous internal stimuli13. Concerning the sleep process, it presents itself as a multifaceted phenomenon devoid of a singular pattern. Consequently, both the quantity and quality of sleep have a substantial influence on human health14. Sleep serves numerous functions within the body, including restoring physical performance, enhancing synaptic plasticity, consolidating memory, and improving mental health15. Moreover, there is evidence that sleep plays a pivotal role in regulating body composition. Numerous scientific reports indicate that sleep deprivation may have an impact on the content of muscle16, fat17, and bone mass18. Furthermore, there is a growing body of literature that recognizes the importance of sleep disturbances in causing negative health consequences. In fact, insufficient or low-quality sleep has been linked to increased risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and cancer19.

The reciprocal relationship between physical activity and sleep has been increasingly emphasized in recent literature, with growing empirical support for a bidirectional model describing this interplay. This model posits that both variables influence one another through a combination of neuroendocrine, behavioral, and physiological mechanisms. For example, physical activity has been associated with improvements in sleep quality20, shorter sleep latency, and greater sleep efficiency, potentially through the regulation of circadian rhythms, thermoregulatory effects, reduced anxiety, and modulation of neuroendocrine factors such as cortisol and melatonin21,23–25. In turn, poor sleep can negatively affect physical activity behaviors, reducing motivation, energy levels, and executive functioning the following day23. This self-reinforcing feedback loop suggests that insufficient physical activity may disrupt the homeostatic and circadian regulation of sleep, while inadequate sleep further reduces physical activity engagement, perpetuating a cycle of dysregulation. Central to this dynamic are neuroendocrine feedback mechanisms, particularly those involving the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the autonomic nervous system, which mediate stress reactivity and metabolic homeostasis26. For instance, regular physical activity may attenuate HPA axis hyperactivity, often observed in individuals with sleep disorders, thereby improving overall sleep architecture27. Despite the theoretical robustness of this bidirectional framework, its application across diverse populations remains limited, and many studies are constrained by observational designs. Therefore, future research should aim to integrate objective assessments of both sleep and physical activity to test the model more rigorously and account for potential moderating factors such as sex, chronotype, or metabolic health status.

Body composition refers to the relative proportions of various tissues in the human body, predominantly fat, fat-free mass, bone, and water28. It is subject to dynamic alterations influenced primarily by energy balance, physical activity, and sleep quality. It is now well established from a variety of studies that not only the content of individual components is important but also maintaining the right proportions between them. Certainly, excessive fat tissue contributes to the onset of numerous diseases29, while inadequate muscle mass correlates with diminished quality of life, decreased physical fitness, and a shortened lifespan30. Additionally, decreased bone mass serves as a significant predisposing factor for bone fractures. Consequently, continuous monitoring of body composition, facilitated by methods such as bioelectrical impedance analysis31,32 or anthropometric measurements33, assumes critical importance, particularly considering the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity.

The rationale for this study is the increasing prevalence of sedentary lifestyles and screen time, which is a major public health problem34. There is evidence in the scientific literature that physical inactivity has a negative impact on various physiological processes, including sleep regulation35. Poor sleep, in turn, contributes to increased fat accumulation and increases susceptibility to obesity and related chronic diseases36. With this in mind, we want to clarify how key health indicators such as sleep quality, body composition and anthropometric indices differ between active and inactive individuals in order to enrich the existing literature in this area. With this study, we also want to raise awareness among people with a sedentary lifestyle of the specific health risks associated with inactivity and highlight the potential benefits of regular physical activity in everyday life. Furthermore, understanding the differences between active and inactive individuals will facilitate the development of targeted interventions aimed at improving well-being and reducing health risks at an early stage.

The above considerations prompted us to carry out this study, which was aimed to detect the differences in body composition, anthropometric indices, and sleep quality between active and inactive young adults as well as explore the relationship between these parameters. Therefore, we posed the following research questions: (1) Are there differences in body composition, anthropometric indices, and sleep quality between active and inactive individuals? (2) Is there a relationship between body composition, anthropometric indices, and sleep quality in the studied groups? Based on the existing literature, we hypothesized that physically active individuals would have a more favorable body composition, anthropometric profiles and better sleep quality compared to inactive individuals. We also expected a significant association between favorable body composition parameters and better sleep quality in both groups.

Material and methods

Study design and ethics approval

The research procedures of this prospective observational study were conducted at the SLfit Training Studio in Sandomierz, Poland. The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical rules of the Helsinki Declaration and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The protocol of this study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Rzeszów, Poland (Approval No. 013/04/2024). Before the commencement of the research each participant was informed about its purpose and stages. Subsequently, each participant provided written informed consent to participate in the study and to the processing of their personal data.

Sample size

Before initiating the research, the minimum sample size for the studied population was calculated using the sample size calculator (“Set Plus” in Statistica 13.3). A fraction size of 0.7, a confidence level of 95%, and a maximum error of 5% were assumed. As a result of the calculations, it was determined that the minimum sample size should be ninety-nine participants. A total of one hundred fourteen participants were recruited in the study. Participants

Participants were included in the study if they met the following criteria: (1) provided written, informed consent to participate in the study, (2) age between 18 and 35 years old, (3) no musculoskeletal injuries in the past 12 months, (4) no surgeries in the past 12 months, (5) fasting on the day of the study. Exclusion criteria included: (1) lack of informed consent to participate in the study, (2) functional status preventing independent locomotion, (3) use of medications and supplements affecting body composition or sleep quality, (4) presence of metal and electronic implants, (5) acute dehydration on the day of assessment, such as that caused by diarrhea or vomiting to minimize potential inaccuracies in BIA measurements despite standardized hydration protocols. Due to not meeting the inclusion criteria (n = 5) or formal deficiencies, such as incomplete or incorrectly completed questionnaires. (n = 9), fourteen participants were excluded from the study. The recruitment process was structured to achieve equal representation in both groups, ensuring balanced sample sizes. Ultimately, one hundred individuals were included in the final analysis. In the participant qualification process for the study, individuals were divided into two equally sized groups: a physically active group consisting of fifty participants (25 males and 25 females) and a physically inactive group also consisting of fifty participants (25 males and 25 females). Participants qualified for the physically active group if they achieved a “high” or “moderate” score on the IPAQ-SF test. Conversely, individuals who scored “low” on the IPAQ-SF test were included in the physically inactive group. Detailed criteria for selecting the level of physical activity are included in Appendix A. Research procedures

The assessment of physical activity level utilized the 7-item International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF)38. The present questionnaire is widely utilized within the young adult population39,40. Participants self-reported their daily time spent engaged in sitting, walking, moderate, and vigorous physical activities, with activities lasting continuously for at least 10 minutes considered for analysis. Responses were processed to compute Metabolic Equivalent of Tasks minutes per week (MET-min/week), offering a comprehensive metric of physical activity. Each activity type was converted to MET-min/week by multiplying the designated coefficient by the frequency (days per week) and duration (minutes per day) of engagement. Cumulative results across activity types yielded weekly totals of physical activity41. Subsequently, participants’ physical activity levels were categorized into low, moderate, and high based on recommendations from the literature38. Participants were categorized into three levels of physical activity (Low, Moderate, High) according to the official IPAQ scoring protocol [29]. The “high” activity level included individuals who met one of the following criteria: (1) vigorous-intensity activity on at least 3 days per week accumulating ≥1500 MET-min/week, or (2) 7 or more days of any combination of walking, moderate, or vigorous physical activity achieving ≥3000 MET-min/week. The “moderate” level included those meeting one of the following: (1) vigorous activity on ≥3 days/week for at least 20 minutes/day, (2) moderate activity or walking on ≥5 days/week for at least 30 minutes/day, or (3) any combination of activities on ≥5 days/week totaling ≥600 MET-min/week. The “low” level comprised individuals who did not meet the criteria for either moderate or high activity levels.

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) questionnaire, which is a self-rated tool designed to evaluate sleep quality and disturbances over the past month42. The PSQI comprises seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21, where higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. PSQI is a widely used scale for evaluating sleep quality in clinical research and everyday practice and demonstrates strong compatibility with polysomnography43. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for internal consistency of the PSQI is 0,83, affirming the scale’s high reliability44. It is also characterized by good validity45.

Body composition analysis was conducted using the InBody 770 analyzer (Figure 2), which employs bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). This method operates based on the electrical properties of tissues and involves measuring the total electrical resistance of the body, which comprises resistance (passive resistance) and reactance (active resistance)46. It is a modern, non-invasive, painless, and reliable method for analyzing body composition, serving as a substitute for dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry47. Body composition analysis was conducted in the morning, in a fasting state, barefoot, and wearing only underwear. On the day preceding the study, participants were informed of the necessity to refrain from food and water intake on the day of the study. The analysis included the following parameters: body weight, total body water, fat-free mass, muscle mass, bone mineral content, and total fat tissue content, with a focus on visceral fat area.Fig. 2InBody 770 analyzer.

InBody 770 analyzer.

Height was measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer, while body mass was obtained from body composition analysis. Participants were weighed to the nearest 0.01 kg and measured to the nearest 0.1 cm48. Waist circumference and hip circumference were measured using an anthropometric tape measure. Waist circumference was measured between the highest point of the iliac crest and the lowest part of the rib arch at the midaxillary line, with measurements taken to the nearest 0.1 cm49. Hip circumference was measured at the largest protrusion of the buttock muscles, with measurements taken to the nearest 0.1 cm. Subsequently, using the collected data, Body Mass Index (BMI), Waist to Hip Ratio (WHR), Waist to Height Ratio (WHtR), and A Body Shape Index (ABSI) were computed50–53.

Statistical analysis

The collected research data underwent statistical analysis using Statistica 13.3 software developed by StatSoft (Cracow, Poland). Descriptive statistics were calculated including the mean, median, higher and lower quartiles, and standard deviation. Both parametric and nonparametric tests were used for the analysis of quantitative variables. The decision to use a parametric test was based on meeting its fundamental assumptions, including the conformity of the variables with a normal distribution, which was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances, which was examined using Levene’s test. To detect differences between groups, the parametric Student’s t-test or alternatively the nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was employed. ANCOVA (Analysis of Covariance) was used to control for the confounding variables of age, and BMI, and the data were stratified by sex. The correlation analysis of quantitative variables was conducted using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. As sleep quality, body composition and anthropometric measures may be confounded by age, sex, and BMI, the correlation analysis was adjusted for these confounders. A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted for the analysis.

Results

Statistical analysis revealed significant differences in body composition parameters between physically active and inactive individuals. Physically active participants exhibited a significantly higher total body water percentage (59.93±4.81; p<0.001) compared to inactive individuals (52.50±3.66%). Similarly, the active group had a significantly lower fat mass percentage (18.11±6.53%; p<0.001).

Moreover, muscle mass percentage and bone mineral content were also significantly higher in the active group (p<0.001 for both), indicating a more favorable body composition profile among physically active individuals. The analysis after controlling age and BMI revealed that all parameters were proved. The same results were obtained after controlling age and BMI in all measures (p*<0.001). Discussion

This study aimed to investigate differences in body composition, anthropometric indices, and sleep quality between physically active and inactive young adults, as well as to explore the interrelationships among these variables. Our findings demonstrated that physically active individuals had significantly lower fat mass, higher skeletal muscle mass, greater bone mineral content, and higher total body water compared to their inactive peers. These findings align with those of Olszewska et al., who reported significantly higher total body water percentages among physically active individuals54. The significantly lower fat mass in active individuals found in our study supports previous findings that regular physical activity is an effective means of reducing obesity55. This difference was particularly pronounced in men, probably due to gender-specific characteristics in fat distribution and lean mass56. Similarly, active participants both males and females had significantly more muscle mass, which is consistent with the known role of physical activity in promoting muscle development and maintenance57. In addition, bone mineral content was higher in the active participants, which is consistent with previous research showing that strength and resistance exercise contribute to greater bone strength and density58. Taken together, these findings highlight the profound impact of regular physical activity on body composition during young adulthood; a pivotal stage in the life course for the formation of enduring health-related behaviors, which are strongly associated with favorable physiological trajectories and a decreased risk of non-communicable diseases in later life.

To better understand the distribution of adipose tissue in the studied groups, the body composition analysis was expanded to include anthropometric indices. In this study, we found that physically active people had significantly lower levels of these variables compared to physically inactive people. These findings suggest that individuals participating in physical activity tended to have lower fat content compared to those in the control group. Similar findings regarding the WHR were obtained in studies by Du et al., who concluded that individuals not meeting the WHO guidelines for moderate and vigorous physical activity had significantly higher WHR values compared to those meeting the criteria59. Concerning the WHtR, our results corresponded with the findings of Lee et al., who found that physical activity was significantly associated with a lower prevalence of obesity based on WHtR, regardless of gender60. A scientifically meaningful finding emerged after adjusting for age and BMI, as significant differences between active and inactive individuals remained for all variables analyzed, with the exception of WHR and WHtR in females. This lack of significance can be attributed to the influence of BMI as a covariate, which can partially explain key obesity indicators such as WHR and WHtR. In addition, it is possible that physical activity in the female cohort studied had a stronger influence on total fat mass or regional fat distribution (e.g. peripheral rather than central fat) than on waist-related indices in particular. This finding suggests also that in females, the relationship between physical activity and central obesity as captured by these ratios may be more complex and possibly influenced by additional physiological or hormonal factors. Previous research has shown that fat distribution patterns in females, particularly abdominal fat compared to gluteofemoral fat, are strongly modulated by hormone status, particularly estrogen levels, which may reduce the direct influence of physical activity on indicators of central adiposity in this group61. In addition, it is possible that lifestyle variables that were not fully captured in our analysis, such as dietary habits, stress or menstrual cycle status, may have confounded these associations. This emphasizes the importance of interpreting anthropometric indices in females with caution, and that further research is needed to better understand the interplay between sex-specific fat distribution, physical activity and sleep quality.

Further analysis showed that physically active people were characterized by an overall better quality of sleep. This finding is consistent with the findings of Mahfouz et al., who found that sleep quality varied significantly with the level of physical activity, with significantly higher PSQI scores observed in physically inactive individuals compared to moderately and highly active ones63. This suggests that individuals lacking physical activity reported subjectively lower sleep quality compared to their physically active counterparts. Similar observations were made by Merella-no-Navarro et al., who found that lower levels of physical activity were associated with poorer sleep quality63. Regarding the mechanisms underlying the impact of physical activity on sleep improvement, the literature has identified several potential pathways. First and foremost, it has been shown that physical activity increases the release of melatonin by the pineal gland, a hormone that makes it easier to fall asleep. This phenomenon contributes to the regulation of the circadian rhythm and the sleep-wake cycle64. Evidence also suggests that engaging in physical activity influences the parasympathetic nervous system. In a meta-analysis conducted by Sandercock et al., it was found that regular exercise enhances vagal nerve modulation, resulting in bradycardia65. This vagal modulation strengthens parasympathetic control, which may contribute to improved sleep quality. In addition, it has been observed that physical activity also affects the limbic system. In this context, research by Magnan et al. suggests that physical activity improves mood and creates a positive feedback loop that promotes greater enthusiasm for exercise, which in turn increases regular participation66. Furthermore, Kapsimalis et al. found that moderate exercise can decrease resting plasma concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines while elevating levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, thus contributing to an enhancement in sleep quality67. Finally, physical activity influences the hypothalamus, helping to regulate body temperature in a manner that promotes sleep for bodily recovery68.

In this study, we also assessed individual components of the PSQI. It was found that individuals regularly engaging in physical activity reported significantly better subjective sleep quality, shorter sleep latency, longer sleep duration, and fewer daytime dysfunctions. These results are partially in agreement with the findings of Narayana et al., who demonstrated that physically active individuals had significantly lower scores in each PSQI component69. In the presented study, no significant differences were observed in terms of sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, and use of sleep medications. One potential explanation for these observations could be the demographic profile of the study participants as the study population consisted of young adults aged 18 to 35. Within this age range, the prevalence of sleep disorders and the need for sleep medications are typically less common compared to older populations70. Following adjustment for age and BMI most sleep-related parameters continued to exhibit significant differences between physically active and inactive individuals. Nonetheless, the previously observed differences in subjective sleep quality and sleep latency among males were attenuated and did not reach statistical significance. This attenuation suggests that the differences originally observed in these two components may have been due to individual variations in BMI or age-related sleep characteristics rather than physical activity per se. In particular, BMI was associated with changes in sleep architecture and perception of sleep quality, possibly masking or obscuring the effect of physical activity in male participants. In addition, subjective components of sleep, such as perceived quality and latency, are known to be influenced by psychosocial and behavioral factors that may not be evenly distributed across activity levels within sex-specific subgroups71.

Further analysis revealed that significant correlations existed between GSS scores (adjusted for age, sex, and BMI) and body composition parameters, particularly in the active group. Higher GSS scores, indicating poorer sleep quality, were associated with lower total body water, fat-free mass, and bone mineral content, as well as higher fat mass. In the inactive group, similar associations were found but were weaker in magnitude. These findings are consistent with those reported by Jurado-Fasoli et al., who observed that GSS scores were negatively associated with bone mineral content, bone mineral density, and fat-free mass, and positively associated with fat mass percentage72. Notably, no significant correlation was found between GSS scores and muscle mass in the inactive group. This lack of correlation may be due to the lower muscle mass in the inactive cohort, where other components of body composition, such as fat mass, have a more pronounced impact on sleep quality. Furthermore, muscle mass may not be as directly influenced by sleep quality in inactive individuals, as compared to the more dynamic and metabolically responsive components such as fat mass and fat-free mass. These findings suggest that while poorer sleep quality is linked to unfavorable body composition, especially in active individuals, the relationship between sleep and muscle mass appears to be less pronounced in those with lower physical activity levels.

To conclude the analysis, associations between GSS scores (adjusted for age, sex, and BMI) and anthropometric indices were examined. Significant positive correlations were observed between GSS scores and both WHR and WHtR in both active and inactive groups, with the association being particularly strong for WHtR. These findings suggest that poorer sleep quality is associated with increased central adiposity. However, no significant association was observed between GSS scores and ABSI in either group. This lack of relationship may reflect the nature of ABSI as a composite anthropometric index that integrates both body size and fat distribution. As such, it may be less sensitive to subtle variations in sleep quality compared to more direct indicators of central adiposity, such as WHR and WHtR, which may better capture metabolically relevant body composition changes linked to sleep disturbances. Notably, the observed link between sleep quality and central adiposity, especially among inactive individuals, underscores the potential role of poor sleep in promoting abdominal fat accumulation - a key contributor to metabolic dysregulation73.

While the relationship between poor sleep quality and increased adiposity is well-documented, it is essential to recognize that this link may not be purely unidirectional. In fact, emerging research suggests a bidirectional relationship, where poor sleep may contribute to increased adiposity, while excess fat may, in turn, negatively impact sleep quality74. Several metabolic and hormonal mediators play a central role in this complex interaction, including cortisol, leptin, and insulin sensitivity. For example, chronic sleep deprivation is known to elevate cortisol levels, a stress hormone that can disrupt metabolic processes, promote fat accumulation, and reduce muscle mass75. Elevated cortisol can impair insulin sensitivity, further promoting fat storage and increasing the risk of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Additionally, poor sleep disrupts leptin and ghrelin signaling, hormones that regulate appetite and satiety, leading to overeating and subsequent weight gain76. Furthermore, sleep quality and duration are crucial in the regulation of circadian rhythms, which govern the timing of metabolic processes and hormonal secretion. Disruptions to the circadian clock can result in dysregulated insulin secretion, increased adiposity, and impaired fat metabolism, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates both metabolic dysfunction and sleep disturbances77. These interactions highlight the need for a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between sleep, body composition, and metabolic health, considering the bi-directional effects and the key role of hormonal and metabolic mediators.

A limitation of this study is the reliance on subjective research tools to assess physical activity and sleep quality, which poses a potential risk of measurement errors due to the subjective nature of this assessment. Although subjective measurement tools were utilized, they are standardized, validated, and widely used in both research and clinical practice, which enhances their reliability and applicability. Nevertheless, we concede that supplementing the PSQI questionnaire with objective sleep measurements, such as actigraphy or polysomnography, would provide more valuable and precise data. Future studies would greatly benefit from the integration of objective measurement tools, such as actigraphy or accelerometers, to enhance the reliability of physical activity and sleep assessments78. For example, actigraphy has been shown to provide more precise estimates of sleep onset, wake times, and sleep fragmentation, while wearable accelerometers can objectively quantify activity levels across various intensity thresholds79. Additionally, the incorporation of heart rate variability (HRV) and polysomnography in subpopulations could further enrich the understanding of sleep–activity interactions by providing physiological markers of autonomic function and sleep stages80. While our findings are consistent with previous literature relying on similar methodologies81, we encourage future research to adopt a multimodal assessment approach that combines subjective and objective methods. Such triangulation would not only improve measurement accuracy but also provide deeper insights into behavioral patterns and physiological mechanisms underlying the observed associations. Another important limitation of this study is the fact that it is a cross-sectional observational study, which limits the possibility of inferring causal relationships between sleep quality, physical activity and body composition. While the observed associations provide valuable insight into potential relationships between these variables, the directionality of these relationships cannot be definitively established. Longitudinal or intervention studies are needed to confirm causality and to better understand the underlying mechanisms that underlie these relationships.

Future research could also investigate how different forms of physical activity are associated with sleep quality. It would be valuable to determine which types of exercise are most effective in enhancing sleep quality. This could involve comparing aerobics, resistance, and mixed forms of exercise to identify their respective effects on various dimensions of sleep, such as duration, efficiency, and sleep architecture. Furthermore, it is valuable to explore what other factors may influence sleep parameters, including whether stress levels determine sleep quality and duration, as well as whether stress levels may affect an individual’s physical activity. In addition to stress levels, examining dietary habits (such as diet type and calorie intake) alongside mental health status could provide valuable insights into factors influencing sleep. Future studies should incorporate detailed dietary assessment, as nutrition plays a critical role in both sleep regulation and body composition. Specific attention should be given to total energy intake, macronutrient distribution, and dietary patterns, such as late-night eating or high glycemic load meals, which have been shown to negatively affect sleep quality and metabolic outcomes82. Furthermore, key nutrients such as tryptophan, magnesium, and omega-3 fatty acids are implicated in the synthesis of sleep-regulating neurotransmitters and hormones, including serotonin and melatonin83. Inadequate dietary control can confound the relationship between sleep and body composition through multiple mechanisms. For instance, poor sleep is associated with dysregulation of hunger hormones such as ghrelin and leptin, increased hedonic eating, and a tendency toward higher caloric intake, all of which may contribute to adiposity and metabolic imbalance84. Therefore, future research should aim to include quantitative dietary intake measures and control for these variables to more accurately assess the independent effects of sleep on body composition and metabolic health. Notably, the existing scientific literature provides also initial evidence that the combination of nutritional measures, such as the consumption of caraway seeds, with aerobic exercise can have synergistic effects that lead to an improvement in both C-reactive protein levels and sleep quality85.

Despite limitations, this study makes a valuable and significant contribution to existing literature. It enriches the understanding of the connections between physical activity, sleep quality, and body composition, The findings provide important data that can support further research in these areas and contribute to a better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of these interactions. The results obtained can serve professionals from various fields such as medicine, physiotherapy, dietetics, and psychology as a strong rationale for implementing and supporting initiatives promoting regular physical activity in society. Furthermore, the data can be utilized to develop effective educational strategies that educate the public on the importance of managing physical activity and sleep for shaping a healthy body composition.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates significant differences in fat tissue levels, anthropometric indices, and sleep quality according to the level of physical activity. Therefore, it can be stated that regular physical activity is associated with potential benefits related to better sleep quality and reduced total and visceral fat tissue, as well as improved anthropometric indicators. However, given the cross-sectional observational design, these findings indicate correlations rather than causal relationships.

To recapitulate the above considerations, we conclude that body composition is sensitive not only to changes in activity levels but also to sleep quality, suggesting that body composition may be associated with multiple factors. Therefore, it is necessary to continue research in this area taking into account other processes that may affect body composition, including stress (mental or physical), eating patterns (different types of diets), work patterns (daily or night) and various types of inactivity including screen time or time spent at school/university. In this context, it would be valuable to conduct randomized controlled trials or longitudinal studies to examine these relationships more thoroughly. This will facilitate a deeper understanding of the complex relationships and processes governing body composition, enabling better management of personal health and psychophysical well-being. Awareness that our body composition is shaped by our decisions and behaviors underscores the importance of identifying the processes that shape it, allowing us to effectively model it towards a healthier composition.

Our findings should be interpreted with caution, as the study did not exclude individuals with sleep disorders or account for chronotype differences. Consequently, the results may not be generalizable to populations with clinical sleep disturbances or extreme chronotypes.
