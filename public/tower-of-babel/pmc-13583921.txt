Symptomatic and Ultrasonographic Improvement in Rheumatoid Arthritis Temporally Associated with Intravenous NAD + Therapy: A Case Report
Authors: Mohamad Rabih Itani, Arije Farhat, Nour Abou Yehya, Lana Ghraizi, Bahaa Bou Dargham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAltered NAD+ metabolism has been described in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but intravenous NAD+ has not been clinically evaluated for inflammatory arthritis.Case presentationA 49‐year‐old woman with a rheumatologist‐documented diagnosis of RA had progressive polyarthralgia, intermittent swelling, marked morning stiffness, and baseline hand and wrist ultrasonography showing active synovitis. After approximately 4 months of inconsistently taken methotrexate, discontinued because of adverse effects, she received four intravenous NAD+ infusions for aesthetic and antiaging purposes: 250 mg in 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride over approximately 1 hour every 2 weeks. She reported rapid pain relief after individual infusions and subsequent improvement in swelling, morning mobility, function, and analgesic use. No infusion‐related adverse events were reported. Nine months later, follow‐up ultrasonography showed no synovitis or Doppler hyperemia. Psychologic support, exercise, and improvement in personal circumstances occurred concurrently.ConclusionThe symptomatic and ultrasonographic changes were temporally associated with intravenous NAD+, but incomplete diagnostic documentation, previous methotrexate exposure, concurrent psychosocial changes, and the uncontrolled retrospective design preclude causal inference. This case is hypothesis‐generating and supports prospective controlled investigation.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease in which early disease‐modifying antirheumatic treatment is important to limit joint damage and disability. 1 NAD+ regulates cellular energetics, redox homeostasis, and NAD+‐dependent signaling. 2 NAD+ metabolism is altered in RA and has been proposed as a therapeutic target, although intravenous NAD+ has not been clinically evaluated for inflammatory arthritis. 3 , 4 We report symptomatic and ultrasonographic improvement temporally associated with intravenous NAD+ administration.

Case Presentation

The patient, a 49‐year‐old Lebanese woman working as a bank branch manager, developed joint pain beginning in the little finger and progressing to migratory polyarthralgia, intermittent swelling, and marked morning stiffness. She later developed a rash over both legs and feet. Her history was notable only for appendectomy. Symptom onset coincided with substantial occupational and family stress, including caring for a son hospitalized for several months and ongoing marital conflict. Detailed examination findings from the original rheumatology visit were unavailable.

Initial complete blood cell count was reportedly normal; markers of inflammation, rheumatoid factor, anticyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies, and other autoimmune serology were unavailable. In August 2024, bilateral hand and wrist ultrasonography showed marked synovial hypertrophy of both wrists and right metacarpophalangeal joints with Doppler hyperemia consistent with active synovitis, together with articular irregularities and distal osteophytes (Figure 1A–C). A rheumatologist diagnosed RA, but the original consultation notes and diagnostic criteria were unavailable; therefore, fulfillment of the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/EULAR classification criteria could not be independently verified. 5

Methotrexate with vitamin supplementation was prescribed. Adherence was initially inconsistent, and the patient reported partial improvement during approximately four months of treatment before discontinuing it because of adverse effects.

Approximately 2 months later, she received intravenous NAD+ for aesthetic and antiaging purposes rather than RA treatment. Each session comprised 250 mg NAD+ in 500 mL 0.9% sodium chloride infused over approximately 1 hour every 2 weeks for four sessions, totaling 1,000 mg over 6 weeks. Around the same time, she divorced, began psychologic support, and resumed regular gym exercise.

She reported rapid pain reduction after individual infusions and, by treatment completion, marked improvement in pain, swelling, morning mobility, function, cold tolerance, energy, mood, and analgesic requirements. All four sessions were completed without reported interruption, dose modification, or adverse events. Nine months later, follow‐up bilateral hand ultrasonography showed no synovitis, Doppler hyperemia, tenosynovitis, significant effusion, erosion, or soft‐tissue abnormality; joint spaces were preserved (Figure 1D–F). No validated disease activity measures, serial markers of inflammation, or follow‐up serology were available. The clinical chronology is summarized in Supplementary Figure 1, and the patient's perspective is presented in Box 1. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report. No data beyond those presented in this article will be shared. Additional patient‐level clinical data are not publicly available because of patient privacy and confidentiality considerations and the scope of the patient's consent for publication.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first peer‐reviewed case report describing RA improvement temporally associated with intravenous NAD+. Human RA research has identified reduced intracellular NAD+, associations with inflammation and disease activity, and improvement of selected inflammatory features after ex vivo NAD+ precursor exposure. 3 However, these findings do not establish that intravenously administered NAD+ reaches relevant intracellular compartments or improves RA. The available human intravenous study assessed metabolite disposition, not rheumatologic efficacy. 4

The temporal sequence and later absence of ultrasonographic inflammation make the observation notable, but causal inference is weak. Alternative explanations include natural disease fluctuation, placebo or expectation effects, retrospective recall, occasional analgesic use, residual effects of methotrexate, and concurrent psychologic, social, and exercise‐related changes. Diagnostic uncertainty, missing baseline serology and disease activity measures, and nonstandardized, nonblinded ultrasonographic comparison further limit interpretation.

This case should therefore be regarded as hypothesis‐generating. Prospective controlled studies should use validated RA outcomes, inflammatory biomarkers, standardized blinded power‐Doppler ultrasonography, NAD+ metabolite measurements, and systematic safety monitoring. Intravenous NAD+ should not be adopted for RA outside research settings.

Conclusion

Symptomatic improvement and the absence of previously documented inflammatory findings on follow‐up ultrasonography were temporally associated with four intravenous NAD+ infusions, but this single retrospective case cannot establish causality or efficacy. Controlled prospective investigation is required.

Patient perspective*

“During the period when my hand symptoms worsened and I was diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis, I was under considerable stress in both my professional and personal life. I work as a bank branch manager, which carries significant responsibilities. At home, I was caring for my children and supporting their education, and my son was hospitalized for several months following an accident. At the same time, I was experiencing ongoing marital conflict and felt emotionally unstable and unhappy in my home environment. I felt that the accumulated stress affected my general health and contributed to a significant decline in my physical and psychological well‐being.

After my divorce, I decided to focus more on myself and my health. I sought psychological support, began exercising regularly at the gym, and started intravenous NAD+ therapy. Following these changes, I noticed an overall improvement in my health. I felt that my joint pain decreased rapidly after each NAD+ session, and I also experienced a clear improvement in my mood. With the continued sessions, the pain became much less frequent and eventually resolved from my perspective.”
