Radiologic analysis of cavum septi pellucidi and cavum vergae in children
Authors: Adrian Drożdż, Mateusz Gadzała, Tomasz Wojciechowski, Bogdan Ciszek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCavum septi pellucidi (CSP) and cavum Vergae (CV) are two major midline intracranial fluid collections. They have been in the area of interest for many years because of their variable size during growth. The present study aimed to develop a new classification of CSP, investigate morphometric changes in both the CSP and CV during development, and assess their correlation with well-known cerebral parameters.MethodsWe analyzed MRI scans of 201 pediatric patients. We divided the sample of individuals into five age groups with approximately equal sizes, including only T2-weighted sequences. Three different methods of CSP classification, Nopoulos, Tsutsumi, and ours, newly developed, were considered.ResultsWe have proposed a new classification of CSP based on the relation between the laminae of the cavum. We observed a very strong correlation (rho = 0.91) between CSP width and length (p < 0.001) but negligible correlations between CSP dimensions and the width of the lateral ventricles (rho < 0.1; p = 0.928). The trends indicate a decrease in CSP size with age (p < 0.001). All cases with present CV were found in patients with laminae CSP completely unadherent (type C, p = 0.001). We have reached almost perfect interrater agreement across measurements.ConclusionsWe have provided a new classification of CSP. It can be considered in every stage of human development as it is not correlated to cerebral diameters. We confirmed no correlation between the width of CSP and the width of the ventricles and biparietal diameter.Keywords: Cavum septi pellucidi, Cavum vergae, Pediatric neuroimaging, Magnetic resonance imaging, Neurodevelopment

Introduction

Cavum septi pellucidi (CSP) and cavum Vergae (CV) are midline intracranial fluid-filled cavities traditionally regarded as anatomical variants rather than true pathological entities. CSP is situated between the two laminae of the septum pellucidum, bordered anterosuperiorly by the corpus callosum and posteroinferiorly by the fornices. CV represents a posterior extension of CSP, with the columns of the fornices and the interventricular foramina commonly described as the anatomical boundary between the two cavities. Although frequently incidental, increasing evidence suggests that the presence, size, and persistence of these cavities may reflect critical aspects of brain development and maturation, thereby conferring clinical and scientific relevance [5, 6, 17].

The formation of CSP begins around the 12th gestational week, when the embryo measures approximately 60 mm. Fusion of the cerebral hemispheres creates a transient cavity in the anterior portion of the longitudinal cerebral fissure. Subsequent rostral development of the corpus callosum progressively separates this space from the fissure, while caudal growth elongates the septal laminae and results in posterior extension, giving rise to CV. Postnatal obliteration of CSP and CV has been attributed to the rapid increase in white matter volume within both cerebral hemispheres, leading to apposition and fusion of the septal leaves, as described by Rakic and Yakovlev [12].

Because the development of CSP is closely linked to the formation of the corpus callosum, hippocampus, and limbic system, its presence and morphology are considered markers of normal cerebral development [23]. Prenatal imaging studies have demonstrated that CSP can be reliably visualized and measured during routine obstetric ultrasonography between 20 and 40 weeks of gestation in more than 98% of cases, making it a valuable and accessible developmental landmark and normative growth charts [14, 21]. Absence, abnormal enlargement, or persistence of CSP beyond early childhood has therefore raised concerns regarding underlying neurodevelopmental disturbances.

In pediatric and adult populations, particular attention has been directed toward enlarged CSP and CV, sometimes referred to as cysts, especially when associated with neurological symptoms or mass effect [15]. Numerous studies have explored potential associations between persistent or enlarged cava and neuropsychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, mood disorders, and cognitive impairment, suggesting that persistent or enlarged CSP/CV may reflect broader neurodevelopmental disturbances rather than benign variants [11, 13, 24]. Despite these observations, the clinical significance of CSP and CV remains controversial, partly due to heterogeneity in definitions, measurement techniques, and classification systems used across studies.

Several attempts have been made to classify CSP and CV based on morphometric criteria or radiological appearance. However, existing classifications are often limited to specific age groups, imaging modalities, or clinical contexts, and they fail to capture the dynamic, developmental nature of these cavities. Importantly, no unified and adaptable classification system currently exists that can be applied consistently across the entire pediatric age spectrum and across different radiological modalities. This lack of standardization hampers comparison between studies and limits the interpretation of CSP and CV findings in both clinical practice and research.

Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to propose a new, comprehensive classification of CSP applicable across developmental stages and imaging techniques, and to analyze morphometric changes of CSP and CV throughout childhood in relation to established cerebral parameters. By addressing existing gaps in the literature, this study aims to clarify the developmental behavior of these midline cavities and to enhance their interpretative value in pediatric neuroimaging.

Material and methods

Population

A retrospective analysis was conducted on a series of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans from 525 patients hospitalized in the Department of Neurosurgery between 2019 and 2021. For patients under 16 years of age, consent for the examination was provided by a legal guardian, whereas for individuals over 16 years of age, consent was obtained from both the legal guardian and the patient. The analyzed material was anonymized prior to inclusion in the study.

From the initial cohort, 116 patients were excluded because their imaging diagnostics did not meet the requirements of the planned measurement protocol. Among the remaining patients, 208 individuals were excluded due to a history of neurosurgical procedures involving brain or skull, or due to congenital or acquired disorders of the central nervous system that altered anatomical relationships within the cranial cavity.

From the eligible group of 201 patients, study participants were assigned to age groups of similar size: under 1 year of age, 1–2 years, 2–5 years, 5–12 years, and 12–18 years. Among the subjects, 44.8% underwent MRI due to abnormalities detected on physical examination, 30.9% due to abnormalities identified in previous imaging studies, 16.9% following traumatic brain injury, and 7.5% after a first episode of seizure.

MR imaging acquisition and protocol

MR images were acquired on a 1.5 T scanner (Philips Achieva, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands). Following a 3-plane localizing and coil-intensity calibration scan, a T2-weighted TSE sequence was obtained with the following parameters: TE = 100 ms, TR = 5061 ms, FS = 1.5, matrix = 512 × 512. All measurements were conducted on axial slices acquired according to the standard protocol, with a thickness of 4 and 0.5 mm of inter-slice gap. After establishing the measurement protocol by first researcher (Rater 1), 30 randomly selected cases were analyzed jointly with a second investigator (Rater 2); these cases were excluded from further analysis. This led to the elimination of certain ambiguities in the measurement methodology and improved the clarity and consistency of the protocol. Subsequent measurements were performed independently by the investigators. In this manner, two datasets were obtained and subsequently used for further statistical analyses. Image evaluation, measurements, and classification into appropriate groups were performed using RadiANT DICOM Viewer software, version 2023.1 (64-bit; Medixant, Poznań, Poland). The software was equipped with a standard distance measurement tool. The collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet in accordance with the developed measurement protocol.

Image analysis

Measurements of CSP were taken on axial slices, at the level of the interventricular foramina. CSP was considered present in any case in which a space with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) signal intensity was visualized between two separate or diverging laminae of the septum pellucidum. Statistics

Statistical analyses were conducted using the R Statistical language (version 4.3.1; R Core Team, 2023) on Windows 10 Pro 64-bit (build 19,045). In the current study, we used a significance level of α = 0.05, entailing a 5% risk of a Type I error. The dataset’s distribution normality was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test, guiding the selection of appropriate statistical techniques for subsequent analyses.

To evaluate the consistency of measurements involving two numeric raters, we employed the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient, specifically ICC3k. This statistic was chosen to assess the degree of agreement among fixed sets of raters, providing a measure of reliability and consistency across assessments.

For the analysis of agreement between two categorical variables, we utilized Gwet’s AC1 coefficient. This coefficient was selected due to its robustness against chance agreement, offering a more stable measure of inter-rater reliability than other commonly used statistics, such as Cohen’s kappa.

Discussion

In this paper, we developed a clear protocol and defined CSP as any space between two distinct, diverging laminae of the septum pellucidum demonstrating a fluid signal on T2-weighted sequences. Moreover, CSP was evaluated on axial sections, which minimizes measurement errors related to slice thickness and gaps between slices. Previous classifications were typically based either on linear measurements or on specific imaging planes or radiological protocols, limiting their applicability across different datasets and developmental stages [4, 11, 19]. In contrast, our classification focuses on the anatomical relationship between the laminae of the septum pellucidum, which form the structural substrate of the cavum itself. This morphology-based approach enables consistent identification of CSP across fetal and pediatric populations as well as across different imaging modalities.

Direct comparison of our results with previously published data is challenging because most earlier studies focused primarily on determining the presence or absence of CSP. Born et al. did not observe significant differences in CSP width between age groups; however, children were analyzed as a single heterogeneous group [2]. Kavak et al. demonstrated that CSP width is greater in male fetuses than in female fetuses [8].

Embryological studies provide an important framework for interpreting these findings. As shown by Rakic and Yakovlev, CSP develops at the fusion site of the cerebral hemispheres as a secondary cleavage [12]. Closure of the cavum results from the growth of adjacent midline structures, particularly the hippocampus and corpus callosum, which gradually bring the septal laminae together [16, 20]. Because these processes occur progressively during fetal and early postnatal development, the morphology of the septal laminae should be interpreted as a continuum rather than a binary presence–absence phenomenon. The classification proposed in this study reflects this developmental continuum, ranging from complete adhesion (type A) to fully separated laminae (type C).

This perspective also helps reconcile discrepancies between anatomical and radiological studies. Post-mortem preparation and cerebrospinal fluid drainage may alter the distension of the laminae and artificially create or enlarge a cavum [17]. Radiological studies, in contrast, often rely on visibility on MRI slices or dimensional thresholds. Shaw et al., in a post-mortem study of 374 brains, considered CSP present only when two clearly separated leaves of the septum enclosed a space at least 1 mm wide extending from roof to floor [18]. Although small triangular cavities at the anterodorsal aspect of the septum pellucidum are frequently observed in post-mortem material, some of the authors ignore these entities [16]. Based on developmental concepts described by Rakic and Yakovlev, our classification recognizes these cavities as part of the physiological developmental spectrum rather than excluding them from analysis.

Radiological studies provide another perspective on CSP recognition. Authors such as Nopoulos et al., Kwon et al., Galarza et al., and Born et al. define CSP as present when visible on at least one coronal MRI slice [2, 9, 11, 13]. Earlier criticism of MRI-based studies concerned limited spatial resolution; however, modern imaging systems and reconstruction software now provide substantially improved image quality. Consequently, post-mortem and radiological studies should be viewed as complementary approaches to the anatomical description of CSP. MRI appears particularly suitable for evaluating this fluid-filled space bounded by thin membranous laminae.

In our classification, cases without a visible cavity were labeled as type A. Small cavities with laminae adherent along more than 50% of their length were classified as type B1, whereas larger cavities with adhesion along ≤ 50% of their length but not completely separated were classified as type B2. Fully separated laminae were classified as type C. Assignment to specific groups was straightforward in all cases, and interrater agreement was very high (Gwet’s AC1 = 0.96, p < 0.001). Type B1 was the most frequent morphology in all age groups except among patients younger than one year, in whom type A predominated. Notably, type C showed a clear decrease across age groups and was absent in the oldest patients. This pattern suggests that fully developed CSP may undergo progressive remodeling during childhood. Longitudinal studies evaluating type C morphology in the same individuals could provide further insight into the maturation of this region, although repeated MRI examinations pose ethical challenges.

Application of previously published classifications demonstrated similar interobserver agreement for the Nopoulos system but substantially lower agreement for the Tsutsumi classification. In our opinion, this difference may be related to the less clearly defined protocol and the more subjective criteria used in the latter system.

Overall, our results demonstrate a gradual decrease in CSP size with age. This observation is consistent with developmental studies indicating that septal laminae fuse in a posterior-to-anterior direction and that the anterior portion typically closes only after birth, resulting in a high prevalence of CSP in premature infants [10, 25]. Jarvis and Griffiths similarly reported no evidence of laminar fusion before 38 weeks of gestation in fetal MRI studies [7].

We did not observe a relationship between CSP dimensions and either ventricular size or overall brain size, represented by the width of the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles and biparietal diameter. Similar findings were reported by Tuma et al., who observed only weak correlations between biparietal diameter and CSP volume [21]. These observations support the hypothesis that CSP, although filled with cerebrospinal fluid, is not part of the ventricular system and develops independently.

Cavum Vergae (CV), also known as Verga’s ventricle or cavum psalterii, represents the posterior extension of cavum septi pellucidi [3, 16]. Although these cavities are not anatomically separated, the coronal plane through the columns of the fornices is typically regarded as the boundary between the anterior CSP and posterior CV [17]. Andréa Verga, an Italian anatomist who is considered the first to describe the posterior of these cavities, proposed the term “aqueduct of the ventricle of Sylvius” for the channel connecting cavities located below the corpus callosum [22]. In our study, CV was evaluated on axial T2-weighted images at the lowest slice showing the body of the lateral ventricles. Morphometric data on CV in pediatric populations remain limited, but the progressive reduction in CV width observed across age groups supports the concept of physiological involution of this cavity during childhood. The near absence of CV in the oldest age group corresponds with classical anatomical observations indicating that persistent CV is uncommon in adults [16].

An important finding of our study was the strong association between CV and type C CSP. All CV cases occurred in the group with fully separated laminae, and this association was statistically significant (p < 0.001). This observation supports the concept that CSP and CV represent elements of a shared developmental continuum of midline cavum structures rather than entirely separate anatomical entities.

From a clinical perspective, a standardized and developmentally applicable classification of CSP may improve radiological interpretation and facilitate the study of neurodevelopmental processes. Owing to its close embryological relationship with the corpus callosum, hippocampus, fornix, and other midline structures, CSP has long been regarded as a marker of normal forebrain development and is routinely assessed during prenatal neurosonography and fetal MRI examinations. The present findings support the concept that CSP and CV should primarily be regarded as developmental anatomical variants. The progressive decrease in CSP dimensions and CV width across age groups, together with the disappearance of type C CSP in older children, is consistent with the physiological process of septal laminae fusion described in embryological and neuroimaging studies. Furthermore, the absence of correlations between CSP dimensions and ventricular width or biparietal diameter suggests that CSP development follows a relatively independent developmental trajectory and does not simply reflect overall brain growth or ventricular enlargement.

At the same time, the clinical significance of CSP remains a subject of ongoing debate. Although CSP is usually considered a normal anatomical variant, persistence of a large cavum has been associated with abnormalities of midline brain development, particularly involving limbic structures such as the hippocampus, fornix, and hypothalamus [17]. Increased prevalence of enlarged CSP has also been reported in several neuropsychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia [16, 24]. These observations suggest that CSP morphology may reflect alterations in the maturation of midline cerebral structures rather than represent a pathological entity per se. Importantly, however, the presence of CSP or CV alone should not be interpreted as evidence of neurological disease.

The analyzed cohort consisted of children who underwent MRI because of clinical indications. Although patients with congenital malformations, hydrocephalus, intracranial mass lesions, and other conditions capable of significantly distorting intracranial anatomy were excluded, the cohort cannot be considered representative of a completely healthy pediatric population. Because of the relatively small sample sizes and substantial heterogeneity of the clinical subgroups, together with the limited possibility of retrospectively verifying some MRI indications, the present study did not investigate associations between CSP/CV morphology and the clinical indications for MRI referral. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted primarily as observations regarding the developmental morphology of CSP and CV rather than evidence for their clinical significance in particular neurological disorders.

Recent studies further suggest that CSP morphology may be relevant in the context of acquired brain injury. Larger CSP and greater CSP length have been associated with exposure to repetitive head impacts and traumatic brain injury. In particular, CSP length exceeding approximately 6 mm appears to be relatively specific for individuals with substantial exposure to repetitive head trauma, particularly in contact sports participants [1]. These findings suggest that CSP morphology, rather than simple presence or absence, may provide clinically relevant information about prior mechanical stress affecting midline brain structures. Future studies combining detailed clinical characterization with standardized morphometric assessment of CSP and CV may help clarify whether the proposed classification has prognostic or diagnostic value beyond its anatomical and developmental significance.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged

First, the study was based on a retrospective analysis of clinically acquired MRI examinations. Although axial T2-weighted sequences allowed reliable visualization of the septal laminae, the use of higher-resolution sequences or isotropic three-dimensional acquisitions could potentially provide more precise morphometric measurements.

Second, the analyzed cohort included only pediatric patients who underwent MRI for clinical indications and therefore cannot be considered fully representative of a healthy pediatric population. The examinations were performed because of abnormalities detected on physical examination, findings identified in previous imaging studies, traumatic brain injury, or first episodes of seizures. Although individuals with conditions capable of significantly altering intracranial anatomy were excluded, it remains possible that subtle neurological or neurodevelopmental abnormalities were present in some participants. Consequently, the present findings should be interpreted as observations derived from a selected pediatric clinical population rather than normative reference data for children with entirely normal neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Another limitation is that detailed analyses of specific clinical diagnoses, neurological symptoms, and developmental outcomes were beyond the scope of the present study. Therefore, we were unable to determine whether particular CSP or CV morphologies were associated with the indications for MRI referral or with specific neurodevelopmental characteristics. Although CSP and CV are generally regarded as developmental anatomical variants and are frequently observed in otherwise healthy individuals, previous studies have suggested that enlarged or persistent cava may be associated with abnormalities of midline brain development, neuropsychiatric disorders, or acquired brain injury. Consequently, our results should not be interpreted as demonstrating that all observed CSP and CV variants are entirely unrelated to adverse clinical outcomes. Future studies integrating detailed clinical, neuropsychological, and developmental assessments will be necessary to better define the clinical significance of the proposed classification.

Another limitation concerns the cross-sectional design of the study. Although age-related trends were observed, longitudinal imaging of the same individuals would provide more direct evidence regarding the natural evolution and potential regression of different CSP morphologies over time. However, such studies are difficult to perform because repeated MRI examinations in healthy children are rarely justified from an ethical perspective.

Finally, although our morphology-based classification demonstrated very high interrater agreement, it still relies on visual assessment of the relationship between the septal laminae. Further validation in independent cohorts including population-based samples and cohorts with specific neurological or neurodevelopmental disorders, as well as across different imaging protocols, would be valuable to confirm the robustness, reproducibility, and clinical applicability of the proposed system.

Conclusions

In our paper, we have provided a new classification of cavum septi pellucidi based on the relation between its laminae instead of the absolute measurements of the cavum. As a result, the clinician may use it in every stage of human development, as it does not correlate with cerebral diameters. We have provided a simple protocol with clear instructions, which other authors can use successfully. We have observed the mean length and width of CSP in different age groups, and it has been proven that there is no relation between the width of CSP and the width of the ventricles and cerebrum. We have found that the CV’s presence is related to fully developed CSP, and its mean width decreases with age.
