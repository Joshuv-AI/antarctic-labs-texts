Cardiac tamponade after pericardiocentesis: a rare but interesting bleeding point
Authors: Zeyi Cheng, Zibo Lin, Hong Xu
Published: 2025

A 65-year-old male presented with chest tightness for half a month, 1 year after undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention. The physical examination revealed normotension and regular heart rate without audible murmurs or peripheral oedema. He has a history of renal insufficiency for more than 10 years, with no urine output, and has been undergoing regular haemodialysis in recent years, 10-year history of Type 2 diabetes; 1 year post-radiofrequency ablation for atrial fibrillation, and previous history of right clavicle fracture plate fixation surgery; see X-ray of the chest (Panel A). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large pericardial effusion (Panel B; Supplementary material online, Video S1), so the cardiologist decided to do pericardiocentesis and catheter drainage. After a short while, the patient’s blood pressure dropped, accompanied by chest tightness and discomfort, and the pericardial drainage fluid was bright red and the amount gradually increased. Cardiac tamponade was considered, and the patient was transferred to our operating room for emergency thoracotomy. Because the patient’s clavicle fracture plate was still there, it was difficult to perform a midline thoracotomy. We performed a reverse L-shaped incision at the lower end of the sternum. Intraoperative findings: there is active bleeding at the puncture site of the pericardial drainage tube (Panel C; Supplementary material online, Video S2), suture the bleeding point, and then insert pericardial and mediastinal drainage tubes. The patient was discharged on the sixth post-operative day without any complications.

Lessons from this case: bleeding from a pericardial artery following pericardiocentesis is a potential complication that requires prompt recognition. Surgical exploration around the drainage tract should be thorough. When median sternotomy is contraindicated, a small reverse L-shaped thoracotomy at the lower sternal end represents a safe and effective alternative.
