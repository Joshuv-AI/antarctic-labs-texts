Quantifying acute care nurses’ experiences of patient handoffs during shift change: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Vivek Kaliraman, Joni L. Watson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Joint Commission established the 2006 National Patient Safety Goals and the 2010 Provision of Care standard requiring an organization to have a standardized process between the patient handoff giver and receiver. Inconsistent communications during patient handoffs increase the patient safety risk. There is limited evidence about the quality of patient handoff communication at shift change between nurses.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional observational study with a 24-question online survey conducted from June 17 through September 15, 2024, to examine hospital nurses’ experience with the quality of patient handoffs at shift change. Ordinal and multivariable regressions analyzed and controlled for specialty, license type, education level, and years of experience. This analysis includes responses from 688 direct care nurses in U.S. hospitals (i.e., acute care nurses). The primary measure was nurses’ experiences of the quality of patient handoffs at shift change including: (a) missed patient care information, (b) information completeness and accuracy, (c) frequency of patient involvement, and (d) problematic shift changes for patients at an organization.Results1029 nurses responded to the survey, and 688 met the survey analysis inclusion criteria. More than 1 in 4 nurses agreed or strongly agreed that important patient care information is often missed during shift change handoffs and that shift changes are problematic for patients in their hospitals. Only 6.3% of hospital nurses noted that the shift change handoffs they receive from the outgoing nurse are “always” accurate, complete, and pertinent to the patients’ conditions, while only 6.4% reported that they “always” engaged and involved patients during shift change handoffs. When stratified and controlled by specialty, license type, level of education, and years of experience, these results vary.ConclusionsNurses in hospitals experience inconsistent quality of patient handoffs at shift change, with only a minority reporting consistently high-quality and accurate handoffs. These findings highlight the normalization of broken, inaccurate, and unsafe shift change reports from direct care nurses’ perspectives, pointing to the need for further research and diversified interventions to support diverse nursing professionals.Clinical trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.

Background

The Joint Commission’s 2017 Sentinel Alert 56 noted that “failed hand-offs are a long-standing, common problem in healthcare.” [1] With approximately 33,679,935 patients admitted across 6120 hospitals in the United States [2]and an average length of hospital stay of 4.5 days [3], approximately 1.75 million nurses [4] conduct over 300 million patient handoffs annually at shift changes.

The quality of shift change handoff communication impacts the quintuple aim of patient care quality/safety, cost of care, patient satisfaction, clinician satisfaction, and equity [5]. Patient handoff – the transfer and acceptance of patient care responsibility between healthcare professionals or settings is complex [1]. The Joint Commission and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) recommend to “standardize critical content to be communicated by the sender during a handoff – both verbally (preferably face to face) and in written form; make sure to cover everything needed to safely care for the patient in a timely fashion; and standardize tools and methods (forms, templates, checklists, protocols, mnemonics, etc.) to communicate to receivers.” [1, 6].

Adherence to structured communication tools remains inconsistent, often dependent on institutional support and the prevailing workplace culture [7, 8]. The rigorous evidence on nurses’ experience of patient handoffs at shift change is sparse. The current body of knowledge includes numerous single-site studies with small samples, short implementation and measurement periods, and a lack of patient and/or system outcomes (e.g., adverse event rates, patient experience scores, etc.). The evidence also overemphasizes self-reported and process fidelity measures, either without inferential statistical analyses or with little to no statistical significance or effect [9–16].

For example, Reisenberg et al.’s [10] 2010 systematic review of 95 articles meeting inclusion criteria focused on nursing handoffs, barriers, and interventions/strategies concluded that most studies were of low quality. Only 20 were research studies, and 85% scored ≤ 8 on a 16-point quality scale. Only 10 studies assessed handoff effectiveness, and the researchers identified eight categories of barriers to effective handoffs (e.g., communication, social/hierarchy, culture/language, lack of standardization, equipment, environmental distractions, time, and human factors). Mardis et al.’s [12] 2016 systematic review of 41 studies revealed only six (15%) measured patient outcomes. Most of the articles combined interventions, making it difficult to determine the effect. Authors also identified that the lack of studies with control groups weakened the literature to determine handoff effectiveness. Buus et al.’s [15] 2017 systematic review of 19 qualitative field studies of naturally occurring nursing shift reports shifted the perspective from a linear information transfer to a decentralized and distributed social clinical care component. The researchers identified that bedside shift reports cannot be treated solely as data exchange moments, and innovations within the space need to consider the team building, contextual problem-solving, and collective sense-making occurring within handoffs.

To provide a unified summary of evidence-based practice and research recommendations, Bressan et al.’s [14] 2020 umbrella review included 17 reviews. They determined that accurate, timely, and relevant patient handoffs improve patient safety while inadequate handoffs contribute to adverse events, miscommunication, and delays in care. Researchers reinforced the nonlinear and complex nature of nursing handoffs, indicating that standardization via tools is helpful while also highlighting that flexibility is key to tailoring implementation to clinical settings and patient needs, while providing ongoing education and support to change staff behaviors.

Originally published in September 2013, the AHRQ “Nurse Bedside Shift Report Implementation Handbook,” [6] routinely cited as a seminal resource, includes limited quality improvement and case study evidence about the shift change communication intervention. Authors across all systematic and umbrella reviews published over the last 15 years called for improved methodological quality of studies and recommended longitudinal and multicenter studies to assess the impact of handoff intervention on patient outcomes [10–12, 14–16, 18]. In addition, the handoff communication language within the published evidence lacks clarity. It includes interchangeable terms such as handoff communication, patient handoffs, nurse handoffs, change-of-shift reports, and bedside shift reports. For this work and manuscript, the research team focused on communication between nurses during shift changes.

While researchers have identified that some patients express concern about privacy or frequency of conversations during shift change handoffs, several studies indicate that patients support and appreciate being a part of shift change handoffs. This may be reflected in patient experience measures, albeit it is missing from high-quality, published evidence [13, 14, 16–20]. The published literature offers mixed views of nurses’ perceptions of shift change handoffs. Evidence shows that nurses perceive shift handoffs as enhancing professionalism while supporting patient safety, quality, and engagement in care [10, 12, 16, 17]. Conversely, the literature also indicates nurses’ dissatisfaction with shift change handoffs due to lack of training, lack of standardized processes, rigid or broken structured processes, technology barriers, inaccurate handoff information, handoff interruptions, and patient involvement requirements [10–17, 19, 20].

Methods

This national survey focused on several aims that align with the quintuple aim [5] and the AHRQ domains [21]. The study investigated acute care nurses’ experiences regarding the quality of information exchanged during patient handoffs at shift changes regarding missed patient care information, handoff information completeness, frequency of patient involvement, and nurse perceptions of problematic shift changes. This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement Guidelines [22].

Design

A cross-sectional observational survey gathered practices, experiences, and perceptions of nurse handoffs at shift changes from nurses across the United States. The 24-question Google Form survey included nine demographic questions, 14 questions about patient handoff experience, and one open-response question about nurses’ thoughts on improving nurse handoffs. It took approximately five minutes to complete. This manuscript includes an analysis of four experience question responses:

These survey questions align with the study aims to provide nurses’ insights on organizations’ and researchers’ calls for consistent shift handoff elements: (1) complete and accurate information via structured yet flexible tools and processes and (2) patient involvement.

While any nurse could complete the survey, they had to meet the following eligibility criteria for study inclusion: (1) work in the United States; (2) work in a hospital setting; and (3) spend a majority (> 50%) of their time providing direct patient care. The researchers considered these participants as “acute care nurses.” The researchers excluded nurses working outside the U.S., those working outside hospital settings, and nurses who did not spend the majority of their time providing direct patient care (e.g., administrative, education, research). Researchers informed participants about the study’s purpose, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Researchers used the Cleveland Clinic Department of Quantitative Health Sciences calculator (https://riskcalc.org/samplesize/) to conduct the sample size power calculation in May 2024, before opening the survey, and included this calculation in the Institutional Review Board application. The Pearl Institutional Review Board reviewed the study design and data collection methods and provided an exempt determination.

Setting and participants

To correctly represent approximately 1.75 million nurses working in U.S. hospitals [23, 24] the research team aimed to accrue responses from 385 acute care nurses. This would power the study at a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of ± 5%. To increase survey participation, participants could win one of 20 $75 Amazon gift cards.

The team distributed the survey through various channels, including direct email invitations to over 5,000 U.S. nurses and healthcare leaders via principal investigators’ historical professional email lists; direct email invitations to all publicly-published email addresses of state affiliate leaders of the American Nurses Association; posting in the online communities of the Oncology Nursing Society (ONS) and the Society of Nurse Scientists, Innovators, Entrepreneurs, and Leaders (SONSIEL); sharing through the American Association of Critical Care Nurses (AACN) research participation site; appearing in Bluem Nursing social media posts and an Adni weekly email newsletter; and widespread community-driven sharing across LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, X, and TikTok. Survey participation increased due to organic social media distribution, particularly after investigators asked several well-known nursing voices and Bluem Nursing to share the survey details with their networks. Zip code responses indicated nursing participation across all 50 U.S. states.

Procedures

Survey question and variables development

Without a standardized, validated nurse handoff survey tool within the literature, the research team developed questions focused on the project aims. The team reviewed national nursing surveys and statistics such as the National Council of State Boards of Nursing (NCSBN) National Nursing Workforce Study [23] and the United States Health Resources & Service Administration (HRSA) National Sample Survey of Registered Nurses (NSSRN) [24] to identify and use standard variable terms and groupings.

After drafting the national survey questionnaire, the researchers pilot-tested and conducted a face-validity survey with 15 acute care nurses who provided direct patient care. The nursing feedback supported clarity, relevance, bias reduction, ease of use, and survey completion time. The researchers refined and improved the survey questionnaire before initiating national data collection. The survey included informed consent details and was open for three months, from June 17 – September 15, 2024.

Statistical analyses

The researchers measured four survey items assessing the quality, accuracy, and patient engagement during patient handoffs between nurses at shift change on a 5-point Likert scale. Investigators coded the Likert scales to numerical equivalents for analysis so that higher numbers related to better handoffs and lower numbers related to worse handoffs (Table 1). The team calculated overall and stratified independent variable summary statistics for each efficacy outcome as median, IQR, and SD. Ordinal regressions tested for associations between the outcome questions and the independent variables. The team controlled for confounding by performing univariable and multivariable regressions for each outcome, selecting reference groups based on either the largest sample size or subject matter expertise, depending on which was most appropriate for each variable. Data tables include all analyses, and researchers detail the multivariable regression results in the manuscript results. The team completed all statistical analyses in R studio [25]. The researchers ran the ordinal regressions using the polr function of the MASS package [26] and assessed significance in all cases at p = 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals.

Data cleaning

As the number of online studies has increased, so has the concern for fraudulent participant responses – particularly when there is a participation incentive – from bots or people repeating or misrepresenting themselves or with lack of focus/engagement, no matter the recruitment method. As such, researchers are responsible for safeguarding participation and detecting fraudulent responses via continuous data monitoring [27–30]. The researchers included several survey questions to help reduce non-genuine responses, including requiring an email address, zip code, birth year, and licensure year. Additionally, the survey form captured the survey completion date and time.

Results

Sample characteristics

1,143 participants consented and responded to the survey. Researchers removed 114 responses due to non-genuine response concerns: 18 for duplicate email addresses; 56 completed within seconds of one another with matching or duplicate data except for email address and a few other fields; 22 with less than 18 years between birth and licensure year; 17 with the same year of birth and licensure; and one that provided nonsensical responses to multiple questions. Of the remaining 1,029 responses, the study excluded all respondents who were not working in a hospital setting and providing direct patient care, resulting in a sample of 688 responses for the data analysis. Of these 688 nurses, 85.3% (n = 587) were licensed as Registered Nurses (RNs), 10.6% (n = 73) as Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs), and 4.1% (n = 28) as (Advanced Practice Registered Nurses (APRNs). The majority indicated baccalaureate education (63%, n = 433), with 20.1% (n = 138) and 16.9% (n = 116) indicating post-baccalaureate and pre-baccalaureate education, respectively. The sample mean age was 35.3 years with an 8.3 standard deviation. The respondents’ mean years of experience (YOE) was 8.7. 26.7% (n = 177) had greater than 10 YOE, 48.9% (n = 325) had 5 to 10 YOE, 13.7% (n = 91) had three to five YOE, and 10.7% (n = 71) had zero to two years of experience. Respondents ranged across more than 15 specialties inclusive of intensive/critical care (26.9%, n = 185), medical/surgical (25.6%, n = 176), pediatrics/neonatology (12.2%, n = 84), emergency care (10.6%, n = 73), obstetrics/gynecology (6.3%, n = 43), operating room/recovery (5.8%, n = 40), and all other (12.6%, n = 87).

Patient care information is missed during shift changes

Half of the nurses working in the hospitals “disagreed” (42.7%, n = 294) or “strongly disagreed” (7.3%, n = 50) with the statement “Important patient care information is often missed during shift changes.” Nurses not involved in direct patient care in the hospital noted that important patient information is missed more often during shift change handoffs (OR = 0.624, p = 0.0026) than nurses involved in direct patient care in the hospital – an incidental finding to the study’s specific aims. Multivariable regression analysis revealed two specialty groups in the hospital - medical/surgical nurses (OR = 0.674, p = 0.0008) and emergency care nurses (OR = 0.671, p = 0.0096) - who indicated that important patient information is missed during shift change communication between nurses. Nurses with baccalaureate (OR = 1.465, p = 0.0023) and pre-baccalaureate education (OR = 1.421, p = 0.0178) are significantly more likely than the post-baccalaureate to disagree that important patient care information is often missed during shift changes compared to nurses with post-baccalaureate education. There were no statistically significant differences among nurses with varying license types or years of experience. See Table 2.

Change-of-shift report almost accurate, complete, and pertinent

Nearly 3/4th of the respondents reported that the information they receive during shift change handoffs is accurate, complete, and pertinent. Incidentally, the information about patient’s condition was reported as more accurate, complete, and pertinent during shift change handoff when received by nurses working in the hospital versus nurses working outside the hospital (p = 0.0002), and for nurses providing direct patient care in the hospital versus not providing direct care in the hospital (p = 0.0099).

There were no significant differences in nurses’ experiences of accuracy and completeness of patient information received during shift change handoffs for nurses working in the hospital by nursing specialty, level of education, or years of experience. However, LPNs indicated they are less likely to receive accurate and complete reports pertinent to patient conditions (OR = 0.614, p = 0.0016). See Table 3.

Minimal patient engagement and involvement during shift change handoffs

Only 6.4% (n = 44) of the nurses working in the hospital reported that patients are “always” engaged or involved by the nurses in shift change handoffs. 19.2% (n = 132) of respondents indicated they included patients “most of the time” in change-of-shift handoffs (Table 1). Nurses in emergency care are significantly less likely to include patients in shift change handoffs (OR = 0.630, p = 0.0042). Nurses in the operating room/recovery most often involved patients during shift change handoff, but this was not statistically significant by multivariable regression analysis. LPNs were more likely to engage patients in shift change handoffs (OR = 1.779, p = 0.0002).

Nurses with greater than 10 years of experience were more likely to engage patients in shift change handoffs while nurses with less than 10-year experience were significantly less likely to engage patients during handoffs: 5–10 YOE (OR = 0.813, p = 0.0459), 3–5 YOE (OR = 0.717, p = 0.0216), and 0–2 YOE (OR = 0.689, p = 0.0227). Shift changes are problematic for patients in the organization

Less than half the acute care nurses “strongly disagreed” (7.3%, n = 50) or “disagreed” (37.1%, n = 255) with the statement “Shift changes are problematic for patients in this organization.” See Table 1. Nurses working in non-hospital settings reported that shift change handoffs are worse for patients at their organization (OR = 0.725, p < 0.0001) versus nurses working in hospital settings.

Nurses not providing direct patient care perceive shift change communication as more problematic for patients in the hospital (OR = 0.68, p = 0.0043). There were no statistically significant differences among nurses with varying years of experience. See Table 5.

Discussion

To the best of the researchers’ knowledge, this is the largest study to document nurses’ experiences of challenges with communication during patient handoffs at shift change. The diversity of the study’s sample by specialty, license type, level of education, and years of experience strengthens the external validity.

Overall, half or more than half of the nurses working in hospitals agreed that shift change handoffs are problematic at their organizations and important patient care information is missed during the handoffs. Medical/surgical and emergency care nurses are more likely to agree that nurses miss essential information during shift changes. Baccalaureate-prepared nurses are more likely to disagree that nurses miss important information during shift changes, which may indicate increased confidence levels in their skills. However, researchers did not see this effect sustained for nurses with post-baccalaureate preparation, so more research is needed to characterize the influence of nurse’s education on their behavior during shift change/ patient handoffs.

Nurses have previously reported varying degrees of patient involvement in shift change handoffs [13, 14, 16, 18–20]. However, this study’s findings provide the first evidence clarifying the variations among nurses. Emergency care nurses are less likely to engage patients during handoffs. Nurses with more than 10 years of experience and those with post-baccalaureate education are more likely to involve patients in shift change reports.

Overall, the closer nurses are to providing direct patient care within hospitals, the less likely they perceive shift change handoffs as problematic for patients and themselves, even when controlling for specialty, license type, level of education, and years of experience. This may reflect the current mixed body of knowledge that nurses are neutrally satisfied with shift change communication [10–17, 19, 20]. It also supports the evidence that direct care nurses have different perspectives on handoff processes and what may be considered “important.” [31] In addition, these incidental findings and disparate views may support patient handoff researchers and implementation clinicians shift to more objective patient handoff quality measures [10–12, 14–16, 18].

This study has limitations to consider when appraising the results. First, self-reported data within cross-sectional observational studies are inherently subjective, introducing variability, imprecision, and bias. Participants can have recall biases such as not accurately remembering, leading to overestimating or underestimating; response biases from more engaged or opinionated individuals; or social desirability biases, answering in ways that reflect what they believe is socially acceptable or favorable rather than providing honest answers [32]. While researchers conducted face validity of the survey questionnaire, participants may have had misunderstandings or misinterpreted the questions [30].

Second, a cross-sectional study is a time-limited snapshot of data. It does not capture changes in attitudes or behaviors over time. In addition, researchers cannot determine cause-and-effect relationships between variables, only associations or correlations [29, 31].

Third, the convenience sample is skewed to a younger population of U.S. nurses. The sample skewness could be due to electronic, online, and social media participant accrual methods. This may have excluded nurses who do not spend time online or within social media channels or are uncomfortable with these methods. While researchers used Google Forms for ease of access and completion, it may have been foreign to some. In addition, participants with a Google account may have had to enter their email addresses twice – once as a Google sign-in and once for the survey requirement, which may have been a barrier for some participants.

Technology may have been a contributing limitation. For example, participants had to provide an email address to be eligible for the Amazon gift cards (to deliver if they won); this may have deterred some individuals from participating in the survey for fear of non-confidentiality. In addition, the researchers tried to partner with various hospitals and health systems to reach their nursing staff, but heightened cybersecurity concerns prevented organizations from sharing the external study link. Considering that the U.S. location was a part of the inclusion/exclusion criteria, these results are not generalizable to geographic locations outside the U.S., which may have different practices, regulations, and staffing models.

Conclusion

Results from this national survey provide new insight into the challenges nurses experience during shift change handoff communication. It is concerning that nurses working in hospitals across all specialties, license type, education level, and years of experience report problems with shift change handoffs.

Nurses working in direct patient care in U.S. hospitals report statistically significant variations in the accuracy, completeness, satisfaction, and patient inclusion in shift change handoffs, underscoring the ongoing challenges with this frequent and vital communication step that directly impacts patient safety and quality of care. Modern, integrated health technology solutions may be one element supporting nurses in standardizing and completing accurate patient handoffs. As handoffs are a part of larger healthcare systems with multiple and interdependent agents, technology must remain flexible to adapt to differing needs across teams and organizations and be a part of broader system views and changes.

The healthcare system has normalized broken, ineffective, and unsafe shift change communication. Considering the number of nursing shift change reports conducted in U.S. acute care settings each year and the number of organizations working to standardize and align handoff communication, nursing change of shift report warrants more research and potentially diversified interventions to support a diverse nursing profession caring for diverse populations.
