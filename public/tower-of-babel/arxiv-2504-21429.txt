Active Learning of Upward-Closed Sets of Words
Quentin Aristote

Abstract
We give a new proof of a result from well quasi-order theory on the computability of bases for upward-closed sets of words. This new proof is based on Angluin’s L* algorithm, that learns an automaton from a minimally adequate teacher. This relates in particular two results from the 1980s: Angluin’s L* algorithm, and a result from Valk and Jantzen on the computability of bases for upward-closed sets of tuples of integers. Along the way, we describe an algorithm for learning quasi-ordered automata from a minimally adequate teacher, and extend a generalization of Valk and Jantzen’s result, encompassing both words and integers, to finitely generated monoids.

Proof.

A minimal finite basis of
U
can always be derived from a non-minimal one by
removing redundant elements: if
U=\left\uparrow{B}\right.
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
with
b_{1}\leq b_{2}
, then still

. Hence, without loss of
generality, we now describe a procedure that computes a non-necessarily
minimal basis.

Start with
B=\varnothing
. Enumerate all the words in
{\Sigma}^{*}
. For each
such

, check if

: because

is
upward-closed,

if and only if
U
intersects the
*
-product

\left\downarrow{w}\right.=\sigma_{1}^{?}\cdots\sigma_{n}^{?}

the oracle. If
w\in U
, add

to

, and just move on to the next word
otherwise.

By construction, it is always true that
B\subseteq U
. Because

is
upward-closed, it is hence also always true that
\left\uparrow{B}\right.\subseteq\left\uparrow{U}\right.=U
. On the other hand, every time a word is added to
B
and
B
changes, it
is possible to check with the help of the oracle whether, conversely,
U\subseteq\left\uparrow{B}\right.
. To check this, notice that it holds if and only if
U\cap(\left\uparrow{B}\right.)^{c}=\varnothing
. But
(\left\uparrow{B}\right.)^{c}
is downwards-closed, and it is a
general fact of WQOs that any downwards-closed set is a finite union of ideals

[
10

(\left\uparrow{B}\right.)^{c}=P_{1}\cup\cdots\cup P_{n}
for some

and some
*
-products

(P_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}

, one
can check whether
U\subseteq\left\uparrow{B}\right.
by asking the oracle whether
U

intersects any of the

’s.

Let us now see how to compute these

’s. Note that the fact that this can
be done means that
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
has the
effective complement property

[
9
, Definition 5]

Theorem

a bit technical, and only recall the two main steps instead.

•

For every

,

•

Then

-products appearing in the

All in all, we always have that

, and every time

as

The main appeal of the above proof is its genericity and modularity: it works in
fact for any WQO that can be shown to be sufficiently effective, in particular
having the aforementionned effective complement property – an effective way to transform
the complement of an upward-closed set into a union of ideals.

What hinders the algorithm from being polynomial-time is the rewriting of
regular expressions into unions of

detail), and the brute-force enumeration of words. While we do not claim to give
another algorithm with better asymptotic complexity, this brute-force
enumeration seems particularly inefficient and it is natural to try to improve
it, with heuristics for instance.

Active learning of quasi-ordered automata

We now describe how active learning of automata can indeed be done in a
quasi-ordered setting. Because we are only trying to learn those languages that
are upward-closed, DFAs are too general for our purpose. We will focus on a
more restricted family of automata:

\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma},{\preceq}\right)\mathclose{}

Proof.

Let

q\sqsubseteq q^{\prime}\iff\forall w\in{\Sigma}^{*},\delta^{*}_{w}(q)\in F\Rightarrow\delta^{*}_{w}(q^{\prime})\in F

Deciding whether

L_{q^{\prime}}

q^{\prime}

, and decide whether it recognizes an empty language. If this is

\delta^{*}_{w}(q)\in F

If this order makes

such

w^{\prime}
.

Here we use that

•

Lemma 8

(structure of the minimal quasi-ordered automata)
.

Let

be the minimal

. Then

1.

(Q,{\sqsubseteq})
is not just a quasi-order, but an order, so that,
except for transitions

that loop on a state, the
underlying graph of the automaton is acyclic;

2.

the initial state is the minimum of
(Q,{\sqsubseteq})
;

3.

unless

is empty, an accepting state is reachable from any state;

4.

there is at most one accepting state, and it is the maximum of

(Q,{\sqsubseteq})
.

Proof.

We implicitly use the Myhill-Nerode theorem, and the quasi-order
constructed in the proof of

.

1.

2.

For every

,

We now consider the problem of learning such a minimal quasi-ordered automaton
from an MAT. For a fixed upward-closed subset
U
of
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
, suppose given
an oracle able to answer two kinds of queries:

•

– given a word
w\in{\Sigma}^{*}
, the oracle
decides whether
w\in U
–

•

and
equivalence queries
– given a quasi-ordered automaton

that
is minimal for the upward-closed language it recognizes, the oracle decides
whether this language is

, and, in the negative case, also outputs a
counterexample word
w\in{\Sigma}^{*}
such that
w
is in
U
but is not

Can

Theorem 9

(active learning of quasi-ordered automata)
.

Suppose given an MAT as above. There is an algorithm that computes the
minimal quasi-ordered automaton recognizing
U
, and its complexity is
polynomial in the size of this minimal automaton and the maximum length of a
counterexample outputted by the oracle.

Proof.

We prove this by giving a polynomial-time reduction to the active learning
problem for DFAs, where
U
is only seen as a regular language, the complexity
result being that of the classical active learning algorithm of Angluin

[
1
]
. We therefore explain how to implement
an oracle in the classical setting, call it
\textsc{Oracle}_{U}
, from the oracle in
the quasi-ordered setting, call it
\textsc{Oracle}_{(U,{\preceq_{*}})}
: one can then
learn the minimal DFA recognizing the regular language
U
in polynomial-time
using the classical active learning algorithm, and then equip this minimal DFA
with a quasi-order in polynomial-time as explained in

7
.

First, any membership query to
\textsc{Oracle}_{U}
can simply be forwarded to

\textsc{Oracle}_{(U,{\preceq_{*}})}
: when it comes to membership queries, the two
oracles have the same computing power. Consider now a DFA
A
minimal for the
language it recognizes, given to
\textsc{Oracle}_{U}
for an equivalence query. By

Proposition

7
, one can then
check in polynomial-time whether
A
is in fact a quasi-ordered automaton:

•

if it is the case we also get the QO structure on
A
, and can then
forward
A
to
\textsc{Oracle}_{(U,{\preceq_{*}})}
for an equivalence query;

•

otherwise, we get some

such that
A
recognizes
w
but
not
w^{\prime}
, and one of these two words must then be a counterexample to
A

recognizing
U
. ∎

4
A proof by active learning of automata

We now give a new proof of
Corollary

3
, by reducing it
to the problem of actively learning quasi-ordered automata.

Theorem 10
.

Fix a finite quasi-ordered alphabet
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma},{\preceq}\right)\mathclose{}
and an upward-closed subset

U
of
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
. Given an oracle that decides for any
*
-product
P

whether
U
intersects
P
, a minimal quasi-ordered automaton recognizing
U

is computable.

Proof.

We reduce this problem to that of actively learning quasi-ordered automata,
for which we gave a polynomial time algorithm in

Theorem

9
. In other words, we describe how to
implement an MAT in the quasi-ordered setting by checking whether
U

intersects some well-chosen
*
-products.

Answering a membership query for a
w=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in{\Sigma}^{*}

can be done by checking whether
U
intersects
\sigma_{1}^{?}\cdots\sigma_{n}^{?}
:
because
U
is upward-closed, this is the case if and only if
w\in U
.
Consider now some minimal quasi-ordered automaton
A=((Q,{\sqsubseteq}),q_{0},\delta,F)
submitted to the MAT for an equivalence query.
We decide whether
A
recognizes
U
in two steps, implicitly using the
properties of

described in

and
assuming
A
does not recognize the empty language.

•

First, we check whether
U
contains the language recognized by
A
. For
this, enumerate all the paths
q_{0}\xrightarrow{\sigma_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\sigma_{n}}q_{n}
in
A
that go from the initial state
q_{0}
to
the accepting state
q_{n}\in F
, that are

–
q_{0}\sqsubset q_{1}\sqsubset\cdots\sqsubset q_{n-1}\sqsubset q_{n}
– and that
are

– for every
q_{i}\xrightarrow{\sigma_{i}}q_{i+1}
,

\sigma_{i}
is minimal among the letters
\sigma
such that

\delta_{\sigma}(q_{i})=q_{i+1}
(see

Figure

6
for an example within the
automaton of
Figure

). Check whether
\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in U
with a membership query. If this is not the case,
\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}
is a counterexample. Otherwise, if no path yields such a

are in
U
as well: if

A
accepts
w
, then, for one of the paths

•

Second, we check whether

is contained in the language recognized by

is not in

neither. For
this, enumerate all the strictly increasing paths
q_{0}\xrightarrow{\sigma_{1}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\sigma_{n}}q_{n}
in
A
that go
from the initial state
q_{0}
to a non-accepting state

one

for an example within
the automaton of
Figure

). Let
\Sigma_{i}\subseteq\Sigma
be the subset of these letters

for which

. Then any word

that is not accepted by

must be in one of the

corresponding to one of

This new proof partially addresses the two concerns highlighted in

5
The Valk-Jantzen lemma for quotients of WQOs

We now move on from quasi-orders on the finitely generated free monoid
{\Sigma}^{*}

of words, to quasi-orders on finitely generated monoids. We thus consider a
monoid
M
equipped with a quasi-order
{\preceq_{M}}
, and generated by a finite
quasi-ordered alphabet
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma},{\preceq}\right)\mathclose{}
: we assume there is a surjective
homomorphism of monoids
e_{M}:{\Sigma}^{*}\to M
that is also a monotone function

\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}\to(M,{\preceq_{M}})
. The surjectivity and monotonicity of
e_{M}

automatically makes
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
into a WQO, and
e_{M}
can be used to
describe the ideals of
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
:

Lemma 11

(ideals of a quasi-ordered monoid)
.

The
ideals of a quasi-ordered monoid
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
generated by
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma},{\preceq}\right)\mathclose{}

are exactly the downward-closures of images of languages specified by

*
-products:

\Idl(M,{\preceq_{M}})=\left\{\left\downarrow{e_{M}[I]}\right.~\middle|~I\in\Idl\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}\right\}

Proof.

This result is an instance of
[
10
, Proposition
5.1]
, but the phrasing differs
slightly so we repeat the proof here for completeness.

On the one hand, if
I
is an ideal of
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
(a language described by a

*
-product) then
\left\downarrow{e_{M}[I]}\right.
is an ideal of
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
. Indeed,

e_{M}[I]
is non-empty and directed because
I
is: for every
x,y\in e_{M}[I]
, write
x=e_{M}(x^{\prime})
and
y=e_{M}(y^{\prime})
with
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in I
; then there
is a
z^{\prime}\in I
such that
z^{\prime}\succeq_{*}x^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}\succeq_{*}y^{\prime}
, so that

e_{M}(z^{\prime})\in e_{M}[I]
,
e_{M}(z^{\prime})\succeq_{M}e_{M}(x^{\prime})
and
e_{M}(z^{\prime})\succeq_{M}e_{M}(y^{\prime})
.
\left\downarrow{e_{M}[I]}\right.
is thus also non-empty and directed, and it is of
course downwards-closed: it is an ideal.

On the other hand, we show that every ideal of
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
is of the
shape
\left\downarrow{e_{M}[I]}\right.
for some
I\in\Idl\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
. To see this, we use the
following characterization of ideals in a WQO: a downwards-closed subset
D

can always be written as a finite union
D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}
of ideals

[
10
, Lemma 2.6(2)]
, and
D
is an
ideal if and only if it is join-irreducible, i.e. for every finite family of
downwards-closed subsets
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
, if
D=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}

then
D=D_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq n

[
10
, Lemma
2.5]
. Consider thus a
downwards-closed subset
D
of
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
. Then
e_{M}^{-1}[D]
is
downwards-closed in
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
, hence
e_{M}^{-1}[D]=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}
for
some
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}\in\Idl\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
. But then

D=\left\downarrow{e_{M}\mathopen{}\left[e_{M}^{-1}[D]\right]\mathclose{}}\right.=\left\downarrow{e_{M}\mathopen{}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}\right]\mathclose{}}\right.=\left\downarrow{\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}e_{M}[I_{i}]}\right.=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\downarrow{e_{M}[I_{i}]}\right.

hence
when
D
is an ideal it must be equal to
\left\downarrow{e_{M}[I_{i}]}\right.
for some
1\leq i\leq n
.
∎

If
P
is a
*
-product that specifies an ideal
I
of
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
, write by
abuse of notation
e_{M}[P]
for the downard-closed set
e_{M}[I]
of

(M,{\preceq_{M}})
. Finitely generated quasi-ordered monoids also enjoy a
Valk-Jantzen-like result:

Theorem 12
.

Fix a finite quasi-ordered
alphabet
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma},{\preceq}\right)\mathclose{}
and an upward-closed subset
U
of a quasi-ordered
monoid
(M,{\preceq_{M}})
generated by
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma},{\preceq}\right)\mathclose{}
. Given an oracle that
decides for any
*
-product
P
whether
U
intersects
\left\downarrow{e_{M}[P]}\right.
, a
finite basis of
U
is computable as soon as
e_{M}
is.

Proof.

We reduce this problem to that of computing a finite basis of the
upward-closed subset
e_{M}^{-1}[U]
of
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
. For this it is enough, by

Corollary

3
, to decide, given a
*
-product
P
,
whether
e_{M}^{-1}[U]
intersects
P
. But
e_{M}^{-1}[U]
intersects
P
if and
only if
U
intersects
\left\downarrow{e_{M}[P]}\right.
: if
x\in e_{M}^{-1}[U]\cap P
, then

e_{M}(x)\in U\cap\left\downarrow{e_{M}[P]}\right.
; conversely, if
x\in U\cap\left\downarrow{e_{M}[P]}\right.
,
then there is a
y\in P
such that
x\leq e_{M}(y)
, and of course
e_{M}(y)\in U\cap\left\downarrow{e_{M}[P]}\right.
as
U
is upward-closed, so that
y\in e_{M}^{-1}[U]\cap P
.

Once a minimal finite basis
B
of
e_{M}^{-1}[U]
is computed,
e_{M}[B]
is a
finite basis of
U
. Note that
e_{M}[B]
need not be a minimal basis, and need
not be effectively reducable to a minimal one, as that would require the
quasi-order
{\preceq_{M}}
on
M
to be decidable, an assumption we have not
made.
∎

is not just an instance of Goubault-Larrecq’s

[

. Indeed, the

) which need not be true of any quotient of the

.

have noticed that nowhere in the proofs of

we actually use the fact
that
\mathopen{}\left({\Sigma}^{*},{\preceq_{*}}\right)\mathclose{}
is a subword ordering or that

is a monoid. We could in
fact have stated

more generally, and it would
then informally read as follows.
Fix a WQO
(X,{\leq})
for which

2
holds, a monotone surjection

, and an upward-closed subset

of
(Y,{\leq^{\prime}})
. Then

(Y,{\leq^{\prime}})
is a WQO and, given an oracle that decides for any

Example 13
.

Let
(\Sigma,{=})
be an alphabet with
k
letters. Then
\mathbb{N}^{k}
, also
known as the free commutative monoid with
k
generators, is a quotient of

{\Sigma}^{*}
: the surjection
\left|-\right|:{\Sigma}^{*}\to\mathbb{N}^{k}
, written
\left|w\right|=(\left|w\right|_{1},\ldots,\left|w\right|_{k})
counts the number of appearances of each letter
in a word. This surjection is monotone from the subword ordering on
{\Sigma}^{*}

to the component-wise ordering on
\mathbb{N}^{k}
. Given a
*
-product

\Sigma^{\prime}_{0}\sigma_{1}^{?}\Sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdots\Sigma^{\prime}_{n-1}\sigma_{n}^{?}\Sigma^{\prime}_{n}
, the downward-closure of its image by
\left|-\right|
is the subset of
elements
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that, for every
1\leq i\leq k
,

•

x_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
if the
i
-th letter appears in
\bigcup_{j=0}^{n}\Sigma^{\prime}_{j}
;

•

x_{i}\leq\left|\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\right|_{i}
otherwise.

Hence the corresponding instance of
Theorem

12
is Valk
and Jantzen’s original 1985 result, except we have now proved it by reducing
it to Angluin’s 1987 active learning algorithm.

Representing elements of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
as
k
-letter words is of course not very
efficient, and what is interesting in
Example

13
is how it
reduces the result of Valk and Jantzen to that of Angluin. But for other non-trivial
monoids, using word representations may still be a sensible choice: a trace
monoid where only a few pairs of letters are allowed to commute, for instance,
is very close to a monoid of words.

6
Conclusion and further work

In this work we have provided a new proof, relying on active learning of
automata, of the generalized Valk-Jantzen lemma for subword orderings. This
reduces in particular the original 1985 result by Valk and Jantzen for

\mathbb{N}^{k}
to Angluin’s 1987
L^{*}
algorithm. Along the way, we have
described a new MAT-based learning algorithm, for quasi-ordered automata, and
have extended Goubault-Larrecq’s generalized Valk-Jantzen lemma to finitely
generated monoids.

We have restricted ourselves to words on finite alphabets, but there are other
WQOs for which the generalized Valk-Jantzen lemma could reduce to an MAT-based
learning algorithm.

•

Words on infinite (or very large) alphabets: this would require regrouping
transitions in quasi-ordered automata by upward-closed subsets, so that they
can still be finitely represented. This use of an additional structure to
represent infinite alphabets finitely also comes up with
nominal
automata

[
4
]
. More generally,
enriched functors could provide a unifying framework for such state-machines
on infinite alphabets: a Myhill-Nerode theorem for topos-enriched automata is
notably given in
[
11
]
.

•

Tree orderings: trees on well quasi-ordered families of terms can
naturally be well quasi-ordered themselves

[
13
]
, their ideals can be
characterized as regular tree expressions
[
8
, §10]
, but “the technicalities are daunting”

[
10
]
. Perhaps an approach based
on the active learning algorithm for tree automata described in

[
7
]
can soothe out these technicalities.
