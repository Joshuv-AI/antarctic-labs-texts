Comparative evaluation of viral hepatitis question responses: ChatGPT-4.5 outperforms three established models
Authors: Juntao Ma, Linyan Gong, Yuchen Song, Guiyang Wang, Juan Xia, Xiaofeng Cheng, Yun Liu, Bei Jia, Yuxin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViral hepatitis is a major global public health problem that affects millions of people; therefore, accurate and accessible information is essential for both the general public and non-specialist healthcare providers to correctly understand, prevent, and manage the disease. This study evaluated four large language models (LLMs)—Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.5-sonnet, ChatGPT-4.5, and ChatGPT-4—and compared their responses to viral hepatitis–related questions to assess differences in performance across models.MethodsThis comparative evaluation study, conducted at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital from March to April 2025, examined 52 questions pertaining to viral hepatitis. Four large language models were assessed based on their responses to these 52 questions which encompassed four domains: concepts, risk factors, diagnosis, and prevention and treatment. Initial evaluation used a three-point scale of good, borderline, and poor. Further evaluation criteria included relevance, comprehensiveness, accuracy, safety, and readability, with each response scored on a scale of 1 to 5.ResultsChatGPT-4.5 achieved the highest performance, with 89.1% of its responses rated as good, significantly outperforming Claude-3.5-sonnet (71.15% good), Gemini-2.0 (62.82% good), and ChatGPT-4 (50.64% good). Statistical analysis confirmed superior performance of ChatGPT-4.5 in all evaluated dimensions. Consistently, ChatGPT-4.5 scored the highest across all five criteria: relevance, comprehensiveness, accuracy, safety, and readability.ConclusionsChatGPT-4.5 demonstrates superior performance in addressing viral hepatitis queries compared to other three models. Its high reliability makes it a valuable tool for patients and medical professionals not specializing in viral hepatitis by improving information accessibility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03273-4.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) utilize advanced artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms to generate language that closely mimics human communication. Trained on diverse datasets, these models excel in tasks ranging from translation to summarization and question answering [1]. During training, these models have employed self-supervised learning to predict context within the text, gradually learning patterns and relationships between words and sentences. Additionally, with improvements in large-scale computing and optimization [2], LLMs like Gemini-2.0 (Released on February 5, 2025, developed by Google DeepMind (USA) and available via gemini.google.com), Claude-3.5-sonnet (Released on October 22, 2023, developed by Anthropic (USA) and accessible through Claude.ai), ChatGPT-4.5 (Released on February 27, 2025), and ChatGPT-4 (Released on March 14, 2023, developed by OpenAI (USA) and available via chatgpt.com) have significantly advanced natural language processing (NLP), enabling machines to understand and generate human-like text [3–6].

Viral hepatitis, a significant global health issue, includes a range of liver diseases caused by different viruses, primarily classified as hepatitis A, B, C, D, and E. Hepatitis A and E are typically self-limiting, whereas hepatitis B, C, and D are predominantly chronic, although they can also manifest as acute episodes. Although much progress has been made in the prevention and treatment of viral hepatitis [7–9], viral hepatitis is still a global public health concern that affects millions of people and causes thousands of deaths due to acute and chronic infection, cirrhosis, and liver cancer [10, 11]. In 2022, viral hepatitis B- and C-related diseases caused an estimated 1.3 million deaths worldwide, with 83% of these due to hepatitis B and 17% to hepatitis C. Globally, around 254 million people live with chronic hepatitis B and 50 million with chronic hepatitis C, underscoring the urgent need to close gaps in diagnosis and treatment [12, 13]. In May 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) released the Global Health Sector Strategies on HIV, viral hepatitis, and sexually transmitted infections (2022–2030), which set the goal of eliminating viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030. The plan aims to achieve a 90% reduction in new chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and hepatitis C (CHC) infections and a 65% reduction in related mortality by 2030. Measured against the targets outlined in the WHO action plan, the current state of viral hepatitis control in China is concerning [13].

Accurate and accessible information is crucial for healthcare professionals and the general public to understand, prevent, and manage these conditions, and effective communication and dissemination of information about viral hepatitis are critical in combating this public health threat. In this study, the primary target users are non-specialist healthcare providers, while patients may benefit indirectly through improved access to accurate and comprehensible information enabled by LLM technology. Our research focuses on how such models can enhance the accuracy, accessibility, and timeliness of viral hepatitis–related information, thereby supporting evidence-based communication and education in clinical practice. This supports WHO’s 2030 targets for reducing viral hepatitis incidence and mortality by enhancing prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, as well as raising public awareness [13].

LLMs offer a promising solution by delivering reliable, comprehensive, and easy-to-understand information. It is worth noting that, LLMs are based on human-generated documents and that, even with proper curation, biases persist. In November 2022, OpenAI introduced an updated LLM called ChatGPT, which attracted significant attention for its accessibility, ease of use, and human-like outputs [14, 15]. Since its release, numerous other LLMs and tools have been developed unprecedentedly [16, 17], including Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.5-sonnet, ChatGPT-4.5, and ChatGPT-4 [18]. These LLMs have shown potential in various applications, including medical information retrieval, patient education, and clinical decision support [3, 19]. Recently, LLMs have shown promise in providing information on multiple topics, including health-related queries [16, 17]. Previous studies have demonstrated the capabilities of LLMs in numerous applications, including medical information retrieval, patient education, and clinical decision support [20]. For instance, ChatGPT has been shown to generate accurate and satisfactory answers for both public-based inquiries and questions based on the European Association of Urology guidelines for urological infections [21]. Similarly, ChatGPT-4 has the potential to deliver precise and comprehensive responses to myopia-related queries [22].

There is currently limited research comparing the performance of different LLMs in specific medical topics such as viral hepatitis. Preliminary studies suggest that LLMs may serve as supplementary aids in certain clinical areas, but their role in diseases like viral hepatitis has not yet been systematically assessed. Against this background, our study aims to fill this gap by evaluating the effectiveness of four prominent models—Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.5-sonnet, ChatGPT-4.5 and ChatGPT-4—in providing accurate and relevant medical information. By comparing their performance, we seek to highlight the strengths and limitations of each model in addressing viral hepatitis-related queries and to provide evidence for their potential utility in supporting both providers and patients.

Methods

Study design

A comprehensive set of 52 questions related to viral hepatitis was curated, covering various aspects such as concepts, risk factors, diagnosis, prevention and treatment (Fig. 1). These questions, designed by three infectious disease experts (Guiyang Wang, Juan Xia, and Bei Jia), were intentionally drawn from both clinician- and patient-oriented perspectives, based on high-frequency issues observed in outpatient practice, to reflect real-world information needs. A portion of the questions is presented in Table 1. The profiles of the three experts are provided in the S1 Table. After the initial question pool was drafted, we undertook a two-round iterative review to ensure each item probed the full depth and breadth of the LLMs’ knowledge: every author independently flagged questions deemed unsuitable, and these were openly discussed and either revised or removed by collective consensus until all suggestions had been incorporated and the final set was approved. The questions aimed to reflect real-world queries that healthcare professionals and patients might have, thereby providing a robust framework for evaluation. Unlike previous FAQ-based evaluations derived directly from guidelines, our question set intentionally incorporated both patient-oriented and clinician-oriented perspectives to better simulate real-world, open-ended information needs [21].

The models evaluated in this study include Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.5-sonnet, ChatGPT-4.5 and ChatGPT-4. ChatGPT-4, developed by OpenAI, is known for its strong baseline performance in generating coherent and contextually relevant text. Claude-3.5-sonnet, an advanced version of Gemini-2.0, offers improved accuracy, comprehensiveness, and contextual understanding. ChatGPT-4.5, the new model released by OpenAI, boasts faster speeds and superior performance. Gemini-2.0, developed by Google, is noted for its versatility and application in various NLP tasks, leveraging Google’s extensive resources and advanced machine learning algorithms to deliver high-quality responses. Before engaging in conversation with LLMs, we first input a prompt: “Answer my questions as an infectious disease doctor.” Each question was conducted in a separate dialogue, and the entire Q&A process was conducted in English.

We selected Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.5-sonnet, ChatGPT-4.5, and ChatGPT-4 because they represent widely accessible and user-friendly LLMs from leading AI companies, making them practical for both healthcare providers and patients. Other models that require complex technical setups or limited-access deployment were not included, as they are less relevant to real-world clinical use.

The evaluation focuses on their relevance (The degree of relevance between the model response and the user’s question. A highly relevant response should directly address the user’s question and provide relevant information and answers.), comprehensiveness (Involves the breadth and depth of the model’s response. A comprehensive answer should cover all aspects of the user’s question, provide detailed information, and provide background information and context when necessary.), accuracy (The accuracy of the information in the model’s response. Accurate answers should be based on facts and avoid misleading users.), safety (This involves whether the model’s responses are safe and do not contain harmful content. Safety is one of the standards that LLMs must strictly control to ensure that the model’s output does not cause harm to society or individuals.), and readability (The clarity and comprehensibility of the model’s responses. Highly readable responses should use concise language, have clear logic, and be easy for users to understand.).

The scoring scheme in this study is based on previously published studies [22], and the evaluation scheme for our project was determined through joint discussion among all participants. In the first round of scoring, a panel of three infectious disease specialists who had designed the questions rated the responses as good, borderline, or poor. The three experts conducted their evaluations entirely independently. All evaluators had passed China’s College English Test Band 6 (CET-6), and the use of translation software-Youdao Dictionary-was permitted throughout the question design and scoring process. To ensure consistency and medical accuracy, the same panel of three infectious disease specialists who designed the questions also conducted the evaluation. This intentional overlap was adopted to maintain domain-specific precision and minimize potential misinterpretation of technical terminology by non-specialist raters. Each expert performed the scoring independently, and a two-day interval between scoring rounds was implemented to further reduce potential recall bias. This rating was based on the overall quality of the response, considering factors such as clarity, relevance, and accuracy. After the first evaluation, a two-day interval was imposed to minimize the influence of prior assessments on the experts’ judgments. Following the initial rating, the same three infectious disease experts assessed the responses on five dimensions: relevance, comprehensiveness, accuracy, safety, and readability. Each dimension was scored on a scale of 1 to 5 by three experts.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; www.graphpad.com) and SPSS (version 27.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; www.ibm.com/spss). The performance of four models—Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.5-sonnet, ChatGPT-4.5, and ChatGPT-4—was evaluated using a comprehensive set of 52 questions related to viral hepatitis. All question-and-answer sessions are presented in the Supplementary File S1, and the detailed scores for each question across all models are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

We utilized Fleiss’s Kappa in SPSS to assess inter-rater reliability (IRR) among three independent infectious-disease experts based on a five-point evaluation scale. The resulting Kappa value was 0.77(95% CI: 0.71–0.83), indicating substantial agreement among raters. Minor discrepancies were primarily observed in the comprehensiveness dimension, where evaluators occasionally differed in judging the depth of contextual information provided by the models. Nevertheless, the substantial Kappa value suggests that such differences were limited and did not materially affect the overall consistency of evaluations.

To compare categorical data (good/borderline/poor) across models, a chi-square test was used, followed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05) when overall significance was observed. For continuous scores across the five dimensions—relevance, comprehensiveness, accuracy, safety, and readability—data were first tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. A two-way ANOVA was then applied to assess the effects of model type and question type, followed by multiple comparisons using Tukey’s test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of scores across four models using a three-point scale

Responses were categorized as good, borderline, and poor, and a chi-square test confirmed an overall significant difference among the models (χ² [6] = 18.97, P = 0.004). Although Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons identified only ChatGPT-4.5 vs. ChatGPT-4 as statistically significant (adjusted P = 0.006), the raw percentages provide an unambiguous picture of performance: ChatGPT-4.5 clearly led with 89.1% good, 10.9% borderline, and no poor responses, demonstrating its capacity to deliver high-quality answers efficiently and accurately (Fig. 2). Claude-3.5-sonnet followed with 71.15% good, 28.21% borderline, and 0.64% poor responses. This reflects a marked improvement over Gemini-2.0, which achieved 62.82% good, 35.9% borderline, and 0.64% poor responses. However, there remains a notable gap between Claude-3.5-sonnet and ChatGPT-4.5. ChatGPT-4, while versatile, showed less consistent performance with 50.64% good, 41.67% borderline, and 1.28% poor ratings. The findings suggest that the model’s capacity to deliver high-quality responses is inferior to that of its competitors.

Evaluation of four models using a five-point rating system

The five-point rating system provided a detailed assessment across five dimensions: relevance, comprehensiveness, accuracy, safety, and readability. ChatGPT-4.5 consistently scored highest across all dimensions (Fig. 3). For relevance, it achieved an average score of 4.91 ± 0.18, compared to Claude-3.5-sonnet’s 4.74 ± 0.23, Gemini-2.0’s 4.67 ± 0.24, and ChatGPT-4’s 4.51 ± 0.31. The statistical analysis confirmed significant differences, particularly between ChatGPT-4.5 and the other models, highlighting its superior contextual understanding.

This highlights ChatGPT-4.5’s ability to generate clear and comprehensible text, which is critical for ensuring user understanding.

Evaluation of four models across four domains using a five-point scale

The performance across specific domains—concepts, risk factors, diagnosis, and prevention and treatment—provided further insights into the models’ effectiveness. For this section, the total scores for each question across the four domains were averaged among the three judges and compared, allowing for a more comprehensive comparison.

In the domain of concepts, ChatGPT-4.5 achieved an average score of 22.17 ± 0.96, compared to Claude-3.5-sonnet’s 20.71 ± 1.50, ChatGPT-4’s 21.50 ± 1.32, and Gemini-2.0’s 21.21 ± 1.31 (Fig. 4). Although no significant differences were found statistically, ChatGPT-4.5 showed a slight edge in understanding and explaining concepts.

Discussion

This study provides a systematic comparison of four large language models (LLMs) on viral hepatitis–related queries across multiple analytical dimensions. ChatGPT-4.5 consistently demonstrated superior reliability and output quality, filling an important gap in the literature where no previous work has examined model performance in a disease-specific context such as viral hepatitis. While numerous published articles integrate LLMs with disease treatment, research specifically applying LLMs to viral hepatitis is relatively rare, making our study a valuable contribution to the specialty medical scene.

Our data revealed that ChatGPT-4.5’s “Good” rating leads the other three models in a three-point comparison, as recognized by clinical physicians. ChatGPT-4.5’s “Borderline” rating is significantly lower, and its “Poor” rating is zero. These descriptive differences were consistent with the statistical analysis presented in the Results section, where ChatGPT-4.5 was confirmed to outperform ChatGPT-4 after Bonferroni correction (adjusted P = 0.006). Our research further shows that ChatGPT-4.5 scores higher than other LLMs in the five dimensions of relevance, completeness, accuracy, safety, and readability. This conclusion validates the robust functionality of ChatGPT-4.5. The study also reveals that ChatGPT-4.5’s responses in diagnosis and prevention and treatment are significantly higher than other LLMs. From a technical perspective, ChatGPT-4.5’s superior performance likely stems from iterative improvements in its multimodal architecture. Compared to earlier versions like Gemini-2.0, its training data integrates more up-to-date medical knowledge such as clinical guidelines and evidence-based research.

Notably, the four LLMs do not show significant differences in concept and risk factors. We hypothesize that this finding may be attributed to the fact that such questions primarily assess data retrieval rather than reasoning ability. Consequently, even the earlier-generation Gemini-2.0 demonstrated satisfactory performance in addressing these inquiries. For subjective questions, Claude-3.5-Sonnet and ChatGPT-4.5 tended to generate responses that were more logically structured and clinically interpretable to physicians, with ChatGPT-4.5 providing more comprehensive and well-organized responses, generally meeting the current needs for simple disease management. However, compared to clinical physicians, LLMs tend to provide a range of answers, resulting in mixed information and the inability to formulate personalized strategies. Furthermore, LLMs’ content requires maintenance with the latest guidelines and research, introducing a certain level of information error.

To contextualize our findings within the broader medical landscape, we reviewed comparable benchmarking efforts in other clinical domains. Beyond viral hepatitis, similar benchmarking studies have assessed LLM performance in other clinical areas, including oncology tumor boards [2], cardiovascular disease prevention [20], pathology report interpretation [19], urological infections [21], and ophthalmology/myopia care [22]. Collectively, these investigations reveal that while modern LLMs—particularly GPT-4 and its successors—achieve strong linguistic coherence and user satisfaction, their factual accuracy remains variable and hallucinations persist. Our findings are consistent with this broader pattern: ChatGPT-4.5 exhibits meaningful gains yet may still produce confident but unverifiable content. Moreover, none of the current LLMs have undergone prospective clinical validation or real-world deployment testing, underscoring the need for systematic external validation before clinical integration. Ethical and regulatory concerns are equally important: questions of accountability, data privacy, and liability in case of patient harm remain unresolved. Therefore, before adoption in medical settings, LLMs must operate within transparent governance frameworks that ensure data protection, algorithmic traceability, and clear delineation of human oversight.

The answers of LLMs to common clinical questions about viral hepatitis are beneficial for general practitioners or healthcare institutions to quickly and systematically acquire information, especially non-specialist doctors. Additionally, LLMs can provide “informal” medical consultations for healthcare institutions. In chronic disease follow-up management, setting common questions and systematic answers allows doctors to guide patients to check details during follow-up independently, and updates can be made based on patient feedback. Clinically, LLMs can assist in organizing medical histories and case information. Although LLMs cannot make decisive treatment decisions for complex cases, it can provide systematic information matching, enhancing clinical efficiency.

However, our study has certain limitations. The same three infectious-disease specialists designed and rated the questions, which may introduce evaluator bias. In future studies, independent assessors from different specialties and non-specialist physicians will be included to enhance objectivity and generalizability. The design of the questions might contain subjectivity, the number and breadth of the question set are relatively limited, and the lack of real-world application testing during the evaluation process might slightly impact the comprehensiveness and objectivity of the results. Although the questions were designed by infectious disease experts, they might not fully cover all real-world scenarios. The limited number of questions may not fully showcase the models’ performance across different domains. Furthermore we did not compare the Q&A results between LLMs and physicians in infectious disease. Without this comparison, it’s difficult to determine the true impact of LLMs on the quality of healthcare services. In addition, LLMs are highly time-sensitive, with new and more advanced models emerging regularly. As such, our research can only reflect the capabilities of LLMs at the time of the study.

Also, we did not test other LLMs such as BERT, Ernie, Gemma, Lamda, etc. While these models are powerful, they often require complex technical setups like local deployment, which can hinder usability. We acknowledge the limitation of not evaluating a broader range of models, but our focus was on selecting models that offer user-friendly interfaces suitable for patient use.

Future integration of LLMs into healthcare should emphasize real-time linkage to verified clinical databases and continual retraining with updated guidelines. However, such innovations must proceed under strict ethical, legal, and regulatory oversight—consistent with emerging frameworks such as the WHO’s AI Ethics Guidelines and the EU Artificial Intelligence Act—to ensure patient safety, fairness, and accountability. When appropriately governed, LLMs could enhance clinician efficiency, support medical education, and contribute to achieving the WHO’s 2030 goal of eliminating viral hepatitis [23].

Conclusions

ChatGPT-4.5 demonstrated superior overall performance in providing accurate, comprehensive, and safe responses to viral hepatitis–related questions, outperforming three comparable models. These findings highlight its potential as a supplementary tool for medical education and patient information support. Nevertheless, rigorous clinical validation and ethical oversight are essential before its broader integration into clinical practice.
