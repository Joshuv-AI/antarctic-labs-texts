Availability, prices and affordability of nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines in China: a national survey
Authors: Wan Tang, Wei Li, Wei Lu, Zhengyi You, Hongdou Chen, Wei Sun, Huanhuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the availability, prices, and affordability of nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines in China.MethodsThis study collected monthly medicine usage data from 2018 to 2023 from the China Medical Economic Information Network (CMEI). The study focused on 12 nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines included in the 2020 edition of the National Reimbursement Drug List for Basic Medical Insurance, Work-Related Injury Insurance, and Maternity Insurance. This study covered 804 public general hospitals in 31 provincial administrative regions in China, among which 537 (66.79%) were tertiary, and 267 (33.21%) were secondary. The adjusted World Health Organization and Health Action International methodology was used to conduct a longitudinal descriptive analysis of the availability and affordability of 12 nationally negotiated antidiabetic drugs in the investigated hospitals. Besides, prices are expressed as the defined daily dose cost (DDDc).ResultsFrom 2018 to 2023, the number of antidiabetic medicines with availability exceeding 50% increased from 1 to 5. The total availability of the 12 medicines was higher in tertiary hospitals than in secondary hospitals. The DDDc of the 12 medicines decreased substantially from baseline, with reduction rates ranging from 22.78% to 90.39%. Affordability improved progressively over the study period, with declines ranging from 43.43% to 97.52%. In 2023, 7 out of the 12 antidiabetic medicines were affordable for the population. However, rural residents had lower affordability of the medicines than urban residents.ConclusionsThe overall availability, affordability and DDDc of 12 nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines improved from 2018 to 2023. However, the overall availability of most antidiabetic medicines in China remained at a low level (less than 50%). Therefore, further efforts should be made to sufficiently and equally benefit patients with diabetes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14780-z.

Introduction

According to the latest report from the International Diabetes Federation, nearly one ninth adults globally (589 million) are now living with diabetes. By 2050, the estimated number of adults with diabetes is projected to reach 853 million [1]. The prevalence rate among adults aged 18 and above stands at 11.9% in China, where there is the largest total number of patients with diabetes globally [2]. Diabetes has been an urgent health issue for China since 1980 [3, 4]. Economic development, aging population, and lifestyle changes are among the reasons for the high incidence of diabetes. For its treatment, lifestyle intervention is a basic therapeutic measure for diabetes and should be carried out throughout the treatment of diabetes. If simple lifestyle intervention fails to bring blood glucose up to the target, medicine therapy should be started in time. The use of hypoglycaemic drugs is an important clinical intervention for controlling blood glucose. Therefore, the availability and affordability of antidiabetic medicines are crucial for patients with diabetes. However, a 2018 study on antidiabetic medicines in Hubei Province, China, found that 70.6% of the surveyed medicines had availability below 50%, and 41.7% of urban patients with diabetes and 74.0% of rural patients with diabetes were unable to afford the medicines surveyed [5]. A 2019 study on oral antidiabetic medicines in Shaanxi Province, western China, found that most antidiabetic medicines in Shaanxi Province failed to meet the World Health Organization (WHO)’s availability target (80% availability) [6]. These findings highlight the urgent need to focus on and improve the availability and affordability of antidiabetic medicines for Chinese residents.

In recent years, novel glucose-lowering medications, including sodium-glucose cotransporter2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, and insulin analogs, have rapidly entered the Chinese market. These medicines effectively control blood glucose and improve cardiovascular outcomes [7, 8], which makes them gradually replace traditional therapies. However, these novel antidiabetic medicines are typically expensive, posing significant economic burdens on patients and society [9]. Given that diabetes, as a highly prevalent chronic condition, requires lifelong pharmacological intervention, these elevated costs severely compromise medication adherence and patients’ willingness to persist with treatment. Consequently, this financial barrier undermines optimal healthcare access for people with diabetes.

To effectively reduce the prices of patented and exclusive medicines and alleviate medication burdens on patients, the national healthcare security department has explored and established a medical insurance access negotiation mechanism since 2017. Through negotiations, high-priced medicines with high clinical value and proven efficacy have been progressively included into the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL) [10]. To date, over a dozen glucose-lowering medications have been incorporated into the NRDL through national medical insurance negotiations, securing their large coverage for patients [11]. However, their inclusion in the NRDL does not guarantee patient access to them. Whether patients can ultimately have access to medicines conveniently and affordably depends on the coordination of multiple factors, including hospital procurement, local policies, and pharmaceutical supply. Therefore, empirical investigation into the final implementation of nationally negotiated medicines within medical institutions is essential to further optimize relevant policies and measures, thereby effectively safeguarding medication access for grassroots populations.

The prices, availability, and affordability are the main criteria to measure whether patients can purchase a medicine at an affordable price [12, 13]. In 2003, the WHO and Health Action International (HAI) jointly developed the WHO/HAI Standard Survey Methodology to assess the accessibility of essential medicines worldwide [14].To date, numerous studies have been conducted globally using the WHO/HAI Standard Survey Methodology. Based on this foundation, China has now established the evaluation method that better suits its national conditions [12, 15]. The scope of the medicines studied has expanded beyond essential medicines to include those previously not covered by the standard survey, such as glucose-lowering medicines [16]. To evaluate the accessibility of nationally negotiated glucose-lowering medicines, this study investigated their availability, affordability and prices across China by using an adjusted WHO/HAI methodology. This study provided the first empirical analysis, from the perspectives of hospital tiers and urban-rural stratification, of the impact of the NRDL policy on the utilization of nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines. It can not only address the gap in nationwide evaluation research on antidiabetic medicines under the NRDL policy framework but also offer a more comprehensive analytical reference for future related studies.

Methods

A longitudinal descriptive analysis was conducted across 31 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in China from January 2019 to December 2023. The study evaluated the availability, prices, and affordability of nationally negotiated glucose-lowering medicines in 804 fixed public general hospitals (including 537 tertiary hospitals and 267 secondary hospitals) in recent years.

Selection of the investigated medicines

A list of 12 medicines was included in this study, consisting of liraglutide injection, which was nationally negotiated in 2017, acarbose tablets, exenatide injection, lixisenatide injection, dapagliflozin tablets, canagliflozin tablets, and empagliflozin tablets negotiated in 2019, and insulin degludec/aspart injection, benaglutide injection, dulaglutide injection, pegylated loxenatide injection and ertugliflozin tablets negotiated in 2020 (Table 1).

Data sources

The Chinese Medicine Economic Information Network (CMEI), one of the largest medicine information service platforms in China covering over 1,500 public hospitals, served as the data source for this study [14, 17]. By virtue of the CMEI, this study systematically collected related data including therapeutic category, generic name, dosage form, brand manufacturer, procurement volume, and procurement price for the 12 nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines.

The DDD value of each generic medicine can be found in the Guidelines for Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification and DDD assignment 2024 [24]. A higher DDDc value indicates that the medicine is more expensive [25].

Affordability

The WHO defines medicine affordability as the multiple of the lowest-paid unskilled government worker (LPGW)’s daily wage represented by the total medicine cost for a standard treatment regimen against a specific disease [26]. According to the WHO/HAI, a medicine is affordable if the cost of a treatment course is less than the daily wage of the LPGW (affordability < 1) [27, 28]. Following previous research [29], due to the lack of data, the daily wage of the LPGW was proxied using the annual disposable income per capita for estimates. In China, the median annual disposable income per capita for all residents is divided by the standard number of workdays (250 days) to estimate the daily wage equivalent [30]. The treatment duration for an acute disease is the total duration of a full course of therapy while that for a chronic disease is 30 days [13, 26]. Prior to their inclusion in the NRDL, the investigated medicines required 100% patient’s out-of-pocket payment. After national negotiations, these medicines were incorporated into Category B of the NRDL with nationwide implementation, but their reimbursement rates varied from place to place (ranging from about 50% to 85%) [31, 32]. Based on established research evidence, this study standardized the reimbursement rate for Category B negotiated medicines at 65% [33]. To eliminate the influence of the assumed reimbursement rate on affordability, this study conducted a sensitivity analyses using the Mann-Whitney U test. According to the National Bureau of Statistics of China (NBSC), the median annual disposable income per capita from 2018 to 2023 stood at 24,336 CNY, 26,523 CNY, 27,540 CNY, 29,975 CNY, 31,370 CNY and 33,036 CNY for all residents; at 36,413 CNY, 39,244 CNY, 40,378 CNY, 43,504 CNY, 45,123 CNY and 47,122 CNY for urban residents; and at 13,066 CNY, 14,389 CNY, 15,204 CNY, 16,902 CNY, 17,734 CNY and 18,748 CNY for rural residents, respectively [34–39].

Results

Availability

The availability of 12 investigated antidiabetic medicines from 2018 to 2023 is presented in Table 2; Fig. 1. The availability rates of all the investigated medicines except acarbose and exenatide demonstrated upward trends against baseline values, among which insulin degludec and insulin aspart’s availability rate rose from 0.33% in 2019 to 67.60% in 2023. In 2023, the availability rates of exenatide, lixisenatide, and benaglutide plummeted to merely 3.11%, 0.30%, and 1.01% respectively. Concurrently, five medicines demonstrated fairly high availability (50% to 80%): insulin degludec and insulin aspart (67.60%), acarbose (70.34%), liraglutide (51.67%), dulaglutide (55.38%), and dapagliflozin (77.11%). The analysis on hospital tier levels (Fig. 2) reveals that most antidiabetic medicines exhibited obvious increases in their availability rates across both secondary and tertiary hospitals. Moreover, their availability rates in tertiary hospitals were generally higher than those in secondary hospitals, which aligned with disparities in resource allocation and clinical capacity for different hospital tiers.

Prices

As shown in Table 3; Fig. 3, in 2023 all the investigated medicines except ertugliflozin demonstrated significant reductions in DDDc values compared with baseline values, with decline rates ranging from 22.78% to 90.39%. Exenatide showed the most pronounced reduction, whose DDDc decreased from 113.29 CNY in 2018 to 10.89 CNY in 2023, representing a 90.39% decline. Notably, DDDc values generally decreased immediately following a medicine’s entry into the NRDL, and the first formal implementation year (post-negotiation year) witnessed the most substantial reductions. Thereafter, DDDc values generally continued to decline gradually or stabilized. For example, the initial DDDc value of benaglutide during the study period was 30.16 CNY. It decreased to 29.90 CNY in the negotiation year (2020) and further dropped to 14.28 CNY in the first implementation year (2021).

Affordability

As shown in Fig. 4, during the study period, while the affordability of ertugliflozin increased (by 12.97%), the affordability of all other investigated medicines decreased significantly, with decline rates ranging from 43.43% to 97.52%. Among them, exenatide showed the largest decrease in affordability (97.52%). As shown in Table 4, all the medicines collected by the research institute were unaffordable during 2018 and 2019. In 2020, excluding ertugliflozin which was not collected, four of the other investigated medicines became affordable: acarbose (0.43), dapagliflozin (0.41), empagliflozin (0.70), and canagliflozin (0.77). In 2021, ertugliflozin (0.49) was added to the list of affordable medications. By 2022, two more medicines became affordable: insulin degludec and insulin aspart (0.98) and exenatide (0.89), achieving 7 affordable medications in total. In 2023, although no new medicines became affordable, the affordability rates of the remaining medicines still decreased. The affordability for urban residents was significantly better than that for rural residents. In 2023, urban residents could afford 10 of the investigated antidiabetic medicines, while rural residents could only afford 5 (Figs. 5). This study used a 65% reimbursement rate to estimate patients’ out-of-pocket costs for those medicines included in the NRDL. To verify the robustness of this assumption, sensitivity analyses were conducted using reimbursement rates of 50% and 85%. The sensitivity analyses confirmed that, although the absolute level of out-of-pocket expenses varied with the reimbursement rates, the core findings of this study remained robust across all scenarios. Specifically, overall medicine affordability showed a trend of improvement, while a significant and persistent affordability gap between urban and rural areas was consistently observed (P = 0.018 < 0.05) (Supplementary Tables 1–2).

Discussion

Diabetes is a global health challenge that affected 529 million people in 2021 and is projected to affect 1.31 billion people by 2050 worldwide [40]. China has the world’s largest patients with diabetes population, with more than 118 million people living with diabetes, accounting for approximately 22% of all suffering from diabetes worldwide [41]. Antidiabetic medicines, as a key means of controlling diabetes progression and lowering blood glucose levels, play a pivotal role in diabetes treatment. Deressa HD [42] found big gaps in availability and affordability of essential medicines (EMs) used for diabetes in central Ethiopia. Policy makers should work to improve access to EMs against diabetes. Caijun Yang’s study on the availability and affordability of oral antidiabetic medicines in Shaanxi Province, China, revealed that most antidiabetic medications failed to meet WHO supply targets [43]. Patients were under considerable economic pressure, and the medication affordability was disproportionate among rural residents. These findings have underscored the need for government-led policy interventions to ensure better access to more affordable antidiabetic medicines for the Chinese people. In response, China has implemented the NRDL negotiation policy and has been progressively expanding the NRDL to cover more and newer antidiabetic medicines.

This study was conducted using the WHO/HAI methodology to evaluate the availability, prices, and affordability of nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines in China, aiming to provide policymakers with the latest data reference for optimizing relevant policies. In this study, it was found that from 2018 to 2023, the overall availability of the investigated antidiabetic medicines showed an upward trend, while their DDDc exhibited a downward trend. The affordability of these medicines for the public has gradually improved, which is consistent with the anticipated outcomes. During the study period, an “inverted U-shaped” inflection point was observed in the availability of GLP-1 receptor agonists such as exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, and benaglutide in 2021. This phenomenon may be primarily attributed to two factors. First, although these medicines were included in the NRDL, they were subject to stringent reimbursement restrictions: for patients with inadequately controlled type 2 diabetes who require oral antihyperglycemic agents (e.g., metformin) or insulin and have a Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m², the initial prescription must be issued by an endocrinologist or a specialist at a secondary or higher medical facility [44]. Second, it may also be attributed to changes in market competition dynamics caused by several factors, including the market impact caused by newer-generation GLP-1 medicines (such as dulaglutide and semaglutide), price reductions of generic medicines, and the implementation of centralized procurement policies. These factors were associated with temporary imbalances or fluctuations in the actual availability of GLP-1 medicines, thereby affecting the stability of their availability. The availability of acarbose in 2023 was lower than that in 2018, which might result from the gradual introduction of various newer antidiabetic medicines. This expansion in treatment options has provided alternative approaches for diabetes management, consequently impacting the demand for earlier-generation medicines such as acarbose. Among the 12 investigated medicines, insulin degludec and insulin aspart demonstrated the most significant increase in availability. As the first fully soluble co-formulation of insulin, it combines ultra-long-acting basal insulin degludec and rapid-acting meal-time insulin analog aspart, which features no requirement for resuspension before injection and an extended duration of action in its advantage [45]. The improved availability of this insulin formulation may benefit a larger population of patients with diabetes.

The empirical analysis of this study found that as of 2023, the availability rates of dapagliflozin, acarbose, and insulin degludec and insulin aspart in tertiary hospitals in China (81.94%, 73.88%, and 74.57%, respectively) were relatively higher than those of other investigated antidiabetic medicines. This disparity may be mainly attributable to the following factors. First, policy and market factors are the primary drivers. Although all these medicines have been included in the national medical insurance, some have not yet entered the national centralized procurement or the Essential Medicines list. Among the 12 investigated antidiabetic medicines, only sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors, acarbose, and insulin degludec and insulin aspart were included in the centralized procurement program [11]. Additionally, only acarbose was listed in the Essential Medicines [46]. Tertiary hospitals generally enforce restrictions on the variety and specifications of medicines, and the introduction of new or high-cost medicines requires stringent approval processes. Consequently, hospitals tend to prioritize stocking the medicines supported by national policies and with clear clinical demand. Second, clinical recognition and prescribing habits also play a significant role. Dapagliflozin, as an SGLT-2 inhibitor, can not only lower blood glucose but also provide proven cardio-renal protective benefits, making it a preferred recommendation in domestic and international guidelines [47]. Acarbose, due to its suitability for Asian dietary patterns, has long been used as a first-line foundational therapy. Insulin degludec and insulin aspart, as a novel insulin analog, is favored by both physicians and patients for its dual-phase mechanism (addressing both basal and postprandial glucose control) and reduced injection frequency [45]. Finally, regional economic disparities significantly impact medicine availability in medical institutions. China’s vast territory and varying levels of development across regions directly affect the accessibility of high-cost innovative medicines at the local level. In summary, the availability of a medicine is associated with multiple factors, including the scope of its indications, patient acceptance (e.g., price and tolerability of adverse reactions), the extent of clinical promotion (e.g., the number of clinical trials conducted), and physicians’ familiarity with the medicine. If a medicine is used infrequently in clinical practice—due to high cost or because it has been replaced by superior alternatives—it may be removed from the hospital’s regular procurement list or placed under temporary procurement, ultimately affecting its availability. Therefore, the current landscape of antidiabetic medicine availability across different levels of hospitals is likely to result from the interplay of policy regulation, hospital management, clinical practices, and regional economic development.

Among the 12 investigated antidiabetic medicines, the DDDc values decreased significantly for all except ertugliflozin. The slight increase in the DDDc value of ertugliflozin may be attributed to a floor effect. It possibly resulted from its relatively late market entry (in 2021), which coincided with China’s implementation of the NRDL policy, leading to artificially suppressed market pricing during its first year on the market. It could also be linked to the volume-price agreements signed between the National Healthcare Security Administration and manufacturers during medical insurance and medicine procurement price negotiations. Besides, during the study period, the number of medicines affordable to the public increased from 0 to 7. These findings collectively indicate a certain correlation between the implementation of the NRDL policy and the economic burden on patients. However, this study also revealed a substantial disparity in the affordability of the investigated medicines between urban and rural areas, with the affordability capacity of rural residents notably lower than that of urban residents. This can provide a solid theoretical foundation for the formulation and optimization of relevant policies by the government in the future. This phenomenon may be attributed to the following reasons. Firstly, the most direct and fundamental cause lies in the “dual disparity” in payment capacity between urban and rural residents. According to publicly available statistical data from the NBSC [34–39], the per capita disposable income of urban residents in China has consistently been significantly higher than that of rural residents in recent years, with the gap approximately exceeding twofold. Simultaneously, disparities exist between the healthcare insurance systems for rural residents and urban employees in terms of funding standards, reimbursement rates, and payment ceilings. As a result, these differences in reimbursement policies have further amplified the economic risks arising from income inequality. Residents in areas with lower reimbursement levels face greater out-of-pocket pressure, which tends to reduce medication adherence, lead to delays or interruptions in treatment plans, and even result in poverty due to illness. Secondly, while payment capacity determines “whether one can afford medication”, physical availability determines “whether medication can be obtained”. The distribution of pharmaceuticals relies on mature and efficient supply chains. In rural areas featuring lower population density and relatively dispersed settlements, higher logistics costs for medicine delivery have made it challenging for primary healthcare institutions to maintain a complete inventory of certain medications. To get access to necessary treatments, patients sometimes have to seek medical care from higher-level medical institutions. This not only indirectly increases residents’ healthcare costs but also substantially reduces medication accessibility. Lastly, implicit differences in healthcare environments and health behaviors between urban and rural areas cannot be overlooked. Prescribing practices among primary care physicians in rural areas are often constrained by factors such as limited access to continuous professional training, less availability of the latest academic information, and inadequate awareness of newer medications. Meanwhile, some rural residents, affected by their economic concerns or insufficient awareness, may be more prone to discontinuing treatment or receiving irregular care. The urban-rural is not the result of a single factor but rather stems from the interplay of residents’ payment capacity, medication accessibility, and the primary healthcare ecosystem. Therefore, this study can provide a robust theoretical foundation for the future formulation and optimization of relevant policies by the government. Building on this, future policy initiatives could adopt a more diversified approach.

This study reports on the utilization of 12 nationally negotiated antidiabetic medicines in 804 public secondary and tertiary general hospitals across China from 2018 to 2023. It features a long duration, broad coverage, and a diverse range of medicines, allowing for a comprehensive evaluation of the implementation of the NRDL policy. However, this study also has certain limitations, such as potential underreporting in hospital procurement data and the absence of data from private hospitals, primary healthcare institutions and retail pharmacies during data collection. Therefore, the dataset will be further expanded in our subsequent studies to provide more objective and robust data support for assessing the implementation of the NRDL policy. In addition, the utilization data were based on procurement data. Although the procurement volume partially reflects the actual demand for and potential consumption of medicines by patients, discrepancies remain between the procurement volume and the actual consumption.

Conclusions

This study has suggested that the implementation of the NRDL policy is associated with improved availability of antidiabetic medicines and a potential reduction in the financial burden on patients with diabetes. However, the availability of many newer antidiabetic medicines remains relatively low, and significant disparities persist in affordability between urban and rural residents. Therefore, the government should introduce targeted policies to address urban-rural inequalities, further optimize the national healthcare security system, and ensure medication access for grassroots populations. Simultaneously, it is crucial to accelerate the establishment of a comprehensive evaluation system for new medicine inclusion, so that more innovative medicines can be promptly incorporated into the national medicine price negotiations. This will lighten the financial burden on patients and promote the comprehensive implementation of nationally negotiated medicines.
