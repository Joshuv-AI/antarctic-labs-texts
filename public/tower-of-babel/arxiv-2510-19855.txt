Two Quantum Algorithms for Nonlinear Reaction-Diffusion Equation using Chebyshev Approximation Method
Manish Kumar, [2pt] Quantum Technology (IAP)IISc Bengaluru

Abstract
We present two new quantum algorithms for reaction-diffusion equations that employ the truncated Chebyshev polynomial approximation. This method is employed to numerically solve the ordinary differential equation emerging from the linearization of the associated nonlinear differential equation. In the first algorithm, we use the 𝗆𝖺𝗍𝗋𝗂𝗑 ​ 𝖾𝗑𝗉𝗈𝗇𝖾𝗇𝗍𝗂𝖺𝗍𝗂𝗈𝗇 \mathsf{matrix\ exponentiation} method (Patel et al., 2018), while in the second algorithm, we repurpose the 𝗊𝗎𝖺𝗇𝗍𝗎𝗆 ​ 𝗌𝗉𝖾𝖼𝗍𝗋𝖺𝗅 ​ 𝗆𝖾𝗍𝗁𝗈𝖽 \mathsf{quantum\ spectral\ method} (Childs et al., 2020). Our main technical contribution is to derive the sufficient conditions for the diagonalization of the Carleman embedding matrix, which is indispensable for designing both quantum algorithms. We supplement this with an efficient iterative algorithm to diagonalize the Carleman matrix. Our first algorithm has gate complexity of 𝖮 ⁡ ( 𝖽 ⋅ 𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( 𝖽 ) + 𝖳 ⋅ 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( 𝖳 / ε ) ) \mathsf{O\big(d\cdot log(d)+T\cdot polylog({T}/{\varepsilon})\big)} . Where d d is the size of the Carleman matrix, T T is the simulation time, and ε \varepsilon is the approximation error. The second algorithm is polynomial in 𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( 𝖽 ) \mathsf{log(d)} , T T , and 𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( 𝟣 / ε ) \mathsf{log(1/\varepsilon)} - the gate complexity scales as 𝖮 ⁡ ( 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( 𝖽 ) ⋅ 𝖳 ⋅ 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒𝗅𝗈𝗀 ⁡ ( 𝖳 / ε ) ) \mathsf{O\big(polylog(d)\cdot T\cdot polylog({T}/{\varepsilon})\big)} . In terms of 𝖳 \mathsf{T} and ε \varepsilon , this is comparable to the speedup gained by the current best known quantum algorithm for this problem, the 𝗍𝗋𝗎𝗇𝖼𝖺𝗍𝖾𝖽 ​ 𝖳𝖺𝗒𝗅𝗈𝗋 ​ 𝗌𝖾𝗋𝗂𝖾𝗌 \mathsf{truncated\ Taylor\ series} method (Costa et.al., 2025). There are two shortcomings of our approach. First, we have not provided an upper bound, in terms of d d , on the condition number of the Carleman matrix. Second, the success of the diagonalization is based on a conjecture that a specific trigonometric equation has no integral solution. However, we provide strategies to mitigate these shortcomings in most practical cases.

1.1
Quantum Algorithms for Linear Differential Equations

Quantum Hamiltonian simulation has been considered a promising avenue for achieving a computational advantage over existing classical simulation methods

1.2
Quantum algorithms for Non-linear Differential Equations

Several natural systems, such as chemical reactions, fluid transport, and population dynamics, are well understood through the use of non-linear equations. For example, the Reaction-diffusion equation (RDE) for chemical systems and the Navier-Stokes equation (NSE) for fluid dynamics are widely studied nonlinear systems. As per the finite difference scheme, spatial discretization of the above two PDEs will produce non-linear ODEs
[
GRS16
]
. Recently, this observation has been used as an opportunity to explore quantum algorithms for such nonlinear systems by designing a quantum nonlinear ODE solver. However, the quantum algorithmic toolkit developed for linear ODEs can’t be directly applied to non-linear ODEs, primarily because quantum dynamics is a linear theory. In a breakthrough result,
a special class
of non-linear ODEs, for a specific range of parameters, has been proven to be efficiently computable on a quantum computer
[
LKK+21
]
. These non-linear ODEs exhibit a special type of non-linearity called polynomial non-linearity. For example, the non-linear ODE

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}{\displaystyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}{\scriptstyle{\frac{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0muu}{\mathrm{d}\mkern 0.0mut}}}=au+bu^{2}\qquad

(1.1)

has quadratic non-linearity. An efficient quantum algorithm exists for this system. One of the key steps of the algorithm is to employ the Carleman linearization, a technique that embeds a non-linear ODE into an infinite system of linear ODEs. Now, the existing quantum algorithm for a linear system of ODEs can solve it by appropriately truncating the ODE system.

has the following properties. First, it is a non-Hermitian matrix. Second, it is
3N
-sparse matrix with local structure (see equation

5.6
to equation

) guaranteed by the No-resonance condition.

We deploy the algorithms by

]
for the Carleman matrix. To this end, we state the three crucial lemmas.

Lemma 4
.

The Carleman matrix
A
can be linearly transformed to map its eigenvalues to the interval
[-1,\ 1]
.

A\mapsto\frac{A-\frac{(\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{min}})}{2}I}{\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}{2}}

(6.15)

Let’s define
\alpha:=\frac{(\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{min}})}{2}
and
\beta:=\frac{(\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{min}})}{2}
. The scaling of time goes as

t\mapsto\beta t

(6.16)

It should be noted that the above scaling doesn’t alter the diagonalization procedure due to the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

The Carleman matrix
A
is diagonalized as
A=V\Lambda V^{-1}
, then the same similarity transformation matrices
V
and
V^{-1}
will diagonalize the scaled matrix.

A\mapsto\frac{A-\frac{(\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{min}})}{2}I}{\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}-\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}{2}}

(6.17)

Proof: Notice

\mathsf{V(A/\beta-\alpha I/\beta)V^{-1}=V(A/\beta)V^{-1}-V(\alpha I/\beta)V^{-1}=\Lambda/\beta-\alpha I/\beta}.

(6.18)

This is also a diagonal matrix.

\square

Consequently, we use the same matrices
V
and
V^{-1}
without worrying about the scaling.

Lemma 6
.

The minimum and maximum values of the Carleman matrix are

\mathsf{\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}=-4D(n+1)^{2}sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}\right)+a}

(6.19)

\mathsf{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}=-N\left(4D(n+1)^{2}sin^{2}\left(\frac{n\pi}{2(n+1)}\right)+a\right)}

(6.20)

\mathsf{Proof}
: We work in a parameter regime with negative eigenvalues. Thus, the maximum eigenvalue of the matrix
A
is the one with the minimum magnitude eigenvalue of
A_{1}^{1}=F_{1}
. The maximum eigenvalue of the matrix
A
is the one with the maximum magnitude eigenvalue of
A_{N}^{N}
.

\square

Since
A=V\Lambda V^{-1}
, we express the analytic equation of the Carleman matrix as

\mathsf{e^{At}=Ve^{\Lambda}V^{-1}}.

(6.21)

Here,

e^{\Lambda t}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda_{1}t}&&&&\\
&e^{\lambda_{2}t}&&&\\
&&\ddots&&\\
&&&e^{\lambda_{n-1}t}&\\
&&&&e^{\lambda_{d}t}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(6.22)

Express each of the exponential functions along the diagonal as a Chebyshev series

\mathsf{e^{\lambda_{i}t}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}C_{k}(t)T_{k}(\lambda_{i})}

(6.23)

These are two crucial results regarding the series that is used to design and analyze the algorithm.

Theorem 6
.

In the Chebyshev series

\mathsf{e^{\lambda_{i}t}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}C_{k}(t)T_{k}(\lambda_{i})}

(6.24)

The coefficients are given by the modified Bessel function of the first kind

:

\mathsf{C_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-tcos\theta}d\theta=I_{0}(t),}

(6.25)

\mathsf{C_{k>0}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-tcos\theta}cos(k\theta)d\theta=2(-1)^{k}I_{k}(t)}

(6.26)

Where,

\mathsf{I_{k(t)}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(t/2)^{k+2s}}{s!(k+s)!}}

(6.27)

See Mathews & Walker’s book
[
WW71
]
for its derivation using complex analysis.

(
[
PP18
]
)
.

Given a simulation time of

\mathsf{r=\frac{e^{5/4t}}{2}+ln(1/\varepsilon)=O(t+log(1/\varepsilon))}

The proof is proved in the paper

]
and it follows as a natural consequence of

Step I (matrix rescaling): The matrix rescaling has a relatively nominal cost because there is a closed-form expression for computing the minimum and maximum eigenvalue of the Carleman matrix due to

6.3
Method II: Quantum Spectral Method

The high-level description of the algorithm is very similar to that of other Quantum linear system-based algorithms. The key difference is that the Carleman ODE is solved using the Chebyshev polynomial-based method.

For the sake of completeness, we briefly summarize the Pseudo-spectral method. The key goal is to solve the homogeneous ODE

\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{y}}}{\mathrm{d}{t}}=A\mathbf{y},\qquad\mathbf{y}(0)=\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{in}}

(6.47)

The goal is to find
\mathbf{y}(t)
for
t\in[0,\ T]
.

Key idea:

Consider a truncated Chebyshev series approximation for the solution
\mathbf{y}(t)
as

\mathbf{y}_{i}(t)\approx\hat{\mathbf{y}}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{r}c_{i,k}T_{k}(t),\quad i\in[{d}]_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}

(6.48)

There are two useful properties of the above consideration.

•

Special points exist in the domain
t\in[0,\ T]
where evaluating the Chebyshev polynomial has a marginal cost. These points are called Chebyshev-Gauss-Lobatto quadrature (CGL) nodes,
t_{l}=\cos\frac{l\pi}{r}
for
l\in[{r+1}]_{0}
. Note the value of the Chebyshev polynomial at CGL nodes
T_{k}(t_{l})=\cos\frac{kl\pi}{r}
.

•

For
\forall i\in[d]_{0}
, the solution components
\mathbf{y}_{i}(t)
satisfy the ODE and initial conditions at these CGL nodes
\{t_{l}\}_{l=0}^{n}
. It would give a system of linear equations solved for the coefficients.

Determining the Chebyshev truncation order:
The criteria for selecting the Chebyshev truncation order
r
are due to the following theorem.

Lemma 9

(Lemma 2 of
[
CL19
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{y}(t)\in C^{\infty}(-1,1)
be the analytic solution of an ordinary differential equation and let the numerical solution be
\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t)
that satisfies the ODE and initial condition for
\{t_{l}=\cos\frac{l\pi}{n}\}_{l=0}^{n}
. Then

\max_{t\in[-1,1]}\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}(t)-\mathbf{y}(t)\|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\max_{t\in[-1,1]}\|{\mathbf{y}}^{(r+1)}(t)\|\left(\frac{e}{2r}\right)^{r}.

(6.49)

If the matrix
A
is diagonalizable,
A=V\Lambda V^{-1}
, then equation 4.21 of
[
CL19
]
gives

\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}(T)-\mathbf{y}(T)\|\leqslant m\kappa_{V}\|\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{in}}\|\left(\frac{e}{2r}\right)^{r}

(6.50)

where,

. Thus, expressing the formula below gives an estimate of the condition number in terms of time

and error

\|\hat{\mathbf{y}}(T)-\mathbf{y}(T)\|\leqslant\left(\frac{\|A\|T}{2}\right)\kappa_{V}\|\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{in}}\|\left(\frac{e}{2r}\right)^{r}

(6.51)

We discuss the classical hardness of the problem. It is unlikely that there can be a classical algorithm having polynomial dependency on all the parameters
