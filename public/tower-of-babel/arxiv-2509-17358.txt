Labeled Chip-Firing on Undirected k k -ary Trees
Ryota Inagaki, Aaron Lin

Abstract
We explore labeled chip-firing on undirected k k -ary trees, trees where every vertex has degree k + 1 k+1 . First, we extend known results for binary trees from Musiker and Nguyen, including the endgame and the locations of the smallest and largest chips, as well as relations between chips at different vertices. Then, inspired by recent work on the binary tree by Inagaki, Khovanova, and Luo, we use these properties to construct an upper bound, which we call the zigzag bound, on the number of stable configurations in labeled chip-firing on k k -ary trees with k ℓ − 1 k − 1 \frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1} labeled chips starting at the root. We further provide a novel lower bound on the number of stable configurations of k k -ary trees, complementing our upper bounds.

Introduction

Chip-firing is a combinatorial process that acts as a discrete approximation of a dynamic system. It was first proposed by Spencer, who described a game in which chips are taken from a pile and split as evenly as possible among neighboring piles
[
15
]
. Later, classical chip-firing was defined in
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
, as a system in which one takes chips from a pile and
fires
one chip to each neighboring pile. Numerous variants, such as the Abelian Sandpile (see
[
3
,
6
,
7
]
) and the chip-firing game on invertible matrices
[
8
]
, and algebraic aspects of the game, such as the critical group
[
4
]
, have been studied. When the chip-firing game is done with
distinguishable
, or labeled chips, numerous basic properties from the unlabeled version of the game no longer hold, motivating a distinct area of study.

1.1
Unlabeled Chip-Firing

Chip-firing has classically been studied in the unlabeled case. Specifically, unlabeled chip-firing is a variant of chip-firing in which indistinguishable chips are placed on the vertices of a graph. We define a
configuration
as a distribution of chips over the vertices of a graph
G=(V,E)
; it can be represented as a vector
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{|V|}
where the entry corresponding to vertex
m
is the number of chips at
v_{m}
. If a vertex has as at least as many chips as neighbors, it can fire and give a chip to each of its neighbors. This process continues, firing as many vertices as possible. If no vertex can fire, then the process has terminated and we say that it has reached a
stable configuration
, which is a configuration of chips where no vertex can fire.

Example 1.1
.

Consider the binary tree below in Figure
1
where the top vertex is a neighbor of itself. Initially, 7 chips are placed at the root. After the root fires three times, the root has 1 chip, and each of its children has 3 chips. The children of the root will then each fire once, resulting in 3 chips at the root and 1 chip at each vertex in layer 3. Lastly, the root fires once, reaching a stable configuration with 1 chip on each vertex in layers 1 through 3.

Figure 1:
An example unlabeled chip-firing process on a binary tree starting with 7 chips at the root.

One important property of unlabeled chip-firing is a commutativity property known as
confluence
, which we formally state below.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 2.2.2,
[
12
]
)
.

Consider configuration
\mathcal{C}
and stable configuration
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. If
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
can be reached from
\mathcal{C}
after finitely many legal firings, then
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is the unique stable configuration that is obtained from
\mathcal{C}
.

1.2
Labeled-chip firing

In this paper, we study a variant of the above game titled
labeled chip-firing
, where the chips are numerically labeled
1,2,\ldots,N
, and a specific firing mechanism is used to determine the direction in which chips are fired. This variant of chip-firing was first studied by Hopkins, McConville, and Propp in
[
9
]
in the context of the one-dimensional lattice. We specifically focus on labeled chip-firing on undirected
k
-ary trees.

Here, we assume that the root vertex has a self-loop and every vertex has
k
children. In this setting, when a vertex
v
has at least
k+1
chips, it can fire, i.e., select a set of
k+1
chips on it and send one to each of its neighbors. The
\lceil\frac{k+1}{2}\rceil
th-smallest of the selected chips will be sent to the parent, and of the remaining chips, the
i
th smallest chip gets sent to the
i
th left child.

Example 1.3
.

Consider the undirected binary tree starting with
7
chips at the root. Figure
2
illustrates one possible procedure that stabilizes the tree. First, we fire chips
5,6,7
from the root. Then we fire
3,4,6
from the root. Then we fire
1,2
, and
4
. Afterwards, we fire
1,3,5
from the left child of the root, and we fire
4,6,7
from the right child of the root. Finally, we fire chips
2,3,6
from the root. We are left with one chip on each vertex in the first three layers of the tree. Since no vertex has at least
3
chips, we have reached a stable configuration.

Figure 2:
A labeled chip-firing process on a binary tree with 7 chips at the root. At each step, we bold the chips that get fired from the vertex.

One key property of labeled chip-firing on
k
-ary trees is that, unlike in unlabeled chip-firing, confluence does not always hold due to the distinguishable chips and firing mechanism; see Example
1.4
. Because of this, there are multiple possible stable configurations, even when we start with the same chips at the root of the tree. This lack of global confluence motivates research on labeled chip-firing.

Example 1.4
.

Figure
3
shows an example of confluence not holding for a binary tree starting with chips
1,2,\dots 2^{3}-1
. The figure shows two distinct stable configurations can be reached from the same initial configuration, demonstrating that confluence does not hold.

Figure 3:
Two possible labeled chip-firing processes starting from the same stable configuration and ending with different stable configurations. This demonstrates that confluence, as in Theorem
1.2
, does not always hold in the labeled chip-firing game on undirected
k
-ary trees.

1.3
Motivations and Roadmap

In
[
14
]
, Musiker and Nguyen study the labeled chip-firing game on the undirected binary tree starting with
2^{\ell}-1
chips at the root, i.e., the
k=2
case, and describe relations between chips that hold for all possible stable configurations. At the end of their paper
[
14
]
, Musiker and Nguyen ask the following:

•

What are the attainable stable configurations?

•

How many are there?

In
[
11
]
, Inagaki, Khovanova, and Luo partially answered the second question in the context of binary trees by proving an upper bound on the number of stable configurations.

In this paper, we answer the second question, but in the more general setting of
k
-ary trees starting with
\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}
chips at the root. We begin in Section 2 by providing formal notations used to describe vertices of
k
-ary trees and describing prior results about unlabeled and labeled chip-firing on binary trees. Next, in Section 3, we prove an upper bound on the number of stable configurations in the
k
-ary tree; we do this by generalizing the method used by Inagaki, Khovanova, and Luo
[
11
]
. In Section 4, we provide a lower bound on the number of stable configurations.

Preliminaries and Prior Results

2.1
Definitions and Notations

We begin with fundamental definitions about trees and then expand into chip-firing on trees.

A
tree
is an acyclic, undirected graph. A
rooted tree
is a tree with a designated vertex as the root. For a vertex
u
, the unique vertex
v
such that
u
and
v
are directly connected by an edge and
v
is closer to the root than
u
is the
parent
of
u
. Since multiple vertices can have the same parent, we denote the collection of vertices that have a vertex
v
as its parent as the
children
of
v
. An
infinite looped
k
-ary tree
is an infinite tree such that every non-root vertex has
k
children, and the root has a self-loop. This ensures that each vertex has degree
k+1
.

For the rest of this paper, assume all trees are infinite. Furthermore, assume that all
k
-ary trees have a self-loop at the root.

Next, we describe how we denote each vertex. We utilize a breadth-first index on the vertices. We begin by indexing the root as vertex
v_{0}
and for each
i
, we label the
j
th leftmost child of
v_{i}
as
v_{ki+j}
. We illustrate this labeling in Figure
4
.

Figure 4:
Vertex labelings for the first three layers of a 4-ary tree.

In this paper, we assume that our initial configuration of chips consists of labeled chips
1,2,\dots,\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}
on the root vertex
v_{0}
. We say that a vertex
v
of this
k
-ary tree can
fire
, i.e., choose
k+1
chips and distribute a chip to each child and parent as follows: send the
\lceil\frac{k+1}{2}\rceil
th-smallest of the selected chips to the parent and, from of the
k
remaining chips, the
i
th smallest chip gets sent to the
i
th leftmost child. A
stable configuration
is a configuration of chips where no vertex in the
k
-ary tree can fire.

We now introduce terminology used to describe relationships between vertices in our
k
-ary tree. We say that vertex
u
is a
straight left descendant
of vertex
v
if
u
can be reached by traveling only along the leftmost children starting at
v
. Similarly,
u
is a
straight right descendant
of vertex
v
if
u
can be reached by traveling only along the rightmost children starting at
v
.

2.2
Prior Results

The property of confluence tells us that whether a configuration stabilizes or not only depends on the initial chip configuration, not the order of firings. Next, we describe some known results about unlabeled chip-firing on trees. We begin with the number of chips on a vertex in each layer in the stable configuration of a
k
-ary tree.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
1
]
)
.

If we start with
N
chips at the root, where
\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}\leq N<\frac{k^{\ell+1}-1}{k-1}
for some integer
\ell
, then for
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
, the resulting stable configuration has
a_{i}+1
chips on each vertex on layer
i+1
, where
a_{\ell-1}\ldots a_{2}a_{1}a_{0}
is the base-
k
expansion of
N-\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}
.

The above theorem implies that if we start with exactly
\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}
chips at the root of a
k
-ary tree, then the stable configuration will have exactly 1 chip on each vertex in layers 1 through
\ell
. Because of this nice stable configuration, we choose to study stable configurations of
k
-ary trees starting with
\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}
labeled chips. We use
N_{k,\ell}
to denote

N_{k,\ell}:=\frac{k^{\ell}-1}{k-1}

for the sake of abbreviation. The above theorem is exemplified in Figure
1
, where each vertex in layers 1 through 3 ends with exactly 1 chip in the stable configuration.

We now provide definitions that we shall use to first state the results found in
[
14
]
about labeled chip-firing on binary trees and then extend them to the setting of undirected
k
-ary trees.

Definition 2.2
.

Consider a stable configuration of
\frac{k^{n}-1}{k-1}
chips on a
k
-ary tree. The
bottom straight left descendant
and
bottom straight right descendant
are the straight left and right descendants at the lowest layer such that they have at least 1 chip in the stable configuration, respectively. The
top straight ancestor
of a vertex

is the vertex closest to the root such that
v
is a straight left or right descendant of its top straight ancestor.

We now proceed with the relevant results in
[
14
]
.

Proposition 2.5

(
[
14
]
)
.

For any vertex whose top straight ancestor is its parent, if the vertex is a left child, then the chip on it is less than the chips on its parent and its right sibling. The opposite result for a right child also holds.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
14
]
)
.

The vertices that are parents of the bottom vertices contain the smallest and largest chips among the subtree of their straight ancestors, excluding the vertices on the bottom layer.

Lemma 2.7

(
[
14
]
)
.

Suppose we start with
2^{\ell}-1
labeled chips at the root of a binary tree. Then, in the stable configuration, chip 2 is located on the parent of the vertex containing chip 1, and chip
2^{\ell}-2
is on the parent of the vertex containing chip
2^{\ell}-1
.

Using the above properties, a bound was then constructed in
[
11
]
on the number of possible stable configurations for a binary tree. For labeled chip-firing on a
k
-ary tree, let
Z_{k,\ell}
denote the number of possible stable configurations with
N_{k,\ell}
chips at the root and let
T_{k,\ell}
denote the number of possible orderings of the chips within a subtree consisting of
\ell
layers in the stable configuration. We now state known bounds for
Z_{2,\ell}
and
T_{2,\ell}
. First, let
A_{\ell}
denote the
Euler zigzag numbers
, or the number of alternating permutations of the set
\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\}
. Further, we define
\beta_{\ell}
and
\gamma_{\ell}
as a shorthand for the following expressions:

\beta_{\ell}=A_{\ell}\binom{2^{\ell}-3}{\ell}\binom{2^{\ell}-\ell-3}{2^{\ell-1}-2,2^{\ell-2}-2,2^{\ell-3}-1,2^{\ell-4}-1,\ldots,1}

and

\gamma_{\ell}=A_{\ell}\binom{2^{\ell}-5}{\ell}\binom{2^{\ell}-\ell-5}{2^{\ell-1}-3,2^{\ell-2}-3,2^{\ell-3}-1,2^{\ell-4}-1,\ldots,1}.

We then have the following bound, known as the zigzag bound.

Theorem 2.8

(Theorem 3.6 of
[
11
]
)
.

If there are
N_{2,\ell}
labeled chips at the root of a binary tree with
\ell\geq 4
, then

T_{2,\ell}\leq 10^{2^{\ell-4}}\beta_{\ell}\prod_{i=4}^{\ell-1}\beta_{i}^{2^{\ell-1-i}}

and

Z_{2,\ell}\leq 10^{2^{\ell-4}}\gamma_{\ell}\prod_{i=4}^{\ell-1}\beta_{i}^{2^{\ell-1-i}}.

3
An Upper Bound on the Number of Stable Configurations

We now derive an upper bound on the number of stable configurations of chips on the undirected
k
-ary tree starting with
N_{k,\ell}
labeled chips at the root.

3.1
Generalizing the Endgame of Musiker and Nguyen

We now expand known results from
[
11
]
and
[
14
]
on binary trees to
k
-ary trees in general. We first begin by finding the set of firings at the end of the labeled chip-firing process where confluence holds on
k
-ary trees. We refer to this set as the
Endgame
. The Endgame on binary trees was used in
[
14
]
to prove Proposition
2.5
and Proposition
2.6
.

First, let
(i,j)
be the
j
th-to-last fire of vertex
v_{i}
, where the 0th-to-last fire of a vertex denotes the last time that vertex fires. Next, define the relation
(i_{1},j_{1})>(i_{2},j_{2})
if
(i_{1},j_{1})
must occur before
(i_{2},j_{2})
. This then creates a poset on the possible moves in a labeled chip-firing process. We now define the Endgame similarly to how it was done in
[
14
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

We call the set of moves
(i,j)
where
\mathrm{layer}(i)+j<\ell
the
Endgame
.

Next, we show that during the Endgame, there is a strict ordering between when the parent and its children fire; this is our generalization of Proposition 4.2 of
[
14
]
. This strict ordering will then allow us to prove that confluence holds on the endgame. Note that for a
k
-ary tree, if a non-root vertex has label
s
, its children have labels
ks+1,ks+2,\ldots,ks+k
and its parent has label
\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{k}\right\rfloor
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider a vertex
i
with
\mathrm{layer}(i)>1
. Then for all
j
such that
\mathrm{layer}(i)+j<\ell
, we have:

•

\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,j\right)<(i,j)<\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,j+1\right)

•

(i,j)
occurs when vertex
i
has
k+1
chips.

Proof.

We prove the above proposition by induction on both
\mathrm{layer}(v_{i})
and
j
. We do this by proving the following steps:

1.

(0,0)<(1,0),(2,0),\ldots,(k,0)
.

2.

If
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,0\right)<(i,0)
, then
(i,0)<({ki+1},0),({ki+2},0),\ldots,({ki+k},0)
.

3.

(ki+1,0),(ki+2,0),\ldots,(ki+k,0)<(i,1)
.

4.

If
(1,j-1),(2,j-1),\ldots,(k,j-1)<(0,j)
, then
(0,j)<(1,j),(2,j),\ldots,(k,j)
.

5.

If
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,j\right)<(i,j)
and
(ki+1,j-1),({ki+2},j-1),\ldots,({ki+k},j-1)<(i,j)
, then
(i,j)<({ki+1},j),({ki+2},j),\ldots,({ki+k},j)
.

6.

If
({ki+1},j-1),({ki+2},j-1),\ldots,({ki+k},j-1)<(i,j)
, then
(i,j)<\left({\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor},j+1\right)

When steps 1, 2, and 3 are proven, we have proven the base case. Proving steps 4, 5, and 6 proves the inductive step.

We now prove each step of the induction.

1.

Suppose
(0,0)
occurs before
(1,0)
. Then, after
(0,0)
, vertex 1 will have at least 1 chip. Further,
(1,0)
will then increase the number of chips at
(0,0)
by another chip, so it will have at least 2 chips at the end, a contradiction. Similarly, we know that
(0,0)<(2,0),\ldots,(k,0)
. Therefore,
(0,0)
must occur when vertex 0 has
k+1
chips, as it needs to end with 1 chip.

2.

Suppose
(i,0)
occurs before
(ki+1,0)
. Then after
(i,0)
occurs, vertex
i
will gain two chips from
(ki+1,0)
and
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,0\right)
, a contradiction. Similarly, we know that
(i,0)<(ki+a,0)
for all
a\in\{2,3,\dots,k\}
. Further, since vertex
i
must end with 1 chip,
(i,0)
must occur when vertex
i
has
k+1
chips.

3.

Suppose
(ki+1,0)
occurs before
(i,1)
. Then after
(ki+1,0)
, vertex
ki+1
will gain two chips from
(i,1)
and
(i,0)
, a contradiction. Therefore,
(ki+a,0)<(i,1)
for all
a\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
.

4.

Suppose
(0,j)
occurs before
(1,j)
. Then since
(2,j-1),\ldots,(k,j-1)<(0,j)
, after
(0,j)
, vertex 0 will keep at least 1 chip, lose
k(j-1)
, gain at least
j
chips from vertex 1, and gain at least
(k-1)(j-1)
chips from vertices
2,3,\ldots,k
. Then vertex 0 will have at least 2 chips, a contradiction. Similarly, we know that
(0,j)<(a,j)
for all
a\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. Therefore, after
(0,j)
, vertex 0 will lose
k(j-1)
chips and gain
k(j-1)
, meaning
(0,j)
must occur when vertex 0 has
k+1
chips.

5.

Suppose
(i,j)
occurs before
(ki+1,j)
. Then after
(i,j)
vertex
i
will lose
(k+1)(j-1)
chips. Further, since
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,j\right)<(i,j)
, vertex
i
will receive at least
j
chips from vertex
\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor
,
j
chips from vertex
ki+1
, and
(k-1)(j-1)
chips from vertices
ki+2,\ldots,ki+k
. Then vertex
i
will have at least 2 chips at the end, a contradiction. Further, since
(i,j)<(ki+1,j),\ldots,(ki+k,j)
, after
(i,j)
, vertex
i
will lose
(k+1)(j-1)
chips and gain
j+k(j-1)=(k+1)j-k
chips, so
(i,j)
must occur when vertex
i
has
k+1
chips.

6.

Suppose
(i,j)
occurs before
\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,j+1\right)
. Then after
(i,j)
, vertex
i
will lose
(k+1)(j-1)
more chips. However, since
(ki+a,j-1)<(i,j)
for all
a\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
, vertex
i
will gain at least
k(j-1)
chips from vertices
ki+1,\ldots,ki+k
and also gain
j+1
chips from vertex
\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor
. Therefore, vertex
i
receives at least
(k+1)j-1
chips, leaving vertex
i
with at least 2 chips at the end, a contradiction. Therefore,
(i,j)<\left(\left\lfloor\frac{i-1}{k}\right\rfloor,j+1\right).

∎

Next, we show that confluence holds on the endgame of a labeled chip-firing process on a
k
-ary tree; this is our generalization of Corollary 4.3 of
[
14
]
.

Corollary 3.3

(Endgame Confluence)
.

For a looped
k
-ary tree with
N_{k,\ell}
chips at the root, once
(0,\ell-2)
has occurred, the order in which vertices fire does not affect the stable configuration.

Proof.

During the endgame, a vertex fires for the
j
th to last time before its parent fires for the
j
th to last time but after its parent fires for the
(j+1)
th to last time. Therefore, if a vertex can fire, none of its children or parents can fire. Further, since a vertex can only fire when it has exactly
k+1
chips, the firing cannot be affected by any of its neighbors. Therefore, the order does not affect the stable configuration.
∎

For example, Figure
6
is a case of the endgame on a 4-ary tree. Notice that there is only 1 possible stable configuration, as confluence holds on the endgame. Furthermore, since confluence holds on the endgame, we now consider a specific order of firings. First, notice that if we have
N_{k,\ell}
chips at the root, then the endgame starts when the root has
k+1
chips, each vertex on layers 2 to
\ell-1
has
k
chips, and each vertex on layer
\ell
or lower has 0 chips.

Figure 6:
A labeled chip-firing process on a 4-ary tree, starting from the endgame and ending at the stable configuration.

Next, we assume that in the endgame, we fire in the following process: we first fire each vertex from 0 to
N_{k,\ell-1}-1
exactly once, then we fire each vertex from 0 to
N_{k,\ell-2}-1
exactly once and we continue so on and so forth until we fire just vertex
N_{k,\ell-(\ell-1)}-1=0
, the root, exactly once.

Notice that in every step, we fire every vertex that can fire once. Denote each of these steps as a
wave
, shown in Figure
7
. We now use this specific firing during the endgame to prove various properties of a stable configuration and later extend the zigzag bound to
k
-ary trees in general.

Figure 7:
The first wave of the endgame for a labeled chip-firing process on a binary tree starting with 7 chips at the root.

Proposition 3.4
.

In labeled chip-firing on an infinite
k
-ary tree with
N_{k,\ell}
labeled chips at the root, the bottom left and right descendants of a vertex contain the smallest and largest chips among its subtree.

Proof.

We only consider the case for the bottom straight left descendant, as the bottom straight right descendant case is analogous. Further, if a vertex
v_{i}
was the leftmost child of its parent, we can instead prove the statement for its parent. Therefore, we only consider the cases where
v_{i}
is not the leftmost child.

First, consider the smallest chip that
v_{i}
contains throughout the firing process. Call this chip
c
. Since
c
is the smallest chip in the subtree rooted at
v_{i}
, whenever it is fired, it will always fire to the leftmost child. Therefore,
c
will always stay among
v_{i}
and its straight left descendants.

Next, from the first move of vertex
v_{i}
in the endgame,
(i,\ell-1-\text{layer}(i))
, vertex
v_{i}
will alternate between sending a chip to its parent and receiving a chip from its parent. Since
v_{i}
isn’t the leftmost child, the chip it receives will be larger than or equal to the chip it sends back, meaning
c
cannot reach
v_{i}
for the first time after
(i,\ell-1-\text{layer}(i))
. This means that
c
is in the subtree rooted by
v_{i}
during the first wave. Since
c
can only be fired to the left, this then means that
c
will reach the end of the first wave, becoming the bottom straight left descendant of
v_{i}
.
∎

This proposition leads us to the following “naive” bound on the number of stable configurations on the undirected
k
-ary tree when starting with
N_{k,\ell}
chips.

Proposition 3.5

(Naive Bound)
.

For
\ell>2
, we have
Z_{k,\ell}
and
T_{k,\ell}\leq(N_{k,\ell}-2)!
.

Proof.

For any undirected
k
-ary tree or any subtree of one, with
N_{k,\ell}
labeled chips, Proposition
3.4
guarantees the locations of the smallest and largest of those chips. Thus, ordering the remaining chips, there are at most
(N_{k,\ell}-2)!
possible stable configurations.
∎

Z_{2,\ell}\leq(N_{2,\ell}-5)!
. This is because, in the context of the stable configuration of the binary tree with
2^{\ell}-1

3.2
The Zigzag Bound

Using properties resulting from the generalization of the Endgame, we now derive a nontrivial upper bound on the number of possible stable configurations, which we call the
zigzag bound
.

Definition 3.6
.

A
zigzag
of length
\ell
is a sequence of vertices
v_{a_{1}},v_{a_{2}},\dots,v_{a_{\ell}}
defined as follows. Establish a starting vertex
v_{a_{1}}
. Then the other vertices are defined as follows. Let
a_{i+1}=ka_{i}+k
if
a_{i}\equiv r\pmod{k}
for
r\in\{1,2,\dots,\lfloor k/2\rfloor\}
, and we let
a_{i+1}=ka_{i}+1
if
a_{1}\equiv r\pmod{k}
for some
r\in\{\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1,\lfloor k/2\rfloor+2,\dots,k\}.

.

3
-ary tree as shown in Figure

In our proof of the zigzag bound for the
k
-ary tree starting with
N_{k,\ell}
chips at the root, we first construct a zigzag of
\ell+1
vertices starting at the root vertex. Then, we notice that for each vertex
v
in the zigzag except for the last one, there are
k-1
children of
v
that are not in the zigzag. We therefore observe that the zigzag splits the tree into
k-1
subtrees, each rooted at the child of the root, with
N_{k,\ell-1}
chips,
k-1
subtrees each rooted at the child of the second vertex, with
N_{k,\ell-2}
chips, and so on.

These zigzags have the following key property:

Proposition 3.10
.

For any vertex whose top straight ancestor is its parent, let its parent have label
v
. Further, let
c_{i}
denote the chip at vertex
v_{i}
in the stable configuration. If the vertex is a left child, then we have the following relation

c_{ks+1}<c_{ks+2}<\ldots<c_{ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor}<\min\{c_{s},c_{ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1},c_{ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor+2},\ldots,c_{ks+k}\}.

If the vertex is a right child, we have

c_{ks+k}>c_{ks+k-1}>\ldots>c_{ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor+1}>\max\{c_{s},c_{ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor},c_{ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1},\ldots,c_{ks+1}\}.

Proof.

We prove the case for which the vertex is a left child, and the opposite case follows analogously.

When vertex
v_{s}
fires for the last time, the chip that vertex
ks+1
receives is less than the chips of its
k-1
siblings. Further, since vertex
v_{s}
is a right child, the chip it receives from vertex
\left\lfloor\frac{m-1}{k}\right\rfloor
, i.e. the parent of vertex
v_{s}
, cannot be smaller than the chip vertex
v_{s}
sent to vertex
ks+1
, so it also cannot be smaller than the chip at vertex
ks+1
.

Further, this also holds for vertices
ks+2,ks+3,\ldots,ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor
, as when vertex
v_{s}
fires, the chip sent to vertex
\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{k}\right\rfloor
is greater than the chips sent to vertices
ks+2,ks+3,\ldots,ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor
. Since vertex
\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{k}\right\rfloor
cannot send back a smaller chip, the chips at vertices
ks+2,ks+3,\ldots,ks+\lfloor k/2\rfloor
must then be smaller than the chip at vertex
v_{s}
.
∎

Proposition 3.12
.

The vertices that are parents of the bottom vertices contain the smallest and largest chips among the subtree of their straight ancestors, excluding the vertices on the bottom layer.

Proof.

Again, consider a vertex
v_{i}
that is the right child of its parent, and let
c
be the second smallest chip sent to its left branch,
v_{ki}
. Inside
v_{ki}
,
c
is fired to the left except when it is fired with the smallest chip, where it is sent up to its parent. This then ensures that
c
always remains among
v_{i}
and its straight left descendants. By a similar argument as seen in the previous proof,
c
is already in
v_{ki}
in the second wave, meaning
c
will go to the parent of the bottom straight left descendant.
∎

We now establish Lemma
3.10
, the key lemma to provide an upper bound on
Z_{k,\ell}
and
T_{k,\ell}
.

Lemma 3.13
.

For a
k
-ary tree with
\ell\geq 3
, we have the following upper bound for
Z_{k,\ell}
and
T_{k,\ell}
:

\binom{N_{k,\ell}-2}{\ell}\frac{(N_{k,\ell}-\ell-2)!}{(N_{k,\ell-1}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-1})!^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-2}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-2})!^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-3})!^{k-1}(N_{k,\ell-4})!^{k-1}\ldots(N_{k,1})!^{k-1}}A_{\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}T^{k-1}_{k,i}.

Proof.

Consider a subtree with
N_{k,\ell}
labeled chips at the root and a zigzag alternating between leftmost and rightmost children starting from the root. Specifically, consider a zigzag of length
\ell
rooted at the subtree. We begin by choosing the possible chips on the zigzag. Since
\ell\geq 3
, we know that the smallest and largest chips cannot lie on the zigzag. Therefore, we can choose the chips in the zigzag in
\binom{N_{k,\ell}-2}{\ell}
different ways. Further, let
A_{\ell}
denote the number of alternating permutations of the set
\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\}
. Then by Proposition
3.10
, there are
A_{\ell}
ways to order the chips in the zigzag, meaning there are a total of
\binom{N_{k,\ell}-2}{\ell}A_{\ell}
ways to select the chips on the zigzag.

Next, we consider the number of ways to order the chips on the vertices, not on the zigzag. Observe that the zigzag splits the initial subtree into
k-1
subtrees of
\ell-1
layers,
k-1
subtrees of
\ell-2
layers,
\ldots
, and
k-1
subtrees of
1
layer. Further, the smallest chip must end up in the leftmost subtree, and the largest chip must end up in the rightmost subtree. Therefore, instead of choosing
N_{k,\ell-2}
and
N_{k,\ell-1}
chips for those subtrees, respectively, we only need to pick
N_{k,\ell-2}-1
and
N_{k,\ell-1}-1
chips. Therefore, there are

\frac{(N_{k,\ell}-\ell-2)!}{(N_{k,\ell-1}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-1})!^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-2}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-2})!^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-3})!^{k-1}(N_{k,\ell-4})!^{k-1}\ldots(N_{k,1})!^{k-1}}

ways to split the remaining chips into the subtrees. Lastly, there are
T_{k,i}
ways to order the chips in a subtree with
i
layers, so there are
\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}T^{k-1}_{k,i}
total ways to order the chips within each subtree. Multiplying all of the terms together then gives the zigzag bound.
∎

Next, we use Proposition
3.13
to provide a closed form bound on
Z_{k,\ell}
and
T_{k,\ell}
. First, we define the following expression to collapse our recursive bound into a direct bound:

\beta_{k,\ell}=\binom{N_{k,\ell}-2}{\ell}\frac{(N_{k,\ell}-\ell-2)!}{(N_{k,\ell-1}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-1})!^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-2}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-2})!^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-3})!^{k-1}(N_{k,\ell-4})!^{k-1}\ldots(N_{k,1})!^{k-1}}A_{\ell}.

We also establish bounds on
T_{k,1},Z_{k,1},T_{k,2},Z_{k,2}
, which we shall use to establish our recursive bound:

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
k\geq 2
. Then,

Z_{k,1}=T_{k,1}=Z_{k,2}=1
and
T_{k,2}\leq k-1
.

Proof.

Recall that
Z_{k,1},T_{k,1}
are the numbers of configurations/placements of chips of the
k
-ary tree and subtree of the
k
-ary tree containing
N_{k,1}=1
labeled chips. There is only one way of placing them.

We find that
Z_{k,2}=1
. To see this, first note that
Z_{k,2}
is the number of stable configurations of
N_{k,2}=\frac{k^{2}-1}{k-1}=k+1
chips. By the rules of firing, we obtain that the median of those
k+1
chips will remain at the root, and all other chips will be on the children of the root and in increasing order from left to right.

We now bound
T_{k,2}
. Let
v
be the root of the vertex that is
v
. We obtain that the chips in the last layer of the subtree are so that 1) the smallest and largest chips in that subtree are always in the leftmost and rightmost child of the root, by Proposition
3.4
. Because of this, we find that there are at most
\frac{k^{2}-1}{k-1}-2=k-1
potential chips that can be at the root of the subtree of 2 layers. Then note that the leftmost vertex in that layer has the smallest chip in that layer, the second leftmost vertex in that layer has the second smallest chip in that layer, and so on. To see this, one can consider the endgame described by Theorem
3.2
; here we know that the chips in the bottommost layer of the subtree were dispersed by the last firing of the root of the subtree. Therefore, we find that the number of stable configurations of the subtree with 2 layers of chips is at most the number of options for chips that can end up at the root. Therefore
T_{k,2}\leq k-1
.
∎

Finally, we state and prove an expression for the upper bound on
Z_{k,\ell}
and
T_{k,\ell}
, the number of configurations.

Theorem 3.15

(Zigzag Bound)
.

For a
k
-ary tree, we have the following upper bound for
Z_{k,\ell}
and
T_{k,\ell}
when
\ell\geq 3
:

T_{k,\ell}\leq(k-1)^{(k-1)k^{\ell-3}}\beta_{k,\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-3}\beta_{k,\ell-i}^{(k-1)k^{i-1}}.

and

Z_{k,\ell}\leq(k-1)^{(k-1)k^{\ell-3}}\beta_{k,\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-3}\beta_{k,\ell-i}^{(k-1)k^{i-1}}.

Proof.

We first observe that
Z_{k,\ell}\leq T_{k,\ell}
. This is because any stable configuration of
N_{k,\ell}
chips on a
k
-ary tree, which is a subtree of itself consisting of
\ell
layers of chips in the stable configuration.

We prove this by strong induction. First, by Proposition
3.13
, we know that

T_{k,3}\leq\binom{N_{k,3}-2}{3}\frac{(N_{k,3}-5)!}{(N_{k,2}-1)!(N_{k,2})!^{k-2}(N_{k,1}-1)!(N_{k,1})!^{k-2}}A_{3}(k-1)^{(k-1)k^{3-3}}=\beta_{k,3}(k-1)^{k-1}\prod_{i=1}^{0}\beta_{k,3-i}^{(k-1)k^{i-1}}.

Next, for the inductive step, we assume that for all
j\in[3,\ell]
, we have

T_{k,j}\leq(k-1)^{(k-1)k^{j-3}}\beta_{k,j}\prod_{i=1}^{j-3}\beta_{k,j-i}^{(k-1)k^{i-1}}.

Next, by Proposition
3.13
, we then know that

\displaystyle T_{k,\ell+1}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}T^{k-1}_{k,j}=(k-1)^{k-1}\beta_{k,\ell}\prod_{j=3}^{\ell}T^{k-1}_{k,j}\leq(k-1)^{k-1}\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{j=3}^{\ell}\beta^{k-1}_{k,j}(k-1)^{(k-1)^{2}k^{j-3}}\prod_{i=1}^{j-3}\beta_{k,j-i}^{(k-1)^{2}k^{i-1}}

\displaystyle=(k-1)^{k-1+(k-1)^{2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-3}k^{j^{\prime}}}\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{m=3}^{\ell}\beta_{k,m}^{k-1+\sum_{j=m+1}^{\ell}(k-1)^{2}k^{j-m-1}}

\displaystyle=(k-1)^{k-1+(k-1)^{2}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{\ell-3}k^{j^{\prime}}}\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{m=3}^{\ell}\beta_{k,m}^{k-1+\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-m-1}(k-1)^{2}k^{j}}

\displaystyle=(k-1)^{k-1+(k-1)(k^{\ell-2}-1)}\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{m=3}^{\ell}\beta_{k,m}^{k-1+(k-1)(k^{\ell-m-1}-1)}

\displaystyle=\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{m=3}^{\ell-1}\beta_{k,m}^{(k-1)k^{\ell-m}}

\displaystyle=(k-1)^{(k-1)k^{\ell-2}}\beta_{k,\ell+1}\prod_{m=1}^{\ell-2}\beta_{k,\ell+1-m}^{(k-1)k^{m-1}}

∎

For fixed
k
, we define the following sequence signifying the zigzag bound:

T_{k,zig}(\ell)=Z_{k,zig}(\ell)=(k-1)^{(k-1)k^{\ell-3}}\beta_{k,\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-3}\beta_{k,\ell-i}^{(k-1)k^{i-1}}.

Like Theorem 3.12 of
[
11
]
, we now prove that the zigzag bound is asymptotically better than that from Proposition
3.5
.

Theorem 3.16
.

For
\ell\geq 4
and
k\geq 2
,
T_{k,zig}(\ell)<(N_{k,\ell}-3)!
.

Proof.

We do a proof by induction. First observe that for
\ell=4
,

\displaystyle Z_{k,zig}(4)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(N_{k,4}-2)!}{4!(N_{k,3}-1)!((N_{k,3})!)^{k-2}(N_{k,2}-1)!(N_{k,2}!)^{k-2}}A_{4}\right)\left(\frac{(N_{k,3}-2)!}{3!(N_{k,2}-1)!(N_{k,2}!)^{k-2}}A_{3}\right)^{k-1}(k-1)^{(k-1)k}

\displaystyle\ <\

\displaystyle\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k^{2}+k)!(k^{2}+k+1)!^{k-2}(k!)(k+1)!^{k-2}}\left(\frac{(k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k!)(k+1)!^{k-2}}\right)^{k-1}(k-1)^{(k-1)k}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)!(k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k^{2}+k)!(k!)^{k}((k+1)!)^{k^{2}-2k}}\left(\frac{(k-1)^{k(k-1)}}{((k^{2}+k+1)(k^{2}+k))^{k-2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ <\

\displaystyle\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)!(k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k^{2}+k)!(k!)^{k}((k+1)!)^{k^{2}-2k}}\left(\frac{(k-1)^{k(k-1)}}{k^{4(k-2)}}\right)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k^{2}+k)(k!)^{k}((k+1)!)^{k^{2}-2k}}\cdot\frac{(k-1)^{k(k-1)}}{k^{4k-8}}

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k^{2}+k)(k!)^{2}}\left(\frac{(k-1)^{k(k-1)}}{k^{4k-8}k^{2k^{2}-4k}}\right)\leq\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)!}{(k^{2}+k)k^{2}}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-1)}{k^{2}(k^{2}+k)}\right)(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-2)!

\displaystyle\ <\

\displaystyle\frac{2}{k}(k^{3}+k^{2}+k-2)!=(N_{k,4}-3)!

Here we used that for any
\ell\geq 3
,
A_{\ell}<\ell!
. This proves the base case of the induction.

Now we prove the inductive step. Observe that by definition,
A_{\ell}<\ell!
. We derive that

\begin{split}&Z_{k,zig}(\ell+1)\\
&=(k-1)^{k(k-1)}\binom{N_{k,\ell+1}-2}{\ell+1}\frac{A_{\ell+1}\cdot(N_{k,\ell+1}-\ell-3)!\prod_{j=3}^{\ell}(T_{k,zig}(\ell))^{k-1}}{(N_{k,\ell}-1)!(N_{k,\ell}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-1})!(N_{k,\ell-1}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-2})!^{k-1}(N_{k,\ell-3}!)^{k-1}\dots(N_{k,1})!^{k-1}}\\
&<\frac{(N_{k,\ell+1}-2)!(N_{k,3}-2)!}{(N_{k,\ell}-1)!(N_{k,\ell}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-1}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-1}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-2}!)^{k-1}(N_{k,\ell-3}!)^{k-1}\dots(N_{k,1}!)^{k-1}}\left(\prod_{j=4}^{\ell}(N_{k,j}-3)!\right)^{k-1}\end{split}.

Here, we use the inductive hypothesis in the last step along with the fact that
T_{k,zig}(3)=\beta_{k,3}=A_{3}\binom{N_{k,3}-2}{3}\frac{1}{((N_{k,2}-1)!)(N_{k,2}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,1}-1)!(N_{k,1}!)^{k-2}}(k-1)^{k-1}<(N_{k,3}-2)!
.

From there, we obtain

\begin{split}&Z_{k,zig}(\ell+1)\\
&<\frac{(N_{k,\ell+1}-2)!((N_{k,3}-2)!)^{k-1}(k-1)^{k-1}}{(N_{k,\ell}-1)!(N_{k,\ell}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-1}-1)!(N_{k,\ell-1}!)^{k-2}(N_{k,\ell-2}!)^{k-1}(N_{k,\ell-3}!)^{k-1}\dots(N_{k,1}!)^{k-1}}\left(\prod_{j=4}^{\ell}(N_{k,j}-3)!\right)^{k-1}\\
&\leq\frac{(N_{k,\ell+1}-2)!}{(N_{k,\ell}-1)!(N_{k,\ell}!)^{k-2}}(k-1)^{k-1}\\
&\leq\frac{(N_{k,\ell+1}-2)!}{k^{k^{\ell-1}+k^{2}-k}}(k-1)^{k-1}\\
&\leq(N_{k,\ell+1}-3)!\end{split}.

with the penultimate inequality being a result of the fact that
\ell\geq 4
and
k\geq 2
.
∎

4
Lower Bounds on the Number of Stable Configurations

Finally, we provide lower bounds for
Z_{k,\ell}
through direct constructions.

For this, we introduce some notations. Given
N_{k,\ell}
chips at the root of a
k
-ary tree, define a
stationary chip
as a designated chip that does not leave the root during the stabilization. We define a
uneasy chip
to be any chip
c
that 1) goes to one of the left children of the root and is larger than the stationary chip or 2) goes to one of the right children of the root and is smaller than the stationary chip. We say that a firing procedure fires
symmetrically
if every firing of the root involves the stationary chip, the stationary chip remains at the root after each firing, and every chip fired to the root is sent back to the child of the root from which it came.

Example 4.1
.

Consider starting with chips
1,2,\dots,7
in an undirected binary tree as in Figure
13
. As in the figure, we fire first the triple
\{5,6,7\}
, then
\{1,3,6\}
, and afterwards
\{2,3,4\}
from the root. Then we fire
1,2,5
from the left child of the root, and we fire
4,6,7
from the right child of the root. Finally, we fire
2,3,6
from the root.

In this stabilization, we say that the chip
3
is the stationary chip, since it stays at the root vertex. We say that chip
5
is an uneasy chip, since it is bigger than the stationary chip, chip 3, and is sent to the subtree rooted at the left child of the root.

A visualization of
(Z_{2,3})^{2}
possible stable configurations with
m-1=3
as the stationary chip and 5 as the uneasy chip on a binary tree with three layers in the stable configuration.

Lemma 4.2
.

For positive integers
k\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 3
, we have the following lower bound for

5
Further Directions

In this section, we discuss conjectures and further questions related to the properties and numbers of stable configurations of labeled chips in the
k
-ary tree.

5.1
Ballot Property

In
[
11
]
, Inagaki, Khovanova, and Luo conjectured that stable configurations of
2^{\ell}-1
labeled chips on a binary tree exhibit the
ballot property
: for every vertex
v
and integer
i
, the
i
th smallest chip to the left of
v
is less than the
i
th smallest chip to the right of
v
. This resembles ballot sequences counted by Catalan numbers
[
16
]
. Assuming this property, the authors established a bound on the number of stable configurations. They confirmed it for
\ell=3
and
\ell=4
, but the validity for higher
\ell
remains uncertain.

In light of this, we conjecture the following generalized ballot property:

Conjecture 5.1
.

Consider any stable configuration resulting from
N_{k,\ell}
labeled chips starting at the root of the
k
-ary tree. Then for each vertex
v
,
i\in\{1,2,\dots,N_{k,\ell-1}\}
and
a,b\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
such that
a<b
, the
i
th smallest chip that is in the subtree rooted at the
a
th leftmost child of
v
is less than the
i
th smallest chip in the subtree rooted at the
b
th leftmost child of
v
.

5.2
The Stable Configurations as a Permutation of
1,2,\dots,N_{k,\ell}

As noted in Musiker and Nguyen, one can view the stable configuration of
N_{k,\ell}
chips at the root as a stable configuration. More specifically, they considered listing all of the chips in the stable configuration of the binary tree from left to right. Now, consider doing this for
k
-ary trees; for odd
k
and for each vertex
v
of the tree, we say that the
\lceil\frac{k}{2}\rceil
nd leftmost child of
v
is to the right of the
v
and if vertex
v
is in a subtree whose root is left of that of a subtree containing
u
then
v
is left of
u
. This is better explained via illustration. We call this permutation the flattened configuration.

Example 5.2
.

Consider the stable configuration of chips in Figure
6
. We read it as the permutation
1,2,3,4,9,5,6,7,8,10,12,14,15,16,17,13,18,19,20,21
of
1,2,\dots,21.

For
k=2
and
n=4
, Patrick Liscio lists out all configurations as permutations derived from the flattened configuration with the number of inversions, i.e., the number of pairs of indices
i,j
such that both
i<j
and the
i
th term of the flattened configuration is greater than the
j
th. Viewing the configurations as permutations, we ask the following questions:

•

What is the maximum number of inversions that can appear in a flattened configuration of
N_{k,\ell}
chips?

•

What permutations and patterns of the flattened configuration are avoided or can show up?

In
[
10
]
, these questions were addressed in the context of labeled chip firing on
directed

k
-ary trees.

For configurations of
2^{3}-1
chips, the maximum number of inversions is
3
. According to
[
13
]
, 25 is the largest number of inversions that appear in a stable configuration of
2^{4}-1
labeled chips on a binary tree. For
n\geq 5
, we do not know the largest possible number of inversions in a stable configuration of
2^{\ell}-1
partly because it is computationally hard to find all of the stable configurations
[
14
]
. Thus, even calculating values for the maximum number of inversions that can appear in the flattened configuration of chips
2^{5}-1
,
2^{6}-1
, and
2^{7}-1
on only binary trees is non-trivial.

For address the first question, one can almost immediately observe that the maximum number of inversions that can appear is less than
\frac{N_{k,\ell}(N_{k,\ell}-1)}{2}
. This is because the permutation
N_{k,\ell},N_{k,\ell}-1,\dots,1
cannot be a flattened configuration of any stable configuration; indeed, chip
1
must be the straight left bottom descendant of the root and chip
N_{k,\ell}
must be the straight right bottom descendant of the root by Proposition
3.4
.

To address the second question, we believe that relations like Theorem
3.10
, which show relative positions of chips based on numerical order, can reveal patterns. Given the limited literature on viewing undirected
k
-ary trees as permutations, Musiker and Nguyen’s results (e.g., Proposition
2.5
) may be a good starting point for exploration.
