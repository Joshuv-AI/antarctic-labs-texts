Incidental identification of neonatal babesiosis: a case report
Authors: Samiksha Tarun, Ian A. Mellis, Abdullah Kilic, Daniel A. Green, Makoto Tokiwa, Gregory J. Berry, Fann Wu, Shivang S. Shah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvaluation of suspected neonatal sepsis rarely considers diagnostic workup for vector-borne illnesses, as these are generally infrequent etiologies in the febrile neonate. Babesiosis -- a zoonosis caused by apicomplexan parasites of the genus Babesia and spread to humans by the Ixodes scapularis tick -- can either be clinically silent, or symptomatic with fever, constitutional symptoms, as well as anemia and thrombocytopenia. We report here a rare case of neonatal babesiosis that was incidentally identified during routine workup for neonatal sepsis.Case presentationA full-term male neonate with fever was admitted to the hospital for sepsis evaluation. On routine complete blood count with manual differential blood smear, parasites were incidentally detected, later identified as Babesia microti. On review of maternal history, there was antenatal history of unexplained thrombocytopenia and anemia, and post-hoc review of peripartum maternal blood smear showed rare intra-erythrocytic parasites, which were confirmed as B. microti by PCR testing of maternal blood. Ultimately, the infant was successfully treated with azithromycin and atovaquone, received a red blood cell transfusion for symptomatic anemia, and remained well at his outpatient follow-up visit.ConclusionThis unusual case highlights the importance of including neonatal babesiosis in the differential diagnosis for neonatal sepsis in endemic regions, including careful review of maternal antenatal exposure history and labs, and consideration of peripheral blood smear in suggestive cases.

Background

Fever in the neonate is a medical emergency due to the risk of neonatal sepsis and its associated morbidity and mortality. Here we describe a febrile neonate with congenital babesiosis, suspected to occur via vertical transmission. Although rare cases of vertically transmitted babesiosis have been reported [1–8], it continues to be an underappreciated pathogen in the neonatal setting. Babesiosis should be on the differential diagnosis for general pediatricians caring for febrile neonates in endemic regions even without known tick bites, particularly when the mother or infant has unexplained anemia and/or thrombocytopenia.

Case presentation

A 26-day old full-term male infant with unremarkable perinatal history presented to the hospital with fever. On the day prior to presentation, the patient’s mother noted that he was fussy and felt warm to touch, later measuring fever to 100.7 °F, prompting presentation to the emergency department for evaluation.

Maternal pregnancy history was uncomplicated. She did not have any flu-like symptoms or any known fevers at any time during the pregnancy. Her pre-natal labs were largely unremarkable. She was found to be colonized with group B Streptococcus and received appropriate peri-partum antibiotics. At the time of parturition, she was diagnosed with chorioamnionitis and had prolonged rupture of membranes. She was also found to have thrombocytopenia (platelet count 69 × 103/uL, reference range 167–374 × 103/uL) and anemia (hemoglobin 8.2 g/dL, reference range 11.2–14.7 g/dL) on the last day of pregnancy. She did not receive any blood transfusions before, during, or after the pregnancy.

The infant lived at home in New York City in northern Manhattan, along with his mother, father, and an elder brother, and the infant himself had no relevant travel or exposure history. The family reported no history of international travel for anyone in the household. Maternal travel history during pregnancy included several short trips to suburban areas of the Northeast, including Connecticut, Westchester County in New York, and northern New Jersey, with trips spread across all three trimesters of the pregnancy. The patient’s mother and family deny any history of any known tick bites.

At presentation, the patient was febrile (T 39.3 °C), with mostly normal age-appropriate vital signs (heart rate 170 beats per minute; respiratory rate 44 breaths per minute; blood pressure 107/68 mmHg [fussy when obtained]; oxygen saturation 95–100% on room air). Physical examination was largely non-focal, without pallor, icterus/jaundice, or hepatosplenomegaly. Initial labs showed mild leukopenia (white blood cells 6.3 × 103 per µL, reference range 7.8–15.91 per µL, thrombocytopenia (platelets 95 × 103 per µL, reference range 248–586 × 103 per µL), and elevated C-reactive protein 39 mg/L (reference range 0–10 mg/L). Lumbar puncture was performed, with cerebrospinal fluid exhibiting benign profile without pleocytosis. Blood culture was also obtained and ultimately found to be sterile. Patient was started on ampicillin and ceftazidime as empiric coverage for typical bacterial pathogens of neonatal sepsis (e.g., group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli) as per American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines [9], and were continued until blood and CSF cultures showed no growth at 48 h.

Complete blood count was also obtained, with manual differential via peripheral smear review incidentally identifying intraerythrocytic ring forms (Fig. 1). Reflex testing for malarial antigens was negative, and parasite load was formally quantified at 5% parasitemia. A presumptive diagnosis of babesiosis was rendered, and Babesia PCR confirmed that the neonate was positive for B. microti.

Post-hoc evaluation of a peripartum peripheral blood smear from the patient’s mother revealed that she had previously undetected, very low (< 1% estimated) parasitemia (Fig. 2) at the time of her concurrent thrombocytopenia and anemia. Subsequent PCR testing, completed at the time of patient’s admission, showed that the patient’s mother was also PCR-positive for Babesia microti.

The infant was diagnosed with neonatal babesiosis. We suspect that the patient was infected with B. microti through vertical transmission given that his mother had antenatal anemia and thrombocytopenia, coupled with perinatal maternal B. microti parasitemia, and because the neonate had low risk epidemiologic profile for post-natal acquisition.

The patient was started on intravenous azithromycin and oral atovaquone [10–13]. He responded well to antimicrobial therapy, rapidly defervescing and clearing parasitemia by day 6 of therapy. He was treated for a total of 10 days, until 2 consecutive blood smears over 48 h were parasite-free. He had symptomatic anemia during hospitalization, requiring one red blood cell transfusion, with both anemia and thrombocytopenia promptly and stably normalizing over time (Fig. 3).

The infant was discharged from the hospital and seen as a follow-up in clinic about 10 days after hospital discharge, where he was noted to be doing well clinically, without fever or fussiness, and with good oral intake. Repeat blood smear at that time was negative for parasites.

Discussion

Clinical symptoms of babesiosis in neonates are variable, ranging from asymptomatic to mild, non-specific symptoms (e.g., fever, irritability, poor feeding) to sepsis-like syndrome [1–8, 12]. Common laboratory abnormalities include anemia, thrombocytopenia, and evidence of hemolysis (e.g., elevated LDH, ALT, bilirubin) [12]. The etiologic agent of babesiosis is the apicomplexan parasite Babesia, an intraerythrocytic protozoan which triggers waves of hemolysis responsible for the clinical symptoms of the disease [12, 14, 15]. In the United States, most cases are caused by Babesia microti, as in this case [12, 15]. Babesia duncani and Babesia divergens can also cause babesiosis albeit less frequently [12]. Babesia species complete their life cycle within two main hosts: (1) rodents, especially the white-footed mouse Peromyscus leucopus; and (2) the Ixodes tick [12, 15] which serves as definitive host. Humans are typically considered dead-end hosts [15] although human to human transmission is well recognized through blood transfusions [15, 16].

Babesiosis in neonates has been previously reported, albeit rarely [1–8]. There have been prior transfusion-associated cases of neonatal babesiosis [17–19], primarily in the northeastern United States, with most cases responsive to standard multi-drug regimens, but more severe cases in premature infants occasionally requiring exchange transfusion. Probable congenital babesiosis (transplacental or perinatal acquisition) has been less frequently reported [3, 7, 8] with most cases arising from the northeastern USA. These cases, like ours, involved presentation within the first month of life, with fever, lethargy or irritability, poor feeding, and pallor/anemia, variable parasitemia (5–15%), and patients were successfully treated with standard multi-drug regimens with some patients requiring blood transfusion.

In viewing this case in the context of prior reports, noteworthy aspects here include mother’s antepartum laboratory abnormalities and her rather benign antepartum exposure history, both of which could be easily overlooked by clinicians. Regarding the former, maternal thrombocytopenia here could be more easily ascribed to the more common pre-eclampsia or HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets), which can be a mimic for babesiosis [20, 21]. Regarding the latter, our patient’s travel history was rather banal, with no obvious high-risk exposure amongst various suburban day trips within the tri-state area (New York, New Jersey, Connecticut), including no significant wooded/grassy area exposure during those trips. However, there is evidence that climate change is expanding the range of the deer tick vector Ixodes scapularis, thus expanding the range for babesiosis [22–24]. National surveillance data has indicated spread beyond classical ranges of the northeast and upper Midwest of the United States [23] to more northern areas such as Maine and New Hampshire. Within the tri-state area of New York, New Jersey and Connecticut, this expanded range has included more inland and more northern areas beyond Long Island.

Taken together, this case highlights that careful review of antenatal maternal history is important in evaluating the differential diagnosis of neonatal fever in endemic regions. Furthermore, this case highlights that vertical transmission may be an underappreciated mode of transmission of this parasite. In this case, retrospective manual evaluation of the mother’s peripartum blood smear showed intraerythrocytic parasites (Fig. 2), which was later confirmed to be B. microti, suggesting likely vertical transmission to the neonate.

This case also highlights the importance of obtaining a blood smear from a diagnostics standpoint during the evaluation for an at-risk infant with late-onset sepsis of unclear source, especially in the setting of neonatal/maternal anemia or thrombocytopenia. Automated complete blood count testing often cannot detect low-level parasitemia, and it was only manual intensive retrospective evaluation that identified the intra-erythrocytic parasites in the mother’s blood. It is imperative that appropriate diagnostic testing be ordered if there is suspicion for parasitemia, i.e., a dedicated blood smear which includes evaluation for parasites on peripheral blood rather than a complete blood count only. The sensitivity of complete blood count tests for parasitemia is undefined. Per the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention guidance, peripheral blood smear parasite evaluations can detect as few as 4 parasites per microliter of blood [https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/diagnosticprocedures/blood/microexam.html]. The specificity of peripheral blood smear parasite evaluations for Babesia species depends on concurrent antigen testing, e.g., malarial antigen tests to rule out Plasmodium species, in combination with morphological evaluation of parasites. Confirmation of babesiosis requires molecular testing, such as PCR for 18 S rRNA gene targets. The highly sensitive ARUP reference laboratory Babesia PCR test used in this case can detect as low as 435 B. microti copies per mL and demonstrated no cross-reactivity with 56 other pathogens tested, per consultation with the reference laboratory. Similar PCR-based assay sensitivity and specificity for B. microti have been reported in the literature by other laboratories, as well [25].

Regarding treatment, despite lack of optimal dosing criteria for neonates, azithromycin and atovaquone combination therapy is the current gold standard [10, 11] due to reduced risk of toxicity compared to treatment with combination of quinine and clindamycin. Exchange transfusion may be considered if parasitemia is > 10% [11] or in case of severe hemolytic anemia [25]. Duration of therapy can vary, short 7 to 10-day regimens are favored for immunocompetent hosts with mild-moderate disease, but extended courses of up to 6 weeks or longer may be needed for those with significant immune compromise [10–13]. There is limited guidance on how frequently to obtain blood smears after appropriate treatment has been initiated; we elected to follow peripheral blood smear every 48 h while patient was admitted and then one at follow-up visit, which was about 10 days after completing treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case suggests that neonatal babesiosis should be a consideration in the broad differential diagnosis for neonatal fever in endemic regions. Clinicians should carefully review maternal antepartum history including exposures and routine laboratory results, and consider obtaining a peripheral blood smear in at-risk infants with late-onset sepsis of unclear source, especially in the setting of neonatal anemia or thrombocytopenia.
