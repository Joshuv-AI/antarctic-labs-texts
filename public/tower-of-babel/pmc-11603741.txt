Impact of longitudinal changes in serum uric acid levels and weight gain on new-onset atrial fibrillation—The Nishimura Health Survey: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jun Munakata, Keitaro Senoo, Mitsuko Nakata, Satoshi Teramukai, Muneaki Kumagai, Miyoko Yamaoka, Hiromi Nishimura, Satoaki Matoba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveUric acid (UA) and obesity are significant risk factors for new-onset atrial fibrillation (AF). Based on the pathogenesis mechanisms of new-onset AF involving obesity and UA, it is possible that UA and weight gain may interact with each other. We investigated the impact of UA and weight gain on new-onset AF using a simple measure of weight change over time, ‘weight gain of ≥10 kg after age 20’.DesignA retrospective cohort study.Setting and participantsWe retrospectively analysed 16 444 Japanese aged over 30 without AF from a cohort of employees undergoing annual health check-up from 2 April 2013 to 30 April 2022. We conducted a landmark survival analysis to assess the impact of longitudinal changes in UA and obesity on new-onset AF. Weight gain was defined as ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ using a standardised self-administered questionnaire.Primary outcome measureSubjects were diagnosed with AF when AF was present in the electrocardiogram or when indicated in a patient interview.ResultDuring a median follow-up period of 3.91 years, 69 new-onset AF occurred (incidence; 1.12/1000 person-years). UA levels were 5.76 (±1.37) in the weight gain group and 4.87 (±1.31) in the no weight gain group, both within normal limits. A multivariable landmark survival analysis including interaction term showed that new-onset AF was significantly associated with age, sex, baseline systolic blood pressure, baseline UA and the interaction term between UA change and weight gain. The interaction term between weight gain and UA change indicates that HR for every UA 1 mg/dL increase was 1.96 (95% CI 1.38 to 2.77) in subjects with weight gain and 0.95 (95% CI 0.61 to 1.48) in those without.ConclusionEven if UA levels are within the normal range, subsequent UA change and weight gain in adulthood have an interactive effect on new-onset AF.Keywords: Weight Gain, Cardiac Epidemiology, Risk Factors, PUBLIC HEALTH, Observational Study, PREVENTIVE MEDICINE

Introduction

Uric acid (UA) is the final product of purine metabolism in humans and is associated with hypertension, diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular diseases.1 2 In addition, several studies show that UA is a risk factor for the new onset and recurrence of atrial fibrillation (AF).3,6 Some studies reported that UA has an antioxidant effect under general conditions in the human body, while it has an oxidative-promoting effect in the cytoplasm and atherosclerotic plaques.7 It is shown that the oxidative-promoting effect of UA causes chronic inflammation, oxidative stress and fibrosis in the atrium,8 9 and those are considered to be involved in the pathogenesis of AF by inducing structural and electrical remodelling in the atrium.8 9 Kalenderoglu et al10 reported that UA is a useful independent predictor of new-onset AF as inflammation parameter. Furthermore, a meta-analysis including 13 studies shows that an UA increase of 1 mg/dL was associated with a 21% increased risk of the new-onset AF.3 Therefore, we have recognised that UA is one of the important risk factors for the new-onset AF.

Several previous studies showed that the indexes which present obesity, such as body weight, body mass index (BMI) and visceral fat are positively correlated with UA,11 12 and obesity is also one of the important risk factors for the new-onset AF. Adipose tissue associated with obesity promotes structural and functional remodelling of the left atrium, induces electrical conduction abnormalities and contributes to the new-onset AF.13 Oxidative stress induced by adipose tissue and activation of the autonomic nervous system are also involved in the pathogenesis of AF.13 Based on the pathogenesis mechanisms of the new-onset AF involving obesity and UA, it is possible that weight gain and UA may interact with each other, and a deeper understanding of this interaction is crucial for the prevention of the new-onset AF.

Recently, the index of ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20,’ which can be easily confirmed by health check-up, has attracted attention because this index may be an indicator of pathological conditions equivalent to obesity. Several studies show that ‘weight gain after age 20’ is an indicator of subsequent disease development.14 15 For example, Ochiai et al14 reported that compared with those without weight gain, those with weight gain have a 31% (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.21–1.42) increased risk of developing chronic kidney disease (CKD) in men regardless of current obesity status. In addition, Cui et al15 reported that this index is not only associated with disease development but also associated with a 67% increased risk of death associated with myocardial infarction. Even for those who are not currently obese, the index of ‘weight gain after age 20’ may be a predictor of subsequent several disease developments.

Thus, UA and obesity alone have been implicated in the development of new-onset AF over time,16 17 but the impact of the interaction between UA and weight gain on the new-onset of AF is not clear. We therefore investigated the impact of UA and weight gain on new-onset AF using a simple measure of weight change over time, ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’.

Methods

Study subjects and study design

The Nishimura Health Study is an ongoing cohort investigation of risk factors for chronic diseases, including hypertension, metabolic syndrome and diabetes mellitus as well as AF.18,20 The Nishimura Clinic (Kyoto, Japan) provides regular health check-ups for employees of various companies. In Japan, annual routine health examinations of employees are legally mandated, and the employers usually pay all or most of the health examination costs. We conducted a retrospective cohort study to assess the association between longitudinal changes in UA and obesity and the new-onset AF during a median follow-up period of 5.99 years from visit 1 and 3.91 years from visit 3. In this study, we calculated the change in each variable from visit 1 to visit 3 and evaluated the association with the subsequent incidence of AF.

The current study included 72 758 subjects who underwent health check-up from 2 April 2013 to 30 April 2022 and agreed to take part in the survey. The exclusion criteria were as follows: missing all laboratory values, three or less health check-ups during research periods, the occurrence of new-onset AF before visit 3, the interval between visit 1 and visit3 was within 1.5 years, the interval between visit1 and visit3 was over 4 years, serum creatinine level was higher than 1.2 mg/dL in men and 1.0 mg/dL in women or unknown at visit 1, age under 30 at visit1, taking hyperuricemia medication at visit 1 and unknown about ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’. Finally, 16 444 subjects were selected as eligible according to the study criteria (figure 1). All procedures of the study were approved by the local research ethics committee and were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was given by all subjects. The present study was approved by the institutional Clinical Research Review Board of the Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (approval number: ERB-C-1512).

Data acquisition and measurement

All subjects provided demographic details. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. After an overnight, venous blood was collected for the measurement of various factors, including fasting plasma glucose (fPG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and triglyceride (TG). Well-trained nurses measured each subject’s blood pressure (BP) once using an automatic monitor. Physicians and nurses administered questionnaires covering personal habits and present illness. Information on ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ was also collected using a standardised yes/no questionnaire.21 Subjects were diagnosed with AF when AF was present in the ECG or when indicated in a patient interview.18 19 The endpoint of the follow-up period for each participant was whichever of the following options occurred first: (1) date of the first AF event, (2) date of the last health examination or (3) 30 April 2022 (censored).

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics including patient background and laboratory data are listed in table 1, divided by with or without ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’. Clinical characteristics are presented as mean ± SD and categorical variables as numbers (percentage). Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, and categorical variables were compared with a χ2 test. Since this study used the data from annual health check-ups, if a subject is diagnosed with AF at his/her regular check-up, the occurrence of AF is considered to be between his/her last visit and the current visit. Such time to event data are called interval censored data. The semiparametric proportional hazard model is not feasible because the time to event is expressed not as a specific time point but as an interval. Thus, we estimated the HR for the new-onset AF using a parametric proportional hazards model that takes into account interval censoring.22 We estimated the baseline hazard parametrically, using a cubic spline model. We conducted landmark analysis to assess the impact of longitudinal change in BMI, systolic BP, HDL-C, TG, fPG and UA on the new-onset AF. The landmark point was set at visit 3, 2–4 years after visit 1 which was the starting point.23

The following variables were analysed as covariates: baseline age, sex, baseline BMI, BMI change, baseline systolic BP, systolic BP change, baseline HDL-C, HDL-C change, baseline logarithmised TG, logarithmised TG change, baseline fPG, fPG change, baseline UA, UA change and ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’. For logarithmised TG change, when TG decreased from visit 1 to visit 3, the absolute value of the change was logarithmised and multiplied by −1. We selected the optimal statistical model that includes an interaction term between ‘obesity’ and ‘UA change’ using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The statistical model with two interaction terms, one between ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ and ‘UA change’ and the other between ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ and ‘BMI change’ had the lowest AIC (online supplemental table 1). A p value of <0.05 is considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS, V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Patient and public involvement statement

No patient was involved in the design and conception of the study.

Results

A total of 16 444 subjects were finally included in the analysis, including 8413 men and 8031 women. During follow-up periods (median 3.91 years from visit 3), AF developed in 69 subjects (57 men and 12 women, incidence 1.12 per 1000 person-years). Baseline characteristics of 16 444 study subjects divided into 2 groups with or without ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ are shown in table 1. UA levels were 5.76 (SD 1.37) in the weight gain group and 4.87 (SD 1.31) in the no weight gain group, both within normal limits. When compared with the mean values of two groups, there are statistically significant differences in baseline age, sex, baseline BMI, BMI change, baseline systolic BP, systolic BP change, baseline HDL-C, HDL-C change, baseline TG, TG change, baseline fPG, baseline UA and UA change.

A multivariable landmark survival analysis using the baseline values of each variable and the change from visit 1 to visit 3 as explanatory variables showed that the new-onset AF was significantly associated with baseline age (HR 1.74 every 5 years (95% CI 1.53 to 1.98)), sex (HR 2.39 (95% CI 1.15 to 4.97)), baseline systolic BP (HR 1.01 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.03)), baseline UA (HR 1.29 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.63)), BMI change (HR 1.42 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.78)), UA change (HR 1.42 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.93)) (table 2).

Multivariable landmark survival model with interaction term showed that HR for every UA 1 mg/dL increase was 1.96 (95% CI 1.38 to 2.77) in subjects with weight gain of ≥10 kg after age 20, while HR for every UA 1 mg/dL increase was 0.95 (95% CI 0.61 to 1.48) in subjects without weight gain of ≥10 kg after age 20. Similarly, HR for every BMI 1 unit increase was 1.66 (95% CI 1.30 to 2.14) in subjects with weight gain of ≥10 kg after age 20, while HR for every BMI 1 unit increase was 1.09 (95% CI 0.73 to 1.62) in subjects without weight gain of ≥10 kg after age 20. Elsewhere, baseline age (HR 1.76 every 5 years (95% CI 1.55 to 2.00)), sex (HR 2.40 (95% CI 1.16 to 4.97)), baseline systolic BP (HR 1.01 (95% CI 1.00 to 1.03)) and baseline UA (HR 1.31 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.65)) were associated with new-onset AF.

Discussion

Summary findings

The present study is a cohort study investigating the impacts of longitudinal change in UA and weight gain on the new-onset AF. Our study showed that the longitudinal increases in UA and weight gain are both significant risk factors for the new-onset AF. Particularly, in a population with weight gain after early adulthood, the longitudinal increases in UA and weight gain have both escalated the risk of the new-onset AF more significantly, even if UA is within the normal range.

Association between UA and the new-onset AF

Two previous studies reported about the impact of UA on the new-onset AF by repeat measurement of UA. These two studies show that long-term hyperuricemia and longitudinal change in UA are significantly associated with the new-onset AF.4 16 It has been pointed out that UA is also associated with other risk factors for AF, such as obesity, hypertension, glucose intolerance and dyslipidaemia.1 2 24 However, these two previous studies did not take into account the impacts of longitudinal changes in other risk factors for AF, such as obesity, hypertension, glucose intolerance and dyslipidaemia. Therefore, it is possible that longitudinal changes in these other risk factors may interact and influence the relationship between UA and the new-onset AF.

We investigated the impact of UA on the new-onset AF, taking into account the impact of longitudinal changes in other risk factors for AF, as well as the baseline status. Our study revealed that each 1 mg/dL of baseline UA was significantly associated with a 29% increased risk of new-onset AF, and each 1 mg/dL of UA change was also significantly associated with a 42% increased risk of the new-onset AF. Our study also showed that longitudinal UA changes are significantly associated with new-onset AF, even after accounting for the impacts of changes in other risk factors for AF. The consistent and significant association between new-onset AF and longitudinal UA change in different populations further improves the reliability of UA as a risk factor for AF.

Some studies reported that UA is also associated with other cardiovascular diseases,25 and that the medication of hyperuricemia reduces the developing risk in some cardiovascular diseases.26 Because our study excluded subjects taking medications for hyperuricemia, further intervention studies are needed to assess the effect of UA reduction on the new-onset AF in the future.

The impact of UA and weight gain on the new-onset AF

Several studies reported UA has a positive correlation with obesity. Cibickova et al11 reported that UA has a positive correlation with BMI (r=0.42) and abdominal circumference (r=0.54). Takahashi et al12 reported that the accumulation of visceral fat correlates with UA(r=0.37). We have previously investigated the noxiousness of obesity.18,20 We know that obesity is one of the important risk factors for the new-onset AF and is a factor highly dependent on lifestyle, as is UA. Weight gain after early adulthood has been attracting attention because it is considered to be equivalent noxiousness to obesity even for those who are not currently obese.14 15 27 In Japan, because we can easily screen high-risk groups for the development of lifestyle-related diseases by using the index of ‘weight gain after age 20’, defining weight gain as 10 kg or more, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare recommends that this index is incorporated into the medical check-up questionnaire.28 It has been reported that ‘weight gain after age 20’ reflects the increase in adipose tissue and the accumulation of adipose tissue.29 30 Several previous studies showed that the index of ‘Weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ has impact on the development and other outcomes of various diseases, and many studies showed the association between ‘Weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ and CKD, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, mortality and so on.14 15 21 27

However, the impact of ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ on the new-onset AF is not clear. Our study therefore revealed that the index of ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ significantly increases the risk of new-onset AF associated with longitudinal UA change. Because this interactive effect was observed regardless of baseline UA as shown in table 2, this study indicates that the management of UA is important to prevent the new-onset AF in the population with ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ even if UA is within the normal range.

Future perspective

An indicator of ‘weight gain of≥10 kg after age 20’ can be obtained from an annual health check-up questionnaire, the results of which are useful for the prevention and early detection of new-onset AF through health check-ups.

Even in Japan, where obesity is less prevalent than in the West, statistically about 50% of Japanese people gain weight more than 10 kg after age 20.31 Therefore, approximately one in two people need to pay adequate attention to the management of UA.

UA is closely related to lifestyle habits such as diet and alcohol consumption, and Koike et al32 reported that dietary guidance such as prevention of excessive purine and fructose intake, limitation of alcohol consumption and adequate fluid intake are effective in reducing UA. Therefore, it may be necessary to recognise that people who have gained weight in adulthood are at risk of developing new AF due to a subsequent increase in UA, even if their UA is within the normal range at the time of a health check-up, and to strengthen dietary guidance.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of the study was that the study design was a retrospective landmark survival analysis, which allowed us to assess the causal relationship between weight gain and changes in UA and the new onset of AF. The results showed that ‘change in UA’ and ‘change in BMI’ had heterogeneous effects on the new onset of AF in those who gained 10 kg or more in weight after the age of 20 years and those who did not. Our study has several limitations. First, information on lifestyle factors (smoking and drinking, menopause in women and so on), regular medication and history of cardiovascular disease and family history were missing, and confounding in these factors were not included in the analysis. Second, the manner and frequency of evaluation of AF during the follow-up period may lead to an underestimation of the incidence of AF. Third, the subject of this study is not composed of the general population, since the subjects are employees of various companies who underwent annual mandatory medical examinations. Fourth, the scope of our study was to observe subsequent changes in participants with normal UA levels, and our results may not apply to patients with hyperuricemia. Fifth, in order to conduct a landmark survival analysis, subjects who had three or less visits were excluded, which resulted in the exclusion of a large number of subjects. Finaly, because our study is an observational study, further intervention studies are needed to assess the effect of UA reduction on the incidence of the new-onset AF.

Conclusion

Even if UA levels are within the normal range, subsequent UA change and weight gain have interactive effects on the new-onset AF. In terms of AF prevention, particular attention may need to be paid to elevate UA in populations that have gained weight after adulthood.
