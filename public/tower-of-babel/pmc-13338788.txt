Frailty and Severe White Matter Lesions Are Risk Factors for Surgical Treatment for Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm in Older Adults
Authors: Fumihiro MATANO, Yasuo MURAI, Takayuki MIZUNARI, Minoru IDEGUCHI, Kenta KOKETSU, Takehiko SASAKI, Toru NISHI, Shigeo YAMASHIRO, Isao DATE, Tomohito HISHIKAWA, Fusao IKAWA, Shigeyuki SAKAMOTO, Katsumi TAKIZAWA, Tohru KOBAYASHI, Kentaro MORI, Junta MOROI, Toshiaki OSATO, Masanori SUZUKI, Hiromu HADEISHI, Yoshiaki SHIOKAWA, Atsushi SAITO, Masaaki UNO, Shigeo SORA, Akitsugu KAWASHIMA, Tohru MIZUTANI, Tomonori TAMAKI, Rokuya TANIKAWA, Akio MORITA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It remains unclear whether frailty and white matter lesions are risk factors for surgical treatment of unruptured intracranial aneurysms in older adults. We conducted a prospective multicenter study of 397 patients aged ≥60 years with unruptured intracranial aneurysms enrolled from 20 hospitals in Japan. Unruptured intracranial aneurysms were treated by either microsurgical clipping or endovascular treatment according to institutional protocols. The primary endpoint was functional outcome at discharge assessed using the modified Rankin scale (mRS). Poor outcome was defined as a deterioration of 1 or more points in the mRS score at discharge compared with the preoperative baseline. Unruptured intracranial aneurysm size ranged from 2.1 to 26 mm (mean, 6.93 mm). Poor outcome occurred in 52 patients (13.1%). There was no significant association between poor outcome and age (p = 0.089) or treatment modality (clipping vs. coiling, p = 0.4739). In multivariate regression analysis, poor outcome was significantly associated with larger unruptured intracranial aneurysm size (p = 0.033), higher Clinical Frailty Scale score (p = 0.006), higher preoperative mRS score (p = 0.039), lower hemoglobin level (p = 0.016), absence of regular exercise (p = 0.046), slower walking speed (p = 0.002), severe white matter lesions (p = 0.001), and lower intraoperative blood pressure (p = 0.032). Severe white matter lesions were associated with a higher risk of postoperative ischemia (p ≤ 0.042) and intracranial hemorrhage (p = 0.0017). These findings indicate that preoperative frailty and severe white matter lesions are significant predictors of poor outcomes after unruptured intracranial aneurysm surgery in older adults. Introduction

Older adults will comprise nearly 20% of the world's population by 2050.1) Presently, the populations of Japan, Western Europe, and the United States are aging rapidly. Simultaneously, the number of older adults with incidentally discovered unruptured cerebral aneurysms has been increasing.2) Unruptured cerebral aneurysms in older adults have a high rupture rate and poor prognosis, and many cases require treatment.3) Conversely, it is often difficult to determine the indications for treatment due to underlying diseases and physical vulnerability.4,5) In other fields of surgical treatment, frailty can be a risk factor for surgical treatment.6) Moreover, cerebral white matter lesions (WMLs) are a risk factor for stroke,7) and our single-center retrospective study found that they are a risk factor for surgical treatment of cerebral aneurysms.8) However, the risk factors for unruptured cerebral aneurysms in older adults remain unclear, and it is important to clarify these factors in populations that are rapidly aging.

This multicenter prospective collaborative study aimed to investigate the characteristics of aneurysms that are common risk factors for treatment and assess whether frailty and severe WMLs could be surgical risk factors.

Materials and Methods

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Nippon Medical School Hospital Research Ethics Committee, Tokyo, Japan (R28-02-720), and was reported based on the criteria from the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement. Informed consent was obtained from each participant, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

Baseline characteristics of the study patients and unruptured intracranial aneurysms

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are shown in Table 1. We conducted a prospective multicenter analysis across 20 institutions involving 397 patients aged ≥60 years with unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) between May 2017 and April 2019, with follow-up until April 2020. The studied group included 103 men and 294 women with a mean age of 70.3 years (range, 60-89 years). UIA size ranged from 2.1 to 26 mm (mean, 6.93 mm), and the primary sites were as follows: anterior cerebral artery, 97 cases; internal carotid artery, 135 cases; middle cerebral artery, 128 cases; basilar artery and vertebral artery, 25 cases; and others, 12 cases (Table 1).

The inclusion criteria were age ≥60 years and UIA diameter ≥2 mm on magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), angiography, or 3-dimensional computed tomography angiography. The choice of treatment strategy, such as clipping or endovascular therapy, was independently determined by each institution. We conducted a preoperative review of the patients' laboratory workup data, imaging results, medical history, lifestyle factors, and frailty indicators, including recent physical condition, usual exercise, and cognitive function. Frailty was assessed preoperatively using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). Cognitive function was assessed preoperatively using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). For all included patients, WMLs were assessed on magnetic resonance imaging using fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images within the first week of admission. The Fazekas classification9) was used, in which grade 0 refers to no WMLs; grade 1 refers to punctate WMLs with a diameter ≤9 mm for a single WML and ≤20 mm for grouped WMLs; grade 2 refers to a single WML with a diameter of 10-20 mm (early confluent) and grouped WMLs with a diameter of 20 mm without connecting bridges between WMLs; and grade 3 refers to confluent WMLs in which the size of a single or grouped WML is ≥20 mm and connecting bridges are present between WMLs. Grades 0 and 1 were defined as mild WMLs, and grades 2 and 3 were defined as severe WMLs (Supplementary Figure 1). In challenging cases, such as cases involving intracranial hemorrhage, we evaluated contralateral WMLs using the same Fazekas classification. Atherosclerosis was defined as the presence of any of the following factors: smoking, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension. Furthermore, we evaluated relationships between parameters according to age, sex, preoperative MMSE score,10) Fazekas classification, and atherosclerosis.

Outcome

The primary outcome was functional status at discharge, assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).11) Poor outcome was defined as a deterioration of 1 or more points in the mRS score at discharge compared with the preoperative baseline. The mRS was also evaluated at 3 months and 1 year postoperatively for follow-up assessment. Postoperative intracranial hemorrhage, seizures, cerebral infarction, contusion, and cranial nerve palsy were also evaluated (Table 2).

For exploratory analysis, we defined the combined frailty score as the sum of the following factors, each scored as 1 point: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score (>3), CFS score (>4), reduced hand grip strength (<19 kg), motor paresis, body weight loss, absence of regular exercise, awareness of slowed walking, walking speed (<1.0 m/s), maximum walking distance (<0.5 km), MMSE score (<26), short-term memory impairment, and severe Fazekas grade. We then examined the relationship between the total score (maximum, 12 points) and postoperative mRS. We also analyzed the relationship between WML grade and postoperative MMSE score and the rate of change between preoperative and postoperative assessments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software for Mac (version 21.0; SPSS, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analysis. Numerical variables were represented as mean ± standard deviation for parametric data, median (interquartile range: 25th-75th percentile) for nonparametric data, or number and percentage, as appropriate. Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Student's t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test were used to compare normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. To assess predictors of poor prognosis, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In the present study, poor outcome (defined as a deterioration of ≥1 point in the mRS score at discharge compared with the preoperative baseline) was observed in 52 patients (13.1%), and a deterioration of ≥2 points in the mRS score was observed in 23 patients (5.8%) at discharge (Table 2).

The major complications were cerebral infarction in 24 cases and hemorrhagic complications in 9 cases. Further, 40 patients (10.1%) presented with asymptomatic complications. Hospital stay ranged from 5 to 86 days (mean, 14.4 days) (Table 2).

Baseline characteristics and laboratory data

Significant correlations were observed between poor outcome and the preoperative number of medications (p = 0.0268) and hemoglobin level (p = 0.0255). No significant correlations were noted between poor outcome and age, sex, body mass index, heart disease, stroke, atherosclerotic disease, or levels of albumin, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, creatine kinase, cholesterol, or creatinine (Table 3).

Physical and cognitive status

Significant correlations were observed between poor outcome and ASA physical status (Bryson and Kellner,12) 2014) (p = 0.0473), CFS score (Rockwood et al.,13) 2005) (p = 0.0287), mRS score ≥2 (p = 0.00108), absence of regular exercise (p = 0.0148), walking speed (p = 0.0379), maximum walking distance (p = 0.0194), MMSE score (p = 0.0105), and short-term memory (p = 0.0126). No significant correlations were noted between poor outcome and grip strength, motor paralysis, weight loss, or awareness of slow walking (Table 3).

Aneurysm characteristics, imaging findings, and treatment

Significant correlations were observed between poor outcome and fusiform aneurysm (p = 0.0123), thrombosis aneurysm (p = 0.0449), severe Fazekas grade (p = 0.0111), operation time (p = 0.0010), and intraoperative blood pressure (p = 0.0370). No significant correlations were noted between poor outcome and aneurysm location, calcification, daughter sac, clipping, or coiling (Table 3).

Multivariate logistic regression analyses

We performed multivariate logistic regression analyses to identify predictors of poor outcome. Hemoglobin level (p = 0.016), ASA physical status (p = 0.004), CFS score (p = 0.006), mRS score (p = 0.039), absence of regular exercise (p = 0.046), walking speed (p = 0.002), MMSE score (p = 0.018), aneurysm size (p = 0.033), severe Fazekas grade (p = 0.001), and intraoperative blood pressure (p = 0.032) were significant predictors of poor outcome (Table 4).

Frailty and outcome

The combined frailty score had a significant correlation with postoperative mRS score (Spearman's correlation, p = 0.048) (Supplementary Figure 2). Severe WMLs, as graded by the Fazekas classification, were significantly correlated with age (p = 0.0013), preoperative MMSE score (p = 0.010), atherosclerotic disease (p = 0.0263), and complications such as ischemia (p ≤ 0.042) and intracranial hemorrhage (p = 0.0017) (Table 5). Severe WMLs had a significant positive correlation with postoperative MMSE score (Spearman's correlation, p = 0.031) (Supplementary Figure 3). Patients with severe WMLs had a decrease of 1.46 points between the preoperative and postoperative MMSE scores, whereas patients without severe WMLs had a decrease of 0.35 points, which showed a statistically significant difference (p = 0.0149).

Discussion

In this prospective multicenter cohort, poor outcomes after UIA treatment in older adults were more strongly associated with preoperative frailty and severe WMLs than with chronological age. These findings underscore the importance of careful patient selection and perioperative risk stratification in older adults.

UIAs were first identified with the widespread use of MRA.14,15) In Japan, the proportion of individuals aged ≥65 years reached 25% in 2013, will exceed 30% by 2025, and will reach 39.9% by 2060.16) In the future, only the proportion of patients aged ≥75 years will continually increase,17) and the incidental detection of UIAs in older adults will continually increase. Our cohort study, according to the Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study of Japan,18) demonstrated that there are larger aneurysms in the elderly population. Additionally, age is a strong risk factor for ruptured aneurysm in Japan.3) Therefore, the proportion of older patients who will undergo surgical treatment will increase. Western European countries are also aging, and the population of older people (defined as over 65 years) in the 27 European Union countries will increase significantly, with an expected increase from 90.5 million at the start of 2019 to 129.8 million by 2050. Therefore, risk management for unruptured cerebral aneurysms in older adults is an important issue globally.

In considering surgical treatment for older patients, clinicians also hesitate because of the higher risk of complications in older patients compared with younger patients.19) However, there are some reports indicating that age is not a risk factor for aneurysm treatment.20) Therefore, it is difficult to conclude that older age is associated with higher surgical risk, because some elderly patients are healthy and others are not. It is necessary to identify elderly patients who are at higher risk for surgery to improve the outcomes of surgical treatment.

The risks of poor surgical outcomes in brain aneurysm treatment can be divided into 2 major categories. The first risk is due to surgical difficulties caused by the aneurysm itself, such as its size, shape, location, and thrombosis, which may represent procedural difficulties with a higher risk of complications, such as ischemic events or rupture during the procedure.21) In our study, aneurysm size, shape, and thrombosis were identified as risk factors for poor outcome.

The second risk is due to the patient's general condition, including comorbidities and systemic complications that could affect the vulnerability of the body and brain. It has been found that systemic disease, including hypertension, renal dysfunction, and heart failure, is a risk factor for poor outcome after surgical treatment.22) However, there are few studies evaluating whether patients' systemic complications, especially systemic fragility (which has been widely reported in other surgical fields in recent years, such as muscle weakness and worsening gait status), are risk factors for cerebral aneurysm treatment. Furthermore, there are few reports on whether brain fragility is a risk factor for cerebral aneurysm treatment.23,24)

Older adults with frailty are defined as individuals who require comprehensive functional assessment, and they have been described as having multiple chronic diseases, psychiatric and psychological problems, and issues related to social isolation,25) as well as requiring a large number of services. It is important to support both physical and social frailty in elderly patients and to maintain a long, healthy life expectancy from a social point of view, but the outcomes of medical and surgical treatments are mainly assessed based on physical frailty.26)

Frailty is recognized as an independent risk factor for poorer outcomes in patients with systemic diseases, regardless of chronological age.27) However, evidence regarding the impact of frailty on surgical outcomes for UIA in elderly patients remains limited, with only 1 nationwide registry-based retrospective study reported to date.24) In the present study, detailed preoperative frailty assessment was identified as a significant risk factor for poor postoperative outcomes, as reflected by lower postoperative mRS scores. In addition, the combined frailty score showed a significant correlation with postoperative functional outcomes. The combined frailty score was newly developed to comprehensively assess multidimensional vulnerability, including physical performance and cognitive function, which have not been sufficiently evaluated by conventional frailty indices. This combined frailty score was used for exploratory purposes and was not intended to replace established frailty assessments.

WMLs and cerebrovascular disease, dementia, or cognitive dysfunction have been widely reported as risk factors for poor neurological outcomes.7,28) Aging reduces vascular elasticity and consequently impairs cerebral blood flow.29) In addition, decreased levels of neurotransmitters have been observed in elderly patients. These age-related changes increase brain vulnerability by reducing tissue elasticity and elevating the risk of postoperative ischemic and hemorrhagic complications, as observed in our study. Intraoperative hypotension in elderly patients with reduced cerebral and vascular elasticity may further contribute to cerebral hypoxia.30) Moreover, in our cohort, even in patients without major complications, severe WMLs were associated with a significant postoperative decline in MMSE scores, reflecting so-called brain aging and underlying patient frailty.

In elderly patients with UIA, the choice between microsurgical clipping and endovascular treatment is typically individualized according to aneurysm characteristics (size, location, neck configuration, morphology, calcification, and thrombosis) and patient-related factors (frailty, comorbidities, baseline function, and anesthetic risk). In general, endovascular treatment tends to be preferred in older or medically vulnerable patients because of its less invasive nature, whereas microsurgical clipping remains an important option for aneurysms with unfavorable endovascular anatomy (e.g., broad neck, complex morphology, or branch incorporation) and for lesions in which durable occlusion is prioritized. Previous studies comparing these modalities in elderly populations have reported that, with appropriate case selection, perioperative mortality and major complication rates are broadly comparable between clipping and endovascular treatment, although the profile of complications may differ (e.g., procedure-related ischemic events vs. hemorrhagic complications), and endovascular treatment may require additional retreatment during follow-up.28,29) In the present study, treatment modality itself was not independently associated with poor outcome. Instead, patient-related factors―particularly preoperative frailty and severe WMLs―showed a stronger association with postoperative functional outcomes, suggesting that patient vulnerability may play a more critical role than treatment modality in determining outcomes in elderly patients.

Conclusion

Frailty and WMLs should be considered by neurosurgeons in the treatment of unruptured cerebral aneurysms in elderly patients and in the determination of indications for treatment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Although it is a multicenter prospective cohort study, the overall sample size is relatively small, particularly for patients who underwent endovascular treatment. In addition, long-term outcomes (e.g., 5-10 years) were not assessed, which limits the ability to evaluate the durability of clinical effects. Furthermore, given the low incidence of poor outcomes in this cohort, technical complications during the procedure―rather than the presence of severe WMLs themselves―may have contributed to poor discharge mRS scores in some patients. This potential confounding factor should be taken into account when interpreting the relationship between WML severity and short-term functional outcomes. To address these limitations, future studies should include a larger sample size, more detailed analysis of intraoperative events, and long-term follow-up (5-10 years) to better elucidate the independent impact of WMLs on clinical outcomes.
