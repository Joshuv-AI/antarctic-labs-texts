Comparative diagnostic performance of motor-evoked potential alarm thresholds for predicting postoperative neurological decline in intradural extramedullary spinal tumor surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Łukasz Antkowiak, Marta Antkowiak, Anna Kasperczuk, Wojciech Kaspera, Marek Mandera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Variability in motor evoked potential (MEP) alarm thresholds limits interpretation of intraoperative monitoring during intradural extramedullary (IDEM) tumor surgery. We aimed to compare predefined MEP thresholds for predicting postoperative neurological decline. PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane were searched from inception to 9 November 2025 for studies evaluating MEP accuracy in predicting postoperative motor deficits. Studies assessing MEP within multimodal intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (mIONM) for predicting neurological deficits were also analyzed. MEP thresholds and diagnostic contingency data were extracted. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) were calculated for complete MEP amplitude loss and 50%-, 70%-, and 80%-level MEP amplitude decline categories. AUROC confidence intervals and p-values were obtained using study-level bootstrap resampling when feasible. Sixteen studies were included. In separate-threshold MEP analyses, 50%-level, 70%-level, and complete MEP amplitude loss criteria significantly predicted short-term motor deficits, whereas 50%- and 70%-level thresholds significantly predicted long-term motor deficits. The 50%-level threshold showed the most favorable short-term profile, with 94.1% sensitivity and 98.4% specificity. In sensitivity analysis, the pooled 70%/80%-level high-threshold group also showed significant diagnostic performance for short- and long-term motor deficit prediction. Within mIONM, the 50%-level threshold showed the most favorable point estimates, although formal testing was unavailable for short-term performance. Significant mIONM diagnostic performance was shown for complete MEP amplitude loss in short-term evaluation and for the 50%-level threshold in long-term evaluation. The 50%-level MEP amplitude decline threshold showed the most favorable profile for short-term motor deficit prediction in MEP-based analyses and favorable estimates within mIONM. Higher thresholds retained diagnostic utility. These findings should be interpreted cautiously because of differences in sample size, tumor location, histology, and outcome reporting between threshold groups.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10143-026-04490-9.

Introduction

Intradural extramedullary (IDEM) spinal tumors represent about one-third of spinal canal tumors, with the majority of them being either meningiomas or nerve-sheath tumors [1]. These tumors typically compress rather than infiltrate the spinal cord parenchyma. Despite their relatively benign character, the surgical removal of IDEM tumors remains classified as a high-risk spinal surgery [2]. Therefore, in recent years, the feasibility of intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) has been studied in that clinical setting, to maximize the safety of IDEM tumor removal procedures [3, 4]. According to our recent meta-analysis, IONM alarms occurred in up to 19.7% of patients undergoing IONM-guided IDEM tumor resection [4]. Although there are no data on the therapeutic benefit of applying IONM in that setting, the predictive value of both multimodal IONM (mIONM) and motor evoked potential (MEP) monitoring has already been demonstrated [4].

However, there is substantial inconsistency in the magnitude of MEP amplitude decline that should be considered alarming and should prompt anesthetic or surgical corrective measures. The MEP amplitude alarm threshold ranges from approximately 50% amplitude decline from baseline to complete MEP amplitude loss. In 2014, Kobayashi et al. [5] proposed a 70%-level decline in MEP amplitude as a highly accurate threshold for predicting postoperative neurological decline. Although their analysis was based on a large cohort of 959 patients, the study was not limited to IDEM tumor patients and did not include other MEP alarm thresholds for comparison.

Therefore, considering the increasing evidence on IONM applicability in IDEM tumor surgery and the substantial variability in MEP alarm thresholds across studies, this study aimed to compare the diagnostic performance of predefined MEP amplitude decline thresholds for predicting postoperative neurological decline.

Methods

Study guidance

When performing this study, the authors strictly adhered to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [6]. The protocol of this study was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database under the identification number CRD420251044622.

Search strategy and criteria

Two authors (Ł.A. and M.A.) independently searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane databases for English-language full-text research articles analyzing the impact of MEP amplitude on IDEM tumor surgery, published from inception until 9 November 2025. The detailed database searching strategy is presented in Supplementary Data 1.

Search results were exported to the Rayyan software [7]. Duplicate removal was performed manually within the Rayyan software. Subsequently, two authors (Ł.A. and M.A.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of each study. Only original studies and case series were included. The study was deemed eligible when it clearly analyzed the MEP amplitude alarm threshold and provided detailed data on true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN) in the purely IDEM tumor cohort. In turn, studies on mixed or undefined groups, those analyzing purely pediatric patients, and those with conflicting or unclear data were excluded. To maximize the completeness of our literature search, the authors screened the reference lists of all eligible papers.

Data extraction

The data extraction included: first author, publication year, study design, period, and MEP alarm criteria. Whenever available, data on the intraoperative anesthesia protocol, including the use of total intravenous anesthesia, volatile agents, and neuromuscular blockade, were also extracted. Based on the detailed study data analysis, we additionally calculated the number of monitored patients, monitorability rate, alarm rate, rescue rate, and the number of permanent MEP/mIONM decline cases, along with the corresponding numbers of short-term and long-term neurological deficits. The methodology for determining the monitorability rate, alarm rate, rescue rate, and the number of permanent MEP/mIONM decline cases was consistent with that described in our previous study [4].

Intraoperative alarm classification

The detailed methodology for alarm classification has already been published in our previous paper and has been consistently applied in the current paper [4]. Our accuracy analyses included only permanent IONM decline (those IONM potential declines that exceeded the predefined alarm threshold and persisted until the end of surgery), and consequently classified as TP, TN, FP, FN, based on postoperative neurological/motor status of the patient. Transient MEP or mIONM changes that recovered after corrective maneuvers were classified as rescue cases and analyzed separately. They were not included as permanent MEP/mIONM declines in diagnostic accuracy calculations unless the monitoring change persisted until the end of surgery.

Quality assessment

Two authors (Ł.A. and M.A.) independently assessed the quality of the included studies using the second version of the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool [8].

Statistical analysis

Python software (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware, United States) was used for all statistical analyses. Accuracy metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), were calculated from TP, TN, FP, and FN events using the methods described in our previously published study [4]. To determine the accuracy of each MEP alarm threshold in predicting postoperative neurological decline, analyses were performed separately for short-term outcomes, defined as those assessed immediately after surgery, and long-term outcomes, defined as those assessed at least 3 months after hospital discharge. The following strategy was implemented:In studies where MEP was the sole modality, each intraoperative event in an individual patient (TP, TN, FP, FN) was linked to the patient’s postoperative motor statusIn studies that used MEP along with other IONM modalities, and for which MEP-related data were available for separate extraction, each intraoperative MEP event in an individual patient (TP, TN, FP, FN) was linked to the patient’s postoperative motor statusIn studies using MEP as part of mIONM, each intraoperative mIONM event in an individual patient (TP, TN, FP, FN) was linked to the patient’s postoperative neurological status

All analyses were performed using publicly available data only. Missing, unavailable, or not reported data were not treated as zero and were excluded from the corresponding calculation. Therefore, denominators differed between variables, threshold groups, and follow-up time points. Short-term and long-term analyses were calculated separately and could include different study-level patient pools, because not all included studies reported both short- and long-term outcomes.

MEP amplitude decline thresholds were harmonized into predefined threshold categories before analysis. Thresholds reported as > 50%, ≥ 50%, or 50% amplitude decline were classified as the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category. Thresholds reported as > 70%, ≥ 70%, or 70% amplitude decline were classified as the 70%-level MEP amplitude decline category. Thresholds reported as > 80%, ≥ 80%, or 80% amplitude decline were classified as the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category. Complete MEP amplitude loss was analyzed as a separate category.

For sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, monitorability rate, alarm rate, rescue rate, permanent MEP/mIONM decline rate, and short- and long-term postoperative deficit rates, uncertainty was estimated using the Jeffreys Bayesian approach. For each proportion (x/n), 95% credible intervals were estimated from the posterior Beta(x + 0.5, n − x + 0.5) distribution.

Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was calculated for each subgroup based on the predefined threshold categories: complete MEP amplitude loss, 50%-level MEP amplitude decline, 70%-level MEP amplitude decline, and 80%-level MEP amplitude decline. Because only aggregated study-level TP, TN, FP, and FN data were available, individual patient-level AUROC analysis and DeLong’s method could not be applied. Therefore, AUROC values were estimated for each threshold group using aggregated diagnostic accuracy data, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using non-parametric bootstrap resampling at the study level with 2000 iterations. Statistical significance was assessed by testing the null hypothesis of AUROC = 0.5 using the bootstrap-derived standard error.

For threshold groups represented by fewer than three independent studies, point estimates of AUROC were calculated; however, 95% confidence intervals and p-values were not estimated because the number of studies was insufficient for reliable bootstrap resampling.

As an additional sensitivity analysis, studies in the 70%-level and 80%-level MEP amplitude decline categories were pooled into one high-threshold group. Diagnostic accuracy metrics, AUROC values, and threshold-group comparisons of monitorability, alarm, rescue, permanent MEP decline, and postoperative motor deficit rates were then calculated for three MEP alarm-threshold categories: complete MEP amplitude loss, pooled 70%/80%-level MEP amplitude decline, and 50%-level MEP amplitude decline.

MEP-based and mIONM-based analyses were not treated as paired patient-level comparisons, because they could include different study-level patient pools, different event definitions, and different availability of extractable TP, TN, FP, and FN data.

To assess potential heterogeneity between threshold groups, as applicable, in terms of tumor location, histological subtype, extent of resection (EOR), monitorability rate, alarm rate, rescue rate, permanent MEP/mIONM decline rate, and short- and long-term postoperative deficit rates, Pearson’s chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact test was applied, as appropriate. Whenever an overall comparison involving more than two groups was statistically significant, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Holm’s correction for multiple testing.

Funnel plots were created and assessed for apparent asymmetry to evaluate potential publication bias. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

The literature search yielded 4582 results, including 1328 from PubMed, 1843 from Embase, and 1411 from Cochrane. After excluding 1211 duplicate records, 3371 studies remained for further evaluation. As a result of title and abstract screening, 3256 records were found to be irrelevant, leaving 115 papers eligible for full-text review. Following a meticulous review of all initially eligible studies, 15 were included in the systematic review. Subsequently, 426 references from 15 included studies were screened, resulting in the identification of 1 additional relevant study. Finally, 16 original studies were included and further analyzed. Study characteristics

Among 16 studies included [3, 9–23] 12 were retrospective [3, 9–15, 18–20, 23] and 4 were prospective [16, 17, 21, 22]. The accuracy of MEP in predicting motor deficits was studied in 12 research articles [3, 9–12, 16–19, 21–23], while MEP as a component of mIONM in predicting neurological deficits was evaluated in 10 papers [3, 9–11, 13–15, 19, 20, 22].

In studies analyzing the accuracy of MEP in predicting postoperative motor deficits, 4 studies were classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category [3, 12, 22, 23], another 4 into the 70%-level MEP amplitude decline category [16–18, 21], 1 into the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category [19], whereas 3 studies applied complete MEP amplitude loss as the alarm criterion [9–11].

In studies assessing the accuracy of mIONM in predicting postoperative neurological deficits, 5 studies were classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category [3, 14, 15, 20, 22], 2 into the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category [13, 19], whereas 3 studies used complete MEP amplitude loss as the alarm criterion [9–11]. Anesthesia protocols were reported in 15 of 16 included studies [3, 9, 11–23], corresponding to 1873/1941 IONM-monitored patients. In all studies with available data, anesthesia was based on total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA), most commonly using propofol combined with remifentanil or fentanyl. One study [22] reported occasional use of an inhalational component, but the protocol was still primarily based on intravenous propofol and fentanyl. Across MEP threshold groups, available anesthesia data therefore did not suggest major differences in the use of TIVA-based regimens. However, the use of neuromuscular blocking agents differed between studies. Some studies avoided muscle relaxants after induction or used them only for intubation [3, 9, 11–14, 18–20, 22, 23], whereas two multicenter studies classified into the 70%-level MEP amplitude decline category reported the use of vecuronium during the procedure [16, 21]. Thus, although the main anesthetic approach was broadly comparable across studies, some heterogeneity remained in the use and reporting of neuromuscular blockade. The details of anesthesia protocols are listed in Supplementary Data 2.

Diagnostic performance of MEP alarm thresholds

We assessed whether different alarm criteria significantly affected MEP accuracy for predicting new postoperative motor deficits. AUROC analyses revealed that, in the primary analysis of separate thresholds, the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline, 70%-level MEP amplitude decline, and complete MEP amplitude loss categories significantly predicted short-term motor deficits (AUROC = 0.96; p < 0.0001, AUROC = 0.89; p < 0.0001, and AUROC = 0.74; p = 0.0002, respectively). The 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category yielded an AUROC of 0.61, but formal statistical testing was not available because this category was represented by only one study. In long-term evaluation, both the 50%-level and 70%-level MEP amplitude decline categories showed significant diagnostic performance (AUROC = 0.86; p < 0.0001, and AUROC = 0.82; p < 0.0001, respectively). The 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category yielded an AUROC of 0.997, but formal statistical testing was not available because this category was represented by only one study. AUROC could not be estimated for the complete MEP amplitude loss group in long-term evaluation because no long-term motor deficits occurred in this subgroup. Because only one study was classified into the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis in which studies in the 70%-level and 80%-level MEP amplitude decline categories were pooled into one high-threshold group. In this analysis, the pooled high-threshold group combining the 70%-level and 80%-level categories showed significant diagnostic performance for both short-term (AUROC = 0.85; p < 0.0001) and long-term motor deficit prediction (AUROC = 0.86; p < 0.0001). Nevertheless, the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category showed the most favorable diagnostic profile for short-term motor deficit prediction. Additionally, we analyzed the histological and anatomical distribution of tumors as well as tumor EOR between different MEP amplitude decline groups. Our analysis showed significant intergroup differences in tumor level, including craniocervical (p < 0.001), cervical (p = 0.045), thoracic (p < 0.001), and lumbar locations (p = 0.029). Histological subtypes also differed significantly, particularly for schwannomas and meningiomas (both p < 0.001), whereas the difference in ependymoma distribution did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.051). Conversely, the EOR did not differ significantly between MEP threshold groups with available data. Gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved in 66/68 patients (97.1%) in the complete MEP amplitude loss group, 153/173 patients (88.4%) in the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline group, and 276/308 patients (89.6%) in the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline group (p = 0.115). EOR data were not available for studies classified into the 70%-level MEP amplitude decline category.

In the corresponding sensitivity analysis after pooling the 70%-level and 80%-level MEP amplitude decline categories into a single high-threshold group, significant intergroup differences remained for craniocervical (p < 0.001) and thoracic tumor locations (p < 0.001), whereas cervical (p = 0.064) and lumbar (p = 0.148) tumor distributions were no longer significantly different. Histological subtype distribution also differed significantly for schwannomas (p < 0.001), meningiomas (p = 0.001), and ependymomas (p = 0.003). EOR did not differ significantly between groups in this pooled high-threshold analysis (p = 0.115). Detailed data for both separate-threshold and pooled high-threshold analyses are provided in Supplementary Data 5.

Diagnostic performance of MEP alarm thresholds within mionm

We aimed to test whether different MEP alarm criteria significantly influence the accuracy of mIONM. AUROC analyses revealed that, within mIONM, the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category yielded the highest point estimates for predicting short-term neurological deficits (AUROC = 1.00), although formal statistical testing was not available because of the limited number of studies and events. Complete MEP amplitude loss significantly predicted short-term neurological deficits (AUROC = 0.83; p = 0.0004), whereas the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category yielded an AUROC of 0.64 without available formal statistical testing. In long-term evaluation, the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category showed significant diagnostic performance (AUROC = 0.88; p < 0.0001). The 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category yielded an AUROC of 0.69, but formal statistical testing was not available. AUROC could not be reliably estimated for the complete MEP amplitude loss group because no long-term neurological deficits occurred in this subgroup. Discussion

Recently, the use of IONM during IDEM tumor resection has gained increasing attention. Our recent meta-analysis on that topic showed that MEP and mIONM accurately predict motor and neurological deficits, respectively [4]. In contrast, SSEP and D-wave failed to achieve sufficiently high accuracy values to be considered adequately precise modalities to guide surgical decisions. However, considering the specific role of D-wave in IDEM tumor surgery, which typically remains combined with MEP monitoring, we concluded that MEP and D-wave should be combined whenever possible to protect the patient from permanent postoperative deficits. While our recent meta-analysis provided novel data on the actual prognostic value of MEP, D-wave, SSEP, and mIONM separately, it was highly limited by the diverse MEP alarm criteria applied. Therefore, in the present study, we aimed to supplement our recent findings with a detailed comparison of the diagnostic performance of predefined MEP alarm thresholds.

Precise determination of IONM modality alarm criteria is crucial for appropriately stratifying intraoperative events to balance patient safety and the maximized extent of tumor resection. There is a paucity of data on optimal alarm criteria for MEP amplitude decline during IDEM tumor surgery, which has led to a lack of recommendations in that field. The only alarm criterion that correlates well with a permanent motor deficit remains the decline in D-wave amplitude of more than 50% [10, 11, 24–28]. However, D-wave data in the present IDEM dataset were limited and should be interpreted with caution. D-wave was included in the monitoring protocol in four studies [9–11, 14], comprising 351 IONM-monitored patients. In most of these studies, D-wave was reported as part of mIONM rather than as an isolated modality, which limited threshold-specific diagnostic accuracy analyses. Therefore, the lower diagnostic performance reported for D-wave in the available IDEM literature [4] should not be interpreted as evidence that D-wave is inferior to myogenic MEP monitoring, but rather as a consequence of sparse and inconsistently reported extractable D-wave data.

Conversely, the lack of recommendations on the MEP and SSEP alarm criteria led to the implementation of a wide range of alarm threshold values reported in recent studies. While SSEP alarm criteria remain roughly comparable across the studies (consisting of > 50% amplitude reduction and/or > 10% latency increase), MEP alarm criteria that force changes in surgical strategy range from complete MEP amplitude loss [9–11], through 80%-level [19] and 70%-level [16–18, 21] to 50%-level [3, 12, 22, 23] amplitude decline. Since changes in MEP alarm criteria affect the balance between FP and FN, we aimed to compare the diagnostic performance of different predefined MEP amplitude thresholds.

Consequently, our primary analysis of separate threshold categories showed that studies classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category demonstrated significant diagnostic performance for predicting motor deficits both immediately after surgery, with a sensitivity of 94.1% and specificity of 98.4%, and at long-term follow-up, with a sensitivity of 78.6% and specificity of 94.3%. In 2014, Kobayashi et al. [5] proposed a 70%-level MEP amplitude decrement as a highly sensitive and specific threshold for predicting motor deficits immediately after surgery. In line with these findings, our primary analysis found that the 70%-level MEP amplitude decline category provided sufficient diagnostic accuracy for both short-term and long-term motor deficit prediction. However, because the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category was represented by only one study, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis pooling the 70%-level and 80%-level MEP amplitude decline categories into a single high-threshold group. This pooled high-threshold category also showed significant diagnostic performance for both short- and long-term motor deficit prediction. Therefore, higher MEP amplitude decline thresholds should not be regarded as diagnostically uninformative. Taken together, these findings indicate that the diagnostic advantage of the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category was most apparent for short-term motor deficit prediction, whereas its long-term performance was comparable to that of the pooled 70%/80%-level high-threshold group. Therefore, although the 50%-level threshold may have clinically relevant diagnostic utility, it should not be interpreted as a universally optimal cutoff because of sparse events, unequal subgroup sizes, and interstudy heterogeneity.

Although these findings enrich the existing literature on the utility of IONM in IDEM tumor surgery, some limitations of these calculations must be highlighted. Firstly, the number of patients included in the threshold-specific diagnostic accuracy calculations differed markedly between MEP alarm-threshold groups and between follow-up time points. In the short-term analysis, extractable TP, TN, FP, and FN data were available for 189 patients in the complete MEP amplitude loss group, 171 patients in the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline group, 913 patients in the 70%-level MEP amplitude decline group, and 205 patients in the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline group. In the long-term analysis, the corresponding numbers were 176, 171, 377, and 276 patients, respectively. These differences reflect the fact that not all included studies reported both short- and long-term outcomes with extractable diagnostic accuracy data. Therefore, comparisons across thresholds and follow-up time points should be interpreted as study-level comparisons based on available data rather than as paired analyses of identical patient cohorts.

Moreover, intergroup analyses indicated that tumor level and histology differed significantly between both MEP and mIONM threshold groups (Supplementary Data 5 and Supplementary Data 7). While the tumor location may impact the type of postoperative neurological deficits, transcranial MEP monitoring may not always capture neurological injury with the same accuracy. In thoracic tumors, postoperative motor decline is more likely to reflect corticospinal tract dysfunction and may therefore be more directly associated with transcranial MEP changes. Conversely, in cervical, conus medullaris, cauda equina, or lumbosacral tumors, postoperative deficits may include segmental paresis or nerve-root-related deterioration, which may not be captured by transcranial MEP monitoring with the same reliability. Similarly, different histological tumor subtypes, particularly schwannomas and meningiomas, may differ in consistency, degree of adhesion and relationship to the spinal cord or nerve roots, potentially influencing both the risk of postoperative neurological deterioration and the risk of intraoperative MEP alerts. Therefore, the apparently favorable diagnostic performance of the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category may partly reflect interstudy differences in tumor distribution rather than the effect of the threshold alone.

Importantly, the available studies did not consistently report TP, TN, FP, and FN events stratified by tumor level, histology, or type of postoperative motor deficit. Therefore, more detailed subgroup analyses distinguishing long-tract motor deterioration from segmental or nerve-root-related deficits were not feasible. For this reason, the present findings should not be interpreted as establishing a universally optimal physiological cutoff applicable to all IDEM tumor locations and histological subtypes. Rather, they indicate that studies classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category demonstrated the most favorable diagnostic performance, particularly for short-term motor deficit prediction, while prospective validation in anatomically and histologically stratified cohorts remains necessary.

Although MEP amplitude loss showed statistically significant short-term diagnostic performance, it remained relatively insensitive compared with lower amplitude-decline thresholds. Therefore, complete MEP abolishment may still fail to detect more subtle intraoperative deterioration and should not be interpreted as a sufficiently sensitive warning criterion.

When analyzed separately, the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category was represented by only one study and therefore produced unstable estimates, particularly in the long-term analysis. The apparently excellent long-term diagnostic values in this subgroup should not be overinterpreted, as they most likely reflect the small number of events and resolution of some short-term deficits rather than robust independent evidence for this threshold. For this reason, the 80%-level category was additionally analyzed together with the 70%-level category as a pooled high-threshold group. The significant diagnostic performance observed in this combined group suggests that higher thresholds retain diagnostic utility when analyzed collectively, but it does not indicate superiority over the 50%-level threshold.

The interpretation of rescue rates is also important when evaluating the clinical relevance of each MEP alarm threshold. In MEP-based analyses, rescue rates differed between separate threshold groups; however, the 50%-level threshold was not associated with the highest rescue rate. Moreover, after pooling the 70%-level and 80%-level categories into a single high-threshold group, rescue rates no longer differed significantly between groups. In mIONM-based analyses, rescue rates were comparable across threshold groups. Therefore, the present data do not provide sufficient evidence that the favorable diagnostic performance observed in studies classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category resulted from a clearly superior therapeutic effect of earlier alarm-triggered corrective interventions. Rather, the advantage of this threshold should be interpreted primarily as diagnostic and predictive, while the potential therapeutic impact of earlier rescue interventions remains to be clarified in prospective studies with standardized reporting of alarm reversibility, corrective maneuvers, and postoperative outcomes.

Similarly, we analyzed patients undergoing mIONM, defined as a combination of at least two IONM modalities, stratified according to the MEP alarm criterion applied.

Consequently, studies classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category within mIONM showed high diagnostic performance for predicting neurological deficits, with 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity in short-term evaluation and 83.9% sensitivity and 91.8% specificity in long-term evaluation. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limitations described above. Conversely, the 80%-level threshold did not demonstrate robust diagnostic performance within mIONM, and complete MEP amplitude loss, although significant in short-term evaluation, could not be assessed for long-term neurological deficit prediction because no long-term events occurred in this subgroup.

Limitations

Because the available evidence was derived from methodologically heterogeneous studies with limited sample sizes, the presented results should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the retrospective design of most included studies introduces uncertainty regarding the reliability and completeness of the reported data.

Although most included studies reported the use of TIVA, anesthetic protocols were not uniformly described in detail across the literature. In particular, the dose, timing, and reversal of neuromuscular blocking agents differed between studies or were incompletely reported. Therefore, interstudy differences in anesthetic management may have influenced baseline MEP amplitudes, monitorability rate, alarm rates, rescue rate, and thus the overall diagnostic performance of specific MEP thresholds. Because the available data were not sufficiently standardized, a formal subgroup analysis according to anesthetic protocol was not feasible. The diversity in the anesthesia protocol and reporting should be considered when interpreting the presented data in the clinical scenario.

Moreover, the analyzed MEP alarm thresholds were not identified from prospectively collected continuous data but rather constituted preestablished values applied in each study included in the meta-analysis. The pooled 70%/80%-level high-threshold analysis should also be interpreted as exploratory, because it was performed to reduce instability related to the small number of studies classified into the 80%-level MEP amplitude decline category rather than to define a new prespecified alarm threshold. Consequently, the performed accuracy calculations did not aim to define a single optimal MEP alarm threshold, but rather to compare the diagnostic performance of various preestablished MEP alarm thresholds used in the available studies. Prospective studies would be necessary to externally validate whether any specific MEP alarm threshold should guide surgical decision-making in IDEM tumor surgery. In addition, variation in patient numbers, tumor histology, tumor location, and the level of detail regarding postoperative deficit type across MEP alarm-threshold groups may have influenced the results. In light of these preliminary data, future higher-level evidence studies are needed to confirm their validity in daily clinical practice. Despite the overall high sensitivity and specificity of MEP for predicting motor deficits and mIONM for predicting neurological deficits, future large prospective cohorts are required to determine whether a lower MEP alarm threshold, such as 50%-level amplitude decline, improves diagnostic performance without increasing clinically irrelevant FP alerts.

Conclusions

Studies classified into the 50%-level MEP amplitude decline category demonstrated the most favorable diagnostic performance for short-term motor deficit prediction in MEP-based analyses and favorable diagnostic estimates within mIONM, although formal testing of short-term mIONM performance was not available. However, the 70%-level threshold and the pooled 70%/80%-level high-threshold group also showed significant diagnostic performance, indicating that higher thresholds retain diagnostic utility when analyzed collectively or in sufficiently powered groups. Given the differences in sample size, tumor location, histology, and outcome reporting between threshold groups, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered definitive evidence of a universally optimal alarm criterion. Prospective, anatomically and histologically stratified studies are required to externally validate whether a 50%-level MEP amplitude decline threshold should guide surgical decision-making during IDEM tumor resection.
