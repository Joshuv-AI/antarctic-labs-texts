Indirect Interactions Driven by Soil Effects Enable Coexistence Among Competing Plant Species
Authors: Ezequiel Antorán, Jaime Madrigal‐González, Rubén Bernardo‐Madrid, Miguel Á. Fernández‐Martínez, Marcelino de la Cruz, Joaquín Calatayud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTReciprocal effects between plants and soil have been proposed as mechanisms that promote coexistence. However, recent theoretical and empirical works have questioned their role in stabilizing coexistence within multispecies communities. In these systems, soil‐mediated indirect interactions may play a pivotal but often overlooked role. We investigate these indirect interactions using an experimental system of two competing shrub species grown in soil conditioned by a mediating third tree species. Tree‐induced shifts in soil microbial communities, metabolites and nutrients boost growth in the weak competitor species while reducing germination in the dominant competitor. Simulations demonstrate that these shifts in plant performance are sufficient to stabilize coexistence, producing simulated spatial distributions consistent with those observed in natural communities. Our findings underscore the role of soil effects in driving indirect interactions that sustain coexistence in diverse plant communities, highlighting the importance of indirect but also positive and negative multitrophic interactions in maintaining biodiversity.

Introduction

How can plants that use similar resources coexist? The long‐standing belief that species competing for the same resources cannot stably coexist has shaped ecological theory through the lens of the competitive exclusion paradigm (Hardin 1960). Yet, plants are not merely resource consumers—they actively modify soil properties and nutrient dynamics through interactions with microbial communities, mediated by processes such as nutrient uptake, litter deposition, root exudation and allelopathy (Wardle et al. 2004; Ehrenfeld et al. 2005; Lebeis et al. 2012; Fujii et al. 2018). These soil modifications can, in turn, strongly influence germination, growth and reproduction (Bonanomi et al. 2005; Veen et al. 2019; Weyens et al. 2009), ultimately shaping individual fitness (Bever et al. 2012; Lau and Lennon 2012). Moreover, plant‐induced changes in soil conditions can affect the performance of neighbouring species, thereby shaping competitive and facilitative interactions within plant communities (van der Putten et al. 2013; Rodríguez‐Echeverría et al. 2013; van de Voorde et al. 2011). These reciprocal interactions—known as plant–soil feedbacks—are increasingly viewed as a potential mechanism for maintaining biodiversity. However, despite their conceptual appeal, both empirical and theoretical evidence remain mixed regarding their role in promoting species coexistence. While some studies suggest that negative feedbacks can limit dominance and facilitate coexistence (Crawford et al. 2019; Mack et al. 2019; van der Putten et al. 2013; Teste et al. 2017; Willing et al. 2024), others indicate that interspecific plant–soil feedbacks alone may be insufficient to maintain coexistence in multispecies communities (Miller et al. 2022; Pajares‐Murgó et al. 2024).

Multispecies communities are characterized by pervasive indirect interactions, which occur when the dynamics between two species are influenced by the presence of a third (Wootton 1994). These interactions significantly affect plant performance (Levine 1999, 2000), influencing competitive and facilitative processes, with far‐reaching implications for community stability and species coexistence (Allesina and Levine 2011; Gibbs et al. 2022; Levine et al. 2017; Molina‐Venegas et al. 2025). Despite the recognized importance of indirect interactions in community dynamics, much of the current understanding of plant–soil feedback and its role in species coexistence stems from studies that isolate single‐species or pairwise interactions (Bever et al. 1997; Peay 2018; Scheublin et al. 2007; Veen et al. 2019; Willing et al. 2024). This focused approach, while informative, inherently overlooks the web of indirect effects that are intrinsic to multispecies communities, potentially contributing to the lack of consensus on the role of plant‐driven soil modifications in promoting species coexistence in natural communities (Mack et al. 2019; Miller et al. 2022; Pajares‐Murgó et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2020). Stabilizing indirect interactions mediated by interspecific plant–soil feedback would entail that shifts in soil biotic and abiotic characteristics caused by one species drive demographic shifts in other plant species that foster coexistence. For instance, soil modifications by a third species may reduce the fitness of dominant competitors, thereby enhancing the persistence of weaker competitors and promoting stable coexistence. These soil modifications by a third species can also create spatial heterogeneity, which is widely recognized as an important factor promoting species coexistence (Amarasekare 2003; Holt 1984). Therefore, unravelling the role of plant–soil feedback in shaping multispecies coexistence requires a comprehensive investigation of its effects through the lens of indirect interactions.

To explore the role of indirect interactions mediated by soil shifts in promoting multispecies coexistence, we investigated an experimental system involving three woody species. Specifically, we examine how soil modifications caused by a mediating tree species (Quercus pyrenaica Willd.) influence the competitive dynamics between two closely related shrub species ( Cistus ladanifer L. and Cistus laurifolius L.). These shrubs are broadly distributed and dominate many shrublands across the Iberian Peninsula (Fernández‐Mazuecos and Vargas 2010). Throughout much of the Mediterranean region, C. ladanifer often dominates, forming nearly monospecific stands. In these areas, the locally rare or weak competitor, C. laurifolius , tends to occur in the presence of Q. pyrenaica (Castroviejo 2020; See Appendix S1 and Figure S1). Quercus species are particularly relevant in this context, as they are well known to induce strong soil modifications through root exudation and litter deposition, with extensive evidence documented for several species of the genus (Aponte et al. 2011; Lasa et al. 2019). This makes Q. pyrenaica an ideal focal species for studying the effects of interspecific plant–soil feedback. Hence, this system provides a relatively simple yet ecologically relevant context to investigate potential indirect mechanisms mediated by soil shifts in stabilizing species coexistence. Its simplicity allows us to perform controlled and detailed experiments while its broad natural distribution ensures that experimental findings can be validated with field observations. By leveraging this widely distributed but experimentally tractable system, our study aims to uncover the soil‐mediated indirect interactions that may underpin coexistence in plant communities.

Results and Discussion

Effects of Soil Conditioning by the Mediating Species on Germination

The majority of evidence linking species coexistence to reciprocal plant–soil interactions is based on their effects on plant growth (Crawford et al. 2019; Teste et al. 2017). Yet, shifts in soil properties are also expected to influence critical demographic plant parameters that may facilitate coexistence beyond growth, such as germination (Dudenhöffer et al. 2018). Therefore, we initially investigated whether soil conditioned by the mediating species affects the germination of the two competing species. To facilitate the link between experimental and observational evidence, we used naturally occurring soils from our study system that were undoubtedly conditioned by a single target species: either Quercus pyrenaica (the mediating species) or Cistus ladanifer (the locally dominant competitor). In addition, we included soils unconditioned by woody species as control soils (see Appendix S2). The germination of the dominant species is significantly reduced in the influence of the mediating species' soil compared to its own soil (Z = 2.55; p = 0.033; Figure 1A; Table S1). Comparison with the control soil reveals that the significantly reduced germination arises from a combination of increased germination in the dominant species' soil and decreased germination in the mediating species' soil (Figure 1A). In contrast, the germination of the weak competitor remains similar across all treatments (p > 0.05 always; Figure 1B and Table S1). These findings show that the mediating species modifies the soil in ways that reduce the germination of the dominant competitor, without affecting the weak competitor. To determine whether these effects are primarily driven by soil biochemistry or living microorganisms, we compared germination responses in sterilized soils. Similar to above results, germination of the dominant competitor was significantly reduced in sterilized soils previously conditioned by the mediating species, compared to those conditioned by the dominant species (Z = 3.297; p = 0.01; Figure 1A). This suggests that the observed effects are likely mediated by shifts in soil biochemistry, such as the accumulation of allelochemicals produced by the mediating species (Meiners et al. 2017). Together, these findings indicate that biochemical soil modifications induced by the mediating species can suppress the dominant competitor, potentially promoting coexistence by reducing competitive asymmetries.

Effects of Soil Conditioning by the Mediating Species on Plant Growth

Although the observed changes in germination may contribute to the coexistence of these competitors, the germination rates of the dominant competitor remain higher than those of the weak competitor (Figure 1B), suggesting that additional mechanisms are involved. Plant–soil feedback is well‐known to affect plant growth, which might also contribute to species performance and coexistence (Crawford et al. 2019; Teste et al. 2017). We therefore investigated whether soil conditioned by the mediating species affects the growth of these closely related species. In contrast to germination, no significant differences are observed in the growth of the dominant competitor across all soil treatments (p > 0.05 in all cases; Figure 2A; Table S2). However, our results reveal that the growth of the weak competitor is significantly enhanced in the mediating species' soil compared to the control and dominant species' soils (p < 0.01 in both cases; Figure 2B; Table S2). Because larger seedlings generally experience lower mortality in semiarid environments (Andivia et al. 2021), this increase in growth likely promotes the persistence of the weak competitor under Mediterranean semiarid conditions. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that plant–soil feedback can drive demographic shifts by influencing distinct stages of plant ontogeny (Dudenhöffer et al. 2018). More importantly, the reduced germination of the dominant species, coupled with the enhanced growth of the weak competitor in soil conditioned by the mediating species, is expected to promote the coexistence of both competitors. These findings underscore how soil‐mediated positive and negative interactions can support biodiversity.

To gain deeper insights, we explored whether growth changes are driven solely by soil biochemistry or also involve shifts in microbial communities. To address this, we conducted a growth experiment using sterilized soils paired with water‐based microbial inoculum derived from live soils in a cross‐experimental design (see Methods). In the dominant competitor, a significant enhancement in growth was observed only when plants were grown in soils conditioned by the mediating species and inoculated with the microbiome of the dominant species. This treatment produced more biomass than both treatments grown in the dominant species' soil, regardless of the microbial inoculum (p < 0.05 for both comparisons; Figure 2C; Table S2). Thus, the dominant competitor appears to benefit from increased nutrient availability in soils conditioned by the mediating species (see Appendix S4 and Figure S2) only when its native soil microorganisms are present. This combination is unlikely to occur under natural conditions because soils conditioned by the mediating species should also carry its characteristic microbial community, consistent with the lack of effects observed in untreated live soils. Similarly, the locally weak competitor exhibits significantly enhanced growth in soils conditioned by the mediating species when paired with microorganisms associated with the mediating species compared with all other treatments (p < 0.05; Figure 2C; Table S2). This experimental setup, which mimics natural conditions, reveals that the enhanced growth of the weaker competitor in soils conditioned by the mediating species arises from the combined effects of shifts in soil biochemistry and microbial communities. Hence, plant growth seems to depend not only on soil chemical properties but also on specific microbial communities to which plant species are likely adapted (Lau and Lennon 2011). Importantly, our findings suggest that shifts in both soil biochemistry and microbial communities are necessary to facilitate the coexistence of these closely related species.

Experimentally Based Expectations Align With the Spatial Organization of Natural Communities

Experimental evidence is most compelling when it directly links to patterns observed in natural communities. If the mediating species modifies soil properties in ways that influence the germination and growth of the competing shrub species, thereby shaping their coexistence, we would expect that: (i) individuals of the mediating species alter both biotic and abiotic soil properties at fine spatial scales, and (ii) at this scale, the dominant and the weak competitor species tend to segregate from and aggregate to the mediating species, respectively. Supporting the first expectation, proximity to individuals of the mediating species significantly shifts the composition of soil nutrients (R 2 = 7.84%; F = 4.08; p < 0.01), metabolites (R 2 = 4.06%; F = 2.03; p < 0.01) and bacterial (R 2 = 5.02%; F = 2.54; p < 0.01) and fungal (R 2 = 3.14%; F = 1.55; p < 0.01) communities. These effects are not linear but occur within short distances from the individuals, with compositional dissimilarities reaching their maximum at approximately 5.73, 6.28, 3.87 and 3.82 m for nutrients, metabolites, bacterial and fungal compositions, respectively (Figure 3A–D). The changes in soil composition at short distances from the tree trunk correspond to areas that remain largely covered by tree litter (Figure S3) throughout most of an individual's lifespan (average age 36.6 years, Appendix S5) and probably characterized by a denser root system. Such conditions strongly suggest that the combination of litter decomposition and root exudations are driving the pronounced modifications in soil composition observed near the tree individuals. This aligns with evidence showing that Quercus spp. strongly modify soil chemical, and biological properties (Aponte et al. 2011; Lasa et al. 2019). Our findings also support the second expectation: the abundance of the locally dominant competitor increases with distance from the individuals of the mediating species (R 2 = 13.6%; F = 24.23; p < 0.01; Figure 3E), whereas the locally weak competitor follows the opposite trend (R 2 = 3.03%; F = 7.53; p < 0.01). This spatial replacement pattern is evident, with the weak competitor prevailing within approximately 3 m from the mediating species and the dominant competitor dominating beyond this distance (Figure 3E). Focusing on species cover instead of individual counts yields similar results (Figure S4). Although the specific soil microorganisms and chemical compounds driving these patterns remain unidentified, the fine‐scale spatial structure of soil properties and species distribution closely aligns with experimental‐based expectations. This supports the idea that soil modifications by mediating species play a crucial role in promoting the coexistence of competing species.

Simulations Predict Spatial Patterns and Stable Coexistence of the Competing Species

Definitive evidence for the role of soil‐mediated indirect interactions in stabilizing coexistence would require experimental data on all relevant demographic parameters (Dudenhöffer et al. 2018). However, obtaining such data—For example, seed production—in the studied woody species is largely unfeasible due to their long‐life cycles and the challenges of maintaining realistic yet fully controlled experimental conditions over extended timescales. We therefore used numerical simulations parameterized with experimental results to assess whether observed changes in germination and growth are sufficient to enable the stable coexistence of both shrub species (see Methods and Appendix S6). We used experimentally derived germination data and inferred survival probabilities from observed growth responses in conditioned soils. This inference was based on the well‐supported assumption that increased growth enhances survival (Andivia et al. 2021).

Our simulations showed that the combination of measured germination probabilities and a slight increase of 0.08 in the survival probability of the weak competitor in soil conditioned by the mediating species—compared to soil conditioned by the dominant competitor—was sufficient to reproduce a spatial abundance pattern closely matching that observed in natural communities (averaged R 2 = 0.92; average RMSD = 0.313; compare Figure 4A and Figure 3E, see also Figure S5 and Table S3). Under this parameterization, both species also maintained stable population sizes over time (Figure 4B, Table S3), establishing a link between the demographic effects of soil modifications by the mediating species, the spatial distribution of the shrub species, and their long‐term coexistence.

Similar to field observations (Figure 3E), the dominant competitor increases in abundance with distance from the mediating species, while the weak competitor aggregates near it (R 2 = 36%; F = 619.5; p < 0.01 for the dominant competitor and R 2 = 22%; F = 389.3; p < 0.01 for the weak competitor). Sensitivity analyses varying the soil‐conditioning extent consistently yielded stable coexistence driven by relatively modest increases in survival probabilities (0.054–0.141) associated with enhanced growth (Appendix S7 and Table S4). Nevertheless, the link between plant size and survival should be interpreted with caution, as the growth experiment spanned only 60 days and therefore captured seedling responses, leaving uncertainty about how these short‐term, soil‐mediated growth differences translate into long‐term survival. Similarly, soil modifications by the mediating species might also influence reproduction (Dudenhöffer et al. 2018) or even the competitive process itself (Willing et al. 2024). Experimental approaches spanning the complete lifespan of the shrub species and incorporating direct competition trials are required to address these questions and to fully disentangle the intricate interplay between competitive interactions and the facilitative and negative effects of soil‐mediated interactions shaping species coexistence. Additionally, the mediating species could also affect competitive dynamics through other mechanisms, such as asymmetric shading or differential overlap in resource use with the shrub species. Nonetheless, our results suggest that only the combined effect of soil‐mediated reductions in germination of the dominant competitor and enhanced growth in the weaker competitor is sufficient to reverse competitive hierarchies and enable coexistence. We also acknowledge that the dynamics of the mediating species were not explicitly included in our framework, and therefore the long‐term coexistence of the shrub species will be conditioned by the longer‐term demographic dynamics of the mediating species. However, Quercus trees are far more long‐lived than the shrubs, with the lifespan of a single tree spanning several shrub generations, which likely explains the congruence between our simulations and the observed field patterns where living trees are present. Overall, these results highlight the role of soil‐mediated indirect interactions in modulating competitive dynamics and underscore their importance in driving species coexistence.

Implications and Future Directions

Our findings provide mechanistic insight into how competing plant species can coexist through soil‐mediated indirect interactions. Acting as mediators, certain plant species can modify both biotic and abiotic soil components in ways that alter competitive dynamics, reducing the performance of dominant competitors while enhancing that of weaker ones. Together, these soil‐mediated effects generate a combination of positive and negative interactions that operate beyond direct competition and can play a central role in structuring species coexistence. Such processes may underlie observational patterns in which rare species more frequently spatially aggregate with nurse plants (Soliveres et al. 2015) or cluster with other rare species (Calatayud et al. 2020), providing a mechanistic basis for general patterns observed in ecological communities that are presumably linked to species coexistence.

Several open questions remain. While our results demonstrate that soil‐mediated indirect effects associated with a mediating species can facilitate species coexistence, the specific processes responsible for altering soil properties—such as root exudation, litter deposition or the timescales over which soil modifications accumulate—remain unresolved. Similarly, because soils were pooled within species prior to the experiment, some among‐individual or spatial variability in soil‐mediated effects may have been reduced, potentially amplifying the influence of rare but strong soil biota (Clark et al. 2025; Smith‐Ramesh and Reynolds 2017). In addition, the long‐term demographic dynamics of the mediating species, as well as potential soil feedbacks from the weaker competitor and their effects on competitive dynamics, warrant further investigation. Our results further suggest that soil modification by the mediating species can reverse competitive hierarchies between the shrub species, a pattern characteristic of coexistence mediated by spatial heterogeneity (Amarasekare 2003; Holt 1984). However, because both shrub species appear to coexist only in soils modified by the mediating species, it remains unclear whether coexistence is primarily driven by the spatial heterogeneity generated by the mediating species or by soil shifts per se, independent of their spatial configuration. Future research should assess whether these dynamics extend to more diverse communities, explore how species differentially respond to microbial communities and soil biochemistry, and evaluate whether soil‐mediated effects are maintained across species ranges and environmental conditions. Such efforts will provide deeper insight into the eco‐evolutionary pathways through which plant–soil feedbacks mediate coexistence, with important implications for understanding how species interactions shape species distributions (Kissling and Schleuning 2015).

More broadly, our study highlight the need to integrate indirect and multitrophic interactions (Allesina and Levine 2011; Chesson and Kuang 2008; García‐Callejas et al. 2023; Molina‐Venegas et al. 2025)—such as those between plants and soil microbes—into coexistence theory, moving beyond a framework centred on direct competition (Chesson 2000). Our results further emphasize that both positive and negative indirect interactions play a fundamental role in promoting species coexistence and sustaining biodiversity (Buche et al. 2025; Calatayud et al. 2020; Molina‐Venegas et al. 2025). Expanding coexistence theory to incorporate the indirect as well as positive and negative interactions will help resolve the diversity paradox, reframing species‐rich communities as the expected outcome of complex ecological interactions rather than an exception (Simha et al. 2022). Overall, our findings reinforce the view that the maintenance of biodiversity is shaped not only from competition but from a web of interacting forces, where soil‐mediated positive and negative indirect effects can play a central role in stabilizing species‐rich plant communities.

Methods

Study Area, Soil and Seed Collection and Treatments

We used two closely related mediterranean shrub species ( Cistus ladanifer and Cistus laurifolius) and a Quercus tree (Quercus pyrenaica) as our experimental system. Given their close phylogenetic relationship, C. ladanifer and C. laurifolius are expected to engage in strong interspecific competition (Violle et al. 2011). Based on local species' abundances, we define C. ladanifer as the dominant competitor, and C. laurifolius as the weak competitor. Quercus pyrenaica is considered the mediating species, as its presence is associated with the occurrence of C. laurifolius within a study area otherwise dominated by C. ladanifer (Appendix S1 and Figure S1). The study area is located at the south of the Sistema Central mountain range in central Spain, at an elevation of 940–1050 m a.s.l. (40°26′ 46″N, 3°42′ 36″W). It features a continental Mediterranean climate characterized by cold winters and mild, dry summers.

Fruits of wild specimens of both Cistus species were collected between September and October 2022 from 10 individuals per species, randomly selected across the study area. After collection, seeds were separated from the fruits, air‐dried under ambient conditions and pooled by species. Immediately before experiments, seeds were exposed to a heating treatment, 100°C during 5 min (Valbuena et al. 1992). Soils were collected from three categories based on vegetation context: areas dominated by C. ladanifer , soils conditioned by Q. pyrenaica, and an area without woody vegetation for over 50 years (see Appendix S2). To separate biotic and abiotic soil effects, subsamples were sterilized by gamma irradiation, and aqueous soil inocula were prepared from both live and sterilized soils following standardized protocols. Detailed methodological procedures are provided in Appendix S2.

Germination Experimental Design and Analysis

To investigate soil effects on the germination of both Cistus species we conducted a controlled germination experiment using five soil treatments. Each treatment involved a soil aqueous inoculum derived from different soil types: (i) soil conditioned by the mediating species, (ii) soil conditioned by the locally dominant species, (iii) unconditioned control soil, (iv) sterilized soil conditioned by the mediating species and (v) sterilized soil conditioned by the dominant species. For each treatment, 10 mL of the corresponding inoculum was pipetted onto the Petri dishes before seed placement. After inoculation, 20 seeds of a single Cistus species were evenly distributed on a moistened filter paper. We established five replicate dishes per treatment. A total of 1000 seeds were used in the experiment (20 seeds × 5 Petri dishes × 5 treatments × 2 species). Petri dishes were incubated in a phytotron under controlled conditions (20°C, 14 h light; 16°C, 10 h dark) for 30 days (April–May 2023). Germination (radicle emergence) was recorded every 3 days, distilled water was replenished as needed, and dishes were fully randomized at each census.

Cumulative germination over time was modelled for each species and soil treatment using a three‐parameter logistic nonlinear mixed‐effects model (see Appendix S3), with Petri dish as a random effect. Models adequately captured sigmoidal germination patterns (median pseudo‐R 2 = 83%; Table S5, Figure S6) and results were consistent using the Gompertz model (Appendix S3); analyses focused on the maximum germination parameter (K), with pairwise treatment comparisons conducted using Z‐tests and FDR‐corrected p‐values, excluding direct comparisons between live and sterilized soils because gamma sterilization altered soil nutrient properties (McNamara et al. 2003). All models were fitted in R (R Core Team 2025) using the nlme package (Pinheiro et al. 2022).

Growth Experimental Design and Analysis

We conducted common garden experiments to explore the effects of soil on plant growth. The experiment consisted of two steps. First, we tested the influence of three provenances of live soils on growth: (i) soils conditioned by the mediating species, (ii) soils conditioned by the dominant species and (iii) control soils. Second, we designed a cross‐treatment experiment to disentangle the individual and combined effects of soil biochemistry and living microorganisms. The cross‐treatment experiment consisted of a total of four treatments, in which sterilized soils of the mediating and dominant species were supplemented with aqueous live‐soil inoculums of the mediating and dominant species to restore microbiological activity: (i) sterilized soil of the mediating species + aqueous inoculum of the mediating species; (ii) sterilized soil of the mediating species + aqueous inoculum of the dominant species; (iii) sterilized soil of the dominant species + aqueous inoculum of the mediating species; (iv) sterilized soil of the dominant species + aqueous inoculum of the dominant species.

In common garden experiments, pre‐heated seeds were sown in sterilized 3.5 × 3.5 cm pots (five seeds per 58 cc pot), with one randomly selected seedling retained after germination. Microbiome cross‐treatments were applied by adding 58 mL of soil aqueous inoculum prior to sowing. We used 20 replicates per each of the seven treatment and two species (280 individuals total), grown for 60 days (April to May) under controlled greenhouse conditions (22°C, 14 h of 230 L μmol m−2 s−1 LED lighting, 46 g m−3 humidity) with automated nebulized irrigation (2 min 3 times daily) and periodic pot randomization. Aboveground biomass was harvested, oven‐dried (60°C, 48 h, Cornelissen et al. 2003) and weighed. Treatment effects were analysed using linear models on log‐transformed biomass with Tukey‐corrected marginal mean comparisons as implemented in the R package emmeans (Lenth 2025).

Field Observations

We further assessed whether the experimental results align with the spatial distribution patterns of the competing shrub species relative to the mediating species observed in natural communities. For this, we performed vegetation inventories around Q. pyrenaica individuals within a ca. 2‐ha area of the study area. Ten adult trees (average age 36.6 years, average DBH 45.9 cm, Appendix S5) were randomly selected, ensuring a minimum distance of 20 m from other Q. pyrenaica individuals. From the base of each selected tree, a 10 × 1 m transect was established in a random direction and subdivided into ten 1 × 1 m sections, where abundance and cover of each Cistus species were recorded. Variation in abundance and cover with distance from Q. pyrenaica was analysed using generalized additive mixed models with Poisson error distribution, a log link function, and tree identity as a random effect, implemented in the mgcv package in R (Wood 2011).

To explore the influence of the mediating species on soil nutrients, metabolites composition and microbial communities, we collected soil samples along the same transects. Sampling was conducted at five points along each transect, beginning at the base of the trunk (0 m) and extending up to 10 m, with a separation of 2.5 m. Soil samples were subsequently analyzed to determine their nutrient, metabolite, and microbial (bacteria and fungi) composition; detailed methodological procedures are provided in Appendix S8.

To assess differences in microbial communities, nutrients and metabolites in relation to tree proximity, we first calculated compositional distances across all soil samples. For microbial communities, we computed Jaccard distances separately for bacterial and fungal ASVs, choosing this presence/absence‐based dissimilarity index to mitigate issues associated with compositional data of ASV counts (Gloor et al. 2017). We excluded taxa present in fewer than five samples, though including these taxa yielded similar results (Appendix S9 and Figure S7). For nutrients and metabolite compositional differences, we used Euclidean distances. We conducted permutational multivariate analysis of variance on compositional distances as a function of spatial distance, as implemented in the vegan R package (Oksanen et al. 2025), incorporating tree identity as a stratification factor. Additionally, we examined where the strongest compositional differences occurred in relation to tree proximity. Specifically, we compared the compositional distances between soil sampled directly beneath tree individuals (0 m) and all the other samples. In this case, we fitted GAMMs of compositional distances as a function of spatial distances, including tree identity as a random factor. We identified turning points as locations where the first derivative of the fitted curve crossed zero.

Individual‐Based Model

We developed an individual‐based model to test whether experimentally observed soil‐mediated demographic differences are sufficient to enable stable coexistence of two competing Cistus species. The model was implemented on a 50 × 50 lattice of 1 m2 cells. We randomly placed 20 individuals of Quercus pyrenaica and assumed that their soil‐conditioning effects extended homogeneously within a circular area of 4 m radius around each individual, corresponding to the maximum distance at which consistent changes in microbial communities, metabolite composition and nutrient availability were observed. A sensitivity analysis using alternative influence radii yielded qualitatively similar results (Appendix S7 and Table S4). We also randomly placed 100 individuals of each competing Cistus species. In cells influenced by Q. pyrenaica, germination and size were assigned according to estimates from Q. pyrenaica–conditioned soils, whereas all other cells followed estimates from Cistus ladanifer –conditioned soils.

Reproduction was simplified by assuming that each adult individual produced one viable seed per year. Seeds were dispersed following a negative exponential kernel, mimicking the short‐range dispersal of the genus (Bastida and Talavera 2002). Germination occurred only when local density did not exceed the carrying capacity (2 individuals m−2), preventing unbounded population growth. Germination probabilities were drawn annually from species × soil–specific uniform distributions bounded by experimental 95% confidence intervals (Table S4).

We assumed that soil effects on survival are mediated through plant size following Andivia et al. (2021). Plant size values were drawn from species × soil–specific uniform distributions based on experimental measurements, using the same 95% confidence intervals. Survival was then modelled as size‐dependent following a logistic function: Pssize=11+e−x+ysize where x = 1.386 yields a basal survival probability of 0.8, consistent with the observed longevity of Cistus species (Oria‐de‐Rueda et al. 2008). The parameter y modulates the effect of plant size on survival; y = 0 implies size‐independent survival, while higher values increase the positive effect of size. Senescence was implicitly included, as individuals were removed from the lattice once annual survival draws failed. Because the size–survival relationship is unknown, we explored 21 values of y (0–2, step = 0.1). For each competing species and soil type, annual survival probabilities were drawn from uniform distributions bounded by the logistic predictions within experimentally observed size ranges (Table S3).

For each value of the size–survival parameter y, we ran 25 simulations of 100 years. All model specifications are detailed in Table S6. We selected the value of y that produced simulated spatial abundance patterns most similar to those observed in the field (Appendix S6 and Figure S5). Under this parameterization, we then assessed whether both competing Cistus species persisted and maintained stable population sizes, which we interpreted as evidence of stable coexistence. See Appendix S6 for a detailed explanation of the simulations and analyses of model output.
