Exploring Public Perceptions of Telenursing: A Q Methodology Study
Authors: Ick‐Jee Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo investigate public perceptions of telenursing in South Korea, an Asian country that is transitioning to the introduction of telenursing and possesses a high level of information and communication technology. This study sought to uncover strategies for the successful introduction and extension of telenursing by identifying the characteristics of existing perceptions.DesignQ methodology was applied.MethodsA total of 188 Q populations were selected from the literature, Internet sources, newspaper articles and in‐depth interviews about telenursing, from which the final 37 Q statements were derived. Forty‐nine people who were expected to have diverse perspectives about telenursing were selected purposively and participated in Q‐sorting. The data were obtained from November 2022 to December 2022 and analysed with principal component analysis and varimax rotations using PQMethod version 2.11.ResultsFive types of perceptions regarding telenursing in South Korea were identified: ‘Factor I: Preference for convenience and efficiency of telenursing, Active acceptance’, ‘Factor II: Complete support for telenursing, Total acceptance’, ‘Factor III: Complete skepticism for telenursing, Total nonacceptance’, ‘Factor IV: Expectance of the supplementary roles of telenursing, Immediate acceptance’ and ‘Factor V: Expectance of the supplementary roles of telenursing, Prolonged acceptance’.Patient or Public ContributionPatients and the public were not involved in this study's design, recruitment and implementation.Keywords: perception, public, Q methodology, telenursing

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic has given rise to many changes in the field of nursing (Chandra et al. 2022; Khan et al. 2020; Kord et al. 2021). The surge in patients has overburdened nurses, further exacerbating the chronic nursing shortage (Auerbach et al. 2024; Ge et al. 2023; Watson et al. 2024). Additionally, the demand for non‐contact nursing services has arisen to minimise the risk of infection transmission. For patients located far from facilities, strategies have been required to overcome challenges regarding accessibility to nursing care, transcending the limitations of time and space (Kord et al. 2021; Purabdollah and Ghasempour 2020).

In recent years, with the development of information and communications technology (ICT), telenursing has attracted considerable attention as a means of effectively responding to these new demands. Telenursing refers to nursing services delivered remotely using ICT. This can be achieved through various technologies, such as wearable devices, phones, short message services, video, email and the Internet (Cloyd and Thompson 2020; Kumar 2011; Kumar and Snooks 2011). Telenursing enables nurses to provide efficient, high‐quality nursing services. In other words, nurses can respond to the diverse needs of patients through remote care and provide nursing services without the constraints of time and distance (Asimakopoulou 2020; Purabdollah and Ghasempour 2020; Siriwardhana et al. 2021). Furthermore, telenursing can reduce the burden of excessive workload on nurses since it can be utilised as a means to support their work (Ramelet et al. 2017; Schwartz et al. 2024; Shany et al. 2017).

Accordingly, telenursing can serve as a useful tool with which to respond to the rapidly changing nursing paradigm and is predicted to become a new avenue for expanding the nursing realm in the digital era (Afik and Pandin 2021; Cloyd and Thompson 2020). Successful telenursing is expected to become a growth engine for future nursing development.

To successfully introduce telenursing, it is first necessary to deeply explore the public's perceptions of it. The introduction of new systems requires sufficient agreement among the members of a society and, therefore, depends on individual perceptions. Consequently, this study aimed to explore public perceptions of telenursing prior to its introduction.

This study was set in South Korea because it is a country with advanced ICT and commercialised 5th generation mobile network (5G) services (Kim et al. 2020; Massaro and Kim 2022). Such advanced ICT has greatly changed the nursing environment in South Korea. Currently, the country is beginning to introduce telenursing; therefore, it was judged that there would be many individuals in South Korea who could offer in‐depth opinions about this practice.

Previous studies investigating perceptions of telenursing have been conducted among nursing students (Glinkowski, Pawłowska, and Kozłowska 2013; Kim, Kim, and Jeon 2021; Poreddi et al. 2021). However, research conducted with the public is lacking, and no study has explored the public's perception of telenursing using the Q methodology. Confirming subjectivity, based on the types of public perception of telenursing, will be useful for policy formulation to ensure its future acceptance and expansion. This study's results can be used as a basis to develop strategies for the introduction and successful establishment of telenursing in countries with similar backgrounds to South Korea, namely, those that are in the transitional period of introducing telenursing and possess advanced ICT.

Methods

Q Methodology was applied to explore societal perceptions. The Q methodology, created by Stephenson in 1953, is a powerful method for researching human subjectivity and an approach to understand inner human experience (Brown 1996). This method is a useful tool that combines qualitative and quantitative research methods to enable the systematic investigation of human subjectivity, such as similarities or differences in individuals' perceptions, beliefs and attitudes towards specific issues (Brown 1996; Watts and Stenner 2012).

Q methodology was adopted because telenursing is a new concept within the field of nursing and there may be individual differences in how the public perceives it. The researcher expected the various beliefs, opinions and ideas of the public regarding telenursing to be understood more objectively through the Q method.

Research Design

In this study, the Q methodology was applied to explore public perceptions of telenursing. The procedure for applying the Q methodology is as follows: (1) generate a ‘concourse’ and develop ‘Q‐set’, (2) select a ‘P‐sample’, (3) conduct the Q‐sort, (4) analyse data and (5) extract and interpret factors (Figure 1).

Q‐methodology steps in this study.

Step 1: Generating a Concourse and Developing Q‐Set

A concourse is a collection of all the statements surrounding a topic. This should include all the possible statements that respondents can make about the topic (Van Exel and De Graaf 2005). A concourse is generated through various methods, such as literature reviews, interviews, expert opinions, Internet data and newspapers. Q‐statements derived from the concourse should cover the subject matter as completely as possible. In other words, the main purpose of the Q‐statement design is to generate a series of items that thoroughly cover the research topic (Brown 1996). Q statements from a range of sources expressing thoughts, attitudes and perceptions of telenursing were collected through the following process.

In the first stage, 93 sentences were derived by reviewing previous studies, Internet data and newspaper articles related to ‘telehealth’, ‘telenursing’, ‘telemedicine’, ‘smart healthcare’ and ‘digital healthcare’. Subsequently, 95 sentences were derived by conducting in‐depth interviews on telenursing with four individuals from different backgrounds, including sex, age and social position. During the interviews, open‐ended questions regarding telenursing were used to elicit diverse opinions from participants, such as: ‘What do you think about telenursing?’ The interviews continued until data saturation was reached. Of the 188 sentences collected, 151 were selected by removing redundant sentences. The researcher reviewed the concourse of 151 statements several times with a nursing professor who had a thorough understanding of the research theme and a Q methodologist who had conducted many Q studies. Next, 46 representative sentences were selected from the 151 sentences; this was reduced to 40 sentences and, finally, 37 sentences were extracted.

It can be said that these last 37 sentences are as comprehensive as possible when it comes to capturing public opinion on telenursing (Table 1).

Pilot studies were conducted with two participants. Sentences that were too long or difficult to understand were modified and complemented, by using simple vocabulary among others. Reliability of Q‐sorting is considered sufficient if the correlation coefficients obtained by two sorts (a test–retest procedure) is 0.80 or more (Brown 1980). In this study, the average r value was 0.8117 based on two participants conducting a test–retest procedure. Therefore, the reliability was confirmed to be appropriate.

Step 2: Selecting P‐Samples

The key issue in a Q‐study is to find various perspectives on the research theme. Therefore, the number of participants was not limited (Brown 1980). It is usually considered that 40–60 participants are appropriate for discovering various perspectives in Q methodological studies (Bartlett and DeWeese 2015; Watts and Stenner 2005). Thus, 49 participants were included in this study. To include individuals with various viewpoints about telenursing, the researchers intentionally selected participants considering various distributions, such as sex, age, education, religion, residential area, interest in ICT, ICT utilisation and social position (Appendices S1 and S2).

Step 3: Q‐Sorting

A total of 37 Q cards with Q statements were forcedly distributed on the Q grid, ranging from ‘Strongly Disagree’ to ‘Strongly Agree’ based on participants' perceptions of telenursing. For the four statements placed at the extremes (+4, −4), the reasons for selection were described in writing analysis (Appendices S2 and S3).

Step 4: Data Analysis

The data were collected between November 2022 and December 2022 in South Korea. The collected data were processed by principal component analysis (PCA) and varimax rotation using PQMethod version 2.11. To determine the ideal number of factors, eigenvalues > 1, explanatory variances > 40% and correlations between factors were considered. Psychosocial situations related to these factors were also reflected. Public perceptions of telenursing in South Korea were divided into five types.

Step 5: Extracting and Interpreting Factors

When interpreting factors, factor arrays, anchor statements, distinguishing statements, participants' responses with the highest factor weights, and their responses to post‐sort questionnaires should be considered (Bartlett and DeWeese 2015). First, the researcher checked how the rankings of the 37 statements were assigned to each factor using factor arrays (Table 1). This confirms how each factor differentiates itself from the others. Anchor statements refer to statements at the +4, +3, −3 and −4 positions of each array and are the rationale for considering these statements as the most important properties of each factor. The researcher continued to differentiate between the factors by comparing the anchor statements for each factor. Distinguishing statements indicated statistically significant statements for each factor. Through these sentences, the unique attributes of each factor were identified more clearly. For each factor, the higher the weight of the factor, the more representative it is of that type. The researcher included participants' responses with the highest factor weights in factor analysis, with the result factor distinctions became clearer. Additionally, the participants' responses to the post‐sort questionnaires were considered when interpreting each factor.

Ethics

The Ethics Review Committee (REDACTED) approved this study (REDACTED). Prior to data collection the researcher explained the study's purpose, survey items and confidentiality to all the respondents who voluntarily agreed to participate. They were advised that they could quit at any time. All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Public perceptions regarding telenursing in South Korea were classified into five types: ‘Preference for convenience and efficiency of telenursing, Active acceptance’, ‘Complete support for telenursing, Total acceptance’, ‘Complete skepticism for telenursing, Total nonacceptance’, ‘Expectance of the supplementary roles of telenursing, Immediate acceptance’ and ‘Expectance of the supplementary roles of telenursing, Prolonged acceptance’.

The five factors explained 56% of the total variance: Factor I, 16%; Factor II, 13%; Factor III, 11%; Factor IV, 8% and Factor V, 8%. Of the 49 participants, 11 loaded on Factor I, seven Factor II, five Factor III, five Factor IV and five Factor V (Appendix S1). Sixteen respondents were not classified by any factor; therefore, they were excluded from the analysis. Among the correlations between factors, Factors I and IV were the highest at 0.4668 and that of the remaining factors, excluding this factor, were 0.3533 or less (Table 2).

Factor I: Preference for Convenience and Efficiency of Telenursing, Active Acceptance

Respondents who were loaded on Factor I highly valued the convenience of not being limited by space and cost‐effectiveness of telenursing, although they preferred existing face‐to‐face nursing (Q14, +4/Q22, +3). When telenursing is implemented, respondents generally intended to use it more than other types (Q13, +4). Compared with other types (Factors II–V), respondents in this type strongly agreed that subtle changes in the patient could be overlooked when using telenursing (Q12, +3) and exhibited relative agreement with the statement that telenursing may help solve the problem of nursing shortages (Q3, +2). They strongly disagreed that telenursing accelerated patient recovery (Q32, −4). They thought it was difficult to communicate and form trusting relationships between patients and nurses through telenursing (Q27, −4/Q20, −3). Furthermore, they strongly agreed that telenursing interventions may be insufficient in emergency cases (Q16, +3). However, they did not agree that telenursing increased the risk of infringement of personal information or privacy (Q4, −2/Q31, −3).

Participant 20 (female, 70 years old), who had the highest factor weight (0.7782) in Factor I, stated the following (Appendix S3):

Communication between nurses and patients in face‐to‐face nursing is more efficient than telenursing. However, telenursing will reduce time wasting and discomfort due to distance. Therefore, it will be very convenient to use.

The distinction between face‐to‐face nursing and telenursing falls under the category of nursing, and it is just a difference in the way nursing services are performed. Therefore, I do not think that telenursing will reduce the quality of nursing care.

I will actively use telenursing. It is a wise decision to introduce such a convenient and efficient system.

In summary, Factor I supported face‐to‐face nursing in principle, but actively utilised telenursing for convenience and efficiency. Therefore, Factor I was named ‘Preference for convenience and efficiency of telenursing, Active acceptance’.

Factor II: Complete Support for Telenursing, Total Acceptance

Respondents who were loaded on Factor II strongly agreed that telenursing would help to ensure the accessibility of healthcare services and improve an individual's quality of life (Q15, +4/Q7, +4). In addition, they agreed on the convenience of not being limited by space with telenursing (Q6, +3/Q14, +3) and that it would help patients live independently (Q18, +3). They strongly disagreed that telenursing would reduce nursing care quality and benefit some individuals only (Q 34, −4/Q33, −3). They also strongly disagreed that age is not a major problem in the use of telenursing (Q 25, −4). Compared with other factors (Factors I, III, IV and V), there was a strong and conspicuous opinion that telenursing could reduce economic and regional health inequalities (Q8, −3/Q23, −3). This was the only factor that disagreed with the opinion that individuals would be reluctant to use telenursing because of technical difficulties (Q10, −2).

Participant 4 (male, 43 years old), who had the highest factor weight (0.7985) in Factor II stated the following (Appendix S3):

Telenursing can be widely used by all classes regardless of economic level or area of residence because of its convenience and easy access. Therefore, the problem of health inequality will be resolved.

Telenursing has the advantage that it can be used in areas where medical services are difficult to access. Therefore, it is expected that the inequality in health care between urban and rural areas can be resolved due to telenursing.

Compared with face‐to‐face nursing, telenursing can be provided more conveniently at any time, regardless of distance. In addition, telenursing guarantees the users' own space. This enables users to be more comfortable when explaining their physical condition.

Telenursing can increase spatial and temporal accessibility. Therefore, telenursing will greatly contribute to increasing the accessibility of healthcare services for the population.

Summarising the above information comprehensively, Factor II predicted that telenursing would solve health inequality between urban and rural dwellers and universal health guarantees for the entire population would be possible. Individuals in Factor II were also completely optimistic about all other aspects of telenursing. Therefore, Factor II was named ‘Complete support for telenursing, Total acceptance’.

Factor III: Complete Scepticism for Telenursing, Total Nonacceptance

Respondents who were loaded on Factor III strongly preferred traditional face‐to‐face nursing (Q17, −4); therefore, they had a completely negative view of telenursing. They strongly agreed that telenursing was less safe than in‐person nursing (Q21, +4) and was the most prominent feature compared with other types (Factors I, II, IV and V). This type strongly disagreed that trust between patients and nurses could be formed through telenursing (Q20, −4). They strongly agreed that telenursing had limitations in providing physical care to patients and would not replace but supplement face‐to‐face nursing (Q30, +4/Q35, +3). They thought that the introduction of telenursing would not solve the problems of nurse shortages and medical errors (Q3, −3/Q19, −3) and would cause an increase in the risk of personal information infringement (Q4, +3). Furthermore, there would be age‐related problems in using telenursing (Q25, −3) and agreed that it would take a long time for telenursing to become universal (Q26, +3). Compared with other factors (Factors I, II, IV and V), Factor III had the highest level of scepticism about whether telenursing services would develop similar to face‐to‐face nursing services.

Participant 45 (female, 61 years old), who had the highest factor weight (0.7727) in Factor III, stated the following (Appendix S3):

It is questionable whether the shortage of nurses can be solved through telenursing.

Regardless of the quality of telenursing, the best and safest way is for nurses to provide in‐person care.

It is thought that patients will have more things to do by themselves when using telenursing. Therefore, I think telenursing will help patients live independently.

Telenursing may not be able to solve all situations in various cases. The risk due to telenursing will be increased.

A notable demographic feature of Factor III is the respondents' social position. The participants consisted of two nurses, two nursing college students (one of whom was a nursing transfer student), and one doctor; that is, 80% of the participants were nurses and nursing students.

Overall, Factor III fully trusted and preferred traditional face‐to‐face nursing. They were distrustful and critical of telenursing safety and also less willing to accept telenursing services. Therefore, Factor III was named ‘Complete skepticism for telenursing, Total nonacceptance’.

Factor IV: Expectance of the Supplementary Roles of Telenursing, Immediate Acceptance

Respondents who were loaded on Factor IV strongly disagreed that telenursing would reduce nursing care quality (Q34, −4). They strongly agreed that telenursing would not replace but supplement face‐to‐face nursing (Q35, +4). Similar to Factor I, they strongly agreed that telenursing could be used anywhere without space limitations and could help reduce medical costs (Q14, +4/Q22, +3). They also strongly agreed that telenursing would contribute to ensuring that healthcare services were accessible to the whole population (Q15, +3). Factor IV participants strongly disagreed that telenursing would form trusting relationships between nurses and patients, and facilitate communication (Q20, −3/Q27, −3). They also strongly disagreed that age would not be considered as a major problem when using telenursing (Q25, −4). Furthermore, when compared with the other factors (Factors I, II, III and V), the individuals in Factor IV strongly believed that it would not take long for telenursing to become universalised (Q26, −3). They also vigorously agreed that telenursing would pose a threat to patients' privacy (Q31, +3).

Participant 40 (female, 35 years old), who had the highest factor weight (0.6730) in Factor IV, stated the following (Appendix S3):

Telenursing is convenient because it is available anytime and anywhere. However, palpation during physical examination, pain assessment, communication of emotions coming from the patient, and so forth is only possible face‐to‐face. Therefore, telenursing without face‐to‐face contact would not be safe.

It will be difficult for emotional exchanges to occur because there is no direct face‐to‐face interaction. It will be difficult to form a deep trusting relationship between patients and nurses. Telenursing cannot provide mental and emotional comfort to patients.

In summary, Factor IV recognised both the advantages and disadvantages of telenursing and had the highest expectations for the complementary role of telenursing. Factor IV participants believed that society would have no choice but to accept telenursing with the conviction that it would be implemented soon. This group believed that the implementation of telenursing should not be delayed. Therefore, Factor IV was named ‘Expectance of the supplementary roles of telenursing, Immediate acceptance’.

Factor V: Expectance of the Supplementary Roles of Telenursing, Prolonged Acceptance

Respondents who were loaded on Factor V, similar to Factor IV, strongly disagreed that telenursing would reduce the quality of nursing care (Q34, −4) and strongly agreed that it would not replace but supplement face‐to‐face nursing (Q35, +4). The individuals in Factor V essentially preferred face‐to‐face nursing to telenursing (Q17, −4) and strongly agreed that it would take a long time for telenursing to become universalised (Q26, +4). They believed that telenursing would improve an individual's quality of life (Q7, +3). However, they strongly agreed that in emergency cases, telenursing intervention might be insufficient, and there would be limitations in providing physical care to patients through telenursing (Q16, +3/Q30, +3). Individuals in Factor V disagreed with the view that age was irrelevant in using telenursing and that it solved the problem of nursing shortages (Q25, −3/Q3, −3). Compared with the other types (Factors I, II, III and IV), Factor V disagreed most strongly that the ICT literacy level was sufficient to introduce telenursing (Q29, −3). Furthermore, unlike other factors, they disagreed that it would be impossible to prevent the introduction and commercialisation of telenursing and agreed that telenursing will benefit only some people (Q1, −2/Q33, +2).

Participant 18 (male, 34 years old), who had the highest factor loading in Factor V (0.7480), stated (Appendix S3):

Currently, there are too many obstacles and limitations to introduce telenursing.

Collaborations with governments and other fields outside the nursing community, technical capabilities to support telenursing enforcement, and individuals' needs for telenursing in society are unclear. Therefore, considering its current state, I think it will be difficult to commercialize telenursing. To enforce telenursing, a more logical and systematic basis should be established based on the consumers' needs. Current information and communication technologies for introducing telenursing have limitations. Furthermore, consent and consultation of public are required. It will take a long time for these things to be resolved.

An important aspect of nursing is continuity. Telenursing is sufficient to achieve continuity of care. In addition, telenursing can solve patients' minor nursing needs. I think that the quality of nursing will be much higher if telenursing is implemented. It will be possible to secure the continuity of nursing care through telenursing. I am convinced that this will lead to an improvement in individuals' quality of life.

In summary, although individuals in Factor V highly valued the complementary role of telenursing, they thought it was unreasonable to expect implementation within a short period of time. Therefore, Factor V was named ‘Expectance of the supplementary roles of telenursing, Prolonged acceptance’.

Discussion

Principal Results

This study used Q methodology to investigate societal perceptions of telenursing in South Korea. Five types of perceptions regarding telenursing were derived from the data.

Before listing the five types, it is important to first establish that the main structures of the public's subjectivity regarding telenursing are explained based on two premises: ‘Positive‐Negative aspects of telenursing’ and ‘Active‐Passive position in accepting telenursing’. First, in terms of the ‘Positive‐Negative aspects of telenursing’, Factors II and III showed opposite characteristics. Factor II emphasised the positive aspects of telenursing, whereas Factor III highlighted its negative aspects. Additionally, Factors I, IV and V accepted both the positive and negative aspects of telenursing. Next, regarding the ‘Active‐Passive position in accepting of telenursing’, Factors I, II and III showed an active position which expressed a user‐centred position in accepting telenursing. Factors IV and V showed a passive position when considering the surrounding conditions in accepting telenursing (Figure 2).

In this discussion, concrete strategies for the introduction and establishment of telenursing are proposed based on the characteristics of each of the five perspectives obtained from the results.

Factor I: Preference for Convenience and Efficiency of Telenursing, Active Acceptance

Factor I valued the convenience and cost‐efficiency of telenursing. Telenursing is convenient and cost‐effective; particularly, post COVID‐19 pandemic, such benefits have emerged (Asimakopoulou 2020; Esmaeilpour‐BandBoni, Gholami‐Shilsar, and Khanaki 2021; Locsin et al. 2021; Shohani et al. 2018). Factor I greatly supported the advantages of telenursing shown in previous studies' results. This group has some belief that telenursing can solve the nursing shortage problem. One of the biggest concerns that Factor I respondents expressed was that with telenursing subtle changes in the patient could be missed, thus affecting the overall recovery speed.

Considering the perspectives associated with Factor I, it is necessary that people who belong to this type have a proper understanding of telenursing. Telenursing can provide extra nursing support that reduces the workload of nurses, thus breaking the cycle that can quickly lead to a nursing shortage (McVey 2023). Additionally, by allowing nurses to spend more time on critical patient care activities, increased patient satisfaction can be achieved (Schwartz et al. 2024). However, telenursing cannot completely replace nursing care. Since the professional activity in nursing fundamentally comprises face‐to‐face components, the extent of the application of telenursing to nursing care will be limited (Marco‐Franco et al. 2023). The nursing community must assist the society in gaining a proper understanding of telenursing to ensure its successful integration. Also, when accepting telenursing, nurses should suggest a reasonable method to appropriately configure the proportion of patient care provided through telenursing within the overall scope of nursing activities.

In addition, when developing medical equipment for telenursing, not only devices that measure a part of the body but also those that can capture changes in the entire body system should be developed. In other words, by enabling the acquisition of information not only regarding specific parts of the patient's body but the condition of the entire body, it is possible to include parts that could be overlooked by non‐face‐to‐face nursing. In addition, it can accelerate patient recovery. The nursing community must build an environment in which telenursing can be successfully introduced by participating in the development of these devices and technologies through multidisciplinary research.

Factor II: Complete Support for Telenursing, Total Acceptance

Factor II represents complete support for telenursing. Individuals of this type recognise and support the role of telenursing in an era of rapidly changing nursing paradigms. They predicted that telenursing would become a tool for realising universal health for all citizens. In other words, Factor II supports fundamental concepts in healthcare field, that is everyone has access to quality and essential health services (Watkins 2014; World Health Organization 2013). However, Factor II perceptions expressed particularly strong concerns that age would be a major problem in using telenursing.

Reflecting on the perceptions of Factor II, the following strategies for revitalising telenursing are proposed. First, it is necessary to understand the extent of technology required to use telenursing by age group. Next, the possibility of providing telenursing in a form suitable for an age‐specific skill level must be considered. Furthermore, measures should be adopted to ensure that appropriate education and guidance are provided to the target, considering individual differences in technology usage levels. The positive perspective of telenursing in Factor II is likely to become the main cognitive structure for introducing and establishing telenursing in the future. Therefore, it is necessary to ensure awareness of the characteristics of Factor II.

Factor III: Complete Scepticism for Telenursing, Total Nonacceptance

The participants in Factor III completely supported face‐to‐face nursing and were sceptical of telenursing. They criticised the safety of telenursing. The five subjects loaded into this type were all medical personnel: two nurses, two nursing students and one doctor. This finding is of great significance in explaining Factor III perspectives. When considering this result, it is necessary to consider care provider‐centred strategies as a proposal to introduce and establish telenursing. Previous studies have shown that nurses have more positive and less negative attitudes towards technological support and demonstrate higher use behaviours if aspects of core nursing (e.g., direct care) are not replaced (Schlicht et al. 2020). Therefore, after classifying the level and type of nursing duties that will preferentially replace telenursing, it will be possible to formulate strategies to replace peripheral rather than core nursing functions (Schlicht et al. 2020).

Considering the perspectives associated with Factor III, the following proposal is recommended: Positive perceptions of the usefulness and usability of technology are correlated with technology acceptance with users showing more positive attitudes towards digital technology than non‐users (Tubaishat 2018). Therefore, it is necessary to provide education and training suitable for telenursing management for nurses and, if necessary, introduce a licensing system (Schlicht et al. 2020). In this context, it is important to train individuals to understand and handle next‐generation health informatics for the operation and management of telenursing (Matsushita 2020). Therefore, nursing education should include integrated specialised technical training that can facilitate the use of future systems to cultivate the next generation of professionals. Finally, it is important for nurses to recognise that their vision and active involvement in telenursing are key elements for its successful implementation (Marco‐Franco et al. 2023).

Factor IV: Expectance of the Supplementary Roles of Telenursing, Immediate Acceptance

The Factor IV group is convinced that telenursing will soon become universal and believes that society has no choice but to introduce telenursing. In the 21st century, the demand for medical services is constantly undergoing rapid changes (Afik and Pandin 2021). A paradigm shift in nursing is required to provide holistic nursing care in these settings and using technology is a suitable strategy (Locsin et al. 2021). Factor IV perceptions are abreast with the times. The participants in Factor IV were concerned about the infringement of patients' privacy. They attached great importance to protecting individuals' privacy.

Security, confidentiality and privacy in telenursing are paramount not only from a nurse–patient interaction perspective, but also to ensure the security of technology (Matsushita 2020). Therefore, legislation related to information security at the national level is essential to establish and develop telenursing systems. Additionally, it will be necessary to establish an academically verified system that includes not only medical and nursing sciences but also information and computational knowledge.

Factor V: Expectance of the Supplementary Roles of Telenursing, Prolonged Acceptance

The participants in Factor V recognise the need for telenursing but do not expect telenursing to be introduced and commercialised within a short period of time. Therefore, it is necessary to actively recognise the feasibility of introducing telenursing by presenting more concrete proposals for the construction of environments and countermeasures.

Considering the perspectives associated with Factor V, the following strategies are proposed for the introduction and establishment of telenursing: Thorough preparation of legal and institutional measures are necessary before introducing telenursing. For telenursing to be introduced, non‐face‐to‐face health management of patients and consultation and education of the patient and family through the cooperation of medical staff must first be legally and institutionally permitted (Matsushita 2020). To achieve this, the nursing community and the government must work together. Additionally, to accelerate the introduction of telenursing, it is necessary to improve the ability of both care providers and users to utilise information. This is also an important issue in improving the understanding of health information and protecting personal information. This will help to identify solutions to the limitations and inadequate measures of telenursing (Matsushita 2020). In addition, in an era of rapid development in information utilisation, building a cloud database that can collectively manage health‐related data and establish information security measures is an urgent task for medical, nursing and related professionals. Financial reserves for the realistic practice of telenursing must be established at the national level.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is that the perceptions of telenursing may differ across cultures. This study was limited to South Korea. Nevertheless, the results can be used as basic data for establishing policies prior to the introduction of telenursing, especially in countries with similar backgrounds to South Korea.

In future, it will be necessary to examine telenursing perceptions in various countries. Furthermore, an in‐depth examination of perceptions of telenursing among nurses or healthcare workers in clinics across various regions should be conducted.

Conclusions

This study found that public perceptions of telenursing were classified in various ways based on two premises: ‘Positive‐Negative aspects of telenursing’ and ‘Active‐Passive position in accepting telenursing’. The author identified existing strategies for the introduction and establishment of telenursing and organised them into different groups based on five types of perception. Moreover, the study considered the differences in the awareness of nursing majors and non‐majors on the same theme by including undergraduate nursing students, clinical nurses and professors at nursing colleges. These results indicated that public perceptions regarding telenursing were not homogeneous; individuals had subjective and multifaceted attitudes and positions depending on each situation and context. This study highlights that the introduction of telenursing is neither feasible nor sustainable without the public's consensus. Therefore, most importantly, a process of fully converging and reflecting the values and preferences of the public is necessary. The introduction and establishment of telenursing in the future will require discriminatory and differentiated measures and strategies according to the characteristics of the public's different perspectives.
