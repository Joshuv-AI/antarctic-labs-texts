Association between bedroom nighttime light pollution and risk of hepatic encephalopathy in hepatocellular carcinoma patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Shaoying Li, Sheng Zhang, Shuaichao Li, Tao Meng, Hailong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLight pollution has been implicated in liver health. This study aimed to investigate the association between bedroom nighttime light pollution and the risk of hepatic encephalopathy (HE) in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). A total of 454 HCC patients were enrolled from communities. Bedroom nighttime light intensity was measured using an illuminometer (lux) at baseline, 2 months, and 4 months. Sleep quality was assessed at these three time points using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. These data at different time points were averaged separately for subsequent analyses. All patients were followed up for 12 months from baseline (unless death occurred), and HCC-related adverse outcomes were recorded. Multivariate logistic and Cox regression were adopted for statistical analysis. The results indicated that higher mean bedroom nighttime light intensity (> 50 lx) was significantly associated with an increased risk of both overt HE and minimal HE. Furthermore, it was associated with impaired liver function, esophagogastric variceal bleeding, and elevated HCC-related mortality. Notably, interaction analysis revealed that age and TNM stage may modify the aforementioned associations to some extent. In conclusion, bedroom nighttime light pollution is linked to an elevated risk of HE and may represent a potential risk factor warranting future validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51269-5.Keywords: Hepatic encephalopathy, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Light intensity, Light pollution, RiskSubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most prevalent malignant tumors worldwide. Globally, approximately 900,000 new HCC cases and more than 800,000 HCC-related deaths are reported annually1. China represents a high-risk region for HCC, accounting for nearly 45% of all new cases worldwide, and the overall 5‑year survival rate of patients remains below 15%2. Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) constitutes one of the most severe complications in patients with HCC and liver cirrhosis, with a clinical spectrum ranging from mild cognitive impairment to a deep coma3,4. It markedly increases mortality risk and serves as an important independent predictor of poor prognosis in HCC patients.

HE is closely linked to multiple pathogenic factors, including severe liver dysfunction, portosystemic shunting, infection, electrolyte disturbances, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, and excessive protein intake5,6. These triggers may induce or aggravate central nervous system dysfunction by exacerbating ammonia metabolic disorders and neurotoxicity. Although several risk factors have been identified, the full pathogenesis and many potential modulators of HE remain incompletely understood.

As an emerging environmental risk factor, artificial light pollution has been repeatedly demonstrated to exert direct and indirect detrimental effects on liver health. Prolonged light exposure can induce hepatocyte necrosis and apoptosis, leading to direct structural damage to hepatic tissue7. It can also promote and worsen metabolic liver injury via melatonin suppression, sphingolipid metabolic disturbance, circadian clock disruption, and the light-gut-liver axis8–10. Furthermore, artificial light pollution disturbs circadian rhythms and impairs neuroendocrine pathways, triggering a cascade of systemic disorders including obesity, metabolic syndrome, depressive‑like behaviors, and oxidative stress11–14.

The development of HE is tightly associated with impaired liver function, metabolic dysregulation, and neurological dysfunction. Notably, the spectrum of injuries induced by artificial light pollution is highly consistent with these core pathogenic mechanisms, implying a potential but uncharacterized association between artificial light pollution and the pathogenesis of HE.

Previous studies have further demonstrated that sleep disturbances can exacerbate HE by disrupting gut microbiota homeostasis and activating inflammatory pathways15–17, and bedroom nighttime light pollution is strongly associated with sleep disorders. Collectively, these findings highlight the urgent need to explore the potential relationship between light pollution and HE.

Accordingly, the present study adopted a prospective cohort design involving hundreds of HCC patients to systematically evaluate the association between bedroom nighttime light pollution and the prognosis of HCC-related HE, with the aim of filling the current knowledge gap in this field.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinxiang Medical University and was conducted in strict accordance with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association18.

Study population

In this study, 1 to 2 large communities were selected from each of the 6 administrative districts under Xinxiang City, Henan Province, China, totaling 10 communities. The study was conducted from July 1, 2021, to June 30, 2024, with the collaboration of community physicians. The brief procedure was as follows: based on the community residents’ health data available to them, community physicians took the initiative to contact potential patients. After obtaining informed consent, researchers conducted household surveys accompanied by community physicians and enrolled subjects who met the following inclusion criteria into the study.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Aged ≥ 18 years, both males and females were eligible; (2) First diagnosed with HCC based on epidemiological history, clinical manifestations, serological indicators, and imaging examinations (particularly abdominal enhanced computed tomography [CT])19; (3) No prior anti-tumor treatment had been administered; (4) Complicated with compensated cirrhosis and no overt HE; (5) Lost the opportunity for radical surgical treatment due to various reasons and planned to receive targeted therapy, immunotherapy, interventional therapy, or other conservative treatments20; (6) Good general condition; for patients with chronic diseases such as hypertension, the conditions must be stably controlled; no other types of malignant tumors, severe infections, or other comorbidities; (7) Have a fixed residence, a stable nighttime sleep environment, no habit of staying up late, and regularly fall asleep before 23:00; (8) Voluntarily agreed to participate in the study, provided relevant medical records, and cooperated with baseline assessment and multiple household follow-up visits within the 1-year follow-up period.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Insufficient evidence for HCC diagnosis; (2) Patients without concurrent cirrhosis; (3) Planned to undergo surgical treatment or had already initiated other types of anti-tumor treatment; (4) Unable to provide complete medical records or with missing key data; (5) Failure to standardly complete bedroom nighttime light intensity measurement; (6) Refusal to participate in follow-up, loss to follow-up, or death due to causes other than HCC during the follow-up period; (7) Significant changes in the sleep environment during the study period.

Baseline data collection

At enrollment (defined as the baseline of this study), researchers collected the following data through face-to-face interviews and medical record reviews: (1) demographic data, (2) personal history, (3) medical history, (4) characteristics of HCC, (5) liver function, and (6) characteristics of HE, all of which were obtained at the time of HCC diagnosis.

In the personal history, “smoking” was defined as intermittent or continuous regular smoking for 12 months within 10 years prior to diagnosis; “Alcohol drinking” was defined as intermittent or continuous regular alcohol consumption for 12 months within 10 years prior to diagnosis. In the medical history, various diseases were confirmed based on diagnostic certificates or treatment records in the medical records.

The diagnosis of minimal hepatic encephalopathy (MHE) was also determined based on medical records. At the time of HCC diagnosis, all patients were at risk of HE and were routinely evaluated by specialist physicians using the Portable Hepatic Encephalopathy Score (PHES), a well-recognized neuropsychological gold-standard tool for the diagnosis of MHE21. Several versions of the PHES scale have been reported, and a unified version is routinely adopted by major local medical institutions in clinical practice, with a total score ranging from − 18 to + 6 points. A PHES ≤ − 4 points was used to define MHE in accordance with the widely accepted criteria in the Asia-Pacific region. According to medical records, all assessments were performed in a quiet, undisturbed environment, and the procedures were conducted in line with international standards.

Assessment of light pollution and sleep disorders

From enrollment (i.e., baseline), a 12-month follow-up was conducted, with the endpoint set as the last day of the 12-month follow-up period or death due to HCC during the follow-up period.

At baseline, the end of the 2nd month, and the end of the 4th month, the nighttime bedroom light intensity (unit: lux) was measured using the UNI-T UT383BT portable illuminometer, which features simple operation and reliable measurement.

On the day of each measurement, researchers delivered the illuminometer to the patient’s home and placed it 1.2–1.5 m above the ground at the head of the bedroom bed, ensuring no obstruction, no direct exposure to bedside lights, and no interference from other light sources. The instrument parameters were set to measure light intensity from 23:00 to 04:30, avoiding interference from early morning daylight. Brief and clear training on operational standards was provided to the subjects or their family members. The subjects turned on the instrument independently before going to bed, and no additional parameter settings were required for the instrument. As previously stated, all subjects had a regular bedtime before 23:00. The illuminometer continuously monitored light intensity until it automatically turned off after 04:30 the next day. On the following day, researchers retrieved the illuminometer, exported the light intensity data recorded between 23:00 and 04:30, verified the data, and retained it for subsequent use.

The average value of the three nighttime light intensity measurements for each patient was used for grouping: patients with an average value > 50 lx were assigned to the exposed group, and those with an average value ≤ 50 lx were assigned to the non-exposed group. This grouping threshold was determined based on the widely recognized gold standard and authoritative evidence in the field of circadian rhythm research. Specifically, the international classic protocol for dim light melatonin onset (DLMO) testing clearly stipulates that ambient light intensity must be controlled below 30–50 lx to avoid disturbing endogenous melatonin secretion22. In addition, multiple recent clinical studies have uniformly defined < 50 lx as the standard dim light environment that does not interfere with circadian rhythms23,24. Meanwhile, nighttime light exceeding this threshold can significantly suppress melatonin secretion, disrupt sleep rhythms, and exert adverse biological effects, further supporting the scientific validity of this cutoff value25,26.

During each measurement, researchers also assessed the patients’ sleep quality over the past month using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)27. The PSQI evaluates 7 dimensions including subjective sleep quality and sleep latency, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. The average value of the three PSQI scores for each patient was used to determine sleep disorders: a score ≥ 7 indicated the presence of sleep disorders, while a score < 7 indicated normal sleep.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation, and between-group differences were evaluated using independent-samples t-test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency (proportion), and between-group differences were analyzed using the chi-square test.

Multivariable logistic regression was performed to evaluate the association between nighttime light pollution and the outcomes. The multivariable model was primarily adjusted for sleep disorders from baseline to the 4th month. Additionally, the model adjusted for variables with between-group differences or potential differences (P < 0.1) across the following categories: baseline characteristics of subjects; baseline characteristics of HCC and HE; treatment measures and daily living conditions during follow-up.

As the three outcome variables “OHE during follow-up”, “Esophagogastric variceal rupture during follow-up”, and “HCC-related death during follow-up” had definite occurrence times, multivariable Cox regression was additionally performed to evaluate the time-dependent association between nighttime light pollution and these outcomes. Covariate adjustment was consistent with that in the main models.

Multivariable logistic regression was also used to analyze the interactive effects of nighttime light pollution with sleep disturbance, advanced age, and TNM stage on prognostic outcomes related to HCC and HE. Nighttime light pollution was dichotomized as exposed/non‑exposed, sleep disturbance as present/absent, advanced age as ≥ 60 years/<60 years, and TNM stage as stage IV/stages I–III. Interaction terms were generated by multiplying the two dichotomous variables. Covariate adjustment was still consistent with that in the main models; PSQI score, sleep disturbance, age, and baseline characteristics of HCC and HE were excluded from the covariate adjustment in the respective interaction analyses due to high collinearity with the interacting factors.

A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the above analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0.

Results

Study population

A total of 477 eligible patients were enrolled in the study at baseline. During follow-up, 10 patients were lost to follow-up, 8 developed other comorbidities, and 5 died from other causes; these patients were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 454 patients (95.2%) completed the entire study protocol.

Based on the average value of the three light intensity measurements, 193 patients were assigned to the exposed group (> 50 lx) and 261 to the non-exposed group (≤ 50 lx).

Characteristics of subjects at baseline

As shown in Table 1, compared with the non-exposed group, the exposed group had significantly older age (P = 0.011), higher body mass index (P = 0.025), and a higher proportion of participants with household income ≤ 10,000 yuan per month (P = 0.034).

Characteristics of HCC and HE at baseline

As shown in Table 2, compared with the non-exposed group, the exposed group had a higher proportion of patients with intrahepatic metastasis (P = 0.040) and tumor capsule invasion (P = 0.032), longer prothrombin time (P = 0.019), and higher blood total bilirubin levels (P = 0.023).

Nighttime light pollution and sleep disorders from baseline to the 4th month

As shown in Table 3, the mean light intensity (the grouping indicator) was significantly higher in the exposed group than in the non-exposed group (P < 0.001). Similarly, the exposed group exhibited significantly higher mean PSQI scores (P < 0.001) and a greater proportion of patients with sleep disorders (P = 0.001).

Treatment measures and daily living conditions during the follow-up

As shown in Table 4, compared with the non-exposed group, the exposed group had a lower proportion of patients with a regular diet (P = 0.013), as well as lower levels of daily protein intake, daily dietary fiber intake, and daily water intake (P = 0.036, P = 0.010, and P = 0.042, respectively).

Outcomes of HCC and HE during and at the end of the follow-up

As shown in Table 5, compared with the non-exposed group, the exposed group had a higher proportion of patients with one HCC outcome, three liver function outcomes, three HE outcomes, and two other adverse outcomes, namely “Maximum tumor diameter increased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.019), “Blood albumin level decreased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.001), “Blood total bilirubin level increased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.003), “Prothrombin time prolonged by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.006), “OHE during follow-up” (P < 0.001), “New-onset MHE at follow-up end” (P < 0.001), “PHES further decreased in baseline MHE patients” (P < 0.001), “Esophagogastric variceal rupture during follow-up” (P = 0.012), and “HCC-related death during follow-up” (P < 0.001).

Association of nighttime light pollution with outcomes of HCC and HE

As shown in Table 6, multivariable logistic regression reported that higher mean light intensity (> 50 lx) was significantly associated with an increased risk of three liver function outcomes, three HE outcomes, and two other adverse outcomes, namely “Blood albumin level decreased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.002), “Blood total bilirubin level increased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.006), “Prothrombin time prolonged by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.018), “OHE during follow-up” (P < 0.001), “New-onset MHE at follow-up end” (P = 0.003), “PHES score further decreased in baseline MHE patients” (P = 0.001), “Esophagogastric variceal rupture during follow-up” (P = 0.027), and “HCC-related death during follow-up” (P = 0.001).

Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analyses indicated that higher mean light intensity (> 50 lx) was not associated with the risk of the four HCC outcomes (all P>0.05).

Time-dependent association of nighttime light pollution with outcomes of HCC and HE

As shown in Supplemental Table 1, multivariable Cox regression reported that higher mean light intensity (> 50 lx) was significantly associated with an increased risk of “OHE during follow-up” (P < 0.001), “Esophagogastric variceal rupture during follow-up” (P = 0.010), and “HCC-related death during follow-up” (P = 0.001).

Association of nighttime light pollution interaction with outcomes of HCC and HE

As shown in Supplemental Table 2, multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed no significant interaction between nighttime light pollution (> 50 lx) and sleep disorders (mean PSQI score ≥ 7) for any of the adverse outcomes (all P > 0.05).

As shown in Supplemental Table 3, multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed that the interaction between nighttime light pollution and advanced age at baseline (≥ 60 years) was significantly associated with an increased risk of “OHE during follow-up” (P = 0.007), “PHES score further decreased in baseline MHE patients” (P = 0.005), “Esophagogastric variceal rupture during follow-up” (P = 0.004), and “HCC-related death during follow-up” (P < 0.001).

As shown in Supplemental Table 4, multivariable logistic regression analyses revealed that the interaction between nighttime light pollution and TNM stage at baseline (stage IV) was significantly associated with an increased risk of “Blood albumin level decreased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.018), “Blood total bilirubin level increased by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.023), “Prothrombin time prolonged by > 10% at follow-up end” (P = 0.040), “OHE during follow-up” (P = 0.005), and “PHES score further decreased in baseline MHE patients” (P = 0.014).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the association between bedroom nighttime light pollution and the risk of HE in patients with HCC, identifying a potentially novel risk factor for predicting this neurological complication, pending further validation in future studies.

The multivariable logistic regression confirmed that higher bedroom nighttime light intensity (> 50 lx) during follow-up was associated with an approximately 1- to 3-fold increased risk of OHE and MHE, as well as a 1.7- to 2-fold increased risk of liver function deterioration, esophagogastric variceal rupture, and HCC-related death. These outcomes, covering multiple clinical dimensions (liver function, HCC complications, and mortality), consistently support the association between nighttime light pollution and adverse prognosis in patients with HCC, markedly enhancing the reliability of our findings. The multivariable Cox regression analysis further demonstrated that this higher bedroom nighttime light intensity was associated with a 1.9- to 2.7-fold elevated risk of OHE, esophagogastric variceal rupture, and HCC-related death in a time-dependent manner, further corroborating our key results.

In addition, the multivariable logistic regression found no significant association between higher bedroom nighttime light intensity and HCC outcomes, including tumor diameter, metastasis, tumor thrombus, and stage progression, which may be explained by the fact that HCC progression is mainly determined by multiple core factors such as underlying liver disease and tumor biological characteristics, with a relatively limited direct effect of light pollution.

To further clarify the heterogeneity in the risk effects of nighttime light pollution, this study comprehensively analyzed its interactions with sleep disturbance, age, and TNM stage. The results showed that the interaction between light pollution and advanced age was mainly concentrated in severe adverse outcomes, including HE, esophagogastric variceal rupture, and mortality, suggesting that advanced age may amplify the harmful effects of light pollution by increasing central nervous system vulnerability and reducing overall systemic compensatory capacity. In contrast, the interaction between light pollution and advanced TNM stage involved both liver function deterioration and HE, indicating that patients with late-stage tumors have inherently impaired liver reserve function, and light pollution can further aggravate hepatocyte injury, promote liver function decompensation, and subsequently increase the risk of HE. These differences suggest that advanced age and TNM stage modify the risk effects of light pollution through distinct pathways. In addition, no significant interaction was observed between light pollution and sleep disturbance, implying that the impact of light pollution on adverse prognosis in HCC patients acts independently of sleep-related pathways. These stratified effects and underlying mechanisms warrant further validation and elucidation in future studies.

Potential confounders included patients’ general conditions (e.g., age, medication history), tumor characteristics (e.g., liver function grade, etiology of HCC, severity of portal hypertension), treatment regimens (e.g., targeted therapy), and lifestyle factors (e.g., diet, sleep, and nighttime activities). In the univariate analysis, we compared all the above potential confounders between groups (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4). Most variables showed no statistically significant between-group differences, indicating good quality of the study population and data. Variables with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) or marginally significant differences (P < 0.1) were adjusted in the multivariable regression models. In addition, strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to control part of the confounding bias at the source.

Of note, this study comprehensively included key indicators reflecting tumor aggressiveness and vascular invasion of HCC in the baseline assessment, including intrahepatic metastasis, tumor capsule invasion, portal vein tumor thrombus, number of tumor lesions, and TNM stage (Table 2). Previous studies have confirmed that invasive features such as vascular invasion, venous compression, and microvascular invasion in HCC can accelerate liver function deterioration and promote the occurrence and progression of HE29,30. In the statistical analysis, we fully considered and adjusted for the confounding effects of the above‑mentioned tumor aggressiveness and vascular‑related factors, thereby reliably ensuring the independence of the main results. In addition, hepatic structural injury and hemodynamic changes related to hepatectomy can also directly affect liver functional reserve, thereby altering the risk of HE and long‑term prognosis31. All patients enrolled in this study had lost the opportunity for radical surgical treatment and only received targeted therapy, immunotherapy, interventional therapy, or other conservative treatments, and surgical‑related confounding factors were excluded at the study design level. Nevertheless, in future studies involving surgical patients, attention should still be paid to the impact of surgery‑related liver injury on liver function and the risk of HE, so as to further improve the prognostic evaluation system.

During the 1-year follow-up period, light intensity was measured three times at baseline, 2 months, and 4 months, with the mean value used as the final indicator for analysis. Patients who stayed up late and those with significant changes in sleep environment during the study were excluded. These measures helped ensure that the measured light intensity closely reflected the patients’ actual daily level.

Bedroom nighttime light pollution may increase the risk of HE in patients with HCC through multiple pathways. Firstly, nighttime light exposure directly suppresses melatonin secretion and disrupts the central circadian rhythm, thereby impairing the hepatic clock and metabolic homeostasis, and exacerbating hepatocyte injury and liver dysfunction32,33. Secondly, circadian disruption and melatonin deficiency aggravate systemic chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, elevate the levels of neurotoxic metabolites related to HE such as blood ammonia, and reduce the clearance capacity of these toxins in the brain tissue34,35. Meanwhile, light pollution may independently of sleep affect neurotransmitter balance and central nervous excitability, further inducing or exacerbating neurocognitive abnormalities associated with HE36,37. These pathways interact synergistically and jointly mediate the association between nighttime light pollution and the risk of HE in HCC patients through multiple dimensions, including deteriorated liver function, impaired toxin metabolism, and increased central nervous system vulnerability.

This study has several limitations. First, although the study participants were recruited from community populations, the study was conducted in only a single city and region with a relatively concentrated population, which somewhat limits the generalizability of the findings to other regions and populations with different socioeconomic and lighting environments. Second, the relatively small sample size may affect the statistical power. Third, a small number of patients were lost to follow-up during the 12-month follow-up period, which may introduce attrition bias and affect the robustness of the association estimates. Fourth, despite standardized operating procedures, home measurements of nighttime light intensity are susceptible to objective biases, such as slight differences in sensor placement, temporary light source interference, and real-time environmental changes, all of which may lead to certain measurement variability. Fifth, this study only observed a clinical association between bedroom nighttime light exposure and the risk of HE and adverse prognosis in patients with HCC. We did not measure objective circadian rhythm markers such as melatonin or cortisol rhythms to verify relevant mechanistic pathways, nor did we perform in vitro or in vivo experiments to explore potential molecular mechanisms. These limitations were mainly constrained by insufficient objective research conditions. Therefore, future multi-center, large-sample studies covering diverse geographic regions are warranted to improve generalizability and statistical power. Meanwhile, comprehensive follow-up management should be implemented to minimize attrition, and stricter automated home light monitoring techniques should be adopted to reduce attrition bias and measurement bias. In addition, basic mechanistic studies combined with detection of objective circadian biomarkers and performance of in vitro and in vivo experiments are needed to clarify the biological link and potential causal relationship, and to provide more robust and sufficient evidence for clinical practice.

In conclusion, this study confirms that relatively high bedroom nighttime light intensity is significantly associated with an increased risk of HE and other adverse prognoses in patients with HCC. This finding provides a new perspective for risk factor screening of HE from the standpoint of environmental exposure and also supplements a potential objective indicator for clinical prognostic evaluation. If further validated by future interventional and basic studies, bedroom nighttime light pollution is expected to serve as an environmental risk factor for predicting HE risk and a modifiable intervention target. Simple and feasible nighttime light avoidance measures may help reduce the risk of adverse events, ultimately benefiting a large number of HCC patients and improving their long-term prognosis.
