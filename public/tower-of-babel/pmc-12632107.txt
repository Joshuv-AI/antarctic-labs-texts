CLOCK-mediated acetylation of NF-κB p65 drives immune evasion in breast cancer
Authors: Xinyu Quan, Na Li, Jie Huang, Yixin Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDisruption of circadian rhythm (DCR) has been connected with breast cancer (BC) susceptibility; whereas it is unclear whether status of key clock genes could be used in predicting BC prognosis, tumor immune microenvironment, and immunotherapy responses.ResultsCircadian clock genes demonstrate significant dysregulation in BC, where elevated CLOCK expression emerges as an independent prognostic factor strongly correlated with adverse clinical outcomes. CLOCK-overexpressing BC cells exhibit enhanced proliferative ability and strong resistance to chemotherapy drugs doxorubicin and gemcitabine. High CLOCK expression correlates with reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and increased M2 macrophage polarization, consistent with increased immune checkpoint molecule PD-L1 expression in the TCGA BC dataset. Additionally, patients with high CLOCK expression display lower Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score. Mechanistically, RNA-sequencing identified suppressed NF-κB, TNF, MAPK pathways, and PD-L1 expression in sh-CLOCK MCF-7 cells. Subsequent in vitro validation demonstrated that CLOCK mediates NF-κB p65 acetylation at K56 site, potentiating its transcriptional activation of PD-L1, thereby facilitating immune evasion in BC.ConclusionsCLOCK functions as a critical prognostic biomarker in BC by promoting tumor proliferation, chemoresistance, and immune evasion. Mechanistically, CLOCK mediates NF-κB p65 acetylation to enhance PD-L1 transcription, promoting immune evasion in BC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-025-00704-0.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC), the most prevalent global malignancy and fifth leading cause of cancer mortality in 2022 [1], shows increasing incidence due to risk factors including aging, elevated estrogen, genetic predisposition, and unhealthy lifestyle factors such as night-shift work [2]. Understanding its molecular mechanisms and identifying biomarkers are crucial for improving prevention and treatment strategies for BC patients.

Chronic circadian rhythm disruption, often caused by irregular light exposure, sleep patterns, or eating schedules, is strongly associated with various diseases, including cancer [3, 4]. Notably, numerous studies displayed that night-shift work and night-owl patterns were closely connected to BC occurrence [5–7] and metastasis [8, 9]. A recent meta-analysis revealed that reduced nightly fasting duration ( < 13 h vs. ≥ 13 h) significantly correlates with increased risk across all BC outcomes [10], also suggesting a potential link between BC progression and circadian gene regulation. Emerging evidence demonstrates that chronic circadian disruption modulates BC stemness and tumor immune microenvironment(TIME) in vivo [11], while exacerbating cancer-induced systemic and neuroinflammation in orthotopic BC models [12]. These findings collectively highlight the crucial role of circadian clock genes in shaping the BC tumor immune microenvironment and influencing therapeutic responses.

Circadian clock genes form intricate intracellular signalling networks regulated by biological rhythms, orchestrating the expression of downstream target genes to maintain cellular homeostasis and metabolic regulation [13]. At the core of this system lies a transcription-translation feedback loop, where the CLOCK-BMAL1 heterodimer translocates to the nucleus and binds E-box elements in target gene promoters, thereby regulating clock-controlled gene expression [14]. This regulatory network involves positive modulators (RORA/B/C) that enhance BMAL1 transcription, while negative regulators (REV-ERBα/β and CRY1/2-PER1/2/3 complexes) suppress CLOCK-BMAL1 transcription. Through this complex regulatory network, circadian clock genes precisely maintain biological rhythm homeostasis. Notably, emerging evidence indicates that CLOCK functions not only as a transcription factor but also exerts circadian regulatory effects through its acetyltransferase activity [15].

Recent research indicates that circadian disruption not only directly promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis but also remodels the TIME, facilitating immune evasion and induce immunotherapy resistance [16]. Hadadi et al. [11] demonstrated that circadian dysregulation drives BC progression by suppressing TIME via the CXCL5–CXCR2 axis. Moreover, emerging studies reveal that core circadian molecules regulate immune checkpoint expression to modulate immune escape. In oral squamous cell carcinoma, PER2 interacts with HSP90 to suppress IKK/NF-κB signalling, downregulating PD-L1 and enhancing T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [17]. In melanoma, RORA inhibit PD-L1 expression and activate cytotoxic T cells, improving immunotherapy efficacy [18]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting circadian-immune crosstalk to overcome immune evasion. However, the mechanisms linking circadian genes, immune checkpoints, and immune escape in BC remain further investigation.

CLOCK is not only a core transcription factor in the circadian rhythm regulatory system but also possesses intrinsic histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity [15]. Through this enzymatic function, CLOCK facilitates chromatin relaxation and transcriptional activation. In addition to histones, CLOCK also acetylates non-histone substrates—for example, acetylation of BMAL1 regulates circadian rhythm [19], while acetylation of ASS1 modulates urea metabolism [20]. Importantly, the acetyltransferase activity of CLOCK plays a critical role in tumor development. CLOCK-mediated acetylation of PRPS1/2 has been shown to enhance nucleotide biosynthesis in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, thereby promoting tumor progression [21]. However, the relationship between CLOCK and tumor immune evasion remains poorly understood. Further elucidation of CLOCK’s role in tumor immunity may uncover novel strategies for enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.

This study aims to systematically investigate the altered expression patterns of specific circadian clock genes in BC and assess their associations with the TIME and immune evasion. Based on circadian gene expression profiles, we constructed a prognostic prediction model and identified CLOCK as a significant predictor of BC outcomes. Further analyses elucidated the molecular mechanisms by which CLOCK promotes PD-L1 expression through acetylation of NF-κB p65 at K56 site, thereby facilitating tumor immune evasion. Collectively, our findings establish crucial evidence that circadian molecular CLOCK drives tumor immune escape by modulating immune checkpoints PD-L1, and provide novel theoretical insights for guiding immunotherapeutic strategies in BC.

Materials and methods

Data collection

RNA-Seq data, SNV data (MuTect2. Variant0. Maf), Illumina Human Methylation 450 methylation data, and clinical follow-up information data for BRCA were downloaded from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). RNA-seq data and clinical information from an another BRCA dataset, Molecular Taxonomy of BC International Consortium (METABRIC) dataset were downloaded from the cBioPortal website (https://www.cbioportal.org/). For clinical data, we removed cases without survival time and survival status.

Characterization of circadian clock genes in BRCA

The differential expression of circadian clock genes between paired tumor and adjacent normal specimens was tested using the Wilcoxon test method. The R package maftools (V2.4.05) [22] was utilized for analyzing and visualizing the mutation data of circadian clock genes among molecular subtypes of BRCA. For the methylation data, the following processes were performed: 1) Complementation of missing values and rows using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm; 2) Removal of CpG sites with cross reflections; 3) Exclusion of methylation sites on chromosome X and Y. The methylation data corresponding to the circadian clock genes were then extracted and used for heatmap clustering analysis.

Survival analysis of circadian clock genes

The RNA Sequencing profiles of circadian clock genes in BC tissues and corresponding clinical follow-up information were extracted from the TCGA-BRCA cohort. Univariate cox regression analysis was performed for each circadian clock gene using the ‘coxph’ function of R package ‘survival (V3.1–12)’, and p < 0.05 was selected as the threshold for filtering. Meanwhile, KM survival analysis was conducted for each gene using the ‘survminer’ package with the optimal cutoff set by the ‘surv_cutpoint’ function. Those genes that met the criterion of both univariate cox regression analysis and KM survival analysis were used for further analyses.

Calculation of circadian clock scores and validation

Multivariate cox regression analysis was further performed for those intersected genes and the correlation coefficient for each gene was obtained, then CC score was calculated for each sample in the TCGA-BRCA cohort based on the correlation coefficients. Both KM survival analysis and ROC curve analysis were then performed to test the survival association and the survival prediction ability of the CC score. An independent dataset METABRIC was employed for external validation. The distribution of the CC score among different clinical characteristics was also compared using the standard student T test or one-way ANOVA analysis.

Immune microenvironment analysis

We evaluated the TCGA-BRCA data for immune infiltration using Single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [23], as implemented in R package GSVA. Immune cell infiltration scores between CLOCK-high and CLOCK-low groups are then compared using a rank sum test (Kruskal-Wallis Test). Correlations coefficients between the infiltrating immune cells and expression of CLOCK were computed by Spearman and distance correlation analyses. PD-L1(CD274), HAVCR2, PDCD1, ITPRIPL1, SIGLEC15, TIGIT, CTLA4, LAG3, and PDCD1LG2 are crucial genes associated with immune regulation. The expression levels of these genes between CLOCK-high and CLOCK-low groups are then compared using a rank sum test (Kruskal-Wallis Test) to understand the functional status of immune checkpoints.

Prediction of immunotherapy response

The similarity of gene expression profiles between patients in the defined subgroups and patients receiving immunotherapy as provided in the available GEO dataset GSE93157 was measured by SubMap gene pattern analysis to indirectly predict the efficacy of immunotherapy in each group [24, 25].

CCK8 cell proliferation assay

Cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a density of 3,000 cells per 200 µL of medium, with six replicates for each condition and four plate repeats over four days. Cell viability was assessed at 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours. After adding10% CCK8 reagent to each well and incubating at 37 °C for three hours. Measure the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell proliferation was determined by subtracting the medium absorbance from the cell absorbance at 450 nm. For doxorubicin (Dox) and gemcitabine (Gem) treatment, cells were seed in 5000 cells per well, after 24 hours, cell was added with indicate concentration of Dox and Gem, cell viability was assessed at 48 hours after drug treatment.

Wound healing assay

Cell migratory capacity was evaluated using a wound healing assay. Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to full confluence. A uniform scratch was created by using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After washing to remove dislodged cells, serum-free medium was added. Wound closure was monitored and recorded at 0 and 48 hours post-scratch using an inverted microscope. All experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated three times to ensure reproducibility.

ClOCK overexpression and CLOCK knockdown MCF-7 cell line established

PLVX-FLAG-CLOCK or pLKO.1-shCLOCK plasmids were transfected into 293T cells to generate lentivirus using Addgene’s protocol. MCF-7 cells were then infected with CLOCK overexpression (CLOCK-OE) or CLOCK knockdown (sh-CLOCK) lentivirus at a 1:1 ratio of viral supernatant to cell culture medium, supplemented with 10 µg/ml Polybrene. To establish stable cell lines, puromycin selection was applied for 7 days. Successful transfection was confirmed via RT-PCR and immunoblot analysis.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from CLOCK knockdown MCF-7 cell line and parental MCF-7 cell line. Library preparation and sequencing were done by Biomaker (Beijing, China) using Illumina Hiseq4000 following a standard protocol. RNAseq analysis was performed using the platform BMKCloud (http://www.biocloud.net). Differentially expressed genes under the threshold Fold Change ≥ 1.2 and FDR < 0.01 were considered as significant. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis and GSEA analysis were then performed to explore the related pathways and molecules with CLOCK.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with EasyScript-cDNA synthesis super Mix (EasyScript, #AE311). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR master mix (Vazyme, #q711-03) on a Real-Time System (Bio-Rad). Primer specificity was confirmed by melt-curve analysis, and relative gene expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal control. Cell were transfected with indicate plasmid for 48 hours, harvest cells in cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, and 10% glycerol) with protease inhibitors on ice for 30 minutes. GST pull-down assay

Recombinant GST-tagged p65 proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21 and purified using glutathione-Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare, #17075601). The FLAG-CLOCK proteins were purified from 293T cell lysates using anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Yeasen, #20585ES08), followed by competitive elution with FLAG peptides (sigma, #F3290). Equal amounts of GST-fusion proteins were incubated with FLAG-CLOCK proteins in cell binding buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40) for 8 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed, and bound proteins were analysed by immunoblotting.

In vitro acetylation assay

Recombinant GST-p65 proteins were incubated with purified FLAG-CLOCK in acetylation buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 10% glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 10 mM sodium butyrate, and 50 μM acetyl-CoA) at 30 °C for 2 h. Reactions were terminated by adding 5× SDS loading buffer and boiling at 95 °C for 5 min. Acetylated proteins were detected by immunoblotting using anti-acetyl-lysine antibodies. To confirm enzyme specificity, control reactions were performed without acetyl-CoA.

DNA constructs and mutagenesis

Target gene fragments were amplified by PCR and cloned into pcDNA3.1 vectors. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme, #C214-01) with validated primers. All constructs were verified by Sangon sequencing.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP were performed by using ChIP Kit Magnetic (Abcam, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells transfected with indicate plasmid of 48 h were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin into 200–500 bp fragments. Lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight with DYKDDDDK tag antibody (proteintech, #20543–1-AP) or IgG (Cell signaling, #2729S) control. Protein-DNA complexes were eluted, reverse-cross-linked, and purified. Enriched PD-L1 promoter DNA was quantified by qPCR and normalized to input (PD-L1 promoter primer, forward: 5’GCACTTTAGGACGGAGGGTC3’, reverse: 5’CTCTACTGCCCCCTAGACCA3’).

Luciferase reporter assay

PD-L1 promoter were cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector. Cells were co-transfected with reporter constructs with FLAG-p65 WT or FLAG-p65 K56R as well as Renilla control. After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Trans Detect Double-luciferase reporter assay kit (EasyScript, #FR201-01-v2) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and detected by SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader and normalized to Renilla signals.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.0). All experiments were independently repeated three times and analysed using Prism 8.0 software. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA test. The statistical results were regarded as statistically significant only when p ≤ 0.05 and statistical significance was defined as *p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001.

Results

CLOCK and RORB construct a prognostic risk model and serve as independent indicators in breast cancer

Circadian clock disruption plays a vital role in BC, initially, we aimed to explore the mutation, methylation, and expression profiles of the known 14 circadian genes in BC. Mutation analysis (maftools) identified circadian gene mutations in 5.17% (51/986) of tumors, with PER2/3, NPAS2, PER1, and CLOCK showing rare mutations ( < 1%), suggesting mutations were not the main expression driver (Supplemental Figure 1a). Next, we found the methylation profiles of circadian clock genes showed an obviously different distribution between normal/tumor samples (Supplemental Figure 1b), and principal component analysis (PCA) and t-SNE analyses also confirmed clear segregation by circadian gene expression (Supplemental Figure 1c-1d). In TCGA-BRCA analysis, 11/13 circadian regulators showed differential expression in tumor comparing paired normal tissues (all except ARNTL and CRY1, Fig. 1a). Only CLOCK, ARNTL2, and RORC were upregulated in BC, while all others (CRY2, PER1/2/3, NR1D1, NR1D2, RORA, RORB) were downregulated (Fig. 1a). To evaluate the prognostic potential of circadian clock genes in BC, we conducted univariate Cox regression analysis. CLOCK and RORB emerged as significant predictors of poor overall survival (OS), with hazard ratios of 1.348 (95% CI: 1.063–1.709) and 1.249 (95% CI: 1.023–1.524), respectively (Fig. 1b). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed that high expression of both CLOCK and RORB genes was associated with significantly worse prognosis in BC patients (log-rank p = 0.0092 for CLOCK; p = 0.012 for RORB; Figs. 1c–1d). We subsequently developed a circadian clock (CC) risk score by incorporating CLOCK and RORB expression levels through multivariate Cox regression. Patients in the high CC_score group exhibited significantly reduced OS compared to the low-score group (log-rank p = 0.0026; Fig. 1e). The model demonstrated good predictive accuracy for 5-year OS (AUC = 0.745; Fig. 1g). Validation in the METABRIC cohort confirmed the robustness of our findings: the high CC_score group maintained worse OS outcomes (log-rank p < 0.0001; Fig. 1f) with consistent 5-year OS prediction (AUC = 0.714; Fig. 1h). Multivariate analysis established CC_score as an independent prognostic factor for OS (Fig. 1i), remaining significant after adjustment for TNM stage, ER, PR, and HER2 status. These results demonstrate that CLOCK and RORB serve as independent prognostic biomarkers in BC, and the established CC_score model effectively predicts patient survival. Building upon these findings, CLOCK and RORB were identified as independent prognostic factors in BC, with higher expression levels correlating with poorer outcomes. Since expression profiling revealed that only CLOCK but not RORB was significantly upregulated in tumour tissues compared to normal breast tissue and CLOCK gene expression was significantly correlated with BC aggressive markers MKI67 (Ki-67) and ERBB2 (Her2) but not RORB (Supplemental Figure 2a-2d), we chose to explore the tumor-promoting role of CLOCK and its in-depth mechanisms in BC.

Doxorubicin (Dox), an anthracycline antibiotic, and gemcitabine (Gem), a pyrimidine nucleoside analog, are commonly used chemotherapeutic agents in BC treatment. To investigate CLOCK’s role in chemoresistance, we examined cell viability following DOX and GEM treatment across a concentration range. CLOCK overexpression significantly enhanced the resistance of MCF-7 cells to both Dox and Gem in higher concentrations (Fig. 2d), whereas CLOCK knockdown potentiated the cytotoxic effects of Dox and Gem on MCF-7 cells across all tested concentrations (Fig. 2e). These comprehensive results demonstrate that CLOCK serves as a critical regulator of multiple oncogenic processes in MCF-7 cells, including cell proliferation, migration, and chemotherapeutic resistance.

Association of the CLOCK with the immune microenvironment and treatment responses

Furthermore, we next predicted whether CLOCK expression is related with breast cancer tumor immune microenvironment. Comparative analysis revealed distinct immune checkpoint expression profiles between high and low CLOCK groups (Fig. 3a). Specifically, CLOCK-high BC patients demonstrated elevated expression of PD-L1 (CD274), HAVCR2, PDCD1LG2, and SIGLEC15 (p < 0.05), while showing reduced levels of ITPRIPL1, LAG3, PDCD1, and TIGIT compared to CLOCK-high patients (p < 0.05, Fig. 3a). Immune checkpoints PD-L1 plays a crucial role in tumor immunosuppression, which is involved in the tumor immune escape or activated immune attack and shows higher expression in CLOCK-high BC group. Analysis of immune cell infiltration revealed that CLOCK-high BC is characterized by an immunosuppressive profile, revealing significantly decreased CD8+ T cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, and Tregs, as well as enhanced M2 macrophage and resting CD4+ memory T cells compared to CLOCK-low group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3b). Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed a consistent negative association between CLOCK expression and infiltration levels of CD8+ T cells, NK cells, Tregs, and DCs (Fig. 3c). Meanwhile, CLOCK expression showed a significant positive correlation with M2 macrophage infiltration (Fig. 3c). High CLOCK expression was associated with markedly reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration (critical anti-tumor immune cells), indicating CLOCK-mediated immunosuppression in the BC microenvironment. Therefore, to assess the impact of CLOCK expression on immunotherapy response in breast cancer patients, we evaluated the TIDE score. Consistent with CLOCK-mediated PD-L1 (CD274) upregulation, the CLOCK-high group exhibited a lower TIDE score (Fig. 3d), which predicts superior response to nivolumab. Clock-regulated NF-κB signalling pathway promotes immune escape and tumor progression

Considering CLOCK’s significant correlation with BC prognosis and immune landscape modulation, we next sought to elucidate its functional contributions and mechanistic underpinnings in BC progression. To further investigate the molecular mechanisms by which CLOCK influences BC progression and immunosuppression, we performed RNA sequencing on CLOCK-knockdown MCF-7 cells and their parental control cells. A total of 4045 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, including 1762 upregulated and 2283 downregulated genes (Fig. 4a). Functional and pathway enrichment analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed significant associations with cell cycle, DNA replication, p53 signalling pathway (Fig. 4b). Moreover, the top 20 KEGG pathway enrichment of downregulated DEGs captured our attention, as it is significantly associated with cell proliferation and immune response which include the TNF signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, antigen processing and presentation pathway, NF-κB signalling pathway signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 4c). The enrichment analysis of downregulated DEGs revealed that immune response pathways were significantly enriched, suggesting that tumor cells may evade host immune surveillance by regulating immune-related pathways. To elucidate whether CLOCK regulates PD-L1 expression and whether this regulation is mediated by NF-κB, we investigated a potential interaction between the CLOCK and NF-κB p65 proteins. Co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated the physical association between endogenous CLOCK and p65 in MCF-7 cells, which was further confirmed by the interaction of exogenous FLAG-tagged p65 with CLOCK (Figs. 5a, 5b). In vitro acetylation assays coupled with LC-MS/MS revealed that CLOCK acetylates p65 at Lys56 site, a phylogenetically conserved residue (Fig. 5f). CLOCK acetylates NF-κB p65 at Lys56 site and promotes PD-L1 transcription

To investigate the role of p65 lysine 56 acetylation in regulating PD-L1 expression, we measured PD-L1 mRNA levels and transcriptional activity in MCF-7 cells after transfected with wild-type p65 (p65-WT) or acetylation-deficient mutant plasmid (p65-K56R). Consistent with prior findings, p65-WT significantly enhanced PD-L1 mRNA level and transcription activity, whereas the acetylation-deficient mutant p65-K56R completely abrogated this effect (Figs. 6a–6b). Chromatin immunoprecipitation further revealed the p65-K56R mutation impaired promoter occupancy at the PD-L1 locus, with concomitant reduction in transcriptional output (Fig. 6c). Furthermore, we next investigated whether CLOCK promotes PD-L1 transcription through p65 acetylation. Both at the mRNA and protein levels, PD-L1 expression analysis demonstrated that CLOCK enhances WT-p65-mediated transcriptional activation of PD-L1, while the p65 K56R acetylation-deficient mutant completely abrogates this promotional effect (Figs. 6d–6f). Discussion

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) reported that there was strong evidence for an association between disrupted circadian rhythm and BC incidence and tumor progression, indicating a key role of clock genes in preventing breast epithelial carcinogenesis of BC [13]. In this study, we identified CLOCK as an independent prognostic factor in BC through integrative analysis of circadian gene expression and BC patient outcomes. We further discovered that CLOCK may potentially drives breast cancer immunosuppressive and promote immune escape by regulating the NF-κB pathway. Mechanistically, CLOCK exerts its acetyltransferase activity to acetylate NF-κB p65 at K56 site, thereby enhancing NF-κB-mediated transcriptional activation of PD-L1. Our study suggested that CLOCK may serve as a prognostic biomarker and a potential guide for immunotherapy in BC.

Normal expression of circadian clock genes is important for maintaining normal cell function in the mammary gland and dysregulated circadian clock gene expression may play a key role in the occurrence and development of BC [3]. Studies have found that PER1, PER2, PER3, CRY2, and NR1D1 are downregulated in BC [28], and the low expression of CRY2 is positively correlated with poor prognosis of BC [29], which is consistent with circadian clock gene expression of BC samples from TCGA in our study (Fig. 1a). CLOCK has been established as a prognostic indicator for glioblastoma and liver cancer [30, 31]; however, its expression pattern and clinical relevance in breast cancer (BC) patients remain poorly defined. Our findings demonstrate that CLOCK is highly expressed in BC and correlates with worse patient outcomes, consistent with its role inglioblastoma and liver cancer.

Our study showed that CLOCK expression in BC was associated with the tumor immune checkpoint and tumor infiltrating immune cells. CLOCK was found to be positively correlated with PD-L1 expression and negative correlated with CD8+ T cells in BC. It means that BC patients with a higher CLOCK expression possess better immune cell infiltration properties and may have better responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors treatment, which is the so-called ‘hot tumor’ [32]. Such a hypothesis was subsequently supported by the fact that the group with higher CLOCK was estimated to show better response to PD-1 inhibitor nivolumab. Enhanced anti-tumor immunity effect of CLOCK has been proved in other types of tumors, CLOCK recruits immunosuppressive microglia and drives immunosuppressive microenvironment in GBM. Conversely, inhibition of CLOCK increases intratumoral infiltration of CD8+ T cells and enhances the efficacy of anti-PD1 therapy [30]. The above studies have shown that CLOCK have immunomodulatory roles mediated lymphocyte infiltration and immune reaction in tumor.

CLOCK has been found to promote NF-κB inflammation signalling [33]. Consistent with this, our RNA-seq and qPCR data confirmed that CLOCK knockdown in MCF7 cells downregulates the NF-κB signaling pathway and its target genes. We speculate that the tumor immune regulation function of CLOCK may be exerted by regulating the NF-κB downstream immune regulatory molecules. In this study, transcriptomic data revealed that the MAPK, AKT, and NF-κB pathways were downregulated in CLOCK-knockdown BC cells. Cell proliferation assays were consistent with these findings, showing that CLOCK overexpression promoted proliferation, and vice versa. Additionally, previous research has proved that active NF-κB promote PD-L1 transcription, thereby enhancing tumor immune evasion [34]. Our results showed PD-L1 was downregulated in CLOCK-knockdown BC cell lines and upregulated in CLOCK-overexpressing cells, leading to the hypothesis that CLOCK may facilitate the transcriptional activation of PD-L1 by the NF-κB axis. PD-L1, an immune checkpoint highly expressed in tumor cells, inhibits the CD8+ T cell attack, leading to immune evasion [35]. Breast cancer patients with high CLOCK expression demonstrate elevated PD-L1 levels and lower TIDE scores, anti-PD1 antibodies are recommended, as it may lead to superior therapeutic outcomes. Therefore, CLOCK expression levels could be used as an important indicator for the survival prognosis and immunotherapy response of BC patients.

We further investigated the molecular mechanism by which CLOCK regulates PD-L1 expression through NF-κB. Current research has identified multiple NF-κB acetyltransferases, including p300/CBP and PCAF, which acetylate p65 to enhance its stability and nuclear translocation, thereby potentiating its transcriptional activity [36, 37]. CLOCK also has been demonstrated to possess acetyltransferase activity, and Mary L. et. previously proved that CLOCK promotes p65 acetylation and enhances p65 transactivation [38]. However, the specific acetylation sites of p65 modified by CLOCK remain unidentified, and the relationship between CLOCK and PD-L1 has not yet been reported. Our results demonstrate that CLOCK promotes acetylation of p65 at K56 site, and this acetylation enhances the transcriptional activation of PD-L1. It has been demonstrated that NF-κB acetylation regulates PD-L1 expression, and HDAC5-mediated deacetylation of p65 suppresses PD-L1 transcription [39]. Existing evidence and our findings collectively demonstrate p65 acetylation promotes PD-L1 transactivation. Importantly, we identified CLOCK as a novel acetyltransferase for p65 at K56 site, and mechanistically proved CLOCK-mediated p65 K56 acetylation drives PD-L1 expression. Existing studies have shown that, multiple core circadian rhythm molecules can regulate PD-L1 expression through various pathways. PER2 can inhibit the nuclear translocation of NF-κB, thereby suppressing PD-L1 expression [17]. RORA binds directly to the PD-L1 promoter and recruits HDAC3 to form a transcriptional repressor complex, thereby epigenetically suppressing PD-L1 expression [18]. These findings demonstrate that modulating the expression of circadian rhythm molecules can influence tumor immune escape, thereby impacting the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. Therefore, our development of the CC_score for predicting BC patient prognosis and immunotherapy response holds significant clinical implications. Targeting circadian rhythm molecules to modulate treatment response in BC represents a promising research frontier with significant clinical potential.

Limitations of our work include the following. First, although our model has been validated using the METABRIC dataset, it lacks support from real-world evidence, and our central hypothesis requires testing in prospective clinical trials. Second, the CC_score, which is calculated based on baseline breast cancer (BC) levels, may change dynamically following various treatments, potentially limiting its clinical applicability. Furthermore, while our study elucidates the role of CLOCK-mediated p65 acetylation in promoting PD-L1 expression, the relationships between CLOCK, PD-L1, the tumor immune microenvironment, and immune escape need further validation in animal models and clinical settings. The clinical significance of the p65 K56 site also remains to be fully established. As circadian clock genes were shown to be critical in BC in our study, and previous studies have demonstrated that drugs targeting biological clock genes, including REV-ERB agonists, ROR agonists, and CRY stabilizers, have shown good anti-tumor effects in different tumor models [40, 41]. We believe that potential chemo reagents that normalize the functions of the circadian clock might be used to reverse the malignant phenotype of tumors, but it still requires intensive validation and stepwise clinical trials.

Conclusions

In summary, elevated CLOCK expression in the TCGA breast cancer cohort is significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes, correlates positively with immunosuppression and immune evasion, and predicts superior response to immunotherapy. Mechanistically, CLOCK promotes immune escape by acetylating the NF-κB p65 subunit at lysine 56 (K56) site, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity and upregulating PD-L1 expression in breast cancer cells. CLOCK may serve as a prognostic biomarker and a guide for immunotherapy in breast cancer.
