Severity of COVID-19 Omicron Variants: A Global Systematic Review
Authors: Deepa Malhotra, Rajeev M. Nepal, Irini Zografaki, Moe H. Kyaw, Pinelopi Nikolopoulou, Rodrigo Sini de Almeida, Santiago M. C. Lopez, Stephen Wiblin, Fraser Williams, Sophie Pope, Isabelle Whittle, Fiona Pearson, Daniel Curcio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe continual emergence of new severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) variants drives the need to update evidence on coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) severity and disease burden, and better understand the impact on prevention, treatment, and healthcare systems.MethodsThis systematic review aimed to determine relative disease severity, through comparative measures of hospitalization, intensive care unit admission and mortality, between SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern emerging since Omicron was first identified. A protocol was registered a priori (PROSPERO ID: CRD42024619193). Systematic searches of MEDLINE and EMBASE databases were conducted in November 2024 and supplemented by conference searches from 2022–2024. Population, Exposure, Comparisons, Outcomes (PECO) criteria were used to screen publications for inclusion. Critical appraisal tools published in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Handbook for Evidence Synthesis were used to assess the risk of bias of the primary studies included. The outcomes associated with Omicron variants, identified by sequencing or predominance periods, included hospitalization, admission to intensive care, death, and various composite endpoints.ResultsThirty-two studies fulfilled the eligibility criteria, most reported on relative disease severity for early Omicron BA.5 (n = 23) and XBB (n = 24) variants. Overall, COVID-19 severity appeared largely comparable across the various Omicron subvariants. Among the subset of studies that directly compared various severity outcomes to earlier SARS-CoV-2 variants (n = 7), some reported modest increases or decreases in severity. However, these differences were generally not statistically significant. Five studies stratifying outcomes by the presence of comorbid conditions noted that comorbidities were predictors of significantly worse COVID-19 disease outcomes (p = 0.000–0.027).ConclusionsOverall, this systematic review found the severity of COVID-19 disease to be comparable among Omicron subvariants. As SARS-CoV-2 subvariants continue to emerge, these results highlight the continuing need for vaccination against SARS-CoV-2 infection alongside early antiviral intervention to support short-term management and long-term reduction of COVID-19-associated morbidity and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01352-1.

Key Summary Points

Among studies directly comparing disease severity outcomes to earlier Omicron subvariants, some reported slight increases or decreases in severity that were not statistically significant.Therefore, COVID-19 disease severity, including hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality, was comparable overall across Omicron subvariants.Presence of comorbidities was a consistent predictor of worse COVID-19 outcomes, regardless of which Omicron subvariant predominated.Our findings highlight a persistent severe COVID-19 risk across Omicron subvariants, particularly among individuals with comorbidities, indicating a need to consider continued surveillance, prevention efforts, and disease management.

Introduction

Many protective and risk factors at the individual, institutional, and societal levels actively influence the clinical course of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [1–3]. The continual emergence of new SARS-CoV-2 variants drives the need to update evidence on the clinical presentation, coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) severity, and disease burden, and clarify the impact of emerging variants on prevention, treatment, and healthcare systems.

Vaccination provided substantial protection against severe COVID‑19 disease outcomes during the early phases of the pandemic [4, 5]. The emergence and peak circulation of the Omicron variant, characterized by immune‑evasive mutations, coincided with reduced observed vaccine effectiveness compared with earlier variants [6–8]. This attenuation likely reflects a combination of immune escape and waning immunity over time; nevertheless, vaccination continues to offer meaningful protection against severe COVID-19 disease [6, 9–12].

Despite the availability of effective SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, COVID-19-related hospitalizations and deaths still persist, particularly among individuals deemed to be at higher risk of severe COVID-19 disease [13, 14].

Similar to SARS-CoV-2 spike protein mutations reportedly increasing transmissibility [15–17], differing COVID-19 disease severity has been observed during the evolution of variants of concern (VOC). Previous research has demonstrated reduced COVID-19 disease severity associated with the Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variant compared to that of the previous variants, such as Delta, including lower rates of hospitalization, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, death, and need for respiratory treatment interventions [18, 19]. As such, research into the impact of emergence of novel SARS-CoV-2 variants on severity of COVID-19 disease is needed in the post-Omicron emergence era.

At the time of writing, several systematic reviews have contributed to the body of evidence regarding older SARS-CoV-2 VOC (up to BA.2 subvariant). Additionally, real-world studies indicate that emerging variants have the potential to impact clinical outcomes [20, 21]. However, at the time this systematic review was performed, no evidence had been published on the impact of emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants beyond BA.2 subvariant [19, 22, 23].

This systematic review aimed to determine relative disease severity, through comparative measures such as hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality between SARS-CoV-2 VOC emerging since Omicron was first identified.

Methods

This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024619193), conducted in alignment with the guidance in the Cochrane handbook for systematic reviews of interventions [24], and is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [25].

Search Strategy

Database searches were conducted on November 26th, 2024 following the development of a comprehensive search strategy within the OVID platform for EMBASE® that was then tailored to reflect the unique search terms used across MEDLINE®. For full search terms, please refer to Supplementary Table 1.

To capture a comprehensive evidence base, supplemental gray literature searches were conducted on April 1, 2025, encompassing conference proceedings from January 1, 2022, to December 31, 2024, for the Congress of the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) and the joint annual meeting of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDWeek). Search terms used for gray literature searches are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Additional citation chaining of identified relevant published systematic reviews meeting eligibility criteria but not directly answering our research question was also conducted to identify further eligible primary research articles (Supplementary Table 3).

Study Eligibility

Two reviewers screened titles and abstracts of citations retrieved from the database searches according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria that were based on the Population, Exposure, Comparisons, Outcomes (PECO) criteria outlined in Supplementary Table 4. Eligible records reported SARS-CoV-2 variant as confirmed by sequencing or determined by study author based on regional variant predominance during the time of study with associated acute COVID-19 outcomes indicative of disease severity (e.g., hospital or intensive ICU admission, mortality). Records meeting eligibility criteria at title and abstract screening were taken forward to full-text publication screening undertaken by the same reviewers according to the same PECO criteria. Throughout the screening process, any conflicts in screening decision were resolved by a third, senior reviewer. Relevant data from full-text publications meeting the eligibility criteria were extracted by one reviewer and validated by a second reviewer. COVID-19 coronavirus disease 2019, SARS-CoV-2 severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2, UK United Kingdom, US United States

Most publications (64%) reported on retrospective cohort studies, and focused on Western regions (Americas and Europe [66%]). Mortality was the most frequently reported COVID-19-related outcomes (n = 17 studies), followed by hospitalization (n = 16), and composite outcomes indicative of disease severity (n = 13). Table 1 summarizes key aspects of included study design and outcome reporting.

There was substantial heterogeneity between patient populations in the 32 included studies. The majority of publications analyzed community or hospitalized populations, while other studies specifically focused on patients at higher risk of severe COVID-19 disease, such as organ transplant recipients, patients with hematologic diseases and older individuals aged ≥ 60 years (Supplementary Table 5).

The majority of included studies reported on the impact of Omicron subvariants BA.5 (n = 23) and XBB (n = 24) variants. No publications reporting on Omicron subvariants beyond JN.1 met the eligibility criteria. Comparison of Disease Severity Between Predominant Variant Periods

As anticipated, the observational studies included in this systematic review were heterogenous in design and reported a variety of outcomes with differing metrics to evaluate associations between exposures and health outcomes. Outcome metrics included use of absolute and proportional incidence, rate/time series, and comparative measures of association to explore the risk of outcomes of interest between variant periods.

Of the 32 publications, 14 reported adjusted results regarding the impact of BA.2 BA.4, BA.5, BN.1, BQ.1, CH.1.1, JN.1, HV.1 and XBB subvariants on disease severity/composite outcomes (n = 6), hospitalization (n = 6), mortality (n = 6), ICU admission (n = 2), and oxygen therapy (n = 1). Twelve (86%) of these studies reported results adjusted for age, and 11 studies by sex (79%), in combination with factors including timepoint, race/ethnicity, comorbidities, and geography. Risk of hospitalization, ICU admission, mortality, and disease severity were comparable across the Omicron subvariants, although two studies reported slight increase in severity with the more recent variants (Fig. 3) [27, 28] and five studies reported a modest decrease in disease severity [29–33]. However, 95% confidence intervals were wide and most spanned 1 on the x-axis indicating no statistically significant difference in severity during the post-Omicron emergence variant predominance periods (Fig. 3). Across these studies, severity was assessed spanning the period from BA.4/5 predominance to JN.1, with comparison between the following exposures of interest BA.4/5, BN.1, XBB, BQ.1, BA.2.75, BA.4.6, CH.1.1, and JN.1.

For hospitalization and mortality, one of the two studies reported a difference in comparative risk between assessed variant periods [34, 35]. Specifically, for hospitalization, increased risk was associated with the earlier variant EG.5 versus HV.1 or JN.1 [28, 34], yet risk was comparable between JN.1 and HV.1. In terms of chronology, these three subvariants were in circulation during 2023, with EG.5 increasing in predominance and assigned a variant of interest by the WHO in August, before being superseded by HV.1 in November [36, 37]. Regarding mortality, two studies directly compared the risk of death between variant periods, with conflicting findings. Reduced mortality risk was associated with the more recent BN.1 subvariants when compared to BA.5 (statistical significance not reported), however no difference was observed during the JN.1 variant period versus pre-JN.1 era [33, 35].

Limited statistical comparison between periods of variant predominance were reported for organ support outcomes (Supplementary Table 5). In the one reporting study, a similar trend in comparable severity was reported for the requirement of oxygen therapy between XBB and JN.1 variants (adjusted OR: 0.83, 95% CI 0.54, 1.28; p = 0.402) [38].

Impact of Comorbidity

Given that comorbidities and pre-existing conditions can lead to worse COVID-19 disease outcomes and disease severity [39], subgroup analyses to assess the potential impact of comorbidity on SARS-CoV-2 variant on disease severity were planned a priori as part of this review.

However, 63% (20/33) of included studies only reported the presence of comorbidities or underlying conditions within population baseline demographics. Further, only five studies stratified outcomes by extent of comorbidity (e.g., number, specific conditions, Charlson Comorbidity Index score) to report the effect of comorbid conditions on outcomes. Where specified, comorbid conditions were highly variable and included underlying organ diseases, hypertension, malignancy, Parkinson’s disease, and type 2 diabetes [40–44]. Of the five studies, none reported on the effect of comorbid conditions between SARS-CoV-2 exposure periods, meaning no statistical comparison was reported on the impact of the presence of comorbidities on COVID-19 severity between differing periods of Omicron subvariant predominance. Discussion

Overall, this systematic review found that COVID-19 disease severity was comparable across the reported Omicron subvariants. In addition, data we were able to ascertain from five studies stratifying outcomes by comorbid conditions highlighted that comorbidities were consistent predictors of worse outcomes among patients regardless of variant predominance period. These findings highlight the ongoing need for both effective vaccination campaigns and antiviral treatments to prevent severe outcomes associated with COVID-19 disease.

Despite the awareness of potential confounders and effect modifiers (e.g., comorbidities) [13], many studies did not adequately report detailed baseline characteristics. Further, most studies were not designed to or did not account for confounding (e.g., through restriction, matching, stratification or comprehensive adjustment during regression), and potential effect modification (e.g., subgroup or interaction analyses) was rarely explored.

Despite the results of this literature review highlighting the overall similarity of COVID-19 disease severity between Omicron subvariants, two studies attributed a modest increase in severity to more recent subvariants, yet these were not statistically significant. This contrasts with more recent studies which have identified some associations between decreased COVID-19 disease severity and more recent SARS-CoV-2 Omicron subvariants. For example, in a 2025 retrospective database study including 130,263 US veterans, authors reported a mild trend for decline in risk of hospitalization odds for JN.1 and KP compared to XBB, (September 2023–October 2024) [45]. Nevertheless, these results were not found to be statistically significant, with wide and overlapping confidence intervals for adjusted rates of COVID-19 hospitalization that were attributed to limited statistical power due to low events rates [45]. Further, the authors did not consider the influence of COVID-19 vaccination and previous infection in their analysis [45].

Research into trends regarding COVID-19 disease severity during pre-Omicron variant waves versus early Omicron variant predominance has also suggested a gradual decrease in severity over time, with the exception of the Delta variant which was associated with more severe COVID-19 [46]. The gradual decrease in severity following Omicron emergence was most likely impacted by increasing population -level hybrid immunity over time due to vaccination and prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, and increasing use of antiviral treatments [47, 48].

Another consideration is the impact of antigenic changes with evolution of SARS-CoV-2 subvariants emergence [49]. While this SLR did not aim to determine factors influencing the virulence of different SARS-CoV-2 strains, it is recognized that spike protein mutations have previously significantly impacted SARS-CoV-2 transmissibility [15–17], and may also play an influencing role in differences in COVID-19 severity between subvariants [15, 16]. The overall similar risk of COVID-19 hospitalization, mortality, ICU admission observed in direct comparisons between post-Omicron emergence subvariants in this systematic review reflects an interplay of population-level fluctuations in immunity (i.e., COVID-19 booster vaccinations and waning over time followed by increased natural immunity in response to newly emerging subvariants) [50], surges in SARS-CoV-2 circulation based on human behaviors (e.g., seasonal peaks in infections), as well as potential influence of gradual antigenic drift [51–53].

Furthermore, population immunity has likely increased and become more heterogeneous over time; however, the durability and waning of individual immunity, particularly as a function of the route of acquisition (vaccination, infection, or hybrid immunity), remain poorly understood. Therefore, COVID-19 severity across SARS-CoV-2 variants must be interpreted in the context of these temporal dynamics of population immunity. As successive variants emerged in populations with progressively higher and more heterogeneous levels of prior immunity, apparent reductions in severity, particularly for later variants such as Omicron, likely reflect differences in immune history and time since last exposure rather than intrinsic reductions in severity alone [19, 54, 55]. Studies that do not adequately account for calendar time, waning immunity, vaccination status, and prior infection may bias comparisons of severity across variants and overstate biological differences.

With these factors persisting over time, our findings underscore the persistent risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes across Omicron subvariants, as well as the elevated risk of severe COVID-19 amongst those with comorbidities. This highlights a need to consider continued COVID-19 disease surveillance alongside prevention and treatment strategies such as variant-adapted vaccines and antiviral use to mitigate this persistent risk. Beyond the need to protect and prevent severe outcomes among individuals, effective COVID-19 vaccination programs are also needed to reduce the short- and long-term burden associated with COVID-19 on healthcare systems and providers [56, 57].

The conduct of meta-analyses to generate pooled estimates of variant effect on well-reported outcomes such as hospitalization and ICU admission was considered. However, the heterogenous study populations, exposures (even when grouped from individual VOCs into broader post-Omicron time periods) and outcome definitions led to too few comparable studies to support a robust synthesis. Therefore, a key consideration for future research following this systematic review could be to focus on analyzing variant data confirmed by genetic sequencing as opposed to assuming variant infection based on predominance periods. Ensuring the accurate capture of variant data would help to avoid incorrect attribution of outcomes to specific variants, which may in turn weaken the validity of study findings. Additionally, at the time of conducting the search strategies for this SR, KP.2, KP.3 and XEC were not reported in any included studies, thus highlighting a knowledge gap in the literature during the search period. Since the conduct of this SR, studies have been published which do report on these more recent Omicron subvariants, thus contributing to bridging this data gap [45, 58]. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to expand the available evidence base, particularly for more recent Omicron subvariants such as LP.8.1, NB.1.8.1, and XFG, for which no relevant publications were identified in this systematic review.

Limitations

Following registration on PROSPERO, the decision was taken to follow a staged approach to identify evidence on adult and pediatric populations (first adults, then pediatrics) to allow for the expansion of search methods to identify pediatric studies. Few studies included in this review were directly comparable either due to their design, population of interest, time horizon and in turn Omicron variants (exposure) of interest. Also, due to the lack of alignment in statistical results format for comparability, the studies comparator was also often in the pre-Omicron variant period, which was outside of the scope for inclusion in this review. Furthermore, this review examined COVID-19 disease severity outcomes across Omicron subvariants and did not systematically evaluate vaccine efficacy and effectiveness, safety or adverse events.

Another limitation of this review is the absence of pre-specified analysis of outcomes stratified by breakthrough infection status. However, breakthrough infection status was inconsistently reported across published studies, limiting our ability to examine associated disease severity. Therefore, these data gaps represent an important area for future research. Interpretation of findings is also limited by varying definition of exposure, with approximately half of study authors defining exposure based on variant predominance during the study period, and the remaining studies using variant sequencing. This is a common and acknowledged limitation of the conduct and synthesis of observational COVID-19 studies.

Similarly, when considering study size, any imbalance in composition between negative and positive SARS-CoV-2 cases warrants consideration. Although such an imbalance does not automatically invalidate severity estimates, testing and selection biases could influence results and distort how severe a variant appears.

Furthermore, only five studies stratified outcomes of interest by comorbid conditions, which alone may not present a holistic overview of COVID-19 disease severity in vulnerable populations.

Additionally, in accordance with the PECO criteria, only English-language articles were eligible for inclusion. Nevertheless, given the breadth of literature available relating to COVID-19, this was not anticipated to have impacted the findings or conclusions from this review. Of the total 1988 abstracts screened for eligibility, only 25 were excluded due to not being available in the English language.

Conclusions

Overall, this systematic review found no conclusive evidence that the severity of COVID-19 disease differs significantly between Omicron subvariants. Although only a limited number of studies assessed the association between similar outcomes and variants of interest, the findings provide valuable insights into the implications of the evolving COVID-19 landscape. Additionally, these findings highlight the persistent need for prevention or early intervention of SARS-CoV-2 infection, in order to decrease COVID-19-associated morbidity and mortality. Ultimately, the results of this study may enhance our understanding of the clinical presentation and burden of emerging VOCs, and clarify whether there is a need to investigate these variants in greater depth.
