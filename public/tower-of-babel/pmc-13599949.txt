Temporal changes in bronchopulmonary dysplasia severity distribution associated with evolving respiratory practices in very preterm infants

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) remains one of the major chronic complications of prematurity and continues to affect a substantial proportion of infants born very preterm.1 Despite improvements in neonatal survival and advances in respiratory care, BPD remains a major cause of long-term respiratory morbidity, neurodevelopmental impairment and increased healthcare utilisation during childhood.2 3 The pathogenesis of BPD is multifactorial and reflects the interaction between structural lung immaturity, inflammation, oxygen toxicity and ventilator-induced lung injury during a critical period of pulmonary development.1 2 As neonatal intensive care practices evolve, changes in respiratory support strategies and perinatal management may influence both the incidence and severity distribution of BPD among preterm infants.4

Over the past two decades, neonatal respiratory management has increasingly focused on minimising lung injury through lung-protective ventilation strategies and greater use of non-invasive respiratory support. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), non-invasive ventilation and less invasive surfactant administration (LISA) techniques have been adopted to reduce exposure to invasive mechanical ventilation, a well-recognised contributor to BPD.5 6 Additional strategies, including optimised surfactant therapy, caffeine administration, careful oxygen targeting and selective use of postnatal corticosteroids, have also been implemented to improve respiratory outcomes in preterm infants.7 8 These evolving approaches may influence respiratory support patterns and the observed severity distribution of BPD.

At the same time, improvements in perinatal and neonatal care have increased survival among extremely preterm infants, who remain at highest risk of developing BPD. As survival improves, the epidemiology of BPD may shift, with potential increases in moderate disease even when severe BPD and mortality remain stable.3 4 Several population-based studies have reported temporal changes in BPD incidence, but fewer investigations have examined detailed respiratory management practices alongside BPD severity within a single neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) across defined clinical periods.4 9 10 Data describing temporal changes in respiratory management and BPD severity from Middle Eastern neonatal populations remain limited, with most evidence derived from North American and European cohorts.3 11

The present study evaluated temporal changes in respiratory management practices and BPD outcomes among infants born before 32 weeks of gestation admitted to a tertiary NICU over a 9-year period. We compared respiratory management patterns and BPD severity across three predefined clinical eras and examined associations between study era and key BPD-related outcomes after adjustment for relevant perinatal factors.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the NICU at King Abdulaziz Medical City, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, a tertiary referral centre providing care for very preterm infants requiring advanced respiratory and cardiovascular support. The study evaluated temporal changes in respiratory management practices and BPD outcomes over a 9-year period.

All infants born before 32 completed weeks of gestation and admitted between January 2017 and December 2025 were eligible for inclusion. Infants with major congenital anomalies affecting cardiopulmonary physiology and those transferred to other hospitals before outcome assessment were excluded.

The study period was divided into three predefined eras to reflect major changes in healthcare delivery and neonatal respiratory practice within the institution. Era 1 represented the pre-COVID period (2017–2019), during which neonatal care was delivered according to established institutional practices. Era 2 represented the COVID period (2020–2022), when pandemic-related operational changes influenced hospital workflow, staffing and clinical service delivery. Era 3 represented the post-COVID period (2023–2025), following relocation of the neonatal service to a newly constructed Women’s Health Hospital. This transition was accompanied by expansion of neonatal intensive care capacity from 40 to 50 intensive care cots within a service comprising 50 intensive care cots and 36 high-dependency beds. During the same period, annual institutional deliveries increased from approximately 9000 to 10 000 births, respiratory therapist staffing was expanded and junior medical staffing and neonatal fellowship positions were increased, while senior consultant staffing remained unchanged. These organisational developments coincided with the progressive adoption of contemporary respiratory management strategies, including greater use of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), introduction of LISA, prolonged non-invasive respiratory support and evolving respiratory pharmacotherapy. The era classification was defined a priori to reflect these distinct phases of organisational development and clinical practice.

The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

NICU respiratory management practices

Respiratory management followed institutional lung-protective ventilation practices. Initial respiratory support was individualised according to gestational age, respiratory effort, oxygen requirement and blood gas status. CPAP was commonly used as primary non-invasive support, while endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were used in infants with significant respiratory distress, apnoea or inadequate ventilation.

Both conventional mechanical ventilation and high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) were available, with HFOV generally reserved for infants with severe respiratory failure or refractory hypercapnia. Non-invasive respiratory support modalities included CPAP, nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation and HFNC. Extubation strategies aimed to facilitate transition from invasive to non-invasive respiratory support where clinically feasible.

Surfactant therapy for respiratory distress syndrome was administered according to clinical and radiographic assessment using conventional intubation, INSURE (INtubation–SURfactant–Extubation) or LISA techniques. Caffeine therapy was routinely administered to infants born before 32 weeks of gestation. Postnatal corticosteroids were used selectively in infants with evolving chronic lung disease, while inhaled nitric oxide was administered in selected infants with pulmonary hypertension or severe hypoxaemic respiratory failure.

Data collection and study variables

Clinical data were extracted retrospectively from the electronic medical record system using standardised definitions. Data included perinatal characteristics, delivery room management, respiratory support practices, respiratory pharmacotherapy and clinical outcomes during hospital admission.

Baseline variables included gestational age, birth weight, sex, mode of delivery, prolonged rupture of membranes, maternal diabetes and hypertension, chorioamnionitis, antenatal steroid exposure and 5-minute Apgar score.

Delivery room management was categorised according to the primary mode of respiratory support at birth. Initial respiratory support following NICU admission was classified as invasive ventilation, including conventional ventilation or HFOV, or non-invasive respiratory support.

Respiratory management during admission included exposure to HFOV, duration of invasive and non-invasive respiratory support, use of HFNC and extubation failure within 72 hours.

Respiratory pharmacotherapy variables included surfactant administration, surfactant delivery approach, postnatal corticosteroid exposure, caffeine therapy and inhaled nitric oxide use.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was BPD severity at 36 weeks of postmenstrual age (PMA), classified according to the evidence-based definition proposed by Jensen et al.4 Grade 1 BPD was defined as nasal cannula support at ≤2 L/min, grade 2 as nasal cannula support at >2 L/min or non-invasive positive pressure support (CPAP or non-invasive ventilation) and grade 3 as invasive mechanical ventilation.

BPD was analysed as any BPD, individual severity grades (grades 1–3) and moderate-to-severe BPD (grades 2–3). Secondary outcomes included death before hospital discharge, the composite outcome of death before hospital discharge or BPD at 36 weeks of PMA and home oxygen therapy at discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for distributional characteristics and are presented as medians with IQRs, while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The proportion of missing data was low for all study variables, and analyses were therefore performed using available-case data without formal imputation.

Comparisons across the three study eras were performed using non-parametric and categorical statistical methods. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate.

Temporal changes in BPD severity across study eras were evaluated descriptively using stratified BPD severity categories and composite respiratory outcomes.

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to examine associations between study era and key respiratory outcomes, including moderate-to-severe BPD (grade 2–3), grade 3 BPD, the composite outcome of death before hospital discharge or BPD at 36 weeks of PMA and mortality before hospital discharge. Regression models were adjusted for clinically relevant baseline variables including gestational age, infant sex, caesarean delivery, complete antenatal steroid exposure and 5-minute Apgar score. These variables were selected a priori because they represent baseline perinatal characteristics that may confound the association between study era and respiratory outcomes. Maternal diabetes, prolonged rupture of membranes and surfactant administration were not included because surfactant administration reflects postnatal respiratory management that evolved across study eras and may lie on the causal pathway between study era and outcome. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed before model fitting and was not considered significant. The earliest study era (2017–2019) was used as the reference category. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs are reported.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.31 (IBM).

Results

A total of 1300 infants born at less than 32 weeks of gestation were assessed for eligibility during the study period. Following exclusion of 41 infants according to predefined criteria before enrolment, 1259 infants were included in the final study cohort. The cohort was stratified into three predefined clinical eras: era 1 (2017–2019; n=529), era 2 (2020–2022; n=318) and era 3 (2023–2025; n=412) (figure 1). Baseline characteristics according to study era

Baseline characteristics differed modestly across the study eras (table 1). Infants born during era 2 had lower gestational age and birth weight compared with eras 1 and 3 (both p<0.001). Caesarean delivery increased from 62.2% in era 1 to 72.3% in era 3 (p=0.001), while maternal diabetes and premature rupture of membranes also increased across eras. Complete antenatal steroid exposure was highest in era 3 (66.6%), whereas infant sex distribution, maternal hypertension, chorioamnionitis and 5-minute Apgar scores remained similar across eras.

Respiratory management practices across study eras

Respiratory management practices changed significantly across the study eras (table 2). HFNC exposure increased progressively from 43.7% in era 1 to 70.6% in era 3 (p<0.001), accompanied by an increase in the duration of non-invasive respiratory support from a median of 42–10 days to 11 (4–29) days (p<0.001). Initial HFOV use increased from 6.6% in era 1 to 13.6% in era 3 (p<0.001). Surfactant administration also increased over time (62.8% vs 77.0% vs 74.7%; p<0.001), while LISA was introduced predominantly during era 3 (7.8%). Postnatal corticosteroid exposure increased from 16.5% in era 1 to 36.4% in era 3 (p<0.001). In contrast, overall HFOV exposure during admission remained similar across eras, suggesting that HFOV was increasingly used as the initial ventilation strategy for selected infants rather than more frequently throughout the overall admission.

BPD severity and oxygen-related outcomes across study eras

The proportion of infants with any BPD increased across the study eras, from 25.2% (118/468) in era 1 to 30.9% (85/275) in era 2 and 37.7% (139/369) in era 3 (p<0.001). This increase was predominantly attributable to grade 2 BPD, which rose from 10.3% (48/468) to 14.2% (39/275) and 22.5% (83/369), respectively (p<0.001). In contrast, grade 1 BPD (p=0.852) and grade 3 BPD (p=0.931) did not differ significantly across eras. Death before hospital discharge was also similar across eras (11.9%, 14.5% and 10.9%, respectively; p=0.331). The composite of death before hospital discharge or BPD at 36 weeks PMA increased from 33.7% (177/526) in era 1 to 40.3% (128/318) in era 2 and 43.9% (179/408) in Era 3 (p=0.005). Home oxygen use at discharge remained infrequent and did not differ across eras (p=0.790) (table 3).

Adjusted associations between study era and respiratory outcomes

After adjustment for gestational age, infant sex, caesarean delivery, complete antenatal steroid exposure, and 5-minute Apgar score, infants born during era 3 had significantly higher odds of moderate-to-severe BPD compared with era 1 (adjusted OR 3.29, 95% CI 2.15 to 5.05; p<0.001) (table 4). In contrast, no significant differences were observed for grade 3 BPD. Era 3 was also associated with higher odds of the composite outcome of BPD or death before 36 weeks of PMA (adjusted OR 2.08, 95% CI 1.47 to 2.94; p<0.001). Significant ordinal per-era trends were observed for both moderate-to-severe BPD and the composite outcome, whereas mortality before discharge remained unchanged across eras.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study evaluated temporal changes in respiratory management practices and BPD outcomes among infants born before 32 weeks of gestation over a 9-year period within a tertiary NICU. Respiratory management practices evolved across study eras, including increased use of HFNC, prolonged non-invasive respiratory support, introduction of LISA, and greater postnatal corticosteroid exposure. Over the same period, BPD incidence increased, primarily driven by an increase in grade 2 disease, while grade 3 BPD and mortality before hospital discharge remained stable. After adjustment for key perinatal factors, infants in the most recent era had higher odds of moderate-to-severe BPD and the composite outcome of death before hospital discharge or BPD at 36 weeks of PMA. Importantly, the observed increase in grade 2 BPD occurred despite stable rates of severe BPD and mortality, suggesting a shift in the distribution of BPD severity rather than a worsening of overall respiratory morbidity. This pattern is likely multifactorial and should be interpreted in the context of differences in patient characteristics between study eras, improved survival of extremely preterm infants, evolving respiratory management practices, and contemporary BPD classification. These findings suggest that temporal changes in respiratory management practices may influence the observed severity distribution of BPD in contemporary neonatal care.

Respiratory management evolved substantially across the study period, reflecting the adoption of contemporary lung-protective strategies in neonatal care. Non-invasive respiratory support increased over time, including greater use of HFNC and longer durations of non-invasive support during NICU admission. These findings align with efforts to reduce exposure to invasive mechanical ventilation, a recognised contributor to ventilator-associated lung injury and BPD.1 2 In parallel, surfactant administration increased across eras, accompanied by the introduction of LISA techniques during the most recent era. These findings are consistent with the wider adoption of minimally invasive surfactant delivery strategies in contemporary neonatal care.6 12 13 Exposure to postnatal corticosteroids also increased, likely reflecting evolving approaches to chronic lung disease and attempts to facilitate extubation in infants with persistent ventilator dependence.8 14 15 Despite these changes, the duration of invasive ventilation and extubation failure rates remained similar across eras, suggesting that increased use of non-invasive respiratory support primarily reflected evolving postextubation management practices rather than greater initial respiratory illness severity. Because the Jensen classification incorporates the mode of respiratory support at 36 weeks of PMA, prolonged dependence on non-invasive respiratory support may have contributed to the observed increase in grade 2 BPD without a corresponding increase in grade 3 BPD.

Against this background of evolving respiratory management, the increase in BPD across study eras was primarily driven by grade 2 disease, whereas rates of grade 3 BPD and mortality remained stable, suggesting a redistribution rather than progression of disease severity. This pattern is likely multifactorial. Although our analyses adjusted for key perinatal characteristics, differences in gestational age and birth weight between study eras, together with improved survival of extremely preterm infants who are inherently at greater risk of BPD, may also have contributed to the observed increase in grade 2 disease. Organisational changes during the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent expansion of our NICU may likewise have influenced respiratory management and clinical decision-making. In addition, greater use and prolonged duration of non-invasive respiratory support, including HFNC, should be considered in the context of the contemporary Jensen BPD classification, which incorporates the mode of respiratory support at 36 weeks of PMA. Consequently, increased use of HFNC may increase the likelihood of meeting criteria for grade 2 BPD without necessarily reflecting more severe underlying pulmonary injury. However, because the Jensen classification is based solely on respiratory support at 36 weeks of PMA, our data cannot determine whether continued HFNC reflected persistent pulmonary disease or differences in respiratory support and weaning practices. Similar associations between respiratory support modality and BPD classification have been described previously.4 9 10 16 Taken together, these findings suggest that temporal changes in grade 2 BPD should be interpreted within the broader context of evolving neonatal respiratory practice and contemporary BPD classification, rather than as evidence of worsening pulmonary disease alone.

These observations are consistent with findings from large population-based neonatal cohorts. Data from the NICHD (Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development) Neonatal Research Network and other studies have shown that improvements in survival among extremely preterm infants have been accompanied by higher rates of BPD among survivors.3 10 17 18 Similar patterns have been reported in international cohorts, where increasing survival of extremely preterm infants has shifted the burden toward chronic respiratory morbidity.9 19 Observational studies have also demonstrated that evolving respiratory care practices, including greater use of non-invasive ventilation and changing oxygen management strategies, may influence the epidemiology and classification of BPD.3 10 19 20 Similar variability in BPD incidence across neonatal units has been described in international databases, where differences in respiratory support practices and oxygen management strategies may contribute to variation in reported BPD rates.20–23 While previous studies have described temporal changes in BPD incidence, fewer have examined detailed respiratory management practices alongside contemporary BPD severity classification within a single NICU over an extended period. Collectively, these findings support the interpretation that temporal changes in BPD reflect both improved survival and evolving respiratory management practices.

One component of the evolving respiratory management strategy was the introduction of LISA during the most recent study era. Although relatively few infants received this intervention, previous studies have shown that minimally invasive surfactant administration reduces exposure to mechanical ventilation,6 12 whereas its effect on BPD has been more variable and appears to depend on the broader respiratory management strategy employed.5 24 Given its limited use within our cohort, it is unlikely that introduction of this technique alone accounted for the observed temporal changes in BPD severity.

This study has several strengths and limitations. It included a large cohort of very preterm infants managed within a single tertiary NICU over a 9-year period, enabling evaluation of temporal changes in respiratory management practices and BPD outcomes across multiple clinical eras within a consistent clinical environment. Detailed respiratory management and clinical outcome data were available from the institutional electronic medical record, and multivariate analyses were performed to adjust for key perinatal factors. The study also provides data from a Middle Eastern neonatal population, which remains underrepresented in studies of BPD and neonatal respiratory management. However, the retrospective single-centre design limits causal inference and may reduce generalisability to units with different patient populations or respiratory practices, although the use of a consistent institutional setting reduced variability arising from differences in clinical protocols and respiratory management approaches. Validated neonatal illness severity scores, such as Score for Neonatal Acute Physiology II (SNAP-II) and Clinical Risk Index for Babies II (CRIB-II), were not routinely collected. Detailed measures of illness severity, ventilator settings and cumulative oxygen exposure were also not consistently available and may have influenced respiratory management decisions and subsequent BPD classification. However, adjustment for important baseline perinatal variables and the availability of early respiratory severity markers, including delivery room intubation and initial HFOV use, partially mitigated this limitation, although residual confounding by baseline illness severity cannot be excluded. In addition, contemporary BPD definitions incorporate respiratory support requirements at 36 weeks of PMA and may therefore be influenced by evolving respiratory support practices over time, which was a central focus of the present study. Finally, long-term respiratory and neurodevelopmental outcomes after hospital discharge were not available, limiting assessment of the longer-term clinical significance of the observed changes in BPD severity.

Conclusions

In this single-centre cohort of infants born before 32 weeks of gestation, respiratory management practices evolved over time, with increased use of non-invasive respiratory support and changes in respiratory pharmacotherapy. Over the same period, BPD increased, primarily due to a rise in grade 2 disease, while grade 3 BPD and mortality remained stable. These findings suggest that temporal changes in BPD may partly reflect evolving respiratory support practices and contemporary classification systems rather than a clear increase in severe pulmonary morbidity.
