Public-Key Quantum Money and Fast Real Transforms
Jake Doliskani, Morteza Mirzaei, Ali Mousavi

Abstract
We propose a public-key quantum money scheme based on group actions and the Hartley transform. Our scheme adapts the quantum money scheme of Zhandry (2024), replacing the Fourier transform with the Hartley transform. This substitution ensures the banknotes have real amplitudes rather than complex amplitudes, which could offer both computational and theoretical advantages. To support this new construction, we propose a new verification algorithm that uses group action twists to address verification failures caused by the switch to real amplitudes. We also show how to efficiently compute the serial number associated with a money state using a new algorithm based on continuous-time quantum walks. Finally, we present a recursive algorithm for the quantum Hartley transform, achieving lower gate complexity than prior work, and demonstrate how to compute other real quantum transforms, such as the quantum sine transform, using the quantum Hartley transform as a subroutine.



1
Introduction

In a seminal paper, Wiesner
[
29
]
introduced the concept of quantum money, where bills are represented by quantum states. In contrast with classical money, quantum money cannot be counterfeited using general copying machines. This is a consequence of a fundamental theorem in quantum mechanics called the no-cloning theorem. Wiesner’s scheme, which in modern terminology is called a private-key quantum money scheme, had significant drawbacks. In particular, in a private-key scheme the bank is required to verify each bill, meaning it must be involved in every transaction. This is the main reason that these schemes are not generally practical.

In 2009, Aaronson
[
1
]
proposed the first concrete proposal for a different type of quantum money, known as
public-key
quantum money, that did not have such drawbacks. In public-key quantum money anyone can verify the bill while only the bank can issue it. Aaronson’s scheme was later broken by Lutomirski et al.
[
24
]
. In the years since, several alternative constructions have been explored
[
2
,
17
,
30
,
19
,
20
,
23
,
31
]
, yet each has either been broken
[
14
,
26
,
7
,
23
]
or relies on non-standard cryptographic assumptions.

Quantum money from group actions and the Fourier transform.

A candidate for secure public-key quantum money based on abelian group actions was recently proposed by Zhandry
[
31
]
. In this scheme, money states correspond to group-action Fourier states, and serial numbers are elements of a group; see Section
6.1
. Verification is performed using a group-action phase kickback unitary that extracts the serial number from the money state. It was later proved by Doliskani
[
15
]
that the scheme is secure in the generic group action model.

1.1
This work

Motivation.

We propose a public-key quantum money scheme, which is an adaptation of Zhandry’s scheme, using the quantum Hartley transform over finite abelian groups. The motivation behind this proposal is multifold. First, as a consequence of using the Hartley transform, the banknotes will have real amplitudes. This is in contrast to the original scheme, in which the banknotes have complex amplitudes. We believe that this might lead to both computational and theoretical advantages. As a concrete example of a theoretical difference between real and complex quantum states, consider the following scenario: given any two real orthonormal bases
\{\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle\}
and
\{\lvert\psi_{j}\rangle\}
of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
, one can easily show that

\sum_{j}\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle=\sum_{j}\lvert\psi_{j}\rangle\lvert\psi_{j}\rangle.

Such an identity does not, in general, hold for complex bases, and this is the main reason Theorem 4.4 of
[
31
]
fails to prove that the scheme is a quantum lightning scheme.

Another motivation behind our instantiation is that this work can serve as a starting point for the use of real quantum transforms in quantum cryptography and, more broadly, in quantum computing. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time the quantum Hartley transform has been successfully used in a significant quantum computing construction. Finally, we hope that this work itself serves as motivation for further research on the optimization of real quantum transforms.

Contributions.

We propose new algorithms for both the verification of the quantum money scheme and the efficient computation of real quantum transforms.

Replacing the quantum Fourier transform with the quantum Hartley transform in Zhandry’s scheme is a straightforward task, as the Hartley transform enjoys essentially the same arithmetic properties as the Fourier transform. However, the verification algorithm under the Hartley transform breaks down, in the sense that it fails to distinguish between certain banknotes. As a result, the verification algorithm accepts some illegitimate banknotes with probability
1
. To address this issue, we propose a new verification algorithm that relies on group action
twists
.

Verification algorithms, in any quantum money scheme, take as input the money state along with its associated serial number. However, we show that the serial number of any given money state can be efficiently computed. To achieve this, we propose a new algorithm based on continuous-time quantum walks.

With regard to efficient real quantum transforms, we present a new algorithm for the quantum Hartley transform that exploits its recursive structure. A similar recursive algorithm was previously proposed in
[
3
]
, based on a well-known decomposition of the Hartley transform. Our algorithm, however, is simpler to implement and easier to analyze, which allows us to provide a more explicit estimate of its gate complexity. We also compare our algorithm to that of
[
22
]
, which uses the quantum Fourier transform as a subroutine. Compared to both these algorithms, we demonstrate that our algorithm achieves lower gate complexity. Finally, we show how to efficiently implement other real quantum transforms using the quantum Hartley transform, by presenting an efficient algorithm for the quantum sine transform that uses the quantum Hartley transform as a subroutine.

Preliminaries

We follow the presentation of
[
15
]
regarding group actions and quantum computation throughout this paper. A finite Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
of dimension
N
is a complex Euclidean space, isomorphic as a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space to
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. An
N
-dimensional quantum state is represented by a unit vector
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
, where
\lvert\cdot\rangle
denotes Dirac notation. In practice, we typically use specific bases for a Hilbert space. For example, when considering a finite group
G
, we work with the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{C}^{G}
, which is a
\lvert G\rvert
-dimensional space spanned by the basis
\{\lvert g\rangle:g\in G\}
.

2.1
Group actions

For a group
G
and a set
X
, we say that
G
acts on
X
if there is a mapping
*:G\times X\to X
that satisfies the following properties:

1.

Compatibility: for every
g,h\in G
and every
x\in X
,
g*(h*x)=(gh)*x
,

2.

Identity: for the identity
1\in G
and every
x\in X
,
1*x=x
.

We use the notation
(G,X,*)
to denote a group
G
acting on a set
X
through the action
*
. A group action is called
regular
if for every
x,y\in X
there exists a unique
g\in G
such that
g*x=y
. In order for a group action
(G,X,*)
to be suitable for algorithmic applications, the group
G
, the set
X
, and the action
*
must adhere to specific properties. This motivates the concept of an
effective group action
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
X
a finite set. A group action
(G,X,*)
is said to be effective if it satisfies the following properties:

i)

There are efficient algorithms for elementary operations such as membership testing, equality testing, sampling uniform elements and group operation for
G
,

ii)

There are efficient algorithms for membership testing and unique representation in
X
, and

iii)

There exists an efficient algorithm for the action
*
.

In this paper, we assume that all group actions are effective and, unless otherwise stated, that we are working with regular group actions. A central problem in group-action cryptography is the discrete logarithm problem for group actions, defined as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Group Action DLP)
.

Let
(G,X,*)
be an effective group action. Given a pair
(x,g*x)
, where
g\in G
, the discrete logarithm problem (DLP) is to compute
g
.

We say that the DLP assumption holds for
(G,X,*)
if no quantum polynomial time (QPT) algorithm can solve the DLP with respect to
(G,X,*)
. A group action for which the DLP assumption holds is referred to as a
cryptographic group action
.

2.2
The Fourier transform

Let
G
be an abelian group. The set of characters of
G
, denoted by
\hat{G}
, is the set of homomorphisms
\chi(a,\cdot):G\to\mathbb{C}
where
a\in G
. If
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{N_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{N_{k}}
then the character
\chi(a,\cdot)
can be explicitly written as

\chi(a,x)=\omega_{N_{1}}^{a_{1}x_{1}}\cdots\omega_{N_{k}}^{a_{k}x_{k}}

where
\omega_{M}=\exp(2\pi i/M)
is a primitive
M
-th root of unity. The Fourier transform of a function
f:G\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

\hat{f}(a)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G\rvert}}\sum_{x\in G}\chi(a,x)f(x).

The quantum Fourier transform of a (normalized) state
\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\lvert g\rangle
is given by
\sum_{x\in G}\hat{f}(x)\lvert x\rangle
. For a regular group action
(G,X,*)
, any subset
S\subseteq G
, any
y\in X
, and any
h\in G
, we define

\lvert S^{(h)}*y\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert S\rvert}}\sum_{g\in S}\chi(g,h)\lvert g*y\rangle.

(1)

There are two orthonormal bases of the space
\mathbb{C}^{X}
. One basis is
\{\lvert x\rangle:x\in X\}
. For a fixed element
x\in X
, this basis is the same as
\{\lvert g*x\rangle:g\in G\}
, which follows from the fact that the action is regular and thus
\lvert X\rvert=\lvert G\rvert
. The other basis is given by the states

\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G\rvert}}\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g,h)\lvert g*x\rangle,\quad h\in G.

These states are simultaneous eigenstates of the group action operation. Specifically, for the unitary
U_{k}:\lvert y\rangle\mapsto\lvert k*y\rangle
, where
k\in G
, we have
U_{k}\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle=\chi(-k,h)\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle
. These states resemble the set of Fourier states over the abelian group
G
. We will also sometimes refer to them as
Fourier states
.

The
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
algorithm.

Given a state
\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle
, there is an efficient algorithm for computing
h
. Specifically, there is a unitary operator that performs the transformation
\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\lvert 0\rangle\mapsto\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\lvert h\rangle
using the
phase kickback
technique. To see this, start with the state
\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\lvert 0\rangle
, apply the quantum Fourier transform to the second register, and then apply the unitary
\sum_{k\in G}U_{k}\otimes\lvert k\rangle\langle k\rvert
to both registers. This results in the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G\rvert}}\sum_{k\in G}\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\chi(-k,h)\lvert k\rangle.

Finally, applying the inverse quantum Fourier transform to the second register yields
\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\lvert h\rangle
.

3
Fast Real Transforms

The Hartley transform.

Let
N
be a positive integer, and let
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
be the additive cyclic group of integers modulo
N
. The Hartley transform of a function
f:\mathbb{Z}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is the function
\mathsf{H}_{N}(f):\mathbb{Z}_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\mathsf{H}_{N}(f)(a)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)f(y),

where
\mathrm{cas}(x)=\cos(x)+\sin(x)
. Like the Fourier transform,
\mathsf{H}_{N}
is a linear operator, and it can be easily shown that it is unitary. The Hartley transform can be defined for general abelian groups. Let
G=\mathbb{Z}_{N_{1}}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{N_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{N_{k}}
be a decomposition of an abelian group
G
into cyclic groups. For a function
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
, the transform
\mathsf{H}_{G}(f):G\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\mathsf{H}_{G}(f)(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G\rvert}}\sum_{y_{1}=0}^{N_{1}-1}\cdots\sum_{y_{k}=0}^{N_{k}-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi a_{1}y_{1}}{N_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{2\pi a_{k}y_{k}}{N_{k}}\Big)f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k}),

(2)

where
(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})\in G
. Let
\alpha(\bm{y})=y_{1}/N_{1}+\cdots+y_{k}/N_{k}
. A compact form of the expression for
\mathsf{H}_{G}
is

\mathsf{H}_{G}(f)(\bm{a})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G\rvert}}\sum_{\bm{y}\in G}\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\langle\bm{a},\alpha(\bm{y})\rangle)f(\bm{y}).

It follows from the identity

\mathrm{cas}(2\pi\langle\bm{a},\alpha(\bm{y})\rangle)=\frac{1-i}{2}\chi(\bm{a},\bm{y})+\frac{1+i}{2}\chi(-\bm{a},\bm{y})

that

\mathsf{H}_{G}=\frac{1-i}{2}\mathsf{F}_{G}+\frac{1+i}{2}\mathsf{F}_{G}^{*},

(3)

where
\mathsf{F}_{G}
is the Fourier transform over
G
. This proves that
\mathsf{H}_{G}^{2}=\mathds{1}
, implying that
\mathsf{H}_{G}
is also a unitary transform. Another variant of the Hartley transform used in the literature is a multiplicative variant defined as

\mathsf{H}_{G}(f)(a_{1},\dots,a_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G\rvert}}\sum_{y_{1}=0}^{N_{1}-1}\cdots\sum_{y_{k}=0}^{N_{k}-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi a_{1}y_{1}}{N_{1}}\Big)\cdots\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi a_{k}y_{k}}{N_{k}}\Big)f(y_{1},\dots,y_{k}).

(4)

This is also a unitary transform as shown by the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

The Hartley transform (
4
) is unitary.

Proof.

If we represent the function
f
as a vector indexed by the elements of
G
, the matrix representing
\mathsf{H}_{G}
is the tensor product of the matrices representing the Hartley transform over each component
\mathbb{Z}_{N_{i}}
of
G
. More precisely,
\mathsf{H}_{G}=\mathsf{H}_{N_{1}}\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N_{k}}
. Since each
\mathsf{H}_{N_{i}}
is unitary, it follows that
\mathsf{H}_{G}
is also unitary.
∎

The quantum Hartley transform over an abelian group
G
is defined as

\mathsf{QHT}_{G}:\sum_{\bm{x}\in G}f(\bm{x})\lvert\bm{x}\rangle\mapsto\sum_{\bm{a}\in G}\mathsf{H}_{G}(f)(\bm{a})\lvert\bm{a}\rangle,

(5)

where we have skipped the normalization for the left hand side state. Here,
\mathsf{H}_{G}(f)
can be either of the transforms (
2
) or (
4
). For a single basis element of the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, the quantum Hartley transform simplifies to

\mathsf{QHT}_{N}:\lvert a\rangle\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y\rangle.

(6)

The sine and cosine transforms.

Two other widely used real transforms are the discrete sine and cosine transforms. There are different versions of these transforms, but here we briefly introduce the first sine transform,

The quantum version of this transform is defined by

\mathsf{QS}_{N-1}^{\text{I}}\lvert a\rangle=\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{y=1}^{N-1}\sin\left(\frac{\pi ay}{N}\right)\lvert y\rangle.

The cosine transform of a function

C_{N+1}^{\text{I}}(f)(a)=\left(\frac{2}{N}\right)^{1/2}\sum_{y=0}^{N}k_{a}k_{y}\cos\left(\frac{\pi ay}{N}\right)f(y),

where

and
k_{j}=1
for

Quantum sine and cosine.

To compute
\mathsf{QS}_{N-1}^{\text{I}}
and
\mathsf{QC}_{N+1}^{\text{I}}

is used, which satisfies the identity

T_{N}^{*}\cdot\mathsf{QFT}_{2N}\cdot T_{N}=\mathsf{QC}_{N+1}^{\text{I}}\oplus i\mathsf{QS}_{N-1}^{\text{I}}.

The action of

where

4
A New Algorithm for
\mathsf{QHT}

Our algorithm for the quantum Hartley transform,
\mathsf{QHT}_{N}
, is inspired by the recursive algorithm for computing the quantum Fourier transform (
\mathsf{QFT}_{N}
). Let us briefly explain how the algorithm for
\mathsf{QFT}_{N}
works. For simplicity, we assume
N=2^{n}
, so that elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
are represented using exactly
n
qubits. The generalization to arbitrary
N
can be achieved by following the same recursive approach. Given
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, the expression for
\mathsf{QFT}_{N}
is written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{QFT}_{N}\lvert a\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\omega_{N}^{ay}\lvert y\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\omega_{N}^{ay}\lvert y\rangle+(-1)^{a}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\omega_{N}^{ay}\lvert y+N/2\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N/2}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\omega_{N}^{ay}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert 0\rangle+(-1)^{a}\lvert 1\rangle)\lvert y\rangle,

(10)

where, in the last equation, we have separated the first qubit for clarity. Let
\lvert a\rangle=\lvert t\rangle\lvert b\rangle
, where
b
is the least significant bit of
a
, so that
a=2t+b
for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{N/2}
. Applying
\mathsf{QFT}_{N/2}
to the first register, we obtain the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N/2}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\omega_{N}^{2ty}\lvert y\rangle\lvert b\rangle.

Next, we apply the phase unitary
P(y,b):\lvert y\rangle\lvert b\rangle\mapsto\omega_{N}^{by}\lvert y\rangle\lvert b\rangle
, and finally, we apply a Hadamard transform to the last qubit. The result is the state in (
10
).

We now present our algorithm for the efficient computation of the quantum Hartley transform
\mathsf{QHT}_{N}
. The idea is to exploit the recursive structure of
\mathsf{QHT}_{N}
, similar to the approach we used for
\mathsf{QFT}_{N}
. To this end, we first rewrite the sum in (
6
) to reveal its recursive nature. We proceed as follows:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=N/2}^{N-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y\rangle.

(11)

The second sum in the right-hand side can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{y=N/2}^{N-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}+\pi a\Big)\lvert y+N/2\rangle

\displaystyle=(-1)^{a}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y+N/2\rangle,

where the second equality follows from the
\mathrm{cas}
angle-sum identity
\mathrm{cas}(\alpha+\beta)=\cos(\alpha)\mathrm{cas}(\beta)+\sin(\alpha)\mathrm{cas}(-\beta)
, and the fact that
\cos(\pi a)=(-1)^{a}
for all
a
. Substituting this into (
11
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\lvert y\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)(\lvert y\rangle+(-1)^{a}\lvert y+N/2\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N/2}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ay}{N}\Big)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert 0\rangle+(-1)^{a}\lvert 1\rangle)\lvert y\rangle,

(12)

where, in the last equality, the most significant qubit has been separated to highlight its role.

We now show how to compute
\mathsf{QHT}_{N}
recursively. Given
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, we once again express
\lvert a\rangle=\lvert t\rangle\lvert b\rangle
, where
b
is the least significant bit, so that
a=2t+b
for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{N/2}
. Suppose we already have an efficient circuit for computing
\mathsf{QHT}_{N/2}
. To compute the Hartley transform of
\lvert a\rangle
, we introduce an ancilla qubit, resulting in the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert t\rangle\lvert b\rangle
. Then

\displaystyle\lvert 0\rangle\lvert t\rangle\lvert b\rangle

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{N/2}}\sum_{y=0}^{N/2-1}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi ty}{N/2}\Big)\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y\rangle\lvert b\rangle

We devote the rest of the paper to constructing a public-key quantum money scheme based on the Hartley transform. In this section, we review the quantum money scheme proposed by Zhandry
[

, which is based on abelian group actions and the quantum Fourier transform. To construct a quantum money scheme based on

6.1
Quantum Money From Group Actions

A public-key quantum money scheme consists of two QPT algorithms:

•

\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})
: This algorithm takes a security parameter
\lambda
as input and outputs a pair
(s,\rho_{s})
, where
s
is a binary string called the serial number, and
\rho_{s}
is a quantum state called the banknote. The pair
(s,\rho_{s})
, or simply
\rho_{s}
, is sometimes denoted by
\$
.

•

\mathsf{Ver}(s,\rho_{s})
: This algorithm takes a serial number and an alleged banknote as input and outputs either
1
(accept) or
0
(reject).

The quantum money scheme is said to be
correct
if genuine banknotes generated by
\mathsf{Gen}
are accepted by
\mathsf{Ver}
with high probability. More formally:

\Pr[\mathsf{Ver}(s,\rho_{s})=1:(s,\rho_{s})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})]\geq 1-\negl(\lambda).

where the probability is taken over the randomness of
\mathsf{Gen}
and
\mathsf{Ver}
. The scheme
(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Ver})
is said to be secure if, given a genuine bill
(s,\rho_{s})
, no QPT algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can produce two (possibly entangled) bills
(s,\rho_{1})
and
(s,\rho_{2})
that are both accepted by
\mathsf{Ver}
with non-negligible probability. More formally:

\Pr\left[\mathsf{Ver}(s,\rho_{1})=\mathsf{Ver}(s,\rho_{2})=1:\begin{subarray}{c}(s,\rho_{s})\leftarrow\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})\\
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})\leftarrow\mathcal{A}(s,\rho_{s})\end{subarray}\right]\leq\negl(\lambda).

We now briefly outline the quantum money construction from
[
31
]
, which is based on abelian group actions. Let
\{(G_{\lambda},X_{\lambda},*)\}_{\lambda\in J}
, where
J\subset\mathbb{N}
, be a collection of cryptographic group actions for abelian groups
G_{\lambda}
, and let
x_{\lambda}\in X_{\lambda}
be a fixed element. The
\mathsf{Gen}
and
\mathsf{Ver}
algorithms are as follows:

•

\mathsf{Gen}(1^{\lambda})
. Begin with the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert x_{\lambda}\rangle
, and apply the quantum Fourier transform over
G_{\lambda}
to the first register producing the superposition

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G_{\lambda}\rvert}}\sum_{g\in G_{\lambda}}\lvert g\rangle\lvert x_{\lambda}\rangle.

Next, apply the unitary transformation
\lvert h\rangle\lvert y\rangle\mapsto\lvert h\rangle\lvert h*y\rangle
to this state, followed by the quantum Fourier transform on the first register. This results in

\frac{1}{\lvert G_{\lambda}\rvert}\sum_{h\in G_{\lambda}}\sum_{g\in G_{\lambda}}\chi(g,h)\lvert h\rangle\lvert g*x_{\lambda}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert G_{\lambda}\rvert}}\sum_{h\in G_{\lambda}}\lvert h\rangle\lvert G^{(h)}*x_{\lambda}\rangle

where
\lvert G^{(h)}*x_{\lambda}\rangle
is defined as in (
1
). Measure the first register to obtain a random
h\in G_{\lambda}
, collapsing the state to
\lvert G^{(h)}*x_{\lambda}\rangle
. Return the pair
(h,\lvert G^{(h)}*x_{\lambda}\rangle)
.

•

\mathsf{Ver}(h,\lvert\psi\rangle)
. First, check whether
\lvert\psi\rangle
has support in
X_{\lambda}
. If not, return
0
. Then, apply
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
to the state
\lvert\psi\rangle\lvert 0\rangle
, and measure the second register to obtain some
h^{\prime}\in G_{\lambda}
. If
h^{\prime}=h
, return

; otherwise return
0
.

From this point forward, to simplify the notation, we make the security parameter
\lambda
implicit, and use
G

G_{\lambda}
,
X
for
X_{\lambda}
, and so on.

The quantum money scheme above can be instantiated using the quantum Hartley transform instead of the quantum Fourier transform. However, this substitution breaks the verification algorithm. has support in
X
. If not, return
0
. Then, apply
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
to the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
, and measure the first register to obtain some
h^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. If
h^{\prime}=h
, return
1
; otherwise return
0
.

Let us take a closer look at the verification algorithm. Suppose
\lvert\psi\rangle
is a genuine money state, say
\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
. The
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
algorithm begins by preparing the state

\lvert\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\lvert u\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}.

It then applies the unitary
\lvert y\rangle\lvert u\rangle\mapsto\lvert(-u)*y\rangle\lvert u\rangle
, yielding:

\displaystyle\lvert\phi\rangle

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert u\rangle\lvert(g-u)*x\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi(g+u)h}{N}\Big)\lvert u\rangle\lvert g*x\rangle.

Finally, the algorithm applies
\mathsf{QHT}_{N}
to the second register. A straightforward calculation shows that the resulting state is

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\Big(\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert h\rangle+\sin\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert-h\rangle\Big)\lvert g*x\rangle.

Rewriting this state using the
\mathrm{cas}
function, we get the state

\displaystyle\lvert\phi_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert h_{+}\rangle+\mathrm{cas}\Big(-\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert h_{-}\rangle\Big)\lvert g*x\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lvert h_{+}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lvert h_{-}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H},

(14)

where
\lvert h_{\pm}\rangle=(\lvert h\rangle\pm\lvert-h\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. This stands in clear contrast to the case when the scheme is instantiated using the quantum Fourier transform. In that case, we obtain the state
\lvert h\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle
, from which
h
can be read off.

Unfortunately, we do not know how to use the resulting state in (
14
) to verify the banknote
(h,\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H})
. In particular, when given the banknote
(h,\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H})
, the above verification algorithm produces the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lvert h_{+}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\lvert h_{-}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H},

(15)

and with the current assumptions on the group action, we lack a method to distinguish between this state and the one in (
14
). This limitation motivates us to consider an additional, commonly used property of the group action, known as
twists
.

7.1
Group Actions with Twists

We show how twists can be used to successfully verify banknotes in the above money scheme. A group action
(G,X,*)
, with a fixed element
x\in X
, is said to support twists if there exists an efficient algorithm that computes the mapping
g*x\mapsto(-g)*x
for any
g\in G
. The twisting map can be efficiently performed in isogeny-based group actions, which are the most widely used cryptographic group actions. We assume that the group actions used to construct the above quantum money scheme support twists.

In the quantum setting, the twist operation is the unitary
\textsc{twist}:\lvert g*x\rangle\mapsto\lvert(-g)*x\rangle
. The following lemma states the result of applying the twist operation to a genuine money state.

Lemma 7.1
.

For any
h\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, the twist unitary maps
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
to
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\textsc{twist}\,\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert(-g)*x\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{-2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert g*x\rangle

\displaystyle=\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}.\qed

As explained at the end of Section
7
, the verification algorithm does not distinguish between the states (
14
) and (
15
). Distinguishing these two states reduces to distinguishing between the money states
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
and
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}
. The following lemma shows that this can be efficiently done using the twist unitary.

Lemma 7.2
.

Given
h\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}\setminus\{0,N/4,N/2,3N/4\}
, there exists a polynomial-time quantum algorithm that distinguishes between the states
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
and
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}
. Specifically, the algorithm is a unitary that acts as the identity on
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
but maps
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}
to
\lvert 1\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}
.

Proof.

The algorithm proceeds as follows. First, we find
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
such that
uh=N/8\bmod N
; based on the condition on
h
, such a
u
always exists. For such a
u
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi(g+u)h}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{2\pi uh}{N}\Big)+\sin\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\mathrm{cas}\Big(-\frac{2\pi uh}{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{\pi}{4}\Big)+\sin\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\mathrm{cas}\Big(-\frac{\pi}{4}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big).

(16)

Similarly,

\mathrm{cas}\Big(\frac{-2\pi(g+u)h}{N}\Big)=-\sqrt{2}\sin\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big).

(17)

Now, consider the unitary
T_{u}:\lvert y\rangle\mapsto\lvert(-u)*y\rangle
. It follows from the above identities that

T_{u}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}\propto\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\cos\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert g*x\rangle,

(18)

whereas

T_{u}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}\propto\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\sin\Big(\frac{2\pi gh}{N}\Big)\lvert g*x\rangle.

(19)

Therefore, applying
T_{u}
results in one of these states, depending on the input state. Both of these states are eigenstates of the twist unitary. The former corresponds to eigenvalue
1
and the latter corresponds to eigenvalue
-1
.

Now, prepare
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
, where
\lvert\psi\rangle
is the input state. Applying the sequence of unitaries
\mathds{1}\otimes T_{u}
,
H\otimes\mathds{1}
,
\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes\mathds{1}+\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert\otimes\textsc{twist}
,
H\otimes\mathds{1}
, and
\mathds{1}\otimes T_{u}
to the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
results in the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
(resp.
\lvert 1\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
) if
\lvert\psi\rangle
is
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
(resp.
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}
).
∎

Remark 2
.

For a general
N
, Lemma
7.2
holds with a slight modification. Specifically, we need to find
u
such that
uh=\lfloor N/8\rfloor
. In this case,
\mathrm{cas}(\pi/4)\approx 1
and
\mathrm{cas}(-\pi/4)\approx 0
, with exponentially small error, so the identities (
16
) and (
17
) also hold up to exponentially small error as well. To ensure the existence of such
u
, we could, for example, assume that
h
is coprime with
N
. Since a random

is coprime with
N
with overwhelming probability, this condition can be efficiently enforced in the
\mathsf{Gen}
algorithm by repeating the procedure until we find an appropriate
h
.

We now give the new verification algorithm that utilizes the twist operation.

Algorithm 2

(
\mathsf{Ver}_{new}
)
.

Input:

Alleged banknote
(h,\lvert\psi\rangle)
where
\lvert\psi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.

Output:

0
or
1

1.

If the support of
\lvert\psi\rangle
is not in

, return
0
.

2.

Apply the
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
algorithm to the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
.

3.

Measure the first register using the observable

\{M_{0}=\lvert h\rangle\langle h\rvert+\lvert-h\rangle\langle-h\rvert,M_{1}=\mathds{1}-M_{0}\},

4.

If the measurement outcome is

, return

.

5.

Apply the inverse of
\mathsf{cmpIndex}

Use Lemma

to check whether the resulting state is

. If yes, return

.

Some explanations about the algorithm are in order. Step

can be implemented using an extra qubit initialized to
\lvert 0\rangle
, which is set to
1
if the values of the first register is not in
X
. This qubit is then measured, and the state is rejected if the measurement outcome is not

. The post-measurement state, which we denote again by

, has support in
X
. Similarly, implementing the measurement in Step

can be done efficiently, as

Theorem 7.3
.

Given a banknote
(h,\lvert\psi\rangle)
, we have

\Pr[\mathsf{Ver}_{new}(h,\lvert\psi\rangle)=1]=\lvert\langle\psi|\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}\rvert^{2}.

Algorithm
2
runs in
\poly(\log N)
operations. Moreover, if
\mathsf{Ver}_{new}
accepts, then the post-verification state is exactly
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
.

Proof.

It follows from the prior discussion that the running-time of the algorithm is
\poly(\log N)
. We now prove correctness. Suppose the state
\lvert\psi\rangle
passes the check in Step
1
, so that it has support in
X
. Since the states
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(u)}*x\rangle,u=0,\dots,N-1
, form an orthonormal basis of the space
\mathbb{C}^{X}
, we can write

\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\alpha_{u}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(u)}*x\rangle_{H},

where
\sum_{u}\lvert\alpha_{u}\rvert^{2}=1
. By the same calculations leading up to (
14
), applying
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
to the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle
results in the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}\alpha_{u}(\lvert u_{+}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(u)}*x\rangle_{H}+\lvert u_{-}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-u)}*x\rangle_{H}).

Steps
3
and
4
projects this state onto

\alpha_{h}(\lvert h_{+}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}+\lvert h_{-}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H})+\alpha_{-h}(\lvert h_{+}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}-\lvert h_{-}\rangle\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}),

where we have ignored the normalization constant for clarity. This projection occurs with probability
\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\alpha_{-h}\rvert^{2}
. Applying the inverse of
\mathsf{cmpIndex}
, we obtain the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\alpha_{-h}^{2}\rvert}}(\alpha_{h}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}+\alpha_{-h}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle_{H}).

The final step of the algorithm projects this state onto
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}

\lvert\alpha_{h}^{2}\rvert/(\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\alpha_{-h}^{2}\rvert)
. Therefore, the probability of the state
\lvert\psi\rangle

(\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\alpha_{-h}^{2}\rvert)\frac{\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\alpha_{-h}^{2}\rvert}=\lvert\alpha_{h}\rvert^{2}=\lvert\langle\psi|\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}\rvert^{2}.\qed

One intriguing question regarding the quantum money scheme above is whether a more “direct” verification algorithm can be designed. In the original scheme, using the quantum Fourier transform, we could directly obtain
h
from the money state
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle
and compare it to the given
h
. However, this approach did not work when we used the Hartley transform: we ended up with the state in (

h
without collapsing the state. To address this, we design an algorithm for computing

that utilizes quantum walks.

8
Computing The Serial Number Using Quantum Walks

In this section, we show how to use continuous-time quantum walks to compute the serial number of a given money state. Quantum walks are quantum analogs of classical random walks and play a fundamental role in quantum algorithms. Similar to the classical case, there are two types of quantum walks: continuous-time and discrete-time, both of which exhibit significantly different behaviors compared to classical random walks. In particular, a key distinction between quantum and classical random walks lies in the wave nature of quantum mechanics. In a quantum walk, interference effects can occur, allowing computational advantage for exploration of the graph compared to classical walks, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
10
,
11
]
.

For a graph
\Gamma
, the dynamics of a continuous-time classical walk on
\Gamma
is described by the differential equation
\frac{d}{dt}q(t)=Lq(t)
, where
L
is the Laplacian of
\Gamma
and
q(t)
describes the state of the walk at time
t
. In the quantum setting, the vector
q(t)
is replaced by a quantum state
\lvert\psi(t)\rangle
and the dynamics of the walk is given by the Schrödinger equation

i\frac{d}{dt}\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=L\lvert\psi(t)\rangle,

(20)

where
L
plays the role of the Hamiltonian of the quantum system. The solution to this differential equation can be written in closed form as:

\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iLt}\lvert\psi(0)\rangle.

In practice, we often (including this work) use the adjacency matrix
A
of
\Gamma
as the Hamiltonian of the walk, so the unitary for the state transition becomes
\exp(-iAt)
.

A discrete-time quantum walk on
\Gamma
can be described using the adjacency matrix
A
by making
A
into a stochastic matrix
[
27
]
. More precisely, we define a matrix
P
as
P_{jk}=A_{jk}/\deg(j)
, where
\deg(j)
is the degree of the vertex
v_{j}
in
\Gamma
. If the
\Gamma
has
N
vertices, the discrete-time quantum walk on
\Gamma
is defined by a unitary operator on the finite Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}
as follows. Define the states

\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\deg(j)}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sqrt{P_{jk}}\lvert j,k\rangle,

and the project and swap operators

\Pi=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lvert\phi_{j}\rangle\langle\phi_{j}\rvert,\quad S=\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}\lvert j,k\rangle\langle k,j\rvert.

Then, a step of the quantum walk is defined by the unitary
W=S(2\Pi-\mathds{1})
.

Simulating continuous-time walks.

It was shown by Childs
[
9
]
and Berry, Childs, and Kothari
[
6
]
that continuous-time quantum walks can be simulated using discrete-time quantum walks. Since we will use their methods, we briefly outline the necessary notations. Given a Hamiltonian
H
of dimension
N=2^{n}
, let
\lVert H\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{i,j}\lvert H_{ij}\rvert
. The Hilbert space on which
H
acts is expanded from
\mathbb{C}^{N}
to
\mathbb{C}^{2N}\times\mathbb{C}^{2N}
by adding an ancilla qubit in the state
\lvert 0\rangle
and duplicating the entire new space. To define the discrete-time walk operator, we begin by defining the orthonormal set of states

\displaystyle\lvert\phi_{j0}\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{\ell\in F_{j}}\lvert\ell\rangle\left(\sqrt{\frac{H_{j\ell}^{*}}{K}}\lvert 0\rangle+\sqrt{1-\frac{\lvert H_{j\ell}^{*}\rvert}{K}}\lvert 1\rangle\right),

(21)

\displaystyle\lvert\phi_{j1}\rangle

\displaystyle:=\lvert 0\rangle\lvert 1\rangle,

where
F_{j}
is the set of indices corresponding to the nonzero elements in column
j
of
H
, and
K\geq\lVert H\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}
is a constant.

Based on the states
\lvert\phi_{jb}\rangle
, we define an isometry
T:\mathbb{C}^{2N}\to\mathbb{C}^{2N}\times\mathbb{C}^{2N}
by

T:=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}(\lvert j\rangle\langle j\rvert\otimes\lvert b\rangle\langle b\rvert)\otimes\lvert\phi_{jb}\rangle.

(22)

The discrete-time quantum walk is then defined by
W=iS(2TT^{*}-\mathds{1})
, where
S
is the swap operator acting as
S\lvert j_{1}\rangle\lvert b_{1}\rangle\lvert j_{2}\rangle\lvert b_{2}\rangle=\lvert j_{2}\rangle\lvert b_{2}\rangle\lvert j_{1}\rangle\lvert b_{1}\rangle
for all
0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq N-1
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in\{0,1\}
. To efficiently simulate the continuous-time quantum walk defined by the Hamiltonian
H
, we need to be able to efficiently apply the isometry
T
and its inverse
T^{*}
, as well as the walk operator
W
. Assuming black-box query access to
H
, we have the following:

Theorem 8.1

(
[
6
, Theorem 1]
)
.

Let
H
be a
d
-sparse Hamiltonian
H
acting on
n
qubits. Then, the unitary
e^{-iHt}
can be approximated to within error
\epsilon
using

O\left(\tau\frac{\log(\tau/\epsilon)}{\log\log(\tau/\epsilon)}\right)

queries to
H
and

O\left(\tau[n+\log^{5/2}(\tau/\epsilon)]\frac{\log(\tau/\epsilon)}{\log\log(\tau/\epsilon)}\right)

additional elementary gates, where
\tau=d\lVert H\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}t
.

8.1
Group Action Quantum Walks

Let
G
be an abelian group and let
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{k}\}\subset G
be a symmetric set, i.e.,
q\in Q
if and only if
-q\in Q
. The Cayley graph associated to
G
and
Q
is a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
, where the vertex set is
V=G
, and the edge set
E
consists of pairs
(a,b)\in G\times G
such that there exists
q\in Q
with
b=q+a
. The adjacency matrix of
\Gamma
can be expressed as

A=\sum_{a\in G}\lambda_{a}\lvert\hat{a}\rangle\langle\hat{a}\rvert,

where
\lvert\hat{a}\rangle
is the quantum Fourier transform of
\lvert a\rangle
. The eigenvalues
\lambda
are given by

\lambda_{a}=\sum_{q\in Q}\chi(a,q).

Note that the eigenvectors
\lvert\hat{a}\rangle
of
A
depend only on
G
and not on the set
Q
.

Cayley graphs can also be constructed using group actions. Given a regular group action
(G,X,*)
with a fixed element
x\in X
and a set
Q=\{q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{k}\}\subset G
, let
\Gamma=(X,E)
be a graph with vertex set
X
and edge set consisting of pairs
(x,y)\in X\times X
such that
y=q*x
for some
q\in Q
. The adjacency matrix of
\Gamma
is

A=\sum_{h\in G}\lambda_{h}\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\langle G^{(h)}*x\rvert,

where
\lambda_{h}=\sum_{q\in Q}\chi(h,q)
. Again, the eigenvectors
\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle
depend only on
G
. This construction of Cayley graphs from group actions generalizes the previous construction. Specifically, if we set
X=G
and the action
*
as group operation, we recover the original construction.

Since the action
(G,X,*)
is regular, the two constructions yield the same graph up to isomorphism. In the first graph, the vertex set is
G
, and the rows and columns of the adjacency matrix are indexed by the elements of
G
, whereas in the second graph, the vertex set is
X
, and the rows and columns of the adjacency matrix are indexed by the elements of
X
. The isomorphism between the two graphs is induced by the bijection

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\phi:&G&\longrightarrow&X\\
&g&\longmapsto&g*x.\end{array}

Assuming the group action is a cryptographic group action, the isomorphism
\phi
is a one-way function: given
g\in G
, it is easy to compute
g*x
, whereas given
(x,g*x)
, it is hard to compute
g
. Therefore, although these graphs are mathematically the same, computational problems based on them require fundamentally different techniques. A notable example is the implementation of quantum walks on these graphs.
1
1

1

Implementations of continuous-time quantum walks in the context of cryptographic group actions have previously appeared in
[
8
,
16
]
, in the setting of supersingular isogeny graphs. These walks can be interpreted as group-action walks.
From this point onward, assume
Q
is a small set, i.e.,
\lvert Q\rvert=\poly(\log\lvert G\rvert)
. In the first graph, we can efficiently implement the walks
e^{-iAt}
even for values of

that are exponentially large in
\log\lvert G\rvert
. This follows from the fact that

e^{-iAt}=\mathsf{QFT}_{G}\sum_{a\in G}e^{-i\lambda_{a}t}\lvert a\rangle\langle a\rvert\mathsf{QFT}_{G}^{*}.

(23)

The quantum Fourier transform
\mathsf{QFT}_{G}
and its inverse can be applied in
\poly(\log\lvert G\rvert)
operations. The diagonal unitary in the middle, which is a phase computation
\lvert a\rangle\mapsto e^{-i\lambda_{a}t}\lvert a\rangle
, is also efficient because
\lambda_{a}
can be computed classically to arbitrary precision in
\poly(\log\lvert G\rvert)
time.

The situation for group actions is less straightforward. While the states
\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle
are analogous to the states
\lvert\hat{h}\rangle
, the involvement of the action
*
in the former introduces computational challenges. Specifically, it becomes hard to apply transformations beyond the action
\lvert y\rangle\mapsto\lvert a*y\rangle
for
a\in G
. Consequently, no decomposition analogous to (
23
) exists in the context of group actions. Instead, we are left with the spectral expression

e^{-iAt}=\sum_{h\in G}e^{-i\lambda_{h}t}\lvert G^{(h)}*x\rangle\langle G^{(h)}*x\rvert.

Despite this limitation, the sparsity and structure of the matrix
A
allow us to demonstrate in the next section that for
t=\poly(\log\lvert G\rvert)
, the walk

can still be efficiently approximated to polynomial accuracy.

Simulating group action quantum walks.

Assume

\Gamma=(X,E)
of the group action

\displaystyle:=\sum_{y\in X}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}(\lvert y\rangle\langle y\rvert\otimes\lvert b\rangle\langle b\rvert)\otimes\lvert\phi_{yb}\rangle

,
b=0,1
, where the first register is an ancilla. Since
\lvert\phi_{y1}\rangle=\lvert 0\rangle\lvert 1\rangle
, the unitary
U_{1}
can be implemented efficiently. The unitary
U_{0}
can be implemented as follows. Since
Q
is a small set, we can construct an efficient unitary
V_{Q}:\mathbb{C}^{Q}\to\mathbb{C}^{Q}
such that
V_{Q}\lvert 0\rangle=\lvert Q\rvert^{-1/2}\sum_{q\in Q}\lvert q\rangle
. Applying the unitary
V_{Q}\otimes\mathds{1}
to the state
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert 0,0\rangle
results in

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lvert Q\rvert}}\sum_{q\in Q}\lvert q\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert 0,0\rangle.

Next, we apply the unitary
V_{1}:\lvert q\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert 0,0\rangle\mapsto\lvert q\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert q*y,0\rangle
. Following this, we uncompute the first register to obtain
\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert\phi_{y0}\rangle
. Uncomputing the first register involves applying the unitary operation
V_{2}:\lvert q\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert q*y,0\rangle\mapsto\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert q*y,0\rangle
, which can be implemented efficiently since
Q
is small and we can recover
q
from the pair
(y,q*y)
by checking against all the elements in
Q
.

To implement
U_{0}^{*}:\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert\phi_{y0}\rangle\mapsto\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,0\rangle\lvert 0,0\rangle
, we first apply
V_{2}^{*}
, followed by
V_{1}^{*}
, and finally
V_{Q}^{*}\otimes\mathds{1}
. Since each of these unitaries can be applied efficiently,
U_{0}^{*}
can also be implemented efficiently. Now, the isometry
T
can be constructed using a conditional unitary
U_{T}
, which applies
U_{0}
or
U_{1}
based on the qubit containing
b
.

Finally, we show how the walk unitary
W=iS(2TT^{*}-\mathds{1})
can be applied efficiently. To do this, we must show that the reflection
2TT^{*}-\mathds{1}
can be implemented efficiently. Observe that

\displaystyle 2\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes TT^{*}-\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=2\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes\sum_{y\in X}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\lvert y,b\rangle\lvert\phi_{yb}\rangle\langle y,b\rvert\langle\phi_{yb}\rvert-\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{y\in X}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,b\rangle\lvert\phi_{yb}\rangle\langle 0\rvert\langle y,b\rvert\langle\phi_{yb}\rvert-\mathds{1}

\displaystyle=U_{T}\Big(2\sum_{y\in X}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}}\lvert 0\rangle\lvert y,b\rangle\lvert 0,0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\langle y,b\rvert\langle 0,0\rvert-\mathds{1}\Big)U_{T}^{*}

\displaystyle=U_{T}(2\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes\mathds{1}_{X,b}\otimes\lvert 0,0\rangle\langle 0,0\rvert-\mathds{1})U_{T}^{*}.

Since
U_{T},U_{T}^{*}
, and
2\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes\mathds{1}_{X,b}\otimes\lvert 0,0\rangle\langle 0,0\rvert-\mathds{1}
can all be applied efficiently, it follows that
2\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes TT^{*}-\mathds{1}
can also be applied efficiently. Now, for any state
\lvert\psi\rangle
,

(2\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert\otimes TT^{*}-\mathds{1})\lvert 0\rangle\lvert\psi\rangle=\lvert 0\rangle(2TT^{*}-\mathds{1})\lvert\psi\rangle.

8.2
Computing The Serial Number

Given a state
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
, we show how to compute
h
using continuous-time quantum walks. For any
u\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
, define a Cayley graph
\Gamma=(\mathbb{Z}_{N},E)
with the generating set
Q=\{-u,u\}
, as described in Section
8.1
. Let
A
denote the adjacency matrix of
\Gamma
. The eigenvectors and corresponding eigenvalues of
A
are
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle
and
\lambda_{h}=2\cos(2\pi uh/N)
, respectively, for
h\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. According to Theorem
8.1
and the subsequent discussion, the unitary
W=e^{iAt}
can be efficiently simulated to exponential accuracy. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

The money state
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
is an eigenstate of
W
with eigenvalue
e^{i\lambda_{h}t}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle e^{iAt}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}e^{i\lambda_{g}t}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(g)}*x\rangle\langle\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(g)}*x|\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in\mathbb{Z}_{N}}e^{i\lambda_{g}t}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(g)}*x\rangle\langle\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(g)}*x\rvert\Big(\frac{1-i}{2}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle+\frac{1+i}{2}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{i\lambda_{h}t}\frac{1-i}{2}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle+\frac{1+i}{2}e^{i\lambda_{-h}t}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(-h)}*x\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{i\lambda_{h}t}\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H},

where the second equality follows from the identity in (
3
), and the last equality follows from the fact that
\lambda_{h}=\lambda_{-h}
.
∎

If we choose
t=\poly(\log N)
, it follows from Lemma
8.2
that we can run the phase estimation algorithm with the unitary
W
and the eigenstate
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{N}^{(h)}*x\rangle_{H}
to compute an estimate
\tilde{\lambda}_{h}
of
\lambda_{h}
such that
\lvert\tilde{\lambda}_{h}-\lambda_{h}\rvert\leq 1/\poly(\log N)
. From the estimate
\tilde{\lambda}_{h}
we can obtain a value
0\leq\theta\leq 1
such that

\Big\lvert\theta-\frac{uh}{N}\Big\rvert\leq\frac{1}{\poly(\log N)}.

Since the phase estimation algorithm can be executed for different values of
u
, we can obtain different estimates of
uh/N
. It was shown in
[
31
]
that for carefully chosen values of
u
, these estimates provide sufficient information to fully recover
h
.
