Efficiency and accuracy of augmented reality guided endodontic surgery: a systematic review
Authors: Boya Tejaswi Naidu, Aditya Shetty, Teena Sheethal Dsouza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndodontic microsurgery (EMS) is a precision-dependent procedure in which accurate localization of the root apex and controlled osteotomy are critical for successful outcomes. Despite advances in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), magnification, and microsurgical techniques, discrepancies between preoperative planning and intraoperative execution remain a persistent challenge, largely due to limitations in depth perception and spatial interpretation. Augmented reality (AR) technology has been introduced as an intraoperative guidance tool that enable real-time superimposition of three-dimensional anatomical data onto the surgical field, with the potential to enhance visual spatial integration and procedural accuracy. However, the effectiveness of AR-assisted EMS remains unclear due to limited and methodologically heterogeneous evidence.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Electronic searches were performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, supplemented by citation searching. Studies evaluating AR EMS were included. Data extraction was performed using a standardized framework, and outcomes were grouped into accuracy, efficiency, safety, operator-related parameters, and treatment completeness. Risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane RoB 2 for randomized studies and ROBINS-I (version 2) for non-randomized studies.ResultsThree studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising one randomized simulation study, one in vitro comparative study, and one ex vivo cadaveric study. AR-assisted workflows consistently demonstrated improved spatial accuracy, reflected by reduced linear and angular deviations during osteotomy and root-end resection. Operator-related outcomes, including confidence and usability, were positively influenced. However, efficiency outcomes were inconsistent across studies, and one study reported increased residual lesion volume, indicating a potential trade-off between precision and treatment completeness. Risk of bias ranged from moderate to high, primarily due to confounding and measurement-related limitations.ConclusionsAR guided EMS demonstrates promising improvements in surgical accuracy and operator interaction however; the current evidence is limited by methodological heterogeneity and lack of clinical validation. AR should be considered a precision-enhancing adjunct rather than a replacement for conventional techniques. Further standardized and clinically relevant studies are required to establish its translational applicability in endodontic practice.

Introduction

Precise execution at a submillimetric level remains a fundamental requirement in endodontic microsurgery, where even minimal deviation in osteotomy or root-end resection can adversely affect surgical outcomes and increase the risk of iatrogenic injury to adjacent anatomical structures. Despite substantial advances in imaging and microsurgical techniques, the translation of virtual treatment planning into accurate intraoperative execution continues to represent a fundamental challenge in endodontic practice. EMS is indicated in cases of persistent apical periodontitis following failed root canal treatment, procedural complications such as ledges or separated instruments that preclude orthograde retreatment, complex root canal anatomies, or the presence of extra-radicular infection and apical pathology requiring direct surgical management [1, 2]. The procedure involves a sequence of highly technique-sensitive steps including flap reflection, osteotomy, root-end resection, retrograde preparation, and sealing, where treatment success is highly dependent on precise localization of the root apex, minimal bone removal, and preservation of surrounding structures such as the maxillary sinus, mental foramen, and neurovascular bundles [1, 2]. However, despite improvements in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), magnification systems, and microsurgical instruments, a discrepancy persists between preoperative planning and intraoperative execution due to limited depth perception, restricted visualization, and the need to mentally reconstruct three-dimensional anatomy from two-dimensional imaging, often resulting in positional inaccuracies and unnecessary bone removal [3–6].

Computer-assisted navigation systems have been introduced to improve surgical accuracy by providing real-time positional feedback based on CBCT-derived planning. Nevertheless, conventional dynamic navigation requires operators to divide attention between the surgical field and an external display, increasing cognitive load and potentially compromising hand eye coordination and procedural efficiency [7, 8]. Augmented reality (AR) technology aims to overcome these limitations by integrating virtual planning data directly into the surgeon’s field of view. This system utilizes preoperative CBCT datasets to generate three-dimensional reconstructions of dental and surrounding anatomical structures, along with planned osteotomy trajectories and depth cues. Through intraoperative registration techniques such as marker-based tracking, surface matching, or sensor-based alignment, these virtual models are spatially synchronized with the patient and displayed using optical see-through head-mounted devices, projection systems, or screen-based interfaces [7, 8].

By integrating virtual anatomical models with the operative field in real time, AR systems provide a “heads-up” visualization that allows simultaneous perception of both real and virtual environments. This enhances spatial orientation, depth perception, and intuitive instrument guidance without requiring gaze diversion from the surgical field [9, 10]. Additionally, integration with dynamic navigation systems enables a hybrid workflow combining quantitative tracking with visual overlays, which may further improve surgical precision and intraoperative control [11]. Existing experimental evidence indicates that AR-guided systems can reduce linear and angular deviations, limit excessive bone removal, and improve operator confidence, particularly in anatomically complex cases and among less experienced clinicians [12, 13].

However, the current evidence base remains limited and heterogeneous, with variations in AR hardware, registration workflows, calibration protocols, and outcome assessment methods that hinder reproducibility and inter-study comparability [7, 8]. Most studies are confined to in vitro or ex vivo models that do not adequately simulate intraoperative conditions such as soft tissue interference, bleeding, restricted access, and patient movement. Furthermore, inconsistencies in accuracy metrics, operator standardization, and reporting of methodological parameters introduce potential bias and limit generalizability [14, 15]. Although AR technology has been explored in other dental domains such as implantology and orthodontics [16–20], its application in endodontic microsurgery remains relatively under-investigated.

Accordingly, a focused synthesis of the available evidence is necessary to determine the clinical relevance of these technologies. The present systematic review aims to evaluate whether AR-guided EMS improves spatial accuracy measured by linear and angular deviation and apex localization error compared with conventional freehand or non-augmented navigation techniques, and to assess its impact on operative efficiency and procedural outcomes. It is hypothesized that AR guidance enhances surgical accuracy and intraoperative control however, current evidence is expected to be constrained by methodological variability and predominantly preclinical study designs.

Objectives

Primary objective

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether Augmented reality guided endodontic surgery improves accuracy (Linear / angular deviation, apex location error) compared with conventional freehand techniques or non-augmented reality techniques.

Secondary objective

The secondary objective was to compare and evaluate operative time and efficiency and document procedural errors or iatrogenic events; describe device types, registration workflows, operator learning and identify methodological limitations and research gaps.

Materials and methods

Selection criteria

Inclusion criteria

Population

Resin/typodont models, 3D printed jaws and cadaveric mandibles/maxillae.

Intervention

Augmented reality system with 3D dynamic navigation integration or CBCT based planning technologies used in endodontic procedures such as apicoectomy, osteotomy and root end resection.

Comparators

3D Dynamic navigation alone, Haptic simulation, Freehand conventional surgery & static guidance.

Outcomes

Primary

Linear deviation(mm), angular deviation (°) and apex location errors.

Secondary

Operative time, over preparation, perforations and operator satisfaction.

Study types

In-vitro studies, Ex-vivo studies, primarily English language published studies.

Exclusion criteria

Population

Clinical cases, extracted human teeth, animal studies, purely computational/simulation models, imaging-only studies without physical models or clinical correlation.

Intervention

Non-augmented reality/Mixed Reality/Virtual reality/ digital guidance techniques, Dynamic navigation only, non-endodontic procedures (implantology, orthodontics, restorative), simulation only without surgical application.

Comparators

No comparator, non-relevant comparator, non-evaluable comparison.

Outcomes

No deviation metrics, no accuracy data, no operative time, no procedural error reporting, no operator related outcomes.

Study types

Clinical (non-randomized) comparative studies, reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, conference abstracts (no full text), technical notes, case reports/series without quantitative data, duplicate studies.

Protocols and registration

This review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The study was registered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) registries under the DOI number Doi: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/7H8DV.

Information sources

A wide scope literature search was performed across databases from the last 10 years till October 2025 for studies published in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science (WoS).

Study selection and data extraction

Following the search, the collected results were analyzed for inclusion as per the pre-defined eligibility criteria with the aid of Rayyan software. Additional articles were identified via hand-searching and manual screening of the references from the included articles (backward snowballing). The title and abstract screening were conducted by two autonomous reviewers following which the included articles underwent a full-text review for further assessment of eligibility. Discrepancies and disagreements were resolved by involving a third reviewer. The detailed process of literature identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion is presented in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1Prisma flowchart

Prisma flowchart

A comprehensive and systematic search strategy was implemented across three databases PubMed (n = 8), Scopus (n = 16), and Web of Science (n = 31) yielding a total of 55 records. To ensure completeness of the search and minimize publication bias, an additional 23 records were identified through citation searching of relevant articles. Following the removal of duplicate records (n = 9), a total of 69 unique records remained and were subjected to the screening phase. During title and abstract screening, 65 records were excluded based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, primarily due to irrelevance to augmented reality–guided endodontic microsurgery, inappropriate study design, or lack of outcome reporting. Full-text evaluation was performed against strict eligibility criteria, including study design, intervention characteristics, and outcome relevance. Of these, two studies had been excluded one due to the use of an ineligible intervention and another due to an ineligible study population while no exclusions were made based on methodological quality at this stage. Ultimately, three studies fulfilled all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the qualitative synthesis. The selection process was conducted in a structured and transparent manner to ensure methodological rigor, reduce selection bias, and enhance reproducibility of the review.

Table 2 represents the extracted variables of the included studies. Martinho et al. (2023) conducted an in vitro split-mouth study on 72 tooth roots using a dynamic navigation system integrated with a head-mounted AR device (HoloLens 2 + X-guide), where fiducial marker–based registration enabled precise tracking of osteotomy trajectories. This study demonstrated reduced global apex deviation (0.52 ± 0.30 mm vs. 1.35 ± 0.82 mm) and lower angular deviation (0.97 ± 0.43° vs. 1.90 ± 1.20°), alongside improved procedural efficiency, indicating enhanced spatial accuracy with AR-assisted navigation. In contrast, Remschmidt et al. (2023) employed an ex vivo cadaver model utilizing a marker less AR system based on HoloLens 2 with depth-sensing and facial tracking for automatic registration, focusing primarily on feasibility and usability outcomes. The study reported a mean operative time of 9 ± 2 min and a high System Usability Scale score (80.4 ± 6.8), reflecting effective integration of AR guidance without quantitative deviation analysis. Yue Chen et al. (2023) implemented a randomized crossover simulation design using 3D-printed alveolar models and a screen-based AR system (tablet with QR marker–based registration), demonstrating reduced volumetric bone removal deviation (5.71 vs. 12.24 mm³) and improved angular precision (11.67° vs. 18.16°), albeit with increased procedural time (408 vs. 322 s). Collectively, the extraction highlights critical methodological variability across studies, including differences in AR hardware (head-mounted vs. screen-based), registration strategies (fiducial, marker less, and marker-based), and operator experience levels, all of which are key determinants influencing the reported accuracy and efficiency outcomes.

As represented in the Fig. 2 -Study outcomes, within the accuracy domain, both Martinho et al. (2023) and Yue Chen et al. (2023) reported consistently lower linear, volumetric, and angular deviations with AR guidance, indicating improved alignment between planned and executed surgical procedures. In the efficiency domain, findings were inconsistent: Martinho et al. (2023) demonstrated reduced operative time with AR integrated into dynamic navigation, whereas Yue Chen et al. (2023) reported increased procedural time in the AR group, likely reflecting differences in workflow integration and operator familiarity. Remschmidt et al. (2023) reported operative time descriptively without a comparator, limiting direct interpretation. In terms of safety, Martinho et al. (2023) found no significant difference in procedural mishaps between groups, suggesting that AR does not adversely affect procedural reliability. For operator-related outcomes, Yue Chen et al. (2023) demonstrated increased confidence among inexperienced operators, while Remschmidt et al. (2023) reported high usability scores, indicating favourable user interaction with AR systems. However, in the treatment completeness domain, Yue Chen et al. (2023) observed increased residual lesion volume in the AR group, suggesting that improved geometric precision may not necessarily translate into complete lesion removal. Overall, the table highlights that AR consistently enhances accuracy and operator interaction, while its effects on efficiency and treatment completeness vary depending on study design and system implementation.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed according to study design using validated Cochrane-recommended tools. The randomized crossover simulation study by Yue Chen et al. (2023) was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, while the non-randomized experimental studies by Martinho et al. (2023) and Remschmidt et al. (2023) were assessed using ROBINS-I (version 2). The results are presented in Fig. 3A (summary graph) and Fig. 3B (traffic light plot).

Fig. 3A&B

A&B

For RoB 2, five domains were evaluated: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results, with judgments categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. For ROBINS-I, seven domains were assessed: confounding, participant selection, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results, with judgments classified as low, moderate, or critical risk of bias. All assessments were performed through structured evaluation of study design, methodological reporting, and outcome assessment procedures, for preclinical studies, confounding was interpreted in relation to operator variability, model standardization, and experimental control.

As shown in Figure A and Figure B, the distribution of risk across domains indicates that confounding and measurement-related domains contributed most to higher risk levels in non-randomised studies, whereas domains such as participant selection and intervention classification were predominantly low risk. Measurement of outcomes and selection of reported results demonstrated moderate risk across studies. At the study level, ROBINS-I assessments showed variation between moderate and high overall risk, whereas the RoB 2 assessment indicated some concerns overall. The traffic light plot (Figure B) visually represents domain-wise judgments for each study, while the summary graph (Figure A) illustrates the proportional distribution of low, moderate, and high/critical risk across all included studies.

Discussion

Synthesis of findings

The present review included three studies only, comprising one in vitro split-mouth investigation, one ex vivo cadaveric feasibility study, and one randomized crossover simulation study using 3D-printed models. Across this limited evidence base, the most consistent signal was improvement in procedural accuracy with AR-assisted workflows. Martinho et al. showed that integrating a HoloLens 2 display with a 3-dimensional dynamic navigation system reduced both translational and angular errors during osteotomy and root-end resection, with lower global apex deviation and lower angular deflection than navigation alone [6]. Similarly, Chen et al. reported that a screen-based AR system reduced volumetric bone removal deviation, root-end resection deviation, and bevel angle deviation compared with freehand surgery, indicating that AR can improve the fidelity of surgical execution even in inexperienced operators [5]. Remschmidt et al. did not provide a comparator-based accuracy dataset, but their cadaveric study demonstrated that AR-guided apicoectomy was technically feasible, with short preparation and operating times and a high usability score, which supports its intraoperative practicality under controlled ex vivo conditions [7]. Taken together, the available data suggest that AR guidance primarily acts as a spatial augmentation tool: it improves the correspondence between the preoperative plan and the executed surgical trajectory, but the current evidence is still too limited to determine whether that precision consistently translates into superior biologic or long-term clinical outcomes.

Comparison to Non-AR navigation modalities

When compared with conventional freehand approaches or non-augmented navigation, the advantage of AR lies in how information is delivered to the operator rather than in the information itself. Dynamic navigation already provides real-time positional feedback, but the surgeon must repeatedly shift attention away from the operative field to interpret a separate monitor, which may increase cognitive load and disrupt hand–eye coordination [3, 4]. In the study by Martinho et al., AR was used as a heads-up extension of dynamic navigation, allowing the surgeon to remain visually aligned with the surgical field while still receiving live positional guidance, and this hybrid configuration produced lower deviation values than navigation alone [6]. Chen et al. used a lower-cost screen-based AR workflow rather than a head-mounted device, yet the same principle applied: virtual apical targets and trajectories were superimposed on the surgical field, allowing more conservative and more accurate access than freehand surgery [5]. Remschmidt et al. further showed that a head-mounted, marker less AR platform could be deployed without external hardware and still provide stable intraoperative visualization, supporting the idea that AR may reduce the ergonomic and cognitive disadvantages inherent to conventional navigation systems [7]. Thus, across the included studies, AR does not replace preoperative planning or navigation rather, it improves the usability of those systems by making the planned anatomy directly visible in the operative line of sight.

Practical considerations and limitations

Despite the promising direction of effect, several methodological and technical constraints limit the interpretability of the findings. First, the studies differed substantially in platform architecture, registration strategy, and validation metric. Martinho et al. used a HoloLens 2 integrated with dynamic navigation and fiducial-based calibration, whereas Chen et al. relied on a tablet-based, marker-assisted, screen-through workflow, and Remschmidt et al. used a marker less head-mounted system based on automatic facial registration and depth sensing [5–7]. These systems are not directly interchangeable, because each introduces different sources of registration error, latency, field-of-view constraints, and user interaction patterns. Second, the evidence base is entirely preclinical or ex vivo, so the models did not capture important clinical variables such as bleeding, soft-tissue mobility, restricted access, patient movement, or intraoperative moisture, all of which may compromise overlay stability and operative precision in real surgery. Third, the outcomes reported were heterogeneous: Martinho et al. emphasized 2D/3D deviation and operative time, Chen et al. reported volumetric error, bevel angle, confidence, and residual lesion volume, while Remschmidt et al. focused on feasibility and usability rather than deviation analysis [5–7]. This heterogeneity makes quantitative synthesis difficult and limits comparability across studies. In addition, sample sizes were modest and operator-related effects were substantial; Chen et al. involved inexperienced residents, whereas Martinho et al. and Remschmidt et al. used trained or experienced operators, meaning that observed benefits may partly reflect learning effects rather than device performance alone [5–7]. For these reasons, the current findings should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence rather than confirmation of clinical superiority.

Recommendations for further future research

Future work should move beyond feasibility and short-term precision metrics toward standardized, clinically relevant validation. A priority is methodological harmonization, studies should use comparable deviation metrics, clearly define registration workflows, and report both translational and angular error in a reproducible manner so that results can be compared across platforms [5–7]. Second, research should incorporate more realistic models that better simulate clinical conditions, including soft tissue, visibility reduction, bleeding, and limited surgical access, because these variables are likely to influence AR performance more than controlled laboratory settings suggest. Third, larger comparative studies are needed to evaluate whether the improved spatial precision observed in simulation reduces procedural complications, preserves bone, shortens learning curves, or improves healing outcomes in patients. Finally, hybrid systems that combine dynamic navigation with AR overlays may deserve particular attention, because Martinho et al. indicate that this configuration can leverage the numerical precision of navigation while reducing the cognitive burden associated with monitor-based guidance [6]. If future studies standardize metrics and include early clinical cohorts, the field will be better positioned to determine whether AR is simply an accuracy-enhancing visualization tool or a genuinely transformative adjunct for endodontic microsurgery.

Conclusion

Despite the limited and primarily preclinical evidence available, augmented reality-guided endodontic microsurgery consistently shows improved spatial accuracy. This is demonstrated by decreased linear and angular deviations in osteotomy positioning and root-end resection across various platforms. These improvements appear to be accompanied by favourable operator-related outcomes, including increased procedural confidence and improved usability, particularly in less experienced operators, suggesting a potential role of AR in augmenting intraoperative visual spatial integration. However, the current evidence is characterized by substantial methodological heterogeneity in study design, AR system architecture, registration protocols, and outcome assessment metrics, which limits direct comparability and precludes quantitative synthesis. Therefore, AR-guided systems should currently be considered as precision enhancing adjuncts rather than definitive alternatives to conventional navigation or freehand techniques.
