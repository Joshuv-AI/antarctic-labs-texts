Increases in suture purchase of same magnitude lead to a higher gap resistance force for barbed sutures used in flexor tendon repair
Authors: Yiğit Umur Cırdı, Ali Eray Günay, Mehmet Ekici, Ömer Tolga Şekerci, Ramazan İlter Ozturk, Safa Gürsoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:There is still no optimal suture material or configuration for flexor tendon repair. Barbed sutures provide an alternative for flexor tendon repair with unidirectional clefts that effectively resist sliding. Increasing the suture purchase results in an expected increase in the tensile force. This study investigated the relationship between suture purchase length and repair strength in flexor tendon. This addresses the question of whether it is possible to achieve sufficient tensile strength compared to a 2-strand polypropylene suture by simply increasing the barbed suture course inside the tendon.Methods:The study involved 24 sheep flexor digitorum tendons, which were divided into 4 subgroups, each comprising 6 tendons. Group P1 utilized polypropylene sutures 1 cm from the incision, while group P2 used it 2 cm away. Group B1 utilized a barbed suture positioned 1 cm from the incision, while group B2 employed a barbed suture 2 cm from the incision. The outcome measures included testing for 2-mm Gap Resistance (2mm-GR) and maximum tensile strength (MTS) using an Instron® 3345K7023 tensile testing device (Norwood, MA, USA).Results:A 2 cm suture purchase length increased by the mean 2mm-GR by 6.9 N for polypropylene and 12.6 N for barbed sutures. Group B2 showed the highest 2mm-GR (20.7 N). Groups P2 and B2 had significantly higher 2mm-GR than groups P1 and B1 (P < .05). The MTS did not differ significantly between the groups (P = .743). Suture breakage occurred in 92% of cases, with knot failure observed in 8%.Conclusion:Extending the purchase length of the barbed suture within the tendon significantly enhances the MTS and 2mm-GR. With further refinement, barbed sutures have the potential to become a favorable option for tendon repair, possibly facilitating early and effective postoperative rehabilitation.Level of Evidence:Level V, Therapeutic Study.

Introduction

The flexor tendon repair technique has evolved rapidly to provide a more durable repair to adapt to aggressive postoperative rehabilitation protocols. Multi-strand repairs and peripheral sutures are now frequently preferred and have become the backbone of flexor tendon treatment.1,2 Suture material strongly influences the biomechanical durability of the tendon repair.3 However, traditional 4- to 6-strand repairs have been shown to increase the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the tendon, therefore creating a bulkiness that makes it difficult for the tendon to glide through the flexor pulleys and adhesions if the repair site is adjacent to pulleys.4,5

The use of barbed sutures in flexor tendon repair was introduced in 1967.6 The barbed sutures have unidirectional clefts. The clefts on this provide resistance by holding the tendon in a perpendicular direction against sliding. The number of clefts buried inside the tendon is responsible for the resistance generated. Therefore, providing an anti-glide effect by anchoring multiple points makes it a valuable alternative for flexor tendon repair.7 The pull-out strength and final failure force were shown to be similar to that of the conventional 4-strand Adelaide repair.8,9 In clinical scenarios where adequate tendon length is available for suture placement, increasing the suture purchase length may offer additional stabilization beyond what is achievable with conventional techniques.

Although the general performance of barbed sutures has been widely studied, no studies have specifically focused on the gain in tensile strength with increased purchase. This study aimed to examine the relationship between the length of the suture inside the tendon and repair strength and its effect on tensile force. Suture purchase length directly influences the strength and reduces the re-rupture rates and makes the more aggressive rehabilitation protocol possible. Furthermore, it reduces overall tendon repair-associated complications.10 It was hypothesized that extending the intratendinous course of the barbed suture would result in a significantly greater improvement in biomechanical repair strength compared to conventional polypropylene sutures.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not required due to the use of deidentified animal cadaveric specimens. All tendon specimens were obtained from animals slaughtered for food production, in compliance with local ethical and legal regulations regarding animal tissue use for research purposes.

Study group

In this study, 24 flexor digitorum profundus tendons of sheep forelimbs from a commercial slaughterhouse were used. All tendons were inspected for abnormalities or impairments, such as degeneration and trauma. The tendons were harvested within 2 hours after slaughter. After dissecting the paratenon, a 10 cm piece of tendon was excised. The sheep were on average 1-1.5 years old at the time of slaughter, with an average weight of 60-72 kg. Each specimen was gradually frozen and kept hydrated at room temperature for 12 hours before surgical intervention. Subsequently, each tendon is bisected. The 2-strand modified Kessler technique was employed to standardize both the intratendinous length of the suture and the influence of the suture material, thereby ensuring comparable mechanical conditions (Figure 1). Tendon repair was performed in consecutive sessions by a single surgeon specialized in hand surgery.

A comparative study employing block randomization was conducted. A computer-generated random sequence was used for allocation, and each of the 24 tendon specimens was assigned a randomized number. An independent researcher, not involved in the testing procedures, allocated the specimens into 4 groups (n = 6 per group). No identifiable information regarding group assignments was available on the specimens during the evaluation. The groups were formed as follows:

Group P1: Polypropylene suture (3/0) (PP) placed 1 cm from the incision gap.

Group P1: Polypropylene suture (3/0) (PP) placed 1 cm from the incision gap.

Group P2: Polypropylene suture (3/0) placed 2 cm from the incision gap.

Group P2: Polypropylene suture (3/0) placed 2 cm from the incision gap.

Group B1: Barbed suture (3/0) placed 1 cm from the incision gap.

Group B1: Barbed suture (3/0) placed 1 cm from the incision gap.

Group B2: Barbed suture (3/0) placed 2 cm from the incision gap.

Group B2: Barbed suture (3/0) placed 2 cm from the incision gap.

Outcome measures

The repaired tendons were subjected to tensile testing using an Instron testing device (Instron, USA). A pre-tension of 2 N was applied to eliminate slack. Pre-conditioning was not applied. Soft tissue clamps were utilized to retain the tendon ends, ensuring a consistent hold. No slippage was detected throughout the test. The process was recorded using a slow-motion camera.

Two key outcome measures were recorded:

2-mm Gap Resistance (2mm-GR): The force at which a 2 mm gap formed, measured in newtons (N).

2-mm Gap Resistance (2mm-GR): The force at which a 2 mm gap formed, measured in newtons (N).

Maximum Tensile Strength (MTS): The peak force at which the tendon ends completely separated, measured in newtons. The failure pattern at the MTS point was documented.

Maximum Tensile Strength (MTS): The peak force at which the tendon ends completely separated, measured in newtons. The failure pattern at the MTS point was documented.

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, an F-ANOVA test was employed to compare the differences between groups using SPSS version 26. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as percentages.

Power calculations

A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using GPower software (version 3.1), employing an ANOVA model. The required sample size was calculated based on measurement differences (in millimeters) along the X, Y, and Z axes between the study groups. This value was consistent with both the observed differences in the current dataset and estimates derived from preliminary measurements in a smaller sample set. Using a significance level of α = 0.05 and a desired power of 0.80, the analysis confirmed that a total of 24 specimens (n = 6 per group) provided sufficient statistical power. The calculated effect size was in agreement with values reported in the literature for comparable biomechanical studies.

Results

A 2 cm suture purchase length increased the mean 2mm-GR value by 6.9 N for polypropylene sutures and 12.6 N for barbed sutures. The percentage increase in 2mm-GR was 57% for polypropylene (group P1 to P2) and 156.7% for barbed sutures (group B1 to B2). The highest 2mm-GR value was observed in group B2, with a mean of 20.7 N (SD: 5.4). Both group P2 and group B2 exhibited significantly higher 2mm-GR values than groups P1 and B1 (P < .05). The failure pattern predominantly involved suture breakage, occurring in 92% (22/24) of the specimens, while knot failure was observed in 8% (2/24) of the cases, with 2 instances in group P2. In all specimens repaired with barbed sutures, failure consistently occurred due to suture breakage at the tendon repair site.

Table 1 presents a comparison of 2mm-GR and MTS averages between the 4 groups (P1, P2, B1, B2), with measurements given in N. For 2mm-GR, group P2 and group B2 show significantly higher values (19.0 N and 20.7 N, respectively) compared to group P1 and group B1 (12.1 N and 8.1 N, respectively), with a P value of <.001 indicating strong statistical significance. The MTS values did not show significant differences between the groups (P = .743) (Figure 2).

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that increasing suture purchase length enhances tensile strength, and that extended suture purchase length with barbed sutures yields MTS and 2mm-GR values comparable to those achieved with the conventional 2-strand Kessler technique using polypropylene sutures. The 2 cm suture purchase length resulted in a 57% increase in the 2mm-GR value for polypropylene and a 156.7% increase for barbed suture. While a 1 cm suture purchase provides lower tensile force in flexor tendon repair, the use of a longer course of barbed suture inside the tendon provided similar 2mm-GR and MTS values compared to conventional repair.

Barbed sutures offer significant potential in surgical applications, particularly in tendon repair, owing to their self-anchoring design that eliminates the need for knots.6 This reduces operative time and avoids complications related to knot bulk and failure, which are critical for preventing suture rupture or tendon retear. Barbed sutures also distribute tension evenly across the wound, minimizing localized pressure and ischemia, both vital factors for promoting tendon healing and recovery.6 Their ability to achieve faster wound closure and consistent tissue approximation helps to prevent gapping, dehiscence, and other complications. Additionally, using less suture material reduces foreign body load, contributing to better cosmetic outcomes and enhanced healing. On the other hand, their external barbs may increase friction between the tendon and the surrounding sheath, thereby elevating the work of flexion.11 In addition, the unique barbed geometry has been associated with a heightened intratendinous inflammatory response compared to smooth sutures.12 Importantly, in revision procedures, retained barbed suture fragments cannot be reversed or untied, often necessitating wider dissection for complete removal, thereby complicating re-entry surgery.9,13 The term “barbed” might raise concern about the increased risk of glove perforation due to the suture’s mechanical design; however, Schwarzkopf et al14 demonstrated that there were no increased perforation rates for barbed sutures. This potential risk can be avoided by using double layer or thicker gloves. Despite some minor limitations, the advantages make barbed sutures a promising and effective tool for tendon repair and other surgical procedures. However, current evidence does not yet support their routine use in all cases. Their application may be beneficial in selected scenarios where their specific advantages outweigh the potential drawbacks.

Biomechanical durability remains a major issue in the routine use of barbed sutures. This study demonstrated that this deficit may be compensated by simply increasing suture purchase. Cao et al10 demonstrated that increasing the purchase length from 4 to 12 mm increased the 2 mm-GR value by approximately 50%. Similarly, Tang et al15 also demonstrated an increased tensile strength as the suture purchase length increases and stated that the optimal suture purchase length is between 7 and 10 mm and showed the tensile strength does not increase significantly after 10 mm. This was attributed to the smooth surface of the PP sutures. The extended suture results in ineffective shrinkage of the tendon and shows no effect on the tensile strength as expected. However, with barbed sutures, taking transitions further from the incision line than the routinely used distances increased the tensile force and made it equal to the conventional PP repair group as hypothesized.

The MTS must be sufficient to allow the hand to perform active motion following repair. In the current study, tendon repair with barbed suture provided MTS results similar to those of conventional repair (P = .743). Likewise, it has been shown that 2-strand barbed and conventional repairs provide similar MTS values with the advantage of a smaller CSA increase.8,16 Similarly, with the 4-strand repair technique, the authors highlighted equivalent tensile force with barbed sutures.9,11 Furthermore, in another study, the maximum pullout force was enhanced by 280% (38N to 145 N) in the 4-stranded barbed suture configuration compared to the 2-strand, whereas polydioxanone suture increased it by 54% only, reaching a similar maximum tensile force eventually (149.5N vs. 145.6N).17 This finding supports the hypothesis that an increased course inside the tendon generates comparable MTS values for barbed sutures. Although the routine use of barbed sutures is not prominently attractive for flexor tendon repair, it is a candidate for ideal tendon repair with the convenience it provides.

Repaired tendons must remain intact when force is applied to endure postoperative mobilization protocols. It has been shown that 24 N force is generated during the flexion of the flexor digitorum profundus in an intact tendon.18 Furthermore, durability of at least 35 N is required to perform unresisted active finger movement.19 Gap formation exceeding 2 mm promotes fibrotic healing of the tendon tissue, contributes to elongation at the repair site, and increases gliding resistance, thereby impairing smooth passage through the pulley system.20–22

Gap resistance force is the keystone for early rehabilitation, and the findings indicated that the increase in the 2mm-GR in barbed suture was higher than that in the conventional method (57.1% vs. 155.5%). In addition, the ultimate gap resistance became equal when the suture purchase length was increased. Consistent with the findings, similar to superior gap resistance forces were obtained when similar strand counts were used.8,11,23 However, the authors concluded that these differences do not provide a significant advantage over the conventional method, and even increased work of flexion may result in increased incidence of failure.11,17,23 Likewise, recent reviews reaffirm these findings, indicating either a greater load to the 2-mm gap for the barbed suture repair or at least statistically comparable loads.12

Previous studies have primarily focused on comparing the biomechanical strength of different suture materials, configurations, and strand counts; barbed sutures appear to be influenced not only by the material properties but also by the length of suture purchase within the tendon.8,24,25 However, it should be kept in mind that tensile force is multifactorial, and the elasticity and surface friction characteristics of conventional sutures may also influence the observed outcomes. In contrast to conventional sutures, the biomechanical strength of barbed sutures is more significantly affected by the distance they traverse inside the tissue. This observation suggests that, in suitable clinical scenarios, increasing the suture purchase length alone may enhance repair strength without requiring changes in suture type or configuration. This study demonstrated that similar tensile strength could be obtained using simpler configurations. As hypothesized, a similar increase in suture purchase length results in a 58.3% increase in 2mm-GR values for PP sutures vs. 157.5% for barbed sutures.

This study has some limitations. As a biomechanical investigation primarily focused on evaluating the tensile strength of barbed suture use in flexor tendon repair, it lacks clinical validation, highlighting the need for further research and clinical trials to confirm its effectiveness. Moreover, future studies incorporating cyclic loading protocols are warranted to better assess the durability and long-term performance of barbed sutures. Lastly, while the study indicates that longer suture purchase lengths improve repair strength, it also highlights that a 1 cm purchase may be inadequate, suggesting that the benefits of barbed sutures are contingent on specific surgical techniques and conditions. Further biomechanical studies are essential to evaluate the long-term outcomes and clinical applicability of the findings.

This study demonstrated that increasing the suture purchase length enhances the 2mm-GR force more for barbed sutures than for PP sutures. Barbed sutures, when used with an extended purchase length, could potentially offer practical benefits, such as eliminating the need for knots and reducing the bulkiness associated with traditional multistrand repairs.
