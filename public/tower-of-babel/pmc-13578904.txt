Myosin II–independent contraction of actin filaments in membrane nanotubes
Authors: Md Arsalan Ashraf, Tapas Singha, Susav Pradhan, Serene Rose David, Pierre Sens, Pramod Pullarkat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceForce generation by actin filaments is a fundamental property of Eukaryotic cells. These filaments can generate pushing forces via polymerization, pulling forces via the action of molecular motor proteins, and can generate filament sliding forces mediated by dynamic cross-linking proteins. Here we demonstrate sustained, cyclic, contractile force generation in actin filaments confined in membrane nanotubes pulled out of axons using optical tweezers. Using pharmacological and genetic perturbations studies, and theoretical analysis we show that the force generation is due to the action of the actin severing proteins belonging to the actin depolymerizing factor (ADF/cofilin) class. Such mechanisms can be relevant in regulating the dynamics of tubular cell structures like filopodia and tunneling nanotubes.

Membrane nanotubes are generated by various cell types to perform important biological processes such as cell locomotion, environment sensing, signaling, intercellular transport, etc. Examples include filopodia generated by motile cells and neuronal growth cones (1), microvilli (2), stereocilia (3), pseudopodia of dendritic cells (4), and tunneling nanotubes that bridge multiple cells (5, 6), including neurons and glia cells (7). The formation and stability of these nanotubes, and in some cases their growth and retraction dynamics, relies on the dynamics of actin filaments and their regulation by actin associated proteins. It is reasonably well established that such nanotubes can be formed by forces generated by sustained polymerization of actin filaments (8, 9). However, how actin dynamics is regulated within such nanotubes and how this regulation is associated with their ability to generate pulling forces are less well understood. The generation of contractile stresses by the actin cytoskeleton generally involves myosin II motor proteins, which also play a central role in most mechanism proposed to explain pulling forces generated by filopodia (10–12). However, experiments and theoretical models have also shown that contractile forces can be generated through the cross-linking, bundling, and turnover of actin filament, without the requirement of myosin motor proteins (13–16). One such crosslinker is anillin, known to be important for the contraction of cytokinesis rings (17). These findings naturally raise the question of whether contractile forces in nanotubes require myosin II activity.

Here we show that membrane tethers drawn out of axons using optically trapped micron-size beads form a very convenient system to investigate actin dynamics within nanotubes. We demonstrate that actin filaments invade these extracted tubes and exhibit sustained growth and retraction cycles within the tube. More remarkably, such tubes exhibit actin-dependent contractile force generation via a mechanism which is independent of myosin II molecular motors. The observed cyclic load and fail behavior is quantified and compared across different biochemical and genetic perturbations to elucidate the mechanism responsible for the observed actin dynamics and force generation within nanotubes. This leads us to propose a mechanism of actin contractile stress generation based on actin depolymerizing factor (ADF/cofilin). We substantiate this proposal by a theoretical model where multiple actin filaments stochastically bind and unbind from the protrusion’s tip while undergoing continuous shrinkage due to cofilin binding. This model quantitatively reproduces the statistics of contractile stress generation and explains the cyclic dynamics and the load-fail behavior in nanotubes.

Results

Extracted Nanotubes Exhibit Load and Fail Cycles.

Membrane nanotubes, also known as tethers, were extracted from axonal shafts of chick dorsal root ganglia neurons using an optically trapped polystyrene bead (Fig. 1A–C). After extraction of the nanotube, the distance between the axonal shaft (base) and the trap was maintained at a length of about 10 µm and the force on the bead was recorded. In many cases (≳50%), after about a minute, a repeating load and fail behavior (active force peaks) like the one shown in Fig. 1D could be observed (more examples in SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In the rest of the cases, where the tethers remained attached, no active force peak could be observed up to ≃10 min. When load-fail cycles were observed, the tether force increase slowly, pulling the bead toward the axon (Fig. 1A), and eventually drops rapidly to a base value. The time evolution of the force build-up varied from cycle to cycle, but whenever the force collapsed back to the base value, the sudden drop in force exhibited a double-exponential decay (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Histograms for the distributions of “rupture time” (trupt), defined as the duration from start of a force event to final collapse to the base value (green horizontal line in Fig. 1D), and the peak force (fpeak) within each cycle as measured from the baseline (blue vertical line in Fig. 1D), are shown in Fig. 1 E and F. The statistics of lag-times between two load and fail cycles was also analyzed (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). It shows an exponential distribution with an average waiting time of order 30 s.

To explore the mechanism for the active force generation, we applied various biochemical perturbations to the myosin motor proteins prior to nanotube extraction, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. No significant changes in the load-fail behavior could be observed after treatments using either the myosin II inhibitor Blebbistatin (20 µM), or with the Rho kinase inhibitor Y-27632, or with the myosin V blocker MyoVin-1 (a combination of MLCK inhibitor ML7 and Rho kinase inhibitor Y-27632 was also tried; see SI Appendix, Fig. S3). However, depletion of ATP (Materials and Methods), stabilization of actin using Jasplakinolide, or prevention of actin polymerization using Latrunculin A completely abolished the load-fail cycles without exemption. These results demonstrate that the repeated occurrence of active force peaks require dynamic actin filaments but molecular motors like myosin II and myosin V are dispensable. As filament turnover seems to be involved, we also tried inhibiting actin nucleators: formins using FH2 domain inhibitor SMIFH2 and Arp2/3 using CK666. These inhibitors failed to abolish the active force peaks (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). It has to be noted that the antiformin drug SMIFH2 has been shown to also inhibit various myosins as well (18). This includes myosin IIA, Drosophila myosins V & VIIa, and bovine myosin X.

Actin Filaments Show Growth and Retraction Cycles.

To explore the role of actin filaments further, we performed fluorescence imaging using cells expressing either LifeAct-mCherry or pCAG-Actin-GFP. As can be seen from the sequence of images and the kymograph shown in Fig. 3A, actin fluorescence exhibit a cyclic growth and decay dynamics within the nanotube (Movie S1). The kymograph shows that the retraction of actin fluorescence occurs with a maximum velocity near the tip, there is negligible velocity toward the base, and no significant retrograde flow during the regrowth phase. A more detailed analysis of the kymographs is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S5. A quantification of the time evolution of the fluorescence intensity within the nanotube is shown in Fig. 3B. These data show the following. i) During the “growth phase,” when the fluorescent region grows toward the tip, the intensity at any time point decays monotonically from the base toward the tip (time point i in Fig. 3B). This shows that multiple filaments invade the tube and the filament density decreases toward the tip. ii) During the “retraction phase,” regions of localized actin build up are often observed (time point ii in Fig. 3B). These peaks of fluorescence intensity can occur anywhere along the tether and they drift toward the base of the tube. iii) For the case shown in Fig. 3B, there is hardly any detectable intensity between the retraction front and the tip of the membrane tube (time point ii). This, along with the occurrence of localized fluorescence peaks, suggests that actin retracts as polymerized filaments before undergoing any subsequent depolymerization and regrowth. iv) After the retraction phase, the intensity becomes more diffused and eventually regains the monotonically decreasing pattern (time point iii in Fig. 3B), and the cycles repeat.

Fig. 3C shows the optical tweezers measurements performed on a fluorescently labeled cell. In this case, the force increase occurs when the actin intensity becomes continuous and the sudden drop in force coincides with a discontinuity in actin fluorescence. Occasionally, we observe nanotubes where no clear break in fluorescence intensity is seen when the force drops. We presume that in such cases there is still a loss of mechanical continuity due to yielding of filaments within the nanotube. Nanotubes frequently exhibit helical buckling as the actin detaches and retracts within the tube (Fig. 3D and Movie S1). The helical buckling as well as the actin growth-retraction cycles can be seen even in extremely long nanotubes as well (>50 μm) and the wavelength of buckling is similar to that seen in shorter tubes (Movie S2).

Since blocking of myosin II or myosin V activity did not prevent the load-fail behavior, we considered the possibility of actin filaments getting pulled by kinesin or dynein motors on axonal microtubules. Disrupting axonal microtubules by exposure to 10 µM Nocodazole for >15 min did not have any noticeable effect on the force behavior. Since such a treatment causes only partial disruption of microtubules in axons (19), we also performed experiments using chick primary fibroblasts transfected with tubulin-GFP. Here we could confirm complete dissociation of microtubules and such fibroblast cells, as well as untreated fibroblasts, exhibited active force dynamics similar to that seen in axons (SI Appendix, Figs. S6 and S7). As will be discussed in more detail later, our experiments suggest that molecular motors are unlikely to be the generators of force within the actin filaments that invade the nanotubes.

Role of ADF/Cofilin in Actin-Mediated Force Cycles.

Since actin stabilization by Jasplakinolide inhibits the force cycles, we investigated the possible role of the actin severing proteins that belong to the ADF/cofilin family (20–24). Chick DRG neurons contain the ADF (Actin Depolymerizing Factor) analogue of cofilin (25). Imaging using a RFP tag to ADF (using pCAG-ADF-RFP) and GFP tag to actin (pCAG-Actin-GFP) reveal a colocalization and highly correlated dynamics of ADF and actin within nanotubes (Fig. 4 A and B and Movie S3). Although it is reported in literature that cofilin binds predominantly to ADP-actin, we do not see any observable lag between the two fluorescence signals. This could be because the filaments crossing the ROI defined in Fig. 4A are probably not freshly polymerized filament and already carry ADF/cofilin. Next, we expressed either a constitutively inactive form of ADF (ADF S3E) (26) or a constitutively active form (ADF S3A) (27), and recorded the force time-series. As shown in Fig. 4C, competition with inactive ADF decreased the peak height while the peak duration is increased (Welch’s t test was performed for statistical significance and significance was assessed at α=0.01). Remarkably, in some cases the peak duration lasted for as much as 250 s with the force remaining close to a saturation value (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Such long rupture times with the force remaining at saturation level were never seen in normal cells. Expression of the active form of ADF, on the other hand, did not show any significant difference in peak height or peak duration compared to control (see Materials and Methods for statistical analysis).

A Stochastic Model with Filament Shrinkage Reproduces the Load-and-Fail Behavior

We propose to analyze the statistics of the load and fail cycles of the nanotube based on a stochastic model involving actin filaments attaching to and detaching from the nanotube tip, and progressive filament shrinkage due to the binding of cytoplasmic actin associated proteins, thereafter labeled ADF/cofilin for conciseness, see Fig. 5A for a sketch of the model. The assumption of protein-induced filament shrinkage can be thought of as a generic mechanism for the generation of pulling forces. We show in SI Appendix that the induction of torsional and/or curvature stress which can be inferred from the helical shape of detached filament (Fig. 3D) can, in the linear regime, be accounted for by an effective shrinkage of the filament’s preferred length. We follow this simplified approach in the model described below. The model is designed to explain the characteristic temporal signature of the force, made of distinct load-and-fail events. The growth or depolymerization of actin filaments not attached to the nanotube tip are not included in the model to limit the number of parameters, and therefore, the model does not capture the time delay between successive loading events. However, our analysis shows (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) that the distribution of lag time between load and fail events follows a simple exponential distribution with an average waiting time of order 30 s, suggesting independent events which could easily be incorporated in a more complete model.

Shrinkage of Individual Filaments.

As the force steadily increases with time between failure events, the binding and generation of strain by ADF/cofilin can be thought of as an almost continuous process. Considering a filament made of N actin subunits, each of length ℓ, the filament length prior to any ADF/cofilin binding is L0=Nℓ. We assume that a single ADF/cofilin can bind to a single actin monomer (28, 29) and decreases its rest (stress free) length by an amount δℓ. The rest length of the filament is thus L=L0(1−ϵδℓ/ℓ), where ϵ is the fraction of subunits carrying ADF/cofilin. The time evolution of ϵ satisfies the kinetic equation ϵ˙=kon(1−ϵ)−koff ϵ, where the ADF/cofilin binding and unbinding rates kon and koff could depend on local properties such as the mechanical stress. For simplicity, we assume here that they do not, so that starting with a ADF/cofilin-free filament at t=0, we have[1]ϵ(t)=konkon+koff1−e−kbt, kb=kon+koff.

Note that the rate kb for the generation of strain in the filament includes both ADF/cofilin binding to actin and the induction of conformation change.

Force Exerted by an Individual Filament.

An empty membrane tether held by an optical trap exerts a force fmemb=2π2κσ on the trap, where σ is the membrane tension and κ is the membrane bending rigidity (30). The length of such tube is then Lmemb=Ltrap−fmemb/Kext, where Ltrap is the (imposed) distance between the center of the trap and the bead position on the undeformed axon prior to pulling, and Kext is an effective stiffness that includes the stiffness of the trap and the bending stiffness of the axon (1/Kext=1/Ktrap+1/Kaxon) (Fig. 5A). An actin filament that reaches the tip of the tether and attaches to it has an initial length L0=Lmemb. As this filament shrinks, the trap force increases: F=fmemb+ff, where the contribution of actin to the force is ff(t)=KeffL0ϵ(t)δℓ/ℓ, where 1/Keff=1/Kext+1/klink+1/kf is an effective stiffness that also account for the stiffness of the actin filament kf and that of the linker between the filament and the tether tip klink. Note that Keff is dominated by the softest element of the system. Finally, the evolution of the force generated by a single filament is[2]ff(t)=fmax1−e−kbt, fmax=KeffLmembδℓ/ℓ1+koff/kon.

This force increases until it either saturates and eventually drops when the filament detaches or drops before saturation. Note that if ADF/cofilin binding and filament shrinkage start before its attachment to the nanotube tip, this merely reduces its shrinkage capacity once attached, which is quantified by the parameter fmax. The value of fmax obtained by fitting the model to the data should therefore be thought of as an effective parameter that includes ADF/cofilin binding and filament modification before its attachment to the nanotube tip.

By choosing a particular rupture kinetics, one may derive the distribution of rupture forces and rupture times as is measured experimentally. As shown in SI Appendix, these distributions can be computed analytically for constant rupture rate, or for mechanosensitive rupture following Bell’s statistics (rate increasing exponentially with the force). Quantitative analysis (detailed in SI Appendix), shows that the single filament picture—even with mechanosensitive rupture—is unable to reproduce the salient features of the force and time probability distributions shown in Fig. 1 E and F, namely the presence of a peak at small force (and time), and an exponential distribution for large force (and time). Indeed, the single filament picture rather predicts an exponential distribution (for constant unbinding rate) or a peak with short range decay (for mechanosensitive unbinding). Furthermore, some peculiar features of the force trace, in particular the existence of partial rupture events after which the force remains above the baseline (Fig. 5 D and D’) suggests that multiple filaments are involved in the force generation process. This is also in agreement with the fluorescence data shown in Fig. 3B.

Force Exerted by Multiple Filaments.

The situation where several filaments can be attached to the tether tip simultaneously involves nontrivial collective effects and must be handled numerically. Indeed, filaments binding at different times shrink at different rates. Slowly shrinking filaments will be compressed by fast shrinking filaments, affecting the net tether force in complex ways. To model this, we assume that a given filament i attaches under no tension at time ti with a length equal to the tether length at the time of attachment L(ti). As ADF/cofilin binds to this filament, its rest length Li(t) decreases, and it pulls on the tip with a force fi(t)=ki(L(t)−Li(t)), where ki accounts for the stiffness of the filament and the linker protein connecting the filament to the tip. The tether force is then F(t)=fmemb+∑ifi(t), which can be written as[3]F(t)=∑iki(Lmemb−Li(t))1+∑iki/Kext,

and the force on the ith filament is expressed as[4]fi(t)=ki(Lmemb−Li)−∑jkj(Lmemb−Lj)Kext+∑jkj.

We note that if all filaments and linkers are identical (ki=k) and bind simultaneously, the total average force is F=nfil f1=nfil k(Lmemb−L1)/(1+nfil k/Kext), where nfil is the number of attached filaments. In the limit of stiff filaments: nfilk≫Ktrap, the total average force becomes F=Ktrap(Lmemb−L1), which is independent of the number of filaments nfil. On the other hand, soft filaments (nfilk≪Ktrap) produce a force (F=nfil k(Lmemb−L1)) proportional to the number of filaments in this situation. The fitting procedure described below is performed assuming stiff filaments.

Numerical Simulations.

We use the Gillespie algorithm (31) to model the system. This algorithm considers agent-based processes that incorporate stochasticity in the individual level for both the binding and unbinding of ADF/cofilins and the attachment and detachment of the filaments on the tether tip. We consider each event as a reaction that occurs in real time based on its respective rate. Let us consider a total number of filaments nfil, with na attached filaments and nd=nfil−na detached filaments at a given time t. We first calculate the total propensity of all reactions, which can be written as[5]Rtotal(t)=kon∑i=1na[Ni(t)−ci(t)]+koff∑i=1naci(t)+ka nd(t) +kr na(t),

where ci and Ni are the number of bound ADF/cofilins and the total number of available sites on the ith filament, respectively. The rupture and attachment rates of a filament are denoted by kr and ka, respectively. Once a reaction occurs, the next reaction—whichever it is—will occur Δt time later which is calculated as Δt= log(1/r)/Rtotal where r∈[0,1) is a random number chosen from a uniform distribution. A reaction occurs with a probability determined by the ratio of the reaction rate to the total rate. For example, the detachment of the ith filament occurs with a probability of kr/Rtotal. Each force evolution with time (until rupture) is considered as an ensemble in our statistical analysis.

The results of the simulation for single filaments with mechanosensitive rupture are shown in SI Appendix. It compares very well with the analytical solution also discussed in SI Appendix. However, as discussed earlier, the single filament case with the mechanosensitive rupture is not sufficient to fit the experimental data (SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and S14). One must therefore invoke the possible binding of multiple filaments.

Best Fit to Experimental Data.

The temporal evolution of the force follows two stochastic processes; ADF/cofilin binding and unbinding on actin filaments, and filament attachment and detachment to the nanotube tip. The former typically results in much smaller fluctuations than the latter, such that the force generated by attached filament can be considered deterministic and characterized by two parameters: the maximum force per filament fmax and the net ADF/cofilin binding rate kb (Eq. 2). Restricting ourselves to linear and force insensitive filament attachment and rupture kinetics, the full stochastic system requires three additional parameters: the maximum number of filament that can be attached at a given time nfil and their attachment and rupture rate ka and kr, respectively. Therefore, in addition to experimentally measurable parameters such as the tether length, membrane force, and trap stiffness, the statistics of the force traces involve five parameters. Initial conditions at the start of an event must also be specified. Here we adopt the simple unbiased choice based for the probability pi that i∈[1,nfil] filaments are bound at the start of a force generation event, based on the different ways i distinguishable filaments can attached on nfil sites: pi=nfili/(2nfil−1). For nfil=3 (see below) this corresponds to p1=p2=3/7 and p3=1/7, but it should be noted that the distributions are not very sensitive to the chosen values of pi, as illustrated by SI Appendix, Fig. S17.

We adopt the following fitting procedure. As force events remaining at the saturation force for a long time or stopping during the initial linear rise are rarely observed (Figs. 1D and 5 and additional example in SI Appendix, Figs. S18–S20), one expects the strain rate to be of order the rupture rate and we fix kb=kr. The four remaining parameters (kr, ka, fmax, and nfil) can be determined with high confidence from the exponential tails of the rupture time and peak force distributions, and from the maximum of the peak force distribution at small force (Fig. 1). As shown in SI Appendix, the exponential tail of the rupture time distribution is primarily controlled by the rupture rate kr and the steady-state number of attached filaments nfil∗=nfil/(1+kr/ka), and is well fitted for kr=1/4 s−1 and nfil∗=3/2. The short time peak of this distribution is mostly influenced by the fact that short events reaching a force <1 pN are not counted in experiments considering noise level of the data, and does not allow for a separate determination of ka and nfil, see SI Appendix, Fig. S16. The exponential tail of the peak force distribution (with kb=kr) leads to the maximum force per filament fmax=4.3 pN, while the maximum of the distribution at small force imposes nfil=3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S16) and therefore ka=kr.

In summary, the best fit, displayed together with the experimental distributions in Fig. 1 E and F, correspond to kr=ka=kb=1/4 s−1, fmax=4.3 pN, and nfil=3. Note that the degree of confidence for the fitting parameter is relatively high, as illustrated by the different examples shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S16. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 5B–D, the broad variety of features of the load and fail behavior seen experimentally are well reproduced by the simulations. An example time series and a more detailed comparison of these are shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S18–S20.

Discussion

Comparison with Filopodia Contraction.

Although membrane nanotubes pulled out of axons resemble filopodia in some ways, the contractile mechanism we observe is distinct from the several mechanisms proposed earlier for filopodial contractility, as summarized below. Based on traction force microscopy, Chan et al. demonstrated that filopodia exhibit repeated load and fail cycles (10). They modeled mechanosensitive contractility by invoking myosin II mediated pulling at the base of the filopodia, although the involvement of myosin II in force generation was not explored in their experiments. In general, the role of myosin II in filopodial contraction is not clear with different studies on different cell types yielding different results (16, 32–34). In our case, multiple drug experiments clearly rule out any role for myosin II in the contraction of extracted nanotubes. Another mechanism proposed by Bornschlögl et al. for pulling force generation in filopodia invokes frictional coupling between the retrograde flow of actin filaments in the cortex and actin filaments extending from the filopodium (11). Such a mechanism where actin filaments in the tube are pulled at the base due to cortical retrograde flow can be ruled out in our case because i) that significant retrograde flow is observed only near the tip and only after actin detachment from the tip, when the pulling force vanishes, and ii) the flow velocity is nearly zero at the base of the nanotubes (see kymograph of Fig. 3A and analysis in SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Unconventional myosins like myosins V & VI have also been tested in the case of retracting phagocytotic filopodia, and knock-out of either of these did not affect retraction, and no force measurement is reported (35). Based on optical tweezers measurements, Farrell et al. have proposed a force generation mechanism that is mediated by end-tracking linkages between the actin filaments and the bead (16). Generally, such linkages occur via formins, and formins can also act as force generators (36). Such mechanisms are unlikely in our case, as inhibition of formins did not abolish the force events (Fig. 2). More recently, based on the rotation of actin filaments inside filopodia, knock-down experiments, and theoretical modeling, Leijnse et al. have argued that unconventional myosins like myosin V & X may be involved in the helical buckling of the actin bundles (12). In our case, pharmacological blocking of myosin V did not have any effect on the active peaks, and we could observe only faint intensity in cells expressing GFP-myosin-X (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Additionally, estimates of force generation by membrane-associated myosins such as myosin X suggest that they are unlikely to produce forces of the scale we observe. Such force would result from the viscous drag associated to membrane flow. With membrane viscosity of order μ=10−9 N.s/m (37) and motor velocity of order v=1μ m/s (38), the force per motor is of order ≃4πμv≃10−2 pN. A force of 10 pN would require about 1,000 motor, or an average distance of 10 nm between motors along the tube, which is hardly compatible with the size of a single motor. Finally, note that walking of membrane bound myosins will generate an equal pushing force on the membrane (see ref. 39) and hence the bead will experience a net zero force.

Several studies performed on filopodia have highlighted the role of actin severing proteins belonging to the ADF/cofilin family in regulating actin dynamics (23, 40, 41). Our experiments using mutant ADF constructs suggest that stresses are generated by ADF/cofilin binding leading to contractile forces in actin bundles, subsequent disruption of filaments, followed by repolymerization. This process is detailed below.

Contractile Force Driven by Crosslinking Proteins.

There is growing evidence that contractile forces in cytoskeletal systems can arise without myosin-driven power strokes (14, 42). Such motor-independent forces can result from actin crosslinking and bundling, with proteins such as fascin and filamin increasing the mechanical interactions within the network. More generally, the degree of crosslinking and their connectivity in the network are key factors in setting the magnitude of the global contraction (43). Experiments on apolar actin networks crosslinked by filamin show that they can phase separate into dense aggregates and the surface tension of the aggregate can generate contractile forces without molecular motors (44). Using a reconstituted system of actin rings, experiment shows that constriction can be driven by the anillin (a crosslinker protein), which causes relative sliding of actin filaments and together with filament turnover, generates contractile forces (17). In addition, in the presence of crosslinkers, depolymerization of the filament in the network can drive contractile forces by reorganizing the network toward a preferred mechanical state (14). Together, these findings indicate that contractile force generation in actin networks can emerge from crosslinker activity, network architecture, and collective material properties rather than from conventional motor-driven sliding.

ADF/Cofilin Based Contractility Hypothesis.

We observe that ADF colocalizes with the dynamic actin within the nanotubes (Fig. 4 A and B). Reduction in ADF activity caused by competition with an inactive form which competes with native ADF results in very long lived force events and reduced peak force when compared to control cells, suggesting a role for ADF in force generation (Fig. 4C). No significant changes to the load-fail response is seen when a constitutively active form of ADF is overexpressed (Fig. 4C). This can be understood if the concentration of the native form of active ADF is already high enough to saturate the filaments.

The ADF/cofilin based mechanism proposed here can account for the sustained cyclic load-fail behavior seen in the nanotubes as follows. ADF/cofilin is known to decorate actin filaments in filopodia and drive disassembly (23, 40, 41). Freshly formed actin filaments are composed of ATP-bound monomers (45) and are prone to ADF/cofilin-induced severing only after they have been converted to the ADP-bound form (46). This causes a delay between the initial growth of actin within the tube, subsequent stress generation due to ADF/cofilin binding, and the eventual severing and disintegration of filaments. The G-actin thus generated undergoes exchange of nucleotide to participate in fresh filament formation, completing the cycle. The time taken for fresh actin to polymerize, hydrolyze ATP, and bind cofilin, gives rise to the “dead-time” between successive force events.

Based on the above observations, We hypothesize that stresses generated by ADF on actin filaments can also account for the measured contractile forces. It is known that the binding of ADF/cofilin results in the generation of torsional stress that eventually causes severing (24, 47–50). The torsional stress manifest as an overtwisting of actin filaments leading to a ≈25% reduction in the actin pitch (41, 48, 49). We argue that this overtwisting of actin filaments is accompanied by a normal contractile stress resulting in a reduction in the length of each actin filament. A correlation between longitudinal stress in actin filaments and cofilin-induced severing was demonstrated using an in vitro system (51). There, an externally imposed longitudinal tension on actin filaments held using optical tweezers markedly decreased the severing probability of cofilin. As a corollary, in the mechanism we propose here, the binding of ADF on actin can induce conformational changes leading to generation of tension when the ends are constrained between boundaries. Such effects are expected due to allosteric reasons, as was predicted based on the experimentally observed plasticity of actin filaments (21, 52). Remarkably, an allosteric force generation mechanism has been reported recently to explain the generation of longitudinal tension in microtubules held in a double optical trap when the microtubule associated protein tau was allowed to bind (53).

While we expect a cofilin-induced shrinkage of the filament, estimating the contractile strain from the overtwisting is not straightforward. Therefore, we adopt a simplified model of effective filament shrinkage to access important measurable quantities related to the statistics of cofilin-induced contractile force generation, namely the maximum expected force per filament and the rate of the force build-up.

Simple Model of ADF/Cofilin-Mediated Contractility.

Here, we propose a simple model where ADF/cofilin binding imposes actin filaments to shrink, generating a pulling force on the tether tip (pictorially summarized in Fig. 6). In this model, actin filaments are assumed to be straight, but in practice, this effect can also be obtained if ADF/cofilin binding imposes a helical twist to the filament (SI Appendix). Owing to the short-time linear increase of tether force with time seen in our experiments, we assume that the shrinkage is constant per bound protein, and that binding follows a first-order kinetics with a constant shrinkage rate kb that eventually saturates to a value corresponding to a maximum pulling force per filament fmax, determined by the ratio of the protein binding to unbinding rates. Fitting this model to experimental probability distribution leads to the values fmax=4.3 pN and kb=1/(4 s).

A schematic showing the different stages of the cyclic load and fail mechanism proposed here. Two example force responses are shown in the Center. (stage 1) Filaments are not attached to the tip and hence force remains at the base value. (stage 2) A few filaments are in contact with the tip and linker proteins. A rise in force is observed as ADF/cofilin progressively decorates the filaments. Note that in reality the filament number decreases from base to tip and this is not detailed here for simplicity. (stage 3) Loss of mechanical integrity of one or a few, but not all, of the filaments leads to either a sudden decrease of slope or a partial drop in force. Decrease in slope (Fig. 5 C and C’) occur when the remaining filaments are not saturated with ADF/cofilin and hence continue to shrink. (stage 4) When all the filaments detaches from the tip, force collapses to baseline value.

Our stochastic model includes ADF/cofilin binding on actin, which generates contraction, and actin attachment and detachment to the tip of the membrane tube. We show that qualitative features of the time-dependent force (such as abrupt partial drops of force that do not reach the baseline, Fig. 5 D and D’ and SI Appendix, Fig. S14) requires that several (at least three) actin filaments can be bound simultaneously to the tether tip. With this assumption, the model is able to quantitatively reproduce the distribution of peak force and rupture time measured experimentally, with attachment and rupture rates ka=kr=1/(4 s). Moreover, certain features of the temporal variation of the force, such as sharp increase and decrease in slope, are also reproduced (Fig. 5 C and C’ and SI Appendix, Fig. S15).

This simple model does not account for the time delay between contraction events, which depend on actin polymerization and associated ATP to ADP conversion, but concentrates on the statistics of the events themselves. Furthermore, the model does not include tension-dependent ADF/cofilin binding/unbinding or actin attachment/detachment. While these features are not necessary to reproduce the peak force and duration statistics, they might nevertheless be important for other features, such as the anticorrelation between peak force and duration when cells are transfected with a constitutively inactive form of ADF (Fig. 4C). Finally, although we cannot rule out additional contributions to force arising from filament bundling via crosslinkers or to retrograde flow due to unconventional myosins, we believe that the experimental observations are best accounted for by this ADF/cofilin based model. The nanotube technique we have employed can be very valuable in assessing actin dynamics and force generation in similar cellular structures. Future force spectroscopy measurements using reconstituted actin filaments and cofilin will be valuable in further exploring the mechanism proposed here.

Conclusion.

In summary, we demonstrate myosin-II independent and actin-based cyclic contractility in nanotubes pulled out of axons. Axons form a convenient system for quantitative force analysis as they lack any significant motility that can corrupt the force data. This allows us to make comparisons between the statistical properties of experimentally and theoretically obtained load and fail responses. The ADF/cofilin based mechanisms we have proposed here can be further tested using in vitro systems using optical tweezers. To what extent such mechanisms are involved in actual filopodial dynamics is also an area for further exploration.

Materials and Methods

Preparing Cell Culture Media.

L-15 medium (Gibco 21083-027) thickened with 6 mg/ml Methyl Cellulose (Colorcon, ID34516) and supplemented with 10% (v/v) Fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10100-147), 6 mg/mL D-Glucose (Sigma G6152), 20 ng/ml Nerve Growth Factor (NGF-7s, 13290-010, Thermo Fisher Sci.) and 10 µl/ml Penicillin-Streptomycin-Glutamine (Gibco 10378-016).

Neuronal Cell Culture.

Chicken eggs were incubated for 8 to 9 d at 37 °C, then the embryo from the egg was dissected in HBSS buffer with Ca++/Mg++ (Gibco, 14025-092) for isolation of Dorsal Root Ganglia (DRG). The extracted DRGs were incubated in 0.25 % Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 15400-054) at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were isolated using centrifugation and the dissociated cells were resuspended in 100 µL OPTI-MEM 1X solution (Gibco, 31985-062) by gentle pipetting. The suspended cells were then plated on glass cover-slips attached to punched 35 mm petri dishes with 2 mL of prewarmed cell culture media and incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h. The glass cover slips were thoroughly cleaned by sonication in a detergent solution (Extran, MA03 Merck, Millipore), rinsed several times with Millipore deionized water, dried, sterilized under UV, and used without any further treatment. The cell culture medium was then replaced with culture medium lacking Methyl Cellulose 30 min before the start of the experiments.

Electroporation method was used for transfecting the cells with external plasmids or siRNA. To do the transfection with plasmid, before culturing the cells 10 µg of plasmid/s of interest is added to the dissociated cell suspension (in 100 µL OPTI-MEM) and electroporated using super electroporator (NEPA GENE, NEPA21 Type II). For siRNA (ADF-1—5′-GUGGAAGAAGGCAAAGAGAUU-3′, Lac Z—5′-CGUCGACGGAAUACUUCGAUU-3′, Integrated DNA Technology) transfection, it was added to dissociated cell suspension in 30 nM concentration before electroporation.

Lifeact-mCherry and pCAG-actin-GFP was used for fluorescence imaging of actin inside membrane nanotubes. Fluorescent mutant of ADF S3A and ADF S3E was not available so it was mixed with empty vector GFP (pCAG-GFP) as a marker for transfected cells. siRNA-ADF was used to inhibit ADF production in the cell and siRNA-LacZ (scrambled RNA) was used as control. GFP-tubulin was used for tagging microtubule.

Pulling Membrane Nano-Tubes from Axons.

A home built optical tweezers was used for nanotube pulling and force measurement experiments. The setup consisted of a 1W CW DPSS Nd:YAG Laser (Laser Quantum, Ventus 1064+MPC6000) and an Olympus IX71 microscope with a UplanFL N 100X/1.3 Oil objective and custom fitted with IR-reflecting dichroics—one to reflect the laser light into the objective and another to detect the backscattered laser light via a side port. The microscope was equipped for fluorescence imaging using an EMCCD camera (Andor, iXon DU-885K). Carboxylated polystyrene beads (Spherotech, 2.8 µm) were trapped and used for pulling membrane nanotubes. A motorized XY positioning stage (PRIOR Scientific, H117) was used to displace the sample with respect to the fixed laser trap. The sample chamber was maintained at 37 °C using a home-developed electric incubator. Displacement of a trapped bead from the fixed center of the trap was measured by imaging the backscattered light on to a Quadrant Photodiode coupled to preamplifiers (OSI Optoelectronics, Spot-9DMI) and read into a computer using a 14 bit DAQ card (National Instruments, USB-6009). Trap calibration was done using the standard Power Spectrum method at different laser powers. The trap stiffness at the 90% laser power (used during experiment) was 100 ± 16 and 90 ± 14 pN/µm along X and Y axes respectively. For some experiments a glass microneedle with motorized micromanipulator (Sutter Instruments) mounted on a Leica SP8 confocal microscope was used to pull nano-tubes. Alternatively, nano-tubes were extracted using optical tweezers and the bead was made to stick nonspecifically to the coverslip by lowering the trap position and then transferred to the confocal microscope for fluorescence imaging of actin dynamics.

Drug Treatments to Cells.

All the drugs were dissolved in DMSO, and used with following concentrations. 1 µM Latrunculin A (Sigma-Aldrich, L5163) to depolymerize f-actin, 10 µM Jasplakinolide (Invitrogen, J7473) to stabilize f-actin, 20 µM Blebbistatin (Sigma-Aldrich, B0560) to inhibit myosin II activity, 50 µM MyoVin-1 (CALBIOCHEM, 475984) to inhibit myosin V activity, 40 µM FASCIN-G2 (Xcess Biosciences, M60269-2S) to inhibit actin cross-linking, 20 µM SMIFH2 (Sigma-Aldrich, S4826) to inhibit formin activity, 50 µM CK-666 (Sigma-Aldrich, SML0006) to inhibit Arp 2/3 activity, 16.6 µM Nocodazole (Sigma-Aldrich, M1404) to depolymerize microtubule, 10 µM Y27632 (Sigma-Aldrich, Y0503) as Rho kinase inhibitor, 20 µM ML-7 (sigma-aldrich, I2764) as a myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) inhibitor. All controls were vehicle (DMSO) controls. Cells were exposed to the drug for about 20 min prior to tether pulling.

ATP depletion was performed by exposing the cells to a medium containing L-15 + 10 mM Sodium azide (Riedel-de Haën 13412) + 10 mM 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (Sigma D6134) for 30 min, and then tethers were extracted.

Statistical Analysis.

As sample sizes were different and variance may not be the same, Welch’s t test was performed for statistical significance. One-tailed Welch’s t test was performed to check the decrease in peak height and increase in peak duration when cells were transfected with ADF-S3E, the obtained P values were 0.00013 and 0.0057 respectively. Two-tailed Welch’s t test was performed to check any change in peak height and peak duration when cells were transfected with ADF-S3A, the obtained P values were 0.0163 and 0.0831 respectively Statistical significance was assessed at α=0.01.
