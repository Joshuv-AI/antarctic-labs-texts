Evaluation of GFAP, UCH-L1 and S100B Blood Biomarkers for the Management of Sports-Related Concussion in Professional Rugby Players: A Prospective Observational Study
Authors: Charlotte Oris, Rémi Gaulmin, Julie Durif, Vincent Goddelière, Bruno Pereira, Damien Bouvier, Vincent Sapin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of sports-related concussion (SRC) in rugby has been rising in recent years. The Head Injury Assessment (HIA) protocol, used to manage SRC, is limited by its reliance on subjective symptom reporting. Blood biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1) and S100B could serve as complementary objective tools for SRC management.ResultsA prospective cohort study was conducted in a French elite professional rugby team (Association Montferrandaise Clermont Auvergne Sports, Association Sportive Montferrandaise Clermont Auvergne). A total of 68 professional rugby players were followed from September 2022 to June 2024. During this study period, 23 SRC episodes with blood samples collected according to the HIA protocol were included. S100B baseline levels remained stable across the season in control players. For GFAP and UCH-L1, all baseline values were consistently below the measurable range limits (GFAP < 30 ng/L and UCH-L1 < 200 ng/L). At 2 h post-match (in the absence of SRC), both S100B and UCH-L1 levels increased significantly and were positively correlated with contact frequency, whereas GFAP levels showed no significant change at this early timepoint. At the 36-h post-match follow-up, concentrations of all three biomarkers had returned to baseline, supporting the use of this timepoint within the validated HIA protocol. At 36 h following SRC, UCH-L1 concentrations remained below the quantifiable range, suggesting that earlier sampling may be required to capture post-injury changes for this biomarker. At this timepoint, an increase in S100B of ≥ 15% from baseline yielded 100% specificity (SP) and 39% sensitivity (SE) for SRC diagnosis. Elevated GFAP concentrations (≥30 ng/L) were observed in some players with SRC, providing 100% SP and 17% SE. Combining S100B and GFAP maintained 100% SP while improving SE to 52%.ConclusionAt 36 h post-match, both GFAP and S100B demonstrated 100% SP for the diagnosis of SRC in professional rugby players. Their integration into routine HIA assessments may offer objective support to clinical decision-making and help enhance player safety.

Background

Sports-related concussion (SRC) is defined as a traumatic brain injury caused by an impulsive force transmitted to the brain during sports or exercise, resulting in transient neurological symptoms that commonly resolve within days and with normal standard neuroimaging [1]. Rugby, a globally popular full-contact sport, has been identified as having a high rate of SRCs [2]. In recent years, the incidence of reported concussions in rugby players has increased significantly, from approximately 4–13 per 1000 player-match-hours [3].

While SRCs can lead to short-term impairments, such as reduced cognitive function and athletic performance, they can also result in serious long-term consequences [4]. Repeated concussions, and even sub-concussive impacts, have been linked to neurocognitive decline, behavioral changes, premature dementia, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy [4]. To reduce these risks, improved diagnostic and management strategies are essential. World Rugby has therefore introduced the Head Injury Assessment (HIA) protocol for the management of SRC in elite professional teams [5]. This protocol, currently based on the 6th edition of the Sport Concussion Assessment Tool (SCAT6) [1], is a three-step process designed to identify, evaluate, and manage head impacts that may result in concussion. The three key stages include: an initial assessment immediately after the injury (HIA-1), a second assessment within around three hours of the injury (HIA-2), and a follow-up assessment 36–48 h later (HIA-3). A diagnosis of SRC is established if concussion criteria are met at any stage of this process [5]. If a concussion is diagnosed, the Graduated Return-to-Play (GRTP) protocol must then be initiated to ensure a safe and stepwise return to training or competition [6]. The implementation of this approach has significantly improved the management of concussed players in elite rugby [6].

However, both the HIA and GRTP protocols rely on subjective symptom reporting, which may introduce variability and increase the risk of symptom under-reporting [7–9]. The use of brain-specific blood biomarkers, such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1) and S100B, may help address these limitations by providing objective information. These biomarkers, recently incorporated into the definition of mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) and validated for routine use in patients with mTBI [10], could enhance the accuracy of SRC assessment and follow-up [7, 11]. Nevertheless, their use within the specific context of professional rugby and the HIA protocol remains poorly defined. In particular, the variations of these biomarkers following a match without SRC, as well as their diagnostic value during SRC follow-up, remain insufficiently understood. Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the evolution of GFAP, UCH-L1, and S100B concentrations in professional rugby players throughout the HIA protocol and to evaluate their potential utility for SRC diagnosis. We hypothesized that players with SRC would exhibit persistent alterations in these biomarkers beyond the changes induced by routine match exposure, and that combining multiple biomarkers could improve the objective identification of SRC and thereby support safer and more individualized return-to-play (RTP) decisions.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Study Design

A prospective observational cohort study was conducted over two consecutive seasons to evaluate the potential utility of blood biomarkers (GFAP, UCH-L1 and S100B) for the management of SRC in players from a French elite professional rugby team. The study was conducted at Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital in collaboration with the Association Montferrandaise Clermont Auvergne Sports (Association Sportive Montferrandaise Clermont Auvergne), Clermont-Ferrand, France, over the 2022–2023 and 2023–2024 professional rugby seasons. Players were categorized into two groups: control players and concussed (at least one SRC diagnosed during the study period) players. SRCs were diagnosed by a nationally certified neurological referent, appointed by the Medical Committee of the French National Rugby League, in accordance with World Rugby’s three-stage HIA diagnostic protocol [5]. This protocol involves a multimodal assessment comprising a clinical examination supported by a symptom checklist, cognitive tests (orientation, concentration, immediate and delayed memory) and a balance assessment. All HIA results were reviewed by a physician officially designated as a national concussion expert by the French Rugby Federation, with recognized expertise in SRC management and HIA procedures. An SRC diagnosis was confirmed only if at least one of the three assessments (immediately pitch-side [HIA-1], 3 h post-impact [HIA-2], or 36–48 h post-impact [HIA-3]) was positive. If all three evaluations were negative, the case was not classified as an SRC.

The study design involved the inclusion of both control and concussed players during season 1, while only concussed players were enrolled during season 2, with a focus exclusively on the post-injury blood biomarker response. Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for ethical medical research involving human subjects. The project was approved by our local Ethics Committee (IRB00013412, “CHU de Clermont Ferrand IRB #1”, IRB number 2022-CF076) with compliance to the French policy of individual data protection. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study.

S100B Assay

Blood samples were collected into serum gel separator tubes, allowed to clot for 30–60 min at room temperature, and then centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Samples were anonymized using a unique participant identifier to ensure confidentiality. Serum samples were aliquoted into cryo-tubes (minimum volume of 500µL per cryo-tubes) and stored at –80°C until analysis. S100B concentrations were measured by electro-chemiluminescence immunoassay on a Cobas e411® analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France). This sandwich immunoassay has a total assay time of 18 min and an analytical measuring range of 0.005–39 µg/L. According to the manufacturer, repeatability coefficients of variation ranged from 1.5% to 2.1%, and intermediate precision coefficients of variation ranged from 2.6% to 2.9% on the Cobas e411® analyzer for the two internal quality control levels. Samples were measured in single determinations using this fully automated routine clinical assay.

GFAP and UCH-L1 Assay

Blood samples were collected into gel separator tubes for EDTA plasma and centrifuged within 10 min (2000g at 4 °C). The blood samples were also anonymized with a unique participant identifier ensuring participant confidentiality. Plasma samples were aliquoted into cryo-tubes (with a minimum volume of 100 µL per cryo-tubes) and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Plasma concentrations of GFAP and UCH-L1 were measured using i-STAT® TBI Plasma cartridges (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL, USA) on the i-STAT® Alinity analyzer. The cartridge required 20 µL of plasma and provided results in approximately 15 min. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) is 23.0 ng/L for GFAP and 70.0 ng/L for UCH-L1. However, the measurable ranges defined by the manufacturer begin at 30 ng/L for GFAP and 200 ng/L for UCH-L1, with upper measurable limits of 10,000 ng/L and 3200 ng/L, respectively. The manufacturer previously established decision thresholds of 30 ng/L for GFAP and 360 ng/L for UCH-L1, to optimize the balance between diagnostic sensitivity and analytical reliability, while ensuring robust performance for identifying intracranial lesions in patients with mTBI. Both GFAP and UCH-L1 assays were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. Samples were measured in single determinations using this fully automated routine clinical assay.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR), where Q1 and Q3 represent the first and third quartiles, respectively. The assumption of normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To compare groups, Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were satisfied; otherwise, Mann–Whitney tests were applied. Homogeneity of variances was assessed using the Fisher–Snedecor test. Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. Furthermore, for each biomarker, the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was estimated using a random-effects model (more precisely linear mixed model) in order to evaluate the effect of rugby on biomarker values over a season in controls and concussed players. The results were interpreted according to the thresholds reported in the literature [12]. In addition to the estimation of ICC, the comparisons between timepoints evaluation were performed using random-effects model. These analyses were completed by multivariable regression models with age, BMI, skin pigmentation and pitch position as adjustment covariates to take into account possible confounding factors. When appropriate, the normality of residuals from these models was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When the assumptions of normality were not met, logarithmic transformations of the dependent variables were performed before analysis. The relationships between continuous variables (i.e., between baseline S100B levels and demographic characteristics, match data, skin pigmentation, and player position in control players; between S100B and UCH-L1 biomarkers with match data (number of contacts and playing time) at H2 and H36 post-match) were explored using correlation coefficients (Pearson or Spearman, according to the statistical distribution). Finally, to evaluate the discriminative ability of serum measurements 36 h after the end of the match, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for was plotted according to the dichotomous variables concussed or non-concussed as per the HIA protocol. To determine the most appropriate threshold, several indexes (Youden, Liu, and Efficiency) were estimated according to the recommendations and to the clinical relevance. Then, sensitivity and specificity were estimated. Because some players experienced more than one SRC during the study period, mixed-effects models were additionally performed by considering the player as a random-effect in order to account for the non-independence of repeated observations and to model both between- and within-player variability. The intra-class correlation coefficient related to the player effect was low, suggesting a limited impact of clustering on the study findings. In addition, a sensitivity analysis excluding players with multiple SRCs was performed to assess the robustness of the results. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software, Version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, US). The tests were two-sided, with a Type I error set at 5%. Secondary analyses were considered exploratory; therefore, confidence intervals were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted cautiously. Emphasis was placed on the magnitude and clinical relevance of observed effects, particularly through effect size estimates such as correlation coefficients and ICC values.

Biomarkers Values in Control Players

For S100B, the median concentration at BC was 0.055 µg/L (IQR: 0.038–0.086 µg/L), compared to 0.24 µg/L (IQR: 0.14–0.29 µg/L) at H2 and 0.050 µg/L (IQR: 0.035–0.081 µg/L) at H36. Discussion

This study highlights the potential value of blood biomarkers, particularly S100B and GFAP, in improving the management of SRC in professional rugby players. The HIA protocol remains the gold standard for SRC diagnosis, but like the GRTP protocol, it is limited by its reliance on some subjective symptom reporting. A recent study found that ~ 20% of elite rugby league players in Australia failed to report concussion-related symptoms during the 2018 and 2019 seasons [9]. In other contact sports, under-reporting rates have been reported to range from 20 to 80% [9, 13–16]. The predominant rationale provided by athletes is their desire to avoid missing game time or training sessions [9]. Incorporating objective physiological measures, such as the determination of blood biomarkers levels, into routine assessment could help address these challenges. The availability of automated assays for individual and rapid determinations of GFAP, UCH-L1 and S100B further supports their potential integration into clinical practice [17].

S100B showed low intra-individual variability throughout the season in control players, with samples collected three times during the season, away from any physical activity. Baseline concentrations were independent of age, weight, size, or pitch position, consistent with previous findings [11]. The only significant association observed was between S100B concentration and skin pigmentation, linked to melanocyte activity [17]. Furthermore, baseline S100B levels were unaffected by the total number of matches played, contact frequency, or cumulative playing time over the season. In concussed players, the ICC value suggested lower stability of S100B levels over time. However, this apparent variability must be interpreted with caution, as the small sample size in this subgroup (n = 10) limits the reliability of the ICC estimate. Importantly, the absence of a significant difference in S100B concentrations across the season indicates that S100B levels show limited variation, even in previously concussed players. This is consistent with our previous study conducted in concussed rugby players [11]. These findings are also concordant with studies in professional rugby players [18, 19] which reported stable S100B concentrations over time. Taken together, these results suggest that S100B levels remain stable, supporting its potential use as a biomarker for SRC assessment. This stability contrasts with the transient increase observed after a single match (without SRC), where S100B levels increased immediately after the match, correlating with physical activity and contact frequency, before returning to baseline within 36 h due to its short half-life [20]. As demonstrated in previous studies, the 36-h post-match timepoint (corresponding to the third step of the HIA protocol for concussed players) is the optimal time to measure S100B for SRC diagnosis, as it minimizes confounding effects related to physical activity and extra-cerebral release [11, 21]. Personalized interpretation based on the PCH36% calculation is also required to reduce well established inter-individual variability and improve the diagnostic accuracy of S100B [11, 21]. In our study, a ≥ 15% increase in S100B at 36 h post-match compared to individual baseline identified SRC with 100% SP and approximately 40% SE. This study is a logical continuation of our preliminary previous study [21], evaluating the diagnostic performance of S100B for SRC in professional rugby players with a cohort including only five SRC cases. In the present study, based on a larger cohort of 23 SRC cases, the results are more robust and further support the diagnostic value of S100B in this context. To our knowledge, aside from these two studies, no other research has assessed the diagnostic performance of S100B in professional rugby players, making these findings particularly relevant and valuable to the rugby community.

An automated assay (i-STAT® Alinity) was used to measure GFAP and UCH-L1, enabling simultaneous detection of both biomarkers and supporting their potential use in the acute evaluation of mTBI [22]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply this method in contact sport athletes, and more particularly in rugby. Across the full competitive season, all baseline values remained below the lower limits of the measurable ranges (GFAP < 30 ng/L and UCH-L1 < 200 ng/L), indicating stable concentrations and absence of detectable individual variability for both biomarkers. Because of these low baseline levels, only semi-quantitative interpretation was possible, excluding the calculation of percentage changes from individual baselines. By contrast to S100B, GFAP and UCH-L1 were independent of skin pigmentation. As a result, GFAP and UCH-L1 were interpreted without individualized adjustment, which may be more practical than the approach required for S100B. At 2 h post-match (in the absence of SRC), UCH-L1 levels increased significantly, similarly to S100B, and correlated with physical activity and contact frequency, before returning to undetectable levels at 36 h. In contrast, GFAP levels remained below 30 ng/L at both 2 and 36 h post-match, suggesting a delay kinetic profile or a higher neuro-specificity compared to UCH-L1 and S100B. Our findings are consistent with those of Bazarian et al. [23], who reported a very acute increase in UCH-L1 levels 45 min after exercise, while GFAP remained unchanged. Their previous study involved 30 male collegiate American football players performing a combined aerobic and resistance workout, in the absence of any head impacts [23]. More recent data have confirmed this pattern for UCH-L1, with elevated concentrations observed 4 h post-exercise, whereas GFAP levels did not increase [24]. The distinct behavior of GFAP compared to S100B and UCH-L1 cannot be attributed to differences in cellular origin, as both GFAP and S100B are astroglial markers, whereas UCH-L1 is neuronal. Taken together, these observations support the rapid and transient kinetic profile of UCH-L1 and the more delayed or impact-specific response of GFAP, providing important context for interpreting biomarker levels at the H36 evaluation point. At 36 h following SRC, all UCH-L1 concentrations were below the quantifiable range, whereas several concussed players showed GFAP concentrations ≥ 30 ng/L. Using this threshold, GFAP alone identified SRC with 100% specificity (SP) and 17% sensitivity (SE). When combined with S100B, sensitivity increased to 52%, highlighting the added value of a multi-biomarker strategy. Finally, within our study design, H36 emerged as the most relevant post-match timepoint for interpreting GFAP and UCH-L1 in relation to SRC. Importantly, H36 is not necessarily the optimal biological window, but it is the only evaluation point available after H2 within the current HIA schedule. H36 represents a kinetic constraint specific to rugby, imposed by the structure and timing of the HIA protocol (to include delayed onset concussion without symptoms at H2). In other sports, or in studies using different sampling kinetics, the optimal timepoint for biomarker interpretation could differ [25].

Our findings suggest that combining S100B and GFAP measurements within the HIA protocol may improve the objective assessment of SRC and potentially contribute to player safety. Given their high specificity in our cohort, an increase in either biomarker may represent a biological signal that should be considered as part of the clinical assessment. In our cohort of 23 concussion cases, 7 players were asymptomatic at 36 h, yet 3 had elevated biomarker levels (S100B > 15% and/or GFAP ≥ 30 ng/L), suggesting ongoing biological changes despite clinical resolution of symptoms. These observations support cautious and individualized clinical management to avoid premature return to play. This study has several limitations. Although the sample size is limited, our cohort of professional rugby players represents a valuable and relevant population for the study of concussion in elite sport. Prospective studies evaluating blood biomarkers in elite rugby players are inherently difficult to conduct because they require longitudinal follow-up and repeated sampling, while only a limited proportion of players will sustain a concussion during follow-up. This explains both the limited cohort sizes and the small number of published studies in this field [11, 18, 19, 21]. Despite this limited sample size, no false-positive results were observed in our cohort, notably with S100B alone and with the combined S100B/GFAP approach. Although these findings require confirmation in larger cohorts, the absence of false positives represents an important signal supporting high specificity.

Another limitation is the analytical performance of GFAP and UCH-L1 measurements using the i-STAT® Alinity platform limiting direct comparison to individual baseline values. As baseline values for GFAP and UCH-L1 were consistently below the measurable range of the assay (< 30 ng/L and < 200 ng/L, respectively), interpretation of these biomarkers remained semi-quantitative. This limitation may explain the differences observed in the diagnostic performance of GFAP and UCH-L1 for SRC when compared with studies using high-sensitivity platforms [27, 28], which report much lower limits of quantification (0.47 ng/L for GFAP and 5.45 ng/L for UCH-L1) [27]. In contrast, the i-STAT® Alinity only reports values above 30 ng/L for GFAP and 200 ng/L for UCH-L1, potentially missing lower-level elevations and therefore reducing sensitivity for SRC diagnosis. However, these high-sensitivity systems are not automated and are not suited for routine clinical use, unlike the i-STAT® Alinity. In addition, the recent development of whole blood determination using the i-STAT® Alinity could be useful to democratize these blood biomarkers in the professional sport environment (pitch, training center…). Lastly, although post-SRC samples were collected at 36 h in accordance with the third time of the HIA protocol, additional sampling at earlier timepoints (e.g., 4, 8, 16, and 24 h post-injury) would provide a more detailed kinetic profile. Previous studies have highlighted multiple relevant timepoints for GFAP sampling after SRC, notably the acute phase (< 6 h), as well as 16–24 and 24–48 h post-injury [27–30]. The acute phase appears particularly informative [27, 28, 30], reflecting the early release of GFAP following injury, while among later time windows, the 16- to 24-h period remains clinically relevant, with GFAP levels generally higher than at more delayed timepoints, at which diagnostic accuracy may be reduced [29]. For UCH-L1, evidence from a large collegiate cohort indicates a modest but significant acute elevation compared with baseline (mean difference ~ 0.5 ng/L), with levels returning to baseline at 24–48 h. This transient pattern highlights the need for early sampling and appropriate analytical sensitivity to capture transient elevations in neuronal injury biomarkers [28]. In this context, it will be useful to evaluate measurements within 12 h of injury, consistent with Abbott’s recommendations for the management of mTBI.

Conclusion

Our study highlights, for the first time, the potential value of blood biomarkers such as S100B and GFAP, when measured at the regulatory steps of the HIA protocol, to improve the management of SRC in professional rugby players. The integration of these biomarkers into routine assessment may provide objective data to guide clinical decisions and enhance player safety, although these findings require confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.
