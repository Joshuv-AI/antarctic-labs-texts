Effects of visual real-time feedback during decline walking on gait parameters: a crossover randomized trial
Authors: Klaus Widhalm, Sebastian Durstberger, Peter Putz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeveloping osteoarthritis is influenced by the control of dynamic functional leg alignment and biomechanical load. While research on level walking with load-related feedback has sparked treatment innovations, there is limited knowledge about the effects of feedback during downhill walking on gait kinematics and kinetics. The potential of external feedback for neuromuscular knee control is unexplored. We examined if real-time feedback affects frontal knee range of motion and load ratio during downhill walking on a treadmill compared to self-paced and matched speed walking.MethodsTwenty-eight healthy participants aged 18 to 35 years performed gait trials with 10° declination on an instrumented treadmill under four conditions: (a) self-paced walking, (b) self-paced walking with an internal focus of attention, (c) self-paced walking with real-time feedback, and (d) speed-matched C without feedback. Main outcomes were compared by repeated-measure ANOVA with predefined contrasts.ResultsSignificant differences were found for the left frontal knee range of motion and both side load ratios, with moderate to large effect sizes. Predefined contrasts revealed that any feedback improved left knee stabilization when compared to no feedback at self-paced walking. Real-time feedback reduced load ratio even more than in the internal focus of attention.ConclusionInterventions that facilitate internal focus of attention and/or real-time feedback may improve frontal knee range of motion and load ratio, indicating an enhanced stabilization of frontal leg alignment. Real-time feedback is preferable to internal focus for decreasing load ratio. Future studies should explore these findings for populations with low physical activity and mild osteoarthritis.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT04763850. Background

Symptomatic osteoarthritis (OA) still is a growing burden in European adults with an estimated prevalence of 3–9% for the over 19 years old population and up to 9–15% in the over 60 years ones [1]. Biomechanical loading of joints has been acknowledged as one main facilitator in the development of knee OA and an effective dosing parameter in the conservative therapy of OA [2, 3]. Only a limited number of studies have demonstrated altered gait parameters in patients with OA during incline walking [4] or differences in kinetic parameters with increasing inclinations in healthy subjects [5]. However, for the musculoskeletal system declined walking is even more demanding, as decelerating body mass results in higher vertical ground reaction forces [6–8]. In particular, increasing declination causes higher joint compression forces at knee and hip joints [9]. Additionally, for the frontal plane, larger mediolateral load distribution excursions and loading velocities have been reported, which are associated with increased knee valgus/varus kinematics [10]. Valgus thrust with a maximum of up to 6° was observed in healthy subjects performing level walking (LW) [11, 12]. Therefore, valgus thrust might be even more present in downhill walking due to higher deceleration forces and an inadequate dynamic control of the functional leg alignment. However, such an increased frontal knee range of motion (fKROM) in analogy to Farrokhi et al. [10] might induce shifted joint compression forces. In this case, the lateral knee compartment is exposed to higher local pressure than the medial one at the initial contact [13].

Given the importance of vertical loading, frontal load distribution, and neuromuscular control in the prevention and treatment of knee osteoarthritis (OA) [14, 15], feedback represents an effective approach for supporting patients [16, 17]. Real-time feedback has been shown to be effective with a moderate retention effect when vertical ground reaction force was provided as parameter in patients following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction [16].

Studies on uphill and downhill walking have been primarily conducted using instrumented ramps [6, 18]. However, few investigations have been carried out on tiltable treadmills with embedded force plates [4, 19, 20]. Mostly results of those studies lack comparability due to the use of different kinetic measurement technologies.

To the best of our knowledge, the effect of motor control interventions such as internal focus of attention [21] and function-targeted real-time feedback [22] on the aforementioned biomechanical factors in declined walking has not been studied previously. Several studies on the effect of different types of real-time feedback during LW have been performed, but even weighted mean differences did not show distinct results concerning the most favorable presentation type [23]. Also, knowledge on the prioritization of internal or external focus of attention for feedback targeting gait modification is controversial and has only been studied in running [24, 25]. Therefore, this randomized four-period crossover trial was conducted with the objective of deepening the insights of the effects of real-time feedback on lower limb gait kinematics and kinetics in declined walking.

The primary research question was whether real-time feedback during declined walking alters the frontal knee range of motion compared to self-paced walking and matched-speed walking. The secondary aspect focused on the effect of lower limb loading during decline walking. For both outcomes, predefined contrasts null-hypothesized that (1) self-paced walking with real-time feedback is equal to speed-matched walking (C = D), (2) self-paced walking with internal focus of attention, and self-paced walking with real-time feedback is equal to self-paced walking (BC = A), (3) self-paced walking with real-time feedback is equal to self-paced walking with internal focus of attention (C = B).

Methods

Study design

This trial was performed at the University of Applied Sciences Campus Vienna (Vienna, Austria) virtual reality lab. Three out of four conditions (self-paced walking, self-paced walking with internal focus of attention, and self-paced walking with real-time feedback) were tested under block randomization of their six possible permutations (ABC, BCA, CAB, CBA, ACB, BAC). Speed-matched walking (condition four) was excluded from the sequence randomization and always was performed following the corresponding self-paced real-time feedback condition. The numbers of participants screened for eligibility, allocated to assessments, and analyzed were recorded by a modified CONSORT flow chart [26] without considering losses to follow-up. Fig. 1 illustrates a flowchart of the trial design.

Ethical approval was obtained via the ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna, Austria (2287/2020). The study was prospectively registered on 21 February 2021 at ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04763850. The full study protocol has been published prior to the conduct of the assessments [27].

Participants

Due to a lack of studies reporting estimates on the effect of real-time feedback during decline walking, the intended sample size was determined at 25 participants, based on the number of participants included in thematic studies, ranging from 14 to 40 [16, 28–30]. A total of 30 participants were planned for recruitment, with a conservative estimate of 15% dropout rate included. Thirty (30) healthy participants aged 18 to 35 years with a body mass index of 18.5 to 29.99 kg/m2, no chronic joint disease, no OA surgery, and no neuro-motor disease were included. Participants with non-physiological or non-symmetrical gait patterns were excluded from the analysis.

Students of a physiotherapy program were recruited and gave written informed consent prior to the assessments. Following, the principal investigator assessed them for possible physical limitations regarding the intervention using a structured health status interview protocol. Sagittal lower limb joint range of motion and functional hamstring flexibility were also assessed as part of the screening for eligibility. Participants were randomly assigned according to a prepared sequence list of the tasks as per protocol [27]. Blinding of participants or researchers was not possible.

Intervention

Initially, body height and inter-anterior superior iliac spine distance as required for the biomechanical model were assessed. Following, retroreflective markers for modelling the Upper Body Plug-In-Gait Marker Set and the Modified Cleveland Clinical Marker Set were applied [31].

Intervention took place on the GRAIL system (Motek Medical, Houten, Netherlands) with participants being secured with a ceiling-mounted safety harness. After an instruction of the treadmill’s self-paced mode, they had a familiarization phase at a 10° declination on the treadmill. Thereafter, three tasks were performed in the order given in the random-sequence list: self-paced decline walking (Condition A), self-paced decline walking with internal focus of attention (Condition B) and self-paced decline walking with real-time feedback (Condition C) followed by speed-matched decline walking (Condition D).

All conditions contained an instruction of the particular task [27], a five-minute familiarization period and a three-minute measurement period. Three ten-second periods of self-paced walking were recorded during the measurement phases. Participants´ movement behavior was continuously observed for arbitrary changes in gait pattern during the intervention. Additionally, they were asked on a regular basis concerning their wellbeing (e.g., blistering of the heel or physical exhaustion).

Outcome parameters

According to the study protocol [27], biometric and functional measures were clinically assessed. Biomechanical parameters were processed using the hip joint center estimation according to Davis [32]. The primary outcome fKROM during the first 30% of the gait cycle and the secondary outcome ground reaction force-loading ratio for the loading response phase (LR), calculated from heel strike to the first peak of the vertical ground reaction force after applying a 12 Hz Butterworth lowpass filter, were calculated for both the right and left leg. Deviating from the protocol, due to dFLA control challenges during the loading-response phase which is the relevant aspect concerning the research question, the fKROM was not calculated for the whole stance-phase. The detailed processing procedure was pre-specified in the related study protocol [27].

Statistical methods

All metric parameters were analyzed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as mean and standard deviation (sd). Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspections of Q-Q plots. Regarding the primary and secondary hypotheses, differences between the four conditions were tested using one-factorial analysis of variance with repeated measures (rmANOVA). The sphericity assumption was tested with Mauchly’s test. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied in case of a violation of the sphericity assumption. Contrasts were used to test the prespecified hypotheses listed under ‘Objectives’ above. Overall rmANOVA results were reported as test statistic F, p-value, and partial eta² (ηp²) as effect size interpreted according to Cohen’s scheme, where 0.01 represents a small, 0.06 a medium, and 0.14 a large effect [33]. For contrast analyses, the point-biserial correlation (r) was calculated as effect size. In an additional exploratory analysis, one-dimensional statistical parametric mapping (1dSPM) was used to test the predefined contrasts for the parameters fKROM and vertical ground reaction force (GRFv) continuously throughout the gait cycle [34]. The Bonferroni-Holm method was to correct for multiple testing. Tests other than those related to the primary and secondary hypotheses are considered exploratory and are consequently exempted from the multiple testing correction [27]. Hence, the contrast with the smallest p-value was considered statistically significant when p < alpha/3 (i.e. 0.017), and the contrast with the second smallest p-value was considered statistically significant when p < alpha/2 (i.e. 0.025). To facilitate the parametric analysis in a homogeneous sample, participants with non-physiological and non-symmetric gait patterns were excluded from the analysis. Further, severe outliers (more than two sd) on outcome parameters were excluded according to the assumption that 95% of the normally distributed parameters will also preserve natural variability. As this two-times standard deviation approach is commonly accepted in biomechanics, but missing sufficient evidence compared to recently developed more complex methods [35, 36], we ran (deviating from the protocol) a secondary analysis with the whole dataset.

Effect modification by the gait characteristics step width, cadence, and walking speed was explored by Pearson correlations for fKROM and LR. Condition A versus B, as well as C versus D, were tested for possible differences in LR by means of t-tests. Two-tailed testing was applied. Alpha was set at 0.05 and exact p-values were reported. A post hoc-power analysis was run in G*Power 3.1.9.7 on the overall repeated measures ANOVAs, based on the observed effect size, one group, four measurements, no correction for nonsphericity, and alpha set at 0.05. The results thereof were reported as post-hoc power and sample size required to achieve a power of 0.8.

Results

Sixteen (16) female and fourteen (14) male healthy physiotherapy students with a mean age of 23.9 (sd: 3.2) years, body weight of 66.97 (sd: 9.98) kg, and a height of 1.73 (sd: 0.09) m performed all conditions. For the primary outcome (fKROM) two participants, and for the secondary outcome (LR) three participants were excluded from the analysis because of outliers outside the two standard deviation range (Fig. 1). Analysis of contrasts (Table 2) revealed no significant differences in BC vs. A and C vs. D, representing negligible effect sizes. However, for left LR, the contrast comparing internal feedback with real-time feedback (B vs. C) showed a significant difference, representing a moderate effect. For right LR, a similar reduction (i.e., improvement) was observed in real-time feedback compared to internal focus of attention (C vs. B), representing a moderate effect.

In the real-time feedback condition (C), step width was greater, whereas cadence and walking speed were lower compared to the other three conditions (Table 1). An exploratory analysis showed non-significant weak-sized correlations (r < 0.3) of these gait characteristics with the fKROM. However, LR was strongly correlated (r = 0.71–0.79, p < 0.01) with walking speed. LR was therefore compared between the conditions A vs. B and C vs. D, where walking speeds were similar. This exploratory analysis revealed a significant difference for left LR between self-paced and internal focus condition ((27) = 2,12, p = 0.043) with a moderate effect (d = 0.40) and no significant difference for right LR (d = 0.28). Also, left and right LR of real-time feedback versus matched speed condition did not show significant differences with a low effect size (d < 0.1).

In the exploratory 1dSPM analysis, the degrees of fKROM differed significantly (p < 0.001) across the RTF, IFF, and SP conditions in the area covering about 70% − 90% of the gait cycle, with RTF resulting in the lowest values (Fig. 2). Peak vertical ground reaction forces were significantly lower in the RTF condition than in the IFF and MS conditions. However, vertical ground reaction forces were significantly higher in the RTF condition during midstance (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study was the first, investigating and comparing the effects on dynamic functional leg alignment of real-time feedback as an external focus of attention and feedback through an instructed internal focus of attention, as commonly used by physiotherapists, during downhill walking on a treadmill.

Albeit the null hypothesis was not rejected by the sample when applying a correction for multiple testing (right LR p = 0.041, r = 0.389), RTF was superior to IFF in reducing the forces exerted during the loading phase for both the left and right leg. Both types of feedback were highly effective in stabilizing the knee in the frontal plane during the loading phase (p = 0.001, r = 0.577) compared to walking without feedback. Surprisingly, this effect was observed only for the left fKROM. Looking at the 1dSPM plots for the fKROM during the stance phase, either no difference or an increase in valgus thrust was statistically detected during the loading response. Thus, during load acceptance, the knee is similarly stabilized in the frontal plane, but around the first GRFv peak, RTF seems to facilitate control of the dFLA, as by visual inspection is slightly reduced (Fig. 3).

However, the clinical relevance of these statistical results must be carefully interpreted, as the parameter fKROM solely appears to be limited in its ability to express dynamic leg alignment in concerns of measurement issues [37–39]. While the reported small values correspond to the limited frontal movement of 11° in healthy knees [40], a minimal detectable change (MDC95) of 7.19° in hypermobile and an inter-session variability of 1.89° in a normal knee population was reported [41]. Also Schwartz et al. stated an inter-therapist error for frontal knee angles of 0.5° and 5° error for the inter-therapist to inter-trial ratio [42]. In a study investigating kinematics during treadmill running a MDC95 of 4.8° for frontal knee excursion was reported [43]. But it has to be pointed out that in this study fKROM was calculated for the relatively short loading phase, whereas most other research reports frontal knee motion for the entire stance phase or gait cycle.

A comparison of our results with those of existing studies is only possible to a limited extent, as fKRoM has not yet been assessed or published in decline walking, and no commonly proven and agreed physiological range for this parameter has been established. One study used similar direct kinematic feedback, in particular investigating the effect of knee adduction angle feedback during treadmill LW, with an average decrease in knee adduction angle of 2° [44]. In other thematic relevant research with focus on modifying frontal knee adduction moment it was shown that presenting real-time feedback on lateral trunk lean during level walking was effective [45], or also visualizing foot progression angle during treadmill walking [15]. Additionally, several studies have been performed with focus on modifying external knee adduction moment by verbal instructions on changing the individual gait pattern or varying step width [46], but kinematic data regarding effects on the dynamic leg alignment are not reported. So, the positive effect of real-time feedback in gait (re-)training is already well established, but to some extent the validation of outcome parameters feedbacked and joint forces being responsible for structural changes in cartilage are still missing. Taking the frontal knee angle into account, as well as joint motions in the hip and pelvis [47], it might be necessary to gain a deeper insight into shifts of joint moments and loads acting on the knee joint [48, 49]. The use of the most appropriate feedback should be considered in those studies [17, 29, 50].

Limitations

We observed a surprisingly well-stabilized dynamic functional leg alignment with small but systematic differences in fKROM during load acceptance during downhill walking, which might be partly due to an increased motor control level of the study population of physiotherapy students. Based on the strong correlation between LR and walking speed, the observed effect favoring RTF over IFF for LR is somewhat confounded by the slightly slower walking speed in the RTF condition (1.32 vs. 1.37 m/s). Furthermore, there was an increased step width in the RTF condition (0.098 m vs. 0.084 m) which may have been caused by a rather challenging visualization of the real-time feedback parameter. However, there were only weak correlations of the fKROM with step width, walking speed, and cadence, and thus the primary outcome (fKROM) should not have been substantially confounded by these discrepancies.

There might also be some motor-learning related limitations. The feedback interventions aimed to improve dFLA control during task execution, which is not comprehensive enough to sustainably improve dFLA control during walking [51]. Therefore, no washout periods were foreseen. The sequence of the tasks was randomized to avoid periodic effects. Another bias could be due to an above average level of motor control of participants, which might differ concerning the performance learning effect induced by the interventions compared to non-exercise proficient participants. In particular, the instructed IFF may not be equally effective in a population with less motor control capacity.

Conclusion

Our study provides novel insights into the feasibility of real-time feedback for dynamic functional leg alignment during declined walking. Internal focus of attention, as instructed by a physiotherapist, can help in improving the stabilization of functional leg alignment. Digital visual feedback applications can enhance the effectiveness of such guided training for the prevention or therapy of knee OA. These findings might help in the conceptualization of innovative exercise therapy for prevention and rehabilitation in subjects with increased valgus thrust. Future research should improve in strengthening clinical relevance and evaluate different types of real-time feedback visualizations for gait (re-)training in patient populations suffering from early-stage OA.
