Preoperative statin use and postoperative mortality in cardiac surgery patients: a retrospective cohort study of the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Xiu Zou, Jianlei Zheng, Jianxing Zhou, Hongqiang Qiu, Xuemei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of preoperative statin use on postoperative survival in cardiac surgery (CS) patients remains controversial. While some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported neutral or harmful effects, real-world evidence from large, granular databases like Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) can provide complementary insights into long-term outcomes and dose-response relationships. This study aimed to investigate the association between preoperative statin use and short- to medium-term postoperative mortality in cardiac surgery patients using a large, real-world database and to explore potential dose-response effects.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized the MIMIC-IV database [2008–2019]. We included adult patients (≥18 years) with an intensive care unit (ICU) stay of >24 hours undergoing their first CS. Patients were divided into preoperative statin users and non-users. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, while secondary outcomes were 30-, 90-, and 360-day mortality. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed, adjusting for demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, and comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, heart failure). Follow-up data were derived from hospital records and related death registry information.ResultsA total of 17,172 CS patients were finally included in the study. Among 17,172 CS patients, 3,647 (21.2%) received preoperative statins. Statin users were older and had a higher burden of comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes compared to non-users. In the fully adjusted model, preoperative statin use was associated with significantly lower in-hospital mortality rates [hazard ratio (HR) =0.708, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.622–0.807, P<0.001]. Similar associations were observed for 30-day (HR =0.826, P=0.001), 90-day (HR =0.829, P<0.001), and 360-day mortality rates (HR =0.884, P=0.002). Dose-response analysis revealed a more significant reduction in mortality rates with high-dose (>40 mg) atorvastatin.ConclusionsIn this large, real-world cohort, preoperative statin use was associated with reduced short- and medium-term mortality rates after CS, with a potential dose-dependent effect. However, given the conflicting evidence from RCTs, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Perioperative management should be individualized, and further research is necessary to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from statin therapy.Keywords: Statins, cardiac surgery (CS), postoperative mortality, Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV), atorvastatin dose-response

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study, based on 17,172 cardiac surgery (CS) patients from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, found that preoperative statin use was significantly associated with reduced postoperative short- and medium-term mortality. After multivariate adjustment, statin users had approximately 29% lower in-hospital mortality (hazard ratio =0.708, 95% confidence interval: 0.622–0.807), with more pronounced protective effects in patients with hyperlipidemia and diabetes. Dose-response analysis indicated that high-dose atorvastatin (>40 mg) further reduced in-hospital and long-term mortality risks. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings.

What is known and what is new?

• Statins are known to exert pleiotropic effects beyond lipid-lowering, including anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant, and endothelial-improving properties, but their impact on CS outcomes remains controversial.

• Using large-scale real-world data, this study confirms that preoperative statin use significantly lowers postoperative mortality and, for the first time in a MIMIC-IV cohort, reveals a more pronounced benefit in subgroups with hyperlipidemia and diabetes as well as a dose–response relationship for atorvastatin.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings provide high‑level evidence supporting the routine use of preoperative statins in CS, especially for patients with hyperlipidemia or diabetes.

• In clinical practice, statin dosing should be individualized based on patient characteristics, and standardized preoperative statin administration should be emphasized to improve surgical outcomes. Future research should further explore optimized strategies for different surgical types and statin agents.

Introduction

Despite advances in surgical techniques, anesthesia management, and perioperative monitoring, postoperative mortality after cardiac surgery (CS) remains a significant concern. The reported mortality rates are 2–5% for elective procedures and substantially higher for complex or emergent surgeries (1,2). Major risk factors include patient demographics, comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, renal dysfunction), and the type and urgency of the procedure. Postoperative complications such as infection, bleeding, arrhythmia, and multiple organ failure further increase the risk of mortality (3,4). Hence, improving perioperative management is persistently challenging in clinical settings. Current international guidelines [e.g., American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC), European Society of Cardiology (ESC)] emphasize comprehensive perioperative risk stratification and optimization but do not provide definitive recommendations on routine preoperative statin use for all patients. It is therefore imperative to conduct clinical studies on effective preoperative intervention strategies, including medication management, to improve patient outcomes. By inhibiting cholesterol synthesis to reduce low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), statin delays the progression of atherosclerosis (5-7). Recent studies have found multiple effects of statins other than lowering lipids, namely, their pleotropic effects (8,9). Statins can inhibit the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway to reduce inflammatory markers and alleviate vascular endothelial inflammation (10). Statins can decrease the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) to protect vascular endothelial cells (11). In addition, statins can inhibit macrophage infiltration, thus lowering the risk of vascular plaque rupture (12). Furthermore, statins can accelerate the release of nitric oxide (NO), relax blood vessels and inhibit platelet aggregation, thereby improving haemodynamics (9).

Although the potential benefits of statins have been supported by fundamental research, controversy persists regarding their effect on postoperative outcomes of CS patients. Observational studies showed a correlation between preoperative statin treatment and lower postoperative mortality, possibly because statins could stabilize plaque and reduce perioperative myocardial infarction (13). However, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reported inconsistent results. Some studies proved statins’ role in significantly reducing postoperative risks of atrial fibrillation (AF) and acute kidney injury (AKI) (14-17). On the other hand, some RCTs found that perioperative statins could not prevent postoperative AF or myocardial damage, and it was correlated with an elevated incidence of postoperative AKI (18). In a prospective RCT study targeting patients on coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), preoperative high-dose statin use failed to significantly reduce postoperative mortality or major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs). Instead, it is related to higher risks of abnormal kidney function (19). Moreover, observational studies found an increased postoperative risk of myopathy or cognitive disorder after statin use, possibly through inhibition of coenzyme Q10 synthesis (20,21). Current studies on the correlation between statins and the prognosis of CS are mostly small-sample RCTs, lacking long-term follow-up data of large-scale populations. Existing studies on statins and CS outcomes have notable limitations. Many are small, single-center RCTs with short follow-up, focusing primarily on CABG patients and short-term complications like AF or AKI. Evidence on long-term mortality and dose-response relationships in a broad, real-world CS population is scarce. The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database offers a unique opportunity to address these gaps. It is a large, contemporary, publicly available critical care database containing granular, time-stamped clinical data from over 40,000 CS patients, including detailed medication administration records, longitudinal laboratory results, and related mortality data for up to one year after discharge. It can be used to comprehensively analyze the association between preoperative statin use and short- to medium-term mortality, explore dose-response effects, and control for a wide array of potential confounders, thus providing real-world evidence to inform and complement the findings from RCTs. In this study, MIMIC-IV data were taken to analyze the correlation between preoperative statin use and postoperative mortality. The real-world study is expected to provide more comprehensive data for optimizing preoperative medication management strategies for CS. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0053/rc).

Methods

Data source

The MIMIC-IV database (version 2.2) is a large, publicly available critical care database comprising de-identified electronic health record data from patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center from 2008 to 2019. It integrates comprehensive clinical information across multiple domains, including patient demographics, vital sign measurements, laboratory test results, medication administration records, procedural codes, caregiver notes, mortality, and follow-up data from hospital records and related state and national death registries. Cohort extraction and data retrieval were performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) queries in PostgreSQL (version 16.4). Patients were identified as undergoing CS if they had corresponding International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision (ICD-9/10) procedure codes during their hospital stay. Having successively finished the online course titled “Protecting Human Research Participants” (record ID: 64278868) organized by National Institutes of Health, the author X.Z. was authorized to access data from the MIMIC-IV database. Owing to the de-identification of data, informed consent was not required. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study population

According to the ninth and tenth revisions of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9/10), patients who underwent CS during hospital stay and met the following criteria were incorporated into the current study: (I) hospital stay >24 hours; (II) age >18 years; (III) only the first surgery record would be considered if the patient had received several cardiac surgeries.

Data extraction

Patient data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database via the structured query language of postgreSQL (version 16.4), including patients’ basic information, vital signs, laboratory test results, complications, medication information, and clinical outcome. Basic information included age, gender, and race; vital signs included respiratory rate, body temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure; laboratory test results included anion gap, urea nitrogen, glucose, and serum creatinine; complications included hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), heart failure, cancer, hyperlipemia, and myocardial infarction; medication information included use of statins before CS or not, name and use dosage of statins.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, defined as death from any cause during the index hospitalization. Secondary outcomes were all-cause mortality at 30, 90, and 360 days after hospital admission. In-hospital mortality was ascertained from hospital discharge records. Post-discharge mortality data were obtained from the Social Security Administration Death Master File and Massachusetts state death records, which are routinely linked to the MIMIC-IV database and provided as part of the database release. This linkage allows us to ascertain vital status for up to one year following hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were indicated by mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR), while group comparisons were carried out through the t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Classification data were indicated by frequency (proportion), while group comparisons were conducted by Chi-squared test, adjusted chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Variables with less than 25% missing data were retained. All the missing values were filled through Mice multiple imputation of R language, and multicollinearity among the variables was investigated.

The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) regarding preoperative statin use were computed utilizing the Cox proportional hazard model, and adjustment was made to several variables. Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age, and race, and on this basis, Model 3 was adjusted for respiratory rate, body temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, anion gap, urea nitrogen, glucose, serum creatinine, chronic renal failure, myocardial infarction, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and hyperlipemia. In addition, stratification analysis was performed by age, gender, race, and specific diseases such as chronic renal failure, myocardial infarction, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and hyperlipemia to investigate the correlation between preoperative statin use and prognosis of CS patients in different subgroups, as well as the potential interactions. The dose effect of preoperative atorvastatin use in lowering postoperative mortality was examined. Three sensitivity analyses were implemented to validate the reliability of the research findings. First, to balance the baseline differences between the statin group and non-statin group after the surgery, matching was performed on age, gender, race, anion gap, urea nitrogen, glucose, serum creatinine, malignant tumor, respiratory rate, heart rate, body temperature, chronic renal failure, diabetes, myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypertension, and hyperlipemia using propensity score matching (PSM) method, and the matched data were re-analyzed. Then, the correlation between preoperative statin use and postoperative mortality was calculated after the patients undergoing CABG and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) were removed. Third, to validate the robustness of our multiple imputation approach, we performed a complete-case sensitivity analysis. The primary fully adjusted model (Model 3) analysis was repeated for in-hospital mortality after excluding all patients with missing data for any of the included covariates.

For the dose-response relationship analysis, the dose of atorvastatin was dichotomized at 40 mg per day. This cutoff was chosen based on standard pharmacological intensity definitions from clinical guidelines (e.g., 2013 ACC/AHA guidelines), where ≤40 mg was considered moderate-intensity and >40 mg (typically 80 mg) was considered high-intensity statin therapy.

All data were analyzed via R 4.2.2 version, with bilateral P value <0.05 meaning statistical significance.

Results

Patient selection and baseline characteristics

As illustrated in Figure 1, 17,172 patients were enrolled in the study and classified into preoperative statin use group (3,647 patients) and non-use group (13,525), and 1,819 (10.59%) died during hospitalization. The median age for CS patients, individuals with preoperative statin use, and those without preoperative statin use was 67.42, 72.92, and 65.65 years, respectively. Among the statin use group, a smaller proportion suffered from cancer (14.0% vs. 86.0%), chronic renal insufficiency (22.9% vs. 77.1%), diabetes (35.2% vs. 64.8%), myocardial infarction (22.7% vs. 77.3%), heart failure (31.2% vs. 68.8%), and hyperlipemia (43.6% vs. 56.4%), while a larger proportion suffered from hypertension (52.9% vs. 47.1%). Males accounted for a higher proportion (57.6% vs. 42.4%) (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis

As illustrated in Figure 2, in different subgroups of age, gender, race, malignant tumor, chronic renal failure, diabetes, myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypertension, and hyperlipemia, an association was observed between preoperative statin use and declined postoperative mortality, which was more significant in patients above 65 years of age, the white race and other ethnic groups (P<0.001). A significant association was noted between preoperative statin use and lower postoperative mortality in patients complicated with hyperlipemia (HR =0.638, P<0.001), but the association was weak in non-hyperlipemia patients (HR =0.828, P=0.02). A significant interaction was noted between hyperlipemia and statin use and postoperative death (P for interaction =0.01). A significant association was found between preoperative statin use and lower postoperative mortality in patients complicated with diabetes (HR =0.635, P<0.001), but the association was weak for non-diabetes patients (HR =0.820, P=0.01). A significant interaction was noticed between diabetes and statin use and postoperative death (P for interaction =0.04). By contrast, a significant association was found between preoperative statin use and lower postoperative mortality in patients with no malignant tumor (HR =0.682, P<0.001), but the association was insignificant for malignant tumor patients (HR =0.922, P=0.48). A significant interaction was found between malignant tumors and statin use with regard to postoperative death (P for interaction =0.02).

Dose analysis

In this study, the patients’ medication data were analyzed to further investigate the correlation between statin dose and postoperative mortality of CS patients. Among the 3,647 patients with preoperative statin use, 1,707 cases, 1,346 cases, 277 cases, 303 cases, and 14 cases were treated with atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin, pravastatin, and lovastatin, respectively. Considering different conventional doses for different statins, this study discussed the impact of atorvastatin calcium (used more frequently) on in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality of patients undergoing CS. To ascertain the influence of drug dose on mortality, atorvastatin patients were classified into a large-dose group (>40 mg) and a small-dose group (≤40 mg) according to clinical medication habits, including 876 patients in the large-dose group and 831 patients in the small-dose group. As illustrated in Table 3, atorvastatin use was associated with a significantly declined in-hospital mortality, showing an obvious dose-response relationship. Compared to the patients who did not use preoperative statins, the HR for in-hospital mortality was 0.73 (95% CI: 0.575–0.928, P=0.01) in the small-dose group (≤40 mg) and was further lowered to 0.678 (95% CI: 0.525–0.875, P=0.003) in the large-dose group (>40 mg). In terms of long-term mortality, the effect of atorvastatin was dose-dependent. As for 30-day mortality, statistical significance was not achieved in either the small-dose group or the large-dose group. However, in terms of 90-day mortality, a significant protection effect was observed in the large-dose group (P=0.008), while HR was 0.859 (P=0.08) in the small-dose group. With regard to 360-day mortality, the large-dose group had a HR of 0.858 (95% CI: 0.738–0.998, P=0.047), showing statistical significance, while no statistical significance was observed in the small-dose group. Overall, atorvastatin, particularly at a high dose, can significantly decrease in-hospital and long-term mortality of CS patients (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Three sensitivity analyses were implemented to verify the reliability of the results. First, the raw data were matched by a proportion of 1:1 using PSM to form 3,610 pairs of patients, thus balancing the baseline differences between death and non-death patients. According to the post-matching analysis, preoperative statin use was correlated with a significantly reduced in-hospital mortality after adjustment for all confounding factors (HR =0.792, 95% CI: 0.695–0.903, P<0.001) (Table 4). Second, since it has been confirmed in some studies that the prognosis of CABG and PCI was correlated with statins (22), patients undergoing the two surgeries were excluded to explore the correlation between CS prognosis and statins. Patients undergoing CABG and PCI surgeries (3,948 persons) were excluded, and all confounding factors were adjusted. The results revealed that statin use was still correlated with lower in-hospital mortality (HR =0.801, P<0.001) (Table 5). Third, a complete-case analysis excluding all patients with any missing covariate data was performed. The results remained consistent with the primary imputed analysis, showing a significant association between preoperative statin use and reduced in-hospital mortality rates (HR =0.807, 95% CI: 0.712–0.915, P<0.001), supporting the robustness of our imputation strategy. Together, these three analyses suggested a significant and robust protective role of statins in lowering in-hospital mortality (Table 6).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort research, based on the MIMIC-IV database, investigated the effect of preoperative statin use in CS on postoperative mortality. The research findings showed an association between preoperative statin use and reductions in in-hospital, 30-, 90-, and 360-day mortality. The subgroup analysis found a more significant protective effect of statins among patients with hyperlipemia and diabetes. The dose analysis suggested a significant advantage of large-dose atorvastatin use in lowering in-hospital mortality and long-term mortality. The finding remained robust after adjustment for multiple confounding factors. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that our findings, derived from an observational design, contrast with some large-scale RCTs. For instance, the STICS trial (Zheng et al.) has found that perioperative use of rosuvastatin does not reduce the risk of adverse outcomes after CS and is associated with an increased risk of AKI (19). Similarly, Billings et al. have reported that perioperative use of high-dose atorvastatin did not prevent AKI and may have increased its incidence (18). These discrepancies highlight the inherent limitations of observational research, particularly the risk of unmeasured confounding and ‘healthy user bias’, which can lead to an overestimation of treatment effects. While we have adjusted for a wide range of covariates, residual confounding may still influence our results.

The research findings herein are consistent with some RCT results. For instance, ARMYDA-ACS trial showed a significantly lower incidence of myocardial infarction and a better short-term prognosis in acute coronary syndrome patients using preoperative atorvastatin (23). Chello et al. found that preoperative atorvastatin use led to a significantly lower level of postoperative inflammatory factors (like interleukin-6 or interleukin-8) and better endothelial function (24). DECREASE-III trial suggested that preoperative statin use led to a considerably lower likelihood of postoperative myocardial infarction and cardiovascular death (25). These findings coincide with pleiotropic mechanisms of statins, including anti-inflammation, antioxidation, plaque stabilization and endothelial function improvement (8-12). These pleiotropic effects suggested the possible role of statins in protecting the heart by alleviating ischemia-reperfusion injury, inhibiting systemic inflammatory response and improving microcirculation before the CS, thus lowering the likelihood of postoperative complications and improving the prognosis of patients on CS (14,26).

The subgroup analysis revealed a significant interaction between diabetes, hyperlipemia, and cancer patients and statin use. Among diabetes patients, statin use was significantly correlated with a lower risk of in-hospital death, possibly because there is a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases among diabetes patients. Statins might exert a significant cardiovascular protection effect on diabetes patients by improving insulin resistance, lowering blood lipids, stabilizing plaque, and resisting inflammation (27). Among individuals with hyperlipemia, statins can not only decrease LDL-C, but also stabilize arterial plaque, reduce inflammatory response, and improve endothelial function. These mechanisms might act together to further reduce postoperative mortality, and the heart protection effect is even greater among hyperlipemia patients. Nonetheless, statins in cancer patients fail to significantly lower death risks. It is possibly related to cancer patients’ complicated pathophysiological state, since cancer-related systemic inflammations and metabolic disorders probably cancel some protective effects of statins (28). These findings suggest that, in clinical decisions, clinicians should fully consider patients’ complications and then provide individualized statin strategies for patients.

According to the dose-response analysis result, small-dose atorvastatin calcium use (≤40 mg) mainly exerted a significant effect on in-hospital mortality, while large-dose use (>40 mg) demonstrated a significant advantage in lowering in-hospital mortality, 90-day, and 360-day mortality. This suggested that statin dose might affect the long-term protective effect. Despite the short-term effect of small-dose statins, a sufficient dose is required for longer-term survival benefits, which coincides with some RCT research findings. For example, the PROVE IT-TIMI 22 trial unraveled a greater advantage of high-dose statins in lowering the likelihood of cardiovascular events compared to low-dose statins (29). Therefore, in clinical practice, statin dose should be adjusted based on patients’ specific conditions to ameliorate treatment outcomes.

This research finding carries important clinical significance. First, it provides further evidence for preoperative statin use in CS. In particular, statin use might lead to greater survival benefits among patients with hyperlipemia and diabetes. Second, the dose analysis result suggests that clinical doctors should adjust statin doses based on specific patient conditions to achieve optimal long-term protection effects.

The difference between our findings and those of neutral RCTs indicates that our findings should be interpreted cautiously. The protective association observed in our study might be partly explained by unmeasured factors such as the indication for statin therapy (e.g., pre-existing cardiovascular disease), better overall adherence to medical therapy, or a healthier lifestyle among statin users-a classic example of ‘healthy user bias.’ Future research should replicate these findings using advanced causal inference methods, such as instrumental variable analysis or target trial emulation, to better control for unmeasured confounding.

The current research also has some limitations. First, the data were derived from a single center. Second, despite the rich clinical information provided by MIMIC-IV database, some variables lacked large data, which might affect the comprehensiveness of the analysis. Third, despite the rich data in the MIMIC-IV database, we are unable to adjust for several important cardiac-specific risk factors that are integral to standard perioperative risk scores such as the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score or the EuroSCORE. These include preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction, the urgency of the surgery (elective, urgent, or emergent), and detailed coronary anatomy. The absence of these variables in a substantial proportion of the cohort represents a potential source of residual confounding, as they are known to be strongly associated with both the likelihood of receiving preoperative statin therapy and postoperative outcomes.

In summary, this study, based on large-sample data, demonstrates a significant correlation between statin use before CS and postoperative mortality, with a more obvious effect observed among specific patient groups. Future studies should further explore the optimal strategies of different statins for different surgery types and different patient groups, thus optimizing perioperative management and improving patients’ prognosis.

Conclusions

This retrospective analysis, based on data from the MIMIC-IV database, found that preoperative use of statins in CS, especially large-dose atorvastatin, would significantly lower postoperative short-term and medium-term mortality, with a more significant effect observed in hyperlipemia and diabetes patients. In the future, statin use strategies should be further optimized for different surgery types and different patient groups to improve perioperative management and patients’ prognosis.
