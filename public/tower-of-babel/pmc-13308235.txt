Kaiso modulates androgen receptor expression in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Stephanie Ali Fairbairn, Kyle Kim, Robert W Cowan, Lindyann R Lessey, Melody Fanaian, Hanad Adan, Sailaja Golamari, Ryan Rattan, Juliet M Daniel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive breast cancer subtype that presents therapeutic challenges due to the lack of expression of biomarkers required for conventional targeted treatments. Although a subset of TNBC tumors respond to androgen receptor (AR) inhibitors, the majority exhibit low AR expression and poor outcomes. These AR-negative tumors have been subclassified as quadruple-negative breast cancer (QNBC). Notably, women of African ancestry (WAA) experience a disproportionate burden of TNBC and higher mortality compared to white women, a disparity not fully explained by socio-economic factors, thus suggesting a genetic susceptibility. The transcription factor Kaiso, previously implicated in TNBC progression and poor survival in WAA patients, may play a key role in QNBC biology.MethodsExpression profiling of TNBC tumors from WAA and white women was performed using publicly available datasets and tissue microarrays. To examine the role of Kaiso in AR regulation, we generated CRISPR-Cas9 Kaiso knockout TNBC cell lines and assessed AR expression using chromatin immunoprecipitation, immunoblotting, RT-qPCR and promoter-reporter assays. Cell viability, migration and invasion assays were used to evaluate the effect of Kaiso knockout on sensitivity to the AR inhibitor enzalutamide.ResultsHigh Kaiso expression combined with low AR expression correlated with poorer survival across breast cancer subtypes, including TNBC. WAA expressed significantly lower AR expression and a higher prevalence of the QNBC subtype. Kaiso associated with the endogenous AR promoter, and Kaiso knockout in TNBC cells increased AR expression while reducing ACSL4, an AR target gene that promotes metastasis through phospholipid remodeling. Notably, Kaiso knockout cells were more sensitive to enzalutamide compared to parental cells and reduced migration and invasion.ConclusionCollectively, our findings implicate Kaiso as a key player in TNBC and QNBC, highlighting its potential as a druggable target for improving outcomes in these aggressive breast cancer subtypes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-026-02299-4.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), Quadruple-negative breast cancer (QNBC), Women of African ancestry, Kaiso, Androgen receptor, Disparities

Introduction

Breast cancer (BCa) remains a major global health burden despite increased awareness, early detection and recent therapeutic advances [1]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which represents 15–20% of all breast cancers in North America, is one of the most clinically challenging BCa subtypes because the tumors lack expression of the estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [2, 3]. TNBC is thus unresponsive to conventional targeted therapies such as tamoxifen and trastuzumab and consequently TNBC patients have reduced overall survival compared to other BCa subtypes. [4].

Recent advances in immunotherapy have shown promising results in treating advanced or metastatic TNBC using immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab and durvalumab, which target the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and the programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), respectively [5, 6]. These inhibitors have also demonstrated increased success when combined with chemotherapy in metastatic TNBC. Unfortunately, most TNBC patients (70–80%) show limited or no response to immunotherapy regimens [7]. Approximately 15–20% of TNBC patients with BRCA1/2 germline mutations are eligible for treatment with PARP inhibitors such as Olaparib [8]. However, the lack of targeted treatment options for most TNBC patients has urged current research to identify other potential druggable targets for these patients.

The Androgen Receptor (AR) has recently emerged as a promising therapeutic target for a subset of TNBC patients because 20–40% of all TNBC diagnoses express high levels of AR [9, 10]. AR-positive TNBC patients enrolled in clinical trials using AR antagonists such as enzalutamide [11, 12] and darolutamide [13] showed favorable outcomes with better overall survival rates than AR-negative patients. Patients with AR-negative tumors do not respond to AR inhibitors and have a shorter disease-free interval, supporting the existence of the quadruple-negative breast cancer (QNBC) subtype [14, 15]. Interestingly, Davis et al. (2018) and others reported that African American (AA) women with breast tumors have reduced AR expression compared to breast tumors from white patients, independent of ER and PR expression [16–18]. Notably, TNBC is also most prevalent in young, pre-menopausal women of African ancestry (WAA) [19, 20] and since socio-economic factors alone do not account for the disparity in TNBC prevalence and mortality in WAA, it is postulated that WAA have an ancestral genetic susceptibility [21–23].

Intriguingly, the transcription factor Kaiso, encoded by the ZBTB33 gene, has been associated with racial disparities in breast cancer outcomes among individuals of African ancestry, with elevated expression strongly correlating with the aggressiveness of TNBC tumors [24–26]. We previously reported significantly higher levels of nuclear Kaiso expression in TNBC tissues from WAA (Nigerian, Barbadian, and AA) compared to TNBC tissues from white women, suggesting a role for Kaiso in TNBC prevalence in WAA [19]. Notably, high Kaiso expression also correlates with the aggressiveness and poor outcomes of prostate cancer in Black men compared to white men [27], suggesting that high Kaiso levels may play a role in the disparities in cancer outcomes in people of African ancestry.

Kaiso, initially identified as a binding partner of the E-cadherin catenin cofactor, p120-catenin, is a multi-faceted POZ-ZF transcription factor that is implicated in vertebrate development and tumorigenesis [28, 29]. Research from our lab [26, 30] and others [31–33] suggests that Kaiso exhibits both pro-oncogenic and tumor-suppressive roles in multiple human cancers and contributes to poor prognostic outcomes. Unlike other POZ-ZF transcription factors, Kaiso exhibits dual-specificity DNA binding – it binds DNA via a sequence-specific Kaiso binding site (KBS) - TCCTGCNA (core sequence: CTGCNA), an unmethylated palindromic TCTCGCGAGA sequence, and methylated CpG dinucleotides [29]. Several Kaiso target genes (cyclin D1, matrilysin, E-cadherin) are associated with tumor initiation, invasion, and metastasis [27, 30, 34] and we previously demonstrated that Kaiso-depleted TNBC cells exhibit decreased motility & invasiveness in cultured cells and mouse xenografts [26]. These Kaiso-depleted cells also exhibited decreased TGFß signaling, a known promoter of the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway that endows cells with increased motility and contributes to tumor progression [26]. Given the prognostic significance of Kaiso and AR in TNBC and their differential expression in WAA, this study aims to elucidate the relationship between Kaiso and AR expression in TNBC and assess the role of Kaiso on the sensitivity of TNBC cells to AR inhibitors.

Collectively, our findings suggest that Kaiso modulates AR expression in TNBC cells and increases their sensitivity to AR inhibitors, highlighting the need for further investigation of Kaiso as a potential TNBC therapeutic target.

Methods & materials

Study population

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) TNBC specimens with corresponding clinical data were collected from Nigeria and Barbados and tissue microarrays (TMAs) were created as previously described. [19] This study used tissues from 47 Nigerian and 43 Barbadian TNBC specimens. A Yale tissue microarray (YTMA-347-4) comprised of TNBC specimens from 15 African American (AA) and 30 white women was purchased from the Yale Developmental Histology Lab. In this study, Nigerian, Barbadian and AA patients are considered WAA. Normal breast tissue, triple-positive breast cancer tissue and the luminal B MDA-MB-361 cell line were used as positive controls.

Cell culture

The TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 (hereafter referred to as MDA-231 and MDA-453, respectively) were kindly donated by Dr. John Hassell (McMaster University, Hamilton, ON, Canada). MDA-453 is classified as an AR-positive TNBC subtype, and MDA-231 is claudin-low with minimal AR expression [35, 36]. Cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling by The Centre for Applied Genomics (TCAG, SickKids, Toronto). STR profiles were compared with a reference database to verify cell line identity. All cultured cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Lonza BioWhittaker, Walkerville, Maryland, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Hyclone, Logan, Utah, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, Grand Island, NY, USA) and maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37◦C. For 5-azacytidine treatment, cells were incubated in serum-supplemented, antibiotic-free, media containing a final concentration of 5 µM 5-azacytidine (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), which was replaced every 24 h for a 72-hour incubation period. For siRNA treatments, MDA-MB-157 (MDA-157) cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 3 × 105 and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were transfected with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (#13778030, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and either a negative control siRNA (#4390846, Thermo Fisher Scientific), or siRNA against Kaiso (# 4392420, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were incubated for 48 h prior to protein and RNA isolation.

CRISPR-Cas9

Kaiso knockout cell lines (Kaiso KO) were created following a modification of the protocol by Bauer et al. [37]. Briefly, single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences [sgRNA1 - AACTCCTTGAATGAGCAACG (sense), sgRNA2 - GCTACAGACATTCAGTACTC (sense), and sgRNA3 - TAACATCACAGAAGAGTCCA (antisense)] were designed using CRISPick [38] and ligated into pSpCas9(BB)-2 A-Puro (PX459) V2.0 (Addgene plasmid #62988), as previously described [39]. MDA-231 and MDA-453 cells were transfected in 6-well microplates with 2.5 µg plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s protocol and subsequently treated 96 h post-transfection for several days with 2 µg/mL puromycin (InvivoGen, San Diego, California, USA) to select for plasmid-containing cells. Media containing puromycin was removed and cells were distributed into 96-well microplates by serial dilution to generate single-cell clones [40]. Single-cell clones were expanded, and Kaiso knockout was confirmed by Sanger sequencing and western blot analyses.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The FFPE tissue microarray sections were de-paraffinized in xylenes and rehydrated as previously described [19], and then subjected to antigen retrieval using a low-pH (pH 6.0) sodium citrate buffer (Sigma Aldrich, Oakville, ON, Canada) at 98 °C for 40 min. After membrane permeabilization (0.05% Tris-buffered saline (TBS)-Tween) and blocking of non-specific staining using normal donkey serum (NDS), tissues were incubated overnight with a mouse monoclonal primary antibody specific for AR (AR-441, #MA5-13426, Thermo Fisher) diluted at 1:50 in a TBS solution containing 5% NDS and 4 drops/mL Biotin (Vector Laboratories, Newark, California, USA). Following multiple washes, endogenous peroxide activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and tissues were incubated with biotinylated donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody (# 715-065-150, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratory, West Grove, Pennsylvania, USA) diluted at 1:1000. Tissues were incubated with Vectastain Elite ABC solution (Vector Laboratories) followed by staining with diaminobenzidine (DAB). Tissues were counterstained with Harris hematoxylin (Sigma, St. Louis, Missouri, USA), differentiated in acid ethanol (0.3% HCl in 70% ethanol), blued in Scott’s tap water substitute, and dehydrated in a gradient of ethanol. The FFPE tissues were then rehydrated in xylenes and mounted using PolyMount (Polysciences Inc, Warrington, Pennsylvania, USA). Images were acquired using the Aperio ScanScope (Leica Biosystems Imaging, Inc., Buffalo Grove, Illinois, USA).

Tissues stained by IHC were blinded, and nuclear AR expression was scored by two independent pathologists (SG, RR), and the scores were averaged to give a final score value. The intensity of staining was scored as negative (0), weak (1), moderate (2), or strong (3) staining intensity. The modified histochemical score (H-score) system was then used to generate a total score for each tissue with values spanning 0 − 300 using the formula: (0 x % negative cells) + (1 x % weak positive cells) + (2 x % moderate positive cells) + (3 x % strong positive cells). H-scores were graphed using the GraphPad Prism software v.8.4.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) to compare protein expression in each patient cohort. Statistical analyses were performed using a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test to compare the AR H-scores among the four patient groups (Nigerian, Barbadian, African American, and white).

Kaplan-Meier curves

Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated based on data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) Research Network [41, 42] and the K-M Plotter website [43]. GraphPad was used to analyze and correlate AR and Kaiso mRNA expression from the TCGA-BRCA patient database. For log-rank statistical tests, p < 0.05 indicated significance.

Immunoblot analysis

Immunoblot analysis was performed using 30 µg of protein as previously described [30]. Primary antibody incubations were performed overnight at 4 °C at the following dilutions: rabbit polyclonal anti-Kaiso antibody (in-house, 1:10,000); mouse monoclonal anti-AR antibody (AR-441, #MA5-13426, Thermo Fisher, 1:150); rabbit-monoclonal anti-ACSL4 antibody (#PA527137, Thermo Fisher, 1:500 and 1:1000), mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin antibody (#A5441, Sigma, 1:10,000), and rabbit monoclonal anti-GAPDH antibody (#2118, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA, 1:5000). Primary antibody signals were amplified with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit or donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody (#7074S and #7076S, Cell Signaling Technology) in a 1:2,000 dilution before visualization using Clarity Western Enhanced Chemiluminescence Substrate and the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Mississauga, ON, Canada). All immunoblot experiments were performed in triplicate. GraphPad was used to visualize the protein fold change after retrieving band intensity values from the Image Lab software (RRID: SCR_014210).

Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cell lines using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using SuperScript™ III First-Strand Synthesis System Kit (Thermo Fisher) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCR was then conducted using QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Luna Universal qPCR Master Mix (#M3003L, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA). All results were normalized to the housekeeping gene B2M (Table 1) and relative quantification was calculated using a comparative threshold cycle (2−ΔΔCT) with an efficiency correction for 3 technical replicates. Graphing and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Unpaired t-tests were used to determine statistical significance.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

MDA-231 and MDA-453 cells were grown to ~ 80% confluence, and fragmented chromatin was extracted and immunoprecipitated as previously described [30]. 50 µL of the isolated chromatin was then precipitated with 5 µg of mouse monoclonal anti-Kaiso 6F antibody (in-house), 4 µg of mouse Kappa monoclonal IgG2a Isotype Control (#ab18413, Abcam Limited, Cambridge, UK) or 4 µg of rabbit anti-Histone H3 antibody (#ab1791, Abcam) antibodies. The fragmented, precipitated DNA was then purified via phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol extraction and resuspended in 50 µL of DNase/RNase-free water for PCR amplification.

PCR amplification

For the ChIP experiments, 5 µL of recovered DNA was subjected to an endpoint PCR reaction containing 2X DreamTaq green PCR master mix (Thermo Fisher), 0.5 mM forward and reverse primers (Table 2), and RNase/DNase-free sterile water to a final reaction volume of 25 µL. PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30s, annealing at 55 °C for 30s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 15 min. After completion of the PCR reaction, 10 µL of each reaction was loaded onto a 1% agarose gel with 0.5 µg/mL ethidium bromide and electrophoresed at 140 V for 40 min in TAE solution. Images were obtained using the Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Promoter sub-cloning

The minimal AR promoter region (-14 to + 2479) was sub-cloned upstream of the Gaussia luciferase gene in a pGLuc Basic vector (New England Biolabs). Insertion of the AR promoter was confirmed by sequencing (Plasmidsaurus, Eugene, Oregon, USA). Kaiso-depletion was achieved by using a pRetroSuper (pRS) vector as previously described [44]. All vectors were purified from methyltransferase deficient dam−/dcm− competent E. coli bacteria for use in minimal promoter-reporter luciferase assays.

Promoter-Reporter Luciferase assay

MDA-231 cells were seeded in a 6-well dish at a density of 3 × 105 and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were then co-transfected with 1 µg pRSV-β-Galactosidase control vector (gift from Dr. Pierre-André Bédard, McMaster University, Hamilton, ON, Canada), 1 µg AR promoter-reporter constructs and either 0.25–0.5 µg of pRS Kaiso plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher) as per the manufacturer’s protocol, in antibiotic-free DMEM media supplemented with FBS. The media was replaced after 18 h and 48 h post-transfection, 20 µL of the culture media and 50 µL of Gaussia Luciferase Flash assay working solution were used to measure the secreted luciferase activity according to the manufacturer’s protocol for the Pierce Gaussia Luciferase Flash Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher). Luciferase activity was recorded as relative light units (RLU) using a GloMax microplate reader (Promega, Madison, WI) and normalized for transfection efficiency using the internal β-galactosidase control. The average luciferase activity from 3 biological replicates was plotted as RLU against the luciferase activity of the empty pGLuc-basic vector control. Statistical significance was determined by unpaired t-tests (p < 0.05).

Immunocytochemistry

50,000 cells were seeded into 8-well chamber slides (#30108, SPL Life Sciences, South Korea) and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were then treated with 10nM DHT (Millipore Sigma, Massachusetts, USA) for 24 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed in methanol pre‑chilled to − 20 °C for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, samples were incubated in CAS‑Block (Thermo Fisher) for 10 min to reduce nonspecific binding. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with a mouse monoclonal anti-AR primary antibody (AR-441, #MA5-13426, Thermo Fisher, 1:50). The following day, samples were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher, 1:1000) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained using DAPI mounting medium (Abcam, UK). Images were acquired using the BioTek Cytation 5 imaging system (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Experiments were performed in duplicate.

Cell viability assay

MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells were seeded using 200 µL of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 0.1 mg/ml penicillin/streptomycin in a 96-well dish at a density of 2 × 103 and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Cells were treated with various concentrations of enzalutamide (#HY-70002, 10 µM-90 µM, MedChemExpress via Cedarlane, Burlington, ON, Canada) for 96 h before cell viability assessment. AlamarBlue cell viability reagent (#DAL1100, Thermo Fisher) was used to assess cell viability according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A DMSO control was included for each experiment and used for normalization. All experiments were performed in duplicate.

Migration and invasion assays

MDA-453 (1 × 106) and MDA-231 (5 × 105) parental and Kaiso knockout (KO1, KO2) cells were seeded in 10 cm cell culture plates overnight. Following attachment, the cells were treated with a DMSO vehicle control or ½ IC50 of enzalutamide and incubated for 48 h. Following treatment, the cells were harvested and replated for migration assays at a density of 5 × 104 (MDA-231) or 1 × 105 (MDA-453) in 100 µl of serum-free media in the upper chamber of the transwell inserts (# 29442-12, Corning, New York, USA) placed into the well of a 24-well plate containing 800 µl of media supplemented with 10% FBS. This served as a chemoattractant and the cells were incubated for 16 h (MDA-231) or 24 h (MDA-453). After incubation, the cells were washed twice in PBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde (v/v) for 10 min and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (w/v) for 10 min at room temperature. The inserts were then washed twice in PBS, the non-invaded cells on the upper chamber of the inserts were removed with a sterile cotton swab, and the invaded cells on the bottom chamber were then imaged under a 4X objective using a Nikon Eclipse Ts2 microscope (Nikon, USA). Images were processed and the area-fraction percentage was calculated using ImageJ 1.54 g (NIH, USA) [45].

This was repeated for the invasion assays; however, the cells were plated after treatment at a density of 1 × 105 (MDA-231) or 2 × 105 (MDA-453) in 100 µl of serum-free media in the upper chamber of the transwell inserts coated with 0.2 mg/mL of Matrigel (#CB40234, Corning). The cells were incubated for 24 h (MDA-231) or 48 h (MDA-453).

Results

WAA had low androgen receptor expression compared to white TNBC patients

This retrospective study used tissue samples collected between 2002 and 2018 from Nigerian (n = 47), Barbadian (n = 43), African American (AA, n = 15), and white (n = 30) TNBC patients (Supplementary Table 3). Since previous studies reported that AR expression is significantly lower in breast tumors from African American patients compared to those from white patients [16, 18, 46], we used IHC to assess AR protein expression in our WAA (Nigerian, Barbadian, AA) and white TNBC patient tumors. Representative tissue cores from each patient cohort revealed nuclear AR expression is higher in white patients, as indicated by the intensity of the brown staining (Fig. 1A). Kruskal-Wallis statistical analyses revealed TNBC tumors from white women expressed nuclear AR at significantly higher levels than those from Nigerian (p < 0.0001), Barbadian (p = 3 × 10− 4), and AA (p = 3.63 × 10− 2) women. There were no significant differences in AR expression among the WAA TNBC tissues (Fig. 1B). Notably, patient tissues from each cohort were very heterogeneous as indicated by the range of H-scores, particularly in white patients.

When compared to tissues from white patients, nuclear AR expression was significantly reduced in WAA TNBC tissues. To determine the percentage of QNBC patients in each cohort, we separated patients into either AR-negative (QNBC) or AR-positive (TNBC) groups. Tissues with no staining (H-score = 0) were considered AR-negative or QNBC and tissues with weak-to-strong staining (H-score > 0) were considered AR-positive or TNBC. The WAA patient cohort consisted of a higher percentage of QNBC tissues (Nigerian = 87%, Barbadian = 81%, AA = 67%), while white patients had the lowest percentage of QNBC tissues (32%) (Fig. 1C).

High Kaiso and low AR correlate with poorer survival in breast cancer patients

Our lab previously reported that WAA TNBC tissues expressed high levels of nuclear Kaiso compared to TNBC tissues from white women [19] and this increased nuclear Kaiso expression correlated with poorer survival. Thus, we investigated the effect of AR expression on the probability of survival using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) in both the broader breast cancer cohort and in TNBC patients specifically. TNBC patients included in this analysis were restricted to cases classified as TNBC according to the molecular subtyping defined by Lehmann et al. 2016 [47]. Expression levels were then dichotomized into “low” or “high” groups using the median expression value as the cutoff. TCGA breast cancer patients (n = 134) with low AR expression had a median survival time of 979 days, which is significantly less (log-rank test, p = 0.002) than patients with high AR expression who had a median survival time of 1661 days (Fig. 2A). This trend in overall survival was also evident when considering only TNBC patients (n = 27, log-rank test, p = 0.04), where the median survival time with low AR expression was 833 days compared to 1104 days in TNBC patients with high AR expression (Fig. 2B).

Considering the correlation of low AR and high Kaiso expression on BCa patient survival, we next assessed two groups based on median expression — low AR/high Kaiso (n = 84) and high AR/low Kaiso (n = 125). Analysis of Kaplan-Meier survival curves revealed that BCa patients with both low AR and high Kaiso expression had significantly worse overall survival than patients with high AR and low Kaiso expression (log-rank test, p = 0.041, Fig. 2C).

Due to the low TCGA TNBC sample size, we also used data from KM Plotter. Survival curves generated using mRNA expression levels in BCa patients (from RNA-seq, n = 2976 and from gene chip, n = 943) revealed that patients with low AR expression had worse overall (log-rank test p = 0.003 and p = 0.005, respectively) and recurrence-free (gene chip, n = 2032, log-rank test, p = 1.2 × 10− 4) survival (Supplementary Fig. 2). Kaplan-Meier survival analyses also revealed a significantly lower recurrence-free survival in patients with low AR/high Kaiso expression compared to those with high AR/low Kaiso expression (gene chip, n = 2032, log-rank test, p < 0.0001, Fig. 2D).

Increased AR mRNA and protein expression in Kaiso knockout TNBC cells

To gain further insight into the Kaiso-AR relationship, we generated stable Kaiso knockout clones of the MDA-453 and MDA-231 TNBC cell lines using specific sgRNAs and CRISPR-Cas9 to target the Kaiso gene, ZBTB33. Two knockout derivatives were created for each cell line (KO1, KO2) to control for clonal and off-target effects. To assess Kaiso’s effect on AR expression, we quantified AR mRNA levels in parental and Kaiso-knockout MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells by RT-qPCR (Table 1). The Kaiso KO derivatives exhibited a significant increase in AR mRNA expression compared to parental TNBC cells (MDA-453 KO1, p = 0.005 and KO2, p = 0.043; MDA-231 KO1, p = 0.005 and KO2, p = 0.0007, Fig. 3A). AR mRNA expression was also significantly increased (p = 0.034) in Kaiso-depleted MDA-157 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). AR expression was normalized to a B2M control (Table 1).

We then performed immunoblot analyses to evaluate AR protein expression in the parental and Kaiso knockout cells (KO1, KO2). As seen in Fig. 3B, Kaiso knockout cell lines did not express any Kaiso protein compared to both MDA-453 and MDA-231 parental cells. Densitometry analysis revealed an ~ 2-fold increase in AR protein levels in MDA-453 KO1 (p = 0.009) and MDA-231 (KO1: p = 0.042, KO2: p = 0.035) TNBC cells and an ~ 1.5-fold increase in MDA-453 KO2 (p = 0.028) TNBC cells relative to the GAPDH (MDA-453) or ß-actin (MDA-231) loading controls (Fig. 3C). These findings were consistent with our pilot study using siRNA against Kaiso in the TNBC cell line, MDA-157. Densitometry analysis indicates that Kaiso was successfully depleted (p < 0.0001) and there was a 2.3-fold increase in AR protein expression (p = 0.033, Supplementary Fig. 3A).

Kaiso associates with the endogenous AR promoter and represses its expression

Due to our findings that Kaiso knockout increased AR mRNA and protein expression in TNBC cells, and in silico analysis using the Eukaryotic Promoter Database [48] revealed that the endogenous AR promoter region (-13 to -4933 upstream of the transcription start site) contains a full KBS, 8 core KBS and 2 CpG dinucleotides (Fig. 4A), we postulated that AR may be a Kaiso target gene. ChIP-PCR analyses using chromatin isolated from MDA-453 and MDA-231 parental cells, and the Kaiso-specific monoclonal 6F antibody demonstrated that Kaiso associates with the endogenous AR promoter region (-1796 to -1125, Fig. 4B). Notably, treatment of MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells with 5-azacytidine did not affect Kaiso’s ability to associate with the AR promoter region, suggesting that Kaiso’s association with the AR promoter is not methyl-CpG-dependent (Fig. 4B). These findings are consistent in MDA-157 cells and across two additional regions in the AR promoter (-2489 to 1777 and − 667 to -13, Supplementary Fig. 3B). We then assessed Kaiso’s ability to regulate AR expression by measuring promoter-reporter activity under the control of a minimal AR promoter. Transfection of MDA-231 cells with a pRS Kaiso plasmid resulted in a dose-dependent increase in promoter activity, with a 2-fold induction at 0.25 µg (p = 0.144) and a 2.8-fold induction at 0.5 µg (p = 0.007) relative to the baseline (Fig. 4C).

Kaiso knockout decreases ACSL4 protein expression in TNBC cells

Given our findings that AR may be a putative Kaiso target gene, we investigated Kaiso’s potential involvement in AR signaling. ACSL4 (long-chain fatty acyl-CoA synthetase 4) has gained considerable interest as a biomarker in QNBC [49] and it is well established that AR represses ACSL4 expression in prostate cancer [50, 51]. The presence of multiple Kaiso binding sites in the ACSL4 promoter led us to explore whether Kaiso was involved in AR signaling and ACSL4 regulation. Using METABRIC [52–54] breast cancer data, we confirmed that AR and ACSL4 are negatively correlated in BCa (n = 2509, Spearman coefficient = -0.48, Fig. 5A). We also noted that Kaiso and ACSL4 are positively correlated (n = 2509, Spearman coefficient = 0.19, Fig. 5A). Next, we investigated the effect of Kaiso knockout on ACSL4 protein expression in MDA-453 and MDA-231 Kaiso KO cells and immunoblot analyses revealed decreased ACSL4 protein expression in MDA-453 and MDA-231 Kaiso KO cells compared to the parental cells (Fig. 5B). Densitometry analysis revealed a significant decrease in ACSL4 protein levels in Kaiso KO MDA-453 (KO1: p < 0.0001, KO2: p = 0.0009), and Kaiso KO MDA-231 (KO1: p = 0.0007), KO2: p = 0.0015) derivatives compared to parental MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells, Fig. 5C). The effect of Dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a potent AR agonist, on Kaiso regulation of AR-mediated repression of ACSL4 was examined using 10nM DHT. There was significant ACSL4 repression in the Kaiso knockout cells compared to the parental cells (Fig. 5B). Densitometry analysis revealed a significant decrease in ACSL4 protein levels in Kaiso KO MDA-453 (KO1: p < 0.0001, KO2: p = 0.0007), and Kaiso KO MDA-231 (KO2: p = 0.0173) derivatives compared to parental MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells (Fig. 5C).

DHT treatment induces nuclear localization of AR in Kaiso knockout TNBC cells

To determine how Kaiso influences AR subcellular distribution in TNBC, we performed immunocytochemistry (ICC) in MDA-453 and MDA-231 parental and Kaiso knockout cells under untreated and DHT stimulated conditions. In the untreated conditions, MDA-231 parental cells showed limited nuclear signal, whereas both Kaiso knockout clones (KO1 and KO2) exhibited visibly stronger AR staining and a marked increase in nuclear AR, reflected by greater overlap between AR and DAPI channels. Although MDA-453 parental cells displayed higher AR levels than MDA-231 parental cells, the relative increase in nuclear localization in the Kaiso knockout clones was comparable between the two cell lines. Treatment with 10 nM DHT further enhanced nuclear AR accumulation in all cell lines, with the most pronounced nuclear increase observed in the knockout clones (Fig. 6).

Kaiso knockout increases the susceptibility of TNBC cells to enzalutamide

To determine the clinical relevance of our findings, we evaluated the impact of Kaiso knockout on the sensitivity of TNBC cells to the AR inhibitor, enzalutamide. Kaiso knockout MDA-453 cells showed increased sensitivity to the drug enzalutamide with IC50 values of 14.95 µM (KO1) and 15.14µM (KO2), compared to 20.29 µM in the parental cells (Fig. 7A). Similarly, Kaiso knockout MDA-231 cells exhibited enhanced sensitivity to enzalutamide with IC50 values of 21.57 µM (KO1) and 16.07 µM (KO2) versus 37.77 µM in the parental cells (Fig. 7B). Given that enzalutamide is a competitive AR antagonist and DHT can modulate AR activity, we also compared the effects of DHT plus enzalutamide on cell viability. As expected, DHT attenuated the inhibitory effect of enzalutamide, resulting in a rightward shift in the dose-responsive curves. The Kaiso knockout cells remained more sensitive to enzalutamide in both MDA-453 (KO1 = 21.38µM, KO2 = 22.71µM) and MDA-231(KO1 = 62.77µM, KO2 = 44.86µM) compared to the parental (MDA-453 = 30.97µM; MDA-231 = 78.57µM) upon DHT treatment.

Kaiso knockout and enzalutamide independently suppress migration and invasion in TNBC cells

We next assessed whether Kaiso loss and AR inhibition by enzalutamide alters the migratory and invasive capacity of TNBC cells. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both Kaiso knockout and enzalutamide treatment on migration in MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells, as well as a significant interaction in both cell lines. Across both cell lines, KO1 and KO2 clones exhibited markedly reduced migration relative to parental controls, and enzalutamide further suppressed migration (Fig. 8A).

For invasion, two-way ANOVA again showed significant main effects of Kaiso knockout and enzalutamide treatment in both MDA-453 and MDA-231 cells. The interaction was significant in MDA-231 but not in MDA-453, indicating that while both factors independently reduce invasion in MDA-453 cells, their combined effect is not synergistic in this context. In contrast, MDA-231 cells displayed a significant interaction, with enzalutamide producing a greater reduction in invasion in the knockout clones than in parental cells (Fig. 8B).

Together, these findings demonstrate that Kaiso loss suppresses both migration and invasion, and that AR inhibition further attenuates these effects, with cell line specific differences.

Discussion

Despite advances in targeted therapies, TNBC remains challenging to treat, particularly in AR-negative or quadruple-negative (QNBC) cases, which are often associated with poorer clinical outcomes and racial disparities [55–57]. Consistent with previous reports [17, 18] indicating lower AR expression among African American (AA) patients, our findings reveal markedly reduced AR expression in our cohort of women of African ancestry – Nigerian, Barbadian and AA TNBC patients – compared to white women. We also observed a significantly higher proportion of the QNBC subtype in WAA compared to white women. This may partially explain the higher mortality rates in WAA, as QNBC presents with higher tumor grades and stage at diagnosis, and is associated with a more aggressive phenotype [16, 18].

Despite the higher occurrence of QNBC reported in WAA patients, we were unable to compare the survival rates for our WAA cohorts due to the limited availability of survival data for Nigerian and Barbadian patients. However, analysis of TCGA and KM Plotter datasets confirmed that low AR expression is associated with poorer overall survival in BCa patients, independent of subtype. Additionally, TNBC patients with low AR expression also exhibited worse survival outcomes compared to those with high AR expression. These findings underscore the prognostic relevance of AR and highlight the need for future studies using tissues from larger cohorts of each BCa subtype with available ancestry and survival information to better understand the therapeutic implications of AR expression in WAA populations. Future experiments could include multiplex IHC to assess the expression and co-localization of Kaiso, AR, and additional markers of interest in race-stratified TNBC tissues. Patient-derived organoids and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models from WAA and white patients could also be used to determine whether Kaiso-AR modulation differentially affects tumor growth and metastasis. Together, these studies can contribute to determining whether the Kaiso-AR interaction contributes to disparities in breast cancer outcomes between WAA and white women.

In addition to AR, we previously demonstrated that the transcription factor Kaiso contributes to the unique molecular characteristics of WAA TNBC [19, 26]. Kaiso’s role in transcriptional regulation of diverse genes suggests that Kaiso could also influence the aggressive phenotype observed in QNBC patients. Our current findings show that Kaiso associates with and represses the AR promoter, suggesting a mechanistic link for the inverse relationship between high Kaiso expression and low AR in QNBC. We further show that Kaiso may suppress AR nuclear localization in TNBC cells. Since nuclear localization is required for AR-mediated transcriptional activity, loss of Kaiso may potentiate downstream AR signaling. Together, these findings suggest that Kaiso-mediated repression of AR expression may contribute to the aggressive phenotype and poorer survival outcomes observed in AR-negative TNBC.

Our successful knockout of Kaiso in TNBC cells and the concomitant increase in AR expression in the Kaiso knockout derivatives compared to parental TNBC cells suggested that Kaiso knockout cells may be more responsive to AR inhibitors such as enzalutamide. We established that knocking out Kaiso increased the sensitivity of TNBC cells to enzalutamide, resulting in reduced cell viability relative to parental cells and this difference in responsiveness persisted even under DHT stimulation. Importantly, these effects were accompanied by reduced migration and invasion in the Kaiso knockout TNBC cell lines in untreated and enzalutamide-treated conditions. These findings suggest that Kaiso loss sensitizes TNBC cells to AR antagonism at both molecular and functional levels, warranting further investigation using in vivo models.

In silico analysis revealed a positive correlation between Kaiso and the AR target gene, ACSL4. Kaiso knockout reduced ACSL4 protein expression, supporting the positive correlation between Kaiso and ACSL4. ACSL4 is inversely correlated with AR, ER and PR expression in breast cancer [49] and is overexpressed in several cancers, including colorectal [58, 59], prostate [60] and liver [61] cancers. Importantly, ACSL4 overexpression has been linked to an aggressive phenotype and poor prognosis in QNBC, with increased expression reported in AA patients [49]. Moreover, ACSL4 inhibition has shown promise in reducing tumor growth and enhancing chemosensitivity in QNBC models [62]. Since our study shows that Kaiso may contribute to ACSL4 regulation, an understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which ACSL4 contributes to QNBC development and progression may be crucial for developing effective treatments. Moreover, our data further implicates Kaiso in QNBC progression and highlights Kaiso’s potential as a therapeutic target. Future studies to elucidate the role of ACSL4 in QNBC may provide insight for developing targeted therapies that inhibit ACSL4 activity.

Given that AR-negative patients do not benefit from AR inhibitors, and that Kaiso may suppress AR expression, we propose that the development of a Kaiso-specific inhibitor may be beneficial to these patients. While inhibiting Kaiso alone may reduce proliferation and metastasis, there may be increased benefit from the synergistic effect of using a Kaiso inhibitor alongside well-established AR inhibitors such as enzalutamide. Our data showing increased enzalutamide sensitivity in Kaiso knockout TNBC cells support this hypothesis.

Conclusions

We postulate that high Kaiso and low AR expression may contribute to the higher mortality rates observed in women of African ancestry, due to the increased prevalence of AR-negative TNBC (QNBC) subtype among these patients. Our study demonstrates that Kaiso represses AR expression and may regulate AR target genes such as ACSL4. We also demonstrated that Kaiso knockout enhances the sensitivity of TNBC cells to AR inhibition. These findings support the development of Kaiso-targeted therapies as a promising strategy for improving outcomes in TNBC patients, particularly among women of African ancestry.
