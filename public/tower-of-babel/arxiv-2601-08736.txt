Note on High Dimensional Spatial-Sign Test for One Sample Problem
Ping Zhao

Abstract
We revisit the null distribution of the high-dimensional spatial-sign test of 6 under mild structural assumptions on the scatter matrix. We show that the standardized test statistic converges to a non-Gaussian limit, characterized as a mixture of a normal component and a weighted chi-square component. To facilitate practical implementation, we propose a wild bootstrap procedure for computing critical values and establish its asymptotic validity. Numerical experiments demonstrate that the proposed bootstrap test delivers accurate size control across a wide range of dependence settings and dimension–sample-size regimes. Keywords: degenerate U U -statistic, elliptical symmetry, high dimensional data, spatial sign, wild bootstrap

Introduction

Testing mean vectors is a cornerstone problem in multivariate analysis, with ubiquitous roles in genomics, finance, neuroimaging, and many other areas where scientific questions are naturally phrased as location effects.
In the classical low-dimensional regime, Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test provides an optimal likelihood-based benchmark under multivariate normality
(
3
)
.
However, modern applications routinely operate in the “large
p
, small
n
” or “large
p
, comparable
n
” regimes, where the sample covariance matrix is singular or ill-conditioned and Hotelling-type procedures become infeasible or unstable.

A fundamental line of work bypasses covariance inversion by constructing quadratic-form statistics based on the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of the sample mean (or mean difference) and suitable bias correction.
Seminal examples include the two-sample statistic of
1
, and the U-statistic refinement of
2
, which has motivated an extensive literature on high-dimensional mean testing. Please see an overview in
4
.

Despite their broad applicability, many asymptotic calibrations in this literature rely on nontrivial restrictions on the covariance eigenstructure (e.g., trace-type conditions that enforce asymptotic Gaussianity of quadratic forms) and typically assume light-tailed distributions with sufficiently high finite moments.
To relax covariance assumptions, several authors have proposed calibration schemes that adapt to non-Gaussian and nonstandard limits, including randomization-based approaches.
In particular,
8
studied feasible high-dimensional randomization tests for mean vectors, and
7
developed approximate randomization tests under weak conditions without imposing eigenstructure constraints in related two-sample settings. Zhang et al.
(
9
;
10
)
developed a
normal-reference
line of tests for high-dimensional mean problems.
In particular,
9
proposed an
L_{2}
-norm based two-sample procedure that remains valid under mild covariance conditions and calibrates the null distribution via a Welch–Satterthwaite
\chi^{2}
approximation, which is designed to accommodate generally non-Gaussian quadratic-form limits.
Building on the same principle,
10
provided a unified normal-reference framework for testing high-dimensional mean vectors across several settings and illustrated its practical performance in applications.
More recently,
13
studied the high-dimensional two-sample Behrens–Fisher problem and proposed a normal-reference
F
-type test, where the null law is approximated by an
F
-type mixture (a ratio of two independent chi-square-type mixtures) with consistently estimated degrees of freedom for implementation.

Heavy-tailed data and outliers present an additional challenge.
In finance and omics studies, distributional departures from Gaussianity are common, and moment-based procedures can suffer size distortions or power loss.
A robust alternative is to replace raw observations by
spatial signs
, which retain directional information while downweighting large radii.
Under elliptically symmetric models, spatial-sign-based procedures are natural and often stable
(
5
)
.

In this direction,
6
proposed a high-dimensional nonparametric test for the mean vector based on spatial signs, and
12
further developed an adaptive spatial-sign-based approach for elliptically distributed high-dimensional data, together with a chi-square approximation for calibration.
However, the limiting null law of spatial-sign quadratic forms can be non-Gaussian under general covariance structures: the limit may involve a mixture of a Gaussian component and an infinite weighted chi-square component, with weights determined by the (normalized) spectrum of the spatial-sign scatter.
Consequently, fixed-form approximations (normal or chi-square) can be inaccurate in regimes where a few leading eigen-directions are non-negligible.

This paper makes two contributions.
First, we reestablish the null limit distribution of the spatial-sign test statistic of
6
under mild conditions on the spatial-sign scatter matrix, allowing for general eigenvalue configurations.
We show that the subsequential limits take a mixed form consisting of a Gaussian part and a weighted chi-square part.
Second, we propose a wild bootstrap procedure that consistently estimates critical values under elliptically symmetric distributions, thereby adapting automatically to all possible mixed limits.
We provide a detailed theoretical justification for the bootstrap consistency and demonstrate via simulations that the proposed method controls empirical sizes accurately across a wide range of covariance structures and distributional scenarios.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the model, the spatial-sign statistic, and the mixed-limit characterization.
Section
3
presents the wild bootstrap and establishes its validity.
Section
4
reports numerical results, and Section
5
concludes with discussions and future directions.

2
Spatial Sign based Test

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
follows the following elliptical symmetric distribution:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+r_{i}\mathbf{\Gamma}\boldsymbol{u}_{i},

(2.1)

where the scatter matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Gamma}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
r_{i}\geq 0
is a scalar radius,
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
is uniform on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
, and
r_{i}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
. We consider the following hypothesis test problem:

\displaystyle H_{0}:\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{0},~~\text{versus},~~H_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mu}\not=\boldsymbol{0}

(2.2)

The spatial sign function is defined as
U(\mathbf{x})=\|\mathbf{x}\|^{-1}\mathbf{x}I(\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0})
. In traditional fixed
p
circumstance, the following so-called ”inner centering and inner standardization” sign-based procedure is usually used (cf., Chapter 6 of
5
)

Q_{n}^{2}=np{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}^{T}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}},

where
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i},\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}=U\left(\mathbf{S}^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}\right),\mathbf{S}^{-1/2}
are Tyler’s scatter matrix (cf., Section 6.1.3 of
5
).
Q_{n}^{2}
is affine-invariant and can be regarded as a nonparametric counterpart of Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test statistic by using the spatial-signs instead of the original observations
\boldsymbol{X}_{ij}
’s. However, when
p>n,Q_{n}^{2}
is not defined as the matrix
\mathbf{S}^{-1/2}
is not available in high-dimensional settings. So
6
defined their test statistic

T_{WPL}=\frac{\sum_{i<j}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}}{\sqrt{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\widehat{\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2}\right)}},

where

\widehat{\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2}\right)}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\sum_{j\neq k}\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{j}-\overline{\boldsymbol{U}}_{(j,k)}\right)\boldsymbol{U}_{j}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{U}_{k}-\overline{\boldsymbol{U}}_{(j,k)}\right)\boldsymbol{U}_{k}^{\top}\right\}

is an estimator of
\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2}\right)
. Here
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}=U(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}=E(\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top})
. And
\overline{\boldsymbol{U}}_{(j,k)}=\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{i\not=j,k}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
. Under some sparity structure assumption of the scatter matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, they establish the asymptotic normality of their test statistic, i.e.
T_{WPL}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
. However, when this assumption are not statisfied, as shown in
12
, the asympotic normality do not hold anymore and the empirical sizes of the
T_{WPL}
test is often larger than the nominal level. For any fixed finite
p
, they show that

\displaystyle n\|\overline{\boldsymbol{U}}\|^{2}\xrightarrow{d}\sum_{r=1}^{p}\lambda_{p,r}A_{r}

where
\lambda_{p,r}
being the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}
and
A_{1},\cdots,A_{p}
being i.i.d
\chi_{1}^{2}
random variables. The above expression also holds for
p=\infty
provided that
\lim_{p\xrightarrow{d}\infty}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\infty}
and
\lim_{p\xrightarrow{d}\infty}\lambda_{p,r}=\lambda_{\infty,r}
for all
r=1,2,\ldots
uniformly where
\lambda_{\infty,r}
’s are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\infty}
.
Then, they used
\chi^{2}
-approximation to approximate the above limited null distribution and reject the null hypothesis if
\chi^{2}_{\hat{d}}>\hat{d}n\|\overline{\boldsymbol{U}}\|^{2}
and
\hat{d}=n/((n-1)\widehat{\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2}\right)})
. Even the
\chi^{2}
-approximation is good choice, however, it is not the exact critical value. In the simulation studies, we found that it always has small size than the nonimal level. So, we would recalculate the limit null distribution of
\sum_{i<j}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
and proposed two wild bootstrap method to calculate the critical value.

2.1
Limit null distribution

Consider

S_{n}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}.

Using
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\right\rVert^{2}=1
, we have the identity

S_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\right\|^{2}-\frac{n}{2}.

(2.3)

Set

\displaystyle\tau:=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2}),\qquad\sigma_{n}^{2}:=\binom{n}{2}\tau,\qquad T_{n}:=\frac{S_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}.

(2.4)

Let
\mathcal{F}_{3}
be the class of functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that are three times continuously differentiable and satisfy

\|f^{(r)}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\qquad r=0,1,2,3.

Define, for real random variables
X,Y
,

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(X)-\mathcal{L}(Y)\right\rVert_{3}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{3}}\bigl|\mathbb{E}f(X)-\mathbb{E}f(Y)\bigr|.

(2.5)

Convergence in
\|\cdot\|_{3}
implies weak convergence. Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}
be i.i.d.
N(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})
, independent of
\{\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\}
. Define

S_{n}^{(G)}:=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}G_{i}^{\top}G_{j},\qquad T_{n}^{(G)}:=\frac{S_{n}^{(G)}}{\sigma_{n}}.

Let
\xi\sim N(0,I_{p})
and define the Gaussian quadratic form

Q_{p}:=\frac{\xi^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}\xi-\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})}}=\frac{\xi^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}\xi-1}{\sqrt{2\tau}}.

(2.6)

Define

\kappa_{4}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(U_{1}^{\top}U_{2})^{4}]}{\bigl(\mathbb{E}[(U_{1}^{\top}U_{2})^{2}]\bigr)^{2}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(U_{1}^{\top}U_{2})^{4}]}{\tau^{2}}.

(2.7)

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume
\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=0)=0
and
\tau=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})>0
. Then

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(T_{n})-\mathcal{L}(T_{n}^{(G)})\right\rVert_{3}\ \leq\ C\,\kappa_{4}^{3/4}\,n^{-1/2},

(2.8)

for a universal constant
C>0
. Consequently, if
\kappa_{4}=o(n^{2/3})
, then

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(T_{n})-\mathcal{L}(T_{n}^{(G)})\right\rVert_{3}\to 0.

Theorem 2.2
.

With
T_{n}^{(G)}
and
Q_{p}
defined in (
2.6
),

T_{n}^{(G)}=Q_{p}+o_{p}(1),\qquad n\to\infty,

(2.9)

uniformly over all positive semidefinite
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}
with
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})=1
.

Based on Theorems
2.1
–
2.2
, we can directly have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorems
2.1
–
2.2
and
\kappa_{4}=o(n^{2/3})
,

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(T_{n})-\mathcal{L}(Q_{p})\right\rVert_{3}\to 0.

Hence the asymptotic behavior of
T_{n}
is determined by
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}
through the Gaussian quadratic form
Q_{p}
, with no eigenstructure assumptions on
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.

According to the Corollary 1 in
7
about the limit distribution of the Gaussian quadratic form, we have the following excat distribution of our test statistic
T_{n}
.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}\geq 0
be eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}
, and define normalized weights

\alpha_{i}:=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}^{2}}}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\sqrt{\tau}},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1.

Along any subsequence for which
\alpha_{i}\to\alpha_{i}^{\star}
for each fixed
i
(allowing infinitely many nonzero limits),

Q_{p}\xrightarrow{d}\left(1-\sum_{i\geq 1}(\alpha_{i}^{\star})^{2}\right)^{1/2}Z_{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{i\geq 1}\alpha_{i}^{\star}\,(Z_{i}^{2}-1)\doteq T_{\infty},

where
Z_{0},Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d.
N(0,1)
. The same subsequential limits hold for
T_{n}
whenever
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(T_{n})-\mathcal{L}(Q_{p})\right\rVert_{3}\to 0
.

Corollary 2.5

(A sufficient condition for asymptotic normality)
.

If

\max_{1\leq i\leq p}\alpha_{i}\to 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{4})}{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})^{2}}\to 0,

then
Q_{p}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
, and hence
T_{n}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)
under
\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(T_{n})-\mathcal{L}(Q_{p})\right\rVert_{3}\to 0
.

Next, we give some discussion about the parameter
\kappa_{4}
.

Proposition 2.6

(A universal bound)
.

For any symmetric distribution on the unit sphere with
\mathbb{E}U=0
and
\tau>0
,

1\ \leq\ \kappa_{4}\ \leq\ \frac{1}{\tau}\ \leq\ p.

Proof.

Let
V:=U_{1}^{\top}U_{2}\in[-1,1]
and
W:=V^{2}\in[0,1]
. By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}(W^{2})\geq\mathbb{E}(W)^{2}
, hence
\kappa_{4}=\mathbb{E}(W^{2})/\mathbb{E}(W)^{2}\geq 1
.
Moreover, since
0\leq W\leq 1
we have
W^{2}\leq W
, so
\mathbb{E}(W^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}(W)=\tau
and

\kappa_{4}\leq\tau/\tau^{2}=1/\tau
.
Finally, for any positive semidefinite matrix with
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})=1
,

\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})\geq 1/p
(minimized at
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}=I_{p}/p
), hence
1/\tau\leq p
.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Spherical case)
.

If
\mathbf{\Sigma}\propto\mathbf{I}_{p}
, then
U
is uniform on
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
and

\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{U}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{2})^{2}]=\frac{1}{p},\qquad\mathbb{E}[(\boldsymbol{U}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{2})^{4}]=\frac{3}{p(p+2)},\qquad\kappa_{4}=\frac{3p}{p+2}\to 3.

Remark 2.8

(When
\kappa_{4}
is typically bounded for ACG directions)
.

For angular central Gaussian (ACG) directions

\boldsymbol{U}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}S/\sqrt{S^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}S}
with
S\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
,
a common high-dimensional regime is the effective-rank condition

\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{2}}\to 0\qquad\bigl(\text{equivalently }r_{\mathrm{eff}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}):=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{2}/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2})\to\infty\bigr).

Under this condition, the quadratic form
S^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}S
concentrates around
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma})/p
,
and one can show that for any deterministic unit vector
a\in\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
,

\frac{\mathbb{E}[(a^{\top}U)^{4}]}{\bigl(\mathbb{E}[(a^{\top}U)^{2}]\bigr)^{2}}=\frac{3p}{p+2}\,(1+o(1))\to 3.

Combined with a mild delocalization condition on
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}

(e.g.,
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{4})/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})^{2}\to 0
), this yields
\kappa_{4}\to 3
.
We emphasize that this argument does
not
require a bounded condition number for
\mathbf{\Sigma}
;
it only requires that the spectral energy of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is not concentrated in finitely many directions.

Remark 2.9
.

Assume the scatter matrix is compound symmetric,

\mathbf{\Sigma}=\sigma^{2}\{(1-\rho)I_{p}+\rho\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\},\qquad-1/(p-1)<\rho<1,

and let
u_{0}=\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{p}
. Write
S=Zu_{0}+\sqrt{1-Z^{2}}\,V
with

S\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{p-1})
, where
Z=u_{0}^{\top}S
and

V\in u_{0}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}^{p-2}
is uniform and independent of
Z
.
For
U=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{1/2}S/\sqrt{S^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}S}
, define
A=u_{0}^{\top}U
and
B=\sqrt{1-A^{2}}
.
Then
U=Au_{0}+B\widetilde{V}
with
\widetilde{V}\in u_{0}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}^{p-2}
uniform
conditional on
(A,B)
.

Let
T=Z^{2}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,(p-1)/2)
and

\gamma=\frac{\rho p}{1-\rho}.

A direct eigen-decomposition yields

A^{2}=\frac{(1+\gamma)T}{1+\gamma T}.

Denote
m_{2}=\mathbb{E}(A^{2})
and
m_{4}=\mathbb{E}(A^{4})
.
For
V:=U_{1}^{\top}U_{2}
with independent copies
U_{1},U_{2}
, we have the exact identities

\mathbb{E}(V^{2})=m_{2}^{2}+\frac{(1-m_{2})^{2}}{p-1},

and

\mathbb{E}(V^{4})=m_{4}^{2}+\frac{6}{p-1}(m_{2}-m_{4})^{2}+\frac{3}{(p-1)(p+1)}(1-2m_{2}+m_{4})^{2}.

Therefore,

\kappa_{4}=\frac{\mathbb{E}(V^{4})}{\{\mathbb{E}(V^{2})\}^{2}}=\frac{m_{4}^{2}+\frac{6}{p-1}(m_{2}-m_{4})^{2}+\frac{3}{(p-1)(p+1)}(1-2m_{2}+m_{4})^{2}}{\left(m_{2}^{2}+\frac{(1-m_{2})^{2}}{p-1}\right)^{2}}.

In the regime
\rho p=O(1)
(including
\rho=0
and the admissible negative correlations),
one has
m_{2}\sim 1/p
and
m_{4}\sim 3/(p(p+2))
, which implies
\kappa_{4}\to 3
.
If
\rho\in(0,1)
is fixed, then
\gamma\asymp p
and
pT\xrightarrow{d}W\sim\chi_{1}^{2}
,
so
A^{2}\xrightarrow{d}\rho W/((1-\rho)+\rho W)
and hence
\kappa_{4}\to K(\rho)\in(1,\infty)
.
In particular,
\kappa_{4}=O(1)
for fixed
\rho>0
.

3
Wild Bootstrap

Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
be the sample spatial median, i.e.

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\|.

Define centered sign vectors
\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}:=U(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
and the empirical sign-scatter

\hat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{U}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{\top}.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be i.i.d. Rademacher variables independent of the data:

\mathbb{P}(e_{i}=1)=\mathbb{P}(e_{i}=-1)=1/2
.
Define the bootstrap pseudo-sample
X_{i}^{*}:=e_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
and

\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{*}:=U(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}^{*})=U(e_{i}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}))=e_{i}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}.

Define

T_{R}^{*}:=\frac{1}{\tau^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{*})^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}^{*}=\frac{1}{\tau^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}e_{i}e_{j}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}.

(3.1)

Note that our bootstrap implementation does not require estimating the scale parameter
\tau
.
This is because
T_{n}
and
T_{R}^{*}
share the same scale factor, which cancels out in the comparison
with the bootstrap critical value and therefore does not affect the rejection rule.
We include
\tau
in the definition of the test statistics solely for theoretical convenience.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\cdot)
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}(\cdot)
denote conditional law/probability given the data
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\cdots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\}
.

To get the consistency of the bootstrap procedure, we need the following assumptions.

Assumption 3.1
.

Define
\zeta_{k}=E\left(r_{i}^{-k}\right),r_{i}=\left\|\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\right\|_{2},\nu_{i}=\zeta_{1}^{-1}r_{i}^{-1}
. We assume that

\zeta_{k}\zeta_{1}^{-k}<\zeta\in(0,\infty)
for
k=1,2,3,4
and all
d
.

Assumption 3.2
.

\limsup_{d}\|\mathbf{S}\|_{2}<1-\psi<1
for some positive constant
\psi
.

Assumption 3.3
.

As
n\to\infty
, the ratio
\kappa_{4}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{4})/\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})^{2}

is bounded:
\sup_{n}\kappa_{4}<\infty
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Condition on the data and define the symmetric matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
by

a_{ii}=0
and, for
i\neq j
,

a_{ij}:=\frac{1}{\tau^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}.

Let
W(e):=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}a_{ij}e_{i}e_{j}
with
e_{i}
Rademacher, and

W(g):=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}a_{ij}g_{i}g_{j}
with
g_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
independent.
Then

\big\|\mathcal{L}^{*}(W(e))-\mathcal{L}^{*}(W(g))\big\|_{3}\leq C\Big(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}a_{ij}^{4}\Big)^{1/4},

for a universal constant
C>0
.
Moreover,

\Big(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}a_{ij}^{4}\Big)^{1/4}=O_{p}(\tau^{-1/4}n^{-1/2}),

hence
\|\mathcal{L}^{*}(W(e))-\mathcal{L}^{*}(W(g))\|_{3}=O_{p}(\tau^{-1/4}n^{-1/2})
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.3
hold.
Then, as
n,p\to\infty
,

\big\|\mathcal{L}^{*}(T_{R}^{*})-\mathcal{L}(T_{\infty})\big\|_{3}\to 0,

if
n\tau\to\infty
.

According to Theorem
3.5
, we know that the wild bootstrap procedure based on Rademacher variables can consistenty estimate the critical value of
T_{\infty}
. By Lemma
3.4
, we also consider the following wild bootstrap method based on normal variables: we randomly generate
g_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
and then consider the following bootstrap test statistic:

\displaystyle T^{*}_{N}=\frac{1}{\tau^{1/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}g_{i}g_{j}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}

(3.2)

Based on the wild bootstrap test statistics
T_{R}^{*}
and
T_{N}^{*}
, we generate
M
bootstrap replicates

\{T_{R,m}^{*}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and
\{T_{N,m}^{*}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, respectively, and use their empirical
(1-\alpha)
-quantiles
as critical values. The null hypothesis is rejected whenever the observed test statistic exceeds the corresponding
bootstrap critical value, i.e.,

T_{n}>\widehat{c}_{R,1-\alpha}\quad\text{or}\quad T_{n}>\widehat{c}_{N,1-\alpha},

depending on whether the
TR
or
TN
procedure is applied.

4
Simulation

We evaluate the finite-sample performance of four procedures: the test of
6
(denoted
WPL
), the adaptive spatial-sign test of
12
(denoted
ZGCZ
), the Rademacher wild bootstrap calibration (denoted
TR
), and the Gaussian wild bootstrap calibration (denoted
TN
).
For each replication, we generate i.i.d. observations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

from an elliptically symmetric model with equicorrelated dependence.
Specifically, we set
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{0}_{p}
and

\mathbf{\Sigma}=(1-\rho)\mathbf{I}_{p}+\rho\,\boldsymbol{1}_{p}\boldsymbol{1}_{p}^{\top},

(4.1)

so that
\Sigma_{jj}=1
and
\Sigma_{jk}=\rho
for
j\neq k
.
We consider
\rho\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}
, sample sizes
n\in\{40,80,120\}
, and dimensions

p\in\{100,200,400\}
.
For each
(n,p,\rho)
configuration, we perform
10{,}000
replications.

We examine three representative regimes.

1.

Normal distribution

Conclusion

This paper clarifies the null behavior of the high-dimensional spatial-sign test of
6
under mild assumptions on the dependence structure, showing that the standardized statistic may converge to a non-Gaussian limit that mixes a normal component with a weighted chi-square component. To address the resulting practical challenge of critical value calibration, we develop a wild bootstrap procedure and prove its consistency. Simulation results further indicate that the proposed approach achieves reliable empirical size control across a broad range of correlation patterns and dimension–sample-size configurations.

Several directions appear particularly promising for future research. First, it would be valuable to extend the theory beyond the i.i.d. setting to dependent observations, including time series and spatial data, where robust sign-based procedures are attractive but the limiting behavior can be more intricate. Second, developing power-optimal or adaptive variants—capable of automatically interpolating between dense and sparse alternatives—could improve sensitivity while retaining robustness. Third, it would be useful to incorporate data-driven estimation of the mixing weights (or low-dimensional spectral summaries of the sign-scatter) to yield analytic critical values and reduce bootstrap cost in ultra-high dimensions. Fourth, extending the framework to multi-sample problems (two-sample location, MANOVA-type settings) and to other sign-based statistics would broaden applicability. Finally, a systematic investigation of robustness under contamination, heteroskedasticity, and model misspecification—together with principled tuning and diagnostic tools—would strengthen practical guidance for real data applications.

6.1
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Define the standardized kernel

w_{ij}(x,y):=\frac{x^{\top}y}{\sigma_{n}},\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n,

and the functional

W(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\sum_{i<j}w_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Then
T_{n}=W(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
and
T_{n}^{(G)}=W(G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})
.

Degeneracy holds because
\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{U}_{i}=\mathbb{E}G_{i}=0
: for any fixed
a\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\mathbb{E}[w_{ij}(\boldsymbol{U}_{i},a)]=\sigma_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{U}_{i})^{\top}a=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[w_{ij}(G_{i},a)]=0.

Moreover,

\sigma_{ij}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[w_{ij}(\boldsymbol{U}_{i},\boldsymbol{U}_{j})^{2}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(U_{1}^{\top}U_{2})^{2}]}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}=\frac{\tau}{\binom{n}{2}\tau}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}.

Define the influence of index
k
by

\mathrm{Inf}_{k}:=\sum_{j<k}\sigma_{jk}^{2}+\sum_{j>k}\sigma_{kj}^{2}=(n-1)\cdot\frac{2}{n(n-1)}=\frac{2}{n}.

Hence

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{Inf}_{k}^{3/2}=n\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^{3/2}=2^{3/2}\,n^{-1/2}.

(6.1)

For
k=1,\ldots,n+1
, define

W_{k}:=W(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k-1},U_{k},\ldots,U_{n}).

Then
W_{1}=T_{n}
and
W_{n+1}=T_{n}^{(G)}
, so for any
f\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
,

\mathbb{E}f(W_{1})-\mathbb{E}f(W_{n+1})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}f(W_{k})-\mathbb{E}f(W_{k+1})\Bigr).

Fix
k
. Let
W_{k,0}
denote the same functional with the
k
th input set to
0
:

W_{k,0}:=W(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k-1},0,U_{k+1},\ldots,U_{n}),

where
w_{ij}(0,\cdot)=w_{ij}(\cdot,0)=0
. Then

W_{k}=W_{k,0}+\Delta_{k},\qquad W_{k+1}=W_{k,0}+\Delta_{k}^{\prime},

with

\Delta_{k}=\sum_{i<k}w_{ik}(G_{i},U_{k})+\sum_{j>k}w_{kj}(U_{k},\boldsymbol{U}_{j}),\qquad\Delta_{k}^{\prime}=\sum_{i<k}w_{ik}(G_{i},G_{k})+\sum_{j>k}w_{kj}(G_{k},\boldsymbol{U}_{j}).

Apply Taylor’s theorem around
W_{k,0}
:

f(W_{k,0}+\Delta)=f(W_{k,0})+f^{\prime}(W_{k,0})\Delta+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(W_{k,0})\Delta^{2}+R,

where the remainder satisfies

|R|\leq\frac{1}{6}\|f^{(3)}\|_{\infty}\,|\Delta|^{3}\leq\frac{|\Delta|^{3}}{6}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(W_{k})-f(W_{k+1})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[f^{\prime}(W_{k,0})(\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{k}^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(W_{k,0})(\Delta_{k}^{2}-(\Delta_{k}^{\prime})^{2})\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}(R_{k}-R_{k}^{\prime}).

The first-order term vanishes because, conditioning on all variables except
U_{k}
(respectively
G_{k}
), each summand in
\Delta_{k}
and
\Delta_{k}^{\prime}
has conditional mean
0
by degeneracy. The second-order term vanishes because
G_{k}
is chosen with matching covariance
\mathbb{E}(G_{k}G_{k}^{\top})=\mathbb{E}(U_{k}U_{k}^{\top})=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}
, so the conditional second moments of
\Delta_{k}
and
\Delta_{k}^{\prime}
coincide.

Hence,

\bigl|\mathbb{E}f(W_{k})-\mathbb{E}f(W_{k+1})\bigr|\leq\frac{1}{6}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}|^{3}+\frac{1}{6}\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}^{\prime}|^{3}.

(6.2)

Using
\mathbb{E}|\Delta|^{3}\leq(\mathbb{E}\Delta^{4})^{3/4}
, it suffices to bound
\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}^{4}
and
\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{k}^{\prime})^{4}
.
Conditional on
U_{k}
, the summands in
\Delta_{k}
are independent across indices (they depend on disjoint collections of
G_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{j}
), and have conditional mean
0
. A standard fourth-moment inequality for sums of independent mean-zero variables yields

\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{k}^{4})\leq C_{0}\Bigl(\mathrm{Inf}_{k}^{2}+\sum_{j\neq k}\mathbb{E}[w_{kj}(\cdot)^{4}]\Bigr)

for a universal constant
C_{0}>0
, where the fourth moments are controlled by
\kappa_{4}
through

\mathbb{E}[w_{kj}(U_{k},\boldsymbol{U}_{j})^{4}]=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(U_{1}^{\top}U_{2})^{4}]}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}=\kappa_{4}\,\frac{\tau^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}=\kappa_{4}\,\sigma_{kj}^{4}.

The Gaussian/mixed terms satisfy analogous hypercontractive bounds, so overall

\mathbb{E}(\Delta_{k}^{4})\leq C_{1}\,\kappa_{4}\,\mathrm{Inf}_{k}^{2}

for a universal constant
C_{1}>0
, and similarly for
\Delta_{k}^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}|^{3}\leq(\mathbb{E}\Delta_{k}^{4})^{3/4}\leq C_{1}^{3/4}\kappa_{4}^{3/4}\mathrm{Inf}_{k}^{3/2},\qquad\mathbb{E}|\Delta_{k}^{\prime}|^{3}\leq C_{1}^{3/4}\kappa_{4}^{3/4}\mathrm{Inf}_{k}^{3/2}.

Plugging into (
6.2
), summing over
k
, and using (
6.1
) yields

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{3}}\bigl|\mathbb{E}f(T_{n})-\mathbb{E}f(T_{n}^{(G)})\bigr|\leq C\,\kappa_{4}^{3/4}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathrm{Inf}_{k}^{3/2}\leq C\,\kappa_{4}^{3/4}\,n^{-1/2},

which is (
2.8
). ∎

6.2
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Using the analogue of (
2.3
),

S_{n}^{(G)}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}\|^{2}.

(6.3)

Let
W_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}
. Then
W_{n}\sim N(0,n\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})
, so
W_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sqrt{n}\,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{1/2}\xi
and

\frac{1}{2}\|W_{n}\|^{2}=\frac{n}{2}\,\xi^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}\xi.

(6.4)

Also,
\mathbb{E}\|G_{i}\|^{2}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})=1
. Write

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}\|^{2}=n+R_{n},\qquad\mathbb{E}R_{n}=0.

Since
\mathrm{Var}(\|G_{1}\|^{2})=2\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}^{2})=2\tau
for Gaussian
G_{1}\sim N(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U})
,

\mathrm{Var}(R_{n})=n\mathrm{Var}(\|G_{1}\|^{2})=2n\tau.

Combining with (
6.3
)–(
6.4
) gives

S_{n}^{(G)}=\frac{n}{2}\bigl(\xi^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}\xi-1\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}R_{n}.

Divide by
\sigma_{n}=\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}\tau}\sim n\sqrt{\tau/2}
:

T_{n}^{(G)}=\frac{\xi^{\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{U}\xi-1}{\sqrt{2\tau}}-\frac{R_{n}}{2\sigma_{n}}=Q_{p}-\frac{R_{n}}{2\sigma_{n}}.

Finally,

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{R_{n}}{2\sigma_{n}}\right)=\frac{2n\tau}{4\sigma_{n}^{2}}=\frac{n\tau}{2\binom{n}{2}\tau}=\frac{1}{n-1}\to 0,

so

6.3
Proof of Lemma
3.4

Proof.

We use a Lindeberg replacement.
Let
(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
be i.i.d. Rademacher and
(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})
be i.i.d.
N(0,1)
,
independent of each other and of the data.
For
k=0,1,\ldots,n
, define the hybrid vector

Z^{(k)}:=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k},\xi_{k+1},\ldots,\xi_{n}),\qquad W_{k}:=\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}Z^{(k)}_{i}Z^{(k)}_{j}.

Then
W_{0}=W(e)
and
W_{n}=W(g)
, and by telescoping,

\Big|\mathbb{E}^{*}f(W_{0})-\mathbb{E}^{*}f(W_{n})\Big|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big|\mathbb{E}^{*}\{f(W_{k-1})-f(W_{k})\}\Big|,\qquad f\in\mathcal{F}_{3}.

Fix
k
. Write
W_{k-1}=\alpha+\xi_{k}\beta
and
W_{k}=\alpha+\eta_{k}\beta
, where

\alpha
and
\beta
are functions of
\{Z^{(k)}_{\ell}:\ell\neq k\}
and the data.
By Taylor expansion of
f(\alpha+t\beta)
around
t=0
up to third order,
and using
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{k})=\mathbb{E}(\eta_{k})=0
,
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{k}^{2})=\mathbb{E}(\eta_{k}^{2})=1
,
and
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{k}^{3})=\mathbb{E}(\eta_{k}^{3})=0
, we obtain

\Big|\mathbb{E}^{*}\{f(W_{k-1})-f(W_{k})\}\Big|\leq C\,\mathbb{E}^{*}(|\beta|^{3})\cdot\Big|\mathbb{E}(\xi_{k}^{4})-\mathbb{E}(\eta_{k}^{4})\Big|\leq C^{\prime}\,\mathbb{E}^{*}(|\beta|^{3}),

since
\mathbb{E}(\xi_{k}^{4})=1
and
\mathbb{E}(\eta_{k}^{4})=3
.
Now
\beta=\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}Z^{(k)}_{j}
.
By Burkholder/Rosenthal and
\|a_{k\cdot}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}^{2}
,

\mathbb{E}^{*}(|\beta|^{3})\leq C\Big(\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}^{2}\Big)^{3/2}
.
Summing over
k
yields

\big|\mathbb{E}^{*}f(W(e))-\mathbb{E}^{*}f(W(g))\big|\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}^{2}\Big)^{3/2}.

Finally, by Hölder,

\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}^{2})^{3/2}\leq(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}^{2})^{3/4}(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}^{4})^{1/4}\asymp(\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^{4})^{1/4}
,
because
\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^{2}=1
by construction.
Taking the supremum over
f\in\mathcal{F}_{3}
gives the first bound.

For the rate, note
|a_{ij}|\leq C\tau^{-1/2}/\sqrt{\binom{n}{2}}\leq C^{\prime}n^{-1}\tau^{-1/2}
and
\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^{2}
is bounded,
hence
\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^{4}\leq(\max_{i<j}a_{ij}^{2})\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^{2}=O_{p}(\tau^{-1}n^{-2})
,
so
(\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}^{4})^{1/4}=O_{p}(\tau^{-1/4}n^{-1/2})
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.5

Proof.

Let
\hat{W}=(\hat{w}_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}

\hat{w}_{ij}=\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{U}}_{j}

Let
\mathbf{g}\sim N(0,I_{n})

3.4

T_{R}^{*}

\hat{A}=Q^{\top}\diag(\hat{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\nu}_{n})Q
