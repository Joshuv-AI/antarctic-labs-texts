Optimal control for phase locking of synchronized oscillator populations via dynamical reduction techniques
Narumi Fujii, Hiroya Nakao

Abstract
We present a framework for controlling the collective phase of a system of coupled oscillators described by the Kuramoto model under the influence of a periodic external input by combining the methods of dynamical reduction and optimal control. We employ the Ott-Antonsen ansatz and phase-amplitude reduction theory to derive a pair of one-dimensional equations for the collective phase and amplitude of mutually synchronized oscillators. We then use optimal control theory to derive the optimal input for controlling the collective phase based on the phase equation and evaluate the effect of the control input on the degree of mutual synchrony using the amplitude equation. We set up an optimal control problem for the system to quickly resynchronize with the periodic input after a sudden phase shift in the periodic input, a situation similar to jet lag, and demonstrate the validity of the framework through numerical simulations.



Synchronization of collective oscillations is crucial in biological systems, such as circadian rhythms.
As the loss of synchronization due to disturbances may impair their functionality, e.g., discomfort from jet lag,
a quick recovery to the appropriate synchronized state is desirable.
Like circadian rhythms generated by many neurons, collective oscillations in real-world systems often involve a large number of elements and are difficult to control due to their high dimensionality.
In this study, we present a framework for controlling the collective phase of synchronized oscillator populations using dynamical reduction techniques and optimal control, which enables control of the oscillator population through low-dimensional reduced dynamics.

II.1
Kuramoto model with a periodic input

The Kuramoto model describes a system of phase oscillators with all-to-all coupling
[
6
]
. We consider a Kuramoto model subjected to a sinusoidal periodic input, described by

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{j}=\omega_{j}+\frac{K}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sin(\phi_{k}-\phi_{j})+z(\phi_{j})p_{0}\sin\Theta(t),~~

\displaystyle(j=1,\cdots,N),

(1)

where
\phi_{j}
and
\omega_{j}
are the phase and the natural frequency of the
j

is the coupling strength,

is the number of oscillators,

is
the individual phase sensitivity function
(a.k.a. infinitesimal phase response function / phase resetting curve)
characterizing the linear response of each oscillator to applied perturbations
[
6
,
41
]
,
p_{0}
is the amplitude of the periodic input, and
\Theta(t)
is the phase of the periodic input that increases with a constant frequency
\omega_{p}
.
We assume that the system is high-dimensional
(N\gg 1)
, the amplitude
p_{0}
of the input is sufficiently small,
and
\omega_{j}
follows a Lorentz distribution,

\displaystyle g(\omega)=\frac{\gamma}{\pi}\frac{1}{\left(\omega-\omega_{0}\right)^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

(2)

where
\omega_{0}
is the central frequency and
\gamma
is the width parameter.
We assume that the input frequency
\omega_{p}
is close to
\omega_{0}
,
and that the individual phase sensitivity function is given by a simple sinusoidal form,
z(\phi_{j})=\sin\phi_{j}

The above Kuramoto model with the lowest-harmonic sinusoidal individual phase sensitivity function can be obtained by phase reduction of a system of globally coupled Stuart-Landau oscillators driven by a periodic input
[
6
,
41
]
.

The complex order parameter characterizing the degree of mutual synchrony of the system, which we also call the complex mean field, is defined as
[
6
]

\displaystyle A(t)=R(t)e^{i\Phi(t)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{i\phi_{j}(t)}.

(3)

Here,
i=\sqrt{-1}
is the imaginary unit, the modulus
R
of
A

gives the amplitude of the complex mean field characterizing the degree of mutual synchrony, and the argument
\Phi
of
A
represents the collective phase of the complex mean field, i.e., the collective phase of the system;

takes a value in
[0,1]
, where
R=0
when the oscillators are incoherent and
R=1
when the oscillators are completely synchronized.

By using
R(t)
and
\Phi(t)
, we can rewrite the interaction term in Eq. (
1
) as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{j}=\omega_{j}+KR(t)\sin(\Phi(t)-\phi_{j})+z(\phi_{j})p_{0}\sin\Theta(t).

(4)

Thus, we can represent Eq. (
1
) formally as a one-body problem driven by the complex mean field and the periodic input.

II.2
Loss of synchronization with the periodic input

In this study, we assume that the system is exhibiting synchronized collective oscillations under the periodic input,
and focus on the recovery of the system from a loss of synchronization with the periodic input.
When the system is synchronized with (phase-locked to) the periodic input, the difference
\Psi(t)=\Phi(t)-\Theta(t)
between the corrective phase
\Phi(t)
of the system and the phase
\Theta(t)
of the periodic input is kept small.
This occurs when the frequency
\omega_{p}
of the periodic input is close to
\omega_{0}
.

We consider a jet lag like situation and introduce a sudden phase shift in the periodic input
in the steady synchronized state of the system with the periodic input, given by

\displaystyle\Theta(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\omega_{p}t\quad(0\leq t<t_{s}),\\
\omega_{p}t+\Delta\Theta\quad(t\geq t_{s}),\end{array}\right.

where
\Delta\Theta
is the phase shift of the periodic input and
t_{s}
is the time at which the phase shift is applied.

Figure
1
shows the time series of the phase difference
\Psi(t)
for several values of the given phase shift,
\Delta\Theta=0.4\pi,~0.5\pi,~0.6\pi,~0.7\pi,
and
0.8\pi
, where the phase shift is introduced at
t_{s}=100
.
Before the phase shift, the phase difference
\Psi(t)
exhibits small-amplitude oscillations around
a certain value
\Psi_{0}

and does not grow, i.e., the collective phase
\Phi(t)
of the system is locked to the phase
\Theta(t)
of the external input.
Note that we observe small-amplitude oscillations of
\Psi(t)
in the phase-locked state because the effect of the periodic input is not averaged over time in the present model; the phase difference converges to a constant only when further averaging is performed on Eq. (
1
)
[
41
]
.
By the introduction of the phase shift of the periodic input at
t_{s}
, the phase difference
\Psi(t)
is suddenly shifted to a different value from
\Psi_{0}
, and then gradually goes back to
\Psi_{0}

We define that the time difference

between the time

at which the input phase is shifted and the time

at which
\Psi(t)
returns to
\Psi_{0}
for the first time after

as the recovery time.
The dependence of the recovery time on the phase shift

is shown in Fig.
2

The recovery time takes a peak around

. In the context of jet lag, this is called the jet lag separatrix, and is considered to be related to the east-west asymmetry in the recovery time from jet lag, as discussed in
[
42
,
43

II.3
Control of resynchronization

In this study, we use optimal control theory to derive the control input that realizes quick recovery from the loss of synchrony with the periodic input after the sudden phase shift, as shown schematically in Fig.
3
.
Since the system is high-dimensional (
N\gg 1
), control of all oscillator phases
by applying each individual oscillator a different input

is impossible.
Therefore, we assume that all oscillators receive a common control input
u(t)
in addition to the periodic input
p_{0}\sin\Theta(t)
as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{j}=\omega_{j}

\displaystyle+KR(t)\sin(\Phi(t)-\phi_{j})+z(\phi_{j})p(t),

(7)

for all
j
, where

\displaystyle p(t)=p_{0}\sin\Theta(t)+u(t).

(8)

We also assume that the net input
p(t)
to the system is weak.
That is, we aim to reestablish phase locking with the periodic input using only a weak control input, so that the control input does not disturb the collective synchronization of the oscillators in the system, which may play important functions in the case of biological systems
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

As our interest is on the collective phase of
mutually synchronized oscillators, in the following sections,
we first consider the continuum limit with

and derive a low-dimensional reduced equation describing the system’s collective dynamics,
and further reduce it to a pair of phase-amplitude equations. We then design

using the optimal control theory.

III
Dynamical reduction

In this section, we briefly review the continuum limit of the Kuramoto model and the OA ansatz, and then perform phase-amplitude reduction of the resulting equation for the complex order parameter.

III.1
Continuum limit

We first consider the continuum limit of the Kuramoto model with an external input

]
.
We introduce the probability density function
P(\phi,\omega,t)
of the phase
\phi

\omega

In the

\displaystyle A(t)

\displaystyle=R(t)e^{i\Phi(t)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\phi^{\prime}}P(\phi^{\prime},~\omega^{\prime},~t)g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}d\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left[\{\omega+KR(t)\sin\left(\Phi(t)-\phi\right)+p(t)\sin\phi\}P\right].

Using Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\Bigg[\Bigg\{\omega+p(t)\sin\phi

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle K\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sin(\phi^{\prime}-\phi)P(\phi^{\prime},\omega^{\prime},t)g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}d\phi^{\prime}\Bigg\}P\Bigg].

Since oscillators with different

III.2
Ott-Antonsen ansatz

Next, we introduce the OA ansatz to reduce Eq. (
11

]

We expand
P(\phi,\omega,t)

\displaystyle P(\phi,~\omega,~t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}P_{m}(\omega,~t)e^{im\phi}.

(12)

Substituting Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{m}

\displaystyle=-im\omega P_{m}-\frac{mp(t)}{2}\left(P_{m-1}-P_{m+1}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{Km}{2}\Big\{P_{m-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{1}(\omega^{\prime},~t)g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle-P_{m+1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}P_{-1}(\omega^{\prime},~t)g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}\Big\}

Ott and Antonsen assumed a special class of

\displaystyle P_{m}(\omega,~t)=\alpha(\omega,~t)^{m},~~(m=1,~2,~\cdots),

(14)

Note that

Plugging this form into Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\alpha

\displaystyle=-i\omega\alpha-\frac{p(t)}{2}\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{K}{2}\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha(\omega^{\prime},~t)g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle-\alpha^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha(\omega^{\prime},~t)^{-1}g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}\Big\}

for

From Eqs. (
9
) and (

\displaystyle A(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha(\omega^{\prime},~t)^{-1}g(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime},

(16)

and since

\displaystyle\alpha(\omega,~t)^{m}=\overline{\left\{\alpha(\omega,~t)^{-m}\right\}}=\left\{\overline{\alpha(\omega,~t)}\right\}^{-m}.

Therefore, Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\alpha=-i\omega\alpha-\frac{p(t)}{2}\left(1-\alpha^{2}\right)+\frac{K}{2}\{\overline{A(t)}-\alpha^{2}A(t)\}.

For the Lorentzian

2

\displaystyle A(t)=\overline{\alpha(\omega_{0}-i\gamma,~t)}

(19)

Thus, the complex order parameter

\displaystyle\dot{A}(t)=\left(\frac{K}{2}-\gamma+i\omega_{0}\right){A}(t)-\frac{K}{2}{A}(t)|A(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{p(t)}{2}\{1-{A}(t)^{2}\}.

(20)

This equation represents two-dimensional dynamics of the complex order parameter

When the perturbation

If

In what follows, we assume that the parameters

Phase-amplitude reduction

\displaystyle=-KR_{0}^{2}r+\text{grad}_{\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{X}(t)}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{X})\cdot\boldsymbol{G}(\boldsymbol{X}(t))p(t)

\displaystyle\simeq-KR_{0}^{2}r+\text{grad}_{\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{\chi}(\varphi(t))}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{X})\cdot\boldsymbol{G}\left(\boldsymbol{\chi}(\varphi(t))\right)p(t)

\displaystyle=-KR_{0}^{2}r+\boldsymbol{I}(\varphi(t))\cdot\boldsymbol{G}(\varphi(t))p(t),

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{I}(\varphi)=\text{grad}_{\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{\chi}(\varphi)}\Gamma(\boldsymbol{X})
is the amplitude sensitivity function (a.k.a. isostable response function)
[
45
,
39
,
46
]
.

For the Stuart-Landau oscillator, the phase and amplitude sensitivity functions can be explicitly calculated as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R_{0}}(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi),

(35)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=KR_{0}(\cos\varphi,~\sin\varphi).

(36)

Then, Eqs. (
33
) and (
34
) are explicitly given as

\displaystyle\dot{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}+\zeta(\varphi)p(t),

(37)

\displaystyle\dot{r}

\displaystyle=-KR_{0}^{2}r+\kappa(\varphi)p(t),

(38)

respectively, where we defined the collective phase and amplitude sensitivity functions as

\displaystyle\zeta(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{R_{0}^{2}+1}{R_{0}}\sin\varphi,

(39)

\displaystyle\kappa(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\frac{KR_{0}}{2}(R_{0}^{2}-1)\cos\varphi.

(40)

Equation (
37
) describes the collective phase dynamics of the system of coupled oscillators under the external input, which will be used to design the control input,
and Eq. (
38
) describes the deviation from the unperturbed collective oscillations, which will be used to evaluate the effect of the external input on the mutual synchrony in the numerical simulation.

IV
Optimal control

In this section, we design the control input
u(t)

to quickly resynchronize the system with the periodic input after the phase shift disturbance as given in Eq. (
II.2
).
We use the reduced phase dynamics for the complex mean field, Eq. (
37
), instead of the original high-dimensional Kuramoto model, Eq. (
7
), to design the control input.

We formulate the optimal control problem
[
37
,
38
]
for the phase difference

\hat{\Psi}(t)=\varphi(t)-\Theta(t)
as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\Psi}(t),~u(t)}~~J=\eta\left(\hat{\Psi}(t_{f})\right)+\int_{t_{s}}^{t_{f}}L\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),u(t)\right)dt,

(41)

s.t.

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\Psi}}=\Delta\omega+\zeta(\hat{\Psi}(t)+\Theta(t))\left(p_{0}\sin\Theta(t)+u(t)\right),

\displaystyle\hskip 7.68222pt\eqcolon f\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t)\right),

(42)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}\left(t_{s}\right)=\varphi\left(t_{s}\right)-\Theta\left(t_{s}\right)=\Phi\left(t_{s}\right)-\Theta\left(t_{s}\right),

(43)

\displaystyle|p(t)|=|p_{0}\sin\Theta(t)+u(t)|\leq p_{0},

(44)

where
\Delta\omega=\omega_{0}-\omega_{p}
.
Here, the initial time of the control is taken as
t_{s}
at which the phase shift is applied to
\Theta(t)
, and
t_{f}
is the terminal time of the control, which is set as
t_{f}=t_{s}+2T_{p}
where
T_{p}
is the period of the
original periodic
input
p_{0}\sin\Theta(t)
.
The constraints are as follows:
Eq. (
42
) gives the dynamics of
\hat{\Psi}
,
Eq. (
43
) is the initial condition,
and Eq. (
44
) is the condition on the range of the control input,
that is, the magnitude of the net external input including
u(t)
does not
exceed the magnitude of the original periodic input
p_{0}
.

The cost function
J
consists of the terminal cost and the stage cost,
both of which are assumed to be
quadratic functions of the sine of the phase difference from the target, i.e.,

\displaystyle\eta\left(\hat{\Psi}(t_{f})\right)

\displaystyle={S_{f}}\sin^{2}\left({\frac{\hat{\Psi}(t_{f})-{\Psi}_{0}}{2}}\right),

(45)

\displaystyle L\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),u(t)\right)

\displaystyle=Q\sin^{2}\left({\frac{\hat{\Psi}(t)-{\Psi}_{0}}{2}}\right)+\frac{W}{2}{u(t)}^{2},

(46)

where
\Psi_{0}
is the target phase difference,

S_{f}>0
is the weight of the terminal cost associated with the phase difference,

Q>0
is the weight of the stage cost of the phase difference,
and
W>0
is the weight of the stage cost of the control input.

To handle the inequality constraint on the range of the input, we use the penalty function method.
We introduce the penalty function as

\displaystyle P(\Theta(t),u(t))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{2}\left(\max\left\{g_{l}(\Theta(t),u(t)),0\right\}\right)^{2},

(47)

where
g_{1}(\Theta(t),u(t))=p_{0}\left(\sin\Theta(t)-1\right)+u(t)
and

g_{2}(\Theta(t),u(t))=-p_{0}\left(\sin\Theta(t)+1\right)-u(t)
.
Introducing the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda(t)
,
the total cost function including the constraints and the penalty is given by

\displaystyle\bar{J}=J+\int_{t_{s}}^{t_{f}}\Big\{\lambda(t)\left(f(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t))-\dot{\hat{\Psi}}(t)\right)

\displaystyle+BP\left(\Theta(t),u(t)\right)\Big\}dt,

(48)

where
B>0
is the weight of the penalty.

The control Hamiltonian of the above optimal control problem is given by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t),\lambda(t)\right)

\displaystyle=L\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),u(t)\right)+\lambda(t)f\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+BP(\Theta(t),u(t)),

(49)

and, from the extremum conditions for
\bar{J}
, we obtain the following set of equations;

\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\Psi}}=f\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t)\right),

(50)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}\left(t_{s}\right)=\varphi\left(t_{s}\right)-\Theta\left(t_{s}\right)=\Phi\left(t_{s}\right)-\Theta\left(t_{s}\right),

(51)

\displaystyle\dot{\lambda}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\Psi}}H\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t),\lambda(t)\right),

(52)

\displaystyle\lambda\left(t_{f}\right)=\frac{d}{d\hat{\Psi}}\eta\left(\hat{\Psi}(t_{f})\right),

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial u}H\left(\hat{\Psi}(t),\Theta(t),u(t),\lambda(t)\right)=0.

(54)

Solving this two-point boundary value problem numerically, the control input
u(t)
is obtained from Eq. (
54
).

The control input
u(t)
also affects the degree of mutual synchrony, i.e., the value of
R(t)
, while it may be undesirable to disrupt the mutual synchrony by the control.
We can use the amplitude equation to evaluate the effect of
u(t)
on
R(t)
.
We here derive the sufficient condition on
r(t)
for the amplitude decay
-KR_{0}^{2}r
to dominate the dynamics of
r(t)
within linear approximation.
That is, we seek the condition that

\displaystyle KR_{0}^{2}|r(t)|\geq|\kappa(\varphi(t))p(t)|

(55)

holds in Eq. (
38
)
for any
\kappa(\varphi(t))
in Eq. (
40
) and

p(t)
in Eq. (
44
).
If Eq. (
40
), Eq. (
44
), and
R_{0}\leq 1
are satisfied, then

\displaystyle\frac{KR_{0}p_{0}}{2}(1-R_{0}^{2})\geq|\kappa(\varphi(t))p(t)|

(56)

holds.
Thus, the sufficient condition on
r(t)
to satisfy Eq. (
55
)
for any
\kappa(\varphi(t))
in Eq. (
40
) and

p(t)
in Eq. (
44
) is given by

\displaystyle KR_{0}^{2}|r(t)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{KR_{0}p_{0}}{2}(1-R_{0}^{2}),

(57)

i.e.,

\displaystyle|r(t)|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p_{0}}{2R_{0}}(1-R_{0}^{2}).

(58)

As long as
r(t)
satisfies Eq. (
57
), the system state approaches the limit cycle even
under the external input
p(t)
containing the control input
u(t)

because the decay term
-KR_{0}^{2}r
dominates.
Here, the condition Eq. (
57
) for the original amplitude

R=\sqrt{X^{2}+Y^{2}}
is obtained by using the amplitude function in Eq. (
32
) as

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{R_{0}^{2}}{\left(R(t)\right)^{2}}\right|\geq\frac{p_{0}}{KR_{0}}(1-R_{0}^{2}).

(59)

The above condition can be expressed as

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle\geq R_{0}\sqrt{\frac{KR_{0}}{-(1-R_{0}^{2})p_{0}+KR_{0}}}\eqcolon R_{+},

(60)

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle\leq R_{0}\sqrt{\frac{KR_{0}}{(1-R_{0}^{2})p_{0}+KR_{0}}}\eqcolon R_{-},

(61)

where we used
-(1-R_{0}^{2})p_{0}+KR_{0}>0
in our parameter setting.
Thus, if
R
satisfies Eqs. (
60
) and (
61
), the system state always approaches the limit cycle,
and the system state is expected to stay within a certain distance from the limit cycle, i.e.
between
R_{+}
and
R_{-}
.

R_{0}

That is, the control input gives only a limited effect on the mutual synchrony and does not degrade it.
Thus, the effect of the control input on the degree of mutual synchrony can be estimated by using the amplitude equation.

VI
Conclusion

We presented a framework to control the synchronization of the high-dimensional Kuramoto model with
a periodic input by applying the optimal control theory to the low-dimensional equation derived by
dynamical reduction methods, i.e., the Ott-Antonsen ansatz and phase-amplitude reduction.
Considering a jet lag like situation, we set up a control problem for the system to recover quickly from the loss of synchronization with the periodic input, and demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed framework by numerical simulations.
In addition, we showed that the control input did not degrade the degree of mutual synchrony by evaluating
the effect of the input using the amplitude equation.

In this study, we treated the simple Kuramoto model as the controlled object.
However, it may not fully capture synchronization in real-world systems.
Many studies have been conducted to extend the Kuramoto model to reproduce more complex dynamics:
for example, the cases where the coupling strength depends on time
[
48
]
, the cases involving higher-order interactions determined by relationships among three or more oscillators
[
49
,
50
]
,
and the cases where interactions include time delays
[
51
]
.
Considering these studies, it is desirable to expand our control framework to more complex systems of coupled oscillators.

Our control framework in the present study is feedforward and calculates the control input beforehand.
Since the dynamical reduction approach can significantly shorten the computation time,
it is also well suited to real-time control methods, such as model predictive control,
which solve the optimal control problem at each time step in real time.
By combining the reduced dynamics with model predictive control, it may be possible to control
high-dimensional systems even in more complex situations, such as trajectory tracking control.
