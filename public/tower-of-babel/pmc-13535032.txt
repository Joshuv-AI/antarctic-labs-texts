Cultural Drivers, Modes of Use, and Perceived Harms of Traditional and Non-Traditional Smokeless Tobacco Use in South Africa: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Senamile P Ngobese, Olalekan A Ayo-Yusuf, Catherine O Egbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSmokeless tobacco (SLT) is used not only for recreation, but also for traditional healing, spiritual practices, and medicinal purposes. Understanding the motivations and lived experiences underlying these diverse forms of use is important for developing culturally-appropriate tobacco control interventions. This study explored the drivers, mode of use, perceived harms and lived experiences associated with traditional and non-traditional SLT use among adults in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological design was used. In-depth interviews were conducted with 25 adults who used SLT primarily for traditional purposes (traditional health practitioners [THPs] and trainees/initiates [n=12]) or non-traditional purposes (n=13). Participants were recruited using convenience, purposive and snowball sampling. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, translated where necessary, and analysed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework aided by NVivo v12.ResultsFour key themes emerged: motivations for SLT use, modes and practices of use, experienced harms, and perceptions of harmfulness. Traditional users described SLT use as part of ancestral communication, initiation into traditional healing, ritual practices, and traditional treatment. Non-traditional users reported using SLT for stress relief, perceived medicinal benefits, sexual enhancement, affordability compared with smoking, and social influence. Multiple modes of use were reported, including oral, nasal, intravaginal, and external ritual use. Participants described health-related, social, and financial harms associated with SLT use. However, some traditional users perceived SLT used within traditional healing contexts as protected from harm through ancestral or spiritual authority.ConclusionSLT use in KwaZulu-Natal is shaped by cultural, spiritual, medicinal, psychological, sexual, and economic motivations. Although participants reported multiple harms, cultural beliefs and perceptions of spiritual protection may influence risk perceptions and continued use. Public health interventions should address both the health risks of SLT and the cultural meanings attached to its use while engaging THPs and communities in developing culturally-acceptable prevention and cessation approaches.Keywords: smokeless tobacco, ancestral practices, traditional healing, non-traditional use, South Africa

INTRODUCTION

Smokeless tobacco (SLT) use remains an important but often overlooked public health challenge. Globally, it is estimated that about 359 million people use SLT products, which are consumed through oral, nasal, or other routes and expose users to nicotine and numerous toxic and carcinogenic compounds. 1 SLT use has been causally associated with cancers of the oral cavity, oesophagus and pancreas, periodontal disease, adverse reproductive outcomes, cardiovascular disease, nicotine dependence and premature mortality.1-3 Although the burden of SLT has been extensively documented in South and Southeast Asia,4,5 its use in many African countries6,7 remains comparatively under-researched despite evidence that it occurs within diverse cultural contexts.

In many African settings, SLT use extends beyond recreational nicotine consumption and is embedded within indigenous systems of knowledge, traditional healing, religious and spiritual practices, and perceived medicinal applications.8-10 It may be used during ancestral communication, initiation rituals, ceremonial practices, or as part of traditional therapeutic prescriptions, while others use it for stress relief, self-medication, sexual enhancement, or social reasons. These diverse contexts suggest that initiation and continued use are often shaped not only by nicotine dependence but also by cultural beliefs, social norms, perceived therapeutic benefits, and spiritual obligations.

South Africa provides an important context for examining these issues. Although cigarette smoking remains the predominant form of tobacco use, national survey data indicate that approximately 4.3% of adults currently use SLT, with prevalence substantially higher among women than men. 11 The higher prevalence of SLT use among women compared with men is important to note, as this sex difference was not noted for other tobacco products, which may be indicative of South African women’s preference for SLT compared to other tobacco products. For instance, particularly in the rural KwaZulu-Natal area, women reported that inserting SLT into their vaginas was believed to please their male partners. 12

KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) province in South Africa is particularly relevant for understanding culturally embedded SLT use because traditional healing remains an integral component of health-seeking behaviour in many communities. 13 Traditional health practitioners (THPs) continue to play important roles not only in the treatment of illness but also as custodians of indigenous knowledge, cultural traditions, counselling, spiritual guidance and ancestral practices. 13 Many people consult THPs alongside biomedical services, particularly for illnesses perceived to have social, cultural or spiritual origins. The province, therefore, provides an appropriate setting for exploring how indigenous and biomedical health systems coexist and how these influence tobacco-related behaviours.

Previous South African studies have described the prevalence of SLT use, identified socio-demographic correlates, and documented selected traditional beliefs associated with its use.8,9,14 International qualitative studies have also explored psychosocial determinants of SLT use in other cultural settings. 10 However, there remains limited qualitative evidence describing how users themselves understand SLT use across traditional and non-traditional contexts, how cultural and spiritual beliefs influence motivations for use, why different routes of administration are adopted, and how these beliefs shape perceptions of harm and continued use.

Kleinman 15 in a seminal paper, argued that health behaviours are interpreted through culturally constructed explanatory models that influence how people understand illness, treatment and health risks. Consequently, behaviours that appear irrational from a biomedical perspective may be perceived as beneficial or even essential within other knowledge systems. This perspective is particularly relevant to SLT use within traditional healing, where perceived benefits may coexist with awareness of biomedical harms. Implementation science similarly recognises that health interventions are more likely to be effective when they are adapted to local cultural contexts and belief systems rather than implemented as uniform approaches. 16 Consistent with Article 12 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, which encourages culturally appropriate public education and awareness programmes, understanding the cultural meanings attached to SLT use is therefore essential for designing effective prevention and cessation interventions. 17

This study therefore explored the drivers, modes of use, perceived harms and lived experiences associated with traditional and non-traditional SLT use among adults in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. By including traditional health practitioners and non-traditional users, the study provides new insights into how cultural beliefs shape tobacco use behaviours, perceptions of harm, and opportunities for culturally responsive tobacco control policies, cessation interventions and harm-reduction strategies.

METHODS

Research Design

A qualitative study underpinned by a phenomenological approach was conducted to explore the lived experiences, motivations, modes of use, and perceived harms associated with smokeless tobacco (SLT) use among adults in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. This approach was considered appropriate because it seeks to understand how individuals experience and interpret a phenomenon within their social and cultural contexts.

Setting

The study was conducted in KwaZulu-Natal, one of South Africa’s nine provinces where traditional healing remains an integral component of health-seeking behaviour in many communities. The province is home to a large network of traditional health practitioners (THPs), who continue to play important roles in healthcare delivery, spiritual guidance, cultural practices, and the preservation of indigenous knowledge. 13 This context provided an appropriate setting for exploring both traditional and non-traditional SLT use.

Participant Selection and Recruitment

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling, supplemented by snowball sampling to identify additional eligible participants within traditional healing networks and among non-traditional SLT users. Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) who currently used SLT for either traditional or non-traditional purposes.

Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which additional interviews yielded no substantially new codes or themes across participant groups.18,19

Participants (with operational definitions detailed below) comprised three groups:

1. Practising traditional health practitioners (THPs);

1. Practising traditional health practitioners (THPs);

2. THP Patients/Trainees (amathwasa) undergoing initiation and apprenticeship into traditional healing following an ancestral calling; and

2. THP Patients/Trainees (amathwasa) undergoing initiation and apprenticeship into traditional healing following an ancestral calling; and

3. Adults who used SLT outside traditional healing practices (non-traditional users).

3. Adults who used SLT outside traditional healing practices (non-traditional users).

Operational Definitions

Those Who Use SLT for Traditional Reasons

For this study, those who use SLT for traditional purposes were defined as people who use SLT for ancestral, ritual, initiation, or for traditional healing-related reasons, including SLT use prescribed, taught, or supervised by traditional health practitioners (THPs). THPs were defined as traditional healers who used SLT as part of their traditional healing practice. Patients of THPs were defined as adults who had consulted with THPs for traditional treatment and had been advised to use SLT as part of that treatment. During the fieldwork, these patients were found to be THP trainees, traditionally called “amathwasa”, meaning ‘initiates’ or ‘trainees’ who were responding to an ancestral or spiritual calling to become THPs. Their inclusion remained appropriate because their SLT use was linked to traditional treatment, ancestral calling, initiation and training within the traditional healthcare system.

Those Who Use SLT for Non-Traditional Reasons

People who use SLT for non-traditional purposes were defined as people who used SLT outside traditional healing prescriptions or supervision. This category referred to SLT users whose use was self-directed, rather than initiated through THP recommendation. Because some participants in this category reported using SLT for medicinal purposes, medicinal use was categorised as “for traditional healing” when prescribed by THPs and was categorised as “medicinal use” when self-prescribed. Although these categories provided a useful organising framework, the study recognised that the boundary between traditional and non-traditional use could be permeable, as some participants reported using SLT across both spiritual and self-directed contexts.

Access to Traditional Health Providers and Their Patients

THPs were accessed through the Traditional Health Practitioners Council in KwaZulu-Natal, which oversees registered THPs in the province. THPs who participated in the study also referred the researcher to other THPs. Patients of THPs were referred by THPs, who also gave oral permission for the researcher to contact them. These patients were later understood to be THP trainees, or initiates in training to become THPs, because they had been advised to respond to ancestral or spiritual calling as part of their treatment process. People called to become traditional healers may experience what has been described as ‘initiation illness’, which may include psychological, bodily or spiritual symptoms that are not resolved through conventional treatment. 20 Although these participants were undergoing training, they had not yet completed initiation and were not permitted to treat patients independently, which distinguished them from practising THPs.

Access to Non-traditional Users of SLT

Non-traditional users of SLT were recruited through an SLT sales point at the study site. The researcher who conducted the fieldwork asked the seller to assist in linking her with buyers who used SLT. Snowball sampling was then used to obtain further referrals to people who used SLT outside traditional healing contexts until data saturation was reached. Data saturation was considered achieved when no additional themes or meaningful insights emerged across participant groups. The final sample size for this group (n=13) was considered appropriate for qualitative inquiry because the purpose was not statistical representativeness, but depth, diversity and information-rich accounts. Evidence from qualitative research indicates that many empirical studies reach saturation within a range of approximately 9 to 17 interviews, depending on the study aim, sample specificity and analytic depth. 21

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years or older and were able to speak English or a Nguni language commonly spoken in KwaZulu-Natal and include the following closely related languages; isiZulu, isiXhosa, seSwati or isiNdebele. THPs had to be traditional doctors or healers who had practised for at least 12 months and were registered according to the South African Traditional Health Practitioners Act 22 of 2007. 22 Patients of THPs had to have consulted a THP and been recommended to use SLT as part of traditional treatment. Non-traditional SLT users had to be using SLT at the time of the study for leisure or reasons other than being recommended by a THP. Current use was defined as either daily or non-daily use of SLT, consistent with public health definitions of current tobacco use. 23 Participants who did not meet these criteria were excluded from the study.

Data Collection

Data were collected from August to October 2023. Study information and consent forms were provided to all participants by the lead author. Information provided include the purpose of the study, conditions of participation, confidentiality issues, voluntary participation, and the participant’s right to withdraw at any point in time. Each participant signed the consent form and provided consent for audiotaping before the interview commenced.

Data were collected through in-depth interviews conducted using a semi-structured interview guide. The guide explored participants’ motivations for SLT use, modes of use, perceived benefits, perceived harms, cultural and spiritual meanings, and experiences associated with continued use (Supplementary File 1). The interview guide was not pilot tested however, interview questions were critically reviewed by the supervisors to the lead author who are co-authors of this paper and together have over 40 years of research experience in tobacco control.

Interviews were conducted in-person by the lead author, supported by a trained research assistant, in locations chosen by participants (in a car, a neighbour’s spare garage and THPs’ practising huts). Interviews were conducted in English and/or isiZulu, according to participants’ preference. They were audio-recorded with permission and lasted 20 – 90 minutes. None of the participants who consented refused or dropped out of the interviews. Interviews conducted in isiZulu were translated into English during transcription by bilingual researchers experienced in qualitative research. Field notes were taken, maintained throughout data collection to document contextual observations and emerging reflections. Data collection and preliminary analysis occurred concurrently, allowing emerging concepts to inform subsequent interviews until thematic saturation was reached. Data Analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and imported into NVivo 12 24 to facilitate data management and analysis. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis approach.18,25 The six phases included familiarisation with the data, generation of initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report.

The lead and last authors first read the transcripts repeatedly to become familiar with the data and to identify initial meanings across participant accounts. Initial codes were generated inductively from repeated reading of the transcripts and were later grouped into broader themes. Although the interview guide provided broad areas of interest, coding remained open to new patterns that emerged from the participants’ own accounts. This approach enabled systematic identification and interpretation of patterns across traditional and non-traditional SLT use.

Analysis was iterative, with ongoing movement between data, coding, and theme development. Members of the research team met regularly to discuss emerging interpretations, challenge assumptions, and refine the thematic framework through reflexive dialogue. Representative quotations were selected to illustrate each theme while reflecting the diversity of participants’ experiences. The final themes include: motivations for SLT use, modes and practices of use, harms experienced, and perceptions or interpretations of harmfulness (Results are summarised in Table 3).

Rigour and Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was established using the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba. 26 Credibility was enhanced through in-depth engagement with participants, verbatim transcription, investigator discussions throughout analysis, and the use of illustrative quotations to support interpretation. Dependability was strengthened through maintaining an audit trail documenting methodological decisions and analytical development. Confirmability was enhanced through reflexive discussions among the research team and maintenance of analytic memos during coding and interpretation. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, and research processes to enable readers to judge the applicability of the findings to other contexts.

Research Team and Reflexivity

The researcher who conducted all the interviews is a Black African female scientist with a master’s degree in medical sciences as her highest qualification. She had previously received training in qualitative research and attained a Good Clinical Practice training certificate, which qualifies her to conduct interviews with the participants. Before this study, the researcher had over 5 years’ experience of conducting qualitative research, including data collection through qualitative interviews. Her master’s research focused on the use of traditional medicine by nurses and patients. The researcher had no relationship with the participants prior to the commencement of the study.

Throughout the study, continuous reflection was maintained regarding how the researcher’s background, assumptions, interactions with participants and relationship to the research context could influence data collection and interpretation. Regular supervisory discussions supported analytical rigour and transparency by creating opportunities to reflect on emerging interpretations, cultural meanings and possible researcher assumptions, while recognising that complete researcher neutrality is neither achievable nor consistent with qualitative inquiry.

Regular reflexive discussions were important because the study required engagement with participants whose SLT use was linked to spirituality, ancestral authority, traditional healing and private bodily practices. Reflexive practice, therefore, helped ensure that participants’ accounts were interpreted with cultural sensitivity and without reducing them to biomedical risk alone. The co-authors (supervisors) are also experts in the field of research (both qualitative and quantitative) and tobacco control and have both taught and conducted several qualitative studies within and outside South Africa, including studies involving SLT use among THPs in a different province (Limpopo) in South Africa.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance for the study was received from the Sefako Makgatho Health Science University Human Research Ethics Committee (SMUREC ‒ H/78/2023: PG) on 9 March 2023. The ethics clearance was then submitted to the Traditional Health Practitioners Council in KZN, after which a presentation was made to inform the members of the council about the study. Permission to access and conduct interviews among THPs was given by the eThekwini District Department of African Traditional Medicine, in the KZN Provincial Department of Health. The study was explained to every participant, who was reassured that their privacy would be respected and confidentiality maintained throughout the study and in the dissemination of the results. All participants were notified that they could stop the interview at any time without any consequences. Code names were assigned to each participant to protect their privacy. Due to logistical constraints, including the geographical distribution of participants, and resource considerations, and limited means of reaching the study participants after the conclusion of data collection, transcripts were not returned to participants for review. To ensure data quality, interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and checked for accuracy by two members of the research team (SPN and COE). The Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research (COREQ) checklist was completed and included as Supplementary File 2.

THPs have established rules governing relationships with trainees (considered their “spiritual children”), including requiring their presence during interviews. To respect this while accessing trainees’ perspectives on SLT use, interviews were conducted with THPs present. Although this may introduce bias, steps were taken to mitigate this: participants were assured there were no right or wrong answers, encouraged to speak freely, and THPs were asked not to comment unless clarification was needed. THPs were also asked to avoid coercing their trainees to participate. Despite this limitation, rich data were obtained. Participant compensation was set at R100 (about $6) per participant, as stated in the informed consent form and in accordance with ethics committee approval. During recruitment of THPs, the research team was informed that their association required compensation of R200 (about $12), equivalent to a standard client consultation fee, for participation in research. This reimbursement was intended to compensate participants for their time and or transport costs rather than to serve as an incentive for participation.

RESULTS

Twenty-five participants were interviewed, representing traditional health practitioners, trainees (amathwasa), and non-traditional SLT users. Participants described diverse experiences that were organised into four interrelated themes: (1) motivations for SLT use, (2) modes and practices of use, (3) perceived harms associated with SLT use, and (4) perceptions of harmfulness. Although presented separately, these themes often overlapped, reflecting the complex cultural, social and behavioural contexts in which SLT use occurred.

Demographics

A total of 25 participants were interviewed in this study (12 who use SLT for traditional purposes (THPs and their patients/trainees; and 13 who use SLT for non-traditional purposes). The majority (n=15) were females, and all participants were of Black African ethnicity. The youngest participant was 19 years old while the oldest was 58 years old. Among non-traditional users, 8 were employed, and 5 were unemployed. All THPs (n=6) were full-time practitioners (using their profession as a source of income) with experience in the field ranging from 1 – 35 years. All THPs reported to have received certificates from their various THP boards as certified and qualified traditional healers. Among the ‘patients’/trainees of THPs, all of them were unemployed (see Table 2).

Summary of Themes and Subthemes

Four major themes emerged from the data and are presented in Table 3. These themes followed a progression from why participants used SLT to how they used it, the harms they associated with its use, and how they perceived the harmfulness of SLT use. Representative quotes for all themes and subthemes are also presented on Table 3.

Motivations for SLT Use

Motivations for SLT use differed according to whether use occurred within traditional healing or outside it. Traditional users largely described SLT as an instrument for spiritual communication, ritual practice and therapeutic care, whereas non-traditional users emphasised stress relief, perceived medicinal benefits, affordability and social influences. Nevertheless, some motivations, particularly self-medication, overlapped across both groups.

Some participants described SLT use as something they did not initiate out of personal preference, but because it was required by ancestral calling or taught during training. THPs described SLT as a medium through which ancestors could be acknowledged, appeased or communicated with. For THP patients/trainees, SLT use was closely connected to initiation and apprenticeship. Participants explained that trainee THPs learned when and how to use SLT under the guidance of their trainer or according to what they understood as ancestral instruction. One participant described seeing an ancestor using SLT in a dream, which was interpreted as guidance on the ‘prescribed’ form of use.

Non-traditional users of SLT mentioned using SLT for various reasons such as stress reduction, medicinal effects (treating headache and flu), sexual use, cost benefits, and social influences. Additionally, some participants indicated SLT to be less expensive than smoking. Moreover, some participants reported sexual uses for enhanced sexual experience. Another participant mentioned that he started using SLT because he saw his grandmother use it.

Modes and Practices of SLT Use

Participants reported multiple modes of SLT use, including oral, nasal, dual, intravaginal and external ritual use. Routes of administration appeared to be determined less by personal preference than by intended purpose, with ritual, medicinal and sexual uses each associated with particular modes of administration. Traditional users described external use and internal use of SLT in ritual contexts. This included consuming it, pouring SLT on the ground, placing it at a spiritual site, using it during ceremonies, adding it to bathing water or using it in practices associated with communicating with ancestors (called ‘ukuphahla’). Therefore, in ritual contexts, SLT was not always consumed internally but was used as spiritually directed.

Oral use involved placing SLT inside the mouth, often under the lip or in the upper part of the mouth. Nasal use involved sniffing or snorting SLT through the nose. Some participants used both oral and nasal routes, depending on the purpose of use, bodily comfort or the effect they sought. For example, some participants linked nasal use to a stronger or longer-lasting bodily sensation, while others shifted to nasal use when oral use caused discomfort.

Some non-traditional SLT users also described using SLT intravaginally, which was linked to sexual beliefs and perceived effects on sexual experience as previously mentioned. Participants reported that it tightens the intravaginal walls and improve sexual experiences for both themselves and their partners.

Factors Influencing the Mode of SLT Use

Participants’ choice of mode was influenced by the purpose of use, spiritual direction, bodily effects and personal preference. For traditional users, mode of use was often explained as dependent on ancestral instruction or the way in which the ancestor was believed to have used SLT when they were alive.

For non-traditional users, mode of use was more often linked to convenience, perceived effect, discomfort, or the specific purpose why SLT was being used. Some participants reported that nasal use was difficult because of sinus-related discomfort, while others shifted between oral and nasal use when one route became painful, as previously mentioned.

Harms Experienced from SLT Use

Participants reported health-related, financial, social and relationship harms associated with SLT use. Health-related harms reported include mouth peeling, sores, irritation, burning sensations, nausea, vomiting, dizziness, dental problems, nasal discomfort and genital discomfort. Participants who used SLT orally described peeling or painful changes inside the mouth, while those who used it nasally reported discomfort in the nose. Participants who discussed intravaginal use described genital discomfort and concerns about bodily effects. Participants described their first-time experiences of SLT use as physically intense and sometimes unpleasant. Reported first-time effects included dizziness, nausea, vomiting and feeling unwell.

Financial damage to the family was another type of harm reported by non-traditional users, who revealed how despite having a difficult financial situation in the family, they still purchased SLT. According to one participant, money that was meant for buying food was often spent purchasing SLT. Social harms were also indicated in participants’ responses, specifically among women respondents who feared that their use of SLT would cause them shame or disgust in the eyes of others, including friends, community members and even their partner.

Perceptions of SLT-Related Harmfulness

Participants’ perceptions of the harmfulness of SLT use were mixed with perceived legitimacy of SLT use shaping their risk perception. Participants’ perceptions of harmfulness were nuanced rather than polarised. Although many recognised the biomedical harms associated with SLT, some distinguished between commercial or recreational use and use within traditional healing contexts, where ancestral authority or spiritual purpose was perceived to reduce or negate harm. While traditional users believed that they would be protected if SLT were to cause them harm since they were directed to use it for traditional purposes (thus reducing the risk perception), non-traditional users reported SLT use as ‘dangerous’ and describe shame, secrecy or concern for harm to their body.

Across all themes, participants described SLT use as a practice shaped by intersecting cultural, spiritual, medicinal, psychological and social influences. While many recognised that SLT could cause harm, perceived therapeutic and spiritual benefits often coexisted with awareness of biomedical risks. These findings highlight the complexity of SLT use beyond conventional models of nicotine dependence and provide the context for interpreting the broader public health implications discussed below.

DISCUSSION

This study explored the traditional and non-traditional lived experiences and drivers of SLT use among adults in KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa as well as reported harms and perception of harm of SLT use. This study provides new insights into the complex cultural, behavioural and health dimensions of smokeless tobacco (SLT) use in South Africa. While previous studies have largely focused on the prevalence and socio-demographic correlates of SLT use, this study demonstrates that its use is embedded within multiple explanatory frameworks encompassing traditional healing, ancestral communication, initiation into traditional healing, perceived medicinal benefits, psychological coping, sexual enhancement and social influences. These findings show that SLT use cannot be understood solely as a nicotine-related behaviour but also as a practice shaped by the interaction between cultural beliefs, indigenous health systems, lived experiences and individual health needs.

Participants described diverse motivations for SLT use that differed between traditional and non-traditional users but also overlapped in important ways. Traditional health practitioners and their trainees primarily viewed SLT as facilitating ancestral communication, ritual practice, initiation and traditional healing, whereas non-traditional users more commonly described its use for stress relief, perceived medicinal benefits, affordability compared with smoking, sexual enhancement and social influence. Similar motivations have been reported in studies from South Africa, Sudan and other settings where SLT use is closely linked to cultural and social practices.8-10 However, our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that these motivations frequently coexist rather than occurring in isolation, reflecting the multiple roles that SLT occupies in users’ daily lives.

The wide range of routes through which SLT was administered further illustrates the complexity of its use. In addition to oral and nasal administration, participants reported topical application and intravaginal use for specific therapeutic, ritual and sexual purposes. While oral and nasal use have been documented previously, 9 the latter practices remain relatively underreported within tobacco control literature despite their potential implications for health and disease prevention. Importantly, the route of administration appeared to be determined largely by the intended purpose of use rather than by product preference alone, highlighting the need for tobacco control research to consider cultural practices alongside product characteristics.

Participants also described a broad range of adverse consequences associated with SLT use, including oral and nasal problems, reproductive concerns, social stigma, relationship difficulties and financial costs. These findings reinforce existing evidence that SLT use is associated with multiple adverse health outcomes extending beyond oral disease.1,3 At the same time, participants frequently described perceived therapeutic benefits, including relief of headaches, nasal congestion, stress and other ailments. Such perceptions have also been reported elsewhere and may contribute to continued use despite awareness of potential harms.8,10 These findings emphasise the importance of addressing perceived benefits, 27 rather than focusing solely on communicating health risks, within cessation interventions.

Perhaps the most important finding of this study is that participants did not necessarily perceive traditional and biomedical understandings of SLT as mutually exclusive. Many acknowledged that SLT could cause adverse health effects while simultaneously believing that its use within traditional healing or ancestral practices conferred therapeutic or spiritual benefits or protection from harm. Kleinman 15 proposed that health behaviours are understood through culturally constructed explanatory models that shape how individuals interpret illness, treatment and health risks. Our findings support this perspective by showing that participants negotiated multiple explanatory systems simultaneously rather than replacing one with another. Consequently, awareness of biomedical risks alone may be insufficient to change behaviour where tobacco use is reinforced by deeply held cultural beliefs, spiritual obligations or indigenous therapeutic practices.

These findings have important implications for tobacco control policy and practice. Article 12 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control recognises the importance of culturally appropriate public education and awareness programmes, 17 while implementation science demonstrates that interventions are more effective when adapted to local contexts and belief systems rather than transferred unchanged across settings. 16 In settings where traditional health practitioners remain influential, prevention and cessation strategies that rely exclusively on biomedical messaging may have limited impact. Rather than viewing traditional practitioners as barriers to tobacco control, they should be considered potential partners in developing effective culturally appropriate prevention and cessation interventions. 28 Engaging traditional healers, community leaders and users themselves may help identify culturally acceptable alternatives for ritual and medicinal practices while correcting misconceptions regarding the health risks associated with SLT. 27

The study also has broader implications beyond South Africa. Across many low- and middle-income countries, tobacco control programmes increasingly operate alongside indigenous health systems and culturally embedded health practices. Understanding how cultural beliefs shape perceptions of risk, benefit and acceptable health behaviour may improve the implementation and effectiveness of tobacco control interventions in these settings. Integrating cultural perspectives into tobacco control does not imply endorsing harmful practices but rather recognising that successful behaviour change requires engagement with the social and cultural contexts in which those behaviours occur.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study has several strengths. By including traditional health practitioners, their trainees and non-traditional users, it provides a comprehensive exploration of diverse experiences that are unlikely to be captured through quantitative surveys. The phenomenological approach enabled participants to describe their lived experiences in their own words, while the inclusion of multiple participant groups enhanced the richness and contextual depth of the findings.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. As with all qualitative research, the findings are intended to provide in-depth understanding rather than statistical generalisability. Snowball sampling may also have limited participant diversity because participants were partly recruited through existing social or practice-based networks. A statistical power analysis was not applicable because this was a qualitative study, and the sample size was instead guided by purposive sampling, participant-category diversity, information-rich cases and thematic saturation.

Although participants represented diverse perspectives, experiences may differ across other provinces and cultural groups within South Africa. In addition, information was based on participants’ self-reports and may have been influenced by recall or social desirability bias. However, the use of in-depth interviews, reflexive analysis and diverse participant groups strengthened the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings.

Another limitation of this study is that the category of participants initially identified as THP patients who were found to also double as THP trainees may have influenced the composition of the traditional-user group, as these participants were more closely embedded within traditional healing systems than initially anticipated. Their perspectives and SLT practices may therefore reflect the beliefs, norms and expectations associated with traditional healer training as against general recipients of traditional health care, potentially limiting comparability with non-traditional SLT users.

In addition, interviews with these THP trainees were conducted in the presence of THPs due to contextual and cultural considerations. The hierarchical and spiritual relationship between THPs and their trainees may have influenced how freely trainees expressed their experiences. To minimize potential interference, THP trainees were encouraged to express their views freely, while THPs were requested and agreed not to answer questions on behalf of their trainees or influence their responses during the interviews.

CONCLUSION

SLT use in South Africa is shaped by an interplay of cultural, spiritual, medicinal, psychological and social influences that extend beyond conventional models of nicotine dependence. The coexistence of indigenous and biomedical understandings of health highlights the importance of culturally responsive tobacco control strategies that acknowledge local belief systems while promoting evidence-based prevention and cessation. Recognising and engaging with these cultural contexts may improve the acceptability and effectiveness of interventions aimed at reducing the health burden associated with SLT use.
