A model with fragments of projective determinacy and failures of 𝖣𝖢 \mathsf{DC}
Sandra Müller, Bartosz Wcisło

Abstract
We describe a construction of a model of second order arithmetic in which (boldface) 𝚷 𝒏 𝟏 \bm{\Pi^{1}_{n}} -determinacy holds, but (lightface) Π n + 2 1 \Pi^{1}_{n+2} - 𝖣𝖢 \mathsf{DC} fails, thus showing that no projective level of determinacy implies full 𝖣𝖢 ℝ \mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}} . The construction builds upon the work of Gitman, Friedman, and Kanovei.



1
Introduction

The Axiom of Determinacy is with no doubt one of the most studied principles in the foundations of mathematics. Proposed in
[
10
]
, it was immediately observed to be incompatible with the Axiom of Choice. Despite of this fact, it produces such a coherent image of what the universe of sets might look like that it is considered to be a serious competitor to its more classical counterpart. Moreover (and perhaps more importantly), under large cardinal assumptions various well-studied classes of “simple” sets actually provably satisfy determinacy, so understanding
\mathsf{AD}
is the key tool in studying these classes.

Even though
\mathsf{AD}
is inconsistent with
\mathsf{AC}
, it is consistent with its classical weakening, Dependent Choice,
\mathsf{DC}
. Very often, the applications of choice actually involve just
\mathsf{DC}
, so this assumption is significantly more important for the customary mathematical arguments than the full choice.

All the current constructions of a model in which
\mathsf{AD}
holds produce a model in which Dependent Choice for real numbers,
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
, holds as well (see
[
17
]
). Therefore, for all what we know
\mathsf{AD}
can actually imply
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
and it is an important open problem whether these two can be separated.

In this article, we provide a (very) partial solution to this question. We show that if we require that determinacy holds for sets of complexity
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{n}
,
n\geq 1
this does not entail that
\Pi^{1}_{n+2}
-
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
holds. More specifically, under (a necessary) large cardinal hypothesis, we show that there exists a model
M
of
\mathsf{ZF}
satisfying (boldface)
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{n}
-determinacy in which there is a relation
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that for all
x
, there exists
y
with
R(x,y)
, but there is no sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
such that
R(x_{n},x_{n+1})
holds for all
n\in\omega
. In particular, it shows that the restriction of
\mathsf{AD}
to any complexity class
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{n}
does not entail
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Let us place this result in some context. By
[
16
]
, we know that
\mathsf{AD}
is independent of the full
\mathsf{DC}
, so the question really concerns the dependent choice on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, by
[
7
]
, if we assume that
\mathsf{AD}
holds and
V=L(\mathbb{R})
, this implies full
\mathsf{DC}
. Our result actually concerns the models of this form, so we know that the counterexamples which we produce in general have to include additional data if we want to push it further.

Now, let us briefly describe the techniques used in our proof. In
[
1
]
Abraham, following
[
4
]
, introduced a forcing which is itself defined inductively, roughly by adding to some initial very canonical subposet of the Sacks forcing further objects which arise as
L
-least generics for the poset constructed so far in given stage
\alpha
. This can be shown to yield various minimality properties. Most importantly, we can ensure that all reals added by this forcing are actually generic for the forcing. This idea was subsequently adapted by Kanovei in
[
6
]
, and further by Gitman, Friedman, and Kanovei in
[
3
]
to produce a model in which
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-
\mathsf{DC}
fails, whereas full countable choice and full
\Sigma^{1}_{\infty}
comprehension holds. More specifically, we can produce a tree of reals, arising from a generic for a certain inductively defined poset such that the reals in the tree are precisely the ones which are generic for all the posets defined in the stages of the inductive process which, in turn, are definable with a formula of second-order arithmetic.

This article is based mostly on two observations: first of all, the construction in
[
3
]
, carried out in
L
, does not really require us to work in that model, but is based on some of its properties shared by many canonical inner models studied in set theory. Second, if the initial model has suitable large cardinal properties, certain amount of determinacy is guaranteed in the resulting model and will pass down to its symmetric extension which is where we actually obtain a failure of projective
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Inner Model Theory

This article will make a crucial use of the notions of
premice
and
mice
. The definitions of these objects are notoriously technical and demanding, hence we will not give them here, as customary in the field. Standard references for them are
[
18
]
and
[
8
]
(note that the latter source, albeit more detailed, contains a significant error pointed out and corrected in
[
14
]
), another nice overview can be found in the introduction of
[
9
]
, see also
[
11
]
. Roughly, they are models containing large cardinals (most importantly for us,
Woodin cardinals
) and satisfying some
L
-like properties. We will explicitly say what we mean by
L
-like in the subsequent sections of this paper.

Other classic notions to which we will refer here are dependent choice for reals,
\mathsf{DC}_{\mathbb{R}}
and the axiom of determinacy for the projective classes
\bm{\Pi^{1}_{n}}
-
\mathsf{Det}
. Crucially for our purposes, certain suitable large cardinal assumptions, roughly the existence of
n
many Woodin cardinals, guarantee that the model satisfies determinacy for the sets in certain levels of the projective hierarchy, specifically
\bm{\Pi^{1}_{n+1}}
-sets. Again, these are very well studied and classical notions, to be found, for instance in
[
13
]
.

Even though we almost completely omit the presentation of mice, we will mention some of the core notions, which play a key role in this paper. Their precise description can be found in the cited sources (note, however, that some of the terminology below is standard, but not yet established in either
[
8
]
or
[
18
]
).

If
V
contains enough large cardinals, then we can build a canonical inner model
L[\vec{E}]
via a
fully backgrounded construction
. Roughly, we build a constructible universe with a sequence of predicates and whenever we see an extender that could fit on a sequence of the current model, we add a predicate denoting it to the sequence and we replace the sequence built thus far with its smallest “Skolem hull” (it is somewhat technical to describe what we really take). If the universe
V
contains
n
Woodin cardinals, then
L[\vec{E}]
also does. Besides that, it satisfies a number of
L
-like properties. Importantly for us, whenever
V[G]
is a forcing extension by a forcing whose size is below the least measurable cardinal,
L[\vec{E}]^{V[G]}=L[\vec{E}]^{V}
. The construction is unique in the sense that at each stage
\alpha
, there is the unique way to extend the
\alpha
-th model according to the definition. Like in the case of Gödel’s
L
, we can build a similar model over extra sets obtaining the model
L[\vec{E}](A)
(which will have analogous properties if the set
A
in question is small enough).

Another key notion which we will use are the models
M_{n}^{\#}(A)
. They are the smallest mice containing
A
and
n
Woodin cardinals. In this paper, whenever we write “
M_{n}^{\#}(A)
exists” we mean that it exists and is
\omega_{1}
-iterable. These models are canonical in the sense that, if it exists,
M_{n}^{\#}(A)
as defined in
V
is the same as
M_{n}^{\#}
as defined in
L[\vec{E}](A)
(also preserving
\omega_{1}
-iterability). On the other hand, the existence of the models
M_{n}^{\#}(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
(with the right amount of iterability) is what is actually needed for projective determinacy to hold.

Suppose that
M
is a premouse, let
\mathscr{T}
be an
iteration tree
on
M
of limit length (like with mice and premice, this is another notion whose definition we skip) and let
b
be a branch through that tree. We define
Q
-structures
of two related sorts: for a tree,
Q(\mathscr{T})
or for a branch
Q(b,\mathscr{T})
. The first one is the largest extension
\mathscr{Q}\unrhd M(\mathscr{T})
in which
\delta(\mathscr{T})
is not definably Woodin (either because, we can find a definable counterexample to
\delta(\mathscr{T})
being Woodin or the model projects below
\delta(\mathscr{T})
) and which is
\omega_{1}
-iterable above
\delta(\mathscr{T})
.

The latter is defined similarly, but this is the largest model of the form
M^{\mathscr{T}}_{b}\mid\gamma
, the limit model of
\mathscr{T}
along the branch
b
, restricted to the largest ordinal
\gamma
such that
\delta(\mathscr{T})
is not Woodin in
M_{b}^{\mathscr{T}}\mid\gamma
. Crucially, we do not require that the model is
\omega_{1}
-iterable above
\delta(\mathscr{T})
. In particular, the definition of
Q(b,\mathscr{T})
has complexity
\Delta_{0}
. See
[
9
]
, Definition 2.2.1 for the precise formulation.

By a
Q
-structure strategy
for the iteration game on a premouse
M
, we mean the (possibly partial) strategy
\Sigma
such that for any tree
\mathscr{T}
of limit length,
\Sigma(\mathscr{T})
is the unique branch
b
of
\mathscr{T}
for which
Q(b,\mathscr{T})=Q(\mathscr{T})
. One can show that this in fact gives a unique branch and that the model obtained as a direct limit along this branch is well-founded.

2.2
Symmetric models

In this paper, we will make crucial use of symmetric extensions. Let
M
be any transitive model of (a fragment of)
\mathsf{ZFC}
and let
M[G]
be its forcing extension, where
G
is generic for a poset
\mathbb{P}
. Recall that a
symmetric extension
of
M
is a model

M\subseteq M^{*}\subseteq M[G]

such that for some normal subgroup
F\unlhd\textnormal{Aut}(\mathbb{P})
of the automorphism group of
\mathbb{P}
,
M^{*}
is the set of
x\in M[G]
such that
x
has a name
\dot{x}
which is invariant under the natural action of
F
on
\mathbb{P}
-names. It is a classical fact that such an
M^{*}
is a model of
\mathsf{ZF}
.

We will extensively use the following simple lemma. The proof is very straightforward, but we include it nevertheless, since we were not able to find an easily accessible reference.

Lemma 1
.

Let
M^{*}\subset M[G]
be a symmetric extension of
M
. Suppose that
X,a\in M^{*}
and let
Y
be a subset of
X
definable in
M[G]
with the parameter
a
. Then
Y\in M^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
M^{*}
be a symmetric submodel of
M[G]
induced by the subgroup
F\unlhd\textnormal{Aut}(\mathbb{P})
. Let
X,a\in M^{*}
and let

Y=\left\{x\in X\mid\ M[G]\models\phi(x,a)\right\}.

Let
\dot{X}
be a name for
X
hereditarily invariant under
F
. In particular, for any
\sigma=\langle y,p\rangle\in\dot{X}
and any
f\in F
,
fy=y
(where
F
acts on names in the natural way). Now, consider the following name:

\sigma=\left\{\langle\dot{x},p\rangle\mid\ \exists q\ \langle\dot{x},q\rangle\in\dot{X}\wedge p\Vdash\phi(\dot{x},\dot{a})\right\}.

The name
\sigma
is clearly hereditarily symmetric. Indeed, if
f\in F
, then

p\Vdash\phi(\dot{x},\dot{a})

iff

f(p)\Vdash\phi(f\dot{x},f\dot{a}),

and by assumption this amounts to

f(p)\Vdash\phi(\dot{x},\dot{a}),

so
\langle\dot{x},p\rangle\in\sigma
iff
\langle\dot{x},f(p)\rangle\in\sigma
.

Now, we claim that
\sigma^{G}=Y
. Indeed, let
x\in\sigma^{G}
and assume that
\langle\dot{x},p\rangle\in\sigma
. Then

p\Vdash\phi(\dot{x},\dot{a}),

so by definition
x\in Y
.

If, on the other hand,
x\in Y
, then there exists a symmetric name
\dot{x}
for
x
and
p\in G
such that
p\Vdash\phi(\dot{x},\dot{a}).
Hence,
\langle\dot{x},p\rangle\in\sigma
and
x\in\sigma^{G}
.

∎

3
Adapting Gitman–Friedman–Kanovei poset

Our work builds heavily on
[
3
]
, which described a construction of a model in which
\Pi^{1}_{2}\text{-}\mathsf{DC}
fails (which in turn, was building upon previous work by Jensen and Abraham). The model was obtained as a symmetric extension:

L\subset N\subset L[G],

where
L
is the constructible universe and
G
is a generic for a certain poset
\mathbb{J}
constructed using specific properties of
L
. In
N
, there is a tree
T
of reals which has no branch. This much can be obtained by much simpler forcing, not utilising the technology originally studied by Jensen. However, the following two properties hold:

•

The tree is exactly the tree of generic reals in
L[G]
.

•

Being generic for the poset
\mathbb{J}
can be defined in a
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-manner.

The above facts use the exact manner in which our forcing
\mathbb{J}
is constructed, which in turn invokes specific properties of
L
. However, even though the construction of
\mathbb{J}
as described in their paper in many places uses the properties of the constructible universe
L
, it can be adapted to other models of set theory, provided they satisfy enough
L
-like properties. We will now isolate specific assumptions we are making about the ground model and give an outline of the construction of
\mathbb{J}
indicating which parts may be treated as a blackbox. Suppose
M
is a model of
\mathsf{ZFC}^{-}
such that:

•

There exists a
\Diamond
-sequence definable via a formula
D
.

•

There exists a well-ordering of the reals definable via a formula
W
.

•

There exists a formula
K
such that for any set of ordinals
x
if
K(x)
holds, then
x
codes a well-founded model
\bar{M}
of
\mathsf{ZFC}^{-}
such that:

–

D(\bar{M})
is a
\Diamond
-sequence and an initial segment of the
\Diamond
-sequence defined by
D
in
M
;

–

likewise,
W(\bar{M})
is a well-ordering of the reals of
\bar{M}
which is an initial segment of the well-ordering defined by
W
in
M
.

•

The formula
K
holds for a club of countable subsets of
\omega_{1}
.
1
1

1

Actually, in Section 5, we will arrange the situation slightly differently: there will be a formula
K_{0}
which will compute the well-ordering and which will be used for the complexity calculations, and another formula

which will say “every real in the model coded by

If a model

satisfies the above properties, let us call it
L
-like

Working in
M
, we will construct a sequence of posets
\mathbb{P}_{\alpha},\alpha<\omega_{1}
. Each of them will be a

, i.e., a subposet of the Sacks forcing satisfying the following conditions:

•

It contains all trees
T_{s}:=\left\{x\in\omega^{\omega}\mid\ s\textnormal{ is a prefix of }x\right\}
, where
s\in\omega^{<\omega}
.

•

It is closed under joins
T\wedge S
, where
T\wedge S
is the maximal perfect tree in

, if such a tree exists, or an empty tree otherwise.

•

It is closed under unions.

The construction itself uses objects which we will not define, but the definitions of which, crucially, do not depend on the ground universe
L
. Specifically, these notions are:

•

A
tree iteration of perfect posets
,
\mathbb{P}(\vec{P},T)
, where
\vec{P}
is (some)
\omega
-iteration of perfect posets and
T
is a tree of height
\omega

[

).

•

A

\mathbb{Q}(\vec{P},T)
, where
\vec{P}
and

[
3

).

•

For any generic

, a

\mathbb{P}^{*}=S(\mathbb{P},H)

[
3
]
).

In the tree iteration, the conditions are roughly finite subtrees of
T
where the nodes are conditions come from
\vec{P}
, i.e., from the usual finite support length
\omega

\mathbb{P}
and we require them to be arranged in a coherent manner. A condition
p
in this forcing is stronger than a condition

, essentially, if the finite tree supporting the iteration is bigger and the conditions are pointwise stronger.

The fusion poset for Sacks forcing is a poset where conditions are pairs
(T,n)
where
T
is a perfect tree and
n
is a natural number. If
(T,n)\leq(S,k)
, then

The name “seal poset” is introduced in this note and seems to have no previous established name. The seal poset takes a poset
\mathbb{P}
, its finite support length
\omega

\vec{P}
, and a generic

\vec{P}
and a generic

The name is justified by the following result:

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be a perfect poset and let
\mathbb{P}^{*}=\mathbb{Q}(\mathbb{P},\omega_{1}^{<\omega})
.

•

Let
\mathscr{A}

-fold iteration

\mathbb{P}^{*}

•

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a maximal antichain for the tree iteration

\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{P}^{*},\omega_{1}^{<\omega}).

\mathbb{P}_{0}

.

The poset
\mathbb{J}

4
A model with failures of
\mathsf{DC}
and
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-determinacy

In this subsection, we show that the construction of the symmetric model from
[
3
]
can be carried over to the
L[U]
setting, resulting in a model with definable failures of
\mathsf{DC}
. Here, there are some differences between our construction and the original one, since dealing with models with a measurable raises the complexity of the defined tree.

Let us introduce some notation. Let
F\leq\textnormal{Aut}(\omega_{1}^{<\omega})
be a subgroup consisting of the order-automorphisms of
\omega_{1}^{<\omega}
as a tree which pointwise fix some countable subtree
T\subset\omega_{1}^{\omega}
with no infinite branch. This subgroup acts naturally on the poset
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{J},\omega_{1}^{<\omega})
by permuting the names on the coordinates.

Let
G\subset\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{J},\omega_{1}^{<\omega})
be a generic filter and let
N
be a
symmetric model
induced by the permutation subgroup
F
, i.e.:

L[U]\subset N\subset L[U][G]

such that

N=\left\{x\in L[U][G]\mid\ x=\dot{x}^{G}\textnormal{, where $\dot{x}$ is hereditarily invariant under }F\right\}.

We want to show that in
N
, there exists a definable tree of reals which has no branch.

Let
T
be the tree of
\mathbb{J}_{n}
-generic tuples of reals added by the forcing
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{J},\omega_{1}^{<\omega})
. Notice that all such tuples from
L[U][G]
are in
N
, since a condition from
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{J},\omega_{1}^{<\omega})
is supported by a finite tree
X
, so it is fixed by any automorphisms fixing a countable tree with no branch extending
X
. Since
\dot{T}
contains all such conditions, it is clearly fixed setwise by
F
(and, in fact, by
\textnormal{Aut}(\omega_{1}^{<\omega}))
. It can be also shown that no branch through
\omega_{1}^{<\omega}
can have a name fixed by all automorphisms fixing a countable tree
S
with no branches — essentially because we can change that branch at nodes which are outside
S
without changing
S
. This locality property was established in Lemma 9.3 of
[
3
]
. Finally, we have to check the complexity of the branch defined in this way.

Proposition 6
.

Let
N,T
be defined as above. Then
T
can be defined as a
\Pi^{1}_{2}
subset of the reals.

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, the tuples of reals in the tree
T
are exactly the tuples which are
\mathbb{J}_{n}
-generic over
L[U]
, so it is enough to show that the latter condition can be expressed in
\Pi^{1}_{2}
manner.

Consider the following statement
\phi(x)
:

”For all well-founded models
M
satisfying
V=L[U]
, if all countable iterates of
M
via
U
are well founded, then for any sequence of sets
(M_{\beta})_{\beta<{\omega_{1}}^{M}}
, at any nontrivial stage of the construction,
x
defines a
\mathbb{P}_{\beta}
- generic filter.”

This formula defines the tuples
x
which are
\mathbb{J}_{n}
-generic over
L[U]
. Now it is enough to check its complexity. Notice that the formula has the form:

\underbrace{\textnormal{for all $X$}}_{\forall}\Big(\underbrace{\textnormal{$X$ codes a well founded model whose all iterates are wellfounded }}_{\forall}\\
\rightarrow\underbrace{\textnormal{there is a (unique) construction of $\mathbb{J}_{n}$ which makes $x$ generic}}_{\exists}\Big).

which is a equivalent to a formula of the form:

\forall(\exists\vee\exists),

so it has complexity
\Pi^{1}_{2}
, as required.

∎

4.1
A measurable cardinal implies determinacy in the symmetric model

In the previous section, we have constructed a symmetric extension of
L[U]
in which
\Pi^{1}_{2}
-
\mathsf{DC}
fails. Now, we will check that (boldface)
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
-determinacy holds in the model. The arguments in this subsection could be replaced by slightly more abstract ones, along the lines of Section
5.1
. However, since the proof of
\bm{\Pi^{1}_{1}}
-determinacy is significantly easier than the proof of projective determinacy, it might be actually instructive to see this case spelled out in a more explicit manner. Therefore, we include the argument for the convenience of the reader.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that
\kappa
is a measurable cardinal. Let
N
be a symmetric extension obtained by forcing
\mathbb{P}
with a group
F
. Then
N
satisfies
\mathbf{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
-determinacy

The proof of this theorem follows the original argument by Martin. We start with a combinatorial Lemma (the proof, in the context of
\mathsf{ZFC}
, can be found for instance in the proof of Martin’s result, Theorem 31.1 in
[
5
]
):

Lemma 8

(Order representation for
\Pi^{1}_{1}
sets)
.

Let
A\subset\omega^{\omega}
. Suppose that there exists a tree
T
such that
A\subseteq[T]
. Then
A
is
\mathbf{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
iff there exists a function
T\ni t\mapsto<_{t}
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

•

For all
t\in T
,
<_{t}
is a linear order on
\{0,1,\ldots,|t|\}.

•

For all
t\subset s
,
<_{t}\subset<_{s}
.

•

For all
x\in\omega^{\omega}
,
x\in A
iff
<_{x}
is wellfounded, where
<_{x}=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}<_{x\upharpoonright n}
.

The ordering from the above lemma is produced in a constructive manner and can be carried out in
\mathsf{ZF}
. Also, the following classic fact can be proved without using choice (a proof in
\mathsf{ZFC}
can be found, for instance, as Proposition 27.1 in
[
5
]
):

Theorem 9

(Closed determinacy)
.

Let
X
be a wellfounded set. Let
A
be a closed subset of
X^{\omega}
in the product topology with
X
discrete. Then the Gale-Stewart game on
A
is determined.

The above theorem probably cannot be prove in a choiceless context for general sets
X
. The point is that using the well order on
X
, in each move of the game, we can pick the smallest position in which we have not yet lost.

Now, in order to prove
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
-determinacy, it is enough to reduce
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
-games to closed games. Let
\kappa
be a measurable cardinal (in
V
). Let
A\subset\omega^{\omega}
be a
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{1}
set. Let us consider the following game: Player I plays pairs of the form
\langle n_{i},\xi_{i}\rangle\in\omega\times\kappa
. Player II plays natural numbers
n_{j}
. I wins if the produced real
x
is in
A
, and, additionally, for any
k\in\omega
, and any
i,j<\frac{k}{2}
the following condition is satisfied:

n_{i}<_{x\upharpoonright k}n_{j}\textnormal{ iff }\xi_{j}<\xi_{j}.

In other words, I has to produce a number in
A
, and additionally witness that
x\in A
by embedding the linear order
<_{x}
in
\kappa
. Since this is a closed game, it is determined. A winning strategy for I in this auxiliary game clearly produces a winning strategy for I in the original game. So it is enough to check that a winning strategy for II in this game yields a winning strategy (for II) in the original game.

By assumption,
\kappa
is a measurable cardinal. Suppose that
U
is a
\kappa
-complete ultrafilter on
\kappa
in
V
. Then

\widehat{U}=\left\{X\in\mathcal{P}(\kappa)^{N}\mid\ \exists X_{0}\in U\ X_{0}\subset X\right\}

is an ultrafilter in
N
. Indeed: take any
Y\in\mathcal{P}(\kappa)^{N}
. Fix a good name
\dot{Y}
for
Y
. Let

Z=\left\{\gamma\in\kappa\mid\ \exists p\in\mathbb{P}\ \langle\gamma,p\rangle\in\dot{Y}\right\}.

If
Z\notin U
, then very few ordinals even have a chance to end up in
Z
and

\kappa\setminus Z\subseteq\kappa\setminus Y\in\widehat{U}.

If, on the other hand,
Z\in U
, then since
\mathbb{P}
is a small forcing, we see that there exists a single
p
such that

Y_{0}=\left\{\gamma\in\kappa\mid\ \langle\gamma,p\rangle\in\dot{Z}\right\}\in U.

Then

Y_{0}\subseteq Y\in\widehat{U}.

This proves our claim. Now, let
U
be a normal measure on
\kappa
in
V
. Let

U_{2}=\left\{X\times X\mid\ X\in U\right\}.

The set
U_{2}
exists both in
V
and in
N
and it is known to be an ultrafilter on
[\kappa]^{2}
in
V
and, by the previous argument, also an ultrafilter in
N
. We define
U_{n}
for
n\in\omega
in a similar manner.

Now, suppose that
\sigma
is a winning strategy for II in the auxiliary game. We will define a winning strategy
\tau
for II in the original game in the following manner: by
\omega
-completeness of
U
, for any tuple
n_{0},\ldots,n_{2k}
, there exists a unique
m
such that:

A_{m}=\left\{\langle\xi_{0},\ldots,\xi_{k}\rangle\mid\ \tau(n_{0},\xi_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{2k},\xi_{k})=m\right\}\in U_{k}.

We let

\tau(n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{2k})=m.

That
\tau
indeed defines a winning strategy can be checked as in the original proof (see Theorem 31.1 in
[
5
]
).

5.1
Projective determinacy in the small forcing extensions of
M_{n}

In order to complete the proof, we still have to verify whether in the model

-determinacy still holds. In this section, we will verify this fact. Unlike in Subsection

, our argument will not follow the original determinacy proof directly, since it is much more technically involved. Instead, we will show how the proof can be used as a blackbox, using classical techniques from inner model theory. We will prove the following Proposition:

Proposition 11
.

Suppose that
M
is a model of
\mathsf{ZFC}
with
n
Woodin cardinals. Let
\mathbb{P}
be a forcing poset with
|\mathbb{P}|
smaller than the least measurable. Let

, where

is a symmetric extension and

. Then in

-determinacy holds.

Theorem 12

(Neeman)
.

Suppose that
M
is a model of
\mathsf{ZF}
which is closed under the
M_{n}^{\#}
operator. Then
\bm{\Pi}^{1}_{n+1}
-determinacy holds in
M
.

Unfortunately, Theorem 2.14 in
[
12
]
states it in the context of
\mathsf{ZFC}

\mathsf{ZF}
(for instance, it is stated in pure

[

]

Lemma 13
.

Let
M
be an arbitrary model of
\mathsf{ZFC}
. Suppose that

M\models\textnormal{For any $A\subset\textnormal{Ord}$, $M_{n}^{\#}(A)$ exists}.

Then for any

M[G]\models\textnormal{For any $A\subset\textnormal{Ord}$, $M_{n}^{\#}(A)$ exists}.

Now, we will prove our proposition.

Proof of Proposition
11
.

Let
\delta_{0}<\ldots<\delta_{n-1}
be the Woodin cardinals of
M
. Since the forcing is small, they are also Woodin in
M[G]
.

Claim I

We will show that for every
X\in M^{*}_{\delta_{0}}(:=M^{*}\cap V_{\delta_{0}})
,
X\subseteq\textnormal{Ord}
,

M^{*}_{\delta_{0}}\models\textnormal{ There exists }M_{n-1}(X)^{\#}.

First, notice that since the forcing is small, there exists
M_{n-1}(X)^{\#}
in
M[G]_{\delta_{0}}.

Within
M[G]
, define:

F:=\left\{\langle A,B\rangle\mid\ L[\vec{E}](A)\models B=A^{\#}\right\},

where
L[\vec{E}](A)
is the fully backgrounded construction in
M[G]
above
A
. Notice that the structure
L[\vec{E}](A)
can be defined correctly in
M[G]
. In the model
M[G]
, the extenders are lift-ups of the extenders from

. By Theorem 5.1 of
[
15
]
, we know that running a backgrounded construction of

will yield a unique partial extender at each step of the construction (actually, Theorem 9.1 of
[

will be extended with a (globally) definable subset at each active step. Using inductively Lemma
1

(L[\vec{E}](A))^{M*}=(L[\vec{E}](A))^{M[G]}
.

Consider now

defined in the same way as

. Notice that since the construction of

is absolute between

and

, we have that for

\langle A,B\rangle\in F

M[G]\models\textnormal{If }\langle A,B\rangle\in F\textnormal{, then }B=M_{n-1}(A)^{\#}.

This finishes the proof of Claim I.

Claim II

Let
X\subseteq\textnormal{Ord}
be an element of

and let

Then

M^{*}_{\delta_{0}}\models Y=M_{k}(X)^{\#}.

Fix

. The definition of an
n
-small premouse is clearly absolute, so we only have to check whether

is
\omega_{1}
-iterable in

and whether it is the smallest such mouse (notice that

, a structure which is not iterable in

could be iterable in
M^{*}
and

, since there are less trees to deal with, but also less branches to respond with).

We will show that
N
is
\omega_{1}
-iterable in

. By induction on
