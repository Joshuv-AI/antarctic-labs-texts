What's old is new: how anti-trans policy mimics the anti-abortion strategy that culminated in Dobbs
Authors: Fiona R Miller, Gray Babbs, Elizabeth Tobin-Tyler, Benjamin P Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLegislation restricting the rights of transgender people has increased dramatically in recent years. This trend echoes the historical arc of anti-abortion legislation. Over the 50 years between the US Supreme Court decisions in Roe and Dobbs, anti-abortion advocates worked to erode abortion access by expanding both the volume and scope of legislative attacks. While many of the bills proposed prior to Dobbs were enjoined pending litigation, their overall effect was to chill the provision of care, and propel a federal legal strategy that dismantled abortion access, paving the way for contemporary abortion bans. The anti-trans movement has followed the same playbook. In considering how to resist these assaults, the pro-abortion movement provides valuable lessons, both through an analysis of its strategic defeats, and of its post-Dobbs resilience. Advocates for trans rights must vigorously affirm the humanity of trans people, mobilize institutional support, and coordinate a strategic states-based defense to resist contemporary anti-trans legislation.

Introduction

Over the past decade, legislative attacks on transgender people's rights have mounted steadily. (Throughout, we use “trans” as an umbrella term to describe all people identifying as transgender, gender nonbinary, and otherwise gender diverse and/or whose gender identity differs from the one most often associated with their assigned sex at birth.) In 2015, 21 anti-trans bills were introduced in state legislatures. By 2025, however, this number reached 1022, including proposals to restrict trans people's access to health care, education, and sports, and 109 anti-trans bills were introduced in the US Congress.1 Meanwhile, the Trump administration has also implemented multiple anti-trans policies. This is not the first time the United States has experienced a rapid barrage of regulations to restrict bodily autonomy. We need look no further than the surge in anti-abortion legislation and litigation beginning in the early 2010s to predict the likely trajectory of anti-trans policy in the coming years, and to provide inspiration for strategies to effectively disrupt it.

The arc from Roe to Dobbs

In 1973, the US Supreme Court decided Roe v Wade, which held that the right to privacy under the Due Process Clause applied to abortion, legalizing it nationwide, although with increasing state authority to restrict abortion with advancing gestational age. Subsequent decisions, however, gradually eroded the protections achieved with Roe. In response, anti-abortion advocates staged an increasing number of regulatory attacks on abortion to see which might pass constitutional muster. This trend accelerated between 2011 and 2015, when states enacted nearly as many abortion restrictions as in the previous 15 years.2 The restrictions affected all aspects of abortion care: they imposed medically unnecessary restrictions on care facilities, required waiting periods prior to abortion, mandated specific ultrasound tests, compelled false counseling by providers, banned certain methods of abortion, and instituted gestational age limits. These restrictions chilled access to care and litigation of them ultimately resulted in Dobbs v Jackson Women's Health Organization, which, in 2022, overturned Roe.

Certainly, Roe never guaranteed abortion access, merely a federal legal framework within which the right to abortion could be defended. In the years just before and after Dobbs, much pro-abortion advocacy focused on increasing equity in abortion access. Moreover, through local and national collaboration, abortion patients, advocates, and providers have demonstrated tremendous resilience since the fall of Roe. Indeed, the annual number of abortions in the United States increased after Dobbs.3 Nevertheless, this resilience comes with many costs, including novel legal risks for patients, providers, and anyone who supports them. Most importantly, abortion bans only compound the inequitable barriers to abortion that existed before Dobbs, meaning that some abortion seekers are still being denied access to care.

Parallels between anti-abortion and anti-trans policy agendas

After Dobbs, and the imposition of abortion bans in many states, anti-abortion policy began to lose some of its power as an organizing issue. It is likely not a coincidence, then, that after Dobbs, legislative attacks on trans people escalated dramatically. By May of 2026, over 750 pieces of anti-trans legislation had been introduced in state legislatures, more than the total number in all of 2024 (Figure 1).1 Should this trend continue, the predictable result is the complete erosion of legal protections for trans people, just as years of anti-abortion policy led to Dobbs.

However, the development of anti-trans legislation parallels anti-abortion legislation not just in volume (Figure 2),4 but in substance and rhetoric. Just as the 2010s saw an increasing variety of assaults on abortion, anti-trans bills now not only restrict private behavior—through bathroom bills, for example—but directly attack trans personhood, such as by restricting access to gender-affirming care.5 Meanwhile, the expansion in anti-trans rhetoric mimics the dehumanizing arc of anti-abortion discourse. Between the early post-Roe years and the 1980s, anti-abortion advocates expanded from an initial focus on the fetus to the argument that pregnant people had to be protected from their own decisions.6 Similarly, “bathroom bills” were centered on protecting the supposedly vulnerable cis person from trans people, while recent attacks on access to gender-affirming health care have also been framed as protecting trans people from themselves.

The way forward

The path to Dobbs highlights what is so dangerous about the present anti-trans movement: the number and scope of anti-abortion attacks made for a successful litigation strategy, while an evolving rhetoric pushed to progressively dehumanize pregnant people. Trans people are poised on the same precipice—particularly since there is no legal precedent for trans people's fundamental right to exist. However, rather than positioning the trans rights movement as fundamentally precarious, the abortion struggle provides lessons for the way forward. First, advocates must be clear that the humanity of our community members is indisputable. Second, institutions need to demonstrate solidarity with trans communities. Finally, as legal precedent alone is insufficient to guarantee equity for trans communities, the response to anti-trans attacks must be grounded in local and national collaboration, as pro-abortion work has been since Dobbs.

Standing unequivocally for the humanity of trans people is the foundation for our other recommendations. Anti-trans legislation rests on the false notion that trans people are a threat both to cisgender people and to themselves, and therefore must be subject to state control. This dehumanizing framing is anathema to a society that values the freedom of all its members. Strategic invocations of bigotry are an increasingly common tactic throughout political discourse. Calling them out and developing solidarity against them is key to resisting them.7

For this solidarity to be effective, powerful institutions—universities, hospitals, media, and other corporations—must support trans communities. Hospitals and health professional societies, in particular, should voice their support for trans patients, and should resist anticipatory compliance with proposed restrictions. When these institutions restrict services,8 contrary to established evidence, they undermine public confidence in health professionals and invite further meddling by people outside the clinician–patient relationship.

We must also commit to collaborative advocacy. Reflecting the pro-abortion movement's resilience since Dobbs, trans rights supporters can use a state-grounded strategy to bolster protections. Shield laws to protect access to gender-affirming care are an important starting point,9 but more is needed for a multilayered defense, such as codifying trans rights into state laws. Collaboration among advocates may involve discussing what has been successful in different settings, analyzing pending litigation, and planning for future directions.

Conclusion

The onslaught of legislative attacks on the trans community resembles the years of anti-abortion attacks that preceded it—in volume, breadth, and rhetoric. While Dobbs was a painful blow, it offers lessons as we continue to build a society that celebrates trans identities, and safeguards bodily autonomy for all.
