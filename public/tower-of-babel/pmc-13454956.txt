The Effect of Radiotherapy on the Microtensile Bond Strength of Dentin in Relation to the Timing of Restoration Procedure
Authors: Phatchara Tangviroon, Chawalid Pianmee, Thanaphum Osathanon, Supapan Wittayanuwat, Wisarut Prawatvatchara, Dusit Nantanapiboon, Hannah Wesley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare microtensile bond strength at different time points relative to fractional irradiation using both etch‐and‐rinse (ER) and self‐etch (SE) adhesive systems.MethodsNinety‐six sound extracted human molars were divided into two control groups (control‐ER and control‐SE) and six irradiated groups. The irradiated groups were subdivided based on the timing of restoration: those restored before irradiation (restoration before irradiation [RBI]‐ER and RBI‐SE) and those restored after irradiation (irradiation before restoration [IBR]‐ER, IBR‐ER delayed, IBR‐SE, and IBR‐SE delayed). The irradiated specimens underwent fractional radiotherapy at 2 Gy per day, reaching a total dose of 70 Gy. All specimens were restored with resin composite at the designated time points relative to irradiation. Microtensile bond strength was measured, fracture patterns were analyzed, and surface morphology was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Data were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests (α = 0.05).ResultsANOVA revealed significant restoration timing and adhesive‐type effects on microtensile bond strength (p < 0.01). Post hoc analysis showed that irradiation reduced bond strength (p < 0.01). In irradiated ER groups, the RBI group had lower bond strength than post‐irradiation restorations (p < 0.01), while in SE groups, bond strength did not vary across time points (p > 0.05). Additionally, significant differences were found in the IBR and IBR delayed groups, with the ER adhesive outperforming the SE adhesive with a (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIrradiation compromised dentin bond strength, resin tag formation and hybrid layer integrity in a manner dependent on the adhesive system used. In the ER adhesive groups, bond strength was significantly higher when restorations were performed post‐irradiation. Conversely, for the SE adhesive groups, the time points of the restoration had no significant effect on bond strength.Keywords: adhesive, cancer, dentin, etch-and-rinse, geriatric, microtensile bond strength, radiotherapy, self-etch, timing

1. Introduction

Radiotherapy was a standard treatment for head and neck cancer (HNRT), yet it often led to complications that severely affected patients’ quality of life [1–3], especially in the oral region. Frequently reported side effects included xerostomia, mucositis, dysphagia, and radiation‐induced caries [2], typically occurring within the first year post‐treatment [4, 5]. While the primary mechanism of radiotherapy was to target and eliminate cancer cells, the radiation also induced structural changes in healthy tooth substrates [6, 7]. When exposure exceeded 30 Gy, enamel hardness decreased, carbonate and mineral deposition were altered, and dentin was compromised by a reduced protein‐to‐mineral ratio [6, 8]. These alterations created significant challenges for adhesion. Consequently, achieving optimal and durable restorations in irradiated teeth required the careful selection of bonding techniques and materials.

Existing literature on adhesion to the irradiated tooth structure remained inconclusive, largely due to methodological inconsistencies such as varied sectioning techniques and radiation protocols (e.g., fractional versus single 70 Gy doses). While some studies reported reduced bond strength for 3‐step etch‐and‐rinse (ER) [9], 2‐step ER [10], and 2‐step self‐etch (SE) systems [11], citing structural damage like protein denaturation and mineral loss, others found no adverse effects. Specifically, conflicting investigations noted that 3‐step ER [12] and 2‐step SE systems [13] achieved bond strengths comparable to those of sound dentin. These adhesive systems, known as gold standard adhesive systems, have demonstrated proven clinical effectiveness through challenges that consider various factors such as the specific adhesive strategy, substrate condition, moisture control, and operative technique [14]. However, there was limited data available on the performance of these established protocols when applied to irradiated dentin substrates. This lack of conclusive evidence highlights the need for standardized testing protocols.

Comprehensive oral rehabilitation for head and neck cancer patients scheduled for radiotherapy typically encompassed restorations, periodontal therapy, and extractions [15]. However, the therapeutic urgency of oncological intervention often necessitated deferring restorative procedures until the completion of radiation therapy. This clinical sequence raised critical concerns regarding the influence of radiotherapy on the dentin bonding interface and delineated the need to determine the optimal postoperative timing for achieving durable adhesive stability. Therefore, the objective of this study was to simulate a HNRT protocol to evaluate the adhesive efficacy of gold‐standard systems (3‐step ER and 2‐step SE) and to investigate the influence of both restorative timings relative to irradiation on dentin microtensile bond strength. The first null hypothesis stated that irradiation would have no effect on the dentin microtensile bond strength (μTBS) compared to the nonirradiated group. The second null hypothesis stated that restorative timing relative to irradiation would not influence μTBS. The third null hypothesis stated that adhesive system type would not influence μTBS, either independently or through its interaction with restorative timing.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Sample Size Calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using Power software (version 3.1) based on effect sizes reported by Arid et al. [16] and Naves et al. [17]. Parameters were established at a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) and a statistical power of 95% (1 − β = 0.95). While the analysis indicated a minimum of eight specimens per group, the sample size was increased to 10 per group to compensate for potential laboratory errors and pre‐testing failures. By including observational specimens, two teeth per group, the adjustment resulted in a final total sample size of 96 teeth.

2.2. Specimen Collection and Allocation

The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University (Approval No. HREC‐DCU 2023‐049; July 2023). Ninety‐six sound‐extracted human third molars were obtained from patients aged 16–30 years. Human third molars extracted for dental treatment purposes will be collected after informed consent has been obtained. Following extraction, teeth were disinfected in a 0.5% chloramine‐T solution at 4°C for 7 days and stored in distilled water for a maximum of 6 months prior to use. Surface integrity was verified using a stereomicroscope (SZ 61, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 10× magnification to confirm the absence of caries, noncarious lesions, restorations, cracks, craze lines, or developmental defects. Specimens were randomly allocated into eight experimental groups (n = 12), comprising 10 teeth for microtensile bond strength (μTBS) testing and 2 teeth for surface morphology analysis (Figure 1).

2.3. Specimen Preparation

The occlusal third of the clinical crown was removed using a low‐speed precision saw (Isomet 1000, Buehler Ltd., Lake Bluff, IL, USA) under water cooling to expose a flat mid‐coronal dentin surface. To create a standardized smear layer, the dentin surfaces were polished with 400‐ and 600‐grit silicon carbide abrasive papers (TOA, Bangkok, Thailand) under water irrigation for 30 s. Specimens (n = 12) were then subjected to the assigned adhesive and irradiation protocols. A single operator performed all bonding procedures in strict accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions (Table 1). Light activation was performed using an LED curing unit (Bluephase G4, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) with an output intensity >800 mW/cm2. A cylindrical plastic matrix, measuring 4 mm. in height and 8 mm. in internal diameter, was positioned on the prepared surface, and a resin composite (Filtek Z350XT, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) was applied using an incremental technique. Each increment was light‐cured for 20 s until a total restoration height of 4 mm was achieved. Following restoration, the specimens were stored in distilled water at 37°C for 24 h prior to microtensile bond strength testing.

2.4. Irradiation Procedure

The irradiation protocol was adapted from previous studies [8, 18]. Throughout the experimental period, all specimens were stored in distilled water at 37°C. Irradiation was administered at Surin Cancer Hospital (Thailand) using an intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) system (Varian RapidArc, Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). To maintain hydration during irradiation, each specimen was encapsulated in wax with a small aperture that permitted the exchange of fresh storage medium; only the excess medium was blotted away to prevent dehydration without desiccating the specimen. The protocol consisted of a daily fractional dose of 2 Gy, delivered 5 days per week, to a cumulative dose of 70 Gy. Each fraction was delivered in under 10 min, as determined from the computed tomography (CT)‐based treatment plan, so that the specimens were out of the hydrated storage medium for less than 10 min per fraction. Dosimetric calibration was verified using a CT simulator (Aquilion LB TSX‐201A, Toshiba Corp., Tokyo, Japan), and treatment planning data were analyzed using the Eclipse treatment planning system (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA, USA). The delayed experimental groups were established to standardize the interval between restoration placement and microtensile bond strength testing, with a 7‐week period corresponding to the irradiation schedule.

2.5. Microtensile Bond Strength Test (µTBS)

Following restoration, specimens were sectioned longitudinally in both mesio‐distal and buccolingual directions using a low‐speed precision saw (Isomet 1000) with water cooling to prevent thermal damage [19, 20]. This sectioning protocol produced composite‐dentin beams (minimum n = 4 per tooth) with a cross‐sectional area of approximately 1.00 ± 0.05 mm2, as verified by a digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan). Beams were secured to a testing jig using a cyanoacrylate adhesive (Model Repair II Blue, Dentsply Sirona, Tokyo, Japan). Microtensile bond strength testing was conducted using a universal testing machine (EZ‐S, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a 500 N load cell at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min until fracture occurred. The mean microtensile bond strength value per tooth was used as the statistical unit; individual beams from the same tooth were not treated as independent observations to avoid pseudo‐replication. Bond strength (σ) was calculated in megapascals (MPa) using the formula σ = F/A, where F is the load at failure (N) and A is the cross‐sectional area (mm2). Pre‐test failures were recorded and excluded from the statistical analysis.

2.6. Mode of Failure and Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Failure modes were classified into four distinct categories: cohesive failure in dentin, cohesive failure in the resin composite, adhesive failure at the interface, and mixed failure [20]. Fractured surfaces of eligible specimens were examined under a stereomicroscope (SZ 61, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 45× magnification to determine the fracture pattern.

For SEM analysis of the adhesive interface, representative specimens were polished sequentially with silicon carbide papers up to 2000‐grit. The surfaces were etched with 37% phosphoric acid for 30 s, rinsed with deionized water, and deproteinized using 10% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) for 10 min to remove the collagen network and expose resin tags. Specimens were then ultrasonically cleaned in deionized water for 10 min and air‐dried for 24 h [21]. For the evaluation of dentin collagen morphology, the protocol was identical, with the exception that the sodium hypochlorite deproteinization step was omitted to preserve the collagen structure.

Prepared specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs using carbon conductive tape and sputter‐coated with a gold‐palladium (Au/Pd) alloy. High‐resolution imaging was performed using a field‐emission SEM (Quanta 250 FEG, FEI Company, Hillsboro, OR, USA). The adhesive layer was evaluated at 3000× magnification, while dentin collagen morphology was assessed at 20,000× and 40,000× magnification.

2.7. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the microtensile bond strength (μTBS) and failure mode distributions. The normality of data distribution was verified using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while Levene’s test was employed to assess the homogeneity of variances. A two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to determine the main effects and interactions of the independent variables: restorative timing (relative to irradiation) and adhesive system type. Significant differences were identified using Bonferroni’s post hoc test. Failure mode distributions were analyzed using the Pearson Chi‐square test (χ 2). All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) using SPSS software (version 29.1.2, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

3. Results

3.1. Dentin Bond Strength Evaluation

The Shapiro–Wilk test verified the normal distribution of the data (p > 0.05). Two‐way ANOVA results (Table 2) revealed significant main effects for both the timing of the restoration and the type of adhesive system on microtensile bond strength (p < 0.01). Furthermore, a statistically significant interaction was observed between these two factors (p < 0.01), necessitating subsequent pairwise comparisons using the Bonferroni post hoc test.

3.2. Mode of Failure Evaluation and Adhesive Interface Analysis

The Pearson Chi‐square analysis of fracture patterns indicated statistically significant differences among the experimental groups (χ 2 = 98.029, p < 0.001). As illustrated in Figure 2, the nonirradiated control groups (ER and SE) were characterized by a predominance of cohesive failures within the resin composite, with minimal adhesive failure. Conversely, irradiation induced a significant shift in the failure mode distribution, with irradiated groups exhibiting a higher frequency of mixed failures, followed by adhesive failures, relative to controls.

SEM of the adhesive interface at 3000× magnification (Figure 3) demonstrated distinct variations in resin tag morphology, hybrid layer thickness, and adhesive integrity. In the ER system, irradiated specimens presented with reduced resin tag density compared to controls; notably, the RBI subgroup exhibited gap formation at the hybrid layer interface. While the SE irradiated groups similarly displayed compromised resin tag formation, the structural consistency of their hybrid layer remained comparable to that of the control group.

4. Discussion

Based on the findings of this study, the first null hypothesis was rejected, as irradiated dentin exhibited significantly lower microtensile bond strength compared to the nonirradiated control group (p < 0.01). The second null hypothesis was rejected, as the influence of restorative timing was dependent on the adhesive system employed. Specifically, the ER system demonstrated significantly higher bond strength when applied after irradiation (IBR) compared to before irradiation (RBI). In contrast, the SE system showed no statistically significant difference in bond strength regardless of the restorative timing relative to the radiation protocol. The third null hypothesis was also rejected, as adhesive system type significantly affected bond strength, with the ER system outperforming the SE system in the post‐irradiation groups (p < 0.05).

Gold‐standard adhesive systems have demonstrated excellent long‐term clinical performance, characterized by low annual failure rates [14] and strong immediate bond strength [22]. This success is primarily attributed to a stable adhesive interface, maintained through both micromechanical retention and chemical interactions between resin monomers and dentin within the hybrid layer [23, 24]. This study selected gold‐standard adhesives for both the ER system (OptiBond FL) and the SE system (Clearfil SE Bond), as their efficacy has been well‐documented with proven clinical performance [14], making them widely recommended as reference adhesives for their respective systems. Based on previous studies [9, 10], the stability of bond strength was anticipated for both adhesive systems following irradiation. However, the results of the present study demonstrated a significant negative impact on bond integrity post‐irradiation.

The RBI group using the ER system exhibited the lowest microtensile bond strength, a reduction that was statistically significant compared to all other groups. This compromise suggests that irradiation contributes indirectly to the degradation of the established hybrid layer. Biscaro et al. [25] noted that such adverse effects are particularly pronounced during the initial fractional dose. Mechanistically, radiation‐induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) trigger the activation of host‐derived matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) within the dentin matrix [26]. In the RBI group, this enzymatic activation likely accelerated the hydrolysis of collagen fibrils and the adhesive interface subsequent to bonding, thereby weakening the hybrid layer’s structural integrity [9, 17, 25].

This proposed mechanism is corroborated by SEM observations, which revealed distinct micro‐gaps beneath the hybrid layer in the RBI ER specimens. Additionally, the quality of resin tags were reduced compared to the control group, suggesting the detrimental effects of irradiation damage. Conversely, the IBR group maintained a comparatively intact hybrid layer. Given that MMP activity typically persists for approximately 8 h post‐activation [26], restorative procedures performed after this window may avoid the acute phase of enzymatic degradation. Furthermore, radiation may induce microporosity in the dentin, increasing its surface energy [16]. This physical change potentially enhances the penetration of the ER adhesive, allowing it to encapsulate the collagen fibrils and beneficially gain micromechanical retention on compromised substrates [6].

The bonding mechanism of SE adhesive systems relied on the chemical interaction between functional monomers, such as 10‐methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10‐MDP) and the hydroxyapatite remaining beneath the smear layer. This interaction effectively integrated the smear layer into the hybrid layer, minimizing collagen exposure and enhancing stability through chemical fixation [23, 27, 28]. However, the adhesive interface remained susceptible to hydrolytic degradation due to the intrinsic moisture in dentinal tubules. This process, characterized by the hydrolysis of the ester moiety in 10‐MDP and the fragmentation of smear layer proteins [29], was significantly accelerated by irradiation, leading to the observed reduction in bond strength compared to nonirradiated controls [16].

Within the irradiated SE groups, no statistically significant differences in microtensile bond strength were observed regardless of restorative timing (RBI, IBR, or delayed). This uniformity suggested that the primary mode of failure was governed by radiation‐induced substrate alterations rather than the timing of the procedure. Previous studies indicated that irradiation significantly reduces the dentin mineral content and the calcium/phosphate ratio [6, 8]. Consequently, the efficacy of 10‐MDP was compromised via two mechanisms: (1) direct reduction in available hydroxyapatite calcium for ionic bonding and (2) inhibition of the 10‐MDP‐Ca salt formation essential for protective nanolayering. Furthermore, irradiation increases the prevalence of denatured proteins, indicated by a decreased amide III ratio [8], which created a hydrophilic barrier that interfered with adhesive infiltration [30]. This interference was confirmed by SEM observations, showing reduced resin tag formation in irradiated SE specimens relative to controls. This is in line with broader evidence that substrate microstructure and material degradation influence adhesive bond strength [31].

Previous research produced conflicting results regarding how radiation affected bond strength, with some studies finding negative impacts [9, 10, 25] and others indicated no significant compromise [32–34]. These inconsistencies were likely attributable to methodological heterogeneity, particularly variations in radiation protocols, adhesive systems, and specimen preparation. Notably, studies involving tooth sectioning prior to irradiation consistently reported more pronounced negative outcomes, suggesting that exposing the dentin substrate directly to radiation might have exaggerated the effects compared to clinical conditions where the tooth was intact.

In this study, we aimed to replicate a specific clinical scenario by strictly adhering to an established fractional radiation protocol [8]. The methodology involved applying a specific fractional dose, performing whole‐tooth irradiation, refreshing the storage media post‐irradiation, and keeping the restored specimens intact prior to irradiation. Despite these controlled conditions, the results demonstrated a significant reduction in microtensile bond strength across all experimental groups. This compromise in adhesion was attributed to radiation‐induced alterations at the adhesive interface, occurring via two primary mechanisms. First, the direct effect of ionizing radiation involved the excitation of target tissue molecules, triggering random interactions along the radiation track that resulted in collagen fiber disruption, monomer bond cleavage [35], and decarboxylation [15, 36]. Second, the indirect effect was mediated by the radiolysis of water molecules within the dentinal tubules. This process generated ROS and free radicals, which initiated oxidative hydrolysis, ultimately degrading the collagen network essential for hybrid layer formation. This structural degradation directly correlated with a significant shift toward adhesive failure, underscoring the destructive impact of radiation on the bonding interface.

To control for the potential confounding effect of hydrolytic degradation during the experimental timeline, the delayed testing group was subjected to a water storage interval equivalent to the duration of the radiation protocol (~2 months). This design permitted a direct methodological comparison with the RBI group. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference in microtensile bond strength between the delayed testing and immediate testing groups for either the ER or the SE system (p > 0.05). This finding indicates that the additional 2‐month storage period did not significantly influence the adhesive outcome in either adhesive system, thereby isolating irradiation as the primary variable affecting bond strength.

Our findings indicate that restoration timing was a critical factor for ER adhesives. Restorations placed before irradiation yielded the lowest bond strength values, while those placed after irradiation proved to be the strongest. In contrast, the SE system showed no statistically significant altered performance regardless of when the restoration was applied. Clinically, if restoration was unavoidable in a patient undergoing radiotherapy, either an ER or a SE adhesive system could be utilized, as the study demonstrated no statistically significant difference between the two protocols. Nevertheless, for restorations performed after the completion of radiotherapy, the use of an ER adhesive system was recommended. This protocol yielded a significantly higher microtensile bond strength compared to all other evaluated scenarios.

Limitation of this study was its in vitro design, which excludes the complete replication of the oral environment in head and neck cancer patients, specifically factors such as radiation‐induced xerostomia and compromised oral hygiene [3]. The utilization of extracted third molars from young adults remains the preferred methodology for optimally standardizing the tooth substrate during experimental interventions, despite the peak incidence of head and neck cancer occurring among individuals aged 40–50 years [5]. Consequently, the structural characteristics of older, potentially sclerotic dentin were not represented. Future investigations should extend to other adhesive categories, including 2‐step ER and universal (1‐step SE) systems. Only one ER and one SE adhesive were evaluated; therefore, these findings should not be generalized to all adhesive systems within these categories. Additionally, research incorporating simulated pulpal pressure, long‐term aging protocols, and regional dentin variability is warranted to better predict clinical performance.

5. Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, the results demonstrate that radiotherapy compromises dentin microtensile bond strength, depending on both the adhesive system type and the restoration timing. Clinical decision‐making regarding adhesive selection depends on the timing of the restorative procedure relative to the radiotherapy. If the restoration is performed prior to radiotherapy, both adhesive systems can be effectively utilized. Conversely, if restorative intervention is required after radiotherapy, clinicians are advised to implement an ER protocol to optimize adhesive retention.
