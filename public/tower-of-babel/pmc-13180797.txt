“Reactions in Leprosy: Updated Insights Into Pathophysiology, Clinical Spectrum, and Therapeutic Approaches—A Narrative Review”
Authors: Bhaskar Gupta, Manesha Singh, Mohamad Goldust, Kinnor Das, Surajit Gorai, Prajwal Pudasaini, Seema Sitaula, Achyut Pokhrel, Esna Thapa, Sunil Jaiswal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsReaction in leprosy is an immune‐mediated acute inflammatory episodes that occur in the natural course of Hansen's disease. Reaction can occur before, during, or after starting multidrug therapy and is a major cause of nerve damage, morbidity, and disability in affected individuals. The aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive overview on the pathophysiology, clinical manifestations, and current management strategies for reactions in leprosy.MethodsA narrative review of published literature was conducted focusing on the risk factors, immunopathogenesis, clinical presentation, severity scoring, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic approaches of Type 1 (reversal reaction), Type 2 (Erythema Nodosum Leprosum), and Type 3 (Lucio phenomenon) leprosy reactions. Relevant studies, guidelines, and reviews were analyzed using the databases PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar from 2005 to 2025, to provide an updated synthesis of knowledge. The keywords used included: “leprosy reactions”, “type 1 reaction”, “type 2 reaction”, “lucio phenomenon”, “immunopathogenesis”, “clinical presentation”, “severity scoring”, and “management”. Articles were screened based on relevance to the topic and selected through consensus among the authors.ResultsType 1 leprosy reaction is a delayed‐type hypersensitivity response due to enhanced cell‐mediated immunity, leading to the aggravation of pre‐existing skin lesions and neuritis. Type 2 reaction is an immune‐complex mediated phenomenon associated with systemic symptoms along with the fresh appearance of reddish subcutaneous nodules. Lucio phenomenon, which is due to endothelial cell injury, usually presents with tender purpura on the extremities which becomes necrotic and ulcerated. Management can be tailored according to the severity of the reactional episode.ConclusionSwift identification of leprosy reaction is important as it can be the first manifestation of leprosy in a large number of patients, and if not treated at an early stage, it may lead to deformity and disability.

Introduction

Reactions in leprosy are acute or subacute inflammatory episodes caused owing to immune responses to Mycobacterium leprae and/or its antigens [1]. Leprosy reactions may be the initial manifestation in 50% of leprosy patients [2]. Reactions can occur in any types of leprosy, except the indeterminate type [3]. If not treated expeditiously, patients may end up with deformity and disability [4]. Appearance of reaction might raise doubts in patients regarding cure of the disease and repeated attacks of reaction might also affect drug compliance [3]. Three types of leprosy reactions have been described, namely Type 1 leprosy reaction, Type 2 leprosy reaction, and Lucio phenomenon which is at times called as Type 3 reaction [5].

Leprosy reactions pose persistent diagnostic and management challenges due to their variable clinical presentations and risk of neurological damage. Despite advances in treatment, early recognition remains difficult, particularly in resource‐limited settings. Information on reactions in leprosy remains scattered and fragmented across immunology, infectious disease, dermatology, and public health domains, making it challenging for frontline clinicians to access a consolidated, practical, and clinically meaningful source. This review aims to fill this gap by creating a single and comprehensive reference that brings together classical concepts, current evidence, and emerging perspectives on reactions in leprosy. Our review intends to serve as a practical, up‐to‐date, and accessible resource for dermatologists, trainees, and primary care workers who frequently come across leprosy reactions. This review synthesizes classical concepts with the latest evidence to serve as a practical, up‐to‐date resource for front‐line clinicians.

Methods

This is a narrative review aimed at providing an updated synthesis of leprosy reactions. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using the keywords “lepra reactions”, “type 1 reaction”, “type 2 reaction”. “Lucio phenomenon”. Relevant English‐language articles published between 2005 and 2025 were included. Priority was given to systematic reviews, guidelines, original research, and high impact narrative reviews. Articles were screened for relevance by all authors, and evidence was synthesized with emphasis on immunological insights, practical management in resource limited settings, and identification of research gaps. Reference selection was based on consensus among the authors to ensure balanced representation of classical concepts and emerging data.

Type 1 Leprosy Reaction (T1R)

Type 1 reaction (T1R) is seen in borderline leprosy patients, mostly Borderline Tuberculoid (BT) and Borderline Borderline (BB) leprosy during first 6 months of commencement of therapy. In Borderline Lepromatous (BL) leprosy, T1R occurs after longer intervals of therapy. Although T1R is characteristic of borderline leprosy, it has been reported in some cases of sub‐polar Lepromatous Leprosy (LLs) as well [6]. Overall prevalence of T1R varies from 9% to 36% [7]. T1R is also called reversal reaction as there is a natural tendency for borderline leprosy to downgrade towards the lepromatous pole, in the absence of treatment [1]. BB patients may upgrade towards tuberculoid spectrum with shift in immunity [8].

Pathogenesis of T1R

Triggering factors of T1R include MDT initiation, borderline leprosy, age > 20 years, positive acid‐fast bacilli (AFB) in smears, extensive disease (multiple cutaneous lesions, multiple nerve trunks) and facial involvement, intercurrent infections, vaccination, chemotherapy, puerperium, presence of anti‐PGL‐I antibodies, stress, trauma, and use of oral contraceptives [5, 6, 7, 9]. Genetic risk factors include Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) in the TNFSF8 locus and missense mutation in LRRK2 gene [10, 11].

T1R is a type of delayed hypersensitivity reaction (Coombs and Gell Type IV) against Mycobacterium leprae. Antigens are released after phagocytosis of lepra bacilli. Antigen presenting cells (APCs) capture and present antigens to lymph nodes stimulating adaptive immune response through Major Histocompatibility Complex II. APCs produce IL‐12 which differentiates CD4 + T cells into effector Th cells. IL‐2 released by effector Th cells stimulates the proliferation of Th cells and CD8 + T cells. CD8 + T cells migrate to peripheral tissues and release perforin and granzymes, that destroys schwann cells. APCs produce IL‐6 and TGF‐B that differentiate naive T cells into Th17 cells. Th‐17 releases IL‐17 causing neutrophil chemotaxis. APCs also produce IFN‐γ which activates macrophages. Activated macrophages produce interferon‐gamma (IFN‐γ), tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), interleukin‐2 (IL‐2), interleukin‐12 (IL‐12), transforming growth factor‐beta (TGF‐β), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) exacerbating tissue damage. The activated immune response is essential for bacterial clearance but causes collateral tissue damage. Neuritis in Type 1 reaction is mainly caused by TNF‐α and IFN‐γ that directly stimulates the type C and A delta fibers [12, 13].

Recent studies emphasize the central role of autoimmune mechanism (molecular mimicry between cytokeratin‐10 and HSP65), autophagy defects, increase in Th17 cells and reduction in Treg cells, in driving tissue damage during reaction episodes in leprosy, offering potential targets for future therapies [12, 13].

Clinical Features of T1R

Cutaneous features of T1R are typified by changes in pre‐existing lesions (Figure 1). Brisk changes in the appearance of some/all skin lesions can be observed. The lesions become erythematous, tumid, shiny, and warm. They tend to resolve with desquamation. In appalling cases, there may be necrosis which may lead to ulceration. Swelling of hands, feet, and/or face or all three is common. Along with swelling, there may be associated tenderness of palms and soles. Systemic disturbances are infrequent, even so, fever and malaise may be present, particularly in reactions associated with downgrading reactions [6]. Appearance of new lesion(s) is fitful and they can be observed usually in downgrading reaction [1].

Type 1 Lepra Reaction. Extension of the pre‐existing lesion on the left cheek along with an increase in the erythema, swelling, induration, and mild scaling.

Nerve involvement in T1R is attributed to increased intraneural edema, granuloma formation within the nerve, and abscess formation in severe cases. Trauma to the nerve is aggravated at sites where nerve trunks are trapped within tunnel‐like structures formed by bone/fascia. There may be pain, tenderness, and rapid swelling of nerves. Pain may be present along the distribution of affected nerve, for example: pain in the medial border of the wrist and/or little finger due to involvement of ulnar nerve. Motor disturbances are common. Tinel sign may be positive, i.e. pressure exerted on affected nerves will cause tingling sensation distally. Examples of nerve damage include: ulnar nerve injury causing claw hand. lateral popliteal nerve injury resulting in foot drop, and facial nerve involvement leading to facial Palsy [14, 15].

According to the duration of symptoms, different types of T1R have been described. Acute: symptoms persisting ≤ 1 month; subacute: 1 month to 6 months; chronic: > 6 months; recent: covers both acute and subacute forms; recurrent: reactions occurring after 3 months of stopping anti‐reaction treatment; and late‐ reversal reaction: reaction occurring any time after completion of multi‐drug therapy (MDT). According to the activity, they can be categorized into upgrading reaction (rapid increase in specific cell‐mediated immunity against Mycobacterium leprae), downgrading reaction (rapid decline in cell‐mediated immunity), and static reaction (without any change in the immunity). Histopathology of T1R

Histopathology of skin with reversal reaction shows edema of dermis and lymphocytes, epithelioid cells, giant cells, and diminished number of lepra bacilli. T1R shows diffuse extracellular edema in and around granuloma and superficial dermis (Figures 2 and 3). Downgrading T1R shows diffuse macrophage infiltration, few lymphocytes, subtle giant cells, and dermal edema on histopathology [17].

Serological Markers of T1R

The serological markers of T1R include higher serum levels of TNF‐α, IFN‐γ, IL‐6, lipoarabinomannan, neopterin, CXCL10, MMP‐9, CD3 + TCRγδ+ cells, and IL‐17 [18].

Differential Dignosis of T1R

The differential diagnosis that needs to be considered while diagnosing T1R include erysipelas, acute urticarial wheals, cellulitis, and insect bite [16]. Rarely, T1R mimicking Thromboangiitis Obliterans has been reported in a patient with Borderline Lepromatous Leprosy [19].

Severity Scale for T1R

Severity is based on the INFIR clinical severity scoring system. It involves assessing three aspects of T1R using 21 items. The first part examines skin involvement by looking at the number of affected lesions, the level of inflammation, and the presence of peripheral edema (Section A). The second part evaluates nerve sensory function in the hands and feet using Semmes‐Weinstein monofilaments and corneal sensation with cotton wool (Section B). The third part assesses motor function in the nerves of the face, hands, and feet using a standard measure of muscle power using MRC grading (Section C). The total score, ranging from 0 to 63, is the sum of scores from sections A, B, and C [1]. The maximum scores for sections A, B, and C are 9, 24, and 30, respectively. A mild T1R is indicated by a score of 4 or less, a moderate T1R by a score between 4.5 and 8.4, and a severe T1R by a score of 9 or more (Table 2) [20].

Management of T1R

Management of T1R can be difficult and needs a stepwise approach. The reactional episode needs grading and MDT needs to be started or continued so that the antigen load can be reduced. Specific treatment of reaction and nerve involvement is then started [16].

Mild cases refer to few skin lesions with features of reaction clinically, without any nerve pain or loss of function whereas severe T1R is characterised by nerve pain/paresthesia, increasing loss of nerve function, fever/discomfort, edema of hands and/or feet, reaction persisting > 6 weeks, skin lesions over face and ulcerative skin lesion [16]. Non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may be sufficient in patients with mild T1R. Evidence suggests that oral corticosteroids in a dose of 0.5–1 mg/kg/day gradually tapered over 20 weeks, remain the mainstay for the management of severe T1R. Other immunosuppressants for management of T1R include low dose methotrexate (5–7.5 mg/week), cyclosporine, azathioprine, and mycophenolate mofetil. The effect of low dose methotrexate in T1R has been attributed to increased adenosine level, reduced level of proinflammatory cytokines, and increased anti‐inflammatory cytokines. Because of slower onset of action and lack of effect on intraneuronal edema, use of azathioprine is recommended in addition to corticosteroids. Infliximab therapy has shown favourable outcome in a patient with Type 1 Reaction [22, 23, 24, 25].

Management of nerve involvement is another challenging issue. In acute phase, rest to the nerve by splinting and padding is necessary. After active phase is over, active and passive exercises, oil massage, short wave diathermy, and ultrasonic therapy can be tried. Non‐Steroidal Anti‐Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDS) for pain relief can be added. Surgical decompression (neurolysis) can be done in case of nerve abscess formation [26].

Type 2 Leprosy Reaction (T2R)

The first clinical and histopathological description of T2R was given by Mosuke Murata, a Japanese leprologist in a German Journal of Pathology in 1912 [27]. T2R is seen in Lepromatous Leprosy or Borderline Lepromatous (BL) leprosy [28]. Incidence of Erythema Nodosum Leprosum (ENL) in LL has been reported to be 50% and 10% in BL cases [29]. It does not occur in relation to therapy, unlike T1R. In contrast to T1R, it occurs late, during the course of treatment [17].

Pathogenesis of T2R

Triggering factors of T2R include LL with a Bacteriological index (BI) > 4, < 40 years of age, coinfections (HIV, tuberculosis, hepatitis B and C, typhoid), pregnancy and lactation (30%–40% of pregnant and lactating women are at risk to develop ENL), intercurrent infections (streptococcal, viral, intestinal parasites, filariasis, malaria), vaccination, psychological stress, trauma/surgical interventions, decreased C4 and elevated anti‐mycobacterium leprae antibodies in newly diagnosed leprosy, a strongly positive Mantoux test, and ingestion of potassium iodide [16].

ENL is an immune complex‐mediated (antigen‐antibody) disease (Coombs and Gel Type 3 reaction) which results in activation of complements (C3a and C5a). C3a and C5a recruit and activate neutrophils. Activated neutrophils release pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐6, IL‐1, and TNF‐α leading to tissue damage [12, 13].

A deficiency of complement protein C4B and lower levels of regulatory T‐cells is associated with an increased risk of ENL. CD4 + /CD8+ ratio is increased in ENL. CD8 + T lymphocytes secrete perforin and granzymes which leads to tissue damage. IFN‐γ is increased in the serum and skin of patients with ENL [29, 30]. Recent studies suggest increased populations of B lymphocytes and plasma cells, formation of Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs), increased expression of Toll‐like receptor 9, decreased expression of Tregs along with increased IL‐17 expression and decreased expression of TGF‐ β in the pathogenesis of ENL [31].

Clinical Features of T2R

Patients present with crops of brightly erythematous tender nodules or plaque of variable size, distributed symmetrically (Figure 4). Skin lesions are warmer, and blanches with slight pressure. The lesions might undergo secondary changes like ulceration and necrosis. There is no change in preexisting leprosy lesion(s) [32]. The lesions are evanescent, which means they stay for 2–3 days [15]. Fresh crops of lesions come in the evening when endogenous cortisol is low. It may be associated with fever, malaise, and edema of face and extremities. When the lesions subside, they desquamate and leave a bluish stain [33]. In severe cases, vesicular/bullous lesions may occur which may break down which is known as Erythema Necroticans. Invaded blood vessels undergo necrosis and obliteration of the lumen causing obliterative angiitis and endarteritis [17, 34]. In the olden days, ENL was called as roseolar leprosy [35]. Numerous systemic features are associated with T2R (Table 4) [16].

T2R can be classified according to onset of symptoms, morphology of lesions, and based on number of episodes. Based on onset of symptoms, T2R can be of cutaneous onset, rheumatic onset, or mixed type [16]. T2R presenting as monoarthritis has been reported in a case report post MDT [36]. Based on the predominance of the lesions, T2R can be nodular, pustular, bullous, ulcerated, necrotic, and erythema multiforme type [15]. Based on episodes, T2R can be classified as new ENL: 'the occurrence of ENL for the first time in a patient with LL'; acute ENL—'a single episode lasting less than 24 weeks while on corticosteroids treatment and in which treatment was slowly withdrawn with no recurrence of ENL whilst on treatment; recurrent ENL—'if a patient experiences second or subsequent episode of ENL occurring 28 days or more after stopping treatment for ENL; and chronic ENL—'if occurring for 24 weeks or more during which a patient requires treatment for ENL either continuously or where any treatment‐free period had been 27 days or less [37]. Histopathology of T2R

ENL lesions show histopathology features of LL along with dense infiltration of superficial and/or deep dermis and/or subcutaneous tissue by neutrophils. Neutrophilic infiltration is superimposed on already existing lepromatous granuloma (Figures 5, 6, 7). Erythema Necroticans show obliterative angiitis and endarteritis. There is local reduction in bacterial load as most bacilli are fragmented and granular. Neuritis associated with T2R leads to inflammatory edema and cellular exudates in the perineum [17].

Serological Markers of T2R

High serum levels of Th17, γδT cells, IL‐6, IL‐17, IL‐10 receptor 1, CCL11, IgM anti‐PGL‐I, IgG anti‐10 kDa hsp antibodies, LID‐1 IgG and IgM, C3d‐associated immune‐complexes, C4, IL‐1B, C‐C motif, CCL11, CCL2, CCL3, CCL5, PTX‐3, CD64, serum amyloid A protein, C‐reactive protein, α‐acid glycoprotein, α2 chain of haptoglobin, and fibrinogen are associated with T2R [18].

Differential Diagnosis of T2R

The most important differential diagnoses of T2R include Erythema Nodosum (EN), Lupus Erythematosus, and Sweet Syndrome [17, 38, 39]. It becomes very important to differentiate between ENL vs EN. It should be kept in mind that ENL lesions are evanescent, more numerous, and can occur in areas other than the lower leg (flexor aspect of arm, medial aspect of thigh, face) [17].

Severity Scale for ENL (ENLIST ENL Severity Scale)

The 10‐item ENLIST ENL Severity Scale (EESS) is the first reliable measure of ENL severity. It enhances our ability to assess and compare patients and their treatments for this challenging complication of leprosy. This scale is valuable for physicians in monitoring and treating ENL, offering a simple application. It serves as an effective outcome measure for treatment studies and facilitates standardization in clinical and laboratory research on ENL. Management of T2R

Like T1R the management of T2R involves a stepwise approach which includes grading of reaction, treatment of precipitating factor, continuation or starting of MDT, specific treatment of T2R, and underlying systemic involvement. For treatment of mild reactions, NSAIDs like acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) 500 mg 6 hourly and colchicine 0.5 mg TDS with a tapering course can be tried. The treatment of severe T2R is more complicated and requires systemic therapy in acute cases, including oral steroids or thalidomide (Table 7) [40, 41].

Immunotherapy

WHO recommends use of oral steroid in severe ENL [21]. Thalidomide is an effective and rapidly acting TNF‐α inhibitor for severe ENL. Cyclosporine, azathioprine, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide pulse therapy, mycophenolate mofetil, apremilast, minocycline, tenidap, TNF‐α blockers (Etanercept, infliximab), plasma exchange, and intravenous immunoglobulin have shown promising results in ENL [12, 13, 42, 43, 44]. Pentoxyphyline is a good option in HIV co‐infection where long‐term corticosteroids can't be given [45, 46]. Newer drugs for T2R include thalidomide derivatives like Lenalidomide and Pomalidomide [47].

MDT is restarted in patients with chronic/recurrent ENL. In patients with high BI and recurrent episodes, regimen including minocycline, clofazimine, and ofloxacin might be effective. Clofazimine is used in chronic ENL due to slower onset of action. Immunotherapy with Mycobacterium indicus pranii (MIP) vaccine has shown potential benefits in preventing and controlling recalcitrant ENL [12, 13].

Type 3 Reaction/Lucio Phenomenon

This type of reaction is confined to untreated patients with diffuse non‐nodular form of LL and is chiefly encountered in Latin Americans [48].

Clinical Features of Lucio phenomenon include initial painful tender red patches (particularly on extremities) which become purpuric. The center of the purpura becomes necrotic and ulcerated which develops black/brown crust (eschar) which then falls off after a few days to leave superficial atrophic scar. On legs, ulceration is larger and more lasting with jagged edges and surrounded by an inflammatory zone. Lesions are more common in legs followed by thigh, forearm, and buttocks. Trunk and face are usually spared. Skin lesions spread in characteristic ascending fashion over the extremities and heal with atrophic scarring. There are no nodules, lymphadenopathy, or arthritis. There is usually high Morphological Index (MI) and Bacteriological Index (BI) [16, 49].

Main event in Lucio phenomenon is endothelial cell injury. Endothelial cells are colonised by AFB. There are endothelial proliferation and marked thickening of vessel walls to the point of obliteration. There is associated angiogenesis, vascular ectasia, and thrombosis. Patients have a singular deficit defense mechanism that permits unhindered multiplication of bacilli and exposure to bacterial antigen to circulating antibody results in vasculitis, infarction, and skin necrosis [49, 50].

On histopathology, there may be ischaemic necrosis of epidermis and dermis, necrotizing vasculitis of small blood vessels in the upper dermis, histiocytic and neutrophilic infiltrate in and around nerve fibers, blood vessels, adnexa, severe focal endothelial proliferation of mid dermal vessels, presence of large number of bacilli in endothelial cells, and thickening of blood vessel wall with obliteration and thrombosis [50].

The important differential diagnosis of Lucio phenomenon includes the cutaneous manifestation of Antiphospholipid Antibody Syndrome (APLAS), and necrotizing variant of ENL (It should be noted that, in ENL fever and constitutional symptoms present) [51, 52]. APLAS can closely mimic Lucio phenomenon both clinically (purpuric/necrotic lesions) and histopathologically (thrombotic vasculopathy), particularly in patients with positive antiphospholipid antibodies. Differentiation requires a thorough clinical history (previous thrombosis, obstetric complications, or autoimmune features), serologic testing for antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti β2 glycoprotein 1), and skin biopsy showing thrombosis without endothelial colonization by acid‐fast bacilli, which is present in Lucio phenomenon [51, 52]. There is no unanimous guideline for the treatment of Lucio phenomena due to its infrequent occurrence. For the treatment of Lucio phenomenon, multibacillary MDT is the preferred treatment. Steroid has a good effect [48]. Thalidomide is considered ineffective [53].

Reactions in Special Situations

Reactions in Pregnancy

Reactions in pregnancy may be associated with alteration in the cell‐mediated immunity as well as changes in the various maternal hormones. T1R reactions usually occur during pregnancy, particularly in the post‐partum period. The restoration of cell‐mediated immunity following delivery can lead to T1R. ENL can occur during both pregnancy and the postpartum period [56]. MDT should be continued during pregnancy and lactation [57]. Oral steroid is a safe option for the treatment of acute episodes of leprosy reaction [56].

Reactions in Children

In children less than 15 years of age, episodes of reaction are common. Among the cases of leprosy reactions reposted in children, most cases are of T1R [58]. Leprosy reactions in children should be managed with low oral corticosteroid therapy (prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day) until resolution with gradual tapering. MDT should be continued [59]. Due to lack of information available on pharmacokinetic properties of thalidomide under 18 years of age, it is not authorized for administration in Type 2 Lepra Reaction (T2R) in children [60].

Reactions in HIV Co‐Infection

T1Rs have been reported as an immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) in HIV‐infected patients on antiretroviral therapy (ART) [61]. A study conducted by Menezes VM et al found ART to be a risk factor for T1R in HIV co‐infected individuals [62]. ENL can too be a manifestation of IRIS in HIV‐positive cases on ART. Prednisolone (1 mg/kg/day) is preferred choice of agent for managing T1R whereas thalidomide has been recommended in T2R except in women of childbearing age and in patients with peripheral neuropathy [63]. In patients with contraindications to chronic oral steroid use, high dose clofazimine can be used as an alternative treatment [64].

Practical Approach in Resource‐Limited Settings

Although advancing science, and newer developments are leading towards molecular diagnostic methods, health care centers in low resource settings still largely rely on clinical morphology, phenotypic characterization and available routine ancillary investigations for diagnostic and treatment proceedings of reactions in leprosy. This discrepancy of availability further adds on to the tumult of diagnostic and therapeutic lag time which further deters the patient's condition. The subtle detection and delineation of involvement of particular cytokine forms the benchmark of precision and personalized treatment with immunotherapy and biologics which are laregely being used in the most advanced and developed parts of the world [65]. On the contrary, the resource poor settings still utilize the conventional cost‐effective treatments viz. corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (methotrexate and azathioprine), clofazimine, and thalidomide which have been a boon to therapeutic practice but need vigilant monitoring and follow‐up, which is often arduous for resource poor settings [66].

Conclusion

A comprehensive understanding of leprosy reactions has evolved significantly, providing insights into their pathogenesis, clinical spectrum, and therapeutic approaches, highlighting the importance of early recognition, timely intervention, and individualized treatment strategies. Despite these advances, key uncertainties remain: (1) lack of reliable biomarkers for early prediction and monitoring of reactions; (2) absence of representative animal models that reproduce the full spectrum of reactional episodes; (3) high quality randomized controlled trials evaluating newer agents in diverse populations; (4) long‐term outcomes and cost‐effectiveness of therapies in low‐resource settings; and (5) the precise role of genetic, microbiome, and environmental factors in reaction susceptibility. Thus, ongoing research addressing these gaps is essential to further optimize prevention and patient outcomes.
