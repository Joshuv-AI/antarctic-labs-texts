A rare and severe case of toxic megacolon: Radiological emergency
Authors: Salma Abddaimi, Hicham Benramdane, Adnane Boubacar, Hamid Ziani, Siham Nasri, Imane Kamaoui, Imane Skiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractToxic megacolon is a rare and life-threatening complication of ulcerative colitis, representing a medical and surgical emergency with a poor prognosis.We report the case of a 31-year-old man with no significant past medical history, admitted for a 15-day history of bloody mucoid diarrhea associated with general health deterioration. Endoscopic and histopathological findings were consistent with a severe inaugural flare of ulcerative colitis. Despite optimal medical management, the clinical course was marked by the abrupt onset of septic shock. Abdominal imaging revealed acute colonic dilatation consistent with toxic megacolon, leading to an indication for emergency subtotal colectomy. The outcome was unfavorable, with the patient’s death occurring in the context of multiorgan failure.Keywords: Toxic megacolon, Ulcerative colitis, Severe acute colitis, Abdominopelvic CT angiography, Imaging, Multiorgan failure

Introduction

Toxic megacolon is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of inflammatory bowel diseases, most commonly occurring in ulcerative colitis. It is characterized by acute, non-obstructive colonic dilatation associated with signs of systemic toxicity and represents a true medical and surgical emergency [1]. The clinical presentation is often severe, combining gastrointestinal symptoms with a systemic inflammatory response, with a high risk of rapid progression to septic shock and multiorgan failure in the absence of prompt and appropriate management [2].

Abdominopelvic CT angiography plays a central role in confirming colonic dilatation, assessing the extent of disease, and identifying potential complications [3]. Management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving gastroenterologists, intensivists, and surgeons, with early surgical intervention indicated in cases of failure of medical therapy or clinical deterioration [4].

Through this case report, we describe a case of toxic megacolon complicating a severe inaugural flare of ulcerative colitis, highlighting the severity of this condition, the importance of early diagnosis, and the challenges of therapeutic management.

Case presentation

The patient was a 31-year-old man with no significant medical or surgical history. He had no recent medication use, no recent travel history, no tuberculosis exposure, and no family history of inflammatory bowel disease or gastrointestinal malignancy.

The disease began 15 days prior to admission, with 7 daily episodes of bloody mucoid diarrhea associated with peri-umbilical abdominal pain and tenesmus, without Koenig’s syndrome or extra-intestinal manifestations. The condition evolved in the context of initial apyrexia, with a rapid deterioration in general condition, including a 6-kg weight loss over 3 weeks, asthenia, and anorexia.

On admission, the patient was conscious, pale, and dehydrated, with a heart rate of 135 bpm, normal blood pressure, and stable respiratory status. Abdominal examination revealed diffuse tenderness without guarding or distension. Rectal examination revealed liquid stools, and no peripheral lymphadenopathy was noted.

Laboratory tests showed marked inflammation (CRP 300 mg/L), neutrophilic leukocytosis, normocytic normochromic anemia (Hb 11.9 g/dL), hypoalbuminemia (26 g/L), and a moderate coagulation disorder.

Abdominopelvic CT angiography revealed diffuse inflammatory thickening of the colon with submucosal edema and intense mucosal enhancement, predominantly involving the rectum.

Rectosigmoidoscopy showed severe erythematous-ulcerative proctitis with deep ulcerations. Histological examination confirmed moderate-to-severe ulcerative colitis, with no evidence of malignancy, CMV infection, or amoebiasis.

The patient was initially managed medically with intravenous corticosteroids, antibiotics, and therapeutic anticoagulation. After approximately 5 days of medical treatment, the patient showed only partial clinical improvement.

However, he suddenly developed septic shock characterized by hyperthermia (40°C), severe hypotension, tachycardia, hypoxemia, and multiorgan failure.

The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit and started on vasopressors, broad-spectrum antibiotics (imipenem-cilastatin and gentamicin), blood transfusion, and correction of electrolyte disturbances. Given the clinical deterioration and radiological findings, an emergency subtotal colectomy with diverting ileostomy was indicated. Despite prompt surgical intervention, the patient died intraoperatively due to refractory multiorgan failure.

Discussion

Toxic megacolon is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of inflammatory bowel diseases, mainly ulcerative colitis (UC). Its prevalence among patients with UC is estimated between 1% and 5%, while mortality remains high, reaching up to 25% in some series, particularly when diagnosis or surgical management is delayed. Several studies have reported that toxic megacolon accounts for approximately 0.4%-2.5% of hospitalizations for ulcerative colitis, although the incidence has decreased in recent decades due to earlier diagnosis and improved medical management. Despite this, it remains one of the most severe complications of inflammatory bowel disease and continues to be associated with significant morbidity and mortality [5].

It represents an acute, non-obstructive dilatation of the colon associated with severe inflammation and a pronounced systemic response. Triggering factors include a severe flare of ulcerative colitis, concomitant infection (Clostridium difficile, CMV), recent corticosteroid therapy, or medications affecting colonic motility [6]. In our case, the patient presented with a severe inaugural flare of ulcerative colitis, without any other identified triggering factor, highlighting that even the first manifestations of the disease can progress to toxic megacolon.

The typical presentation includes bloody diarrhea, abdominal pain, tenesmus, and signs of dehydration. Systemic manifestations such as tachycardia, hyperthermia, hypotension, or altered general condition reflect systemic involvement. In our case, the patient initially presented with bloody-mucoid diarrhea, peri-umbilical abdominal pain, and rapid deterioration of general condition, which subsequently progressed to septic shock and multiorgan failure, illustrating the potentially fulminant course of this complication. Biologically, a marked inflammatory syndrome, hypoalbuminemia, and coagulation abnormalities are common [7].

The diagnosis of toxic megacolon is based on a combination of clinical, biological, and radiological criteria. The classical criteria described by Jalan include radiological evidence of colonic dilatation greater than 6 cm associated with at least 3 of the following: fever above 38°C, heart rate greater than 120 bpm, leukocytosis above 10,500/mm³, or anemia. In addition, at least one of the following must be present: dehydration, altered mental status, electrolyte disturbances, or hypotension. These criteria help clinicians identify patients at high risk of deterioration and guide early therapeutic decisions [7].

Abdominopelvic CT angiography is the reference examination for evaluating toxic megacolon. It allows confirmation of acute non-obstructive colonic dilatation, precise measurement of colonic diameter, assessment of wall thickening, presence of submucosal edema, and mucosal enhancement, as well as identification of potential complications. Among these, colonic perforation, mesenteric thrombosis, intra-abdominal collections, or wall necrosis are major prognostic factors guiding surgical management [8].

The severity of the condition must be carefully evaluated in order to determine the optimal timing of surgical intervention. Clinical indicators such as persistent tachycardia, high fever, worsening abdominal pain, progressive colonic dilatation, and signs of systemic toxicity suggest failure of medical treatment. Radiological findings including colonic dilatation greater than 8-9 cm, pneumoperitoneum, or evidence of colonic ischemia also represent poor prognostic factors. In such situations, early surgical management is recommended to prevent catastrophic complications such as perforation or irreversible septic shock.

Initial management relies on intensive medical measures, including fluid and electrolyte correction, corticosteroid therapy, antibiotics, and anticoagulation if indicated. However, failure of medical treatment, clinical deterioration, or the onset of signs of shock necessitates urgent surgical intervention, most often a subtotal colectomy with diverting ileostomy [9].

Ulcerative colitis itself is a chronic inflammatory disease of the colon characterized by relapsing episodes of mucosal inflammation. In addition to toxic megacolon, several other complications may occur during the course of the disease. These include severe acute colitis, massive gastrointestinal bleeding, colonic perforation, colorectal cancer, and extra-intestinal manifestations affecting the joints, skin, eyes, and hepatobiliary system. The occurrence of toxic megacolon represents one of the most severe complications and requires rapid recognition and multidisciplinary management [9].

In the present case, the patient developed toxic megacolon during the inaugural severe flare of ulcerative colitis, rapidly progressing to septic shock and multiorgan failure despite intensive medical and surgical management. This case highlights the importance of early recognition of severity criteria and timely surgical decision-making in order to improve patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Toxic megacolon is a severe complication of ulcerative colitis, and imaging, particularly abdominopelvic CT angiography, plays a crucial role in confirming the diagnosis, assessing the extent of lesions, and guiding urgent management.

This case illustrates that toxic megacolon may occur during an inaugural severe flare of ulcerative colitis and can rapidly progress to life-threatening complications such as septic shock and multiorgan failure. Early recognition of severity signs and timely multidisciplinary management are essential to improve patient outcomes.
