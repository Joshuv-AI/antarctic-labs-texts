The relationship of grip and three-point pinch strength to ball velocity and elbow varus torque in high school pitchers
Authors: Solomon Isi, Brittany Dowling, Arden Shen, Alexander Hodakowski, Dave Heidloff, Jeremy Alland, Grant E Garrigues, Nikhil N Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand and forearm strength play an essential role in modulating and controlling forces across the elbow during pitching. In particular, grip and pinch strength reflect functional capacity of the flexor–pronator mass, which contributes to both performance and dynamic stability of the elbow. While diminished grip strength is often used clinically to monitor recovery following shoulder and elbow injury, values in adolescent pitchers are lacking. Furthermore, the relationship between grip and pinch strength with ball velocity and elbow varus torque remains poorly defined.MethodsThirty-eight healthy high school pitchers (16.0 ± 0.9 years, 183.3 ± 6.0 cm, 78.1 ± 9.2 kg) were included in this study. Bilateral grip and three-point pinch strength were assessed prior to pitching. Pitchers were then analyzed using 3D motion capture (480 Hz) and ball velocity was recorded with Trackman throwing fastballs. The pitch with the greatest ball velocity was used in the analysis. Paired t-tests compared dominant and non-dominant grip and pinch strength, and linear regression examined relationships between strength, ball velocity, and elbow torque (α = 0.05).ResultsDominant arm grip strength was greater than the non-dominant throwing arm (P = .001), while pinch strength showed no side to side differences (P = .696). Dominant grip strength demonstrated weak associations with height (r2 = 0.221, P = .002), weight (r2 = 0.217, P = .002), and ball velocity (r2 = 0.116, P = .021). No significant relationships were found between grip strength and elbow varus torque. Pinch strength was not associated with height, weight, age, elbow varus torque, or ball velocity.DiscussionWhile forearm musculature is thought to enhance medial elbow stability, grip and pinch strength were not associated with elbow varus torque in high school pitchers. These results suggest that static hand strength measures may not reflect the complex, dynamic demands placed on the elbow during pitching. Further evaluation may be necessary to determine secondary impact such as load and subsequent fatigue on the flexor pronator complex.ConclusionGrip and pinch strength values in high school pitchers may serve as reference data for clinicians assessing adolescent pitchers. Grip strength demonstrated a weak association with ball velocity; however, neither grip nor pinch strength was significantly related to elbow loading. This highlights the need for future work examining dynamic forearm muscle function in injury risk, recovery, and performance.Keywords: Baseball, Throwing, Overhead athlete, Forearm, Biomechanics, Strength

Elbow injuries are prevalent among adolescent baseball pitchers, with pain reported in 30%-50% of players.11 Approximately 25% of youth and high school pitchers sustain a diagnosed arm injury during their playing careers, with surgical intervention rates increasing as pitchers advance through their career.34 The medial ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) functions as a primary stabilizer of the elbow during the repetitive high-stress motion of pitching. Elevated elbow varus torque during pitching has been identified as a major contributing factor to UCL injury,12 leading to cumulative microtrauma that compromises ligament integrity.1,14 Injury to the UCL has been shown to negatively impact pitching ball velocity and accuracy.28

The pitching motion is a complex, coordinated full-body sequence that transfers energy through the kinetic chain to the throwing arm.16 This motion produces high varus torque at the elbow, which can increase medial joint gapping with repetitive loading. The flexor-pronator muscles, particularly the flexor digitorum superficialis and the flexor carpi ulnaris, play a crucial role in dynamic stabilization of the medial elbow against valgus torque and reduces medial joint gapping.3,19,21 Park and Ahmad32 demonstrated in cadaveric elbows that activation of these muscles dynamically stabilized the elbow against torque. Pitchers with symptomatic valgus instability exhibited decreased flexor pronator mass activity, further underscoring the protective contribution of these muscles to elbow stabilization during pitching.13,16 In addition, with increased emphasis on ball spin, the impact of grip strength on medial elbow pathology remains incompletely understood.

Because a direct measurement of flexor-pronator activity is difficult clinically, grip and pinch strength are often used as surrogate markers of forearm muscle force generation. These low-cost, clinically accessible measures of hand and forearm muscle function have been used as indicators of a general muscular strength and functional status.6,15,28,46 In baseball, 30 high-level amateur baseball pitchers were studied to quantify the progression of grip strength, muscle soreness, and pain threshold perception throughout the course of a simulated 75-pitch game.40 The authors found grip strength in the dominant arm declined by 12.66%, while muscle soreness increased by 25.88%. Moreover, pitch velocity and throwing accuracy declined with higher pitch counts. Muscle fatigue, particularly in the flexor carpi ulnaris and flexor carpi radialis, has been associated with decreased elbow stabilization and mechanical deterioration of the pitching motion.29,45 These findings support the notion that grip and pinch strength may be useful predictors in assessing musculoskeletal health and function in pitchers.

Despite the mechanical demands of holding the ball during a throw and the associated activation of forearm musculature that contributes to medial elbow stabilization, relatively few studies have examined whether grip or pinch strength relates to ball velocity or elbow varus torque during pitching. Harada et al17 found no association between grip strength and elbow injury in youth baseball players (9-12 years). Similarly, in professional baseball pitchers, grip strength was not found to be a predictor of injury over the course of a season.10 Hattori et al18 noted a non-significant trend toward increased medial elbow joint space gapping with lower grip strength in high school baseball players. Given these findings, the direct role of strength in pitching biomechanics and injury risk remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Grip strength and pinch strength are quick, non-invasive measures that may offer practical utility in screening and monitoring the throwing motion of athletes. If relationships exist between these simple strength metrics and important pitching variables, such as ball velocity or elbow loading, they could inform performance, training, and injury-prevention strategies. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate bilateral grip and pinch strength values in high school pitchers. In addition, we aimed to examine their relationships with ball velocity and elbow varus torque. It was hypothesized that greater grip and pinch strength would be associated with higher ball velocity and lower elbow varus torque during pitching.

Materials and methods

Thirty-eight high school pitchers were included in this study (mean ± standard deviation: 16.0 ± 0.9 years old, 183.3 ± 6.0 cm, 78.1 ± 9.2 kg). The following requirements were used for inclusion of the pitchers in this study: 1) at the time of testing, pitchers were on a high school or club team; 2) pitchers had no record of serious injury (requiring >2 weeks of rest or rehabilitation) in the past six months; and 3) pitchers had been cleared to participate in baseball activities by their physician. Each pitcher over the age of 18 provided written informed consent. For participants younger than 18 years, verbal assent was obtained from the pitcher along with written consent from a parent/guardian. Data were de-identified prior to distribution, qualifying for exemption from institutional board review under federal guidelines.

Data were collected over the course of one testing session. Testing took place within two weeks of the end of the summer competitive season, such that all pitchers were still throwing off the mound and had not yet concluded their fall training. Pitcher's demographic data were recorded: height, weight, age, and injury history. All strength measurements were collected by a single certified athletic trainer with 21 years of experience (D.H.).

Bilateral grip strength and pinch strength were assessed prior to the pitching session. To measure grip strength, a Vald Dynamometer (VALD Performance; Brisbane, Queensland, Australia) with the grip attachment was used. Grip strength testing was conducted using the standardized position recommended by the American Society of Hand Therapists. The pitcher was standing, with his shoulder adducted so that his arm was by his side with elbow flexed 90°, and the forearm and wrist in a neutral position. A pinch gauge (Baseline Pinch Gauge 12-0200, White Plains, NY, USA) was used to measure three-point pinch. Pinch testing was conducted with the pitcher's shoulder abducted 90°, elbow flexed to the pitcher's preferred arm slot at ball release, with no rotation of the head, and holding the gauge between the thumb, index, and middle fingers. This position was selected as it mimics the pitcher's arm positioning while throwing a ball. The index and middle fingers were specifically chosen because they are the last fingers to touch the ball and are thus critical for generation of velocity and spin rate. For each test, 3 trials were recorded bilaterally with 30 seconds of rest in between each trial, and the highest value for each test was used in the analysis.

Pitching evaluation was conducted as previously described.8,25 The pitchers were given unlimited time to warm up with their preferred routines to pitch at maximal effort. Once warmed up, 46 reflective markers were placed on anatomic landmarks to create a full body model. Position coordinate data of the reflective markers were collected with a three-dimensional motion-capture system using a 10-camera system (Qualisys, Goteborg, Sweden) at 480 Hz. The global coordinate system was established based on International Society of Biomechanics standards: Y was vertically upward, X was perpendicular to Y (positive toward home plate), and Z was the cross-product of X and Y.47

Following marker application, pitchers were allowed more time to pitch from a mound to feel ready to pitch at maximum effort. Pitchers were instructed to pitch a regular bullpen session (ie, a structured pitching session from a mound consisting of 15-30 pitches) with game-like effort. Pitchers threw from a regulation turf mound to a 9-hole net placed behind the home plate at regulation distance (18.44 m). Pitchers threw a variety of pitch types, but only fastballs were used in this analysis. Ball velocity was collected using Trackman (Vedbaek, Denmark) which was positioned behind the home plate. The fastball pitch with the fastest ball velocity for each pitcher was used for the analysis to represent the “best” performance, as reported in prior investigations.7,25,28,31

All data processing steps were performed using custom MATLAB scripts (The Mathworks, Natick, MA, USA) as previously described.8,25 Data from the markers were filtered by a low-pass filter (fourth-order, zero-lag Butterworth filter, cutoff frequency: 27.2 Hz).9 Throwing-arm kinetics were computed using a recursive Newton-Euler inverse dynamics algorithm, progressing from distal to proximal segments (hand-forearm → upper arm → trunk).25 Segment kinematics and segment inertial properties served as inputs to solve the equations for net intersegmental forces and torques at each joint. The peak elbow varus torque (Nm) was reported as the maximum value that occurred between foot contact and ball release.

Data were reported as mean and standard deviations. Normality testing was conducted using Shapiro-Wilk test, with all variables normally distributed. Differences between dominant and non-dominant grip and pinch strength measures were compared using paired t-tests. Simple linear regression models were run to evaluate dominant grip and pinch strength as predictors for age, weight, ball velocity, and elbow varus torque for a total of 8 separate regressions. Spearman rank correlation coefficients were interpreted as weak (≤.30), moderate (.30-.50), or strong (≥.50).20 The alpha level was set at 0.05. All data analyses were performed using MATLAB (version 2024a, Mathworks).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between grip and three-point pinch strength and their influence on ball velocity and elbow varus torque in high school baseball pitchers. Despite the critical role of the forearm musculature in stabilizing the elbow joint during the high-stress mechanics of pitching, data on grip and pinch strength in this athletic population are lacking. Our findings demonstrate that while grip and pinch strength varied widely among pitchers, greater strength in these domains was modestly associated with increased ball velocity and no association with elbow varus torque. By establishing reference values for grip and three-point pinch strength, this study provides a foundation for future research and clinical screening practices aimed at optimizing performance and potentially mitigating injury risk in adolescent throwing athletes.

Our grip strength values were generally higher than those previously reported in high school pitchers. Tajika et al37 examined 133 Japanese high school pitchers and reported the average grip strength values of 39.4 kg for dominant and 38.6 kg for non-dominant throwing arms. Similarly, Nakata et al30 found that the oldest group of youth pitchers in their cohort (average age 13.6 years) demonstrated grip strength values ranging from 32.1 to 32.9 kg. In contrast, studies involving older or more advanced pitchers have reported substantially greater grip strength. For example, in a broad cohort of baseball players aged from 13 to 50 years (average 21.4 years), average grip strength was 55.7 kg on the dominant arm and 53.8 on the non-dominant arm.30 In professional pitchers (age not reported), grip strength was reported as high as 64.5-65.3 kg. Anthropometric variations may partially explain these differences between studies, as the pitchers in our study were taller and heavier than those in previous high school studies but smaller than the professional pitchers. Prior research has shown that fatigue-induced declines in grip strength may increase the risk of throwing-arm injury in professional pitchers30; therefore, in-game grip strength monitoring could be a useful indicator to assess injury risk. While none of the prior studies reported normalized grip strength values to allow for comparison across different participant groups, our values normalized by bodyweight were similar to the estimated normalized values from the high school pitchers in the earlier studies by Tajika et al37 and Nakata et al30 (60%-62% body weight [BW]), suggesting our findings are proportionally consistent despite absolute differences.

Comparison of pinch strength with previously reported values is limited, as only a few studies have assessed pinch strength in baseball pitchers. Two investigations of Japanese high school pitchers reported pinch strength values of 8.8-9.4 kg,37,39 similar to the current study results. However, our values were slightly lower than those reported in Japanese pitchers (11.5% BW vs. 13.5%-14.3% BW, respectively) when normalized to percent BW. These discrepancies may be due to differences in testing position. Previous research demonstrated a relationship between elbow positioning and grip strength6,15,46; therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that pinch strength may also be influenced by arm positioning. In the current study, pitchers were tested with the shoulder abducted 90° and the elbow positioned according to each pitcher's reported release point, whereas the previous studies tested pitchers with the shoulder adducted and the elbow flexed 90°, with the forearm in a neutral rotation (no supination/pronation). Future research should investigate how arm positioning affects pinch strength measurements in throwing athletes, as positioning differences may play a role in measurement accuracy.

In this study, grip strength was positively correlated, albeit weakly, with ball velocity. Previous reports have identified stronger associations (r = 0.906) between grip strength and pitch ball kinetic energy in youth baseball players (6.4-15.7 years old),30 while this relationship did not exist in collegiate pitchers.5 This suggests that grip strength may contribute more substantially to velocity at earlier stages of development, rather than at higher levels of play. This may be in part due to variable levels of development in younger athletes as compared to collegiate athletes and relative consistently higher level of velocity in players who have achieved collegiate baseball status. Other factors such as rotator cuff strength, kinetic chain efficiency, lead-leg extension, and pelvis-trunk separation likely play a larger role in generation of ball velocity. A broader review across various overhand throwing sports reported a wide range of weak to strong correlations (r = 0.22-0.62) between handgrip strength and throwing velocity.4 This suggests that grip strength supports the rapid, rotational, and torque-demanding actions involved in pitching. One other possible explanation for the weaker relationship observed in advanced pitchers is that younger athletes may rely more on distal muscle groups, including those in the forearm and hands, to compensate for underdeveloped proximal strength and mechanics. Another consideration is that traditional grip strength testing, typically performed using a “power” grip,49 may not accurately reflect the fine motor control and finger positioning used to grip and release a baseball. A more sport-specific, precision-based grip assessment may better capture the hand and forearm contributions to throwing performance.

While some prior investigations have also reported relationships between finger or pinch strength and pitch velocity,26 our findings demonstrated only modest associations between grip strength and ball velocity. In a study using sensor-embedded baseballs to measure finger force and rate of force development at ball release, Yeh et al48 reported no relationship between grip strength and ball velocity, but they found both pinch strength (r2 = 0.49, P = .04) and finger strength during pitching (r2 = 0.57, P = .01) had a significant relationship with ball velocity in Taiwanese college pitchers. More recently, studies using sensor-embedded baseballs have characterized the magnitude and timing of forces produced by individual finger during a pitch and demonstrated that finger force production varies by digit and contributes to ball spin characteristics.23,35 These findings suggest that dynamic finger forces generated during a pitch represent a sport-specific neuromuscular function that is distinct from maximal isometric grip or pinch strength. Consequently, while grip and pinch strength may reflect general hand strength and contribute modestly to ball velocity, they do not fully capture the coordinated, high-speed finger force production required during pitching. This may also explain why maximal grip and pinch strength were not associated with elbow varus torque in the present study.

Despite previous reports highlighting the important role of forearm musculature in the dynamic stability of the medial elbow,22,38,41 we did not observe a relationship between grip or pinch strength and elbow varus torque. Grip and pinch strength have been proposed as surrogate measures of forearm muscle function, particularly of the flexor-pronator mass, which serves as an active dynamic stabilizer against valgus stress at the elbow. Musculoskeletal modeling studies have further demonstrated that the flexor-pronator musculature contributes substantially to resisting valgus loading during pitching. Buffi et al2 used a forward dynamic model to show the contribution of flexor-pronator mass substantially reduced the valgus load experienced by passive stabilizers, such as the UCL. Van Tright et al42 expanded on this model to suggest that coordinated activation of multiple forearm muscles, rather than maximal force generated by any single muscle group, is required to provide dynamic elbow stability during the throwing motion. Cadaveric and in vivo studies have demonstrated that activation of these muscles can reduce medial elbow joint space gapping.24,32,33,43,44 Shitara et al36 used static ultrasound imaging of the medial elbow joint space in 121 high school pitchers and reported a significant reduction in medial elbow joint space with full grip hand contraction. Similarly, Pexa et al33 found that maximal grip contraction reduced medial elbow gapping. However, these studies assessed low-load, static, or quasi-static conditions. During pitching, the flexor-pronator muscles are subjected to high-velocity eccentric loading rather than sustained isometric contraction.

Evaluation of only maximal isometric grip or pinch strength may not fully capture the muscle function required to resist valgus stress during dynamic throwing. Conversely, in a prospective study of professional pitchers, the mean grip strength was not different between pitchers who sustained a shoulder or elbow injury and healthy pitchers.10 These authors also reported that change in grip strength over the course of a season was not different between injured and healthy pitchers. This is surprising as it has been well documented in the literature that poor stabilization of the medial elbow can place high school pitchers at greater risk of UCL injury and medial elbow joint laxity.10,17 The lack of association between grip strength and elbow varus torque in our study most likely reflects the complex and multi-factorial nature of elbow loading during pitching. Parameters such as throwing mechanics, arm positioning, body mass index, humeral torsion, and limb length have all been implicated in influencing elbow varus torque.7,14,27 It is likely that grip and pinch strength represent only one component of a broader kinetic chain and may not independently account for variance in joint and UCL loading. In addition, the timing and coordination of forearm muscle activation, particularly eccentric control during the transition from arm cocking to arm acceleration during the pitch, may be more important than peak force capacity alone. Along with chasing spin rate, the height of seams and tackiness of a baseball may be related to how hard pitchers grip the ball. Seams are lower now in baseballs of Major League Baseball than in past years, compared to higher seams at lower levels of play. In addition, there are differences in the tackiness of baseballs between countries, where Japanese baseballs are tackier than the baseballs in the United States, which may play a role in the grip strength required to control the ball during pitching. Future research should consider incorporating dynamic measures of forearm muscle function, such as rate of force development or electromyographic analysis during pitching, to better assess their contributions to elbow stabilization and injury risk.

This study is not without limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between grip or pinch strength and performance or injury risk. All data were collected at a single time point, which prevents us from assessing how changes in strength or biomechanics over a season may influence outcomes in terms of performance or injury. Second, the study sample was limited to 38 high school pitchers from a single training facility, which may affect the generalizability of the normative strength values to broader populations, including pitchers at different developmental stages, competition levels, or geographic locations. While grip and three-point pinch strength are often used as proxies for forearm musculature, they may not fully reflect dynamic muscle function, especially during high-velocity eccentric contractions experienced in pitching. In addition, we did not assess other potential contributing factors to elbow loading, such as range of motion, scapular control, or detailed pitch mechanics, which may confound the observed relationships. All strength tests were performed isometrically, in a controlled clinical setting which may not replicate the demands of real-time pitching mechanics. Finally, grip strength may not only influence elbow stability but also the muscles of the forearm, which can be prone to injury. With increased emphasis on ball spin, the relationship between grip strength and these muscles needs further evaluation. Future longitudinal studies incorporating dynamic measures and multi-factorial assessments are warranted to further clarify the role of grip and pinch strength in throwing performance and injury risk.

Conclusion

This study provides grip and three-point pinch strength values in high school baseball pitchers and explores their relationship to pitching performance and elbow loading. Our findings suggest that grip strength and pinch strength are weakly associated with ball velocity; however, they do not independently predict elbow varus torque. These results underscore the importance of evaluating distal strength as one component within the broader kinetic chain, particularly in younger athletes who may rely more heavily on forearm musculature for force generation.
