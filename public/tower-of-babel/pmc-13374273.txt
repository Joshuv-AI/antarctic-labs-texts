Computational immunoinformatics and molecular modeling approaches for designing a multi-epitope vaccine against Babesia microti
Authors: Muharib Alruwaili, Intisar Alruwaili, Yasir Alruwaili, Hasan Ejaz, Bi Bi Zainab Mazhari, Muhammad Tahir ul Qamar, Muhammad Umer Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBabesia microti is an emerging tick-transmitted agent causing babesiosis in humans, becoming a major concern to public health due to the lack of a vaccine. In silico methods such as reverse vaccinology and immunoinformatics offer a convenient tool for identifying promising vaccine targets. Using computational immunoinformatics and molecular modelling tools, the current study aims to develop a multi-epitope vaccination against B. microti. For identifying the antigenic proteins, subtractive proteomics, NCBI, and PPGA were used, whereas for their screening, ProtParam (ExPASy), VaxiJen 2.0, AllerTOP v2.0, and BLASTp were utilized. For B-cell epitope prediction, IEDB (BepiPred 2.0) was used, and for T-cell epitope prediction, NetMHCpan 4.1 was used, along with other characteristics such as antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and solubility, which were predicted later. A multi-epitope vaccine construct was designed using the selected epitopes linked together with the help of EAAAK and GPGPG linkers, with β-defensin as an adjuvant. For structural modeling and refinement, Scratch Protein Predictor and GalaxyRefine were used, followed by validation using PROCHECK. Further, molecular docking was done using ClusPro, while molecular dynamics simulations were done using AMBER 22. In addition, immune simulation and cloning were conducted. From a total of 3601 proteins, four non-homologous and non-allergenic proteins were selected for use in the design process, from which a total of seven epitopes were selected (MHC class I − 4; MHC class II − 3). Structural analysis of the designed vaccine revealed good stability, which further increased when the vaccine was modified using disulfide bonds. Population coverage revealed that the vaccine had good global coverage (99.74%). Docking revealed good binding of the designed vaccine with the toll-like receptors TLR-2 and TLR-4. Analysis of molecular dynamics and binding energies of TLR-2 (-135.11 kcal/mol), and TLR-4 (-157.27 kcal/mol) receptors further supported the stability of complexes. In summary, the in silico and biophysical studies show that the designed multi-epitope vaccine for B. microti may have desirable stability, immunogenicity, and positive interaction with TLR-2 and TLR-4 receptors. Introduction

Human babesiosis is a growing tick-borne illness associated with many different Babesia organisms found in tropical zones within the ecosystem [1]. Babesia and Theileria parasites are intracellular organisms within the Apicomplexa phylum along with the Plasmodium parasites and exhibit similar microbiological and pathological features. There are many Babesia organisms, but some have been identified as responsible for causing diseases in human beings [2]. Human babesiosis infections are most frequently caused by Babesia microti (B. microti ) [3]. This infection is common in the northeast and north Midwestern of the United States, with sporadic cases described in other moderate areas around the world. B. microti is mainly transmitted to humans through bites of Ixodes scapularis ticks; however, it may also be spread through blood transfusions, organ transplants, and during childbirth [4]. B. microti infection results in mild to moderate illness, although some individuals may remain asymptomatic. Severe forms of the diseases can arise among immunocompromised patients including those with asplenia, cancer, HIV/AIDS, neonates, adults over aged 50 years o, and individuals receiving immunosuppressive therapy [5].

Furthermore, Babesia is among the most important tick-borne parasites that are widely spread and transmitted by ticks around the globe, with a considerable effect on the field of human and animal health. With over 500 million cattle at risk of infection worldwide, the incidence is growing more significant in the medical field. Babesiosis characteristically presents with symptoms such as fever, anemia, hemoglobinuria, and splenomegaly [6]. Even with therapy, the illness can progress to severe side effects including multiple organ failure, disseminated intravascular coagulation, acute hemolytic anemia, and acute respiratory distress syndrome, all of which are often deadly. The infection is more severe in asplenic animals and humans than in those with an intact spleen due to the importance of spleen in the clearance of parasites to control the illness. Both innate and adaptive immunity make up the defense against babesiosis [7].

During the pathogenesis of B. microti, different immune reactions, including proinflammatory cytokines, the cytoadherence of infected red blood cells, and hemolytic anemia, are considered key pathological mechanisms [8]. The diagnosis of B. microti is suspected on the epidemiological and clinical features, and the positive case is confirmed by serology, smear examination, and molecular-based diagnostic tests. Atovaquone, azithromycin, or clindamycin and quinine demonstrate good efficacy for babesiosis treatment. However, this could pose challenges in severely immunocompromised individuals, as acute fulminant disease may be life-threatening, and extended antibiotic treatment could result in antibiotic resistance [9].

Due to the increase in resistance among parasitic species, vaccine design is the key approach in tackling parasitic infections. Vaccine development encompasses various approaches, each employing distinct methods to elicit an immune response [10]. Human babesiosis is a mainly documented etiological agent of emerging zoonotic infection in different countries. Specifically, no approved vaccine has been available against babesiosis currently, and limited preventative approaches include avoiding vectors. Nonetheless, a multi-epitope-based vaccine would represent a major breakthrough in the fight against this rapidly developing illness [10, 11]. A vaccine to tackle babesiosis could be based on multi-epitope vaccine approaches to provide both innate and adaptive immunity against B. microti. Recently, numerous novel approaches for the creation of vaccines against B. microti have been suggested, but epitope-based vaccines maximize therapeutic efficacy with minimal adverse effects [12].

Conventional vaccine development methods are often constrained by time restraints, safety, and scaling issues that might delay the rapid development of vaccines [13–15]. To overcome limitations, immunoinformatics and reverse vaccinology have become powerful and effective strategies for modern vaccine design. Through the integration of computational methods for antigen selection, epitope prediction, and immune profiling of vaccine constructs, immunoinformatics has become a potent and effective approach for the rational design of next-generation vaccine development in recent years [16]. This paradigm may drastically cut down on the time and cost required for conventional vaccine development by systematically finding highly immunogenic B and T-cell epitopes from pathogen proteomes [17].

This study employed an immunoinformatics-driven pipeline that incorporated important reverse vaccinology concepts to design a vaccine formulation with several epitopes that is more immunogenic [18]. By identifying suitable antigenic targets, evaluating their immunological properties, and developing a stable and potent vaccine candidate, the researchers aim to further advance the development of innovative preventive measures against the target disease [19, 20]. The current study finds potential vaccine candidates from B. microti proteins using reverse vaccinology, immunoinformatics, and biophysical framework. The specific objectives of this study are to screen antigenic proteins using immunoinformatics filters, create a multi-epitope vaccine construct based on T-cell epitopes obtained from B cells, and evaluate its immunogenicity [21–23]. This research’s primary contribution is to develop a vaccine candidate with anticipated safety, stability, and immunological activity, which offers a basis for further experimental validation against B. microti.

Research methodology

The methodology flow for designing a vaccine against B. microti is depicted in Fig. 1.

The schematic representation of an in silico approach for multi-epitope vaccine construct against B. microti. The process commences with the extraction of the target protein sequences, followed by primary sequence and physicochemical properties analysis. Next, epitope prediction and prioritization, multi-epitope vaccine design, 3D structure prediction, and validation were performed using standard quality assessment methods, including Ramachandran plot analysis. Furthermore, biophysics analyses, such as interaction assessment, was conducted to evaluate functional relevance

Sequence retrieval and analysis

The whole proteome sequence was acquired from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database [24]. Core proteins were investigated using the parasitic pan-genomics analysis (PPGA) software. Fast clustering was conducted for investigations using USESEARCH in the PPGA program [25]. Physicochemical characteristics of the protein sequences were conducted using the ProtParam software on the ExPASy web portal, and unstable proteins were filtered out, while only stable proteins were retained [26–28]. Antigenic properties of the protein sequences were assessed using the VaxiJen 2.0 server [29]. A cut-off value of 0.4 was applied to assess antigenicity prediction. Non-antigenic probable proteins were filtered out, and only antigenic proteins were chosen [30]. The probable antigenic protein sequences were analyzed for allergenicity using AllerTOP v.2 [31], and the allergenic protein sequences were removed; the non-allergic proteins were considered for BLASTp analysis [32]. BLASTp was performed for both human proteomes and gut microbiota databases using a threshold value of 1e-10 for homology-free analysis. Selection of the BMN2 protein was done based on its role in parasite biology and host-parasite interaction. The non-similar proteins were selected after BLASTP analysis, whereas the similar proteins were eliminated [33]. Only extracellular proteins were selected for subcellular localization; proteins that were not similar to human and normal flora proteins were utilized. CELLO v2.5 (Subcellular Localization Predictor) was used for subcellular localization prediction [34].

Epitope prediction and screening

Initially, IEDB, an online resource, was used for linear B-cell epitope prediction from particular proteins [35]. Predictions for epitopes were done using the “Bepipred Linear 2.0” method from the database [36]. Similarly, predictions for T-cell epitopes were performed using B-cell epitopes by applying NetMHCpan 4.1 methodology for MHC-I and MHC-II [37]. All the HLA alleles were considered for T-cell epitopes prediction. Core peptide predictions for MHC classes I and II were chosen based on their low percentile scores and high degree of conservancy [38]. In addition, potential vaccine candidates were screened for various immunoinformatics parameters including antigenicity, allergenicity, water solubility, toxicity, and MHC binding [21]. This screening process was carried out through VaxiJen 2.0 server [29], AllerTOP v.2.0 server [39], Peptide solubility calculator, ToxinPred [40], and MHCPred 2.0 respectively. Furthermore, for designing a vaccine, these filtered peptides were considered based on their corresponding analysis by immunoinformatics [30]. Population coverage of the selected peptides was further investigated using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) [41].

Vaccine designing and processing

Multi-epitope vaccines work on stimulating immunity to fight several different epitopes. Individual components of antigenic proteins that activate the immune system are called epitopes [42]. Such vaccines will improve immune efficiency by covering all the aspects of pathogens like viruses, bacteria, and parasites [43]. The GPGPG linkers were employed to connect epitopes sequentially, whereas EAAAK linkers were employed to link the adjuvant with the antigenic structure. The GPGPG linker acts as a junctional epitope blocking linker, and hence preserves the epitopic specificities, whereas the rigid EAAAK linker ensures that proper folding of the structure. A Peptide Solubility Calculator, ToxinPred [40] and MHCPred 2.0 were used for this purpose. Additionally, in the process of designing a vaccine, these selected epitopes were considered based on their immunoinformatics analyses [30]. Finally, for population coverage analysis of the selected epitopes, the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) was used. Beta-defensin adjuvant was used in the proposed model to enhance its efficacy [21]. GPGPG designed by Livingston et al. (2002) serves as an excellent universal linker for the vaccine model. Several studies have indicated that this linker helps in activating HTL response. It is essential for multi-epitope vaccines’ formation. Furthermore, it helps in blocking junctional immunogenicity and increasing epitope immunogenicity [44].

Structure prediction analysis

Following sequence design, Scratch Protein Predictor was used to model the protein’s structure due to its accuracy in predicting de novo modelling of proteins that lacks homologous template [45]. The structure was further optimized by removing the additional loops through the use of Galaxy refine software [46]. Models were subsequently created through the use of GalaxyRefine2 on the GalaxyWEB bioinformatics platform [47]. Subsequently, the structure underwent further validation through disulfide bond engineering for enhanced stability, where the engineered structure was constructed through the replacement of vulnerable amino acids with cysteine amino acids [10]. Disulfide bond engineering was carried out using the Design 2.0 software package [48]. In addition, the vaccine antigen was analyzed for physicochemical and immuno informatics characteristics [49].

The secondary (2D) structure properties of the vaccine construct were investigated using the PDB sum generate tool. First, the 3D structure of the vaccine construct was generated using the Scratch Protein Predictor tool and saved in the PDB format. The PDB format was then submitted to the PDBsum tool to obtain detailed schematic depictions of the vaccine secondary structural elements. The server assesses atomic coordinates to identify α-helices, β-sheets, β-hairpins, turns, and coil regions, giving the findings as a 2D topology diagram [50].

Structural validation analysis

The optimized version of the vaccine structure was subjected to analysis using the PROCHECK module of the SAVES server version 6.0 to assess the stereotypical nature of the vaccine protein and its structural integrity [51, 52]. The optimized PDB structure, as well as the modified vaccine structure with cysteine engineering, were separately loaded on the server for further analysis by Ramachandran plots, which were performed to determine the backbone dihedral angles (φ and ψ) and to categorize each residue into one of the following categories: most favored, additionally allowed, generously allowed, and disallowed. It was found from Ramachandran plot analysis that the optimized protein structure is stable because the majority of the residues fall into the most favored category.

In silico codon optimization and cloning

Codon optimization was performed to enhance the effectiveness of gene expression in the multi-epitope vaccine by considering the E. coli K12 strain using the Java Codon Adaptation tool (JCat). Changes were made to the nucleotide sequence to cater to codon usage bias of the host while avoiding independent transcription terminations via Rho protein, prokaryotic ribosomal binding site sequences, and recognition sequences of restriction endonucleases. The CAI and GC content were evaluated to assess the feasibility and stability of the generated RNA. Restriction enzyme recognition sequences of DraIII and BstEII were added to the 5’ and 3’ ends, respectively, to facilitate directional cloning into the vector pET-29a(+). In silico analysis was conducted using SnapGene software [38], which revealed successful cloning of the optimized gene in the MCS of the vector in the right direction. The gene of interest was found to be inserted downstream of the T7 promoter alongside other regulatory elements such as the lac operator and 6×His tag.

Molecular docking and interaction analysis

The association between the vaccine and the immune receptor (TLR-4 and TLR-2) was analyzed in the course of the docking study. Toll-Like Receptor 2 (TLR-2) and Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR-4) were selected as potential immune receptors to target because of their role in recognizing Pathogen Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPS) and activation of the innate immune system due to parasitic infection. The docking was carried out using the ClusPro server [53]. In order to analyze and improve the vaccine design’s 3D structure before docking, the UCSF Chimera software was used [54]. The chosen immune cell receptors were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database by entering PDB IDs for both of them (for TLR-2, PDB ID: 2Z7X and TLR-4, PDB ID: 8s78). In order to prepare the structure of the receptors, all unnecessary ligands and water molecules were removed using the BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer software. The improved 3D structure was then saved in PDB format [55]. After optimization of both vaccine and immune receptor’s structures, both of the PDB files were uploaded to the ClusPro server for docking [53].

After the docking of molecules, the interaction of the vaccine with the receptors was studied by PDBsum Generate [50]. This software was employed for analyzing the types of intermolecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and nonbonded interactions between the vaccine structure and the receptor. The outcome of PDBsum Generate helped in obtaining an insight into the interaction of binding residues.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis

A 200-ns production cycle of MD simulation was used to further examine the chosen top docked complexes. In order to ascertain the vaccine construct binding stability for the TLR-4 and TLR-2 receptors over time, this investigation was essential. All phases of MD simulation were carried out using AMBER 22 [56]. During the process of system preparation, libraries and parameters for the complex system of receptors TLR-4 and TLR-2 were prepared, and the construct of the vaccine was created via the Antechamber program. The complex was introduced into a solvation box of TIP3P (12 Å dimensions) for its immersion with the help of Leap program. Additionally, the interactions between molecules within the system were simulated using the ff14SB force field and the complex inside the water box. Twenty-five Na+ counterions were added to neutralize the system. Secondly, the next pre-processing step involved making the system ready for the simulation run. This was carried out sequentially, with the system’s energy being decreased first. First, 500 cycles were used to decrease the energy of the hydrogen atoms. Next, 1000 cycles were used to minimize the water box under a 200 kcal/mol Å² restriction. The production phase entailed simulation of the trajectories through 200 ns. The generated trajectories were analyzed statistically by employing the CPPTRAJ software [19]. Whether the complexes would be stable or not, the following AMBER parameters were analyzed. The RMSD parameter was employed to analyze whether the complex structures were stable or not. Meanwhile, the RMSF parameter was employed to investigate the flexibility of the residues. Finally, the generated trajectory was viewed through Xmgrace software. Moreover, through 100 frames, the binding energy of the complex was calculated using the MMPBSA.py module [57].

Immunogenicity assessment of multi-epitope vaccine construct

Immunogenicity of the multi-epitope vaccine construct was determined by carrying out simulations using C-ImmSim 10.1 [58], a web-based tool used to conduct immune system simulations through mammalian interactions of the thymus, lymph node, and bone marrow. The immune response to the multi-epitope vaccine construct was evaluated using the C-ImmSim server. The multi-epitope vaccine construct sequence was subjected to C-ImmSim with the default setting and simulation run with default parameters. After which, important immunological variables from the C-Immune simulation , such as antigen concentration, IgM, IgG subclass antibody titers, B and T cell population dynamics, cytokines like IFN-γ and IL-2, and immune memory, were evaluated. The output fly results were analyzed to evaluate the immunogenicity of the vaccine construct.

Results

Subtractive proteomic investigation

Figure 2 represents the subtractive proteomics approach for vaccine candidates in B. microti. In the first stage, the dataset consist of 3601 proteins, obtained from the NCBI database, from which 14 proteins were recognized by the subtractive approach. The stability prediction of the above proteins by the ProtParam tool revealed that 6 proteins were unstable and were not included in the next process. The remaining 8 proteins were subsequently evaluated through VaxiJen 2.0 software to predict their antigenicity, and 1 protein was found to be non-antigenic and was eliminated. Additional screenings with AllerTOP v2.0 for allergen potential resulted in three proteins being ruled out as possible allergens. The result obtained from running the pipeline showed a set of four proteins was selected as candidates due to their ability to be antigenic, stable, and non-allergenic. The number of proteins decreases with each successive step of the filtration process, showing the strictness of the criteria.

Immunoinformatic screening and selection of vaccine candidates

Immunoinformatic approach was applied to examine the potential T-cell epitopes for their antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and solubility in water. Epitopes having antigenic behavior (VaxiJen score > 0.4), being non-allergenic, non-toxic, and having high solubility in water were chosen.

As a result of multiple selections, seven strong epitopes were selected, four of which represented MHC Class I epitopes, whereas the other three represented MHC Class II epitopes. These selected epitopes possessed high immunogenicity as well as broad HLA binding ability, making them eligible for designing multi-epitope vaccine candidates.

The chosen epitopes, together with their antigenicities and immunochemical properties, are described in Table 4. The selected epitopes had high antigenicity scores (0.4517 to 1.2589) and were found to be free from allergenicity, toxicity, and exhibited good solubility in water, which further justified their use for eliciting a good immune response.

Population coverage analysis

The population coverage analysis shows that the seven epitopes chosen for vaccine development have good global HLA coverage. The combined MHC class I and class II epitope set showed a high degree of population coverage (99.74%). However, while there was a higher contribution of MHC class I epitopes in most countries’ population coverage, class II population coverage showed higher variance. This is due to the known allelic diversity of HLA-DR, DP, and DQ molecules. In addition, the population coverage was 77.15% in Algeria (Arab), 98.75% in American Samoa, and 99.07% in Argentina. Notably, the combined analysis improved overall population reach compared to individual classes alone, indicating the applicability of the vaccine construct to a variety of human ethnicities. Despite the good global coverage, however, there was some regional disparities. For example, poor class II coverage was documented in areas of South Asia, like Pakistan and Sri Lanka, while poor combined or class I coverage was noted in particular populations like Paraguay, Wales, and certain people in the Middle East. On the other hand, regions like East Asia and Europe have shown high combined coverage. This suggests possible allelic differences which need to be optimized in terms of epitope design for better population coverage. Supplementary Table S1 displays the population coverage data, which indicates broad theoretical population coverage for several countries.

2D and 3D structural analysis of the vaccine construct

Structural prediction

Predictions of the 3D structure of the designed multi-epitope vaccine were done employing de novo approach (software used: Scratch Protein Predictor), and the result is shown in Fig. 4. Structure refinement

The 3D structure obtained was further optimized using the GalaxyWEB (GalaxyRefine) server to enhance the quality of the model by optimizing the side chain packing and minimizing energy. The optimization was evaluated based on RMSD, MolProbity score, clash score, rotamer quality, and favorable regions in the Ramachandran plot. Figure 5 and Table 5 showed considerable improvement in all the refined models, especially in the form of decreased values for MolProbity score, clash score, and improvement in favorable Ramachandran regions. The original model had poor stereochemistry (MolProbity: 4.042; Clash Score: 205.5; Favorable Regions: 84.3%), but all the refined models had better stereochemistry and lesser steric clashes.

2D and 3D structural analysis

The 2D structural analysis of the vaccine construct revealed a well-defined secondary structure comprising both stable and flexible regions. The predicted 3D model reveals a compact core 3D structure accompained by extended loop regions, signifying incomplete folding with inherent flexibility. Further, the secondary structure represents the presence of multiple α-helices, H1, H3, and β-strands distributed along the sequence, making short β-sheet elements. Furthermore, several β-turns and γ-turns were predicted, along with a β-hairpin motif, representing localized conformational flexibility. Significantly, disulfide bonds were predicted, which are likely to improve structural stability by reinforcing the 3D conformation. Disulfide engineering and structural stability analysis

The disulfide engineering approach was conducted through the Disulfide by Design 2.0 web server, which helps enhance the stability of the multi-epitope vaccine. Several pairs of residues were selected (Supplementary Table S2) from the results of geometrical and energy considerations, such as Chi3 angles, bond energies, and Cα-Cα distance values. Based on stringent criteria (Chi3 ~ ± 87° to ± 97°, energy < 2.5 kcal/mol), three residues, Lys26-Glu28, Cys33-Arg38, and Gly37-Cys40, were chosen to be replaced by cysteines since they satisfy the geometrical and energy requirements well. Some residue pairs like Asp75-Gly81 were not chosen because they had lesser favorable geometry or structural considerations. Figure 7 A illustrates the native configuration, whereas Fig. 7B display the disulfide-modified version with the added bonds marked by yellow lines. Such bonds are expected to limit local conformational flexibility; however, the results are still computational and need experimental confirmation. The PROCHECK analysis demonstrated the increase in residues falling into the most favorable areas on the Ramachandran plot, from 39.3% in the native form to 91.6% in the engineered protein. This observation indicates possible improvement of the protein backbone geometry after induction of mutation.

In silico cloning

The in silico cloning procedure indicated that the vaccine construct had been successfully cloned into the pET-29a(+) vector as shown in Fig. 8. It was observed that the fragment was cloned in the multiple cloning site in a directionally oriented fashion. The recombinant plasmid map showed that the DNA sequence was situated downstream from the T7 promoter region, hence proving its efficacy to be transcribed at high levels in E. coli expression systems. In silico validation analysis also confirmed the absence of any internal restriction sites in the cloned DNA fragment for the chosen restriction enzymes (DraIII and BstEII), ensuring the reliability and consistency of the cloning protocol. The recombinant plasmid carried important regulatory sequences such as the lac operator required for induction and the 6×His tag located at the C-terminal end. In conclusion, these findings indicate the successful incorporation of the designed vaccine construct into the pET-29a(+) vector.

Molecular docking analysis

Computational docking of vaccine and TLR-4 and TLR-2

The ClusPro server produced 30 distinct clusters during the docking evaluation of the vaccine with the TLR-4 immune cell receptors, indicating numerous potential molecular interactions. Cluster 0 had the highest number of members (71), indicating a dominant and structurally consistent binding mode. In contrast, several small clusters (e.g., clusters 2, 7, and 23) had more negative weighted scores, suggesting relatively more favorable molecular interactions. The lowest-energy poses ranged from − 806.0 to -1097.9 across clusters, reflecting the variability in the stability of the interactions. Importantly, these ClusPro scores are ranking metrics rather than precise measures of binding strength; therefore, the results were interpreted as potential indications of receptor binding and interface complementarity, not actual immune activation.

For the vaccine with the human TLR-2, ClusPro generated 17 clusters, representing various probable interaction orientations. Cluster 0 contained the most members (168), indicating a dominant and consistent binding mode, while several other clusters (e.g., clusters 4, 6, and 14) had more negative weighted scores, indicating comparatively more favorable interactions. The lowest binding energy scores ranged from − 784.9 to -978.3 across clusters, showing variability in interaction stability. As with TLR-4 docking, these ClusPro scores are ranking metrics rather than direct measures of binding affinity and were interpreted conservatively to suggest potential receptor interactions and interface complementarity rather than functional immune activation. The vaccine’s docking results with the selected immune receptors, TLR-4 and TLR-2, are listed in Supplementary Tables S3-S4, respectively. Molecular docking interaction analysis

The molecular docking interaction demonstrates effective binding between the engineered vaccine molecule and TLR-4, wherein the vaccine molecule binds well inside the ligand-binding site of the receptor protein with favorable surface complementarity, as indicated in Fig. 9A. The stability of this complex structure is further supported by the presence of an intricate network of intermolecular interactions such as hydrogen bonding, salt bridging, and non-covalent interactions as shown in Fig. 9B. The existence of multiple interactions between charged residues (e.g., Asp, Glu with Lys, Arg) implies significant electrostatic forces, while hydrogen bonds provide specificity and orientation for binding. The role of disulfide bonds in stabilizing interactions between molecules is minor; however, the whole interaction profile implies the ability of the vaccine molecule to establish a stable complex with the target receptor, TLR-4.

Based on docking presentation, the multi-epitope vaccine complex (pink) docks successfully and is properly oriented within the TLR-2 (blue) site because the vaccine fits well inside the site, indicating proper complementary geometry and surface features (Fig. 10A). The interaction diagram presented in the Fig. 10B demonstrates a network of molecular interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and non-bonded interactions. The interaction map indicates an array of connections between the complex including hydrogen bonds and nonbonded interactions, which are needed for proper positioning and stability of the complex. Electrostatic interactions via salt bridges formed by oppositely charged residues (such as Asp/Glu with Lys/Arg) help in enhancing the interaction between the two proteins. Despite the low occurrence of disulfide linkages in interactions, their existence may help add to the stability of the regions involved. The above mentioned interactions indicate that a stable complex is formed by the vaccine construct and TLR-2, which could be an indicator of the complex’s immune receptor recognition capability.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

Root mean square deviation (RMSD)

According to the RMSD analysis, both vaccine-TLR complexes equilibriated after an initial equilibration phase; however, the docked complex of vaccine and TLR-4 exhibited lower deviation (~ 2.3–2.6 Å) and reduced fluctuations than those of the vaccine-TLR-2 complex. The vaccine and receptor TLR-4 complex stabilized at 2.5 Å, indicating a more rigid, stable interaction, whereas the TLR-2 complex showed higher deviations (~ 2.8–3.4 Å) and noticeable fluctuations due to the flexible loop region. These RMSD findings suggest that the designed vaccine forms a stronger binding interaction with TLR-4 and TLR-2 immune cell receptors, highlighting the vaccine’s potential for stronger receptor interaction. RMSD study of the multi-epitope vaccine construct and immune cell receptor complexes during a 200 ns molecular dynamics simulation. The vaccine complexes with the TLR-4 (black) and TLR-2 (orange) reach equilibrium within the first 30 ns, then stabilize

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)

From the RMSF plot analysis of the vaccine-TLR-4 and vaccine-TLR-2 complexes, it was evident that the two complexes retain their overall structural stability, as most of the residues have a fluctuation value of less than 1.5 Å, showing stability in the protein’s backbone structure (Fig. 12). There were some notable peaks in areas of flexibility, especially in the regions 40–70 and 160–180. Notably, the vaccine-TLR-2 complex has high flexibility, with a notable peak of about 5.5 Å in the N-terminal part. On the other hand, the vaccine-TLR-4 complex shows comparatively less fluctuations at the residue level, with medium levels of fluctuation (approximately 3.5–3.8 Å), which implies that its dynamics are more restricted. The findings indicate that although both complexes are structurally stable, the vaccine-TLR-4 complex is more rigid than the vaccine-TLR-2 complex, whose local flexibility is mostly found in the loops and termini of the protein.

Calculation of vaccine-receptor binding energy

Calculation of binding free energy has been done, and it was found that the values of the binding energy were negative in both cases. In case of the vaccine-TLR-2 complex, the binding energy value is -135.11 kcal/mol, while in case of vaccine-TLR-4 complex, it is -157.27 kcal/mol. The negative value of the binding energy indicates that there is a good interaction between the model and the receptor. Supplementary Table S7 depicts the energy parameters and the calculated net binding energy.

In summary, the evaluation of RMSD, RMSF, and binding energy gives a comprehensive assessment, showing that the two complexes are stable and energetically viable. The TLR-4 complex being relatively more stable due to comparatively lower RMSD and more favourable binding free energy values, although RMSF indicated localized flexibility in certain regions.

The C-ImmSim server predicted a strong humoral and cellular immune response with a mixed Th1/Th2 profile, evidenced by elevated IFN‑γ, IL‑2, and IL‑4, together with a controlled CD8⁺ cytotoxic T‑cell response. Discussion

B. microti has been implicated in several serious complications, such as ARDS, DIC, and organ dysfunction, particularly of the liver and kidneys, and the majority of the cases have been found in immunocompromised hosts [59]. However, B. microti infection can also occur in immunocompetent patients because the organism invades and replicates in human erythrocytes. Additionally, despite the intact immune system, B. microti is identifiable by the immune system; however, the parasite has evolved an immune evasion strategy to escape immune destruction [60]. The urgent need for developing a vaccine against B. microti infection has been highlighted. Several B. microti organisms are observed, some of which cause serious infections, whereas others cause opportunistic infections among immunocompromised hosts [61]. This work aimed to develop a multi-epitope vaccine against B. microti infection through selecting antigenic epitopes derived from essential core proteins [62]. However, it is an enormous trial for traditional vaccinologists [63]. In comparison to traditional methods, reverse vaccinology (RV) can conduct batch analyses of thousands of biological parameters more quickly and with greater reliability, hence opening the door for novel advances in vaccination research and development [64].

Earlier studies have reported a novel multi-epitope vaccine construct based on the whole genomic polyprotein of coxsackievirus A10 through the use of immuno informatics and reverse vaccinology approaches. For the development of a multi-epitope vaccine, fifteen MHC-I and five MHC-II epitopes were selected from the B-cell antigenic regions due to their strong immunogenicity. The selected epitopes were linked to β-defensins, HIV-TAT, PADRE, and 6 × His as adjuvants by incorporating optimal linkers. The constructed vaccine contained 390 amino acid residues and possessed favorable physicochemical properties like antigenicity and stability. Moreover, the vaccine construct was predicted to cover a large range of global population, which is an indication of its high potential efficacy in humans [65].

For the design of the multi-epitope vaccine, 3,601 proteins were collected from the NCBI database and analyzed through immunoinformatics techniques [61]. The redundant protein sequence were eliminated, and the non-redundant core protein sequence was employed for subsequent analysis. In subsequent immunoinformatic analysis, probable antigenic proteins were chosen, whereas the remaining non-antigenic proteins were rejected since only antigenic proteins could activate the immune system and therefore can be considered for vaccine preparation [66]. Vaccines construct must not be allergenic. The allergenic proteins were removed, leaving only the non-allergenic ones for consideration. The human similar proteins were removed to avoid toxicity in the body, while the human non-similar proteins were kept for consideration [67].

In another previous study, a mutation-based reverse vaccinology pipeline was developed for an epitope-based vaccine construct to combat multiple strains of Lassa virus (LASV) using the Glycoprotein of LASV for optimal recognition by the immune cells [68]. Furthermore, the proteins selected for epitope prediction should be surface-localized [69]. In the subcellular localization analysis, all the surface-localized proteins were considered for epitope prediction [70]. Four proteins were shortlisted for epitope prediction using a variety of immunoinformatics and subtractive proteomics filters. The selected proteins possess desirable vaccine characteristics [67].

Epitope prediction was performed for the chosen proteins, while epitope choice is very important for designing vaccines [71]. The first step involved ranking of the epitopes based on the lowest percentile value and conservation. Furthermore, immunoinformatics characteristics such as antigenicity, allergic potential, solubility characteristics, and toxic effects assessment were used to filter the epitopes [72]. The purpose of screening the epitopes was to prevent allergies and hypersensitivity in the host body and to stimulate an immunological response to the vaccine. In addition, both cell-mediated and humoral immunity are necessary in order to fight off this present and future infection by activation of B and T lymphocytes. The ability of MHC* 0101 alleles to bind epitopes is adequate to optimize population coverage worldwide [73]. In the current experiment, the analysis of MHC* 0101 binding capability of these epitopes was carried out and only those which bind to MHC* 0101 alleles were taken for vaccine design purpose [38, 74]. In another study, an integrated mutation-based immunoinformatics approach was carried out for designing a vaccine against the epitope-based HIV subtype C. B and T cell epitope prediction was conducted and an immuno informatics analysis carried out for the targets [65].

In order to evoke the immune response from the immune receptors and activate them, a model vaccine must bind to immune receptors. This study investigated how the model vaccine interacted with immune receptors (TLR-2 and TLR-4) via molecular docking [66]. Moreover, the stability of dynamic interaction is vital for the generation of both humoral and cellular immune responses [75, 76]. In order to ensure dynamic motion of the complexes during docking, MD simulations were done. Moreover, the results obtained after simulation demonstrated that the binding of the immune receptor and the vaccine construct form a stable complex [77]. Besides, the formation of humoral and cellular immune response generated by host simulation is anticipated from the suggested vaccine construct model. The vaccine design model is solely based on computation and biophysical considerations. Therefore, at this point, it is purely theoretical and computational in designing a vaccine against B. microti. Experimental evidence needs to be generated before it can be considered further for clinical trials.

Conclusion

B. microti represents a significant public health risk because of the growing number of vulnerable populations, underdiagnosis, and insufficient awareness. Using immunoinformatics and reverse vaccinology techniques, the current research developed a multi-epitope vaccine design with antigenic properties and non-allergenic or non-toxic effects against target parasites. The findings obtained from biophysical investigation indicate that the developed multi-epitope vaccine exhibits a high affinity for various innate immune receptors in the host and that the vaccine-receptor complex remains dynamically stable under physiological conditions . Moreover, the designed vaccine shows promise in stimulating both humoral and cellular immunity. Additionally, the C-immune analysis suggests potential immunogenicity of the vaccine construct. In conclusion, even though this potential vaccine seems to provide significant protection against B. microti, it is crucial to carry out both in vitro and in vivo testing to fully assess its safety and effectiveness. This inclusive method will help guarantee that the vaccine is not only effective but also safe for widespread use.
