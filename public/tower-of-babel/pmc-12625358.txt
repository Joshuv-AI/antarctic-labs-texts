Clinical utility of CBC-Derived inflammatory markers for predicting IVIG resistance: findings from a Korean infant Kawasaki disease cohort
Authors: Young Tae Lim, Jung Eun Kwon, Yeo Hyang Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesKawasaki disease (KD) in infants presents unique clinical challenges, including higher rates of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resistance and coronary complications. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of inflammatory markers derived from complete blood count, specifically neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), and pan-immune inflammation value (PIV), for IVIG resistance in infants under 12 months with KD.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 261 infants diagnosed with KD between January 2014 and December 2024. The predictive performance of inflammatory markers was assessed using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Logistic regression analysis identified independent predictors of IVIG resistance.ResultsThe IVIG resistance was observed in 67 patients (25.7%); MLR demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy (area under the curve [AUC] 0.776, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.706–0.845), followed by NLR (AUC 0.738, 95% CI: 0.670–0.806) and PIV (AUC 0.734, 95% CI: 0.658–0.811). Background

Kawasaki disease (KD) is an acute febrile vasculitis primarily affecting children under 5 years of age and is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in developed countries. Without timely treatment, coronary artery lesions (CALs), including coronary artery (CA) dilation and CA aneurysms (CAAs), develop in up to 25% of cases [1]. Although first described over five decades ago, KD’s pathogenesis remains unclear, and diagnosis persists in relying on established clinical criteria [2].

Despite standard treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and aspirin (ASA), approximately 15–25% of patients exhibited IVIG resistance [3], which is strongly associated with increased CAL risk. Consequently, early identification of IVIG-resistant patients is crucial for optimising management and outcomes [4].

Infants under 12 months, particularly those under 6 months, are considered high-risk in recent American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines [1] due to more frequent incomplete presentations, subtler clinical signs, elevated CAL risk, and increased IVIG resistance [5–7]. These factors highlight the importance of early detection and timely intervention in this vulnerable group [8].

KD is characterised by systemic vasculitis with significant inflammatory involvement. Systemic inflammation is increasingly recognised in disease pathogenesis and prognosis, and composite inflammatory markers derived from complete blood count (CBC) have shown predictive value. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), reflecting the interplay between nonspecific inflammation and physiologic stress, has emerged as a reliable prognostic indicator in various cardiovascular conditions [9]. The monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) reflects the balance between innate and adaptive immunity; the pan-immune inflammation value (PIV) is a novel marker that integrates neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts relative to lymphocytes [10, 11]. Although these markers are readily obtainable through routine blood tests, their role in predicting IVIG resistance in KD, especially in infants, remains underexplored.

This study aimed to assess the predictive value of NLR, MLR, and PIV for IVIG resistance in infants under 12 months with KD, offering the potential for early risk stratification and improved initial treatment strategies in this high-risk population.

Methods

Study population and design

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at two tertiary centres (Kyungpook National University Hospital and Kyungpook National University Children’s Hospital, Daegu, Korea). The study included 261 infants under 12 months diagnosed with KD between January 2014 and December 2024. Infants who received initial treatment elsewhere or had underlying comorbidities, including congenital heart disease, immunodeficiencies, or chronic inflammatory diseases, were excluded. Medical records of all eligible patients were reviewed retrospectively.

Diagnosis and treatment

The diagnosis of KD followed the AHA guidelines—2004 criteria for cases before 2017 and the updated 2017 guidelines thereafter [2, 12]. Complete KD was defined as fever for ≥ 5 days (with the first day counted as illness day 1) and ≥ 4 of the five principal clinical features: mucocutaneous changes (lip erythema or cracking, strawberry tongue, and/or erythema or both), bilateral nonexudative conjunctival injection, rash, extremity changes (erythema and oedema in the acute phase or periungual desquamation in the subacute phase or both), and cervical lymphadenopathy (≥ 1.5-cm diameter). Incomplete KD was diagnosed in patients with ≥ 5 days of fever and at least two compatible signs or unexplained fever for ≥ 7 days with supportive laboratory or echocardiographic findings, such as CAA.

All patients received standard treatment with IVIG (2 g/kg) and concurrent ASA (30–50 mg/kg/day) until afebrile. The ASA dose was reduced to 3–5 mg/kg/day once the patient remained afebrile for 48 h.

IVIG resistance was defined as recrudescent or persistent fever occurring ≥ 36 h after completing the initial IVIG infusion [2]. Patients meeting this criterion received second-line therapy, either a repeat IVIG dose (exact dosage) or intravenous methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg/day for 3 days).

Evaluation of coronary artery lesions (CALs)

Echocardiography was performed 1–2 days after admission (acute phase) and repeated 6–8 weeks post-treatment (convalescent phase) to assess coronary artery involvement. Coronary artery dimensions were standardised using body-surface-area-adjusted z-scores. In this study, coronary artery aneurysms (CAAs), defined as z-score ≥ 2.5, were assessed as the primary coronary outcome. This threshold was selected based on the 2017 AHA guidelines, which classify z-score ≥ 2.5 as aneurysms requiring more intensive long-term management and thromboprophylaxis [2]. While the AHA guidelines define dilation as z-score 2.0 to < 2.5, we focused on aneurysms (z-score ≥ 2.5) as these lesions have greater prognostic significance, with higher persistence rates and increased risk of long-term complications compared to mild dilation, which often resolves spontaneously [2]. CAAs were identified when z-score ≥ 2.5 was documented in any of the following arteries on echocardiography performed both in the acute phase and the convalescent phase: left main coronary artery, left anterior descending coronary artery, or right coronary artery.

Data collection and analysis

Patients were categorised as IVIG-responsive or IVIG-resistant. Pre-treatment demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were compared between groups. Fever duration was calculated as the number of days from fever onset to blood sampling, which was performed immediately before IVIG administration. Variables included age at onset, sex, KD type (complete/incomplete), presence of CALs, and pre-treatment laboratory parameters: white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte percentages, absolute counts of neutrophils (ANC), lymphocytes, and monocytes, haemoglobin, platelet count, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin, total bilirubin, sodium, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP).

Inflammatory markers

Three inflammatory markers were calculated using the following formula:

NLR = ANC / Lymphocyte count.

NLR = ANC / Lymphocyte count.

MLR = Monocyte count / Lymphocyte count.

MLR = Monocyte count / Lymphocyte count.

PIV = [(ANC × Platelet count × Monocyte count) / Lymphocyte count]

PIV = [(ANC × Platelet count × Monocyte count) / Lymphocyte count]

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) based on distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages). Group differences between IVIG-responsive and IVIG-resistant groups were assessed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

The predictive performance of NLR, MLR, and PIV for IVIG resistance was analysed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The area under the curve (AUC), optimal cut-off points, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated.

Logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of IVIG resistance. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. To avoid multicollinearity, neutrophil and lymphocyte percentages, ANC, and lymphocyte counts were excluded due to their inclusion in NLR. Similarly, monocyte percentage and counts were excluded due to their correlation with MLR. Both MLR and PIV were dichotomised based on optimal cut-off values from ROC analysis. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) statistical software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyungpook National University Chilgok Hospital (IRB No. 2025-01-007) and Kyungpook National University Hospital (IRB No. 2025-01-007). Both IRBs waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective design.

Results

This study included 261 patients with KD, comprising 194 (74.3%) IVIG-responsive and 67 (25.7%) IVIG-resistant cases. Incomplete KD was diagnosed in 141 patients (54.0%). The mean age was 7.16 months, and 65.9% were male. The duration of the median fever before the initial laboratory assessment and IVIG administration was 4 days (interquartile range [IQR] 3–5) in both groups, with no significant difference (p = 0.308). Age (7.19 ± 2.77 vs. 7.07 ± 2.73 months, p = 0.760), sex (male 64.4% vs. 70.1%, p = 0.456), and incomplete KD proportions (56.2% vs. 47.8%, p = 0.257) were comparable between IVIG-responsive and IVIG-resistant groups (Table 1).

Compared to the IVIG-responsive group, IVIG-resistant patients had substantially higher neutrophil percentage (61.35% vs. 52.28%, p < 0.001) and ANC (8.88 vs. 7.16 × 10³/µL, p < 0.001), but lower lymphocyte percentage (26.35% vs. 35.94%, p < 0.001) and lymphocyte count (4.07 vs. 5.21 × 10³/µL, p < 0.001). Monocyte percentage and count were also significantly elevated (all p < 0.05).

All inflammatory markers differed significantly between groups. Median NLR (2.40 vs. 1.39, p < 0.001), MLR (0.27 vs. 0.17, p < 0.001), and PIV (1079.06 vs. 441.68, p < 0.001) were considerably higher in IVIG-resistant patients. CRP levels were elevated in the IVIG-resistant group (7.14 vs. 5.36 mg/dL, p = 0.012). Among blood chemistry parameters, only total bilirubin differed significantly (0.49 vs. 0.37 mg/dL, p = 0.002) (Table 2).

In preliminary logistic regression, continuous MLR and PIV values produced excessively high odds ratios (ORs), hindering clinical interpretation. Consequently, both variables were dichotomised using their ROC-derived cut-offs for subsequent analyses.

Univariate analysis revealed that elevated MLR (OR 5.89, 95% CI 3.23–10.75), PIV (OR 4.93, 95% CI 2.73–8.93), total bilirubin (OR 3.35, 95% CI 1.81–6.22), NLR (OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.33–2.04), and CRP (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13) were significantly associated with IVIG resistance (all p < 0.05) (Table 4).

In multivariate analysis, high MLR remained an independent predictor with the highest odds ratio among the evaluated markers (OR 3.45, 95% CI 1.66–7.14, p = 0.001), followed by elevated PIV (OR 2.30, 95% CI 1.14–4.67, p = 0.020) and total bilirubin (OR 1.96, 95% CI 1.02–3.79, p = 0.045). However, after adjustment, NLR and CRP were no longer significant (p = 0.393 and p = 0.089, respectively). These findings indicate that elevated MLR, PIV, and total bilirubin are independent predictors of IVIG resistance in KD.

Moreover, coronary artery aneurysms (z-score ≥ 2.5) occurred substantially more frequently in those resistant to IVIG than in those responsive to it (16.4% vs. 5.2%, p = 0.007) (Table 1).

Discussion

Infants with KD, particularly those under 6 months of age, represent a high-risk group with distinct diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [1, 2]. They experience higher rates of IVIG resistance [5, 13] and coronary complications [6, 13] compared to older children, necessitating focused clinical attention. This study aimed to identify robust predictors of IVIG resistance in this vulnerable subgroup, addressing a critical gap in KD management.

Our findings indicate that MLR demonstrated discriminative ability for predicting IVIG resistance in infants with KD (AUC 0.776), performing better than NLR (AUC 0.738) and PIV (AUC 0.734) among the evaluated markers. Although NLR demonstrated significance in univariate analysis, it lost its predictive value after multivariate adjustment, whereas MLR > 0.21 remained a significant independent predictor (OR 3.447).

A critical methodological strength of this study is the uniform median fever duration of 4 days before initial laboratory assessment and IVIG administration in both groups (p = 0.308), eliminating disease duration as a confounding factor. Therefore, differences in MLR, NLR, and PIV likely reflect inherent variations in inflammatory response rather than temporal disease progression. These findings support the validity of these CBC-derived markers, particularly MLR, as accurate predictors of IVIG resistance in infant KD.

MLR’s relatively better performance among the evaluated markers aligns with current insights into KD pathogenesis. KD involves heightened innate immunity, particularly monocyte/macrophage activation, and suppression of acquired immunity, especially T-cell responses [14, 15]. Monocytes/macrophages play a central role in KD pathogenesis [16], with CD14 + CD16 + inflammatory monocytes increase during acute KD and correlate with disease severity [17]. These monocytes can differentiate into CD11c + macrophages crucial in coronary arteritis development [18], and patients with CALs show increased monocyte activation and inflammatory cytokine production [16]. In our study, MLR >0.21 was associated with a 3.4-fold increased risk of IVIG resistance, suggesting that enhanced innate immune activation contributes to treatment resistance. Furthermore, the effectiveness of IVIG in reducing inflammation is partly mediated through its effects on monocyte/macrophage function [19], suggesting that patients with higher MLR might require more aggressive initial therapy. These findings support MLR as a practical marker that reflects the key pathophysiological processes underlying IVIG resistance in infant KD. In addition to CBC-derived markers, elevated total bilirubin was an independent predictor, likely reflecting hepatic involvement in the context of severe systemic inflammation.

Previous attempts to predict IVIG resistance in KD have focused on various clinical and laboratory parameters. The Kobayashi, Egami, and Sano scoring systems, widely used in Japan, incorporate parameters such as sodium, AST, CRP, platelet count, and neutrophil percentage [20–22]. However, these scoring systems have demonstrated limited predictive value in non-Japanese populations [23]. Furthermore, these three Japanese scoring systems showed limited effectiveness in predicting IVIG resistance among Korean infants with KD [24]. There remains a need for more reliable predictors, particularly in infants, where conventional scoring systems may not be as applicable.

MLR and PIV have emerged as valuable inflammatory markers in various diseases. Initially recognised as a prognostic marker in oncology, particularly for hematologic and solid malignancies [25]. MLR’s utility has expanded to inflammatory and cardiovascular diseases, reflecting systemic inflammation and immune response [10]. Incorporating platelet count, neutrophil percentage, and monocyte count, PIV was introduced as a novel prognostic biomarker in metastatic colorectal cancer [26] and has since been explored in cancers and cardiovascular diseases [27, 28]. Our finding that these markers predict IVIG resistance in infant KD adds to their clinical relevance. Notably, an optimal PIV cut-off value of 726.68 was identified, differing from Yi et al.‘s value of 1387.83 in patients with KD [29]. This discrepancy might be attributed to our focus on infants under 12 months, highlighting the importance of age-specific reference values for inflammatory markers.

While our findings suggest that MLR has moderate discriminative ability for predicting IVIG resistance, it should not be considered a definitive standalone test. Rather, MLR should be interpreted as part of a multifactorial risk assessment approach, integrated with clinical presentation, other laboratory parameters, and echocardiographic findings. Clinicians should use MLR to complement, rather than replace, comprehensive clinical evaluation when identifying high-risk infants who may benefit from intensified initial therapy or closer monitoring.

Several limitations must be considered. First, the retrospective, single-centre design may introduce selection bias. Second, our findings, based solely on Korean infants under 12 months, may not be generalisable to other age groups or ethnic populations. Third, the proposed cut-off values were derived from ROC analysis within this cohort and require prospective validation in independent, diverse populations before clinical application. Further external validation studies across different ethnic groups, age ranges, and healthcare settings are essential to confirm the generalisability and clinical utility of these markers.

Conclusions

Among CBC-derived inflammatory markers, MLR demonstrated moderate predictive value for IVIG resistance in infant KD, suggesting its potential as a simple, cost-effective tool for early risk stratification, pending further validation. These readily accessible markers may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk infants who could benefit from intensified initial therapy or closer monitoring. While these findings offer valuable insights for managing infant KD, further prospective, multicentre studies are warranted to validate these markers across diverse populations and determine their optimal clinical use in paediatric care.
