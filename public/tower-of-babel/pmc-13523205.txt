Chlorogenic acid mitigates calcium oxalate monohydrate crystal-induced epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition in renal epithelial cells via suppression of HSP60 expression and p38 signaling
Authors: Paleerath Peerapen, Somsakul Phuangkham, Wanida Boonmark, Visith Thongboonkerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a prominent process involved in kidney fibrosis. Calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM), a crystalline composition predominantly found in renal stones, is one of the known stimuli that can trigger the EMT process. Chlorogenic acid (CGA), a phenolic hydroxycinnamate naturally found in several plants (especially coffee beans), has several health benefits, including antifibrotic properties. However, the mechanisms underlying its preventive effects against COM-induced EMT in renal epithelial cells remain unclear.MethodsHerein, Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) renal epithelial cells were exposed to COM crystals without or with CGA pre-/cotreatment and were subjected to various assays, including morphological examination, measurement of cell spindle index, transepithelial resistance (TER) measurement, immunofluorescence staining of EMT markers, and western blotting of p38 signaling proteins. Additionally, label-free quantitative proteomics using nanoLC-ESI-LTQ-Orbitrap MS/MS and functional enrichment analyses were carried out, followed by functional validation of the essential role of heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) using a small interfering RNA (siRNA)-based knockdown method.ResultsAs expected, COM induced the EMT process as shown by increases in spindle index and mesenchymal marker (vimentin) but decreases in TER and epithelial marker (ZO-1). Mechanistically, these changes were accompanied with activation of p38 signaling. However, CGA pre-/cotreatment successfully prevented all these COM-induced changes. Label-free quantitative proteomics revealed significantly altered levels of 167 cellular proteins caused by COM. Among these, 55 proteins were completely preserved by CGA. Protein–protein interaction network illustrated HSP60 as a hub of these interacting proteins. While COM increased the HSP60 level, CGA successfully preserved this protein at its basal level. Moreover, knockdown of HSP60 expression by small interfering RNA could also prevent COM-induced EMT and p38 activation.ConclusionsThese data indicate that CGA mitigates COM-induced EMT via suppression of HSP60 expression and p38 signaling activated by COM crystals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00975-z.

Introduction

Kidney fibrosis is a common pathological phenomenon preceding chronic kidney disease (CKD) regardless of the etiology [1]. This phenomenon is characterized by excessive production and irregular deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) within kidney tissues [2, 3]. Myofibroblasts are mesenchymal cells that play active roles in producing ECM for wound/tissue repair during renal injury and inflammation [3, 4]. Other than resident fibroblasts, epithelial and endothelial cells are the origins of myofibroblasts via transformation processes, namely epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), respectively. Dysregulation of wound/tissue repairing processes after renal injury/inflammation causes excessive accumulation of ECM and may lead to CKD [5].

Nephrolithiasis (renal stone disease) is a common kidney disease with a prevalence of approximately 11% in the USA [6] and 11.4% in China [7]. This disease is generally considered a risk factor for CKD [8]. Calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM) is the main crystalline material of renal stones [9]. Several studies have reported that COM crystals affect many cell types, including various immune cells [10–14], fibroblasts [15], and renal epithelial cells [16–18]. Additionally, secreted products from COM-exposed epithelial cells [19] and macrophages [20] can activate renal fibroblasts. COM exposure also leads to oxidative stress [21, 22], mitochondrial dysfunction [21, 23, 24], tight junction defects [25], cell senescence [21], and EMT [16].

Several previous studies have attempted to investigate the beneficial effects of diverse bioactive compounds, such as epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) [26], quercetin [27], curcumin [28], and chlorogenic acid (CGA) [29], on the alleviation of kidney fibrosis. CGA is a group of phenolic hydroxycinnamates naturally found in several plants, especially coffee [30, 31]. It is an ester compound formed by the reaction of trans-cinnamic acid (e.g., caffeic acid, ferulic acid, coumaric acid) and quinic acid. Among a variety of CGA forms, an ester compound derivatized from caffeic acid and quinic acid, namely 5-caffeoylquinic acid (5-CQA), is the major compound found in dietary plants [30]. Therefore, the term “CGA” is frequently referred to as 5-CQA [30]. This compound has several health benefits, including antiinflammatory, antioxidative, antidiabetic, and antifibrotic properties [32–34].

Even with the known antifibrotic property, the mechanisms underlying the preventive effects of CGA against COM-induced EMT in renal epithelial cells remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the protective roles of CGA and the mechanisms underlying its preventive effects against COM-induced EMT in renal epithelial cells. MDCK renal epithelial cells were exposed to COM crystals without or with CGA pre-/cotreatment and were subjected to various assays, including morphological examination, measurement of cell spindle index, transepithelial resistance (TER) measurement, immunofluorescence staining of EMT markers, and western blotting of p38 signaling proteins. Additionally, label-free quantitative proteomics and functional enrichment analyses were carried out, followed by functional validation of the essential role of heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) using a small interfering RNA (siRNA)-based knockdown method.

Materials and methods

Cell cultivation

MDCK renal epithelial cells (ECACC; cat. no. 00062107) were maintained in a growth medium composed of Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 60 μg/ml streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 60 U/ml penicillin G (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell death analysis

An equal number of MDCK cells (~1.5 × 105 cells/well) in the growth medium was added into each well of a 6-well plate (Corning Inc.). After overnight cultivation, the growth medium was refreshed without (control) or with an addition of CGA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at the final concentration of 0.5, 5, 50, or 500 µM. After 24-h incubation, all attached and floating cells were harvested and subjected to a cell death assay based on flow cytometry as described in [19] and Supplementary Methods.

Cell intervention by COM crystals without or with CGA pre-/cotreatment

COM crystals were prepared according to the protocol described previously [35, 36]. An equal number of MDCK cells (~1.5 × 105 cells/well) in the growth medium was added into each well of a 6-well plate. After overnight cultivation, the growth medium was refreshed without or with the addition of CGA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at the final concentration of 50 µM (selected after the cell death assay as described above). After 24-h incubation, the cells without CGA pretretment were refreshed with the growth medium without addition of both CGA and crystals (control group) or without addition of CGA but with COM crystals (at the final concentration of 100 µg/ml) (COM group), whereas the CGA-pretreated cells were refreshed with the growth medium with addition of both CGA (at the final concentration of 50 µM) and COM crystals (at the final concentration100 µg/ml) (CGA + COM group). After another 24-h incubation, the cells were examined with various assays as follows.

Measurement of cell spindle index

After differential treatments, cell images were captured under an inverted phase-contrast light microscope (Nikon). Cell lengths and widths were measured from 100 cells in at least 10 random fields per sample using the NIS-Elements D (v. 5.42.01) software (Nikon). Functional and pathway enrichment analyses

Functional enrichment analyses based on Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and interrelation of the enriched GO terms were performed using ClueGO (v. 2.5.10) and CluePedia (v. 1.5.10) tools in Cytoscape (v. 3.10.3). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were performed using ShinyGO (v. 0.85) and visualized by SRplot tool. To build the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network, a list of differentially expressed proteins was queried against the STRING tool (v. 12.0). The network was then imported to the Cytoscape tool for visualization.

siRNA-mediated HSP60 knockdown

Expression of HSP60 was knocked down using siRNA transfection against HSP60 (siHSP60) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or scrambled siRNA (siControl) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as described in [40, 41] and Supplementary Methods. The siControl- and siHSP60-transfected cells were then subjected to various assays as described above.

Statistical analysis

The data were collected from three independent experiments using independent biological samples and are reported as mean ± SEM. Unpaired Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test were employed to compare between two groups and among three groups, respectively. The statistically significant threshold was set at a p-value of < 0.05.

Results

Screening for an appropriate concentration of CGA

To find the appropriate concentration of CGA for MDCK renal cells, cell death was quantified using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated annexin V/propidium iodide staining and flow cytometry-based cell death assay. After 24-h incubation with CGA at the final concentration of 0.5, 5, 50 or 500 µM, all the cells were collected and stained with FITC-conjugated annexin V and propidium iodide. The fluorescence signals were acquired by flow cytometric analysis of at least 10,000 cells/sample. The results showed that CGA at 500 µM caused a significant increase in cell death, whereas its other concentrations did not affect the cell death (Fig. 1). CGA prevented COM-induced EMT

To investigate the effects of CGA on the prevention of COM-induced EMT, MDCK renal cells were exposed to COM crystals for 24 h without (COM group) or with 24-h pre-/cotreatment of 50 µM CGA (CGA + COM group), whereas the cells without both CGA pre-/cotreatment and COM crystals served as the control (control group). The cell morphology was then evaluated. COM caused a change in the cell shape to appear spindle-like, whereas CGA pre-/cotreatment could prevent such a change (Fig. 2A). The morphological alteration was confirmed by measurement of cell spindle index, which revealed a significant increase in the spindle index in the COM-exposed group, while the CGA + COM group could preserve the cell spindle index at the basal level (as of the control group) (Fig. 2B). TER across the cell monolayer was also measured to investigate the cell barrier function. The result showed that TER significantly declined in the COM-exposed cells, whereas CGA pre-/cotreatment could prevent such an effect of COM crystals on TER (Fig. 2C). Alterations in EMT markers, including ZO-1 (epithelial marker) and vimentin (mesenchymal marker), were evaluated using an immunofluorescence assay. The results demonstrated disorganization of ZO-1 in the COM-exposed cells, whereas the ZO-1 organization was successfully preserved in the CGA + COM group (Fig. 2D). Quantification of ZO-1 fluorescence intensity showed that ZO-1 was significantly lower in the COM-exposed cells, but could be preserved by the CGA pre-/cotreatment (Fig. 2F). Cellular proteome analyses

Proteomic analyses of cellular proteins extracted from control, COM, and CGA + COM groups were performed via nanoLC-ESI-LTQ-Orbitrap MS/MS. Cellular proteome profiles were retrieved from triplicate runs of each of three independent biological samples, yielding 941, 945, and 952 proteins identified in control, COM, and CGA + COM groups, respectively (Supplementary Tables S1–S3). Principal component analysis (PCA) using intensity data from label‐free quantification (LFQ) of all replicated runs clearly showed the distinct patterns derived from the three different groups (Fig. 4A). LFQ and multiple comparisons among the three groups revealed 167, 126, and 94 proteins with significantly differential levels in control versus COM, COM versus CGA + COM, and control versus CGA + COM comparative pairs, respectively (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S4). A Venn diagram summarized the number of all these significantly differential proteins from the three comparative pairs (Fig. 4C). From these results, 55 differential proteins were common between the control versus COM and COM versus CGA + COM comparative pairs, without an overlap with those of the control versus CGA + COM comparative pair (Fig. 4C). Discussion

EMT is a prominent process involved in organ fibrosis [42] and cancer metastasis [43]. It is the process in which epithelial cells are transformed to fibroblast-like mesenchymal cells with losing their epithelial structure and functions but gaining mesenchymal characteristics. EMT is also associated with excessive production and deposition of ECM [42]. Several cellular mechanisms are involved in the EMT process, such as cytoskeletal re-organization [44–46], cell–cell junctional alterations [47], and focal adhesion changes [44, 48]. EMT can be induced by several stimuli, including drugs [49, 50], oxidative stress [51, 52], high glucose [53, 54], oxalate [37, 38, 55, 56], and COM crystals [16].

In the present study, the protective roles of CGA against COM-induced EMT were investigated. Firstly, the optimal concentration of CGA was determined by searching for the highest but nontoxic concentration of CGA on renal epithelial cells prior to all following experiments. In fact, the physiologic concentration of CGA in the human body varies, depending on its bioavailability and amount of ingestion. A previous study has demonstrated that the maximal plasma concentration of non-metabolized 5-CQA is 2 nM after an intake of instant coffee (200 ml, containing approximately 120 µmol of 5-CQA) [57], whereas 24-h urine and ileal fluid contain approximately 1.1 and 64 µmol of 5-CQA, respectively [58]. However, higher concentrations (0.5–500 µM) have been used in several previous in vitro studies [34, 59, 60]. Therefore, this range of in vitro concentrations (0.5–500 µM) was used in our present study. At 500 µM, CGA, however, caused severe cytotoxicity (as indicated by the increased cell death). Thus, CGA at 50 µM (without obvious cytotoxic effects found) was chosen for all the following experiments in this study.

Our results demonstrated the protective properties of CGA against EMT in renal epithelial cells triggered by COM crystals. While COM crystals led to elongation of renal epithelial cells, CGA preserved the proper morphology of these cells. CGA also rescued the decline of TER upon COM crystal exposure. Additionally, CGA attenuated COM-induced ZO-1 downregulation and vimentin upregulation. The data in this study were in the same line as those found in many previous studies. The beneficial roles of CGA in EMT prevention have been studied mostly in breast cancer [61–65]. The in vitro study using breast cancer cell lines has reported that CGA-rich extract from Capparis zeylanica L. roots inhibits cell proliferation and the EMT process [63]. Another in vitro study in MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 breast cancer cells has revealed impairment of an EMT signaling pathway by CGA, accompanied with diminished cell migration and invasion activities [64]. Additionally, CGA suppresses mesenchymal marker vimentin, while increasing epithelial marker ZO-1 in MCF-7 breast cancer cells, accompanied with lower cell migration activity [65]. A study on ovarian cancer has also found that CGA reduces cell migration, together with suppressing the EMT signaling pathway in OVCA433 ovarian cancer cells [66].

In addition, the roles of CGA in the prevention of EMT have also been documented in fibrotic organs, including the liver and lung. A study in liver fibrosis has suggested that CGA-rich extract of Lonicera japonica ameliorates hepatic EMT by reversing the increased vimentin and decreased E-cadherin in mice with liver fibrosis induced by carbon tetrachloride [67]. A study in mice with bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis has demonstrated that CGA prevents pulmonary EMT by inhibiting the increased vimentin and decreased E-cadherin in lung tissues [60]. In concordance, an in vitro study in A549 lung epithelial cells has revealed that CGA also prevents transforming growth factor-β1-induced EMT [60]. Additionally, CGA attenuates EMT in diabetic retinopathy in streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice [68]. In addition to reversing the EMT protein markers, CGA rescues TER deterioration in tumor necrosis factor α-triggered EMT in APRE19 retinal pigment epithelial cells [68].

Activation of the p38 pathway by COM crystal exposure, as reflected by an increase in the phosphorylated form of p38, has previously been shown in renal epithelial cells [39, 69, 70]. There are several lines of additional evidence reporting the activation of the p38 signaling pathway in EMT triggered by various stimuli in renal epithelial cells [71, 72]. For example, cadmium ion triggers the EMT process in Caki-1 renal clear cell carcinoma cells through the p38 signaling pathway [71]. In concordance, a recent study has reported that nephropathogenic infectious bronchitis virus (NIBV) can induce EMT in renal epithelial cells via the p38 pathway [72]. The role of CGA in p38 suppression has also been reported in several models. CGA-rich coffee pulp extract suppresses lipopolysaccharide-induced phosphorylation of several signaling molecules, including extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38, in RAW 264.7 macrophage cells [73]. CGA inhibits p38 activity in chronic stress-induced intestinal injury [74]. Dextran sodium sulfate-induced ulcerative colitis in mice is also ameliorated by CGA via suppression of the p38 signaling [75]. Consistently, our data demonstrated that CGA could prevent COM-induced EMT via suppression of the p38 signaling.

To explore the molecular mechanisms governing the preventive effects of CGA against COM-induced EMT, we performed label-free quantitative proteomics to discover significantly altered proteins induced by COM crystals that were successfully preserved by CGA pre-/cotreatment. The analysis revealed 55 proteins that might serve as the target proteins of CGA treatment. These 55 target proteins of CGA treatment participated in several biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, and pathways that might contribute to the EMT process, such as regulation of substrate adhesion-dependent cell spreading, actin filament depolymerization, cell adhesion molecule binding, actin filament binding, cadherin binding, cell-substrate junction, actin cytoskeleton, cell cortex, tight junction, cell-extracellular matrix interactions, and degradation of beta-catenin by the destruction complex. Additionally, pathway enrichment analyses also suggested that the target proteins, i.e., serine/threonine-protein phosphatase 2A catalytic subunit alpha isoform (PPP2CA), proteasome subunit beta type-4 (PSMB4), 26S proteasome non-ATPase regulatory subunit 6 (PSMD6), spectrin beta chain, nonerythrocytic 1 (SPTBN1), and septin-7 (SEPTIN7), were involved in MAPK signaling cascades. Among these, serine/threonine-protein phosphatase 2A works as an upstream regulator of p38 MAPK [76, 77]. Interestingly, our study revealed that the level of this protein was reduced by COM exposure but was rescued by CGA. Our functional and pathway enrichment analyses also found the involvement of the target proteins in the regulation of oxidative stress, response to oxidative stress and oxidative phosphorylation, suggesting the antioxidant properties of CGA against COM-induced EMT.

In PPI network analysis of the 55 target proteins of CGA treatment, HSP60, which was increased by COM exposure but was reduced to its basal level by CGA pre-/cotreatment, showed the greatest number of interacting proteins and was thus considered a hub of these CGA-target proteins. The increased HSP60 expression induced by COM crystals was consistent with a previous study on COM-induced cytotoxicity in renal cells [78]. More importantly, suppression of the increased HSP60 expression has been reported to be associated with the alleviating activity effect of CGA against acute liver failure caused by acetaminophen in mice [79].

HSP60 (also called chaperonin 60 or CPN60), encoded by HSPD1 gene, is a chaperone protein that is responsible for stress response to maintain cellular homeostasis and structural integrity [80]. HSP60 is predominantly localized inside the mitochondria, but can also be found in the cytoplasm and plasma membranes [81]. Its canonical function in mitochondria is to regulate protein folding and trafficking [82, 83]. However, several noncanonical functions of HSP60 are also found outside mitochondria to regulate cell growth, apoptosis, and metastasis [81, 83]. The upregulation of HSP60 in kidney fibrosis and injury has been reported in several studies. For example, the increased level of HSP60 has been found in renal tissue of patients with diabetic kidneys, correlated with oxidative stress and kidney fibrosis [84]. HSP60 is also upregulated in mice with diabetic kidneys and high glucose-exposed HK-2 renal cells, both of which are accompanied with the increased level of a mesenchymal marker, fibronectin [85]. Additionally, renal abundance of HSP60 significantly increases in cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity [86, 87]. Other than the kidney, the association of HSP60 upregulation and fibrosis has also been reported in other organs, including the lung and liver. This protein is upregulated in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis mice [88], silica nanoparticle-induced interstitial pulmonary fibrosis in rats [89], and silica nanoparticle-injured BEAS-2B bronchial epithelial cells and MRC-5 fibroblasts [89]. The increased level of HSP60 is also found in high-fructose diet-induced liver fibrosis in mice [90] and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis with fibrosis in rats [91]. Additionally, the increased HSP60 correlates with downregulation of ZO-1 and occludin in imidacloprid-caused intestinal barrier damage [92]. Moreover, the association of p38 activation and increased expression of HSP60 has been found in several other conditions, including hydrogen peroxide-induced oxidative stress in HeLa cells [93], lead-induced hepatotoxicity [94], cadmium-induced liver necroptosis [95], and fluoride-induced hepatic oxidative stress [96]. We therefore hypothesized that HSP60 might play an essential role in COM-induced EMT and the preventive mechanism by CGA.

Our results from siRNA-mediated HSP60 knockdown experiments confirmed that HSP60 knockdown could prevent the EMT process induced by COM crystals. These results were consistent with many lines of evidence in diverse disease models. The study in nasopharyngeal carcinoma cell lines has demonstrated that a reduction of HSP60 expression is involved in gemcitabine- and RQR309-suppressed EMT process, cell migration, and cell invasion [97]. The study on high glucose-induced renal tubular epithelial cell dysfunction has revealed that knockdown of HSP60 prevents high glucose-enhanced oxidative stress and fibronectin expression [41]. Additionally, the suppression of HSP60 by lycopene [86] and dimethylthiourea [87] can rescue kidney injury caused by cisplatin. We also found that HSP60 knockdown could prevent p38 activation by COM crystals. This was consistent with several previous studies, in which the reduction of HSP60 expression correlates with p38 suppression. Avicularin (a flavonoid in citrus fruits) suppresses p38 activity and reduces HSP60 expression in lead-induced hepatotoxicity [94]. Rutin prevents cadmium-enhanced p38 activity and HSP60 expression in chicken liver [95]. Caffeic acid abolishes HSP60 and p38 upregulation in fluoride-induced hepatic oxidative stress [96]. Moreover, knockdown of HSP60 in microglia leads to decreased phosphorylated p38 and reduced inflammation [98]. These data strengthen that HSP60 plays a critical role in COM-induced EMT and the preventive activity of CGA.

To the best of our knowledge, little is known about the roles of CGA in nephrolithiasis. Only a few recent studies have reported the potential association between the protective effects of CGA and renal stone formation [99, 100]. However, several previous studies have reported the protective effects of a variety of bioactive compounds against renal stone formation by inhibiting EMT processes [55, 70, 101–103]. Therefore, our data implicated that CGA may also prevent renal stone formation by inhibiting EMT processes. Nevertheless, the direct effects of CGA on COM stone formation processes, e.g., COM crystallization, growth, aggregation, and crystal-cell adhesion, were not analyzed herein and thus should be further elucidated. Also, another limitation of this study is the lack of in vivo validation to confirm the protective effects of CGA obtained from our in vitro assays.

Summary

We demonstrated herein that COM caused EMT in MDCK renal epithelial cells, as illustrated by increases in spindle index and vimentin expression, whereas TER and ZO-1 expression declined. However, CGA successfully abolished all these EMT features. This anti-EMT effect of CGA correlated with suppression of p38 signaling enhanced by COM crystals. In addition, proteomics data, particularly from functional enrichment analyses, suggested that HSP60 might play a crucial role in the preventive effect of CGA against COM-induced EMT. Functional validation using siRNA-mediated HSP60 knockdown confirmed that the suppression of HSP60 expression successfully abolished the EMT processes induced by COM crystals. These data indicate that CGA mitigates COM-induced EMT in renal epithelial cells via suppression of HSP60 expression and p38 signaling activated by COM crystals.
