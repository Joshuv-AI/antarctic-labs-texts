Tomato SlARF5 participate in the flower organ initiation process and control plant height
Authors: Qingfang Lin, Jianyong Wang, Jiaxin Gong, ZiZi Meng, Yuting Jin, Lei Zhang, Zhiliang Zhang, Jing Sun, Lei Kai, Shilian Qi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant height is a critical agronomic trait closely linked to yield, primarily regulated by Gibberellins (GA) and auxins, which interact in complex ways. However, the mechanism underlying their interactions remain incompletely understood. In this study, we identified a tomato mutant exhibiting significantly reduced plant height. Through gene cloning and bulked segregant analysis (BSA) sequencing, we found that the mutant gene corresponds to the tomato auxin response factor gene SlARF5/MP. Here, we show that overexpression of SlARF5/MP significantly enhances plant height. Additionally, treatment with GA3 restored the plant height of the mutant to wild-type (WT) levels, indicating that GA content is a key factor influencing plant height. We also observed significant upregulation of GA-biosynthesis genes, including GA2-oxidases GA20ox3 and GA20ox4, as well as the GA3 biosynthesis gene GA3ox1, in SlARF5-overexpressing plants. Furthermore, we demonstrated that SlARF5 directly binds to SlGA2ox3, which mediates the conversion of GA3 to inactive GA, therebyregulating its expression. Our findings suggest that SlARF5 modulates GA3 metabolism by regulating GA synthesis genes, ultimately leading to alterations in plant height.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05707-z.Keywords: Tomato, Plant height, Auxin response factor, Gibberellins, Flower development

Introduction

Plant height significantly influences plant architecture, lodging resistance, and yield performance [1]. As a critical agronomic trait, it is regulated by a variety of hormones, including strigolactone, auxin, gibberellin, brassinosteroid, and cytokinin [1–4], as well as by environmental factors. Among these hormones, gibberellin (GA) exerts the greatest impact on plant height [5]. Variations in plant height typically attributed to mutations in genes that regulate GA production, metabolism, or signaling pathways [1]. The GA biosynthesis gene family includes GA20ox and GA3ox genes, which play pivotal roles in GA synthesis. Recent studies have demonstrated that overexpressing of GA20ox1 in maize and Agapanthus praecox can lead to increased plant height [6, 7]. Furthermore, DELLA proteins, key repressors in the GA signaling pathway, have been shown to induce dwarf phenotypes when their function is enhanced, as observed in Arabidopsis and maize [8].

Auxin is a key hormone that frequently interacts with GA in the regulation of plant height [1]. It plays a vital role in plant growth and development, particularly in height regulation [9]. Mutations in critical genes involved in auxin signal transduction or biosynthesis pathways can lead to variations in plant height. For example, loss-of-function mutations in the auxin signaling receptor genes TRANSPORT INHIBITOR RESPONSE1/AUXIN SIGNALING F-BOX (TIR1/AFB) result in dwarf or semi-dwarf phenotypes in rice [10]. In contrast, overexpression of the auxin response factor gene OsARF19 also associated with dwarfism [11]. Moreover, alterations in auxin efflux carrier proteins or polar auxin transport proteins in rice similarly affect plant height. Increased expression of OsPIN5, for instance, diminishes plant height by modulating auxin transport and distribution [12].

Auxin response factors (ARFs) and auxin/INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID (Aux/IAA) proteins are key transcriptional regulators in the auxin signal transduction pathway [13]. Alterations in either ARFs or Aux/IAA proteins can lead to a variety of auxin-related phenotypes. ARFs represent a novel class of transcription factors characterized by three conserved domains: the DNA-binding domain (DBD), middle region (MR), and carboxyl-terminal dimerization domain (CTD). The MR domain regulates the expression of early auxin-responsive genes, including GH3, Aux/IAA, short auxin-up RNA (SAUR), and lateral organ boundaries-domain (LBD) genes [14]. The DBD domain, which specifically binds to the Auxin Response Elements (AuxRE) motif, is crucial for target gene selection [15].

ARFs exhibit a wide range of temporal and spatial expression patterns, contributing to various developmental processes. For instance, ARF5 (also known as MONOPTEROS [MP]) is essential for the formation of embryonic roots and flowers, as well as for other developmental auxin responses [16, 17]. In tomato, ARF5 is highly expressed in leaves, flowers, and early immature green fruit (8 DPA), indicating its role in the development of these tissues and organs [18]. In Arabidopsis, mutations in ARF5/MP lead to flower defects [16]. ARF5/MP can directly activate the expression of LEAFY (LFY), ANT, and AIL6/PLT3 in Arabidopsis inflorescences, specifying floral fate [16]. Similarly, in tomatoes, SlMP influences flower development [19]. Alon Israeli and colleagues found that SlMP promotes the initiation and growth of leaves, leaflets, and floral organs. Despite the extensive characterization of AFR5 functions, its role in regulating plant height is not well understood, and the precise mechanism by which SlARF5 modulates auxin/GA interactions remains unclear.

Previous research has shown that SlARF5 is strongly upregulated by auxin, indicating its role in mediating auxin responses [18]. Additionally, SlARF5 has been reported to interact with SlDELLA, a repressor in the GA signaling pathway, suggesting a link to GA signaling. In this study, we screened a tomato mutant exhibiting significantly reduced plant height and abnormal flower development. BSA sequencing revealed a mutation in the SlARF5 gene. We found that plants overexpressed SlARF5 exhibited significantly greater height compared to WT plants. Our study demonstrated that the expression levels of genes associated with GA synthesis were downregulated in SlARF5 mutants, resulting in decreased GA content and dwarf phenotypes. Conversely, in SlARF5 overexpressed plants, the GA synthesis genes SlGA20ox3, SlGA20ox4, and SlGA3ox1 were upregulated, potentially increasing GA content and plant height. Furthermore, SlARF5 directly regulated the expression of SlGA2ox3, which is involved in GA metabolism. These findings suggest that SlARF5 modulates plant height by influencing GA metabolism.

Results

Screen of mutant

To investigate the mechanism regulating plant height in tomatoes, we screened an EMS-mutant library and identified a mutant with significantly reduced plant height (Fig. 1a). Statistical analysis revealed that the height of the mutant plants was significantly lower than that of the wild type (WT) plants (Fig. 1b), leading us to provisionally name this mutant dwarf-1. In addition to reduced plant height, the dwarf-1 mutant exhibited abnormal flower development compared to the WT plants (Fig. 1c). While the WT plants had intact sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels, the dwarf-1 mutant displayed varying degrees of defects in sepals, petals, and stamens. Specifically, aside from a fixed carpel, other floral organs, including sepals, petals, and stamens, were often absent or fused together (Fig. 1c). Further analysis of the data revealed that in WT plants, the most common number of floral organs-sepals, petals and stamens-was five, accounting for 70.97% of the observations. In contrast, among the mutant plants, the predominant counts for sepals, petals and stamens were zero, with sepals (0) accounting for 24.22%, petals (0) for 56.46%, and stamens (0) for 50% (Table 1). These findings indicate that dwarf-1 mutant exhibits a distinct floral organ deletion phenotype, particularly characterized by loss of petals and stamens. This suggests that the mutant gene may play a critical role in the development of these floral organs. Based on these results, we hypothesized that the mutant gene is likely involved in both plant height regulation and flower organ development.

To investigate the abnormal flower meristems and impaired initiation and growth of flower organs, we utilized scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to visualize flower development in WT and dwarf-1 mutant plants (Fig. 2). The results revealed that the initiation of sepals, stamens, and petals was significantly affected in the dwarf-1 mutant. Mutation in auxin response factors ARF5 caused the stunted plants and malformed flowers

To identify the potential mutant gene in the dwarf-1 mutant, we hybridized the dwarf-1 mutant with WT micro-tom tomato, constructed a segregating F2 population, extracted genomic DNA from both the mutant and WT pools, and performed BSA sequencing. The F1 hybrid of the dwarf-1 mutant and WT tomato did not exhibit the dwarf-1 flower development malformation phenotype, suggesting that the mutant gene is a recessive mutation. Furthermore, in the segregating population, plants displaying the mutant phenotype accounted for approximately one-third of the total, further supporting the conclusion that the mutation is recessive and likely represents a single gene alteration.

Bioinformatics analysis identified a signal peak on chromosome 4 (Fig. 3a) and further pinpointed a candidate region at the end of chromosome 4 (from 63.51 to 65.62 Mbp) harboring three candidate SNPs: one in the intergenic region, one synonymous variant in Solyc04g081590, and one (location: Chr4: 65,315,966) in the second intron of Solyc04g081240 causing a splice donor variant (Fig. 3a). The mutation was located in the gene Solyc04g081240, which encodes the auxin response factor ARF5/MP, a well-characterized member of the ARF family extensively studied in Arabidopsis, known to involve in leaf shape development in tomatoes. We designated the dwarf-1 mutant as arf5-1. Sequencing revealed a splice donor variant in the mutant SlARF5 genome compared to the WT, specifically a G to A substitution occurring after the second exon, potentially leading to incorrect RNA splicing (Fig. 3a). Then we amplified cDNA of the SlARF5 gene from the arf5-1 mutant using RT-PCR and confirmed the mutated form of the mRNA through Sanger sequencing. The arf5-1 mutant exhibited a 116 bp deletion in the coding sequence (CDS) sequence from positions 105 to 220 (Fig. S2a), resulting in a protein sequence missing 117 amino acids (Fig. S2b).

We also identified another mutant with a similar phenotype to arf5-1, designated arf5-2. Genomic analysis revealed that the mutation was also located in SlARF5 but differed from arf5-1, featuring a G to A substitution at the start of exon 4 in the genomic sequence, which could potentially affect RNA splicing (Fig. S3a). This mutation resulted in CDS in arf5-2 that lacked four bases compared to the WT (Fig. S3b), leading to early termination, and only express 116 amino acids (Fig. S3c). These sequence alterations in the mutants are likely to cause the abnormal function of DBD domain. Since the DBD domain is crucial for the functional diversity of ARF proteins, changes in this domain likely affect the normal functioning of ARF5.

Overexpression of SlARF5 increase the plant height

To further investigate the biological function of SlARF5 in tomatoes, we constructed an overexpression vector of SlARF5 and transformed it into WT tomato micro-tom plants. Following kanamycin resistance screening, we obtained four homozygous plants with a single T-DNA locus, designated OE6, OE7, OE10, and OE11. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis (qRT-PCR) demonstrated that the expression levels of SlARF5 in OE6, OE7, and OE10 were significantly higher than those in WT plants, with increases of 12.7, 23.9 and 17.2 times, respectively (Fig. 4a, b). We observed the plant height phenotype at DAG28 for WT, mutant arf5-1, arf5-2, and overexpressing plants, OE6 and OE10. The heights of OE6 and OE10 plants were significantly greater than that of WT plants, while the height of mutant plants remained significantly shorter (Fig. 4c and d). There were no differences in the number of stem internode among the mutants, OE plants and WT plants (Fig. 4e). However, the transgenic lines exhibited longer stem internodes, particularly in internodes 4 and 5, whereas the mutant showed slightly shorter internodes 3 and 4 compared to WT plants (Fig. 4e). We also observed that the flowering time of the mutant plants was significantly delayed compared to the wild type, whereas the flowering time of the overexpression plants was slightly earlier than the wild type (Fig. 4c and Fig. S4a). However, there was no significant difference in the number of pre-flowering leaves among the groups (Fig. S4b). These observations suggest that plant height is primarily influenced by changes in stem internode elongation. Overall, these results indicate that SlARF5 affects plant height by modulating internode elongation in tomatoes.

Among the auxin response genes identified, several early auxin response genes, including SlIAA3, SlIAA13, SlIAA16, and SlIAA27, were notably present. Additionally, multiple SAUR genes, such as SlSAUR14, SlSAUR32, SlSAUR42, and SlSAUR52, were identified, supporting previous research that SlARF5 regulates the expression of early auxin genes. The auxin transporter SlPIN2 was also downregulated in the arf5-1 mutant, indicating that SlARF5 is associated with the auxin signaling pathway and may influence various aspects of plant growth by modulating these auxin-related genes.

In the Gene Ontology (GO) cluster of gibberellin-related genes, many genes associated with GA synthesis were found to be downregulated, including GA20ox1, GA3ox1, and GA3ox2. To further investigate the changes in GA signaling pathway genes in arf5-1 mutants, we assessed the expression of these genes in WT plants, arf5-1 mutants, and overexpressing OE6 and OE10 plants using qRT-PCR. The results demonstrated that genes involved in the early stage of GA synthesis (SlKS, SlKAO, SlKO) as well as later stage (GA20 oxidase: SlGA20ox1, SlGA20ox2, SlGA20ox3, SlGA20ox4; GA3 synthesis genes: SlGA3ox1, SlGA3ox2) were significantly downregulated in the arf5-1 mutant. Conversely, in the overexpressing plants OE6 and OE10, SlKS, SlGA20ox3, SlGA20ox4 and SlGA3ox1 were upregulated (Fig. 5c). These findings indicate that GA biosynthesis is repressed in arf5-1 mutants while enhanced in SlARF5 overexpressing plants OE6 and OE10.

arf5-1 mutant has decreased GA3 content

Given the significant downregulation of GA synthesis genes in the arf5-1 mutant, we hypothesized that GA content might be reduced compared to the WT. To test this, we utilized high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine the GA3 content in the shoot apical meristem (SAM) of WT and arf5-1 mutant plants. As anticipated, GA3 levels were significantly reduced in the arf5-1 mutant (Fig. 6a), which correlates with the decreased expression of GA synthesis genes. We speculate that the reduced GA3 content may be responsible for the dwarf phenotype observed in the arf5-1 mutant.

To verify this hypothesis, we conducted GA3 spray experiments on both arf5-1 mutant and WT plant. The results indicated that a 20 µM concentration of GA3 significantly increased plant height, restoring it to the levels observed in WT plants (Fig. 6b). Statistical analysis further revealed that the height of arf5-1 mutant plants could be rescued to that of WT plants under normal conditions without GA3 application (Fig. 6c). These findings suggest that the dwarf phenotype of the arf5-1 mutant is indeed caused by GA3 deficiency.

SlARF5 directly regulates the SlGA2ox3 expression

Since SlARF5 influences the expression of GA signaling pathway genes, we further investigated the regulatory mechanisms involved. Previous studies suggest that ARF5/MP preferentially bind to the motif 5’-TGTCGG-3’ rather than the canonical AuxREs 5’-TGTCTC-3’ [20]. Additionally, the DBD domain of ARF5/MP can recognize three types of AuxRE motifs: inverted repeats (IR), direct repeats (DR), and overturned repeats (ER). ARF5/MP has a strong affinity for two AuxREs separated by specific base pair spacing, such as IR7-8 and IR18, as well as DR4-5 [20, 21]. Analysis of the genomic sequences of GA signaling pathway genes revealed that SlGA2ox3 contains two conserved AuxRE motifs that are relatively consistent among Solanaceae species (Fig. 7a). To determine whether SlARF5 can directly regulate SlGA2ox3 expression, we conducted a yeast one-hybrid assay, which confirmed that SlARF5 can bind directly to SlGA2ox3 (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, we examined the expression of SlGA2ox3 gene in WT, arf5-1 mutant and SlARF5-overexpressing plants (OE6 and OE10). The results indicated that SlGA2ox3 was upregulated in the arf5-1 mutant and downregulated in SlARF5 overexpressing plants (Fig. 7c). These findings demonstrate that SlARF5 can directly binds to the AuxRE motifs of SlGA2ox3 and thereby regulating its expression.

Discussion

DBD domain of SlARF5 is essential for the development and initiation of flower organs, and the regulation of plant height

Although ARFs have evolved over hundreds of millions of years, they retain several conserved domains, including the DBD domain, the MR domain, and the C-terminal interaction domain (previously known as domain III/IV) [23]. Evidence suggests that the protein structure of ARFs can function autonomously, enabling them to act in isolation [24, 25]. As a well-studied member of the ARF family, ARF5/MP shares this conserve structural framework. Crystal structure analysis reveal that ARF DBDs consist of a B3 domain and two dimerization domains (DD) flanked by the B3 domain [15]. Furthermore, these studies indicate that ARF DBDs homodimerize through their DD domain. Phylogenetic comparisons based solely on DBDs sequences closely resemble those of full ARF proteins, suggesting that the functional diversity of ARF proteins is primarily linked to their DBD proteins [15].

The arf5-1 mutant is characterized by a deletion of 117 amino acids at the N-terminus, which affects the structure of the DBD. Notably, the B3 domain within DBD appears to be an insertion into the DD domain. In Arabidopsis ARF5/MP, the B3 domain spans residues 154–260, while in tomato SlARF5, it covers residues 144–250. His suggest that the sequence preceding residue 144 corresponds to the DD domain. We hypothesize that the 117 amino acids missing in the arf5-1 mutant belong to the DD domain, and their deletion likely disrupts the normal functioning of DBD. Specifically, the loss of this DD domain may impair the ability of SlARF5 DBD to homodimerize when binding to target genes. Consequently, we propose that the flower development and plant height phenotypes observed in the arf5-1 mutant could attributed to the loss of DBD functionality.

SlARF5 modulates the expression of early auxin related genes and GA-signaling genes

Auxin is a crucial hormone that influences various aspects of plant growth and development. The auxin signaling pathway facilitates rapid flucuations in auxin concentration within plant cells, subsequently affecting the expression of downstream genes [26]. This signaling involves three protein families, the F-box-containing auxin receptors TIR1/AFB, ARF transcription factors, and Aux/IAA proteins. In the absence of auxin, Aux/IAA proteins inhibit ARF activity. When auxin binds to TIR1/AFB, it triggers the 26 S proteasome-dependent degradation of Aux/IAA, releasing ARFs to regulate auxin-responsive genes by binding to their promoters [27–29]. Our transcriptome data indicate that SlARF5 is associated with GA synthesis (Fig. 6c). Both GA and auxin play pivotal roles in regulating plant height [1]. Furthermore, ARFs and AUX/IAA proteins can directly induce the expression of GA catabolic genes during GA-related organ elongation [26]. Although the regulation of GA biosynthetic enzymes by auxin is evolutionarily conserved, the specific targets may vary across species and tissues. For instance, auxin has been shown to induce the expression of GA20ox1 and GA3ox1 in the pea fruit pericarp [30–32].

The MR domain of ARFs plays a crucial role in regulating the expression of early auxin-responsive genes, including Aux/IAA, GH3, SAUR, and LBD [14]. In our study, among the genes regulated by SlARF5, the auxin-responsive genes predominantly included IAA proteins and SAURs. IAA proteins serve as primary inhibitors of ARF activity, and the impaired function of SlARF5 in the arf5-1 mutant resulted in the downregulation of IAA proteins. We hypothesize that the disruption of auxin signaling in the arf5-1 mutant contributed to the decreased transcript levels of IAA proteins. Additionally, SAURs represent the largest family of early auxin response genes, functioning to induce cell elongation and promote plant growth by regulating cell wall acidification [33]. The downregulation of these genes can lead to reduced plant cell elongation, which ultimately affects plant height and results in dwarfism.

GA3 content as the main contributor to SlARF5-mediated plant height control

GA influences plant height primarily by affecting cell elongation and division [3]. Auxin’s impact on plant height often coincides with other growth traits, such as leaf angle, leaf shape, and branch number [9, 34]. In the arf5-1 mutant, notable dwarfism also accompany by significant floral deformities. Additionally, the leaf shape in the arf5-1 mutant is simpler compared to the WT plants, aligning with prior findings that SlARF5/MP regulates leaf morphology in tomatoes [19]. While auxin deficiency is known to cause dwarfism in plants [35–37], exceptions exist; for instance, elevated levels of the growth-regulating factor GROWTH-REGULATING FACTORS (GRFs) gene OsGRF7 in rice lead to semi-dwarf phenotypes without a corresponding decrease in auxin levels, indicating that high auxin can also cause dwarfism [38]. Interestingly, in rice, high auxin levels are often associated with a more upright leaf angle, and in SlARF5 overexpressing plants, the leaf angle was indeed smaller than that of the WT (data not shown). We speculate that the SlARF5 mutation may disrupt normal auxin level in the arf5-1 mutant, resulting in various growth and developmental phenotypes, which will be elaborated upon in subsequent sections.

Exogenous treatment with GA3 successfully restored the plant height of the arf5-1 mutant to that of WT plants (Fig. 6b-c), confirming that the dwarf phenotype is linked to GA levels. Additionally, expression levels of several GA synthesis genes including GA20ox3, GA20ox4, and GA3ox1, were increased in SlARF5 transgenic plants (Fig. 5c), suggesting that SlARF5 influences GA synthesis. Conversely, all genes related to GA synthesis were downregulated in the arf5-1 mutant, correlating with its reduced GA content and dwarfing phenotype. The upregulation of SlGA2ox3 in the arf5-1 mutant (Fig. 7c) further indicates enhanced GA metabolism. Notably, GA3ox1 which catalyzes the final step of the GA3 synthesis pathway, was significantly downregulated in the arf5-1 mutant and upregulated in SlARF5 overexpressing plants OE6 and OE10 (Fig. 5c). These findings confirm that the SlARF5 mutation reduces GA3ox1 expression, thus impeding GA3 synthesis. Moreover, the upregulation of SlGA2ox3, which encodes a GA2-oxidase that converts active GA3 into inactive GA (Fig. 7c), further supports the decrease of GA3 in the arf5-1 mutant. Analysis of GA3 content between WT and arf5-1 mutant plants also corroborated these results (Fig. 6a). Therefore, the downregulation of SlGA3ox1 and the upregulation of SlGA2ox3 collectively indicate reduced GA3 accumulation in the arf5-1 mutant. Combined with the restoration of height upon GA3 application, we conclude that the dwarfing phenotype of the arf5-1 mutant is likely due to decreased GA3 level.

Based on our findings, we propose a model in which SlARF5 regulates plant height through the modulation of GA2ox3 expression (Fig. 8). Under normal conditions, optimal auxin levels enable SlARF5/MP to oligomerize with itself or other SlARFs via its functional DD domain, facilitating interactions with unknown regulatory factors to suppress the expression of target genes, including SlGA2ox3. In the arf5-1 mutant, the absence of the DD domain disrupts normal homodimerization of the DBD domain, preventing it from binding to the AuxREs motif of SlGA2ox3, thereby leading to its upregulation. This upregulation accelerates the conversion of GA3 to inactive, resulting in decreased GA3 content in the mutant. Conversely, SlARF5 enhances the expression of SlGA3ox1, SlGA20ox3, and SlGA20ox4, promoting GA accumulation, particularly GA3, which subsequently influences plant height.

A proposed model of SlARF5 regulates the plant height

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

Tomato plants used in this study is Solanum lycopersicum.cv. Micro-Tom, seeds of arf5-1 mutant (TOMJPE8733-1) and arf5-2 mutant (TOMJPW4559) were purchased from the Tomato Mutants Archive (http://tomatoma.nbrp.jp/). The growth environment is under the following conditions: 16 h light/8 h dark cycle, 25 °C temperature, 50% relative humidity and 200 umol m− 2 s− 1 intensity light.

Phenotype characterization

In order to calculate the height of the plants, we measured the height of the plants growing in the soil for 28 days. The height of cotyledon was taken as the starting position. Three biological replicates, each repeat of the number of different plants is not less than 10. To assess the sepal, petal, stamen and carpel phenotypes in each flower, more than 30 flowers (WT, 30, arf5-1 mutant, 62) in bloom from different plants per genotype were analyzed.

Scanning electron microscopy

Floral meristem (FM) from different periods were soaked in ethanol solution with gradient concentration (including 50%, 75%, 95%) for dehydrating the sample. Each concentration was treated for 15 min, and then 100% ethanol was treated twice for 20 min each time. The samples after ethanol gradient treatment were dried at critical point with liquid CO2 in a critical point dryer (Hitachi 3400 N) after plating. These samples were developed by LEICA S8AP0 scanning electron microscope.

Bulked segregant analysis (BSA)

After determining that the mutant phenotype is stable and no longer separated, the bulked segregant analysis was performed. First, we backcross the dwarf-1 mutant with WT tomato to obtain the F1 hybrid seeds. Then the F1 hybrids were then sown and self-bred to generate the F2 mapping population. According to plant height and flower development phenotype, 30 dwarf-1 mutant individuals and 30 WT individuals were selected for subsequent genomic DNA extraction. The method of DNA extraction was CTAB. DNA is extracted from each sample independently, and after the concentration is measured by Nanodrop, the same amount of DNA is mixed into a dwarf-1 mutant pool, a WT pool. These two pools were used for subsequent BSA sequencing. We identified the causal mutation using a mapping-by-sequencing strategy as described by Garcia [39].

Plasmid construction and generation of transgenic plants

For overexpression experiments, the full-length SlARF5 coding sequence was amplified from tomato“Micro-TOM”. The SlARF5 full-length coding sequence was cloned into the expression vector pHellsgate8 using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit according to the manufacturer manual (Vazyme, China) [40]. Primer sequences used for amplification are listed in Supplementalry Table S1. The construct was transferred into A. tumefaciens GV3101 and tomato (Micro-Tom) genetic transformation was conducted following the method of Liang [41]. The transgenic plants obtained were screened with kanamycin. Positive homozygous seedlings were used for subsequent phenotypic identification.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

RNA sequencing from wild type and arf5-1 mutant was performed on RNA pooled from entire SAM isolated from DAG10 (days after germination) tomato plants. Samples were harvested from three plants in two independent replications. Samples were used for RNA extraction (FastPure Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (RC401-01, Vazyme)). One microgram of total RNA was used for cDNA library. The libraries were produced and sequenced by Illumina HiSeq2500/x. Then clean reads were mapped to the ITAG3 tomato reference genome (version 3.0) using HISAT2 [42]. Normalization and significant calling was performed using Deseq2 package in R), and genes whose expression was more than twofold that of the WT (P < 0.05) were considered differentially expressed.

qRT-PCR

WT tomato plants, the arf5-1 mutant and the transgenic overexpression plants were sampled for RNA extraction (RC401-01, Vazyme). cDNA was synthesized from 2 to 5 µg of total RNA using a HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qRT-PCR with gDNA Eraser (Vazyme, R223-01). cDNA products were diluted into proper concentration and used as the template for the qRT-PCR. The 2M5 Hiper Realtime PCR Super Mix (Mei5Bio, MF797-01) was used. qRT-PCR was performed using the BioRad CFX384. The results were calculated using Bio-Rad CFX Manager software. With SlACTIN as an internal control, the relative expression of each gene was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer pairs used for qRT-PCR were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Yeast one-hybrid assay

The SlGA2ox3 promoter and exon sequence (designated P1 and P2) was amplified from gDNA with specific primers and inserted into the pLacZi-2µ vector to generate the bait construct P1 and P2. Then, the full-length ARF5 open reading frames (ORFs) were amplified with gene-specific primers and cloned into the pJG4-5 vector to generate prey vectors. The prey vector and the bait vector were cotransformed into the EGY48 yeast strain, after which the yeast cells were allowed to grow for 3 d at 30℃ on SD/-Ura/-Leu. Then the cells were transferred to the SD/-Ura/-Leu media supplemented with X-Gal. Each interaction was tested three times in parallel in three independent experiments. The primer pairs used for Y1H were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Determination of GA3

To measure endogenous GA3 contents, the SAM of 20-day-old plant were collected with three independent biological replicates. The samples were sent to Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co.,Ltd for GA3 measurement using HPLC methods.

Spray experiment

After wild type, mutant and overexpressed plants (DAG10) grew in soil, they were sprayed with GA3 (20 µM). The control group was sprayed with water. Spray every 2 days. After spraying 18 days (DAG28), phenotypes were photographed and the data was statistic.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analyses were performed using SPSS 22 Software (SPSS Inc., USA). The mean values were further indicated at P ≤ 0.05(*) using Duncan’s multiple range test for significant difference analysis.
