Maternal hyperhomocysteinemia promotes adverse pregnancy outcomes via mtDNA-cGAS-STING signaling-mediated trophoblast mitophagy
Authors: Chaoqun Liu, Zhuoqiao Gao, Jianyin Chen, Yunyan Yang, Qiaolin Xiao, Shaohui Deng, Yumei Zhang, Wenen Su, Tao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) is a recognized risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes. However, the underlying mechanisms precipitating placental dysfunction, particularly the potential involvement of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), remain largely elusive. This study aims to investigate the association between maternal HHcy and adverse pregnancy outcomes mediated by reduced placental mtDNA copy number (mtDNAcn), and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms driving HHcy-induced placental dysfunction.MethodsWe prospectively investigated the associations between maternal HHcy, placental mtDNA copy number (mtDNAcn), and adverse pregnancy outcomes using data from the PEOH (Prenatal Environments and Offspring Health) birth cohort. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, human placental tissues, maternal HHcy rat placentae, and HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cells were utilized to assess HHcy-induced mitochondrial damage, PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, and downstream inflammatory signaling.ResultsEpidemiological analysis revealed that maternal HHcy was significantly associated with increased risks of preterm birth and low birth weight, with reduced placental mtDNAcn serving as a critical mediator. Mechanistically, HHcy exposure induced severe mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy across human placentae, maternal HHcy rat placentae, and HTR-8/SVneo cells. This unrestrained mitophagic flux precipitated the leakage of mtDNA into the cytosol, which directly engaged and activated the cGAS-STING pathway, leading to placental sterile inflammation and subsequent placental dysfunction via upregulating COX-2 and pro-inflammatory cytokines.ConclusionMaternal HHcy exposure contributes to adverse pregnancy outcomes by reducing placental mtDNAcn and triggering a cascade of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagic disruption, cytosolic mtDNA release, and subsequent cGAS-STING-driven sterile inflammation and placental dysfunction, underscoring the need for clinical Hcy monitoring and early intervention.Keywords: Homocysteine, Mitochondrial DNA copy number, Adverse pregnancy outcomes, Mitochondrial damage

Introduction

Elevated maternal homocysteine (Hcy) levels are increasingly linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) such as preeclampsia, miscarriage, preterm birth, and fetal growth restriction, although findings vary across studies [1,2], the diagnostic threshold for hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) in pregnancy remains debated [3], and the mechanisms underlying its association with these complications are highly complex [4]. Epidemiological studies have reported a 37.2% prevalence in the general population [5], with a rising trend observed in recent years. Furthermore, predominant dietary patterns featuring high consumption of methionine-rich animal products (meats, eggs, dairy) correlate with elevated plasma Hcy concentrations in the general population [6]. This dietary profile, coupled with pregnancy-associated increases in folate and vitamin B12 requirements, may affect HHcy status in pregnant women.

The placenta serves as the primary materno-fetal interface, with trophoblasts acting as the core functional cells governing placental development [7], nutrient transport, and fetal growth through functional integrity, which are highly susceptible to oxidative stress [8]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that HHcy can induce oxidative damage [9]. Recent studies further reveal that Hcy triggers calcium-mediated disruption of mitochondrial function and dynamics in endothelial cells and elicits osteoblast dysfunction through mitochondrial oxidative stress [10]. Mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNAcn) is an effective biomarker to evaluate the effects of cationic metals on mtDNA integrity and function [11]. Wang et al. [12] found that reduced placental mtDNAcn was significantly associated with an increased risk of preterm birth. However, whether HHcy impairs placental mtDNA integrity and trophoblast mitochondrial function to drive adverse pregnancy outcomes remains unclear.

If mitophagy is inadequate to eliminate damaged mitochondria or becomes hyperactivated, the impaired mitochondria will generate excessive mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) and release mtDNA into the cytoplasm, which is termed cytosolic mtDNA [13]. Notably, mitophagy degrades impaired mitochondria via the canonical PINK1/Parkin pathway [14]. Subsequently, excessive cytosolic mtDNA acts as an endogenous danger signal that activates innate immune pathways [15], among which the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway is a key regulatory axis [16]. Activation of this axis triggers TBK1-IRF3 signaling, leading to inflammatory cytokine secretion and amplification of inflammatory responses [17]. Additionally, activation of this axis in this context may also upregulate cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) expression [18], thereby exacerbating abnormal prostaglandin synthesis, placental ischemia, and a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. Such inflammatory imbalance represents a critical pathological mechanism underlying placental dysfunction [19]. However, whether HHcy directly participates in this regulatory cascade requires further experimental validation.

This study aims to investigate the associations between maternal HHcy exposure, placental mtDNAcn, and adverse pregnancy outcomes in the PEOH (Prenatal Environments and Offspring Health) birth cohort, as well as to clarify the underlying molecular mechanism by which HHcy impairs PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, thereby activating the mtDNA-cGAS-STING signaling pathway in placental trophoblasts.

Methods

See supplementary material online for the detailed methods section.

Patient collection

This study made use of the Prospective Birth Cohort Study on Prenatal Environments and Offspring Health (PEOH cohort), which was conducted in Guangzhou, China. A total of 4928 pregnant women were initially recruited in PEOH cohort. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the subjects of our study were shown in Fig. 1. In total, 2386 pregnant women who had undergone a cord blood Hcy test were included in the study. In this study, baseline characteristics and clinical information of pregnant women, along with the birth weight of newborns, were gathered. Moreover, at the time of delivery, 3 mL of umbilical cord blood was collected using EDTA anticoagulant tubes. The anticoagulant was then mixed with the blood sample by slowly turning it up and down repeatedly. At the same time, placental tissue samples were collected during labour using standardised procedures. The samples were stored in an ultra-low temperature refrigerator at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. HHcy was defined as plasma Hcy levels above 15 μmol/L. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangdong Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention and registered with the China Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR-ROC-17013496). Each participant was provided with a comprehensive overview of the study and signed an informed consent form.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study subjects.

Animal study and treatment

Healthy adult Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (SPF grade, approximately 8 weeks old) were used in this study. Following one week of acclimatization, the rats were paired at a female-to-male ratio of 2:1 for overnight cohabitation. Subsequently, female rats were subjected to daily morning examinations for the detection of a vaginal plug and the presence of spermatozoa in a vaginal smear. The identification of a vaginal plug or a sperm-positive smear confirmed successful mating and that day was designated as gestational day 0 (GD 0). Pregnancy was further validated by monitoring the progressive increase in maternal body weight. Pregnant rats were randomly assigned to two experimental groups (n = 12 per group) using a random number table. The control group received a standard basal diet and regular drinking water from one week prior to mating until delivery. A single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of an equal volume of physiological saline was administered three days before mating as a vehicle control. The HHcy group was supplemented with 2% (w/v) l-methionine (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat. No. M9625) in their drinking water from one week prior to mating until delivery to induce hyperhomocysteinemia; all other housing conditions and saline treatments were identical to the control group. Maternal clinical signs and activity were monitored daily. Fasting blood glucose and necessary hematological/biochemical parameters were measured during mid-gestation (GD 7-10) and late-gestation (GD 17-19). At the time of delivery, pregnancy outcomes, including gestational length, litter size, birth weight, and survival rate, were comprehensively evaluated and recorded for each group. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2023-12-10-01).

Cell culture

HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA, Cat. No. C11995500BT) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Everygreen, Hangzhou, China, Cat. No. 13011-8611) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA, Cat. No. 15140122) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. HTR-8/SVneo cells were treated with ADU-S100 (1 μM, InvivoGen Cat.No. tlrl-nacda2r-01), H-151 (0.5 μM, InvivoGen, Cat.No. inh-h151), MitoQ (1 μM, MedChemExpress, Cat. No. HY-100116A) or Hcy (100 μM, MedChemExpress, Cat. No. HY-W010347).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD, and the differences between two groups were compared by t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The composition of categorical variables was presented as frequencies and proportions, and the Chi-Squared test or Fisher's exact test was used to analyse differences between groups. Logistic regression analysis was employed to investigate the association between maternal Hcy levels and the occurrence of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Linear regression was used to analyse the association between maternal Hcy levels and placental mtDNAcn. Mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether the relationship between Hcy and adverse pregnancy outcomes was mediated by mtDNAcn. Image J was used to analyse the results of immunofluorescence, JC-1 assay, MitoSOX and Western blot. All graphs were generated using R software (version 4.4.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2). Two-sided tests were used for all tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Association between maternal HHcy exposure and adverse pregnancy outcomes

We defined umbilical cord blood Hcy >15 μmol/L as HHcy exposure group, with overall prevalence of 13.83% in the study population (Table 1). Moreover, compared to the control group, the HHcy group had significantly more women aged ≥35 (P < 0.05), more with ≥3 pregnancies (P < 0.05), fewer experiencing cold/fever during pregnancy (P < 0.001), higher rates of spontaneous and induced abortion history (P = 0.006 and P = 0.036, respectively), and a higher prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus (P < 0.001). Logistic regression analyses was performed to explore the association between maternal HHcy exposure and adverse pregnancy outcomes (Fig. 2). The HHcy group showed significantly higher incidence rates of preterm birth (10.91% vs 4.43%, P < 0.05) and low birth weight (9.10% vs 4.23%, P < 0.05) compared to controls. In the crude model (Model 1), HHcy exposure was associated with significantly increased risks of both preterm birth (OR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.74-3.93) and low birth weight (OR = 2.26, 95% CI: 1.45-3.45). These associations remained statistically significant in the adjusted model (Model 2), with significant risks for preterm birth (OR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.85-4.38) and low birth weight (OR = 2.16, 95% CI: 1.35-3.36). However, no significant differences were found between groups for placental abruption (0.30% vs 0.43%) or fetal growth restriction (3.64% vs 2.72%), with all P-values > 0.05. Neither regression model revealed significant risk increases for these latter outcomes. These findings suggest that maternal HHcy exposure was significantly associated with increased risks of preterm birth and low birth weight, but not with placental abruption or fetal growth restriction.

Mediating effect of placental mtDNAcn on the association between maternal HHcy and adverse pregnancy outcomes

We evaluated the association between umbilical cord blood Hcy levels and placental mtDNAcn. Fig. 3A depicts their correlation, revealing that increased placental mtDNAcn is associated with decreased umbilical cord blood Hcy levels (R2 = 0.259, P < 0.001). Fig. 3B further quantifies and compares placental mtDNAcn between the control group and the HHcy group. Consistent with the correlation analysis, placental mtDNAcn in the HHcy group was significantly lower than that in the control group. Furthermore, we assessed the intracellular distribution of mtDNA by quantifying cytosolic mtDNA levels in the clinical placental samples. As shown in Fig. 3C, placental tissues from the HHcy group exhibited significantly elevated cytosolic mtDNA content compared with the control group. Building on these findings, we performed a mediation analysis to elucidate the biological mechanism linking Hcy to adverse pregnancy outcomes. The proportion of the total effect mediated by mtDNAcn was 0.248 (95% CI: 0.013-0.558), suggesting that mtDNAcn explained a considerable proportion of the link between Hcy and adverse pregnancy outcomes (STable 2). Average Causal Mediation Effects (ACME) were also statistically significant (Estimate: 0.025, 95% CI: 0.001-0.051, P = 0.036). Additionally, the direct effect of Hcy on adverse pregnancy outcomes was 0.074 (95% CI: 0.038-0.111) (Fig. 3D–E).

HHcy exposure induces mitochondrial damage in placental trophoblasts

Trophoblasts form the critical maternal-fetal interface responsible for nutrient and gas exchange, and disruption of their mitochondrial function can profoundly affect fetal development. To further clarify this relationship, we observed the effect of HHcy exposure on mitochondrial function in human placental trophoblast tissue, subsequently validating these findings in a maternal HHcy rat model in vivo and HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cells in vitro. HTR-8/SVneo cells were pretreated with different concentrations of Hcy (0, 10, 50,100, 200 and 500 μM) for 24 h, CCK-8 and LDH release assays showed that 100, 200, and 500 μM Hcy for 24 h significantly reduced cell viability and increased LDH activity, with an IC50 value of 242.2 μM (Fig. 4A–C). Additionally, treatment with 100 μM Hcy also decreased mtDNAcn and increased cytosolic DNA accumulation in HTR-8/SVneo cells, this concentration was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 4D). Further detection of 8-OHdG revealed that its levels were significantly elevated in the HHcy group compared with the control group (Fig. 4E). Because excessive mitochondrial ROS may impair mitochondrial respiration and function, we used immunofluorescence (JC-1 for Δψm and MitoSOX Red for mtROS) in human placental trophoblast tissue, maternal HHcy rat placentae, and HTR-8/SVneo cells. We observed that HHcy exposure significantly increased mtROS and caused marked mitochondrial dysfunction across all three models (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4F–K). These findings indicated that HHcy exposure induced mitochondrial oxidative stress, leading to mtDNA release into the cytoplasm.

HHcy exposure triggers excessive mitophagy in placental trophoblasts via the PINK1/Parkin pathway

Mitochondrial damage triggers excessive mitophagy and stimulates PINK1 kinase activity, which in turn activates Parkin-mediated mitophagy-a process triggered by mitochondrial membrane potential loss and aggregated proteins. Immunofluorescence analysis of HHcy-exposed placental tissue demonstrated a marked reduction in the mitochondrial marker TOM20 (green) concomitant with increased LC3B (red) signal (Fig. 5A), indicative of augmented autophagosome formation around mitochondria. This observation was corroborated by elevated LC3B mRNA expression and LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio, accompanied by decreased p62 and TOM20 protein abundance (Fig. 5J–P). Moreover, HHcy-exposed placentae displayed pronounced upregulation of PINK1 and Parkin protein by immunofluorescence analysis, with PINK1/Parkin staining strongly colocalizing with sites of TOM20 loss (Fig. 5D–G), and concordant increases in PINK1 and Parkin mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 5M–P). Parallel in vivo studies in a maternal rat model of HHcy and in vitro studies in HTR-8/SVneo cells reproduced these findings (Fig. 5B, C, E, F, H, I, K, L, N, O, Q, R), demonstrating that HHcy exposure preferentially activates the mitophagy machinery through the PINK1-Parkin pathway. Importantly, in HTR-8/SVneo cells, co-treatment with the mitochondrial protectant MitoQ effectively suppressed HHcy-induced changes, leading to a marked reduction in LC3B-II conversion and Parkin expression at both the protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 6A and B). Furthermore, MitoQ significantly attenuated HHcy-triggered mitochondrial DNA leakage into the cytosol (Fig. 6C). To genetically validate these findings, we performed siRNA-mediated silencing of PINK1 and Parkin. Consistently, the knockdown of these key mitophagy mediators significantly abolished HHcy-induced excessive mitophagy and markedly reduced the accumulation of cytosolic mtDNA (Fig. S1A and C). These genetic results further corroborate that HHcy-induced mtDNA leakage is specifically mediated through the PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagic pathway. To further clarify whether HHcy-induced mitophagy represents an activated flux or a blockade of lysosomal degradation, we performed a mitophagic flux analysis in HTR-8/SVneo cells using the lysosomal inhibitor Chloroquine (CQ). Co-staining with MitoTracker Green and LysoTracker Red revealed that HHcy increased the colocalization between mitochondria and lysosomes, which was quantified by the Pearson's correlation coefficient (Fig. 6E). Notably, the addition of CQ led to a further significant accumulation of these colocalized signals. This accumulation effect demonstrates that HHcy triggers an active and excessive mitophagic flux rather than merely impairing the degradative pathway. We next investigated the specific mechanism underlying mitochondrial DNA release, focusing on the role of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). The Calcein-AM/CoCl2 quenching assay showed that HHcy exposure significantly promoted mPTP opening, whereas this effect was effectively reversed by the mPTP inhibitor Cyclosporin A (CsA) (Fig. 6F). Consistently, JC-1 staining showed that the HHcy-induced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential was significantly restored upon CsA intervention (Fig. 6G). Most importantly, qPCR analysis revealed that the leakage of mtDNA into the cytosol was markedly suppressed by CsA (Fig. 6D). These results collectively indicate that HHcy triggers mitochondrial damage and subsequent cytosolic mtDNA accumulation through the excessive opening of the mPTP.

HHcy exposure leads to cytoplasmic DNA accumulation and cGAS-STING pathway activation in placental inflammatory responses

Mitochondrial dysfunction triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which activate the innate immune system. Given the central role of the cGAS-STING pathway in inflammatory pathogenesis, we examined the expression of its key components. Crucially, we validated the activation of this pathway across human placental tissues and a maternal HHcy rat model. In both in vivo models, Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in the protein levels of cGAS, p-STING, p-TBK1, p-IRF3, and COX-2 (Fig. 7A and B). To further validate this mechanism in vitro, we treated HTR-8/SVneo cells with HHcy and compared them with control cells, finding that HHcy treatment enhanced activation of the STING signaling pathway, as evidenced by increased protein levels of cGAS, p-STING, p-TBK1 and p-IRF3 (Fig. 7C). Activation of this signaling pathway further induced the upregulation of COX-2 at the protein level, thereby amplifying the placental inflammatory response. To investigate pathway involvement, pharmacological modulators ADU S100 (STING activator) and H-151 (STING inhibitor) were applied. Our results demonstrate that the cGAS-STING pathway is highly responsive to both pharmacological modulation and Hcy exposure. STING activation with ADU S100 robustly upregulated core pathway genes including STING, TBK1, and IRF3, as well as the downstream inflammatory effector COX-2, an effect synergistically enhanced by Hcy. Conversely, STING inhibition with H-151 strongly suppressed pathway gene expression, yet this inhibitory effect was partially reversed by Hcy (Fig. 7D). To provide supporting evidence, we performed genetic validation by knocking down cGAS or STING using siRNA. Our results showed that silencing these core components effectively blocked the transcriptional activation of downstream markers (Fig. S1B) and rescued the functional impairments induced by HHcy. Specifically, wound healing assays revealed that STING silencing restored cell migration (Fig. 7E), while Western blot analysis confirmed the recovery of functional proteins including MMP9 and GLUT1 (Fig. 7F). Placental sterile inflammation could alter trophoblast secretion of inflammatory mediators and hormones, synergistically contributing to adverse pregnancy outcomes, particularly preterm delivery. ELISA analysis showed HHcy treatment elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1 in HTR-8/SVneo cells, whereas this secretory response was significantly attenuated in the cGAS or STING knockdown groups (Fig. 7G–J, Fig. S1D), without disrupting hormonal regulation of estrogen and progesterone levels (Fig. 7K–L). Collectively, these findings suggest that HHcy exposure activates cGAS-STING signaling to drive sterile inflammation without altering hormones, which may increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes.

Discussion

Our study of the PEOH cohort found a 13.83% prevalence of HHcy among pregnant women and identified maternal HHcy as an independent risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes, specifically for preterm birth and low birth weight, highlighting elevated Hcy as a substantial metabolic challenge during pregnancy. More importantly, we found that the association between maternal HHcy and adverse pregnancy outcomes is mediated by a significant reduction in placental mtDNAcn. As a key quantitative indicator of mitochondrial functional integrity, placental mtDNAcn is closely tied to placental function [20], given that mitochondrial abnormality is a major driver of placental dysfunction. Recent research has observed a 14.8% reduction in preterm birth risk for each 0.1-unit increase in preconception mtDNAcn in maternal peripheral blood [12]. Collectively, our results align with and extend prior evidence regarding the role of HHcy-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in placental pathophysiology.

Placental trophoblast mitochondria serve as the central energy hub that fuels critical cellular processes [21], and a decrease in mtDNAcn along with functional impairment may trigger a cascade of pathological events, disrupting placental homeostasis and ultimately contributing to adverse pregnancy outcomes [22]. Across human placental tissues, maternal HHcy rat placentae, and HTR-8/SVneo cells, we found that HHcy exposure induces trophoblast mitochondrial oxidative stress and dysfunction, causing reduced mtDNAcn and cytosolic mtDNA accumulation. This mitochondrial injury activates the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway, suggesting HHcy-induced mitochondrial oxidative damage acts as the upstream trigger for mitophagy activation. Although mitophagy initially serves a protective role by eliminating damaged mitochondria, its sustained hyperactivation paradoxically exacerbates trophoblast injury, thereby contributing to placental dysfunction [15]. Zhang et al. [23]observed a similar effect that Hcy can repress mtDNA transcription, causing mitochondrial dysfunction in primary neurons and PC12 cells. Another study also indicated that HHcy disrupts placental homeostasis by impairing lysosomal and autophagic function in C57BL/6J mice, as evidenced by the atypical accumulation of lysosomes and autophagosomes in HHcy-exposed placentae compared with controls [24].

Studies demonstrate that mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired clearance mechanisms lead to the accumulation of cytosolic mtDNA, a potent trigger for inflammatory responses [25,26]. Upon sensing cytosolic mtDNA, cGAS activates the STING pathway, driving robust interferon (IFN) production and amplifying local inflammation. While the placental cGAS-STING pathway is classically recognized as a critical antiviral defense mechanism against DNA viruses and retroviruses [27], emerging evidence highlights its broader role in mediating sterile inflammation during metabolic pregnancy complications. For instance, Zhou et al. [28] reported that chemerin is upregulated in gestational diabetes mellitus placenta and inhibits the cGAS-STING signaling pathway in a time-dependent manner, which may serve as a potential protective mechanism. Hcy is a well-documented pro-inflammatory factor known to promote cytokine expression across diverse cell types [29,30]. Consistent with the established role of the cGAS-STING pathway in placental antiviral defense, HHcy aberrantly activates this pathway in trophoblasts, triggering a pro-inflammatory cytokine response without disrupting steroidogenesis. Importantly, our functional assays demonstrated that HHcy-induced STING activation is a direct driver of trophoblast cellular failure, as evidenced by significantly impaired migratory capacity and reduced expression of key functional markers including MMP9 and GLUT1, all of which were successfully rescued by STING silencing. Although decidual immune cells are traditionally considered potent responders in the cGAS-STING axis, our consistent findings in purified HTR-8/SVneo cells and the spatial distribution of activation markers within the trophoblast layers suggest that trophoblasts represent a significant and functional cellular site of HHcy-induced inflammation. This cell-autonomous response may play a substantial role in the impairment of critical placental functions such as migration and nutrient transport, though the relative contributions of other cell types remain an important area for further investigation. Furthermore, beyond the cGAS-STING axis, mtDNA is also a well-recognized ligand for Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), which can trigger pro-inflammatory responses through the MyD88-dependent signaling pathway. Given the significant cytosolic mtDNA accumulation and potential extracellular release observed in our study, it is plausible that HHcy might also impact TLR9 signaling to amplify placental sterile inflammation. Although our genetic rescue experiments highlight the cGAS-STING pathway as a critical mediator of trophoblast injury, the potential contribution of TLR9-mediated responses and the possibility of synergistic interplay between these two pathways in sensing the released mtDNA deserve further investigation in the context of hyperhomocysteinemia.

This study has several limitations. First, our single-center study in Guangzhou may be subject to selection bias, as the local demographic profile may not fully represent the broader national population. Second, the measurement of Hcy was conducted using umbilical cord blood rather than maternal blood. Although cord blood Hcy is fetal in origin, it is highly correlated with maternal Hcy levels due to efficient placental transfer, making it a reliable surrogate for maternal exposure during late pregnancy. While cord blood Hcy directly reflects the metabolic environment of the fetal-placental unit at birth, maternal Hcy monitoring throughout pregnancy might provide additional insights into early-gestation exposures. Third, the lack of data on maternal folate/B12 levels and genetic polymorphisms may leave some residual confounding. However, maternal HHcy serves as a robust integrated biomarker that reflects the combined impact of both genetic and nutritional factors. Furthermore, including these upstream determinants alongside Hcy in the multivariate models could potentially introduce multicollinearity and over-adjustment bias, as Hcy acts as the proximal metabolic stressor directly linked to placental injury. Fourth, we acknowledge that this study does not definitively establish trophoblasts as the sole source of the observed placental redox and inflammatory changes. The placenta is a complex organ containing diverse cell populations, including endothelial, stromal, and various immune cells, which are also known to be potent responders to oxidative stress. While our experiments in purified HTR-8/SVneo cells demonstrate that trophoblasts can independently mount a cGAS-STING response that leads to functional failure, our bulk placental tissue analysis represents a collective response of multiple cell types. Therefore, the precise degree to which other cellular compartments contribute to the overall HHcy-induced redox phenotype remains to be fully elucidated. Finally, although we have now integrated an in vivo HHcy pregnancy rat model to validate the PINK1/Parkin-cGAS-STING axis, inter-species differences in placental structure between rodents and humans must be acknowledged. Future meta-analyses and comprehensive systematic investigations are warranted to further confirm these mechanistic pathways and evaluate the consistency of our findings across diverse populations.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that maternal HHcy exposure is associated with reduced placental mtDNAcn, which mediates the link between HHcy and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Mechanistically, HHcy-induced impairment of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy in trophoblasts causes mtDNA release from damaged mitochondria, which activates the cGAS-STING cascade and provokes pro-inflammatory responses. These findings highlight the importance of routine maternal Hcy monitoring and early intervention during pregnancy to mitigate HHcy-related placental dysfunction and adverse pregnancy outcomes.
