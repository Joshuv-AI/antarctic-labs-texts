Clinical characteristics, antithrombotic strategies, and outcomes in patients with atrial fibrillation and coronary heart disease undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention: a multicenter real-world study in Chongqing, China
Authors: Naiyue Bao, Jiacan Wu, Zhuo Li, Chengcheng Li, Hua Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) with coronary artery disease (CAD) requiring percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) — including both acute and chronic presentations — is common, yet the optimal antithrombotic regimen remains unclear. This study aimed to characterize clinical features, current antithrombotic therapy, and discharge prognosis in this population.MethodsData were collected from three independent annual cohorts of patients with atrial fibrillation and coronary heart disease undergoing PCI (2013, 2018, and 2023). First, cross-sectional comparisons across the three years were performed to examine temporal trends in clinical characteristics and discharge antithrombotic therapy. Predictors of discharge antithrombotic therapy were then analyzed in the 2023 cohort. Second, the 2023 cohort was prospectively followed for a median of 17 months (interquartile range 9–24 months; range 0.5–32 months) to evaluate clinical outcomes according to different antithrombotic regimens and to identify independent risk factors.ResultsOf the 1,062 participants, 479 underwent longitudinal follow-up (median 17 months, IQR 9–24; range 0.5–32). Independent predictors of intensified antithrombotic therapy included recent PCI, proton pump inhibitor use, and statin use; independent predictors of antiplatelet therapy included recent PCI, age, liver dysfunction, left atrial diameter, proton pump inhibitor use, heart failure, and non-paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. During follow-up, anticoagulant therapy showed a trend toward higher MACCEs risk compared with intensified antithrombotic therapy (HR 1.47, 95% CI 0.97–2.24, P=0.073) and antiplatelet therapy (HR 1.33, 95% CI 0.79–2.22, P=0.280), with no significant difference between the latter two (HR 1.15, 95% CI 0.71–1.88, P=0.567). Multivariate Cox regression identified liver dysfunction, diabetes, and HAS-BLED score as independently associated with MACCEs, while higher hemoglobin and statin use were associated with lower risk.ConclusionsOver the years, more AF with CHD patients after PCI received post-discharge antithrombotic therapy. Liver dysfunction, diabetes, and a high HAS‑BLED score were independently associated with MACCEs, while statin use was associated with lower risks of all‑cause death, MI, and ischemia‑driven revascularization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05957-9.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Clinical features, Chronic coronary syndrome, Antithrombotic therapy, Prognosis

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation is the most common arrhythmia worldwide, and coronary artery disease (CAD) is the most common cardiovascular disease globally [1]. The two are closely related, often coexist [2], and may require PCI to improve angina symptoms or reduce the risk of subsequent myocardial infarction and death [3]. Anticoagulant therapy can significantly improve the prognosis of patients with atrial fibrillation [4]. Guidelines recommend dual antiplatelet therapy after coronary stent implantation [5]. However, when both conditions coexist, anticoagulation therapy becomes particularly challenging: patients require both anticoagulant therapy to prevent stroke and antiplatelet therapy to prevent stent thrombosis, which inevitably increases the risk of bleeding [6]. Over the years, the antithrombotic regimen for patients with atrial fibrillation combined with coronary heart disease undergoing PCI has never stopped evolving. In 2010, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) recommended that patients with atrial fibrillation undergoing PCI should complete triple therapy, including VKA, aspirin, and clopidogrel [7]. The What is the Optimal antiplatelet and anticoagulant therapy in patients with oral anticoagulation and coronary StenTing (WOEST) study first reported that dual antithrombotic therapy, including VKA and clopidogrel, can significantly reduce myocardial infarction and bleeding events in atrial fibrillation patients after PCI [8]. Since then, there has been a myriad of opinions regarding antithrombotic regimens for atrial fibrillation patients after PCI, with an overall tendency to shorten the duration of triple therapy, while dual therapy, especially dual therapy containing novel oral anticoagulants, has gained great prominence [9–11]. A previous study in South Korea showed that in patients with atrial fibrillation receiving drug-eluting stent implantation, long-term use of triple antithrombotic therapy significantly increased the risk of bleeding without a significant reduction in MACCEs [12]. However, data on the comprehensive evaluation of antithrombotic strategies in Asian atrial fibrillation patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention remain limited. This study aims to dynamically collect baseline data and antithrombotic regimens of patients with atrial fibrillation and coronary heart disease undergoing PCI at different time points within the same region, summarize and evaluate the clinical characteristics of these patients. In addition, the prognosis of patients receiving different antithrombotic regimens will be followed to explore factors affecting these regimens and their impact on post-PCI outcomes in atrial fibrillation patients. These findings are expected to provide real-world evidence to support more standardized post-PCI management of atrial fibrillation patients.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study adopts a multicenter design, integrating cross-sectional and prospective study methods. The study protocol is as follows:

In five-year cycles, based on uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria, inpatient records were extracted for patients diagnosed with atrial fibrillation combined with coronary heart disease who had completed PCI in 2013, 2018, and 2023.

Inverse Probability Weighting (IPTW) was first used to balance baseline characteristics between patients receiving different antithrombotic therapies to enhance intergroup comparability. Subsequently, patients using different discharge antithrombotic regimens were followed longitudinally for a median of 17 months (interquartile range 9–24 months; range 0.5–32 months) to evaluate the efficacy and safety of each antithrombotic regimen. Finally, independent risk factors affecting patient prognosis were further investigated. Follow-up continued until the occurrence of the first prespecified endpoint event or the end of the study (Fig. 1). Relevant disease diagnoses were determined using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplementary Table 1). Inclusion criteria: (1) hospitalized during the periods of 2013, 2018, or 2023; (2) adult patients with atrial fibrillation and coronary artery disease (including both acute coronary syndrome and chronic coronary syndrome) who underwent PCI and were eligible. Exclusion criteria: (1) death during hospitalization or unstable vital signs; (2) unable to cooperate to complete this study for various reasons; (3) not prescribed antithrombotic therapy at discharge (Fig. 1). Antithrombotic therapy

Patients were categorized according to their discharge prescription only, representing the initial antithrombotic strategy intended by the clinician [13]. Reviewing discharge records for antithrombotic treatment plans, they are generally divided into anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents. Among them, oral anticoagulants (OAC) alone are divided into warfarin (a vitamin K antagonist) and NOACs (non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants). OAC combined with a single antiplatelet therapy is classified as dual antithrombotic therapy (DAT), while OAC combined with two antiplatelet therapies is classified as triple antithrombotic therapy (TAT). Both DAT and TAT are considered intensified antithrombotic therapies. Two antiplatelet drugs constitute dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), while a single antiplatelet drug constitutes single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT). In subsequent survival analyses, DAPT and SAPT are combined as antiplatelet therapy.

Clinical endpoints and follow-up

Major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) were defined as a composite endpoint of all-cause death, stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) or systemic embolism (SE), myocardial infarction (MI), or ischemia-driven revascularization (IDR) [14]. Myocardial infarction(MI) was defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction [15]. Ischemic stroke(IS) was diagnosed as an acute episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal cerebral, spinal, or retinal infarction, documented by imaging (CT or MRI). Transient ischemic attack (TIA) was defined as a focal cerebral ischemic event with symptoms lasting <24 hours. Systemic embolism (SE) was defined as new-onset acute limb ischemia or objective evidence of sudden loss of blood flow to a limb or organ [16]. Ischemia-driven coronary revascularization (IDR) included any coronary revascularization performed during follow‑up in the setting of myocardial infarction, as well as cases with worsening symptoms and evidence of myocardial ischemia. Bleeding events were recorded according to the Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) criteria [17]. All clinical outcomes were adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee. In addition, this study explored the outcomes of cardiogenic and non-cardiogenic rehospitalization to further investigate the clinical management of patients with atrial fibrillation and coronary artery disease after PCI.

Data collection

The baseline data of participants included: gender, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, body mass index (BMI), type of atrial fibrillation, type of coronary heart disease, medical history (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart failure, liver dysfunction, history of stroke, history of bleeding), medication history (types and doses of proton pump inhibitors and statins), the most recent time of completed PCI (within one year or more than one year), CHA2DS2-VASc score, HAS-BLED score, cardiac ultrasound at admission (left ventricular ejection fraction), and laboratory indicators (hemoglobin concentration, liver transaminases, creatinine, and INR, etc.).

Statistical analyses

We assumed the missing data were missing at random and performed multiple imputation via chained equations (using the mice package in R version 4.5.2). The random seed was set to 123, and 5 imputed datasets were generated, each undergoing 10 iterations. Model convergence was verified by plotting trace plots of the imputed variables over the iterations. The original missing status of the imputed variables is shown in Supplementary Table 2. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA), the Kruskal-Wallis H test, the Mann-Whitney U test, or the t-test; categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. To identify factors associated with the choice of anticoagulation strategies at discharge, we employed an exploratory variable selection method. First, we performed univariate logistic regression analyses on each candidate variable. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis, as well as those clinically considered likely to influence anticoagulant use, were included in a LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression model to address multicollinearity and avoid overfitting. The optimal penalty parameter λ was selected using 10-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients in the LASSO model were further incorporated into a conventional multivariate logistic regression model to calculate odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals. It should be noted that this sequential screening method constitutes an exploratory analysis and may be subject to selection bias; therefore, the results in Table 2 should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory conclusions. For each comparison group, we used a multivariate logistic regression model to estimate the propensity score for receiving a specific antithrombotic therapy. The selection of covariates was based on two sources: (1) variables identified as independent predictors in the aforementioned multivariate logistic regression models; and (2) clinically important factors consistently recognized in previous clinical practice as being associated with the choice of antithrombotic therapy in patients with atrial fibrillation and PCI, regardless of their statistical significance in this exploratory analysis. The selected covariates included: sex, age, alcohol consumption, smoking status, body weight, history of stroke, history of bleeding, type of coronary artery disease, type of atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart failure, abnormal liver function, proton pump inhibitor use, statin use, serum creatinine level, International Normalized Ratio (INR), hemoglobin concentration, HAS-BLED score, CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, and left ventricular ejection fraction. Stabilized weighting was used for calculations (treatment group: marginal probability/propensity score; control group: marginal probability/(1-propensity score)). This method was chosen to balance model parsimony with clinical relevance. The balance of covariates before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD); an SMD < 0.1 indicated good balance. For covariates that remained unbalanced after weighting, the researchers further incorporated them into a weighted Cox proportional hazards model and used a double robust estimation method to evaluate the associations of different antithrombotic treatment strategies on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACCEs) and other outcome measures (see Supplementary Figure 1). We analyzed the weighted cohort data using Kaplan-Meier survival curves to assess the association between different antithrombotic treatment regimens and MACCEs and bleeding events, and performed between-group comparisons using the log-rank test. Eight variables that remained unbalanced after weighting (including diabetes, abnormal liver function, history of stroke, type of coronary heart disease, type of atrial fibrillation, hemoglobin concentration, HAS-BLED score, and history of statin use) were included in a doubly robust Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent predictors of each endpoint. Study results are presented as adjusted hazard ratios (AHR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Finally, two sensitivity analyses were conducted: first, a sensitivity analysis was performed treating the hospital as a confounding factor; second, after removing samples with missing values prior to multiple imputation, the doubly robust Cox proportional hazards model was rerun. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.2. Given the observational nature of this study, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, all reported associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal.

Results

This study included a total of 1,062 participants (2013: 114; 2018: 469; 2023: 479), with a median follow-up time of 17 months. In 2023, among the total sample of 519 people, 30 were unable to complete the follow-up, and the outcome data of 479 patients were ultimately included.

Clinical characteristics and antithrombotic status of participants in different years

The baseline characteristics of patients in 2013, 2018, and 2023 are shown in Table 1. The average age was approximately 75 years, with no significant difference across years (P = 0.177). The gender distribution remained stable over time (P = 0.725). Over the years, the proportion of patients with hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure all decreased significantly (P < 0.001). The CHA₂DS₂‑VASc score showed an overall downward trend (P < 0.001), while the HAS‑BLED score showed a significant upward trend (P < 0.001). The distribution of atrial fibrillation types also changed significantly (P < 0.001). Paroxysmal atrial fibrillation became the main type, increasing from 38.6% in 2013 to 66.4% in 2023, while the proportion of permanent atrial fibrillation decreased significantly. Regarding coronary artery disease, the number of diagnosed coronary heart disease patients increased year by year, but the proportion experiencing myocardial infarction decreased from 71.9% to 23.6% (P < 0.001). Most notably, the discharge antithrombotic strategies have changed significantly over the past decade (P < 0.001). The use of oral anticoagulant monotherapy increased from 12.3% in 2013 to 40.5% in 2023. Both dual and triple antithrombotic therapies showed an increasing trend, especially dual antithrombotic therapy. In contrast, standalone antiplatelet therapy has decreased significantly over the past ten years.

Baseline characteristics according to different years

Over the past decade, the antithrombotic treatment strategies for patients with atrial fibrillation combined with coronary heart disease undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention have undergone significant evolution. In 2013, vitamin K antagonists (mainly warfarin) dominated anticoagulant therapy, and the use of novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs) was very limited. By 2018, NOACs had gradually become the predominant option, represented by rivaroxaban, dabigatran, and edoxaban. It is worth noting that Chinese patent medicines were also frequently used in 2018, which may reflect regional medication habits at the time. By 2023, the use of NOACs had further expanded, and dosing had become more varied, reflecting clinicians' tendency toward individualized antithrombotic therapy. At the same time, discharge antithrombotic strategies have also undergone major changes. In 2013, antiplatelet therapy was absolutely mainstream, applied in nearly 80% of patients. By 2018, dual and triple antithrombotic therapy increased significantly, reaching peak values of 29.9% and 20.5%, respectively. However, by 2023, triple therapy had decreased to 9.2%, while oral anticoagulant monotherapy became the most common strategy, accounting for 40.5%. This trend is highly consistent with contemporary guidelines emphasizing a balance between ischemic protection and bleeding risk, and preferring long-term use of lower-intensity regimens. These findings highlight the rapid spread of evidence-based concepts in Chinese clinical practice, which in recent years has clearly shifted toward NOAC-based antithrombotic strategies and a preference for dual therapy over triple therapy (Fig. 2).

Independent predictors of different antithrombotic regimens at discharge

To identify factors independently associated with the choice of antithrombotic strategy, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted using oral anticoagulant monotherapy as the reference group (Table 2).

Comparison of intensified antithrombotic therapy with oral anticoagulant monotherapy

Receiving PCI within one year was the strongest predictor of receiving intensified antithrombotic therapy rather than oral anticoagulant monotherapy (odds ratio [OR] = 71.69; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 32.57–157.80; P < 0.001). Concurrent use of proton pump inhibitors (OR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.33–4.53, P = 0.004) and statins (OR = 4.36, 95% CI: 1.02–18.53, P = 0.046) was also significantly associated with a higher likelihood of receiving intensified antithrombotic therapy.

Comparison of antiplatelet therapy with oral anticoagulant monotherapy

Similarly, receiving PCI within one year was the strongest predictor for receiving antiplatelet therapy (OR = 41.86, 95% CI: 17.96–97.54, P < 0.001). Increasing age (per year, OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.03–1.11, P = 0.001) and the presence of liver dysfunction (OR = 6.98, 95% CI: 1.82–26.68, P = 0.005) were independently associated with an increased likelihood of receiving antiplatelet therapy. PPI use also remained a significant predictor (OR = 2.43, 95% CI: 1.27–4.65, P = 0.007). In contrast, several factors were associated with a decreased likelihood of receiving antiplatelet therapy. For example: enlarged left atrial diameter (per millimeter, OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.89–0.98, P = 0.007), heart failure (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.23–0.90, P = 0.025), and persistent atrial fibrillation (OR = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.19–0.91, P = 0.027) (Table2).

Clinical outcomes and independent risk factors associated with different antithrombotic regimens

The follow-up results in 2023 are shown in Fig. 3. First, in 2023, the 479 participants who received different antithrombotic regimens (anticoagulation alone, intensified antithrombotic therapy, and antiplatelet therapy) were processed using inverse probability weighting based on baseline characteristics, as detailed in Table 3 (Detailed baseline characteristics before weighting are shown in Supplementary Table 3). Subsequently, a double robust Cox analysis was conducted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of different antithrombotic regimens, namely: Solo anticoagulation therapy (n = 188), intensified antithrombotic therapy (dual antithrombotic, triple antithrombotic) (n = 171), and antiplatelet therapy (dual antiplatelet, single antiplatelet) (n = 111). The weighted log-rank test is used for pairwise comparisons. Figure 4 shows the cumulative event rates of MACCEs, all-cause mortality, TE (IS+SE), CE (MI+IDR), bleeding, heart-related readmission, and non-heart-related readmission during the 24-month follow-up period in different antithrombotic groups. Between anticoagulant therapy and intensified antithrombotic therapy, there was no significant difference in MACCEs event-free survival among the groups (P = 0.074). The curve showed a trend of increased event incidence in the anticoagulant group, although it did not reach statistical significance. Similarly, the risk of all-cause mortality was comparable across the treatment groups. TE event-free survival showed a significant difference. Compared with single anticoagulant therapy, patients receiving intensified antithrombotic therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk of TE (P = 0.007). The incidence of CE was significantly lower with intensified antithrombotic therapy compared with anticoagulant therapy alone (P = 0.010). Antiplatelet therapy also showed a significantly reduced risk of CE (P = 0.041). There was a marginally significant difference in bleeding-free survival between anticoagulant therapy and intensified antithrombotic therapy (weighted log-rank test P = 0.059); however, compared with antiplatelet therapy, intensified antithrombotic therapy was associated with a significantly increased risk of bleeding (P = 0.012). There were no significant differences in heart-related or non-heart-related rehospitalizations among the groups.

Prognosis of atrial fibrillation patients with coronary heart disease undergoing PCI treatment

A double robust Cox regression model was used to analyze the independent influencing factors for each outcome, and the results are shown in Fig. 5. Liver dysfunction (HR=4.28, 95% CI 1.97–9.26, P<0.001), diabetes (HR=1.81, 95% CI 1.14–2.87, P=0.011), and HAS-BLED score (HR=1.23, 95% CI 1.01–1.50, P=0.038) were independent risk factors for MACCEs, while higher hemoglobin levels (HR=0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, P=0.005) and a history of statin use (HR=0.36, 95% CI 0.15–0.87, P=0.024) were protective factors. Liver dysfunction (HR=8.55, 95% CI 3.03–24.17, P<0.001) and HAS-BLED score (HR=1.52, 95% CI 1.16–1.99, P=0.002) were also significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality risk, while statin use (HR=0.25, 95% CI 0.08–0.78, P=0.017) was associated with reduced all-cause mortality risk. Intensive antithrombotic therapy significantly increased bleeding risk compared to antiplatelet therapy (HR=2.03, 95% CI 1.01–4.07, P=0.046). Anticoagulation alone significantly increased the risk of TE compared to intensive antithrombotic therapy (HR=2.13, 95% CI 1.10–4.15, P=0.026); non-paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (HR=0.33, 95% CI 0.12–0.93, P=0.036) and higher hemoglobin levels (HR=0.96, 95% CI 0.97–1.00, P=0.029) were associated with a lower TE risk. Anticoagulation alone significantly reduced the risk of CE compared to intensive antithrombotic therapy (HR=0.25, 95% CI 0.07–0.90, P=0.033) and antiplatelet therapy (HR=0.18, 95% CI 0.04–0.77, P=0.021); a history of statin use (HR=0.13, 95% CI 0.03–0.54, P=0.005) was a protective factor, while diabetes (HR=4.07, 95% CI 1.03–16.01, P=0.045) significantly increased the risk of CE. Permanent atrial fibrillation was an independent risk factor for non-cardiogenic rehospitalization (HR=5.50, 95% CI 1.25–24.32, P=0.025).

Subgroup analysis

To assess the consistency of the efficacy of different antithrombotic strategies across subgroups, we conducted subgroup analyses based on pre-specified clinical characteristics, including age (<60 years vs. ≥60 years), sex (male vs. female), PCI timing (within 1 year vs. more than 1 year), anemia (yes vs. no), diabetes (yes vs. no), history of stroke (yes vs. no), history of proton pump inhibitor use (yes vs. no), HAS-BLED score (≥3 vs. <3), C score (high risk: male ≥2 points, female ≥3 points vs. low risk: male <2 points, female <3 points), type of atrial fibrillation (paroxysmal vs. non-paroxysmal), and type of coronary heart disease (confirmed vs. myocardial infarction). Using anticoagulant therapy alone as the reference, the effects of intensified antithrombotic therapy and antiplatelet therapy were separately evaluated in each subgroup using a Cox regression model, and interaction P values were calculated. Subgroup analyses showed that the effects of different antithrombotic strategies varied in certain subgroups. Specifically, the association of intensified antithrombotic therapy with a lower risk of thromboembolism was significantly stronger in men than in women (interaction P = 0.04); whereas regarding the increased risk of clinical events, the effect of intensified antithrombotic therapy compared to antiplatelet therapy was more pronounced in patients aged ≥60 years (interaction P = 0.03). For bleeding events, the trend of increased bleeding risk with intensified antithrombotic therapy was more evident in patients aged ≥60 years (interaction P = 0.05) and in men (interaction P = 0.09), even though the interaction did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, in analyses of MACCE and all-cause mortality, no significant interactions were observed among subgroups (all interaction P > 0.05). Overall, except for potential modifying effects of sex and age on certain outcomes, the main study results were relatively robust across different subgroups, with no significant interactions observed with other clinical characteristics (Supplementary Table 4−8).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted two sensitivity analyses, including stratified analysis by adding the hospital variable and excluding participants with missing covariate data, and the results were consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Tables 9−10).

Discussion

Our research dynamics analyzed data collected regularly from atrial fibrillation patients with coronary heart disease who underwent PCI between 2013 and 2023, examining the changes and progress in antithrombotic strategies for these comorbid patients, the possible factors influencing clinicians' choice of antithrombotic strategies, as well as the clinical outcomes and independent risk factors of each antithrombotic regimen.

Trends in antithrombotic strategies over the past decade

We have always firmly believed that anticoagulant therapy for atrial fibrillation patients can significantly reduce the occurrence of adverse events. Moreover, the importance of antiplatelet therapy for patients with coronary heart disease after PCI is also beyond doubt [18]. However, when encountering patients with atrial fibrillation combined with coronary artery disease after PCI in clinical practice, the antithrombotic strategy is often complex and variable, and is by no means simply additive [19]. Japanese studies show that from 2014 to 2018, the use of oral anticoagulants in Japan increased from 15% to 100%, and the use of TAT increased from 10% to over 75%; however, another study also from Japan shows that from 2014 to 2016, Japanese doctors were more inclined to choose direct oral anticoagulants, with the proportion increasing from 15% to 35%, while the proportion choosing warfarin decreased from 22% to 8%, TAT usage decreased from 15% to 5%, and the proportion of OAC monotherapy or SAPT increased [20, 21]. In our study, we observed that from 2013 to 2023, the proportion of anticoagulation alone steadily increased, reaching an impressive 40.5% by 2023, with a significant rise in the use of novel oral anticoagulants, reflected in both the types and doses. In contrast, antiplatelet therapy (including DAPT and SAPT) has gradually declined in proportion over the past decade. The intensified antithrombotic strategies, including dual and triple antithrombotic therapy, showed a more complex pattern, with both reaching their peak proportions in 2018. However, the proportion of DAT remained essentially unchanged over the following five years, while TAT showed a significant decline during the five years after 2018, likely related to concerns about bleeding events.

Factors affecting antithrombotic therapy strategy

This study found, through multivariate logistic regression analysis, that percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 1 year is the strongest predictor of patients receiving intensified antithrombotic therapy. This result is clinically reasonable: patients who have recently undergone PCI face an acute risk of in-stent thrombosis, and clinicians often prefer to combine dual antiplatelet therapy with oral anticoagulants to reduce ischemic events. This trend is consistent with previous studies, especially after interventions for acute coronary syndrome or complex coronary lesions, where intensified antithrombotic strategies are considered standard management [23, 23]. As Castiello et al. noted in a recent review, for patients undergoing complex PCI, antithrombotic strategies should be tailored to each patient’s ischemic risk profile, taking into account factors such as left main lesions, bifurcation lesions, chronic total occlusions, and multiple stent placements. For patients with such comorbidities, the choice between triple and dual antiplatelet therapy requires careful balancing of thrombotic and bleeding risks, with a focus on limiting the duration of triple therapy to the shortest clinically acceptable period [24]. It is noteworthy that a history of proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use and statin use are also positively associated with the choice of intensified antithrombotic therapy. PPIs are commonly used in clinical practice with antiplatelet drugs to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, and the high usage rate in the intensified antithrombotic group may reflect clinicians' consideration of gastrointestinal protection when deciding on stronger antithrombotic regimens [25–27]. The high usage rate of statins in the intensified antithrombotic therapy group may relate to the patients’ history of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, suggesting that these patients have a higher overall cardiovascular risk and require more aggressive lipid-lowering and antithrombotic strategies [28].

When analyzing antiplatelet therapy (including DAPT and SAPT), PCI within one year, age, hepatic dysfunction, and history of PPI use were all positively associated with the choice of antiplatelet therapy. Among them, patients with hepatic dysfunction had a significantly increased likelihood of being prescribed antiplatelet therapy, which may be related to clinicians' concerns about the bleeding risk of oral anticoagulants in patients with abnormal liver function [29]. Advanced age was also associated with the choice of antiplatelet therapy, likely reflecting clinicians’ cautious approach to long-term anticoagulation in the elderly [30]. Interestingly, patients with enlarged left atrial diameter, heart failure, and persistent atrial fibrillation were significantly less likely to receive antiplatelet therapy. Left atrial enlargement and persistent atrial fibrillation often indicate a higher risk of stroke, while heart failure itself is an important component of the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score [31–33]. Therefore, in these patients, clinicians are more inclined to prioritize anticoagulation or intensified antithrombotic therapy rather than antiplatelet therapy for the prevention of thrombotic events. This choice aligns closely with current atrial fibrillation management guidelines [34].

In summary, in this study, the choice of antithrombotic therapy was not random but influenced by multiple factors, including patient clinical characteristics, procedural exposure, and concomitant medications. This reflects clinicians' careful balancing of thrombotic and bleeding risks.

Clinical follow-up results of various antithrombotic strategies

For MACCEs, all-cause mortality, bleeding, cardiovascular re-hospitalization, and non-cardiovascular re-hospitalization, no statistically significant differences were observed across antithrombotic strategies. However, with regard to thromboembolic events, anticoagulation therapy alone appears to be associated with a higher risk of thromboembolism compared to intensive antithrombotic therapy; conversely, the risk associated with antiplatelet therapy appears to be numerically lower than that of anticoagulation therapy alone. These findings are counterintuitive from a biological perspective and suggest the possible presence of residual confounding factors related to indications: Patients receiving monotherapy with anticoagulants had higher baseline risk factors for thromboembolism (e.g., older age, higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, history of stroke, larger left atrium), whereas those receiving antiplatelet therapy were primarily patients who had recently undergone percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and were presumed to have a lower risk of thromboembolism. Furthermore, unmeasured or inadequately measured confounding factors (such as frailty, cognitive status, and medication adherence) likely remain. Therefore, these observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a comparison of treatment efficacy. Although intensified antithrombotic therapy (including dual and triple antithrombotic regimens) theoretically increases bleeding risk, it showed a trend toward a lower risk of TE compared with anticoagulation alone, suggesting a possible association with lower ischemic risk in patients with higher thrombotic risk. This finding is hypothesis-generating and requires confirmation in future studies. This result is consistent with the previously observed concept of "balancing ischemic and bleeding risk," and also suggests that intensified antithrombotic therapy may still have value in certain subgroups, which was confirmed in our subgroup analysis [35, 36]. Notably, for CE events (including myocardial infarction and ischemia-driven revascularization), anticoagulation monotherapy was associated with significantly lower CE risk compared with the other two groups in this cohort. This finding is consistent with the concept of a "de‑escalation" strategy, i.e., gradually transitioning from intensified antithrombotic therapy to anticoagulation monotherapy in patients with stable atrial fibrillation and coronary artery disease, especially those with relatively controllable ischemic risk. However, given the observational design and the fact that the MACCE primary endpoint did not reach statistical significance, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating rather than definitive evidence of superiority. Future randomized studies are needed to confirm the optimal duration and patient selection for de‑escalation therapy [37]. In addition, we identified liver dysfunction as a strong predictor of MACCEs and all-cause mortality. This finding is consistent with existing literature, as patients with liver dysfunction often have coagulation disorders, abnormal drug metabolism, and higher bleeding risk, which should be a key consideration in antithrombotic therapy decisions [38]. We also observed that diabetes was associated with a higher risk of MACCEs and CE events, further validating previous studies identifying diabetes as a key factor in residual cardiovascular risk. Even under adequate antithrombotic therapy, diabetic patients still face higher risks of cardiovascular ischemic events, indicating the need for enhanced comprehensive management for these patients, including glycemic control and optimized antithrombotic strategies [39]. The HAS-BLED score was associated with increased risks of MACCEs and all-cause mortality. As a tool for assessing bleeding risk, the HAS-BLED score may reflect poorer overall health and more comorbidities in high-scoring patients rather than indicating bleeding risk alone. This finding suggests that patients at high bleeding risk are inherently at high risk for cardiovascular events and mortality, and antithrombotic decisions should be made with individualized consideration [40]. Hemoglobin levels were inversely correlated with MACCEs, bleeding events, and TE events. That is, anemic patients have higher incidences of adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular outcomes, as well as increased risk for thromboembolic events. This may relate to inadequate myocardial oxygen supply, greater severity of underlying diseases, and increased bleeding risk after antithrombotic therapy [41]. Statin use was associated with lower risks of MACCEs, all-cause mortality, and CE events. This result further emphasizes the important role of statins in secondary prevention of coronary artery disease and suggests that statins may be associated with improved outcomes, possibly through anti-inflammatory effects and plaque stabilization, as discussed in previous studies [42]. The impact of atrial fibrillation type on outcomes varied. Persistent atrial fibrillation was associated with reduced TE risk, whereas permanent atrial fibrillation was associated with a lower risk of CE events. Interpret these results cautiously, as small sample sizes, confounding variables, or selection bias in treatment may affect them. Nonetheless, the trend suggests distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and therapy responses across atrial fibrillation types, warranting further study.

Impact and application in clinical practice

Evidence is limited regarding the prognosis of different antithrombotic strategies in Asian patients with atrial fibrillation and coronary heart disease undergoing PCI. Our study aims to address this gap. We collect longitudinal data from these patients using long-term dynamic assessment and follow-up. We describe changes in antithrombotic therapy over the years and identify predictors affecting prognosis. In addition, our study is based on real-world multicenter data, reducing single-center selection bias and enhancing generalizability. The study examines the evolution and current status of antithrombotic therapies, and provides hypothesis-generating insights into the associations between different regimens and clinical outcomes. These findings may complement existing trial evidence and inform future research on post-PCI management in this population.

Limitations

However, this study has some limitations. First, this study is a retrospective study, which has certain selection bias; second, because this study is based on real clinical data, some subgroup sample sizes are small, and the follow-up period is short, which limits the comprehensive assessment of efficacy and safety as well as the generalizability and applicability of the results; third, although we adjusted for confounding factors using methods such as IPTW, double-robust Cox regression analysis, and subgroup analysis, the study population was highly heterogeneous (including patients with recent PCI and prior PCI). There were fundamental differences in indications among the treatment groups, and residual confounding could not be completely ruled out. Moreover, the variable selection approach used to identify predictors of discharge antithrombotic regimens (univariate screening followed by LASSO and then multivariable logistic regression) is exploratory and may introduce selection bias and model instability; the results presented in Table 2 should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis‑generating rather than confirmatory. Therefore, all findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects; fourth, we were unable to follow up on patients' adherence, changes in antithrombotic regimens, and physical condition after discharge. However, the majority of clinical events in this study occurred for the first time in the early post-discharge period. Specifically, during a median follow-up period of 32 months, a total of 479 patients experienced adverse events, of which 198 (41.5%) occurred within the first 12 months, 171 (35.8%) occurred between months 13 and 24, and only 108 (22.7%) occurred between months 25 and 32. Thus, nearly 80% of events were concentrated in the first two years, with the highest incidence occurring in the first year. In the early post-discharge period (particularly the first 3–6 months), the medication regimen at discharge best reflects the patient’s actual treatment status, as clinically significant adjustments to antithrombotic therapy typically occur after this time window. In summary, although residual confounding due to later changes in treatment regimens or reduced adherence cannot be completely ruled out, this limitation has a limited impact on the main conclusions of this study, and the overall robustness of the results is credible. In addition, several other clinically relevant factors were not captured, including PCI complexity (e.g., number of stents, lesion characteristics such as bifurcation, left main, or chronic total occlusion) and clinician decision-making processes (e.g., subjective perception of frailty or bleeding risk not fully reflected by HAS-BLED). These unmeasured factors may have introduced residual confounding; fifth, this study was conducted only in Chongqing, China, which limits the external validity of our findings to other regions or populations with different healthcare systems or ethnic backgrounds; sixth, due to the sample size, we could not further classify bleeding events; seventh, prospective follow-up was conducted only in the 2023 cohort, not in the 2013 or 2018 cohorts. This is because the long interval since the initial hospitalization resulted in a high proportion of lost-to-follow-up patients in the earlier years (e.g., due to changes in contact information, death, or relocation), and the participating research centers differed across these three years, making it impossible to establish a consistent multicenter longitudinal cohort. Therefore, data from 2013 and 2018 were used only for cross-sectional trend analysis. We call for future studies to incorporate detailed assessments of PCI complexity and dynamic follow-up of antithrombotic treatment to better address these limitations.

Conclusion

This study indicates that under the current guideline framework, among patients with atrial fibrillation combined with coronary heart disease undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, the use of intensified antithrombotic therapy and anticoagulants has increased. The choice of antithrombotic strategy is closely related to patients' clinical characteristics, and there are significant differences in ischemic and bleeding risks among different regimens. Intensified antithrombotic therapy was associated with fewer ischemic events but a higher bleeding risk. Anticoagulation alone was associated with a higher rate of thromboembolic events, so individual risks must be weighed in decision-making. Clinical factors such as liver and kidney function, diabetes, hemoglobin, and statin use are also associated with prognosis, underscoring the need for comprehensive assessment. Therefore, when developing individualized antithrombotic regimens, the patient's underlying diseases, bleeding risk, ischemic risk, and medication history should be considered comprehensively to achieve an optimal balance between risks and benefits.
