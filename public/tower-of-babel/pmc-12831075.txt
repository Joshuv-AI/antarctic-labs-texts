Precision, Personalization, and Progress: A New Era in Foot and Ankle Surgery
Authors: Soheil Ashkani-Esfahani, Lercan Aslan
Published: 2025

Dear AOTT Readers,

For years, AOTT has featured important contributions in foot and ankle surgery.1-3 In this special issue, we hope to speak directly to surgeons who “live” this field, sharing not only today’s evidence, but also a glimpse of what may define tomorrow’s practice.

Foot and ankle surgery resides where biomechanics meets real life. In no other region do we ask so many joints, packed into such a small volume, to deliver both precision and resilience, every minute of every day. During a single gait cycle, the foot must behave like two different structures: a “flexible adaptor” at initial contact and midstance, then a “rigid lever” for push-off. Subtalar motion and its coupling with the midfoot help coordinate this transformation, unlocking the foot to accommodate terrain and then locking it to transmit force efficiently. This elegant choreography is also why small deviations in alignment, joint congruence, and stability can produce outsized consequences in pain, function, and long-term degeneration.

Over the last decade, our specialty has entered a period of rapid acceleration, driven by advanced imaging, evolving surgical techniques, and meaningful engagement with industry. We are seeing a shift from surgeon-dependent craftsmanship toward a model built on measurement, reproducibility, and personalization, supported by a fast-growing scientific literature. The flood of new studies, spanning deformity, arthroplasty, arthrodesis, sports injuries, neuropathic reconstruction, and trauma, does more than report results: it changes what we consider “standard,” what we know to measure, and how we judge success.

Functional imaging becomes the default: The rise of weight-bearing CT

“If you cannot measure it, you cannot improve it.” Lord Kelvin

A key inflection point has been the wider use of weight-bearing CT (WBCT). For decades, we learned to infer three-dimensional, load-dependent problems from two-dimensional, non–weight-bearing images. WBCT flips that paradigm: it shows alignment, joint relationships, and subtle instability under physiologic loading, closer to how the foot actually behaves when patients stand and walk. WBCT enables more structured planning and more honest postoperative auditing, especially in complex hindfoot alignment, peritalar instability patterns, progressive collapsing foot deformity, syndesmotic pathology, and impingement. As WBCT use expands, the next challenge is standardization: consistent acquisition protocols, validated measurements, and shared definitions that allow meaningful comparisons across centers.4,5

Planning and execution get closer: Patient-specific instrumentation

“We shape our tools, and thereafter our tools shape us.” John Culkin

In parallel with WBCT, digital planning has matured. Patient-specific instrumentation (PSI), particularly visible in total ankle arthroplasty, represents more than a technological add-on; it reflects a mindset: plan carefully, define alignment goals in 3D, and execute with fewer uncontrolled variables. While comparative outcome data will continue to evolve, PSI has already influenced how surgeons think about component positioning, deformity correction, reproducible resections, and workflow efficiency.5

Importantly, these tools are especially meaningful for surgeons early in their practice. High-level outcomes in ankle reconstruction often hinge on small details like alignment targets, the quality of joint preparation, implant positioning, and compression strategy, that traditionally improve with patient volume and time. PSI and structured planning pathways can shorten the route to consistent execution, not by replacing judgment, but by translating established principles into repeatable steps and reducing avoidable variability.

“Universal” guides in midfoot fusion: Lapidus and the central tarsometatarsal (TMT) joints

“One Ring to rule them all.” J.R.R. Tolkien

A parallel development, quietly reshaping midfoot surgery, is the emergence of universal, procedure-specific instrumentation systems for midfoot arthrodesis, including Lapidus and second-third TMT fusions. These guide-based workflows are designed to standardize key steps such as joint preparation, alignment control, compression trajectory, and implant positioning, often in a time-efficient and technically consistent manner that may also reduce radiation exposure. For surgeons earlier in their practice, universal guides can help deliver benchmark-level technical accuracy more reliably by converting best-practice concepts into reproducible intraoperative steps. In short, this is not about “leveling” experience; it is about strengthening reliability.

Patient-specific implants and 3D printing: Expanding options in salvage and revision

“Excellence is never an accident.” Aristotle

Beyond guides, the field is also seeing expanded use of patient-specific implants, often enabled by 3D printing. Custom solutions for bone loss, revision settings, limb salvage, and complex deformity are increasingly reported and increasingly practical. These implants do not replace fundamentals; they demand them. When planned thoughtfully on integrating fixation strategy, biological environment, and alignment goals, patient-specific implants can extend reconstructive options for patients who previously had limited paths forward.6

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS): Smaller incisions, broader impact

“Perfection is achieved, not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away.” Antoine de Saint-Exupéry

Minimally invasive strategies in foot and ankle surgery have matured beyond the narrow goal of “improving union.” When indications are appropriate, MIS can reduce soft-tissue disruption and postoperative morbidity, translating into better early comfort, less swelling and fewer wound-related complications, and faster functional recovery. The functional benefit is not merely theoretical: by preserving local biology and minimizing scar burden, especially around the ankle and hindfoot where the soft-tissue envelope is unforgiving, MIS may support earlier rehabilitation and a smoother return to daily activities. In addition, patient-perceived outcomes matter: smaller incisions often improve cosmesis and satisfaction, which can be particularly meaningful in younger or active patients. As MIS expands across select arthrodesis, osteotomy, and fixation procedures, the field’s responsibility is to match innovation with discipline, defining where MIS truly adds value, maintaining alignment and fixation principles, and reporting outcomes that reflect what patients care about most: pain, function, recovery experience, and durability.7

Union as the currency of success

“Nature cures; the physician assists.” Hippocrates

Despite all new technology, one truth remains simple: union matters. Whether we are performing ankle or hindfoot arthrodesis, tibiotalocalcaneal reconstruction, midfoot stabilization, or revision fusions, the success of our work often depends on achieving a stable, well-aligned fusion in patients whose biology is not always friendly.

Bone stimulation and biologic adjunct strategies are increasingly incorporated into high-risk pathways. The evidence base varies by modality and indication, but the direction is clear: in compromised hosts, we increasingly treat fusion as a mechanical and biological challenge, and we deploy tools accordingly, without letting adjuncts substitute for meticulous joint preparation, alignment, and stable fixation.8

AI and machine learning: The next accelerator of precision

“All models are wrong, but some are useful.” George E. P. Box

No contemporary overview is complete without artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML). Foot and ankle surgery is particularly well-suited to data-driven tools because it is data-rich: radiographs, CT/WBCT, MRI, repeated measurements, and outcomes linked to comorbidities and constructs. Early promise is already tangible for automated measurements, segmentation and alignment quantification, deformity classification support, risk stratification, and outcome prediction models. The best way to frame AI is not as replacement, but as augmentation.9

As we adopt AI, the responsibility is the same as for any new implant or technique: prove utility, understand failure modes, and validate across populations. Used well, AI can become a powerful ally in education and decision support, helping teams converge faster on reliable planning and consistent execution.

A growing ecosystem: Why funding and infrastructure matter

“Alone we can do so little; together we can do so much.” Helen Keller

Innovation does not occur in isolation. It is sustained by training pipelines, multicenter collaboration, and research support. AOFAS annual-report figures help illustrate the broader momentum behind our specialty. Across recent years, AOFAS and its foundation show rising assets alongside a high and increasing share of foundation income attributed to corporate program and grant support. AOFAS total assets increased from roughly $3.24M in 2020 to $4.50M in 2024, and the foundation from about $9.24M to $13.02M. The message is not about numbers; it is about capacity: the ability to fund research, support education, build registries, and accelerate high-quality science that turns innovation into outcome-improving practice.

“The truest guide in life is science.” Mustafa Kemal Atatürk

To follow pioneers today does not mean copying yesterday’s methods. It means inheriting their principles: standardize where it improves reliability; respect biology; use technology responsibly; and contribute back through transparent reporting, honest limitations, and collaborative science.
