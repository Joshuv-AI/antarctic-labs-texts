Secondary Safety Control for Systems with Sector Bounded Nonlinearities [Extended Version] footnoteinfo
Yankai Lin, Michelle S. Chong, Carlos Murguia

Abstract
We consider the problem of safety verification and safety-aware controller synthesis for systems with sector bounded nonlinearities. We aim to keep the states of the system within a given safe set under potential actuator and sensor attacks. Specifically, we adopt the setup that a controller has already been designed to stabilize the plant. Using invariant sets and barrier certificate theory, we first give sufficient conditions to verify the safety of the closed-loop system under attacks. Furthermore, by using a subset of sensors that are assumed to be free of attacks, we provide a synthesis method for a secondary controller that enhances the safety of the system. The sufficient conditions to verify safety are derived using Lyapunov-based tools and the 𝒮 \mathcal{S} -procedure. Using the projection lemma, the conditions are then formulated as linear matrix inequality (LMI) problems which can be solved efficiently. Lastly, our theoretical results are illustrated through numerical simulations.

Introduction

Communication networks are now used extensively in industrial control systems. While they make the operation and maintenance of control systems much more efficient, they also render the systems vulnerable to malicious attacks injected to sensors and actuators. Many incidents caused by cyber attacks have been reported in recent years such as
, e.g., the StuxNet malware incident
[
9
]
. Therefore, it is of significant importance to develop tools that ensure safe operation of control systems.

One way to deal with cyber attacks is to detect the attacks and then mitigate them, see
[
15
,
11
]
for results on linear systems. This approach ensures security using redundancy and typically requires a large number of observers to completely nullify the effect of attacks. Moreover, in
[
41
]
, it is shown that intelligent adversaries can use the available system knowledge to inject malicious signals into the system while remaining stealthy. In this work, we do not detect the presence of attacks, but aim to ensure
safety
of the system under the assumption that a subset of sensors and actuators are well protected. Specifically, we design a controller such that the states of a nonlinear system with sector bounded nonlinearities always remain within a given set, which we call the
safe set
, despite attacks on the other sensors and actuators. This safety requirement is particularly relevant in safety-critical applications such as power, water and transportation systems, where operating outside the safe set may lead to economic losses and fatalities.

There are various approaches to safety verification and control of dynamical systems such as model checking
[
23
]
, formal methods
[
25
]
, barrier function or barrier certificate based methods
[
3
,
34
,
40
]
, and reachability analysis
[
32
,
5
,
16
]
. In this work, we follow an approach towards safety verification similar to the barrier certificate method based on forward invariance of the safe set
[
6
]
. Namely, if we are able to find a subset of the safe set that is forward invariant, then the states always remain within the safe set provided the initial condition is also within the forward invariant subset. Barrier certificate methods allow using Lyapunov methods to devise sufficient conditions that guarantee safety. Therewith, the extensive computations of abstractions or Hamilton-Jacobi-Isaacs equations commonly found in reachability-based methods can be avoided. As an alternative approach, the control barrier function (CBF) method provides sufficient conditions to guarantee safety in terms of the online solution of quadratic programmes (QPs) to compute control actions
[
3
]
. On the other hand, the problem of finding the appropriate CBF for general nonlinear systems is still a challenge, as it is associated with identifying the appropriate safe set
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we consider the setup where a
primary controller
has already been designed to achieve certain control objectives. However, a subset of sensors and actuators are subject to potential attacks. To ensure safety, a dynamic output feedback controller, which we call the
secondary controller
, is designed using a subset of sensors known to be attack-free (for instance those close to the plant or computing units are secured by encryption and/or authentication protocols). The role of the secondary controller is to modify the closed-loop dynamics such that the modified system is ‘less’ vulnerable to sensor and actuator attacks. Fig.
1
shows the configuration considered in this work. We choose to work with quadratic Lyapunov-like functions which allow us to formulate sufficient conditions for forward invariance of the given safe set in the form of matrix inequalities
[
13
,
14
]
.

The main contributions are as follows:

1.

An analysis framework that allows assessing the safety of a class of nonlinear system (with sector-bounded nonlinearities) under state-dependent sensor and actuator attacks in terms of the solution to a set of linear matrix inequalities (LMIs).

2.

Built on this framework and the projection lemma, we devise a synthesis tool for designing secondary linear dynamic output controllers that steer reachable sets induced by cyberattacks to safe regions of the state space.

In comparison to relevant work in the literature:

1.

Our work is different from CBF-based methods for safety-critical systems in
[
2
,
3
]
, where state feedback control laws are computed based on a QP. In this work, we synthesize a dynamic output feedback linear controller, thereby avoiding the potential difficulties of measuring all states. In
[
1
]
, an observer-based method is proposed to address output feedback challenges of CBF-QP-based design. However, our work still differs from
[
1
]
in the sense that we consider attack signals with state-dependent bounds and we allow for general (non observer-based) dynamic controllers. For systems with sector bounded nonlinearities, observer-based control design is non-trivial as the separation principle might not hold. In addition, while CBF-QP is an optimization-based controller that minimizes a convex function of the input at every time step, our proposed method can be designed offline to achieve other objectives such as
\mathcal{L}_{p}
performance, which might not be easily solvable using CBF-QP, see Section
3.4
for details.

2.

The problem setup is novel in the sense that we design the secondary controller which runs in parallel with an (existing) primary controller to ensure safety using a subset of sensors that are free of attacks. Although there exist works, including
[
43
,
44
]
, that consider output feedback control designs under attacks, we consider a substantially different problem setup. In our problem, we focus on safety challenges of control systems under attack signals, which has not been studied within the nonlinear partial measurements setup considered here.

3.

Compared to the safety filters approach summarized in
[
42
]
, our proposed primary/secondary control scheme uses only a subset of reliable sensors. In the safety filters setting, corrupted measurements will also go to the filter. This motivates us to consider a configuration that separates the primary and secondary controller. Moreover, we consider the case of output feedback which is more general than the state feedback case considered in
[
42
]
and references therein.

4.

Compared to the conference version
[
27
]
, we extend the results on linear systems by considering a class of nonlinear systems with sector bounded nonlinearities. Additionally, in this work we allow the attack signals to depend on the states of the system while they are assumed to be bounded in
[
27
]
. Moreover, the change of variables used in
[
27
]
to convexify the sufficient conditions for safety are not applicable to the nonlinear case. Therefore, in the synthesis part, we derive a new set of sufficient conditions for safety-aware controller synthesis using the projection lemma to decompose the non-convex programme into two convex ones.

Problem formulation

2.1
System description

We consider the following class of systems

\begin{split}\dot{x}_{p}&=A_{p}x_{p}+G_{p}\hat{\phi}_{p}(H_{p}x_{p})+B_{p}u,\\
y_{p}&=C_{p}x_{p},\end{split}

(1)

where
x_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}}
is the state, and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
and
y_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
are the input and output, respectively. For the nonlinear term
G_{p}\hat{\phi}_{p}(H_{p}x_{p})
,
H_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{p}\times n_{p}}
,
G_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times q_{p}}
, and
\hat{\phi}_{p}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{h_{p}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q_{p}}
. We assume that there is a
primary
controller which has been designed a priori according to some desired system specifications, for instance, stabilization and/or robustness. We consider general primary controllers of the form,

\begin{split}\dot{x}_{1}&=A_{1}x_{1}+G_{1}\hat{\phi}_{1}(H_{1}x_{1})+B_{1}(y_{p}+a_{y}),\\
u_{p}&=C_{1}x_{1}+D_{1}(y_{p}+a_{y})+a_{u},\end{split}

(2)

where
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
,
H_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{h_{1}\times n_{1}}
,
G_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times q_{1}}
, and
\hat{\phi}_{1}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{h_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}}
. In the case where linear controllers are used,
\hat{\phi}_{1}
can be set to zero. We consider additive false-data injection attack to sensor measurements,
a_{y}
, and control signals,
a_{u}
. For simplicity, we collect all attack signals in the stacked vector
a=(a_{u},a_{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}+n_{y}}
. We further assume that the interconnection of the primary controller (
2
) with system (
1
), in the absence of attacks (
a_{y}=0
and

), is forward complete.

Since the primary controller (
2
) is pre-designed without being aware of the attacks, the security and safety of the closed-loop may be compromised. Therefore, we propose introducing a
secondary controller
, that runs in conjunction with the primary controller. The secondary controller uses a subset of the sensors and actuators, which are either available locally or known to be safeguarded against malicious manipulation (e.g., via encryption or watermarking). The secondary controller considered in this work takes the following form:

\begin{split}\dot{x}_{2}&=A_{2}x_{2}+B_{2}y_{s},\\
u_{s}&=C_{2}x_{2}+D_{2}y_{s},\end{split}

(3)

where
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
. The signal
y_{s}=C_{s}y_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{C}}
is obtained from the set of sensors that are available to the secondary controller and safeguarded from attacks, where
C_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{C}\times n_{y}}
is a sensor selection matrix, and
u_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{E}}
is the secondary control input signal. We consider dynamic output feedback controllers as they cover a more general and wider class of controllers including the class of static feedback controllers, observer-based, and PID controllers. Although we could in principle formulate the problem for more general nonlinear dynamic controllers, the synthesis of such controllers would be computationally costly and intractable in some cases. Using linear controllers allows exploiting a variety of synthesis techniques available in the literature (e.g., convexifying coordinate transformations, the projection lemma, and semidefinite programming).

The secondary controller additively perturbs the control signals of the primary controller as follows

u=u_{p}+E_{u}u_{s},

(4)

where
E_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}\times n_{E}}
is a selection matrix that dictates the set of secondary control inputs (safeguarded from attacks) that will be fed back to the plant (
1
). As shown in Fig.
1
, the secondary controller uses uncompromised sensors and actuators only, as it is co-located with the plant (i.e., not connected to the network) and we assume that the local sensors and actuators are not subject to network attacks. Consequently, no attack signals appear in (
3
). The goal of the secondary controller (
3
) is to ensure that when the overall closed-loop system (
1
)-(
4
) is subject to cyber attacks, the safety of the closed-loop can be ensured, i.e., the states of the closed-loop system (
1
)-(
4
) remain within a given safe set for all
t\geq 0
.

Remark 1

Matrices
(E_{u},C_{s})
denote the design resources available to the system architect. In the best possible case, we have

which means there exists no sensor attacks and all sensors are somehow made available to design the secondary controller ( which is not needed in this extreme case). In general,

In future research, we will investigate how to optimally choose the sensors to secure and make them available to the secondary controller.

2.2
Safe sets and attack signals

We briefly introduce the notion of robust positive invariant set for dynamical systems
[
6
, Definition 2.2]
.

Definition 1

Consider the system,

\dot{x}=f(x,w)

(11)

with state
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and exogenous input
w\in\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{w}}
. The set
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be robustly positively invariant (RPI) for the system (
11
), if for all initial states
x_{0}\in\mathcal{O}
and all
w\in\mathcal{W}
state trajectories satisfy
x(t)\in\mathcal{O}
for all
t\geq 0
.

We assume throughout the paper the existence of an ellipsoidal safe set of the form:

\mathcal{E}(\Xi)=\{\zeta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}+n_{1}}\ |\ \zeta_{1}^{\top}\Xi\zeta_{1}\leq 1\},

(12)

where
\Xi\succeq 0
. Safe sets capture portions of the state space where systems integrity is guaranteed (e.g., no collisions, explosions, spills, etc.). The safe set (
12
) implies that the state of the secondary controller,
x_{2}
, is not taken into account for safety evaluation. We believe this assumption is reasonable, since safety of the states of the plant are often the primary concern in most applications. For simplicity, we further assume that the safe set
\mathcal{E}(\Xi)
is centred around the origin. This is without loss of generality as the same analysis can be performed with trivial changes as done in the preliminary version
[
27
]
.

Remark 4

Ellipsoidal safe sets have also been used in existing literature for security and safety, see
[
35
,
32
]
for instance. If the safe set has another geometrical shape, (
12
) can be used as an inner approximation of the true safe set. One possible way to deal with safe sets induced by certain classes of nonlinear functions is by using sum-of-squares programming, see
[
28
]
for example.

To verify the safety of the closed-loop system (
5
), we aim to find a robust positive invariant set for (
5
) which is also a subset of the safe set
\mathcal{E}(\Xi)
.

It is shown in
[
41
]
that cyber attackers often seek to inject malicious signals while remaining stealthy and undetected. To characterize such attacks, we assume the attack signals satisfy the following assumption.

Assumption 2

For all time, the attack signal,
a
, satisfies the following inequality:

\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{1}\\
a\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}&Q_{2}\\
Q_{2}^{\top}&Q_{3}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\zeta_{1}\\
a\end{bmatrix}\leq 1,

(13)

for some matrix
Q_{2}
and symmetric matrices
Q_{1},Q_{3}
satisfying

\begin{split}&Q_{3}\succ 0,\\
&\mathcal{Q}:=Q_{2}^{\top}Q_{3}^{-1}Q_{2}-Q_{1}\succeq 0.\end{split}

(14)

Remark 5

Compared to
[
27
]
, condition (
13
) covers the situation where the attack signal,
a
, depends on the states
\zeta_{1}
of the plant and the primary controller due to attacker’s potential knowledge of
\zeta_{1}
. Specifically, the upper bound of the attack is allowed to grow with the norm of the states. Since the secondary controller is set to be attack-free, the attack is assumed to be independent of the state
x_{2}
. The first condition in (
14
),
Q_{3}\succ 0
, ensures that the attack signal at any time is upper bounded. If
Q_{3}=-I
, then
|a|
will be lower bounded instead. On the other hand, the second condition
Q_{2}^{\top}Q_{3}^{-1}Q_{2}-Q_{1}\succeq 0
is enforced for analysis purposes and will be used to convexify the conditions derived later in the paper. The latter assumption is without loss of generality. To see this, suppose
\mathcal{Q}\succeq 0
does not hold, then one can find a
\bar{Q}_{1}\prec 0
such that
\bar{Q}_{1}-Q_{1}\prec 0
. If (
13
) is satisfied with
Q_{1}
, it also satisfies the inequality with

replaced by
\bar{Q}_{1}
. Moreover, if
Q_{3}\succ 0
, then
Q_{2}^{\top}Q_{3}^{-1}Q_{2}-\bar{Q}_{1}\succeq 0
also holds. An example of admissible attacks satisfying Assumption

is given by

,

, and

. In this case (
13
) is equivalent to
|a|^{2}\leq 1+|\zeta_{1}|^{2}
.

.

Remark 6

Condition (
13
) models the resource the attacker has to continuously launch attacks without being detected. While some existing works detect attacks with unbounded norms, see
[
21
,
29
]
for example, we focus on the safety of the controlled system in the presence of attacks. Although equivalent or similar assumptions are adopted by some existing works, in particular,
[

,
[
44
, Assumption 1]
,
[

, Section IV.D]
,
[

[

3

We start this section by introducing the

Lemma 1
(Section 2.6.3 in

Let

F_{i}(\xi):=\xi^{\top}T_{i}\xi+2b_{i}^{\top}\xi+v_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,p,

such that for all
\xi

F_{0}(\xi)-\sum_{i=1}^{p}\tau_{i}F_{i}(\xi)\geq 0,

then, we have
F_{0}(\xi)\geq 0
for all
\xi
such that
F_{i}(\xi)\geq 0
,
i=1,\ldots,p
.

3.1
Safety verification

We first set

and

and verify the safety of the closed-loop system with the primary controller (

V_{1}(\zeta_{1})=\zeta_{1}^{\top}P_{1}\zeta_{1}

Proposition 1

Consider the closed-loop system (

3.4
Optimization perspectives

For fixed constants

Example 3.3
.

Find the worst admissible attack signals.

A method to find out the worst possible attack that can be dealt with by the closed-loop systems (

is a positive definite matrix, this is equivalent to minimizing the volume of the ellipsoid induced by

that the volume of the latter ellipsoid is proportional to

Lemma 3.4

(Lemma 4,
[
32
]
)

Given any positive definite matrix

, the following holds

\det[R]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}^{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[R]^{\frac{n}{2}}.

(56)

Moreover,
\arg\min[\mathrm{Tr}[R]^{\frac{n}{2}}]=\arg\min[\mathrm{Tr}[R]]
.

As a result, one can set

subject to the feasibility conditions (

\text{Tr}[Q_{3}]
in this case dictates the amount of resources an attacker has to invest to violate the safety constraints of the system and serves as a possible metric of how secondary controller improves the safety of the overall system. Note that, if (
17
) and (

) are solved for given
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\alpha_{4}
, the addition of the new variable

Remark 4.1
.

In this example, the synthesized controller takes a high-gain form which normally suffers from robustness issues against measurement noises. However, this will not be a problem since we assume the secondary controller uses reliable and perfect measurement which reflects the fact that natural disturbances and noises are normally considered small compared to adversarial signals. Another implementation issue is the potential peaking of the control input. One way to deal with it is to replace the linear map
E_{u}
with a properly designed saturation function, which will introduce an extra nonlinearity of dead-zone function into the plant. Fortunately, as shown in
[
39

[
38
]
. However, the switching between controllers is beyond the scope of this work, but is an interesting future endeavour.

5
Conclusions

We have provided a framework for analysis and synthesis of safety-aware controllers of a class of nonlinear systems subject to sensor and actuator attacks. By using reliable feedback, a secondary controller synthesis problem is formulated as feasibility problems of matrix inequalities. Although the initial synthesis problem is not convex, we give sufficient conditions in both linear and nonlinear case to render the problem tractable for existing convex optimization solvers. A numerical example of the Josephson junction model is simulated, where a secondary controller is designed to guarantee safety which can not be achieved by the primary controller alone. An interesting future work is to investigate how
C_{s}
and
E_{u}
impact the system and how to optimally select them.
