Remarks on stochastic cloning and delayed-state filtering
Tara Mina, Lindsey Marinello, John Christian

Abstract
Many estimation problems in robotics and navigation involve measurements that depend on prior states. A prominent example is odometry, which measures the relative change between states over time. Accurately handling these delayed-state measurements requires capturing their correlations with prior state estimates, and a widely used approach is stochastic cloning (SC), which augments the state vector to account for these correlations. This work revisits a long-established but often overlooked alternative—the delayed-state Kalman filter—and demonstrates that a properly derived filter yields exactly the same state and covariance update as SC, without requiring state augmentation. Moreover, the generalized Kalman filter formulation provides computational advantages, while also reducing memory requirements for higher-dimensional states. Our findings clarify a common misconception that Kalman filter variants are inherently unable to handle correlated delayed-state measurements, demonstrating that an alternative formulation achieves the same results more efficiently.

Introduction

Many state estimation problems involve measurements that depend on prior states.
This dependency is oftentimes either due to processing delays or because the measurement itself depends upon state information over multiple time steps, such as with odometry measurements.
It is critical in such situations to properly account for the correlations between past states and the measurements.
This brief work aims to bring new clarity to the various approaches used to solve this problem and the relationships between them.

In robotics, measurements that depend on past states—–referred to here as
delayed-state measurements
—–most commonly arise in the context of odometry,
where observations describe how a robot’s pose changes over time.

Odometry measurements can be used within a simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) framework or in a map-free approach.
In situations where real-time map construction is not important, the map-free approach is sometimes attractive due to its reduced computational complexity.

As a relative measurement, a fundamental characteristic of map-free odometry is that each measurement inherently depends on both the current and previous robot state.

I-A

The History and Lexicon of Delayed-State Filtering

Over the years, various terms have been introduced to describe similar—or, oftentimes, equivalent—techniques in delayed-state filtering.
This usually happens by accident rather than on purpose.
Unfortunately, however, the introduction of different terms to describe the same idea creates confusion, misattribution of discovery, and makes it more difficult to locate relevant prior works.
In this section, with an intent to clarify the relationships between similar ideas, we provide (1) an overview of the most common terms (2) a summary of some important historical connections.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the earliest formulation of a Kalman filter variant designed to handle delayed-state measurements appeared in the work of Brown and Hartman (1968)
[
24
]
.
Their derivation introduces modified update equations for the recursive Kalman filter that explicitly account for correlations between the measurement and the prior state uncertainty.
Although the 1968 paper did not assign a specific name to this generalized Kalman filter, Brown later refers to this filter as the
delayed-state Kalman filter (DSKF)

[
26
]
in the context of GPS delta-range measurement processing and in his textbook on Kalman filtering, co-authored with Hwang
[
18
]
.

Alternatively, one can augment the Kalman filter’s state vector by including the prior state, thereby directly representing the measurement model as a function of the augmented state vector.
This approach preserves the familiar Kalman filter structure, which is more commonly recognized.
This technique is popularly termed as
stochastic cloning
, coined by Roumeliotis and Burdick (2002)
[
29
]
.

However, a deeper examination of the literature reveals that the stochastic cloning approach predates the term.
In fact, Brown and Hartman (1968) describe a method of “twisting the model” for the delayed-state measurement scenario in order to conform to the standard Kalman filter formulation, arriving at the same mathematical expressions that were later proposed for stochastic cloning
[
29
]
.
At the time, Brown and Hartman (1968) refer to this as the “double-state approach”
[
24
]
, but then ultimately focus on deriving a recursive update form for the delayed-state Kalman filter using the original state vector.
While they note that both formulations should yield equivalent results, this equivalence was not explicitly demonstrated, as it was not the focus of their work and analysis—an observation that motivates a more explicit comparison in the present work.

In the literature, two related contexts arise in the delayed-state measurement problem.
First, a measurement may depend only on a single prior state but is received or processed at a later time. This occurs frequently in practice for real-time onboard filtering, as measurements are inherently delayed relative to the current estimation time.
Second, a measurement may simultaneously depend on multiple states at different times — for instance, in odometry measurements that relate states across time.
The first scenario is a special case of the second, more general formulation.
Both the delayed-state Kalman filter (DSKF) and stochastic cloning (SC) have been used in prior works to address both the specific case of delayed measurements and the more general case of multi-state measurements.

Various terms have been used in the literature to describe techniques equivalent to
stochastic cloning
, where the state vector is augmented with prior states, to address the delayed-state measurement problem.
One related term is the
stochastic cloning Kalman filter (SC-KF)
[
37
]
.
The
multi-state constraint Kalman filter (MSCKF)
[
19
]
similarly augments the filter’s state and covariance matrix with copies of prior states and associated covariances at the times when camera measurements were taken.
An equivalent framework to the MSCKF has also been referred to as the
stochastic cloning sliding-window EKF
[
38
]
.
Related approaches involving cloning and augmentation of prior states have also been referred to in prior works as
latency compensation
[
39
]
, the
augmented state extended Kalman filter (AS-EKF)
[
40
]
, and
reduced-order Kalman filtering with relative measurements
[
41
]
.
Indeed, this list is not meant to be exhaustive, but rather to illustrate the range of terminologies that effectively describe the same underlying method.

A related term that is used in the context of delayed measurements is
measurement extrapolation

[
42
]
or
Larsen’s method

[
43
]
.
This approach derives a linear expression that maps a delayed measurement—dependent on a past state—to the current time step, thereby relating the past measurement to the current state and motivating the term “extrapolation.”
The resulting correlation between measurement noise and state uncertainty is then explicitly modeled and incorporated in the Kalman filter update.
Similar to the delayed-state Kalman filter, this approach handles the resulting correlations directly within the filter equations, rather than through state augmentation.
While specifically designed for delayed measurements, this approach can also be framed within the more general delayed-state Kalman filter formulation
[
18
]
.

Given the variety of terminology that has been used for closely related techniques, in this work we adopt the widely used term
stochastic cloning (SC)
to refer to the augmentation of the state vector with prior states, and we use Brown’s terminology,
delayed-state Kalman filter (DSKF)
, for the generalized Kalman filter formulation that explicitly accounts for correlations between the prior state and measurements when handling delayed-state measurements.

II
Traditional Kalman Filter

II-A

Dynamics and Observation Models

For a linear, discrete Kalman filter
[
44
,
18
]
, we suppose linear dynamics propagate the state at time
t_{j}
to time
t_{k}>t_{j}

\displaystyle x_{k}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{k,j}x_{j}+B_{k}u_{k}+G_{k}w_{k},

(1)

where
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the state at time
t_{k}
,
u_{k}
is the action, and
w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{k})
is the Gaussian process noise with covariance
Q_{k}
.

\Phi_{k,j}
is the state transition model from
t_{j}
to
t_{k}
,
B_{k}
is the control-input model, and
G_{k}
is the noise coupling matrix, mapping the process noise to its effect in the state.

Observations are modeled as a linear function of the state

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=H_{k}x_{k}+v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(2)

where
H_{k}
is the linear observation model and
v_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R_{k})
is the Gaussian measurement noise with covariance
R_{k}
.

II-B

Predict and Update Steps

Given the state estimate and covariance at
j<k
, the predicted state and covariance at
t_{k}
is evaluated as

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}=\Phi_{k,j}{\hat{x}}_{j}+B_{k}u_{k},

(3)

\displaystyle{P}^{-}_{k}=\Phi_{k,j}{P}_{j}{\Phi^{\top}_{k,j}}+S_{k},

(4)

where

{P}_{j}
is the covariance of the prior estimate
\hat{x}_{j}
,
the superscript
-
signifies terms predicted forward in time, and the process noise covariance is

\displaystyle S_{k}\triangleq G_{k}Q_{k}G^{\top}_{k}.

(5)

After obtaining the set of observations
\tilde{y}_{k}
, the Kalman filter evaluates the measurement innovation
\delta\tilde{y}_{k}
and covariance
\Upsilon_{k}

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{y}_{k}=\tilde{y}_{k}-H_{k}{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k},

(6)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{k}=H_{k}{P}^{-}_{k}H^{\top}_{k}+R_{k},

(7)

then maps this information to the state space via the Kalman gain
K_{k}
in order to evaluate the updated state and covariance

\displaystyle K_{k}={P}^{-}_{k}H^{\top}_{k}\Upsilon^{-1}_{k},

(8)

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}^{+}_{k}={\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+K_{k}\delta\tilde{y}_{k},

(9)

\displaystyle{P}^{+}_{k}=(\mathbb{I}-K_{k}H_{k}){P}^{-}_{k}(\mathbb{I}-K_{k}H_{k})^{\top}+K_{k}R_{k}K^{\top}_{k},

(10)

where
+
denotes quanities updated with the corresponding observation at the given time step, and
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix.

III
Delayed-State Dependence in Sequential Filters

A fundamental assumption in conventional sequential filtering is that the state follows a
Markov process

[
45
]
.
In other words, the random process
(x_{k}:k\in\mathbb{T})
describing the state over the set of times
\mathbb{T}
satisfies the
Markov property
, allowing for the conditional independence relation

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{x_{k+1}|x_{k},x_{k-1},\cdots,x_{0}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{x_{k+1}|x_{k}\right\}.

(11)

Sequential filters oftentimes assume the observations
(\tilde{y}_{k}:k\in\mathbb{T})
follow a
hidden Markov model
(HMM) with respect to the state, satisfying a similar conditional independence relation

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\tilde{y}_{k}|x_{k},x_{k-1},\cdots,x_{0}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\tilde{y}_{k}|x_{k}\right\}.

(12)

Indeed, the Kalman filter observation model in Eqs. (
2
) and (
12
) is only a function of the current state,
which inherently follows a HMM, due to its conditional independence from prior states, given the current state.

A delayed-state dependency poses a problem for sequential filtering, since it breaks the HMM assumption.
For example, LiDAR odometry provides a relative motion measurement between two positions at different times,
t_{k}
and
t_{j}

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{k}=(p_{k}-p_{j})+v_{k},

(13)

with
p_{k}
denoting the position state at time
t_{k}
.
As written, the observation model
(x_{k}:k\in\mathbb{T})
no longer follows a HMM.

IV
Stochastic Cloning Kalman Filter

Stochastic cloning (SC)
[
29
]
handles delayed-state dependencies by augmenting the state vector to regain Markovity

\displaystyle\breve{x}_{k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}x_{j}^{\top}&x_{k}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(14)

While it is possible to augment the state with prior states from multiple times—and the extension to an arbitrary number of times is straightforward—for clarity, we focus on the case involving two times in this work.

The linear measurement with delayed-state dependence can be modeled with respect to the augmented state in Eq. (
14
) as

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\breve{H}_{k}\breve{x}_{k}+v_{k}.

(15)

Indeed, for the LiDAR odometry case in Eq. (
13
), letting
{x}_{k}=p_{k}
enforces the augmented measurement matrix to be
\breve{H}_{k}=[-\mathbb{I}~\mathbb{I}]
.
Thus, by re-defining the state, the observation model re-follows a HMM with respect to the augmented state.

IV-A

Predict Step

For the predict step, the augmented state estimate becomes

\displaystyle{\breve{\hat{x}}}^{-}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{\hat{x}}^{+}_{j}\\
{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n},

(16)

thereby concatenating (or “cloning”) the previous state estimate at time
t_{j}
with the original predicted state estimate.
The dynamics for the augmented system can be represented as

\displaystyle{\breve{\hat{x}}}^{-}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}&\mathbb{I}_{n}\\
\mathbf{0}_{n}&\Phi_{k,j}\end{bmatrix}{\breve{\hat{x}}}^{+}_{j}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
B_{k}\end{bmatrix}u_{k},

(17)

\displaystyle\triangleq\breve{\Phi}_{k,j}{\breve{\hat{x}}}^{+}_{j}+\breve{B}_{k}u_{k},

(18)

where
\mathbf{0}_{n}
is an
{n\times n}
matrix of zeros and
\mathbb{I}_{n}
is the
{n\times n}
identity matrix.
The augmented predicted covariance is

\displaystyle{\breve{P}}^{-}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{P}^{+}_{j}&{P}^{+}_{j}\Phi^{\top}_{k,j}\\
\Phi_{k,j}{P}^{+}_{j}&{P}^{-}_{k}\end{bmatrix},

(19)

The covariance prediction can also be represented in the traditional Kalman filtering formulation as

\displaystyle{\breve{P}}^{-}_{k}=\breve{\Phi}_{k,j}{\breve{P}}^{+}_{j}\breve{\Phi}^{\top}_{k,j}+\breve{S}_{k},

(20)

\displaystyle\breve{S}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{n}&\mathbf{0}_{n}\\
\mathbf{0}_{n}&S_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

IV-B

Update Step

With the augmented state, the update step can be performed in an identical manner as the traditional Kalman filter using the delayed-state-dependent measurement model in Eq. (
15
).
The innovation is computed with the augmented state as

\displaystyle{\delta\tilde{y}}_{k}=\tilde{y}_{k}-\breve{H}_{k}{\breve{\hat{x}}}^{-}_{k}

(22)

\displaystyle{\Upsilon}_{k}=\breve{H}_{k}{\breve{P}}^{-}_{k}\breve{H}^{\top}_{k}+R_{k},

(23)

then mapped to the state space for the Kalman update as

\displaystyle\breve{K}_{k}={\breve{P}}^{-}_{k}\breve{H}^{\top}_{k}{\Upsilon}^{-1}_{k},

(24)

\displaystyle{\breve{\hat{x}}}^{+}_{k}={\breve{\hat{x}}}^{-}_{k}+\breve{K}_{k}{\delta\tilde{y}}_{k},

(25)

\displaystyle{\breve{P}}^{+}_{k}=(\mathbb{I}-\breve{K}_{k}\breve{H}_{k}){\breve{P}}^{-}_{k}(\mathbb{I}-\breve{K}_{k}\breve{H}_{k})^{\top}+\breve{K}_{k}R_{k}\breve{K}^{\top}_{k}.

(26)

Note that the update step acts on the complete augmented state.
However, from Eq. (
17
), the cloned state will be discarded in the next predict step.
Thus, to reduce computational cost, one can only update the last
n
elements of the augmented vector, corresponding to the original state of interest

\displaystyle\breve{K}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\cdot&\breve{K}_{k,k}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

(27)

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}^{+}_{k}={\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+\breve{K}_{k,k}{\delta\tilde{y}}_{k}.

(28)

V
Delayed-State Kalman Filter

The delayed-state dependency can also be handled by explicitly modeling the correlation between the measurement and prior state.
Traditional Kalman filters assume these are conditionally uncorrelated given the current state, implying uncorrelated process and measurement noise at time
t_{k}
.
This section outlines the derivation of a generalized version of the Kalman filter
[
18
]
, called the
delayed-state Kalman filter
, which introduces additional terms in the Kalman gain to account for these dependencies before the update step.

To begin, we use Eq. (
15
) to define our measurement model

\displaystyle\tilde{y}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{H}_{j,k}&{H}_{k,k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{{x}}_{j}\\
{{x}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}+v_{k},

(29)

where
{H}_{j,k}
is the measurement matrix coefficient at time
t_{k}
with respect to the prior state at time
t_{j}
.
The corresponding innovation, based on measurements up to time
t_{j}
, becomes

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{y}_{k}=\tilde{y}_{k}-\begin{bmatrix}{H}_{j,k}&{H}_{k,k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\hat{x}}^{+}_{j}\\
{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

We now seek to express the innovation in terms of the predicted state error, in order to derive the statistics of the delayed-state measurement innovation.
This allows us to subsequently derive the state uncertainty after the DSKF update.
In many real-world systems, the state transition model
\Phi_{k,j}
is invertible, allowing
\Phi_{j,k}
to be expressed as its inverse.
Thus, we express the prior state in terms of the predicted state as

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}^{+}_{j}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{j,k}\left({\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}-B_{k}u_{k}\right).

(31)

Substituting Eq. (
31
) into Eq. (
30
) gives

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\tilde{y}_{k}-\left({H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}+{H}_{k,k}\right){\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}B_{k}u_{k}.

(32)

Using our measurement model from Eq. (
29
), we can express the innovation in terms of the true state

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{H}_{j,k}&{H}_{k,k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{{x}}_{j}\\
{{x}}_{k}\end{bmatrix}+v_{k}

\displaystyle\quad-\left({H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}+{H}_{k,k}\right){\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}B_{k}u_{k},

(33)

Using Eq. (
1
), the true prior state can be represented in terms of the the current state as

\displaystyle x_{j}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{j,k}\left(x_{k}-B_{k}u_{k}-G_{k}w_{k}\right).

(34)

Replacing this expression for
x_{j}
in Eq. (
33
) gives us

\displaystyle\delta\tilde{y}_{k}=\left({H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}+{H}_{k,k}\right){\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}-{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}G_{k}w_{k}+v_{k},

(35)

\displaystyle{\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}\triangleq{x}_{k}-{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}.

(36)

The generalized Kalman filter state update and error is

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}^{+}_{k}={\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+\mathcal{K}_{k}{\delta\tilde{y}}_{k},

(37)

\displaystyle{\delta\hat{x}}^{+}_{k}\triangleq x_{k}-{\hat{x}}^{+}_{k}=x_{k}-{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}-\mathcal{K}_{k}{\delta\tilde{y}}_{k},

(38)

where
\mathcal{K}_{k}
denotes a general Kalman gain, derived later after deriving the state update covariance.
For simpler notation going forward, let us also define the following terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{k}\triangleq{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{k}\triangleq{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}+{H}_{k,k},

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{k}\triangleq{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}S_{k},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{k}\triangleq\mathcal{J}_{k}S_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}^{\top}+R_{k}.

(40)

Substituting Eq. (
35
) into Eq. (
38
) and simplifying gives us

\displaystyle{\delta\hat{x}}^{+}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\left({H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}+{H}_{k,k}\right)\right){\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{K}_{k}{H}_{j,k}\Phi_{j,k}G_{k}w_{k}-\mathcal{K}_{k}v_{k}

(41)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right){\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}G_{k}w_{k}-\mathcal{K}_{k}v_{k}

(42)

Note that the predicted state error
{\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}
and process noise
w_{k}
are zero-mean, and using Eq. (
1
) have a cross-covariance of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}w^{\top}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Phi_{k,j}{\delta\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}+G_{k}w_{k}\right)w^{\top}_{k}\right],

(43)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{k,j}\mathbb{E}\left[{\delta\hat{x}}^{+}_{j}w^{\top}_{k}\right]+G_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[w_{k}w^{\top}_{k}\right],

(44)

\displaystyle=G_{k}Q_{k},

(45)

where the last simplification step is due to the independence of the prior state error
{\delta\hat{x}}^{+}_{j}
from the current process noise
w_{k}
.

We now evaluate the state update covariance using Eq. (
42
), noting that the measurement noise is uncorrelated with the prior process noise and state error

\displaystyle{P}^{+}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right){P}^{-}_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)G_{k}Q_{k}\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}G_{k}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}G_{k}\right)Q_{k}G^{\top}_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}G_{k}\right)Q_{k}\left(\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{J}_{k}G_{k}\right)^{\top}+\mathcal{K}_{k}R_{k}\mathcal{K}^{\top}_{k}.

(46)

Using Eq. (
5
), we reintroduce the process noise covariance
S_{k}
and rearrange terms to reintroduce
\mathcal{N}_{k}
and
\mathcal{R}_{k}
from Eq. (
40
)

\displaystyle{P}^{+}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right){P}^{-}_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)^{\top}+\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)\mathcal{N}^{\top}_{k}\mathcal{K}^{\top}_{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{N}_{k}\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{H}_{k}\right)^{\top}+\mathcal{K}_{k}\mathcal{R}_{k}\mathcal{K}^{\top}_{k},

(47)

where the corresponding Kalman gain can be found to be
[
18
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left({P}^{-}_{k}\mathcal{H}^{\top}_{k}-\mathcal{N}^{\top}_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\mathcal{H}_{k}{P}^{-}_{k}\mathcal{H}^{\top}_{k}-\mathcal{N}_{k}\mathcal{H}^{\top}_{k}-\mathcal{H}_{k}\mathcal{N}^{\top}_{k}+\mathcal{R}_{k}\right)^{-1}.

(48)

Notice that if
{H}_{j,k}=0
(i.e., the measurement does not depend on the prior state), then both
\mathcal{J}_{k}
and
\mathcal{N}_{k}
become zero, while
\mathcal{H}_{k}\to H_{k}
and
\mathcal{R}_{k}\to R_{k}
, recovering the traditional Kalman filter update in Eq. (

) and classical Kalman gain in Eq. (

VI
Equivalence of Both Delayed-State Filters

We now prove the equivalence of stochastic cloning and the delayed-state Kalman filter by induction.
The base case is trivial, as both filters start with the same belief.
Assuming equivalence at time
t_{j}
, we show equivalence is maintained at the next update time
t_{k}
.
Section
VI-A
shows equivalence of the updated state estimate, and Section
VI-B
demonstrates the equivalence of the covariance update.

VI-A

Equivalence of State Update

Let us examine the state update for both filters in Eqs. (
28
) and (
37
).
Given the prior updated estimate
{\hat{x}}^{+}_{j}
, both delayed-state filters yield the same predicted state
{\hat{x}}^{-}_{k}
, since the prediction step follows the same state dynamics.
The innovation
{\delta\tilde{y}}_{k}
is also identical, so verifying the equivalence of the Kalman gain confirms the equivalence of state updates.

We first examine the stochastic cloning Kalman gain
\breve{K}_{k,k}

\breve{K}_{k}

From Eq. (

\displaystyle\breve{K}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cdot&\cdot\\

Replacing Eq. (

{P}^{+}_{j}

Through some algebra, we write out the matrices to get

\displaystyle\breve{K}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\cdot\\

Extracting the bottom
n
rows to get the Kalman gain associated with the state update

\displaystyle\breve{K}_{k,k}

Recognizing that

VIII
Conclusion

Stochastic cloning (SC) is a widely used method for processing delayed-state measurements—such as odometry—in robotics by augmenting the current state with a prior one.
This approach is often justified by the common (mistaken) assertion that Kalman filters, with the state defined at a single time, cannot fully account for cross-time correlations.
This work demonstrates that a properly derived Kalman filter, like the lesser-known but well-established delayed-state Kalman filter (DSKF), yields identical state and covariance updates to those of SC, but without the need for artificial state augmentation.
Furthermore, the more general delayed-state Kalman filter is shown to require fewer computations and, in many cases, less memory to produce the same estimation solution.

To be clear, we are not suggesting that one method is better or worse than the other—after all, they produce the exact same result and implementation preferences may vary.
Rather, we aim to explicitly demonstrate and remind the community that: (1) state augmentation is not the only way to properly account for correlations and (2) it is sometimes an inefficient framework.
Assertions that Kalman filter variants cannot properly account for correlations are simply not true.
