A simple and effective hydrogel dressing for advanced management of full-thickness skin wound by multi-functional strategies
Authors: Deqiao Dong, Lujia Mao, Zhenmiao Qin, Yueping Guo, Jiaqi Yu, Xue Hu, Jie He, Shiquan Feng, Min Zhang, Yan Liu, Yanan Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In clinical practice, wound treatment continues to be a significant concern. The wound healing process is intricate and continuous. Hemostasis is the initial stage and the primary variables influencing wound healing include bacterial infection and oxidative stress. To tackle these issues, a novel hydrogel dressing was constructed, composed of chitosan hydrochloride (CSCl), hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC), glucose and resveratrol nanoemulsion (RES-NE). The innovative RES-NE loaded chitosan hydrogel (RN-CH) features controllable gelation time, rapid self-healing property, appropriate injectability, and strong tissue adhesion ability. Additionally, it exhibits excellent biocompatibility, antioxidant property, antibacterial activity, and significant hemostasis capability. Pharmacodynamic experiments reveal that RN-CH can effectively promote full-thickness wounds healing by modulating inflammation, mitigating oxidative stress, and facilitating early angiogenesis and collagen formation.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03803-2.

Introduction

The human skin is the largest organ in the body, performing vital physiological roles such as controlling fluid balance, regulating body temperature, and guarding against the infiltration of harmful germs from outside the body. Additionally, it is one of the most susceptible tissues to damage, therefore being prone to trauma [1]. Over the past few decades, our understanding of trauma physiology and treatment has improved remarkably. Unfortunately, even today trauma remains a significant contributor to mortality and disability worldwide, representing a persistent public health challenge [2]. The wound healing process is complicated, involving many cellular and biological mechanisms, and can typically divided into four stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [3]. Early studies once believed that keeping skin wounds dry and uncovered could promote healing. With the development of the first generation of biofilms, it was found that dressings could significantly accelerate the wound healing efficiency [4]. Thereafter, numerous studies on the construction of dressings with different structures and functions for wound repair have emerged, such as electrospun fibers, foams, sponges, films and hydrogels [5–8]. Among them, hydrogel dressings not only have good hydrophilicity and permeability, but also can load drugs or bioactive molecules to achieve functions such as sterilization and promoting cell proliferation [9–11]. As a type of wound dressing with excellent properties, hydrogel has garnered significant interest from many researchers. Especially in recent years, studies on hydrogel wound dressings have shown an obvious increasing trend [12].

Among hydrogel-forming materials, chitosan (CS) has good prospects for development and application. CS is the sole positively charged alkaline polysaccharide found in nature, exhibiting excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, hemostatic properties, antibacterial efficacy, and capabilities in promoting wound healing [13, 14]. The limited water solubility of CS restricts its potential applications. The modification of CS by incorporating various hydrophilic functional groups can solve this problem [15]. CS modified by hydrochloride not only increases its water solubility but also enhances its antibacterial efficacy, making it suitable for use in the biomedical field [16]. To create a hydrogel with particular properties, CS is often used in conjunction with other polymer materials, and hydroxyethyl cellulose (HEC) is one of them.

HEC is a type of cellulose ether produced through the alkalization of cellulose using sodium hydroxide, followed by the etherification process involving ethylene oxide [17]. HEC can be dissolved in cold water, hot water, strong and weak acid/bases, and some organic solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide and dimethylformamide. It is the third largest cellulose derivative after carboxymethyl cellulose and hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose [18]. HEC possesses numerous outstanding excipient properties, such as non-toxicity, good mechanical properties, high water retention, film formation, dispersion, emulsification, etc., and is commonly used as thickener of hydrogels [19]. HEC-derived dressings emulate the characteristics of the native extracellular matrix microenvironment, thereby enhancing cellular adhesion and proliferation. According to previous research, a novel temperature-responsive hydrogel, formulated from CS, graphene oxide, β-glycerophosphate (β-GP) and HEC, was utilized as a delivery system for progranulin and its derivative Atsttrin. This hydrogel creates a distinctive biochemical and biomechanical environment that promotes the recovery of diabetic fractures [20]. Qu et al. used CSCl, β-GP and HEC to prepare a blank hydrogel, which was loaded with α-tocopherol liposome to combat oxidative stress and enhance the survival rate of cardiomyocytes [21]. In the CSCl/β-GP/HEC hydrogel system, HEC can lower the concentration of β-GP to create a temperature-sensitive hydrogel. In our study, we found that the CS/HEC/glucose system can also form an injectable hydrogel. Its crosslinking mechanism might be due to the following two reasons: the Van der Waals interaction between macromolecular of CS and HEC; a large number of hydroxyl groups of glucose (uniformly mixed in CS and HEC solution) are connected with the amino groups on CS and the hydroxyl groups on HEC through a series of hydrogen bonds, which further pull the macromolecular closer and gradually form hydrogels. In theory, the biocompatibility of the above hydrogel will be greatly improved compared with that of the CSCl/β-GP/HEC system.

To improving the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of hydrogels, resveratrol (RES) is a good candidate. RES is a natural polyphenolic stilbene compound derived from various plants, including polygonum cuspidatum, grapes, and mulberries [22]. It is a plant antitoxin synthesized by plants themselves in response to external stimuli. The chemical name of RES is 3–4’−5-trihydroxystilbene. It has both cis and trans configurations, of which the trans isomer is more stable and exhibits greater biological active [23]. RES demonstrates a variety of pharmacological effects, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-infective, neuroprotective, cardiovascular protective, and hepatoprotective properties [24–26]. However, its poor water solubility and susceptibility to light-induced decomposition result in low bioavailability and instability, which limit its application in biomedicine field. Nanoemulsions are expected to solve these problems [27]. Nanoemulsions represent a thermodynamically stable system with an average particle size ranging from 50 to 200 nm, prepared by shear, high-pressure homogenization or ultrasound by combining appropriate proportions of water, oil, emulsifier and coemulsifier [28].

In this study, we developed a novel multifunctional hydrogel dressing designed to address multiple challenges in wound healing simultaneously. Although the role of RES-loaded hydrogel formulations in wound healing has been reported [29–32], to the best of our knowledge, the whole new injectable hydrogel composed of CSCl/HEC/glucose has not been reported elsewhere. The prepared RES-NE loaded chitosan hydrogel (RN-CH) synergistically integrates good swelling, self-healing, injectability, and robust tissue adhesion, which are critical for clinical application and wound conformability, with a multifunctional therapeutic profile. In addition, the hydrogel demonstrated effective antibacterial properties against Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA). Cytocompatibility assessment and histocompatibility evaluation confirmed the excellent biocompatibility of RN-CH. The antioxidant characteristics of RN-CH were validated through in vitro experiments involving free radical scavenging as well as intracellular antioxidant assessments. The hemostatic properties of RN-CH were examined using a liver hemorrhage model. The impact of RN-CH on promoting wound healing was verified through wound contraction, epidermal thickness, collagen coverage, and immunofluorescence staining of inflammatory factors by using a full-thickness skin wound model in mice. The results show that RN-CH is a promising hydrogel wound dressing and is expected to be further developed to treat wounds. To the best of our knowledge, the entirely new injectable hydrogel composed of CSCl/HEC/glucose has not been reported elsewhere.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of RES-NE

RES-NE was formulated using triglyceride triacetate as oil phase, polyoxyethylene castor oil EL-60 as emulsifier, PEG 400 as co-emulsifier and water as aqueous phase. Glycerin was added as an osmotic pressure regulator. First, RES was dissolved in a mixture of triglyceride triacetate, polyoxyethylene castor oil EL-60, PEG 400 and glycerin. Second, an appropriate amount of water was added to the above oily solution while maintaining stirring at 50℃ for 30 min. Then the crude emulsion obtained was further subjected to homogenization through a microfluidization high-pressure homogenizer 4 times at 31,500 psi (NanoGenizer-30 K, Genizer, USA) to produce a nanoemulsion.

The morphology of RES-NE was analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7800, Hitachi, Japan). The RES-NE sample was appropriately diluted with water and applied to carbon-coated copper grids, followed by negative staining with a drop of 2% phosphotungstic acid solution. After drying, the samples were examined and photographed by TEM at 100 kV. A dynamic light scattering highly sensitive zeta potential and particle size analyzer (DLS, NanoBrook 90plus PALS, Brookhaven, USA) was used to examine the particle size and zeta potential of RES-NE, blank nanoemulsion (Blank-NE), and RES aqueous suspension. For each sample, measurements were made three times, and average results were calculated.

The 7-day accelerated stability experiment of RES-NE was executed under the conditions of 4 °C (protected from light), 30 °C (protected from light), and 30 °C (exposed to light) by a drug stability test chamber (BXZ-250SI, Shanghai Boxun Medical Biological Instrument Corp, China). The changes in drug content, particle size, and zeta potential of RES-NE were investigated respectively.

Preparation and characterization of RN-CH

CSCl was first dissolved in 0.45% D-anhydrous glucose solution to obtain a 2% solution, with 0.3% NaHCO3 as pH regulator. HEC was dissolved in 0.45% D-anhydrous glucose solution to obtain a 2.75% solution, with 0.6% NaHCO3 as pH regulator. HEC was added to the CSCl solution in accordance with the predetermined mass ratio and mixed uniformly to get blank hydrogel (abbreviated as CH). Similarly, RES-NE was incorporated into the CH precursor solution to get RN-CH. The gelation time of CH and RN-CH was determined by a tube inversion method at 37 ℃ [29]. When the solution stopped flowing by inverting the vial, the state was characterized as gel, while the liquid state that flows was referred to as sol.

The fully gelled complete gelation hydrogel (approximately 1 g) was measured to determine its wet weight, referred to as Ww. Following freeze-drying in a vacuum freeze dryer, the dry weight of the hydrogel was recorded and designated as Wd. The water content of the hydrogel was then calculated using the specified formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{Water content}}(\%)=({W}{\text{w}}-{W}{\text{d}})/{W}{\text{w}}\times100$$\end{document}

In vitro swelling studies of CH and RN-CH were performed in the presence of PBS at room temperature. Pre-weighed hydrogel (~ 1 g) was taken in its liquid form, placed in a 37 °C water bath until gelled, and then 5 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) buffer was added. After 24 h, the PBS buffer was discarded, and the surface water was removed with filter paper. The hydrogel before and after swelling was weighed. The swelling percentage was determined by comparing the weight change observed before and after the swelling process.

Cryo-scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM) images of samples were obtained using SU3500 SEM equipment (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) with a QuorumP P3010 cryopreparation system (Quorum Technologies, East Sussex, UK). Hydrogel samples were sliced into pieces about 1–2−2 mm. Initially, the samples were submerged in nitrogen slush for rapid freezing and subsequently moved to a cryo-preparation chamber under vacuum. There, they underwent freeze fracture, followed by sublimation at −80 °C for 15 min, and were then platinum-coated at 10 mA for 60 s. Finally, imaging was performed at −175 °C and 10 kV utilizing secondary electron signals.

The time when the hydrogel ceased to flow was recorded as the gelation time. To analyze the chemical structure of the hydrogel and the intermolecular interactions, CSCl powder, glucose powder, and HEC powder were each mixed with an appropriate amount of KBr and pressed into tablets. The physical mixture containing glucose, CSCl, and HEC, along with the blank hydrogel lyophilized product, underwent this process. Following drying, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Nicolet IS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was employed to scan the spectra over the range of 400 cm−1 to 4000 cm−1. The pH value of hydrogels was assessed using a pH meter (PHSJ-3 F, Inesa Scientific, Shanghai, China) equipped with a planar pH composite electrode (E-201-P, Inesa Scientific, Shanghai, China).

In vitro RES release

The RES release from RN-CH was executed according to previous literature [30]. Briefly, a total of 2 g of RES suspension loaded chitosan hydrogel (RES-CH) or RN-CH was placed in a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 3500 and immersed in 100 mL of PBS at pH 7.2, which contained 0.5% (w/v) Tween 80, and incubated at 37 °C while shaking at 100 rpm. At different time points, 1 mL of the supernatant was withdrawn and replaced with 1 mL of fresh medium. The concentration of RES was determined using HPLC (Ultimate3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, and the volume of injection was set at 10 µL. The eluent was monitored with an ultraviolet absorption detector at 305 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 42% methanol and 58% water, with a flow rate of 1 mL/min, and maintained at a detection temperature of 35 °C. Adhesive property of RN-CH

The adhesion characteristics of RN-CH was assessed through a visual adhesion experiment, and it adhesive strength was quantitatively evaluated using lap shear testing on porcine skin [31]. First, the adhesion characteristics of the hydrogels were assessed through a visual adhesion experiment. The qualitative analysis involved observing the adhesion of hydrogels to various biological tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney of mouse and porcine skin), as well as to abiotic materials (glass, rubber and metal), by determining whether the hydrogels maintain adhesion or detached. Subsequently, the adhesive strength of the hydrogels was quantitatively evaluated using lap shear testing on porcine skin. Briefly, fresh porcine skin was immersed in PBS at pH 7.4, the fat layer was removed, and the skin was cut into rectangular pieces measuring 5 cm by 2.5 cm. The hydrogel was applied between two samples of porcine skin, with a gel area of 1.5 cm × 2.5 cm. Once bonded, the lap shear test was performed in tensile mode using a universal testing machine (SHK-A102, Suzhou Jianzhuo Instrument Technology, China), at a tensile rate of 5 mm·min−1, and the maximum tensile strength was recorded. All tests were repeated 3 times.

Rheological properties of RN-CH

The viscoelastic and mechanical characteristics of the hydrogels were examined through rheological measurements conducted at room temperature. These assessments were carried out using a Discovery HR-2 rheometer (TA Instruments, US) equipped with a 60 mm diameter stainless-steel parallel plate rotor. The linear viscoelastic region of hydrogels was detected under oscillation amplitude sweep mode performed from 1% strain to 1000% strain. The gelation behavior was studied by frequency sweep experiment performed from 0.1 to 10 Hz under 1% strain. The shear-thinning behavior of the hydrogel was assessed through a steady flow sweep, with shear rates varying from 0.1 to 400 s−1. The hydrogel self-healing performance was evaluated through a cyclic strain test with oscillatory strains alternatively switched from 1% to 1000% at the shear frequency of 1 Hz.

Injectable and self-healing properties of RN-CH

The injectable and self-healing properties of hydrogel were double validated by macroscopic visual observation. For the injectable experiment, the hydrogel close to gelatinization was slowly pushed through a syringe with a 20 G needle, and words such as HMC were written on the glass dish. For the self-healing experiment, two different color hydrogels were cut in half, the obtained four hydrogel blocks were put together and placed for 30 min, then the hydrogel was picked up with tweezers to observe its connection and integrity.

Hemolysis assay

Hemolysis activity test of hydrogels was conducted according to previous literature [31]. Erythrocytes were separated by centrifuging rat blood with anticoagulants at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The isolated erythrocytes were washed three times with PBS and subsequently diluted to a final concentration of 5% (v/v) using PBS. A mixture of hydrogel (300 µL) and erythrocyte stock (700 µL) was prepared in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. Triton X-100 at a concentration of 0.1% served as the positive control, while PBS served as the negative control. Following incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well microplate. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX, Bio-Tek). Intracellular ROS-scavenging evaluation

According to previous literature [29], the reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit (DCFH-DA and DHE) was used to assess the effect of RN-CH on the production of intracellular ROS in L929 cells subjected to oxidative stress. DCFH-DA, upon oxidation by intracellular ROS, is converted into the fluorescent compound dichlorofluorescein (DCF), which emits green fluorescence. This fluorescence serves as an indicator for quantifying the levels of ROS present within the cells. Intracellular superoxide anion can dehydrogenate DHE to produce ethidium, which can bind to DNA or RNA to produce red fluorescence. Firstly, a hydrogel sample (0.1 g, wet weight) was immersed in 1 mL of complete cell-culture medium and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a light-shielded, after which the supernatant was collected as the hydrogel extract. Concurrently, L929 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, and maintained at 37℃ in 5% CO2 for 24 h. Culture medium containing H2O2 (200 µM) was used to induce cellular oxidative stress and served as the control (+) group. Hydrogel extract containing H2O2 (200 µM) comprised the experimental group, and culture medium without H2O2 served as the control (-) group. After 24 h, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. The cells were then incubated with either 20 µM DCFH-DA or 5 µM DHE solution for 30 min. Then the DCFH-DA/DHE solution was removed and 400 µL 4% paraformaldehyde was added to each well to fix the cells. After 10 min, the fixing solution was removed and the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 10 min. The entire procedure was carried out at 37 °C in the dark; specific light-shielding measures included switching off the lighting system and wrapping the plate in aluminum foil during transport. Fluorescence images were captured using inverted fluorescence microscopy (OLYMPUS IX51 + DP80) in the dark, and the fluorescence intensity of DCF/ethidium was subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software.

In vitro antibacterial assay

The antibacterial properties of hydrogels were qualitatively assessed through the co-culture of materials with gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and MRSA) and gram-negative bacteria (E. coli and PA). Initially, the antibacterial activity was evaluated by the plate colony counting method. S. aureus, MRSA, E. coli and PA were incubated in 10 mL LB medium for 12 h (37℃, 100 rpm). For S. aureus and E. coli, 200 µL hydrogel was placed in a 24-well cell culture plate, to which 10 µL of bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was added onto the surface of the hydrogel. After co-cultured for 30 min, 1 ml PBS was added per well and continued to culture for 2 h at 37℃. For MRSA and PA, 500 µL hydrogel was placed in a 24-well cell culture plate, to which 100 µL of bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was added onto the surface of the hydrogel. After co-cultured for 1 h, 1 ml PBS was added per well and continued to culture for 1 h at 37℃. The bacterial were re-suspended with PBS and 100 µL of bacterial suspension was coated to LB agar plates and incubated in a bacterial incubator at 37℃ for 24 h. For the control group, the same volume of bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was added to the empty 24-well culture plate, the subsequent experimental steps were the same as above. The colonies on the plate were photographed and counted. In vitro hemostatic assay

The whole blood-clotting ability test was performed referring to the previous literature with some modifications [35]. 1 mL hydrogel was introduced in the bottom of a sterile centrifuge tube; after gelation, 200 µL of heparin sodium anticoagulated whole blood and 20 µL of 0.2 M CaCl2 aqueous solution were dropped on the surface of the hydrogel, incubated at 37℃ for 10 min, then 20 mL water was added to the centrifuge tube to disrupt red blood cells that had not been trapped in the thrombus. The group without hydrogel was served as positive control. The supernatant was collected and the optical absorbance measured at 540 nm was executed using a microplate analyzer (SynergyHTX, Bio-Tek). where Ahydrogel and Apositive are the absorbance values of the test group and positive control group, respectively. In addition, in order to better observe the coagulation phenomenon of the hydrogels, heparin sodium anticoagulated blood (2 drops) was brought into direct contact with petri dish, commercially available gauze (0.5 × 0.5 cm), CH or RN-CH (~ 100 µL). After 5 min, 2–3 drops of water were added on top to observe the hemolysis phenomenon.

In vivo hemostasis experiment

In vivo hemostasis experiment was conducted using a rat-liver hemorrhage model according to reference [36]. Sprague-Dawley rats (SD, 200 ± 20 g, male), obtained from Changsha Tianqin Biotechnology Co., Ltd (SCXK 2022-0011), were utilized to assess the hemostatic efficacy of RN-CH. Briefly, rats were anesthetized by injecting 3% wt pentobarbital sodium, and then secured on a surgical corkboard. An abdominal incision was made to expose the liver, and serous fluid surrounding the liver was carefully removed. A pre-weighed filter paper placed on a polyethylene film was positioned beneath the liver. Hemorrhaging from the liver was induced using a 20 G needle angled at approximately 30° relative to the corkboard, followed by the immediate application of 100 µL of hydrogel to the bleeding site via a syringe; each group contained 3 rats. Photographs were captured with a digital camera at intervals of 0, 10, 30, 90, and 180 s. After three minutes, the weight of the filter paper, now containing absorbed blood, was recorded and compared to that of a control group (no treatment after pricking the liver).

In vivo wound healing of RN-CH

In vivo wound healing was evaluated using a full-thickness skin defect model, as indicated in previous literature [37]. A total of 24 Kunming (KM) mice (aged 6–8 weeks, weighing 20–25 g) were obtained from Hunan Slack Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (SCXK 2019-0004). An intraperitoneal injection of 0.8% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) was administered to induce anesthesia, followed by the creation of a full-thickness wound measuring 8 mm in diameter on the shaved dorsal skink. The wounds were treated with 100 µL of normal saline, a commercial hydrogel (URGO hydrogel, Germany), CH, or RN-CH over a period of 15 days, with 6 animals per group. The hydrogels were changed every 3 days, and wound images were taken on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15. Histopathological and immunological analysis

Histological and immunofluorescent staining were conducted to evaluate vascular remodeling and inflammatory conditions in wound tissue during healing process. Brifly, mice were euthanized (inhalant isoflurane overdose followed by cervical dislocation) on days 6 and 15, after which regenerated skin samples were excised and collected. The samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated using a gradient of ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared and subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration for H&E and Masson trichrome staining to assess the structure and composition of the neotissue. All sections were analyzed and imaged using a microscope (Nikon ECLIPSEE100, Japan). ImageJ software was employed to quantify the blue-stained area to determine the proportion of collagen deposition.

The regenerated skins from the wound site were also excised on the 6th and 15th day for immunofluorescence (IF) staining. The expression of anti-inflammatory factor (IL-4) and pro-inflammatory factor (IL-6) was detected by immunofluorescence method after sectioning to access changes in the wound microenvironment. The effect of hydrogels on angiogenesis was observed by immunofluorescence staining of platelet-endothelial cell adhesion molecule (CD31) in wound tissue at day 15. Briefly, the sections were stained with anti-IL-4 antibody, anti-IL-6, antibody and anti-CD31 antibody, respectively. CY3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG was used as the secondary antibody to reveal IL-4, IL-6, and CD31 expression. The nuclei were counterstained using a DAPI-containing mounting solution. Observations were conducted using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse ci, Japan). Finally, the fluorescence intensity of each parameter was statistically analyzed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as mean (± SD). Biological replicates (n ≥ 3) were performed in all experiments. Differences between groups were assessed using t-test analysis and one-way ANOVA analysis. Results and discussions

Preparation and characterization of RES-NE

This study adopted the high-pressure microjet method to produce RES-NE. The optimal procedure for the preparation of RES-NE included 0.175 g RES, 1.4 g triglyceride triacetate, 2 g polyoxyethylene castor oil EL-60, 0.6 g PEG 400, and 0.65 g glycerin, with water finally added to 25 mL. As shown in Fig. 1A, RES-NE had a uniform and transparent appearance, just like water. The TEM image of RES-NE (Fig. 1B) exhibited a uniform circular dot-like structure with no aggregation or adhesion, and the average diameter was 22.84 ± 3.88 nm. From the results of DLS (Fig. 1C), the average particle size of RES-NE (17.05 ± 0.59 nm) was slightly larger than that of Blank-NE (13.99 ± 0.43 nm), mainly on account of RES loading. The original particle size of RES suspended in water was 4292.05 ± 612.13 nm, and the PDI was 0.111 ± 0.099. RES exhibits poor water solubility; formulating RES into a nanoemulsion can effectively disperse it, increase the drug loading capacity, and thereby potentially enhance its druggability. The particle size of RES-NE obtained from TEM was slightly larger than that obtained from DLS, which might be due to the sample preparation procedure on the copper plate, where the three-dimensional spherical RES-NE deformed into two-dimensional circle, leading to an increase in particle size. Moreover, the zeta potentials of RES-NE and Blank-NE were 6.45 ± 0.56 mV and 2.45 ± 0.94 mV, respectively (Fig. 1C).

The 7-day accelerated stability experiment of RES-NE was executed under the conditions of 4 °C (protected from light), 30 °C (protected from light), and 30 °C (exposed to light), the changes in drug content, particle size, and zeta potential of RES-NE were investigated respectively, with the results shown in Tab. S1. When RES-NE was stored under various conditions for 7 days, the drug content remained unchanged in the group stored at 4 °C in the dark, decreased to 96.87% in the group stored at 30 °C in the dark, and dropped to 85.61% in the group stored at 30 °C under light (4500 lx). It can be seen from the results that RES-NE is relatively stable under the condition of 4 °C and protected from light, which is consistent with the properties of RES.

Preparation and characterization of hydrogels

By using the gelation time and hydrogel hardness as the primary indications, the hydrogel formulation was tested and optimized. A suitable gelation time is essential for hydrogel applications. Gelling too quickly can hinder needle penetration, while gelling too slowly reduces its adhesiveness, making it difficult to firmly adhere to wounds. In the case of CH, when the weight ratio of 2% CSCl and 2.75% HEC was 3:0.7 (m:m), the hydrogel had a suitable gelation time of 10 min with higher gel strength. In the case of RN-CH, when the weight ratio of 2% CSCl, 2.75% HEC, and RES-NE was 3:0.7:1 (m::m:m), the hydrogel had a suitable gelation time of 15 min. Figure 1D-E show photographs of RN-CH before and after gelation, respectively. It was speculated that the addition of RES-NE diluted the crosslinking network of CH, resulting in a gelation time of RN-CH that was 5 min longer than that of CH. Therefore, both the developed CH and RN-CH exhibited suitable gelation times for wound dressing applications.

It is well accepted that hydrogels with high water content might promote wound healing by maintaining continuous hydration at the specific site, which aids in both wound healing and epithelialization [38, 39]. According to Fig. S1, the water content of CH and RN-CH was up to 90%, which is comparable to that of the extracellular matrix in nature. When hydrogels are introduced into the bodily fluid environment, they swell to varying degrees. Therefore, it is important to maintain the hydrogel’s structure at the proper swelling ratio [40]. As shown in Fig. 1F, the swelling ratios of CH and RN-CH were 19% and 17%, respectively. The results demonstrated that both hydrogels possessed the capability to absorb exudates from the wound locations. An ideal hydrogel dressing must exhibit excellent fatigue resistance, endure specific mechanical stresses, and preserve its structural integrity, given that the skin is frequently in motion, thereby ensuring effective and ongoing wound care [41]. As shown in Fig. S2 and Video S1, RN-CH can endure a specific level of pressure, and once the pressure was alleviated, it was able to revert to it is original condition.

The three-dimensional porous architecture of hydrogel has the potential to enhance hemostasis and wound healing by aiding in the absorption of interstitial fluid and blood, as well as facilitating the transport of nutrients to cells [42]. As shown in Fig. 1G, all cryo-SEM images of CH and RN-CH exhibited a homogeneous porous structure with inherent interconnectivity (pore size: 0.5–50 μm), the difference in pore size of CH and RN-CH was insignificant, and the corresponding statistical result was summarized in Fig. 1H.

The cross-linking principle of the hydrogel was analyzed using FTIR (Fig. S3). The C-O stretching vibration peak of glucose at 1108 cm− 1 and the C-H bond at 2946 cm− 1 are distinctly visible in the mixture but not in the hydrogel. This absence may be attributed to hydrogen bond cross-linking between the hydroxyl groups of glucose and the polar groups in CSCl and HEC. Furthermore, the amide II peak, corresponding to the N-H bending vibration of CSCl at 1519 cm− 1, shifts leftward to 1526 cm− 1 in the hydrogel. This shift may also be associated with the formation of hydrogen bond cross-linking involving the amino groups. Consequently, the formation of hydrogen bonds appears to be the primary mechanism driving gel formation.

Intact skin maintains a natural acidity with a pH level ranging from 4 to 6. However, following an injury, the wound’s pH increases due to leakage from the surrounding microvessels, approaching a pH of approximately 7.40, which is conducive to bacterial infections. In the process of restoring the barrier function of wounds, re-establishing an acidic environment can alleviate the microbial burden and lower the risk of bacterial colonization [43]. As shown in Fig. 1I, the average pH values of CH and RN-CH were 6.61 ± 0.03 and 6.59 ± 0.01, respectively. Thus, RN-CH is capable of creating an acidic environment in the early stages of wound healing to prevent bacterial infections, which is beneficial for wound healing.

In vitro release and degradation of RN-CH

The shortcomings of conventional drug administration can be addressed by engineered nanomaterials, such as improve the stability and solubility of medications [44]. Wound dressings with controlled release and precise therapy functions is an efficient approach for wound healing [45]. As shown in Fig. 1J, the drug release rates of RES-CH (where RES was loaded into CH as a group) and RN-CH after 96 h were about 60% and 82%, respectively, suggesting that CH could effectively reduce the release rate of RES release. It is worth noting that the RES release from of RN-CH is significantly more than that from RES-CH (P < 0.01) indicating that formulating of RES into an oil-in-water nanoemulsion can effectively improve the dispersion of RES, so that it can be better released to play the best effect in the wound site. The particle size of RES-NE was significantly smaller than that of the RES aqueous suspension (17.05 ± 0.59 nm vs. 4292.05 ± 612.13 nm), indicating that the nanoemulsion formulation greatly enhanced the degree of drug dispersion, thereby facilitating drug release.

Appropriate degradation performance is an important indicator of hydrogel wound dressings [46]. The hydrogel was immersed in PBS or PBS containing 0.05 mg/mL lysozyme. The degradation curve of CH and RN-CH is shown in Fig. S4. Results showed that the hydrogels swelled first and then degraded. On the 8th day, the degradation rates of all hydrogels in PBS (containing lysozyme or not) were lower than 50%, indicating that the hydrogels indeed can degrade in the physiological environment, and the degradation rate was suitable to meet long-term drug requirements for wound healing as well. After 8 days, the morphology of hydrogels following the degradation experiment was examined using SEM. As shown in Fig. S5, compared with the control group, the three-dimensional network structure of CH and RN-CH had a small part of cracking and collapse, but the main framework of the hydrogel still existed obviously.

Adhesive behavior of RN-CH

As a superior material for the repair of skin wounds, good adhesion performance is necessary. When RN-CH (dyed with methylene blue) was placed on the finger joint, it could withstand the bending of the finger joint at 0°, 45°, 90° and 135° without falling off, maintaining excellent adhesion properties (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the hydrogel was also affixed to a range of materials, and the adhesion performance was documented visually through a digital camera. As shown in Fig. 2B, RN-CH had excellent adhesion to tissues, such as the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and mouse skin, and it also had certain adhesion to non-biological materials such as rubber ear suction balls, metal medicine spoons, glass bottles and EP tubes (Fig. S6). The adhesive properties of RN-CH were also investigated by using porcine skin, which had similar properties to human skin tissue. The results showed that RN-CH could firmly adhere to the surface of porcine skin without falling off due to distortion, bending and stretching. RN-CH attached to porcine skin could even withstand the impact of running water, indicating that the hydrogel had excellent adhesion properties (Fig. 2C, Video S2 and S3). Furthermore, fresh porcine skin was used to mimic human skin, and the lap-shear method was employed to assess the hydrogels’ adhesive strength to the porcine skin. It was observed from Fig. 2D-F that the adhesive strength of CH and RN-CH was 8.48 ± 1.30 kPa and 10.86 ± 1.00 kPa, respectively. The free amino groups present in the composition of hydrogels can form hydrogen bonds with tissue surfaces, thereby imparting advantageous adhesive properties to the materials [47]. The findings showed that RN-CH has a positive adhesiveness, which might securely shield wounds from outside stimuli, prevent bacterial infection and greatly speed up the healing process.

In general, the strong adherence of the hydrogel to the wound may complicate its removal, potentially causing secondary damage during dressing change. Therefore, an appropriate amount of hydrogel was injected into the wound of the mouse using a syringe and removed with tweezers after gelling. As shown in Fig. S7, the gelled hydrogel adhered firmly to the wound yet was easy to remove.

Rheology of RN-CH

Strain amplitude and frequency amplitude sweeps were conducted to examine the rheological properties and elastic behavior of RN-CH. Formation of the hydrogel is indicated when the storage modulus (G′) clearly surpasses the loss modulus (G′′). The results of the strain amplitude sweep of CH and RN-CH, as shown in Fig. 3A, showed that the strains corresponding to the intersection point between G′ and G′′ curve were 423% and 762%, respectively. This indicates that the hydrogel network collapsed at these points. The results of frequency amplitude sweep analysis showed that CH and RN-CN exhibited consistent elasticity with a gradual increase in angular frequency (Fig. 3B). Thus, the structure integrity of both hydrogels remained intact when subjected to suitable pressure. The G′ of RN-CH was significantly lower than CH, which might be related to the addition of RES-NE, diluting the cross-linking density of the hydrogel.

Injectability is an important requirement of hydrogels in clinic. Injecting hydrogels with shear-thinning property directly into the intended region of damage can prevent gaps, precisely fill the wound, and ensure full contact [48]. As shown in Fig. 3C and Video S4, both CH and RN-CH showed shear-thinning ability by decreasing viscosity at high shear rates, which indicated that the hydrogels were injectable. One example was that RN-CH could be injected through a 20 G needle to form a “HMC” shape, and it maintained its shape after injection without dissolving, clogging or breaking. Results presented in Fig. 3D indicate that the fractured structure of CH and RN-CH is capable of rapid recovery and can sustain its hydrogel state for a minimum of three cycles. Both hydrogels remained in the gel state when subjected to a shearing strain of 1%. However, at a shearing strain of 1000%, the hydrogel network experienced collapse. Following this, when 1% shear strain was reinstated, the hydrogel network was re-transformed to a gel state. In the second and third cycles, although G′ did not recover to the level of the first cycle, it was also much higher than the G′′, which proved the formation of gel. In order to visually verify the self-healing properties of RN-CH, two different colors of RN-CH were cut and placed together. Within 30 min at room temperature, RN-CH showed self-healing ability and could eventually be lifted. It exhibited a progressively indistinct transition from blue to transparent (Fig. 3E). The results presented above demonstrate that the developed hydrogels exhibited excellent injectable and self-healing characteristics, establishing a solid basis for their future application as wound dressings.

Hemocompatibility of hydrogels

Optimal blood compatibility is essential for the utilization of biomaterials. First, the blood compatibility of hydrogels was evaluated using a hemolysis test. The pictures presented in Fig. 4A clearly demonstrate the distinct color variation between the two groups of hydrogels, the negative group (PBS) and the positive group (Triton X-100). The CH and RN-CH groups exhibited a light red coloration, akin to that of the negative group, whereas the positive group displayed a bright red color. The hemolysis ratios observed in both hydrogel groups were below the critical safe hemolysis ratio of 5%, as established for biomaterials in the ASTM standard (F756–2008) [47].

In vitro cell compatibility for hydrogels

The utilization of hydrogels as wound dressings necessitated a high level of cytocompatibility. The proliferation and migration of fibroblasts mainly occur in the post wound healing stage. Therefore, to further evaluate the cytocompatibility of CH and RN-CH, a direct contact assessment was conducted between L929 cells and hydrogels. First, the most suitable RES-NE concentration was assessed by CCK-8 assay. As show in Fig. 4B, when the RES concentration is below 40 µM, L929 cells exhibit good viability. Finally, 30 µM was chosen as the dosing concentration for the following cell experiments.

Then, the cytotoxic effects of CH and RN-CH were assessed visually through live/dead staining, where green fluorescence indicated live cells and red fluorescence signified dead cells. The majority of L929 cells exhibited normal morphology across all samples. It was noted that there was no significant difference in cell density between the CH and RN-CH groups and the control group (Fig. 4C). In addition, after being incubated with L929 cells for 1, 3 and 7 days at 5% CO2 and 37℃, both hydrogels demonstrated no significant cytotoxic effects, with cell viability exceeding 80% as indicated by the CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 4D). Therefore, it can be concluded that CH and RN-CH demonstrate favorable cytocompatibility and are suitable for use as dressings in wound healing applications.

Cell migration assay

The impact of hydrogels on the migration of L929 cells was assessed using a scratch assay (Fig. 4E-F). The quantity of migrating cells in the RN-CH group was markedly greater than that observed in the control group. Furthermore, the capacity of the samples from each group to enhance the migration of L929 cells was ranked as follows: RN-CH > CH > Control. The mobility of RN-CH reached 70% or more within 24 h, which indicated that RN-CH had the strong ability to promote cell migration.

Antioxidant activity of hydrogels

ROS play a dual role in wound healing. ROS are generated transiently during normal wound healing to facilitate debridement and support immune defense [49]. However, in pathophysiological conditions characterized by impaired healing (such as diabetic wounds), ROS production becomes excessive and prolonged, overwhelming the endogenous antioxidant capacity, which can trigger inflammation and the formation of fibrotic scars [50]. Biomaterials engineered to possess free radical scavenging capabilities can significantly enhance the wound healing process. Herein, in vitro antioxidant activity of RN-CH was evaluated by scavenging assays for DPPH and ABTS radicals. As shown in Fig. 5A-B, CH showed minimal DPPH or ABTS radical scavenging activities. Fortunately, RN-CH exhibited high DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities of 73% and 95%, respectively. The reaction color of each sample with DPPH and ABTS working solution is shown in Fig. 5C; the color of the CH group was close to that of the control group, while RN-CH showed a color similar to RES or RES-NE. All results indicated a strong scavenging ability of RN-CH against DPPH and ABTS free radicals.

The levels of intracellular superoxide anions and ROS induced by H2O2 (under the condition of hydrogel extracts or not) were detected by DHE staining and DCFH-DA staining, to evaluate the hydrogel’s efficacy as a prospective antioxidant treatment. From Fig. 5D, in the control (+) group, which was treated with H2O2, there was a significant presence of strong green fluorescence (DCFH-DA) and intense red fluorescence (DHE). Conversely, the control (-) group, which did not receive H2O2 treatment, exhibited minimal fluorescence. This observation suggests that a substantial amount of ROS was generated in L929 cells following co-culture with 200 µM H2O2. In the CH group, distinct red fluorescence (DHE) and green fluorescence (DCFH-DA) were observable in the vicinity of or within the cell nucleus (blue). After treatment with RN-CH extracts, the intracellular ROS level showed a significant reduction, comparable to that observed in the control (-) group. In addition, the fluorescence intensity of the CH group was lower than that of the H2O2-treated group, indicating that CH also had some antioxidant properties. This may be associated with the presence of active amino and hydroxyl groups within the molecular chain of chitosan [51]. In the RN-CH group, the fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced. The addition of RES enhanced the antioxidant effect of the hydrogel, and the synergistic antioxidant effect of RES and CS could effectively alleviate the oxidative stress caused by H2O2. The fluorescence intensity assessed using ImageJ (Fig. 5E-F) demonstrated consistent results. Taken together, these results indicated that RN-CH has the ability to scavenge excess ROS in the wound microenvironment.

In vitro antibacterial assay

Infections caused by bacteria in wounds lead to inflammation and extend the duration of the healing process [52]. The use of a wound dressing that possesses antibacterial properties is highly important in practical application. The antibacterial efficacy of the wound dressings was assessed against S. aureus and E. coli using the colony-counting method. As shown in Fig. 6A, compared with the control group, the bacterial colonies in the CH and RN-CH groups were significantly reduced, especially in the RN-CH group. It was shown that the antibacterial ratios of CH and RN-CH against S. aureus and E. coli were all above 70% (Fig. 6B); notably, RN-CH presented a better antibacterial ability. The antibacterial experiments of the hydrogel against MRSA and PA yielded the same results (Fig. S8). The microscopic morphology of the bacteria after contact with the hydrogels was further observed by SEM. As can be seen from Fig. 6C, after being treated with CH or RN-CH, bacterial cell structures collapsed and shrunk, indicating the antibacterial property of the hydrogels. The antibacterial mechanism of CH and RN-CH was shown in Fig. 6D and the remarkable antibacterial effect may be ascribed to several factors: (1) The antibacterial properties of CS can be explained by the electrostatic interaction between positively charged CS and negatively charged bacterial membrane, resulting in structural impairment and subsequent cell death [53]. (2) RES can inhibit the stability of bacterial biofilm and the synthesis of bacterial flagella, reduce the clustering effect and movement characteristics of bacteria, and can reversibly bind to bacterial ATPase, thereby inhibiting the hydrolysis of bacterial ATP and the synthesis of ATP, thus affecting the metabolism of bacteria [54]. All the results indicated that both CH and RN-CH exhibited remarkable antibacterial properties, suggesting their potential use in wound treatment.

In vitro and in vivo hemostatic performance of the hydrogels

Bleeding is an unavoidable consequence of full-thickness skin defects, and achieving prompt hemostasis is crucial during the initial phase of wound healing. In the petri dish and gauze groups, the blood dispersion in the water was plainly evident (Fig. 7A), showing poor hemostasis properties. However, the water in the CH and RN-CH groups remained relatively clear, indicating that they had a high capacity for blood coagulation. A lower BCI value indicates a reduced clotting time and improved clotting efficiency [55]. The BCI values of CH and RN-CH were 44.1 ± 1.3% and 27.0 ± 3.4% (Fig. 7B), respectively. Therefore, RN-CH exhibited superior blood coagulation capabilities compared to CH.

In vivo rat-liver hemostasis experiments were also conducted to validate the hemostatic performance of the hydrogels. A needle was used to puncture the surface of the liver to induce bleeding, thereby simulating the visceral bleeding that frequently occurs during surgical procedures (Fig. 7C). Hydrogels were then utilized to cover the bleeding site to achieve hemostasis. By comparing the weight of the filter paper before and after bleeding in the control, CH and RN-CH groups, and quantifying the blood loss in each group, the results were shown in Fig. 7D-E. A notable disparity in blood loss was observed between the hydrogel-treated group and the control group. Only a minimal amount of blood appeared on the filter paper in the two hydrogel groups, and the RN-CH group lost less blood than the CH group. Whereas the control group produced a significant quantity of blood stains. The above results showed that both CH and RN-CH had a rapid hemostatic effect on acute liver bleeding and could effectively absorb wound exudate. CH had a certain degree of adhesion and could form a physical barrier over the wound. Loading RES-NE into CH effectively improved its in vivo hemostatic ability. There are several factors that account for the effective hemostatic properties of these hydrogels. The robust adhesive properties of the hydrogels enabled them to bond with and seal the wound, thereby minimizing blood loss and activating the coagulation system to halt the bleeding. Secondly, positively charged CS has been shown to possess the capability of attracting negative charged erythrocytes, thereby facilitating platelet aggregation [56]. Additionally, phenolic hydroxyl groups from RES may interact with platelets, erythrocytes and plasma fibronectins, leading to the activation of platelets, aggregation of erythrocytes, finally resulting in the formation of clots [57]. Therefore, RN-CH exhibited excellent hemostatic performance.

In vivo wound healing performance of the hydrogels

Given the appropriate injectability, biocompatibility, ROS scavenging capability, antibacterial ability, and hemostasis properties of RN-CH, we further evaluated the efficiency of the hydrogels as a wound dressing on a full-thickness skin wound model in vivo. The schematic diagram of the pharmacodynamic experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 8A. Typical wound photographs and wound areas on days 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 are presented in Fig. 8B-C. Apparently, RN-CH exhibited a significantly accelerated wound healing rate compared to both the control group and the commercial hydrogel group. As shown in Fig. 8D, wounds treated with RN-CH almost completely healed (approximately 98%) after 15 days; in contrast, the wound closure rates for the control, commercial hydrogel, and CH groups were only 82%, 92%, and 87%, respectively. It is important to highlight that wounds treated with RN-CH exhibited the most rapid healing rate among all groups.

The assessment of biosafety is a vital criterion for the clinical use of hydrogel wound dressings. Consequently, the systemic toxicity of these hydrogels has been evaluated. H&E images of the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney for various groups on day 15 are presented in Fig. 8E; the microstructures of the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney in mice from both the CH and RN-CH groups were distinct, with cellular morphology exhibiting a regular arrangement. Furthermore, there were no observations of hemorrhage, necrosis, or inflammatory cell infiltration, which demonstrating that CH and RN-CH did not exhibit any potential systemic toxic effects on the major organs.

Histological evaluation of wound regeneration

Re-epithelialization is often utilized as a key indicator for assessing the healing process of skin wounds [58]. To evaluate the healing effects of each group throughout the healing process, H&E staining was conducted on day 6 and day 15, and the findings aligned with the noted wound healing rates. The skin structures observed in all groups on day 6 exhibited a disordered appearance, suggesting the existence of skin defects (Fig. 9A). On day 15, the wounds in the control group had not fully healed, whereas obvious squamous epithelium was observed in the wounds in the RN-CH group, and dense connective tissue similar to that of normal skin dermis was observed under the epithelium. Furthermore, as compared to CH, RN-CH enhanced the quality of wound healing by facilitating the growth of muscle fibers, hair follicles, and sweat glands.

The level of collagen within granulation tissue serves as a key indicator of the wound healing process [59], and Masson trichrome staining was utilized to assess this healing progression. Collagen appeared as blue, while keratin and muscle fibers stained red [60]. As depicted in Fig. 9B, the RN-CH group exhibited a more compact and well-organized collagen structure on the 15th day, as indicated by a more intense blue hue compared to the control group. Additionally, a quantitative analysis of the area ratio of regions occupied by collagen was conducted (Fig. 9C), which further supports the observation of enhanced collagen deposition across all treatment groups. Notably, RN-CH exhibited significantly greater collagen deposition and a more compact arrangement compared to both the commercial gel and CH. Our results demonstrated that RN-CH could effectively promote collagen deposition, which was conducive to accelerate wound healing.

The expression of inflammation and angiogenesis markers

IL-6 and IL-4 are recognized as multifunctional cytokines that play a crucial role in regulating inflammation, immune responses, acute phase reactions and hematopoietic functions. Their activity is closely associated with the intensity of the inflammatory response [3, 61]. To further illustrate the mechanism by which RN-CH promoted wound healing, immunofluorescence assays for IL-6 and IL-4 were performed to assess skin wound repair on days 6 and 15. IL-4 functions as an anti-inflammatory cytokine capable of inducing macrophage polarization [62]. In contrast, IL-6 serves as a marker of inflammation and plays a crucial role in modulating the immune response, as well as influencing cell survival and apoptosis [63]. On day 6, all groups showed obvious expression of IL-4 and IL-6, indicating that the wound was in the stage of inflammation. The expression of IL-4 at the wound site in the RN-CH group was significantly upregulated (Fig. 10A-B). IL-6 in the control, commercial hydrogel and CH groups still showed a certain expression after 15 days of treatment. Meanwhile, the expression of IL-6 at the wound site in the RN-CH group was significantly downregulated (Fig. 10C-D). In conclusion, RN-CH could enhance the levels of IL-4, while simultaneously suppressing the expression of IL-6. This modulation transforms the wound microenvironment from a pro-inflammatory state to an anti-inflammatory state, thereby facilitating the process of wound healing.

Accelerated angiogenesis positively influences wound healing and contributes to the restoration of skin function in cases of full-thickness skin defects [64]. Immunofluorescence staining of CD31 was used to assess neovascularization and maturation [65, 66]. Immunofluorescence analysis of CD31 on day 15 is presented in Fig. 10E-F. Combined with the results of histomorphology, it could be seen that wounds in RN-CH group had entered the remodeling stage and were close to complete healing. Compared with the control, commercial hydrogel and CH groups, the expression of CD31 in the RN-CH group was significantly down-regulated, indicating that the number of capillaries in the new tissue of RN-CH group was significantly reduced, which was consistent with the results of histomorphology.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully created a novel hydrogel wound dressing (RN-CH) designed for hemostasis, anti-oxidation, anti-inflammatory and antibacterial to promote wound healing. Hydrogel composed of CSCl-HEC-glucose is a whole new discovery and its synthetic process is simple and green. The superior tissue adhesion properties of hydrogels enhance their competitiveness in the realm of wound dressing applications. In addition, RN-CH has favorable injectable and self-healing properties, allowing it to fill irregular wounds and maintain the integrity of the hydrogel. More importantly, RN-CH exhibits a qualified hemolysis rate and excellent biocompatibility. Notably, RN-CH can effectively eliminate intracellular ROS (induced by H2O2) and enhance the migration of L929 cells effectively. Furthermore, CH and RN-CH demonstrated remarkable antibacterial efficacy against E. coli and S. aureus, and the addition of RES-NE further improved the antibacterial performance. Meaningfully, RN-CH accelerates wound healing in a mouse full-thickness wound model by facilitating epidermal regeneration and promoting organized collagen deposition. The immunofluorescence results showed that RN-CH further promoted wound regeneration by inhibiting IL-6 production and promoting IL-4 expression. The RN-CH group showed lower CD31 expression compared to the other groups, confirming that the wounds in this group had entered the remodeling stage.

The simplicity and scalability of the green synthesis process, combined with the multifunctional performance of RN-CH, suggest strong potential for clinical translation, particularly in clinical emergency hemostasis and wound care. Although RN-CH shows promising results in murine models, which may not fully recapitulate human wound healing pathophysiology, further studies in larger animal models are necessary to better evaluate its efficacy and safety prior to clinical trials. Long-term biosafety and potential immune responses also require more comprehensive investigation. Overall, this study developed a brand-new multifunctional hydrogel that demonstrates great promise as a next-generation wound dressing. Future efforts will focus on addressing these limitations and scaling up the fabrication process for potential clinical application. therapeutic profile.
