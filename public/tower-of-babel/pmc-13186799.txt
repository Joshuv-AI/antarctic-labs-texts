Clinical utility of the Oakland score in rectal bleeding: Implications for admission decisions and cost efficiency
Authors: Mehmet Fuat Çetin, Mehmet Emin Gönüllü, Erman Yekenkurul, Fatih Gürsoy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRectal bleeding is a frequent cause of emergency admissions, yet objective criteria for hospitalization remain limited. The Oakland Score was developed to identify low-risk patients suitable for outpatient management.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study analyzed 346 patients presenting with rectal bleeding between 2015 and 2025. Patients were categorized as low-risk (Oakland ≤ 8, n = 142) or high-risk (Oakland ≥ 8, n = 204). Clinical parameters, transfusion, tranexamic acid (TXA) use, and total hospital costs were compared.ResultsHigh-risk patients were older (64.2 ± 14.2 vs 52.4 ± 14.1 years, p < 0.001), had lower hemoglobin (10.6 ± 2.2 vs 12.6 ± 1.9 g/dL, p < 0.001), lower systolic pressure (116.1 ± 17.0 vs 124.3 ± 15.2 mmHg, p < 0.001), and higher heart rate (92.5 ± 14.1 vs 83.2 ± 11.6 bpm, p < 0.001). Hospital admission (91.2% vs 40.8%), transfusion (35.3% vs 5.6%), and TXA use (73.5% vs 28.2%) were significantly greater in high-risk patients (all p < 0.001). The Oakland Score correlated positively with total cost (r = 0.55, p < 0.001) and length of stay (r = 0.52, p < 0.001). ROC analysis showed excellent discrimination for hospital admission (AUC = 0.86, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe Oakland Score reliably predicts both clinical severity and healthcare burden in patients presenting with rectal bleeding. Integrating this simple and objective tool into emergency department protocols can help physicians identify low-risk patients who may be safely discharged, thereby reducing unnecessary hospitalizations, optimizing resource use, and improving overall cost-effectiveness of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05128-4.Keywords: Oakland Score, Rectal bleeding, Hospitalization, Transfusion, Cost analysis

Introduction

Rectal bleeding is a frequent cause of emergency department visits and represents a broad clinical spectrum ranging from benign anorectal diseases to life-threatening conditions [1, 2]. Although hemorrhoids, fissures, and fistulas account for many cases, serious etiologies such as diverticular disease, angiodysplasia, inflammatory bowel disease, and colorectal malignancy must also be considered. Because the clinical presentation often overlaps and laboratory findings may initially be inconclusive, distinguishing patients who require hospitalization from those who can be safely discharged remains a major challenge [3, 4].

The decision to admit patients with lower gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding is largely based on physician experience, medicolegal concerns, comorbid conditions, and social factors rather than objective criteria. This variability can lead to unnecessary hospitalizations, redundant testing, and increased healthcare expenditures. Consequently, reliable and reproducible risk-stratification tools are needed to guide evidence-based decisions in the management of rectal bleeding [5–7].

Several scoring systems have been proposed for this purpose, including the Strate, BLEED, and NOBLADS scores [8–10]. However, these models were primarily developed from retrospective cohorts and have shown limited predictive value in real-world practice. In 2017, Oakland and colleagues introduced the Oakland Score, a simple and validated risk assessment tool designed to identify patients with lower GI bleeding who can be safely managed as outpatients [11]. The score incorporates routinely available clinical variables such as age, sex, hemoglobin concentration, hemodynamic parameters, previous bleeding episodes, and digital rectal examination findings. Subsequent validation studies demonstrated that patients classified as low-risk (Oakland ≤ 8) rarely experience adverse outcomes or require urgent intervention [12, 13].

Despite these validations, important gaps remain regarding the real-world implementation of the Oakland Score. In routine practice, admission decisions are often influenced by comorbidities, frailty, clinician risk perception, and healthcare system factors, which may lead to hospitalization of patients who are formally classified as low risk. Moreover, although several studies have evaluated clinical outcomes associated with risk stratification in lower GI bleeding, data integrating risk scores with detailed cost analyses and potentially avoidable admissions remain limited, particularly outside Western healthcare systems.

Integrating the Oakland Score into emergency department decision-making has the potential not only to improve clinical risk stratification but also to optimize healthcare resource utilization and reduce unnecessary hospital admissions. However, evidence evaluating its combined clinical and economic impact within the Turkish healthcare setting is lacking. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the clinical utility of the Oakland Score in patients presenting with rectal bleeding, to examine its association with hospitalization, transfusion, and treatment strategies, and to perform a comprehensive cost analysis from an emergency service perspective. Additionally, the study sought to quantify potentially avoidable admissions among low-risk patients and to estimate the associated financial burden on the healthcare system under real-world clinical practice conditions.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as a retrospective, single-center, observational analysis conducted at the Department of General Surgery, Düzce University Faculty of Medicine, Türkiye. Patients who presented to the emergency department with rectal bleeding between January 2015 and June 2025 were identified through the institutional hospital information system. The study protocol was approved by the Düzce University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 06.10.2025, Decision No: 2025/264). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Given the retrospective nature of the study, no patient intervention or additional procedure was performed. Patient consent was waived by the ethics committee. All data were de-identified before analysis to ensure compliance with the national regulations on personal data protection (Law No. 6698). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

Study population

The study included adult patients aged 18 years or older who presented to the emergency department with rectal bleeding (hematochezia), regardless of whether they were ultimately admitted or discharged from the emergency department. Patients were identified using diagnostic codes and emergency department records.

Exclusion criteria were: diagnosis of upper gastrointestinal bleeding, hemodynamic instability requiring immediate emergency intervention, and incomplete or inconsistent medical records.

A total of 346 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. According to the Oakland scoring system, patients were stratified into two groups: Group 1 with an Oakland score ≤ 8 (low-risk group) and Group 2 with an Oakland score ≥ 8 (high-risk group). This stratification enabled evaluation of the clinical utility of the Oakland score in guiding admission decisions, therapeutic interventions, and healthcare cost outcomes.

Data collection

Data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records. Demographic variables included age and sex. Clinical parameters recorded at presentation included systolic blood pressure, heart rate, and documented presence of melena or syncope. Laboratory data focused on admission hemoglobin levels.

Information on comorbidities—including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, and other chronic systemic diseases—was obtained from documented medical history. Digital rectal examination (DRE) findings were extracted from standardized emergency department and surgical consultation notes and were recorded as binary variables (presence or absence of visible blood). Given the retrospective design, DRE findings reflected routine clinical documentation and were not subject to protocolized assessment; potential inter-observer variability was therefore acknowledged.

The Oakland score was calculated for each patient using age, sex, prior hospitalization for lower gastrointestinal bleeding, DRE findings, hemodynamic parameters, and hemoglobin level.

Treatment-related variables included use of tranexamic acid, blood transfusion requirement, and endoscopic or surgical interventions during hospitalization. Hospital disposition (admission or discharge), length of hospital stay, re-presentation within 30 days, and in-hospital mortality were recorded.

Definition of inappropriate admission

Inappropriate admission was defined as hospitalization of patients classified as low risk (Oakland score ≤ 8) who did not require blood transfusion, urgent endoscopic or surgical intervention, or experience in-hospital mortality during the index admission. This definition was used to estimate potentially avoidable hospitalizations from a clinical and economic perspective.

Cost assessment

A comprehensive cost analysis was performed using institutional hospital billing records. Cost components included laboratory investigations, imaging studies, medications (including tranexamic acid), bed occupancy, and procedural interventions. Laboratory costs comprised hematology, biochemistry, and coagulation tests; imaging costs included ultrasonography, computed tomography, and endoscopic procedures; medication costs reflected all drugs administered during the hospital stay; hospitalization costs were calculated based on length of inpatient stay.

All costs were initially recorded in Turkish Lira and converted to United States dollars (USD) using the mean official exchange rate at the time of hospitalization. Cost estimates reflect institutional charges and were not adjusted for inflation across the study period.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was to determine the relationship between the Oakland score and hospital admission status, aiming to assess whether low-risk patients could be safely managed without inpatient admission. Secondary outcomes included the association between Oakland score and transfusion requirement, tranexamic acid use, length of hospital stay, total hospital cost, and in-hospital mortality. In addition, the study examined the potential role of the Oakland score in identifying unnecessary admissions and excessive healthcare expenditures.

Data management and quality control

All collected data were cross-checked by two independent researchers to ensure accuracy and consistency. Any discrepancies between electronic and paper-based records were resolved through consensus. The dataset had a completeness rate above 95%, and missing data were handled through pairwise deletion without imputation.

All files were stored on an encrypted, password-protected institutional database accessible only to the research team. Patient identifiers were permanently removed, and each case was assigned a unique study code to maintain anonymity and confidentiality throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables, median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data, and numbers with percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and the Pearson chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Correlation analyses between the Oakland score and continuous clinical parameters were conducted using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients according to data distribution. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative performance of the Oakland score in predicting key binary outcomes, including hospital admission (yes/no), transfusion requirement (yes/no), and composite adverse outcomes. The area under the curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated, and optimal cutoff values were determined using the Youden index.

To identify independent predictors of hospital admission, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed by including variables with a p value < 0.10 in univariate analyses. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and overall model performance was evaluated using Nagelkerke R2. Potential multicollinearity among variables included in the multivariate model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 2.5 considered acceptable. Given the potential influence of baseline health status on admission decisions, a predefined sensitivity analysis was conducted in patients without documented comorbidities to assess whether the Oakland score retained predictive performance in a non-frail population. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A priori sample size estimation was performed using G*Power version 3.1 software. Assuming a medium effect size (d = 0.3), a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired power (1–β) of 0.80, a minimum sample size of 88 patients per group (total n = 176) was required. The final cohort of 346 patients provided a post hoc statistical power exceeding 0.95, confirming that the study population was adequately powered to detect clinically meaningful differences[14].

A total of 346 patients who presented with rectal bleeding were included in the study. Among them, 142 (41.0%) patients had an Oakland score ≤ 8 (Group 1) and 204 (59.0%) had an Oakland score ≥ 8 (Group 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we evaluated the real-world clinical applicability of the Oakland Score in patients presenting with rectal bleeding, focusing not only on traditional clinical outcomes but also on healthcare utilization and cost implications. Our findings demonstrated that patients with higher Oakland scores were significantly older, exhibited greater hemodynamic instability, had lower hemoglobin levels, and experienced markedly higher rates of hospitalization, transfusion, and tranexamic acid use. Importantly, these patients also generated substantially greater healthcare expenditures, underscoring the combined clinical and economic relevance of structured risk stratification in lower gastrointestinal bleeding.

The Oakland Score was developed as an objective tool to support safe discharge decisions in patients with lower GI bleeding by integrating readily available clinical and laboratory variables. The original derivation and validation study by Oakland et al. reported that patients with scores ≤ 8 could be safely managed as outpatients, with adverse outcomes occurring in fewer than 5% of cases [11].

Subsequent external validation studies across diverse healthcare systems—including the United Kingdom, United States, and Asia—have consistently demonstrated strong discriminative performance, with reported AUC values ranging from 0.81 to 0.88 [15, 16]. In line with these findings, our study yielded an AUC of 0.86 for predicting hospital admission, providing the first evidence supporting the external validity of the Oakland Score within a Turkish population.

Beyond validation, our study adds to the existing literature by highlighting the gap between score-based risk classification and real-world clinical decision-making. Despite being classified as low risk (Oakland ≤ 8), a substantial proportion of patients were still admitted to hospital. Stratified analyses revealed that comorbidity burden played a major role in these decisions, with admission rates rising from 22.0% in low-risk patients without comorbidities to 66.7% among those with at least one chronic condition. This finding suggests that clinicians often prioritize baseline health status, frailty, and perceived vulnerability over bleeding severity alone when determining admission, reflecting legitimate concerns related to patient safety, medicolegal risk, and system-level constraints.

These observations are further supported by our multivariate analysis, in which comorbidity burden emerged as an independent predictor of hospital admission alongside the Oakland Score itself. While the score effectively summarizes bleeding-related risk, our results emphasize that it should be viewed as a decision-support tool rather than a replacement for clinical judgment, particularly in patients with complex medical backgrounds. This insight helps explain why potentially avoidable admissions persist even in settings where validated risk scores are available.

Hemoglobin concentration remained a key driver of both clinical severity and admission decisions in our cohort. Consistent with prior studies, lower hemoglobin levels were strongly associated with higher Oakland scores, transfusion requirement, and hospitalization [9, 17, 18]. Similarly, abnormalities in vital signs—specifically reduced systolic blood pressure and elevated heart rate—reflected hemodynamic compromise and contributed to higher-risk classification, reinforcing the physiological basis of the Oakland model. Notably, the independent association of melena with hospital admission suggests that clinicians may overweight certain cues suggestive of active bleeding when making disposition decisions, potentially allowing individual components to dominate the composite score in real-world practice.

The role of tranexamic acid in lower GI bleeding continues to be debated. Although TXA was used more frequently in high-risk patients in our study, its administration was not associated with improved short-term outcomes, echoing findings from large randomized trials such as HALT-IT, which failed to demonstrate mortality benefit and raised concerns regarding thromboembolic risk [19, 20]. These data suggest that TXA use in lower GI bleeding should be individualized rather than routine.

From a health economics perspective, our findings demonstrate a clear association between Oakland score, length of hospital stay, and total healthcare costs. High-risk patients incurred approximately 3.5-fold higher hospital expenditures compared with low-risk individuals, largely driven by inpatient bed occupancy, diagnostic testing, and procedural interventions. Importantly, a notable proportion of admissions among low-risk patients were potentially avoidable, highlighting an opportunity for cost containment through structured risk stratification. The conceptual budget impact illustrated in Fig. 1 underscores how score-guided admission strategies could optimize resource utilization without compromising patient safety, particularly in high-volume emergency care settings. However, translating score-based discharge decisions into routine practice may be limited by access to urgent endoscopy, institutional admission culture, medicolegal concerns, and social support constraints—factors that likely contributed to hospital admissions among patients classified as low risk in our cohort.

In addition to bleeding severity, transfusion requirement has increasingly been recognized as a surrogate marker of overall disease burden, clinical complexity, and healthcare resource utilization. Prior studies have demonstrated that transfusion need is closely associated not only with short-term morbidity but also with prolonged hospitalization and increased costs across diverse clinical settings [21, 22]. In this context, our findings that transfusion was required in more than one-third of high-risk patients—and was rare among those with Oakland scores ≤ 8—further support the clinical relevance of the score in identifying patients likely to consume substantial healthcare resources. These observations reinforce the concept that transfusion requirement reflects a composite of bleeding severity, physiological reserve, and comorbidity burden rather than a single isolated clinical parameter.

Moreover, the relationship between risk stratification, transfusion practices, and downstream outcomes highlights the importance of structured decision-support tools in contemporary emergency care. As healthcare systems face increasing pressure to balance patient safety with cost containment, objective risk scores such as the Oakland Score may serve as a pragmatic framework to support consistent and transparent admission decisions. By identifying patients at low risk for transfusion, intervention, or clinical deterioration, score-guided pathways may help clinicians safely limit unnecessary inpatient care while preserving resources for patients with higher acuity needs [23]. Importantly, such tools should be integrated into clinical workflows as adjuncts to—not replacements for—physician judgment, particularly in patients with complex comorbid profiles.

Despite these strengths, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective and single-center design inherently carries a risk of selection bias and limits control over data completeness and standardization. Although electronic medical records were carefully reviewed, certain clinical variables—such as estimated bleeding volume, stool characteristics, or clinician-perceived bleeding severity—may not have been consistently or uniformly documented, potentially influencing admission decisions.

Second, data regarding time-to-endoscopy and definitive bleeding etiology were not consistently available for all patients. Both factors are clinically relevant, as delayed endoscopic evaluation and underlying bleeding sources (e.g., diverticular disease, angiodysplasia, malignancy) may substantially affect management strategies, length of stay, and resource utilization. The absence of these data limits our ability to assess how procedural timing or etiology-specific risk influenced admission practices and outcomes.

Third, the economic analysis was based on institutional billing records and reflects costs within a single healthcare system. Cost data were not adjusted for inflation across the 10-year study period, nor were indirect costs or post-discharge expenditures captured. As a result, absolute cost estimates may not be directly generalizable to other institutions or healthcare systems with different reimbursement structures, although relative cost differences between risk groups remain informative.

Fourth, the low number of in-hospital mortality events limits the statistical power to draw firm conclusions regarding mortality prediction. While mortality occurred exclusively in the high-risk group, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and primarily viewed as descriptive rather than inferential.

Finally, we did not perform direct head-to-head comparisons between the Oakland Score and other established lower gastrointestinal bleeding risk scores, such as BLEED or NOBLADS. Such comparisons could provide additional context regarding relative predictive performance and clinical utility. Nevertheless, the primary aim of the present study was to evaluate the real-world applicability and economic implications of the Oakland Score rather than to establish comparative superiority among risk models.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the Oakland Score is a valuable and practical tool for risk stratification in patients presenting with rectal bleeding, with meaningful implications for both clinical decision-making and healthcare resource utilization. While real-world admission decisions are influenced by comorbidities and clinician judgment, structured use of the Oakland Score may help identify low-risk patients suitable for outpatient management, thereby reducing unnecessary hospitalizations and associated costs. Prospective, multicenter studies incorporating implementation strategies and clinician behavior are warranted to further define the role of score-guided care pathways in lower gastrointestinal bleeding.
