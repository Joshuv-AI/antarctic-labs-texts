Metric Double Complements of Convex Sets
Douglas S. Bridges

Abstract
In constructive mathematics the metric complement of a subset S S of a metric space X X is the set − S -S of points in X X that are bounded away from S S . In this note we discuss, within Bishop’s constructive mathematics, the connection between the metric double complement, − ( − K ) -(-K) , and the logical double complement, ¬ ¬ ⁡ K \lnot\lnot K , where K K is a convex subset of a normed linear space X X . In particular, we prove that if K K has inhabited interior, then − ( − K ) = ( ¬ ¬ ⁡ K ) ∘ -(-K)=\left(\lnot\lnot K\right)^{\circ} , that the hypothesis of inhabited interior can be dropped in the finite-dimensional case, and that we cannot constructively replace ( ¬ ¬ ⁡ K ) ∘ (\lnot\lnot K)^{\circ} by K ∘ K^{\circ} in these results.

Proposition 24

The following are equivalent.

(i)

If
K
is a convex subset of
\mathbf{R}
with inhabited
interior, such that
-(-K)
is located, then
-(-K)=K^{\circ}
.

(ii)

The law of excluded middle.

(iii)

If
K
is a convex set with inhabited interior in a normed
space
X
, then
-(-K)=K^{\circ}
.

Proof.
Let
p
be a proposition such that
\lnot\lnot p
, and define

K\equiv\left[0,1\right]\cup\left\{x\in\left[0,2\right]:p\right\}\text{.}

Then
\left[0,1\right]\subset K\subset\left[0,2\right]
, so
K^{\circ}
is inhabited, and
K=\left[0,2\right]
if and only if
p
. If
x\
and

y
belong to
K
, then either both
x
and
y
are in
\left[0,1\right]
,
or else one of them belongs to
\left\{x\in\left[0,2\right]:p\right\}

and
K=\left[0,2\right]
; in either case,
\left[x,y\right]\subset K
,
so
K
is convex. If
\left[0,2\right]\cap-K
is inhabited, then
\lnot p
, which is absurd; so
\left[0,2\right]\cap-K=\varnothing
. Since

\left[0,2\right]
is closed and located in
\mathbf{R}
, Lemma

6
shows that
\left(0,2\right)=\left[0,2\right]^{\circ}=-(-K)
, which is therefore located. Moreover,
3/2=-(-K)
, so if (i) holds,
then
3/2\
must belong to
\left\{x\in\mathbf{R}:p\right\}
and therefore

p
holds. It follows that if (i) holds, then
(\lnot\lnot p\Rightarrow p)

for every proposition
p
; whence (i) implies (ii). Referring to Theorem

16
, we see that (ii) implies (iii). It is trivial that (iii)
implies (i).
\square

Proposition
