Interferometric discrepancy between the Schrödinger and Klein-Gordon wave equations due to their dissimilar phase velocities.
Frank V. Kowalski

Abstract
The Schrödinger equation predicts interference when a beamsplitter’s trajectory includes a segment where its speed exceeds the phase velocity of a free non-zero rest mass particle that is in a momentum eigenstate. Such interference is neither possible for electromagnetic waves nor for eigenstates of momentum in the non-relativistic limit of the Klein-Gordon equation since the speed of the beamsplitter cannot exceed the phase velocity of the wave. The dual behavior of reflection and transmission in this case is discussed for dielectric and diffracting beamsplitters.

For a free particle of mass
m
, the phase velocity of a plane wave solution (momentum eigenstate) of the the Schrödinger equation is
v_{g}/2
, where
v_{g}
is the group or particle velocity. In the non-relativistic limit of the Klein-Gordon equation the phase velocity is, in this case,
c^{2}/v_{g}
where
c
is the speed of light. Yet, their probability density functions, PDFs, are expected to not differ in the non-relativistic domain. It is often remarked that the phase velocity in quantum mechanics is not a physically meaningful quantity since it can exceed the speed of light and carries no information.

However, the phase difference between two paths is measurable, generating interference that is evident in the PDF at the output port of an interferometer. The significance of the phase velocity in interferometry is often concealed by using a snapshot method for determining the difference in phase between two paths; time is fixed while the number of wave crests is compared along the two paths. The retarded phase method, however, emphasizes the importance of the phase velocity in such calculations.
kowalski2010

For example, let the phase at the input port of an interferometer be given by
-\omega_{0}t
. The phase at the output port along one path is then
-\omega_{0}t_{ret}
, where
t_{ret}
is the retarded time for that path (the time at which the wave crest at the output port was at the input port). For light traversing a static interferometer
t_{ret}=t-t_{o}
, where the wave crest transit time to the output port is
t_{o}=L/c
and
L
is the distance that it travelled. Other examples of the retarded phase method, using the dispersion relations for the free particle Schrödinger and Klein-Gordon eqns., are found in the literature.
kowalski2010

One advantage of this method is that the phase along time dependent path lengths is more easily determined. An example is a path that involves reflection from a mirror whose position is a nontrivial function of time. This approach can reveal interferometry that might be nonintuitive when considering only the snapshot method.

A contrast can be drawn between these wave equations and the acoustic wave equation for the apparatus shown in fig.
1
. The acoustic wave crest speed is independent of the BS speed while a shock wave appears in front of the BS as it exceeds the speed of a wave crest (due to the compression in the density of the medium). For a quantum system, the BS’s motion induces a change in the reflected wave’s properties, but not the transmitted wave’s. No shock wave occurs since these are not waves associated with a pressure variation in a medium.

The quantum results can be derived using two separate approaches: (1) transformation to and from the rest frame of the BS, or (2) the application of the retarded phase method in the lab frame. The former method is straightforward to analyze since it only involves static boundary conditions. However, this transformation to and from the BS frame for the Schrödinger equation requires a unusual phase factor.

During most of its trajectory the BS is either at rest or moving at constant velocity. A small duration, however, involves acceleration, the effects of which are approximated as a sequence of momentarily co-moving inertial frame interactions between the wave and the BS. The calculation utilizing these transformations between the lab and BS frame is outlined next.

Consider first this interference in the lab frame for the Schrödinger equation while the BS moves at constant speed
V

v_{g}/2\ll V
(both the BS and wave crests move in the positive direction). The BS interacts with two waves in fig.

Consider case (I) after transforming to the rest frame of the BS. The wave crests that move in the direction of the BS in the lab frame now move toward the BS in its frame. The reflected and transmitted waves are determined in this BS frame. The results are then transformed back to the lab frame where both incident and reflected wave crests now move in the direction of the BS. In the lab frame the reflected wave crests move faster than the BS and in front of the BS (shown as the wave train associated with the longest dashed arrow in fig.
1
(b) and (c)) while the transmitted wave crests move slower than the BS and are left behind it. These lagging wave crests have the same frequency,
mv_{g}^{2}/(2\hbar)

mv_{g}/\hbar

m(2V-v_{g})^{2}/(2\hbar)

m(2V-v_{g})/\hbar

Consider case (II) in the frame of the BS. The incident wave crests move faster toward the BS than in case (I). Their reflection in the BS frame is then transformed back to the lab frame where these crests move in front of the BS and much faster than in case (I). These reflected wave crests have frequency,
m(2V+v_{g})^{2}/(2\hbar)

m(2V+v_{g})/\hbar

The wave crests transmitted through the BS in the lab frame have the same frequency,
mv_{g}^{2}/(2\hbar)

mv_{g}/\hbar

The other two amplitudes are associated with wave crests that reflect in both cases (I) and (II) and travel in front of the BS with different wavevectors and frequencies. Their interference (beat frequency) at the detector lasts only until the BS comes to rest, at which time the interference shown in fig.

The effect of acceleration of the BS on the wave crest motion is treated as a sequence of momentarily co-moving inertial frame interactions. In each of these sequential interactions the wave crests that transmit through the BS are unaffected by interaction with the BS. In this approximation these transmitted wave crests are unaffected not only by constant BS speed but also by the acceleration of the BS.

Contrast this calculation with that using the Klein-Gordon equation. Boundary conditions are applied in the BS frame after a Lorentz transformation from the lab frame. Following reflection, the wave’s parameters are transformed back to the laboratory frame, where the reflected phase velocity is

For case (I) in the lab frame the Klein-Gordon wave crests that are reflected travel faster than the BS at any point in its trajectory and therefore cannot be overtaken by motion of the BS. One might question how this interference differs from a BS trajectory in which the BS is removed from the beam path at the upper BS position and the later moved into the beam path at the lower position. This can be modelled as an upper shutter that reflects wave crests until it is opened at
t_{1}
, corresponding to the removal of the BS from the beam path. At a later time,
t_{2}
, a beam splitting shutter is then activated at the lower position allowing both the wave crests reflected from the upper shutter to pass through it while at the same time reflecting the wave crests that are incident upon it from the source below.

The opening of the shutter generates a decrease in the amplitude of the reflected wave that can be described approximately as a step function envelope superimposed on the reflected wave train.
moshinsky
;
martin
This decrease in amplitude or edge of the step function moves at the group velocity through these wave crests (faster than the speed of a wave crest in the Schrödinger equation). It transmits the information that the shutter is open.

Similarly, activation of the beamsplitting shutter at the lower position generates a change in amplitude of the wave crests reflecting from it. However, now the amplitude variation (or step function envelope superimposed on the wave train) is at the front of these reflected wave crests.

Wave crests reflected from the upper shutter that are transmitted through the lower BS shutter interfere with wave crests reflected from the lower BS shutter. This yields static interference that exists only during the overlap of these wavegroups.

The beginning of this overlap occurs at
t_{2}=t_{1}+\alpha\tau
where
\tau
is the group transit time between shutters and the constant
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
. The overlap continues until the information that the first shutter is open arrives at the lower BS at
t_{1}+L/v_{g}
. The duration of this interference,
\tau(1-\alpha)
, is the same for both the Schrödinger and Klein-Gordon equations since it involves the group velocity (which is the same for both equations in the non-relativistic limit) and not the phase velocity. The Schrödinger and Klein-Gordon equations then predict the same interference between these wavegroups at the detector.

Another shutter example involves EM waves propagating in vacuum. If
t_{2}-t_{1}<L/c
then the wavegroup reflected from the first shutter overlaps with that reflected from the second shutter at the second shutter while no overlap occurs for
t_{2}-t_{1}>L/c
. For interference to occur with the BS replacing the shutters, the effective speed of the BS (when removed and then inserted into the beam) must exceed the speed of light. Thus, repositioning the BS out of, around, and then back into the beam cannot cause EM wave interference, consistent with the spacetime diagram shown in fig.
1
, even though interference is possible with the shutters.

Consider again the system shown in fig.
1
, focusing on the effects of BS acceleration. In the Klein-Gordon eqn. the initial acceleration of the BS generates an increasing chirp in the reflected wave crests. Reflection from the BS, in the Schrödinger eqn., involves two waves; those coming directly from the source and those that the BS is overcoming. All such wave crests move in front of and faster than the BS. The deceleration generates a decreasing chirp for the reflected wave crests. The chirps carry the information that the slab accelerated and constitute the beginning and end of a wavegroup whose boundaries are defined by these frequency chirps (not by the changes in amplitude of the wave train). These wavegroups interact with the detector only before the BS comes to rest. The interference depicted in fig.
1
, solely a feature of the Schrödinger equation, is observed only after the BS comes to rest.

The crucial factor in how the BS interacts with a wave train is its ability to function simultaneously as both a mirror (reflecting the wave) and as an invisible component (allowing unimpeded transmission). During the BS’s trajectory it generates a wavegroup when it acts like a mirror while the wave train remains in an eigenstate of momentum in transmission. No information about the BS trajectory is contained in the beam transmitted through the BS or left behind it. Yet, the reflection/transmission duality property of the BS facilitates interference when the BS again comes to rest. The trajectory of the BS is indeterminate from this interference.

Experimental verification critically depends on the BS behaving as described above. A thick aluminum slab, which acts as a BS for neutrons, is used to illustrate problems that can arise. Retro-reflection occurs at the interfaces, while transmission propagates through the slab’s moving medium; both processes are characterized by an index of refraction. Prior studies have investigated this system for constant velocity slab motion.

Acceleration of the slab in the direction (or in the opposite direction) of wave crest motion increases (or decreases) the frequency of the transmitted wave. The transmitted wave train then acquires frequency chirps during the duration of the acceleration and deceleration stages of the slab trajectory. The frequency of the transmitted wave train during the constant speed part of the trajectory does not change.
