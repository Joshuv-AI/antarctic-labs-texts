Association between the atherogenic index of plasma and major adverse cardiovascular events in individuals with metabolic syndrome: findings from the UK biobank
Authors: Yuanjia Hu, Wenlong Li, Jingyuan Nie, Changhong Zhang, Jing Zhou, Zuhai Hu, Hanwen Zhou, Xun Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) has been widely investigated in relation to cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, its association with major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) among individuals with metabolic syndrome (MetS) remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate the association of AIP with incident MACE and its component endpoints among individuals with MetS.MethodsWe analyzed data from 131,736 UK Biobank participants with MetS who were free of MACE at baseline. The primary outcome was MACE, defined as the first occurrence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), cardiovascular death (CV death), unstable angina (UA), stroke, or heart failure (HF). Cumulative incidence was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, and the association between baseline AIP and MACE was explored with multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Additionally, nonlinearity in this association was examined with restricted cubic splines (RCS). The incremental predictive value of AIP for MACE was evaluated using the change in C-index (C-index), the net reclassification index (NRI), and the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). Robustness of the findings was evaluated in prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ResultsDuring a median of 13.4 years of follow-up, 19,140 of the 131,736 participants with MetS experienced MACE (14.5%). Each standard deviation (SD) increase in AIP was associated with higher risks across multiple cardiovascular endpoints in this population. In fully adjusted models, AIP was associated with MACE (hazard ratio (HR), 1.07; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.05,1.08), AMI (1.16; 1.12,1.19), and UA (1.16; 1.13,1.19), with no clear evidence of association for stroke (1.03; 1.00,1.06), CV death (1.01; 0.97,1.05), or HF (1.01; 0.98,1.03). Kaplan–Meier curve analysis demonstrated significant differences in the incidences of MACE, AMI, UA, CV death, and HF across AIP groups in the entire cohort (all log-rank P < 0.05), whereas stroke showed no significant difference (log-rank P > 0.05). RCS analyses indicated U-shaped associations for MACE, AMI, HF, and CV death and a J-shaped association for UA (all P for nonlinearity < 0.05), whereas the association with stroke was linear (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Moreover, AIP improved reclassification metrics (NRI/IDI) and discriminative ability (C-index). Sensitivity analyses corroborated these primary findings.ConclusionsThis observational study showed that higher AIP was associated with increased risks of MACE and its component endpoints, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for risk stratification among individuals with MetS.Graphical abstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-03010-1.Keywords: Major adverse cardiovascular events, Metabolic syndrome, Atherogenic index of plasma, UK Biobank, Risk stratification

Research insights

What is currently known about this topic?

CVD risk is elevated in individuals with MetS.

Although AIP is an established marker of CVD risk, evidence on its associations with MACE in MetS remains unclear.

What is the key research question?

Is AIP associated with incident MACE in individuals with MetS, and could 10-year risk prediction be improved if AIP were added to PREVENT/SCORE2?

What is new?

AIP is significantly associated with the risk of incident MACE and its component endpoints (AMI and UA) in adults with MetS.

Adding AIP to current 10-year CVD risk equations could improve prediction and stratification.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Integrating AIP into routine assessment for patients with MetS may enable earlier risk identification and targeted preventive strategies, with potential to reduce MACE.

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide [1]. In Europe, CVD causes more than four million deaths each year [2]; ischemic heart disease and stroke remain among the leading contributors to disease burden [3], posing a serious threat to population health and life expectancy. CVD is characterized by an insidious onset and high rates of disability and mortality. Once major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), such as myocardial infarction or stroke, occur, they impose a substantial burden on patients, families, and society. Accordingly, early identification of, and intervention for, individuals at high risk of CVD are crucial [4].

Among individuals at high risk for CVD, metabolic syndrome (MetS) merits particular attention. MetS is a clustered state of metabolic abnormalities characterized by central obesity, elevated blood pressure, dysglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [5]. With the global epidemics of obesity and type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of MetS continues to rise, affecting roughly one quarter of adults worldwide [6, 7]. Insulin resistance is regarded as the central pathophysiological mechanism of MetS and is closely linked to abdominal obesity, dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, hypertension, and a chronic inflammatory state [8]. MetS is closely associated with the onset and progression of CVD: it not only accelerates atherosclerosis and markedly increases the risk of MACE such as myocardial infarction and stroke, but also shows a linear increase in cardiovascular event rates with a greater number of MetS components [9, 10]. A meta-analysis reported that MetS is associated with roughly a twofold higher risk of incident CVD (Relative Risk(RR) ≈ 2.35), a ~ 2.4-fold higher risk of CVD mortality, and a ~ 1.5-fold higher risk of all-cause mortality [11]. Therefore, individuals with MetS constitute a high-risk population for CVD, with a substantially higher risk of MACE than the general population, underscoring the need to investigate risk biomarkers to enable more precise risk stratification and intervention.

In addition, MetS is characterized by insulin resistance and atherogenic dyslipidemia, manifested by elevated triglycerides(TG), reduced HDL-C, and an increased proportion of small dense low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (sdLDL-C). The atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), calculated as log₁₀(TG/HDL-C), reflects this lipid phenotype, correlates inversely with LDL particle size [12], and is associated with cardiovascular risk [13]. Numerous studies have shown that AIP has strong predictive performance for CVD and MACE, serving as a novel biomarker of cardiovascular risk [14, 15]. In the general population, AIP is an independent predictor of CVD events and remains significantly associated with future MACE risk even after adjustment for traditional risk factors [16]. Among patients with coronary artery disease, the highest AIP group has an approximately 63% higher risk of MACE than the lowest group, and elevated AIP is associated with increased risks of myocardial infarction (RR ≈ 2.21) and cardiac death [17]. Data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicate that individuals with AIP >0.24 have a 29% higher risk of all-cause mortality than those with AIP ≤ 0.24, independent of traditional lipid indices such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and HDL-C [18]. Moreover, elevated AIP is associated with increased risks of other adverse cardiovascular events, including ischemic stroke [19], and is closely linked to multiple metabolic diseases. Taken together, as a convenient lipid-derived index, AIP effectively reflects the degree of metabolic dysregulation and atherosclerotic risk, demonstrating independent and reliable value in CVD risk prediction; in particular, among high-risk individuals with MetS, AIP may help address the limitations of traditional risk-assessment tools.

Given that existing studies have largely been based on general populations or patients with coronary artery disease and that sample sizes and follow-up are limited. To address this gap, using the large prospective UK Biobank(UKB) cohort, we systematically evaluate the associations between AIP and incident MACE among adults with MetS and its component outcomes (acute myocardial infarction (AMI), stroke, cardiovascular death (CV death), unstable angina (UA), and heart failure (HF]), and assess the incremental value of adding AIP to contemporary 10-year cardiovascular risk equations such as the AHA Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events(PREVENT) equations and Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation(SCORE2) [20, 21]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the association between AIP and incident MACE in a MetS population, and the findings may facilitate earlier identification of high-risk individuals and support targeted preventive strategies, potentially reducing the occurrence of MACE.

Methods

Study population

This study was based on the UKB, a large population-based prospective cohort. Between 2006 and 2010, UKB recruited more than 500,000 volunteers aged 40–69 years at 22 assessment centers across England, Wales, and Scotland. At baseline, all participants underwent a comprehensive health assessment, including a detailed touch-screen questionnaire, physical measurements, and collection of blood and urine samples, with long-term follow-up achieved via linkage to electronic health records. The protocol was approved by the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent. This study was conducted under UKB application number 160,957.

We identified 153,294 individuals in UKB who met the diagnostic criteria for MetS. Within the MetS subset, we sequentially excluded participants with missing HDL-C required to compute the AIP (n = 2,622) and those with a history of MACE at or before baseline (n = 18,936). In total, 131,736 participants were included in the analysis.

Definition of MetS

MetS was defined according to the 2005 joint criteria established by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the American Heart Association/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute [22]. A diagnosis of MetS requires meeting at least three of the following five components: (1) increased waist circumference: ≥102 cm in European men or ≥ 88 cm in European women; ≥90 cm in non-European men or ≥ 80 cm in non-European women. Thresholds follow IDF recommendations and are applied according to self-identified ethnicity [23]. (2) elevated triglycerides: TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L. (3) reduced HDL-C: <1.0 mmol/L in men or < 1.3 mmol/L in women, or treatment for low HDL-C. (4) elevated blood pressure: systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg, or antihypertensive treatment. (5) elevated fasting glucose: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.6 mmol/L or Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 39 mmol/mol, or glucose-lowering therapy. Since the UKB cohort primarily measured non-fasting glucose, which is influenced by recent dietary intake and consequently shows higher variability, HbA1c was employed as a surrogate marker. In accordance with American Diabetes Association guidelines, an HbA1c level exceeding 5.7% (39 mmol/mol) was used to define hyperglycemia [24]. The variables used to define the MetS components, along with their UKB field IDs, are listed in supplementary Table S1.

Definition of AIP and MACE outcome

The primary exposure was AIP, calculated as log₁₀ (TG / HDL-C), with TG and HDL-C measured in mmol/L. The primary outcome was composite MACE [25], defined in UKB as the first occurrence of any of the following five events: hospitalization for AMI ((International Classification of Diseases) ICD-10 I21 or I23); hospitalization for ischemic stroke (I63, I64), intracerebral hemorrhage (I61), or subarachnoid hemorrhage (I60); hospitalization for UA (I20); hospitalization for HF (I50); or CV death (ascertained using the UKB death registry derived variable “Date Death CVD”). The composite endpoint integrated inpatient electronic health records (based on primary or secondary ICD-10 diagnosis codes in hospital episode statistics databases) and fatal events recorded in national death registries. During follow-up, the first eligible nonfatal hospitalization or CV death was considered to meet the MACE endpoint.

Covariates

Covariates were grouped into three domains: sociodemographic variables (age, sex, ethnic, educational level, household income level, employment status, and the Townsend Deprivation Index(TDI); lifestyle factors (smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, and sleep duration); and clinical and laboratory measures (SBP, DBP, LDL-C, insulin medication, cholesterol medication, and blood pressure medication). Detailed categorizations of covariates are provided in the supplementary Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into quartiles of AIP (Q1–Q4). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± standard deviation (SD)) and compared across groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), whereas non-normally distributed variables are presented as median interquartile range [M (IQR)] and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test. Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages, n (%), and group differences are evaluated using the chi-square (²) test.

Missing data for all variables is summarized in Supplementary Table S3, and the distribution of missing values across study variables is displayed in Fig. S1. In the primary analysis, mixed-type missingness was addressed using random-forest imputation with missForest in R (ntree = 500; maxiter = 10). Cumulative incidence curves for MACE, stroke, AMI, CV death, UA, and HF were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier survival curve, and between-group differences were compared with the log-rank test. Associations between AIP and the primary and secondary endpoints were examined using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). The appendix reports the number of events, person-years, and incidence rates to better assess statistical power and interpret effect sizes. Three progressively adjusted models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 further adjusted for ethnicity, education level, household income, employment status, and TDI; and Model 3 additionally adjusted for alcohol consumption, physical activity, sleep duration, LDL-C, SBP, and DBP, insulin medication, cholesterol medication, and blood pressure medication. To assess potential multicollinearity among covariates, we calculated variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all variables included in the fully adjusted model, and no substantial collinearity was detected (all VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold; Table. S4). Dose–response relationships between AIP (modeled continuously) and each endpoint were fitted using RCS. To determine the optimal number of RCS knots, models with 3, 4, and 5 knots were fitted and compared using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), and the model with the lowest AIC was selected for the final knot specification. Knot locations were set according to Harrell’s recommendations using datadist to derive empirical quantiles: for three knots at approximately the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles; for four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles; and for five knots at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles. Nonlinearity was assessed with anova.rms by partitioning the AIP effect into linear and nonlinear components, and both the overall and nonlinearity P values are reported. We modeled the nonlinear relation between AIP and MACE using covariate-adjusted Cox models with RCS. The number of knots (3, 4, or 5) was selected by the minimum AIC, and knot locations followed Harrell’s recommendations using empirical quantiles from datadist. The turning point was defined as the minimum of the adjusted hazard ratio curve, and its 95% CI was estimated via nonparametric bootstrap (B = 2000). Detailed information on the inflection point point is reported in Supplementary Table S5. To assess robustness, subgroup analyses were performed by sex, age group, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, educational level, and household income level, and interactions were tested. Furthermore, we assessed the incremental predictive and risk-stratification value of adding AIP to the AHA PREVENT (2023/2024) and ESC SCORE2 (2021) base models using change in Harrell’s C-index (C-index), categorical net reclassification improvement (NRI) at 5% and 10% 10-year risk thresholds, category-free NRI, and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), reporting 95% confidence intervals.

Sensitivity analyses included: (1) multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption using the MICE framework implemented with miceforest, generating 10 imputed datasets with 5 iterations per dataset, passively recalculating AIP within each dataset, and pooling estimates using Rubin’s rules. (2) A complete-case analysis restricted to participants with complete data on exposure, outcome, and covariates; and (3) repeating the main analysis after excluding participants who developed MACE within the first two years of follow-up; (4) fitting Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models treating death as a competing risk for all outcomes (all-cause death for nonfatal endpoints, and non-cardiovascular death for MACE and cardiovascular death). (5) To compare the consistency of baseline characteristics and outcomes between MetS groups defined by FPG and HbA1c, we constructed contingency tables and fitted Cox models for 5-year MACE. (6) To test whether the U/J association was driven by reverse causality, medication use, or residual confounding, we conducted three sensitivity analyses: (i) excluded participants with MACE in the first two years of follow-up; (ii) excluded all medication users; and (iii) refit models with alternative exposure parameterizations.7) Building on Model 3, we additionally adjusted for statin use and CKD and repeated the primary analysis to assess the robustness of the results in the HF population. All sensitivity analyses used the same modeling and adjustment strategy as the primary analysis, and their results were compared with the main findings. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.2), and two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The participant selection flow is shown in Fig. 1 total of 131,736 participants were included in the final analysis, of whom 49.7% were men and 50.3% were women, with a mean age of 58.18 years. Participants were categorized into four groups (Q1–Q4) according to baseline AIP quartiles. AIP was categorized into quartiles based on its distribution in the analytic sample (N = 131,736): Q1 [− 0.808,0.101], Q2 (0.101,0.251], Q3 (0.251,0.410], and Q4 (0.410,1.398]. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) showed that, compared with Q1, Q4 had a significantly higher proportion of men (70.0% vs. 28.8%, P < 0.001), a lower mean age (56.61 vs. 59.34 years, P < 0.001), a higher proportion of current smokers (particularly those smoking ≥ 15 cigarettes/day), and a slightly higher proportion with at least a high-school education. With respect to physiological and biochemical measures, Q4 exhibited marked metabolic disturbances: SBP, DBP, LDL-C, TG, fasting glucose, HbA1c, CRP, and uric acid were all significantly higher than in Q1 (P < 0.001), whereas HDL-C was significantly lower (P < 0.001). In addition, waist circumference and body mass index (BMI) were significantly higher in Q4 than in the other groups. The proportion of participants meeting four or five MetS components was highest in Q4.

Association between AIP and outcomes

Association between AIP and MACE

Over 1,642,106 person-years of follow-up, 19,140 MACE events occurred (incidence rate 11.66 per 1000 person-years; 95% CI: 11.49–11.82) (Supplementary Table S6 and Table S7). In the MACE analysis, the Kaplan-Meier curves (Fig. 2A) showed that the highest AIP quartile (Q4) consistently had a greater cumulative risk than lower AIP groups, indicating a close association between higher AIP and increased MACE risk, with a statistically significant difference (log-rank P < 0.001). In multivariable Cox models (Table 2), when modeled continuously, each 1 SD increase in AIP was associated with a 5% higher risk of MACE in Model 1, which adjusted for age and sex (HR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.04–1.07, P < 0.001). In Model 2, which further adjusted for ethnicity, education, household income, employment status, and TDI, each 1 SD increase in AIP was associated with a 4% higher risk of MACE (1.04, 1.03–1.06, P < 0.001). In Model 3, which additionally adjusted for alcohol consumption, physical activity, sleep duration, LDL-C, SBP, and DBP, each 1 SD increase in AIP was associated with a 7% higher risk of MACE (1.07, 1.05–1.08, P < 0.001). In analyses by AIP quartiles, MACE risk rose with increasing AIP; in Model 3, Q3 vs. Q1 yielded HR = 1.09 (1.05–1.14, P < 0.001) and Q4 vs. Q1 yielded HR = 1.18 (1.13–1.23, P < 0.001). RCS analyses further demonstrated a significant nonlinear dose–response relationship between AIP and MACE risk (P overall < 0.001; P nonlinear = 0.002, k = 4), with an overall U-shaped pattern (Fig. 3A). Adding AIP to current 10-year CVD risk equations improved prediction and stratification (supplementary Table S8 and S9). In PREVENT, the C-index increased from 0.6592 to 0.6621 with a ΔC-index of 0.0029 (0.0022–0.0037), the continuous NRI was 0.0874 (0.0720–0.1020), the IDI was 0.0009 (0.0008–0.0010), and the categorical NRI improved at 5% and 10% thresholds with estimates of 0.0021 (0.001–0.003) and 0.0039 (0.0001–0.0070). In SCORE2, the C-index increased from 0.6514 to 0.6545 with a ΔC-index of 0.0031 (0.0023–0.0040), the continuous NRI was 0.0890 (0.0740–0.1041), the IDI was 0.0009 (0.0008–0.0010), and the categorical NRI improved at 5% and 10% with estimates of 0.0031 (0.0020–0.0042) and 0.0068 (0.0029–0.0107).

Association between AIP and AMI

Kaplan-Meier (K-M) analysis (Fig. 2B) showed that the cumulative risk of AMI in Q4 was consistently higher than in the other groups throughout follow-up (log-rank P < 0.001). Cox regression (Table 2) indicated a close association between higher AIP and increased AMI risk; per 1 SD increase in AIP, AMI risk rose by 16% (1.16, 1.12–1.19, P < 0.001). By quartiles, risk increased with AIP: Q2 (1.13, 1.03–1.23, P = 0.009), Q3 (1.29, 1.18–1.41, P < 0.001), and Q4 (1.48, 1.35–1.61, P < 0.001). RCS analysis (Fig. 3B) showed a nonlinear relationship between AIP and AMI risk (P overall < 0.001; P nonlinear = 0.002; k = 4), with an overall U-shaped curve.

Association between AIP and UA

KM curves (Fig. 2C) showed that the cumulative risk of UA in Q4 was significantly higher than in Q1 throughout follow-up (log-rank P < 0.001). As shown in Table 2, Cox regression indicated that each 1 SD increase in AIP was associated with a 16% higher risk of UA (1.16, 1.13–1.19, P < 0.001). In analyses by quartile, risk rose stepwise with higher AIP: Q2 vs. Q1 (1.10, 1.03–1.18, P = 0.007), Q3 (1.25, 1.16–1.34, P < 0.001), and Q4 (1.45, 1.36–1.56, P < 0.001) had higher risks. RCS analysis further revealed a J-shaped nonlinear association between AIP and UA risk (P overall < 0.001; P nonlinear = 0.013; k = 4; Fig. 3C).

Association between AIP and CV death

In the CV death analysis, KM curves showed that the cumulative risk in Q4 was higher than in Q1 (4.0% vs. 1.5%, log-rank P = 0.006; Fig. 2D). Cox regression (Table 2) indicated that no significant increase in risk was observed whether AIP was modeled as a continuous variable (per 1 SD: 1.01, 0.97–1.05, P = 0.540) or by quartiles. Compared with Q1, the HRs for Q2, Q3, and Q4 were 0.96 (0.86–1.07, P = 0.430), 0.97 (0.87–1.08, P = 0.560), and 1.06 (0.95–1.18, P = 0.280), respectively, none of which reached statistical significance. RCS analysis showed that both the overall and nonlinear terms were significant (P overall = 0.012; P nonlinear = 0.004; k = 3; Fig. 3D), but the curve displayed a U-shaped pattern.

Association between AIP and stroke

K–MKM analysis (Fig. 2E) showed no association between AIP and stroke risk (log-rank P = 0.250). As reported in Table 2, Cox analysis indicated that AIP was not significantly associated with stroke whether modeled continuously or by quartiles (per 1 SD: 1.03, 1.00–1.06; P = 0.089); in quartile analyses, the HRs for Q2, Q3, and Q4 vs. Q1 were 1.00 (0.91–1.09; P = 0.919), 1.06 (0.97–1.15; P = 0.196), and 1.05 (0.96–1.14; P = 0.337), respectively. RCS analysis likewise found no significant linear or nonlinear association (P overall = 0.190; P nonlinear = 0.501; k = 3; Fig. 3E), further supporting the absence of a significant relationship between AIP and stroke risk.

Association between AIP and HF

Risk analysis for HF showed a trend opposite to the other outcomes: KM curve analysis revealed that the lowest AIP quartile (Q1) had the highest cumulative risk, while the highest quartile (Q4) had the lowest. (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2F). Cox regression indicated no significant association between AIP modeled continuously and HF risk (per 1 SD: 1.01, 0.98–1.03, P = 0.590), whereas in quartile analyses the Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups had HRs of 0.96 (0.90–1.03, P = 0.260), 0.96 (0.89–1.03, P = 0.250), and 1.02 (0.95–1.10, P = 0.510). RCS analysis demonstrated a significant U-shaped association between AIP and HF risk (P overall = 0.007; P nonlinear = 0.002; k = 3; Fig. 3F).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Significant heterogeneity was observed for MACE by physical activity (P for interaction = 0.009), with progressively stronger associations from low to high activity (1.13 [1.02–1.25], 1.24 [1.15–1.35], and 1.31 [1.03–1.67]). The sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the primary analysis, confirming the robustness of our conclusions. After multiple imputation (m = 10), AIP remained positively and consistently associated with the outcome: the HR per 1 SD was 1.04,1.07, and per quantile increase (linear trend) was 1.04–1.07 (all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S10). In quartile analyses, Q4 vs. Q1 was consistently significant (1.12–1.19, P < 0.001), Q3 vs. Q1 was significant in the fully adjusted model (1.09, P < 0.001; borderline in Models 1–2), and Q2 vs. Q1 was not significant (Supplementary Table S11). After excluding all individuals with missing covariate data, the results were likewise stable; after adjustment for covariates, the direction, magnitude, and significance of the associations between AIP and MACE were consistent with the primary analysis (supplementary Table S12). After excluding participants who developed MACE within the first two years of follow-up, the main results remained stable, with HRs and 95% CIs highly concordant and statistical preserved (P < 0.05; supplementary Table S13). In Fine–Gray models with death as the competing event, AIP remained positively associated with the subdistribution risk of MACE, AMI, and UA (sHR = 1.12 (1.06–1.18), 1.55 (1.37–1.75), and 1.25 (1.17–1.34), all P < 0.001), whereas associations with HF (0.95 (0.87–1.05), P = 0.350), CV death (0.88 (0.75–1.03), P = 0.116), and stroke (1.01 (0.88–1.15), P = 0.923) were not significant; the overall pattern was consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S14). At the hyperglycemia component level, agreement between HbA1c ≥ 39 mmol/mol and FPG ≥ 5.6/6.1 mmol/L was 80.3%/83.3% with κ = 0.294/0.284 (Supplementary Table S15). When the remaining components were integrated to form the full MetS diagnosis, agreement rose to 94.4%/95.3% with = 0.872/0.892 (Supplementary Table S16). With identical covariate adjustment, the association of MetS with 5-year MACE was essentially unchanged across definitions (HR 1.49–1.50, all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table S17), supporting robustness. After excluding participants who experienced MACE within the first 2 years of follow-up, the nonlinear association between AIP and MACE remained significant (Model 3 χ²=8.24, df = 3, P = 0.0413). (Supplementary Table. S18–20) Excluding all users of lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, and insulin therapies yielded similar results (χ²=6.55, df = 2, P = 0.038). Results were consistent under alternative exposure parameterizations: when modeled as a standardized continuous variable, each 1 SD increase in AIP was associated with 1.06 (1.04–1.08; P < 0.001); quartile analyses and median-of-quartile trend tests were significant; and in the full model the spline fit was superior to a linear term (χ²=8.24, df = 3, P = 0.041). Overall, the U or J-shaped association was robust to removing early events, excluding medication users, and varying exposure specification; moreover, after excluding medication users and early events the curve was U-shaped (Fig. S2 and S3). After additionally adjusting for statin use and chronic kidney disease on top of the fully adjusted Model 3, AIP showed no independent association with incident heart failure, consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table. S21).

Discussion

Leveraging the large prospective UKB cohort, we systematically evaluated the associations between plasma AIP and the risks of MACE and its component events. Results showed that higher AIP was significantly and positively associated with the risks of MACE, AMI, and UA. RCS analyses further revealed U-shaped nonlinear relationships of AIP with MACE and AMI, and a J-shaped relationship with UA (P nonlinear ≤ 0.001). Notably, associations of AIP with CV death and stroke were not statistically significant (HR ≈ 1.0, P > 0.05), while an opposite trend was observed for HF, with the lowest AIP quartile (Q1) exhibiting the highest cumulative risk (log-rank P < 0.001). Additionally, adding AIP to current 10-year CVD risk equations yielded modest but statistically significant improvements in predicting MACE. These findings support integrating AIP into routine assessment for patients with MetS to enable earlier risk identification and targeted preventive strategies, with potential to reduce MACE.

In individuals with MetS, we found that higher baseline AIP is an independent predictor of increased risks of MACE, AMI, and UA. This conclusion aligns with prior evidence from studies in coronary heart disease, hypertension, and the general population. For example, Alifu et al. [26] reported in patients with chronic coronary syndrome that AIP independently predicts long-term MACE, with cardiovascular event risk rising markedly when AIP exceeds 0.24. Abdu et al. [27] likewise showed in patients with myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries that those in the highest AIP quartile had roughly twice the risk of MACE compared with the lowest quartile. In a nationwide large cohort [16], individuals in the highest AIP quartile also exhibited a significantly elevated future risk of cardiovascular events. Prospective studies in Chinese populations [28, 29] similarly indicated that elevated AIP predicts a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, with increased incidence of events including HF [30]. Collectively, these findings indicate that AIP is a reliable predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes across diverse populations.

AIP reflects the ratio of plasma TG to HDL-C and serves as a composite lipid metric characterizing atherosclerotic risk [31]. Elevated AIP may promote the occurrence of cardiovascular events through multiple mechanisms. First, AIP is closely related to the proportion of sdLDL-C; higher AIP indicates a higher proportion of sdLDL-C particles, which are more prone to oxidation and foam-cell formation, thereby facilitating atheromatous plaque development [32]. Second, HDL-C plays a key role in reverse cholesterol transport, antioxidation, anti-inflammation, and endothelial protection [33], whereas higher AIP is typically accompanied by lower HDL-C levels, further attenuating these protective effects [34]. In addition, elevated AIP often reflects the presence of insulin resistance and dysglycemia. Both cross-sectional and cohort studies have shown that higher AIP is strongly associated with insulin resistance and the incidence of type 2 diabetes [35]. Insulin resistance leads to increased triglycerides and reduced HDL-C, thereby aggravating atherogenic dyslipidemia. Moreover, a high-AIP state is frequently accompanied by systemic oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, factors that jointly promote plaque formation and instability [36]. Thus, elevated AIP captures an adverse lipid profile characterized by high TG and low HDL-C, and it increases the risk of cardiovascular adverse events through multiple pathways.

For HF, we observed that the lowest AIP quartile (Q1) paradoxically had the highest HF risk, with a clear overall U-shaped association. After multivariable adjustment, the association between AIP and HF was no longer statistically significant. This finding is consistent with evidence from prospective cohorts showing that MetS itself is an independent driver of new-onset HF through central obesity, elevated blood pressure, insulin resistance, and chronic low-grade inflammation, rather than through any single lipid indicator [37, 38]. It is noteworthy that in populations with less severe metabolic disturbance or in single-risk-factor cohorts, persistently high AIP or a high triglyceride to HDL-C ratio can predict subsequent HF or cardiovascular events, as demonstrated in recent studies of hypertensive or general populations [29]. The absence of a similarly clear positive association in our study therefore most likely reflects a paradoxical lipid pattern under conditions of heavy metabolic load: when obesity, insulin resistance, fatty liver, renal impairment, and lipid-lowering treatment coexist, lower lipid or AIP levels do not necessarily indicate lower HF risk and may instead signal a phenotype with greater metabolic consumption, more active inflammation, or higher overall disease burden. This interpretation parallels the lipid paradox described in HF, but in our setting it emerges in the context of MetS with complex comorbidities rather than in patients with established HF [39]. Consequently, our results support the view that AIP in MetS should be interpreted against the background of global metabolic, inflammatory, and treatment profiles, and should not be read in the simple unidirectional way that is often applied to general populations.

In our analysis, AIP was not independently associated with incident stroke. One plausible explanation is outcome heterogeneity. Prior multicenter case–control data have shown that the pattern and strength of associations between circulating lipids, lipoproteins and apolipoproteins and stroke differ across etiological subtypes, with clearer and more positive associations for large-artery atherosclerotic ischemic stroke than for cardioembolic or hemorrhagic stroke [40]. Recent analyses in individuals with Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic stages 0–3 have also described a non-linear, threshold-like relationship between AIP and stroke, indicating that the AIP signal can be detected only within a limited segment of the metabolic spectrum [41]. When all stroke types are modelled together in a metabolically overloaded MetS population, in which hypertensive, renal and inflammatory pathways also contribute to stroke, the atherothrombotic component that AIP actually reflects is likely to be diluted.

In this study, the main source of subgroup heterogeneity was the number of MetS components. Large prospective cohorts have shown that cardiovascular risk rises stepwise as each additional MetS component is added, indicating that MetS is not an all-or-none condition but rather a cumulative metabolic disturbance in which new pathogenic pathways are progressively engaged [42]. Our findings were consistent with this pattern: among participants with only three MetS components, the associations between AIP and stroke and between AIP and HF were inverse or neutral, whereas in those with four or five components, the lipid-driven signal was attenuated, and in HF the direction even tended to reverse. This suggests that AIP primarily captures the triglyceride/low-HDL related atherothrombotic component, but as the MetS burden increases, additional non-atherosclerotic pathways such as long-standing hypertension, renal impairment and chronic inflammation contribute more strongly to stroke and HF, so the proportion of risk that can be explained by AIP becomes smaller and may disappear in the 4–5 component subgroups.

Further analyses showed nonlinear associations between AIP and multiple cardiovascular outcomes, with U-shaped or J-shaped curves at the extremes. Large studies in community-based U.S. populations and in patients with hypertension have likewise observed U-shaped relationships between AIP or related lipid indices and cardiovascular outcomes [43, 44]. This pattern is biologically plausible. At the upper end of AIP, the index usually reflects an excess of triglyceride-rich remnant lipoproteins and a higher burden of apoB-containing particles; large Danish cohort and Mendelian randomization studies have demonstrated that an increment in nonfasting remnant cholesterol confers a substantially higher risk of ischemic heart disease independent of HDL cholesterol, indicating that this limb of the curve is consistent with a remnant/apoB-driven, inflammation-prone atherogenic pathway [45, 46]. At the lower end, a very low AIP may indicate extremely low TG and cholesterol or disproportionately high HDL-C, which can occur in catabolic or malnourished states or in the so-called high-HDL risk paradox [47, 48]; human genetic studies of SCARB1 have shown that loss-of-function variants can markedly raise HDL-C while paradoxically increasing coronary risk, supporting the interpretation that this segment of the distribution may capture HDL quantity–function discordance rather than a truly protective lipid phenotype [49]. Moreover, the CANTOS trial demonstrated that targeting the interleukin-1β axis reduces recurrent cardiovascular events without altering lipid levels, which is consistent with the notion that the high-AIP segment may in part be mediated through inflammation-sensitive mechanisms rather than lipids alone [50]. Consequently, both excessively high and excessively low AIP may be detrimental to cardiovascular health, and in clinical assessment physicians should be alert to cardiovascular risks concealed at the extremes of AIP and consider proactive management in individuals with persistently high AIP. Taken together, our nonlinear analyses suggest that a moderate AIP level may be a metabolically favorable zone, whereas deviation toward either extreme likely reflects activation of distinct biological pathways and therefore warrants clinical attention.

This study has several notable strengths. First, leveraging the broadly representative, large prospective cohort of UKB, we included over 130,000 MetS participants, ensuring ample sample size and robustness of the findings. We not only systematically evaluated the association between AIP and MACE, but also performed detailed analyses on specific component endpoints, including AMI, UA, HF, stroke, and CV death, thereby improving the comprehensiveness and clinical relevance of our findings.

However, this study also has several limitations. As an observational analysis within a prospective cohort, residual confounding and reverse causation cannot be fully eliminated, and causal inferences cannot be drawn. AIP and covariates were primarily obtained from single baseline measurements, which fail to capture long-term dynamics and may underestimate the impact of temporal variation in AIP on cardiovascular outcomes. Moreover, certain endpoints such as stroke exhibit substantial etiologic heterogeneity, and the lack of further differentiation among stroke subtypes in this study may affect the interpretation of the results. The UKB cohort is predominantly White, so external generalizability to other racial/ethnic groups and to the general population requires further validation. For rare outcomes such as CV death, limited events within subgroups may have reduced statistical power. Medication use was complex among some participants; although we adjusted to some extent, residual effects of medications on the AIP–cardiovascular risk association cannot be fully excluded.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that elevated AIP levels in individuals with MetS independently associated an increased risk of MACE, particularly in patients with advanced MetS, and reveal U-shaped/J-shaped nonlinear relationships. As a low-cost metric derived from routine lipid profiles, AIP enhances risk stratification and helps identify high-risk cases requiring intensified management. Our findings further underscore the clinical value of AIP in cardiovascular risk assessment among MetS patients. Future studies should involve long-term follow-up in more diverse populations to establish clinical cut-off points, elucidate underlying mechanisms, and verify through interventional trials whether lowering AIP can effectively reduce the incidence of clinical events.
