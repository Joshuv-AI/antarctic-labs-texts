Weak solutions and incompressible limit of a quasi-incompressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard model for viscous two-phase flows
Mingwen Fei, Xiang Fei, Daozhi Han, Yadong Liu

Abstract
. We study a quasi-incompressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard coupled system which describes the motion of two macroscopically immiscible incompressible viscous fluids with partial mixing in a small interfacial region and long-range interactions. The case of unmatched densities with mass-averaged velocity is considered so that the velocity field is no longer divergence-free, and the pressure enters the equation of the chemical potential. We first prove the existence of global weak solutions to the model in a three-dimensional periodic domain, for which the implicit time discretization together with a fixed-point argument to the approximate system is employed. In particular, we obtain a new regularity estimate of the order parameter by exploiting the partial damping effect of the capillary force. Then utilizing the relative entropy method, we establish the incompressible limit—the quasi-incompressible two-phase model converges to model H as the density difference tends to zero. Crucial to the passage of the incompressible limit, due to the lack of regularity of the pressure, are some non-standard uniform-in-density difference controls of the pressure, which are derived from the structure of the momentum equations and the improved regularity of the order parameter.

Introduction

In this article, we study a
diffuse interface model
(also referred to as
phase-field
model
) for two incompressible, immiscible fluids of different densities and viscosities. Let
T>0
,
Q_{T}\coloneqq\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,T)
.
The model consists of the quasi-incompressible Navier–Stokes equations coupled with the Cahn–Hilliard equation in
Q_{T}
:

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{u})+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\big(S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})\big)-(\nabla p+\phi\nabla\mu),

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\alpha\Delta\mu_{p},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\mathrm{div}(\phi\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\Delta\mu_{p},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=F^{\prime}(\phi)+\Lambda^{2s}\phi,

(1.1e)

\displaystyle\mu_{p}

\displaystyle=\mu+\alpha p,

(1.1f)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\rho(\phi)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\phi+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+1,

with the initial conditions

(1.1g)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}|_{t=0}=\mathbf{u}_{0},\ \phi|_{t=0}=\phi_{0},\quad\text{in $\mathbb{T}^{3}$}.

Here the unknowns
(\phi,\mathbf{u},p,\mu):Q_{T}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
are the order parameter (volume fraction difference), the mass-averaged (barycentric) mixture velocity, the pressure and the chemical potential, respectively. The function
F
denotes the homogeneous Helmholtz free energy of double-well type.
The constant
\varepsilon\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}-1
measures the relative difference of the two densities
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
. Without loss of generality,
\varepsilon
is assumed to be negative, i.e.,
\rho_{2}<\rho_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\alpha\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}{\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}\in(0,1).

The matrix

\displaystyle S(\phi,\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u})\coloneqq 2\eta(\phi)\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}-\frac{2}{3}\eta(\phi)(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})\mathbf{I}

denotes the Newtonian stress tensor with the symmetric gradient
\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathbf{u}+(\nabla\mathbf{u})^{\top})
, where

\displaystyle\eta(\phi)=\frac{\nu-1}{2}\phi+\frac{\nu+1}{2}

is the viscosity of the mixture and

\nu>0
stands for the constant viscosity ratio of the two fluids.

In (
1.1d
), for
s>0
the fractional Laplacian operator in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
is defined by

\displaystyle\Lambda^{2s}\phi(x)=(-\Delta)^{s}\phi(x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}|k|^{2s}\widehat{\phi}(k)e^{ix\cdot k}

with

\widehat{\phi}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}e^{-ix\cdot k}f(x)\mathrm{d}x.

By direct computation one has

\displaystyle\widehat{\Lambda^{2s}\phi}(k)=|k|^{2s}\widehat{\phi}(k),\ k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

We refer to
[
38
,
47
,
48
,
49
]
for the derivation of such models when
s=1
. In particular, if
\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}
, the quasi-incompressible model (
1.1
) reduces to the classical
model H

[
37
,
39
]
for two-phase flows of matched densities.

The total energy
E
of (
1.1
) is defined as

(1.2)

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{u},\phi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\frac{1}{2}\rho|\mathbf{u}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(F(\phi)+\frac{1}{2}|\Lambda^{s}\phi|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Then smooth solutions of (
1.1
) fulfill the following energy dissipation law

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(\mathbf{u},\phi)=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left(2\eta(\phi)\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}-\frac{2}{3}\eta(\phi)(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\nabla\mu_{p}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where one recalls the fact

\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}:\mathbb{D}\mathbf{u}-\frac{1}{3}(\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u})^{2}\geq 0.

Note that (
1.1b
) and (
1.1f
) imply

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi+\mathrm{div}(\phi\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\mathrm{div}\mathbf{u}.

Recalling the definition of
\rho
in (
1.1f
) and
\alpha=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2+\varepsilon}
, one recovers the continuity equation (conservation of mass)
[
2
,
48
]

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathrm{div}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0.

1.1.
Literature review and current contribution

Diffuse interface fluid models assume a thin diffusive layer of finite thickness separating two fluids inside which the two fluids mix due to chemical diffusion. Because they allow for smooth interface topological transitions (such as bubble merging or splitting), diffuse interface models have become an important tool in the study of interfacial dynamics of multiphase flows. For incompressible two-phase flows of nearly matched densities, the model is known as Model H
[
37
,
39
]
taking the form of the incompressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard equations. There is a vast literature dedicated to the analysis and simulations of Model H which we refrain from reviewing. In the case of unmatched densities, Lowengrub and Truskinovsky
[
41
]
derived a
quasi-incompressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard system, utilizing the concentration as the order parameter with the density present in the Cahn–Hilliard equation. In
[
47
]
the volume fraction is adopted as the order parameter, which greatly simplified the Cahn–Hilliard equation. Recently, Shokrpour Roudbari et al.
[
48
]
re-derived the quasi-incompressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard model, i.e. model (
1.1
) with
s=1
, based on the mixture theory and the Coleman-Noll procedure. In
[
49
]
, a unified derivation and comparison of the known diffuse interface models were explored from the physical point of view. It is also possible to derive solenoidal phase field fluid models by volume-averaging the velocity, cf.
[
8
,
9
,
14
,
16
,
18
]
and
[
5
,
7
,
21
,
33
,
34
]
for relevant models and analytical results. Finally for a diffuse interface model for compressible fluids, Abels and Feireisl
[
6
]
constructed weak solutions globally in time, and the low Mach number limit is justified in
[
10
]
by the relative entropy method.

In order to describe the long-range interactions among particles for the phase transition phenomenon, nonlocal versions of Cahn–Hilliard equation were employed extensively. One motivation for considering a nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard equation comes from its original physical formulation
[
17
]
. In that setting, the Laplace operator appearing in the chemical potential was initially replaced by a spatial convolution term specifically designed to capture long-range particle interactions.
From this perspective, it seems to be more faithful to the underlying physical phenomena to employ the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}\phi
, which can be exactly represented as a convolution integral for smooth functions.
For the Cahn–Hilliard equation with the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta)^{s}\mu
and
(-\Delta)^{\sigma}\phi
, Akagi, Schimperna and Segatti
[
13
]

showed the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions and long-time behavior of solutions in the case that
0<s,\sigma<1
, and the singular limits as either
s
or
\sigma
tends to zero. Ainsworth and Mao
[
12
]
later investigated the global weak solutions of the fractional Cahn–Hilliard equation when
s=1
and
\frac{1}{2}<\sigma\leq 1
.
Readers are also referred to other related modelling and analytical results (mostly with spatial convolutions) on nonlocal Cahn–Hilliard equation
[
4
,
15
,
20
,
32
,
35
,
42
,
44
]
, and nonlocal Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard system
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
40
]
.

The major difficulty associated with the quasi-incompressible model (
1.1
), compared to Model H, lies in the fact that the velocity field is no longer divergence-free and the pressure enters the chemical potential. As observed in
[
2
]
, the low temporal regularity of the pressure prevents further regularity gains of the order parameter. In particular the usage of the logarithmic potential could not be justified. For weak solutions of the model by Lowengrub and Truskinovsky, Abels
[
2
]
employed the
p
-Laplacian with a slightly modified potential to ensure that the order parameter does not deviate from its physical bounds too much. Existence of local strong solution is established in
[
3
]
.

It is noted that once the densities of two fluids are matched, the model reduces to the classical model H. This is also observed formally in
[
2
]
. However, the limit passage from quasi-incompressible models to Model H is once again out of reach due to the lack of regularities mentioned above. A related quasi-incompressible model derived from mixture theory was investigated by Feireisl, Lu and Málek
[
28
]
, where they studied the incompressible limit with the same parameter as our ratio
\alpha=\frac{\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}}{\rho_{2}+\rho_{1}}
, see
[
28
, (2.13)]
. Moreover, Giorgini
[
34
]
showed a stability result for the strong solutions to the Abels–Garcke–Grün model derived in
[
8
]
and model H
[
39
]
in terms of the density values, which provides a convergence result for the diffuse interface model with volume-averaged velocity. It is essential that in our case, pressure enters the chemical potential equation (
1.4e
), demanding additional estimates for it. Finally, for diffuse interface models, the incompressible limit results are quite limited. Here we complement with two known results for two-phase flows by Feireisl, Petcu and Pražák
[
31
]
and Abels, Liu and Nečasová
[
10
]
, where the former one justified the incompressible limit of a compressible Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn model with low Mach number via the relative entropy method, and the latter one addressed a compressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard counterpart.

The purpose of current contribution is to study the existence of weak solutions to the quasi-incompressible two-phase flow model (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{T}^{3}
. In particular, we aim at generalizing the analysis obtained in
[
2
]
with the
p
-Laplacian to the current fractional Laplacian setting. Moreover, we are going to present a first convergence result of the incompressible limit for such quasi-incompressible models for two-phase flows with small density differences.

Several remarks are in order.

Remark 1.4
.

It is noted that for the weak solutions, we derive an additional higher-order regularity (
1.14
), compared to standard Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard equations, cf.
[
2
,
5
]
. Here we found that in fact, the capillary force provides partial damping effects for the order parameter
\phi

\phi\nabla\mu\sim\phi\nabla\Delta\phi
, which is roughly third-order in
\phi

\phi
, cf. Lemma

. Furthermore, it is crucial to employ the higher-order regularity of

in further analysis of the imcompressible limit due to the lack of regularity of pressure, cf. Remark
1.7

Remark 1.5
.

Typically, one shall derive a physically reasonable range for
\phi
in
(-1,1)
by emplying the singular potential, e.g., the Flory–Huggins potential (logarithmic type)

\displaystyle F(s)=\frac{\vartheta}{2}\Big[(1+s)\log(1+s)+(1-s)\log(1-s)\Big]-\frac{\vartheta_{0}}{2}s^{2},\quad s\in[-1,1]

for
0<\vartheta<\vartheta_{0}
, cf.
[
1
,
5
,
7
,
34
]
.
Analogous to
[
2
]
where the Lowengrub–Truskinovski model
[

]
was analyzed, in our case a singular potential is not applicable as well. Since the pressure

enters (

leads to the crucial issue for the estimate of

in some Lebesgue space. Thanks to the higher-order fractional operator

with

Here is a comment on the incompressible limit.

Remark 1.7
.

For the incompressible limit, there are in principle three ways to rigorously justify it. First one concerns the weak solutions replies on the weak convergence method by uniform estimates directly. This does not work right now due to the lack of regularity of pressure. The second one is to derive the error estimates for the strong solution of original and limit systems, which may demand delicate energy estimates. This can be one of the further steps rendered from the authors previous work on strong well-posedness
[
26
]
. The last one is based on the so-called weak-strong principle, meaning the weak solution coincides with the unique strong solution as long as the latter one exists. The general argument to justify it is based on the relative entropy method, which is exactly the idea of current contribution. The main step is defining the relative (modulate) entropy (energy) that measures the distance of weak and strong solutions, and then deriving certain estimates for the remainders and closing it with Grönwall’s inequality. Readers are referred to e.g.
[
10
,
27
,
29
,
31
]
for the applications in fluid mechanics.

The main difficulty is that one can not derive a uniform estimate independent of
\alpha
for the pressure in some Lebesgue space
L^{q}(Q_{T})
with
q>1
. In contrast to the incompressible limit of compressible fluids, the remainder terms involving the pressure cannot be directly controlled in the present setting. Fortunately, one shall derive new uniform-in-
\alpha
controls for the pressure with the momentune equation and the improved regularity of
\phi
, cf. Lemmas
5.3
,
5.4
, which are critical to take the incompressible limit.

\displaystyle\leq C(T^{\prime},D)\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\Lambda^{s}\phi-\Lambda^{s}\phi_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\tau}\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\right).

Submitting (
5.3
)–(
5.54
) into (
5.3
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\bigg[E(\phi_{\alpha},\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}|\phi,\mathbf{u})\bigg]_{t=0}^{t=\tau}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\nabla\mu_{p,\alpha}-\nabla\mu|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\frac{C_{1}}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,\tau;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(T^{\prime},D)\alpha+C(T^{\prime},D)\int_{0}^{\tau}E(\phi_{\alpha},\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}|\phi,\mathbf{u})(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(5.55)

\displaystyle\quad+(C(T^{\prime},D)+C_{2})\int_{0}^{\tau}E(\phi_{\alpha},\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}|\phi,\mathbf{u})(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Finally, we apply the Grönwall’s inequality to (
5.3
) and derive

\displaystyle E(\phi_{\alpha},\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}|\phi,\mathbf{u})(\tau)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\nabla\mu_{p,\alpha}-\nabla\mu|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t+\frac{C_{1}}{2}\|\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}-\mathbf{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,\tau;H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))}^{2}

(5.56)

\displaystyle\leq C(T^{\prime},D)\bigg(\alpha+E(\phi_{\alpha},\mathbf{u}_{\alpha}|\phi,\mathbf{u})(0)\bigg).

This completes the proof of Theorem
5.1
. ∎

Appendix A
Technical Tools

A.1.
Weak Neumann Laplace operator

This part is adapted from
[
3
]
, one can also see
[
26
]
.

Define the orthogonal projection onto
L^{2}_{(0)}(\mathbb{T}^{3})=\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3}):\bar{f}\triangleq\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{3}|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0\}
by

\displaystyle P_{0}f\triangleq f-\bar{f}.

Furthermore, we define a Hilbert space
H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
by

\displaystyle H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})=H^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\cap L^{2}_{(0)}(\mathbb{T}^{3})

with the following inner product

\displaystyle(c,d)_{H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}=(\nabla c,\nabla d)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})},\ \ \forall c,d\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}).

The weak Neumann-Laplace operator
\Delta:H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))\rightarrow H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))
is defined by

(A.1)

\displaystyle-\langle\Delta u,\varphi\rangle_{H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}),H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}=(\nabla u,\nabla\varphi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})},\quad\text{for $u,\varphi\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})$},

where
H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})=\big(H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\big)^{\prime}
.
By the Lemma of Lax-Milgram, for every
f\in H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
there is a unique
u\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})

such that
\Delta u=f
.

Moreover, we embed
H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
and
L^{2}_{(0)}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
into
H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
in the standard way by defining

\displaystyle\langle c,\varphi\rangle_{H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}),H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}=\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}c(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\quad\text{for $\varphi\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}),c\in L^{2}_{(0)}(\mathbb{T}^{3})$},

which gives us a useful inequality

(A.2)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}^{2}\leq\|f\|_{H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\|f\|_{H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})},\forall f\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}).

Furthermore, assuming
\Delta u=f
for
f\in H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\leq\|f\|_{H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}.

Finally, we define
\mathrm{div}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\rightarrow H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
as

(A.3)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{div}f,\varphi\rangle_{H_{(0)}^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{3}),H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}=-(f,\nabla\varphi)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})},\quad\text{for $\varphi\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})$},

which and (
A.1
) imply that
\Delta u=\mathrm{div}\nabla u
for
u\in H_{(0)}^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
.

In this part, we show some auxiliary results.

Lemma A.1
.

[

Let
X,Y
be two Banach spaces satisfying that
Y\hookrightarrow X

densely. For

Similar to

Lemma A.2
.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
. Then for every
f\in(W_{(0)}^{2,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))^{\prime}
, we can find a unique solution
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
such that

\displaystyle(u,\Delta\varphi)=\left\langle f,\varphi\right\rangle_{(W_{(0)}^{2,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))^{\prime},W_{(0)}^{2,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}

for all
\varphi\in W_{(0)}^{2,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3}):=\{f\in\varphi\in W^{2,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3}):\bar{f}=0\}
. Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C_{p}

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{(W_{(0)}^{2,p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{T}^{3}))^{\prime}}.

Next, we show following important results about
\Lambda^{2s}
that will used to show the existence of approximate solutions.

Lemma A.3
.

The pseudo-differential operator
\Lambda^{2s}:H^{s}\rightarrow H^{-s}
is of type M (see
[
45
]
). For
\lambda>0
, we can derive that
\lambda+\Lambda^{2s}:H^{s}\rightarrow H^{-s}
is bijective with strongly continuous inverse.

Proof.

We can easily show for
u,v\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda^{2s}u-\Lambda^{2s}v,u-v\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}=\|\Lambda^{s}(u-v)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geq 0

and

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda^{2s}(u+(h+t)v),v\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}-\langle\Lambda^{2s}(u+tv),v\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}\leq h\|v\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}^{2},\text{ for }h>0.

Hence, we can prove that
\Lambda^{2s}
is of type M by
[
45
, Lemma 2.1 in Chapter II]
.

In order to prove that
\lambda+\Lambda^{2s}
is bijective. We first verify that

\displaystyle\lambda\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}+\langle\Lambda^{2s}u,u\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}=\lambda(u,u)_{L^{2}}+(\Lambda^{s}u,\Lambda^{s}u)_{L^{2}}\gtrsim\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}^{2},

hence we can get that
\lambda+\Lambda^{2s}
is coercive. By
[
45
, Lemma 2.1 and Corollary 2.2 in Chapter II]
, we then deduce that
\lambda+\Lambda^{2s}
is surjective. Besides, we can prove that

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda^{2s}u-\Lambda^{2s}v,u-v\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}=0\ \text{if only if $u=v$.}

Combining the above results, the proof of the first part of this lemma is completed.

Now we begin to show strongly continuous inverse of
\lambda+\Lambda^{2s}
. We assume that

(\lambda+\Lambda^{2s})u_{n}=f_{n},(\lambda+\Lambda^{2s})u=f
and
f_{n}\rightarrow f
in
H^{-s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
, then we have

\displaystyle\|u_{n}-u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda\langle u_{n}-u,u_{n}-u\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}+\langle\Lambda^{2s}u_{n}-\Lambda^{2s}u,u_{n}-u\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}

\displaystyle=\langle f_{n}-f,u_{n}-u\rangle_{H^{-s},H^{s}}\rightarrow 0(n\rightarrow\infty),

i.e.,
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
.

Hence, we get the desired results.
∎

The following lemma ensures the energy inequality of the limit passage.

Lemma A.4
.

[

]

Let
E:[0,T)\rightarrow[0,\infty)

\displaystyle E(0)\varphi(0)+\int_{0}^{T}E(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)dt\geq\int_{0}^{T}D(t)\varphi(t)dt

The following lemma shows that for given

Lemma A.5
.

Let

,
s>\frac{3}{2}
. Then there is an extension
F\in C^{3}(\mathbb{R})
fulfilling
F(\phi)\geq 0
,

such that such that

\displaystyle-1-\theta<\phi<1+\theta

if
\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{3}|}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\phi\,\mathrm{d}x=:\overline{\phi}\in(-\infty,+\infty)

(A.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Big(F(\phi)+\frac{1}{2}|\Lambda^{s}\phi|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\leq R.

Proof.

Firstly, we can note that

\displaystyle\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}

\displaystyle=|\hat{\phi}(0)|^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},|k|\geq 1}|\hat{\phi}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{T}^{3}|^{2}|\overline{\phi}|^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},|k|\geq 1}|\hat{\phi}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathbb{T}^{3}|^{2}|\overline{\phi}|^{2}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3},|k|\geq 1}|k|^{2s}|\hat{\phi}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{T}^{3}|^{2}|\overline{\phi}|^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}|\Lambda^{s}\phi|^{2}dx
