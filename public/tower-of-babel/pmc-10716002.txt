Deep learning signature based on multiphase enhanced CT for bladder cancer recurrence prediction: a multi-center study
Authors: Hexiang Wang, Meng Zhang, Jianguo Miao, Feng Hou, Yunqing Chen, Yonghua Huang, Lei Yang, Shifeng Yang, Chencui Huang, Yancheng Song, Haitao Niu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAccurate stratification of recurrence risk for bladder cancer (BCa) is essential for precise individualized therapy. This study aimed to develop and validate a model for predicting the risk of recurrence in BCa patients postoperatively using 3-phase enhanced CT images.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 874 BCa patients across four centers between January 2006 and December 2021. Patients from one center were used as training set, while the remaining patients went into the validation set. We trained a deep learning (DL) model based on convolutional neural networks using 3-phase enhanced CT images. The resulting prediction scores were entered into Cox regression analysis to obtain DL scores and construct a DL signature. DL scores and clinical features were then used as deep learning radioclinical signature. The predictive performance of DL signature was assessed according to concordance index and area under curve compared with deep learning radioclinical signature, clinical model and a widely accepted staging grading system. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS) were also predicted in order to further assess survival benefits.FindingsDL signature showed strong power for predicting recurrence (concordance index, 0.869; area under curve, 0.889) in validation set, outperforming other models and system. In addition, we divided RFS and OS into high and low risk groups by selecting appropriate cutoff values for DL signature, and calculated cumulative recurrence risk rates for both groups.InterpretationOur proposed DL signature shows promising potential as clinical aid for predicting postoperative recurrence risk in BCa and for stratifying the risk of RFS and OS, which can be applied to guide personalized precision therapy.FundingThere are no sources of funding for this manuscript.Keywords: Bladder cancer, Deep learning, Recurrence, Risk stratification, Prognosis

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Accurate stratification of bladder cancer (BCa) recurrence risk is important for precise individualized therapy. Radiomics is making substantial progress in addressing various challenging clinical problems. We searched pubmed and the Web of Science for terms up to December 2021 (“Bladder cancer”, “Bladder neoplasms”, “Muscle-invasive bladder cancer”, “Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer”, “Radiomics”, “Machine learning”, “Deep learning”, “Computed tomography”, “Cross-sectional imaging”, or “Enhanced scanning”), without language restrictions. No study has yet reported successful prediction of BCa recurrence using end-to-end deep learning (DL) signature extracted from multiphase CT images.

Added value of this study

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicenter study based on end-to-end DL signature from multiphase CT images to predict BCa recurrence. Based on our results, we developed and validated DL signature to predict postoperative recurrence risk in BCa patients, and compared with clinical model and widely accepted staging grading system, highlighting the validity of DL signature.

Implications of all the available evidence

DL signature can be used to predict the risk of recurrence, stratify the risk of RFS and OS after BCa surgery, and guide personalised precision therapy, showing great potential as a clinical adjunct.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) is one of the most prevalent epithelial tumors of the urinary tract.1 Although treatment of BCa has improved across a wide range of therapeutic schedules, such as intravesical chemotherapy, surgery, and immunotherapy, the recurrence rate within 5 years remains high.2,3 Patients with non-muscular invasive BCa (NMIBC) may be treated via transurethral resection of bladder tumor, but have a reported recurrence rate of 61% within 2 years after surgery.4 In contrast, for patients with muscle-invasive BCa (MIBC), the recurrence rate within 2 years is in the 5%–50% range, even after radical bladder resection.5 To improve the postoperative survival of BCa patients, patients with high risk of recurrence should be offered more aggressive and effective therapeutic treatment in addition to close follow-up monitoring.6 It is therefore critically important that BCa patients be accurately stratified according to their level of recurrence risk, so as to guide more efficient and personalized treatment.

Patients suffering from recurrent superficial BCa may undergo a repeat resection or receive intravesical treatment,7,8 but these options can reduce their quality of life.9, 10, 11, 12 Identifying a high recurrence risk of BCa is important for deciding whether more aggressive treatment is needed. Predicting postoperative BCa recurrence risk solely based on the experience of the treating physician may be affected by subjectivity.13 Several prediction tools have been developed by researchers. The most widely used clinical scales for assessing BCa recurrence risk were established by the Club Urológico Español de Tratamiento Oncológico (CUETO)14 and by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC).15 Recently, Hyung Suk Kim proposed novel nomograms for predicting recurrence and progression in Asian patients.16 But these tools only target primary NMIBC patients while overlooking MIBC patients, thus limiting their clinical application.16,17 The staging system proposed by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) can also be applied to predict the risk of postoperative recurrence in BCa patients; however, it is relatively inaccurate and fails to incorporate relevant information, such as the latest tumor markers.18 Consequently, a reliable predictive model for assessing the risk of BCa recurrence is urgently needed to perform precise individual therapy and prolong survival.

Radiomics has extensively exploited big data information of images by capturing subtle visual cues, which allows diagnostic and prognostic assessment of specific regions of interest (ROI).19 Currently, radiomics is making substantial progress in tackling various challenging clinical problems.20 For example, Xu et al.21 built a predictive nomogram for individualized stratification of BCa recurrence based on multiparametric MRI, which performed well in predicting recurrence risk within 2 years of surgery. In a preliminary study, Qian et al.6 showed that, when combined with important clinical risk factors, radiomic features extracted from multiphase CT can predict the recurrence of BCa within 2 years postoperatively. In addition, deep learning (DL) signature automatically derived by convolutional neural networks (CNN) perform robust image discrimination across large datasets, which is increasingly gaining attention and leading to numerous applications.22 Notwithstanding the significant progress summarized above, no prior study has reported successful prediction of BCa recurrence using end-to-end DL signature extracted from multiphase CT images acquired across multiple centers.

The aim of our study was to develop and validate a model for predicting the risk of postoperative recurrence in BCa patients using DL signature from multicenter data. Furthermore, we made comparisons with clinical model and a widely accepted staging grading system to illustrate the validity of our model in predicting recurrence.

Materials and methods

Patient and tumor baseline characteristics

This multicenter retrospective study collected data on BCa patients from four centers (the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University, the Puyang Oilfield General Hospital and the Affiliated Qingdao Central Hospital of Qingdao University). Images were collected between January 2006 and December 2021, and all patients had a postoperative pathological diagnosis. The current study was conducted with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines.23 The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) BCa confirmed by surgery; (2) complete clinicopathological data; and (3) CT scans performed within 20 days prior to surgery and complete records of the CT images used to evaluate lesions. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete or missing CT images and clinicopathological data; (2) lesions with hard-to-define borders; (3) presence of other unrelated malignancies; and (4) there is metastasis during diagnosis. We included a total of 874 BCa patients. The Tumor-Node-Metastasis classification was assigned after surgery in accordance with the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual 8th edition. Pathological results were acquired by two pathologists with 11 years and 7 years of experience working in urological diseases. Our study was reported in accordance with the STROCSS criteria24 and STARD-2015 guidelines (equator-network.org).

Ethics

All participating centers’ Institutional Review Board approved the study protocols with the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University No. QYFY WZLL 28166, Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University No.2023-526, the Puyang Oilfield General Hospital No. 203-05-0019-E01, and the Affiliated Qingdao Central Hospital of Qingdao University No. KY2022193765. The informed consent was waived because of the multicenter retrospective nature of this study and due to all procedures were part of routine care.

Follow-up

Recurrence-free survival (RFS) time was calculated as the outcome from the time they received operation until local recurrence in the bladder or the last follow-up visit, or recurrence-free death. Overall survival (OS) was calculated as the period from the time of surgery to death or the last follow-up visit. Patients were followed up every 3–6 months after surgery for the first 2 years, and then every 6 months. Tracking data were collected from medical records, including physical exams, images, telephone inquiries, and Medicare records. These data were reviewed on June 30, 2022.

Image acquisition

All patients underwent pre-treatment CT scans using spiral CT scanners. The settings used for CT scanning are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Three phase images were collected for each patient: corticomedullary phase (25 s), nephrographic phase (75 s), and excretory phase (300 s).

Patient and CT morphological characteristics

Age and gender of all patients were recorded. All diagnostic images were interpreted independently and retrospectively by two radiologists with 10 and 6 years of experience, neither of whom was aware of any clinical or pathological data. If there were differences, they were resolved through discussion. The recorded characteristics were as follows: (1) maximum diameter (in centimeters); (2) number of tumors; (3) shape (cauliflower-shaped, papillary, mounded); (4) bladder wall thickness; (5) location of tumor lesions (trigone, lateral wall, posterior wall, anterior wall, parietal wall, lower wall, multiple areas); (6) calcification (present or absent); (7) cystic necrosis (present or absent); (8) tumor boundary (present or absent); (9) stalk (present or absent); (10) extramural infiltration (present or absent); (11) CT value of lesion in corticomedullary phase; (12) CT value of lesion in nephrographic phase; and (13) CT value of lesion in excretory phase.

Segmentation of region of interest

We performed three-dimensional ROI segmentation of the tumor with ITK-SNAP open-source software (v.3.8.0; http://www.itksnap.org). Two experienced radiologists outlined the ROI, layer by layer, along the edge of cancer lesions within bladder tissue from preoperative CT images. When ROI delineation was controversial between the two radiologists, a consensus was reached after discussion between them.

Medical image pre-processing

For the 3-phase CT images, a generalized scale method was first performed to standardize the intensity of pixels.25 Centers of ROI region in the layer where the largest lesion lies were selected for alignment. To reduce errors caused by manual delineation, nephrographic phase and excretory phase were selected in the same layer as the first corticomedullary phase at the time of scanning. Then, the largest ROI region of each patient's tumor was cropped to reduce the size of image, and for preservation of edge's features, a rectangle area 1.05 times as long as the maximum length and width of the ROI area was cropped respectively. For patients with multiple tumors, the largest tumor was selected for analysis. The cropped images were converted to gray-scaled single-channel images. To ensure smooth convergence of model training, pixel values were normalized from [0, 255] to [0, 1],26 and stacked into three-channel images that could be input simultaneously during the training process. To implement uniform formatting, all images were reshaped to a size of 120 pixels × 120 pixels. DL models require large amounts of data for training, making data augmentation on training set essential to avoid overfitting and increase model robustness.27 We applied flipping and rotation to all images in our training set.28 Flipping included both horizontal and vertical symmetrical flips, while rotation involved increments of 15° from 0° to 360°, such as rotations at 15°, 30°, 45°, and so on. This procedure didn't change the characteristics of the tumor. Images that were not generalized scales but had been subsequently processed were also used to train more models and contribute to the final prediction.

Recurrence prediction model based on 3D-CNN deep learning

This study used a 3D-CNN architecture based on 3-phase CT images to predict the recurrence risk of postoperative BCa (Supplementary Figure S1). The network consisted of a total of four 3D convolutional layers with kernel sizes of 12 × 12 × 3 × 3, 5 × 5 × 3 × 6, 5 × 5 × 6 × 16, and 5 × 5 × 16 × 120. Pooling layers were applied after the first, second, and third convolutional layers. Two sets of fully connected layers (120 and 60 units) act as classifiers for the entire CNN to improve the learning ability of the model. Batch normalization was added between the two fully connected layers, which served not only as a regularization process, but also to speed up the training process and improve model accuracy. To balance the dataset, our model takes the Focal Loss multiplied by weights, and adds the imbalance weights incrementally.29 The unbalanced weight ratio of recurrence to non-recurrence was set at 400:100 and the weight of the recurrence category was increased by 3.5 per epoch, as defined by the following equation:

pˆ is prediction probability, Wt is the imbalance weights of different categories, ct is the change in imbalance weight of each category per epoch, γ >0 is an adjustable factor when prediction is accurate, (1-pt) tends to be 0, otherwise it tends to be 1 to enhance the importance of the category of classification error and achieve the purpose of imbalance treatment. As the value of γ increased, the strength of the unbalanced processing became stronger. In this experiment, the value of γ is 2.

The model output is a two-dimensional vector such as [0, 1], representing the predicted probability of non-recurrence is 0 and the predicted probability of recurrence is 1. During training, we used Stochastic Gradient Descent with a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 without decay for 100 epochs, and a batch size of 1024. The loss curve for the training process is shown in the Supplementary Figure S2. During convergence, the loss remains stable between 0 and 0.0005, reaching convergence around the 100th epoch. We trained, tuned, and tested the 3D-CNN on an Intel i7-12700H 14-core CPU and an RTX 3060 12 GB GPU using PyTorch (Version: 2.0.0, https://pytorch.org/) and CUDA (Version 11.6.0, Nvidia). Early stopping was used to prevent overfitting. Multiple models were trained for combinatorial forecasting based on voting method. By combining models, classification thresholds and the model weights can be easily tuned to adjust the importance given to recursive or non-recursive categories. We used class activation maps (CAM) to monitor the suspected bladder tumor areas selected by the network to make decisions regarding recurrence or non-recurrence.30

Model development and validation

Input prediction scores from the 3D-CNN to Cox regression analysis to get DL scores for constructing the DL signature. Clinical data and CT morphologic features associated with BCa recurrence were analyzed using univariate Cox regression with p < 0.05. The variables included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis were those with p < 0.05 as independent risk factors for recurrence of BCa, and were included in the construction of clinical model. We integrated DL scores with selected clinical risk factors through univariate Cox regression and multivariate Cox regression and input them into Cox regression analysis to develop a deep learning radioclinical signature (DLCS). In addition, Cox regression analysis was executed on AJCC system. We evaluated model performance using concordance index (C index), area under the curve (AUC), and integrated Brier score (IBS). The C index measures predictive performance.31 The DeLong test was applied to compare different Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. To estimate prediction errors, IBS was calculated with the “Boot632plus” splitting method. For the purpose of calibrating the model, we derived calibration curves with 1000 iterations of the bootstrap sampling method. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to select the model with the best net clinical benefit. The analysis codes are accessible in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/QDUmiao/DL_for_BCa.git).

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.2, http://www.R-project.org) and SPSS statistical software (version 26.0, IBM). We used Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed data. Normally distributed data were tested using Student's t-tests and chi-square tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. A series of sensitivity and specificity values were obtained by plotting the ROC curve and calculating the Youden's index (Youden's index = sensitivity + specificity − 1), with the maximum value of the Youden's index being the optimal cut-off point for DL score.32 Patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups for the corresponding survival analysis. We used the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank tests to estimate RFS and OS probabilities over five years for individuals in two different risk groups.33 A p value < 0.05 is regarded as statistically significant. R packages “survminer” (version 0.4.9) and “survival” (version 3.5–5) were used.

Role of the funding source

There are no sources of funding in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Discussion

Accurate prediction of recurrence risk is beneficial for risk stratification and treatment selection of BCa patients. This multi-center study had two objectives. For the first objective, we developed and validated a 3D-CNN architecture based on 3-phase enhanced CT images for predicting the risk of recurrence of postoperative bladder cancer. For the second objective, we compared all predictive models to identify the optimal model. It was found that DL signature delivered excellent predictive power for recurrence (C index and AUC were 0.869 and 0.889 in validation sets, respectively), smaller prediction error (IBS value was 0.070 in validation sets), better calibration ability, and superior clinical benefit compared with others, demonstrating its stability and generalizability.

When compared with previous studies, our study was innovative in the following respects: (1) it included more comprehensive data from 874 patients with bladder cancer; (2) it pooled data from four different research centers, with data from three of them serving as external validation set, which enhanced the reliability of the experimental approach; (3) feature extraction in our model was not confined to hand-crafted features and DL features, but based on DL scores derived from the end-to-end output of a multi-phase CT CNN; (4) we built and validated DL signature, DLCS model, clinical model, and AJCC system, and explored the additional value of the optimal model in predicting survival; and (5) we studied the application of the DL signature to recurrence prediction for both MIBC and NMIBC patients, which carries potential applicability to a wide range of clinical settings. Not only that, to estimate the added value of DL signature for NMIBC patients and MIBC patients for RFS prediction, we also divided the patients into two subgroups based on tumor infiltrating muscle status in pairs, and investigated the ability of DL signature to stratify patients in both groups (Supplementary Figure S5).

In recent years, significant progress has been made in radiomic analysis, alongside advances in radiographic imaging. An earlier study found that radiomic features extracted from multiphase CT images could be used to predict the recurrence risk of BCa postoperatively.6 However, it was single-center, adopted a small sample size, and lacked an end-to-end architecture for BCa recurrence prediction. In the present study, we used a CNN for end-to-end training in a supervised manner, greatly simplifying the training process. Because the accuracy of the DL signature is largely influenced by image acquisition parameters, we apply a generalized scale approach to balance images from different centers.25 The number of patients who did not relapse was significantly greater than those who did; therefore, we used a loss function to increase the weight of the non-recurrence group and improve model performance.29 Our dataset was heterogeneous in terms of image acquisition parameters and clinical features, which reflects the actual clinical situation, suggesting that DL methods may be sufficiently robust and generalizable for real-world applications. The activation maps show that the high-risk group is predominantly red (Fig. 2D), indicating that salient regions may be identified effectively with the help of mapping information, which is of great significance in patient stratification. This is in line with related studies on tumor prognosis.34 It was inferred that the highlighted areas from activation maps may be associated with cancer progression.

Some studies35,36 have reported that clinical data such as gender, age at the time of first surgery, histological grade, number of tumors, tumor size, and operation choice may be predictors of BCa recurrence. Widely accepted clinical prognostic models currently in use are EORTC, CUETO, and Korea nomogram,14, 15, 16 but their application has only been studied in relation to NMIBC patients while overlooking MIBC patients, and their performance needs to be improved. In our study, we combined data from both MIBC and NMIBC patients to construct the DL signature. It outperformed traditional risk factors and commonly used grading systems, which were not sufficiently accurate in predicting the risk of BCa recurrence after surgery. The TNM staging we used was not associated with recurrence in this study, possibly related to the fact that these features were based on imaging reports. p-values in Table 2 show that there was no significantly statistical difference between the AUC values of DL signature and DLCS, but DL signature delivered better predictive performance, indicating that clinical factors did not assist DL signature significantly. In summary, DL signature constructed from multiphase CT images can achieve superior prognostic power compared with clinical factors and AJCC system.

Most guidelines recommend a risk-stratified surveillance program for BCa early on, to improve patient prognosis and prolong survival.37, 38, 39 If all patients were to receive postoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy, this would be regarded excessive for those who do not need it.40 However, if patients considered to have promising outcomes do not benefit from subsequent neoadjuvant therapy and do not receive follow-up treatment, the prognosis cannot be guaranteed. BCa carries a high risk of recurrence postoperatively. In this study, we compared RFS across risk groups (low and high risk), with p values less than 0.001. In the validation cohort, the cumulative recurrence rates at 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years were 0.4%, 2.7%, 7.4%, and 12.2%, respectively, in the low-risk group, whereas the cumulative recurrence rates at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years were 17.2%, 44.8%, and 59.7%, respectively, in the high-risk group. In the combined cohort, the cumulative recurrence rates in the low-risk group were 0.1%, 0.8%, 2.5% and 3.9% at 6 months, 1 year, 2 years and 3 years, respectively. The recurrence rates in the high-risk group were 23.2%, 43.3%, 62.2% and 74.2%, respectively. Low-risk patients can be followed up moderately. For high-risk patients, more intensive follow-up and more aggressive treatment are needed. Postoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy should not be abandoned for these high-risk patients. In addition, we explored the validity of DL signature in predicting OS for BCa. In this study, DL signature not only identified recurrence risk but also predicted OS. Patients with higher DL scores had poorer OS. Given these promising results, it is anticipated that DL signature developed in this study may find clinical application in the future as a prognostic tool for guiding treatment selection.

Our study had some limitations. First, it was retrospective and included sets from four centers, making it prone to selection bias and inherent bias. Larger datasets from prospective studies will consequently be necessary to confirm our findings. Second, all patients involved in this study came from China. Therefore, large-scale multicenter studies will be necessary to comprehensively evaluate the generalizability of the DL signature, with a view towards applying it to patients from other ethnicities. Third, manually outlining ROI regions requires expertise, and is heavily influenced by subjective experience. We plan to adopt a newly proposed unsupervised segmentation method based on fuzzy clustering in future studies.41 Finally, the absence of studies between DL signature and potential genetic features calls for further investigation of possible radiological–genomic correlations in BCa patients.

In conclusion, we designed a DL signature with accessible, non-invasive, and effective results for predicting postoperative recurrence in BCa. Our comparison with other models showed that this novel DL signature can yield superior prediction of BCa recurrence risk. Furthermore, the DL signature is effective in predicting RFS and OS in BCa patients. We recommend it be adopted in future efforts at predicting the postoperative recurrence of BCa. Future work will be necessary to confirm the applicability of the DL signature in clinical decision making.
