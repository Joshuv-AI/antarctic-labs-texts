CoralSCOP-LAT : Labeling and Analyzing Tool for Coral Reef Images with Dense Mask
Yuk-Kwan Wong, Ziqiang Zheng, Mingzhe Zhang, David Suggett, Sai-Kit Yeung, [1em] Hong Kong University of Science, Technology

Abstract
Images of coral reefs provide invaluable information, which is essentially critical for surveying and monitoring the coral reef ecosystems. Robust and precise identification of coral reef regions within surveying imagery is paramount for assessing coral coverage, spatial distribution, and other statistical analyses. However, existing coral reef analytical approaches mainly focus on sparse points sampled from the whole imagery, which are highly subject to the sampling density and cannot accurately express the coral ambulance. Meanwhile, the analysis is both time-consuming and labor-intensive, and it is also limited to coral biologists. In this work, we propose CoralSCOP-LAT , an automatic and semi-automatic coral reef labeling and analysis tool, specially designed to segment coral reef regions (dense pixel masks) in coral reef images, significantly promoting analysis proficiency and accuracy. CoralSCOP-LAT leverages the advanced coral reef foundation model to accurately delineate coral regions, supporting dense coral reef analysis and reducing the dependency on manual annotation. The proposed CoralSCOP-LAT surpasses the existing tools by a large margin from analysis efficiency , accuracy , and flexibility . We perform comprehensive evaluations from various perspectives and the comparison demonstrates that CoralSCOP-LAT not only accelerates the coral reef analysis but also improves accuracy in coral segmentation and analysis. Our CoralSCOP-LAT , as the first dense coral reef analysis tool in the market, facilitates repeated large-scale coral reef monitoring analysis, contributing to more informed conservation efforts and sustainable management of coral reef ecosystems. Our tool will be available at https://coralscop.hkustvgd.com/ .

Related Work

2.1
Coral Analysis

Traditionally, coral researchers manually analyze vast quantities of coral reef images
[
39
]
. The random point count method
[
23
]
has been employed, wherein points are randomly scattered across the whole image, and each point is identified based on the underlying semantics. However, the sparse point analysis is both time-consuming and labor-intensive. More importantly, it does not guarantee accuracy
[
17
]
due to its reliance on random sampling and it is inevitable to introduce the over-/under- estimation, overlooking significant features (the irregular boundaries) of coral reefs. Further works combined image classification with coral identification to promote analysis efficiency. These coral reef classification algorithms (
e.g.
, CoralNet
[
4
,
9
]
) mainly focus on image classification, classifying cropped image regions into pre-defined categories/semantics. However, the image regions could contain multiple coral reef identities/species and the image classification cannot describe the coral reef boundaries. Another line of coral reef analytical approaches is to perform sparse-to-dense conversion
[
34
,
35
]
, converting sparse point annotations to dense coral masks. CoralSeg
[
2
]
and PLAS
[
34
]
proposed to perform sparse-to-dense conversion based on Superpixels
[
6
]
to obtain dense coral masks as pseudo ground truth for optimizing dense segmentation algorithms. However, such sparse-to-dense conversion would fail under adverse conditions and still require human efforts to label the sparse points. Besides the sparse-to-dense conversion, the dense semantic coral reef segmentation algorithms
[
21
,
45
,
40
,
26
]
were developed based on the labeled coral reef datasets
[
5
,
16
,
47
]
with dense coral masks. However, these datasets with dense coral reef masks are limited in data scale or fixed to a few semantic categories. CoralSCOP
[
43
]
proposed the first coral reef segmentation foundation model, which demonstrated a strong generalization ability to unseen coral reef images and could automatically produce reasonable coral reef masks. Thus, in this work, we propose to develop a simple and efficient coral reef labeling and analysis tool based on this powerful coral reef segmentation model.

CoralSCOP-LAT

3.1
Workflow Overview

In this section, we provide a detailed overview of our
CoralSCOP-LAT
, which is depicted in
2
. Users could create separate projects to manage the coral reef images and analyze the coral reef images. We offer hierarchical functionalities for coral reef labeling and analysis. The main procedures of our tool include:

•

Project creation
. Users select a set of coral reef images to analyze.
CoralSCOP-LAT
stores the selected coral reef images into a single project, saving the annotation and analytical results.

•

Project preparation
. After project creation,
CoralSCOP-LAT
conducts a series of data preparation procedures (including generating image embeddings for interactive labeling and automatically yielding coral reef masks) to promote labeling and analysis efficiency. This data preparation is only required
once
for each project.

•

User-aligned analysis
. After the preparation stage, users could design their own label sets (with a strong flexibility to satisfy wide user requirements) to analyze the interests of regions. Meanwhile, the users could validate the automatically generated coral reef masks and assign the semantics from the defined label sets to validated coral reef masks. More importantly,
CoralSCOP-LAT
enables
interactive
labeling and analysis to refine the coral masks and obtain real-time coral statistics.

•

Visualization and result export
. After user-aligned analysis, users could visualize the coral reef statistics for any coral reef image or all the images belonging to the whole project to gain high-level insights. The users could export the statistics to various formats (
e.g.
,
JSON
and
EXCEL
). Finally, the generated coral reef masks with semantics could also be exported to optimize semantic coral reef segmentation algorithms.

3.2
Project Creation

To begin with, the users are required to create one project to better manage the coral reef images. Users could select coral reef images from different locations to save them in one project. At the same time, the users could add or delete any image to the whole project. Once the project is created, all the meta information is restored in the
project.json
file. Users could restore all the required information by loading an existing project to continue previous work.

For image formats,
CoralSCOP-LAT
supports a wide range of image formats (including JPEG, WEBP, JPG, PNG, TIFF, and so on), ensuring compatibility with diverse imagery sources. We also add the filtering mechanism to automatically filter out non-image files.
project.json
stores all the configuration metadata, including annotation progress, coral mask filtering configurations (for removing some low-quality automatically generated coral reef masks described in Sec.
3.3
), and other project settings.

After creating a new project,
CoralSCOP-LAT
conducts
project preparation
to generate all the required intermediate files for interactive analysis and automatically yield coral reef masks.

3.3
Project Preparation

After the project creation, we perform the project preparation, which mainly involves two procedures:
automatic coral mask generation
and
intermediate file preparation
.

For the former mask procedure,
CoralSCOP-LAT
automatically identifies and segments coral regions within each image, reducing the human efforts to draw coral regions manually. We adopted the coral reef foundation model CoralSCOP
[
43
]
as our backend to yield high-quality coral reef masks and ensure a strong generalization ability to unseen coral reef images. To reduce the computation time,
CoralSCOP-LAT
detects the presence of a Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) on the user’s system. If a compatible GPU is available, the software utilizes its parallel processing capabilities to significantly accelerate the whole segmentation process.

Figure 3:
Setting Page of
CoralSCOP-LAT
, which allows user to configure mask display by area, confidence, and overlapping degree

CoralSCOP could automatically generate multiple predictions for coral reef regions. To filter out those unnecessary and low-quality coral reef masks, we provide a filtering mechanism based on the user requirements. To enhance the simplicity and clarity,
CoralSCOP-LAT
provides a parameterized configuration to filter unsatisfactory masks based on three criteria, as shown in Figure
3
:

•

Minimum Area
: Masks below a specified area threshold are filtered out, ignoring those small areas.

•

Confidence Level
: Each mask generated by CoralSCOP is associated with a confidence score. By filtering out masks with confidence scores below a certain threshold, we can only maintain those high quality coral reef masks.

•

Degree of Overlapping
: Masks with high Intersection over Union (IoU) with other masks are filtered to avoid redundant masks.

The users could manually adjust these three criteria to obtain required coral reef masks.

The latter procedure generates the required intermediate image embedding (stored as
.npy
files) to enable interactive coral reef labeling and analysis. The users could also add masks interactively. It is worth noting that
CoralSCOP-LAT
supports adding masks for both corals and non-coral objects (
e.g.
, rocks, algae, sea urchins, sea cucumbers, and other marine organisms). It means that the users could also utilize our tool to analyze other non-coral benthic communities.

CoralSCOP-LAT

This folder structure ensures that all pertinent data is encapsulated within the project folder, allowing users to pause and resume their work at any point without the need to repeat initial processing steps. The preservation of project settings and progress information fosters a seamless and efficient analysis workflow.

3.4
User-aligned Analysis

In this procedure, we perform user-aligned analysis. First of all, the users could create their
label set
one by one or directly load the existing JSON file to load the label set to meet their requirements. The label set is stored in
project.json
. Please note that the users can add any categories to the label set at any time. The users could only remove one semantic category when there are no masks assigned to this semantic category.

Mask validation and semantic assignment
. Then the users could perform mask validation and semantic assignment. Considering the automatically generated coral reef masks would introduce
false positives
or inaccurate coral reef masks with unsatisfactory boundaries. The users could validate the automatically generated coral reef masks and remove those wrong masks. After the mask validation, the automatically generated mask lacks semantics, indicating that the generated masks are all coral reef masks without fine-grained details (
e.g.
, genus or species). The users could select multiple coral reef masks at one time and assign one semantic category from the label set to all the selected coral reef masks. In this way, we could promote the analysis efficiency significantly.

Adding new masks
. Meanwhile, there are some
false negatives
, some missed coral reefs within the coral image. We enable interactive labeling to support adding masks according to user requirements. The users could add or refine the coral reef masks and assign the semantic category to the newly generated coral reef masks simultaneously. Please also note, our
CoralSCOP-LAT
could support adding masks for both coral and non-coral regions.

Besides the label set, we also perform
status monitoring

[
31
]
of the coral reefs. We define three coral statuses:
Healthy
,
Bleached
, and
Dead
to support more domain-specific analysis. The users could also opt to hide this function and only focus on the semantic coral reef understanding. This customization provides a comprehensive understanding of coral conditions and helps in detailed ecological assessments.

Finally,
CoralSCOP-LAT
provides a wide range of functionalities to facilitate user-aligned coral reef labeling and analysis:

•

Simple user interaction
.
CoralSCOP-LAT
minimizes operations needed to generate dense pixel-level annotations. Users can assign labels to each mask with simple mouse clicks on the targeted masks because the raw image is displayed with overlayed segmentation masks on an interactive interface, shown in Figure
5
, where users can easily recognize the coral region. This feature enables users to accurately associate specific coral areas with their corresponding species and health states.

•

Real-time statistic report
. As users assign semantics to all the coral reef masks,
CoralSCOP-LAT
generates real-time statistical reports, which include graphic visualizations of biologically meaningful metrics such as coral coverage, species distribution, bleaching percentage, and so on. An example is shown in Figure
6
. These insights support ecological research and monitoring, providing valuable data for conservation efforts.

•

Mask editing and batch labeling
. To maintain accuracy,
CoralSCOP-LAT
enables users to refine segmentation masks by adding or removing them, ensuring precise labeling. Users can apply labels to multiple masks simultaneously, saving time on repetitive tasks.

Comparison of Existing Tools

In this section, we compare

5.1
Identification Accuracy

Figure 7:
Cumulative MAE comparison between
CoralSCOP-LAT
and CPCe

We evaluated the accuracy of dense coral reef identification using
CoralSCOP-LAT
and CPCe. Cumulative Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is adopted to reveal the total error users might encounter at the end of their analysis. We collected 493 coral reef images with ground truth segmentation with coral reef ground truths for comparison.

The MAE for evaluation is defined as:

\text{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|,

(1)

where
N
is the total number of pixels in image,
y_{i}
is the ground truth value, and
\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted value.

•

CoralSCOP-LAT
: To facilitate large-scale comparison, we directly utilized the results generated from CoralSCOP as the prediction results of
CoralSCOP-LAT
. It represents the
lower bound
of
CoralSCOP-LAT
, where the user does not refine any prediction results when using our
CoralSCOP-LAT
.

•

CPCe: Since the code is not available, we implement an evaluation program to reproduce the analyzed result from CPCe. We first create a 2D array with the same dimension as the image and classify all pixels to be non-coral as the initialization. We then randomly sample 5,000 points (which is the maximum number of points CPCe can generate without crashing the tool) in the 2D array and classify them as coral or non-coral based on the ground truth segmentation mask, serving as the sparse segmentation mask result.

We then calculate the cumulative MAE, presented in Figure
7
, which demonstrates a significant improvement of
CoralSCOP-LAT
, where it reduces cumulative error by 6.4 times. The superior performance of
CoralSCOP-LAT
is attributed to its ability to provide dense coral segmentation. In contrast, CPCe’s sparse point based analytical results, inherently limit its accuracy, where the under-/over- estimation is inevitable. Consequently,
CoralSCOP-LAT
achieves remarkably better coral reef identification results compared with CPCe.

5.2
Analysis Time Cost

Figure 8:
Time time cost comparison between CPCe, CVAT and
CoralSCOP-LAT
.

CPCe requires users to manually classify each generated point, which can be labor-intensive and time-consuming. Although CPCe offers the option to outline the contour of a coral region and automatically identify the sparse points within the contour, this process still demands a significant time cost to accurately trace the coral reef boundaries. In contrast,
CoralSCOP-LAT
enhances labeling and analysis efficiency with an interactive user interface that allows users to simply click on the target masks and identify the semantic categories. This action automatically updates all pixels within the mask to the same label, drastically reducing human efforts.

To empirically assess these differences, we invited 15 volunteers to annotate 50 images using the provided tools, where the number of candidates are distributed evenly to use CPCe, CVAT, and
CoralSCOP-LAT
. The ground truth coral region and corresponding genus are provided as the reference to ensure all candidates have the same knowledge level to perform the analysis. Each image had an average resolution of
2356\times 1508
pixels and was presented in the same order. We measure the time required for each user to complete the annotation process of each image, and measure the average time consumed. As illustrated in Figure
8
, the results indicate that
CoralSCOP-LAT
significantly improves the time cost of the analysis process by 5.1 times. Users were able to complete the task more quickly with
CoralSCOP-LAT
, demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing the time required for high-resolution image annotation.

5.3
Annotation Flexibility

Annotation flexibility is crucial for the annotation tools to provide user-aligned analysis, as it reflects the ability to align with user expectations.
CoralSCOP-LAT
and CPCe provide varying levels of user control over annotations.

•

CPCe limits user control in point annotations. Once random points are generated, users cannot modify their locations, even if they do not adequately cover the coral region. Users can only increase the number of points or regenerate all points, without the ability to specify their locations, restricting CPCe’s ability to meet user expectations.

•

CoralSCOP-LAT
offers users full control over mask annotations. Users can freely add or remove masks using the
ADD MASK

Discussions and Conclusion

Discussions
. Currently, our
CoralSCOP-LAT
adopts the CoralSCOP model as the backend to automatically generate coral reef masks to speed up the coral reef labeling and analysis. However, the generated coral reef masks are without semantics to guarantee the generalization ability to unseen coral reef images and the flexibility of user-defined label set to satisfy a large range of domain requirements. After the interactive labeling and analysis, the users could export the generated masks with semantics for training their own semantic coral reef segmentation algorithms. Considering the strong coral reef segmentation ability of CoralSCOP, it is more efficient to further fine-tune the foundation model to the user-defined label set in the few-shot setting. Once the fine-tuning is finished, the trained counterpart will automatically produce coral reef masks with user-defined semantics. We aim to combine the further training procedure into our tool and we leave these as our future work.

Furthermore, underwater images are notably known for their color distortion and degraded visibility. The degraded image quality will lead to analysis efficiency. The underwater image enhancement algorithm could be combined to promote the visibility of the coral reef images. The users could also adjust the color balance, contrast, and light of the selected image regions.

Conclusion
. We proposed the first dense segmentation based coral reef labeling and analysis tool for coral reef images in the market.
CoralSCOP-LAT
could automatically yield coral reef masks for reducing human efforts to analyze the whole coral reef images. We have designed hierarchical and versatile functions to enable the users to do the mask validation, semantic assignment, adding new masks, coral status monitoring, statistic visualization and result export.
CoralSCOP-LAT
is the first tool supporting automatic and semi-automatic dense coral reef annotation and analysis.
