Mitochondrial-associated PCD signature defines immune landscape, therapeutic sensitivity, and prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xiao-qin Luo, Yi-min Cao, Yang Peng, Jing Dong, Zhi-yong Chen, Lu-shan Xiao, Wei-zhen Wang, Mei-ling Ai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial-associated programmed cell death signatures (MPCDS) have emerged as critical indicators of cancer progression and treatment response. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), mitochondrial dysfunction and dysregulated PCD pathways are prevalent, yet the application of MPCDS in HCC remains underexplored. This study aimed to identify and characterize the HCC MPCDS, evaluating its prognostic value. We integrated data from TCGA-LIHC and HCCDB18 cohorts, identifying 351 HCC MPCDS genes through differential expression analysis and intersection with known MPCDS genes. We constructed and validated a prognostic model based on 56 out of the 351 genes, regarding their remarkable influence in HCC survival (Cox p-value < 0.0001) using Cox regression and machine learning algorithms. Our analysis revealed distinct clusters with significant differences in survival outcomes, immune landscapes, somatic mutation, and drug sensitivity. The machine learning model demonstrated robust predictive accuracy for HCC prognosis (1-, 3-, 5-year AUC = 0.976, 0.984, 0.976). Clinical HCC samples were collected for experimental validation of the top-ranked genes, KIF2C and CDK4, using qRT-PCR. The results confirmed that the genes were significantly upregulated in tumor lesions. These findings underscore the importance of HCC MPCDS as a prognostic biomarker and highlight its potential for guiding personalized treatment strategies in HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04153-z.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of liver cancer and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Despite advances in diagnostic techniques and therapeutic strategies, the prognosis for HCC patients remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 30% [2, 3]. This grim outlook is largely attributed to the high incidence of recurrence and metastasis, as well as the limited effectiveness of current treatments. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets that can improve the prognosis and treatment outcomes for HCC patients [4].

Mitochondria are essential organelles involved in various cellular processes, including energy metabolism, apoptosis, and cellular signaling [5]. In recent years, the role of mitochondria in cancer development and progression has garnered significant attention. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various cancers, including HCC [6]. Programmed cell death (PCD) is a critical process that maintains tissue homeostasis and prevents the development of cancer [7]. Dysregulation of PCD pathways has been observed in many types of cancer, including HCC [8]. The interplay between mitochondrial function and PCD pathways is complex and has been shown to influence cancer cell survival and response to therapy [9].

Given the carcinogenetic potential of mitochondrial imbalance, previous studies have identified mitochondrial-associated PCD signatures (MPCDS) in lung adenocarcinoma [9]. The signature has shown potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target. However, the application of MPCDS in HCC has not been extensively explored. Given the critical role of mitochondria and PCD in cancer biology, it is plausible that MPCDS could serve as valuable biomarkers for HCC prognosis and therapeutic response.

In this study, we aimed to identify and characterize the mitochondrial-associated PCD signature in HCC (HCC MPCDS) and evaluate its prognostic value. By comprehensive bioinformatics analyses to construct and validate an MPCDS-based prognostic model, we sought to explore the biological pathways, immune landscapes, and drug sensitivity associated with HCC MPCDS, thereby providing novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying HCC MPCDS and identifying potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for this deadly disease.

Methods

Data sources and processing

The study analyzed two hepatocellular carcinoma datasets recording the sequencing data of tumoral and normal liver tissues. Data from TCGA-LIHC (The Cancer Genome Atlas Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma) cohort was downloaded from the XENA database under “gene expression RNAseq” (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/?cohort=TCGA%20Liver%20Cancer%20(LIHC)&removeHub=https%3 A%2 F%2Fxena.treehouse.gi.ucsc.edu%3A443). The HCCDB18 cohort was obtained from the HCCDB database (http://lifeome.net:809/#/download). R package Limma was utilized to screen the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the two datasets with the criteria of |Log2FC|>1(log2 fold change), Qvalue < 0.05(Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p-value), intersecting to generate 2216 common DEGs.

Mitochondria-related genes and PCD(MPCD)-related genes (2558 in total) were previously reported by Pengpeng Zhang’s group [9]. According to the report, DEGs of lung adenocarcinoma from multiple cohorts were combined to identify the mitochondrial-related (MitoCarta 3.0) and programmed cell death (PCD) related genes through correlation analysis. Subsequently, Cox regression analysis and various machine learning algorithms were used to construct and optimize the model, which ultimately generated Mitochondrial-Associated Programmed Cell Death Signatures (MPCDS) with prognostic value, and their performances were validated in multiple independent cohorts. In the current study, genes of mitochondrial-associated PCD in HCC were generated by intersecting our DEGs with previously reported MPCDS, which returned 351 genes in total.

The datasets were then merged using the R package “sva” to remove batch effects (Supplementary Fig. 1). The merged cohort contained 574 samples and expression data of 17,661 genes, but only 566 samples were eligible for survival analyses. Among 351 HCC MPCDS, 330 genes were cooperated in the expression data, and 2 of them were ineligible for univariate analyses.

HCC MPCDS phenotyping

Fifty-six genes were selected from a total of 328 candidates through univariate regression analyses with Cox p-value < 0.0001. R package ConsensusClusterPlus was used to cluster samples of the merged cohort according to their expression of candidate genes. Gene expression patterns of three clusters were compared and plotted by ggplot2 and pheatmap. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the clusters using the prcomp function of R, and the results were plotted by ggplot2. The correlations of clusters with patients’ overall survival were analyzed using R packages survival and survminer.

Pathway analyses

Hallmark pathway information was downloaded from the Msigdb database. Pathway analyses were performed using the R package GSVA. GSVA (Gene Set Variation Analysis) calculated the enrichment scores of various pathways for the clusters, and pathways presenting significant differences were plotted by R package pheatmap.

ssGSEA(single sample gene set enrichment analysis) is one of the functions of GSVA package and was utilized to assess immunocyte infiltration and cell stemness. ssGSEA assesses genes of particular pathways according to their expression in a single sample, thereby calculating the enrichment scores of these pathways by their gene components.

Somatic mutation, immunity analyses, and drug sensitivity prediction

Samples from TCGA-LIHC dataset were clustered according to their expression of candidate genes and subjected to mutational analyses utilizing R package maftools. Somatic variants in TCGA-LIHC cohort were provided in the Mutation Annotation Format (MAF). The package maftools was also used to analyze the somatic variants between two groups categorized by the machine learning model.

Immunity analyses were conducted using R package IOBR. The package provides 8 approaches to evaluate immune infiltration, namely MCPcounter, EPIC, xCell, CIBERSORT, IPS, ESTIMATE, SVR, and TIMER, and each returns the prediction of immunocyte distribution in given groups.

Drug sensitivity information was downloaded from GDSC (Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer, https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). The website provides the correlation between gene expression and drug sensitivity in various tissues annotated by IC50(half maximal inhibitory concentration) and AUC data. R package oncoPredict was utilized to predict the IC50 value of every case in low- and high-risk groups against anti-tumor drugs. IC50 was used to quantify the therapy resistance of the sample.

Construction of machine learning model

A total of 328 candidate genes were further screened by univariate regression analyses with the HR (hazardous ratio) and Cox p-value in TCGA-LIHA, HCCDB18, and the merged cohort. Genes with Cox p-value < 0.001, HR > 1 were classified as “badgene” and Cox p-value < 0.001, HR < 1 “goodgene”. The analyses harvested 77 significant genes in total.

We assigned TCGA-LIHC cohort training set and the other two validation sets. The training cohort was subjected to 10 classical algorithms: random forest (RSF), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), gradient boosting machine (GBM), survival support vector machine (Survival-SVM), supervised principal components (SuperPC), ridge regression, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), CoxBoost, Stepwise Cox, and elastic network (Enet). Given the dimensionality reduction and variable screening function of RSF, LASSO, CoxBoost, and Stepwise Cox, we combined them with other algorithms to generate new machine-learning approaches. Each model was tested in the validation cohorts, and the Cindex (Concordance Index) of all cohorts was plotted in a heatmap using ComplexHeatmap package. We further assessed the survival predicting efficiency of the best performance model using survival analyses and ROC curve with the help of R packages pROC, survival, and timeROC. Using the same methods, the performance in validation cohorts was also evaluated.

SHAP (shapley additive explanations) and DepMap analyses

SHAP provides global or local interpretations of the model by calculating SHAP values and presenting them through visualization. It replies to how features affect prediction and enhance model interpretability. In the study, SHAP was conducted using R package fastshap, and results were visualized by ggplot2, including SHAP summary and dependence plot. The top 10 genes recognized by SHAP were subjected to DepMap (The Cancer Dependency Map, https://depmap.org/portal/home/) analysis, which has been widely employed to investigate the influence of certain genes on cell growth and biological functions. The dataset documents the gene necessity, i.e., Gene Effect, assessed by large-scale loss-of-function screening using CRISPR. Gene Effect is defined as the fold change of cell viability after gene knockdown or knockout in comparison with control groups. A negative value indicates that the given gene is necessary or important, as its expression interference impaired cell proliferation. In this circumstance, the absolute value represents the importance of the gene in cell survival and functionality; in other words, the dependence of the cell on the gene. When the value is approaching zero, the gene is less crucial to cell viability. The gene effect is positive in a few cases, which suggests that the gene impedes cell growth and proliferation.

Spatial transcriptome analyses

Two HCC spatial transcriptome data, namely VISDS000507 and VISDS000519, were obtained from CROST (A Comprehensive Repository of Spatial Transcriptomics, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/crost/home). With the help of an R package seurat, the spatial expression of key MPCD genes and main immune components was mapped on the tissue image.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analyses

Tumor and non-tumor samples were collected and used under the approval of the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital (Approval number: NFEC-2012-K3), and all aspects were in accordance with criteria established by the Declaration of Helsinki. The informed consent was obtained from all patients. The total RNA of biopsies was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription was performed by reverse transcription kit (Thermo#K1622). The quantitative PCR assay was performed using a 2×PCR Master Mix (Roche), and the following amplification procedure was set as 95℃ for 10 min, 95℃ for 15 min, and 60℃ for 1 min for 45 cycles. RT-qPCR was performed on the ABI Q6 Flex Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). mRNA expression values were calculated using 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to U6.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed on R Studio (R version 4.2.2) with the corresponding packages. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using survival. Student’s t-tests were utilized to compare the variant distribution between the two groups, and the results were analyzed by ggplot2. Heatmap plotting was performed utilizing the pheatmap package.

Results

Identification and clustering of mitochondrial-associated programmed cell death signatures in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC MPCDS)

To screen influential genes involved in Mitochondrial-Associated PCD(MPCD), we selected a total of 2216 common differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the TCGA-LIHC and HCCDB18 cohorts with the criteria of |Log2FC|>1 and Qvalue < 0.05 (Supplementary Table 1). By intersecting these DEGs with the mitochondrial-associated PCD signature (MPCDS) genes previously reported by Pengpeng Zhang’s group [9], we obtained 351 HCC MPCDS genes (Fig. 1A). Among these, 330 genes were successfully matched in the expression data of the cohorts, and 328 genes were eligible for further univariate regression analyses. These analyses identified 56 genes with a Cox p-value of less than 0.0001, and only three of them were protective genes for HCC survival, as predicted by the hazard ratio (Fig. 1B). All these influential HCC MPCDS genes were then used to cluster the merged cohort samples, resulting in three distinct clusters (Fig. 1C). Survival analysis revealed significant differences in overall survival among these clusters (Fig. 1D). In detail, cluster C showed longer overall survival, while cluster B showed shorter survival than cluster (A) The expression profiles of the candidate genes across the clusters were compared, which exhibited significant distinctions (Fig. 1E F). Typically, 49 genes were comparatively unregulated in cluster B but downregulated in cluster C, including AURKA, BIRC5, CDK4, CDKN2A, CDKN3, E2F1, EZH2, FANCD2, G6PD, HMGB2, UBE2C, and so forth. In addition, ACADL, ABCA9, and OGDHL expression increased in cluster C but decreased in cluster B These gene expression patterns indicated that HCC MPCD was profoundly activated in cluster B, which might play certain roles in shortening the survival time of these HCC patients.

Immune landscapes and stemness of HCC MPCDS clusters reflected their malignancy

Pathway analyses using GSVA revealed significant differences in hallmark pathways among the clusters (Fig. 2). In comparison with cluster A, cluster B showed enhanced enrichment for mitosis-related processes and glucose metabolism, including DNA repair, mTORC1 signaling, unfolded protein response, E2F targets, and G2M checkpoint. Unlike cluster B, cluster C was enriched for metabolism- and oxidative stress-related pathways, including bile acid metabolism, xenobiotic metabolism, peroxisome, fatty acid metabolism, adipogenesis, and reactive oxygen species pathway. In the investigation into the genetic force driving the differentiation in their inclination to different biological processes, we performed mutational analyses of TCGA-LIHC cohort grouped by the clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2). Apart from cluster A, clusters B and C harbored the most frequent mutation at TP53 and CTNNB1, respectively, followed by TIN. MUC4, MUC16, and ADGRV1 were common mutated hotspots in both clusters, while TP53 was less mutated in cluster C. The analyses reveal that cluster A was the mixture of clusters B and C, which was also manifested by the PCA plot (Fig. 3A).

Next, we analyzed the HCC MPCD clusters in terms of immune components and stemness (Fig. 3B-D). As compared by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA), cluster C exhibited significantly higher infiltration of activated B cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, neutrophils, effector memory CD8 T cells, and type 1 T helper cells (Fig. 3B), but fewer type 2 T helper cells. In contrast, cluster B demonstrated a higher proportion of activated CD4 T and type 2 T helper cells. Multiple approaches revealed that three HCC MPCD clusters harbored varied stemness and immune microenvironments (Fig. 3C&D). Specifically, 22 out of 26 stemness-related signatures identified cluster B with markedly higher stemness (Fig. 3C). The diversity of the HCC MPCD clusters in pathway activation, immune infiltration, and versatility might be associated with their divergence in malignant phenotype and fatal events.

Machine learning model constructed on MPCD signature scores survival prediction for HCC patients

In order to expand and optimize the predictive performance of the MPCD signature in HCC patients, we select key HCC MPCD genes for model construction. Univariate regression analyses were used to screen 328 eligible genes with the criteria of Cox p-value < 0.001 and HR ≠ 1, and identified bundles of genes (Fig. 4). By intersecting these genes, we harvested 67 “badgenes” (HR > 1) and 10 “goodgenes” (HR < 1) and subjected them to machine learning model construction using TCGA-LIHC cohort (Fig. 5A). Among the original and combined methods, random forest had the best performance as evaluated by C-index (Fig. 5A) and was selected for further validation. Variables selected by the RSF model were ranked by their importance (Fig. 5B) and used for constructing the scoring model. The training cohort was divided into two groups according to the scores calculated by the model, and Kaplan-Meier analyses confirmed that the scoring model successively distinguished the survival fraction and fatal risk (Fig. 5C; p-value < 0.0001). ROC curves (Fig. 5D) presented that the model maintained stable efficiency (Fig. 5E) in survival prediction from 1-(AUC = 0.976), 3-(AUC = 0.984) to 5-year (AUC = 0.976). The model was further validated in the HCCDB18 (Fig. 6A) and merged (Fig. 6B) cohorts, demonstrating consistent performance across different datasets.

SHAP analysis provided insights into each gene’s contribution to the model’s prediction and accordingly ranked the genes (Fig. 7A). Taking TXNRD1, the most influential gene of the model, as an example, the SHAP value surged sharply as its eigenvalue increased from 0 to nearly 3(Fig. 7B). DepMap analysis was performed on the top ten genes to assess their importance in cell viability and function (Fig. 7C). According to the ridge plot, SFN, MSH2, KIF2C, and CDK4 were essential genes for cell fate and functioning. Further, we collected several pairs of HCC biopsies and conducted qRT-PCR to assess the practical expression of HCC MPCD genes. KIF2C and CDK4 were both “badgenes” and presented higher expression in tumor and adjacent non-tumor liver tissues (Fig. 7D&E), which validated their oncogenic role in HCC pathology. The results indicated that the selected genes significantly influenced the model’s predictive power and were crucial for invasive behaviors as well as HCC prognosis.

HCC MPCD scoring model immune landscape and spatial transcriptome analysis

Given that HCC MPCD clusters were associated with various pathway activations (Fig. 2), somatic mutations (Supplementary Fig. 2), and immune landscapes (Fig. 3), we continued to investigate their association with the HCC MPCD scoring model. The immune landscapes of the high- and low-risk groups classified by the RSF model were compared, revealing significantly varied patterns of immune composition (Fig. 8A) and chemokine abundance (Fig. 8B). Unlike HCC MPCD clustering, where cluster A was the intermediate phenotype of two other clusters, risk groups exhibited polarized divergence in somatic mutation (Supplementary Fig. 3A&B). Compared with the low-risk group, the high-risk group harbored more frequent mutations at TP53, INTS1, TGFBRAP1, EVC2, DNAH8, ADGRL2, DVL1, KCNQ2, KIF20B, and NR3C2(Supplementary Fig. 3C). In light of this, we analyzed the correlation of risk groups and drug sensitivity. Using the GDSC dataset, we predicted IC50 values of anti-tumor drugs in the cases and compared the groups (Fig. 9). IC50 values of uprosertib, selumetinib, SCH772984, SB505124, SB216763, Nutlin-3a, NU7441, LJI308, JAK1_8709_1718, doramapimod, AZD8186, AZD6482, AZD2014, and AZD1208 were higher in the high-risk group, while those of BI.2536, sepantronium bromide, ML323, MK 1775, and GCD0810 were higher in the low-risk group. The expressions of key HCC MPCD genes and immune components were mapped on tissue images, providing a spatial context for the identified gene signatures (Fig. 10A-B) and colocalization of these genes and certain immunocytes, further supporting their role in HCC progression and immune response.

Discussion

Our comprehensive analysis of the MPCDS(Mitochondrial-Associated Programmed Cell Death Signatures) in HCC has revealed significant insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying this aggressive malignancy. The identification of a distinct set of genes associated with mitochondrial function and PCD pathways not only highlights their prognostic value but also underscores their potential as therapeutic targets. This study builds upon recent advancements in understanding the complex interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and cancer progression, particularly in the context of HCC.

The clustering of HCC MPCDS genes into distinct subgroups with varying prognostic outcomes aligns with emerging evidence that mitochondrial alterations can drive tumorigenesis and influence patient survival. Cluster B, with higher expression of AURKA, BIRC5, CDK4, CDKN2A, CDKN3, E2F1, EZH2, FANCD2, G6PD, HMGB2, and UBE2C, exhibited the shortest survival time, suggesting a strong link between aggressive tumor behavior and unleashed mitotic processes. For instance, AURKA is essential for mitotic entry and centrosome maturation [10]; BIRC5 encodes survivin, which is involved in cell division and inhibits apoptosis [11]; CDK4 is a key regulator of the cell cycle, promoting cell proliferation [12]; E2F1 is a transcription factor that drives cell cycle progression [13]. The relatively higher expression of these genes in cluster B suggests that cells in this cluster may have a higher proliferative capacity and better survival ability compared to those in cluster C. Notably, BIRC2 has recently been identified as a key player in immune evasion in HCC, with its high expression correlating with poor survival and resistance to immunotherapy [14, 15]. This finding underscores the potential of targeting mitochondrial-associated genes to disrupt tumor-promoting pathways and enhance therapeutic efficacy.

The immune landscapes of the HCC MPCDS clusters further elucidate the complex relationship between mitochondrial function, immune response, and tumor progression. Cluster C, characterized by higher infiltration of activated immune cells, suggests a more favorable immune environment conducive to antitumor activity. To specify, effector memory CD8 T cells and Th1 cells, responsible for killing of tumor cells directly or through cytokine secretion, indicate a strong cytotoxic anti-tumor immunity [16], not mentioning the tumor eradication of activated B cells [17], effector memory CD4 T cells [18], and neutrophils [19]. On the contrary, immunocytes contributing to immune escape and tolerance were enriched in cluster B, especially type 2 T helper (Th2) cells [20]. Though infiltrated by activated CD4 T cells that can contribute to antitumor immunity, their function can be modulated by the presence of Th2 cells [16]. In this cluster, the Th2 dominance might lead to a shift towards an immunosuppressive environment, potentially reducing the effectiveness of the antitumor response This observation is consistent with studies demonstrating that an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, often associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, can contribute to poor prognosis in HCC [21–23]. The differential expression of immune checkpoints and the variation in immune cell infiltration highlight the potential for personalized immunotherapy strategies based on the MPCDS profile.

The HCC MPCDS clusters shared some similarities with the phenotypes defined by mutational frequencies. Damaged p53 functionality has been established with more aggressive features [24], including increased genome instability, higher grade of pathological stage, compromised expression of mature hepatocyte markers, shorter overall survival, and increased risk of tumor relapse. In accordance, HCC MPCDS clusters B, featuring the highest frequency of TP53 mutation, were associated with the worst survival (Fig. 1D). According to previous reports, TP53 mutation was related to an immunosuppressive microenvironment [25], as demonstrated by increased myeloid-derived cells, B cells, regulatory T cells, and T follicular helper cells, rather than cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8 + T cells) [26]. In our study, cluster B demonstrated increased CD4 + T, memory B, and type 2 T cells (Fig. 3B); otherwise, no difference was found across three clusters in the infiltration of activated CD8 + T cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells. It was worth noting that cluster B featured high scores of stemness in comparison with the other two, in line with the documentation that TP53 inactivation was strongly associated with HCC stemness [27]. As the TP53 mutation was related to immune evasion, the CTNNB1 mutation was linked to immune exclusion [28], characterized by low infiltration of cytotoxic and antigen-presenting immune cells; however, CTNNB1-activated HCC manifested well-differentiated tumors with low proliferation and thus had longer survival than TP53-mutated cases [24]. Cluster C in our study harbored a higher mutation rate of CTNNB1 and longer survival. Besides, drastically decreased abundance of activated CD4 + T cells was observed in cluster C (Fig. 3B), suggesting the link between immune exhaustion [29] and low HCC MPCDS activity. More importantly, metabolism reprogramming was indicated by GSVA results in clusters C. The cluster was inclined to bile acid metabolism (Fig. 2), consistent with the accepted notion that CTNNB1 activation enhances fatty acid-derived metabolisms and thereby involves hepatic mitochondrial homeostasis [28]. The findings strengthen the tie between the breakdown of mitochondrial homeostasis and HCC MPCDS, corroboratively highlighting the meaning of HCC MPCDS.

The machine learning model constructed in this study, which demonstrated high predictive accuracy for HCC prognosis, represents a significant advancement in the field. Integrating multiple machine learning algorithms allowed for identifying key genes that significantly influence patient outcomes. This approach not only enhances our ability to predict prognosis but also provides a framework for identifying potential therapeutic targets. The identification of genes such as TXNRD1 [30] and SFN [31] as top contributors to the model’s predictive power further supports their biological significance in HCC.

The spatial transcriptome analysis conducted in this study offers a novel perspective on the spatial organization of key HCC MPCD genes and immune components in HCC tissues. The colocalization of these genes and immunocytes provides a spatial context for their roles in tumor progression and immune response. This spatial information is crucial for understanding the microenvironmental interactions that drive HCC development and progression.

In the context of recent clinical advancements in HCC treatment, our findings hold significant implications for the development of targeted therapies. Identifying HCC MPCDS genes with high predictive value for drug sensitivity suggests the potential for personalized treatment strategies. For instance, the differential sensitivity to drugs such as uprosertib and selumetinib [32] between high- and low-risk groups highlights the potential for tailored therapeutic interventions based on the MPCDS profile.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the study is inherently retrospective, relying on public databases (TCGA-LIHC and HCCDB18), which may introduce selection and ascertainment biases. Second, although internal cross-validation was performed, a fully independent external cohort is lacking, potentially restricting the generalizability. Third, the clinical covariates (e.g., etiology, treatment history) were not harmonized across datasets, limiting multivariable adjustment. Finally, functional validation was restricted to qRT-PCR of two genes in a small biopsy set; thus, mechanistic and interventional studies are warranted to confirm causality before clinical translation.

Moreover, the integration of our findings with emerging therapeutic approaches, such as immunotherapy and targeted therapy, could further enhance treatment outcomes. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies in improving survival rates in HCC patients. The identification of HCC MPCDS genes as potential biomarkers could facilitate the selection of patients most likely to benefit from these treatments, thereby optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving patient outcomes. Although the current model was built without clinical covariates to ensure data completeness, a prospective, multi-center cohort with fully annotated clinicopathological parameters is expected to integrate these variables into a mixed-effects Cox framework.

Conclusion

Our study comprehensively analyzed the mitochondrial-associated PCD signature in HCC, revealing its prognostic value and potential as a therapeutic target. The identification of key genes and pathways involved in HCC progression, along with their association with immune landscapes and drug sensitivity, underscores the importance of targeting mitochondrial dysfunction in cancer treatment. Certain key genes were confirmed by experimental analysis that they were upregulated in HCC. Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger cohorts and exploring the therapeutic potential of targeting HCC MPCDS genes. The integration of multi-omics data and advanced computational approaches will further enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying HCC and pave the way for the development of more effective treatments.
