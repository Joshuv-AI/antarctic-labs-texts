Curbing the tide of “paper mills”: A risk-based integrated governance for public health databases
Authors: Jing Wang, Yibo Wu
Published: 2026

Main text

Large-scale public health databases like the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), are increasingly being misused. Researchers generate large volumes of low-quality papers by mechanically combining variables and conducting shallow correlation analyses while using AI tools to bypass plagiarism checks.1 A recent methodology and cross-sectional study published in The BMJ revealed that nearly 10% of original cancer research papers, exceeding 260,000 articles, exhibited textual signatures consistent with retracted paper-mill outputs.2 Crucially, this study found that the prevalence of such “flagged” papers has grown exponentially in high-impact journals (top 10% by impact factor), highlighting that traditional peer review is struggling to curb the tide of industrialized fabrication.2 The Retraction Watch database has documented tens of thousands of retractions linked to systematic fraud. Recent analyses further reveal that paper mills are increasingly exploiting publicly accessible datasets, such as NHANES and FAERS, to generate large volumes of low-utility, single-association studies. Meanwhile, the United2Act initiative, a multi-stakeholder collaboration supported by COPE and STM, has highlighted the unintended consequences of open-data policies. While these policies have significantly advanced equity and transparency, they have also created opportunities for industrialized research misconduct. Consequently, the initiative calls for robust, coordinated governance involving database curators, publishers, and research integrity organizations.

In response, journals such as Frontiers and PLOS now often reject submissions relying on these databases unless the authors provide external experimental validation or institutional supplementary data. Simultaneously, databases such as jPOST are addressing these issues by developing data quality assessment indices and establishing specialized data journals to promote responsible use. The UK Biobank also issued a statement suspending data access until download restrictions are in place. A 2024 study offers a comprehensive overview of ongoing efforts at both the publisher and organizational levels to combat paper mills. These efforts include layered detection tools, such as network analysis and image forensics, as well as shared toolkits developed by independent organizations and practical checklists designed for editors and readers.3

Despite these efforts, Dr. Richardson shows that the entities enabling scientific fraud at scale remain large and resilient and continue to grow rapidly. They achieve this growth primarily through tactics such as journal hopping and broker networks.4 Therefore, to combat paper mills, it is essential to establish a collaborative governance framework involving journals, databases, and other stakeholders. Dr. Sanderson described a major multi-stakeholder initiative involving funders, publishers, and research institutions, aimed at systematically addressing the commercial practices behind the production of fraudulent academic papers. He emphasized that countermeasures must shift from isolated efforts by individual journals to a coordinated, industry-wide response.5

Building on these existing studies, we believe a risk-based automated screening and information-sharing system should be established, leveraging the technological feasibility demonstrated by recent AI models. The BMJ study mentioned above successfully utilized a fine-tuned BERT (bidirectional encoder representations from transformers) model to classify paper mill products with 0.91 precision using only titles and abstracts.2 Although this example demonstrates feasibility, any automated risk-scoring system is likely to be reverse-engineered quickly. Paper mills adapt rapidly by paraphrasing content, distributing queries across multiple accounts, or mimicking the patterns of legitimate submissions. Recent evaluations indicate that even the most advanced AI detectors remain vulnerable to hybrid adversarial edits. This vulnerability underscores the need for continuous retraining and sustained hybrid human-AI oversight. To address these challenges, we propose a more robust ensemble machine-learning framework (Figure 1). The approach integrates natural language processing of manuscript content with behavioral anomaly detection at the database level.

A risk-based integrated governance framework for paper mills

The proposed system features a deliberately multi-layered and adaptive architecture. Potential adversarial strategies, such as reverse-engineering risk-score thresholds, employing sophisticated paraphrasing combined with behavioral mimicry, or leveraging proxy and VPN networks for distributed querying, are countered through several integrated mechanisms. First, the system employs an opaque ensemble architecture that combines graph neural networks for query-pattern analysis with isolation forests for outlier detection. This approach is significantly more difficult to circumvent than conventional single-modality classifiers. Second, continuous retraining pipelines rapidly incorporate encrypted and de-identified misuse feedback from partner journals, often within days of confirmation. Third, quarterly closed-door consortium meetings enable participating institutions to share newly observed high-risk behavioral patterns. Fourth, the model undergoes bimonthly dynamic recalibration of weights and thresholds, informed by rigorous adversarial stress-testing simulations. These built-in adaptive mechanisms allow the framework to stay ahead in the evolving arms race, rather than merely reacting to it.

At the database level, the first step is to establish a refined mechanism for detecting anomalous access and usage patterns. The system continuously monitors a range of multi-dimensional behavioral indicators. These include unusually high volumes of variable extraction from the same IP address or account within a short time frame, multiple author accounts accessing data from the same IP range, access frequency, the strength of variable correlations, temporal clustering of queries, cross-account behavioral fingerprinting, consistency with an author’s ORCID-linked publication history, and alignment with the declared research objectives. Based on these indicators, an automated risk-scoring engine applies ensemble methods to generate a composite risk score ranging from 0 to 1, along with categorical risk flags (low, medium, or high). The engine integrates graph neural networks to analyze query patterns and isolation forests to detect outliers. Risk thresholds are dynamically calibrated through continuous monitoring of false positives. This approach helps minimize the misclassification of legitimate high-volume users, such as those conducting large cohort studies, while maintaining equity in open science.

A lightweight API can then be developed to enable journal submission systems to query these risk signals during manuscript submission. After authenticating via ORCID iD or institutional login, authors receive a unique cryptographically signed data usage number managed as a time-limited and revocable token, to prevent circumvention through proxy accounts or newly created identities. When authors enter the data usage number during submission, the journal system calls the API, which then returns only a de-identified risk rating and type code, without disclosing any user-identifiable information. This enables a privacy-compliant risk alert for the journal.

Journals integrate these risk signals directly into their manuscript-handling workflows. High-risk submissions automatically trigger an enhanced review process. This may require authors to upload complete analysis code and data, provide additional ethical approval documentation, or have statisticians prioritize review to evaluate model validity. To protect due process and open-science principles, authors whose submissions are flagged receive a transparent 14-day window to appeal the decision. During this period, they may submit supplementary evidence (e.g., raw code, full variable lists, analytical intent justification). Appeals are then reviewed by an independent panel drawn from the international consortium, following clearly pre-published criteria.

The system is intentionally designed to protect researchers from resource-limited settings, early-career scientists, and the Global South rather than to penalize them. It employs a multi-dimensional risk model that incorporates equity-weighted features, such as ORCID-linked publication history, institutional affiliation size, and declared research objectives. These features help prevent the misclassification of legitimate high-volume queries submitted by collaborative consortia or under-resourced research teams. In addition, annual public dashboards will report stratified false-positive rates according to researcher demographics and geographic regions. This transparency will support independent oversight and allow for rapid recalibration if any subgroup is found to be disproportionately affected. If a manuscript is confirmed to originate from a paper mill, the journal may securely return the encrypted submission IP address, data usage identifier, and key misuse characteristics to the shared database. This process helps refine the risk identification model. All data exchanges are fully de-identified and governed by formal agreements that strictly limit their use to improving peer review processes. In addition, participating parties must destroy the data after a specified period. Database providers and journals should hold regular closed-door meetings, ideally on a quarterly basis, to share insights into emerging high-risk behavior patterns and collaboratively update monitoring protocols and review guidelines.

Governance of the shared infrastructure is overseen by an independent international consortium modeled after United2Act working groups. This body comprises database providers, journal publishers, research-integrity experts, and representatives from organizations such as COPE. The consortium adjudicates rating disputes, secures sustainable funding through public grants and member contributions, and ensures full compliance with GDPR, HIPAA, and equivalent frameworks by using pseudonymized data flows and mandatory Data Protection Impact Assessments. To ensure applicability beyond NHANES, the framework adopts an open-source API specification and modular adapters, enabling phased integration with global databases while maintaining a unified risk taxonomy.
