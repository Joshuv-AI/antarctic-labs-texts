Knife-assisted endoscopic full-thickness resection of deeply invasive esophageal adenocarcinoma: a case series
Authors: Pieter Jan Poortmans, Maria Eva Argenziano, Michele Montori, Andrea Sorge, Lynn Debels, Lobke Desomer, David James Tate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsEsophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) incidence is rising globally, and current therapeutic options carry substantial morbidity and mortality. We report the feasibility and early outcomes of knife-assisted endoscopic full-thickness resection (kFTR) as a palliative local control in nonsurgical candidates.MethodsThree patients with suspected locally advanced distal EAC (clinical tumor stage T1b-T2), no nodal or distant disease, and high Charlson Comorbidity Index scores were unfit for surgery and declined chemoradiotherapy. All consented to kFTR: a submucosal pocket was created cranial to the suspected invasive area, the muscle-retracting sign was identified, and the muscularis propria was incised to access the periesophageal space. The lesion was dissected laterally and resected en bloc. A fully covered metal stent was then placed.ResultsPatients resumed oral intake on day 5 without major adverse events. All had EAC pathologic tumor stage 1b with invasion into the deepest layer of the submucosa; 2 had positive vertical margins. One remained disease-free (24 months); 2 had local recurrence by 6 months but remained asymptomatic at last follow-up (10 and 14 months).ConclusionsIn carefully selected symptomatic, inoperable patients, kFTR achieved short-to-medium term symptom control for locally advanced EAC with acceptable safety.

Background

Esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) is increasing in incidence. For lesions with deep submucosal (SM) invasion or invasion into the muscularis propria (MP), esophagectomy remains a standard curative option but carries considerable morbidity and nontrivial mortality. Definitive chemoradiotherapy represents the standard nonsurgical option, although not all patients are suitable candidates because of comorbidity or anticipated toxicity. Untreated progressive EAC causes debilitating dysphagia and bleeding. In this context, endoscopic approaches may provide symptom relief when curative therapies are declined or contraindicated.

Knife-assisted endoscopic full-thickness resection (kFTR) adapts endoscopic SM dissection tools to achieve deliberate full-thickness excision using a pocket-guided approach. The technique has been described in the rectum and upper gastrointestinal subepithelial tumors but has not been widely reported in EAC.1, 2, 3 We report a focused case series of 3 consecutive multimorbid patients treated with pocket-guided kFTR for symptomatic, deeply invasive distal EAC, emphasizing technique, resources, and early clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patients and staging

Three patients presenting with dysphagia were referred with suspected deeply invasive distal EAC (clinical tumor stage T1b-T2 according to the eighth edition TNM staging of esophageal cancers,4 corresponding to deep SM invasion [T1b] up to invasion of the MP [T2]) who had no distant or nodal disease on endoscopic ultrasound or positron emission tomography—computed tomography (PET-CT) scan (Table 1). All 3 patients were treatment-naïve at the time of presentation. Considering comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index scores, 6-10 points5), all patients were deemed unfit for esophagectomy after multidisciplinary discussion at a tertiary referral center for esophageal surgery. In addition, all patients declined chemoradiotherapy after extensive discussion. Alternative palliative treatment options, including permanent esophageal stent placement versus attempting kFTR, were presented. Risks and benefits were discussed, and all 3 patients elected to proceed with kFTR (Fig. 1). Key demographics, comorbidities, and tumors are summarized in Table 1.

Technique and equipment

All procedures were performed with the patient under general anesthesia using a high-definition gastroscope (GIF-EZ1500 with EVIS X1 processor; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and CO2 insufflation. Lesions were lifted with a standardized injection solution: 500 mL succinylated gelatin (Gelofusine 4%, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany), 5 mL indigo carmine 0.4%, and 1 mg epinephrine. A DualKnife J (1.5 mm; Olympus) was used during each procedure, and in some cases, a TriangleTipKnife J (Olympus) was also used, both connected to a VIO 3 generator (Erbe Elektromedizin, Tübingen, Germany). The TriangleTipKnife J was used for more controlled hemostasis and the use of spray coagulation during SM dissection. The DualKnife J was preferred for controlled incision of the MP with optimal visualization using EndoCut Q effect 2 and for further dissection in the periesophageal space.

After marking outside lesion margins with soft coagulation, an SM pocket was created 10 to 15 mm away from the cranial EAC margin in the orientation of the suspected deeply invasive component (Figs. 2 and 3A), that is, the area of maximally disrupted vascular pattern and/or ulceration (Video 1, available online at www.igiejournal.org). When reaching an area showing the muscle-retracting sign (Fig. 4),6, 7, 8 indicating either deep SM invasion or MP invasion but obstructing further SM dissection, we made an incision into the MP at a distance of 3 mm away until the periesophageal space was reached (Fig. 3A). Continuous clear saline-immersion together with a saline-exchange device (12F [F gauge] nasogastric tube attached to the endoscope with intermittent low-pressure suction) was used to increase precise visualization and dissection in the periesophageal space. Repetitive lifting injections expanded the periesophageal space, increasing the distance from surrounding structures for safe dissection.

SM dissection lateral to the suspected deeply invasive component was then performed to further isolate it (Fig. 3B). The MP was subsequently incised around the suspected deeply invasive component. With the help of external traction (orthodontic rubber band on wire clipped to the proximal edge of the specimen), the mucosal incision and SM dissection were completed, providing an en bloc, full-thickness resection of the EAC (Figs. 1 and 3C). For hemostasis, soft or spray coagulation was used via the knife.

A fully covered self-expandable metal stent (Niti-S, TaeWoong Medical, Goyang, South Korea) was placed in each patient, tailored to cover the entire defect and provide sealing of the esophagus. Proximally, the stent was fixed to the esophageal wall using over-the-scope stent fixation (stentfix OTSC, Ovesco Endoscopy AG, Tübingen, Germany). If the resection defect extended into the lower esophageal sphincter and/or cardia, the stent was also fixed to the mucosa of the cardia using through-the-scope clips (Instinct Plus, Cook Medical, Bloomington, Ind, USA). Fluoroscopy with intraluminal contrast injection was used to demonstrate sealing of the esophagus by the stent.

Results

The cohort consisted of 3 patients: 2 males aged 64 and 84 years, and 1 female aged 82 years (Table 1). The median total procedure duration was 240 minutes (range, 180-270 minutes). Capnoperitoneum developed in 1 patient during the procedure, requiring percutaneous needle decompression. No other adverse events occurred during the procedures.

A 7-day course of empiric antibiotics was administered. An esophagogram on postoperative day (POD) 2 confirmed sealing, and patients resumed liquids on POD5, advancing to semisolids while the stent remained in situ. The median length of hospital stay was 7 days (range, 5-8 days).

All postoperative adverse events were prospectively recorded and graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification (CDC) of surgical adverse events.9 During the 30 days after the procedure, no major adverse events (CDC grade III-V) were identified.

Stent extraction was performed between 6 and 8 weeks (median = 6 weeks) after the resection, and complete resolution of the muscle defect was observed in all patients. In 1 patient, a symptomatic stenosis occurred 2 weeks after the stent extraction, which was successfully treated with serial balloon dilations (CDC grade IIIb).

Histopathologic examination revealed a deeply SM invasive adenocarcinoma (pathologic tumor stage 1b with invasion into the deepest layer of the submucosa—invasion into the deepest one-third of the submucosa) in all patients. In 2 cases, the EAC was poorly or moderately differentiated without lymphovascular invasion. Both were removed en bloc, and 1 had tumor-free (negative) resection margins (R0), yet the other had positive deep resection margins (R1 vertical). One case showed an undifferentiated, mucinous EAC with lymphovascular invasion and positive deep resection margins (R1 vertical). In both cases, the positive deep resection margins were detected away from the full-thickness area in areas with poor tumoral cohesion and mucus pools in the deep margin in the mucinous EAC.

Postprocedural follow-up included 3-monthly structured clinical assessments, upper endoscopy (including resection scar biopsies), and PET-CT. One patient has remained disease-free and asymptomatic (after the balloon dilations) to date (24 months) and reports good quality of life (subjectively assessed). The remaining 2 patients (with positive deep resection margins) developed local recurrence at 6 months. Both declined further treatment and have remained asymptomatic to date (10 and 14 months), reporting good quality of life. No nodal or distant disease was detected during recorded follow-up in any of the patients.

Discussion

This focused case series demonstrates that pocket-guided kFTR can be performed safely in carefully selected multimorbid patients to achieve symptomatic relief and complete histologic assessment in situations where surgical or chemoradiotherapeutic options are contraindicated or declined. In such cases, the procedure aims to maintain or increase quality of life by local disease control rather than achieve curative resection, supporting kFTR as a potential palliative local control strategy.

Although full-thickness excision aims to prevent deep margin involvement, 2 of 3 resections showed positive vertical margins. This likely reflects the complex morphology and diffuse infiltrative nature of poorly differentiated and undifferentiated deeply invasive EAC. Microscopic tumor extension may exceed the macroscopic or fibrotic boundaries visible endoscopically (the muscle-retracting sign), despite intentional incision of the MP several millimeters from the invasive front.

The decision to perform kFTR in these cases should be interpreted within the specific context of symptomatic patients deemed unsuitable for surgery or chemoradiotherapy. All patients in this series presented with dysphagia and consented to kFTR as a palliative treatment over other palliative options such as stent placement. The additional benefit of kFTR compared with palliative stent placement alone lies in the immediate debulking of tumor tissue, which may reduce the risk of tumor-related bleeding, the lower likelihood of stent malfunction because of overgrowth or migration, and potentially extending the period of local disease control. In contrast, patients treated with stent placement alone seldom regain normal swallowing function and frequently experience adverse events related to long-term stent presence, including tumor ingrowth, overgrowth, and fistulization to adjacent structures. Although any survival advantage remains speculative, 1 patient in this series experienced a prolonged symptom-free interval (24 months to date) beyond what would be expected with stent placement alone.

Endoscopic cryotherapy may also palliate symptoms and reduce tumor burden, with the advantages of shorter procedure times and minimal invasiveness. However, cryotherapy does not yield a histologic specimen, may require repeated sessions, and is less suitable for large or fibrotic lesions. In contrast, kFTR provides both immediate mechanical relief and a complete tissue specimen for pathologic staging without losing the potential of being a curative treatment, albeit at the cost of higher procedural complexity, longer intervention time, and increased resource utilization.

Potential adverse events of the technique include periprocedural capnoperitoneum and postprocedural stricture formation. The recurrence observed in cases with positive deep margins emphasizes that, in this setting, kFTR should be regarded primarily as a palliative intervention offering local control and symptomatic benefit, rather than a definitive oncologic therapy.

In conclusion, pocket-guided kFTR may represent a minimally invasive option for carefully selected patients with deeply invasive EAC managed at expert centers who are unfit for standard therapy. The present findings suggest technical feasibility and short-term symptom improvement, but further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are required to define its role within the palliative treatment spectrum.
