Antifungal, antioxidant, and antibacterial activities of C. macrostachyus plant leaves extracts using different solvents
Authors: Birhanu Ayalew Tebeje, Ayalew Temesgen Wodajo, Shewaye Lakew Mekuria, Gizachew Mulugeta Manahelohe, Estifanos Ele Yaya, Mesfin Redi Abshiro, Melesse Ababay Assege, Getaneh Worku Moges
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The need for natural product-based bioactive plants with synergistic antioxidant and antibacterial properties is rapidly increasing worldwide. This study focuses on evaluating the antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal potential of C. macrostachyus Hochst. Ex Delile, which is frequently used in traditional settings to treat inflammatory conditions and infectious problems. The DPPH assay was used to evaluate the antioxidant activity; the antibacterial activity was tested against four microorganisms using an agar well diffusion assay, and the antifungal activity was tested against six fungal pathogens through a potato dextrose agar medium. Different chemical compounds were identified using HPLC and UV-Visible spectroscopic methods. The methanol extract showed better antioxidant capacity, with an IC50 value of 40.07 mg/mL compared with the other solvent extracts (p < 0.001). From all solvent extracts, the antibacterial activity test revealed potent antibacterial activity of the methanol extracts against E. coli and K. pneumoniae, with zones of inhibition of 22 ± 1.37 mm and 26 ± 1.18 mm, respectively. The chloroform extract also showed promising antifungal activity towards strawberry gray mold (20.3 ± 1.8 mm inhibition) which is greater than that of the other solvent extracts. The general data showed that the leaf part of the plant can be used for pharmaceutical, agricultural and food prevention applications due to its richness of antioxidants, antibacterial, and antifungal agents.

Introduction

The rapid increase in antimicrobial resistance and the increasing prevalence of oxidative stress-related health problems are becoming challenging health problems worldwide. The combined problem underscores the need to develop multipurpose therapeutic drugs that exhibit both antioxidant and antimicrobial effects1. Due to their traditional medicinal acceptance, which persists for extended periods, and the presence of diverse secondary metabolites, plant-based natural products exhibit firm bioactivity profiles with various active compounds2.

Concerning this idea, a tree native to the central, eastern, and southern parts of Africa, C. macrostachyus Hochst. Ex-Delile has significant potential3. Different parts of the plant are commonly used in traditional settings to treat inflammatory conditions and infectious diseases. The plant is frequently used to treat fever, gastrointestinal problems, and skin infections4,5. Various studies also reported the presence of particular phytochemicals like plenolics, flavonoids, diterpenoids, and triterpenoids in different parts of this plant responsible for its bioactivities5–7. In addition to its traditional use, there are limitations in the scientific pharmacological findings of the plant, particularly those related to solvent-based bioactivity determinations of the leaf part.

The current study is based on the assumption that the biological activity of the leaf extract of C. macrostachyus highly depends on the solvents used for extraction, because the polarity of the solvent determines the phytochemical families that can be recovered and the level of concentration8–10. Each phytochemical family possesses different antibacterial, antioxidant and antifungal mode of action. The polarity series are intentionally selected, compounds that have lower polarities like lipophilic terpenoids and fatty acids that are responsible for membrane targeting antimicrobial activities are extracted from the plant via chloroform11 and those compounds with medium polarities like flavonoids and some alkaloids which showed radical scavenging activates are isolated by acetone. On the other hand, more polar constituents, such as tannins, phenolics, and saponins, are isolated using more polar solvents, including methanol and water, which are known for their antimicrobial and redox properties12.

Some studies have shown that oxidative stress and microbial proliferation are closely linked, and that identifying antioxidants with greater effectiveness can reduce bacterial activity by enhancing the body’s defensive capacity13. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the synergy and relationship between the antioxidant and antimicrobial potential of C. macrostachyus as a natural source. Previous studies conducted on this plant mainly focus on the general antimicrobial studies, whereas this study differs in its aim and scope.

This study aims to isolate different crude extracts from the leaf parts of C. macrostachyus by using various organic solvents and to investigate the antioxidant capacity of these solvent extracts via a 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay; to evaluate the antibacterial and antifungal activities of the extracts towards selected pathogens; and to correlate the bioactivity results with the polarity and possible phytochemicals present in the samples via HPLC and UV‒visible spectroscopy. This method contributes to ongoing research on multiple natural agents that can have simultaneous antioxidant and antimicrobial effects.

Results

Phytochemical screening

Phytochemical screening tests of the plant extracts revealed the presence of biologically active and necessary secondary metabolites from different solvent extracts. According to the study results, the methanolic extract contains alkaloids, terpenoids, flavonoids, polyphenols, tannins, and steroids. Phenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids are also present to some extent in the acetone extract (Table 1). Phenolic compounds are the major phytochemicals found in the chloroform crude extract. The presence of flavonoids and phenolic compounds showed the plant its high antioxidant potential14 and the antimicrobial activity are also due to the presence of terpenoids and alkaloids15. Earlier quantitative studies confirms that the presence of diterpenoids (Floridolide A and Hardwickic acid) from the bark, triterpenes (Betuline, Zeorin, and Lupeol) from the stem bark and twigs, phenolic compounds (Benzoic acid, methyl gallate, Lichexanthone) from the twigs5.

Total phenolic contents (TPC)

The total phenolic concentration of the C. macrostachyus extract was determined using the Folin method, with a calibration curve prepared from different Gallic acid concentrations. The results are expressed as mg of Gallic acid per gram of crude extract (Fig. 1). The TPC of the C. macrostachyus plant extract, extracted with methanol and water, was found to be 155.28 ± 0.32 mg GAE/g and 122.0 ± 0.2 mg GAE/g, respectively, with strong statistical significance (p < 0.001). However, the total phenolic concentration decreased with the decreasing polarity of the solvent extracts: 98.5 ± 0.45 mg/GAE/g for the acetone extract and 85 ± 0.327 mg/GAE/g for the chloroform extract. These results revealed that the methanol extract of C. macrostachyus leaves is rich in polyphenolic compounds.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

The aluminum chloride method was employed to determine the total flavonoid concentration of the extracts using a calibration curve of different quercetin concentrations, and the results are expressed as milligrams of quercetin per gram of crude extract. The TFC content of the crude plant extract, when extracted with methanol, was greater (573 ± 6.24 mgQE/g) than that of the other solvent extracts, with values of 487.70 ± 7.05, 367 ± 9.72, and 272 ± 6.76 mgQE/g for the water, acetone, and chloroform solvent extracts, respectively. Overall, these results indicate that the plant is very rich in flavonoids and the statistical data (p < 0.001) showed that polarity of solvent plays a key role in which, more polar solvents contains higher TPC and TFC when compared with less polarity solvents (Fig. 1).

The TFC of C. macrostachyus is much more greater than TFC of P. atlantica fruit extract 93.7500 ± 0.2946 mgQE/g14, Grantia aucheri (Boiss.) ethanolic extracts at vegitable stage 41.83 ± 0.45, flowering stage 69.32 ± 0.53 mgQE/g, and seeding stage 29.35 ± 0.14 mgQE/g a16. On the other hand the total phenolic content of the leaf part of C. macrostachyus is still lower than P. atlantica fruit extract which is 227.0392 ± 1.6198 mgGAE/g14.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of methanol, chloroform, water, and acetone extracts of the C. macrostachyus sample was determined via the most commonly used method to assess the antioxidant potential, which is the DPPH radical scavenging assay. In this assay, there is discoloration, and the extent of discoloration is due to the presence of an antioxidant, which is due to its hydrogen-donating ability; as a result, the absorbance decreases as the radical changes from DPPH to DPPH-H at 517 nm17.

DPPH free radical scavenging activity is widely used in assessing the potential of free radical scavenging capacity because of the ease of the reaction. The DPPH assay relies on the measurement of the reducing ability of an antioxidant toward DPPH. This capacity can be measured by a decrease in absorbance or by using electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy18. The DPPH assay is a reaction-based assay in which hydrogen abstraction and electron transfer are involved in the reaction pathway. The results of the DPPH assay revealed that the methanol, water, and acetone extracts had relatively high antioxidant activity with relatively low IC50 values. The antioxidant capacity of the chloroform extract was significantly lower than that of the other crude extracts (Fig. 2). The scavenging activity also increased with increasing extract concentration. The antioxidant activity could be attributed to the presence of phenolic compounds19.

HPLC profiles of the extract and UV-visible spectra

Figure 3 shows the chromatogram of the crude extract of C. macrostachyus at 327 nm. This wavelength was selected because the major peaks showed maximum absorption at this wavelength. The eluted compounds were detected mainly in the ranges of 5–10 min, 10–16 min, and 24–37 min, indicating that the compounds had different polarities. Based on the HPLC chromatogram (Fig. 3) and UV-Visible spectral data (Fig. 4), compounds with relatively high phenolic and flavonoid contents were observed.

Chlorogenic acids are eluted as phenolic acids at ~ 5–10 min via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and at a maximum absorbance of ~ 325 nm via UV-Visible spectroscopy, as reported in a previous study20. Caffeic acid was co-eluted in the same way as phenolic acids and confirmed at the same wavelength21. Rutin was also detected ~ 15 min as a flavonoid glycoside at a maximum of ~ 325 nm (band II) and 325 nm (band I) 22. The retention time of 15 min is a fingerprint of quercitrin/ iso-quercitrin near 325 nm. Quercetin was retained later, at approximately 25 min. At 375 nm, it exhibited the same absorption as a less polar flavonol, as supported by a study by Zhang22.

The results of the present study revealed that hydroxycinnamic acids, such as caffeic acid and chlorogenic acid, are likely present. In addition to these phenolic acids, flavonoids such as rutin/quercetin glycoside, quercetin, and flavonols are present. The presence of these compounds revealed that the methanolic extracts are more active in combating oxidative stress and microbial resistance.

Antibacterial activity

In the present work, the extracts obtained from the leaves of C. macrostachyus showed strong activity against most of the tested bacterial strains. The results were compared with those of standard gentamicin. Antibacterial activity tests of the crude extract of leaves of C. macrostachyus were evaluated by measuring the average zone of inhibition against the tested organism23.

The negative control, DMSO, had no inhibitory effect on any of the test strains. The positive control, gentamycin, showed a zone of inhibition for the selected bacterial strains. The results of the extraction of C. macrostachyus leaves are expressed as the mean value ± standard deviation (SD) of the growth inhibition zone diameter.

The results revealed the higher antibacterial activity of the C. macrostachyus methanolic extracts against E. coli and K. pneumoniae, with zones of inhibition of 22 ± 1.37 mm and 26 ± 1.18 mm, respectively (Fig. 5). On the other hand, no activity was observed for C. macrostachyus chloroform and acetone plant extracts against Bacillus bacteria (Fig. 6). This is due to low concentration of bio active phytochemicals or due to robust cell structure and their spore forming ability of Bacillus species that cam make resistant to such extracts24. The extract produced significant differences across all bacterial strains. Most comparisons yielded p < 0.001, while a few demonstrated p < 0.01 or p < 0.05, indicating strong statistical significance with R2 value 0.978.

Discussion

The tests of the total phenolic, flavonoid, antioxidant, antifungal, and antibacterial activities of C. macrostachyus’ four solvent extracts of dried leaves revealed that there were medicinally active compounds in the studied methanolic, chloroform, acetone, and water extracts of C. macrostachyus. The plant exhibits excellent antioxidant activity; in particular, the methanolic extract demonstrates relatively greater antioxidant activity, characterized by a lower IC50 value. The results showed that C. macrostachyus can be a good source of antioxidant compounds that help relieve oxidative stress. In addition, the water extracts also showed good antioxidant activity, unlike the chloroform and acetone extracts.

All the solvent extracts exhibited a range of antibacterial activities against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Besides its antioxidant activity, the methanolic extract also showed strong antibacterial activity relative to the other solvent extracts, which are rich in phenolic compounds. This extract showed average zones of inhibition ranging from 19 to 26 mm, with potent inhibition of K. pneumoniae at 26 ± 2.0 mm, and notable inhibition of E. coli at 22 ± 1.3 mm in length. The methanolic extract is the only extract that inhibits Bacillus bacterial strains. The results showed that this extract contains various compounds with broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. The chloroform extract also exhibited significant antibacterial activity against certain bacterial strains, including K. pneumoniae and E. coli, with average inhibition zones of 22 ± 0.5 mm and 18.6 ± 0.5 mm, respectively. Chloroform and acetone extracts did not exhibit activity toward Bacillus. When compared with previously studied reports, the antibacterial activity of methanol extract showed higher result on Bacillus strains (19.5 ± 0.5 mm) than previous study by Cyrus et al.25 reported 15.6 mm. on the other hand, antibacterial study on C. macrostachyus by sendeku et al.26 reports that the chloroform and methanol extract showed 7.00 ± 1.0 mm and 8.33 ± 1.53 mm zones of inhibition against E.coli respectively. But this study revealed higher inhibition zones (18.5 ± 0.5 mm and 22 ± 1.3 mm zones of inhibition) against chloroform and methanol extracts respectively.

The antioxidant properties of C. macrostachyus extracts might be related to their chemical composition, which includes bioactive phytochemicals and high levels of phenolic acids, terpenoids, and flavonoids. C. macrostachyus can be considered a good source of antioxidants that help prevent many diseases caused by oxidative stress, e.g., diabetes mellitus, carcinomas, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases 5,27.

In recent years, various studies have been conducted on phenolic and flavonoid-rich natural products with antioxidant activities. The reason behind this is the presence of hydroxyl group attachments in flavonoids and phenolic compounds, which are highly responsible for reducing oxidative stress. Phenolic compounds are good electron donors because their hydroxyl groups can directly contribute to antioxidant actions. Phenolic compounds play a significant role in preventing oxidative stress by inhibiting free radicals and decomposing peroxides, as demonstrated by various studies28,29.

Recent studies have also shown that leafy plants that contain relatively high flavonoid phenolic contents have potential antioxidant activity, and this activity is associated with a decreased incidence of cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and neural diseases 29–31.

Additionally, the results of the HPLC chromatogram of the C. macrostachyus plant extract at 327 nm in Fig. 3 and UV‒visible spectral readings in Fig. 4, revealed the presence of mixed polarities of the compounds in the plant extract. The results of the present study indicate that hydroxycinnamic acids, which are caffeic acid and chlorogenic acid, are present. In addition to these phenolic acids, flavonoids such as rutin/quercetin glycoside, quercetin, and flavonols are present. The occurrence of these compounds revealed that the methanolic extracts are more active toward oxidative stress and microbial resistance.

The presence of these known phenolic compounds (chlorogenic acid and quercetin) on the C. macrostachyus leaf extracts helps to enhance the antibacterial and antifungal activities in the methanol and chloroform extracts by microbial cell walls disruptions, inhibiting the growth of fungus, and generating oxidative stress to alter permeability of membrane32,33. In addition, the strong antioxidant phenolic compound, chlorogenic acid, can prevent oxidative stress and support the growth of microbials by scavenging free radicals34. The potency of antifungal and antioxidant activity of the crude extracts is much lower than their purified forms which indicate their presence are in a minimal amount.

The antifungal activity of the extracts revealed various responses to the tested fungal pathogens. Among the four solvent extracts, the chloroform crude extract of C. macrostachyus showed promising antifungal activity toward strawberry gray mold (20.3 ± 1.8 mm inhibition). In addition, the extract exhibited relatively moderate antifungal activity against the pathogens of cucumber anthracnose (8.2 ± 2.0 mm inhibition) and rice sheath blight (4.0 ± 1.2 mm). This result suggests that the chloroform extract of the leaf part of C. macrostachyus can be utilized as a potential antifungal agent against the aforementioned fungal pathogens, particularly Botrytis cinerea.

The methanolic and acetone extracts of the plant showed moderately greater antifungal activity against rice blast disease, with inhibition zones of 18.3 ± 1.2 mm and 11.1 ± 1.3 mm, respectively. These findings suggest that these extracts function as natural agents that suppress rice blast.

The acetone and water plant extracts also had similar, slight effects on strawberry mold pathogens and cucumber anthracnose. In addition, four of the plant extracts had no positive effects on the apple Valsa canker or tomato early blight fungal pathogens. Although some C. macrosachyus leaf extracts have shown good activity against various fungal pathogens, they are less active than Osthole, Streptochlorin, and Boscalid, which serve as positive controls.

Genrrally, the methanol and chloroform extracts C. macrostachyus showed measurable antioxidant, antibacterial and antifungal effects, their inhibition levels are comparably lower than those of standard drugs. So it can be said that they have modest but encouraging bioactivity.

Methods

Plant material collection and extraction

Fresh leaves of C. macrostachyus were collected from the northwestern part of Ethiopia, specifically from the Amhara region, near Tseda village, located approximately 339174.6 latitude and 1,381,260 longitudes, with an elevation of 2344 m above sea level, in August 2024 (Fig. 7). The plant was identified and authenticated by Mr. Abiyu Enyew Molla, a botanist at the University of Gondar. A voucher specimen, BAT001/2024, was given and deposited in the herbarium of the Biology Department.

C.macrostachyus is treated as a least concern (LC) plant under IUCN 3.1 (2019 assessment) and it is not listed on I, II, and III CITES trade restriction appendices.

Map of the study area in which the plant C. macrostachyus was collected (generated by using ArcGIS 10.7.1 (https://desktop.arcgis.com/en/index.html).

The collected plant parts were dried at room temperature (27 °C) without exposure to sunlight for two weeks and ground into a powder by an electric grinder. The powdered material (300 g) was subjected to subsequent extraction via different solvents with increasing polarity (chloroform, acetone, methanol, and water) via maceration with occasional shaking on an orbital shaker (72 h each)35. The extracts were filtered (Whatman No. 1 filter paper), concentrated under reduced pressure in a rotary evaporator, and stored at 4 °C until further use36.

Phytochemical screening

The presence and absence of secondary metabolites, which are responsible for the bioactivities of the extracts, were determined via qualitative phytochemical analysis. This test was conducted via standard methods such as alkaloids (Dragendorff’s test), flavonoids (AlCl₃ test), tannins (ferric chloride test), terpenoids (Salkowski test), saponins (foam test), and phenolics (Folin‒Ciocalteu assay)37.

Determination of total phenolic content

First, 1.2 mL of sodium carbonate (7.5%) and a volume of 1.8 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu were added to 50 µL of sample extract within a 5-minute interval. Then, the incubation was continued for 120 min, and the absorbance was measured at 760 nm (R2 = 0.99). For the determination of the total phenolic concentration, a calibration curve generated with gallic acid was used, and the results are expressed as mg of gallic acid per gram of crude extract38.

Determination of total flavonoid content

Four milliliters of distilled water, 0.3 mL of 5% NaNO2, and 0.3 mL of 10% AlCl3 were added to 100 µL of crude extract. In this mixture, 2 mL of 1 N NaOH and 2.5 mL of distilled water were added after 6 min. The absorbance was then measured at 510 nm to determine the total flavonoid content using a calibration curve with quercetin (R2 = 0.99), and the results are expressed as milligrams of quercetin per gram of crude extract39.

Antioxidant activity assay

DPPH radical scavenging assay

The antioxidant potential was evaluated via the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay40. The free radical scavenging activities of C. macrostachyus were investigated based on its ability to reduce DPPH. The antioxidant potential of the plant extracts was evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay, as described by Cuvelier et al. (1995), with slight modifications40. In a 500 mL volumetric flask, 0.25 mg of DPPH was dissolved in a minimal amount of methanol. The flask was filled with methanol after the DPPH was completely dissolved. The control solution was prepared by adding 1 mL of methanol to 25 mL of DPPH solution.

Additionally, an ascorbic acid stock solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of ascorbic acid in 100 mL of methanol. A calibration curve was generated by adjusting different concentrations, 10, 25, 50, 100, 150, and 200 mg/L, from the stock solution. Three milliliters of standard ascorbic acid and 2 mL of DPPH solution were added to each test tube. Then, the test tubes were shielded with aluminum foil and placed in the dark for approximately 30 min. For the DPPH samples, the final volume of each solution was adjusted to 4 mL with 80% aqueous methanol. This mixture was kept in a dark place for 30 min. After the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The extract concentration that yields 50% inhibition (IC50) was calculated based on the plot of inhibition (%) against extract concentration and compared with the IC50 of ascorbic acid, serving as a positive control18.

HPLC and UV‒visible spectral analysis

For the HPLC analysis of the crude extract, an Agilent LC-25AT HPLC instrument with an Agilent ZORBAX SB C18 column (5 μm×4.6 mm×250 mm) was used. The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and methanol (B), with gradient elution (0.01 min, 10% B; 0.01 ~ 60 min, 10% B→90% B; 60 ~ 73 min, 90% B→10% B). The sample size was 1 mL, and the injection volume was 10 µL. Each sample was run for 60 min. The column temperature was 30 °C, and a DAD detector was used with an optimal detection wavelength of 327 nm.

Antimicrobial activity

Microbial strains

The extracts were tested against four different bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus) and gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli, Bacillus, and K. pneumoniae) were used.

Agar well diffusion assay

The agar well diffusion (NCCLS, 2022) method was used to assess the antimicrobial activity of the plant extract. Nutrients were prepared, and the composed Müller‒Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) was added to different Petri dishes. Then, bacterial dilutions were prepared by using the 0.5 McFarland turbidity standards and streaked step by step on several agar plates. The negative control for the bacterial strains was DMSO. Wells were made on agar plates via aseptic techniques to immerse the C. macrostachyus plant extract. The Petri dishes were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the zone of inhibition was measured41.

Antifungal activity

Preparation of potato dextrose agar medium

Potato dextrose agar medium (PDA) was prepared by mixing 200 g of potato, 1000 mL of water, 18 g of agar, and 20 g of dextrose, after which the mixture was sterilized at 121 °C and 1 MPa for 20 min. The target extract was weighed and dissolved in DMSO to prepare a stock solution at a concentration of 25 mg/ml. DMSO without any drugs was used as a blank control42. The stock solution was also added to the sterilized PDA medium to prepare a drug-containing medium at a concentration of 50 µg/mL (50 mL). The mixture was evenly distributed into three Petri dishes, placed in a laminar flow hood for UV sterilization for 15 min, and then cooled. Next, after the test fungus was activated, it was transferred to the medium via a hole, after which it was placed in a 25 °C incubator for cultivation. During the incubation period, the growth of the mycelium was observed until the diameter of the blank colonies reached approximately 60–65 mm. The growth diameter of the mycelium of the fungi in the presence of different target compounds was measured. The control plates were arranged without drugs and with commercial drugs (Boscalid, osthole), with the same treatment methods and amounts of solvent used as the experimental group. Each sample was tested in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data were analyzed via ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (p < 0.05) in GraphPad Prism 9.0 and Origin 2025b software.

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed different compounds that are responsible for their essential biological activities. Different solvent extracts of the plant inhibited the growth of various bacteria at varying levels. The methanolic crude extract exhibited both significant antioxidant and antibacterial activity, while the chloroform extract showed relatively higher antifungal activity against specific fungal pathogens. The higher total phenol and flavonoid content, along with the presence of hydroxycinnamic acids and flavonoid compounds, provides an opportunity to further isolate curative lead compounds and conduct in vivo studies from C. macrostachyus leaf extracts.
