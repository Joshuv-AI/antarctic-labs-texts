Incidence and risk factors of obstetric anal sphincter injuries: a retrospective study
Authors: Felwa Faisal Althaher, Deama Saeed Alghamdi, Heba Emad Bayoumi, Lamees Fadhely Alshanqity, Samera Fahad Al Basri, Nashwa Fahad Aldardeir, Hanan Mohammed Shamrani, Feras Mohammed Allagany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND: Obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS) are severe complications of vaginal deliveries with long-term physical and psychological sequelae. Data on the prevalence and risk factors of OASIS are limited in Saudi Arabia. This study aimed to assess the incidence of OASIS annually from 2017 to 2020, identify associated maternal and delivery factors, and investigate patients' characteristics related to OASIS Grades.OBJECTIVE: To evaluate the annual incidence of OASIS from 2017 to 2020, identify maternal and delivery-related risk factors, and assess characteristics associated with different OASIS grades.DESIGN: Single-center, retrospective observational studySETTING: King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi ArabiaMETHODS: Medical records of all vaginal deliveries from 2017 to 2020 were reviewed. The study included 112 patients with singleton vaginal deliveries complicated by OASIS, out of a total of 12 081 deliveries. Exclusion criteria included preterm births and incomplete records. Data on maternal demographics, obstetric history, labor type, mode of delivery, episiotomy use, and neonatal outcomes were collected. The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) classification was used to grade OASIS severity. Chi-square and t-tests were used for analysis.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURES: Annual incidence of OASIS, distribution of OASIS grades, and associations between clinical variables and injury severity.SAMPLE SIZE: 112 cases of OASIS among 12081 vaginal deliveriesRESULTS: The incidence of OASIS was 0.93%, showing a decreasing trend from 2017 to 2020. Most patients were Saudis (78.6%), primigravidae (58%), and nulliparous (67%). Spontaneous vaginal delivery occurred in 73.2% of cases, with episiotomies performed in 62%. Grade III tears were observed in 79.5% of cases, whereas 8.0% were classified as Grade IV. No significant differences were observed between Grades III and IV in terms of maternal and delivery characteristics, except for higher estimated blood loss in Grade IV cases. Incomplete documentation was noted in repair techniques and episiotomy details.CONCLUSION: Incidence of OASIS shows a decreasing trend in both the number of cases and the incidence rate over the four years between 2017 and 2020. Further research is necessary to address documentation gaps and refine regional prevention and management practices.LIMITATION: Retrospective design and missing values limited the ability to analyze some variables thoroughly.Keywords: Obstetric anal sphincter injury, perineal tear, retrospective study, risk factor, vaginal delivery complication

INTRODUCTION

Perineal tears are common injuries sustained during vaginal delivery. They occur as the perineum may stretch and tear when the fetal head passes through the birth canal.1 Perineal tears are classified according to severity. First-degree tears, the mildest form, involve only the vaginal mucosa or outer perineal skin. These typically heal spontaneously without the need for stitches. Second-degree tears extend deeper to affect the vaginal mucosa and perineal muscles. These often require sutures to heal properly. Third-degree tears involve the anal sphincter complex. They are further divided into subtypes: involvement of <50% of the external sphincter muscle is grade 3a, >50% of the external sphincter muscle is grade 3b, and both the external and internal sphincter muscles is grade 3c. These injuries—particularly those arising during delivery—are known as obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS).2

The prevalence of OASIS worldwide is estimated to range from 0.25% to 7.31%, with a higher incidence reported among primiparous women.3 A previous study, in Saudi Arabia, reported that 29.6% of women experienced third- or fourth-degree lacerations during vaginal deliveries.4 Another Saudi study reported an OASIS incidence rate of 0.4% with 85 of 19,374 women who experienced vaginal deliveries in the period 2014–2019 having third (N=81) and fourth (N=4) degrees of perineal tears.5

OASIS usually results from multiple risk factors. The risk rises markedly with instrumental deliveries (forceps or vacuum), prolonged or difficult labor, and post-term pregnancies, where increased birth weight, reduced amniotic fluid, decreased perineal elasticity, and greater medical intervention collectively heighten the likelihood of severe perineal tears.6,7

Persistent perineal pain resulting from perineal damage can cause negative effects on a woman's daily activities and her quality of life. Women with OASIS may suffer from urinary and fecal incontinence. More than 50% of OASIS patients are reported to have anal incontinence, regardless of menopausal status. Losing bowel and bladder control is both distressing and difficult to manage.8 Also, OASIS may cause constipation and pain during bowel movements due to decreased gastrointestinal emptying.9 Some cases are complicated by vaginal wall prolapse, in which the vaginal walls lose support and protrude into the vaginal canal, causing severe discomfort. Moreover, OASIS contributes to sexual dysfunction and dyspareunia.6

In patients with perineal injuries, the physical and emotional aspects are significantly influenced. These patients often experience anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal, as they may avoid engaging in activities due to embarrassment or complex symptoms. OASIS can also impact a woman's ability to fulfill her role as a mother positively, as she may have difficulty fulfilling childcare responsibilities whilst recovering from perineal damage. Consequently, these effects may lead to OASIS syndrome.10,11

Studies found that the effects of perineal damage can persist for years, even a decade or more postpartum. Some women may have persistent anal incontinence and other symptoms two decades later. Anal incontinence at nine months postpartum has been observed as a predictor of long-term complications in OASIS cases.12

Due to the adverse effects of OASIS and limited data about its prevalence and risk factors in Saudi Arabia, this study was conducted to assess the incidence of OASIS at a tertiary care hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, between 2017 and 2020. It also aimed to assess maternal, obstetric, and delivery factors linked to OASIS and to compare characteristics between grade III and IV cases to identify risk factors and guide prevention and management strategies in Saudi Arabia.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective study was conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH) in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. This tertiary care hospital has a capacity of 406 beds and serves a diverse patient population. We reviewed the medical records of women with singleton vaginal deliveries at the hospital between January 2017 and December 2020. Patient confidentiality was preserved by the de-identification of all collected data. During data extraction, identifiable information was omitted. The study was approved by the Unit of Biomedical Ethics, approval number 746-23 last April 23, 2025.

Study population

The study included women who experienced singleton vaginal deliveries at KAUH between January 2017 and December 2020. To ensure the study's relevance to full-term pregnancies, women who delivered preterm births were excluded from the study. A total of 11969 cases were excluded from analysis due to absence of OASIS or meeting exclusion criteria such as preterm birth, elective cesarean without labor, or documentation gaps.

Data collection

Data was extracted from the electronic medical records system at KAUH. Variables were categorized into maternal demographics, obstetric history, labor and delivery characteristics, birth outcomes, and details specific to OASIS. Maternal demographic variables included age at delivery, nationality, ethnicity, smoking status, and body mass index (BMI). Obstetric history included gravidity, parity, previous obstetric complications such as OASIS, and comorbidities.

Labor and delivery characteristics involved mode of labor (spontaneous, augmented, or induced), mode of delivery (spontaneous vaginal, ventouse-assisted, or forceps-assisted), use of analgesia (e.g., epidural or pethidine), duration of the first stage of labor, episiotomy use, and gestational age at birth. Birth outcomes focused on birth weight, Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity and Respiration (APGAR) scores, and birth complications other than OASIS. Data on OASIS included the severity of tears (grades III and IV), repair methods, suture materials, location of repair (e.g., labor room or operating room), and the duration of the repair procedure.

Diagnosis of OASIS was clinically made by the senior physician. In cases where the diagnosis was made by the senior resident, it was confirmed by the senior physician. Grading of OASIS was done according to the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) classification.13 Grade III OASIS is characterized by a partial or total disruption of the anal sphincter muscles, which can affect either the external or internal anal sphincters. Anal sphincter muscle dysfunction combined with a rectal mucosal tear was referred to as grade IV. OASIS data were verified against KAUH medical records and identified using codes O70.2 and O70.3 of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9 Codes.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corp, Chicago, IL, USA). The study variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables, including maternal, neonatal, and delivery characteristics, were compared according to OASIS grade using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test. Continuous variables, such as maternal BMI, estimated blood loss, and hemoglobin level, were compared between groups using independent t-tests, based on OASIS severity. Annual incidence rates over the period of January 2017 to December 2020 were reported as cases per 1000 births. Poisson regression was performed with the number of OASIS cases as the outcome and year as a continuous predictor, using the natural logarithm of total births per year as an offset. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at P<.05.

Delivery characteristics of OASIS patients

Delivery parameters show that the majority of patients (69.6%) experienced spontaneous labor, while 18.8% had labor augmented, and 11.6% underwent induced labor. DISCUSSION

This retrospective study collected medical records of women with singleton vaginal deliveries at a tertiary hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, between 2017 and 2020 to assess the incidence and risk factors for OASIS.

Incidence of OASIS

Our study identified an OASIS incidence rate of 0.93% among 12 081 deliveries, which is comparable to the lower range reported globally (0.25–7.31%) 3 but lower than the 1.2% reported by Mahgoub et al14 and the 0.43% in a prior Saudi study conducted by Al Ghamdi in 2020.5 This trend aligns with findings by Al Ghamdi, who declared that selective episiotomy and senior staff attendance during difficult deliveries contribute to reduced rates.5 Troko-Alawale et al further pointed to the importance of follow-up care, including endoanal ultrasonography, to identify asymptomatic defects.15

Demographic characteristics

The majority of OASIS cases in our study involved Saudi nationals (78.6%), with Arabs being the predominant ethnic group (71.4%). This is consistent with the study by Al Ghamdi, which also had a predominantly Arab cohort.5 Nevertheless, Mahgoub et al14 and Ramm et al3 reported increased OASIS risk among Asians. Such differences may reflect anatomical differences and varying delivery practices across populations. Smoking status, largely unknown in our cohort, was unlikely to affect OASIS incidence significantly, as supported by other studies that did not establish smoking as a major risk factor.3,5,14,16

Obstetric and labor characteristics

Most of the OASIS patients in the current study were primigravida (58.9%) and nullipara (67.0%). This agrees with findings by Mahgoub et al,14 who also reported an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 4.19 for nulliparity. The association of nulliparity with OASIS is well-documented in the literature, largely attributed to increased perineal resistance during first-time vaginal deliveries.16–19 Additionally, prolonged second-stage labor, observed in some of our cases, is a recurring risk factor across studies, including Meyer et al,18 who utilized a case-control approach to identify labor-related risks, and van Bavel et al,19 who analyzed a large registry-based cohort in the Netherlands. Ramm et al further demonstrated that OASIS risk elevates with prolonged second-stage labor.3

The role of episiotomy in OASIS development remains debated. In our study, 62.5% of women underwent episiotomy, though its protective effect was not statistically significant. Similar findings were obtained by Mahgoub et al14 and Loewenberg-Weisband et al,17 who found that women did not benefit from episiotomy during spontaneous vaginal deliveries. On the other hand, Verghese et al,20 who performed a systematic review and meta-analysis, reported that mediolateral episiotomy decreased the risk of OASIS by 33% in nulliparous women during vaginal deliveries. They concluded that proper technique and training in episiotomy administration are crucial to decreasing OASIS incidence. Our findings call for further stratification by episiotomy type to clarify its impact.

Instrumental deliveries

Instrumental deliveries intensify perineal strain, contributing to anal sphincter injuries.1 These procedures were observed in 26.8% of cases in this study, predominantly ventouse-assisted. Similarly, Troko-Alawale et al identified forceps deliveries as a significant risk factor. Their retrospective analysis of 69 cases revealed a significant relationship between forceps use and higher rates of endoanal defects, diagnosed via ultrasonography (P=.004).15 Ramm et al confirmed these findings, demonstrating that vacuum-assisted deliveries increased OASIS risk fourfold compared to spontaneous vaginal deliveries.3 The protective role of mediolateral episiotomy during instrumental deliveries, as reported by van Bavel et al,19 was not evident in our study. Loewenberg-Weisband et al similarly observed no significant association between episiotomy and reduced OASIS incidence during assisted deliveries.17

Neonatal and maternal factors

Neonatal factors, including birth weight, were pivotal in OASIS risk. The mean birth weight of 3.22 kg in our study partially aligns with findings by Mahgoub et al,14 who reported increased OASIS risk for birth weights >3.5 kg. Other studies also found that macrosomia is a major contributing factor to OASIS.16,21,22

Maternal BMI averaged 28.2 kg/m². While the difference between Grade III (27.9±6.28) and Grade IV (32.8±9.62) cases was not statistically significant (P=.508), the higher BMI observed in Grade IV cases may have clinical relevance. This aligns with van Bavel et al, who demonstrated that lower BMI was protective against OASIS.19 Differences in maternal weight and height across populations may explain these variations. Hemoglobin levels in our study were also not predictive of OASIS severity, a finding that aligns with Al Ghamdi, who investigated maternal laboratory parameters in their retrospective analysis.5

Gestational age and prolonged pregnancy

Gestational age averaged 40.8 weeks in our study, with post-term pregnancies showing increased OASIS rates. This agrees with the hypothesis that post-term pregnancies lead to larger neonates and reduced perineal elasticity, as proposed by Balachandran et al in their review of perineal trauma risks.21 Meyer et al and Ramm et al similarly identified prolonged pregnancies as a risk factor for OASIS.3,18

Analgesia

Anesthesia practices were inconsistently documented in our cohort, with pethidine being the most common agent used. This highlights the need for standardized analgesia practices, as emphasized by Troko-Alawale et al, who stress that such standardization can significantly improve patient outcomes.15

Maternal and labor factors by OASIS severity

Estimated blood loss was higher in Grade IV cases, aligning with Troko-Alawale et al, who reported increased complications in more severe tears.15 Labor parameters, such as episiotomy use and mode of delivery, showed no significant variations, although induced labor approached statistical significance. These findings suggest that while certain maternal and labor factors influence OASIS risk, their role in determining severity remains less clear.

Clinical implications

A notable decrease in OASIS incidence occurred and the cause may be multifactorial. Potential contributors include improved perineal protection methods during vaginal delivery, greater use of episiotomy in specific instances, enhanced intrapartum monitoring, and more rigorous documentation standards. In addition, the use of instrumental deliveries was reduced or improved when their use was necessary. Moreover, changes in patient demographics, workforce management techniques, or reporting methodologies throughout this timeframe may have impacted the noted decrease. The decline may also reflect broader advancements in obstetric practices and institutional initiatives, such as structured training programs for the staff, implementation of protocols for delivery, and strengthened quality improvement policies within the hospital.

Study strengths and limitations

The strengths of the study include a large sample size and detailed analysis of maternal, neonatal, and labor factors. By focusing on a region with limited existing research on OASIS, it addresses a critical gap in the literature specific to Saudi Arabia. The four-year data range allowed for the identification of temporal trends, including the significant decline in OASIS incidence over time. These findings provide valuable baseline data for future regional studies. However, the study was limited by incomplete documentation. The lack of data on specific types of episiotomies restricted the ability to evaluate their protective effects comprehensively. Furthermore, the retrospective design may have introduced selection bias, and findings are confined to a single tertiary care center, limiting generalized applicability. Although multivariable analysis would provide stronger evidence by adjusting for potential confounders, there were only a limited number of Grade IV OASIS cases. This precluded reliable logistic regression modeling and may restrict the ability to draw causal inferences. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to confirm our findings with multivariable-adjusted estimates. In addition, the comparison between Grade III and Grade IV OASIS cases is limited by the small number of Grade IV OASIS cases (n=9), which reduces the statistical power.

Future research recommendations

Future studies should examine the optimal episiotomy techniques and the application of manual perineal support in varied delivery contexts. Repair protocols should be standardized to improve outcomes and ensure consistent care. Research into long-term maternal and neonatal outcomes is essential for understanding the broader implications of OASIS. Multicenter prospective studies can expand knowledge on OASIS risk factors and prevention strategies, providing predictive models applicable to diverse populations and healthcare settings. Focus should also be placed on addressing regional gaps, particularly in underrepresented areas such as the Middle East.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that most patients were primigravida and nulliparous, underwent episiotomy, and had third-degree perineal tears. None of the maternal or labor factors were associated with OASIS severity. In our study period of 2017–2020, OASIS incidence declined, suggesting that preventive measures like manual perineal support and restricted episiotomy may be effective. Nevertheless, gaps in documentation, such as the type of episiotomy used or specific repair methods, limited deeper analysis. Additionally, a lack of data on long-term maternal outcomes restricts understanding of the broader effect of these injuries. Future research should focus on filling these gaps by standardizing documentation, exploring tailored interventions, and conducting multicenter studies. Regional studies, particularly in the Middle East, are crucial. Such efforts would enhance global understanding and improve care practices in OASIS cases.
