Asymptotic well-calibration of the posterior predictive p p -value under the modified Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
Yueming Shen

Abstract
The posterior predictive p p -value is a widely used tool for Bayesian model checking. However, under most test statistics, its asymptotic null distribution is more concentrated around 1/2 than uniform. Consequently, its finite-sample behavior is difficult to interpret and tends to lack power, which is a well-known issue among practitioners. A common choice of test statistic is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with plug-in estimators. It provides a global measure of model-data discrepancy for real-valued observations and is sensitive to model misspecification. In this work, we establish that under this test statistic, the posterior predictive p p -value converges in distribution to uniform under the null. We further use numerical experiments to demonstrate that this p p -value is well-behaved in finite samples and can effectively detect a wide range of alternative models. Keywords: Asymptotic properties; Goodness of fit; Model checking; Posterior predictive p p -value



1
Introduction

Model checking is a crucial step in Bayesian analysis. Among the available tools, the posterior predictive
p
-value (ppp) has become widely adopted due to its simplicity and flexibility. It was introduced by
Guttman 1967
and
Rubin 1984
, and later popularized by
Gelman et al. 1996
. Suppose we posit a parametric sampling model
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{\bm{\theta}}:\bm{\theta}\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}\}
for observed data
y_{\mathrm{obs}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, along with a prior
\pi(\bm{\theta})
on the parameter
\bm{\theta}
. To evaluate the compatibility between
\mathcal{P}
and the data, we consider testing the composite null hypothesis
H:Y\sim P_{\bm{\theta}}
,
\bm{\theta}\in\Theta
. Let
t(Y):\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be a test statistic where larger values indicate greater discrepancy. Let
p_{\bm{\theta}}(y)
denote the density of
P_{\bm{\theta}}
under the Lebesgue measure,
\pi(\bm{\theta}\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}})
denote the posterior distribution, ppp is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{ppp}(y_{\mathrm{obs}})=pr^{m(y\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}})}[t(Y)\geq t(y_{\mathrm{obs}})],

where the reference distribution for
Y
is the posterior predictive distribution

\displaystyle m(y\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}})=\int_{\Theta}p_{\bm{\theta}}(y)\pi(d\bm{\theta}\mid y_{\mathrm{obs}}).

Smaller
p
-values provide stronger evidence against the null hypothesis.

Ppp can be viewed as the posterior mean of the frequentist
p
-value

\displaystyle pr^{m(y\mid\bm{\theta})}[t(Y)\geq t(y_{\mathrm{obs}})],\quad m(y\mid\bm{\theta})=p_{\bm{\theta}}(y).

This perspective offers a natural and intuitive interpretation, making ppp appealing and accessible to practitioners. Structurally, ppp resembles the prior predictive
p
-value introduced by
Box 1980
where the prior predictive distribution is used as the reference distribution:

\displaystyle pr^{m(y)}[t(Y)\geq t(y_{\mathrm{obs}})],\quad m(y)=\int_{\Theta}p_{\bm{\theta}}(y)\pi(d\bm{\theta}).

However, unlike the prior predictive
p
-value which requires a proper prior, ppp only requires the posterior to be proper. This allows for the use of improper priors, which is advantageous when prior information is limited or unavailable. In practice, ppp is computationally efficient and easily integrates into the Bayesian workflow. Estimation typically proceeds via:

1.

Sampling from the posterior distribution of
\bm{\theta}
;

2.

Generating posterior predictive datasets;

3.

Computing test statistics on both the observed and the posterior predictive datasets.

Among these steps, posterior sampling is generally the most computationally demanding but is required for Bayesian inference regardless. The remaining steps are relatively straightforward for most parametric models, making ppp a practical choice for assessing model fit.

Despite its popularity, ppp is known to be
conservative
for many test statistics, i.e., its null distribution is more concentrated around 1/2 than a uniform. This arises from a ”double use” of data – the same data is used both to estimate the posterior distribution and to assess model discrepancy. As a result, ppp tends to produce systematically larger
p
-values, which reduces its power to detect model misspecification.

Ideally, we seek
p
-values that are uniformly distributed under the null. We refer to such
p
-values are
well-calibrated
, as they can be properly interpreted as the probability of obtaining data at least as extreme as the observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. However, under composite hypotheses such as
H
, exact well-calibration across all parameter values under the null is generally unattainable. Instead, we aim for a weaker property,
asymptotic well-calibration
, which requires the
p
-value to converge in distribution to uniform as the sample size
n
increases.
P
-values that are asymptotically well-calibrated typically also behave well in finite samples.

Well-calibration is fundamentally a frequentist concept. Bayesians with subjective prior information might not be concerned with well-calibration under all parameter values under a composite null. However, in the absence of strong prior information, Bayesians often adopt reference priors to maximize learning from the data, or use weak, convenient priors for practicality. In such cases where the prior primarily facilitates analysis rather than encodes subjective beliefs, well-calibration remains important even for Bayesians, as it ensures the reliability of the
p
-value under any reasonable prior. This is the setting we focus on in this article.

Test statistics play an important role in the behaviors of the
p
-values. Without concrete alternative models for comparison, the
modified Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test
is a popular test statistic which provides global assessment of data-model compatibility for real-valued observations. Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}\sim P_{\bm{\theta}}
be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) real-valued random variables. The KS test quantifies the discrepancy between the empirical distribution of the sample and
P_{\bm{\theta}}
. It is defined as
\sqrt{n}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert P_{n}(y)-P_{\bm{\theta}}(y)\rvert
, where
P_{n}
denotes the empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF). Replacing
\bm{\theta}
with an estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
yields the modified KS statistic

\displaystyle T_{n}=\sqrt{n}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert P_{n}(y)-P_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}(y)\rvert.

The scaling by
\sqrt{n}
ensures that under proper assumptions,
T_{n}
converges in distribution to a continuous random variable, rather than a point mass at zero.

Asymptotic properties of ppp under
T_{n}
, denoted as ppp(KS), remain unexplored.
Robins et al. 2000
studied the asymptotic behaviors of a number of
p
-value procedures for a class of asymptotically normal test statistics. They showed that ppp is generally asymptotically conservative, but achieves asymptotic well-calibration if the asymptotic mean of the test statistic is constant in
\bm{\theta}
. These theories provide valuable guidance on the use of ppp, but they do not extend to test statistics that are not asymptotically normal. Under the null,
T_{n}
converges in distribution to the supremum of a mean-zero gaussian process
(
Van der Vaart 2000
)
, hence it is not asymptotically normal, and not covered by results of
Robins et al. 2000

In this work, we establish that under regularity conditions, ppp(KS) is asymptotically well-calibrated. In Section
2
, we present the main theorem and the proof. We show that a sufficient condition for ppp to be asymptotically well-calibrated is to have the test statistic converge in distribution to a continuous random variable under contiguous alternatives
P_{\bm{\theta}_{n}}
, for any
\bm{\theta}_{n}=\bm{\theta}_{0}+O(\bm{1}/\sqrt{n})
. We apply Le Cam’s third lemma to show that this condition holds for
\sqrt{n}(P_{n}(y)-P_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}(y))
for any single
y
. We then use empirical process theory to extend this result uniformly in
y
to establish the condition for the modified KS test. In Section
3
, we use numerical experiments to study the finite-sample behaviors of ppp(KS). We demonstrate that it is well-behaved under the null, and generally exhibits good power against a variety of alternative models. We conclude with discussions in Section
4
.

2
Asymptotic well-calibration

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}

(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))

\mathcal{B}

P_{\bm{\theta}}

\bm{\theta}

{\kern 0.0pt\Theta}^{\mathrm{o}}

A1.

p_{\bm{\theta}}(y)

\bm{\theta}
for every

\bm{\theta}\mapsto P_{\bm{\theta}}
from
\mathbb{R}^{k}

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}}\lvert P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{h}}(y)-P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y)-A_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\mathbf{h})(y)\rvert=o(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert)\quad\text{as }\mathbf{h}\to\bm{0}.

(1)

A2.

There exists
\epsilon>0
such that
E_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}[\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}\lVert s(\bm{\theta})\rVert]<\infty
, where
B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{0})
denotes the

\bm{\theta}_{0}
.

A3.

Elements of the Fisher information matrix
I(\bm{\theta})=E_{\bm{\theta}}[s(\bm{\theta})s(\bm{\theta})^{\top}]
are well-defined and continuous in
\bm{\theta}
, and
I(\bm{\theta}_{0})
is non-singular.

A4.

Let
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{M}
denote the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE),
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}^{M}-\bm{\theta}_{0})=O_{P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}(1)
.

A5.

Let
\lVert P-Q\rVert
denote the total variation distance between distributions
P
and
Q
,
\mathbf{Y}_{n}=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
denote the data vector, and
N({\mu},\Sigma)
denote normal distribution with parameters
{\mu}
and
\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\lVert\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{Y}_{n})-N(\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{M}_{n},I(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}/n)\rVert\overset{P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}{\to}0.

A6.

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}
admits the following expansion:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}=\bm{\theta}_{0}+\frac{1}{n}I(\bm{\theta}_{0})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{0})+o_{P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}}(\bm{1}_{k}/\sqrt{n}).

Theorem 1
.

Under assumptions
A1
-
A6
, ppp(KS) is asymptotically well-calibrated.

Remark 1
.

A1
specifies the required differentiability conditions. These are typically satisfied by continuous distributions from the exponential family. The key requirements are:

1.

Fréchet differentiability, for results on the asymptotic distribution of
\sqrt{n}(P_{n}-P_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}})
(Theorem 19.23,
Van der Vaart 2000
).

2.

Quadratic mean differentiability (QMD) of the null model at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, for local asymptotic normality to invoke Le Cam’s third lemma.

While it is easy to check for differentiability of
p_{\bm{\theta}}(y)
, it is not as straightforward to verify Fréchet differentiability. If
P_{\bm{\theta}}
is twice differentiable in
\bm{\theta}
, let

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{a}(y)=\frac{\partial P_{\bm{\theta}}(y)}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=a}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times k},\quad\ddot{P}_{a}(y)=\frac{\partial^{2}P_{\bm{\theta}}}{\partial\bm{\theta}\partial\bm{\theta}^{\top}}\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=a}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}

denote the Jacobian and Hessian of
P_{\bm{\theta}}
at
a
. The following are sufficient conditions for
P_{\bm{\theta}}
to be Fréchet differentiable at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, with Fréchet derivative
A_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(\mathbf{h})(y)=\dot{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y)\mathbf{h}
:

(a)

There exists
M_{1}>0
such that
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert\dot{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y)\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}\leq M_{1}
, where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}
denotes the maximum norm, and
\lVert\mathbf{B}\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}=\max_{i,j}\lvert\mathbf{B}_{ij}\rvert
for any matrix
\mathbf{B}
. This guarantees
A_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}
to be a bounded linear map because for all
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert\dot{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y)\mathbf{h}\rvert\leq M_{1}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_{\infty}
, and

(b)

there exists
\epsilon>0
,
M_{2}>0
such that
\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert\ddot{P}_{\bm{\theta}}(y)\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}\leq M_{2}
.
This is sufficient for equation (
1
) because by Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{h}}(y)-P_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y)-\dot{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y)\mathbf{h}\rvert\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert\mathbf{h}^{\top}\ddot{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{y}}(y)\mathbf{h}\rvert\leq M_{2}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^{2}=o(\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert),

where
\bm{\theta}_{y}=t_{y}\bm{\theta}_{0}+(1-t_{y})(\bm{\theta}_{0}+\mathbf{h})
, for some
t_{y}\in[0,1]
.

A sufficient condition for (b) is that there exists a continuous function
g:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert\ddot{P}_{\bm{\theta}}(y)\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\lVert g(\bm{\theta})\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}.

This is because continuity guarantees the existence of
\bm{\theta}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}\lVert g(\bm{\theta})\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}
so that
M_{2}
can be set to
\lVert g(\bm{\theta}^{*})\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}
. Similarly, a sufficient condition for (a) is to have a function
h:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{1\times k}
, such that
\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert\dot{P}_{\bm{\theta}}(y)\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\lVert h(\bm{\theta})\rVert_{\mathrm{max}}
.

Remark 2
.

A2
ensures
s(\bm{\theta})
is locally uniformly integrably bounded at
\bm{\theta}_{0}
, so that we can apply Leibniz’s rule to interchange integral and differentiation below, which is needed in the proof:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{y}s(\bm{\theta}_{0})p_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(u)du=\int_{-\infty}^{y}\frac{\partial P_{\bm{\theta}}(u)}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}du=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\int_{-\infty}^{y}P_{\bm{\theta}}(u)du\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}}=\dot{P}_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}(y).

A sufficient condition for

\displaystyle\lvert s_{j}(\bm{\theta})\rvert\leq g_{j}(\bm{\theta})+h_{j}(Y),\quad E_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}[h_{j}(Y)]<\infty.

(2)

This is because by continuity of

\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*}=\argmax_{\bm{\theta}\in\bar{B}_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}g_{j}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle E_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\left[\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\theta}_{0})}\lvert s_{j}(\bm{\theta})\rvert\right]\leq g_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{*})+E_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}[h_{j}(Y)]<\infty,

which guarantees that

A3
states standard regularity conditions on the Fisher information matrix. Overall

and

are mild. They hold for commonly used continuous distributions, including those in the exponential family.

Remark 3
.

A4
and
A5
are regularity conditions for posterior concentration at

.

A6

Next, we outline the intuition behind the proof of the theorem. For any fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}
, the test statistic
Z_{n}(y)=\sqrt{n}(P_{n}(y)-P_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}(y))
converges in distribution to a mean-zero normal random variable. Therefore, by Theorem 1 in
Robins et al. 2000
, ppp is asymptotically well-calibrated under
Z_{n}(y)
for each fixed
y
. The key challenge is to extend this result uniformly overall
y
such that it holds for
T_{n}=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\lvert Z_{n}(y)\rvert

1
-
3
). We verified that
Z_{n}(y)
satisfies these conditions for any fixed
y

\sqrt{n}(P_{n}-P_{\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}})
is asymptotically tight, ensuring the conditions hold uniformly in
y

1
-
3
provide a framework that could potentially extend these results to other test statistics that are not asymptotically normal. We present the lemmas below. See Appendix
A
for proof of the theorem, and Appendix
B

Let

be a test statistic with CDF

, define

L1.

3
Numerical experiments

We used simulation studies to evaluate the finite-sample performance of ppp(KS). Our results demonstrate that it is well-behaved under the null under different priors and estimators, and it has good power against a variety of alternative models.

Consider testing for the null hypothesis
H:Y_{i}\sim
i.i.d.
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
(shape, rate parameterization). Without a specific alternative for comparison, the modified KS test would be a reasonable global measure of discrepancy for model checking. We first studied the null distribution of ppp(KS) with data generated from
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{0}=2,\beta_{0}=5)
. We considered two estimators: the MLE, and the posterior mean, four sample sizes:
n=10,20,100,500
, and two sets of priors:

1.

Good
priors: loosely centerd at the true parameters

•

\pi(\alpha)\sim TN(2.5,16,0,\infty)
, a normal with mean 2.5, variance 16, truncated to the positive part of the real line;

•

\pi(\beta)\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(1,1)
.

2.

Bad
priors: chosen such that
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
are roughly at the 97.5% and 2.5% percentiles respectively

•

\pi(\alpha)\sim TN(1,0.5,0,\infty)
;

•

\pi(\beta)\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(3,1.25)
.

Assumptions
A1
-
A6
hold under this setup, hence Theorem
1
applies, and ppp(KS) is asymptotically well-calibrated. To see why the assumptions are satisfied: For
A1
, continuous differentiability of the density function is obvious. And it can be shown that all elements of the first and second derivatives of the gamma CDF with respect to the parameters can be upper bounded by continuous functions of the parameters, hence the sufficient conditions for Fréchet differentiability discussed in Remark
1
hold; Gamma distribution belongs to the exponential family, and all the standard regularity conditions such as
A2
and
A3
hold; The MLEs are CAN estimators and admit the expansion in
A6
, hence
A4
holds; All the priors we considered are continuous distributions with positive density over the entire parameter space, hence Bernstein-von Mises theorem applies. Consequently,
A5
holds and the posterior means also satisfy the expansion in
A6
. See Appendix
C
for a detailed proof.

For each sample size
n
, we simulated 1,000 datasets using
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
. For each simulated dataset and each prior, we performed Bayesian model fitting using
rstan
to obtain 1,000 posterior samples (1,000 burn-in, followed by 5,000 iterations, thinned every 5 iterations). We then generated posterior predictive datasets, and computed the modified KS test for both the observed and posterior predictive datasets to approximate ppp(KS). See Figure
1
for simulation results.

Figure 1:
Null distributions of ppp(KS) under two priors, two estimators, and four sample sizes.

With a reasonable prior, the null distributions of ppp(KS) closely resemble uniform, even with a sample size as small as
n=10
(see plots 1 and 3). When the priors are misspecified, the posterior means are biased towards the prior, particularly for small sample sizes. This bias increases the discrepancy between the empirical distribution of the data and the fitted distribution, resulting in larger values of the modified KS test. This discrepancy is more pronounced for the observed data than for the posterior predictive datasets, which leads to smaller
p
-values, as illustrated in plot 2. However, as
n
increases to 100, these effects are largely washed away by the data. With
n=500
, the null distributions of ppp(KS) are again approximately uniform.

Using frequentist estimators such as the MLE in the modified KS test may offer advantages over Bayesian estimators like the posterior mean. If the MLE can be easily estimated, it eliminates the need for Bayesian model fitting on the posterior predictive datasets, hence is more computationally efficient. Additionally, as shown in the last plot of Figure
1
, MLE is not affected by poor priors, which makes the results more robust to prior misspecification. For complex problems where the MLE is difficult to compute reliably, Bayesian estimators remains a viable alternative.

We considered several alternative models to study the power of ppp(KS):

1.

Weibull model with shape =
\alpha_{0}
, and scale =
1/\beta_{0}
.

2.

Lognormal model with
\mu=0
,
\sigma=0.5
.

3.

Gamma generalized linear model (GLM),

\displaystyle Y_{i}\mid x_{i}\sim Gamma\left(\alpha_{0},\frac{\alpha_{0}}{x_{i}\theta_{0}+\alpha_{0}/\beta_{0}}\right),

(4)

with
\theta_{0}=0.5
, and
x_{i}
’s are known fixed covariates generated from lognormal with
\mu=0.5
,
\sigma=1
. Here the mean of
Y_{i}
is
x_{i}\theta_{0}+\alpha_{0}/\beta_{0}
instead of
\alpha_{0}/\beta_{0}
under the gamma model.

We considered two other test statistics for comparison. Let
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\hat{\beta}_{n}
denote estimators for
\alpha
and
\beta
, which can be the MLE or the posterior mean:

1.

Chi-squared test, another popular omnibus test. Under
H
, it is defined as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(Y_{i}-\hat{\alpha}_{n}/\hat{\beta}_{n})^{2}}{\hat{\alpha}_{n}/\hat{\beta}_{n}^{2}}.

Deviations from the null model are indicated by extreme values in either direction. Consequently, the corresponding
p
-value is two-sided, with both small and large values providing evidence against the null.

2.

Score test under the gamma GLM. The GLM in (
4
) is a larger parametric model
P_{\alpha,\beta,\theta}
which contains the null model
P_{\alpha,\beta}
, and we recover
H
when
\theta=0
. In this situation,
Robins et al. 2000
and
Wang and Xu 2021
showed that the following score test exhibits particularly good power against the larger model:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\frac{\partial\log f(\mathbf{Y}_{n};\hat{\alpha}_{n},\hat{\beta}_{n},\theta)}{\partial\theta}\Bigg|_{\theta=0}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{n}^{2}}{\hat{\alpha}_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}Y_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}.

Larger values suggest greater discrepancy between the null and the data.

We used the good priors and
n=100
for simulations to compare the performances of ppp under these three test statistics across different models. Figure
2
presents results with the posterior mean as the plug-in estimator. Results with the MLE are very similar, hence omitted here.

The first plot displays the null distributions, all of which are approximately uniform, indicating that ppp is well-behaved under all three test statistics. Under alternative models, we seek
p
-values with high power, which translates to right-skewed distributions under the modified KS test or the score test, and either right-skewed or left-skewed distributions under the chi-squared test. The second plot presents results under the gamma GLM. While all three test statistics are able to detect model misspecification, the score test is the most powerful as it is designed for this alternative. However, it lacks sensitivity to other types of alternatives as illustrated in the last two plots. In contrast, both the modified KS test and the chi-squared test are more versatile, effectively identifying a broader range of alternative models.

4
Discussion

The posterior predictive
p
-value is a convenient tool for Bayesian model evaluation, but its conservatism has raised concerns among practitioners.
Robins et al. 2000
showed that ppp can be asymptotically well-calibrated for a class of asymptotically normal test statistics, provided the asymptotic mean of the test statistic is constant in the parameter
\bm{\theta}
. Our work extends this theory to the modified KS test, a versatile omnibus goodness-of-fit test. Unlike the test statistics considered by
Robins et al. 2000
, the modified KS test is not asymptotically normal; instead, it converges in distribution to the supremum of a mean-zero gaussian process under the null. We leverage its connection to asymptotic normality to establish the asymptotic well-calibration of ppp(KS) through Le Cam’s third lemma and empirical process theory. Moreover, our Lemmas
1
-
3
provide a pathway for potentially extending these results to other test statistics that are not asymptotically normal. We further used numerical experiments to show that ppp(KS) is well-behaved under finite sample, and that it is versatile to detect a variety of alternative models.

The classical KS test is only applicable to i.i.d. random variables. For more complex settings such as regression models where the
Y_{i}
’s are independent but not identically distributed, or for multivariate data
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, a generalized modified KS test statistic
\tilde{T}_{n}
can be used:

\displaystyle\tilde{T}_{n}=\sqrt{n}\sup_{y\in[0,1]}\lvert y-F_{n,\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}(y)\rvert,\quad F_{n,\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1(P_{i,\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{n}}(Y_{i})\leq y),

where
P_{i,\bm{\theta}}
is the CDF for
Y_{i}
which may depend on covariates
x_{i}
. A potential direction for future research is to extend our theory to this setting.

Over the years, several alternative Bayesian
p
-value procedures have been developed to address the conservatism issue of ppp.
Bayarri and Berger 2000
introduced the conditional predictive
p
-value and the partial posterior predictive
p
-value which are asymptotically well-calibrated, as demonstrated by
Robins et al. 2000
;
Hjort et al. 2006
proposed calibrating ppp using the prior predictive distribution, while
Wang and Xu 2021
suggested calibration with the posterior predictive distribution to retain asymptotic uniformity; More recently,
Moran et al. 2019
and
Li and Huggins 2022
independently proposed using separate subsets of the data to compute the posterior and measure model discrepancy, addressing the double-dipping issue of ppp. While these alternative procedures often exhibit better properties in terms of well-calibration and power, they typically come at the cost of ease of use, increased computation time, or reduced efficiency in utilizing information. With this note, we highlight the important role that test statistics play in the properties of
p
-values. For test statistics such as the modified KS test, and those discussed in
Robins et al. 2000
, practitioners can confidently use ppp without resorting to more sophisticated procedures. It remains an active area of research to develop new procedures and characterize test statistics that yield asymptotically well-calibrated Bayesian
p
-values with good power.

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\gamma(\alpha,\beta y)}{\partial\alpha^{2}}=\int_{0}^{\beta y}u^{\alpha-1}(\log u)^{2}e^{-u}du\leq\int_{0}^{\beta y}u^{\alpha+1}e^{-u}du\leq\Gamma(\alpha+2).

Next, for the partial derivative with respect to
\beta
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\beta}=\frac{\partial\gamma(\alpha,\beta y)}{\partial\beta}=\beta^{\alpha-1}x^{\alpha}e^{-\beta y}\leq\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{\beta}e^{-\alpha}.

Here we recognize the kernel of
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha+1,\beta)
, hence we can bound it by plugging in
y=\alpha/\beta
, which is the mode of this unimodal density. Let
\psi(\alpha)
denote the digamma function, the first order partial derivatives of
P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\psi(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\gamma(\alpha,\beta y)+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\beta}.

They can be bounded by the following continuous functions of
(\alpha,\beta)
:

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\alpha}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\psi(\alpha)\rvert+\alpha

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\left\lvert\frac{\partial P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\beta}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{\beta}e^{-\alpha}.

The second order partial derivatives of
P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)
are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\psi(\alpha)^{2}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}-\frac{\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\right)\gamma(\alpha,\beta y)-2\frac{\psi(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\alpha}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\partial^{2}\gamma}{\partial\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\alpha\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\psi(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)}{\partial\beta^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{\partial^{2}\gamma}{\partial\beta^{2}}.

They can be bounded similarly, and we omit the details here. Overall, this shows that the sufficient conditions discussed in Remark
1
hold, therefore
P_{\alpha,\beta}
is Fréchet differentiable.

A2.

To show
A2
holds, we will show that the sufficient condition discussed in Remark
2
is satisfied. The score function

\displaystyle s(\alpha,\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\log\beta-\psi(\alpha)+\log Y\\
\alpha/\beta-Y\end{pmatrix}.

Hence element-wise,

\displaystyle\lvert s(\alpha,\beta)\rvert\leq\begin{pmatrix}\log\beta+\psi(\alpha)+\log Y\\
\alpha/\beta+Y\end{pmatrix},

and both
\log Y
and
Y
are integrable. Therefore
A2
holds.

A3.

Conditions on the Fisher information matrix clearly hold for the gamma distribution.

A4.

Under the gamma distribution, MLEs for
\alpha
and
\beta
are CAN estimators. Therefore
A4
holds.

A5.

The priors we considered are all continuous with positive density on the entire parameter space. Also the parameters of a gamma distribution are identifiable such that for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\inf_{\lVert(\alpha,\beta)-(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\rVert>\epsilon}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\lvert P_{\alpha,\beta}(y)-P_{\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}}(y)\rvert>0.

Therefore Lemma 10.4, and hence Theorem 10.1 the Bernstein-von Mises theorem in
Van der Vaart 2000
holds.

A6.

Both
\log Y
and
Y
are square integrable. Therefore as discussed in Remark
4
, this is sufficient to invoke Theorem 5.39 in
Van der Vaart 2000
to conclude that the MLEs admit the expansion stated in
A6
. By
A5
, the same expansion applies to the posterior means.
