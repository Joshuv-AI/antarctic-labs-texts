Fluent but Culturally Distant: Can Regional Training Teach Cultural Understanding?
Dhruv Agarwal, Anya Shukla, Sunayana Sitaram, Aditya Vashistha

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are used around the world but exhibit Western cultural tendencies. To address this cultural misalignment, many countries have begun developing “regional” LLMs tailored to local communities. Yet it remains unclear whether these models merely speak the language of their users or also reflect their cultural values and practices. Using India as a case study, we evaluate five Indic and five global LLMs along two key dimensions: values (via the Inglehart–Welzel map and GlobalOpinionQA) and practices (via CulturalBench and NormAd). Across all four tasks, we find that Indic models do not align more closely with Indian cultural norms than global models. In fact, an average American person is a better proxy for Indian cultural values than any Indic model. Even prompting strategies fail to meaningfully improve alignment. Ablations show that regional fine-tuning does not enhance cultural competence and may in fact hurt it by impeding recall of existing knowledge. We trace this failure to the scarcity of high-quality, untranslated, and culturally grounded pretraining and fine-tuning data. Our study positions cultural evaluation as a first-class requirement alongside multilingual benchmarks and offers a reusable methodology for developers. We call for deeper investments in culturally representative data to build and evaluate truly sovereign LLMs.



1
Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) now power applications used by millions of users across the world. Despite their global reach, a growing body of work demonstrates that the dominant frontier models systematically encode Western values, beliefs, and knowledge
Cao et al. 2023
;
Johnson et al. 2022
. This cultural skew raises concerns about cultural hegemony, homogenization, and quality-of-service harms for users in non-Western contexts
Qadri et al. 2023
;
Agarwal et al. 2025
.

To mitigate these harms, researchers and practitioners have begun training
regional
language models that are intended for a specific linguistic or geographic community. Recent examples include Nanda, a Hindi LLM aimed at Hindi-speaking users
Choudhury et al. 2025
, and Jais, an LLM for Arabic-speaking communities
Sengupta et al. 2023
. These models are typically trained on large corpora of local-language text and evaluated on standard NLP benchmarks that assess reasoning, classification, generation, and summarization capabilities in target languages
Ahuja et al. 2023
.

Multilingual benchmarks, however, primarily test
linguistic fluency
and
language understanding
. Fluency and understanding alone do not guarantee
cultural competence
: an understanding of the values, norms, and practices embedded in a language community
Havaldar et al. 2023
. Consequently, even regionally trained models can generate culturally incongruent outputs. For instance, an Arabic model associated prayer with alcohol consumption, which is at odds with norms in Arabic-speaking societies
Naous et al. 2024
.

Although multilingual evaluation is now standard for regional models, no prior work has systematically assessed whether such models are culturally appropriate for the audiences they purport to serve. We ask:
Does training LLMs on local-language data endow them with the cultural competence required to serve their intended users?
Insights from this question can inform the design of future multilingual and multicultural training regimes.

We address this question through a detailed case study of India as a non-Western culture. We compare several
Indic
LLMs—models explicitly fine-tuned on Indian languages—with global foundation models such as the GPT, Llama, and Gemma series. India offers an ideal setting because (i) the rapid proliferation of Indic models provides a diverse test-bed, and (ii) Indian cultural norms differ substantially from those typically represented in Western-centric training corpora.

We evaluate models along two key dimensions of cultural competence outlined by Hofstede’s cultural onion
Hofstede 1991
:
values
and
practices
. To probe values, we use the Inglehart–Welzel cultural map
Inglehart and Welzel 2005
and GlobalOpinionQA
Durmus et al. 2023
. To assess practices, we use CulturalBench
Chiu et al. 2024
, which tests factual knowledge of cultural norms, and NormAd
Rao et al. 2024
, which measures adaptation to social situations.

Across all four tasks, Indic models aligned more closely with US culture than with Indian culture, and regional fine-tuning sometimes
reduced
performance on India-specific questions. We also tried diverse prompting strategies to help the model elicit Indic knowledge, but they provided negligible gains. Overall, an average US respondent is still a better proxy for Indian cultural values than any Indic model we tested. These results call for new training paradigms and benchmarks that center cultural competence alongside linguistic fluency. Our main contributions are:

1.

A multi-dimensional evaluation framework.
We operationalize Hofstede’s cultural onion by curating existing surveys and datasets, enabling joint assessment of values and practices in regional models.

2.

A large-scale empirical study.
We evaluate ten models (five Indic, five global), yielding the first empirical picture of how regional fine-tuning affects cultural alignment.

2
Related Work

From multilingual to
multicultural
NLP.

Early efforts to broaden NLP beyond English focused on increasing
language
coverage in pre-training and evaluation (e.g., XTREME
Hu et al. 2020
and XGLUE
Liang et al. 2020
). Yet a growing body of research argues that linguistic diversity alone is insufficient as LLMs often inherit Western-centric assumptions from their web-scale training data
Cao et al. 2023
;
Johnson et al. 2022
;
Adilazuarda et al. 2024
.
Hershcovich et al. 2022
formalize this distinction, proposing a four-part framework—linguistic form, common ground, aboutness, and values—within which cross-cultural differences can arise. Subsequent work has documented such bias in question answering, sentiment analysis, situated applications, and other tasks across many languages
Ochieng et al. 2024
;
Naous et al. 2024
;
Agarwal et al. 2025
.

Measuring cultural knowledge and values.

Several benchmarks probe whether LLMs encode culturally-grounded knowledge
Chiu et al. 2024
;
Fung et al. 2024
;
Huang and Yang 2023
. For example, datasets such as CultureBank
Shi et al. 2024
curate questions about food, historical events, and etiquette. Recent work extends this to
adaptability
: for example, NormAd
Rao et al. 2024
tests whether models can apply cultural knowledge to social scenarios. A complementary direction evaluates alignment of models with national
value systems

Arora et al. 2023
;
Masoud et al. 2025
.
For example,
Tao et al. 2024
find a strong Anglo-Protestant drift in GPT-family models. Recent multilingual vision–language benchmarks such as M5
(
Schneider and Sitaram 2024
)
extend these assessments to multimodal settings.

Efforts towards cultural alignment.

In light of these shortcomings, researchers have explored interventions to improve cultural alignment. Simple prompting strategies (e.g., inserting cultural identity cues) yield limited gains
Mukherjee et al. 2024
, while post-training on culturally representative data has shown more promise
Li et al. 2024
. With growing commercial interest in AI, companies have also started building regional LLMs such as Sarvam in India, Jais in the UAE, Sakana in Japan, etc. Yet despite their regional positioning, these models are evaluated only on multilingual benchmarks that test fluency but not cultural performance (e.g., see technical reports
Choudhury et al. 2025
;
Kallappa et al. 2025
).

3
Methodology

3.1
Tasks and Datasets

In his
cultural onion
framework,
Hofstede 1991
categorized culture into two main dimensions:
values
and
practices
. We probe each dimension using two tasks, yielding a total of four tasks in our study.

3.1.1
Values

Value Orientation (Cultural Map).

The World Values Survey (WVS) is a large-scale, cross-national study that administers over 600 questions to participants in 120 countries every five years to examine global variation in human values
Haerpfer et al. 2022
.
Inglehart and Welzel distilled ten of these questions into two latent dimensions:
traditional vs. secular-rational
and
survival vs. self-expression

Inglehart and Welzel 2005
. These two dimensions form the axes of a cultural map that positions each country within a shared value space. An example is shown in Figure
5
in Appendix
A
. This framework allows us to place models in the same cultural space as human societies, allowing us to assess their proximity to various cultural groups. Following
Tao et al. 2024
, we administer these ten items to each model and locate the model on the cultural map.

Opinion Alignment.

While the cultural map reveals a model’s average value orientation, this task assesses how well a model reflects the
distribution
of opinions in a population.
We use GlobalOpinionQA
Durmus et al. 2023
, a QA dataset constructed from the Pew Global Attitudes Survey. Each question is annotated with human response distributions from various countries. An example question from the dataset is shown in Table
2
in Appendix
B
. We filter the dataset to include 94 questions where Indian and American responses disagree moderately (Jensen-Shannon similarity
\leq 0.6
). For each question, we compute the similarity between a model’s predicted distribution and the distributions from both countries to quantify which country’s distribution the model better reflects.

3.1.2
Practices

Cultural Knowledge.

CulturalBench
Chiu et al. 2024
is a QA benchmark dataset designed to evaluate factual cultural knowledge. It consists of 1227 questions about customs, language, and behaviors from 45 global regions. Each question is verified by five human annotators. An example question in the dataset is:
What do Singaporeans often append at the end of their sentences to show a form of exasperation? A. yah B. yo C. eh D. leh.

We filter this dataset to include 46 India-specific and 20 US-specific questions. Each model’s accuracy is measured on both sets.

Cultural Adaptation.

While CulturalBench directly probes raw cultural knowledge in a model, NormAd
Rao et al. 2024
tests a model’s ability to apply cultural knowledge in social etiquette scenarios.
An example question is:
Sarah went to her friend’s home for dinner. When food was served, she started eating with her left hand. Was what she did socially acceptable? Yes, no, neither?

Questions are phrased with three levels of cultural context: (1)
Country
only (e.g., “in India”), (2)
Value+Country
(e.g., “in India, where there is respect for hygiene in dining”), and (3)
Rule-of-Thumb
, which makes norms explicit (e.g., “avoid eating with your left hand”). The
Country
context requires the most socio-cultural reasoning, whereas RoT reduces to a mere entailment task. We use 29 Indian and 49 American-context questions.

3.3
Evaluation Methodology

Prompting Strategies.

To provide models a fair chance at evoking Indian values, we use four prompting strategies based on prior work
Adilazuarda et al. 2024
;
Durmus et al. 2023
:

1.

Default
: Ask the question in English, as originally written.

2.

Demographic
: Add a system prompt like “You are an average person in
x
country.”

3.

Cross-lingual
: Translate the question to Hindi, keeping instructions in English.

4.

Hindi
: Translate both question and instructions to Hindi (dropped later due to poor instruction-following).

All prompts are zero-shot. Hindi translations were done using machine translation (using GPT-4o).

Decoding.

To reduce invalid outputs from models with brittle instruction-following capabilities, we used random sampling with a temperature of 0.3 instead of greedy decoding. To account for this randomness, we repeat each prompt multiple times and average across trials (details below). Experiments were run on Google Cloud Platform using a combination of Nvidia T4 and A100 GPUs.

Cultural Map.

We ask each of the 10 WVS questions using ten distinct system prompts from
Tao et al. 2024
(e.g., ‘‘You are an average human being responding to the following survey question.’’), and sample ten responses per prompt to account for decoding variability, resulting in 100 responses per question. To ensure valid outputs, we also appended an instruction to prevent the model from abstaining and, for models with poor instruction-following, added explicit formatting instructions.
2
2

2

We modified prompts only for this task, where a valid response to every question is necessary for plotting the map. In all other experiments, we used a uniform prompt across models and treated invalid responses as incorrect.

GlobalOpinionQA.

We follow the methodology of
Durmus et al. 2023
to measure how closely a model’s opinion distribution aligns with that of Indian and American respondents.

Let
Q={q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}
be the set of questions and
O_{q}={o_{1},\ldots,o_{k}}
the answer options for question
q\in Q
. Let
P_{c}(o_{i}\mid q)
denote the probability that respondents from country
c
select option
o_{i}
, as annotated in the dataset. We filtered the dataset to retain only those questions for which the Indian and US response distributions differ moderately, i.e.,
\operatorname{Sim}(\text{India},\text{USA})\leq 0.6
, where
\operatorname{Sim}
is
1-
Jensen–Shannon distance.

For each model
m
and question
q
, we compute the model’s answer distribution
P_{m}(o_{i}\mid q)
by biasing the logits toward the valid options and applying a softmax. We then compute
\operatorname{Sim}_{q}(m,c)
, the similarity between the model’s distribution and that of country
c\in\{\text{India},\text{USA}\}
.
Finally, we define the
Cultural Alignment Differential (CAD)
as:

\operatorname{CAD}_{q,m}=\operatorname{Sim}_{q}(m,\text{India})-\operatorname{Sim}_{q}(m,\text{USA})

(1)

A positive CAD indicates greater alignment with Indian responses, while a negative value indicates greater alignment with US responses. We report the average CAD for each model computed over all filtered questions. To control for position bias, we present each question using all possible answer option permutations (up to 24 per question) and average the results across these permutations.

CulturalBench

We use the prompt template provided by
Chiu et al. 2024
and report accuracy on India and US questions. We repeat each question for all possible answer option permutations (
4!=24
) and five trials for each permutation, resulting in
24\times 5=120
runs per question.

NormAd

We ask each question under the three of context levels (Country, Value+Country, and Rule-of-Thumb) using the prompt template provided by
Rao et al. 2024
. We report accuracy on each context for Indian and American norms. For each context, we run
3!=6
answer permutations, repeated 5 times, resulting in 30 total runs per question per context.

5
Discussion

Our central question was whether region-specific training produces language models that are
culturally
and not merely
linguistically
aligned with their target community. Across four complementary evaluations, the answer is consistently negative. Indic models fluently
speak
Indian languages, yet they reason, express opinions, and recall cultural facts much like Western-centric models, and sometimes
worse
. Below, we unpack the broader implications, diagnose likely causes, and chart a path forward.

Why evaluate “small” regional models at all?

One might argue that current regional LLMs are too small (7–12B parameters) and far less capable than frontier models to be relevant. Why, then, should we evaluate these models? However, two factors make this evaluation not just necessary, but urgent. First, these models are explicitly marketed as culturally grounded, designed to serve regional user bases, yet are evaluated only on linguistic tasks. Our results make a clear case that regional language fluency
\neq
cultural competence, and we hope this work encourages developers to treat cultural evaluation as a first-class concern. Second, these early regional models are prototypes — they form the foundation for much larger investments. For example, companies like Sarvam (India), Sakana (Japan), and others worldwide are already building frontier-scale sovereign models. Before pouring resources into scaling, it is imperative to ask whether these prototypes have succeeded at their core promise. It is prudent to detect cultural brittleness
before
building frontier-scale versions which could fossilise the same biases.

Training data and sovereign models

In our results, ablations against Indic models’ base checkpoints show that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on local-language data leaves cultural knowledge unchanged or degraded (Table
1
). This suggests that fine-tuning may obscure subtle cultural priors that the base models might have acquired.

Technical reports reveal that almost every Indic model relies on instruction sets that are either translated from English or tiny compared to the Western-centric pre-training data they attempt to counterbalance. For example,
nanda-10b
,
airavata-7b
, and
gajendra-7b
machine-translate English instruction datasets into Hindi or other Indic languages, with limited native Hindi corpora (e.g., 6k Hindi WikiHow articles.
aya-expanse-8b
includes a sizeable, genuinely multilingual corus, yet it tilts US-centric in our evaluation.
krutrim-2-12b
seems to be pre-trained from scratch by an Indian company, yet remains strongly US-leaning.

Thus, we hypothesize that the bottleneck lies not in model architecture or instruction tuning, but in the imbalance of pretraining and fine-tuning data. Web-scale corpora are overwhelmingly Western, and fine-tuning on a thin slice of translated regional languages does not offset that prior. We caution model developers that building “sovereign” models is guided by Richard Sutton’s
bitter lesson
: more data usually beats clever algorithms.

Culturally aware for
whom
?

Both global and regional models face the challenge of pluralism: culture is plural both across the world and even within a nation-state. Multiple, often conflicting, cultural norms coexist within the same geography. This raises the question, who should models represent? Rather than prescribing a fixed cultural identity, we argue that
adaptability
should be the goal
Saha et al. 2025
. Just as humans infer each other’s backgrounds from subtle cues, future work should measure whether a model can infer and adjust to cultural cues embedded in a user’s prompts (e.g., honorifics, code-switch patterns). This interacts with research on personalization, but inferring and adapting to a user’s cultural background is useful to fill the gaps in available data about the user.

The case for expansive, community-rooted cultural benchmarks.

If we are to build culturally resonant models, we must be able to evaluate them. All four benchmarks we used together cover only 46 Indian knowledge questions, 29 etiquette vignettes, and 94 value items. Cultural benchmarks are limited in size and scope, even for India, one of the more represented non-Western regions. Building the next generation of cultural evaluations is a participatory data-collection challenge: partnering with local scholars, NGOs, and crowd communities (e.g., Karya
6
6

6

https://www.karya.in
) to elicit situated norms and perspectives that never reach digital platforms. Culturally-aware AI, at its heart, is a race to reach people in their situated, lived realities.

6
Conclusion

Regional language fluency does not imply cultural competence. Across four evaluations, five Indic LLMs failed to align better with Indian values and practices than global models, and sometimes performed worse. Prompting offered little to no improvement, highlighting the limits of small-scale or translated local data in overcoming Western pretraining bias. Our findings position cultural alignment as a core evaluation goal, not an optional add-on. Building truly sovereign LLMs will require large, high-quality,
untranslated
regional corpora and training objectives that prioritize cultural grounding alongside linguistic ability. Without such efforts, regional models risk replicating the very biases they aim to avoid.

Limitations

Our analysis is constrained by the availability of regional models. DeepSeek is the only frontier-scale sovereign LLM not trained in the West, but no such model exists yet for India that we could add to our evaluation set. The cultural benchmarks used, though multiple, are limited in size. We mitigated this through multiple experiments and prompting strategies, but future work would benefit from larger datasets and longitudinal training checkpoints to better isolate when and where cultural knowledge degrades. We do not foresee any risks of this work; in fact, this work is part of a larger effort to build culturally-aware models that can serve users from diverse backgrounds equitably.
