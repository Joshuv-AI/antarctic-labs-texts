The Use of In Vivo Reflectance Confocal Microscopy for the Diagnosis and Classification of Melasma
Authors: Nilay Duman, Banu Yaman, Göktürk Oraloğlu, Işıl Karaarslan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
In recent years, in vivo reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) draws attention as a noninvasive method to precisely detect abnormal pigment deposits, providing additional cytological details for the diagnosis, classification, and treatment monitoring of melasma. This article aims to review the RCM findings of melasma and classify melasma with RCM findings.

Introduction

Melasma is a common acquired pigmentary disease caused by abnormal deposition of melanin in the skin, often occurring in women with Fitzpatrick skin Phototype 3-5.[1] Although the etiopathogenesis is not completely understood, pregnancy, heavy use of birth control pills, prolonged sun exposure, and the use of certain drugs such as tetracycline or antimalarial drugs are risk factors for the development of melasma.[1] It is most commonly seen on the face and occasionally on the neck and forearms. Both increased melanocyte count and melanin production cause excessive pigmentation in melasma.[1]

Melasma is difficult to treat and evaluation of the anatomical location of the affected area, skin phototype, age, disease duration, mode of onset, and possible aggravating factors are important for effective treatment.[2] The choice of the best therapeutic approach for melasma lesions depends on determining the distribution and depth of melanin deposition.[3] Until recent years, Wood’s light has been used frequently for melasma classification and treatment follow-up. Under Wood’s light, melasma is classically classified into three main types; epidermal type (deposition of melanin in the epidermal layers), dermal type (presence of melanin and melanophages in the dermis), and mixed type (combination of the first two types).[456] However, numerous studies have shown that Wood’s light may not be accurate enough to identify the location of pigment deposition. Discordance has been shown between Wood’s light and histology in comparative studies.[78] Therefore, new methods of correlation with histology are needed for classification. In this context, reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) is a promising noninvasive method to precisely detect abnormal pigment deposits, providing additional cytological details for diagnosis, classification, and treatment monitoring in melasma patients.[39]

In Vivo Reflectance Confocal Microscopy Findings of Melasma

RCM is a noninvasive imaging method that allows in vivo imaging and microscopic analysis of tissue with high resolution and contrast without dissecting the tissue.[10]

RCM enables in vivo microscopic analysis of the skin, providing real-time imaging of the epidermis and upper dermis to a depth of 200-300 μm with near histological resolution.[11] In RCM, images are obtained by detecting individual backscattered photons from the optical section, and the contrast is caused by differences in the refractive indices and sizes of the cell organelles.[1213]

The strong signal and bright contrast observed in RCM are due to keratin, collagen, or melanin.[12] This is why RCM is particularly useful in pigment-related conditions such as hyperpigmentation.[14]

RCM images allow the identification and semi-quantitative evaluation of melanin in the epidermis, basal layer, and upper dermis, and the classification of melasma. In recent years, it has been shown that RCM can be an adjuvant method in the classification of melasma. In addition, RCM can also be used to visualize dynamic skin processes and allows in vivo monitoring of skin lesions after invasive and noninvasive treatments.[15] However, in addition to these capabilities, RCM has some limitations such as the inability to assess the deep dermis, the need for a highly trained and skilled dermatologist or pathologist to interpret imaging, the cost of the device, and the inability to be widely available.

There are few studies in the literature on the analysis of RCM in melasma. According to the RCM findings, melasma is classified into two main types, the epidermal and mixed type, which is in full agreement with histology.[1617] In Liu et al.’s study[16] in which they examined 200 melasma cases with RCM, 143 (71.5%) of 200 cases were classified as epidermal and the remaining 57 (28.5%) were classified as mixed-type. In epidermal-type melasma lesions, more intense melanin density is observed in the epidermis compared to the surrounding normal skin.[18] In addition to this, an increase in dermal melanophages is observed in mixed-type melasma lesions. These findings observed in RCM show a correlation with histology. Unlike Wood’s examination, pure dermal-type melasma was not identified with RCM.[1819] In melasma lesions defined as dermal-type with Wood’s light, there is a significant epidermal pigment in RCM analysis while less dermal pigment is present.[3]

Moreover, RCM analysis is useful in evaluating basal melanocytic activity in melasma patients. Kang et al.[20] showed activated melanocytes in six of 26 patients with melasma with RCM, a finding that could previously only be evaluated with electron microscopy.

In the RCM analysis of melasma lesions, melanin deposition is observed at the suprabasal level and the dermo-epidermal junction.[2122] Increased pigmentation in the epidermis is observed as dense refractory keratinocytes scattered in the spinous layer [Figure 1].[16] The increase in pigment in the epidermis creates the ‘mottled pigmentation’ pattern [Figure 2].[14]

At the dermo-epidermal junction, the junctional keratinocytes and activated melanocytes filled with melanosomes form either prominent bright papillary rings around the dermal papillae or a hyper-refractile cobblestone pattern in the flattened basal layer.[16] In the presence of preserved rete ridge structures, a bright papillary ring structure is observed.[4] Shiny papillary rings at the dermo-epidermal junction constitute the polycyclic papillary contour finding [Figure 3a].[18] This is caused by the elongated epidermis and bridging of the club-shaped cristae. The presence of these findings can be explained by ultraviolet damage, which also plays a role in the etiopathogenesis of melasma. In lesional and perilesional normal skin, dense refractile dermal papillary rings are prominently observed on the forehead and upper lip, while in lesions in the cheek region, they are not usually observed. Especially in cheek lesions, the sudden transition from the spinous layer to the papillary dermis can be observed due to flattened rete ridges. In addition, an increase in the number and activity of melanocytes at the dermo-epidermal junction and dendritic or polymorphic-shaped melanocytes may also be seen in some cases.[16]

In mixed-type melasma lesions, in addition to epidermal and dermo-epidermal junction findings, increased pigmentation and melanophages are observed in the dermis [Figure 4]. Melanophages are observed as round or polygonal bright refractile structures within dermal collagen fibers in the superficial dermis [Figure 3b]. Their distribution within the lesion is heterogeneous. In some cases, solar elastosis findings in the form of less refractile, wavy structures and increased vascular structures in the form of dark, rounded, or tubular structures can also be observed.[20]

Conclusion

In conclusion, RCM has reduced the need for biopsy in melasma. The in vivo evaluation of the entire lesion is an advantage over biopsy.[20] This finding shows that a reliable classification can be made by evaluating the epidermal/dermal melanin pigment in the whole lesion. In addition, RCM analysis is useful in monitoring response to therapy.
