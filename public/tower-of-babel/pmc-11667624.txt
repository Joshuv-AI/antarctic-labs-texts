Application of machine learning and neural network models based on experimental evaluation of dissimilar resistance spot-welded joints between grade 2 titanium alloy and AISI 304 stainless steel
Authors: Marwan T. Mezher, Alejandro Pereira, Rusul Ahmed Shakir, Tomasz Trzepieciński
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The use of a composite welded joint consisting of titanium and austenitic stainless steel metals is evidently a favourable selection for industrial applications employing the resistance spot welding (RSW) operation. Nevertheless, achieving a high-quality welded joint proved challenging owing to the properties of the diverse range of materials' used. To improve the quality of dissimilar welded joints, the welding parameters should be selected precisely. With that in mind, the current research endeavoured to figure out the ideal RSW parameters for a dissimilar resistance spot-welded joint between grade 2 titanium alloy (Ti) and AISI 304 austenitic stainless steel (ASS) with equal and unequal thicknesses of 0.5 and 1 mm. The RSW cases based on the selected thickness were referred to as the following: similar thickness of 1 mm for Ti and ASS as case E, dissimilar thickness of 0.5 mm for Ti and 1 mm for ASS as case F, dissimilar thickness of 0.5 mm for ASS and 1 mm for Ti as case I, and similar thickness of 0.5 mm as case J. Tensile shear force, failure mode, and micro-hardness were the metrics used to assess the dissimilar joint's soundness. The RSW variables utilized in the scope of the present investigation were “welding current, pressure, welding time, squeeze time, holding time, and pulse welding”. Models from gradient boosting, CatBoost, and random forest machine learning (ML) algorithms were used to guarantee an accurate analysis, along with the artificial neural network regressions. Therefore, the ML and artificial neural network (ANN) models were trained using real data collected from 100 experimental RSW samples conducted under different RSW process parameters. Various transfer and training functions were applied with the multilayer perceptron employing the feed-forward-back propagation approach when building the ANN models. Also, for the first time in the RSW field, an estimation of the relative importance of the RSW variables regarding the predicted shear force is presented in the current study. Evaluating the experimental findings demonstrated that the highest shear force was 2.183, 2.589, 1.708, and 1.851 kN for cases E, F, I, and J. The micro-hardness data indicated that the nugget zone had a much higher hardness than the heat-affected zone (HAZ) and base metal (BM) zones, wherein case F showed the peak nugget hardness in comparison to the other cases. The best prediction model was found to be the ANN model when training the conjugate gradient with the Polak-Ribiere updates (Traincgp) training function with the hyperbolic tangent sigmoid transfer function (Tansig) with the mean squared error (MSE) and correlation coefficient (R2) values recorded as 0.01886 and 0.94973, respectively. However, the random forest algorithm gave the second best prediction of the MSE while the CatBoost and gradient boosting algorithms were third and fourth, respectively. The automotive and aerospace sectors are anticipated to benefit from the present research in applications associated with body component production that result in a superior strength-to-weight ratio.

Introduction

The automobile and aerospace industries are deeply concerned about reducing the weight of vehicles and aircraft in light of the present fuel and environmental crises caused by strict government regulations and high gas prices [1]. To achieve this, the structural design of vehicles and aircraft often makes use of lightweight materials, including composites, titanium, magnesium, and aluminium alloys. Using materials that have dissimilar hybrid components allows the use of each material's unique benefits [2].

Composites made of stainless steel and a titanium alloy might be useful in the aircraft and automobile sectors. A combination of high strength and low weight is required, and the properties of these materials fulfil this requirement [1,2]. One promising strategy for reducing spacecraft mass is the partial replacement of steel components with titanium and its alloy [3]. It is essential to weld the steel and titanium. Indeed, the process of welding these metals is challenging due to the generation of brittle structures inside the weld [4]. Titanium alloy and steel welds have been attempted using brazing and pressure welding techniques like impulse pressure diffusion [5], friction welding [6], explosive welding [7], and diffusion bonding [8]. Having said that, there is a lack of published studies on the topic of resistance spot welding (RSW) with these two materials. In addition, other studies have applied the friction welding process to join different aluminium alloys [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

The mechanical performance of dissimilar joints made of commercial titanium and ASS grade 304 with an aluminium insert was conducted by Tu et al. [13]. Their findings demonstrated that even though the shear force was 5.38 kN, the failed joints still experienced interracial failure. Mansor et al. [14] investigated the dissimilar micro-RSW of Ti-6Al-4V and ASS 316L. The results of the tensile shear test indicated that all of the joint samples fractured in an interfacial failure (IF) mode, with the peak shear force for each specimen being 378.25 N. When Li et al. [15] examined the RSW of AA 6061-T6 and commercial titanium, they reported that the weld current significantly influences the tensile shear performance of the joints, while the electrode force and welding time had negligible impacts.

The combination of AISI 304 and commercial titanium was welded by Santhakumari et al. [16] via upset butt-welding. Their results demonstrated that brittle intermetallic phases cause a decrease in elongation at the welded joints. Taufiqurrahman et al. [17] analysed the hardness and shear force of dissimilar ASS 316L/Ti6Al4N RSW joints. Interfacial mode generation remained despite a shear force of 895 N and hardness of 367.3 HV. According to research by Rana et al. [18], friction-stirred welded joints made of AISI 316L/Ti6Al4V could reach a maximum shear force of 554 N/mm. The hardness was improved by 25 % and 15 % with respect to the top plates of AISI 316L and Ti6Al4V, respectively. According to the findings of Chen et al. [19], the joint strength between Ti6Al4V and stainless steel 201 reached a peak value of 150 MPa. Furthermore, the failure of the sample occurred specifically at the intermetallic compound (IMC) layer. In a similar vein, Cheng et al. [20] performed studies using various welding techniques, such as brazing, metal inert gas (MIG), and tungsten inert gas (TIG), to join Ti grade 4 and ASS 304. Fractures were seen at the interface along the IMC layer in all samples, as described. A further investigation conducted by Ref. [21] using the laser welding technique revealed that the fractured joints of Ti/ASS 304 exhibit a similar pattern of brittle failure caused by the development of IMCs at the contact. The friction stir welding procedure of ASS 304/commercial pure Ti was examined by Fazel et al. [22]. The shear strength of 330 N/mm was reported as a maximal record.

Using the pressure joule-heat forge welding method, Shotri et al. [23] examined the joining mechanism of Ti6Al4V/ASS 316L steel rods. It was shown that when the forging force is increased, the deformation behaviour of the dissimilar joints is reduced and the interfacial stresses are increased. The microstructure analysis showed that ASS had α+β phases with refined annealed austenite and extended Ti α-grains. Through pulse laser welding and an electron beam, Adomako et al. [24] found that at the interface of the fractured area of the Ti6Al4V/SS436 joint, there were IMCs such as FeTi and Fe2Ti. Similar to this, FeTi and Fe2Ti IMCs were found within ASS 304L/Ti6AL4V by Kumar et al. [25] during the vacuum diffusion bonding and electrical resistance welding procedures. By using rotating friction and forge load, Balasubramanian et al. [26] and Kumar et al. [27] joined commercial pure Ti and Ti5Al2.5Sn to AISI 304L. The joint strength results ranged from 150 to 270 MPa. Linear friction welding was used by Astarita et al. [28] with a frequency and oscillation amplitude of 2.5 mm at 50 Hz. A high-temperature, uncontrolled flash was seen in the martensitic structures close to the titanium weld contact.

Wang et al. [29] used diffusion welding experiments to join titanium and 17-4 PH stainless steel by varying the temperatures range. They concluded that the maximum shear strength and micro-hardness were 163 MPa and 693 HV. The microstructure development of the interface of CP-Ti and ASS 304 friction stir-welded butt joints was examined by Gotawala et al. [30]. As a result of welding, Ti-Fe IMCs emerged on each side of the titanium/steel contact. Although the interface temperature and the number of IMCs within the weld may decrease with a low tool speed, the failure strain offers undesirable findings. Szwajka et al. [31] analysed the microstructure and the mechanical properties of RSW joints of Ti6Al4V and DP600 steel.

The evaluation of the quality of the RSW joint relies on the concurrent interaction of several factors, including welding current, welding, holding, and squeeze times, welding pressure, and pulse welding. Determining the impact of a one parameter on joint quality while neglecting other factors poses significant challenges in practical applications. Hence, several methodologies such as analytical modelling, analysis of variance (ANOVA), artificial neural networks (ANNs), machine learning (ML), and deep learning approaches, and artificial intelligence are used [32]. ANNs and ML are the most prevalent of the approaches listed, and how they function is well understood. Artificial intelligence (AI) approaches have gained in popularity for addressing welding process issues, with ANNs and ML being the most prominent. The performance of ANNs is influenced by various parameters, including the hidden layer number [33], the training approach [34], the number of neurons in the hidden layers [35], the input data quality [36], the activation functions [37], and the test dataset size.

Using the mean absolute error (MAE), Siamakmanesh et al. [38] used ANNs to forecast the grain size and hardness of friction-stirred Ti joints. The MAE was 4.7731 %. For assessing the residual stresses, Qin et al. [39] applied a convolutional NN model for evaluating the correlation coefficient (R2) and MAE of around 0.95 and 6.8, respectively, for laser-welded ASS 304 joints. Peng et al. [40] suggested a back-propagation NN-based model for residual stress measurement. Using the predictions from an ANN and finite element method (FEM), Baruah et al. [41] assessed the thermal profile of arc-welded samples. Nejad et al. [42] utilized an ANN to forecast the fatigue crack rate and fracture toughness of AA2024-T351 welded joints that were stirred by friction. They executed this by altering the hidden layer neurons number. Another investigation that utilized ANNs and ML to estimate the fatigue life of flash butt joints was conducted by Sheng et al. [43]. With R2, MSE, MRE, and MAE reported as 0.865, 0.134, 0.872, and 0.171, respectively, the results demonstrated outstanding performance.

Martin et al. [44] employed an ANN to assess the effects of AISI 304's RSW aspects on the pitting corrosion behaviour of the material. Their results show that controlling overfitting during training and carefully selecting hidden layer neurons are necessary for effective prediction. Wan et al. [45] found that back propagation outperformed a probabilistic NN for fracture force assessment. In their study, Mezher et al. [46] used both experimental and ANN studies to assess the RSW of grade 2 titanium alloy. Using the results of an FEM and a multilayer perceptron (MLP) approach, Ghafarallahi et al. [47] divided the welding diameter into subsets. The ANN model's weld diameter estimates were much more accurate than those of the FEM model. Arunchai et al. [48] used ANN for RSW to predict the weld quality based on the NN observations. The accuracy of the model's predictions was 95 %. Mezher et al. [49] applied an FEM and experimental investigation to the AISI 316L resistance spot-welded joints. Considering fracture topography characteristics, Macek et al. [50] investigated the fractured surface of maraging steel under low cycle fatigue. The results verified that the fatigue life values using the core material volume were within the scatter band and on the whole fracture surface. Based on reconstructing crack propagation in microstructural details by examination of fracture surface topographies, Kobayashi and Shockey [51] introduced a novel approach for fracture surface analysis called fracture surface topography analysis (FRASTA).

Presenting the above facts demonstrates that there is no such paper on dissimilar Ti grade 2/AISI 304 that has analysed all the RSW parameters simultaneously using ANNs, ML algorithms and experimental tests in the same investigation without omitting any parameter. The RSW parameters include welding current, welding, holding, and squeeze times, pressure, and pulses per weld. In addition, the authors are aware of no prior research that has examined a dissimilar RSW joint based on an equal and unequal thickness of Ti grade 2 and ASS 304 using random forest, CatBoost, and gradient boosting ML algorithms. Besides this, the absence of a mathematical equation for measuring shear force based on an ANN model has driven the authors to present this study. Moreover, the experimental observations evaluated the impact of the aforementioned RSW variables on the shear force, micro-hardness, and failure mechanism.

Shear force and hardness tests

This study used the tensile shear force experiment for analysing the quality of the RSW joints and computing the highest fractured shear force required to break the dissimilar grade 2 Ti/AISI 304 joints. Tensile shear force testing was performed on each sample in cases E, F, I, and J to measure the peak shear force and select the best sample based on the force and displacement data from each experiment. Utilizing a universal testing apparatus capable of a maximum load of up to 50 kN at room temperature and a speed of 5 mm/min, the shear force was assessed. Prediction models

In the current investigation, different regressions such as ANNs, random forest, CatBoost, and gradient boosting were applied to evaluate the prediction performance.

ANN modelling

The neural network notion originated from McCulloch and Pitts's investigation in the 1940s. Essentially, they hypothesized that ANNs were capable of computing numerous mathematical and logical calculations. Tens of thousands of academics across disciplines have recently focused on neural networks. In general, it is clear that neural networks are interconnected with every scientific discipline. Healthcare, aerospace, defence, the arts, cinematography, music, and a host of industrial technologies are among the many fields that embraced ANNs. Neural networks enhance their performance via data learning rather than relying on presumptions while creating their model. A common thought is that neural networks are simplified models of the way the brain really processes neural input [53,54]. The neural network method has various uses, but one is training a model to accurately predict process dynamics using input and output data [55,56].

Welding operations are one example of an area where this approach excels, given that it is exceedingly challenging to acquire a comprehensive knowledge of physical mechanics. With the use of training data, a neural network is able to pick up new capabilities. The input variables are the first step in the process, and the last step is to compare the forecasted and actual outputs. The network then adjusts the value of the linkages within the layers. The operation is halted when the network generates favourable outcomes for the training set. Applying a validation set that is excluded from the training set to assess the network is another way to enhance network convergence.

Multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network

A network's topology consists of its components' structure, connections, inputs, and outputs. An ANN's topology may be defined by its input and output layer numbers, the neuron numbers within every layer, and the types of transfer functions that connect them [57]. The structure of an ANN typically includes an input layer, an output layer, and at least one hidden layer. Each layer of the network comprises numerous neurons. The neurons number in the input layer is equivalent to the number of input variables, whereas the neuron number in the output layer corresponds to the output number linked with each input. The transfer function [58] enables the neurons in the layers to transmit weight both forwards and backwards between the layers. This research used a back-propagation learning technique to train an ANN model employing an MLP architecture. As stated in Equation (1), the MLP is defined as follows:(1)y=f(net)=(∑i=1nwix+b)where:

Training a neural network on a dataset involves optimizing the network's parameters to generate an accurate prediction map. Training functions are just another name for the many optimization techniques available. In order to teach a network to recognize and respond to a given input, an algorithm known as the training function must be implemented. The training performance is affected by a number of factors, including the target performance, the trained data set, and the biases and weights. Defining a fitting training function for the network is one obstacle to creating quick, accurate, and excellent predictions. Data distribution

Predicting the outcome of welded joints may be done by using the outcomes of the RSW joints as objectives. Training, validation, and test datasets are necessary partitions created from actual data. Creating separate datasets for training and testing has the potential to significantly impact the accuracy of any model. Shahin et al. [59] hold the view that the ratio of data from various subsets does not seem to have any clear relationship. Zhang et al. [60] state that the dividing ratio is a big difficulty with datasets and that, generally speaking, there is not yet a solution for this problem. In accordance with [61,62], 70 % of the data was allocated for training purposes, 15 % assigned for testing and 15 % for validation in their research. The present study used a dividing ratio of 80 % for training data and 20 % for testing data, which was kept for final testing. The 80 % of data that was used for the training stage was divided into: 70 % for training, 15 % for testing, and 15 % for validation. The actual data for the RSW joints was collected from 100 samples taken from the experiments, and this served as the data for training and testing.

Gradient boosting machine learning algorithm

One of the most effective ML algorithms, the gradient boosting decision tree, learns a function by mapping inputs to targets using recognized input and target variables. Its ability to automatically handle sophisticated connections and relations among defined inputs [63], its better interpretability than other ML approaches [64], and being computationally simple, make it suitable to use and implement, generating verified forecasting findings in an actual and practical environment [[65], [66], [67]]. Consequently, it is being used as the basis for embedded failure prediction methods. The gradient boosting algorithm is implemented as both a regressor and a classifier. This is a collective learning predicative algorithm in which each step tries to enhance the errors of the preceding step in a structure known as a decision trees. Using this algorithm with a depth of 6, a minimum sample division of 2, 1000 trees, and least-squares loss, the shear force of the welded joints in the present investigation was predicted. This is illustrated in Fig. 7. Aiming to reduce error, least squares loss identifies predictive points and applies them to the line that has already been fitted. The profundity of a regression tree indicates the number of divisions that can be generated before a prediction is made. To make weak learners strong, gradient boosting trains models consecutively, with each model fixing the errors of the one before it. Gradient boosting derives its gradient from its ability to minimize the loss function through gradient descent. In essence, the boosting approach is an iterative process that involves learning nodes, fitting findings to the data set, analysing errors between predicted and real values, and then learning the already acquired nodes. In addition, this loop continues until the iteration number that was previously specified is achieved. The gradient boosting model uses the same data division (80 % for training and 20 % for testing) as the ANN.Fig. 7Structure of the gradient boosting ML algorithm.Fig. 7

CatBoost machine learning algorithm

A high-performance, open-source gradient boosting technique specifically designed to effectively handle categorical data is CatBoost [68]. In particular, CatBoost provides a number of benefits for datasets that include categorical data, which may be difficult for conventional algorithms to handle. Among the several benefits of CatBoost is its built-in support for categorical features, which eliminates the need for pre-processing methods such as one-hot encoding or label encoding [69]. In addition, CatBoost provides efficient encoding by using a technique called target-based encoding with statistical approaches to convert category variables into numerical values in a way that avoids over-fitting. It is possible to reduce the occurrence of over-fitting by processing the data so that each iteration of the model relies only on previous data to make predictions. This approach works especially well with smaller datasets. It is not necessary to do substantial hyper parameter adapting to modify the parameters since CatBoost performs well even with default values [70]. In its default configuration, CatBoost generates 1000 trees with two leaves, perfectly symmetrical binary trees with six levels of depth. The automatic calculation of the learning rate is done under the constraints of the iteration number and the features of the training dataset. Maximum effectiveness is achieved when the learning rate is automatically set. Reducing the number of iterations and increasing the learning rate can speed up training. The CatBoost model has used the same data split as the ANN and gradient boosting models, with 80 % of the data assigned for training and 20 % for testing.

Random forest machine learning algorithm

Random forest is a commonly used and potent ensemble ML approach that integrates multiple decision trees during training and produces the mode (classification) or mean prediction (regression) of the individual trees. It was introduced by Adele Cutler and Leo Breiman in 2001. The algorithm's benefits include accurate prediction, capable of dealing with data in high dimensions that exhibit non-linear input effects and interactions, and the ability to prioritise inputs based on their significance and contribution. On the basis of a decision tree, the random forest model generates an interpretable model for regression and classification tasks. Based on the feature values, it divides the data into subsets and makes a series of decisions to arrive at a final prediction. Bagging is employed by random forest, which entails the construction of a decision tree for each training data subset by replacing variables. This assists in decreasing variance and preventing over-fitting. Improving the algorithm's predictive power required adjusting three configuration settings. A minimal number of data points at a leaf, also known as the node size, is employed to avoid over-fitting. The node size, the maximum number of randomly chosen inputs to be evaluated at each split node and the number of trees are the three setup parameters [71,72]. A random subset of features is selected at every split in the construction of the decision tree, rather than all of the features being considered. This results in the introduction of additional randomness and the ability to generate trees that are less correlated. A decision tree is generated for each bootstrapped dataset. A random subset of features is selected at each node during the tree construction process, and the optimal split among these features is determined. The hyper-parameters have a related influence on the effectiveness of the ML model, as well as the patterns and speed at which the algorithm recognizes and analyses data [73]. The random forest model applied the same data distribution as the ANN, gradient boosting, and CatBoost models. Utilizing cross-validation is decisive for ideal forecasting performance when tuning hyper-parameters on the datasets. This investigation employed a ten fold cross-validation strategy. The data was partitioned into ten subgroups, and in each iteration, one subgroup was designated for testing the trained model [74,75]. Performance metrics

Selecting appropriate validation metrics is crucial for assessing the accuracy of a predictive model, and incorporating real-world data is vital for enhancing the model's performance. This research conducted a comparison and validation of several training and transfer functions to assess and quantify the level of good match between the real and forecasted findings. The selection of a suitable validation standard is of the utmost importance and presents a significant challenge when evaluating outcomes and reducing errors. To evaluate performance, all ANN, CatBoost, gradient boosting, and random forest models trained and tested in this research were compared by employing favourable metrics. The following metrics were utilized: mean squared error (MSE), root mean squared error (RMSE), mean error (ME), mean absolute error (MAE), mean relative error (MRE), and the coefficient of determination (R2). A high R2 value, close to 1, implies excellent performance, whereas low values of MSE, RMSE, and MAE, around 0, imply minimal error. In this situation, consistent performance can be guaranteed, and conversely, a substantial difference between the RMSE and MAE observations indicates important discrepancies in the error distribution. In addition, the RMSE and MAE provide a more precise assessment compared to other validation measures. While the MAE offers more stability, the RMSE is more responsive to errors. Results and discussions

Joint mechanical properties

The incorporation of spot-welded joints into the design of a structure guarantees that the shear force is the dominant load experienced by the welds whenever the joints undergo tension or compression. The welds might be subject to tension loading, shear stress, or both. In the former scenario, the loading direction would be perpendicular to the joint's plane [76]. It is important to note that spot welds utilized in automotive construction may exposed to shear stress owing to the rotation or relative motion of neighbouring sheets, and tensile loads caused by separation forces operating perpendicular to the sheets while carrying out their functions [77,78]. However, the RSW quality is primarily evaluated by the shear force of the RSW joints [79]. To fulfil this requirement, this investigation used four Ti/St304 welded cases with equal and unequal thicknesses for obtaining the tensile shear force, meaning that there are 25 samples for each case. The RSW cases based on the selected thickness as shown in Table 4 were referred to as the following: similar thickness of 1 mm for Ti and St 304 as case E, dissimilar thickness of 0.5 mm for Ti and 1 mm for St 304 as case F, dissimilar thickness of 0.5 mm for St 304 and 1 mm for Ti as case I, and similar thickness of 0.5 mm as case J.

The RSW parameters were tested using the DOE method on the samples from each instance to determine the shear force. Failure mechanism

The failure mechanism of RSW joints has a fundamental effect on their mechanical properties [[84], [85], [86], [87], [88]]. There are four recognized modes of failure for spot welds, as listed in Refs. [89,90]: interfacial (IF), pull-out (PF), partial interfacial (PIF), and partial pullout-partial width (PP-PW). The shape of the fusion zone (FZ), sheet metal thickness, and the ratio between nugget zone hardness and failure spot hardness throughout shear load experiment are used to identify the fracture mode of RSW specimens [91].

The mechanism of failure of the test samples was examined in the present investigation. Regardless of the welding case and the parameters used, the findings of the shear load tests always show the same failure mode: interfacial failure (IF). Consequently, the metallographic properties of the fractured surface were examined using only one test specimen. Sample 14 from case E was selected from among the other welded samples due exhibiting to the its highest shear force.

Fig. 20 shows micrographs of the surface of the titanium side fracture caused by tensile shear force. As seen in Fig. 20a, fracture initiation can be observed beginning at the HAZ border and extending from the HAZ towards the middle of the weld nugget. This crack spreads throughout the faying surfaces between the welded sheet metals in a direction perpendicular to the applied force, as shown in Fig. 20b. Because of the fracture, the shear force and weld strength would decrease due to interfacial failure. The same phenomenon took place when the surface of spot-welded DP600 steel that had fractures was studied by Ref. [92].Fig. 20The interfacial failure mode under microscope inspection.Fig. 20

The interfacial failure mode under microscope inspection.

SEM analysis of the interface failure of the Ti side in Fig. 21 shows, as seen in Fig. 21a, that a portion of the fracture surface displays a cleavage facet indicating that a brittle fracture occurred. A river-like shape is shown in Fig. 21a, which is caused by the crack's propagation throughout the tensile shear stress experiment. Typically, the fracture will separate into terrace-like stages because of the fact that each grain in the FZ has distinct orientations from one another. These cracks processes are characterized by cleavage fracture, a kind of trans-granular brittle fracture. Observing Fig. 21b, it is evident that some secondary cracks were generated in the weld nugget. Fig. 21c demonstrates the presence of some elongated dimples with a white colour which is thought to be the main reason for the improving shear force in the current study compared to the previous study [14], and this is confirmed by the XRD analysis in Fig. 22. Examination of the fractured surface of AISI side as shown in Fig. 22a, reveals several cracks and a river-like shape on the fractured stainless-steel side, demonstrating the occurrence of a brittle fracture. Also, the presence of cleave facets observed in Fig. 22b, c, and d on the stainless-steel side are proof of a trans-granular fracture generated between the welded joints over the grain boundary as the fracture continues to expand. A cleavage fracture, a common kind of brittle overload fracture, is another name for this type of fracture.

Crack initiation area origin, carbide precipitation, is another factor that leads to the formation of these fractures. Welded joints are brittle because of this. It is worth mentioning that the melting point of each metal is different, meaning that titanium melts at approximately 1668 °C, while AISI 304 melts at around 1400–1450 °C. Additionally, there is a variation in the thermal expansion between these metals, with AISI 304 having a thermal expansion approximately twice that of titanium. All these factors caused unequal volume expansion and thermal stress, which in turn led to the occurrence of brittle fractures. Moreover, the resistivity of the Ti is different to that of the St, causing the shrinkage rate of the two metals to be different and generating pores and cracks after the solidification is completed.

Micro-hardness distribution

Evaluation of the hardness distribution along the RSW joints is of the utmost importance. Owing to the different temperature generation suffered by the weldment, several phases will emerge and be divided into three main regions: base metal, heat-affected zone (HAZ), and nugget zone (NZ). Because of this, the hardness values in certain areas will differ. This paper conducted a hardness test using the micro-hardness Vickers method, applying a force of 2.98 N. The test consists of 20 indentations spaced 0.5 mm apart across the welded joint. Hardness measurements were carried out in the present study on the three chosen RSW samples of instances D, G, and H. These three welded samples were taken from the joints that showed the maximum, middle, and smallest shear force, in that order, according to the measurements of shear load.

In the present paper, the hardness determinations were applied to the three chosen specimens of cases E, F, I and J. These three samples were taken according to the shear load test by selecting the joints that exhibited the minimum, middle, and peak shear load, respectively. The hardness measurements were conducted by starting with the BM, then proceeding to HAZ on the titanium side, reaching the NZ, and proceeding to the HAZ and BM on the AISI 304 side.

Importantly, the interfacial fracture developed owing to the brittle behaviour of the weldments, making the hardness reach its maximum values.

Additionally, the varying cooling rates experienced by the NZ and HAZ may contribute to the hardness improvement in these regions. Throughout the RSW operation, the ratios at which the temperature decreases gradually change from the area where the heat source is most concentrated, known as the NZ, to the HAZ, which is located further away from the heat source, and finally to the BM, which is the area furthest from the heat source. As a result of this factor, the nugget zone undergoes more rapid cooling rates in comparison to other regions, resulting in an elevation in the hardness of the NZ.

Conclusion

The dissimilar joints between grade 2 titanium alloy and AISI 304 of 0.5 and 1 mm thickness were welded using the RSW process by applying different levels of the RSW parameters. The RSW parameters were welding current, pressure, welding time, squeeze time, holding time, and pulse welding, which were used with different welding cases by achieving an equal or unequal thickness. Moreover, the joint quality accuracy was assessed based on the ANN, gradient boosting, CatBoost, and random forest models. The shear force observations showed that case F yielded the highest value of 2.589 kN, while case I showed the second highest shear force of 2.183 kN. The maximum shear forces in cases I and J were 1.708 and 1.851 kN, respectively. Predictions of the accuracy of joint quality using different models such as ANN, gradient boosting, CatBoost, and random forest provided incredible observations about how RSW affects the predicted shear force. The ANN model achieved the most favourable results when trained and tested with Traincgp and Tansig, with MSE and R2 values of 0.01886 and 0.94973, respectively. Nevertheless, the Trainbfg and Purelin functions demonstrated the ANN model's worst prediction, with MSE and R2 values of 0.25501 and 0.64662, respectively. The random forest proved to have the second-best prediction accuracy, with an MSE of 0.039798 and an R2 of 0.832732. CatBoost was the third-most accurate model, with an MSE of 0.039958 and an R2 of 0.832061. Finally, the gradient boosting model yielded satisfactory results, however, these were not as promising as the results obtained with the random forest and CatBoost models. The performance metrics of MSE and R2 with gradient boosting were 0.059862 and 0.74841, respectively. In this research the relative importance metric was used for the first time to assess the impact of each RSW parameter on the shear force. Sheet thickness was the most effective parameter in the random forest, CatBoost, and gradient boosting models, with values of 31.94 %, 31.55 %, and 33.94 %, respectively. This study also provided a new mathematical equation for estimating the shear force based on the weights and biases of the ANN model. Lastly, the micro-hardness of the highest, medium, and lowest shear force specimens from cases E, F, J, and I were analysed. Case F exhibited the greatest hardness, while case E showed the second-highest. Cases J and I were third and fourth, respectively.
