Ultra-miniature and sensitive optical fiber-tip optomechanical resonant photoacoustic spectroscopy gas sensors
Authors: Taige Li, Pengcheng Zhao, Peng Wang, Shangming Liu, Linhao Guo, Wei Jin, A Ping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOptomechanical resonator (OMR)-based photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) sensors have garnered significant attention for ultra-sensitive and selective trace-gas detection. However, it remains challenging for optomechanical resonant PAS (OMR-PAS) sensors to achieve both high sensitivity and miniature size for trace-gas detection with minimal consumption in space-constrained environments. Here, we present a monolithically designed optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor, in which a micrometer-scale planar-spiral-spring OMR (PSS-OMR) is 3D micro-printed on the end face of optical fiber, enabling ultrasensitive gas sensing with nanoliter-level consumption. Compared to off-resonance operation, this fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor operating in resonance mode produces a significantly stronger photoacoustic signal, improving the signal-to-noise ratio by more than five times. The sensor can detect acetylene gas at 55 ppb without the need for an additional photoacoustic cell. Its response time can be as fast as 0.2 s. This ultra-miniature and highly sensitive fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor may become a powerful tool for various trace-gas monitoring applications.Keywords: Optical fiber gas sensors, Photoacoustic spectroscopy, Fabry-Pérot cavity, Optomechanical microresonator, 3D micro-printing

Introduction

Owing to the advantages of fast response, high sensitivity and selectivity, optical gas sensors based on laser absorption spectroscopy (LAS) have become increasingly indispensable tools for trace-gas detection and monitoring in various applications, including environmental surveillance, reaction measurement, livestock monitoring, and medical diagnosis [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Although many different LAS techniques, such as cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS) [10], [11], Raman spectroscopy [12] and photothermal spectroscopy [13], [14], have been demonstrated for trace-gas sensing, photoacoustic spectroscopy (PAS) holds its promise in pursuing ultracompact gas sensors as its sensitivity breaks loose from the length of light absorption path [15]. Conventional PAS gas sensors use electrical microphones, such as capacitive (electret or condenser) piezoelectric [16], MEMS transducers [17], [18], and quartz tuning forks (QTFs) [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], to detect light-absorption-induced acoustic waves for quantitative measurement of gas concentration. However, PAS gas sensors using electrical microphones are not well suited for remote gas monitoring or deployment in complex or harsh environments, and the combination of electrical and optical components limits the miniaturization of the entire device.

These technological bottlenecks can be overcome by using optical fiber PAS gas sensors, in which electrical microphones are replaced by optical fiber-based microphones. Typically, an optical Fabry-Perot (FP) interferometric acoustic transducer with a diaphragm [24], [25] or cantilever [26], [27] is assembled at the output of optical fiber for PAS signal detection. To achieve a very high sensitivity, one can either utilize the acoustic resonance within the photoacoustic cell (PAC) [28], [29] or leverage the mechanical resonance of diaphragm or cantilever to create optomechanical resonators (OMRs) for enhanced PAS signal detection [30], [31], [32], [33]. Compared to resonant PAC-based schemes, OMR-based resonance enhancement holds promise of operating without the need for an additional PAC and thus has attracted particular attention in the development of compact PAS sensors. For instance, Tan et al. demonstrated an all-fiber PAS sensor that employs a ceramic sleeve-top multi-layer graphene OMR [30]. By leveraging the resonant characteristics of the graphene diaphragm, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was improved tenfold compared to the non-resonant state, achieving a limit of detection (LoD) of ∼120 parts-per-billion (ppb) acetylene (C2H2). On the other hand, cantilever-based FP interferometric acoustic sensors have relatively more flexibility in tailoring mechanical resonance, higher linear response and larger dynamic range [31]. For instance, Guo et al. proposed a high-sensitivity cantilever-enhanced photoacoustic spectroscopy (CEPAS) gas sensor that integrates a silicon cantilever mounted on a cm-scale stainless-steel shell outside an optical fiber ferrule. Combined with a non-resonant photoacoustic cell, it achieved a LoD of ∼0.2 ppb C2H2 when the cantilever operated at its mechanical resonance [32]. To solve the tradeoff between small dimension and high sensitivity, our group devised a miniature optical fiber optomechanical resonant PAS (OMR-PAS) gas sensor based on a planar-spiral-spring OMR (PSS-OMR) that is directly 3D micro-printed on the end-face of a fiber-optic ferrule. Assisted with a non-resonant PAC, the sensor demonstrated the detection of C₂H₂ gas at very low concentrations, achieving a LoD of approximately 45 ppb in resonant mode [33]. However, the overall size of these optical fiber PAS sensor systems is typically about several millimeters to centimeters mainly subject to the dimensions of the OMR and PAC, which thus hinders their applications for trace-detection with minimal consumption in space-constrained environments.

Very recently, Ma et al. demonstrated an ultracompact optical fiber-tip PAS gas sensor by assembling a polymer membrane on a short section of capillary at the end-face of an optical fiber. It is a monolithically designed PAS sensor integrated with both a micrometer-scale PAC and FP micro-interferometer, achieving a very low LoD of ppb level in the detection of C2H2 gas [25]. However, the mechanical resonance frequency of such a membrane is as high as ∼520 kHz, where PAS signal is weak due to the damping effects from gas and heat flow in the cavity. Consequently, the sensor operated at a non-resonant frequency, i.e., 14 kHz, instead of its resonant frequency, thereby not benefiting from resonance amplification.

In this work, we present a monolithically designed optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor for trace-gas detection with nanoliter-level sample consumption. Compared to our previously reported OMR-PAS gas sensor [33], this sensor features a miniaturized micro-PAC integrated PSS-OMR with a smaller size (120 μm in diameter) and thinner microbeams (3 μm in thickness), which is directly 3D microprinted onto the end face of a standard single-mode fiber (SMF). This new design results in significantly enhanced acoustic sensitivity and improved acousto-mechanical coupling efficiency. Consequently, instead of using a pump/probe split mode, this sensor can combine pump and probe lights within a single optical fiber to achieve highly sensitive trace gas detection without the need for an additional external PAC. Experimental results show that, compared to off-resonance operation, the PAS gas sensor's signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) can be greatly enhanced by the mechanical resonance of PSS-OMR up to 550 %. This ultraminiature fiber-tip sensor can highly sensitively detect low-concentration C2H2 gas at ppb levels, and its response time is as short as below 0.2 s.

Principle and analysis

Working principle of the optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor

Fig. 1(a) shows the schematic design of optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor for trace-gas monitoring. The sensor uses a pump-probe sensing scheme, in which an SMF is used to deliver both the pump and probe lights into the micro-PAC integrated OMR-PAS gas sensor. The central part of the PSS-OMR is a thin-film reflector that, together with the end face of the SMF, forms a micrometer-scale FP interferometer (FPI). When gas molecules diffuse into the micro-PAC, they absorb the pump light modulated at a specific frequency and undergo non-radiative relaxation, generating periodic heating and consequently acoustic waves. The generated weak acoustic wave, i.e., photoacoustic (PA) wave, can excite the vibrational resonance of the fiber-top PSS-OMR. Such an acoustic wave-induced resonant vibration of the suspended microdisk, i.e., thin-film reflector, may cause periodic variations of the FPI’s length, reaching their maximum amplitude when the modulation frequency matches the resonance frequency of the PSS-OMR and then can be read out by an interferometric means. A photograph of the optical fiber-tip sensor is shown in Fig. 1(b).

Numerical simulation of the PSS-OMR

Mechanical responses of the designed PSS-OMRs were numerically analyzed by using commercial FEM software, i.e., COMSOL Multiphysics. The PSS-OMRs are made of SU-8 resin, which has the advantages of low optical absorption, good mechanical strength, excellent chemical resistance, and high thermal stability. Its Young’s modulus and Poisson ratio used in simulation are 3.4 GPa and 0.22, respectively [34]. A combined solid mechanics and pressure acoustics analysis was conducted to investigate the frequency response of the PSS-OMRs and their corresponding surface displacements, in which material damping was included to account for intrinsic loss. An isotropic loss factor of 0.035 was used in the simulation, with which the Q factor of simulated frequency response closely matches the measured value.

Fig. 1(c) shows the typical simulated frequency response of a PSS-OMR under the excitation of 1-Pa acoustic pressure. To mitigate thermal damping effects from the gas and heat flow in the cavity, the width and thickness of the microbeams of PSS-OMR are chosen to be 6 μm and 3 μm, respectively, achieving a relatively low resonance frequency of 105 kHz and enabling a strong PA signal. The quality factor (i.e., Q factor) of the fundamental resonance is calculated to be about 20. The simulated displacement of the thin-film reflector under resonance is ∼13.9 nm, which is about 33.2 times larger than that under a 1-Pa static pressure (i.e., ∼0.419 nm). Notably, the micromechanical resonance of such a PSS-OMR can be flexibly customized by tailoring the width or thickness of its microbeams. Fig. 1(d) summarizes the calculated displacements of the PSS-OMRs with different microbeam widths and thicknesses at the fundamental vibration resonance under the excitation of 1-Pa acoustic pressure. The displacement increases with the decreases of the microbeam width and thickness, while the resonant frequency alters oppositely.

Materials and methods

Materials

EPON SU-8 resin was purchased from HEXION Ltd. The photoinitiator octoxyphenylphenyliodonium hexafluoroantimonate (OPPI) was purchased from Arctom Scientific LLC. Tributylamine (TBA) was used as an inhibitor. The weight ratios of SU-8 resin, OPPI, and TBA are 1: 4 wt%: 0.4 wt%, respectively. The concentrations of SU-8 in photoresist solutions for bottom and upper layers were 20 % and 25 %, respectively. 2-(2H-benzotriazol-2-yl)-4,6-bis(1-methyl-1- phenylethyl)phenol, called as Tinuvin 234, was used as UV absorption agent to enhance the vertical discernibility. Its weight ratios to SU-8 for bottom and upper layers were 0.1 % and 0.4 %, respectively.

3D micro-printing of the micro-PAC-integrated OMR-PAS sensor head

The micro-PAC-integrated OMR-PAS sensor head was fabricated on the end face of an optical fiber by using an in-house 3D micro-printing technology [33], [35], [36]. Fig. S1 shows the flow chart of printing processes. The fabrication started from deposition of a uniform thin layer of SU-8 on the fiber end-face by dip-coating process. Then, the sample was baked in an oven at 65 °C for 5 min and followed by 95 °C for 15 min to evaporate solvent. After soft-baking, with the assistance of the digital camera-based machine vision metrology, the sample was automatically moved to the pre-aligned position for optical exposure process. The image data sliced from the 3D model of the design were used to dynamically generate UV patterns to expose the SU-8 film on the fiber end face. The exposure time for the first (i.e., bottom) layer was about 40 s, which is associated with the UV light intensity of 54.3 mW/mm2. After exposure, the sample was post-baked by a blast drying oven. The sample was developed by propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate (PGMEA) and carefully cleaned by a low surface tension solvent methyl nonafluorobutyl ether (C5H3F9O) [37]. The fabrication processes were repeated for the second (i.e., upper) layer of the designed sensor head. After the two-round fabrication processes, the sample was hard-baked at 120 °C for 60 min.

Experimental setup for the testing of acoustic response

The acoustic response of the fabricated optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor was tested in air by using a home-made setup, as shown in Fig. S2. A piezo loudspeaker driven by a signal generator was used to generate acoustic waves. A narrow-linewidth probe laser was employed for the measurement of high-frequency acoustic responses using intensity demodulation method. The probe wavelength was tuned to a quadrature point of the sensor’s reflection spectrum. The reflected light was coupled to a photodetector (PD; Newport 2053) via an optical circulator, where its phase change was measured interferometrically, converted to electric signal and analyzed by a digital oscilloscope. The passband of the PD was set in the range from 10 kHz to 300 kHz. A commercial microphone was mounted near the optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor to measure acoustic pressure for calibration. The piezo loudspeaker, the optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor, and the calibration microphone were placed in an acoustic isolation box to suppress noise influence from the external environment.

Experimental setup for the testing of OMR-PAS gas sensors

The fabricated optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor was tested by using a pump-probe experimental setup, as shown in Fig. S3. Gas concentration measurements were conducted through a combination of wavelength modulation and second harmonic (2 f) detection techniques. The pump beam was generated by a 1533-nm distributed feedback (DFB) laser (FITEL FRL15DCWD-A81–19560-C) and subsequently amplified using an erbium-doped fiber amplifier (EDFA, Amonics AEDFA-EX-27-B-FA). The pump wavelength was slowly scanned across the absorption line P(13) of C2H2 gas and meanwhile modulated sinusoidally at a higher frequency (i.e., f is 10.5 or 52.5 kHz) to produce acoustic waves. The probe light was provided by a tunable semiconductor laser (TSL; Santec, TSL-570) with a linewidth of about 100 kHz, and its output wavelength was tuned to a quadrature point of the sensor’s reflection spectrum, specifically around 1548.2 nm (as shown in the inset of Fig. 2(b)). In the experiments, both the pump and probe lights were coupled into a single optical fiber using a 1530/1550-nm wavelength-division multiplexer (WDM; Flyin Optronics). The light reflected back from the sensor was split into two paths: one path was connected to a PD (Newport, 2053-FC), with the signal acquired by a data acquisition card (DAQ; National Instruments, USB-6002). The DC component of this signal was used by a software-based proportional–integral–derivative (PID) controller to stabilize the probe light wavelength at the sensor’s quadrature point. The other path was sent to a balanced photodetector (BPD; Newport, 2117-FC), where it was compared with the reference light directly from TSL to generate the 2f-PAS signal, which was demodulated by using a commercial lock-in amplifier (LIA) from Zurich Instruments (MFLI 500 kHz) with a frequency range of 0 ∼ 500 kHz. During testing, the OMR-PAS gas sensor was placed inside a small cylindrical gas chamber with an inner diameter of 5 mm and two outlets for gas exchange.

Preparation of gas samples

Gas mixtures of different concentrations were prepared by mixing calibrated gas samples, i.e., 1010 ppm C2H2 and high-purity N2 gas, with two mass flow controllers at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure.

Experimental results

3D micro-printed fiber-top PAC-integrated OMR-PAS sensor head

Fig. 2(a) shows the scanning-electron microscopy (SEM) images of PAC-integrated OMR-PAS sensor head fabricated on the end-face of a SMF. The whole structure has a height of ∼90 μm and diameter of 120 μm, which is precisely aligned with the SMF end-face. The lower layer of the sensor head is a ∼40-μm-high three-petal base with three microchannels, allowing gas molecules flowing into the PAC. The diameter of its inner cavity is ∼80 μm. The upper layer consists of a thin-film reflector connected to the supporting pillars by three spiral microbeams, each 6 μm wide and 3 μm thick. Although narrower and/or thinner microbeams could further enhance sensitivity, these dimensions were selected as a compromise based on the limitations of our current optical 3D micro-printing processes. The length of each microbeam is about 106 μm. The thin-film reflector and the end-face of SMF form a micrometer-scale FPI. The measured reflection spectrum of the FPI is shown in Fig. 2(b). The free spectral range (FSR) and fringe contrast are ∼13.3 nm and ∼8 dB, respectively, at the wavelength of around 1550 nm. By applying a fast Fourier transform (FFT) to the measured reflection spectrum, the length of the FP microcavity was estimated to be about 84 μm.

Testing of the sensor’s acoustic responses

The measured frequency response of the OMR-PAS sensor to acoustic waves is shown in Fig. 2(c). The fundamental resonant frequency was measured at approximately 105 kHz, which agrees well with the simulation result. By fitting the resonance peak with a Lorentzian profile, the full width at half maximum (FWHM) is determined to be ∼5.07 kHz, resulting in an estimated Q factor of about 20.7. A comparison between the measured frequency response and the simulation result is given in Fig. S4(a).

Fig. 2(d) shows the measured time-domain signals of the optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor at its resonance frequency of 105 kHz when the applied acoustic pressures are 10, 20, 50, and 100 mPa, respectively. Fig. 2(e) shows the amplitudes of the sensor output signals as a function of applied acoustic pressure under resonant (colored line) and non-resonant conditions. The output signals exhibited a linear dependence on acoustic pressure in both cases. The measured sensitivity of the sensor at resonance is ∼144 mV/Pa, compared to ∼1.53 mV/Pa under non-resonant conditions, indicating that the resonance can enhance the sensor’s acoustic sensitivity by 94 times. The measured waveforms and their power spectral densities calculated by using FFT are shown in Fig. S5. A SNR as high as 45 dB was achieved under a 100-mPa acoustic pressure, yielding an estimated noise-equivalent acoustic sensitivity of ∼10.7 μPa/Hz1/2. For comparison, the measurements were repeated at a non-resonant frequency of 21 kHz, resulting in a sensitivity of ∼154.3 μPa/Hz1/2.

Results of photoacoustic gas sensing

The detection limit of the OMR-PAS gas sensor was tested by continuously injecting N2-balanced 1010 C2H2 gas into the chamber. Fig. 3(a) presents the 2 f lock-in output signals acquired by scanning the pump wavelength across the P(13) absorption line of C2H2 at different input pump power levels when the modulation frequency is 21 kHz (i.e., under off-resonance operation), while Fig. 3(b) shows the signals measured at the frequency of 105 kHz (i.e., at resonance mode). The LIA time constant is 100 ms and a filter slope of 18 dB/Oct, corresponding to a detection bandwidth of 0.9375 Hz. The peak-to-peak amplitudes of these 2 f signals (i.e., PAS signal) against the input pump power levels are shown in Fig. 3(c). In both cases, the peak-to-peak 2 f signal amplitudes exhibit a linear relationship with the pump power. Notably, compared to off-resonance operation, the sensitivity of the sensor operating at resonance mode was enhanced by ∼14.1 times.

The system noise was measured when the pump wavelength was fixed at P(13) line and the gas chamber was filled with pure N2. The LIA detection bandwidth is 3.125 Hz. To assess the minimum noise equivalent concentration (NEC) of the fabricated optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor in C2H2 gas detection, Allan-Werle deviation analysis was conducted [38], [39] using the measured 2-hour noise data. As shown in Fig. 3(d), under off-resonance operation with an input pump power of ∼210 mW, the SNR for 10-s integration time calculated from the measured data was ∼1050, whose corresponding NEC and noise equivalent absorption (NEA) were ∼962 ppb C2H2 and ∼1.01 × 10−6 cm−1, respectively. The NEC decreased with an increase of integration time up to 538 s, at which the NEC reached a minimum of 144.6 ppb C2H2, corresponding to a NEA of ∼1.52 × 10−7 cm−1. Fig. 3(e) presents the measured result of the sensor under resonance operation at the same pump power. The SNR for 10-s integration time was ∼4146, whose corresponding NEC and NEA are ∼243.6 ppb C2H2 and ∼2.56 × 10−7 cm−1, respectively. Compared with off-resonance operation, the SNR is amplified by ∼3.95 times due to the resonance of PSS-OMR. The NEC further decreased with the increase of integration time. When the integration time is 356 s, the NEC reached a minimum of 55 ppb C2H2, corresponding to a NEA of ∼5.78 × 10−8 cm−1, indicating the remarkable benefit of optomechanical resonance in enhancing the gas sensor’s detection ability.

A comparative analysis of the SNRs for the operations at 21 kHz and 105 kHz with an integration time of 10 s is shown in Fig. 3(f). The SNRs at the resonance frequency are remarkably higher than these at the non-resonant frequency, reflecting the benefit of resonance amplification. As shown in the inset, the amplification factor quickly increased when the pump power increased from 10 mW to 30 mW, then gradually declined with further increases. When the pump power was ∼30 mW, the SNR at 105 kHz was ∼1445, whose corresponding NEC reached sub-ppm level, and it is about 5.5 times of the SNR measured at the frequency of 21 kHz. For comparison, the SNR measured at 21 kHz was ∼1050 even when a much higher pump power of ∼210 mW was used. It indicates that the resonance amplification mechanism can substantially improve power efficiency to achieve a high SNR at a relatively low pump power.

The dynamic range of the optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor was tested by filling C2H2 of different concentrations into the gas chamber at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. The peak-to-peak values of these 2 f signals with respect to C2H2 concentration are given in Fig. 4(b). The measured results show that the peak-to-peak signal values exhibit a good linear relationship with C2H2 concentrations ranging from 10 to 1010 ppm, whose coefficient of determination R2 is 0.9986.

The response time of the gas sensor was tested by sequentially filling the gas chamber with pure N2, 1010 C2H2, and then pure N2 gases at a flow rate of 100 standard cm³ /min (SCCM), while the pump wavelength was fixed to the center of P(13) absorption line of C2H2. Since the maximum gas path length in the sensor head is less than 125 µm, the time of gas flowing across the sensor head is no longer than 1.5 ms, which is sufficiently shorter than the estimated response time to avoid potential impact on response time testing. The LIA detection bandwidth is 3.125 Hz. Fig. 4(c) presents the real-time recording of the 2 f signal, which has been normalized by removing the baseline and scaling them against the maximum value. The response time, specifically the time required for reaching 90 % (i.e., rise time) and descending to 10 % (i.e., fall time) of the maximum value, are around 165 ms and 94 ms, respectively. The long-term stability of the sensor system was tested by continuous measurement of 1010 C2H2 in a laboratory environment. As shown in Fig. 4(d), the measured fluctuation of the peak-to-peak 2 f signal is ±1.5 % over 1.5 h.

Notably, compared to our previously reported OMR-PAS sensor [33], which requires the use of a 5-mm-long external PAC for acoustic focusing to achieve a LoD of 45 ppb, this monolithically integrated fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor has not only a much smaller size, which enables the reduction of gas consumption by more than three orders of magnitude (i.e., from 1.4 μL to <1.0 nL), but also a comparable LoD, i.e., 55 ppb, that was achieved without the use of an additional PAC. Moreover, when comparing their NNEAs and response times, the new fiber-tip OMR-PAS sensor demonstrates better performance, achieving an NNEA of approximately 2.3 × 10⁻⁷ cm⁻¹ ·W·Hz⁻¹ ᐟ² and a response time of less than 0.2 s, while our previously reported sensor exhibited an NNEA of about 3.1 × 10⁻⁷ cm⁻¹ ·W·Hz⁻¹ ᐟ² and a response time of under 0.3 s.

These significant advancements can be mainly attributed to two contributions: i) The new monolithic design of the sensor head. The direct fabrication of a micro-PAC integrated PSS-OMR on fiber end-face enables a complete spatial overlap between the micro-PAC and the FP microcavity and thus maximally enhances the acousto-mechanical coupling efficiency. This is particularly important for PAS sensors operating at tens of kilohertz, as the corresponding acoustic waves have wavelengths ranging from millimeters to centimeters that typically result in low acousto-mechanical coupling efficiency and pose significant challenges for the miniaturization of PAS sensors. ii) A much thinner PSS-OMR achieved by improved 3D micro-printing process. Compared to the PSS-OMR in previous work, the thickness of the PSS-OMR in this work has been reduced from 10 μm to 3 μm. From Fig. 1(d), we can see that the sensitivity (in terms of the acoustic-induced displacement) can be enhanced by ∼22 times for such a thickness change when the width of microbeam is kept to the same (i.e., 6 μm). Discussion

Notably, the localized heating caused by the gas absorption of pump laser, known as the photothermal (PT) effect, leads to a change of refractive index (RI) of the gas medium. Therefore, one can infer that the PT effect also contributes to the phase change of the probe light in FP cavity and thus conduces to the interferometric readout, especially when the pump light power is high. To evaluate the contributions of PT effect on the measured interferometric readout, we fabricated a reference photothermal interferometric (PTI) sensor whose thin-film reflector is suspended by using short and wide beams, as shown in Fig. S6(a). From the measured reflected spectrum and its corresponding FFT result, as shown in Fig. S6(b), the cavity length of the reference sample is ∼88 μm, which is nearly identical to the fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor. Since this reference PTI sensor has negligible response to acoustic wave, it can be used to assess the PT contribution in the OMR-PAS gas sensor.

A comparison of the peak-to-peak 2 f signals at the frequencies of 21 kHz and 105 kHz measured by using the two sensors under different pumping power is shown in Fig. 5(a). The peak-to-peak 2 f signal at 21 kHz measured by using the reference PTI sensor is higher than that measured at 105 kHz, which is believed to be resulted from the reduced PT-induced RI modulation at higher thermal modulation frequencies [40]. When comparing the 21-kHz 2 f signals of the two sensors, it can be seen that the PT effect contributes non-negligibly; however, the OMR-PAS gas sensor has a relatively stronger signal, which means that the PA effect has played a more significant effect. It becomes more evident if one compares the 105-kHz 2 f signals of the two sensors. These results reveal that PA effect plays a dominant role in the OMR-PAS gas sensor, especially when the sensor works in its resonant mode. It also indicates that the performance of OMR-PAS gas sensors can be further improved if their PA effect is enhanced, e.g., by improving thermoacoustic conversion efficiency.

(b) Comparison of the measured SNRs of the OMR-PAS sensor and the reference PTI sensor at the frequencies of 105 kHz and 21 kHz, respectively. Noise levels were assessed using 10-s integration time.

The measured SNRs at the two frequencies (i.e., 21 kHz and 105 kHz) measured by using the two sensor heads are presented in Fig. 5(b). One can see that, as the same with the measured peak-to-peak 2 f signals, the SNRs at the frequency of 105 kHz (i.e., the resonance frequency of PSS-OMR) of the OMR-PAS sensor are far beyond the SNRs measured at the frequency 21 kHz and those measured by using the reference PTI sensor head at these two frequencies. It further confirms the superior role of the PSS-OMR’s resonance in enhancing the signal of PAS gas sensors.

Notably, the target gas of C2H2 was chosen as it is a representative analyte for optical fiber-based LAS technology. Our optical fiber-tip PAS gas sensor can be used to detect other gases, such as CH₄, H₂S, and NH₃, as long as the pump laser is adjusted so that its output wavelength matches the absorption line of the target gas, and the single-mode optical fiber operates effectively at that wavelength. Moreover, the good performance achieved by the OMR-PAS sensor is largely attributed to both excellent optical transparency and good mechanical strength of SU-8 resin used in fabricating the PSS-OMRs. The low optical absorption of SU-8 enables the sensor to operate under high pump power, while its good mechanical strength allows for resonant vibration with relatively low damping losses. Therefore, it can be anticipated that the sensor’s performance could be further enhanced by optimizing the optical and mechanical properties of the SU-8 material, for example, through nanocomposite technology by embedding with silica nanoparticles [41].

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed a small-size high-performance optical fiber-tip PAS gas sensor based on a miniaturized PSS-OMR. A 3D micro-printing technology has been utilized to directly print both the PSS-OMR and a micrometer-scale PAC on the end face of a standard single-mode optical fiber to create a monolithically designed optical fiber OMR-PAS gas sensor. Experimental results have demonstrated that, enhanced by resonance amplification mechanism, this optical fiber-tip OMR-PAS gas sensor can highly sensitively detect low-contraction C2H2 gas down to ppb levels without the need of using an additional PAC. Moreover, the semi-opening design of this OMR-PAS gas sensor enables a very fast response in gas monitoring, whose response time can be less than 0.2 s. Such a small-size, high-sensitivity and fast-response gas sensor offers new opportunities for real-time monitoring of trace gas fluctuations in confined environments, such as operando in-reactor monitoring and intravascular diagnostic applications.
