Impact of facility based versus home based growth monitoring and promotion programs on child nutritional status in rural Bangladesh
Authors: Muttaquina Hossain, Md Ahshanul Haque, A S G Faruque, Md M Islam Bulbul, Tahmeed Ahmed, Ulla Ashorn, Per Ashorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe delivery platform for growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) programs to improve child nutrition remains debated. This study compared the impact of facility-based plus non-governmental organization supported home-based GMP (FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP) versus facility-based GMP (FGMP) on child nutritional status in rural Bangladesh. Using a quasi-experimental design, 1,519 children aged 6–12 months were enrolled in each program and assessed at baseline and after 12 months. The primary outcome was linear growth (length-for-age Z-scores, LAZ), while secondary outcomes included weight-for-age (WAZ), weight-for-height (WHZ), stunting, wasting, and underweight prevalence, minimum dietary diversity, minimum meal frequency, and caregivers’ complementary feeding practices. Child linear growth (Difference-in-difference, D-I-D: -4.5; 95% CI: -15.2, 6.1) and weight (D-I-D: -3.5; 95% CI: -11.7, 4.6) faltered in both groups, with no significant differences between the programs. Stunting (D-I-D: 2.8%; 95% CI: -1.3, 7.1) and wasting (D-I-D: 0.43%; 95% CI: -3.2, 4.1) prevalence increased equally across programs. Underweight prevalence rose more in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP (D-I-D: 4.7%; 95% CI: 1.7,7.8) than in FGMP programs. Caregivers in FGMP areas showed slight improvements in complementary feeding practices (D-I-D: -1.4%; 95% CI:-6.5, 3.7) and significantly improved meal frequency (D-I-D: -4.7%; 95% CI: -9.1,-0.3). No significant differences were observed in minimum dietary diversity (D-I-D: -1.5%; 95% CI: -6.1, 3.1) or acceptable diet (D-I-D: -2.0%; 95% CI: -6.6, 2.6) between the programs. Findings highlight the need to strengthen both FGMP and FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP programs by actively engaging caregivers and incorporating the influential role of family members and local social networks, which appeared to have a stronger impact on child feeding practices than GMP providers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33899-3.Keywords: GMP, Home, Facility, Children, Feeding practices, Nutritional status, BangladeshSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Child malnutrition remains a critical public health issue in many low and middle-income countries, including Bangladesh1,2. Especially in rural areas, where access to healthcare facilities is often limited, the challenge of ensuring adequate child nutrition is particularly pronounced2. Growth Monitoring and Promotion (GMP) programs address this service gap through several key approaches tailored to rural settings3,4. GMP programs identify early signs of malnutrition or growth faltering by regularly monitoring children’s growth parameters5. This regular growth monitoring (GM) allows for timely interventions to prevent more severe health issues. In addition, the “promotion” component of GMP programs provides caregivers with essential nutrition education, including proper child-feeding and caring practices, which empowers caregivers with the knowledge and tools needed to improve their children’s nutritional status6.

Traditionally, GMP programs are conducted in healthcare facilities, where trained health professionals can closely monitor children’s growth and provide caregivers with direct counseling and support6. However, facility-based GMP (FGMP) programs face significant logistical challenges in rural settings, including transportation barriers, limited healthcare infrastructure and resources, and the burden of time and cost for caregivers3,5,7,8. As a result, the reach and effectiveness of FGMP programs are often constrained7,9–11.

Home/community-based GMP (HGMP) programs are being implemented for some time as an alternative approach due to the challenges in FGMP services8,12–15. By bringing growth monitoring and promotion services directly to children’s homes or to their communities, these programs aim to overcome barriers associated with FGMP care. HGMP programs can provide caregivers with more personalized and continuous support, potentially leading to better adherence to recommended feeding practices and improved nutritional outcomes for children5,14. However, most home/community-based GMP services remain isolated and lack linkage with the broader health system, posing challenges to sustainability and scalability5,15. Consequently, the outcomes of HGMP services exhibit variability across diverse contexts and regions8,14,16.

Despite the theoretical advantages of HGMP programs, there is limited empirical evidence comparing their effectiveness with traditional FGMP programs in improving the nutritional status of children16. In Bangladesh, FGMP programs are operational for decades with questionable effectiveness15. To overcome this, a non-governmental organization (NGO) supported governments FGMP program and provided home-based GMP services to strengthen the GMP program in selected districts of rural communities in Bangladesh. However, the effectiveness of FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP programs is not known. This study aims to fill this gap by evaluating the effectiveness of FGMP vs. FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP programs in rural Bangladesh. Precisely, we assess how these two approaches impact the nutritional status of children, providing insights into the most effective strategies for addressing child malnutrition in resource-constrained settings. Understanding the comparative effectiveness of these GMP approaches is crucial for informing public health policies and optimizing resource allocation in rural Bangladesh and similar contexts. By identifying the more effective model, this study seeks to contribute to developing more efficient and impactful interventions to combat child malnutrition, ultimately improving the health and well-being of children in rural communities.

Materials and methods

Study design, period, and site

A community-based quasi experimental study employing a mixed-method approach was conducted in six rural sub-districts of Bangladesh’s Mymensingh district from August 2019 to December 2020. In this area, the Government administered GMP services via health facility platforms, notably community clinics (CC). These CCs, community-based government healthcare facilities, cater to the health, family planning, and nutrition needs of 6,000 or more households17. The detailed methodology of this study is published elsewhere18. FGMP services were available in both FGMP and FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas.

Mymensingh is similar to other Bangladeshi districts with its predominantly rural, agrarian population, along with comparable literacy rates and demographic patterns. We selected the Mymensingh district based on the following criteria: (A) time of implementation of the programme matching with the study timeline; (B) staff and logistics in CCs supported by a NGO; and (C) an adequate number of CCs in subdistricts to satisfy the sample size18. Six sub-districts were selected to understand the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and FGMP service delivery models. Three subdistricts were selected where an NGO supplemented the Government’s FGMP services with home-based initiatives. Concurrently, three neighboring sub-districts were chosen where only the Government’s FGMP services were available, without an NGO’s involvement. We compared child nutritional status in these two settings over 12 months. This approach allowed for a comparative analysis of the impact of the GMP program in varied contexts within the Mymensingh district.

The home and facility-based areas are matched based on some socio-demographic characteristics to avoid selection bias18. Initially, study-specific general information was collected on each CC and its catchment area through discussions with three groups of residents: (1) Community health care providers (CHCPs), (2) mothers/caregivers of under-two children, and (3) members of the CC-associated community group (CG). Discussions focused on: (1) GMP service availability, (2) availability of other nutrition services at the CCs for women and children, (3) components of GMP provided by CHCP, and (4) sociodemographic status of people living in the CC catchment area. One point was given to a response for each discussion category if it contained a positive response. All the points were then summed up to generate a combined score for each CC in each of the GMP areas. The majority (more than 90%) of the CCs in both GMP areas scored 3 out of 4, which underscores the similarities in characteristics among CCs. In the next stage, 30 CCs were randomly selected by computer-generated simple random sampling from the total of 72 CCs in the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP subdistricts. The 30 FGMP CCs were then individually matched with the 30 FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP CCs based on the closest scores. Thus, the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP-to-FGMP CC ratio was 1:1.

Selection and recruitment of study participants

The study included children aged 6–24 months and their mothers/caregivers in Community clinics’ catchment areas. Eligibility criteria encompassed children currently free from known acute/chronic illnesses, caregivers reported severely stunted, wasted, or underweight children were excluded due to ethical obligation as the study would not provide treatment or management, not enrolled in similar programs, and whose caregivers provided consent. The youngest child in each household was designated as the index child, with twins excluded to prevent potential information biases. In case of participant absence, the next eligible household was approached during data collection. The same mother-child pair were interviewed after the next 12 months in a closed cohort manner, meaning the children lost for any reason were not replaced with another participant.

Components of GMP

The GMP program was designed to include regular check-ups for child growth, happening every three months at health facilities. The components of GMP outlined in this section represent the ideal elements of a GMP program as it should be, rather than reflecting the practical realities of implementation. Health workers were advised to counsel mothers and caregivers on their children’s nutrition, measure the weight, length, or mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of all 6–24 months old children, and plot the results on a growth monitoring chart in the GMP card. If health workers identified a child was acutely ill or not growing well during GMP he/she should refer the child to nearest medical/treatment center6.

Facility- based GMP (FGMP) program

The GoB has implemented a GMP program in its CCs since 2011. Each CC in the current study was meant to have three healthcare staff responsible for providing GMP to every child under two years of age, whether they visited for illness or routine check-ups. The government had no child tracking system in the government health facilities during the study period.

FGMP + NGO-supported home-based GMP (FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP) program

During the study period in Mymensingh, a single NGO partnered with the Government to support the GMP program in certain sub-districts. The NGO provided extra support by engaging its staff to provide home-based GMP services. They offered GMP sessions at home for children using a tracking system to ensure all children were reached by the NGO every 3 months with GMP. The NGO aimed to improve GMP utilization by creating counseling tools and ensuring a steady supply of GMP cards to caregivers at home. The GMP components were aligned with the previously outlined methodology6.

GMP service disruption during COVID-19

Due to the surge in COVID-19 infection countrywide, the Government imposed a countrywide lockdown from March 26th to May 30th. During the lockdown period, the GoB CC health facilities providers measured the weight and occasionally length of sick children who came to CCs. The CC service providers stopped measuring well-child at CC to reduce the risk of COVID-19 infection. The regular GMP services at CCs were resumed after the lockdown period. The NGO stopped the home-based GMP program during the COVID-19 lockdown period and never resumed it even after the lockdown was withdrawn; due to the withdrawal and relocation of funds from supporting GMP to COVID-19 prevention services by their donor. However, FGMP services resumed in the NGO-supported area once the lockdown was lifted.

Measurement of child’s nutritional status

Locally trained research assistants measured the length and body weight of the children using an infantometer and digital baby weighing scales following standard procedures19. The child’s weight was measured using the Seca 727 Baby Scale with a precision of 10 g. Child length was measured using a Seca Infantometer with 1 mm accuracy. Measurements were taken in the morning, with minimal clothing and without shoes. The scales were calibrated daily with standard apparatus/tools, and two repeated measurements were taken to ensure precision. Child nutritional status was determined by anthropometric indices for under-five children as length-for-age (LAZ), weight-for-height (WHZ), and weight-for-age (WAZ), and prevalence of stunting, wasting and underweight, as per World Health Organization (WHO) 2006 guidelines19. We analyzed child nutritional status using both continuous anthropometric z-scores—LAZ, WAZ, and WHZ—and their corresponding binary indicators: stunting (LAZ < -2), underweight (WAZ < -2), and wasting (WHZ < -2)19. Continuous scores were used to capture subtle changes in growth over time, which may not be reflected in binary classifications. Binary indicators were included to provide a clear interpretation of the public health burden and to align with national and global reporting standards. Presenting both types of measures offers a more comprehensive understanding of child growth and nutritional outcomes.

Measurement of caregivers’ age-appropriate child complimentary feeding practice (ACFP) indicators

Caregivers’ ACFP were defined as caregivers’ timely introduction of complementary feeding to their children and daily required minimum numbers of frequent and diverse meals as WHO recommended20. Four major complementary feeding indicators [(introduced to complementary foods (ISSSF), has a sufficiently diverse diet (MDD), and has a minimum number of frequent feed (MMF) and minimum adequate diet (MAD)] were used to construct the caregiver’s appropriate complementary feeding practices using a previously validated tool20,21. The mother was asked to recall her child’s food items intake including breastmilk for the preceding 24 h of interview, then the food items were grouped in eight different food groups as recommended by WHO20.

Food group scores: The food group scores were constructed by summarizing the eight food groups mentioned under minimum diverse diet (MDD). For each of the eight food groups, one point was added if the child consumed any food in the last 24 h before the survey20. The eight food groups were: breastmilk, grains/roots/tubers/plantains, legumes/nuts, dairy products, flesh foods (meat, fish, poultry, liver/organ meats), eggs, vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables, and other fruits and vegetables.

Outcome and confounder variables

This study focused on detecting the impact of FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and FGMP programs on the child’s attained length-for-age (LAZ). We also considered weight-for-age (WAZ), weight-for-height (WHZ), prevalence of stunting, wasting, and underweight as secondary outcomes for child nutritional status. Another important secondary outcome is the ACFP index. We also considered ISSSF, MDD, MAD, MMF, and food group scores as secondary outcomes.

Covariates that were identified as potential confounders included in the models: mother’s education in years, asset index, child age, child morbidity, and child complementary feeding counseling.

Sample size and sampling technique

We hypothesized that the LAZ of children 6–24 months of age would improve by 20% in the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP compared to the FGMP arm over the study period. For sample size calculations, several assumptions were made: the mean LAZ for children aged 6–12 months in the FGMP arm was − 1.8 with an SD of 1.3; 80% power; 5% significance level; 0.02 intracluster correlation coefficient; and 15% dropout rate22. Based on these assumptions, a sample size of 50 per cluster, 1510 per arm, and 3020 mother-child pairs in total was calculated. The study used a 60-cluster design (i.e., CCs and their catchment area) with 30 clusters per arm23. A total of 3038 mother-child pairs were enrolled, and 215 participants were lost, leaving 2823 participants for the final assessment. The final analysis included data from 2823 participants. The dropout rate was 7%, lower than the anticipated 15%, so the study power was maintained.

To complement the survey findings, only a few relevant participants (n = 36) were interviewed for the qualitative part during the first and final assessments. The qualitative participants were selected purposively from the program areas to supplement the quantitative findings, focusing on gathering in-depth insights into factors influencing caregiver’s child feeding practices. This approach was chosen to ensure a diverse range of perspectives, particularly those not captured through the quantitative surveys, such as caregivers’ source of knowledge, personal experiences, and beliefs related to child feeding practices. The number of qualitative interviews was decided following an iterative process of achieving information saturation, meaning we continued the interviews until no new information was received.

Data processing and analysis

Quantitative data

Fourteen data collectors conducted quantitative surveys with mothers and caregivers using a pre-tested semi-structured questionnaire. The first assessment occurred between August and November 2019, and the final between September and December 2020. As mentioned, the survey covered household sociodemographic, child anthropometry (length, weight), and caregivers’ feeding practices. Child morbidity information was collected by asking caregivers if the child had suffered from fever, common cold, runny nose, diarrhea, pneumonia, earache or itching within the 15 days preceding the interview. Data collectors also observed drinking water sources and toilets. Improved toilets included flush toilets, ventilated pit latrines, pit latrines with a slab, or composting toilets, while improved water sources were tube wells22. The wealth index was derived from household assets24. Data was entered and analyzed using MS Access and STATA 18. Data were checked for normality using Skewness/Kurtosis tests, and outliers were managed according to standard statistical/data analysis procedures. Categorical variables were summarized using proportions and frequencies. Normally distributed continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. Linear regression was applied for continuous outcomes (LAZ, WAZ, WHZ, food scores), while log-binomial regression was used for binary outcomes (stunting, underweight, wasting, ACFP, ISSSF, MMF, MDD, MAD). To adjust for baseline differences, a difference-in-difference (D-in-D) analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of the 12-month FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP versus FGMP programs on child nutritional status and complementary feeding practices. Due to a lack of true control group, we considered the FGMP-only group as a control group for D-I-D analysis. Initially, the unadjusted D-in-D estimate was obtained using the Stata diff package. Subsequently, multiple regression analysis was conducted, adjusting for maternal education (in years), asset index, and potential confounders, including child age, child morbidity, complementary feeding counseling, and the cluster effect. The interaction between time and study group was considered the D-in-D estimate. Results were considered significant with p-values less than 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative approaches were applied to enhance the quantitative survey findings. The qualitative interviews were carried out by one trained moderator and one interviewer whose native language was Bangla. Interviews were conducted in Bangla and later translated into English. Open-end questionnaires and guidelines were used for qualitative interviews. These tools included questions on caregivers’ child complementary feeding knowledge, perception, and practices, caregivers’ perception of child health and nutritional status, community clinic health care providers and NGO workers’ child feeding knowledge and counseling, and GMP service-related information. Two qualitative researchers employed a thematic analysis for all qualitative information. Qualitative analysis included thematic descriptions, analysis, and quotations from respondents. Transcribed interviews were coded based on predetermined themes, subthemes, and emergent issues. Trustworthiness was ensured through four principles: credibility, transferability, conformability, and dependability25. Intercoder or synchronic reliability was utilized to gauge the agreement between independent coders of the data. An agreement was assessed during the analysis when the researchers independently coded the same interviews. Triangulation was conducted between methods and participants, validating information by triangulating data from different approaches.

Ethics declarations

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr, b) approved the study (PR-17123, version 1.02, dated March 08th, 2018). The icddr, b’s IRB completely adheres and aligns with the Declaration of Helsinki. All research documents, and activities, including informed consent forms, were developed and implemented as per icddr, b’s IRB guidelines/regulations. Written informed consent with full disclosure about the study was taken from the participants and/or their legal guardians before all interviews. The respondents’ privacy, anonymity, and confidentiality of data/information identifying the study participants were strictly maintained. Only the principal investigators of this study had access to information on personal identification and other sensitive information that the respondents provided. The study was prospectively registered in January 2019 at ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT03824756).

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

Between August and December 2019, 3038 mother-child dyads were enrolled in this study, evenly split between the FGMP vs. FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP program areas. The participants included in the cohorts and analysis workflow are described in Fig. 1, based on the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations with Nonrandomized Designs (TREND)26, and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram27 (Supplementary file). During the final assessment period, 91 participants from FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and 124 participants from FGMP areas dropped out, mostly due to migration to other areas and child deaths. The migration was more in FGMP areas. However, the total dropouts were 7%, less than anticipated 15%, so the study power was maintained.

Participant flow diagram.

During the initial assessment, sixteen qualitative in-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted with government health facility workers, NGO workers, and 20 mothers of enrolled children. Fifteen IDIs with health facility workers and 15 IDIs with mothers of enrolled children were conducted during the final assessment.

The socio-demographic status was reported among the participants from both areas at enrolment without dropouts (N = 3038). At enrolment, children’s mean age was nine months, with a 1:1 male-to-female ratio in both groups (FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP vs. FGMP: 52% vs. 50%). Children’s nutritional status (LAZ, WAZ and WHZ, and prevalence of stunting, wasting and underweight) and caregivers’ age, sex, and education level distributions were similar between the groups during the initial assessment period (Table 1). Almost all mothers were housewives. Approximately 98% of household members identified as Muslim, with 94% and 92% having improved toilets in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and FGMP service areas, respectively (Table 1). As per the wealth index, 31% of caregivers in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas and 36% in FGMP areas were from poor families (Table 1).

Impact of FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP versus FGMP program on child nutritional status

Changes in child LAZ over time

From initial to final assessment, children in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP programs showed declines in mean LAZ from − 1.3 to -1.7. Children in FGMP programs also experienced reductions in mean LAZ from − 1.4 to -1.7 over the study period. The mean LAZ for children in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas was 4.5 lower than that of children in FGMP areas (difference-in-difference, (D-I-D): -4.5, 95% confidence interval (CI): -15.1, 6.1, p = 0.39), with no statistically significant differences between the programs.

Changes in child WAZ and WHZ over time

During the 12-month study period, children in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP programs showed declines in key anthropometric Z-scores. The average WAZ decreased from − 1.1 to -1.5, and the average WHZ decreased from − 0.5 to -0.8 from the initial to final assessment period, as the child grew up. Children in FGMP programs also experienced reductions in WAZ from − 1.1 to -1.4, and WHZ from − 0.4 to -0.8. The mean WAZ and WHZ for children in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas were also lower compared to those in FGMP areas, but did not significantly differ between the groups (WAZ, D-I-D: -3.5, 95% CI: -11.7, 4.6, p = 0.97; WHZ, D-I-D: 0.22, 95% CI: -13.5, 13.9, p = 0.18) (Table 2).

Changes in children with stunting, wasting, and underweight prevalence

The prevalence of stunted, wasted, and overweight children in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP programs increased from 26% to 37%, 7% to 11% and 20% to 29%, respectively, from the initial to final assessment period. In FGMP areas, the prevalence of stunted, wasted, and overweight children increased from 28% to 36%, 6% to 9%, and 20% to 26%, respectively, over the study period. The D-I-D analysis showed a 2.8% point increase in prevalence of stunted children in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas compared to FGMP areas over the 12-month study period (D-I-D: 2.8%, 95% CI: -1.3, 7.0, p = 0.18). The prevalence of wasted children increased in both GMP programs over time, but the changes between GMP programs were negligible and statistically non-significant (D-I-D: 0.43%, 95% CI: -3.2, 4.1, p = 0.82). Child underweight prevalence significantly increased by 5% points in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP compared to FGMP over time (D-I-D: 4.7%, 95% CI: 1.7, 7.8, p = 0.003) (Table 2).

Impact of FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP versus FGMP program on the caregivers’ complementary feeding practices

At the initial assessment, 24% of caregivers in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas and 16% in FGMP areas fed age-appropriate complementary foods to their children, which increased to 50% and 45% at final assessment, respectively. The proportion of caregivers providing appropriate complementary foods to their children did not differ between the 2 GMP programs over the study period (D-I-D: -1.4%, 95% CI: -6.5, 3.7, p = 0.58).

At the initial assessment, children in both FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and FGMP areas consumed food from an average of three food groups per day, which increased to four during the final assessment period (Table 3). The number of food groups consumed did not differ between the 2 GMP programs over time (D-I-D: -0.12, 95% CI: -0.3, 0.02, p = 0.09).

In FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas, 24% of children had a sufficiently diverse diet (MDD) at the initial assessment, which increased to 47% by the final assessment. In FGMP areas, MDD increased from 17% to 42% over the same period. MDD did not significantly differ between the programs (D-I-D: -1.5%, 95% CI: -6.1, 3.0, p = 0.51) over the study period.

The proportion of children who received the age-appropriate minimum required number of meals per day (MMF) increased from 80% to 94% in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and 75% to 95% in FGMP programs. The children from the FGMP program had a 5-percentage point increase in daily meal frequency compared to their counterparts (D-I-D: -4.7%, 95% CI: -9.1, -0.3, p = 0.04) over the study period.

In FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas, the proportion of children receiving MAD increased from 24% to 47% from the 1st to the final assessment. In FGMP areas, the proportion of children with MAD increased from 16% to 42%. However, the proportion of children with MAD did not differ over time between FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and FGMP programs (D-I-D: -2.0%, 95% CI: -6.6, 2.6, p = 0.38) (Table 3).

Qualitative findings on child feeding practices

Qualitative interviews were conducted with government healthcare providers, NGO workers, and mothers/caregivers to supplement the survey findings on child-feeding practices. Before the pandemic, Government FGMP healthcare providers regularly counseled mothers/caregivers on child-feeding practices during GMP visits, both individually and in group sessions. Four out of eight caregivers reported receiving such counseling before the pandemic. However, these services were significantly reduced during the pandemic, and by the final assessment, none of the caregivers cited FGMP programs as a source of child-feeding information. Instead, mothers/caregivers in FGMP areas consistently relied on family, friends, physicians, and rural informal healthcare providers for knowledge on child-feeding throughout the study period. Similarly, NGO workers provided regular counseling on child-feeding practices at home and during courtyard meetings before the pandemic, but these services ceased entirely during the pandemic. After NGO services stopped, mothers/caregivers in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas relied on family members, relatives, and friends for child-feeding knowledge.

Qualitative findings on GMP service disruption during COVID-19

Qualitative interviews with CHCPs and caregivers revealed substantial disruption in GMP service delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic across both FGMP and FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas. In FGMP settings, one provider shared, “We did not arrange any sessions during COVID, and most of the pregnant women avoided visiting the facility.” (CHCP, FGMP area). Another explained, “At that time, we only provided primary health care services but no GMP, to avoid COVID-19 contamination.” (CHCP, FGMP area). Similarly, a provider from an NGO-supported area stated, “During COVID, there were no GMP activities in the clinic and community. Seven months later, the activities were resumed in clinics, but no one from NGO came to my home to measure my child.” (CHCP, FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP area).

Caregivers from both FGMP and FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP areas echoed these challenges. Three caregivers from NGO-supported areas noted, “No one visited my home for GMP during COVID, and I didn’t know where to take my child for measurement (growth monitoring).” (Caregiver, FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP area). Another caregiver shared, “The baby’s measurement was only taken once by the NGO workers before the COVID pandemic, but they haven’t gone any further or offered any advice or service (GMP) anymore.” (Caregiver, FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP area). Mothers from FGMP areas also reported disruptions: “There were no activities to measure children’s weight and height in our area during the COVID lockdown.” (Caregiver, FGMP area).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the impact of FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and the Government’s FGMP programs on child nutritional status over 12 months. Despite improvements in complementary feeding practices, average LAZ, WAZ, and WHZ declined in both groups, and the prevalence of stunting, underweight, and wasting increased over time. These trends suggest that GMP interventions, as currently implemented, may not be sufficient to address the complex and multifactorial drivers of undernutrition, especially in resource-constrained settings28. While positive changes in MMF, MDD, and MAD were observed, these cannot be directly attributed to the GMP programs. Many caregivers reported relying on family, friends, or physicians rather than GMP personnel for feeding advice. This reliance suggests that family members and other influential community members should be included in counseling sessions to improve child-feeding practices29.

The decline in child growth indicators in both GMP programs groups is consistent with findings from other studies conducted during the COVID-19 crisis in LMICs, where interruptions in health services and increased stress have negatively affected child nutrition outcomes30. Notably, the increased child growth faltering in FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP group is of interest although the differences between groups were not significant, which differs from studies where HGMP programs yielded better or similar outcomes12–14. This may indicate that HGMP programs are more vulnerable to disruptions, such as those caused by the pandemic31. However, the key nutritional outcome of child LAZ did not significantly differ between the groups after 12 months of intervention, consistent with studies from India and South Africa13,32,33. Structured nutrition programs like GMP often help caregivers to improve child-feeding practices in LMIC12,14,30. The improvement in the caregiver’s complementary feeding practices in both GMP programs aligns with this result14. The FGMP program showed improvements in caregivers’ child-feeding practices, which contrasts with studies favoring HGMP programs in LMICs12–14. This discrepancy between our study in rural Bangladesh and other LMIC suggests that the setting of GMP services, whether FGMP or FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP—can impact their effectiveness.

There are several biological and sociological factors behind the study’s observed outcomes. The child’s MMF was significantly better and the prevalence of underweight was significantly lower in the FGMP compared to FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP program from initial to final assessment period. This change may be partly related to more regular contact with health providers in FGMP settings, which could offer more structured opportunities for counseling34. However, this interpretation is speculative, as we did not directly assess service quality or consistency in this study. Moreover, in the absence of a control group without any GMP intervention, it is difficult to determine the extent to which these changes can be directly attributed to the programs themselves. In contrast, the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP program faced financial challenges due to lack of donor support in maintaining program continuity and caregiver engagement, particularly during the pandemic when home visits were limited30,31,35,36.

The greater decline in linear growth observed in the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP group could also reflect the combined effects of reduced access to nutrition services and increased caregiver stress during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in the absence of continued NGO support during that period37,38. Qualitative data support this interpretation, with providers and caregivers reporting complete suspension of GMP activities during lockdowns and confusion about where to access services. Even after activities resumed, caregivers noted that services were minimal and lacked counseling, underscoring the fragility of outreach-based GMP delivery. These findings highlight how fragile GMP delivery systems were during the pandemic and underscore the need for resilient, adaptable service models that can maintain core functions and communication with caregivers in times of crisis.

Despite the improvements in feeding practices, the prevalence of malnutrition increased in both groups during the study period. This finding suggests that improving feeding practices alone may not be sufficient and highlights the need for more comprehensive, multi-sectoral interventions that address not only caregiver behavior but also food insecurity, maternal nutrition, healthcare access, and environmental conditions affecting child growth in rural Bangladesh39. The improvement in child MMF and underweight prevalence observed in government FGMP programs suggest that government health systems are more resilient and effective in maintaining child nutrition and underweight, especially during crises like the COVID-19 pandemic40.

Public health strategies should consider strengthening the continuity and resilience of GMP service delivery particularly during emergencies. Recommendations include integrating family members into counseling efforts, ensuring regular community outreach, incorporating home visits during service disruptions, and training frontline workers to provide consistent, high-quality counseling. In settings where NGO support phases out or is interrupted—as seen during the COVID-19 pandemic—establishing government-led contingency plans and leveraging digital or mobile platforms could help maintain essential nutrition services for children37,41,42.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the implementation of GMP programs in rural Bangladesh. However, it does not provide conclusive evidence that these programs improved child nutritional status during the study period. While improvements in caregiver feeding practices are promising in both the FGMP and NGO-supported HGMP + FGMP programs, they did not translate into improved nutritional outcomes. Notably, the FGMP program showed relatively better outcomes for MMF and improved underweight prevalence over time. These findings underscore the need for strengthened and more resilient GMP service delivery models—particularly during periods of disruption like the COVID-19 pandemic. Future strategies should prioritize consistent support to frontline providers, integrate family and community members into nutrition counseling, and design flexible approaches that can sustain essential services during emergencies.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it was conducted in only a few subdistricts of rural Bangladesh, excluding urban areas, potentially compromising the representation of the country’s overall GMP program implementation scenario. The unprecedented nature of the pandemic and the resulting program disruptions were beyond control and could have affected the outcomes. Although the data were collected in 2019–2020, the key features of GMP service delivery and the broader challenges facing child nutrition in rural Bangladesh remain largely unchanged, making the findings relevant for current program planning and policy discussions. Furthermore, reliance on caregivers’ and healthcare providers’ self-reported data introduces potential recall bias, and the generalizability of the findings may be limited to similar contexts and populations. A few more factors may have influenced the validity and reliability of the study findings. These include selection bias due to non-randomized sampling, exclusion of children with known chronic illness, severely malnourished children, and twins, and the disruption of GMP services during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our study included only apparently healthy, disease-free children, which might introduce a selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to children with underlying health conditions. To address selection bias, we matched socio-demographic parameters using a community scorecard during the selection of study areas and randomly selected participants into clusters. The GMP service was disrupted entirely in the FGMP + NGO-supported HGMP and partially in the Government FGMP program during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the improvements in caregivers’ complementary feeding practices in both groups may have mitigated the impact of program disruption. The study’s rigorous observation and robust design support the validity and reliability of the findings.
