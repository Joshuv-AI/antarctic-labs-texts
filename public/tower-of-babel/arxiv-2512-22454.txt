Comparing Object Detection Models for Electrical Substation Component Mapping
Namish Bansal, Haley Mody, Dennies Kiprono Bor, Dante Groccia, Edward J. Oughton

Abstract
Electrical substations are a significant component of an electrical grid. Indeed, the assets at these substations (e.g., transformers) are vulnerable to hazards such as hurricanes, flooding, earthquakes, and geomagnetically induced currents (GICs). Because failures can have significant economic and public safety implications, identifying key substation components is essential for quantifying vulnerability. Unfortunately, traditional manual mapping of substation infrastructure is time-consuming and labor-intensive. Therefore, an autonomous solution utilizing computer vision models is preferable, as it offers greater convenience and efficiency. In this study, we train and compare 16 models on a manually labeled dataset of US substation images. These models include 12 You Only Look Once (YOLO) models, 2 Roboflow Detection Transformer (RF-DETR) models, and 2 Cascade R-CNN models. RF-DETR-large achieved the highest overall detection performance with mAP@50 and mAP@50:95 scores of 0.881 and 0.632, respectively. Across all models, alternate energy systems were detected most accurately, while transformers and reactors were more difficult to identify due to their smaller size and greater visual variability. Applying our best-performing model to nationwide imagery yielded approximately 22,591 component detections across 11,083 unique substations within the United States. These detections were broken down by state and Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) regions, with Florida (2,478 detections) and Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO; 4,329 detections) having the largest number of detections in their respective categories.

Manually mapping out substations is not a feasible solution due to the sheer volume of the grid and its labor-intensive and time-consuming nature. Additionally, the fragmented nature of the grid leads to inconsistent data collection and labeling practices across regions, making consistency and standardization difficult across datasets

Beyond single-class detection, RF-DETR also achieved higher precision in multi-class detection, with a mean average precision at an intersection over union (IoU) threshold of 0.50 (mAP@50) of 0.830, compared to the other models. The achieved efficiency in distinguishing between classes that need to be detected and obstacles makes it optimal for component localization under high spatial density
(
Sapkota et al., 2025
)
. Recent transformer architectures such as RF-DETR, Fine-grained Distribution Refinement (D-FINE), and Light-Weight Detection Transformer (LW-DETR) have established new Pareto bounds over convolutional neural network (CNN)-based YOLO models on general benchmarks like Common Objects in Context (COCO) and Roboflow 100 for vision-language models (RF100-VL)
(
Peng et al., 2024
;
Chen et al., 2024
;
Robinson et al., 2025

.

Cascade-based detectors have also occasionally been applied to substation equipment monitoring. One study proposed an infrared image recognition algorithm for power transmission and substation equipment built on an improved Cascade region-based convolutional neural network (R-CNN) framework for automatic recognition of electrical devices such as transformers and switches in substation environments
(
Li et al., 2026
)
. Additionally, on the Northeastern University surface-defect detection (NEU-DET) dataset, the standard Cascade R-CNN model was used as a comparison point to an enhanced version; the mAP increased to 75%, an increase of almost 9.2 percentage points over the unmodified Cascade R-CNN. Similarly, in another evaluation of defect detectors for photovoltaic equipment components, Cascade R-CNN was one of the eight benchmarked models. While it successfully detected all defect instances present, it showed reduced bounding-box localization precision relative to newer detectors, at times splitting a single defect into two separate detections
(
Jia et al., 2024
)
. However, studies relating to Cascade-based models detecting electrical substation components are often rare; our study aims to address this gap by comparing Cascade R-CNN against YOLO and RF-DETR models.

In addition to dataset size, environmental conditions often pose challenges for reliable object detection. Some types of visual obstructions can include fog, smoke, mist, or even blurry images. In a past study
(
Zhang et al., 2022
)
, the Synthetic Foggy Insulator Dataset (SFID) was utilized to introduce synthetic fog and assess detection performance under environmental obstacles. They created a new model called Foggy Insulator Network (FINet), built on the YOLOv5 framework, enhanced with a channel attention mechanism to detect infrastructure on the SFID.

To ensure a fair evaluation, all models were trained and tested using a single, consistent dataset, minimizing discrepancies when comparing precision and efficiency of different models. However, to fully evaluate models, future research should account for these data consistency and bias issues.

To include a more comprehensive review of power substation research, existing studies only evaluate a single model or a single model family
(
Yiğit, 2025
)
. For example, a 2024 study
(
He et al., 2024
)

However, the study was limited to the YOLO family only and did not include transformer-based or two-stage detectors, failing to compare whether YOLO could truly outperform other model families.

Furthermore, work that is focused on aerial power infrastructure detection has tended to evaluate models in isolation rather than comparatively. A previous research study modified YOLOv8 to detect electrical pylons from satellite imagery, comparing it against baseline YOLOv8 but not against alternative architecture families. )
.

Based on the review carried out, our study aims to fill a major gap in the literature: component-level identification, specifically, the detection of individual electrical sub-components. To do so, we are evaluating 16 model configurations: YOLOv8, YOLOv11, and YOLOv12, each in nano (n), small (s), medium (m), and large (l) variants, RF-DETR Base and Large, and Cascade R-CNN with 50-layer and 101-layer residual network (ResNet50 and ResNet101) backbones on a single dataset.

Model Training and Substation Mapping

In this section, we aim to develop and train models that can identify and differentiate between substation components. Our goal is to determine which model is the most accurate and precise, as well as to compare efficiencies in training time and inference speed. From there, we want to use the selected mapping model to make predictions on substations across the contiguous United States (CONUS), using imagery from the National Agriculture Imagery Program (NAIP).

3.1
Model Preparation

3.1.1
Dataset Creation

Our dataset will be built using open-source imagery of U.S. substations and transmission lines. Specifically, we will utilize a full-stack web-based geographic information system (webGIS) user interface, which leverages Google Earth’s satellite and aerial imagery
(
Bor et al., 2026
)
. Using this website, we will be able to extract images of electrical substations, which we can train our model on.

3.1.2
Dataset Labeling

From this application, we will collect 250 images for each component. As individual images of substations could contain more than one image class, some images were included in multiple class collections. Each image typically contains multiple substation components, which requires individual labeling. We will manually annotate each image by drawing precise boxes around each substation component present in the picture and assigning the appropriate class label, as shown in Figure (
2
b). We will consolidate these images into one dataset, and after removing any duplicate images across class-specific conditions, we will create a dataset of 1,389 images.

3.1.3
Pre-Processing

We will split our dataset of 1,389 images into training, validation, and testing data (via a 75-15-10 split), resulting in a total of 1,042 training, 210 validation, and 137 test images
(
Ghahremani et al., 2025
;
Egi et al., 2026
;
Vrsalovic et al., 2025
)
. Before training, to help ensure consistency and enhance model performance, we will apply standardized preprocessing techniques to the dataset
(
Sapkota and Karkee, 2025
;
Pottier and Lau, 2025
)
. This consists of resizing and auto-orienting the images. Several machine learning models, including YOLO, require images to have a fixed image size. To accommodate this, the images will be resized to 640x640 using letterbox padding, which will put black edges around the images to resize them while maintaining the original aspect ratio of the image, as shown in Figure (
2
c). Auto-orientation will also be applied to the images to correct any misalignments, ensuring that all the components appear in standardized positions
(
Huang et al., 2024
;
Baig et al., 2025
;
Mishra et al., 2025
)
.

3.2
Model Training

After creating a dataset, it is necessary to determine which models the dataset will be trained on. We will utilize various model architectures, including single-stage, two-stage, and transformer-based architectures, to determine the optimal model for this case. Specifically, we will select one family that uses each architecture: YOLO for a single-stage approach, Cascade for a two-stage approach, and RF-DETR for a transformer-based approach
(
Cheng et al., 2026
;
Carion et al., 2020
)
. Comparing these architectures will allow us to evaluate their differences in inference speed and precision.

3.2.1
YOLO Models Overview

YOLO is a single-stage object detection model with advanced data augmentation, a focal loss function, and a PyTorch-based architecture for optimization. Due to the speed at which YOLO models can be trained and YOLO’s prevalence in object detection, we will utilize YOLO to showcase the effects of changing model speed and model generation. To demonstrate this, we will use YOLOv8, YOLOv11, and YOLOv12, each in nano/small/medium/large variants
(
Ramos and Sappa, 2025
;
Pan et al., 2025
;
Chaman et al., 2026
;
Shalash et al., 2026
;
Ali et al., 2026
)
.

The YOLO family has been widely applied across many domains. In the electrical substation component area specifically, many studies have utilized the YOLO family to detect electrical substation components. For example, one study applied YOLOv8 to real-time aerial inspection of high-voltage power line components using UAV footage, reaching speeds of over 243 frames per second (fps) on a graphics processing unit (GPU) while accurately detecting insulator caps and line hardware
(
Bellou et al., 2024
)
. Similarly, another study modified YOLOv8 to detect electrical pylons from optical satellite imagery, including a fast spatial pyramid pooling module with dynamic snake convolution and large selective kernels (DSLSK-SPPF), and an efficient multiscale convolution detection head (EMS-Head) to improve detection of complex shapes
(
Chi et al., 2024
)
. In insulator-specific work, another study proposed adaptive path fusion YOLO (APF-YOLO), an enhanced model with a corresponding fusion neck, improving detection of diverse insulator sizes in UAV imagery
(
Aitelhaj et al., 2025
)
. In addition to power infrastructure detection, YOLO models have been used for construction safety monitoring for personal protective equipment (PPE) and near-miss detection
(
Ali et al., 2026
)
and in medical imaging for fracture detection in X-rays
(
Ferdi, 2025
)
.

The commonality regarding substation infrastructure studies in this subject is that they share a limitation of evaluating/comparing YOLO models in isolation or comparing only within the YOLO family. For example, one study utilized five YOLO generations on power equipment but did not include any outside detection models such as transformer-based or two-stage detectors
(
He et al., 2024
)
. By including YOLO versions 8, 11, and 12 with all variants, we can compare inference speed with precision and compare against Cascade models and RF-DETR.

3.2.2
RF-DETR Overview

RF-DETR was developed and released by Roboflow
(
Robinson et al., 2025
)
. Most recently developed, the RF-DETR model is based on the DINO version 2 (DINOv2) backbone and is especially useful for detecting images in highly cluttered areas. RF-DETR is the sole model to achieve 60+ mean Average Precision when trained on the COCO dataset as well, highlighting not only its precision but its efficiency as well
(
Robinson et al., 2025
)
. We will utilize two models in the RF-DETR family: RF-DETR and RF-DETR-large.

In a direct comparison with YOLOv12, one study found that RF-DETR outperformed YOLOv12x, YOLOv12l, and YOLOv12n in both single-class and multi-class detection of green fruit in an intricate environment, achieving a precision of 0.946 and an mAP@50 of 0.830
(
Sapkota et al., 2025
)
. Similarly, another study utilized RF-DETR for container damage detection
(
Kumar, 2025
)
. In medical imaging, one study applied RF-DETR to mitotic figure detection, achieving an F1 score (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) of 0.789 with a recall of 0.839 and precision of 0.746
(
Giedziun et al., 2025
)
.

We will include RF-DETR because its DINOv2 backbone is specifically designed for cluttered, multi-class images where objects are tight and close together, which is often the case in substation imagery. Additionally, its DINOv2 backbone has demonstrated strong performance on domain generalization benchmarks, outperforming prior real-time models on RF100-VL, a benchmark specifically designed to measure generalization across real-world detection tasks
(
Robinson et al., 2025
)
. This makes it a strong candidate for detecting electrical substation imagery.

3.2.3
Cascade Models Overview

The Cascade R-CNN architecture uses a sequential chain of detection trained on progressively higher IoU thresholds. This “cascade” method utilizes the outputs of one stage as the input for the next, utilizing ResNet50 and ResNet101 backbones
(
Cai and Vasconcelos, 2018
)
.

Within the electrical substation domain, Cascade R-CNN has proven to be highly effective for the detection of intricate or smaller components. For instance, one study implemented a Cascade R-CNN framework to detect defects in transmission line components, including vibration dampers and insulators, finding that the ResNet101 backbone achieved the highest detection precision
(
Zhao et al., 2023b
)
. Similarly, another study developed an improved Cascade R-CNN model incorporating the shifted-window transformer version 2 (Swin-v2) to address scale variation in UAV images of transmission line components
(
Dong et al., 2023
)
. Beyond power infrastructure, Cascade R-CNN has been applied to food recognition benchmarks, where Cascade models outperformed other models, and to remote sensing instance segmentation tasks for large multi-channel aerial imagery
(
Carvalho et al., 2021
)
.

By training Cascade R-CNN with ResNet50 and ResNet101 alongside YOLO and RF-DETR models, we can directly compare the architectures and assess whether the progressive improvements translate into increased model precision.

3.2.4
Model Implementation and Evaluation

To train the selected models, the annotated dataset will be exported and converted into the YOLO PyTorch plain-text (TXT) format for our YOLO-based models and the COCO JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) format for Cascade and RF-DETR. All models will be implemented in PyTorch using a GPU-accelerated environment and trained for up to 50 epochs. Training performance and model checkpoints will be logged across iterations. Following training, detection accuracy will be evaluated across both individual component classes and full-model performance using mAP at an IoU threshold of 0.50 (mAP@50) and mAP averaged over IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 in increments of 0.05 (mAP@50:95). Average precision for an individual class at an IoU threshold of 0.50 is denoted AP50.

3.3
Model Bootstrapping

We recognize that one run of the model testing data does not accurately reflect model performance. Therefore, to effectively quantify the uncertainty associated with the modeling approach, we will perform bootstrapping utilizing the 91 test images of the models with the following per-class breakdown: alternate energy systems have 54 annotations, circuit breakers have 153 annotations, transformers have 61 annotations, and reactors have 58 annotations. We will resample the test images with replacement 1,000 times, calculating the mAP@50, mAP@50:95, and per-class AP50 values for each substation component category. This will produce a distribution of scores, from which we will generate a 95% confidence interval (CI) of values, which will be included in our result graphs in Section 4.

3.4
Substation Mapping

To observe the effectiveness of our model, we will utilize YOLOv8-Small for mapping out the number of components in substations across the United States of America. To do this, we will use a pre-made dataset of substations found using open-source Earth mapping imagery and import a comma-separated values (CSV) file of their coordinates.

From there, we will use the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) NAIP to extract images from each coordinate. A square of approximately 150 m x 150 m will be extracted, and RGB imagery will be extracted from the most recent available imagery from NAIP. NAIP provides nationwide orthophotography at approximately 0.6 m spatial resolution (the length and width each pixel covers), which makes it suitable for identifying large substation components while remaining realistically publicly accessible. The mapping dataset contains 11,085 images at 2048 pixels.

We will take these images and run inference on them using the mapping model. We aggregate the predicted components from these images by state using the Census 2018 1:500,000 boundaries and by FERC Order 1000 transmission planning region using the supplied planning-region polygons. Geographic summaries retain images assigned to both a state or the District of Columbia (DC) and a planning region. Two images (identifiers 832 and 6153), containing five retained detections, fall outside the planning-region polygons and are excluded from these summaries. State densities use Census land area, and planning-region densities use the area of the dissolved polygons using coordinate system 5070 in the European Petroleum Survey Group (EPSG) registry; both are reported per 100 km
2
.

4
Results

4.1
Model Training Results

In this section, we examined the results of the model training and determined a model to use for substation mapping.

To evaluate model performance, we used two object detection metrics: mAP@50 and mAP@50:95, or Mean Average Precision, measuring how accurately the model detected and localized objects across all the classes by averaging precision scores across recall levels. The @50 threshold means a detection is counted as correct if the predicted bounding box overlaps with the true bounding box by at least 50%. mAP@50:95 is a stricter metric that averages performance across ten overlap thresholds ranging from 50% to 95% in 5% increments. AP50 considers an IoU threshold of 0.50 between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes, whereas mAP@50:95 averages across multiple IoU thresholds, rewarding detectors that achieve more accurate localization across varying levels of strictness
(
Tian et al., 2024
)
.

Figure 3:

The figure above shows the mAP@50 and mAP@50:95 scores for all 16 models with their respective confidence intervals (CI). As shown in Figure (
3
a), RF-DETR Large achieved the highest overall mAP@50 of 0.881, CI [0.814, 0.938], followed by RF-DETR Base at 0.826, CI [0.757, 0.884]. The RF-DETR family also achieved the highest overall mAP@50:95, with RF-DETR Large achieving an mAP@50:95 of 0.632, CI [0.585, 0.679], and RF-DETR Base achieving an mAP@50:95 of 0.5245, CI [0.478, 0.572]. RF-DETR’s heightened performance compared to the other models is due to its heavily trained DINOv2 backbone and ability to perform well in complex and obstructed areas.

The next best-performing model family was YOLO. In Figure (
3
), YOLO versions 8, 11, and 12 are shown, plotted for each model size (nano/small/medium/large). The best performing model was YOLOv8s, with an mAP@50 of 0.623, CI [0.541, 0.701] and mAP@50:95 of 0.420, CI [0.363, 0.477], whereas the worst performing model was YOLOv12l, with an mAP@50 of 0.422, CI [0.361, 0.481], and an mAP@50:95 of 0.243, CI [0.202, 0.286].

A trend emerged where, for the most part, smaller model architectures and older models performed better on this dataset. In YOLOv11 and YOLOv12, the nano-sized YOLO version had the highest mAP@50 (0.605, CI [0.523, 0.689] for YOLOv11, and 0.596, CI [0.517, 0.672] for YOLOv12) and mAP@50:95 values, followed by the small, medium, and large values. In YOLOv8, the small version performed the best, followed by the medium value, but the nano version was within 0.020 mAP@50 and 0.018 mAP@50:95 of the small value. This result can be attributed to overfitting. As our dataset is relatively smaller, larger models can overtrain on the data, resulting in a worse overall performance.

Additionally, we observed that older YOLO model architectures performed better than newer ones on this dataset. The average mAP@50 for YOLOv8 was 0.584, which was higher than YOLOv11’s mAP@50 of 0.551 and YOLOv12’s mAP@50 of 0.515. The average mAP@50:95 for YOLOv8 was 0.386, which was higher than YOLOv11’s mAP@50:95 of 0.351 and YOLOv12’s mAP@50:95 of 0.322. The reason older YOLO architectures perform better is that our dataset is relatively small and its domain is relatively unique, so the architectural complexity of newer YOLO models doesn’t translate into improved performance, as consistent with prior studies
(
Sengun et al., 2025
)
.

4.2
Most Accurately Detected Components

Based on our model training, we found that the most accurately detected component was alternate energy systems, with an average per-class AP50 of 0.570. Alternate systems are often the most visually distinct and have a repetitive rectangular and rack-like structure, allowing the models to detect them consistently regardless of model type. Circuit breakers had a lower average, with an average per-class AP50 of 0.528, whereas the least accurately detected were reactors, with an average AP50 of 0.460, and transformers with an average AP50 of 0.347. They are smaller and less visually distinctive, making the models harder to detect.

5
Discussion

Here, we aim to go back and review the results of the model training and inference mapping in the context of the initial research questions.

1.

What is the best object detection model that can be utilized to identify and differentiate between substation components effectively?

As shown in Figure (
3
), RF-DETR Large achieved the highest mAP@50 among the sixteen models tested, reaching an mAP@50 of 0.881. Among the YOLO family, the YOLOv8-Small variant performed the best at 0.623, outperforming the other YOLOv11 and YOLOv12 variants.

2.

What components are most accurately detected and why?

Across all 16 models, alternate energy systems were the most accurately detected class, reaching the highest AP50 of 0.951. Since alternate systems tend to be large and share repetitive rack-like structures, their appearances are clearer and easier for models to detect. Transformers and reactors were the most difficult classes, averaging 0.347 and 0.46, respectively. Especially since these components tend to be smaller, the models did not detect them as well. Circuit breakers fell in the middle range, performing moderately across all model families. Overall, we have observed that object size, their distinctiveness relative to their background, and their uniformity affect how well the models detect them.

Detection models, regardless of what type they are, usually require clear and well-defined features to draw accurate bounding boxes and assign them to the correct classes. Larger components like alternate energy systems occupy more pixels and have more consistent recognizable shapes across images, giving the models more to learn from. Smaller components like reactors and transformers are harder to distinguish due to their smaller size and the higher variability present in their dataset.

3.

Can we effectively count the total number of nationwide substation components in the United States and analyze them by both state and Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 1000 planning regions?

Using the mapping model, we obtained 22,591 component detections across the 11,083 sampled substation images retained in the geographic analysis within the United States.

According to Figure (
5
a), Florida led the nation with 2,478 total components, almost double the next closest state (Iowa with 1,247 components). Across these top-10 states, Circuit Breakers and Transformers made up the majority of the component mix, with Alternate Energy contributing smaller shares in states like Colorado and Tennessee. Furthermore, in Figure (
6
b), we found that a small cluster of states, mostly in the Southeast and Central U.S., had the highest component counts. Figures (
5
b) and (
6
a) supported the notion that MISO, PJM, and SERTP had the highest component counts of 4,329, 4,252, and 3,832, respectively, which had almost double the number of components as the next FERC Order 1000 planning region cluster (FRCC). A third, much smaller group of FERC Order 1000 planning regions, NGC, CAISO, ERCOT, etc., has half of the previous; NYISO was the only outlier with 46 components.

Together, these results confirmed that our detection pipeline could identify individual components and analyze them by both state and FERC Order 1000 planning region.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we evaluated the effectiveness and efficiency of 16 object detection model configurations spanning YOLOv8, YOLOv11, YOLOv12, RF-DETR, and Cascade for the automated mapping of substation components, including transformers, reactors, circuit breakers, and alternate energy infrastructure. We performed a literature review to identify past/current gaps in substation detection methodology. Our study aimed to address these gaps by using the same dataset to train all three model families and to compare the models. Our dataset included images taken from open-source imagery of U.S. substation equipment.

All in all, the RF-DETR Large model achieved the highest mean average precision (mAP) of 0.881, with a CI of 0.814 to 0.938, while YOLOv8-Small performed best among the YOLO family, with the highest overall detection accuracy of 0.623 with a CI of 0.541 to 0.701. While YOLO variants offered significantly faster inference speeds, RF-DETR Large achieved the highest precision and well-rounded performance across all substation components. Our use of the mapping model on national-scale imagery demonstrated their practical, real-world utility, rather than serving as a baseline comparison. Ultimately, this research not only offers a clear, evidence-based foundation for selecting detection models suited to specific precision and speed constraints but also a framework for automated substation infrastructure counts for the future.
