Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy in critical illness: A practice survey of intensivists in Australia and New Zealand
Authors: Mairi H Northcott, Ashwin Subramaniam, Daryl Jones, Andrew Casamento, Yasmine Ali Abdelhamid, Deepak Bhonaghiri, Krishnaswamy Sundararajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe current percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) practice across Australian and New Zealand (ANZ) intensive care units (ICUs), quantify perceived procedural exposure among intensivists and trainees, and evaluate perceptions of competency and training adequacy.DesignCross-sectional, anonymous, online survey of 11–16 questions, depending on clinical role, investigating: frequency of PDT, demographics of respondents, their experience in performing and supervising PDT in 2024 and their opinion about processes for training and maintaining procedural competency.SettingANZ ICUs.ParticipantsCollege of Intensive Care Medicine trainees and specialists.ResultsOf 321 respondents, 270 complete individual survey responses were analysed. Data from 23 ICUs indicated a median PDT rate of 0.41% (interquartile range [IQR]: 0.20–0.96%) of ICU admissions. Most clinicians performed two (IQR: 0–3) PDTs in 2024 and supervised a further two (IQR: 0–3). While most specialists felt confident performing or supervising PDT, fewer believed they performed enough to maintain competency, and many reported insufficient trainee opportunities. Procedural exposure was unevenly distributed: consultants performed significantly more PDTs than trainees (median [IQR]: 2 [1–3] vs. 1 [0–2]; p = 0.004) yet only five respondents recalled procedural frequency that would have met published annual thresholds (based on expert opinion) for maintaining competency.1 Free-text analysis identified four themes: declining volume of practice; concerns about skill fade and limited trainee exposure; support for supplemental training activities; and uncertainty over whether PDT should remain a universal ICU competency.ConclusionsPDT is now a low-volume and variably performed procedure across ANZ ICUs. Survey respondents reported that this is affecting training and maintenance of competency.Keywords: Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy, Procedural competency, Practice survey, Intensive care

Introduction

Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT) is an elective procedure performed by intensive care unit (ICU) doctors to facilitate weaning from mechanical ventilation, protect the upper airway, and assist with secretion management. First described by Ciaglia in 1985,2 PDT rapidly became established in critical care practice. Early multicentre studies demonstrated that 10.7% of mechanically ventilated patients in ICU received a tracheostomy,3 and subsequent studies over the past three decades have described a variable frequency of between 6% and 20%.[3], [4], [5], [6], [7] More recently, the frequency of PDT appears to be declining. An Australian study demonstrated that the proportion of ICU patients receiving tracheostomy halved to 4% between 2004 and 2014,8 while a large analysis from the United States similarly reported a decline in the frequency of tracheostomy since 2008.9

Despite these trends, no contemporary multicentre studies in the last decade have evaluated the current frequency or practice patterns of PDT in critically ill patients across Australian and New Zealand (ANZ). Anecdotally, reduced frequency of PDT in ANZ is reducing opportunities for procedural exposure. Diminishing procedural volume risks impairing skill development by reducing the ability to train registrars and to maintain procedural competency.

Concerns regarding increased complication rates have also emerged. Over the last 3 decades, several studies have reported higher complication rates amongst patients who underwent PDT when compared to surgical tracheostomy.[10], [11], [12], [13]

Workforce data highlight the scale of potential impact. In 2024, there were nearly 1500 College of Intensive Care Medicine (CICM) fellows and an estimated trainee cohort of approximately 1600, 196 of whom were newly registered in 2024.14 Maintaining procedural competence across such a workforce is challenging given declining opportunities for hands-on training.

Guidance on competency thresholds is inconsistent. A recent CHEST special report recommended that trainees should perform at least 20 PDTs to establish basic competencies and that dedicated operators should perform at least 10 PDT per year to maintain competencies.1 In contrast, the ANZICS Consensus Statement (2014) suggests only that trainees should perform “a sufficient number” of PDTs under close supervision to be deemed competent, with no standards for ongoing competency.15 The CICM Workplace Competency Assessment required trainees to perform at least one PDT at independent or oversight level on the entrustment scale to be deemed competent.16

Given declining frequency, variable training standards, and emerging concerns about complications, there is a critical need for contemporary data describing PDT practice in ANZ. To address this, we undertook this study aimed at characterising current practices surrounding PDT in critical illness in ANZ.

Methods

Study design, setting, survey development, and distribution

A web-based questionnaire was developed to survey ICU doctors across ANZ regarding current practices of PDT in critically ill patients during 2024 (Supplementary Appendix 2). The survey was designed following a literature review, refined through expert input, and piloted. The survey piloted 10 ICU specialists and trainees from the Royal Melbourne Hospital and Royal Adelaide Hospital. There were no significant changes made after the pilot. We used a mix of closed-ended responses as a drop-down menu using a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = unsure, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree) and open-ended responses as free text. Additionally, branching logic was used to explore specific responses. This anonymous questionnaire, consisting of 11–16 questions, was administered using SurveyMonkey™ (Momentive Inc., San Mateo, California, USA) (Supplementary Appendix 2).

Doctors practicing in adult ICUs across ANZ were invited to participate. Distribution occurred via the electronic mailing list of the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society (ANZICS) and through advertisement on the CICM website. The questionnaire remained open for eight weeks with a reminder sent two weeks after the initial distribution.

Data collection and management

Data were collected anonymously and participation was entirely voluntary. No financial incentives were offered. Completion of the survey was considered as implied consent as outlined on the survey landing page. Responses from the survey were exported from SurveyMonkey™ to a password-protected computer. We collected demographic data, including age, gender, country, state, years of experience, training stage, ICU type, and recency of ICU experience. Respondents could only access the survey once, and no duplicate responses were found on manual review of the data.

Heads of Unit (HOUs) who completed the survey were asked to provide data on the frequency of PDTs within their units, while Supervisors of Training (SOTs) were asked about the number of trainees in their respective units. All participants were invited to provide quantitative and qualitative information regarding their current PDT practices.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using primarily descriptive methods. Survey responses were reported as percentages of valid responses. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were summarised using median and interquartile range (IQR). We presented the findings as overall and by job title (HOU, SOT, other consultants and senior registrars). The 5-point Likert scale responses were compared using Chi-square tests. The Likert scale responses were converted to ordinal numerical values (1–5) and these median scores were displayed using radar plots. The nonparametric comparisons across job titles were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. All analyses were conducted as complete case analyses and any missing data were handled by a case-wise deletion in the individual analyses. If a respondent’s survey data were incomplete, erroneous, or approximated, their response was discarded prior to analysis of any of the data. No partial responses were included in our analysis. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Version 30 (Armonk, NY), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Free-text responses were analysed using an inductive thematic approach. Responses were independently coded by two investigators, with codes iteratively grouped into themes through discussion and consensus. Discrepancies were resolved through review to ensure consistency and rigour in theme development.

Ethics

This survey was approved via the quality assurance pathway by the Critical Care and Perioperative Services Department of the Central Adelaide Local Health Network (CAHLN QIP Register ID5971). We then sought approval for publication of the data from the Research Ethics and Governance Office of CALHN Research Services (CALHN Research Office Reference Number 21767).

Results

A total of 321 ICU clinicians anonymously responded to the survey. This represents approximately 43% of the ANZICS medical membership who were sent this survey.17 Of these, responses from 270 (84.1%) had required data for analysis. Responses were excluded if incomplete, used approximations in the numerical data, or contained typographical errors such that they were uninterpretable. Of the respondents, 78.9% (n = 213) were intensivists; of these, 8.9% (n = 24) were HOUs and 16.3% (n = 44) were SOTs.

Demographics

Most survey respondents identified as male (71.5%; n = 193) and 41.1% (n = 111) had more than 15 years’ experience in ICU. A total of 89.3% were from Australia, with the highest proportion from Victoria (33.3%; n = 90), followed by New South Wales (16.7%; n = 45) and Queensland (15.9%; n = 43). Fifty percent (n = 135) of respondents worked less than full time. Only a very small proportion (7.8%; n = 21) had greater than 3 months leave in 2024. Almost all (99.3%) respondents worked in a unit accredited for CICM trainees and 73.7% (n = 199) worked in tertiary public hospital settings (Table 1).

Frequency of PDTs

Twenty-three HOUs from across a variety of ICUs across ANZ responded with number of ICU admissions and number of PDTs performed in their units in 2024, respectively. It was not specified the source used to obtain these data. Across these units, there were 40,220 ICU admissions in 2024. During these admissions, 675 tracheostomies were performed. 53.8% (363/675) of these tracheostomies were PDTs (ranged between 40% and 56% across the range of hospitals). This equated to 7.8 PDTs per 1000 ICU admissions (ranged between 2.8 and 9.7 across the type of hospitals; Supplementary Table 1). There was variability in the frequency of PDT in 2024 across the units (median = 8, IQR: 2.5–24). PDTs were performed in a median of 0.41% (IQR: 0.20–0.96%) of all ICU admissions (median of centre-level proportions), and the pooled patient-level proportion was 0.93% (375/40,220).

Operator data

Overall, the respondents performed a median of one PDT (IQR: 0–3) as the primary operator (Table 2). This significantly varied across the respondents. Consultants (which includes HOU, SOTs and non-HOU, non-SOT consultants) reported higher numbers of PDTs as the primary operator, when compared to senior registrars (p = 0.004) (Fig. 1a). Similarly, the respondents supervised a median of two PDTs (IQR: 0–3). This also varied between consultants and senior registrars (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1b).

Declining case volume and changing practice

Respondents consistently reported that PDT frequency has seen notable reductions over time: “our tracheostomy rates have gone down substantially…patients are benefiting but trainees are struggling”. This decline was attributed to earlier extubation, increased use of non-invasive ventilation and high flow nasal oxygenation, and changes in case mix: “previous groups like spinal cord injured also not getting traches”. Practice varied widely amongst units, with some tertiary centres performing 30–40 PDTs annually and others reporting “only two PDTs in the last three years”. Respondents reported tracheostomies being outsourced to ear, nose, and throat (ENT)or other surgical specialities"the culture in my unit is…risk-averse…[they] refer to ENT if even slight risk of complications". This variability and overall reduction in case volume were viewed as major barriers to training and maintenance of competence.

Competence, training, and skill maintenance

Concerns about recency of practice and skill fade were common, particularly among consultants who perform only one or two PDTs each year. One noted, “I average two per year…but for me to maintain my skill means trainees get less opportunity”. While historically experienced clinicians felt somewhat protected, newer fellows may not reach adequate exposure: “Our current trainees are lucky if they get into double figures by fellowship”. Suggested competency thresholds ranged from ∼10 procedures for basic proficiency to ∼20 for supervision, reflecting the belief that PDT requires a “very specific feel earned after about 20 procedures”. Variability in supervision practices has also created inconsistent training opportunities. “As a trainee…there is significant variability in consultant willingness to perform/supervise percutaneous tracheostomies”. These tensions highlight the challenge of balancing trainee learning with safe consultant practice in a shrinking procedural environment.

Education, simulation, and credentialing

Simulation-based learning and structured credentialing were frequently proposed as essential countermeasures to declining clinical exposure. Respondents described using mannequins, wet labs, and animal models: “each trainee performs up to four PDTs on an ovine model per year”, as well as refresher courses to address skill fade. Many called for clearer expectations from CICM, including logbooks, recency requirements, or periodic recertification. Medicolegal concerns were also raised: “I fear it would be hard to justify in a court of law should there be a complication” without maintained competency. Overall, respondents supported formalised simulation and credentialing frameworks to ensure safe practice as procedural volumes decrease.

Future directions and systems-level considerations

Views diverged on whether PDT should remain a core skill for all intensivists. Some deemed it “an essential part of intensive care practice”, while other consultants and trainees argued for its removal from core competencies: “We do not do enough … please remove PDT as a requirement for fellowship”. Many noted that increasing trainee and consultant numbers now compete for scarce opportunities: “rarely performed with multiple candidates keen to practise when it arises”. Proposed solutions included concentrating PDT within high-volume operators, strengthening collaboration with ENT, and embedding formal guidelines such as the ANZICS PDT consensus statement. Patient safety remained a core concern for respondents, with one emphasising the bronchoscopist’s role as a critical “second operator”. Collectively, these perspectives reflect an uncertain future for PDT in ICU as a universal competency and highlight the need for system-level decisions about how the procedure should be integrated into modern intensive care practice.

Discussion

Key findings

In this survey of ICU medical practitioners across ANZ, we identified several important insights into current PDT practice, training exposure, and perceived competency. The frequency of PDTs across data provided from contributing units was low, with a median of only 0.41% of all patients admitted to ICU undergoing the procedure in 2024. This limited procedural volume was reflected in marked variation in operator experience and trainees consistently reported insufficient opportunities to achieve or maintain competency. A recent single-centre Australian study further noted that only half of all tracheostomies were performed percutaneously.13 Free-text responses further highlighted substantial declines in volume of practice over time, heterogeneity in practice between units, and the growing influence of alternative airway strategies and surgical services, all of which were viewed as key barriers to training. Respondents strongly supported the role of structured education, simulation, and clearer credentialing or scope of practice requirements as potential solutions yet divergent views emerged regarding whether PDT should remain a universal CICM training competency and whether it should be concentrated among high-volume specialist practitioners.

Comparison with previous studies

Our reported frequency of PDT was significantly lower when compared to a similar setting a decade prior (4%).8 Whilst most respondents indicated that performing and supervising PDTs remained part of their current practice, for most this occurred only infrequently during 2024. Several factors likely contribute to this apparent decline. ICU case mix and ventilation practices have evolved, with shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and improved weaning and improved weaning strategies reducing the need for tracheostomy in general.18,19 The increased utilisation of non-invasive ventilation20,21 and high-flow nasal oxygenation22 as first-line or bridging modalities has also reduced the pool of patients progressing to tracheostomy. In addition, less invasive surgical techniques are being favoured and sedation with long-acting agents such as midazolam has decreased.23 There is no clear evidence demonstrating benefit from early tracheostomy24 and clinicians may therefore defer the decision until 10–14 days or longer, by which time many patients may be suitable for primary extubation. System constraints may also play a role. Australia has a relatively modest number of ICU beds per capita,25 meaning that high occupancy, rapid turnover, and increasing patient complexity can limit the availability of clinicians with appropriate experience, time, and resources to perform PDTs safely within the unit. In such circumstances, tracheostomy may be deferred or referred to ENT surgeons for surgical tracheostomy, often related to increased body habitus or concurrent long-acting antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents. These practice and systems-level pressures offer a plausible explanation for the markedly lower frequency observed in our study.

Implications for clinicians and policy makers

Senior consultants and HOUs generally have greater cumulative experience and are more likely to be involved in the limited number of PDTs that occur, reinforcing their confidence. Junior consultants may perform PDTs infrequently, heightening uncertainty about their own recency of practice and their unit’s capacity to train CICM trainees. Trainees, experiencing the scarcity of opportunities most directly, consistently described significant challenges in achieving competence. While current clinicians collectively report significant cumulative experience, a substantial proportion of these experienced operators may reach retirement age in the next decade.

While reduced case numbers may reflect improved patient outcomes (e.g., earlier extubation, increased use of non-invasive ventilation), this has affected procedural exposure for both consultants and trainees. As PDT has become a less frequent procedure in ANZ ICUs, there is a need for a change in approach to safeguard training and competence. With increasing numbers of CICM fellows and trainees, maintaining PDT as a mandatory competency for all trainees to learn seems to be challenging. A volume-outcome relationship has been observed with lower rates of tracheostomy per intensivist and this may leave our patients and our profession vulnerable to risk, medical error, and complications.26,27

Expert opinion suggests that trainees require approximately 6–20 PDT procedures to be deemed competent1,6 and that ICU consultants may need to perform more than 10 PDTs per year to maintain competency.1 Among our 270 respondents, only five would have met these criteria in 2024. Several respondents described the emergence of “self-selected” group of ICU specialists within their units who perform most PDTs. The rationale being that this group maintains higher procedural exposure, whilst others have effectively opted out of including PDT in their scope of practice.

Training models may also need to shift accordingly, focussing trainee exposure on bronchoscopy, management of tracheostomy emergencies, and tracheostomy weaning, with procedural insertion taught similarly to advanced airway skills rather than as a universal competency. More widespread use of simulation-based training and wet-lab accreditation as complementary pathways would centralise live procedural opportunities while maintaining readiness, reducing skill fade, and ensuring trainees attain safe, supervised competence in PDT.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. As with all survey-based research, the findings are subject to recall and sampling bias, and the accuracy of results depends on the precision of self-reported data. Because responses were anonymous, we were unable to link individual exposure to centre-level procedural volume or numbers of specialists and trainees. Incomplete or inconsistent entries could not be clarified, necessitating the exclusion of 51 submissions that may have otherwise contributed useful insights. As previously discussed, the chance of duplicate responses being inadvertently included was mitigated by single-use email links and review of the individual responses but, cannot be completely excluded.

The survey response rate was 43%, which is lower than reported in other practice surveys of PDT.6 Accessing the entire population of CICM specialists and trainees across ANZ remains challenging. Our survey was distributed through the ANZICS membership email distribution list, advertised on the CICM website, and shared through contacts of the investigator group. Therefore, there will undoubtedly be a proportion of specialists and trainees across ANZ that were unaware of our survey and had no opportunity to participate. Important data may have been missed as a result.

A further limitation is the absence of data on the proportion of ICU patients requiring invasive mechanical ventilation, which precludes true estimate of PDT rates. As a result, the true rate of tracheostomy among ventilated patients is likely to be higher than the reported 0.41% (centre-level) and 0.86% (patient-level) estimates, limiting direct comparison with prior studies.

The sample was weighted toward Victoria where ANZICS has the most medical members, which accounted for one-third of respondents, with fewer responses than expected from other states with comparable or larger populations.17 This suggests uneven survey dissemination or variable engagement across jurisdictions. Frequency data for PDTs were available from only a small subset of units, approximately 10% of ICUs across ANZ, limiting the generalisability of procedural frequency estimates. A larger, more systematic data collection effort would be required to confirm whether the low PDT frequency observed in this survey reflects broader binational practice.

Future directions

Further research in this area is required. A registry-based cohort study of tracheostomy practice in ANZ ICUs could more fully characterise the frequency of PDT, the variation among units, proportion of PDT vs ST, and the rate of complications. Concentrated procedural practice, structured simulation, and clear competency standards are suggested areas for further investigation.

Conclusions

This survey demonstrates that PDT is now a low-frequency and variably performed procedure across ANZ ICUs, resulting in limited trainee exposure and uneven maintenance of consultant competence. At the same time, growing numbers of ICU trainees and specialists may be further diluting individual opportunities for training and maintenance of competency. These trends indicate that current training expectations are no longer aligned with clinical reality. A revised and more targeted approach, incorporating concentrated procedural practice, structured simulation, and clear competency standards, may help to ensure safe and sustainable PDT practice for future intensivists.
