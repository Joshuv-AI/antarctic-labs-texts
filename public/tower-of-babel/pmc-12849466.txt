Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, and the third leading cause of cancer death in China [1, 2]. Treatment options for intermediate and advanced-stage hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) are limited, and targeted therapies are ineffective due to tumor heterogeneity [3, 4]. Therefore, exploring the molecular mechanisms of HCC pathogenesis and identifying reliable tumor driver genes are crucial for subsequent drug development.

As the primary mRNA modification, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) regulates gene expression by influencing RNA splicing, stability, nuclear export, localization, translation, and degradation. RNA fate is determined by the coordinated actions of “writers” (METTL3), “erasers” (ALKBH5, FTO), and “readers” (IGF2BP31-3, YTHDF1-3) [5, 6]. Insulin like growth Factor 2 mRNA binding protein 3 (IGF2BP3) recognizes mA modifications on target gene mRNAs (e.g., in collaboration with METTL3), thereby enhancing their stability and translation efficiency [7]. The m6A modification of mRNA is mainly catalyzed by the core methylase complex METTL3-WTAP-METTL14 [6]. WTAP is a key regulatory subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex, directly binding to METTL3 to promote complex assembly and nuclear localization. WTAP binds to METTL3 and recruits it to specific regions of the target mRNA (such as the 3’UTR), achieving regional selective methylation [8, 9].

Current research indicates that the IL17 receptor (IL17R) in the interleukin family exhibits specific m6A modification [10]. Our previous studies have found that IL4R may be regulated by m6A modification. IL4R is overexpressed on the surface of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and various tumor cells [11–13]. After binding to ligands (such as IL-4/IL-13), it activates STAT3 through JAK kinase phosphorylation [14, 15]. STAT3 is widely overexpressed and persistently activated in HCC (e.g., phosphorylated STAT3/p-STAT3), and its activation status is significantly associated with tumor progression, invasion and metastasis, and poor patient prognosis [16, 17]. As a transcription factor, STAT3 regulates downstream target genes through transcriptional regulation, driving HCC cell proliferation, anti-apoptosis, cell cycle progression, and conferring chemotherapy resistance to tumor cells [18].

In this study, we demonstrated that IGF2BP3 promotes the tumorigenesis of HCC both in vitro and in vivo through m6A modification. Mechanistically, IGF2BP3 binds to and stabilizes the IL4R mRNA 3’UTR, thereby activating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. STAT3 promotes the nuclear retention of METTL3 by transcriptionally upregulating WTAP, thereby coordinating the m6A modification of IGF2BP3 in HCC.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Transcriptome and clinical data were downloaded from the TCGA database (https://cancergenome.nih.gov/). IGF2BP3 knockout (GSE270702), MeRIP (GSE110320), and RIP-METTL3 (GSE110320) data were obtained from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Reagents and antibodies

The antibodies used in this study were as follows:

anti-IGF2BP3 (Protientech, Cat. 14642-1-AP, USA); anti‐IL4R (Protientech, Cat. 28331-1-AP, USA); anti-p-JAK2 (Invitrogen, Cat. 44-426G, USA); anti-p-STAT3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. 9131, USA); anti-JAK2 (Santa Cruz, Cat. sc-390539, USA); anti-STAT3 (Protientech, Cat. 10253-2-AP, USA); anti-WTAP (Protientech, Cat. 60188-1-Ig, USA); anti-METTL3 (Protientech, Cat. 15073-1-AP, USA); anti-Tubulin (Proteintech, Cat. 11224-1-AP, USA); anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat. 10494-1-AP, USA); anti- Histone H3 (Proteintech, Cat. 17168-1-AP, USA).

The experimental reagents used are as follows:

Actinomycin D (MedChem Express, Cat.HY-17559, USA); RIPA lysis (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0013C); PMSF (Beyotime, Cat. No.# ST506), crystal violet solution (Solarbio Cat. No. G1062); 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0099).

Cell line acquisition and cell culture

HCC cell lines (HepG2 and Huh-7) were obtained from Procell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Tumor cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, USA). All cells were plated into culture dishes and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Lentivirus construction and transfection

The OE-IGF2BP3, OE-WTAP, shIGF2BP3, shIL4R, and shSTAT3 lentiviruses were designed and purchased from Hanbio (Shanghai, China). Lentivirus infection was conducted using the Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lentivirally transfected cells were subjected to treatment with 5 µg/ml puromycin for a duration of 24 h to select stable cell lines. The transfection efficiency was then confirmed using RT-qPCR and western blot analysis. The shRNA sequence used in this study were:

shIGF2BP3#1: 5′-CGGTGAATGAACTTCAGAATT-3′;

shIGF2BP3#2: 5′-GCAGTTGTAAATGTAACCTAT-3′;

shIL4R#1; 5′-CCCTGACAATTACCTGTATAA-3′;

shIL4R#2: 5′-GCATTGTCTACTCAGCCCTTA-3′;

shSTAT3#1: 5′-GCAAAGAATCACATGCCACTT-3′;

shSTAT3#2: 5′-CATCTGAAACTACTAACTTTG-3′.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed utilizing RIPA lysis buffer, which was supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, China). BCA protein assay kit was used to determine protein concentration (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Proteins were denatured by boiling in 5× SDS-PAGE loading buffer at 100 °C for a duration of 5 to 10 min. After the boiling process, proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was incubated with 5% milk for 2 h to block non-specific binding, followed by three washes with TBST. Subsequently, it was treated with the primary antibody and left to incubate overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG, Proteintech, China) for one hour at room temperature. After performing three additional washes with TBST, protein bands were visualized utilizing a Bio-Rad detection system. Original western blot images are showed in Supplementary Figure S6.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the cells utilizing TRIzol™ reagent (ThermoFisher, MA, USA). The cDNA was subsequently reverse transcribed utilizing using HiScript Synthesis kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-PCR was performed using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system.

The amplification primers:

IGF2BP3 forward: 5′-CCAAGCTAGACAAGCACTAGAC-3′;

IGF2BP3 reverse: 5′-GCGGCCATTTCATCAGGGA-3′.

METTL3 forward: 5′-TTGTCTCCAACCTTCCGTAGT-3′;

METTL3 reverse: 5′-CCAGATCAGAGAGGTGGTGTAG-3′.

IL4R forward: 5′-CGTGGTCAGTGCGGATAACTA-3′;

IL4R reverse: 5′-TGGTGTGAACTGTCAGGTTTC-3′.

WTAP forward: 5′-CTTCCCAAGAAGGTTCGATTGA-3′;

WTAP reverse: 5′-TCAGACTCTCTTAGGCCAGTTAC-3′.

STAT3 forward: 5′-ACCAGCAGTATAGCCGCTTC-3′;

STAT3 reverse: 5′-GCCACAATCCGGGCAATCT-3′.

GAPDH forward: 5′-CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC-3′;

GAPDH reverse: 5′-AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG- 3′.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

HCC cells were plated in 96-well plates, and 200 µL of culture medium (10% FBS) was added to each well. Following cell counting, 1000 cells were introduced into each well. After allowing sufficient time for stable cell attachment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for one hour. Optical density (OD) values were measured every 24 h using a microplate reader set to a wavelength of 450 nm.

Colony formation

After preparing a 6-well plate, add 2 mL of DMEM medium and 1,000 HCC cells to each well. Incubate the cells for two weeks while closely monitoring their growth. Once the cells reach an appropriate density, fix them with 4% paraformaldehyde and stain with a 0.1% crystal violet solution. Count the cell colonies using ImageJ software.

Transwell migration assay

For the transwell invasion assay, 5 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a 24-well plate in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and resuspended in serum-free medium. For the migration assay, these cells were subsequently introduced into the upper chamber of an uncoated transwell insert. In contrast, for the invasion assay, the membrane of the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel diluted at a ratio of 1:8. Five hundred microliters of culture medium supplemented with 25% FBS was added to each lower chamber. Following incubation at 37 °C for a duration of 24 to 48 h, cells were examined microscopically for evidence of migration or invasion.

mRNA stability assay

HCC cells were cultured in 6-well plates and subsequently treated with 5 µg/mL actinomycin D for durations of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6 h. Following treatment, RNA was extracted. The levels of IL4R mRNA transcripts were assessed based on the mRNA half-life and normalized to GAPDH.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein isolation

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were isolated utilizing nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction reagents in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (Beyotime, China) and subsequently analyzed through Western blotting.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

RIP assays were conducted in accordance with the instructions provided by the Geneseed RIP kit. In summary, magnetic beads were incubated with anti-m6A/IGF2BP3/IgG antibodies prior to their addition to cell lysates. The bound complexes were subsequently subjected to thorough washing, elution, purification, and analysis via RT-qPCR. The enrichment of precipitated RNA was normalized against the input control.

ChIP assays

Briefly, after cross-linking with formaldehyde for 6 min, HCC cells were quenched with 125 mM glycine for an additional 6 min. DNA strands were fragmented into sizes ranging from 200 to 500 base pairs through sonication. Cross-linked protein-DNA complexes were precipitated using STAT3 antibodies alongside control IgG antibodies. The immunoprecipitated DNA was subsequently analyzed via qPCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

After incubating in triplicate on a 24-well plate and allowing for 24 h of quiescence, 200 ng of the luciferase reporter gene along with various treatment plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000. Dual-luciferase reporter gene assays were conducted 48 h post-transfection utilizing a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

Tumor xenograft model

The ethical approval for the animal experiments conducted in this research was obtained from the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Tianjin First Central Hospital. Male BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were sourced from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. Treated HepG2 cells were suspended in 100 µl of PBS mixed with Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio and subsequently injected into the axillary regions of the nude mice. Tumor volume was measured daily using the formula: Total tumor volume (mm³) = L × W²/2, where “L” represents tumor length and “W” denotes tumor width.

Statistical analysis

The Kaplan–Meier method was employed for survival analysis. All statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted using R software version 4.4.10 and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). Student’s t-test was utilized to compare two groups, while one-way and two-way ANOVA were applied to evaluate differences among multiple groups. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables are expressed as counts and proportions. NS indicates not significant. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05, with *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 considered statistically significant.

Results

Abnormally high expression of IGF2BP3 is associated with poor prognosis in HCC

To explore the cancer-promoting mechanism of m6a-related genes in HCC, we used high-throughput technology to screen key regulatory genes. Among the 26 m6a-regulated genes, IGF2BP1-3 and METTL3 were found to be significantly upregulated in the TCGA-LIHC database and were speculated to be core genes promoting cancer (Fig. 1A). Survival analysis showed that IGF2BP3 and METTL3 were associated with poor prognosis, while IGF2BP1 and IGF2BP2 had no statistical significance (Figs. 1B, C and S1A, B). PPI network analysis found that IGF2BP3 and METTL3 play a regulatory role in the regulatory network center in liver cancer (Fig. 1D, E). Importantly, pan-cancer analysis showed that IGF2BP3 and METTL3 are highly expressed in a variety of tumors compared with normal tissues, including HCC, suggesting that they may play an important role in promoting cancer (Fig S1C, D). Similarly, paired analysis suggested that IGF2BP3 and METTL3 were significantly more highly expressed in liver tumor tissues than in normal tissues (Fig. 1F, G). We then examined the protein and mRNA levels of IGF2BP3 and METTL3 in normal cells and HCC cell lines. The results showed that IGF2BP3 and METTL3 were indeed significantly overexpressed in tumor cells, particularly HepG2 and Huh-7 (Fig. 1H, I). Interestingly, IGF2BP3 expression levels were higher than METTL3, a finding also observed in single-cell sequencing data (Fig. 1J–L). Therefore, we selected the IGF2BP3 gene and HepG2 and Huh-7 cells for further studies. Taken together, these results demonstrate that m6a modification is aberrantly active in HCC and is associated with poor patient prognosis.

IGF2BP3 promotes the malignant phenotype of tumors in HCC cells

To investigate the role of IGF2BP3 in HCC, we created tumor cell lines with silenced and overexpressed IGF2BP3, confirming knockdown and overexpression efficiencies via qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig S2A–D). Colony formation assays demonstrated that the overexpression of IGF2BP3 significantly enhanced tumor cell proliferation, while the knockdown of IGF2BP3 resulted in a marked inhibition of cell proliferation (Fig. 2A, B). Similarly, CCK-8 assays indicated that silencing IGF2BP3 led to a reduction in tumor cell viability, whereas the overexpression of IGF2BP3 produced an opposing effect (Fig. 2C, D). In addition, Transwell experiments confirmed that IGF2BP3 overexpression promoted tumor cell migration, while IGF2BP3 silencing inhibited tumor cell migration (Fig. 2E, F). To investigate IGF2BP3’s role in HCC cell proliferation in vivo, we injected HepG2 cells transfected with viral vectors that either silenced or overexpressed IGF2BP3 into nude mice. The results showed that knocking down IGF2BP3 reduced tumor volume and weight, while its overexpression promoted tumor growth (Fig. 2G, I). These data underscore the crucial role of IGF2BP3 in enhancing HCC cell growth both in vivo and in vitro.

IGF2BP3 activates the JAK2-STAT3 pathway through m6a modification of IL4R mRNA in HCC

GSEA was used to explore the mechanism of action of IGF2BP3 in HCC, and the results showed a close relationship between the JAK/STAT signaling pathway and IGF2BP3 (Fig. 3A). Therefore, we used Western blotting and luciferase reporter assays to detect JAK2/STAT3 signaling activity. The results showed that silencing IGF2BP3 significantly inhibited p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 protein expression (Fig. 3B), and STAT3 transcriptional activity was also significantly suppressed (Fig. 3C). To gain a deeper understanding of the regulatory mechanism of IGF2BP3, we analyzed the data of KO-IGF2BP3, RIP-METTL3, and MeRIP to identify potential substrates (Fig. 3D). Among them, IL4R was of particular interest because of its important role in activating the STAT3 signaling pathway [19–21]. We then examined whether IL4R was involved in the regulation of STAT3 activity by IGF2BP3. As expected, IL4R knockdown significantly inhibited IGF2BP3-induced STAT3 activation (Fig. 3E, F). IGF2BP3 is an RNA-binding protein that promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis by regulating the RNA stability of downstream genes, especially as a “reader” of m6A modification [6]. RT-qPCR and WB showed that IGF2BP3 knockdown significantly inhibited the mRNA and protein levels of IL4R (Fig. 3G), suggesting that IGF2BP3 may enhance the expression of IL4R by stabilizing its mRNA. SRAMP, an m6A site prediction tool [22], identified distinct m6A sites in IL4R at single-base resolution (Fig. 3H). To investigate the impact of m6A modification on gene expression, we conducted RIP-RT-qPCR. The results indicated that IL4R mRNA was significantly enriched in the precipitates obtained using m6A-specific and IGF2BP3-specific antibodies (Figs. 3I-G and S3C, D). mRNA half-life assays demonstrated that the silencing of IGF2BP3 accelerated the decay of IL4R mRNA, whereas the overexpression of IGF2BP3 exhibited an opposing effect (Figs. 3K, L and S3E, F). In summary, the data show that IGF2BP3 stabilizes IL4R mRNA 3’UTR via m6A modification, activating the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway in HCC.

STAT3 regulates the nuclear localization of METTL3 through WTAP

Correlation analysis showed that METTL3/IGF2BP3 and STAT3 were significantly positively correlated in HCC (Fig S4A). Subsequently, we investigated whether STAT3 plays a potential role in regulating the expression and/or function of METTL3. Interestingly, RT-qPCR and WB analyses revealed that silencing STAT3 did not influence METTL3 expression (Fig S4B, C). Previous studies indicate that METTL3 is mainly found in the nucleus, where it regulates mRNA splicing and export through its methyltransferase activity [23, 24]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of STAT3 on the subcellular distribution of METTL3 and found that STAT3 knockdown reduced the nuclear distribution of METTL3 and increased its expression in the cytoplasm (Fig. 4A, B). Similarly, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that STAT3 silencing reduced METTL3 nuclear expression (Figs. 4C and S4D), suggesting that STAT3 may bind to METTL3 to influence its cellular distribution. However, Co-IP did not show physical association between STAT3 and METTL3 (Fig. 4D). Previous studies have confirmed that WTAP is primarily located in the cell nucleus [25], acting as a key recruitment factor for METTL3 and mediating the nuclear localization of the METTL3-METTL14 complex. WTAP interacts with METTL3, driving METTL3 into nuclear patches and enhancing its nuclear localization [26]. If WTAP is deficient, the nuclear localization of METTL3 is disrupted [25]. WTAP recruitment is essential for METTL3 nuclear localization because WTAP binds to METTL3 only in a specific complex state, which contributes to the enzymatic activity and function of METTL3 in the nucleus [27]. RT-qPCR and WB assays confirmed that STAT3 silencing suppressed the mRNA and protein levels of WTAP (Fig. 4E, F). Therefore, we hypothesized that STAT3 may influence the nuclear localization of METTL3 via WTAP. Importantly, subcellular localization and immunofluorescence analysis showed that WTAP overexpression blocked shSTAT3-induced MEETL3 nuclear export (Figs. 4G–I and S4E). Collectively, STAT3 modulates the nuclear localization of METTL3 through WTAP.

STAT3 enhances the expression of WTAP by promoting its transcription

Studies have reported that STAT3 can transcriptionally regulate WTAP [7]. JASPAR and NCBI predicted three sites where STAT3 bind to the WTAP promoter region (Fig. 5A). ChIP assay was performed using specific primers to amplify three regions containing the corresponding binding sites. The data showed that STAT3 can occupy Region 1 of the WTAP promoter (Figs. 5B and S5A). Next, we constructed deletion 1–3 mutants based on sites 1–3 and performed luciferase reporter assays. Deletion of Region 1 significantly inhibited the transcriptional activity of WTAP, while deletions of regions 2 and 3 did not affect the transcriptional activity of WTAP (Figs. 5 C and S5B). The luciferase rescue experiment demonstrated that STAT3 functions through region 1 of the WTAP promoter, rather than regions 2 and 3 (Fig S5C, D). These findings suggest that STAT3 stimulates WTAP transcription by binding to Site 1. Next, we assessed the levels of m6A in HepG2 and Huh-7 cells following STAT3 knockdown. The results indicated a downregulation of m6A levels upon STAT3 knockdown (Fig. 5D). Notably, we found that overexpression of WTAP effectively mitigated the reduction in m6A levels induced by STAT3 knockdown, whereas overexpression of METTL3 only partially countered this decrease (Fig. 5D). This discrepancy may be attributed to the inability of METTL3 overexpression to compensate for the impaired nuclear speckle localization resulting from WTAP deficiency. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that STAT3 modulates the methyltransferase activity of METTL3 through its interaction with WTAP.

Knockdown of STAT3 can inhibit IGF2BP3-induced tumor malignant progression

To explore whether STAT3 is involved in IGF2BP3-induced tumorigenesis, we performed in vivo and in vitro experiments. CCK-8 showed that STAT3 knockdown inhibited IGF2BP3-increased cell viability (Fig. 6A). Clone formation assay demonstrated that the knockdown of STAT3 significantly inhibited cell proliferation induced by IGF2BP3 (Fig. 6B). Transwell assay demonstrated that STAT3 silencing could inhibit IGF2BP3-enhanced cell migration (Fig. 6C). Flow cytometry showed that STAT3 silencing could restore IGF2BP3-induced cell apoptosis (Fig. 6D). To investigate the role of STAT3 in the tumor-promoting effects of IGF2BP3 in vivo, we used an animal model in which HepG2 cells were transfected with viruses that overexpressed IGF2BP3 and knocked down STAT3. The results demonstrated that the increase in tumor volume and weight induced by IGF2BP3 could be effectively reversed through the knockout of STAT3 (Fig. 6E–G). In conclusion, the inhibition of the STAT3 pathway has the potential to reverse IGF2BP3-driven malignant progression of HCC both in vivo and in vitro.

Discussion

m6A modification affects the expression of key oncogenes by regulating RNA metabolism (stability, translation, splicing, etc.), driving the occurrence and development of tumors [28–31]. Studies have reported that IGF2BP3 significantly promotes the malignant progression of HCC cells through an m6A-dependent mechanism. Knockdown of IGF2BP3 can inhibit tumor cell growth [32, 33]. However, the mechanism by which IGF2BP3 promotes HCC progression remains unclear.

IGF2BP3, as an oncogenic protein in HCC, activates multiple pathways such as proliferation, metastasis, immune escape, and anti-ferroptosis through m6A-dependent RNA stability regulation, promoting tumor progression and being closely associated with poor prognosis. Its molecular mechanism involves post-transcriptional regulation of key target genes (such as MCM5, VEGF, TFAP2A, CCL5, etc.) and activation of oncogenic signaling pathways [34–39]. Therefore, IGF2BP3 is a potential prognostic marker and therapeutic target for HCC.

In our study, we found that IGF2BP3 and METTL3 are highly expressed in HCC and correlate with poor patient prognosis. We confirmed that IGF2BP3 promotes HCC progression through m6A modification. Mechanistically, IGF2BP3 reads and stabilizes the 3’ UTR of IL4R mRNA, thereby activating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. STAT3 transcriptional upregulation of WTAP blocks nuclear export of METTL3, which enhances m6A.

modification activity. In HCC, knockdown of STAT3 significantly inhibited IGF2BP3-induced tumorigenesis both in vitro and in vivo.

STAT3 regulates the expression and function of enzymes involved in m6A modification. In gastric cancer, the AGAP2-AS1/WTAP/STAT3 pathway is activated, and STAT3 participates in regulating m6A modification-related processes, promoting cell proliferation and migration, and contributing to inflammation-mediated carcinogenesis [40]. In drug-resistant breast cancer cells, FTO (m6A demethylase) does not participate in m6A modification of STAT3 mRNA but can activate the STAT3 signaling pathway; overexpression of FTO enhances STAT3-mediated drug resistance [41].

Importantly, our findings indicate that IL4R serves as an m6A-modified substrate of IGF2BP3, which activates the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway and facilitates tumorigenesis and progression. Notably, silencing STAT3 effectively abolished the tumorigenic effects induced by IGF2BP3 in HCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrate that IGF2BP3 binds and stabilizes IL4R mRNA, thereby activating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. STAT3 promotes the nuclear retention of METTL3 via WTAP, which coordinates the m6A modification of IGF2BP3 in HCC (Fig. 7).
