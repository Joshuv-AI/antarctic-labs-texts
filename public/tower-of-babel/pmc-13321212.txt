Impact of Radionecrosis and Local Recurrence on Overall Survival After Stereotactic Radiosurgery for Brain Metastases
Authors: Jim X. Leng, Muzamil Arshad, David J. Carpenter, Jamiluddin Qazi, Christina Huang, Lucas Serra, Eugene Vaios, Zachary J. Reitman, Scott R. Floyd, John P. Kirkpatrick, Peter E. Fecci, Steven J. Chmura, Julian C. Hong, Paul W. Sperduto, Joseph K. Salama, Trey C. Mullikin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRadionecrosis (RN) is a dose-limiting toxicity after stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) for brain metastases and is hard to differentiate from treated tumor progression. The outcomes of patients with RN following SRS are poorly described, particularly compared with those with local central nervous system progression. Therefore, we characterize outcomes of patients who developed RN versus those with treated tumor recurrence in the absence of out-of-field intracranial progression after SRS.Methods and MaterialsThis cohort study included patients who completed an initial course of SRS between 2015 and 2020. The primary outcome was overall survival (OS) by the presence of RN or treated tumor local recurrence (LR), analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Binomial logistic regression was used to test parameters associated with RN or LR.ResultsA total of 1383 patients were included; 758 (55%) were women, and the median age was 63.4 years. Common primaries were lung (n = 757, 55%), breast (203, 15%), and melanoma (118, 9%). At a median follow-up of 8.7 months, 143 patients (10%) had concern for intracranial progression or RN at irradiated sites. Following a multidisciplinary evaluation, 96 patients were ultimately diagnosed with RN and 47 with LR. Patients with LR were diagnosed earlier after SRS than those with RN (median 5.8 vs 8.3 months; P = .0087), and they had worse OS than those with RN (median 15.2 vs 40.4 months; P < .001). Patients symptomatic for either LR or RN presented earlier after SRS than asymptomatic patients and had worse OS. In binomial logistic regression, RN was associated with single-fraction SRS (odds ratio [OR], 0.38; P = .012), receipt of immunotherapy after SRS (OR, 0.32; P < .014), and a longer interval from SRS (OR, 0.94; P = .028).ConclusionsIn this multi-institutional cohort study of brain metastasis patients treated with SRS, those with RN had a significantly better OS than those with LR. These data inform surveillance, diagnosis, and prognosis of these patients.

Introduction

Brain metastases are the most frequently diagnosed brain tumors, with a continuing increase in incidence due to advancements in treatments that lead to longer patient survival.1 While survival of patients with brain metastases was historically poor, it has improved with contemporary management that includes selected surgical intervention, radiation therapy, and many systemic therapies.2,3 Notably, the 2020 update of the Graded Prognostic Assessment for brain metastases showed improvements in survival across all included diagnoses compared with 2012,4 with significant advances in patients with brain metastases from breast, lung, and melanoma primaries. With the increase in incidence and the increasing clinical courses of patients with brain metastases, appropriate and effective management of potential treatment-related toxicities remains a significant challenge for physicians.

The current standard of care for patients with limited brain metastases and good performance status is stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS), either definitively or postoperatively after surgical resection.5, 6, 7 Treated tumor control is high, with 1-year estimates of 80% to 90%.8, 9, 10 However, a proportion of patients eventually develop either tumor recurrence or radionecrosis (RN). Both of these conditions can cause significant management challenges and can lead to progressive clinical deterioration and neurologic decline.11 Differentiating imaging findings between RN and local recurrence (LR) is difficult due to overlapping characteristics and the lack of a gold-standard diagnostic test.

Prior studies have focused on the diagnostic and treatment-related features of RN (with an incidence rate ranging from 5%-25% after SRS),12, 13, 14 while noting the diagnostic difficulties in differentiating RN from treated tumor recurrence in clinical management.15, 16, 17 However, clinical outcomes, especially overall survival (OS), for patients who experience RN versus local tumor progression are poorly characterized. A better understanding of these patients’ prognoses can guide multidisciplinary management, including surgery, reirradiation, systemic therapy, and supportive care at the time of progression. Here, we examine a multi-institutional cohort of 1383 patients who were treated with SRS for brain metastases and report the differential outcomes between patients who developed RN versus those with tumor LR after treatment.

Methods and Materials

In this institutional review board-approved retrospective cohort study, deidentified patient data were used, and a waiver of informed consent was granted. Adult patients treated from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2020, with an initial course of SRS for brain metastases were identified consecutively at 2 institutions. This timeframe was chosen to reflect the contemporary management of brain metastases, including the use of immune checkpoint and molecular targeted therapies (TT). Both single- and multifraction SRS courses were included, and patients who had undergone prior surgical resection or whole-brain radiation therapy (WBRT) were included.

Demographic, treatment-specific, and clinical parameters were obtained by chart review. We defined the oligometastatic disease state as the presence of 1 to 5 metastases (including intracranial lesions) at the time of SRS.18 Time-to-event was defined as the date of SRS completion for each patient. Linear accelerator-based SRS was performed at both institutions, with best practices used in treatment planning.19 After SRS, surveillance included brain magnetic resonance imaging every 2 to 3 months in the first year, with the exact interval between scans left to the discretion of the multidisciplinary treatment team. We defined intracranial progression (ITCP) as any clinical concern for either a treated tumor or distant ITCP. Patients had local in-field progression (IFP) if the initial post-SRS concern for progression was limited to 1 or more previously irradiated brain metastases, including pseudoprogression (eg, RN). This was further categorized as local intracranial tumor recurrence or RN by a multidisciplinary team of neuro-radiologists, radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, neurosurgeons, and pathologists at each study site, based on a review of serial radiologic and clinical data and clinical consensus. Tissue diagnosis confirmation, if available, was reported for both scenarios. Patients were defined as having distant ITCP if progression was due to the development of new brain metastases that were separate from treated locations. Patients with an initial concern for ITCP at both local (in-field) and distant intracranial sites were excluded from the present analysis. Symptomatic ITCP (SITCP) was defined as ITCP concurrent with new or progressive neurologic symptoms, which correlated with the specific intracranial region of progression.

Differences in baseline demographic characteristics were assessed using 2-sided t tests and χ2 tests. Clinical endpoints of OS, freedom from ITCP (FF-ITCP), and freedom from SITCP were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. A log-rank test was used to compare differences in time-to-event outcomes. Patients were censored at the time of death for OS and FF-ITCP analyses, and at the time of death or asymptomatic ITCP for the freedom from SITCP analysis. To address immortal time bias, a landmark analysis was conducted using a landmark time of 24 months. For patients with local IFP, binomial logistic regression was used to identify parameters associated with LR or RN. All data analyses were performed using R statistical software (v4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), including the SurvCorr R package (version 1.0). This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Results

In this cohort study, 1383 patients from 2 institutions were included. Table 1 shows the patient characteristics and RN and LR rates. At the time of SRS, the median age was 64.3 years (IQR, 55.4-72.1). The cohort consisted of 758 women (55%) and 625 men (45%). The baseline Karnofsky Performance Status score was ≥90 in 631 patients (46%) and 70 to 80 in 598 patients (43%). The most common primaries included lung (n = 757, 55%), breast (203, 15%), and melanoma (9%). Prior to SRS, 361 patients (26%) underwent surgical resection followed by SRS to the resection cavity; 142 (10%) received WBRT; 693 (50%) received chemotherapy; 327 (24%) received immunotherapy (IT); and 304 (22%) received TT. At the time of SRS, 648 patients (47%) had oligometastatic disease, and 470 (34%) had controlled extracranial disease. Multiple brain metastases were treated in a single course of SRS in 738 patients (53%), including 247 (18%) with 2 lesions, 308 (22%) with 3 to 5 lesions, and 183 (13%) with 6 or more brain metastases. Single-fraction SRS was used in 718 (52%) patients. The median planning target volume (PTV) for all treated brain metastases was 6.6 cm3 (IQR, 1.7-23), and 4.9 cm3 (IQR, 1.2-17.7) for the largest treated metastasis per patient. At a median follow-up of 8.72 months (IQR, 3.25-19.68), local IFP or distant ITCP was observed in 698 patients (50%). This preceded 492 of the 1000 overall observed deaths (49%). Local IFP limited to 1 or more previously irradiated brain metastases was observed in 143 patients (10% of the overall cohort). This was determined to be RN in 96 patients (67%) and LR in 47 (33%), representing rates of 6.9% and 3.4% in the overall cohort, respectively. Pathological confirmation was available for 28 of 96 RN cases (29%) and 22 of 47 tumor LR cases (47%). Compared with patients who experienced RN, patients with LR were less likely to receive single-fraction SRS (P = .004), had a larger total PTV and PTV of the single largest brain metastasis (P = .008 and P = .006, respectively), received less IT after SRS (P = .009), had a shorter duration between SRS and radiographic progression (P = .004), and were more likely to be symptomatic at the time of ITCP (LR 48% vs RN 27%; P = .035).

OS among all patients after SRS (Fig. 1A) was 9.9 months (95% CI, 9.1-11.1). There were 258 patients (19%) who died without documentation of disease progression, and their median survival was 2.2 months (95% CI, 1.9-2.5). The 1125 patients with documented progression had a median survival of 13.9 months (95% CI, 12.2-15.7) (Fig. E1). For patients with local IFP, those with LR had significantly worse OS compared with those with RN, with a median OS of 15.2 months (95% CI, 12.2-24.5) versus 40.4 months (95% CI, 33.0-not reached) (P < .0001; Fig. 1B), respectively. With a landmark time of 24 months, 81 patients who were not followed until this time were excluded, leaving 62 patients (50 with RN and 12 with LR). Stratified by neurologic symptoms at the time of progression, symptomatic patients had significantly worse OS in both the RN and LR groups compared with asymptomatic patients (Fig. 1C, D). In the RN group, symptomatic patients had a median OS of 31.8 months (95% CI, 10.8-not reached), and asymptomatic patients had a median OS of 62.6 months (95% CI, 33.3-not reached; P = .015). Similarly, in the LR group, symptomatic patients had a median OS of 12.2 months (95% CI, 6.5-29.7), and asymptomatic patients had a median OS of 21.3 months (95% CI, 13.8-33.7; P = .045).

To characterize the timing of intracranial events, FF-ITCP was analyzed across the whole cohort (Fig. 2A), among patients with either RN or LR (Fig. 2B), and stratified by the presence of neurologic symptoms at the time of local IFP (Fig. 2C). Overall, FF-ITCP was 8.8 months across the whole cohort (95% CI, 7.7-9.5). LR occurred earlier than RN, with a median of 5.8 months (95% CI, 4.6-7.2) compared with 8.3 months (95% CI, 6.9-9.5; P = .0087). Symptomatic events occurred earlier than asymptomatic events for both RN and LR. For RN, this was 6.0 months (95% CI, 4.6-9.0) versus 8.9 months (95% CI, 7.8-10.9), and for LR, this was 4.0 months (95% CI, 2.2-6.2) versus 6.9 months (95% CI, 5.9-9.5). Discussion

In this multi-institutional cohort study of patients who were treated with SRS for brain metastases, we found that patients who developed treated tumor recurrence had significantly worse OS compared with those who developed RN as an initial clinical concern for post-SRS ITCP. We also found that tumor recurrences occurred earlier than RN. Additionally, patients with either RN or local tumor progression presenting with symptoms had inferior OS compared with those without new or worsening neurologic symptoms. These findings are important and among the first to characterize survival differences between patients with RN and those with tumor progression, and between patients with intracranial symptoms and asymptomatic patients. Further, these data complement prior studies primarily aimed at characterizing the risk factors for RN and LR and highlight the importance of distinguishing between the 2 entities early in post-SRS surveillance.

Our finding that RN occurs later after SRS than treated tumor progression is important, as it can aid physicians in surveilling these patients. In line with prior studies, this timing serves as a reference for clinicians attempting to address the ongoing challenge of determining whether patients with suspicious imaging findings have RN versus treated tumor progression.13,20 We also identified other factors associated with RN in the binomial logistic regression, including single-fraction SRS, receipt of IT after SRS, and a longer interval from SRS, which are consistent with prior studies.21,22 For the majority of patients in this analysis, we determined whether cases were RN or LR based on serial clinical and imaging follow-up. Pathological confirmation, through either surgical resection or biopsy, was available for only 47% of patients with tumor LR and 28% of patients with RN in our series. This is consistent with current practice, as there is no gold standard differentiating test, with identification of these events through radiologic and clinical factors remaining the prominent diagnostic approach.15,23 Future techniques using radiomics and advanced modalities, such as perfusion magnetic resonance imaging, are promising17,23,24 and will need to be validated to aid in the accurate and expedient diagnosis of RN and LR. Given the prognostic significance of the events reported here, these innovations in diagnostic radiology can prove especially impactful.

Notably, we also identified longer survival in patients ultimately diagnosed with RN compared with those with treated tumor progression. We carefully selected this patient population to include those without extracranial progression to isolate intracranial effects. In this selection, the exact OS of all patients with brain metastases and RN may differ from that shown here if they also have extracranial progression. The difference in survival between patients with RN and those with tumor LR likely reflects a combination of more treatment-resistant tumor histology and the need for enhancements to salvage interventions, such as resection, WBRT, laser interstitial thermal therapy, or repeat SRS. Markedly, those who did not develop new or worsening neurologic symptoms at the time of RN had a median OS of 5.2 years, representing a population of long-term survivors of brain metastases.

We also found differences in symptom presence between patients with RN and those with central nervous system progression. The presence of new or worsening neurologic symptoms at progression was associated with tumor LR, usually occurring earlier than RN (8.9 vs 6 months), likely reflecting earlier imaging evaluation of new neurologic symptoms before routine surveillance imaging. These findings highlight the importance of detecting LR or RN early, before symptoms develop. Coupled with the potential for longer-term survival in RN patients following SRS, this also highlights the need to improve RN treatments, as these patients’ quality of life can be significantly improved for some time.

Strengths and limitations

This analysis is inherently limited by its retrospective nature. However, these data should be generalizable to institutions that provide multidisciplinary care for patients with brain metastases. The results reflect outcomes after an initial course of SRS and are not generalizable to subsequent courses of SRS that may be used in ITCP. Fortunately, the rates of LR (3.4%) and RN (6.9%) in this cohort were low, but this translated into relatively small event counts for our outcomes of interest. Therefore, we limited our regression models to univariate analyses. That said, this patient cohort is sizable and likely reflective of modern practice, thereby making the results meaningful. Additionally, with an emphasis on patient-level survival outcomes, lesion-specific data and outcomes related to individual doses and volumes for each treated lesion were not analyzed and were outside the scope of this study. Future prospective studies should emphasize capturing detailed information on neurologic symptoms and granular data on specific systemic therapies with high intracranial activity. Overall, the strengths of this study, including a large, multi-institutional cohort, enabled high event rates of death and disease progression, allowing rigorous characterization of survival among patients who develop local IFP after an initial course of SRS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this cohort study of patients with brain metastases treated with SRS suggests that patients who develop RN present symptoms later and have longer OS compared with those who develop tumor LR. Further, the detection of RN and progression in the absence of symptoms are associated with better outcomes. The data inform surveillance and prognosis for patients who develop RN or tumor LR after SRS.
