Copula‐Stein Discrepancy: A Generator‐Based Stein Operator for Archimedean Dependence
Agnideep Aich, Ashit Baran Aich

Abstract
Kernel Stein discrepancies (KSDs) have become a principal tool for goodness-of-fit testing, but standard KSDs are often insensitive to higher-order dependency structures, such as tail dependence, which are critical in many scientific and financial domains. We address this gap by introducing the Copula-Stein Discrepancy (CSD), a novel class of discrepancies tailored to the geometry of statistical dependence. By defining a Stein operator directly on the copula density, CSD leverages the generative structure of dependence, rather than relying on the joint density’s score function. For the broad class of Archimedean copulas, this approach yields a closed-form Stein kernel derived from the scalar generator function. We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis, proving that CSD (i) metrizes weak convergence of copula distributions, ensuring it detects any mismatch in dependence; (ii) has an empirical estimator that converges at the minimax optimal rate of O P ( n − 1 / 2 ) O_{P}(n^{-1/2}) ; and (iii) is provably sensitive to differences in tail dependence coefficients. The framework is extended to general non-Archimedean copulas, including elliptical and vine copulas. Computationally, the exact CSD kernel evaluation scales linearly in dimension, while a novel random feature approximation reduces the n n -dependence from quadratic O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) to near-linear O ~ ​ ( n ) \tilde{O}(n) , making CSD a practical and theoretically principled tool for dependence-aware inference.



1.  Introduction

The problem of measuring the discrepancy between a generative model and a set of observed data is fundamental to statistical inference and machine learning. Kernel Stein Discrepancies (KSDs) have emerged as a powerful class of tools for such goodness-of-fit (GoF) testing, particularly in high-dimensional settings where normalizing constants are intractable
(
Liu et al. 2016
;
Chwialkowski et al. 2016
)
. These methods construct a “Stein operator” based on the score of the target distribution (i.e., the gradient of its log-density) and leverage the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) to define a metric for sample quality
(
Gorham and Mackey 2017
)
.

However, a critical limitation of standard KSDs is that they operate on the score of the joint probability density. This often renders them insensitive to the rich, higher-order dependence structures that are crucial in many domains. For instance, in finance and hydrology, properly modeling the tail dependence, the propensity for multiple variables to experience extreme events simultaneously, is paramount, yet this is a feature that joint-density-based methods can easily miss
(
Breymann et al. 2003
;
Patton 2012
)
. Indeed, recent work has shown that standard KSDs can fail to detect non-converging samples precisely because their kernels decay too rapidly in the tails
(
Gorham and Mackey 2017
)
, a problem our dependence-focused approach is designed to address.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing a new paradigm for Stein-based GoF testing that is explicitly dependence-aware. We achieve this by building our discrepancy on the principles of copula theory. A copula is a function that isolates the dependence structure of a multivariate distribution from its marginal distributions
(
Sklar 1959
;
Nelsen 2006
)
. By defining a Stein operator directly on the
copula density
rather than the joint density, we introduce the Copula-Stein Discrepancy (CSD), a novel metric designed to be maximally sensitive to misspecifications in the dependence structure. For the broad and flexible class of Archimedean copulas, our approach is particularly powerful, yielding a closed-form Stein kernel derived directly from the copula’s scalar generator function
(
Genest and Rivest 1993
)
.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce the Copula-Stein Discrepancy (CSD) and derive a new Stein operator and corresponding closed-form kernel tailored to copula densities.

•

We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis, proving that CSD (i) metrizes weak convergence of copula distributions; (ii) has an empirical estimator that converges at the minimax optimal rate of
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
; and (iii) is provably sensitive to differences in tail dependence coefficients.

•

We demonstrate the generality of the CSD framework by extending it beyond Archimedean families to any copula satisfying mild regularity conditions, including important classes like elliptical and vine copulas.

•

We establish the practical viability of CSD by proving its exact computation scales linearly in dimension and developing a random feature approximation that reduces the sample complexity from quadratic to nearly linear, enabling its use on large-scale datasets.

This work positions CSD as a principled, practical, and powerful tool for dependence-aware model diagnostics and goodness-of-fit testing. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section
2
discusses related work. Section
3
provides background on KSDs and copulas. Section
4
introduces all the notations and assumptions used throughout the paper. Section
5
formally introduces the CSD, its operator, and kernel. Section
6
establishes its key theoretical properties. Section
7
extends the framework to general copulas, and Section
8
details its computational properties and scalable algorithms.

2.  Related Work

Our work builds upon and contributes to two major lines of research: kernel-based goodness-of-fit testing and the classical literature on copula diagnostics.

2.1
Kernel Stein Discrepancies

The foundation of our method is the Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD), introduced by
Liu et al. 2016
and
Chwialkowski et al. 2016
. These works combined the principles of Stein’s method
(
Barbour and Chen 2005
)
with the power of RKHSs to create GoF tests that do not require knowledge of the target’s normalizing constant. The framework has since been extended to measure MCMC sample quality
(
Gorham and Mackey 2017
)
and to compare models with latent variables
(
Kanagawa et al. 2019
)
. The primary innovation of CSD is its target of analysis. Whereas existing KSDs operate on the score of the joint density
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
, CSD operates on the score of the copula density
c(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})
. This fundamental shift allows CSD to isolate and test the dependence structure, a feature to which standard KSDs are often insensitive. Furthermore, while the quadratic complexity of KSDs is a known challenge, we follow the line of work on scalable kernel methods
(
Rahimi and Recht 2007
;
Jitkrittum et al. 2017
)
to develop a nearly linear-time random feature approximation specifically for our Copula-Stein kernel.

2.2
Goodness-of-Fit Testing for Copulas

There is a rich classical literature on GoF testing for copulas, which largely relies on statistics derived from the empirical copula process
(
Genest and Rémillard 2004
)
. Many of these are “blanket” or omnibus tests that compare the empirical copula to a hypothesized parametric form using Cramér-von Mises or Kolmogorov-Smirnov type statistics
(
Genest et al. 2009
;
Fermanian 2012
)
. Other notable approaches include tests based on positive-definite bilinear forms
(
Panchenko 2005
)
and the information matrix equality
(
Huang and Prokhorov 2014
)
.

CSD contributes a new class of GoF tests to this literature with several key advantages. First, as a discrepancy metric, its value provides a quantitative measure of misspecification, whereas many classical tests only yield a p-value. Second, our framework avoids the need for smoothing parameters or potentially invalid parametric bootstrap procedures
(
Genest and Rémillard 2008
)
by leveraging the wild bootstrap techniques common in the KSD literature. Most importantly, CSD is specifically designed to be sensitive to features like tail dependence, a known challenge highlighted in financial applications
(
Malevergne and Sornette 2003
)
. While specialized tests for extreme-value dependence exist
(
Kojadinovic and Yan 2011
;
Genest and Segers 2009
)
, CSD provides a general framework with theoretical guarantees on its sensitivity to tail coefficients for any copula family.

2.3
Copula Modeling and Diagnostics

Our work also serves as a diagnostic tool for the vast literature on copula modeling. Since the seminal work on rank-based inference
(
Genest et al. 1995
)
, copulas have been used to build sophisticated semiparametric models in econometrics and time series analysis
(
Chen and Fan 2006
;
Chen et al. 2006
;
Patton 2006
)
. The development of highly flexible models, such as vine copulas
(
Czado et al. 2012
)
, has further increased the need for reliable diagnostic tools and GoF tests
(
Schepsmeier and Czado 2011
)
. CSD provides a principled and scalable diagnostic to assess the fit of these complex models, complementing existing estimation techniques by providing a rigorous check on the specified dependence structure.

3.  Background: Copulas and Stein’s Method

Our work builds a bridge between two distinct areas of statistical methodology: the theory of copula-based dependence modeling and kernel-based goodness-of-fit testing using Stein’s method. We briefly review the essential concepts from both fields.

3.1
Copulas and Dependence Modeling

Copulas are functions that separate a multivariate distribution into its marginal distributions and its dependence structure. By Sklar’s Theorem, any joint cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F
with marginal CDFs
F_{1},\dots,F_{d}
can be written as
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=C(F_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,F_{d}(x_{d}))
, where
C:[0,1]^{d}\to[0,1]
is a unique copula
(
Sklar 1959
)
. This allows the study of dependence on the unit hypercube, free from the influence of the marginals.

A particularly useful and broad class are Archimedean copulas, which are constructed from a single univariate function
\varphi:[0,1]\to[0,\infty]
called a generator. The generator must be continuous, strictly decreasing, and convex, with
\varphi(1)=0
. The copula is then defined via its pseudo-inverse
\varphi^{[-1]}
as:

\displaystyle C(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})=\varphi^{[-1]}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varphi(u_{j})\right).

(1)

This parsimonious construction can model a wide variety of dependence patterns, including different forms of tail dependence, simply by changing the generator
\varphi
. For instance, the Clayton copula exhibits lower tail dependence, the Gumbel copula exhibits upper tail dependence, and the Frank copula exhibits none
(
Nelsen 2006
;
Joe 2014
)
. The existence of an analytic generator is the key property we will exploit to construct our novel Stein operator.

3.2
Kernel Stein Discrepancy

Stein’s method
(
Stein 1972
)
provides a powerful recipe for constructing metrics between probability distributions
(
Barbour and Chen 2005
)
. The core idea is to find a
Stein operator
,
\mathcal{T}_{p_{0}}
, associated with a target density
p_{0}
, such that for any suitable test function
f
, the operator has zero expectation under the target:
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{0}}[\mathcal{T}_{p_{0}}f(X)]=0
. The Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD) defines a metric by choosing the test functions from the unit ball of an RKHS and solving for the supremum, which results in a closed-form kernel test
(
Liu et al. 2016
;
Chwialkowski et al. 2016
)
. Our work adapts this powerful KSD framework from the domain of joint densities to the domain of copula densities.

5.  The Copula-Stein Discrepancy

The central goal of this section is to construct a dependence-aware goodness-of-fit metric relative to a target copula
C_{0}
. Throughout, we work under Assumptions
1
(and, when invoked for metrization results, Assumption
2
).

5.1
From IPM to a Computable Kernel

Following the framework of integral probability metrics (IPMs)
(
Müller 1997
;
Zolotarev 1983
)
, we define a discrepancy as the worst-case difference in expectation over a class of test functions
g\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
:

\text{CSD}(C,C_{0})=\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1}\left|\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)]-\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C_{0}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)]\right|.

(2)

The key to making this definition practical is the construction of an operator
\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}
that satisfies a crucial mean-zero property with respect to the target distribution
C_{0}
. We design our operator based on the principles of Stein’s method
(
Stein 1986
;
Liu et al. 2016
)
.

Definition 5.1
(Copula-Stein Operator)

For a target copula
C_{0}
with density
c_{0}
, define

\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}(u)+g_{j}(u)\,\partial_{u_{j}}\log c_{0}(u)\right].

(3)

Lemma 1
(Stein identity and boundary conditions)

Under
A1
–
A2
, for any
g\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
with each
g_{j}
vanishing on the faces
\{u_{j}=0\}
and
\{u_{j}=1\}
,

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C_{0}}\!\big[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)\big]=0,\quad\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\big(\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}+s_{j}g_{j}\big),\ \ s_{j}=\partial_{u_{j}}\log c_{0}.

Proof.
For each
j
,

\int\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}(u)\,c_{0}(u)\,du=\Big[g_{j}(u)\,c_{0}(u)\Big]_{u_{j}=0}^{u_{j}=1}-\int g_{j}(u)\,\partial_{u_{j}}c_{0}(u)\,du.

Boundary vanishing implies the bracketed term is
0
, and
\partial_{u_{j}}c_{0}=s_{j}c_{0}
. Summing over
j
yields
\int\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g\,c_{0}\,du=0
, i.e.
\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)]=0
.

Using Lemma
1
, the zero-mean property immediately simplifies (
2
). For an empirical measure
C_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{U_{i}}
from a sample, the second expectation vanishes, leaving

\text{CSD}(C_{n},C_{0})=\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U_{i})\right|.

(4)

While simpler, the supremum over the infinite-dimensional unit ball in
\mathcal{H}^{d}
remains intractable. Our second key tool is the reproducing property of the RKHS
(
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2004
)
, which allows us to represent the operator’s action as an inner product with a single “witness function.”

Proposition 5.1
(Witness Function Representation)

For any
u\in(0,1)^{d}
, there exists a witness function
\xi_{C_{0}}(u)\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(u)=\langle g,\xi_{C_{0}}(u)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}

(5)

for all
g\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
.

Proof.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the scalar RKHS with kernel
k
, so
\mathcal{H}^{d}
is the
d
-fold product with

\langle g,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle g_{j},h_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.
By reproducing and closure under partials,
\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}(u)=\langle g_{j},\,\partial_{u_{j}}k(u,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
and
g_{j}(u)=\langle g_{j},\,k(u,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. Hence

\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big\langle g_{j},\;\partial_{u_{j}}k(u,\cdot)+(\partial_{u_{j}}\log c_{0}(u))\,k(u,\cdot)\Big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\big\langle g,\,\xi_{C_{0}}(u)\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}},

with component
\xi_{C_{0}}(u)_{j}:=\partial_{u_{j}}k(u,\cdot)+s_{j}(u)\,k(u,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
. Thus
\xi_{C_{0}}(u)\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
.

This witness representation allows us to solve the supremum in (
4
) analytically. The absolute value is redundant for
C_{n}
since
\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g]=0
by Lemma
1
. By Hilbert space duality, the supremum is attained when
g
aligns with the mean witness, converting the expression into a norm; squaring yields a closed-form V-statistic with the Copula-Stein kernel.

Theorem 1
(Closed-Form CSD)

The squared Copula-Stein Discrepancy for the empirical copula
C_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{U_{i}}
is

\text{CSD}^{2}(C_{n},C_{0})=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j}),

(6)

where
k_{C_{0}}(u,v)=\langle\xi_{C_{0}}(u),\xi_{C_{0}}(v)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
is the Copula-Stein kernel.

Proof.
By Lemma
1
,

\text{CSD}(C_{n},C_{0})=\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U_{i})\right|=\sup_{\|g\|\leq 1}\left|\left\langle g,\;\mu_{n}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\right|,\quad\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{i}).

By Cauchy–Schwarz, the supremum equals
\|\mu_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
(attained at
g=\mu_{n}/\|\mu_{n}\|
when
\mu_{n}\neq 0
). Then

\text{CSD}^{2}(C_{n},C_{0})=\|\mu_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}^{2}=\left\langle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{i}),\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j}\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{i}),\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}},

which identifies
k_{C_{0}}(u,v):=\langle\xi_{C_{0}}(u),\xi_{C_{0}}(v)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
.

The kernel
k_{C_{0}}(u,v)
is the computational heart of our method. We provide its explicit formula below.

5.2
A practical test

In practice, we estimate the discrepancy by the V-statistic form

\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j}),

which aligns with our asymptotic theory (Sec.
6
) and our implementations (Sec.
8
). We obtain critical values via the
wild bootstrap
:
draw
w_{i}
i.i.d. Rademacher and compute

T^{\ast}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{i}w_{j}\,k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j}).

Repeat
B
times (e.g.,
B=1000
) and reject for

\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}>q_{1-\alpha}\{T^{\ast}\}
.
This preserves the degeneracy and yields valid level-
\alpha
tests; see
Chwialkowski et al. 2016
.

Remark 5.1

The unbiased U-statistic
\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j})
is a common variant; we adopt the V-statistic for consistency with theory and streaming/GPU algorithms.

Proposition 5.2
(Explicit Kernel Formula)

Let
s(u)=\nabla\log c_{0}(u)
denote the copula score function. The Copula-Stein kernel admits the explicit form:

k_{C_{0}}(u,v)=s(u)^{T}s(v)k(u,v)+s(u)^{T}\nabla_{v}k(u,v)+s(v)^{T}\nabla_{u}k(u,v)+\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}^{T}k(u,v)).

(7)

Proof.
From the witness function definition in Proposition
5.1
:

\xi_{C_{0}}^{(j)}(u)=\frac{\partial k(\cdot,u)}{\partial u_{j}}+s_{j}(u)k(\cdot,u),

(8)

where
s_{j}(u)=\frac{\partial\log c_{0}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}
.

The Copula-Stein kernel is:

\displaystyle k_{C_{0}}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\langle\xi_{C_{0}}(u),\xi_{C_{0}}(v)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\langle\xi_{C_{0}}^{(j)}(u),\xi_{C_{0}}^{(j)}(v)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\langle\frac{\partial k(\cdot,u)}{\partial u_{j}}+s_{j}(u)k(\cdot,u),\frac{\partial k(\cdot,v)}{\partial v_{j}}+s_{j}(v)k(\cdot,v)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(11)

Expanding each inner product using bilinearity:

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\left\langle\frac{\partial k(\cdot,u)}{\partial u_{j}},\frac{\partial k(\cdot,v)}{\partial v_{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+s_{j}(u)\left\langle k(\cdot,u),\frac{\partial k(\cdot,v)}{\partial v_{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right.

(12)

\displaystyle\quad+s_{j}(v)\left\langle\frac{\partial k(\cdot,u)}{\partial u_{j}},k(\cdot,v)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+s_{j}(u)s_{j}(v)\langle k(\cdot,u),k(\cdot,v)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\bigg].

(13)

Now we apply the reproducing property of the RKHS. For any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
and points
a,b
:

\displaystyle\langle k(\cdot,a),k(\cdot,b)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=k(a,b),

(14)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial k(\cdot,a)}{\partial a_{j}},k(\cdot,b)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial k(a,b)}{\partial a_{j}},

(15)

\displaystyle\left\langle k(\cdot,a),\frac{\partial k(\cdot,b)}{\partial b_{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial k(a,b)}{\partial b_{j}},

(16)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial k(\cdot,a)}{\partial a_{j}},\frac{\partial k(\cdot,b)}{\partial b_{j}}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}k(a,b)}{\partial a_{j}\partial b_{j}}.

(17)

Applying these identities:

\displaystyle k_{C_{0}}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}\partial v_{j}}+s_{j}(u)\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial v_{j}}+s_{j}(v)\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}}+s_{j}(u)s_{j}(v)k(u,v)\right]

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(u)s_{j}(v)k(u,v)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(u)\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial v_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(v)\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}\partial v_{j}}.

(19)

Using vector notation:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(u)s_{j}(v)

\displaystyle=s(u)^{T}s(v),

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(u)\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial v_{j}}

\displaystyle=s(u)^{T}\nabla_{v}k(u,v),

(21)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}s_{j}(v)\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}}

\displaystyle=s(v)^{T}\nabla_{u}k(u,v),

(22)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}\partial v_{j}}

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}^{T}k(u,v)).

(23)

Therefore:

k_{C_{0}}(u,v)=s(u)^{T}s(v)k(u,v)+s(u)^{T}\nabla_{v}k(u,v)+s(v)^{T}\nabla_{u}k(u,v)+\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}^{T}k(u,v)).

(24)

5.3
Application to Archimedean Copulas

To make the kernel practical, we need an analytic form for the score function
s(u)
. For the broad and flexible class of Archimedean copulas, this score can be derived directly from the generator
\varphi_{0}

(
McNeil and Nešlehová 2009
)
.

Proposition 5.3
(Archimedean Score Function)

Let
C
be an Archimedean copula on
(0,1)^{d}
with generator
\varphi:(0,1]\to[0,\infty)
strictly decreasing and twice differentiable (Assumptions
1
), and let
\psi=\varphi^{-1}
. For
u\in(0,1)^{d}
, set

t\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{d}\varphi(u_{k}).

Then the coordinate-wise score of the copula density
c
is

\;\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\log c(u)\;=\;\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})\,\frac{\psi^{(d+1)}(t)}{\psi^{(d)}(t)}\;+\;\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u_{j})}{\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})}\;,\qquad j=1,\dots,d.

Proof.
The standard density form is

c(u)\;=\;\psi^{(d)}(t)\;\prod_{k=1}^{d}\!\big(-\varphi^{\prime}(u_{k})\big),\qquad t=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\varphi(u_{k}).

Taking logs,

\log c(u)=\log\psi^{(d)}(t)+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\log\!\big(-\varphi^{\prime}(u_{k})\big).

Differentiate w.r.t.
u_{j}
and use the chain rule:

\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\log c(u)=\Big(\partial_{t}\log\psi^{(d)}(t)\Big)\,\frac{\partial t}{\partial u_{j}}\;+\;\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\log\!\big(-\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})\big).

Since
\partial t/\partial u_{j}=\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})
and
\partial_{t}\log\psi^{(d)}(t)=\psi^{(d+1)}(t)/\psi^{(d)}(t)
while

\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\log\!\big(-\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})\big)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u_{j})/\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})
, the claim follows.

We used
\frac{d}{du}\log(\varphi^{\prime}(u))=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u)/\varphi^{\prime}(u)
since
\varphi^{\prime}(u)<0
for Archimedean generators.

Remark 5.2

Let
w=\psi(t)
. Then
\partial_{t}=\frac{1}{\varphi^{\prime}(w)}\partial_{w}
, so one can equivalently write

\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\log c(u)=\Big[\varphi^{\prime}(w)\,\partial_{t}\log\psi^{(d)}(t)\Big]\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})}{\varphi^{\prime}(w)}+\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u_{j})}{\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})}=\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})\,\partial_{t}\log\psi^{(d)}(t)+\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(u_{j})}{\varphi^{\prime}(u_{j})}.

One should avoid replacing
\partial_{t}\log\psi^{(d)}(t)
by ad-hoc identities in terms of
\varphi^{(\cdot)}(w)
unless one explicitly invokes the inverse-derivative

This analytic score makes the CSD kernel highly efficient. We can further show that for common choices of base kernels, the cost per evaluation is linear in the dimension.

Proposition 5.4
(Computational Complexity)

For separable kernels
k(u,v)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}k_{1}(u_{j},v_{j})
, each evaluation of the Copula-Stein kernel
k_{C_{0}}(u,v)
requires
O(d)
operations.

Proof.
For a separable kernel, the gradients and Hessian have special structure:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}}

\displaystyle=k_{1}^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})\prod_{i\neq j}k_{1}(u_{i},v_{i}),

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k(u,v)}{\partial v_{j}}

\displaystyle=k_{1}^{\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})\prod_{i\neq j}k_{1}(u_{i},v_{i}),

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}k(u,v)}{\partial u_{j}\partial v_{j}}

\displaystyle=k_{1}^{\prime\prime}(u_{j},v_{j})\prod_{i\neq j}k_{1}(u_{i},v_{i}).

(27)

The computational cost breakdown:

1.

Computing
k(u,v)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}k_{1}(u_{j},v_{j})
:
O(d)
operations

2.

Computing score functions
s_{j}(u)
for all
j
:
O(d)
operations (using Proposition 3.4)

3.

Computing gradient terms
\nabla_{u}k(u,v)
and
\nabla_{v}k(u,v)
:
O(d)
operations each

4.

Computing trace term
\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}^{T}k(u,v))
:
O(d)
operations

5.

Final combination in kernel formula:
O(d)
operations

Total:
O(d)
operations per kernel evaluation.

6.  Theoretical Properties

Having established the Copula-Stein Discrepancy, we now develop the theoretical foundations that justify its use as a principled statistical tool. Our analysis answers a sequence of fundamental questions: (i) Is CSD a valid metric that uniquely identifies the target copula? (ii) Does its empirical estimator converge at an optimal rate? (iii) Does it possess the claimed sensitivity to tail dependence? and (iv) Is the convergence rate fundamentally optimal? We answer each of these affirmatively in the following theorems.

6.1
Metrization and Consistency

The foundational property of any discrepancy measure is that it vanishes if and only if the compared distributions are identical. For CSD, this equivalence extends to characterizing weak convergence of probability measures on the unit hypercube.

Theorem 2
(Detectability)

Under Assumptions
1
(A1–A4),

\mathrm{CSD}(Q,C_{0})=0
if and only if
Q=C_{0}
.

Lemma 2
(Stein solution in the RKHS)

Under
A1
–
A3
, for any bounded–Lipschitz
h
with
\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h]=0
,
there exists
g_{h}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g_{h}=h
and

\|g_{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq C\|h\|_{\mathrm{BL}}
.

Proof.
Construct
g_{h,1}
by solving
\partial_{u_{1}}g_{h,1}+s_{1}g_{h,1}=h-\sum_{j\geq 2}(\partial_{u_{j}}g_{h,j}+s_{j}g_{h,j})

with zero trace at
u_{1}\in\{0,1\}
; the growth bound on
s_{1}
(
A1
) and Lipschitz
h
yield a uniform bound.
Approximate
g_{h,1}
by elements of
\mathcal{H}
using
A3
and pass limits through
\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}
by dominated convergence.

Theorem 3
(Metrization of weak convergence)

Let
(Q_{n})
be probability laws on
(0,1)^{d}
. Under
A1
–
A3
,

\mathrm{CSD}(Q_{n},C_{0})\to 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q_{n}\Rightarrow C_{0}.

Proof.
The forward direction follows from boundedness and continuity of
\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g
when
g\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
and
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1
, together with the Portmanteau theorem and the Stein identity under
C_{0}
in
A2
. We prove the reverse direction.

Fix any bounded Lipschitz
h:(0,1)^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Write
\bar{h}\equiv h-\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h]
. We claim there exists
g_{h}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g_{h}=\bar{h}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|g_{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq C\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{BL}},

(28)

for a constant
C
depending only on
c_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}
. This is a Stein equation for the operator
\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}
.

We construct
g_{h}
by solving a first order linear equation componentwise along the
u_{1}
coordinate with zero boundary data. Let
s_{1}=\partial_{u_{1}}\log c_{0}
. Define, for
u\in(0,1)^{d}
,

g_{h,1}(u)\equiv\int_{0}^{u_{1}}\bar{h}(t,u_{2:d})\,\exp\!\Big\{\int_{t}^{u_{1}}s_{1}(r,u_{2:d})\,dr\Big\}\,dt,\qquad g_{h,j}\equiv 0\ \text{for }j\geq 2.

A direct differentiation shows
\partial_{u_{1}}g_{h,1}+s_{1}g_{h,1}=\bar{h}
, hence
\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g_{h}=\bar{h}
. The boundary value
g_{h,1}(0,u_{2:d})=0
and
g_{h,1}(1,u_{2:d})
is finite due to
A1
and the boundedness of
h
.

We bound
\|g_{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
. Since
\mathcal{H}
is dense in
C^{1}
on compact subsets by
A3
, approximate the scalar map
u_{1}\mapsto g_{h,1}(u_{1},u_{2:d})
uniformly by
\mathcal{H}
functions for each fixed
u_{2:d}
and pass limits under the integral sign using dominated convergence, valid under
A1
. Standard RKHS interpolation bounds then give

\|g_{h,1}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C_{1}\|\bar{h}\|_{\infty}+C_{2}\mathrm{Lip}(h)\leq C\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{BL}},

where
C
depends on
\sup|s_{1}|
on compact subsets and on the kernel constants. Hence
g_{h}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}
and (
28
) holds.

By (
28
) and the Stein identity,

\mathbb{E}_{Q_{n}}[h]-\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h]=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{n}}[\bar{h}]=\mathbb{E}_{Q_{n}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g_{h}]\leq\mathrm{CSD}(Q_{n},C_{0})\,\|g_{h}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq C\,\|h\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\,\mathrm{CSD}(Q_{n},C_{0}).

Since
\mathrm{CSD}(Q_{n},C_{0})\to 0
, we get
\mathbb{E}_{Q_{n}}[h]\to\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h]
for all bounded Lipschitz
h
. This is equivalent to
Q_{n}\Rightarrow C_{0}
.

6.2
Finite‐Sample Convergence and Optimality (Detailed Proof)

Theorem 4
(Finite‐Sample Convergence Rate)

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}C_{0}
, and define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}\;=\;\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j}).

(29)

Under Assumptions
1
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}\bigr]=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C_{0}}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U,U)\bigr],

(30)

and hence
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
.

Proof.
We write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j})\Bigr]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j})\bigr].

(31)

Split into diagonal (
i=j
) and off‐diagonal (
i\neq j
) terms:

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{i})\bigr]++\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j})\bigr]\Bigr].

(32)

For
i=j
, by identical distribution,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{i})\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C_{0}}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U,U)\bigr],

(33)

so the diagonal part contributes

\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\times n\;\mathbb{E}[k_{C_{0}}(U,U)]=\tfrac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}[k_{C_{0}}(U,U)].

For
i\neq j
,
U_{i}\perp U_{j}
and by the witness‐function representation

\displaystyle k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j})=\langle\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{i}),\xi_{C_{0}}(U_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}},

(34)

together with Lemma
1
,
imply
\mathbb{E}[k(U_{i},U_{j})]=0
.
Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\bigl[n\,\mathbb{E}[k(U,U)]+n(n-1)\cdot 0\bigr]=\frac{1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[k_{C_{0}}(U,U)\bigr],

(35)

as claimed.

Corollary 6.1
(Optimal High‐Probability Convergence Rate)

Under the unified assumptions, the CSD estimator satisfies

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}=O_{P}\bigl(n^{-1/2}\bigr).

(36)

Proof.
Decompose
\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}=D_{\text{diag}}+D_{\text{off}}
with

\displaystyle D_{\text{diag}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{i}),\quad D_{\text{off}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j}).

(37)

We already have
\mathbb{E}[D_{\text{off}}]=0
and
\mathbb{E}[D_{\text{diag}}]=O(n^{-1})
.
Classical U-statistic theory for a degenerate kernel shows that
\text{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2})=O(n^{-2})
. Then Chebyshev’s inequality gives, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}\bigl(|\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]|\geq\epsilon\bigr)\leq\frac{\text{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2})}{\epsilon^{2}}=O(n^{-2}).

(38)

Since
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
, it follows that
\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}=O_{P}(n^{-1})
.
Finally, taking square roots (a continuous mapping) yields

\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}=O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
.

The preceding results establish that CSD is a well-behaved and statistically optimal estimator. However, its primary motivation is to overcome the blindness of traditional discrepancies to important dependence structures. We now prove that CSD is, by construction, sensitive to the crucial property of tail dependence.

6.3
Sensitivity to Tail Dependence

The distinguishing feature of CSD lies in its ability to detect differences in copula tail behavior that are invisible to conventional discrepancies. We formalize this intuition through our main theoretical contribution.

Theorem 5
(Tail-dependence sensitivity)

Assume
A1
–
A4
. Fix
d=2
. Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be copulas with well defined lower tail coefficients
\lambda_{L}(C_{1})
and
\lambda_{L}(C_{2})
. Let
\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{2}}(C_{1})\equiv\mathrm{CSD}(C_{1},C_{2})
denote the copula Stein discrepancy that uses
C_{2}
as target. If
\lambda_{L}(C_{1})\neq\lambda_{L}(C_{2})
then

\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{2}}(C_{1})\;>\;0.

The same conclusion holds for the upper tail with
\lambda_{U}
and a witness localized near the upper corner.

Proof.
We write
C_{0}\equiv C_{2}
for brevity. We prove the lower tail case. The idea is to solve the Stein equation for a lower-tail indicator and evaluate the resulting witness under
C_{1}
.

Fix a small
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
B_{\varepsilon}\equiv[0,\varepsilon]^{2}
. Let
\rho_{\delta}
be a standard
C^{\infty}
mollifier on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with support in a ball of radius
\delta
. Define
h_{\varepsilon,\delta}\equiv\mathbf{1}_{B_{\varepsilon}}*\rho_{\delta}
restricted to
(0,1)^{2}
, and then center it under
C_{0}
by
\bar{h}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\equiv h_{\varepsilon,\delta}-\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h_{\varepsilon,\delta}]
. The function
\bar{h}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is bounded Lipschitz uniformly in
\delta
with unit sup norm and Lipschitz constant of order
\delta^{-1}
.

By the Stein equation construction in the proof of Theorem
3
, there exists
g_{\varepsilon,\delta}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
with

\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\bar{h}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|g_{\varepsilon,\delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\leq C\|\bar{h}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\|_{\mathrm{BL}}\leq C^{\prime}\delta^{-1}.

Therefore,

\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{2}}(C_{1})\ \geq\ \frac{\mathbb{E}_{C_{1}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g_{\varepsilon,\delta}]}{\|g_{\varepsilon,\delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}}\ =\ \frac{\mathbb{E}_{C_{1}}[\bar{h}_{\varepsilon,\delta}]}{\|g_{\varepsilon,\delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}}\ \geq\ \frac{\mathbb{E}_{C_{1}}[h_{\varepsilon,\delta}]-\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h_{\varepsilon,\delta}]}{C^{\prime}\delta^{-1}}.

Let
\delta\downarrow 0
. Since
h_{\varepsilon,\delta}\to\mathbf{1}_{B_{\varepsilon}}
pointwise with bounded convergence, we obtain

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\mathbb{E}_{C_{i}}[h_{\varepsilon,\delta}]=\Pr_{C_{i}}\big(U\in B_{\varepsilon}\big)\qquad(i=0,1).

Hence

\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{2}}(C_{1})\ \geq\ c\,\delta\,\big\{\Pr_{C_{1}}(U\in B_{\varepsilon})-\Pr_{C_{0}}(U\in B_{\varepsilon})\big\}\quad\text{for all small }\delta.

We now send
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
while coupling
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)
so that
\delta(\varepsilon)\to 0
and
\varepsilon/\delta(\varepsilon)\to 0
. This keeps the Lipschitz penalty negligible relative to the tail mass. Using the definition of
\lambda_{L}
in
A4
and the monotonicity of
t\mapsto\Pr(U\in[0,t]^{2})
,

\frac{\Pr_{C_{i}}(U\in B_{\varepsilon})}{\varepsilon}\ \longrightarrow\ \lambda_{L}(C_{i})\qquad(i=0,1).

Therefore for small
\varepsilon
,

\Pr_{C_{1}}(U\in B_{\varepsilon})-\Pr_{C_{0}}(U\in B_{\varepsilon})\ =\ \big(\lambda_{L}(C_{1})-\lambda_{L}(C_{0})\big)\,\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon).

Choose the sign of the witness by replacing
h_{\varepsilon,\delta}
with its negative if needed, so the difference above is nonnegative. Then for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
,

\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{2}}(C_{1})\ \geq\ c\,\delta(\varepsilon)\,\big(\lambda_{L}(C_{1})-\lambda_{L}(C_{0})\big)\,\varepsilon+o\big(\delta(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon\big).

Since
\lambda_{L}(C_{1})\neq\lambda_{L}(C_{0})
, the right side is strictly positive for small
\varepsilon
. Thus
\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{2}}(C_{1})>0
.

The upper tail case is identical after mapping
u\mapsto 1-u
.

Proposition 6.1
(Quantitative tail sensitivity for Clayton,
d=2
)

Let
C_{\theta}
be the Clayton copula on
(0,1)^{2}
with parameter
\theta>0
. For any
\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}
, there exists a constant
c(\theta,\theta^{\prime},\mathcal{H})>0
such that

\mathrm{CSD}_{C_{\theta^{\prime}}}(C_{\theta})\ \geq\ c(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\,|\lambda_{L}(C_{\theta})-\lambda_{L}(C_{\theta^{\prime}})|.

Proof.
Follow the proof of Theorem
5
with
C_{0}=C_{\theta^{\prime}}
and recall that
\lambda_{L}(C_{\theta})=2^{-1/\theta}
. The Clayton score has explicit form
s(u)=\nabla\log c_{\theta^{\prime}}(u)
with polynomial growth near the origin, so the constants in the Stein-equation construction in the proof of Theorem
3
are finite. The lower bound follows by the same localization and limiting argument with the proportionality constant depending on
\theta^{\prime}
and the RKHS constants.

Remark 6.1
(Optional quantitative bound)

If one additionally assumes regular variation of the generator near the boundary,
e.g.
\varphi_{0}(1-x)\sim ax^{\kappa}
with
\kappa>0
, and matches the RKHS boundary rate
from Theorem
3
, a quantitative lower bound

\mathrm{CSD}\geq c\,|\lambda_{L}^{(1)}-\lambda_{L}^{(2)}|^{\alpha}
can be derived.
We omit this optional refinement here.

Finally, to complete our theoretical analysis, we prove that the
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
convergence rate is not merely an artifact of our estimator, but is the fundamental statistical limit for this problem
(
Tsybakov 2009
)
.

Having established the
O(n^{-1})
convergence rate, we now prove this rate is minimax optimal, confirming that no estimator can achieve faster convergence.

Theorem 6
(Minimax risk for the squared discrepancy)

Let
\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}
be any estimator of
D^{2}(Q)\equiv\mathrm{CSD}(Q,C_{0})^{2}
based on an i.i.d. sample of size
n
from
Q
. Work over the local nonparametric class

\mathcal{Q}\equiv\big\{Q_{\theta}:d\mathrm{TV}(Q_{\theta},C_{0})\leq\kappa\,n^{-1/2},\ |\theta|\leq\theta_{0}\big\},

constructed by exponential tilts along a bounded function with zero mean under
C_{0}
.
Under
A1
–
A3
there exist constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
such that

c\,n^{-2}\ \leq\ \inf_{\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}}\ \sup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}\ \mathbb{E}_{Q}\big[\big(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}-D^{2}(Q)\big)^{2}\big]\ \leq\ C\,n^{-2}.

Hence the minimax mean squared error for the squared discrepancy is of order
n^{-2}
.

Proof.

Upper bound.
Your Section 6 already proves that under
Q=C_{0}
,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=O(n^{-1})
and
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2})=O(n^{-2})
for the natural U statistic estimator. This gives
\mathbb{E}[(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}-0)^{2}]=O(n^{-2})
. The same calculation holds uniformly over a
\mathrm{TV}
ball of radius of order
n^{-1/2}
by a standard contiguity argument, which yields the stated
C\,n^{-2}
bound.

Lower bound.
Use a two point argument. Pick
h\in C^{\infty}_{c}((0,1)^{d})
with
\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h]=0
and
\|h\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. For small
|\theta|
, define a path

\frac{dQ_{\theta}}{dC_{0}}(u)\ \equiv\ 1+\theta\,h(u).

This is a valid density for
|\theta|
small enough since
h
is bounded and centered. Along this path,

D(Q_{\theta})=\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{\theta}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g]=\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}\big[(1+\theta h)\,\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g\big]=\theta\sup_{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}\big[h\,\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g\big],

since
\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g]=0
. By the Riesz representation in
\mathcal{H}^{d}
, the supremum equals
\|\Gamma h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}
where
\Gamma
is the bounded linear functional
g\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{C_{0}}[h\,\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g]
. Hence
D(Q_{\theta})=|\theta|\,L
with
L=\|\Gamma h\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}>0
for a suitable choice of nonzero
h
. Therefore

D^{2}(Q_{\theta})=\theta^{2}L^{2}.

Consider the hypothesis test
H_{0}:Q=Q_{+\theta}
versus
H_{1}:Q=Q_{-\theta}
with
\theta=\kappa n^{-1/2}
. The likelihood ratio between
Q_{+\theta}
and
Q_{-\theta}
over
n
observations has
\chi^{2}
divergence of order
\theta^{2}n=O(1)
. By Le Cam’s method,

\inf_{\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}}\frac{1}{2}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{Q_{+\theta}}\big[\big(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}-\theta^{2}L^{2}\big)^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}_{Q_{-\theta}}\big[\big(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}-\theta^{2}L^{2}\big)^{2}\big]\Big\}\ \geq\ c_{0}\,\big(D^{2}(Q_{+\theta})-D^{2}(Q_{-\theta})\big)^{2}\,\big(1-\mathrm{TV}(Q_{+\theta}^{n},Q_{-\theta}^{n})\big).

The total variation term is bounded away from one by the
\chi^{2}
bound. Since
D^{2}(Q_{+\theta})=D^{2}(Q_{-\theta})=\theta^{2}L^{2}
, the modulus of continuity argument gives instead

\sup_{Q\in\{Q_{0},\,Q_{\theta}\}}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\big[\big(\widehat{\mathrm{CSD}}_{n}^{2}-D^{2}(Q)\big)^{2}\big]\ \geq\ c_{1}\,\big(D^{2}(Q_{\theta})-D^{2}(Q_{0})\big)^{2}=c_{1}\,\theta^{4}L^{4}=c_{1}\,\kappa^{4}L^{4}\,n^{-2}.

This gives the stated
c\,n^{-2}
lower bound with
c=c_{1}\kappa^{4}L^{4}
.

6.4
Summary of Theoretical Guarantees

The theorems in this section provide a complete theoretical justification for CSD as a tool for copula analysis. We have established that CSD:

1.

Is a valid metric, as it metrizes weak convergence on the space of copula distributions (Theorem
3
).

2.

Is statistically optimal for finite samples, with its empirical estimator converging at the minimax optimal rate of
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
(Theorem
4
, Corollary
6.1
, and Theorem
6
).

3.

Is sensitive to fine-grained dependence, provably distinguishing between copulas with different tail dependence coefficients (Theorem
5
).

Together, these results confirm that CSD is a principled, practical, and powerful tool for dependence modeling.

7.  Extension Beyond Archimedean Families

Our analysis so far has leveraged the convenient generator-based form of Archimedean copulas. However, the CSD framework is far more general. The key insight is that our method does not depend on the Archimedean structure itself, but only on the analytical properties of the copula’s score function, which are captured by our Unified Regularity Conditions (Assumption
1
). In this section, we formally show that all our main results apply to any copula family satisfying these conditions, thereby extending CSD’s applicability to a wide range of important models.

7.1
Generality of the CSD Framework

To extend the framework, we first note that the Copula-Stein operator can be defined for any copula
C
with a sufficiently regular density
c
:

\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\partial g_{j}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}+g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial\log c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\right].

(39)

We now confirm that the key properties of CSD hold true in this general setting.

Theorem 7
(Zero-Mean Property for General Copulas)

Under Assumptions
1
(A1–A4), for any test function
g\in C_{c}^{1}((0,1)^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)]=0.

(40)

Proof.
By definition, the expectation is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)]

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\frac{\partial g_{j}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}+g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial\log c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\right]c(u)\,du

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{(0,1)^{d}}\left[\frac{\partial g_{j}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}c(u)+g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\right]du.

(42)

For each
j
, we apply integration by parts to the first term in the integral, integrating with respect to
u_{j}
:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)^{d}}\frac{\partial g_{j}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}c(u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)^{d-1}}\left[g_{j}(u)c(u)\right]_{u_{j}=0}^{u_{j}=1}du_{-j}-\int_{(0,1)^{d}}g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\,du,

(43)

where
du_{-j}
denotes integration over all coordinates except
u_{j}
. The boundary term,

\int_{(0,1)^{d-1}}[g_{j}(u)c(u)]_{u_{j}=0}^{u_{j}=1}du_{-j}
, vanishes due to Assumption
1
, which states that test functions in the RKHS vanish appropriately at the boundary.

Therefore, the integration-by-parts identity simplifies to:

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1)^{d}}\frac{\partial g_{j}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}c(u)\,du=-\int_{(0,1)^{d}}g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\,du.

(44)

Substituting this back into the expression for the expectation, the two terms for each
j
cancel out:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim C}[\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(U)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[-\int_{(0,1)^{d}}g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\,du+\int_{(0,1)^{d}}g_{j}(u)\frac{\partial c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}\,du\right]=0.

(45)

Theorem 8
(General Copula-Stein Kernel)

Under Assumptions
1
(A1–A4), let k:
[0,1]^{d}\times[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
characteristic kernel. The General Copula-Stein kernel is given by:

k_{C}(u,v)=s(u)^{T}s(v)k(u,v)+s(u)^{T}\nabla_{v}k(u,v)+s(v)^{T}\nabla_{u}k(u,v)+\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}^{T}k(u,v))

(46)

where
s(u)=\nabla\log c(u)
is the copula score function.

Proof.
The proof is identical to the Archimedean case (Proposition
5.2
), as it only relies on the witness function representation and the reproducing property of the RKHS, which hold for any smooth copula density.

7.2
Extension of Main Theoretical Results

We now prove that our main theoretical guarantees extend to general copulas under the regularity conditions.

Theorem 9
(Metrization for General Copulas)

Under Assumptions
1
(A1–A4),
k
is characteristic and
C^{2}
, for any sequence of probability measures
(Q_{n})
on
[0,1]^{d}
:

\text{CSD}(Q_{n},C)\to 0\quad\text{if and only if}\quad Q_{n}\Rightarrow C.

(47)

Proof.
The proof follows the same structure as Theorem
3
, but requires verification that the function class
\mathcal{F}_{C}=\{\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g:\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1\}
satisfies the conditions for the Portmanteau theorem under the general regularity assumptions.

Step 1: Boundedness on Compact Sets.

For any
g
with
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}\leq 1
:

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(u)|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}(u)+g_{j}(u)s_{j}(u)\right]\right|

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[|\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}(u)|+|g_{j}(u)||s_{j}(u)|\right].

(49)

Using the reproducing property (from Assumption
1
), we have
|g_{j}(u)|\leq M_{k}
and
|\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}(u)|\leq M_{k}^{\prime}
on any compact subset
K\Subset(0,1)^{d}
. From Assumption
1
:

\displaystyle|s_{j}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq\|s(u)\|\leq L\left(1+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\min\{u_{j}^{-\beta},(1-u_{j})^{-\beta}\}\right).

(50)

On the compact subset
K
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\min\{u_{j},1-u_{j}\}\geq\epsilon
for all
u\in K
and
j
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in K}|s_{j}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq L(1+d\epsilon^{-\beta})=:M_{s}(K).

(51)

This gives uniform boundedness on compact subsets:

\sup_{u\in K}|\mathcal{T}_{C_{0}}g(u)|\leq d(M_{k}^{\prime}+M_{k}M_{s}(K)).

(52)

Step 2: Equicontinuity.

For
u,v\in K
with
\|u-v\|<\delta
, the equicontinuity follows from:

•

Uniform continuity of
g_{j}
and
\partial_{u_{j}}g_{j}
(from the kernel properties in Assumption
1
)

•

Uniform continuity of
s_{j}
on compact subsets (from Assumption
1
)

The argument proceeds exactly as in the Archimedean case.

Step 3: Convergence-Determining Property.

The key observation is that the growth condition in Assumption
1
ensures that functions in
\mathcal{F}_{C}
remain bounded on compact subsets, while the boundary condition in Assumption
1
ensures proper behavior near the boundary. The characteristic property of the kernel (from Assumption
1
) then guarantees that
\mathcal{F}_{C}
is convergence-determining.

The remainder of the proof (both directions of the equivalence) follows the same pattern as Theorem
3
.

Theorem 10
(Finite-Sample Convergence for General Copulas)

Under the unified assumptions
1
, let
\widehat{\text{CSD}}_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k_{C}(U_{i},U_{j})
where
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\sim C
are i.i.d. Then:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\text{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=O(n^{-1}).

(53)

Furthermore, if
\mathbb{E}_{C}[k_{C}(U,U)^{2}]<\infty
, then for sufficiently large
n
:

\mathbb{P}(|\widehat{\text{CSD}}_{n}^{2}|\geq t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\right)

(54)

for appropriate
\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

Step 1: Mean Square Error Analysis.

The decomposition into diagonal and off-diagonal terms proceeds exactly as in Theorem
4
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\text{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[k_{C}(U_{i},U_{j})]

(55)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[k_{C}(U_{i},U_{i})]+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}[k_{C}(U_{i},U_{j})].

(56)

For
i\neq j
, by independence and the zero-mean property (Theorem
7
):

\mathbb{E}[k_{C}(U_{i},U_{j})]=\mathbb{E}[\langle\xi_{C}(U_{i}),\xi_{C}(U_{j})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{d}}]=0.

(57)

Therefore:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\text{CSD}}_{n}^{2}]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[k_{C}(U,U)]=O(n^{-1}).

(58)

Step 2: Moment Bound Verification.

We need to verify that
\mathbb{E}_{C}[k_{C}(U,U)]<\infty
under our assumptions. From the kernel formula:

\displaystyle k_{C}(u,u)

\displaystyle=\|s(u)\|^{2}k(u,u)+2s(u)^{T}\nabla_{u}k(u,u)+\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{u}^{T}k(u,u)).

(59)

Each term is finite in expectation:

•

\mathbb{E}[\|s(U)\|^{2}k(U,U)]<\infty
by Assumption C4 and kernel boundedness

•

\mathbb{E}[|s(U)^{T}\nabla_{u}k(U,U)|]<\infty
by Cauchy-Schwarz and C2

•

\mathbb{E}[|\text{tr}(\nabla_{u}\nabla_{u}^{T}k(U,U))|]<\infty
by kernel regularity

Step 3: Concentration Inequality.

The concentration bound follows from the same U-statistic theory, with the uniform boundedness now guaranteed by Assumptions C1-C2 on compact subsets where most of the probability mass lies.

7.3
Examples of Non-Archimedean Copulas

To illustrate the practical scope of our extension, we provide examples of important non-Archimedean copula families that satisfy our regularity conditions.

Example 7.1
(Elliptical Copulas: Gaussian case)

Consider the Gaussian copula with correlation matrix
\Sigma

(
Fang et al. 1990
)
:

C_{\Sigma}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})=\Phi_{\Sigma}\!\big(\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(u_{d})\big),

(60)

where
\Phi_{\Sigma}
is the multivariate normal CDF and
\Phi^{-1}
is the standard normal quantile.

Let
z(u)=(\Phi^{-1}(u_{1}),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(u_{d}))
. The copula density is

c_{\Sigma}(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(\Sigma)}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,z(u)^{\top}\!\big(\Sigma^{-1}-I\big)\,z(u)\Big).

(61)

Hence

\log c_{\Sigma}(u)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\,z(u)^{\top}\!\big(\Sigma^{-1}-I\big)\,z(u)-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\Sigma.

Since
\partial_{u_{j}}z_{j}(u)=1/\phi(z_{j}(u))
and

\nabla_{z}\log c_{\Sigma}(u)=-(\Sigma^{-1}-I)z(u)
, the
j
-th score is

s_{j}(u)=\frac{\partial\log c_{\Sigma}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}=-\,\frac{\big[(\Sigma^{-1}-I)\,z(u)\big]_{j}}{\varphi_{\mathrm{N}}\!\big(z_{j}(u)\big)},\qquad z_{j}(u)=\Phi^{-1}(u_{j}),

where
\varphi_{\mathrm{N}}(x)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\exp(-x^{2}/2)
is the standard normal PDF (to avoid collision with the random feature map
\phi
in Sec.
8
) and
[\cdot]_{j}
extracts the
j
-th component.

This score satisfies the growth conditions in Assumption
1
.

Example 7.2
(Vine Copulas)

Consider a C-vine copula constructed from bivariate copulas
(
Bedford and Cooke 2002
)

C_{j|1,\ldots,j-1}
for
j=2,\ldots,d
. Under appropriate regularity conditions on each bivariate component, the resulting multivariate copula density satisfies our conditions.

The score function involves derivatives of the conditional copula densities, which can be bounded using the same techniques as long as each bivariate component satisfies analogous regularity conditions.

Example 7.3
(Mixture Copulas)

Consider a mixture of copulas
(
Clemen and Reilly 1999
)
:

C(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}C_{k}(u)

(62)

where
w_{k}>0
,
\sum_{k}w_{k}=1
, and each
C_{k}
satisfies Assumption
1
.

The mixture density is
c(u)=\sum_{k}w_{k}c_{k}(u)
, and the score function is:

\frac{\partial\log c(u)}{\partial u_{j}}=\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}c_{k}(u)\frac{\partial\log c_{k}(u)}{\partial u_{j}}}{\sum_{k}w_{k}c_{k}(u)}.

(63)

If each component copula
C_{k}
satisfies the conditions in Assumption
1
, then the mixture also satisfies them.

7.4
Computational Considerations

The extension to general copulas preserves the computational efficiency of our approach, with the main difference being the evaluation of the score function
\nabla\log c(u)
.

Proposition 7.1
(Computational Complexity for General Copulas)

For copulas where the score function
\nabla\log c(u)
can be evaluated in
O(d)
time, each evaluation of the General Copula-Stein kernel requires
O(d)
operations for separable base kernels.

Proof.
The kernel evaluation formula is identical to the Archimedean case (Theorem
8
), so the complexity analysis follows immediately from Proposition
5.4
.

This computational efficiency makes our extension practical for high-dimensional applications beyond the Archimedean setting.

7.5
Summary of the Extension

This section demonstrates the generality of the CSD framework. By relying on the foundational properties outlined in our unified assumptions rather than a specific generative structure, we establish that CSD is not merely a tool for Archimedean copulas but a broadly applicable method. The examples of Elliptical, Vine, and Mixture copulas confirm that our framework encompasses many of the most important and widely used dependence models in modern statistics.

8.  Computational Complexity and Fast Algorithms

While the theoretical properties of CSD are compelling, its practical adoption hinges on computational feasibility. This section establishes that CSD is a tractable tool for modern, large-scale data analysis. We structure our analysis by addressing a series of key computational challenges: first, the dependence on dimension
d
in the exact kernel computation; second, the quadratic
O(n^{2})
dependence on sample size
n
; and finally, practical implementation on modern hardware including GPUs. For each challenge, we provide a rigorous analysis and an efficient algorithmic solution.

8.1

We begin with a detailed analysis of the computational cost for exact CSD evaluation, extending our earlier complexity results with complete algorithmic details.

Theorem 11
(Detailed Complexity Analysis)

O(d)

\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{j}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}k_{1}(u_{i},v_{i})=\frac{\partial k_{1}(u_{j},v_{j})}{\partial u_{j}}\prod_{i\neq j}k_{1}(u_{i},v_{i})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}k_{1}(u_{i},v_{i})\cdot\frac{\partial k_{1}(u_{j},v_{j})/\partial u_{j}}{k_{1}(u_{j},v_{j})}

(75)

This avoids recomputing the full product for each derivative.

Complexity:

•

Steps 1-6:
O(d)
kernel evaluations

•

Steps 7-14:
O(d)
gradient computations

•

Steps 15-24:
O(d)
score function evaluations

•

Steps 25-28:
O(d)
final combinations

Total:
O(d)
per kernel evaluation,
O(n^{2}d)
for full CSD.

The algorithms for exact CSD computation are efficient with respect to dimension, but their
O(n^{2}d)
complexity is prohibitive for datasets with a large number of samples
n
. This quadratic scaling is a common bottleneck in kernel methods. To overcome this, we move from an exact computation to an efficient approximation based on random features
(
Rahimi and Recht 2007
)
, a powerful technique for scaling kernel methods to large datasets.

8.3
Random Feature Approximations for Scalable CSD

While the
O(n^{2}d)
complexity of the exact CSD is acceptable for moderate
n
, large‐scale applications demand sub‐quadratic methods. We achieve this via a random‐feature approximation that reduces the sample‐complexity to nearly linear in
n
.

Setup.
Let
\phi:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbf{R}^{m}
be a random feature map for the base kernel
k
, satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\phi(u)^{\top}\phi(v)\bigr]=k(u,v),

(76)

and denote by
\nabla_{u}\phi(u)\in\mathbf{R}^{m\times d}
its Jacobian. Recall the
witness‐function
in the RKHS,

\displaystyle\xi_{C}(u):=\left(\partial_{u_{j}}k(\cdot,u)+s_{j}(u)k(\cdot,u)\right)_{j=1}^{d}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}.

(77)

We now build a finite‐dimensional surrogate of
\xi_{C}(u)
.

Definition 8.1
(Random‐Feature Witness)

For each
u\in[0,1]^{d}
define the
random‐feature witness

\displaystyle\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(u):=\mathrm{vec}\left(\nabla_{u}\phi(u)+\phi(u)s(u)^{\top}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{dm},

(78)

where
s(u)=(s_{1}(u),\dots,s_{d}(u))^{\top}
is the copula score. Here

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}\phi(u)+\phi(u)\,s(u)^{\top}\;\in\;\mathbf{R}^{m\times d}

(79)

has columns

\displaystyle\tilde{\xi}_{C}^{(j)}(u)=\partial_{u_{j}}\phi(u)+s_{j}(u)\,\phi(u),\quad j=1,\dots,d,

(80)

and
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
stacks its columns into a vector in
\mathbf{R}^{dm}
.

Assumption 3
(RF smoothness)

The base kernel
k
is
C^{2}
and admits a random feature map
\phi
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\phi}[\phi(u)^{\top}\phi(v)]=k(u,v)
and differentiation under the expectation is valid:

\mathbb{E}_{\phi}[\partial_{u_{j}}\phi(u)^{\top}\phi(v)]=\partial_{u_{j}}k(u,v),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\phi}[\partial_{u_{j}}\phi(u)^{\top}\partial_{v_{j}}\phi(v)]=\partial_{u_{j}}\partial_{v_{j}}k(u,v).

This holds for Gaussian/RBF features with
C^{2}
kernels.

Theorem 12
(Unbiased RF–CSD)

Under Assumption
3
,
define
\tilde{\Xi}_{C_{0}}(u)=\mathrm{vec}\!\left(\nabla_{u}\phi(u)+\phi(u)\,s_{0}(u)^{\top}\right)\in\mathbf{R}^{dm}
.
Then for any sample
U_{1:n}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\!\left[\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\Xi}_{C_{0}}(U_{i})\Big\|_{2}^{2}\,\bigg|\,U_{1:n}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k_{C_{0}}(U_{i},U_{j}).

Proof.
Since

\displaystyle\widehat{D}_{n,\text{RF}}^{2}=\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(U_{i})\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(U_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(U_{j}),

(81)

linearity of expectation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\widehat{D}_{n,\text{RF}}^{2}\mid U_{1:n}\bigr]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(U_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(U_{j})\bigr].

(82)

It therefore suffices to show, for any fixed
u,v
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(u)^{\top}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(v)\bigr]\;=\;k_{C_{0}}(u,v).

(83)

Write

\displaystyle\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(u)^{\top}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(v)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\bigl[\partial_{u_{j}}\phi(u)+s_{j}(u)\phi(u)\bigr]^{\top}\bigl[\partial_{v_{j}}\phi(v)+s_{j}(v)\phi(v)\bigr].

(84)

Taking
\mathbb{E}_{\phi}
inside the sum and using the RF‐properties

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\phi(u)^{\top}\phi(v)\bigr]=k(u,v),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\partial_{u_{j}}\phi(u)^{\top}\phi(v)\bigr]=\partial_{u_{j}}k(u,v),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\phi}\bigl[\partial_{u_{j}}\phi(u)^{\top}\partial_{v_{j}}\phi(v)\bigr]=\partial_{u_{j}}\partial_{v_{j}}k(u,v),

(85)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\phi}[\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(u)^{\top}\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(v)]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Bigl[\partial_{u_{j}}\partial_{v_{j}}k(u,v)\;+\;s_{j}(u)\,\partial_{v_{j}}k(u,v)\;+\;s_{j}(v)\,\partial_{u_{j}}k(u,v)\;+\;s_{j}(u)s_{j}(v)\,k(u,v)\Bigr],

(86)

which is exactly the Copula‐Stein kernel
k_{C_{0}}(u,v)
by Proposition 3.7.
Substituting back into the conditional expectation completes the proof.
Finally, computing each
\tilde{\Xi}_{C}(U_{i})
requires
O(md)
to form
\phi(U_{i})
and
\nabla_{u}\phi(U_{i})
plus an
O(md)
combine with
s(U_{i})
, totaling
O(nmd)
.

8.7
Summary of Computational Viability

Our computational analysis confirms that CSD is a practical tool for modern data science. We have shown that the exact kernel evaluation scales linearly with dimension, which is optimal. For the challenge of large sample sizes, we developed a theoretically-sound random feature approximation that achieves a nearly linear
O(nmd)
complexity. Finally, we outlined how both the exact and approximate methods can be dramatically accelerated using parallel hardware
(
Nickolls et al. 2008
)
and optimized for memory-constrained environments. This combination of theoretical guarantees and computational efficiency makes CSD a powerful and scalable method for real-world applications.

9.  Conclusion and Future Work

9.1
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced the Copula-Stein Discrepancy (CSD), a novel framework that fundamentally shifts the paradigm of goodness-of-fit testing from joint-density-based methods to dependence-structure-aware approaches. By constructing a Stein operator directly on copula densities, CSD addresses a critical blind spot in existing kernel-based methods: their insensitivity to higher-order dependence structures that are crucial in many scientific and practical domains.

Theoretical Contributions.
Our theoretical analysis provides the first comprehensive treatment of Stein methods in the copula domain. We established that CSD: (i) metrizes weak convergence on copula distributions, ensuring it detects any mismatch in dependence structure; (ii) achieves the minimax optimal convergence rate of
O_{P}(n^{-1/2})
; and (iii) is provably sensitive to tail dependence differences, with explicit lower bounds on the discrepancy.

Methodological Advances.
For Archimedean copulas, we derived closed-form expressions for the CSD kernel. Crucially, we proved that our framework extends far beyond this special case to any copula satisfying mild regularity conditions, encompassing elliptical, vine, and mixture copulas. This generality positions CSD as a unified tool for dependence modeling.

Computational Innovation.
We addressed the practical challenges of kernel methods through multiple algorithmic contributions, including exact computation with linear scaling in dimension and random feature approximations that reduce sample complexity from
O(n^{2})
to nearly linear time. These advances make CSD viable for modern large-scale applications.

CSD represents more than a new statistical test—it embodies a paradigm shift toward structure-aware inference. This paper has established the complete theoretical foundation and the practical, scalable algorithms necessary for dependence-centric model diagnostics in the era of complex, high-dimensional data.

9.2
Future Work

The theoretical framework established in this paper opens several high-impact research directions that span methodology, applications, and long-term vision:

9.2.1
Immediate Extensions

Two-Sample Testing.
Developing a CSD-based two-sample test for comparing dependence structures would address a fundamental need in exploratory data analysis.

Parameter Estimation.
Using CSD as an objective function for copula parameter estimation presents a compelling, robust alternative to maximum likelihood, particularly for tail dependence parameters.

Model Selection.
Extending CSD to select between competing copula families offers a principled approach to structural model selection based on discrepancy minimization.

9.2.2
Methodological and Theoretical Innovations

Deep Generative Models.
Incorporating CSD as a regularization term in VAEs or GANs could improve their ability to capture complex dependencies.

High-Dimensional Theory.
Extending our analysis to the regime where dimension
d
grows with sample size
n
is an important next step.

Streaming Algorithms.
Developing online versions of CSD for streaming data would enable real-time dependence monitoring in applications like algorithmic trading.

9.2.3
Empirical Validation and Applications

While this work establishes the theoretical foundations, systematic empirical validation represents a crucial future step. We envision several key directions:

Benchmark Studies.
Comprehensive simulation studies comparing CSD against established copula goodness-of-fit tests would be needed to quantify its practical advantages in finite samples, with a focus on power to detect tail dependence misspecification.

Real-Data Applications.
The framework can be validated in domains where tail dependence is critical, such as financial risk modeling, hydrology, and neuroscience, to demonstrate its ability to reveal insights missed by traditional methods.

Software Implementation.
The development of open-source implementations in R and Python will be essential for facilitating adoption and enabling reproducible research.

9.2.4
Long-Term Vision

Automated Dependence Discovery.
In the long term, CSD could form the core of algorithms that automatically discover and characterize dependence structures in complex datasets.

Integration with Causal Inference.
Exploring connections between CSD and causal discovery could lead to new approaches for identifying causal relationships based on changes in dependence structure.

Quantum Extensions.
As quantum machine learning develops, extending CSD to quantum probability distributions could provide new tools for quantum state verification and dependence modeling.
