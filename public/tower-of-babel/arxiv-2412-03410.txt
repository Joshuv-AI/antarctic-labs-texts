Free-Space Optical Modulation of Free Electrons in the Continuous-Wave Regime
Cruz I. Velasco, F. Javier García de Abajo

Abstract
The coherent interaction between free electrons and optical fields can produce free-electron compression and push the temporal resolution of ultrafast electron microscopy to the attosecond regime. However, a large electron–light interaction is required to attain a strong compression, generally necessitating short light and electron pulses combined with optical scattering at nanostructures. Here, we theoretically investigate an alternative configuration based on stimulated Compton scattering, whereby two counterpropagating Gaussian light beams induce energy jumps in a colinear electron beam by multiples of their photon-energy difference. Strong recoil effects are produced by extending the electron–light interaction over millimetric distances, enabling a dramatic increase in temporal compression and substantially reshaping the electron spectra for affordable laser powers. Beyond its fundamental interest, our work introduces a practical scheme to achieve a large temporal compression of continuous electron beams without involving optical scattering by material structures.

In this work, we theoretically demonstrate strong temporal modulation of e-beams in free space under CW operation conditions. We rely on stimulated Compton scattering between two counterpropagating light beams and an e-beam that shares the same optical axis (Fig.
1
). This configuration enables long millimetric interaction regions over which the amplitude of inelastically scattered electron components can build up to produce a sizeable spectral and temporal modulation. In the nonrecoil approximation (i.e., assuming a constant electron velocity), this scheme leads to electron energy combs completely analogous to those observed in PINEM. However, electron recoil becomes important for long interaction distances, leading to severe corrections in the electron spectra and enabling a dramatically increased degree of temporal compression. We discuss the characteristics of these unexplored physical effects and present simulations showing strong modulation for practical choices of CW electron- and light-beam parameters.

Results and discussion

We consider a Gaussian e-beam moving with velocity

Although single-photon emission or absorption by a moving electron is kinematically forbidden in free space, inelastic Compton scattering with a zero net balance of photon exchanges can conserve energy and momentum. In the present configuration, stimulated processes dominate and result in photon exchanges between the two light beams with a probability proportional to the number of photons in each of them (i.e., the product of the beam powers). Nonstimulated processes in which photons are deflected from the optical axis should contribute negligibly. Energy and momentum are then conserved under the condition

\displaystyle\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}\frac{1-v/c}{1+v/c}.

(1)

III
Conclusion

In conclusion, we have presented a feasible scheme for free-space free-electron optical modulation in the CW regime relying on the increased length of electron–light interaction that is achieved when electron and light beams share the same propagation axis. Stimulated Compton scattering is the underlying mechanism of interaction, requiring phase matching between the electron and light fields over long propagation distances. This condition is broken for electron components that undergo a large number of photon exchanges, thus resulting in electron spectra that differ dramatically from those observed in conventional PINEM experiments. This type of phase mismatch leads to a cutoff in the number of sidebands as well as a large correction in the degree of temporal compression, which is dramatically increased for a suitable combination of laser power and frequency. Our scheme is compatible with existing electron-optics setups, which could simultaneously combine temporal compression and lateral focusing down to the attosecond–sub-Åmström range. Beyond the emergence of such strong nonrecoil effects, our results support the use of free-space optical modulation as a practical scheme to temporally compress free electrons in free space in a CW regime.

\displaystyle{\rm i}\pi z_{0}\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\Big[B_{jj^{\prime}}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(s_{j}k_{j}+s_{j^{\prime}}k_{j^{\prime}})(z-vt)}+B_{jj^{\prime}}^{*}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}(s_{j}k_{j}+s_{j^{\prime}}k_{j^{\prime}})(z-vt)}\Big]{\rm e}^{-z_{0}|\Delta_{j}-\Delta_{j^{\prime}}|},

where we have disregarded the Gouy phase
{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\arctan(z/z_{0})}
and defined
\Delta_{j}=\omega_{j}/v-s_{j}k_{j}
,
A_{jj^{\prime}}=e^{2}({\bf E}_{j}\cdot{\bf E}_{j^{\prime}})/2\hbar mv\gamma\omega_{j}\omega_{j^{\prime}}
, and
B_{jj^{\prime}}=e^{2}({\bf E}_{j}\cdot{\bf E}^{*}_{j^{\prime}})/2\hbar mv\gamma\omega_{j}\omega_{j^{\prime}}
. We note that the
A
and
B
terms correspond to nonresonant (two-photon absorption or emission) and resonant (Compton scattering) processes, respectively. Assuming the phase-matching condition
\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}\,(1-v/c)/(1+v/c)
[Eq. (
1
)], we have
\Delta_{j}-\Delta_{j^{\prime}}=0
for all values of
j
and
j^{\prime}
. In addition, if
z_{0}
is large compared with the optical period (i.e., assuming that the interaction takes place over many optical cycles of the incident laser fields), we can neglect the
A_{jj^{\prime}}
terms and write Eq. (
A1.2
) as

\displaystyle\phi(z,t)=\phi_{\rm inc}(z,t)\;{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\chi}\exp\Big\{-\beta{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\Omega(z/v-t)}+\beta^{*}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\Omega(z/v-t)}\Big\},

(A1.3)

where
\Omega\equiv\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
defines the quantum of frequency exchanges between the light and the electron, while
\chi=2\pi(B_{11}+B_{22})z_{0}
is a global phase that we can ignore in this study. The wave function in Eq. (
A1.3
) has the same form as in PINEM
García de Abajo and Di
Giulio 2021
, but with a electron–light coupling parameter now defined as

\displaystyle\beta=2\pi{\rm i}B_{12}z_{0}=\frac{2\pi{\rm i}e^{2}c}{\hbar mv\gamma}\,\frac{1+v/c}{1-v/c}\frac{{\bf E}_{1}\cdot{\bf E}_{2}^{*}}{(\rm NA_{1})^{2}\omega_{1}^{3}}.

(A1.4)

Like in PINEM, using the Jacobi-Anger expansion, we can recast Eq. (
A1.3
) into Eq. (
4
):
\phi(z,t)=\phi^{\rm inc}(z,t)\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\chi}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\;{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\ell\Omega(z/v-t)}
with
\alpha_{\ell}=J_{\ell}(2|\beta|){\rm e}^{{\rm i}\ell\arg\{-\beta\}}
, showing that an initially monochromatic electron adquires energy sidebands corresponding to energy exchanges
\ell\hbar\Omega
with a probability
J^{2}_{\ell}(2|\beta|)
.

Appendix 2
Solution of equation (
2
) including recoil effects

To account for electron recoil, we must solve Eq. (
A1.1
), including the
\partial_{zz}
term. We obtain a semi-analytical solution by slicing the interaction region into
N
segments of length
\Delta z=L/N\ll z_{0}
centered at positions
\l_{n}=-L/2+(n+1/2)\Delta z
with
n=0,\cdots,N-1
, and approximating
1+(z/z_{0})^{2}\approx 1+(l_{n}/z_{0})^{2}
as a constant within each of them. In practice, we consider
z_{0}
to be small compared with
L
(i.e., the interaction occurs within a small region in between the two mirrors of Fig.
1
), so we take a large value of
L
to guarantee a negligible electron–light interaction at
z=\pm L/2
. Using the same vector potential and notation as in Sec.
Appendix 1
, Eq. (
A1.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\bigg[{\rm i}(\partial_{t}+v\partial_{z})+

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{2m\gamma^{3}}\partial_{zz}\bigg]\,\phi(z,t)\approx

(A2.1)

\displaystyle-v\sum_{jj}\Big[A_{jj^{\prime}}^{(n)}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(s_{j}k_{j}+s_{j^{\prime}}k_{j^{\prime}})z-{\rm i}(\omega_{j}+\omega_{j^{\prime}})t}+A_{jj^{\prime}}^{(n)*}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}(s_{j}k_{j}+s_{j^{\prime}}k_{j^{\prime}})z+{\rm i}(\omega_{j}+\omega_{j^{\prime}})t}\Big]\phi(z,t)

\displaystyle+v\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\Big[B_{jj^{\prime}}^{(n)}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(s_{j}k_{j}-s_{j^{\prime}}k_{j^{\prime}})z-{\rm i}(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j^{\prime}})t}+B_{jj^{\prime}}^{(n)*}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}(s_{j}k_{j}-s_{j^{\prime}}k_{j^{\prime}})z+{\rm i}(\omega_{j}-\omega_{j}^{\prime})t}\Big]\phi(z,t)

within segment
n
, where
A_{jj^{\prime}}^{(n)}=A_{jj^{\prime}}/[1+(l_{n}/z_{0})^{2}]
and
B_{jj^{\prime}}^{(n)}=B_{jj^{\prime}}/[1+(l_{n}/z_{0})^{2}]
are proportional to
A_{jj^{\prime}}=e^{2}({\bf E}_{j}\cdot{\bf E}_{j^{\prime}})/2\hbar mv\gamma\omega_{j}\omega_{j^{\prime}}
and
B_{jj^{\prime}}=e^{2}({\bf E}_{j}\cdot{\bf E}^{*}_{j^{\prime}})/2\hbar mv\gamma\omega_{j}\omega_{j^{\prime}}
(same coefficients as in Sec.
Appendix 1
).

At this point, we write the ansatz

\displaystyle\phi(z,t)=\phi_{\rm inc}(z,t)\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(t)\;{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\ell_{1}\omega_{1}(t-z/c)+{\rm i}\ell_{2}\omega_{2}(t+z/c)}

(A2.2)

with the initial condition
\alpha_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(-\infty)=\delta_{\ell_{1}0}\delta_{\ell_{2}0}
(i.e., an incident monochromatic electron). By introducing Eq. (
A2.2
) into Eq. (
A2.1
), we find a system of coupled first-order differential equations for
\alpha_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}(t)
. The discretization in
z
translates into an analogous discretization in time so that we approximate
z\approx l_{n}
in the vector-potential profile at times during which the electron is traversing interval
n
[i.e., for
t_{n}^{-}<t<t_{n}^{+}
with
t_{n}^{\pm}=(l_{n}\pm\Delta z/2)/v
]. We find the analytical solution

\displaystyle\alpha(t_{n}^{+})=\exp\big\{-{\rm i}M^{(n)}\Delta z\big\}\cdot\alpha(t_{n}^{-}),

where a matrix notation is used with
(\ell_{1}\ell_{2})
acting as matrix indices, and the matrix
M^{(n)}
has time- and position-independent elements

\displaystyle M^{(n)}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2},\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{2}^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\bigg[(\ell_{1}-\ell_{2})\omega_{1}\bigg(\frac{1}{v}-\frac{1}{c}\bigg)+\frac{\hbar(\omega_{1}\ell_{1}-\omega_{2}\ell_{2})^{2}}{2mc^{2}v\gamma^{3}}\bigg]\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle+\Big[B_{11}^{(n)}+B_{11}^{(n)*}\Big]\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{2}}+\Big[B_{22}^{(n)}+B_{22}^{(n)*}\Big]\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{2}}+2B_{12}^{(n)}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\ell_{1}+1)}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}(\ell_{2}-1)}

\displaystyle+2B_{12}^{(n)*}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\ell_{1}-1)}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}(\ell_{2}+1)}-A_{12}^{(n)}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\ell_{1}+1)}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}(\ell_{2}+1)}-A_{12}^{(n)*}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\ell_{1}-1)}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}(\ell_{2}-1)}

\displaystyle-A_{11}^{(n)}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\ell_{1}+2)}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{2}}-A_{11}^{(n)*}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}(\ell_{1}-2)}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}\ell_{2}}-A_{22}^{(n)}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}(\ell_{2}+2)}-A_{22}^{(n)*}\delta_{\ell_{1}^{\prime}\ell_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}^{\prime}(\ell_{2}-2)}.

The final wave function is obtained by the concatenation of all intervals, leading to

\displaystyle\alpha(\infty)=\exp\big\{-{\rm i}M^{(N-1)}\Delta z\big\}\cdots\exp\big\{-{\rm i}M^{(1)}\Delta z\big\}\cdot\exp\big\{-{\rm i}M^{(0)}\Delta z\big\}\cdot\alpha(-\infty).

(A2.3)

This approximation fulfills the condition that nonresonant processes (i.e., with
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}\neq 0

A1.1
)], as shown in Supplementary Fig.
S1
. The remaining resonant coefficients permit us to obtain the post-interaction coefficients
\alpha_{\ell}=\alpha_{\ell,-\ell}(\infty)
in the final wave function

\displaystyle\phi(z,t)=\phi^{\rm inc}(z,t)\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\chi}\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}\alpha_{\ell}\;{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\ell\Omega(z/v-t)}

[Eq. (
4
)]. We obtain converged solutions by limiting the number of sidebands to
|\ell_{1}|,|\ell_{2}|\ll 2|\beta|
, where
\beta
is the nonrecoil coupling coefficient [Eq. (
A1.4
)].
