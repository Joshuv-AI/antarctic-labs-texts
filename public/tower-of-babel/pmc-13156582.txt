Dramatically changing intracranial arteriopathy due to antiphospholipid syndrome: A case report
Authors: Hideki Nakajima, Yu Sato, Tetsushi Yago, Kazuhiko Tsuda, Hidenori Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune thrombophilia that rarely presents with progressive intracranial large-artery stenosis or occlusion, making early recognition difficult when vascular lesions mimic atherosclerotic disease. A 74-year-old female receiving edoxaban for atrial fibrillation presented with progressive left hemiparesis due to severe right internal carotid artery (ICA) stenosis. Angioplasty improved cerebral blood flow, but recurrent ischemic stroke occurred 3 months later due to restenosis, which was treated with repeat angioplasty and Wingspan stenting. Over the next year, she developed progressive right middle cerebral artery (MCA) stenosis that eventually led to occlusion, requiring superficial temporal artery-MCA bypass. After perioperative interruption of edoxaban, she developed in-stent thrombosis and bypass graft thrombosis. Laboratory testing revealed triple-positive antiphospholipid antibodies, confirming primary APS. Systemic heparinization followed by apixaban resulted in complete resolution of in-stent thrombosis, improved bypass flow, and partial MCA recanalization. This case illustrates that APS can present as a dramatically changing and potentially reversible intracranial arteriopathy, emphasizing the need to suspect APS in patients with recurrent or unexplained intracranial thrombosis.Keywords: Antiphospholipid syndrome, Cerebral infarction, Digital subtraction angiography, Intracranial arterial disease, Intracranial stent, Magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an autoimmune thrombophilia that can cause arterial and venous thrombosis [1]. Ischemic stroke occurs in approximately 20% of patients with APS, but progressive intracranial stenosis or occlusion is uncommon and may mimic atherosclerotic disease [[2], [3], [4]]. Early recognition is challenging, particularly when vascular lesions evolve despite standard antithrombotic therapy. Endovascular treatment such as balloon angioplasty and Wingspan stenting is an established option for symptomatic intracranial stenosis [5]. However, in-stent restenosis and thrombosis remain important complications, especially in patients with unrecognized hypercoagulable states [6,7]. Here, the authors report a rare case of dramatically changing and partially reversible intracranial arteriopathy ultimately diagnosed as APS, which was characterized by progressive internal carotid artery (ICA) and middle cerebral artery (MCA) diseases, delayed in-stent thrombosis, and bypass graft thrombosis after transient perioperative interruption of anticoagulation.

Case report

A 74-year-old female receiving edoxaban 60 mg/d for atrial fibrillation presented with progressive left hemiparesis lasting for 3 days. On admission, the patient was alert, and neurological examination revealed left hemiparesis (manual muscle testing [MMT] 3/5). Diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DWI) and arterial spin labeling magnetic resonance imaging (ASL) showed scattered acute border-zone infarctions and extensive decreased cerebral blood flow (CBF) in the right cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1A and B). Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) showed attenuated flow signals in the right ICA and MCA (Fig. 1C). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) confirmed severe stenosis of the C2 portion of the right ICA (arrowhead; Fig. 1D), whereas the right MCA was patent without stenosis (Fig. 1E). Laboratory data were unremarkable except for mild prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT: 58s). Aspirin 100mg/day, clopidogrel 75mg/d, edaravone 60 mg/d, and argatroban 60 mg/d were started.

On day 3, she developed left hemispatial neglect, suggesting hemodynamic deterioration, and therefore urgent percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) using a Gateway balloon 3×9mm (Stryker Neurovascular, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was performed (Fig. 1F–H). On the following day, ASL showed improved CBF in the right cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 1I), and MRA showed restoration of the right ICA and MCA flow (Fig. 1J). Her neurological deficits improved, with left hemiparesis recovering to MMT 4/5, and she was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital with modified Rankin Scale (mRS) 3 one month after onset.

Three months later, the patient was readmitted with impaired consciousness. DWI showed scattered acute infarctions in the right frontal lobe (Fig. 2A), and MRA showed reduced flow signals in the right ICA and MCA. DSA revealed pseudo-occlusion of the right ICA at the previously treated segment (Fig. 2B). Repeat PTA followed by Wingspan stenting (4.5 × 20 mm, Stryker Neurovascular, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was performed (Fig. 2C and D). On the day after stenting, MRA and ASL demonstrated restored ICA/MCA flow and improved CBF in the right cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 2E and F). The patient continued oral medication with aspirin 100mg/day, clopidogrel 75mg/day, and edoxaban 60mg/day, and discontinued aspirin 3 month after stenting.

Seven months after stenting, follow-up MRA revealed new stenosis of the M1 segment of the right MCA (arrow, Fig. 3A). Clopidogrel was switched to prasugrel due to suspected poor platelet inhibition. One and a half years after stenting, she developed recurrent transient left hemiparesis. MRA showed right MCA occlusion (Fig. 3B), and DWI showed new infarctions (Fig. 3C). DSA showed not only right MCA occlusion but also moyamoya-like vessels formation (Fig. 3D). ASL and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) confirmed decreased CBF and impaired cerebrovascular reserve (Fig. 3E–H). Superficial temporal artery (STA)-MCA bypass was performed after temporary discontinuation of edoxaban. MRA on postoperative day 1 confirmed bypass patency (double arrow; Fig. 4A), and DWI showed no acute cerebral infarction (Fig. 4B). ASL demonstrated improved CBF in the right cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 4C). Edoxaban oral administration was resumed on postoperative day 4.

Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) after superficial temporal artery (STA)-middle cerebral artery (MCA) bypass. On day 1 after STA-MCA bypass, MRA confirms patency of both bypass grafts (double arrow; A), diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) shows no new cerebral infarction (B), and arterial spin labeling MRI (ASL) demonstrates improved cerebral blood flow (CBF) in the right cerebral hemisphere (C). On day 8 after bypass surgery, MRA shows reduced blood flow in the bypass grafts (D), DWI demonstrates new infarctions in the right frontal lobe (E), and ASL shows decreased CBF in the right cerebral hemisphere (F).

On postoperative day 8, left hemiparesis worsened, and MRA revealed decreased bypass flow (Fig. 4D). DWI showed new infarctions in the right frontal lobe (Fig. 4E), and ASL demonstrated decreased CBF in the right cerebral hemisphere (Fig. 4F). DSA revealed in-stent thrombosis (double arrowhead; Fig. 5A) and reduced bypass graft flow (Fig. 5B). Laboratory tests showed APTT >150s, and cross-mixing test showed an immediate inhibition pattern, suggesting the presence of antiphospholipid antibodies (aPLs). The aPL tests revealed immunoglobulin G (IgG) anti-cardiolipin (aCL) antibody 6.5 U/mL, anti-β2-glycoprotein I (GPI) antibody 34.3 U/mL, and lupus anticoagulant (LA) 1.9. Autoimmune screening revealed that antinuclear antibody (ANA), anti-ds-DNA, anti-Sm, and anti-SS-A/SS-B antibodies were negative, and that complement levels were within normal limits. Rapid plasma reagin and treponema pallidum hemagglutination assay were also negative. Considering in-stent thrombosis and bypass graft thrombosis due to primary APS, edoxaban was discontinued and systemic heparinization (10000 IU/day) was started. On postoperative day 21, follow-up DSA showed resolution of in-stent thrombosis (Fig. 5C), improved bypass flow (Fig. 5D), and partial MCA recanalization (Fig. 5E). Heparin therapy was discontinued, and apixaban 10mg/day was started because warfarin was avoided due to fatty liver. The patient remained free of recurrent stroke and was transferred to a rehabilitation hospital with mRS 3. At 12 weeks later, repeat testing showed persistent elevation of 3 aPLs (IgG aCL antibody 22.2 U/mL, anti-β2-GPI antibody 67.1 U/mL, and LA 2.2) and confirmed primary APS. One year after bypass surgery, prasugrel was discontinued, and no cerebrovascular events have occurred for the following 2 years.

On day 8 after STA-MCA bypass, DSA reveals in-stent thrombosis within the right internal carotid artery stent (double arrowhead; A) and reduced blood flow through the bypass grafts (B). On postoperative day 21 during systemic heparinization, follow-up DSA demonstrates resolution of in-stent thrombosis (C), improved blood flow through the bypass grafts (D) and partial recanalization of the right MCA (E).

Discussion

This case highlights a dramatically changing intracranial arteriopathy that progressed despite standard antithrombotic therapy. The patient initially improved after balloon angioplasty for severe right ICA stenosis but later developed restenosis requiring Wingspan stenting, followed by progressive MCA stenosis and eventual occlusion. After STA-MCA bypass, both in-stent thrombosis and bypass graft thrombosis occurred after transient perioperative interruption of anticoagulation. Ultimately, APS-directed therapy resulted in complete resolution of in-stent thrombosis and partial recanalization of the native MCA (Fig. 6). This sequential course demonstrates that APS-related intracranial vasculopathy can be progressive yet partially reversible under appropriate treatment. APS was not initially suspected in this case because of the patient’s advanced age, coexistence of atrial fibrillation, and imaging findings mimicking intracranial atherosclerotic disease. In retrospect, the occurrence of early restenosis, progressive arterial changes despite adequate antithrombotic therapy, and recurrent thrombotic events should have raised suspicion for an underlying hypercoagulable condition such as APS.

APS is an autoimmune thrombophilia characterized by persistent aPLs–aCL, anti-β2-GPI, and LA–that promote thrombosis through endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, complement activation, and interference with endogenous anticoagulant pathways [2,8,9]. APS is classified as either primary or secondary, and is most commonly associated with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [8]. Clinically, APS is defined by the triad of arterial or venous thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, and persistent positivity of aPLs [1]. In the present case, autoimmune serology including ANA, anti-ds-DNA, anti-Sm, and anti-SS-A/SS-B antibodies was negative and complement levels were normal, indicating primary APS rather than secondary APS associated with SLE or other systemic autoimmune diseases. This distinction is clinically relevant because it supports a predominantly thrombotic rather than inflammatory mechanism of the intracranial arteriopathy, which aligns with dramatic vascular changes observed in our patient–progressive stenosis, in-stent thrombosis, bypass graft thrombosis, and partial recanalization after APS-directed anticoagulation.

Although ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack are the most common neurological manifestation, APS can also produce progressive large-artery stenosis or occlusion through chronic endothelial injury and intimal hyperplasia, a process often referred to as “APS-related vasculopathy” [8,9]. This intracranial arteriopathy typically coexists with thrombotic mechanisms, producing a combination of acute thrombosis, delayed restenosis, and fluctuating arterial patency [3,10]. In the present case, the progression from ICA stenosis to MCA occlusion despite ongoing edoxaban administration, followed by partial spontaneous recanalization after APS-directed treatment, strongly suggests that both thrombotic and proliferative mechanisms contributed to the dramatic vascular changes.

The Wingspan stent is a self-expanding, open-cell nitinol device designed for intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis [11]. While it offers flexibility and radial force, its structural characteristics may predispose to delayed endothelialization and local wall shear stress, which can contribute to in-stent thrombosis and restenosis [12,13]. Reported risk factors of the in-stent thrombosis and restenosis include high pre-procedural stenosis severity, long stenotic lesion, vessel angulation, and inadequate antiplatelet response [6,7,14,15]. Our patient exhibited 2 risk factors–severe ICA stenosis, and clopidogrel resistance. Importantly, APS-related hypercoagulability likely amplified these risks, creating an environment highly susceptible to delayed in-stent thrombosis. The occurrence of thrombosis shortly after perioperative interruption of edoxaban suggests that APS had been clinically active before its formal diagnosis and that temporary anticoagulant withdrawal in such patients may precipitate catastrophic events.

Another notable feature was the development of moyamoya-like collateral vessels after MCA occlusion. APS-related chronic endothelial injury can induce intracranial arteriopathy and stimulate collateral vessel formation in response to cerebral hypoperfusion [16,17]. These collateral networks resemble those seen in moyamoya disease radiographically but represent an adaptive and compensatory response rather than a primary moyamoya arteriopathy [3,9,10]. The subsequent partial recanalization of the MCA and improved bypass flow after APS-directed anticoagulation further support that the moyamoya-like vessels reflected a reversible vasculopathy secondary to APS rather than a fixed occlusive disorder.

Management of APS depends on the antibody profile and clinical severity, with triple-positive aPLs representing the highest-risk subgroup [9]. Multiple trials, including the TRAPS study, have demonstrated that direct oral anticoagulants are inferior to warfarin for preventing arterial thrombosis in high-risk APS [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Accordingly, current guidelines recommend warfarin (prothrombin time-international normalized ratio 2.0-3.0 or higher for arterial events) as the first-line therapy, with heparin bridging during periods when anticoagulation must be interrupted [22]. In this case, warfarin was avoided due to hepatic dysfunction. Therefore, systemic heparinization was initiated, followed by direct oral anticoagulation (DOAC) therapy under careful clinical monitoring. Edoxaban was initially used for atrial fibrillation. After the diagnosis of APS and thrombotic complications, anticoagulation was switched to apixaban, considering its stable pharmacokinetic profile and broader clinical experience in secondary thrombosis prevention, although evidence comparing DOACs in APS remains limited. The patient’s vascular lesion improved markedly after APS-specific anticoagulation, supporting the partially reversible nature of APS-related intracranial arteriopathy.

Similar cases of APS-related intracranial arteriopathy have been reported; however, clear longitudinal demonstration of dynamic progression followed by partial reversibility after anticoagulation, as observed in this case, remains limited [2,3]. Although immunosuppressive therapies, including corticosteroids, rituximab, and plasma exchange, may be considered in APS with systemic inflammation or catastrophic presentations, the vascular manifestations in our patient were purely thrombotic without evidence of systemic autoimmune activity [22]. Therefore, anticoagulation remained the mainstay of treatment, resulting in improvement of vascular lesions. This further supports that APS-related intracranial arteriopathy in this case was primarily driven by thrombosis rather than inflammatory vasculitis. Overall, the present case emphasizes the importance of recognizing APS early in patients with recurrent intracranial thrombosis, considering device-related and systemic hypercoagulable risks when using intracranial stents, and employing individualized perioperative antithrombotic strategies to prevent severe complications.

Conclusion

The present case illustrates that APS can present as a dramatically changing intracranial arteriopathy characterized by progressive stenosis and occlusion, in-stent thrombosis, and bypass graft thrombosis. The vascular lesions in this patient progressed despite antithrombotic therapy but improved after APS-specific anticoagulation, indicating that APS-related intracranial arteriopathy can be partially reversible. Recognizing APS as a potential cause of recurrent or unexplained intracranial thrombosis is essential, particularly when vascular lesions evolve despite appropriate treatment against arteriosclerotic lesions. Careful perioperative planning and individualized long-term anticoagulation are critical to preventing further thrombotic complications in patients with high-risk APS undergoing intracranial revascularization.
