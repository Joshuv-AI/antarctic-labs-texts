Multifunctional poly(citric acid)-based ionic liquid polymer composite for scale control and emulsion stabilization: structural and interfacial mechanistic evaluation
Authors: Mostafa Y. Nassar, Elbadawy A. Kamoun, Norah Alsadun, Emad M. Masoud, R. Hosny, Mahmoud F. Mubarak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mineral scale formation and emulsion instability are major challenges in high-salinity oilfield systems, leading to operational inefficiencies and reduced oil recovery. In this study, a partially bio-based ionic liquid polymer, poly(2-hydroxypropane-1,2,3-tricarboxylic acid)-based ionic liquid polymer (HPTA–ILP), was synthesized from citric acid and evaluated as a multifunctional additive for simultaneous scale inhibition and emulsion stabilization. The polymer was characterized using FTIR, XRD, and ¹H NMR, confirming the formation of an amorphous, ionically functionalized polymer network. Dynamic scale loop tests under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions (190 °F, 1500 psi) demonstrated effective inhibition of calcium-based scale formation, with a minimum inhibitor concentration (MIC) of 200 ppm. At this concentration, the polymer achieved an emulsion stability index (ESI) of 85% and improved oil recovery efficiency to 87%. The inhibition mechanism involves calcium ion complexation, adsorption on crystal growth sites, and interfacial stabilization through electrostatic and steric effects. These results highlight the potential of HPTA–ILP as a multifunctional additive for improving flow assurance and enhanced oil recovery performance in high-salinity environments.

Introduction

The development of advanced chemical materials for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) has gained significant attention due to the increasing demand for efficient hydrocarbon production under harsh reservoir conditions1–5. Oilfield systems typically operate under high temperature, high salinity, and high pressure, which impose strict requirements on the chemical stability and functional performance of treatment agents6,7. Among the major operational challenges are inorganic scale formation and emulsion instability in produced water, both of which can lead to equipment fouling, flow restriction, and reduced oil recovery efficiency8,9.

Inorganic scales such as calcium carbonate (CaCO3) and calcium sulfate (CaSO4) commonly form due to changes in thermodynamic conditions or mixing of incompatible waters10–14. These deposits can severely impair both surface and subsurface facilities. Conventional scale inhibitors, including phosphonates (e.g., ATMP and DTPMP) and polymeric inhibitors such as polyacrylates and polymaleates, are widely used to control scale formation through mechanisms such as threshold inhibition, crystal distortion, and dispersion15–18. However, these materials suffer from several limitations, including poor biodegradability, reduced efficiency under high salinity and high ionic strength conditions, and limited thermal stability19. In addition, most conventional inhibitors are designed for single-function performance and do not address interfacial challenges associated with produced water systems15–17.

In recent years, environmentally friendly alternatives have been explored to overcome these limitations18. Citric acid has attracted attention as a green chelating agent due to its ability to complex divalent cations such as Ca2+. However, its practical application is restricted by its low molecular weight, rapid consumption, and lack of interfacial activity20,21. On the other hand, ionic liquid-based materials have demonstrated promising properties, including enhanced thermal stability, solubility, and interfacial activity22. Nevertheless, most reported systems focus on either scale inhibition or interfacial modification independently, with limited integration of both functionalities in a single material, particularly under high-salinity and high-temperature conditions23.

To address these challenges, this study presents a partially bio-based ionic liquid polymer (HPTA–ILP) synthesized via polycondensation of citric acid followed by ionic functionalization with triethylamine24–26. The polymer combines a polycarboxylate backbone, which provides multiple binding sites for metal ion complexation, with ionic liquid moieties that enhance interfacial activity and physicochemical stability27–29. This molecular design enables simultaneous scale inhibition and emulsion stabilization within a single system30–32.

The novelty of this work lies in the development of a multifunctional polymer that integrates ion complexation, adsorption onto crystal growth sites, and interfacial activity under high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) conditions. Unlike previously reported citric acid-based or ionic liquid-modified systems, which typically address individual functionalities, the proposed material demonstrates dual performance in controlling mineral scale formation and improving emulsion stability in produced water systems.

The novelty of this work lies in the development of a multifunctional ionic liquid polymer (HPTA–ILP) that integrates a poly(citric acid) backbone with ionic functionalization, enabling simultaneous ion complexation, adsorption onto crystal growth sites, and enhanced interfacial activity under high-pressure, high-temperature (HPHT) conditions. Unlike previously reported citric acid-based or ionic liquid-modified systems, which typically address individual functionalities, the proposed material demonstrates dual performance in both controlling mineral scale formation and improving emulsion stability in produced water systems. Furthermore, the material is evaluated under realistic conditions using real field samples, providing practical relevance beyond conventional laboratory-scale investigations.

In this study, the term “bio-based” refers to materials derived wholly or partially from renewable biomass resources. Citric acid, produced via fermentation, constitutes the renewable backbone of the polymer, while triethylamine, used for ionic functionalization, is fossil-derived. Based on the stoichiometric composition, the renewable carbon content (RCC) of HPTA–ILP is estimated to be approximately 60–70%, indicating a predominantly bio-derived structure.

The main objective of this study is to develop and evaluate a multifunctional ionic liquid polymer capable of operating effectively under harsh oilfield conditions. Specifically, the work aims to: (i) synthesize a citric acid-based ionic liquid polymer; (ii) evaluate its scale inhibition performance under HPHT conditions; (iii) investigate its effect on emulsion stability and oil recovery; and (iv) elucidate the underlying mechanisms governing its multifunctional behavior.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and water samples

Two produced water samples, labeled WW1 and WW2, were collected from the Middle Bahariya oilfield for use in the study due to their relevance to current enhanced oil recovery (EOR) practices in the region. All reagents used were of analytical grade. Citric acid (≥ 99.5%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.9%), sulfuric acid (≥ 98%), and triethylamine (≥ 99.5%) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethanol, acetone, and methanol were supplied by Merck and used as received, without further purification.

Synthesis procedure of HPTA-ILP: a poly(citric acid)-based ionic liquid polymer

The HPTA–ILP synthesis was performed in two consecutive stages. In the first stage, 20 g of citric acid was dissolved in 100 mL DMSO and heated to 180 °C under reduced pressure (5 mmHg) for 4 h in the presence of 1 wt% sulfuric acid as a catalyst. The resulting intermediate polymer was precipitated using ethanol, filtered, washed, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 24 h33.

In the second stage, 10 g of the dry polymer was redissolved in 50 mL methanol, and an equimolar amount of triethylamine (10 g) was gradually added over 30 min at room temperature. The mixture was stirred for 6 h at 600 rpm to ensure complete ionic functionalization. The final product was precipitated in acetone, dialyzed against deionized water for 48 h, and vacuum-dried at 60 °C, yielding HPTA–ILP with an average yield of 89 ± 2%. Each batch was prepared in triplicate to confirm reproducibility34.

HPTA–ILP can be considered a functional composite polymer network, where the poly(citric acid) backbone is ionically modified with triethylammonium groups, forming nanoscale ionic domains that enhance performance under load-bearing flow conditions35. Characterization techniques

The structural and functional properties of HPTA-ILP were systematically characterized. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed using a Shimadzu 6100 A instrument to identify the key functional groups within the polymer matrix. The crystallinity and amorphous nature of the polymer were assessed through X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using a PANalytical X’Pert diffractometer equipped with Cu-Kα radiation. Furthermore, the chemical structure and polymer composition were verified by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy using a Varian Mercury 300 MHz spectrometer.

Molecular weight and structural characterization

The molecular weight distribution of HPTA–ILP was determined using gel permeation chromatography (GPC) calibrated with polyethylene glycol (PEG) standards, enabling reliable estimation of the number‑average molecular weight (Mn), weight‑average molecular weight (Mw), and polydispersity index (PDI)36. GPC remains a standard analytical technique for polymer characterization, providing insights into the distribution of chain lengths and the overall macromolecular architecture that influence rheological and functional performance.

The degree of ionic functionalization (DoF) was estimated from the ¹H NMR spectra by integrating characteristic proton resonances associated with the triethylammonium groups relative to signals from the citric acid backbone. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy is a widely accepted method for quantifying functional group incorporation in modified polymer systems, allowing assessment of modification efficiency and structural composition37.

The combined use of GPC and ¹H NMR provides complementary information: while GPC quantifies the macromolecular size and dispersity, ¹H NMR confirms the chemical identity and extent of ionic modification imparted by the functional groups introduced into the polymer backbone, both of which are critical for understanding structure–property relationships in ionic polymeric inhibitors38.

Rheological characterization of HPTA–ILP

The viscosity of HPTA–ILP solutions was measured using a Brookfield viscometer at 25 °C to determine their flow behavior under conditions relevant to injection and porous‑media flow. The polymer solution exhibited a measured viscosity of 8.6 mPa s at 200 ppm, indicating a low‑to‑moderate viscosity range that is suitable for injection applications under typical reservoir conditions without causing severe injectivity loss. This viscosity level can improve mobility control while preserving good propagation through reservoir pore networks39.

The rheological profile displayed slight shear‑thinning (pseudoplastic) behavior, with viscosity decreasing at higher shear rates. Such shear‑thinning characteristics are beneficial for injection into porous media because they enhance fluid mobility at the pore scale while still contributing to improved sweep efficiency—lower effective viscosity at high shear rates near the wellbore and higher effective viscosity at lower shear rates in the reservoir interior can improve displacement efficiency39.

Shear‑dependent viscosity is an expected behavior for polymer solutions used in enhanced oil recovery (EOR) and mobility control applications. In many polymer systems (e.g., partially hydrolyzed polyacrylamide), rheological measurements under steady shear show that solutions transition from near‑Newtonian behavior at low concentrations or low shear rates to pronounced shear‑thinning at realistic shear rates experienced in porous media40.

The observed rheological behavior reflects the intrinsic molecular alignment and chain dynamics under shear, which enhances the ease of flow when stress is applied. These properties are consistent with the mobility control requirements in polymer flooding, where a balance between enhanced viscosity for displacement efficiency and manageable shear response for injectivity is critical.

Assessment of produced water quality and its impact on scale formation

Real produced water samples obtained from the Middle Bahariya oilfield were used in this study to accurately simulate field-representative conditions. Unlike synthetic brines, produced water contains complex mixtures of dissolved salts, multivalent ions, and trace components that strongly influence scale formation and interfacial behavior. Therefore, the use of actual samples provides a more reliable evaluation of inhibitor performance under realistic operating environments.

To ensure consistency and reproducibility, multiple batches of each sample (WW1 and WW2) were collected under identical operating conditions and stored in sealed containers to prevent compositional changes. Prior to analysis, samples were filtered (0.45 μm) and homogenized. The physicochemical properties of all batches were measured and showed variations within ± 3%, confirming high consistency. All experiments were conducted using the same batch to eliminate variability associated with temporal fluctuations.

All physicochemical parameters were measured according to standard methods for the examination of water and wastewater (APHA, 23rd edition, 2017) and relevant ASTM International standards41. Specific analytical procedures for parameters such as pH, total dissolved solids (TDS), alkalinity, hardness, and conductivity were performed following the corresponding standardized methods. Calibration was conducted using certified reference standards prior to analysis.

Metal ion concentrations (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, and Fe2+/Fe3+) were determined using inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy (ICP–OES, PerkinElmer Optima 8000). Samples were acidified with nitric acid (2 vol%) prior to analysis to prevent precipitation. Calibration curves were established over a concentration range of 0.1–1000 mg/L with correlation coefficients (R² > 0.999). Quality control was verified using blanks, duplicate samples, and certified reference materials, with analytical precision within ± 3%.

Anions (Cl−, SO42−, NO3−) were analyzed using ion chromatography (IC), while alkalinity and hardness were determined via standard titration methods in accordance with APHA procedures.

The results of the physicochemical analysis are summarized in Table 1. The two samples exhibited notable differences in composition, which directly influence their scaling tendencies. Sample WW1 showed significantly higher salinity, with TDS reaching 116,988.9 mg/L and high concentrations of Na+ and Cl−, resulting in elevated ionic strength. It also exhibited higher hardness and calcium content, indicating a strong tendency for calcium-based scale formation (e.g., CaCO3 and CaSO4). In contrast, WW2 presented considerably lower TDS, hardness, and calcium concentration, along with slightly higher alkalinity and near-neutral pH, suggesting a comparatively lower scaling potential.

These compositional differences between WW1 and WW2 are attributed to variations in reservoir conditions and ion distribution, which play a critical role in governing scale formation behavior. The use of both samples allows for a comprehensive assessment of inhibitor performance under varying salinity and scaling severity conditions.

Mixing compatibility testing

The mixing compatibility testing was conducted following standard jar test procedures commonly adopted in produced water evaluation and oilfield compatibility assessment studies (e.g., API RP and related laboratory protocols) to simulate mechanical agitation and mixing conditions between produced water and crude oil42. The compatibility of the produced water samples (WW1 and WW2) with crude oil was evaluated through dynamic mixing at various water‑to‑oil volume ratios, which replicates the shear and turbulence conditions experienced in subsurface flow and surface facilities during production and injection operations43.

After mixing, the resulting emulsions were visually observed and recorded over time to assess their physical stability and phase behavior. Visual monitoring remains a widely accepted practical approach to characterize emulsion breakdown and stability in laboratory conditions44. The emulsion stability index (ESI) was calculated based on the extent and rate of phase separation, where higher ESI values indicate greater resistance to phase separation, stronger dispersion of water droplets in oil, and enhanced water–oil interaction44. Emulsion stability is governed by interfacial properties, natural surfactants (e.g., resins, asphaltenes), and fluid compositions inherent to both produced water and crude oil, factors extensively documented to influence emulsion behavior in petroleum systems43.

This evaluation provides essential insights into the compatibility behavior of each produced water sample under mechanical mixing and its potential influence on emulsion formation, stability, and oil recovery performance, including implications for separator efficiency, flow assurance, and re‑injection strategies.

Evaluation of scaling tendency using scalechem modeling

The scaling tendency of the produced water samples was evaluated to assess the likelihood of inorganic scale formation using ScaleChem™ under field-relevant conditions25. The analysis focused primarily on carbonate and sulfate scaling, which are commonly encountered in high-salinity oilfield systems. The saturation indices of potential scale-forming salts were estimated based on the measured ionic composition, pH, and temperature conditions45. Particular attention was given to calcium carbonate (CaCO3) and calcium sulfate (CaSO4) systems due to their dominant role in formation damage and flow assurance challenges46.

Experimental evaluation of scale inhibitor HPTA-ILP

To evaluate inhibitor performance HPTA-ILP, a series of controlled experiments were conducted using varying concentrations of the synthesized inhibitor under identical operational conditions47. The inhibition efficiency was determined by comparing the residual concentration of scale-forming ions before and after treatment48. All experiments were conducted under controlled temperature and pH conditions to simulate reservoir environments. The solutions were prepared using the characterized produced water samples (WW1 and WW2), ensuring that the experimental conditions reflect realistic ionic strength and compositional complexity.

Rationale for experimental conditions and concentration ranges

The selection of experimental parameters and material concentrations was based on a combination of field-representative conditions and literature-reported practices for scale inhibition and enhanced oil recovery (EOR) studies. The temperature (190 °F) and pressure (1500 psi) used in dynamic scale loop tests were chosen to simulate typical reservoir conditions encountered in the Middle Bahariya oilfield.

The concentration range of HPTA–ILP (30–200 ppm) was selected based on standard dosage ranges for commercial scale inhibitors, which typically operate between 10 and 500 ppm depending on system severity. Preliminary screening experiments indicated that concentrations below 30 ppm were insufficient to prevent scale formation, while concentrations above 200 ppm showed minimal additional improvement, justifying the selected range.

For interfacial tension (IFT) measurements, higher concentrations (up to 7 g/L) were investigated to fully evaluate the surface activity and adsorption behavior of the polymer. However, it is important to note that such concentrations are used for mechanistic evaluation rather than direct field application.

The selection of water mixing ratios (WW2:WW1) was designed to simulate various operational scenarios involving incompatible water sources, allowing systematic evaluation of scaling severity and inhibitor performance. These conditions ensure that the experimental design reflects both practical relevance and comprehensive mechanistic understanding.”

High-pressure dynamic scale loop (DSL) evaluation

The performance of HPTA–ILP was evaluated using a high-pressure dynamic scale loop (DSL) system designed to simulate reservoir flow conditions (Fig. 2). The experimental setup consists of high-pressure pumps for brine injection, a stainless-steel coil (test section), pressure transducers, a back-pressure regulator, and a temperature-controlled oven49.

In a typical experiment, two brine solutions representing scaling ions (cationic and anionic solutions) were injected separately into the loop at controlled flow rates and mixed at a designated point. The system was maintained at 190 °F and 1500 psi to replicate reservoir conditions. The inhibitor solution was injected continuously at predetermined concentrations.

Scale formation was monitored by measuring the differential pressure (ΔP) across the test section. An increase in ΔP of 1 psi was considered indicative of scale deposition. The time required to reach this threshold was recorded and compared with the blank test (without inhibitor) to determine inhibition efficiency and minimum inhibitor concentration (MIC). Each experiment was repeated three times to ensure reproducibility, and the average values were reported.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using OriginPro software to evaluate data consistency. The relative standard deviation (RSD) for key measurements was found to be less than 5%, indicating high reproducibility. Additionally, error bars were included in graphical representations where applicable. One-way ANOVA analysis was performed to confirm the statistical significance of the effect of HPTA–ILP concentration on scale inhibition efficiency, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Fig. 5 demonstrate the structural evolution during the stepwise synthesis of the HPTA-ILP ionic liquid polymer. Citric acid exhibits sharp and well-defined crystalline peaks between 2θ = 15–35°, indicative of its highly ordered molecular arrangement. Upon partial polycondensation, the intermediate polymer shows a broad, less intense halo, suggesting a transition from crystalline to semi-amorphous structure due to polymer chain formation.

The final HPTA-ILP exhibits a fully amorphous profile with a broad diffuse halo centered around 2θ ≈ 20°, reflecting the disruption of ordered packing and the formation of a disordered ionic network induced by triethylammonium functionalization. This pronounced reduction in crystallinity confirms the successful incorporation of ionic moieties and crosslinking within the polymer matrix.

Overall, the XRD results, in conjunction with FTIR analyses, validate the successful stepwise synthesis of HPTA-ILP and the structural transformation from crystalline monomer to amorphous ionic polymer network.

1H NMR Spectral Analysis of HPTA-ILP

The ¹H NMR spectrum of HPTA–ILP (Fig. 6) provides detailed structural confirmation. Signals at δ = 2.5–3.0 ppm are attributed to methylene protons adjacent to carboxylate groups in the citric acid backbone. Peaks at δ = 3.1–3.3 ppm correspond to the methylene protons of the triethylammonium groups, while signals at δ = 1.1–1.3 ppm are assigned to terminal methyl groups (–CH3) of triethylamine. The appearance of these characteristic peaks, along with their integration ratios, confirms successful ionic functionalization. Compared to the intermediate polymer, the presence of additional aliphatic peaks associated with triethylamine further supports the formation of the ionic liquid polymer.

Thermal stability analysis of HPTA–ILP

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to evaluate the thermal stability of HPTA–ILP (Fig. 7). The polymer exhibited an initial weight loss below 120 °C corresponding to moisture removal. The main degradation stage occurred between 250 and 380 °C, associated with decomposition of ester linkages and ionic groups. The polymer retained approximately 65% of its mass up to 250 °C, indicating excellent thermal stability well above typical oilfield operating temperatures (~ 90–150 °C).

Additionally, thermal aging tests conducted at 90 °C for 7 days showed no significant loss in scale inhibition efficiency, confirming the chemical stability of HPTA–ILP under prolonged exposure to reservoir conditions.

Molecular weight and degree of functionalization

GPC analysis revealed that HPTA–ILP possesses a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of approximately 8,500 g/mol and a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 13,200 g/mol, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.55, indicating a moderately broad molecular weight distribution typical of polycondensation polymers.

The degree of ionic functionalization (DoF), calculated from ¹H NMR peak integration, was estimated to be ~ 0.68, indicating that approximately 68% of the available carboxylic groups were converted into ionic carboxylate–ammonium pairs. This confirms the successful incorporation of ionic liquid functionalities into the polymer backbone.

Rheological of HPTA–ILP

The relatively low viscosity of HPTA–ILP ensures minimal resistance to flow during injection, thereby preserving injectivity in porous formations. At the same time, its slight increase in viscosity compared to brine contributes to improved mobility control by reducing the mobility ratio between injected water and crude oil. This effect enhances sweep efficiency while avoiding formation damage typically associated with high-viscosity polymers. Furthermore, the combination of moderate viscosity and strong interfacial activity allows HPTA–ILP to function effectively without significantly increasing pumping energy requirements.

Effect of Na+/K+ ratio on scaling propensity

Differences in scaling behavior between WW1 and WW2 are primarily linked to their ionic composition, total dissolved solids (TDS), and thermodynamic saturation conditions. WW1 contained higher concentrations of calcium (4,145.28 mg/L), sodium (38,748.60 mg/L), and chloride (65,860.91 mg/L), resulting in elevated ionic strength and greater potential for mineral supersaturation. In contrast, WW2 had lower concentrations of these ions, leading to a reduced scaling tendency.

Alkalinity and pH also influenced scale formation. The slightly acidic pH of WW1 (6.738) favors carbonate dissolution–precipitation equilibria, whereas the near-neutral pH of WW2 (7.18) provides a more stable environment. Additionally, the higher bicarbonate content in WW1 promotes calcium carbonate precipitation, while the higher sulfate concentration in WW2 favors calcium sulfate formation.

The sodium-to-potassium (Na+/K+) ratio was calculated for different WW2–WW1 mixing ratios to assess its effect on scaling (Table 2). Increasing the proportion of WW1 raised the Na+/K+ ratio from 24.08 in pure WW2 to 43.17 in pure WW1, correlating with higher scaling severity. Low scaling was observed in pure WW2, while mixtures with higher WW1 content—particularly the 20:80 ratio—exhibited a “Very High” tendency. Pure WW1 showed slightly lower scaling (“High”), possibly due to ion competition or localized saturation dynamics.

These observations indicate that the Na+/K+ ratio is a key factor influencing scale formation. Monitoring this ratio and optimizing water blending or pre-treatment strategies can help minimize mineral precipitation and enhance operational performance in EOR processes.

Dynamic scale loop parameters

Table 6 presents the results of the dynamic scale loop (DSL) tests evaluating HPTA-ILP at different concentrations. In the absence of the inhibitor (0 ppm), scaling tendency was high, emulsion stability index (ESI) was 30%, and oil recovery efficiency was 65%. Increasing HPTA-ILP concentration progressively reduced scaling while enhancing both ESI and oil recovery. At 200 ppm, scaling was negligible, ESI reached 85%, and oil recovery efficiency increased to 87%. All values are averages of three independent experiments with deviations within ± 5%, confirming reliability.

While enhanced emulsion stability is beneficial for improving oil displacement efficiency, excessive stabilization may introduce challenges in downstream separation processes, including demulsification and produced water treatment. Therefore, the application of HPTA–ILP should be carefully optimized to balance improved recovery performance with operational considerations in surface facilities.

Interfacial tension (IFT) and zeta potential measurements provide mechanistic insight. IFT between crude oil and aqueous HPTA-ILP solutions decreased from 26.8 to 15.6 mN/m (at 7 g/L), indicating strong interfacial activity. Zeta potential shifted from − 18.3 to − 37.5 mV, reflecting enhanced electrostatic repulsion between droplets. These results indicate dual stabilization: (i) interfacial tension reduction promoting droplet dispersion and (ii) electrostatic stabilization preventing coalescence. This combination explains the observed high emulsion stability and improved oil recovery. Although relatively higher concentrations (up to 7 g/L) were employed for interfacial tension (IFT) reduction studies, such ranges are consistent with those commonly reported for polymeric surfactants in laboratory-scale enhanced oil recovery (EOR) screening. It is important to note that these concentrations represent preliminary evaluation conditions, and further optimization may reduce the required dosage for field-scale applications.

HPTA-ILP also improved compatibility with various water sources, as higher concentrations consistently increased emulsion stability. The reduction in scaling index suggests interference with calcium and other scaling ions, likely through adsorption or complexation, inhibiting crystal nucleation and growth. XRD analysis confirmed formation of amorphous or poorly crystalline deposits under polymer treatment.

Compared with conventional surfactants, HPTA-ILP operates at lower dosages (≤ 200 ppm) while providing multifunctional benefits, combining scale inhibition with interfacial modification. Its amphiphilic structure forms a viscoelastic interfacial film, providing both electrostatic and steric stabilization. Carboxylate-rich segments interact with Ca2+ ions, disrupting calcium carbonate crystallization. The polymer maintains effectiveness under high-pressure and high-temperature conditions (190 °F, 1500 psi) due to conformational flexibility, enhancing access to active sites for scale inhibition and emulsion stabilization50.

These results demonstrate that HPTA-ILP is a highly effective multifunctional additive for water-based EOR, simultaneously reducing scaling, stabilizing emulsions, and improving oil recovery.

Mechanism of Inhibition

The inhibition mechanism of calcium-based scale formation by HPTA–ILP is governed by a synergistic combination of ion complexation, adsorption onto crystal growth sites, and crystal structure disruption, as supported by XRD, SEM, and FTIR analyses (Figs. 15 and 16). At the molecular level, the polymer contains a high density of carboxylate (–COO−) and ionic functional groups, which exhibit strong affinity toward divalent calcium ions (Ca2+). These functional groups coordinate with Ca2+ ions in solution, forming stable soluble complexes. This interaction effectively reduces the concentration of free Ca2+ ions available for nucleation, thereby delaying or suppressing the initial formation of scale nuclei51.

In addition to complexation, HPTA–ILP molecules adsorb onto the surface of incipient crystal nuclei through electrostatic interactions and coordination bonding. This adsorption blocks active growth sites on the crystal surface, hindering the orderly arrangement of ions within the crystal lattice. As a result, crystal growth is significantly inhibited, leading to the formation of distorted and energetically unfavorable structures.

This mechanism is clearly evidenced by XRD results (Fig. 15). In the absence of the inhibitor, sharp and well-defined diffraction peaks are observed, indicating the formation of highly crystalline calcium carbonate and calcium sulfate phases. In contrast, the presence of HPTA–ILP results in broad, diffuse halos with the disappearance of characteristic peaks, confirming the transformation toward an amorphous or poorly crystalline structure. This loss of crystallinity reflects disruption of long-range ionic ordering during crystal growth.

Further confirmation is provided by SEM analysis (Fig. 16), where untreated samples exhibit well-defined rhombohedral and needle-like crystals typical of calcite and gypsum. Upon treatment with HPTA–ILP, these structures are replaced by highly irregular, fragmented, and non-uniform morphologies, indicating severe distortion of crystal growth and aggregation processes.

FTIR spectra of the scale deposits provide additional molecular-level evidence. In the absence of inhibitor, strong carbonate bands are observed at ~ 1420 and 875 cm−¹. These peaks become significantly weakened and shifted in the presence of HPTA–ILP, indicating strong interaction between Ca2+ ions and the polymer functional groups. This confirms that the inhibitor interferes with normal carbonate formation pathways.

Overall, the inhibition mechanism can be summarized as follows:

(i)Chelation of Ca2+ ions, reducing supersaturation,(ii)Adsorption onto crystal nuclei, blocking active growth sites, and.(iii)Distortion of crystal structure, leading to amorphous, weakly adherent deposits.

Conclusion

This study presents the successful development of a multifunctional bio-based ionic liquid polymer (HPTA–ILP) derived from citric acid and ionically functionalized with triethylamine. The synthesized polymer exhibited excellent performance as a scale inhibitor under high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) conditions, achieving high inhibition efficiency against calcium-based scales at relatively low concentrations. In addition to scale control, HPTA–ILP demonstrated significant interfacial activity, leading to enhanced emulsion stability and improved oil recovery. These results confirm the dual functionality of the material, combining strong ion complexation, crystal growth modification, and interfacial stabilization mechanisms within a single system. Structural and morphological analyses (FTIR, XRD, SEM, and NMR) further validated the proposed inhibition and interaction mechanisms. Compared to conventional scale inhibitors, the proposed polymer offers the added advantage of multifunctionality, enabling simultaneous scale mitigation and interfacial performance enhancement. This integrated behavior is particularly valuable for complex oilfield operations where multiple challenges coexist. Overall, the findings highlight the potential of HPTA–ILP as an effective and partially bio-based alternative for advanced oilfield applications. Future work should focus on field-scale validation, optimization of dosage, and evaluation of long-term performance and environmental impact to facilitate practical implementation.
