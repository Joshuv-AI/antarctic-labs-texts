The virology of Omicron: pathophysiology, immune regulation, and clinical impact of SARS-CoV-2 sub variants
Authors: Nistha Mishra, Tanya Goel, Nisarg Gangani, Heerak Chugh, Bhavesh Kevadiya, Manisha Tiwari, Snigdha Singh, Jai Gopal Sharma, Ramesh Chandra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince its emergence in late 2019, SARS-CoV-2 has evolved into multiple variants with distinct genetic and clinical features. Among them, the Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) and its sublineages BA.2.75, JN.1.8, and KP.2 have shown enhanced transmissibility and immune evasion, while generally exhibiting reduced lower respiratory tract pathogenicity compared to earlier variants, thereby continuing to pose significant challenges to public health. In India, these variants have significantly shaped the trajectory of the pandemic, necessitating focused evaluation of their biological and clinical impact. This review aims to provide a comprehensive study on the virology, pathophysiology, and systemic manifestations of Omicron and its emerging subvariants upto July 2025. We discuss their mechanisms of entry and replication, interaction with ACE2 and TMPRSS2 receptors, and evasion of host immune responses. Particular emphasis is placed on multi-organ involvement beyond the respiratory system, including neuro-respiratory dysregulation, cardiovascular complications, hepatic injury, gastrointestinal disturbances, and renal dysfunction. Furthermore, we evaluate the effectiveness of available vaccines, antiviral therapies, and diagnostic tools, alongside emerging clinical strategies such as vagus nerve stimulation, thermal modulation, and respiratory muscle training. By integrating molecular insights with clinical outcomes, this review highlights the multifaceted and systemic nature of Omicron-induced disease. We underscore the urgent need for variant-specific immunisation, early intervention strategies, and robust genomic surveillance to mitigate long-term sequelae and guide preparedness for future outbreaks.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, Covid-19, Omicron, Transmissibility, Mutation, Vaccines

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) declared Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) as a pandemic on 11th March. COVID-19 is caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [1, 2] Since its outbreak, SARS-CoV-2 has gone through extensive genetic diversification, giving rise to multiple variants of concern (VOCs) such as Alpha, Beta, Delta, and, more recently, ‘Omicron’. Each variant has exhibited distinct transmissibility, pathogenicity, and immune evasion features, shaping successive waves of infection worldwide [3, 4]. First detected in November 2021 in South Africa, the Omicron variant (B.1.1.529) rapidly spread across the globe, driving the third wave in India by late December 2021 [5–7]. Omicron is characterized by an unusually high number of mutations in the spike protein, enabling enhanced transmissibility and significant immune escape. Over time, Omicron has diversified into multiple sublineages (e.g., BA.1, BA.2, BA.5, BA.2.75, JN.1, KP.2), some of which continue to fuel periodic surges and reinfections despite vaccination and prior immunity [8, 9]. Although, Omicron infection is often associated with reduced lower respiratory tract involvement compared with earlier variants, it has demonstrated broader systemic manifestations, affecting the cardiovascular, neurological, gastrointestinal, hepatic, and renal systems [10]. The persistence of post-acute sequelae and the potential for recurrent surges accentuate the need for continued vigilance and adaptation of clinical and public health strategies [11–15].

This review aims to synthesise current knowledge on the virology, systemic pathophysiology, and clinical implications of Omicron and its subvariants, with a particular focus on the Indian context. We discuss viral entry mechanisms, immune evasion strategies, multi-organs involvement, and emerging therapeutic approaches, highlighting the urgent need for variant-adapted immunisation, early interventions, and sustained genomic surveillance.

Evolution and outbreak of Omicron subvariants

Unlike earlier SARS-CoV-2 variants, Omicron did not arise directly from known circulating lineages but appears to have originated from a cryptic lineage. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including prolonged infection in immunocompromised individuals (such as those with uncontrolled HIV), persistence in isolated populations with limited surveillance, or evolution in non-human reservoirs such as rodents and deer [16]. Genomic analysis revealed an unprecedented ~ 60 mutations, with 30 located in the spike protein and 15 within the receptor-binding domain (RBD), markedly exceeding the mutation load of the Delta variant. These genetic changes underpin Omicron’s enhanced transmissibility and immune evasion, yet with reduced pathogenicity in the lower respiratory tract compared with Delta [17, 18].

Omicron diversified into multiple sublineages with distinct epidemiological and clinical profiles. BA.1 was the earliest lineage, followed by BA.2, which displayed ~ 28 unique mutations, including key changes in the RBD, and demonstrated 1.5-fold higher transmissibility than BA.1. BA.2 was termed the “stealth variant” due to challenges in RT-PCR detection arising from the absence of the S gene target. Subsequently, BA.3 and BA.5 were reported, although their global impact was more limited. Among these, BA.2.75, first identified in India and Nepal in early 2022, attracted significant attention for its exceptional ACE2-binding affinity, driven by mutations such as N460K. This sublineage spread rapidly to over 25 countries and raised concerns over reduced susceptibility to monoclonal antibody therapies [19].

Recombinant and derived subvariants have further expanded Omicron’s genetic landscape. XBB, formed through recombination of BA.2.75 and BJ.1, demonstrated strong immune evasive properties, while BA.5-derived lineages such as BF.7 showed high transmissibility and global spread. More recently, JN.1, a descendant of BA.2.86, and KP.2, part of the FLiRT lineage defined by mutations F456L and R346T, have emerged as dominant drivers of regional outbreaks, including in India. While generally associated with moderate disease characterised by fever, cough, and sore throat, these newer subvariants continue to challenge existing immunity and highlight the virus’s capacity for ongoing adaptation [20, 21].

The evolutionary trajectory of Omicron underscores several critical points: (i) rapid diversification driven by high mutation rates and immune pressure; (ii) emergence of recombinants that complicate detection and immunity; and (iii) persistence of immune-evasive sublineages that can trigger regional surges despite widespread vaccination. In India, BA.2.75 and JN.1 have been particularly influential, reshaping reinfection dynamics and prompting the need for variant-adapted vaccines and sustained genomic surveillance. Together, these findings highlight the unpredictable yet patterned evolution of SARS-Cov-2 and the continued requirement for global vigilance [15, 20, 22, 23]. Omicron virus entry and infection mechanisms

Binding of virus is the initial step of infection; the Omicron variant differs from other VOCs in its use of ACE 2 and TMPRSS2 for entry into host cells as shown in (Fig. 2) [26]. In order to adapt to the host epithelial cells of the human respiratory tract, the Omicron entry process has undergone a fundamental change toward TMPRSS2-independent fusion, representing a major shift in replication properties of virus [12]. Omicron can enter through either route, by blocking the fusion of the cell surface mediated by TMPRSS2 and the cathepsin-mediated endosomal entry. However, Omicron prefers endosomal route as its spike protein is more efficiently processed by cathepsins, broadening its tropism for epithelial cells in the upper respiratory tract where TMPRSS2 expression is lower. This shift clarifies the reason why Omicron replicates faster in upper airway epithelium cells than in lung alveolar cells, thereby increasing its transmissibility and rapid spread via efficient infection of tissues that function as key sites for virus entrance and release [13]. Omicron S protein undergoes 12 changes to basic amino acids (Lysine, Arginine, and Histidine), resulting in the production of additional amino groups (-NH3+), which promote protonation and increase sensitivity to endosomal hydrolytic enzymes such cathepsin B/L.13 Also, in SARS-CoV-2 variants, syncytia are formed when infected host cells expressing the spike protein fuse with neighboring ACE2-expressing cells. This process requires pre-cleavage of the spike protein at the S1/S2 junction. However, in the case of the Omicron variant, researchers observed attenuated syncytia formation despite mutations enhancing spike cleavage. This reduced capacity for inducing syncytia may be clinically significant, as syncytia formation is associated with greater severity of disease [15].

Schematic representation of entry and infection mechanism of SARS-CoV-2 omicron variant into human host upper respiratory tract cells. “Created with BioRender.com”

The affinity of the viral spike RBD for ACE2 is a crucial factor regulating SARS-CoV-2 infectivity. While soluble ACE2 (sACE2) can bind to the spike protein and potentially serve as a decoy, overexpression of ACE2 on host cell surfaces promotes viral entry. More than any other variant of SARS-CoV-2, the Omicron variant easily employs ACE2 for entry, and cells with ACE2 overexpression allow highly efficient infection, demonstrating that Omicron’s principal receptor and portal of entry is ACE2 [27]. Compared to the Alpha or Delta variant, the Omicron spike protein binds to human ACE2 more strongly. The improved binding affinity for receptors correlates with the increased Omicron viral infection and transmission. SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant primarily infects the upper airway epithelial cells which express low levels of TMPRSS2. A virus first attaches to ACE2 via spike protein in the endosomal entry route, and then the viral-ACE2 complex is swallowed into the endosomes through endocytosis. Omicron-S protein undergoes structural modifications because of the Omicron-S/ACE2 complex, which may increase the spike’s vulnerability to proteolytic enzymes. The pH-dependent endosomal or lysosomal protease ‘Cathepsin L’ works inside the endosome to aid in the cleavage of Omicron-S and activate the S protein for fusing inside the endosomal membrane [14]. The virus uncoils its capsid and releases its genome in the cytoplasm of the host cell through a pore created by fusing viral and endosomal membranes. Open Reading Frame (ORF1a and ORF1ab), two large genes encoded by viral genome are translated to produce pp1a and pp1ab polyproteins, which are co-translationally and post-translationally processed and cleaved by virus-encoded papain-like protease (PLpro) into non-structural protein (nsps) that form the viral replication- transcription complex [9]. PL pro is thought as a crucial enzyme that helps evade host antiviral immune responses by cleaving proteinaceous post-translational modification viral polyproteins into a usable replicase complex, which facilitates viral replication and transmission [28]. The replication-transcription complex (RTC) controls transcription and replication; replication is initiated in virus-induced double membrane vesicles (DMVs) that are derived from the ER, which eventually combine to form complex convoluted membranes. The full-length negative-sense RNA and subgenomic (sg) RNA are produced using the incoming positive-strand genome as a template. Both accessory proteins and structural proteins- spike (S), envelope (E), membrane (M), nucleocapsid (N) are produced through sgRNA translation. Following the production of viral structural proteins, nucleocapsids are assembled in the cytoplasm of the host cell; structural proteins translocate into ER membrane and transmit through ER-to-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), where interaction with N-encapsulated, newly produced genomic RNA results in budding into lumen of secretory vesicle containing new viral particle. Finally, new virions produced are secreted by infected cells by exocytosis [29].

Systemic pathophysiology and organ involvement

Pulmonary involvement

As the SARS-CoV-2 enters the body, it initially shows its effect on the alveolar epithelial cells of lungs, where it binds to ACE2 receptors, and triggers local viral replication and hinders the immune responses [30]. In early stages of infection, the virus damages type I and II pneumocytes, reducing surfactant production and compromising alveolar integrity [30]. Overall, this leads to impaired oxygen diffusion, due to which/the CO2 concentration increases in the body as shown in (Fig. 3), leading to problems in alveolar-capillary permeability, further leading to ground-glass opacities (GGO) and progressive hypoxia [19].

Illustration of the pathological changes in alveolar tissue during SARS-CoV-2 infection [36]. “Created by the authors”

As the alveolar inflammation intensifies, the immune system recruits macrophages and neutrophils to the lungs, leading to the release of proteases (Fig. 3), pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [20]. The continuous increment of these immune responses can lead to alveolar collapse, formation of fibrinous hyaline membranes, and pulmonary edema, representing the symptoms of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [20]. As a result, oxygen demand increases and so does the mechanism of respiration because the gas exchange becomes increasingly inefficient. The pulmonary damage occurred due to COVID-19 is not just limited to alveoli itself rather it can cause severe complication like Microvascular Thrombosis [22, 23]. It leads to silent hypoxia and ventilation-perfusion mismatch, leading to the formation of small blood-vessel clots. These clots can develop even in patients who have mild imaging findings, and this suggests the importance of constant clinical monitoring [31]. Despite such severe diseases, some patients tend to survive, but their healing process is affected significantly due to the dysregulated repair mechanisms. Increased activation of fibroblasts and collagen deposition causes pulmonary fibrosis,33 leading to long-term restrictive lung diseases including exertional dyspnea, and impaired diffusion capacity.

As patients suffering from COVID-19 gets hospitalized, the first clinical priority is stabilizing respiratory function, as the lungs are the primary site of inflammatory amplification and viral replication. Pulmonary involvement can act as a leading cause of downstream cardiovascular, immunological and neurological complications [31]. Therefore, early management focuses on respiratory support through oxygen therapy, prone positioning, and, when indicated, mechanical ventilation to prevent progression to multi-organ failure. Depending on the stage of infection, treatment might include antivirals or immunomodulatory agents, emerging evidence suggests that immune regulation, may be more effective in certain phases of the disease. Moreover, most patients receive corticosteroids as part of standard care to mitigate excessive inflammation [32, 33].

Recent meta-analyses estimate that post-COVID pulmonary fibrosis develops in approximately 7% of survivors, with rates rising up to 20–72% among patients who experience severe disease requiring mechanical ventilation or ICU admission. The risk and severity of fibrosis vary depending not only on the intensity of the initial lung injury but also on the viral subvariant involved, host factors such as age and comorbidities, and genetic predispositions. Emerging evidence suggests that certain SARS-CoV-2 subvariants may differentially influence fibrotic outcomes through varied immune responses and tissue damage pathways. These sequelae contribute to long-term respiratory morbidity and emphasize the importance of early intervention and rehabilitation for high-risk cohorts [34, 35].

Neuro-respiratory axis dysfunction

Silent hypoxia and feedback loop impairment

Due to the damage in the respiratory system, the nervous system also gets affected. It is the most clinically intriguing and physiologically underexplored topic of COVID-19, particularly during the Omicron-dominant era. A patient suffering from COVID may suffer from hypoxia, experiencing dangerously low oxygen levels,40 however, does not show any expected signs of respiratory distress. This relation among hypoxia and subjective dyspnea raises many questions regarding the integrity of the neuro-respiratory feedback loop. Mechanism of action explains how the brainstem holds the ability to sense and respond to the reduced oxygen levels through chemoreceptor input [37]. But SARS-CoV-2 can damage the coordination early and might damage the interaction due to infection. Because of this, there are chances that a patient might suffer from neuroinflammatory processes, which affects the brain stem and peripheral chemoreceptors [38]. For a patient suffering from COVID, it is necessary to stabilize the neuro-respiratory axis before CNS disruption becomes irreversible.

Interventions: tVNS, respiratory muscle training (RMT), and prone positioning

Clinicians try to maintain this coordination using the promising adjunctive strategy known as non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS) [39]. This method has shown potential as it modulates systemic inflammation through the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. Clinical trials like ‘NCT04368156’ (registered on 2020-04-20) explore the use of tVNS to reduce respiratory symptoms, improve the neuro-respiratory coordination and control the cytokine release in alveoli due to COVID-19.43 As the afferent vagal pathways get activated, tVNS help to recalibrate the lost control of CNS over autonomic stability, inflammation, and respiratory rhythm, particularly in patients suffering from early respiratory complications without any signs of respiratory distress [39].

Similarly, respiratory muscle training (RMT), is also useful to reinforce both voluntary and involuntary control [31]. RMT tends to strengthen our diaphragm and internal and external intercostal muscles with the help of special breathing exercises or resistance devices [40]. Overall, it improves gas exchange mechanisms, tidal volume, and neuromuscular feedback (Fig. 4). Studies in early COVID-19 rehabilitation have shown that RMT improves oxygenation, reduces dyspnea, and stops deconditioning even before mechanical ventilation is necessary [41]. Moreover, by maintaining respiratory muscle memory and feedback signaling, RMT can help by protecting against neuro-mechanical coordination loss, which is the most recognized symptom in patients suffering from long COVID and post-viral fatigue [41].

Neuro-Respiratory Feedback Loop in COVID-19 affected individuals and its treatment [45]. “Source: Created by the authors”

Another physiologically favorable method is prone positioning, which involves placing the patient face-down, improving oxygenation and optimizing ventilation-perfusion matching by enhancing dorsal lung expansion and reducing alveolar over distension. It maintains and restores the neuro-respiratory feedback loop effectively. It was primarily used in the management of mechanically ventilated patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS),47 but it has since been adopted for COVID-19 patients to improve respiratory mechanics and oxygenation [42]. Prone positioning tends to increase alveolar recruitment, optimize ventilation-perfusion matching, and improve oxygenation without the requirement for invasive support [43]. Moreover, prone positioning has shown promises to improve pulmonary mechanics in the dorsal lung regions and relieve pressure from the ventral surface at the same time improving gas exchange efficiency. It is a great idea to implement prone sessions early in patients suffering from COVID-19 as it may reduce the progression to respiratory failure [43].

Overall, using these techniques can act as the basis of a conceptual model that can be proposed as a strategy to prevent the damage to the neuro-respiratory loop (Fig. 4). These techniques explain the importance of stabilizing alveolar mechanisms, muscular coordination, and neural control in the early symptomatic stages of COVID-19, even before classical symptoms of respiratory distress appear. Instead of treating hypoxia reactively, this approach tries to prevent CNS overload, long-term complications and respiratory failure [38]. Although, using each of these techniques independently has shown benefits, using them as combination strategies as an undefined early care protocol has not yet been formally investigated [44]. Further studies should investigate their synergistic potential, particularly in outpatient or home-based environments, where early support can prevent hospitalization and reduce the chances of long COVID [45].

Neuroinflammation and CNS modulation

The primary site of target for SARS-CoV-2 is the respiratory tract of the human body. Despite that, it holds the ability to affect other body parts including the central nervous system (CNS), either directly via viral neuroinvasion or indirectly through systemic inflammation and cytokine dysregulation [38]. Patients suffering from COVID-19 multiple times, including variants and sub variants, have shown the presence of various neurological symptoms that range from acute effects like acute encephalopathy, delirium, and seizures to chronic effects such as anxiety, brain fog, cognitive dysfunction, dysautonomia and insomnia [38]. It is crucial to understand that COVID-19 can have effects on other organs like the brain, causing neuroinflammation with both acute and chronic effects. The main reason of neuroinflammation is the activation of astrocytes and microglia in response to damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), systemic cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, or viral particles [46]. As COVID-19 severity increases, the resultant systemic inflammatory response can disrupt the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). This disruption impairs the barrier’s tight junctions and endothelial function, resulting in increased BBB permeability. Consequently, inflammatory cells and mediators gain access to the CNS, promoting neuronal hyperexcitability, neurotransmitter imbalances, oxidative stress, and further neuroinflammation [46, 47]. This cascade likely contributes to the acute neurological manifestations as well as the persistent low-grade neuroinflammation observed months to years after infection in some patients with long COVID. Managing this neuroinflammatory state is therefore critical to preventing long-term cognitive and neuropsychiatric sequelae [46].

One therapeutic strategy to treat challenges of neuroinflammation is the use of low-dose benzodiazepines, like ‘clonazepam’ or ‘lorazepam’. Benzodiazepines bind to GABA-A receptors and inhibit GABAergic neuron signaling in the CNS [48]. Binding to GABA-A receptors reduces neuroinflammation as the activation of these receptors decreases the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which stabilizes the neuronal firing, and increases anxiolysis and sedation in agitated or cognitively impaired patients [48, 49]. For the patients suffering from COVID-19, benzodiazepines work as a short-term neuro stabilization medicine that will reduce agitation, delirium insomnia, or long COVID related cognitive dysfunctions [48]. Although, these agents cannot treat the viral infection directly, they can protect neural integrity by limiting neuroinflammation and allowing recovery from neuroimmune dysregulation.

Implementation of CNS-targeted drugs such as ‘benzodiazepines’ can be considered as part of comprehensive COVID-19 recovery protocols, especially for patients that faced a long exposure of neurocognitive symptoms and acute encephalopathy [50]. Interventions must be personalized and time-sensitive, and the ideal time to initiate will be during the early recovery phase before CNS damages and changes become irreversible. Although use of benzodiazepines hold risk of dependence and sedation, its short-term, and low-dose usage can provide meaningful improvements in function and quality of life of a patient [50]. Overall, the implementation of neuroinflammation focused strategies explains how important it is to recognize the brain as a vulnerable and modifiable target of viral pathogenesis, especially for patients suffering from COVID-19.

Cardiovascular complications

There also exist various cardiovascular complications which can be considered as one of the most severe and multifaceted outcomes of COVID-19. This mainly happens among elderly patients and those who are already suffering from pre-existing heart conditions like hypertension, diabetes, or other neurodegenerative diseases [51]. Even when the virus is initially characterized by its major impact on the lungs, it is clearly evident that SARS-CoV-2 can also cause some major damages to the cardiovascular system, both directly and indirectly [51]. Some of the clinical problems include arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, deep venous thrombosis (DVT), heart failure, myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and, in extreme cases, cardiogenic shock [52]. For the younger patients, or the ones who have never been affected, these problems can occur without any prior cardiac history, which highlights the systemic nature of the infection. These cardiovascular problems often arise from a various pathophysiological process that includes autonomic nervous system dysregulation, direct viral damage of cardiac tissues, immune-mediated injury, widespread endothelial dysfunction, and systemic metabolic stress [52]. It is important to note that various complications like silent myocardial infarctions or constant postural tachycardia in long COVID symptoms might not be detected in standard respiratory related tests, which makes cardiac monitoring an important part of accurate and comprehensive COVID-19 care.

While curing the cardiovascular problems in COVID-19, it is necessary to diagnose early and implement supportive strategies. Effective usage of Electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring can help in detection of arrhythmias like atrial fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia [53]. Similarly, cardiac biomarkers such as BNP and troponin can help in early detection of myocardial injury or strain. Patients who suffer from neuro-respiratory stress are likely to suffer from autonomic instability. For them, the usage of low-dose beta-blockers can help better control excessive sympathetic activation. Moreover, usage of anticoagulation therapies with the knowledge of D-dimer levels and thrombotic risk profiles, have proven to be valuable in preventing DVT and pulmonary embolism [53]. For patients with damaged cardiac output, echocardiography acts as an important tool to check the structural changes and left ventricular function.

Despite having appropriate care, cardiovascular monitoring remains crucial during post-infection recovery, as many patients tend to develop symptoms that can lead to autonomic dysregulation, unresolved inflammation, or endothelial injury [54]. It is necessary to understand the manifestations of clinical trials and different detailed mechanisms through which the heart gets damaged.

While studying the pathophysiology of COVID-19 related cardiovascular problems, it is important to note that it is highly complex. It involves a mixture of autonomic, immune, viral, vascular, and metabolic mechanisms. The cardio effects start when the virus enters through ACE2 receptors, which are present in huge amounts in the lungs, cardiomyocytes, vascular endothelial cells, and pericytes [55]. As the SARS-CoV-2 binds to these receptors, it can enter inside and lead to direct infiltration and damage cardiac tissues. This triggers cardiomyocyte apoptosis, local inflammation, and subsequent fibrosis [55]. This is the reason for the development of myocarditis and cardiomyopathy even in patients who are not suffering from significant respiratory symptoms.

Cardiovascular complications can extend beyond the coronary vessels. In the majority of cases, patients develop DVT and pulmonary embolism because of endothelial injury, venous stasis, and systemic coagulopathy [56]. These problems can cause strain on the right ventricle, further leading towards ventricular failure. This mainly happens in patients who are already experiencing hypoxia. Involvement of the autonomic nervous system can further complicate the scenario [56]. Injury that causes inflammation to the central or peripheral autonomic nervous pathways can damage the cardiovascular health by causing arrhythmias and blood pressure variability. As some of the long term COVID symptoms, the ANS dysfunction can often lead to problems like postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), fatigue, and exercise intolerance [56]. These are also the signs of an ongoing cardiac-neural dysregulation (Fig. 5).

Elderly patients and those suffering from neurodegenerative disorders like Parkinson’s disease are more likely to suffer from neuro-respiratory and cardiac failure combined. In these cases, damaged brain stem reflexes reduce the compensation of the respiratory system during hypoxia, increasing the myocardial oxygen demand-supply mismatch. When a patient also suffers inflammation and vascular instability, there are increased chances that patient might suffer from cardiogenic shock [57]. A specific high-risk scenario can be a patient who is already suffering from a heart disease like aortic valve calcification and stenosis. In such patients, progressive calcium deposition in the aortic valve reduces the blood flow, which limits cardiac reserve under normal conditions. Eventually, when COVID-19 increases, the body goes through systemic demand, suffering from fever, tachycardia, and hypoxia. Moreover, various other fixed obstructions from aortic stenosis prevent compensatory cardiac output [57]. All these symptoms are responsible for increased damage to the heart, eventually leading to cardiovascular collapse.

Hepatic manifestations

Hepatic dysfunction occurs in over 50% of hospitalized COVID-19 patients, ranging from mild enzyme elevation to severe acute liver injury [59]. Liver damage results from direct viral effects, immune-mediated injury, microvascular thrombosis, systemic hypoxia, and drug-induced hepatotoxicity [43]. SARS-CoV-2 infects liver cells primarily via ACE2 receptors, highly expressed in cholangiocytes, leading to bile duct injury and mild hepatocellular damage [59, 60]. Cytokine storm characterized by elevated IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β further disrupts hepatocyte metabolism, induces apoptosis, and impairs mitochondrial function. Hypoxic hepatitis (shock liver) exacerbates damage, particularly in critically ill patients [43]. Drug-induced liver injury from agents like remdesivir and corticosteroids also contributes [61]. Microvascular thrombosis and cholestatic injury have been documented in hepatic vessels and bile ducts, suggesting a pro-thrombotic milieu and bile flow disruption. Additionally, gut-liver axis dysfunction via SARS-CoV-2-induced intestinal barrier disruption, dysbiosis, and endotoxemia may amplify hepatic inflammation [62]. Clinically, liver injury manifests as mild-to-moderate transaminase elevation, with occasional rises in bilirubin, ALP, or GGT levels. Severe cases can develop coagulopathy due to impaired hepatic synthesis of clotting factors, contributing to systemic instability. Persistent transaminitis post-acute phase suggests possible links to metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) driven by ongoing systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [63].

Special attention is warranted for vulnerable groups such as pediatric patients with MIS-C, elderly individuals, and post-transplant patients, who face increased hepatic complication risk [60]. Gastrointestinal tract (GI tract) involvement

The gastrointestinal tract is frequently affected in COVID-19 due to high ACE2 receptor expression on small intestinal enterocytes, making it a direct target for SARS-CoV-2 entry and replication [64]. Viral infection disrupts the intestinal lining, leading to symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, which may precede respiratory signs especially in pediatric or immunocompromised patients. SARS-CoV-2 RNA can persist in stool samples, raising concerns about fecal-oral transmission and prolonged gut viral shedding in long COVID [65].

GI pathology is further aggravated by intestinal barrier disruption and immune dysregulation, facilitating microbial translocation and systemic inflammation via the gut-liver axis [66]. Dysbiosis characterized by loss of anti-inflammatory species and increased pro-inflammatory bacteria contributes to persistent GI symptoms and post-acute sequelae [67]. Severe cases may develop microvascular thrombosis, mesenteric ischemia, and intestinal necrosis, complicating diagnosis and treatment [68]. Supportive care focusing on fluid and electrolyte balance, careful medication use, and management of dysbiosis with pre/probiotics is essential [69]. GI involvement represents a critical component of COVID-19 pathophysiology, influencing not only acute disease but also chronic symptomatology and long COVID management. In the majority of cases, particularly in the severely infected patients suffering from coagulopathy or shock also tend to suffer from microvascular thrombosis, mesenteric ischemia, and intestinal necrosis (Fig. 7) [70]. Imaging studies have shown pneumatosis, intestinal wall thickening, and even perforation (series of holes) in rare cases.

Renal complications

The kidneys are among the most affected non-respiratory organs in COVID-19, with acute kidney injury (AKI) occurring in approximately 17–46% of hospitalized patients, especially severely ill cases [52, 71]. AKI is associated with high mortality rates, reaching up to 52% in affected COVID-19 patients [71, 72]. The pathophysiology involves direct viral infection of renal tubular epithelial cells and podocytes via ACE2 receptors, resulting in acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and cytopathic damage. Additionally, the systemic cytokine storm, characterized by elevations in IL-6 and TNF-α, causes immune-mediated glomerular and vascular injury [64]. Contributing factors also include systemic hypoxia, hypotension, volume depletion, microvascular thrombosis, and disseminated intravascular coagulation, compromising renal perfusion and filtration. Clinical manifestations range from proteinuria and hematuria to oliguria or anuria in severe cases. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) and intermittent dialysis are commonly employed in critically ill patients [73].

Patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes, or hypertension show increased vulnerability to AKI and worse outcomes. Renal transplant recipients are at risk of graft dysfunction and viral dissemination due to immunosuppression. Management includes volume resuscitation, renal-protective measures, cautious use of nephrotoxic drugs, and balanced corticosteroid and anticoagulation therapy. Not all patients fully recover post-AKI; many progress to chronic kidney disease or end-stage renal disease, mandating early detection and intervention [74, 75]. Diagnosis of SARS-CoV-2 and Omicron variants

The early and accurate diagnosis of COVID-19 with fast and sensitive methods is essential to control the fatal consequences of the infection that makes its rapid diagnosis very challenging. There are various factors involved in the successful detection of the virus such as the time of testing from the onset of illness, the concentration of virus in the sample, the quality of the specimen collected from a person, its mechanism of processing, and the precise formulation of the reagents in the test kits. Due to the complexity of SARS-CoV-2, it is recommended to employ combined technologies to make a precise and specific diagnosis [77].

Most used PCR and antigen-based rapid diagnostic test (Ag-RDT) assays can detect the virus (Table 1). It has been reported that PCR assays targeting three viral genes (commonly ORF1ab, N, and S genes) may fail to detect the S gene in samples infected with certain Omicron subvariants due to the characteristic spike gene deletion (Δ69–70). This phenomenon, known as S gene target failure (SGTF) or S gene dropout, can be used as a proxy marker to presumptively identify Omicron infections in diagnostic testing. However, definitive variant identification requires genomic sequencing, as similar deletions may be present in other variants. It has been proposed as a shorthand way of differentiating Omicron from dominant S gene positive Delta variant. However, for the confirmation, sequencing and genotyping are required as this deletion can also be found in other variants of SARS-CoV-2 [78].

In contrast to Omicron BA.1, BA.1.1, BA.3, BA.4, and BA.5, the BA.2 lineage lacks the 69–70 deletion, also known as the “stealth” variety because it is linked to SGTF. This makes it difficult to follow its transmission via SGTF detection. The direct detection of viral proteins (called rapid antigen tests) or human antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 antigens (called rapid antibody tests) form the principle of lateral flow immunoassay (LFIA). Since the occurrence of Omicron, many RATs that were used to detect previous variants as a first line of detection have been evaluated for their efficacy to detect Omicron and its sub variants [79]. It’s been suggested that an oropharyngeal sample along with the naso-pharyngeal sample increases sensitivity of the RAT [80].

Clinical manifestation and long COVID

Classical interventions during the initial COVID-19 waves included a combination of non-pharmaceutical measures such as mask mandates, hand hygiene, physical distancing, lockdowns, and contact tracing with early therapeutic protocols utilizing repurposed drugs (e.g., hydroxychloroquine, azithromycin), supportive care (oxygen therapy, prone positioning), and antivirals like remdesivir. Clinical management focused on timely hospitalization, use of corticosteroids in hypoxic patients, and prevention of thrombotic complications with anticoagulation [84]. These strategies underpinned public health responses globally prior to the emergence of immune-evasive variants and remain the basis for layered pandemic control, especially in resource-limited settings [85, 86].

Omicron infections manifest symptoms that are slightly different from the delta SARS-CoV-2 variant. It has been discovered that the Omicron form causes more asymptomatic infections than other variants, which might aid in the virus’s covert spread [29]. Regardless of immunization status, the two symptoms that were consistently more common among Omicron cases than delta cases were sore throat and hoarse voice [87]. Early reports of very minor symptoms in Omicron-infected people and animals are consistent with Omicron variant’s minimally enhanced NF- κB pathway activation whereas in case of TMPRSS2 mediated pathway, an increased in the activation of the NF- κB, suggests that syncytial formation was the primary trigger for the pro-inflammatory pathway to become active [14]. The Omicron variant demonstrates markedly reduced syncytia formation compared to other SARS-CoV-2 variants, which helps explain its association with milder acute disease and less severe lung pathology in infected patients. Syncytia are linked to tissue damage, inflammatory responses, and increased disease severity. Long COVID or persistent post-COVID syndrome can still develop after Omicron infection. This syndrome is characterized by ongoing physical, neurological, and medical symptoms such as immunosuppression, multi-organ fibrosis (lungs, heart, vasculature), cognitive impairment, and other chronic health problems despite mild initial symptoms. Thus, while Omicron infection often presents with milder acute disease, the risk of prolonged sequelae remains significant and requires clinical vigilance [13, 88]. Anosmia or dysgeusia (i.e., smell and taste dysfunctions), cough, dyspnea, fever, hair loss, musculoskeletal symptoms (joint pain, myalgia, fatigue), dyspnea (i.e., shortness of breath), cardiac and gastrointestinal issues are common symptoms, among which fatigue and dyspnea are most prominent [89–92]. These symptoms might also last for up to six months after the first symptom onset. This illustrates long COVID involves multiple organ systems, reflecting the multifaceted nature of the disease. Therefore, significantly worsened quality of life and increased mortality are serious consequences of pathologic fibrosis of the organs and vascular system [93]. There have been multiple reports of relapses or reactivations of SARS-CoV-2 in recovered patients, as latent viruses can reactivate during the period [94].

However, given Omicron’s extensive genetic diversification, therapeutic efficacy cannot be presumed uniform across all subvariants [95]. The emergence of sublineages such as BA.2, BA.2.75, BA.5, XBB, JN.1, and KP.2 has led to varying degrees of sensitivity and resistance to available therapies [9, 96]. Notably, several mAb treatments effective against early Omicron strains have lost efficacy against more immune-evasive subvariants such as BA.5 and XBB, necessitating careful selection of antiviral or immunomodulatory therapies according to the circulating variant profile [97–100]. Likewise, Vaccine protection broadly preserved for severe disease but reduced due to antigenic drift/immune escape [97]. This evolving interplay highlights the importance of variant-adapted therapeutics and underscores the urgent requirement for continuous genomic surveillance to inform optimal clinical management for both current and emergent Omicron subvariants [101]. Rapid antigen test (RAT) sensitivity can vary by subvariant, with generally comparable performance for Omicron BA.2, BA.5, XBB, and Delta, though test sensitivity may decline for subvariants with altered antigen-to-RNA. Vaccines continue to provide some protective efficacy against severe outcomes, but breakthrough infections occur at higher rates with subvariants showing strong immune escape, such as XBB and JN.1; updated mRNA and bivalent boosters offer improved (yet sometimes transient) protection against these circulating subvariants [102–104].

Clinically, Omicron infections have resulted in lower risk of oxygen therapy and invasive ventilation compared to Delta, accompanied by milder overall severity and more prominent upper airway symptoms like sore throat or cough, especially for BA.1 and JN.1 [18, 105]. Omicron SARS-CoV-2 variant has a different clinical presentation, with a lower hospitalization rate, fewer symptom count, and a shorter duration of persistent acute symptoms [33].

Emerging treatment modalities

Controlled thermal modulation

Fever plays a crucial role in the infected human body (such as COVID), because it correlates with host immune response. Fever is considered the best physiological, naturally developed defense mechanism because it increases the activity of leukocytes, improves antigen presentation, and supports the release of type I interferons which contribute to faster clearance of the virus [106]. Although, several patients suffering from COVID, especially older adults or individuals with less immunity, might face blunted febrile responses or even suffer from hypothermia, particularly in early symptomatic stages [107]. Lack of a proper thermoregulatory response can lead to autonomic imbalance, immune dysfunction, or direct relation and interference of a virus with hypothalamic signaling [106]. This becomes the main concern and increases the possibility of fever-mimicking interventions, which aim to restore a physiologically optimal body temperature that can provide a supportive advantage during early infection.

Controlling the temperature of the human body (Controlled Thermal Modulation) to cause the fever is considered as a non-pharmacological method, which elevates core body temperature safely and steadily [108]. This method is used for patients who are unable to increase the core temperature to an adequate fever. Such methods involve the usage of heated pads, radiant heat devices, thermal blankets, or warm water applications that are usually applied during early symptomatic or pre-hypoxic phases [108]. The mechanism behind using these interventions is to increase and stimulate immune activity via localized or systemic warming and follow the immune-activating effects of endogenous fever.

Mechanistic rationale says that the core temperature of the body increases within the range of 38.0 ~ 38.5 °C to improve cytokine coordination, leukocyte trafficking, and T cell proliferation, while limiting viral replication [109]. Moreover, studies on COVID and influenza indicate that even small increments in body temperature can suppress the activity of viral polymerase and improve interferon-stimulated gene expression [110]. For patients suffering from COVID-19 and are hypothermic, there are chances that their natural immunity might not be enough to suppress the viral activity before the virus damages the defence, and as a result it is necessary to apply warming protocols early in the disease intervention. Moreover, Controlled Thermal Modulation can help with mucociliary clearance, and reduce bronchial reactivity. It improves patient comfort with those suffering from mild-to-moderate disease [111]. Such benefits are really necessary for elderly individuals, who tend to have less thermogenic capacity and weaker immune systems. It is necessary to note that these interventions must be implemented with proper clinical supervision, because excessive or prolonged hyperthermia (>39 °C) can cause increment in metabolic demand, further causing dehydration, and cardiovascular problems [111]. Controlled Thermal Modulation can be used as an adjunctive early-stage supportive therapy, particularly in non-hospitalized or immunologically vulnerable patients (Fig. 9). Moreover, it also plays an important role in home-care protocols, where access to pharmaceutical antivirals is limited. Further research is necessary to understand the preferred and optimal duration, thermal range, and timing for such interventions [112]. Along with protocols, it is necessary to follow a standard supportive care that can clarify their role in improving host defenses, patient outcomes, and reducing viral persistence.

Experimental immuno-stimulants

Most COVID-19 cases comprise a specific population with those having weaker immune responses, struggling to increase their immune response to an adequate amount. These include elderly patients, or those with primary immunodeficiency disorders. For these patients, infection from COVID tends to persist for extended periods, with immense risk of chronic viral exposure, severe diseases, or opportunistic infections. For these high-risk patients, it is necessary to provide an innate and adaptive immune response. Pharmacologically, it is possible to represent a promising adjunctive strategy apart from antiviral therapy and vaccination.

Granulocyte-Colony Stimulating Factor (G-CSF) is one of the most promising, pharmacologically verified factors that can be used as immunostimulants [113]. G-CSF stimulates the activity of bone marrow so that it can produce and release more neutrophils, which increases early immune responses and phagocytic activity (Fig. 10) [113]. This method is widely used in the field of oncology for reducing the duration of chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. As COVID-19 cases increased, retrospective studies during the pandemic suggested that G-CSF can reduce further progression of other severe disease related to COVID-19 like neutropenia, specifically when administered during the early symptomatic phase [113]. Although, caution is necessary, as inappropriate use of this method in patients can lead to cytokine-mediated damage.

Similarly, Interleukin-7 (IL-7) and its analogs hold the ability to restore T cell homeostasis in lymphopenic patients [27]. IL-7 is a cytokine crucial for the survival and expansion of memory T cells. Various different studies, such as IL-7-COVID clinical trial, explain how recombinant IL-7 therapy increases CD4 + and CD8 + T cell amounts in the body, improving antiviral responses without causing excessive inflammation [27]. This is a crucial factor in COVID-19 cases where patients suffer from persistent lymphopenia with delayed viral clearance. There are various other agents that are being investigated. These agents are the category of experimental immuno-stimulants, and hold the potential to improve immune competence in those patients that are not able to respond effectively to the virus such as COVID [114]. These agents are not used for the general population but instead gets used for targeted application in immune-dysregulated or persistently infected individuals, as the antiviral and supportive therapies might not be sufficient [114]. These immunostimulants must be considered as part of the initial treatment phase for COVID-19 management strategies. They must be guided through the patient’s immunological profiles, along with their clinical history, lymphocyte and neutrophil counts, and interferon signatures. Implementation of these agents by following proper protocols can provide a valuable lifeline to patients by reducing their risk of viral persistence, poor vaccine response, and inflammatory damage.

Nanotechnology-based interventions

Nanotechnology has emerged as a valuable tool in curing infectious diseases such as COVID-19. Its application in the diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of SARS-CoV-2 has been crucial, especially because of the sudden evolution of variants such as Omicron and its sublineages [115]. Nanoparticles offer precise, efficient, and targeted biomedical interventions, proving their biocompatibility, and multifunctionality. Deployment of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as carriers for mRNA vaccines like ‘BNT162b2’ (Pfizer-BioNTech) and ‘mRNA-1273’ (Moderna) is considered the most prominent successes of nanomedicine [116]. These nanoparticles protect the fragile mRNA strands from degradation and improve cellular uptake. This allows for subsequent immune activation and effective spike protein translation.

Apart from vaccines, other nanostructures like gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) and graphene-based nanomaterials are being explored for rapid diagnostics. It allows for sensitive viral RNA detection in lateral flow assays and biosensors because of its unique electrical and optical properties [117]. Similarly, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and zinc oxide nanoparticles have shown broad-spectrum antiviral activities by disrupting viral envelope, which shows the potential for inhalable formulations or surface disinfection [118]. Therapeutically, liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles have been used for the delivery of antiviral agents (remdesivir and favipiravir) to the lungs, improving drug bioavailability and minimizing systemic toxicity. Moreover, there exists other methods through which Targeted delivery can be achieved [119]. For instance, using magnetic nanoparticles that can localize therapeutic agents to inflamed and infected tissues via external magnetic fields.

Today, nanomedicine continues to evolve, and its integration with CRISPR/Cas systems, immunotherapy, and real-time biosensing helps in fighting against a pandemic such as COVID-19 (Table 2). However, is it necessary to address certain challenges like immunogenicity, large-scale manufacturing, and regulatory hurdles for clinical translation [120].

Antiviral medications as treatment modality

Although the Omicron variant exhibits substantially increased transmissibility, Omicron variant is associated with reduced rates of hospitalization and case fatality compared to the Delta variant [122]. In mild to moderate infections, medications including remdesivir, molnupiravir, and nirmatrelvir were found to be 50% effective against the Omicron strain (Table 3) [88, 123]. Ritonavir boosted nirmatrelvir is anticipated to be effective against all Omicron sub variants, although there are no clinical efficacy data available at this time. However, this regimen might not be the best option for all patients because ritonavir-boosted ‘nirmatrelvir’ has the potential for substantial drug-drug interactions with concurrent medicines [90]. A recombinant human mAb that binds to the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and neutralizes the virus is ‘bebtelovimab’. It exhibits activity against a variety of SARS-CoV-2 variations, including the Omicron and its sub variants. Bebtelovimab was given an EUA by the FDA to treat mild to moderate COVID-19 in non-hospitalized adults and children patients older than 12 years [124]. Another viral RdRp inhibitor is ‘molnupiravir’ (MK-4482 or EIDD-2801), a prodrug of the nucleoside analogue ‘EIDD-1931’ (-D-N4-hydroxycytidine), which was initially created to treat various RNA viruses such as influenza. The use of this orally accessible medication in patients with mild to moderate COVID-19 has recently been approved in the UK [98, 132]. These studies collectively demonstrate that the Omicron variant does expedite the change in COVID-19 treatment. Vaccine-induced antibodies appeared to be less successful in blocking BA.4 and BA.5 than they were blocking early Omicron strains, such as BA.1 and BA.2. As per researchers, this made vaccinated individuals along with booster doses, susceptible to repeated Omicron infections [90]. Even individuals with hybrid immunity from vaccination and prior infection with Omicron BA.1, struggled to make antibodies against BA.4 and BA.5 infections. This antibody escape was a result of mutations L452R and F486V in the spike protein of Omicron variant. Comparing BA.4/5 to BA.1 and BA.2, a study claims that BA.4/5 exhibits a degree of immunological escape from the vaccine/BA.1 response [91].

The Omicron BA.2.75 subvariant have a wider dissemination than other sub-variants, indicating a greater resistance to medications and immunizations. There are nine different mutations, including R493Q and G446S mutations in the surface proteins primarily the S1 spike proteins, which allow cell entry and attachment easier, provide a platform for mutation of different variants of Omicron and enable it fight the immune system that has been acquired either metabolically or by vaccination [92]. The ability of BA.2.75 to evade immunity explains how BA.2.75 can spread to regions where BA.2 infections have already been detected [4]. Studies suggest that in case of omicron primary immunization with two doses of the BNT162b2 or ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 vaccine offered only little protection against symptoms of the omicron variant disease. After either the ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 or BNT162b2 primary course, a BNT162b2 or mRNA-1273 booster significantly enhanced protection, but that protection gradually decreased [125]. However, the vaccination offered in India which mainly consist of two vaccinations Covishield and covaxin did not cause the symptoms to get severe in the reported cases of omicron.

The importance of community awareness and adherence to Covid Appropriate Behavior (CAB) was stressed by health officials, along with intensified efforts for immunization. Notably, an Omicron-specific mRNA-based booster vaccine, ‘GEMCOVAC-OM’ was introduced in India to combat the variant’s impact. Omicron-specific mRNA-based booster vaccine marks India’s first utilization of mRNA technology created using the country’s own platform technology by Gennova.

Oral antivirals such as nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (Paxlovid) and molnupiravir retain broad activity against Omicron sublineages, maintaining reductions in mortality and hospitalization across BA.1, BA.2, BA.5, and XBB, though ongoing surveillance for antiviral resistance is required [126, 127]. Vaccine-induced immunity is less potent against certain subvariants, booster doses also retain effectiveness against severe disease across Omicron subvariants, but rates of symptomatic breakthrough infection are notably higher for BA.5 and XBB when compared with BA.1, attributable to continued spike protein evolution [128]. Recent data affirm lower neutralizing antibody titers for XBB.1.5 and JN.1 despite updated boosters, with variant-adapted mRNA vaccines showing moderate protection from symptomatic infection and robust protection against hospitalization [129]. These evolving patterns underscore the need for flexible therapeutic strategies and ongoing integration of emerging clinical data to guide best practices.

Future strategies for preventing Omicron variant

Considering the spike mutations of omicron virus, it is of particular concern due to its high ability to spread easily among people and its resistance to current treatments. Furthermore, early diagnosis of the infection and timely quarantine of the infected individuals are crucial factors that will minimize the spread of disease during pandemic. Immunization proves to be a beneficial tool that will provide vital protection both for the individual and for the community, decelerating the spread of disease, saving lives and pave the way of recovery, and therefore must be encouraged. Despite the existence of many highly effective vaccines, a therapeutic agent is still needed to combat the emergence of new viral mutations. It is almost certain that the Omicron variant of SARS-CoV-2 won’t be the last one, so to gain a better understanding, the emergence of this virus still requires constant clinical surveillance and extensive research studies, both on clinical and laboratory levels.

Conclusion

The second wave of COVID-19 in India had a profound impact on public health and underscored the necessity for ongoing investigation into the evolution of SARS-CoV-2. The emergence of the Omicron variant, characterized by extensive spike protein mutations and altered viral tropism, significantly changed the clinical presentation of infection. Compared to the Delta variant, Omicron has demonstrated reduced replication in the lower respiratory tract but greater affinity for the upper airways, which contributed to increased transmissibility and relatively milder pulmonary outcomes. However, its notable immune-evasive properties have enabled recurrent waves, even amid high rates of prior infection and vaccination.

Importantly, Omicron and its sublineages have broadened the recognized pathophysiological profile of COVID-19. Accumulating clinical evidence highlights widespread systemic involvement, with documented effects on cardiovascular, neurological, hepatic, gastrointestinal, and renal systems, as well as persistent post-acute sequelae including autonomic dysfunction and cognitive impairment. These findings reinforce the conceptualization of COVID-19 as a systemic viral disease, rather than one confined to the respiratory tract. India’s public health strategy was characterized by extensive vaccination campaigns, the deployment of evolving therapeutic modalities, and robust enhancements in epidemiological surveillance has played a significant role in attenuating the most severe outcomes of the pandemic. However, the continual emergence of highly immune-evasive SARS-CoV-2 variants, including BA.2.75, EG.5.1, and JN.1, continues to challenge existing vaccine-mediated and therapeutic protection, necessitating the ongoing evolution of response strategies [8, 96].

Three critical priorities emerge for future preparedness. Firstly, comprehensive genomic and immunological surveillance is essential to enable early detection of shifts in viral transmission patterns and immune escape. Secondly, vaccination efforts should focus on the rapid development and equitable dissemination of variant-adapted and broadly protective vaccines. Thirdly, clinical management protocols must adopt an integrative approach attuned to the multisystem nature of COVID-19, with an emphasis on early recognition and intervention to mitigate organ-specific sequelae and improve long-term outcomes. India’s experience exemplifies the broader global imperative: SARS-CoV-2 is likely to persist as an evolving pathogen, and proactive, adaptive approaches rooted in scientific vigilance, enhanced surveillance, and systemic understanding will be fundamental to safeguarding population health in the face of ongoing and future variants.
