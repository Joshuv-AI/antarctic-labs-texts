Astrocyte-centered temporal resilience at the brain–behavior–immunity interface: sleep–circadian disruption and perioperative cognitive vulnerability in older adults
Authors: Wei Fan, Shuai Han, Cunjing Wang, Ju Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perioperative cognitive vulnerability in older adults reflects interactions among inflammatory stress, altered brain state, sleep–circadian disruption, and recovery capacity. This narrative review examines astrocyte-centered temporal resilience as a hypothesis-generating framework for integrating these brain–immune–behavior processes. Astrocytes participate in sleep-pressure signaling, circadian regulation, neurometabolic support, inflammatory responses, extracellular homeostasis, and clearance-related physiology; however, these functions operate within broader neuronal, microglial, vascular, endocrine, and environmental networks. Aging may reduce the temporal precision and adaptive range of these interacting systems, thereby limiting recovery after surgical and anesthetic stress. Postoperative delirium, delayed neurocognitive recovery, and postoperative neurocognitive disorder may therefore partly reflect impaired re-coordination of sleep–circadian, immune, metabolic, and behavioral processes, although direct evidence for an astrocyte-specific causal pathway in perioperative populations remains limited. We synthesize evidence concerning astrocytic clocks, inflammatory–metabolic stress, sleep fragmentation, circadian misalignment, anesthesia-induced sleep-like states, glymphatic and aquaporin-4-related physiology, and hospital time ecology. We also distinguish mechanistic evidence from associative clinical findings and consider whether perioperative chronodisruption acts as a mediator of later cognitive vulnerability, a marker of pre-existing susceptibility, or both. The term “astrocyte-centered” is used as an organizing perspective rather than to imply exclusive cellular causation. This framework is intended to guide future studies of temporal phenotyping, intervention timing, and exploratory immune–temporal–behavioral target engagement rather than to define currently validated clinical biomarkers or treatment strategies.

Introduction: perioperative cognitive vulnerability as a brain–immune–behavior recovery problem

Perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND) in older adults are clinically heterogeneous and cannot be explained solely by the magnitude of surgical or anesthetic exposure. Under apparently similar perioperative conditions, some patients recover rapidly, whereas others develop postoperative delirium, delayed functional recovery, or more persistent neurocognitive symptoms (Evered et al., 2018; Lander et al., 2025; Saczynski et al., 2012; Safavynia et al., 2022). Frailty, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, vascular burden, sleep disturbance, subtle cognitive impairment, and latent neurodegenerative susceptibility may all reduce the capacity to restore stable brain function after surgery (Barreto Chang et al., 2023; Kong et al., 2022). For consistency, the term “older adults” is used in this review primarily to refer to individuals aged 65 years or older. When individual studies applied different eligibility thresholds, their original study-specific age definitions are retained.

Current mechanistic models of PND emphasize systemic and central inflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, glial activation, synaptic impairment, metabolic stress, and large-scale network dysconnectivity (Kong et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022). These mechanisms are important but are often considered without fully accounting for the temporal organization of recovery. Sleep–wake history, circadian phase, day–night amplitude, and the ability to re-establish coordinated physiological rhythms may influence how perioperative stress is translated into cognitive outcomes. The two-process model describes sleep–wake regulation as the dynamic interaction between a homeostatic component (Process S) and a circadian component (Process C). Process S represents sleep pressure, which accumulates with time awake and dissipates during sleep, thereby influencing sleep propensity and intensity. Process C is the approximately 24-h timing signal generated principally by the central circadian pacemaker and coordinated with peripheral clocks; it modulates the timing of sleep and wakefulness partly independently of prior wake duration. Their interaction shapes sleep timing, continuity, and depth as well as daytime alertness (Borbély et al., 2016). Both processes are commonly altered with aging, which is characterized by greater sleep fragmentation, reduced slow-wave activity, dampened circadian amplitude, and less stable entrainment (Scullin and Bliwise, 2015; Taillard et al., 2021).

Astrocytes provide one biologically plausible cellular perspective through which sleep–circadian and neuroimmune processes can be considered together. They participate in extracellular glutamate and potassium regulation, neurometabolic support, inflammatory signaling, vascular coupling, sleep-pressure regulation, circadian timekeeping, and clearance-related physiology (Bellier et al., 2025; McKee et al., 2020). These functions may make astrocytes sensitive to the combined effects of aging, inflammation, altered arousal state, and circadian disruption. However, astrocytes operate within interacting neuronal, microglial, oligodendroglial, vascular, endocrine, and peripheral immune systems. The present framework therefore does not assume that astrocytes are the exclusive or primary cause of perioperative cognitive vulnerability. Instead, it uses astrocyte-linked functions as an organizing lens for examining how temporal and neuroimmune recovery may become destabilized.

The perioperative period concentrates several potentially disruptive exposures within a short recovery interval. Surgical injury activates inflammatory, metabolic, neuroendocrine, and autonomic responses; anesthesia and sedation alter arousal and network state; and postoperative care commonly disrupts light exposure, activity, feeding, and sleep continuity (Campbell and Figueiro, 2024; Cao et al., 2022). In patients who already have reduced physiological and temporal reserve, these exposures may interfere with the re-establishment of coordinated sleep–circadian, immune, metabolic, and behavioral recovery. Postoperative delirium and other PND phenotypes may consequently represent not only exposure-related complications but also clinically observable manifestations of impaired recovery capacity. This interpretation remains hypothesis-generating, particularly because direct human evidence linking astrocyte-specific temporal dysfunction to perioperative cognitive outcomes is limited.

To inform this narrative and conceptual review, structured, non-systematic searches of PubMed/MEDLINE and the Web of Science Core Collection were conducted from database inception through April 18, 2026. Search concepts covered astrocytes; sleep homeostasis and circadian rhythms; neuroinflammation and aging; anesthesia, surgery, postoperative delirium, and perioperative neurocognitive disorders; aquaporin-4 and glymphatic or clearance-related physiology; and hospital time ecology. Mechanistic studies, perioperative human investigations, systematic reviews, and landmark articles relevant to the proposed framework were prioritized, with reference-list screening used to identify additional studies. Article selection was iterative and based on relevance; no formal eligibility protocol, risk-of-bias assessment, evidence grading, or meta-analysis was undertaken. Human perioperative evidence is emphasized where available, whereas experimental and adjacent clinical findings are used to establish biological plausibility rather than direct perioperative causation.

Astrocyte-linked sleep–circadian and neuroimmune regulation in the aging brain

Astrocytes participate in several processes relevant to sleep–circadian organization and neuroimmune homeostasis, but these functions operate within multicellular networks that include neurons, microglia, oligodendroglia, vascular cells, and peripheral immune and endocrine signals. This section therefore evaluates astrocyte-linked mechanisms as one component of temporal regulation rather than as an exclusive determinant of perioperative resilience.

Astrocytic molecular clocks and circadian coordination

Astrocytes harbor cell-autonomous molecular clocks whose outputs influence neurometabolic tone, inflammatory responsiveness, extracellular homeostasis, and network excitability (McKee et al., 2020). At the molecular level, circadian timing is organized around CLOCK–BMAL1 activation and PER–CRY negative feedback, with REV-ERB and ROR contributing to phase stability and rhythmic amplitude (Takahashi, 2017). In astrocytes, these molecular components are functionally relevant because they help time metabolic, inflammatory, and homeostatic support functions. Experimental studies further demonstrate that astrocytic clocks can influence circadian behavior, supporting their participation in tissue-level temporal organization (Brancaccio et al., 2019).

At the tissue level, circadian timing coordinates the daily organization of activity, rest, repair, and adaptation. Aging is associated with reduced circadian amplitude, weaker entrainment, and greater internal desynchrony, which may weaken the temporal organization that supports cognitive stability (Hastings et al., 2023; Taillard et al., 2021). Astrocytes may represent one route through which reduced temporal organization is translated into altered local circuit support. In addition to receiving timing information from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), astrocytes participate in local circadian timekeeping. Within the SCN, astrocytic glutamatergic signaling and regulation of extracellular GABA contribute to the coordination of circadian network activity (Brancaccio et al., 2017; Patton et al., 2023).

Astrocyte-linked circadian regulation also intersects with neurometabolic support, extracellular homeostasis, inflammatory responsiveness, and clearance-related physiology. Astrocyte–neuron metabolic cooperation is temporally organized, and disruption of astrocytic circadian function may impair the alignment between neuronal demand and local support capacity (Bonvento and Bolaños, 2021). Circadian organization also influences perivascular exchange and interstitial solute movement. However, clearance-favorable periods are likely to emerge from interactions among sleep state, circadian phase, vascular dynamics, extracellular-space regulation, and astrocyte-associated perivascular mechanisms rather than from glymphatic transport or aquaporin-4-related physiology alone (Hablitz et al., 2020; Kress et al., 2014; Sriram et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2013).

Sleep homeostasis, local support, and aging-related remodeling

Astrocytes contribute to the biology of Process S by participating in the translation of prior wakefulness into local sleep pressure and by supporting aspects of circuit recovery during sleep (Bellier et al., 2025; Carvalhas-Almeida and Sehgal, 2025). One widely discussed mechanism involves astrocyte-associated ATP release and extracellular adenosine accumulation, which can promote slow-wave activity following sustained wakefulness (Bellier et al., 2025; Haydon, 2017). Nevertheless, astrocytic regulation of sleep homeostasis should not be reduced to adenosine accumulation alone. Astrocytes also integrate arousal-related neuromodulatory signals through intracellular calcium responses and extracellular adenosine dynamics across sleep–wake transitions. These findings support a model in which astrocytes participate in Process S through both adenosine-linked sleep pressure and dynamic arousal-state integration (Bojarskaite et al., 2020; Ingiosi et al., 2020; Taylor et al., 2025; Vaidyanathan et al., 2021).

Sleep homeostasis is expressed not only through increasing sleep pressure but also through coordinated changes in excitability, synaptic strength, and oscillatory organization. Through their effects on glutamate uptake, potassium buffering, lactate provision, and calcium-dependent signaling, astrocytes may contribute to the conditions under which sleep supports network recovery rather than mere quiescence (Bellier et al., 2025; Bonvento and Bolaños, 2021). Slow-wave activity provides a physiological context for synaptic recalibration and hippocampo-cortical communication, while circadian modulation of neuron–astrocyte interactions influences synaptic plasticity in hippocampal area CA1 (McCauley et al., 2020). In aging or inflammatory states, these astrocyte-linked support functions may become less efficient, such that the restorative quality of sleep may be impaired even when total sleep duration is not substantially reduced (Bellier et al., 2025; McCauley et al., 2020).

Aging may further alter the temporal precision and adaptive range of astrocyte-linked support. Clock-regulated pathways intersect with neuroinflammatory, metabolic, and neurodegeneration-related mechanisms. YKL-40 (CHI3L1) is a primarily astrocyte-associated glycoprotein that is regulated by the astrocyte circadian clock and is linked to glial inflammatory responses; REV-ERBα is a clock-regulated nuclear receptor that couples circadian timing to inflammatory gene regulation; and the REV-ERBα–NFIL3–CD38 axis links circadian clock output to brain NAD + metabolism and tau-related stress (Griffin et al., 2019; Lananna et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2025). Experimental evidence indicates that aging and disease-related pathology can remodel rhythmic programs associated with immunometabolism, proteostasis, and reactive-state biology (Nakamura et al., 2011; Sheehan et al., 2025). Rather than complete failure of astrocytic clocks, a possible consequence is reduced temporal precision, weaker output fidelity, and less reliable alignment of local support functions with changing neuronal and physiological demands (Hastings et al., 2023).

These age-related changes should be interpreted within a broader multicellular context. Reduced slow-wave activity, circadian dampening, vascular dysfunction, microglial priming, metabolic inflexibility, endocrine alterations, and neuronal network vulnerability may all interact with astrocyte-linked dysfunction. Astrocytic temporal remodeling may therefore amplify or modify pre-existing vulnerability rather than independently generate the complete perioperative neurocognitive phenotype.

In summary, experimental evidence supports astrocyte involvement in molecular timing, sleep-pressure signaling, neurometabolic support, extracellular homeostasis, and clearance-related physiology. These functions provide a plausible link between Process S, Process C, and local circuit stability. However, most evidence is derived from experimental or non-perioperative settings, and direct evidence that astrocyte-specific temporal dysfunction determines perioperative cognitive outcomes in humans remains limited. Perioperative perturbations of temporal and neuroimmune recovery

The perioperative period concentrates inflammatory–metabolic stress, anesthesia- and sedation-related state changes, and disruption of environmental time cues within a short recovery interval (Campbell and Figueiro, 2024; Cao et al., 2022). These exposures are embedded within neuroendocrine and autonomic stress responses and may converge on sleep–circadian, neuroimmune, vascular, metabolic, and glial processes. Their relative contributions and causal ordering remain uncertain, and astrocyte-linked mechanisms should be considered one component of this broader response network.

Inflammatory–metabolic burden

Disease-related burden and surgical injury expose the brain to combined inflammatory and metabolic stress (Liu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2020). Surgical inflammation may influence the brain through blood–brain barrier dysfunction, humoral and vascular pathways, and neural afferent signaling (Subramaniyan and Terrando, 2019; Yang et al., 2020). When systemic and central inflammatory signaling is sufficiently pronounced, microglia–astrocyte interactions may promote reactive states that impair synaptic support, extracellular homeostasis, and network stability (Cao et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2020). Zhu et al. (2024) further showed that MMP-9 inhibition attenuated postoperative neurocognitive impairment while preserving blood–brain barrier integrity and rescuing AQP4 mislocalization, providing experimental support for a possible link among inflammatory–barrier injury, altered astrocyte-associated AQP4 organization, and clearance-related dysfunction. These findings are consistent with the possibility that inflammatory stress disrupts astrocyte-linked support functions, although direct temporal and cell-specific evidence in human perioperative populations remains limited.

Perioperative inflammation and metabolic stress may impair the alignment of neurometabolic support with fluctuating neuronal demand and disrupt sleep continuity and circadian organization, thereby reducing the temporal conditions that support recovery (Bonvento and Bolaños, 2021; Campbell and Figueiro, 2024; Subramaniyan and Terrando, 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Associations of perioperative inflammatory mediators with postoperative delirium are consistent with this possibility. Brattinga et al. (2022) reported that surgery-related increases in interleukin-10 (IL-10), a counter-regulatory cytokine, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), an acute inflammation- and tissue-injury-related protein, were associated with postoperative delirium. Noah et al. (2021) identified associations between postoperative delirium and higher preoperative concentrations of interleukin-6 (IL-6), which reflects pro-inflammatory cytokine activity, and neopterin, which reflects interferon-γ-related monocyte/macrophage activation. These circulating mediators are not brain- or astrocyte-specific and should be interpreted as indicators of broader immune–behavior coupling.

Anesthesia- and sedation-related state modulation

Anesthetics and sedatives profoundly alter arousal, network dynamics, autonomic tone, metabolic demand, and neuromodulatory signaling while the aging brain is already exposed to inflammatory–metabolic stress (van Zuylen et al., 2022). From a temporal-resilience perspective, the key issue is not simply suppression of consciousness, but how the induced brain state interacts with postoperative sleep, circadian re-entrainment, and astrocyte-mediated recovery processes.

Clinical observations suggest that anesthesia may influence early postoperative sleep characteristics (Chen et al., 2025; van Zuylen et al., 2022). Intraoperative electroencephalographic phenotypes may also be clinically informative because they reflect both drug effect and baseline circuit vulnerability. Likhvantsev et al. (2024) showed in a systematic review and meta-analysis that intraoperative burst suppression was associated with higher postoperative delirium risk in older patients. A research implication is that intraoperative brain-state measures may need to be interpreted in relation to baseline vulnerability and subsequent recovery rather than as isolated drug effects.

Astrocytes may participate in this state transition, although the human perioperative evidence remains indirect. Experimental and translational work indicates that anesthetic exposure can influence astrocytic calcium signaling, purinergic pathways, glutamate uptake, potassium buffering, and inflammatory responsiveness (Cao et al., 2022). These mechanisms are biologically relevant to arousal-state sensing, network synchrony, metabolic support, and extracellular homeostasis. In aging brains with reduced reserve, anesthesia may therefore affect not only neuronal excitability but also astrocyte-linked processes that contribute to sleep homeostasis, circadian timing, and local support.

The relationship between anesthesia and clearance-related physiology is likely to be state- and agent-dependent. In experimental models, dexmedetomidine-containing anesthetic conditions can enhance glymphatic solute transport or intrathecal drug delivery relative to less favorable anesthetic states (Benveniste et al., 2017; Lilius et al., 2019). However, the perioperative relevance of these findings depends on what happens after anesthesia, when inflammation, pain, immobility, residual drug effects, and nighttime disruption may interfere with consolidated sleep and circadian re-entrainment. Anesthesia and sedation may therefore modify the temporal conditions of postoperative recovery, but agent-specific effects, causal pathways, and their clinical relevance require prospective validation.

Hospital time ecology

Hospital time ecology is often treated as secondary to surgical trauma or anesthetic exposure, yet it may represent a relatively modifiable component of postoperative recovery. Hospital wards and intensive care unit (ICU) environments commonly provide weak daytime entrainment, insufficient mobilization, irregular feeding, nighttime light exposure, repeated awakenings, noise peaks, and fragmented care schedules (Devlin et al., 2018; Knauert et al., 2023). These conditions flatten day–night contrast when the aging brain most needs consolidated temporal cues.

These environmental conditions are relevant to the proposed framework because astrocyte-linked support functions, like neuronal, vascular, and endocrine processes, are embedded within sleep–wake and circadian context. Weak daytime activation and insufficient nighttime protection may prolong the mismatch between behavioral state, central timing signals, local metabolic demand, and clearance opportunity (Campbell and Figueiro, 2024). In this setting, the problem is not merely poor subjective sleep, but a broader collapse of temporal organization: daytime becomes insufficiently activating, nighttime insufficiently restorative, and the aging brain loses external structure that helps stabilize both Process C and Process S.

This environmental flattening may also help explain why postoperative cognitive instability is often fluctuant, worse at night, and slow to normalize after the immediate surgical event has passed. At the environmental level, modification of ICU design has been associated with changes in delirium occurrence and circadian melatonin profiles in a proof-of-concept pilot study (Spies et al., 2024). Clinical evidence is otherwise consistent with this interpretation: Lu et al. (2019) found in a systematic review and meta-analysis that sleep and circadian interventions were associated with lower delirium incidence after surgery, and Chen et al. (2026) showed in a prospective cohort study that untreated preoperative sleep disturbance predicted both early delirium and persistent postoperative neurocognitive impairment in older adults undergoing major noncardiac surgery. Together, these findings justify further evaluation of hospital time ecology as a modifiable contributor to postoperative sleep–circadian and cognitive recovery. They do not, however, establish that the observed clinical effects are mediated specifically through astrocytes.

In summary, surgical inflammation, anesthesia-related state changes, and hospital time ecology may converge on sleep–circadian and neuroimmune recovery. Their effects are likely mediated through interacting neuronal, glial, vascular, endocrine, and environmental pathways. Astrocyte-linked mechanisms represent one biologically plausible component of this network rather than a singular causal pathway.

From postoperative brain–behavior instability to longer-term cognitive vulnerability

Acute postoperative brain–behavior instability is associated with adverse cognitive and functional outcomes, but its biological interpretation remains uncertain. The purpose of this section is not to propose a direct progression from surgery or delirium to dementia. Instead, it considers whether acute perioperative instability and longer-term cognitive vulnerability may share sleep–circadian, inflammatory, metabolic, synaptic, or clearance-related interfaces (Fong et al., 2015).

Acute postoperative instability as a clinically informative signal

A growing body of evidence shows that postoperative delirium and related early postoperative instability are associated with worse long-term outcomes, including steeper cognitive decline, greater functional dependence, and in some cohorts a higher later risk of dementia diagnosis (Huang et al., 2021; Saczynski et al., 2012). Yet the most informative feature may be less the categorical presence of delirium itself than the broader phenotype of unstable recovery. Fluctuating attention, disordered sleep–wake organization, variable orientation, impaired memory encoding, and delayed return to baseline function together suggest a brain that is struggling to re-establish coordinated physiological and cognitive stability after stress (Jin et al., 2020).

Within the proposed framework, these manifestations may indicate difficulty re-establishing sleep–circadian organization and coordinated glial–neuronal support after stress. However, fluctuation is not specific to astrocyte dysfunction or temporal dysregulation and may also arise from medication effects, pain, infection, organ dysfunction, sensory deprivation, or pre-existing neurological vulnerability (Jin et al., 2020). Acute instability should therefore be interpreted as a clinically informative but biologically nonspecific marker of impaired recovery.

Postoperative trajectories nevertheless remain heterogeneous. Some patients recover rapidly after brief instability, whereas others develop delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR) or postoperative neurocognitive disorder (pNCD) despite resolution of overt delirium (Evered et al., 2018; Suraarunsumrit et al., 2024). This divergence may reflect differences in baseline reserve, exposure burden, complications, and the efficiency with which physiological and behavioral organization is restored. Temporal dysregulation is therefore one candidate contributor, but not a sufficient explanation on its own.

Shared neuroimmune–temporal interfaces and longer-term vulnerability

Outside the perioperative setting, fragmented sleep, weakened rest-activity rhythms, reduced circadian amplitude, and unstable phase relationships are consistently associated with cognitive decline and incident dementia more robustly than crude measures such as total sleep time alone (Musiek et al., 2018; Tranah et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2026). These observations are relevant because they point to a broader vulnerability architecture in which temporal organization, rather than sleep duration alone, shapes cognitive resilience. The implication is not that perioperative neurocognitive disorders and neurodegenerative diseases are the same process, but that they may share temporally sensitive biological interfaces.

Three candidate interfaces are especially relevant to the proposed framework. First, inflammatory and metabolic stress may weaken the temporal conditions required for coordinated recovery, particularly when circadian amplitude and sleep continuity are already reduced. Second, synaptic and oscillatory stability may become more fragile when sleep homeostasis and circadian timing are uncoupled, limiting the capacity for network recalibration after stress. Third, clearance opportunity may be reduced or mistimed in aging brains with impaired paravascular transport and less reliable day–night organization (Campbell and Figueiro, 2024; Musiek and Holtzman, 2016; Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020). These domains are not independent pathways, but overlapping interfaces through which sleep–circadian disruption may interact with aging-related neurocognitive vulnerability.

This relationship may be bidirectional. Aging-related pathology may degrade circadian output and fragment sleep–wake organization before overt dementia becomes clinically apparent, while impaired temporal organization may in turn amplify inflammatory, metabolic, synaptic, and clearance-related stress (Gao et al., 2023; McKee et al., 2020; Sheehan et al., 2025). From this perspective, the perioperative period is important because it compresses multiple temporally destabilizing inputs into a short and clinically observable window. The value of this framework is therefore to identify shared vulnerability interfaces, not to collapse perioperative instability into neurodegenerative disease.

Alzheimer's disease provides a useful reference context for this shared-interface model, but it should not be treated as the inevitable endpoint of perioperative vulnerability. Astrocytic clock outputs intersect with inflammatory, metabolic, synaptic, proteostatic, and clearance-related pathways implicated in AD, and aging or amyloid pathology may remodel astrocytic circadian output programs in ways that alter how temporal information is translated into cellular support (Griffin et al., 2019; Lananna et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2025; McCauley et al., 2020; McKee et al., 2020; Sheehan et al., 2025). For perioperative brain–behavior–immunity research, these convergent observations indicate shared biological domains rather than a continuous disease pathway. They do not establish that perioperative chronodisruption causes neurodegeneration, that postoperative delirium represents an early stage of Alzheimer's disease, or that astrocytic clock dysfunction is a common causal mechanism. Disease-specific longitudinal studies are required to distinguish shared susceptibility from causal progression (Fong et al., 2015; Musiek and Holtzman, 2016).

In summary, acute postoperative instability may identify patients with reduced recovery capacity, but it should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct progression from surgery to dementia. Perioperative and longer-term cognitive vulnerability may share sleep–circadian, inflammatory, synaptic, metabolic, and clearance-related interfaces, while causality, cellular specificity, and disease specificity remain unresolved.

Unresolved questions and boundaries of inference

Several boundaries of inference constrain the proposed framework. First, astrocytic circadian dysfunction may be more plausibly interpreted as an amplifier or modifier of pre-existing vulnerability than as a primary cause of the complete perioperative neurocognitive phenotype. Experimental studies demonstrate that astrocytes possess cell-autonomous clocks and that astrocytic clock components can influence circadian behavior, inflammatory signaling, metabolism, and neuronal support (Brancaccio et al., 2019; Griffin et al., 2019; Lananna et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2025). However, current human perioperative evidence does not demonstrate that disruption of astrocytic clocks is necessary or sufficient for postoperative delirium, delayed neurocognitive recovery, or postoperative neurocognitive disorder. A more defensible model is that astrocyte-linked timing mechanisms may modify how inflammatory, vascular, metabolic, and reserve-related burdens are translated into postoperative instability.

Second, the contribution of AQP4 to rhythmic clearance should not be equated with an AQP4-determined glymphatic mechanism. Brain clearance and glymphatic physiology have been proposed as potential links between sleep–circadian disruption, aging, perioperative stress, and cognitive vulnerability (Nedergaard and Goldman, 2020; Ren et al., 2021). Aging impairs paravascular clearance, sleep is associated with increased metabolite removal, and glymphatic and lymphatic fluid movement varies with time of day (Hablitz et al., 2020; Kress et al., 2014; Xie et al., 2013). Nevertheless, these observations do not establish that rhythmic clearance is driven primarily by circadian changes in AQP4 expression or polarization. Frey et al. (2025) found no clear day–night difference in AQP4 expression or perivascular polarization. Clearance-related physiology is therefore more appropriately considered an emergent property of sleep state, vascular pulsatility, extracellular-space dynamics, ionic conditions, and astrocyte-associated perivascular organization. Its perioperative relevance and measurable human surrogates remain uncertain.

Third, anesthesia-induced sleep-like activity should not be equated with restorative sleep. Some anesthetic or sedative states produce electroencephalographic features resembling non-rapid eye movement sleep, and dexmedetomidine-containing conditions can enhance glymphatic transport or influence sleep- and SCN-related pathways in experimental models (Benveniste et al., 2017; Lilius et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2024). Dexmedetomidine has also reduced delirium incidence in selected clinical populations (Qu et al., 2023; Su et al., 2016). These findings do not demonstrate that pharmacologically induced states reproduce the full functions of physiological sleep or that improved delirium outcomes are mediated through circadian or astrocytic mechanisms. Comparative studies integrating electroencephalography, sleep architecture, circadian phase, inflammatory resolution, and cognitive recovery are required.

Finally, perioperative chronodisruption may function as a mediator of later cognitive impairment, a marker of pre-existing vulnerability, or both. Observational evidence linking delirium with adverse long-term outcomes is compatible with both interpretations but does not establish directionality (Tesfaye et al., 2025). In a mediator model, acute sleep loss, circadian misalignment, inflammatory–metabolic stress, and impaired clearance could contribute biologically to incomplete recovery. In a marker model, patients who develop delirium or persistent sleep–wake instability may already have lower reserve, vascular disease, latent neurodegenerative pathology, or pre-existing circadian fragility (Fong et al., 2015; Pereira et al., 2021). Current observational associations cannot reliably distinguish these possibilities, and the two models are not mutually exclusive. Longitudinal studies beginning before surgery and incorporating repeated temporal, immune, and cognitive measurements are needed to assess directionality.

In summary, the framework is best treated as hypothesis-generating. The most defensible current interpretation is that astrocyte-linked temporal processes may amplify, modify, or reveal vulnerability within a broader multicellular system. Prospective perioperative studies are required to distinguish causal mediators from correlates and to determine whether the proposed mechanisms provide explanatory value beyond established clinical risk factors. Future translational research directions: temporal phenotyping, recovery windows, and exploratory target engagement

The immediate translational value of this framework lies in generating testable research designs rather than defining validated clinical tools. Many of the proposed immune, glial, temporal, and clearance-related measures lack sufficient validation in perioperative populations for routine risk stratification, treatment selection, or monitoring. Future studies should first establish their feasibility, reproducibility, temporal stability, incremental predictive value, and relationships with clinically meaningful outcomes.

Temporal phenotyping and recovery-window design

One research priority is to determine whether preoperative sleep–circadian phenotyping improves risk characterization beyond age, frailty, comorbidity, sensory impairment, polypharmacy, and baseline cognition (Jin et al., 2020). Candidate measures for prospective evaluation include actigraphy-derived rest–activity regularity and circadian amplitude, sleep diaries, brief sleep-health questionnaires, chronotype assessment, screening for sleep-disordered breathing, and time-stamped observations of daytime alertness and nighttime fragmentation (Buysse, 2014; Huang et al., 2025; Leung et al., 2015). These measures should presently be considered research variables rather than components of a validated perioperative screening battery. Studies should determine whether they provide reproducible and clinically meaningful information beyond conventional predictors. Acute sleep loss on the night before surgery may also represent a proximal marker of perioperative neurocognitive vulnerability, although its incremental predictive value requires prospective validation (Leung et al., 2023).

Intervention timing also requires prospective evaluation. More broadly, chronobiology and chronomedicine emphasize that biological timing and dosing time may modify treatment responses, although their specific relevance to perioperative cognitive recovery remains insufficiently established (Cederroth et al., 2019; Ruben et al., 2019). Pre-existing sleep–circadian disruption may reduce tolerance to inflammatory and anesthetic stress, whereas the first 24–72 h after surgery represents a plausible period of heightened vulnerability because pain, inflammation, residual drug effects, immobility, nighttime care disruption, and weak environmental zeitgebers commonly coincide (Chen et al., 2026; Devlin et al., 2018; Knauert et al., 2023; van Zuylen et al., 2022). This interval should be treated as a candidate recovery window rather than an established optimal intervention period. Existing trials of sleep-oriented interventions provide a rationale for evaluating this period, but heterogeneity in intervention components and mechanistic outcomes limits conclusions regarding the optimal timing and pathway of benefit (Tang et al., 2023). After discharge, persistent sleep fragmentation, flattened rest–activity rhythms, or delayed cognitive recovery may indicate incomplete recovery, but their prognostic significance requires validation against baseline measurements and longer-term outcomes.

Exploratory target-engagement measures

A second research priority is to determine whether temporally oriented interventions alter the biological and behavioral processes they are intended to affect. Pharmacologic chronomodulation has shown potential delirium-preventive effects, but current evidence does not establish engagement of circadian or astrocyte-linked mechanisms (de Oliveira et al., 2026). Delirium incidence and cognitive test scores remain clinically important outcomes, but they do not by themselves demonstrate engagement of sleep–circadian, immune, or glial pathways (Lahiri et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2024).

Future mechanistic studies could therefore evaluate linked immune–temporal–behavioral measures while prespecifying which variables are primary, exploratory, or hypothesis-generating. The candidate measures below were selected to sample complementary components of the proposed framework rather than to represent a validated or astrocyte-specific biomarker panel. Time-stamped interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), and neopterin assessments may capture systemic cytokine activity, the acute-phase response, and cell-mediated immune activation, respectively. YKL-40/CHI3L1, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B) may provide indirect and nonspecific information about glial or astrocyte-associated inflammatory responses, reactivity, or cellular injury, whereas neurofilament light chain (NfL) reflects neuroaxonal injury. Extracellular-vesicle microRNAs may provide exploratory regulatory signatures, although their cellular origin and perioperative specificity remain uncertain (Brattinga et al., 2022; Cho et al., 2024; David-Bercholz et al., 2022; Lananna et al., 2020; Leng et al., 2025; Noah et al., 2021). Temporal measures were selected to capture complementary aspects of behavioral rhythmicity, endocrine timing, and environmental input and may include actigraphy-derived rest–activity regularity, sleep fragmentation, circadian amplitude or phase-related measures, melatonin or cortisol timing, nighttime awakenings, and day–night light exposure. Behavioral outcomes may include delirium severity and duration, attentional fluctuation, delayed neurocognitive recovery, postoperative neurocognitive disorder, and functional recovery (Buysse, 2014; Chen et al., 2026; Evered et al., 2018; Sugg et al., 2024; Suraarunsumrit et al., 2024). Imaging-based measures of glymphatic or clearance-related function should be interpreted particularly cautiously because currently available proxies remain indirect and methodologically contested (Ringstad, 2024).

These measures should not be described as validated astrocyte-specific biomarkers. Most are influenced by multiple tissues, cell types, and biological processes, and their perioperative kinetics, specificity, reference ranges, and incremental clinical value remain uncertain. Their present role is to support mechanistic triangulation in carefully designed studies rather than routine clinical decision-making.

In summary, temporal phenotyping, recovery-window design, and linked immune–temporal–behavioral measures represent priorities for future research. The proposed immune, glial, temporal, and clearance-related measures are not currently validated for routine perioperative risk stratification, treatment selection, or assessment of treatment response.

Synthesis, limitations, and clinical implications

This review places perioperative cognitive vulnerability within a brain–immune–behavior model of recovery and adds a timing-sensitive glial perspective to established mechanisms involving neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, metabolic stress, vascular pathology, synaptic impairment, and latent neurodegenerative vulnerability. The proposed framework does not replace these mechanisms or assign exclusive causal primacy to astrocytes. Rather, it considers whether astrocyte-linked functions may participate in the coordination of sleep pressure, circadian timing, neurometabolic support, inflammatory regulation, extracellular homeostasis, and clearance-related physiology during recovery from surgical stress.

Several limitations restrict the strength of inference. Direct human evidence linking astrocyte-specific temporal dysregulation to perioperative cognitive trajectories remains limited. Much of the mechanistic support derives from experimental studies or adjacent literatures in sleep biology, circadian neuroscience, glial physiology, and neurodegeneration. Associations among sleep disruption, circadian instability, inflammatory biomarkers, delirium, and later cognitive outcomes do not establish causal direction or cellular specificity. In addition, the proposed concept of temporal reserve has not yet been validated as a distinct clinical construct, and many candidate measurements lack standardized perioperative thresholds or established incremental value.

Sex is an important biological variable that remains insufficiently characterized within the proposed framework. Available perioperative evidence suggests that sex-related differences may exist, but their direction and magnitude may depend on the surgical population and clinical context. In a prospective cohort of patients undergoing hip fracture repair, male patients had a higher incidence of postoperative delirium than female patients even after adjustment for preoperative risk factors (Oh et al., 2016). In a large national cohort of older adults undergoing major noncardiac surgery, Lander et al. (2025) assessed sex differences in the association between postoperative delirium and adverse outcomes only in a post hoc analysis. Neither study was designed to determine whether sex modifies sleep–circadian physiology, candidate glial or astrocyte-linked measures, or immune–temporal recovery pathways. Current evidence is therefore insufficient to determine whether the proposed temporal vulnerability framework differs systematically between female and male patients. Future studies should prespecify sex-stratified and interaction analyses and examine whether sex modifies associations among age, frailty, inflammatory responses, sleep–circadian phenotype, and postoperative cognitive trajectories.

At present, the principal clinical implication is to recognize sleep–circadian organization and recovery timing as potentially relevant dimensions of perioperative brain health, while avoiding premature biomarker-guided or astrocyte-targeted clinical recommendations. The framework primarily supports prospective studies that combine conventional clinical risk assessment with repeated immune, temporal, and behavioral measurements. Such studies should determine whether temporal phenotyping improves prediction, whether modifiable perioperative exposures influence recovery trajectories, and whether changes in candidate biomarkers correspond to meaningful cognitive or functional benefit.

In conclusion, astrocyte-centered temporal resilience is best regarded as a hypothesis-generating and non-exclusive framework. Its value will depend on whether future perioperative studies can demonstrate temporal specificity, cellular relevance, reproducibility, and clinical utility beyond established predictors of postoperative neurocognitive outcomes.
