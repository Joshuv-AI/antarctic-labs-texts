A new Isospora species (Apicomplexa: Eimeriidae) from Taiwan Blue Magpie (Urocissa caerulea) (Passeriformes: Corvidae) with the first complete corvid coccidian mitogenome
Authors: Sijia Yu, Peihang Hong, Tzu-Chun Chou, Chao-Min Wang, Shyun Chou, Cheng-Hung Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Passerine birds are highly susceptible to Isospora infections, yet the coccidian fauna of endemic Taiwanese avifauna remains largely unexplored. Here, we describe Isospora kwongchungi n. sp. (Apicomplexa: Eimeriidae) from the Taiwan Blue Magpie (Urocissa caerulea, Corvidae), utilizing an integrative taxonomic approach. Morphological characterization was complemented by Sanger sequencing of the nuclear 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene (1,679 bp) and Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long-read sequencing of the complete mitochondrial genome (6,251 bp). Sporulated oocysts are subspherical to spherical (28.9 × 26.4 μm) with a smooth, single-layered wall, possessing both a polar granule and a distinct oocyst residuum. Sporocysts are ovoid (22.3 × 13.6 μm) and feature a knob-like to bubble-shaped Stieda body, a rounded substieda body, and a compact ellipsoidal sporocyst residuum. While the 18S rRNA gene phylogeny exhibited limited intrageneric resolution, the concatenated tree of three mitochondrial protein-coding genes robustly placed I. kwongchungi n. sp. in a clade with Isospora spp. from the invasive Javan myna (Acridotheres javanicus, Sturnidae) and the American robin (Turdus migratorius, Turdidae). This study establishes a molecular anchor for corvid-associated Isospora and highlights the efficacy of combining traditional and next-generation sequencing in resolving the evolutionary dynamics of wildlife parasites.

Introduction

The family Eimeriidae Minchin, 1903 represents the most speciose protist taxon within the phylum Apicomplexa Levine, 1970, with approximately one-third of all described apicomplexan species assigned to this single family (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997; Duszynski, 2011). Approximately 98% of these species are known exclusively from their direct, homoxenous life-cycle stages, chiefly through the production of environmentally resistant oocysts expelled with host feces (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997). The sporulated oocyst, although structurally limited in the number of available diagnostic characters, has historically constituted the principal criterion for taxonomic identification within the family. In particular, the numerical arrangement of sporocysts and sporozoites within the oocyst has been widely applied to circumscribe individual coccidian genera (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997; Berto et al., 2014; Votýpka et al., 2016). Recent phylogenetic investigations, however, have revealed substantial incongruence between morphology-based classifications and inferred evolutionary relationships, demonstrating that several traits formerly regarded as taxonomically diagnostic are in fact synapomorphies shared among multiple non-related genera (Carreno et al., 1998; Chou et al., 2020, 2021; Tokiwa et al., 2025).

Among the genera most profoundly affected by this taxonomic incongruence is Isospora Schneider, 1881. Originally circumscribed on the basis of a disporocystic-tetrasporozoic oocyst organization, the study of Isospora has been beset by persistent taxonomic confusion arising from an over-reliance on convergent morphological characters and a historically incomplete understanding of the parasite's multifaceted life cycle. This dependence on oocyst morphology artificially united phylogenetically disparate lineages within a single genus, ultimately yielding a demonstrably polyphyletic assemblage (Barta et al., 2005; Morrison, 2009; Chou et al., 2020; Tokiwa et al., 2025). Subsequent taxonomic revisions integrating morphological and molecular phylogenetic evidence clarified fundamental distinctions among these lineages: the presence of a Stieda body (SB), now recognized as a synapomorphy uniting eimeriid Isospora, confirmed their affinity with Eimeriidae (Carreno and Barta, 1999), while molecular phylogenies consistently placed SB-lacking coccidia previously accommodated within Isospora outside Eimeriidae, firmly within the family Sarcocystidae (Poche, 1913; Franzen et al., 2000; Barta et al., 2005; Tokiwa et al., 2021, 2022a, 2022b, 2025; Hong et al., 2025b). As currently constituted, genus Isospora is principally recorded from birds and reptiles, with comparatively few confirmed records from amphibians (Barta et al., 2005; Berto et al., 2011; Schoener et al., 2013; Megía-Palma et al., 2016; Knight et al., 2018; Tokiwa et al., 2025).

Within avian hosts, Passeriformes are particularly susceptible to Isospora infections and experience high mortality during epizootic events, a consequence of the intricate and frequently host-specific coevolutionary relationships between these parasites and their passerine hosts, as well as the multifactorial mechanisms underlying pathogenesis (Tenter et al., 2002; Schrenzel et al., 2005; Kubiski et al., 2022). The resulting disease, collectively designated isosporosis or coccidiosis, encompasses a broad pathological spectrum ranging from subclinical oocyst shedding without apparent morbidity to severe enteric and systemic illness characterized by progressive weight loss, hemorrhagic enteritis, marked lethargy, and acute mortality (Duszynski and Upton, 2001; Greiner, 2008).

Despite Taiwan's rich avifaunal diversity, investigations of avian coccidia on the island remain comparatively sparse. Pioneering parasitological surveys conducted by Prof. Jiunn-Shiow Wang and colleagues during the 1990s documented Isospora spp. from Eurasian Tree Sparrow (Passer montanus) (Tung et al., 2007), and more recently a long-read mitochondrial genomics approach has been applied to Isospora from sympatric passerine hosts (Hong et al., 2026b). Both studies reported concurrent mixed infections with multiple Isospora species in individual hosts, a phenomenon similarly documented across other geographic regions (Schrenzel et al., 2005; Dolnik et al., 2009). Such mixed infections have historically impeded morphological species delineation and complicated downstream molecular analyses. The integrated workflow combining single-step PCR amplification with Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) long-read sequencing for recovery of complete mitochondrial genomes from Eimeriidae, established by Hong et al. (2026b), has substantially resolved this methodological barrier. The Taiwan Blue Magpie (Urocissa caerulea) (Passeriformes: Corvidae), a corvid endemic to Taiwan, has to date received no parasitological attention with respect to coccidian infections. The present study applies this ONT-based workflow to characterize Isospora species from U. caerulea, with the aim of formally describing a novel species through integrated morphological, biological, and molecular phylogenetic analyses.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and preparation

Between April and June 2025, six U. caerulea were admitted to the wildlife clinic of the Wild Bird Society of Taoyuan following public rescue. The cohort comprised three adults of undetermined age and sex and three juveniles, all of which received veterinary care throughout the rehabilitation period. Adult individuals achieved clinical stability during hospitalization and were released at separate timepoints following individual fitness assessments. Fecal samples were opportunistically collected during routine health monitoring. Upon confirmation of oocyst positivity by direct light microscopy, fresh fecal material from infected individuals was transferred to 1.5 mL sterile microcentrifuge tubes containing 2.5% (w/v) aqueous potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) and stored for subsequent morphological and molecular analyses.

Morphological analyses

Fecal samples preserved in 2.5% K2Cr2O7 were maintained at room temperature to facilitate sporulation. Sporulation progress was assessed daily and the time to complete sporulation was recorded. Oocysts were examined in wet mount preparations following previously described procedures (Tokiwa et al., 2017). Morphometric analyses were conducted using a Zeiss Axioscope 5 light microscope (ZEISS Group, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with brightfield and differential interference contrast optics. Photomicrographs were acquired with a Tekfar Digital Camera (TEKFAR SCIENCE, Taichung, Taiwan) and processed in ImageJ (Schneider et al., 2012) for quantitative morphometry. All measurements are reported as means with observed ranges in parentheses (μm). Morphological description followed established conventions (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997), with structural terminology standardized according to Wilber et al. (1998). Oocyst parameters recorded included length (L), width (W), L/W ratio, micropyle (M), micropyle cap (MC), oocyst residuum (OR), and polar granule (PG). Sporocyst parameters included L, W, L/W ratio, Stieda body (SB), substieda body (SSB), parastieda body (PSB), sporocyst residuum (SR), sporozoites (SZ), refractile body (RB), and nuclei (N) within sporozoites. Composite line drawings were prepared using Procreate v5.3.15 (Savage Interactive, Hobart, Australia) on an iPad Air 5.

Molecular analyses

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.1 g of K2Cr2O7-preserved fecal sediment per sample. Prior to extraction, samples were homogenized in 1 mL saturated sodium chloride solution by vortexing, followed by a 1 min incubation at room temperature. The resulting supernatant (200 μL) was processed with the QIAamp® DNA Powersoil Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany); mechanical lysis was carried out on a TissueLyser LT® (Qiagen) at 50 Hz for 3 min.

Partial 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene sequences were amplified by PCR using the forward primer CRYPTOF (5′- AAC CTG GTT GAT CCT GCC AGT -3′) (Medlin et al., 1988) in combination with the reverse primer H18SR (5′-TGA TCC TTC YGC AGG TTC ACC TAC G-3′) (designed in this study). Thermocycling conditions followed those employed in previous coccidian studies from this laboratory (Hong et al., 2025b). Amplification products were purified and subjected to bidirectional Sanger sequencing (Genomics, New Taipei, Taiwan). Raw chromatograms were assembled and trimmed in Geneious Prime 2025.0.2 (Biomatters Limited, Auckland, New Zealand; https://www.geneious.com), and consensus sequences were queried against the NCBI GenBank nucleotide database using BLASTn for preliminary taxonomic assignment.

Complete mitochondrial genomes were amplified using the primer pair EmCMF (5′-RCC AGG CGT AAA AAG CGT TCG TTC-3′) and EmCMR (5′-ATG GAT GGA TAA CAC TCG GCT CTT C-3′), which has been validated for broad-spectrum amplification of Eimeriidae mitogenomes (Hong et al., 2026b). Long-range PCR was performed with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche Molecular Systems, Pleasanton, USA) under conditions described previously (Hong et al., 2026a, 2026b). Amplicons were purified with AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, USA) and quantified on a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Purified products were normalized to approximately 100 fmol for sequencing library construction following an optimized protocol (Hong et al., 2025a, 2026a, 2026b). End repair and dA-tailing were performed with the KAPA EvoPrep kit (Roche), with subsequent steps adhering to the manufacturer's instructions for the Native Barcoding Kit 24 V14 (SQK-NBD114.24, ONT, Oxford, UK). Libraries were loaded onto a MinION Spot-On Flow Cell (FLO-MIN114, R10.4.1; ONT) and sequenced on the MinION Mk1D platform (ONT). Real-time basecalling was performed using the super-accuracy model within MinKNOW v25.05.12.

De novo assembly and annotation of the mitogenome

Basecalled FASTQ files were imported into Geneious Prime 2025.0.2 for downstream processing. To selectively enrich for reads of coccidian origin, all reads were mapped against a curated reference database of Eimeriidae mitochondrial sequences (https://doi.org/10.17632/rty9vxkgsd.3) using minimap2 v2.24 (Li, 2018) with the map-ont preset. Successfully mapped reads were extracted, and redundant sequences were removed with Dedupe v38.84 (BBTools; https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/). Read quality was assessed with FastQC v0.12.1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/), and quality trimming was applied using BBDuk v38.84 (BBTools) at a Q20 threshold. De novo assembly was performed with Flye v2.9.1 (Lin et al., 2016; Kolmogorov et al., 2019), configured with a target coverage depth of 30×, a minimum overlap of 1,000 bp, and an expected assembly size of 6,200 bp. The draft assembly was subsequently polished with Medaka (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka) using reads remapped to the draft as input, yielding the final mitogenome sequence.

Mitogenome annotation was conducted in Geneious Prime using the recently published Eimeria tenella mitogenome (Accession number: PP464288), whose annotation has been enhanced by RNA-seq evidence (Kruth et al., 2025), as the reference. Fragmented rRNA genes and the three protein-coding genes (PCGs) conserved in apicomplexan mitogenomes, comprising cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 3 (cox3), and cytochrome b (cytb), were identified and manually curated, with start codon assignments adjusted in accordance with established conventions for Eimeriidae (Ogedengbe et al., 2013). The annotated mitogenome was visualized using Proksee (https://proksee.ca) (Grant et al., 2023).

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic relationships were inferred from two independent datasets: a nuclear ribosomal dataset comprising 18S rRNA gene sequences, and a mitochondrial dataset consisting of the three concatenated PCGs (cytb, cox1, and cox3). Publicly available sequences from Eimeriidae and related coccidian taxa were retrieved from GenBank and combined with sequences generated in the present study. Multiple sequence alignments were produced in MAFFT v7.490 (Katoh and Standley, 2013) using the E-INS-i algorithm for the 18S rRNA gene dataset and a translation-based alignment strategy for PCGs, both implemented within Geneious Prime. The optimal nucleotide substitution model for the 18S rRNA gene alignment, and for each partitioned PCG, was selected using ModelFinder v2.2.0 (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017) as integrated in PhyloSuite v2 (Zhao et al., 2025), under the corrected Akaike Information Criterion; the GTR + F + I + G4 model was recovered as the best-fit model for all datasets. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed by both Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML). BI analyses were executed in MrBayes v3.2.7a (Ronquist et al., 2012) via PhyloSuite, with two parallel Markov Chain Monte Carlo runs of 50 million generations each, sampled at 1000-generation intervals, and the initial 25% of samples discarded as burn-in. ML analyses were conducted in IQ-TREE v2.2.0 (Nguyen et al., 2015) within PhyloSuite, with nodal support evaluated by 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (Minh et al., 2013). All final phylograms were visualized and annotated in Geneious Prime 2025.0.2.

Class: Conoidasida Levine, 1988.

Order: Eucoccidiorida Léger and Duboscq, 1910.

Family: Eimeriidae Minchin, 1903.

Genus: Isospora Schneider, 1881

Type host: Taiwan Blue Magpie, Urocissa caerulea Gould, 1863 (Aves: Passeriformes: Corvidae)

Sporocysts and sporozoites: Two sporocysts per oocyst (Fig. 1B–E), ovoid in shape, with a smooth, colorless, single-layered wall; and measured L × W (n = 51): 22.3 ± 1.62 × 13.6 ± 1.16 (L: 20.1–28.1; W: 10.9–16.0); L/W ratio: 1.6 ± 0.11 (1.5–2.0); SB knob-like to bubble-shaped, located at the narrower end of each sporocyst (Fig. 1C–F). SSB rounded, situated directly beneath the SB (Fig. 1D–F). PSB: absent. SR present, ellipsoidal, membrane-bound, containing fine granules, formed by progressive consolidation during sporulation; scattered granules present peripheral to the SR mass (Fig. 1C–F). Four SZ per sporocyst; SZ elongate, arcuate, with a tapering anterior end and a bluntly rounded posterior end; SZ initially bowed (Fig. 1B), becoming increasingly sinuous as sporulation proceeds, ultimately curving around and enveloping the SR (Fig. 1D–F). Each SZ contains two RBs; posterior RB larger than the anterior RB (Fig. 1C–F). N not discernible by light microscopy.

Site of infection: Unknown. Oocysts were recovered from fecal samples.

Sporulation: Exogenous. Complete sporulation was achieved within 5 days at room temperature in 2.5% (w/v) K2Cr2O7 solution.

Prevalence: Found in 1/6 (16.67%) specimens of the type host examined for oocysts.

Cross-transmission: No experimental or natural transmissions confirmed.

Pre-patent and patent period: Undetermined. Oocysts were detected consistently throughout the 15-day hospitalization period of the confirmed adult host.

ZooBank registration: E151CC54-B27D-4698-BB24-2B6BF5470B6D.

Etymology: The specific epithet kwongchungi is a Latinized genitive noun derived from the given name Kwong-Chung of Professor Kwong-Chung Tung (National Chung Hsing University). This nomenclature is chosen in recognition of his pivotal role in advancing wildlife conservation in Taiwan, alongside his seminal contributions to the education of exotic animal medicine and veterinary parasitology, and his extensive research on coccidian biodiversity.

Material deposited: Phototypes (Bandoni and Duszynski, 1988) were deposited at the Meguro Parasitological Museum, Meguro, Tokyo, Japan under MPM Coll. Nos. 25563. Sequences representing 18S rRNA gene (accession number: LC928813) and complete mitochondrial genome (accession number: LC928814) were deposited in DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ).

Remarks: Among the Isospora species hitherto described from Corvidae hosts, I. kwongchungi n. sp. most closely resembles I. calocitta and I. cyanocoracis in oocyst shape (subspherical) and overall dimensions (Table 1). Nevertheless, I. kwongchungi is readily distinguished from both by the combination of a single-layered oocyst wall and the presence of a prominent PG and a well-developed OR; in contrast, both I. calocitta and I. cyanocoracis possess a bi-layered oocyst wall and lack an OR entirely. The only Corvidae Isospora known to share the combination of both PG and OR with I. kwongchungi is I. coronoideae, from Australian Raven (Corvus coronoides). Molecular and phylogenetic analysis

A total of 1,679 bp of the 18S rRNA gene was successfully amplified and sequenced from the fecal material of the infected host. BLASTn queries against the NCBI GenBank nucleotide database revealed that the closest match was an unnamed Isospora sp. (MW649140) from American Robin (Turdus migratorius), sharing 99.3% pairwise identity. Among formally described species, the highest similarity was recorded with I. picoflavae (MW618926) from Northern Flicker (Colaptes auratus luteus) (96.8%). The sole Corvidae Isospora with an available 18S rRNA gene sequence, I. coronoideae (MK530653.1) from Corvus coronoides, shared 94.9% identity with the new species.

The complete mitochondrial genome of I. kwongchungi n. sp. (Fig. 3) was assembled at a mean coverage depth of 11,273× into a circular molecule of 6,251 bp, with a GC content of 34.3%. BLASTn comparison of the complete mitogenome indicated highest similarity to Isospora sp. from Turdus migratorius (MW645338; 99.0% identity), followed by Isospora sp. from Javan Myna (Acridotheres javanicus) (LC909086), previously reported from Taiwan (98.8% identity) (Hong et al., 2026b). Discussion

The present study reports the first coccidian species formally described from U. caerulea, an avian endemic of Taiwan belonging to the family Corvidae. Through the integration of conventional morphometric characterization with an ONT long-read mitogenomic workflow, I. kwongchungi n. sp. is established as a morphologically and genetically distinct species, supported by both nuclear 18S rRNA gene and concatenated mitochondrial PCGs phylogenies. The species is characterized by subspherical to spherical oocysts bearing a single-layered wall, a co-occurring annular PG and compact ellipsoidal OR, and ovoid sporocysts with a knob-like to bubble-shaped SB subtended by a rounded SSB. These features, taken in combination, differentiate I. kwongchungi n. sp. from all previously described Corvidae Isospora species, and the molecular data confirm that it represents a lineage genetically discrete from its nearest congeners available in public databases.

Several early-described Corvidae Isospora, including I. garruli, I. nankinovi, I. nucifragae, I. rochalimai, I. monedulae, I. rodhaini, and I. schwetzi, were established exclusively on the basis of oocyst dimensions with no sporocyst characterization and no molecular data (Table 1). The inadequacy of such descriptions has been recognized as a pervasive obstacle to taxonomic progress within Eimeriidae (Duszynski and Wilber, 1997; Knight et al., 2018), and the present study reinforces the view that reliable species delineation requires the combined application of morphometric, ultrastructural, and molecular evidence. The sporocyst residuum morphology of I. kwongchungi, a compact, membrane-bound ellipsoidal mass composed of consolidated granules, contrasts notably with the scattered-granule SR type observed in I. coronoideae and I. calocitta (Upton et al., 1995; Liu et al., 2019). The functional significance of SR morphological variation across Isospora species remains to be established.

The clinical and biological context of the infection described here merits detailed consideration. The type host individual, an adult U. caerulea admitted to the wildlife clinic on 4 May 2025, presented with weakness and marked pectoral muscle atrophy but exhibited no overt gastrointestinal signs throughout its 15-day hospitalization, during which oocyst shedding was detected consistently. This clinical presentation is consistent with a subclinical or low-pathogenicity form of enteric isosporosis, characterized by persistent oocyst shedding without systemic manifestations. Such localized infections are well-documented across diverse passerine taxa, particularly among immunocompetent adult hosts in the absence of severe physiological or environmental stressors (Greiner, 2008). The life cycle of passerine Isospora encompasses two recognized forms: an intestinal cycle producing enteric coccidiosis through direct invasion of the intestinal epithelium, and an extended systemic cycle involving hematogenous dissemination of merozoites to mononuclear cells of visceral organs, historically designated Atoxoplasma (Barta et al., 2005; Keckeisen et al., 2024). Sporulated oocysts become infective following exogenous sporulation under ambient conditions and are transmitted via the fecal-oral route (Box, 1970, 1977). Following ingestion by a susceptible host, sporozoites excyst and penetrate the intestinal epithelium, with extraintestinal meronts subsequently establishing in mononuclear phagocytes of the liver, spleen, and lungs in species with systemic life cycles (Schrenzel et al., 2005; Oliveira et al., 2018). The systemic form is associated with severe lymphoproliferative disease and high mortality, particularly in juvenile or immunocompromised birds, and has been implicated in significant morbidity in zoological collections (Terio and Adkesson, 2019; Flach et al., 2022). In the present case, toltrazuril was administered during the hospitalization period, consistent with current recommendations for antiprotozoal management of isosporosis in passerine birds (Madani et al., 2018; Terio and Adkesson, 2019), though complete oocyst clearance was not achieved prior to release. Whether I. kwongchungi n. sp. is capable of systemic extraintestinal development in U. caerulea remains unknown, as histopathological examination was not performed in this rehabilitation context.

The phylogenetic data generated in the present study provide substantive evidence relevant to the question of host specificity in passerine Isospora. The 18S rRNA gene phylogeny recovered low nodal support within the avian Isospora clade, consistent with the locus's well-documented limited intrageneric resolution (Schrenzel et al., 2005; Keckeisen et al., 2024). The extreme structural conservation of the 18S rRNA gene frequently yields shallow polytomies, rendering it fundamentally inadequate for resolving fine-scale cospeciation events (Kubiski et al., 2022). This inherent limitation is further exacerbated by the high prevalence of mixed coccidian infections in passerines; conventional Sanger sequencing of such multi-strain samples often generates chimeric amplicons, severely confounding phylogenetic inference (Hong et al., 2026b). Consequently, integrating more rapidly evolving mitochondrial loci is critical to accurately disentangling the complex evolutionary history of these parasites. The robustly supported mitochondrial PCG phylogeny grouped I. kwongchungi with Isospora spp. from A. javanicus (Sturnidae) and T. migratorius (Turdidae), directly challenging strict cospeciation models. The phylogenetic affinity with the A. javanicus isolate is ecologically significant, as the A. javanicus is a widespread invasive species highly sympatric with U. caerulea in Taiwan, raising the possibility that ecological proximity and shared microhabitats, rather than host phylogenetic relatedness, may have facilitated parasite exchange between these co-occurring lineages. The polyphyletic distribution of Isospora spp. from A. javanicus and S. canaria across divergent mitochondrial subclades further corroborates the absence of obligate family-level host specificity in passerine coccidia. Analogous ecology-driven incongruences between host and parasite phylogenies have been documented in rodent-associated Eimeria, the phylogenetic sister group to avian Isospora (Kvičerová and Hypša, 2013; Trefancová et al., 2021). Taken together, these findings provide preliminary evidence consistent with the hypothesis that ecological opportunity may represent an important driver of host range evolution in avian coccidia, a pattern increasingly supported by theoretical frameworks of ecological fitting (Araujo et al., 2015; D'Bastiani et al., 2023) and empirical observations across diverse passerine assemblages (Schrenzel et al., 2005; Kubiski et al., 2022; Keckeisen et al., 2024).

Certain methodological limitations inherent to opportunistic wildlife sampling circumscribe the conclusions that can currently be drawn. The infection described here derives from a single confirmed individual within a small rehabilitation cohort, precluding any robust estimation of population-level prevalence or infection intensity in wild U. caerulea. The successful clinical recovery and release of this individual prevented post-mortem examination, leaving the specific sites of endogenous development and the potential for systemic extraintestinal merogony unconfirmed. Phylogenetically, the comparatively sparse representation of complete Eimeriidae mitogenomes in public databases, particularly from Corvoidea hosts, restricts the depth of inference attainable at this stage; while the cox1 divergence of 93.6–94.6% from available Corvidae Isospora sequences substantiates species-level distinctiveness, the macroevolutionary hypotheses advanced above remain provisional pending systematic expansion of corvid coccidian mitogenomic resources. Addressing these gaps requires systematic, multi-locality fecal surveillance utilizing the established ONT mitogenomic workflow (Hong et al., 2026b) on U. caerulea and sympatric passerines. Longitudinally, monitoring rehabilitation cohorts is necessary to clarify how host age, seasonality, and physiological stress govern oocyst shedding dynamics. Finally, the absence of molecular data for the majority of historically described Corvidae Isospora represents a critical gap that can now be addressed using modern non-lethal sampling and long-read sequencing approaches, offering the prospect of substantially revising the taxonomy and evolutionary history of this coccidian lineage.
