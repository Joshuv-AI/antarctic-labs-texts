Hotter and Colder Than Unruh: Probing the Kappa Rindler Vacuum with an Accelerated Detector
Arash Azizi

Abstract
We study the response of an accelerated Unruh-DeWitt detector to a one-parameter family of “kappa Rindler” vacua, which generalize the standard Unruh effect. These states, parameterized by κ \kappa , continuously interpolate between the Rindler ( κ → 0 \kappa\to 0 ) and Minkowski ( κ = 1 \kappa=1 ) vacua. We find the detector registers a perfect thermal bath at a tunable temperature T κ = κ ​ T U T_{\kappa}=\kappa T_{U} . This result establishes a framework for environments perceived as both “hotter” ( κ > 1 \kappa>1 ) and “colder” ( κ < 1 \kappa<1 ) than the standard Unruh temperature. We establish this thermality by demonstrating the KMS condition for the Wightman function and by analyzing the associated particle creation process. Furthermore, we visualize the spacetime structure of the created field quanta, revealing an intuitive link between the κ \kappa -controlled symmetry of the modes and the perceived temperature. Our work provides a comprehensive framework for a modulated Unruh effect, bridging formal QFT with clear visual intuition.

While most studies consider a detector interacting with the standard Minkowski vacuum, this represents only one specific state within a vast landscape of possibilities. 2
Derivation of the Excitation Rate

To gain a more physical intuition for the thermal response, we can derive the result by directly calculating the state of the field after the detector becomes excited. We use first-order time-dependent perturbation theory to find the final state of the system, assuming the initial state is
|\Psi_{i}\rangle=|g\rangle\otimes|0_{\kappa}\rangle
, where the detector is in its ground state and the field is in the kappa vacuum.

2.1
The Interaction Hamiltonian

The UDW detector is a two-level system with ground state
\ket{g}
and excited state
\ket{e}
, separated by an energy gap
\omega
. Its interaction with a massless scalar field
\Phi
is described by the Hamiltonian
UnruhWald1984
;
Louko2008
:

H_{\text{int}}(\tau)=g\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Phi(\tau)\left(\sigma^{\dagger}e^{i\omega\tau}+\sigma e^{-i\omega\tau}\right),

(1)

where
g
is the coupling constant,
\sigma^{\dagger}=\ket{e}\bra{g}
, and
\sigma=\ket{g}\bra{e}
.

The final state
|\Psi_{f}\rangle
after the interaction is given by

|\Psi_{f}^{(1)}\rangle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tau H_{\text{int}}(\tau)|\Psi_{i}\rangle.

(2)

To study the excitation of the detector, we are interested in the component of the Hamiltonian (
1
) proportional to the raising operator
\sigma^{\dagger}=|e\rangle\langle g|
, which takes the detector from the ground state to the excited state.

The scalar field
\Phi
can be decomposed into Right-Traveling Wave (RTW) and Left-Traveling Wave (LTW) components.
The field expansion for this component in the
\kappa
-vacuum is given by Eq. (3.17) of
Azizi2023JHEP
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}}}\bigg(\theta(-u)(-u)^{i\Omega}e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\mathcal{B}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\theta(u)u^{i\Omega}e^{-\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\mathcal{B}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\text{h.c.}\bigg),

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{LTW}}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}}}\bigg(\theta(-v)(-v)^{i\Omega}e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\widetilde{{\cal B}}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\theta(v)v^{i\Omega}e^{-\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\widetilde{{\cal B}}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\text{h.c.}\bigg),

(3)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}=8\pi\Omega\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa}\right)
is a normalization factor, and
{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}
and
\widetilde{{\cal B}}_{\Omega,\kappa}
are the annihilation operators associated with the RTW and LTW
\kappa
-vacuum
|0_{\kappa}\rangle
.

Here, we consider the detector in the
right Rindler wedge
, hence, the relevant components of field in that region read:

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}_{\text{RTW}}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}}}(-u)^{-i\Omega}e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\mathcal{B}_{\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\Phi^{-}_{\text{LTW}}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}}}v^{-i\Omega}e^{-\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\widetilde{{\cal B}}_{\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger},

(4)

where the superscript “-” denotes the negative mode contribution.

For a detector on an accelerated trajectory in the right wedge, its proper time
\tau
is related to the light-cone coordinates by
-au=e^{-a\tau}
and
av=e^{a\tau}
. Then we have

\displaystyle|\Psi_{f,\text{ RTW}}^{(1)}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{ig}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tau\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\left[\frac{e^{-a\tau}}{a}\right]^{-i\Omega}\,e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}e^{i\omega\tau}\,\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}_{\Omega,\kappa}\ket{0_\kappa}\ket{e}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tau\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Omega}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{\Omega,\kappa}}}\,a^{1+i\Omega}\Omega\,e^{i(a\Omega+\omega)\tau}e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}\,\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}_{\Omega,\kappa}\ket{0_\kappa}\ket{e}.

(5)

The integral over proper time yields the crucial energy-conservation delta function:

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\tau\,e^{i(\omega+a\Omega)\tau}=\frac{2\pi}{a}\delta\left(\Omega+\frac{\omega}{a}\right).

(6)

This delta function forces the Rindler frequency of the created quantum to be
\Omega=-\omega/a
. This is a key feature of the Unruh effect: the excitation of the detector (energy gain
\omega
) is accompanied by the creation of a field quantum with negative Rindler frequency.

The final state after the interaction, projected onto the detector’s excited state, is found to be

|\Psi_{f,\text{ RTW}}^{(1)}\rangle=I(\omega,a,\kappa)|1_{-\frac{\omega}{a}}\rangle_{\kappa}\otimes|e\rangle,

(7)

where
|1_{-\frac{\omega}{a}}\rangle_{\kappa}=\mathcal{B}_{-\frac{\omega}{a},\kappa}^{\dagger}|0_{\kappa}\rangle
is the state of a single created kappa photon and
I(\omega,a,\kappa)
is its complex amplitude. The probability of this transition is proportional to the modulus squared of this amplitude,
|I(\omega,a,\kappa)|^{2}
. For a derivative-coupled detector, the calculation yields

I(\omega,a,\kappa)=\frac{-2\pi g\omega}{\hbar a^{1+i\omega/a}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\pi\omega}{2\kappa a}}}{\sqrt{8\pi\frac{\omega}{a}\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\omega}{\kappa a}\right)}},

and hence,

|I(\omega,a,\kappa)|^{2}=\frac{\pi g^{2}\omega}{\hbar^{2}a}\frac{1}{e^{\frac{2\pi\omega}{\kappa a}}-1}.

The transition rate
\mathcal{R}
is obtained by dividing by the (infinite) interaction time and properly accounting for phase space factors. The final result for the rate contains the characteristic Planck factor for a thermal distribution:

\mathcal{R}_{g\to e}\propto\frac{1}{e^{\omega/T_{\kappa}}-1},

(8)

where we can read off the perceived temperature:

T_{\kappa}=\frac{\kappa a}{2\pi}.

(9)

This result, derived from the particle creation picture, perfectly matches the temperature found from the KMS condition and the response function method, providing a complete and consistent physical picture.

3
Particle Content and Bogoliubov Transformations

In this appendix, we analyze the relationship between different kappa vacua by calculating the particle content of one vacuum state as perceived by an observer adapted to another. This is achieved by using the Bogoliubov transformation that connects the creation and annihilation operators of two different kappa-vacua, characterized by parameters
\kappa
and
\kappa^{\prime}
.

We begin with the Bogoliubov transformation relating the operators:

{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa^{\prime}}=\frac{\text{sgn}(\Omega)}{\sqrt{\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa}\right)\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)}}\left[\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\right){\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)\right){\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger}\right].

(10)

For clarity, we can write this transformation in the standard form
{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa^{\prime}}=\alpha_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger}
. The number of
\kappa
-particles of frequency
\Omega
present in the
\kappa^{\prime}
-vacuum is given by the expectation value:

N_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}(\Omega)=\langle 0_{\kappa}|{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa^{\prime}}^{\dagger}{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa^{\prime}}|0_{\kappa}\rangle.

(11)

Substituting the transformation yields:

N_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}(\Omega)=\langle 0_{\kappa}|(\alpha_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}^{*}{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger}+\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}^{*}{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa})(\alpha_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}+\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger})|0_{\kappa}\rangle.

(12)

Since the annihilation operator
{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}
acting on the vacuum state gives zero, i.e.,
{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}|0_{\kappa}\rangle=0
and its conjugate
\langle 0_{\kappa}|{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger}=0
, the only non-vanishing term is the one proportional to
|\beta_{\Omega}|^{2}
:

N_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}(\Omega)=\langle 0_{\kappa}|\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}^{*}{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa}\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger}|0_{\kappa}\rangle=|\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}|^{2}\langle 0_{\kappa}|[{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa},{\cal B}_{-\Omega,\kappa}^{\dagger}]|0_{\kappa}\rangle.

(13)

The commutator for fields in an infinite volume results in a Dirac delta function,
[{\cal B}_{\Omega},{\cal B}_{\Omega^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})
, leading to a
\delta(0)
divergence. This term is understood as being proportional to the total volume of space. The physically meaningful quantity is the particle number per mode (or density), which is obtained by regularizing this divergence. Thus, the particle spectrum is proportional to the Bogoliubov coefficient
|\beta_{\Omega}|^{2}
:

\boxed{\quad\langle N_{\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}}(\Omega)\rangle\propto|\beta_{\Omega,\,\kappa\kappa^{\prime}}|^{2}=\frac{\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}-\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa}\right)\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa^{\prime}}\right)}.\quad}

(14)

As expected, if the two vacua are the same (
\kappa=\kappa^{\prime}
), the particle number is zero.

3.1
The Rindler Limit: Recovering the Unruh Effect

We can now recover the celebrated result for the Unruh effect by considering the particle content of the Minkowski vacuum (
\kappa=1
) as seen from the perspective of a Rindler observer. This corresponds to taking the limit where the Rindler operators are defined by
\kappa^{\prime}\to 0
. In this limit, the arguments of the hyperbolic sine functions containing
1/\kappa^{\prime}
become large, allowing for the approximation
\sinh(x)\approx\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(x)e^{|x|}
.

The squared Bogoliubov coefficient becomes:

\langle N_{1,0}(\Omega)\rangle\propto\frac{\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(\Omega)e^{\frac{\pi|\Omega|}{2}(\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}}-1)}\right]^{2}}{\sinh(\pi\Omega)\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{sgn}(\Omega)e^{\frac{\pi|\Omega|}{\kappa^{\prime}}}\right]}=\frac{\frac{1}{4}e^{\pi|\Omega|(\frac{1}{\kappa^{\prime}}-1)}}{\frac{1}{2}\sinh(\pi\Omega)e^{\frac{\pi|\Omega|}{\kappa^{\prime}}}}=\frac{e^{-\pi|\Omega|}}{2\sinh(\pi|\Omega|)}.

(15)

This simplifies to the familiar Bose-Einstein distribution. For
\Omega>0
:

\langle N\rangle\propto\frac{e^{-\pi\Omega}}{e^{\pi\Omega}-e^{-\pi\Omega}}=\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\Omega}-1}.

(16)

And for
\Omega<0
:

\langle N\rangle\propto\frac{e^{\pi\Omega}}{-(e^{\pi\Omega}-e^{-\pi\Omega})}=\frac{1}{e^{-2\pi\Omega}-1}.

(17)

This result precisely recovers the thermal spectrum for Rindler particles in the Minkowski vacuum, confirming that
2\pi
here plays the role of the inverse Unruh temperature
a/T_{U}
(in units where
a=1
), and validating the general transformation.

4
The Wightman Function in
\kappa
-Rindler Spacetime

The detector’s response is governed by the field’s two-point Wightman function,
W(\Delta\tau)=\langle\Phi(\tau_{1})\Phi(\tau_{2})\rangle
, evaluated along its worldline. In (1+1) dimensions, the massless scalar field decomposes into independent right-traveling (RTW) and left-traveling (LTW) components,
\Phi=\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u)+\Phi_{\text{LTW}}(v)
, where
u=t-x
and
v=t+x
are light-cone coordinates. The total Wightman function is the sum of the contributions from each component,
W_{\kappa}=W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}+W_{\kappa,\text{LTW}}
. Our primary objective is to compute this total function by first calculating the contribution from the RTW component and then extending the analysis to the LTW component.

4.1
Field Expansion and the Wightman Function of the RTW Component

We begin by considering the right-traveling component of the field operator,
\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u)
. The Wightman function for this sector is given by the vacuum expectation value:

W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})=\langle 0_{\kappa}|\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u)\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u^{\prime})|0_{\kappa}\rangle.

(18)

Then, using Eq (
3
) we have,

\langle 0_{\kappa}|\Phi(u)\Phi(u^{\prime})|0_{\kappa}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega d\Omega^{\prime}\frac{e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{2\kappa}}e^{\frac{\pi\Omega^{\prime}}{2\kappa}}}{{\cal N}_{\Omega,\kappa}{\cal N}_{\Omega^{\prime},\kappa}}\langle 0_{\kappa}|{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa}{\cal B}_{\Omega^{\prime},\kappa}^{\dagger}|0_{\kappa}\rangle(-u)^{i\Omega}(-u^{\prime})^{-i\Omega^{\prime}}.

Applying the commutation relation
[{\cal B}_{\Omega,\kappa},{\cal B}_{\Omega^{\prime},\kappa}^{\dagger}]=\delta(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})
, the integral simplifies. The initial double integral over
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
reduces to a single integral:

\displaystyle W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\frac{e^{\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa}}}{8\pi\Omega\sinh\left(\frac{\pi\Omega}{\kappa}\right)}\left(\frac{u}{u^{\prime}}\right)^{i\Omega}.

(19)

To evaluate this integral, we perform a rescaling of the integration variable, letting
\Omega\to\kappa\Omega
, where
\kappa>0
is a real parameter. This yields:

W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\frac{e^{\pi\Omega}}{8\pi\Omega\sinh(\pi\Omega)}\left[\left(\frac{u}{u^{\prime}}\right)^{\kappa}\right]^{i\Omega}.

(20)

This manipulation reveals a crucial property: the Wightman function for the RTW component in the
\kappa
-vacuum is directly related to that in the Minkowski vacuum (
\kappa=1
) via a coordinate transformation. Specifically,

\boxed{\quad W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})=W_{1,\text{RTW}}(u^{\kappa},u^{\prime\kappa}).\quad}

(21)

This relation allows us to compute the simpler Minkowski case first and then generalize the result to the
\kappa
-vacuum.

Calculation in Rindler Coordinates

To proceed, we evaluate the contribution of the RTW component along the worldline of a uniformly accelerated observer. For an observer in the right Rindler wedge with acceleration
a
, the proper time
\tau
is related to the light-cone coordinate
u<0
by
-au=e^{-a\tau}
. For two points on the same trajectory, we have:

\frac{u}{u^{\prime}}=e^{-a(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}\quad\implies\quad\left(\frac{u}{u^{\prime}}\right)^{\kappa}=e^{-a\kappa(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}.

(22)

Let us first evaluate the Wightman function for the Minkowski case (
\kappa=1
). While the integral for
W_{1,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})
can be computed directly, it is simpler to use the standard plane-wave expansion for the field in the Minkowski vacuum
|0_{M}\rangle
:

\displaystyle W_{1,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle 0_{M}|\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u)\Phi_{\text{RTW}}(u^{\prime})|0_{M}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\nu d\nu^{\prime}\frac{e^{-i\nu u}}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu}}\frac{e^{+i\nu^{\prime}u^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{4\pi\nu^{\prime}}}\underbrace{\langle 0_{M}|a_{\nu}a_{\nu^{\prime}}^{\dagger}|0_{M}\rangle}_{\delta(\nu-\nu^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d\nu}{\nu}e^{-i\nu(u-u^{\prime})}.

(23)

This is a standard integral, see the appendix
A
for detailed derivation, which evaluates to:

\displaystyle W_{1,\text{RTW}}(u,u^{\prime})=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln(u-u'-i\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big(\theta(u-u^{\prime})\ln(u-u')+\theta(u^{\prime}-u)(\ln(u'-u)-i\pi)\Big).

(24)

Now, we transform this expression to Rindler coordinates. Defining the proper time difference
\Delta\tau=\tau^{\prime}-\tau
, we find (see details in appendix
B
):

W_{1,\text{RTW}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\{\ln\frac{2}{a}-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\ln\left[\sinh\left(-\frac{a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\epsilon\right]\right\}.

(25)

Now we are ready to find the Wightman function for the kappa mode. It is almost analogous to the above calculation with some minor differences. Since

\displaystyle-u\to(-u)^{\kappa}=\frac{e^{-\kappa a\tau}}{a^{\kappa}},\qquad v\to v^{\kappa}=\frac{e^{\kappa a\tau}}{a^{\kappa}}.

(26)

Hence, one can observe the final Wightman function for the RTW for the kappa mode reads

\boxed{\quad W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\{\ln\frac{2}{a^{\kappa}}-a\kappa\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\ln\left[\sinh\left(-\frac{\kappa a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\epsilon\right]\right\}.\quad}

(27)

4.2
Contribution of the Left-Traveling Wave

The analysis for the left-traveling wave (LTW) component,
\Phi_{\text{LTW}}(v)
, follows an analogous procedure. The LTW component is a function of the light-cone coordinate
v=t+x
. The Wightman function for this sector is:

W_{\kappa,\text{LTW}}(v,v^{\prime})=W_{1,\text{LTW}}(v^{\kappa},v^{\prime\kappa}).

(28)

We must evaluate this contribution along the same accelerated trajectory. For the observer in the right Rindler wedge, the coordinate
v
is related to proper time
\tau
by
av=e^{a\tau}
. For the Minkowski case (
\kappa=1
), we have
v/v^{\prime}=e^{a(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}
. The Wightman function is:

\displaystyle W_{1,\text{LTW}}(v,v^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\Big(\theta(v-v^{\prime})\ln(v-v')+\theta(v^{\prime}-v)(\ln(v'-v)-i\pi)\Big).

(29)

Transforming to Rindler coordinates and using the identity
e^{a\tau}-e^{a\tau^{\prime}}=2e^{a\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}}\sinh\left(\frac{a(\tau-\tau^{\prime})}{2}\right)
, analogous to our calculation in the appendix
B
we find:

\displaystyle W_{1,\text{LTW}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\{\theta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\left[\ln\left(\frac{e^{a\tau}-e^{a\tau^{\prime}}}{a}\right)\right]+\theta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)\left[\ln\left(\frac{e^{a\tau^{\prime}}-e^{a\tau}}{a}\right)-i\pi\right]\right\}.

(30)

This can be written compactly using the
i\epsilon
prescription as:

W_{1,\text{LTW}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\{\ln\frac{2}{a}+a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\ln\left[\sinh\left(-\frac{a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\epsilon\right]\right\}.

(31)

Similarly, one may find the following expression for the Wightman function for the LTW in kappa mode

\boxed{\quad W_{\kappa,\text{LTW}}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\{\ln\frac{2}{a^{\kappa}}+a\kappa\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\ln\left[\sinh\left(-\frac{\kappa a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\epsilon\right]\right\}.\quad}

(32)

4.3
The Full Wightman Function for the Kappa-Vacuum

The total Wightman function is the sum of the contributions from the right- and left-traveling waves,
W_{\kappa}=W_{\kappa,\text{RTW}}+W_{\kappa,\text{LTW}}
. Let us first sum the Minkowski contributions. A remarkable cancellation occurs: the terms proportional to
\tau+\tau^{\prime}
vanish upon summation. The total result is can be found by adding (
27
) and (
32
):

\boxed{\quad W_{\kappa}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left\{\ln\frac{2}{a^{\kappa}}+\ln\left[\sinh\left(-\frac{a\kappa\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\epsilon\right]\right\}.\quad}

(33)

4.4
KMS Condition and
\kappa
-Deformed Temperature

A state is in thermal equilibrium at inverse temperature
\beta
if its correlation function satisfies the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition, which for a bosonic field is the statement of periodicity in imaginary time
Kubo1966
;
Martin_Schwinger1959
:

W(\Delta\tau)=W(\Delta\tau+i\beta).

(34)

To check this for our derived Wightman function, we test for periodicity by performing the analytic continuation
\Delta\tau\to i\beta
in the argument of the hyperbolic sine function. This transformation yields:

\sinh\left(-\frac{a\kappa}{2}i\beta\right)=-i\sin\left(\frac{a\kappa\beta}{2}\right).

(35)

The periodicity of the Wightman function requires the argument of the sine function to be an integer multiple of
\pi
. For the fundamental period, which defines the temperature, we set:

\frac{a\kappa\beta_{\kappa}}{2}=\pi.

(36)

This allows us to identify the inverse temperature
\beta_{\kappa}
of the
\kappa
-Rindler vacuum. The temperature
T_{\kappa}=1/\beta_{\kappa}
is then found to be directly proportional to the deformation parameter
\kappa
:

\beta_{\kappa}=\frac{2\pi}{a\kappa}\implies T_{\kappa}=\frac{a\kappa}{2\pi}.

(37)

This result indicates that the temperature experienced by an accelerated observer is modified in
\kappa
-Minkowski spacetime, a significant departure from the standard Unruh effect where the temperature is
T_{U}=a/2\pi
.

This gives the inverse temperature
\beta_{\kappa}
for the
\kappa
-vacuum:

\beta_{\kappa}=\frac{2\pi}{a\kappa}.

(38)

The corresponding temperature experienced by the accelerated observer is:

T_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{\beta_{\kappa}}=\frac{a\kappa}{2\pi}.

(39)

This result shows that the Unruh temperature is scaled by the factor
\kappa
.

A Spectrum of Thermal Vacua: Visualizing the Unruh Effect

6
Conclusion

We have investigated the response of a uniformly accelerated UDW detector to the family of kappa vacuum states. We have shown that the detector’s response is rigorously thermal with a perceived temperature
T_{\kappa}=\kappa T_{U}
. This was first established formally by verifying the KMS condition for the vacuum’s Wightman function, and then re-derived from a quantum optics perspective that highlights the particle creation process. We emphasized that this process is sourced by negative Rindler frequency modes and results in a non-local “kappa photon” predominantly located in the causally disconnected Rindler wedge, a key feature discovered by Unruh and Wald.

This work establishes the kappa vacuum as a versatile theoretical tool, providing a continuous parameter
\kappa
to smoothly transition between the cold Rindler vacuum (
\kappa=0
), the familiar Minkowski vacuum (
\kappa=1
), and hotter-than-Unruh thermal environments (
\kappa>1
). Our findings generalize the Unruh effect and provide a concrete framework for studying observer-dependent phenomena. Future work could extend this analysis to higher-order processes

Appendix A
Finding the integral
f(k)

A.1
Integral Evaluation via Contour Integration

In our calculations, we require the evaluation of the integral
f(k)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}
. We can determine the value of
f(k)-f(-k)
using contour integration. First, we express this difference as a single integral over the real axis:

\displaystyle f(k)-f(-k)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}-\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{izk}}{z}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}+\int_{-\infty}^{0}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}=P.V.\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}.

(40)

where P.V. denotes the Cauchy Principal Value. We evaluate this integral by closing the contour in the complex plane. The choice of contour depends on the sign of
k
.

For
k>0
, the term
e^{-izk}=e^{yk}e^{-ixk}
decays in the lower half-plane (
y<0
). We use a contour consisting of the real axis from
-R
to
R
(indented with a small semicircle of radius
\epsilon
around the origin) and a large semicircle of radius
R
in the lower half-plane. By Cauchy’s theorem, the integral over this closed contour is zero. The integral over the large semicircle vanishes as
R\to\infty
. Thus, the principal value is given by the negative of the integral over the small indented semicircle (which traverses from
\theta=\pi
to
0
):

\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace P.V.\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{dz}{z}e^{-izk}=-\int_{\pi}^{0}\frac{i\epsilon e^{i\theta}d\theta}{\epsilon e^{i\theta}}e^{-i(\epsilon e^{i\theta})k}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\to 0}-\int_{\pi}^{0}id\theta=i\pi.

(41)

For
k<0
, the integrand decays in the upper half-plane, and closing the contour there yields a result of
-i\pi
. We can combine these two results using the signum function:

\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace f(k)-f(-k)=P.V.\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}=-i\pi\sgn(k).

(42)

A.2
Integral Evaluation via Differentiation

An alternative method to evaluate
f(k)
is to first differentiate with respect to
k
and then integrate the result. Differentiating under the integral sign gives:

f^{\prime}(k)=\frac{d}{dk}\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}=-i\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,e^{-izk}.

(43)

To ensure convergence of the integral, we introduce a regulator
\epsilon>0
, effectively taking
k\to k-i\epsilon
:

\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\,e^{-iz(k-i\epsilon)}=\left[\frac{e^{-iz(k-i\epsilon)}}{-i(k-i\epsilon)}\right]_{0}^{\infty}=\frac{1}{i(k-i\epsilon)}.

(44)

Thus, the derivative is

f^{\prime}(k)=-\frac{1}{k-i\epsilon}.

(45)

Integrating with respect to
k
gives
f(k)
:

f(k)=-\ln(k-i\epsilon)+C,

(46)

where C is an integration constant. The complex logarithm can be expressed using the Heaviside step function
\theta(k)
:

\ln(k-i\epsilon)=\theta(k)\ln k+\theta(-k)(\ln|k|-i\pi).

(47)

This gives the explicit form of the function:

f(k)=-[\theta(k)\ln k+\theta(-k)(\ln|k|-i\pi)]+C.

(48)

We can verify this result by checking the value of
f(k)-f(-k)
:

\displaystyle\lx@nobreakspace\lx@nobreakspace f(k)-f(-k)

\displaystyle=[-\theta(k)\ln k-\theta(-k)(\ln|k|-i\pi)]-[-\theta(-k)\ln|k|-\theta(k)(\ln|k|-i\pi)]

\displaystyle=-i\pi(\theta(k)-\theta(-k))

\displaystyle=-i\pi\sgn(k).

(49)

This provides a consistent result. By setting the integration constant
C=0
(a common choice in regularization schemes), we obtain the final expression for the integral used in the main text:

\boxed{\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}dz\frac{e^{-izk}}{z}=-\ln(k-i\epsilon)=-\theta(k)\ln k-\theta(-k)(\ln|k|-i\pi).\quad}

(50)

Appendix B
Wightman function in terms of Rindler coordinate

Here we present the detail of calculation of writing the Wightman function in terms of the Rindler coordinate. We consider the RTW here (we omit the subscript RTW for convenience), which the field is just a function of
u
. We already presented the Wightman function in terms of light-cone coordinates
u,u^{\prime}
. We are interested to find
u-u^{\prime}
. One may write

\displaystyle-a(u-u^{\prime})

\displaystyle=e^{-a\tau}-e^{-a\tau^{\prime}}=e^{-a\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}}e^{-a\frac{\tau-\tau^{\prime}}{2}}-e^{-a\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}}e^{a\frac{\tau-\tau^{\prime}}{2}}

\displaystyle=-e^{-a\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}}2\sinh\left(a\frac{\Delta\tau}{2}\right),

(51)

where
\Delta\tau=\tau^{\prime}-\tau
. Hence

\displaystyle\ln(e^{-a\tau'} - e^{-a\tau})

\displaystyle=\ln 2+\ln\left(\sinh\left(a\frac{\Delta\tau}{2}\right)\right)-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right).

\displaystyle\ln(e^{-a\tau} - e^{-a\tau'})

\displaystyle=\ln 2+\ln\left(-\sinh\left(a\frac{\Delta\tau}{2}\right)\right)-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right).

(52)

Therefore the Wightman function can be found as

\displaystyle W_{1}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\theta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\left[\ln 2+\ln\left(\sinh\left(-\frac{a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)\right)-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\theta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)\left[\ln 2+\ln\left(\sinh\left(\frac{a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)\right)-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)-i\pi\right]\bigg\}.

(53)

Since
\theta(x)+\theta(-x)=1
, then

\displaystyle W_{1}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\bigg\{

\displaystyle\ln\frac{2}{a}-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\theta(\Delta\tau)\big(\ln\sinh\left(\frac{a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\pi\big)

\displaystyle+\theta(-\Delta\tau)\ln\sinh\left(\frac{-a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)\bigg\}.

(54)

Using the following property

\ln\Big(\sinh x-i\epsilon\Big)=\theta(x)\ln\sinh x+\theta(-x)\Big(\ln\sinh(-x)-i\pi\Big),

(55)

we finally find:

\boxed{\quad W_{1}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left\{\ln\frac{2}{a}-a\left(\frac{\tau+\tau^{\prime}}{2}\right)+\ln\left[\sinh\left(-\frac{a\Delta\tau}{2}\right)-i\epsilon\right]\right\}.\quad}

(56)
