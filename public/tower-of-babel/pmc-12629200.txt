Transition of pseudorabies virus from latency to reactivation state selectively triggered by pathogenic bacteria
Authors: Dan Yang, Miao Yang, Shibang Guo, Huihui Guo, Yafei Li, Yan Kuang, Hang Li, Yibo Zhang, Siyi Zhang, Qingyun Liu, Huanchun Chen, Xiangru Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pseudorabies virus (PRV) causes severe morbidity and mortality in pigs and must be eradicated from pig populations. The increasing PRV infections in humans—resulting in severe encephalitis, neurological sequelae, and death since 2017 in China—have also posed a public health risk. PRV can establish latency in pigs, potentially leading to disease outbreaks upon reactivation. However, little is known about the PRV reactivation process, particularly in cases with other pathogens coinfection. We found that extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli and Streptococcus suis, common zoonotic bacteria found in pigs, can trigger PRV reactivation, and bacteria up-regulated CXCL1 to enhance STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, which triggers PRV reactivation by regulating the transcription of early viral genes. Effective booster immunization significantly reduced PRV reactivation among pigs. Our work highlights the risk of bacterial coinfections on PRV reactivation, emphasizing the importance of adequate immunization for controlling PRV within pigs and reducing zoonotic transmission.

INTRODUCTION

Pseudorabies virus (PRV), first identified in 1902, exerts a substantial impact on the global swine industry (1). This double-stranded DNA virus belongs to the family Herpesviridae and Varicella genus, infecting various mammals with pigs serving as natural hosts and reservoirs (2, 3). In adult pigs, PRV primarily causes respiratory symptoms, reproductive disorders, and abortion, leading to substantial losses in the swine industry. In neonatal piglets, PRV infection can lead to severe central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, including tremors, salivation, ataxia, and seizures. Infected piglets typically die within 24 to 36 hours, with a case fatality rate approaching 100% (4). In addition to pigs, non-natural hosts such as cattle, sheep, dogs, cats, and mink may also exhibit similar clinical signs following PRV infection, including neurological symptoms, pruritus, and eventual death (5–7). Since 2017, China has reported 31 cases of human PRV infection confirmed by sequencing (8). These patients exhibited clinical symptoms of viral encephalitis, which ultimately resulted in death or various neurological sequelae (9, 10). In 2021, we successfully isolated the first human-derived PRV strain, hSD-2019/1, from a human case of viral encephalitis (11). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that hSD-2019/1 is closely related to current pig PRV variants in China and exhibits higher pathogenicity. Considering the showed ability of PRV to cross species and its role in causing severe neurological diseases with high disability rates in multiple human infections, its public health risk cannot be overlooked.

Similar to other members of the alpha herpesvirus family, PRV establish latency in the peripheral nervous system (PNS), particularly in the trigeminal ganglion (TG) (12, 13). In infected pigs, the viral genome persists in sensory neurons without generating infectious particles (14). PRV infection typically begins by replicating in the epithelial cells of the pig’s nasal cavity and oropharyngeal mucosa, followed by the spread of the virus to PNS neurons. Viral particles are retrogradely transported to the sensory nerve terminal ganglia, where they establish lifelong latent infections. During the establishment of latency, the virus enters cells through fusion with the axon terminal and is retrogradely transported to the nucleus, ultimately entering a latent state (15–17).

Although regional outbreaks of PRV in China have decreased due to preventive measures such as immunization, latent infection remains a substantial and ongoing concern. Swine farm surveys indicate a prevalent activation of PRV latency during winter and spring seasons (12, 18, 19). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) testing conducted in slaughterhouses revealed a high prevalence of latent PRV infection, with the gB gene detected in 100% of brainstem samples, 92% of olfactory bulb samples, and 85% of TG samples (20). Another study reported an ~75% rate of latent infection in the medulla oblongata, while data from Shandong Province, China, similarly indicated a high rate of PRV latent infection (21). Numerous studies have showed that latent viral infections can be reactivated upon exposure to specific stimuli or stress (22–25). The reactivation process involves the viral genome overcoming the compact heterochromatin structure, the nuclear import of VP16, the extensive transcription of both early and late genes, and the synthesis of infectious viral particles. The viral particles produced during reactivation are anterogradely transported along axons, ultimately reaching the axon terminals in the nasopharynx and eyes, where they are released (15, 26). This dynamic process represents a substantial challenge for the prevention and control of PRV.

In addition to the well-known characteristics of latent infection, PRV is frequently coinfected with other pathogens in intensive pig farming environments (27–29). Notably, the combined infection of PRV and extraintestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (ExPEC) is often underappreciated. In recent years, ExPEC has emerged as a major bacterial pathogen, posing a severe threat to both human and animal health (30). ExPEC strains are capable of causing a wide range of extraintestinal infections, including those affecting the urinary tract, CNS, circulatory system, and respiratory system (31). It remains a major health concern due to its high incidence, rapid progression, and high mortality rate. In China, numerous studies have documented the frequent isolation of ExPEC from clinical samples in the swine industry, resulting in substantial economic losses (32). In our previous work, we showed that ExPEC infection up-regulates multiple cytokines, disrupts blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and alters the peripheral and central microenvironments. Specifically, ExPEC infection elevates cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor–α, IL-17A, and IL-22, which increase BBB permeability as infection progresses (33). In addition, ExPEC-induced the secretion of macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) by brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs) exacerbates inflammation and further weakens the BBB (34). Our recent studies also revealed that ExPEC-secreted α-hemolysin causes an influx of extracellular Ca2+, disrupting the Hedgehog signaling pathway in BMECs, impairing communication between astrocytes and BMECs, and contributing to BBB dysfunction (35). These findings underscore the substantial impact of ExPEC infection on inflammatory cytokines, secreted proteins, and BBB intercellular communication, facilitating pathogen invasion into the CNS. However, whether these changes in the microenvironment influence latent PRV reactivation remains unclear and requires further investigation.

Based on this background, we developed both in vitro and in vivo models of latent PRV infection to evaluate the risk and underlying mechanisms of pathogenic bacterial coinfections that trigger viral reactivation. We reported that ExPEC and Streptococcus suis (S. suis) exhibited great potential to reactivate the latent PRV, in which the bacteria-induced CXCL1 mediating the phosphorylation of signal transducers and activators of transcription 3 (STAT3) played indispensable roles. We also exploited the natural host pigs and provided evidence of the superior protective effects of vaccine immunization on preventing PRV reactivation induced by pathogenic bacterial infections. Our research findings provide scientific and theoretical support for the rational and effective mitigation of latent PRV reactivation in veterinary clinical practice and offer substantial insights and scientific references for efficient prevention and control of PRV in large-scale pig farms, thereby minimizing the risk of PRV infection among the contact people in the future.

RESULTS

PRV establishes a quiescent infection in N2a cells maintained by ACV and IFN-α

We used the mouse neuroblastoma cell line Neuro-2a (N2a) as host cells for our experiments. Differentiation of N2a cells was achieved by reducing the serum concentration in the culture medium from 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to 2% and supplementing with 5% Opti-MEM to support normal cellular metabolism (36–38). After 24 hours of culture, the differentiated N2a cells displayed prominent long protrusions, characteristic of a neuron-like phenotype (fig. S1, A and B). To facilitate the monitoring of quiescent infection and reactivation of PRV in N2a cells, we engineered a recombinant PRV strain, HuBXY/2018-mCherry, by inserting the mCherry fluorescent reporter gene upstream of the VP26 protein terminator. At 2 days postinfection (dpi) with HuBXY/2018-mCherry, the infectious supernatant was replaced with medium containing acyclovir (ACV) and interferon-alpha (IFN-α) to suppress viral replication and spread, thereby establishing a drug-induced quiescent infection, as depicted in Fig. 1A (39, 40). Daily renewal of this medium maintained PRV in a stable quiescent state for ~12 days. Once red fluorescence was no longer observed, ACV and interferon-α (IFN-α) were withdrawn. The quiescently infected cells were then cultured in a standard medium for an additional 4 days to mimic the establishment of a stable quiescent or latent state.

To determine the optimal infection dose, we introduced HuBXY/2018-mCherry into N2a cells at various multiplicities (2, 1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01, and 0.005 MOI) while maintaining constant concentrations of ACV (200 μM) and IFN-α (10 μg/ml) (Fig. 1B). On day 8 of continuous exposure to ACV and IFN-α, fluorescence observations and intensity measurements were performed (Fig. 1C). HuBXY/2018-mCherry demonstrated productive replication at higher multiplicities of infection (MOIs of 2, 1, 0.5, 0.1, and 0.05), whereas replication ceased at MOIs of 0.01 and 0.005. Notably, the group infected at an MOI of 0.01 exhibited significantly elevated latency-associated transcript (LAT) transcript levels (Fig. 1D).

N2a cells were infected with HuBXY/2018-mCherry (MOI = 0.01) and then cotreated with ACV and IFN-α until day 12. The absence of red fluorescence during this period indicated effective suppression of viral replication. On day 12, ACV and IFN-α were withdrawn, and the cells were monitored until 16 dpi ensure that HuBXY/2018-mCherry had entered a stable quiescent state. RNA samples were collected on 16 dpi, and qPCR analysis was performed to assess the transcript levels of the LAT and the immediate early gene (IE180). The results revealed that LAT transcript levels were significantly higher than those of IE180, with LAT transcription being ~694-fold greater than IE180 transcription (Fig. 1E). Notably, the transcription of the late genes VP16 and gD was not observed. These findings provide valuable insights into the dynamics governing drug-induced latent HuBXY/2018-mCherry infection, indicating that HuBXY/2018-mCherry can be maintained in a stable quiescent infection in N2a cells under ACV and IFN-α treatments.

ExPEC and S. suis infections selectively induce HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation in N2a cell

Under normal physiological conditions, neurons typically reside in a sterile environment, making them resistant to bacterial infections. However, under certain circumstances, specific bacteria can breach the BBB, gaining access to neurons. To investigate the influence of pathogenic bacteria on the reactivation of quiescent PRV, we tested a range of bacteria commonly found in clinical pig farms, including Glaesserella parasuis (G. parasuis), S. suis, enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), and ExPEC using the N2a cell model described previously. After confirming that the HuBXY/2018-mCherry had been established quiescent state in N2a cells, the cells were exposed to various bacterial strains for 2 hours. After the infection period, the bacterial supernatant was removed and replaced with fresh medium containing antibiotics to eliminate residual bacteria. Between 1 to 5 dpi, we monitored cell morphology and viral fluorescence by microscopy. As shown in Fig. 1F, G. parasuis serotype 5 strains—G. parasuis–CF7066, G. parasuis–Nagasaki, and G. parasuis–SH0165—all failed to induce the appearance of red fluorescence, and the cells retained normal morphology with no signs of cytotoxicity. S. suis serotype 2 strains, S. suis–SC19 and S. suis–P1/7, did not initiate HuBXY/2018-mCherry expression, whereas S. suis–43 showed a reappearance of red fluorescence on day 4 postinfection, indicating reactivation and induction of cytopathic effects (CPEs) (Fig. 1G). Bright-field images showed normal cell morphology and no signs of bacterial cytotoxicity. We additionally validated two other S. suis serotype 2 strains, S. suis–47 and S. suis–66, and both similarly induced the reexpression of HuBXY/2018-mCherry in quiescently infected cells (fig. S1, C and D). Besides, infection with ETEC-C83549, ETEC-E66, and ETEC-10667 did not yield any observable red fluorescence (Fig. 1H). Despite treatment with chloramphenicol (25 μg/ml) at 2 hours post infection (hpi), cultures infected with ETEC-C83549 and ETEC-10667 displayed partial cell shedding between 48 and 96 hpi, indicating some cytotoxicity. In contrast, the ExPEC-RS218 and ExPEC-PCN033 (both were isolated from brain tissues) successfully induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry to express the red fluorescent protein in N2a cells on 3 dpi (Fig. 1I), and bright-field imaging confirmed that cell morphology remained intact with no evidence of bacterial cytotoxicity. To determine whether PRV particles reactivated during ExPEC infection are capable of productive infection, we inoculated supernatants obtained after viral reactivation onto PK-15 cells, a well-established cell line highly susceptible to PRV and commonly used for viral isolation, propagation, and titration. At 48 hpi, fluorescence microscopy revealed typical signs of PRV infection in PK-15 cells, including red fluorescence, cellular swelling, rounding, and the development of characteristic CPE, as indicated by red arrows (fig. S1E). Concurrently, median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assays demonstrated that the viral titer reached 102.08/0.1 ml following reactivation and increased to 102.87/0.1 ml after 48 hours of propagation in PK-15 cells (fig. S1F). In contrast, supernatants derived from quiescently infected cells did not induce red fluorescence or any observable morphological changes associated with infection in PK-15 cells after 48 hours (fig. S1G). These results indicate that the HuBXY/2018-mCherry particles reactivated by ExPEC infection exhibit infectious properties and are capable of initiating productive infection under the experimental conditions used. In addition, we validated the impact of E. coli isolates obtained from the lungs (ExPEC-PCN042 and ExPEC-PCN079), liver (ExPEC–XT-10 and ExPEC-Avian004), and intestines (InPEC-13566 and InPEC-10017) on the reactivation of quiescent HuBXY/2018-mCherry. The results showed that both ExPEC-PCN042 and ExPEC-PCN079 facilitated the transition of HuBXY/2018-mCherry from a quiescent state to productive infection in neuronal cells (fig. S1, C and D).

To further investigate the impact of different pathogenic bacteria on PRV reactivation, we collected samples on 5 dpi with various bacteria for DNA and RNA extraction and analysis. As shown in Fig. 2A, PRV-gE detection supported that G. parasuis–CF7066, G. parasuis–Nagasaki, and G. parasuis–SH0165 did not induce gE genome replication [ns (not significant), P > 0.05]. Similarly, S. suis–SC19 and S. suis–P1/7 did not trigger gE genome replication as well, whereas S. suis–43 significantly increased the gE copy numbers. Likewise, ETEC-C83549, ETEC-E66, and ETEC-10667 failed to elevate gE re-replication, while in contrast, the infections of ExPEC-RS218 and ExPEC-PCN033 resulted in a substantial elevation in gE copy numbers, ~500 to 1000 times higher than those in the quiescent cells. In addition, HuBXY/2018-mCherry mRNA transcription detection indicated that only S. suis–43, ExPEC-RS218 and ExPEC-PCN033 infections led to the concurrent transcription of IE180, EP0, VP16, and particularly the key late gene gD (Fig. 2B). These data indicate that bacterial pathogens may selectively trigger the reactivation of quiescent PRV in neuronal cells, among which both ExPEC and S. suis strains exhibit the greatest potential.

ExPEC challenge triggers PRV transition from latency to lytic infection in mice

In addition to establishing an in vitro neuronal cell model, we also developed a mouse model of latent PRV infection. As depicted in Fig. 3A, 6-week-old BALB/c mice were passively immunized through intraperitoneal injection (IP) of PRV antibody–positive serum (with a neutralizing antibody titer of 1:230). Following a 30-min interval, the footpads of the mice were inoculated with a lethal dose (30 LD50) of the PRV variant strain HuBXY/2018 (GenBank: MT468549.1). The day of inoculation was designated as day 0, and oral swabs were collected from the mice for viral excretion analysis on each subsequent day until day 56. The analysis of oral swab assays revealed that viral excretion peaked on 3 dpi and subsequently declined, with no detectable virus by day 10 (fig. S2A). By day 56, the survival rate of the mice was 66% (fig. S2B). Three surviving mice were randomly selected for the assessment of PRV latent infection. RNA extracted from their brain, cerebellum, and TG exhibited the LAT transcripts in all tissues. Notably, the TG exhibited the highest levels of LAT transcripts, ~130 times greater than the levels of IE180 transcripts (Fig. 3B). No VP16 or gD transcripts were detected in any tissue, indicating that HuBXY/2018 had successfully entered the latent stage.

On day 56 post-HuBXY/2018 infection, following a previously described protocol, we administered a tail vein injection of the immunosuppressant dexamethasone (DEX, 2.75 mg/kg) as a positive control for PRV reactivation (41). Subsequently, at 12-hour post-DEX administration, DNA and RNA were extracted from the TG and brain (n = 3). qPCR analysis revealed a significant increase in gE copies in both tissues compared to the latent group [phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)–treated group; Fig. 3C). The mRNA assay results revealed active transcription of HuBXY/2018 lytic and replication-associated genes (IE180, EP0, and VP16) (fig. S2C). Notably, the transcription level of VP16, a gene expressed during late-stage reactivation in the TG, reached up to 105 copies per 50 ng of RNA, confirming HuBXY/2018 reactivation from latency.

Furthermore, we investigated the symptoms and histopathology outcomes resulting from ExPEC-PCN033 [5 × 106 colony-forming units (CFU)] triggered HuBXY/2018 reactivation. Following ExPEC-PCN033 infection (tail vein injection) at intervals of 6, 24, 48, and 72 hours, mouse symptoms were monitored and tissues were collected for bacterial and viral analyses (n = 3). At the initial stage of infection (6 hpi), mice displayed disheveled coats. By 24 and 48 hpi, symptoms progressed to include curling behavior and reduced alertness, leading to tremors, eventually culminating in convulsions and labored breathing by the end of the observation period (72 hpi). Mice in the ExPEC-PCN033–infected group harbored significantly higher copies of the gE genome in both the TG and brain compared to the PBS-injected group (Fig. 3D). Upon euthanasia, examination revealed severe brain tissue hemorrhage, which was further confirmed through hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining techniques (Fig. 3E). Mice in the ExPEC-PCN033–treated group, which underwent infection-induced reactivation of latent HuBXY/2018, developed severe cerebral hemorrhage (Fig. 3E, 1), whereas PBS-treated mice (HuBXY/2018 persistent latent group) showed no detectable brain damage (Fig. 3E, 2). Bacterial counts in the brain peaked at 1.6 × 104 CFU/g at 6 hpi, followed by a gradual decline at 24, 48, and 72 hpi (fig. S2D). Viral particle analysis revealed an early increase in the TG at 24 and 48 hpi, which returned to baseline levels by 72 hpi (fig. S2E). Similarly, viral loads in the brain were comparable to PBS controls at 6 hpi but then increased significantly at 24, 48, and 72 hpi (fig. S2F). At 72 hpi, immunohistochemical staining detected HuBXY/2018 antigen only in ExPEC-PCN033–treated mice, whereas both PBS-treated and ExPEC control mice remained negative (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these data suggest that HuBXY/2018 establishes a stable latent infection in mice and may be reactivated by ExPEC-PCN033.

Vaccination effectively suppresses the excretion of PRV and reduces the viral load in pigs

Vaccination remains the primary strategy for controlling PRV in veterinary practice. To assess the impact of vaccine immunization on PRV viral loads and latency, 39 healthy piglets received a gene deletion vaccine (∆TK/∆gE/∆gI) on their first day of birth (Fig. 4A). On day 35, they were divided into three groups: group A (15 piglets received an intramuscular injection of PBS), group B (15 piglets received a second intramuscular dose of the ∆TK/∆gE/∆gI vaccine), and group C (9 piglets received the same second vaccine dose). Serum samples were collected at 50 days of age to evaluate variations in antibody levels among the groups. Notably, no significant differences in gB antibody levels (S/N values) were observed between groups A and B (Fig. 4B). However, group B exhibited significantly higher neutralizing antibody production compared to group A (Fig. 4C), indicating enhanced antibody response following intensive immunization. At 50 days of age, piglets in groups A and B were intramuscularly injected with PRV (5 × 106 TCID50), and oral swabs were collected on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, 25, 35, and 40 postinfections to quantify viral particle copy numbers. Piglets in groups C did not receive a viral challenge and served as the unchallenged, vaccination-only control. In group A, viral excretion was detectable in the oral cavity on 1 dpi (6 of 15), peaked on 7 dpi (14 of 15), substantially decreased on 25 dpi (3 of 15), and became undetectable by days 35 and 40, implying a latency period for the virus (Fig. 4D). Conversely, piglets in group B consistently maintained lower virus loads in oral swabs, with viral excretion completed by day 25 (Fig. 4E), marking an end to the viral release period ~10 days earlier than observed in group A. Meanwhile, blood samples from group A showed consistent PRV detection from days 5 to 25, peaking at day 5 (12 of 15) before gradually decreasing thereafter (Fig. 4F). In contrast to this pattern seen in group A, however, we observed virus particles at much lower rates on days 5 (3 of 15) and 15 (2 of 15) only in group B (Fig. 4G); no detection occurred between days 25 and 40. Collectively, because pigs can carry PRV for life once infected, these data indicate that intensified immunization would substantially reduce viral loads within both the pig’s bloodstream and their oral cavity following challenge with PRV, thereby favoring maintenance of the virus in a latent state.

Infection with ExPEC induces reactivation of latent PRV in immunized pigs

To further confirm the impact of bacterial infections on the reactivation of latent PRV, we infected five pigs via transauricular venous injection from groups A and B (Fig. 4A) with 1.5 × 109 CFUs of ExPEC-PCN033, commonly found in 43 to 53% of swine farms (42–44). As a positive control for PRV reactivation, five additional pigs from each group were administered DEX (2.75 mg/kg) (45). The day of infection was designated as day 0, with stress-free intervals provided throughout the study period. Clinical observations and oral swab collections were conducted over 5 days. Viral content analysis revealed that in group A, ExPEC infection led to persistent viral shedding for 4 days, with peak excretion reaching 1.58 × 104 copies/g DNA before declining to baseline by day 5 (Fig. 5A). In group B, PRV was detected on day 3 (one of five) and day 4 (two of five) post-ExPEC infection, peaking at 1.41 × 104 copies/g DNA, and then was undetectable (Fig. 5B). Among the DEX-treated group, PRV was detected in the oral cavity of three pigs (three of five) in group A on day 1, with a peak of 4.26 × 102 copies/g DNA, while only one pig continued shedding on day 2 (Fig. 5C). The presence of PRV particles in the oral cavity was noted in two of the five pigs in group B on day 3 following DEX induction, with a maximum detection level recorded as being at ~3.40× 102 copies/g DNA (Fig. 5D). These observations suggest that ExPEC infection facilitate renewed shedding of PRV.

On day 5 post-ExPEC and DEX treated, blood samples were collected to assess gE and gB antibody levels, as well as the neutralizing antibody. In group A, ExPEC infection led to a significant decrease in optical density at 630 nm values, indicating significantly elevated levels of gE and gB antibodies, similar to the effects of DEX treated (Fig. 5E). In group B, there was a significant increase in gE antibody levels after ExPEC infection, while the levels of gB antibodies remained unchanged. Moreover, no significant changes in the levels of gE and gB antibodies were observed in group B following DEX-treated (Fig. 5F). In group C, gE antibody levels in the blood remained consistently negative (S/N > 0.4) following ExPEC infection and DEX stimulation, and there was no significant difference in gB antibody levels compared to the PBS-injected group (Fig. 5G). Group A pigs exhibited an increase in neutralizing antibody levels from 1:120 to 1:414 after ExPEC infection and from 1:67 to 1:163 after DEX treated (Fig. 5H). For pigs in group B, ExPEC infection led to a substantial increase in neutralizing antibody levels from 1:192 to 1:747, and DEX stimulation resulted in a significant increase from 1:109 to 1:169 (Fig. 5I). Group C exhibited persistently low neutralizing antibody titers (Fig. 5J). These results suggest that the reactivation of PRV within the host induces the production of specific neutralizing antibodies.

Similarly, we evaluated the tissue viral load in pigs infected with ExPEC and those exposed to DEX stimulation in groups A and B. The results showed that, compared to the pigs injected with PBS, PRV particles could be detected in every tested tissue from group A pigs post-ExPEC infection, including the brain (three of five), cerebellum (three of five), TG (four of five), lungs (two of five), submandibular lymph nodes (four of five), and tonsils (three of five) (Fig. 5K). Likewise, after DEX stimulation, PRV particles were detected in the TG (four of five), lungs (two of five), submandibular lymph nodes (two of five), and tonsils (four of five). In contrast, group B pigs post-ExPEC infection only showed the presence of PRV virus particles in the brain (two of five), cerebellum (two of five), TG (two of five), and lungs (two of five), with no detection observed in submandibular lymph nodes and tonsils (Fig. 5L). Similarly, the presence of PRV virus particles was exclusively identified within the TG (two of five) and submandibular lymph nodes (two of five) after DEX stimulation for group B. These implied a better protective tendency of booster immunization against the reactivation of the latent PRV particles. The colocalization of PRV signals was observed in the nuclei of the brain and TG in group A pigs (Fig. 5M; indicated by red arrows), but not in PBS-treated pigs. These findings indicated that ExPEC trigger the reactivation of latent PRV in pigs, even in pig populations with good vaccination programs.

CXCL1 is substantially up-regulated and plays a crucial role in ExPEC-triggered PRV reactivation

To identify host transcripts up-regulated in neurons supporting PRV reactivation, we conducted comparative RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) between the HuBXY/2018-mCherry quiescent/latent samples and the ExPEC-induced reactivation samples (96 hpi) in the N2a cell (Sequence Read Archive, accession no. PRJNA1151217). The RNA-seq data showed high reproducibility within each group of samples (fig. S3A). In comparison to the latent group, 187 genes were significantly up-regulated, and 152 genes were significantly down-regulated in the HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation group (Fig. 6A). Among these up-regulated genes, CXCL1 exhibited a particularly significant increase (log2 fold change > 2, adjusted P value (P adj.) < 0.05, FPKM > 10), with a read count of 428, which was notably higher than latency group (Fig. 6B). In the N2a cell model, we collected samples at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours after ExPEC infection, covering the period before HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation. CXCL1 remained highly transcript at all time points, peaking at ~138-fold increase over the cell control at 6 hpi (Fig. 6C). Neither ExPEC-control nor PRV-control infection produced a similar up-regulation of CXCL1 (Fig. 6D). These results suggest that CXCL1 may be involved in HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation.

Next, to evaluate the role of CXCL1 in ExPEC-induced PRV reactivation, we used CRISPR-Cas9 to generate CXCL1 knockout (CXCL1-KO) cell clones (fig. S3, B and C). We then established HuBXY/2018-mCherry quiescent infection in both wild-type (WT) and CXCL1–knockout (KO) cells using a previously described method. In the CXCL1-KO cells, the LAT transcript was ~78-fold higher than the IE180 (Fig. 6E), indicating the establishment of quiescent infection. ExPEC was subsequently added into these cells, and fluorescence analysis revealed that ExPEC (MOI = 1) successfully reactivated HuBXY/2018-mCherry in N2a-WT cells (Fig. 6F). In contrast, ExPEC infection at MOIs of 1, 5, and 10 failed to trigger reactivation in CXCL1-KO cells (Fig. 6F). These findings were further indicated by viral genome (gE) copy number analysis (Fig. 6G).

During the quiescent infection phase (days 0 to 11), HuBXY/2018-mCherry was similarly maintained in N2a-WT and CXCL1-KO cells (Fig. 6H). However, while HuBXY/2018-mCherry efficiently proliferated in N2a-WT cells under nondrug-treated conditions, no substantial viral proliferation was detected in CXCL1-KO cells (Fig. 6I). These observations provide preliminary evidence that CXCL1 knockout may limit HuBXY/2018-mCherry proliferation in neuronal cells. Under natural infection conditions, confocal microscopy showed that at 24 hpi, gB signals in N2a-WT cells localized to the plasma membrane, causing cell lysis and spreading to adjacent cells, whereas in CXCL1-KO cells, gB remained perinuclear with no detectable release (fig. S3D). qPCR showed that extracellular viral genome copies did not increase over time in CXCL1-KO cultures compared to N2a-WT (fig. S3E), and transmission electron microscopy showed cytoplasmic accumulation of viral particles in CXCL1-KO cells versus membrane-associated and extracellular particles in N2a-WT cells (fig. S3F). It should be noted that these results were obtained under natural infection conditions and do not involve the quiescent/reactivation process. Together, these findings suggest that CXCL1 knockout reduced the release of the PRV virion, a possibility that merits further mechanistic investigation.

To further support the role of CXCL1 in ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation, we constructed the pEGFP-CXCL1 plasmid for CXCL1 overexpression (fig. S3G). The results showed that pEGFP-CXCL1 restored ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation, whereas cells transfected with the pEGFP vector remained mCherry negative (Fig. 6J). Consistent with this, only the pEGFP-CXCL1 + ExPEC group showed a significant increase in viral genome copy number (Fig. 6K) and elevated transcription levels of gD after ExPEC challenge (fig. S3H).

CXCL1 facilitates PRV reactivation by regulating STAT3 phosphorylation levels

After conducting Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on the differentially expressed genes identified by RNA-seq, we observed that the significantly enriched GO terms primarily pertained to DNA packaging and chromatin assembly (fig. S4A), while the KEGG pathways were mainly involved various immune response–related signaling pathways (fig. S4B). Notably, the CXCL1 gene was found to be included in seven significantly up-regulated KEGG pathways as depicted in fig. S4C, including the “viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor” signaling pathway. This pathway is commonly associated with viral immune escape, immunomodulation, and viral replication and spread within host cells. Key signaling molecules involved in these processes, such as STAT3, have been reported by several studies as important regulators of viral infection. In the N2a-WT cell model, ExPEC-induced STAT3 phosphorylation (p-STAT3) was substantially increased in HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation samples (fig. S4D). In addition, p-STAT3 levels rose steadily over time following ExPEC infection until the reactivation of HuBXY/2018-mCherry (Fig. 7A and fig. S4, E and F). In addition, we showed that CXCL1 could effectively induce p-STAT3 in a time-dependent manner, either through exogenous addition (2.5 ng/ml) or endogenous overexpression (Fig. 7B and fig. S4G), and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assays revealed a substantial accumulation of p-STAT3 in the nucleus of N2a-WT cells during ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation. However, in contrast, stimulation with ExPEC did not result in substantial translocation of p-STAT3 into the nucleus in CXCL1-KO cells (Fig. 7C). These findings suggest that CXCL1-mediated STAT3 phosphorylation and subsequent nuclear translocation may be involved in HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation.

We subsequently investigated the impact of inhibiting STAT3 activation on HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation. Following the establishment of a quiescent infection in N2a-WT cells, the cells were pretreated with or without 50 μM S3I-201(NSC 74869) for 12 hours to suppress the STAT3 signaling pathway before ExPEC infection (fig. S5A). Fluorescence microscopy showed that ExPEC induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)–treated cells, whereas S3I-201 treatment abolished this reactivation (Fig. 7D). Consistently, both the gE copy numbers and transcriptional levels of IE180, EP0, and gD significantly increased in N2a-WT cells upon ExPEC-triggered reactivation, while treatment with S3I-201 effectively suppressed both the increase in gE copy numbers (Fig. 7E) and gD transcription (fig. S5B). These findings suggest that p-STAT3 activation may play a critical role in ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation.

We next generated dual-luciferase reporter constructs containing the IE180 or EP0 promoter and assessed STAT3’s impact on their activity. Transfection of pCAGGS-HA-STAT3 significantly increased luciferase activity driven by both promoters compared to the pCAGGS-HA-vector control (Fig. 7, F and G), suggesting that STAT3 may bind these promoter regions to regulate transcription. We then predicted the potential STAT3-binding motif within the IE180 and EP0 promoter regions (fig. S5C) and generated mutants of these sites based on the pGL3-IE180-promo and pGL3-EP0-promo constructs (fig. S5D). The expression of pCAGGS-HA-STAT3 significantly enhanced luciferase activity driven by IE180-promo-WT, while IE180-promo-Mut remained unchanged (Fig. 7H). A similar pattern was observed for EP0-promo-WT and EP0-promo-Mut (Fig. 7I), indicating that STAT3 regulates these promoters via specific binding sites. Furthermore, S3I-201 treatment effectively blocked ExPEC-induced activation of IE180-promo-WT and EP0-promo-WT but did not affect their mutant counterparts.

To validate these observations, we examined the regulation of the IE180 and EP0 promoters by endogenous p-STAT3 using Cleavage Under Target & Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) technology. In N2a-WT cells undergoing ExPEC-induced reactivation of quiescent HuBXY/2018-mCherry, p-STAT3 was significantly enriched at the IE180 promoter, whereas CXCL1-KO cells showed no such enrichment (Fig. 7L). A similar pattern was observed at the EP0 promoter (Fig. 7M). These findings suggest that p-STAT3 may bind specific sites within the IE180 and EP0 promoters to enhance their activity, thereby promoting reactivation from a quiescent state.

In vivo interference of CXCL1 substantially reduces HuBXY/2018 reactivation from latency in mice

This study further explores the role of CXCL1 in bacteria-induced PRV reactivation through in vivo gene interference targeting CXCL1. To this end, a small interfering RNA (siRNA) specifically targeting the CXCL1 gene was designed and synthesized, with the target sequence shown in Fig. 8A. After establishing a HuBXY/2018 latency in mice, CXCL1-siRNA was delivered using an in vivo siRNA delivery system, with two administrations (Fig. 8B). Mice were subsequently infected with ExPEC via tail vein injection, and samples were collected 72 hpi for analysis. CXCL1 levels in the serum of mice were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The results showed that ExPEC infection significantly elevated CXCL1 levels in serum, with the greatest increase observed in the ExPEC-induced PRV reactivation group (Fig. 8C). In contrast, CXCL1-siRNA treatment substantially reduced CXCL1 levels in the serum of ExPEC-infected mice (Fig. 8D). SYBR Green–based qPCR revealed a significant up-regulation of CXCL1 transcripts in both the mouse brain (Fig. 8E) and the TG (Fig. 8F) following ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018 reactivation. However, in the CXCL1-siRNA treatment group, CXCL1 mRNA levels in both the brain and TG were significantly reduced following ExPEC infection, confirming the effective in vivo interference of CXCL1 expression. Histopathological examination of brain tissues through H&E staining revealed substantial pathological alterations in the meninges of ExPEC-infected mice (Fig. 8G). In the control group, ExPEC infection caused mild meningeal thickening and slight congestion. In the HuBXY/2018 latency group, ExPEC infection induced more severe meningeal lesions, including marked thickening, separation, and prominent congestion (indicated by red arrows). However, in the CXCL1-siRNA treatment group, no substantial meningeal abnormalities were detected after ExPEC infection, suggesting that CXCL1 interference effectively alleviates the meningeal damage caused by bacterial infection. The qPCR analysis of viral loads revealed that ExPEC infection markedly triggered HuBXY/2018 reactivation in the TG, leading to a substantial increase in viral copy numbers. Treatment with CXCL1-siRNA significantly reduced gE copy numbers in the TG (P < 0.001) (Fig. 8H). A similar pattern was observed in brain tissues (P < 0.01) (Fig. 8I). These findings underscore the critical role of CXCL1 in ExPEC-induced reactivation of latent HuBXY/2018. The interference of CXCL1 expression through CXCL1-siRNA not only decreases CXCL1 levels in serum and neural tissues but may also suppress HuBXY/2018 reactivation and alleviate the associated pathological damage.

DISCUSSION

Now, PRV is widely prevalent among swine populations worldwide, particularly in regions such as Asia, South America, and Europe. Although some countries have successfully eradicated PRV from domestic pigs, infections in wildlife, especially among feral pigs, continue to occur frequently (46, 47). In recent years, pseudorabies has gained attention in the context of large-scale pig farming in China, leading to the implementation of preventive measures such as immunization and subsequent reduction in disease prevalence (29, 48). Despite substantial progress in PRV control, several challenges persist. For example, pigs harboring latent infections may not exhibit overt symptoms but can experience reactivation when stressed or immunosuppressed, triggering periodic pseudorabies outbreaks. Therefore, a systematic investigation into PRV latency and reactivation is imperative. Identifying and comprehending the triggers for latent virus reactivation can elucidate the biological mechanisms governing the transition from latency to active infection. This understanding can guide the implementation of measures on pig farms aimed at mitigating disease risks and enhancing both herd and public health.

The potential of clinical pathogens to trigger reactivation of quiescent PRV

In this study, we used N2a cells to model PRV quiescent infection and reactivation. N2a cells are a well-established system for investigating herpesvirus infection, replication, and latency. For instance, PRV infection drives autophagosome formation in N2a cells, which enhances viral replication (49). Inoculation of N2a cells with PRV at a low MOI (0.01) reliably establishes latency in these cells (50). Thyroid hormone (T3), acting through its nuclear receptor, modifies histones and remodels chromatin in N2a cells, thereby influencing herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) gene expression, replication, and the balance between latency and reactivation (51). Neuron-specific miR-9 reduces HSV-1 replication and gene expression in N2a cells, and the ectopic expression of miR-9 inhibits HSV-1 reactivation in neuronal cells (52). Bovine herpesvirus type 4, a γ-herpesvirus, also establishes persistent infection in N2a cells (53). Collectively, these findings indicate that N2a cells provide a useful system for investigating herpesvirus latency and reactivation. Following the infection of N2a cells with HuBXY/2018-mCherry and subsequent treatment with antivirals ACV and IFN-α, we observed characteristic features of quiescent HuBXY/2018-mCherry infection, including suppressed red fluorescent protein expression, elevated LAT gene transcription, and the absence of VP16 and gD gene transcription. These findings are consistent with those observed during latent infection by most α-herpesviruses (54–56). To assess the impact of pathogenic bacterial factors on HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation, we collected field strains of G. parasuis, S. suis, ETEC, and ExPEC, which are commonly detected in large-scale pig farms, to infect the established cell models (30, 57–60). In this work, not all strains induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation from the quiescent state, suggesting that different pathogens have differentiated abilities in triggering virus reactivation. Specifically, ExPEC-PCN033, ExPEC-RS218, ExPEC-PCN042, ExPEC-PCN079, S. suis–43, S. suis–47, and S. suis–66 substantially induced reactivation of quiescent HuBXY/2018-mCherry, whereas G. parasuis–CF7066, G. parasuis–Nagasaki, G. parasuis–SH0165, S. suis–SC19, S. suis–P1/7, ETEC-C83549, ETEC-E66, ETEC-10667, InPEC-13566, and InPEC-10017 failed to induce HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation. We hypothesize that this difference may be closely related to the biology of the pathogen. ExPEC is a zoonotic pathogen that can cross the BBB (61, 62). We have shown that its α-hemolysin disrupts TGFBRII/Gli2 signaling within the BBB, impairing intercellular communication (35). Moreover, ExPEC infection induces astrocyte expression of miR-155 and miR-146a via a Toll-like receptor–mediated nuclear factor κB feedback loop, amplifying the local neuroinflammatory response (63). This inflammatory microenvironment may serve as a key trigger for reactivation from a quiescent state. By contrast, InPEC and ETEC remain confined to the gut and target epithelial cells, with little interaction with the neural environment required for HuBXY/2018-mCherry quiescent infection and reactivation, thus limiting their capacity to trigger reactivation (64, 65). S. suis is also a zoonotic pathogen that causes meningitis and affects the CNS (59, 66). However, it is worth noting that not all type 2 S. suis strains were able to induce HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation in this study. Further analysis revealed that S. suis–43, S. suis–47, and S. suis–66, which were able to induce reactivation, were relatively less virulent strains, while S. suis–SC19 and S. suis–P1/7, which could not induce HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation, were more virulent strains. Whether there is a correlation between the virulence of pathogens and the ability to trigger PRV reactivation remains unclear, and it is necessary to expand the number of S. suis strains and the range of serotypes for further verification. In addition, G. parasuis primarily causes fibrinous pleuropneumonia and arthritis in pigs; although neurological signs can occasionally occur, respiratory and joint lesions predominate. Moreover, our subsequent experiments demonstrated that G. parasuis infection did not substantially or persistently up-regulate CXCL1 transcription, which may be one of the key reasons for its inability to induce HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation.

We also noted that, in the N2a cell model, infection with ExPEC-PCN033 and ExPEC-RS218 resulted in abundant EP0 and gD transcription, while IE180 transcription remained relatively low, particularly in the ExPEC-PCN033 group. We believe that this discrepancy is likely due to our sampling time point, when cells were harvested when we detected viral fluorescence under the microscope. At this stage, most virions have matured and late-gene expression predominates, while IE180 is typically expressed earlier in the infection cycle, for example, ICP0 is highly expressed during the initial phase of DEX-induced BoHV-1 reactivation (67, 68). Conversely, in the ETEC-infected group, both ETEC-C83549 and ETEC-10667 up-regulated IE180 transcription but did not increase gE copy or induce VP16 and gD transcription. This finding indicates that, although IE180 serves as the principal immediate-early transcription factor of PRV, full viral reactivation depends not only on IE180 but also critically on the complementing activity of proteins such as VP16 packaged within the virion. Studies have shown that the VP16-complex formed by VP16 with the host factors HCF-1 and Oct-1 is the molecular “switch” that determines whether the virus enters lytic replication or remains latent state (15).

Vaccination substantially reduces PRV reactivation risk in pigs

We attempted to induce PRV reactivation in immunized pigs through ExPEC infection and DEX stimulation. Our data indicate that both ExPEC and DEX effectively triggered the reactivation of latent PRV in pigs. Among pigs that received two vaccine doses, PRV was detected in the TG of only two of five animals following ExPEC infection (fig. S6A), suggesting that booster vaccination can reduce the occurrence of PRV reactivation. Despite the immunosuppressive effects of DEX, treatment with ExPEC infection and DEX resulted in a substantial increase in PRV-specific antibodies in pig serum, as evidenced by elevated S/N titers and neutralizing antibody levels. This phenomenon can be attributed to the reactivation of memory B cells generated after the initial PRV infection upon subsequent ExPEC infection. With viral replication, memory B cells undergo a secondary immune response characterized by rapid proliferation and production of antibodies against PRV. DEX has been shown to up-regulate IE180 expression and promote PRV replication in neurons via glucocorticoid receptor (GR) binding to multiple response elements within the IE180 promoter (69). This mechanism may explain DEX’s ability to reactivate PRV in pigs. Notably, we detected LAT transcripts in TG from all pigs, including vaccine controls, indicating that the ∆TK/∆gE/∆gI vaccine strain can establish latency independently of TK/gE/gI (fig. S6, B and C), consistent with prior reports (70). However, in the vaccine group, neither DEX nor ExPEC induced VP16 expression (fig. S6D), indicating that although the vaccine strain establishes latency, it cannot reactivate, underscoring its safety. Moreover, preexisting PRV present in the TG prevents subsequent infections from establishing latent infections due to limited neuronal cells supporting latency (70, 71). Although quantification of latent neurons after one or two vaccinations was not performed, this plausible explanation aligns with observed lower virus reactivation rates following double vaccination.

CXCL1-STAT3 signaling mediates ExPEC-induced PRV reactivation

The chemokine CXCL1, also known as GRO-α, is a pivotal mediator of inflammation. In our study, we observed that CXCL1 was the most prominent cytokine during ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation. Previous studies have also implicated CXCL1 in the recurrence of herpes simplex keratitis (72). By using monoclonal CXCL1-KO neuronal cells, we found that CXCL1 expression induced by ExPEC infection plays a critical role in PRV reactivation. In the absence of CXCL1, the replication of HuBXY/2018-mCherry was delayed, resulting in reduced viral particle release and a decreased reactivation rate following ExPEC infection (fig. S5E). Furthermore, CXCL1-deficient cells exhibited elevated LAT expression levels compared to N2a-WT cells after HuBXY/2018-mCherry infection, indicating that CXCL1 deficiency may facilitate the maintenance of a quiescent infection. Notably, the overexpression of pEGFP-CXCL1 in CXCL1-KO cells rescued ExPEC-induced reactivation of HuBXY/2018-mCherry (Fig. 6J), whereas CXCL1 overexpression in N2a-WT cells further promoted viral replication upon reactivation (fig. S5F). This enhancement was supported by an increase in viral plaque area (fig. S5G) and a significant rise in viral genome copy numbers (fig. S5H). In mouse model, reducing CXCL1 expression appeared to limit ExPEC-induced HuBXY/2018 reactivation, as indicated by lower viral loads and less severe meningeal lesions. These observations suggest that CXCL1 serve as a target for modulating PRV reactivation. However, validating this mechanism will require systematic time-course analyses and molecular-labeling experiments.

The p-STAT3 levels were substantially higher in PRV-reactivated cells than in latently infected cells; however, p-STAT3 was markedly suppressed by ExPEC infection in neurons lacking functional CXCL1 (fig. S5I). Treatment with the S3I-201 effectively inhibited ExPEC-induced reactivation of HuBXY/2018-mCherry. The role of STAT3 in viral infection and pathogenesis has been extensively explored, particularly in infections caused by viruses such as varicella-zoster virus (VZV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and HSV-1. In these contexts, STAT3 functions as a proviral host factor (73). For instance, VZV induces STAT3 phosphorylation in infected cells both in vitro and in vivo, and this activation promotes infection by up-regulating the anti-apoptotic protein survivin. Elevated survivin expression suppresses apoptosis, thereby facilitating viral replication (74). Similarly, during HCV infection, core proteins of the virus directly interact with and activate STAT3, while inhibition of STAT3 markedly reduces HCV RNA levels, highlighting its role as a proviral host factor. STAT3 also promotes dengue virus replication and facilitates immune evasion by modulating type I and type III interferon responses. These findings underscore the critical role of STAT3 in regulating viral life cycles (75). To investigate the role of STAT3 in PRV reactivation, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays and found that STAT3 binds to specific motifs within the IE180 promoter, the first gene transcribed during infection, and the EP0 promoter, which encodes a multifunctional transcriptional activator (76). Notably, PRV strains lacking EP0 failed to reactivate in response to DEX (77). CUT&Tag analysis revealed that, upon phosphorylation, STAT3 accumulates in the nucleus of N2a-WT cells and markedly enhances IE180 and EP0 promoter activity. In contrast, CXCL1-KO cells showed no substantial STAT3-mediated regulation of these promoters. Together, these findings indicate that a CXCL1-STAT3 signaling axis contributes to PRV early gene expression and supports reactivation from latency. As part of our future research directions, we intend to perform CUT&Tag sequencing in host cells during both latent and reactivated PRV infection to construct a genome-wide binding profile of p-STAT3. This will allow for a comprehensive analysis of its binding specificity and regulatory functions across the entire PRV genome. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) can phosphorylate the GR at a previously uncharacterized hormone-independent site (Ser134), where phosphorylation acts as a molecular sensor that reprograms GR’s transcriptional output (78). This finding raises the possibility that, in addition to the CXCL1-STAT3 axis, CXCL1 may also activate MAPK to modulate GR function and thereby influence PRV reactivation, a mechanism that merits in vivo validation.

In summary, we established N2a cell lines and mouse and pig models to study latent PRV infection. Our findings highlight bacterial infection as a critical trigger for PRV reactivation, with CXCL1 emerging as a central host factor in this process. ExPEC infection activates STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation, which in turn regulate early PRV gene promoters and drive viral reactivation. Coinfections are commonly observed in pig farming, providing multiple stimuli for PRV reactivation. Although vaccination effectively reduces morbidity caused by PRV infection, it does not completely prevent latency and subsequent reactivation, which complicates efforts to eradicate PRV. This study emphasizes the crucial role of vaccination in preventing outbreaks of PRV. Biosecurity measures should address not only external factors but also the hidden and easily overlooked pig-carried pathogens, such as latently infected PRV and the common bacteria.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell, virus, and bacterial preparation

The PK-15 and N2a cells were obtained from the Typical Culture Preservation Center of China (Wuhan, China). The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco Life Technologies, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gemini, Woodland, USA) at 37°C with 5% CO2. G. parasuis was statically cultured in tryptose soya agar medium (TSA, Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) medium supplemented with 5% newborn calf serum (NBS, TIANHANG, Hangzhou, China) and 7% nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD, Solarbio, Beijing, China), incubated at a temperature of 37°C. S. suis was statically cultured in TSA medium containing 5% NBS at a temperature of 37°C. ETEC and ExPEC (PCN033, GenBank: CP006632.1) were cultured with agitation (200 rpm) in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium at 37°C. Relevant information of the selected strains is provided in table S1.

Construction of the PRV recombinant strain HuBXY/2018-mCherry

To develop a convenient indicator for tracking viral proliferation in cells, we constructed a recombinant fluorescent PRV strain for subsequent detection of latent infection and reactivation. The recombinant plasmid was generated via homologous recombination by inserting a fusion protein linker (GGTGGAGGCGGGTTCAGGCGGAGGTGGGCTCT) and the mCherry fluorescent tag at the 3′ end of the UL35 gene (encoding the VP26 protein) in the PRV variant strain HuBXY/2018. This recombinant plasmid was transfected into PK-15 cells, which were subsequently infected with the PRV variant strain HuBXY/2018 at a MOI of 0.001. After 24 hpi, mCherry fluorescence was visualized using fluorescence microscopy. Following five rounds of plaque purification, we successfully obtained the HuBXY/2018-mCherry strain, which carried the mCherry fluorescent tag, facilitating real-time tracking of viral infection.

Cellular models of quiescent HuBXY/2018-mCherry infection

The N2a cells were seeded into 12-well plates to determine the optimal infective dose of HuBXY/2018-mCherry. Once the cells reached 70% confluence, they were infected with various MOIs, including 2, 1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01, and 0.005 MOIs for a duration of 2 hours. Subsequently, the infective medium was removed, and the infected cells were cultured in a medium containing ACV (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) and IFN-α2a human recombinant (IFN-α, PROSPEC, Rehovot, Israel) for 8 days. The fluorescence distribution was observed and documented using an OLYMPUS IX73 fluorescence inverted microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo Metropolis, Japan). In addition, the fluorescence intensities (at wavelengths of 579 and 616 nm) associated with viral expression were quantified using a multifunctional enzyme marker (Spark, Tecan, Swiss Confederation).

Following determination of the optimal infective dose, the aforementioned procedure was replicated, and the infected cells were maintained in a medium containing ACV and IFN-α for a total of 12 days (37°C, 5% CO2). Subsequently, ACV and IFN-α were removed from the culture medium, and the quiescently infected cells were cultured in standard medium for an additional 4 days. To induce HuBXY/2018-mCherry reactivation, the quiescently infected cells underwent a 1-hour starvation period, followed by the addition of pathogens at an MOI of 1 for G. parasuis, S. suis, ETEC, and ExPEC. After inoculation, the supernatant was discarded, and the appropriate antibiotics were added to inhibit bacterial growth: cefotaxime (50 μg/ml) for ExPEC, kanamycin (50 μg/ml) for S. suis, penicillin and streptomycin (100 μg/ml) for G. parasuis, and chloramphenicol (25 μg/ml) for ETEC from 3 to 5 dpi following daily using a fluorescence microscope for up to 5 dpi. No bacterial proliferation was observed during this period. Viral fluorescence was monitored and recorded using an OLYMPUS IX73 fluorescence inverted microscope from 3 to 5 dpi following pathogenic bacterial infection of host cells.

Mouse models of latent PRV infection

We used 6-week-old female BALB/c mice for the study. The mice were administered an intraperitoneal injection of PRV antibody–positive serum 30 min before the HuBXY/2018 challenge. PRV-positive high-titer serum from pigs, which has a neutralizing titer of 1:230 against the HuBXY/2018 strain. Following footpad injection for HuBXY/2018 infection, oral swab samples were collected daily to detect the viral particle gE gene, while mouse survival was concurrently monitored. On day 56 post-HuBXY/2018 infection, surviving mice underwent an induced reactivation assay. Immunohistochemical experiments were conducted using porcine polyclonal antibody (1:100). All animal experimental procedures (HZAUMO-2022-0220) were approved and performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Huazhong Agricultural University, adhering to the standard requirements outlined in the International Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Animals.

Porcine model of latent PRV infection

One-day-old male piglets were vaccinated via nasal drops with a gene deletion vaccine (∆TK/∆gE/∆gI). At 35 days old, the piglets were divided into three groups: group A (15 piglets) received intramuscular injections of PBS; group B (15 piglets) received intramuscular injections of the gene deletion vaccine (∆TK/∆gE/∆gI); and group C (9 piglets) served as a control group receiving intramuscular injections of the gene deletion vaccine. When the piglets reached 50 days old, groups A and B were inoculated with PRV via intramuscular injection, while group C received intramuscular injections of PBS. All three groups were kept in strictly isolated environments. After PRV inoculation, oral cavity and blood samples were collected for viral excretion assessment. DEX or ExPEC was administered through marginal ear vein injection to induce reactivation. Subsequently, oral cavity samples were collected for five consecutive days. After 5 days, all pigs were euthanized, and tissue samples including the TG, brain, cerebellum, lungs, tonsils and submandibular lymph nodes were harvested. Half of each sample was fixed in formalin, while the other half was stored at −80°C for future use. All animal experimental procedures (HZAUSW-2023-0011) were approved and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the IACUC of Huazhong Agricultural University and adhered to the standard requirements outlined in the International Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Animals.

ELISA and neutralizing antibody test

Blood samples were collected from the pigs, and the sera obtained were subjected to ELISA using a PRV-gB and PRV-gE antibody detection kit (KeQian, Wuhan, China). To determine the neutralizing antibody titer, the test serum was incubated at 56°C for 30 min in a water bath. Subsequently, serial twofold dilutions were prepared and mixed with 200 TCID50 of HuBXY/2018. After a 60-min incubation at 37°C (5% CO2), 100 μl of PK-15 cells was added to the diluted sera mixture. The cell mixture was then incubated at 37°C (5% CO2) for 5 to 7 days. The results were calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

Quantitative PCR

To evaluate the expression of the PRV gene during latency using qPCR, RNA was extracted from N2a cells. Latent samples were incubated for 12 dpi in the presence of ACV and IFN-α, followed by an additional 4-day incubation without ACV and IFN-α. After completion of the incubation period, the medium was removed, and TRIzol reagent (Aidlab, Beijing, China) was added to each sample. The samples were then incubated at room temperature (25° ± 1°C) for 5 min before being collected and stored at −80°C until RNA purification. For assessment of reactivated samples, various pathogenic strains from different species with a MOI of 1 were individually introduced to latently infected samples. On 6 dpi, RNA was collected and stored at −80°C. RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The qPCR analysis was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) using a 2 × SYBR Green qPCR mix (Yeasen Shanghai). Each cDNA sample underwent analysis for IE180, EP0, VP16, gD, and LAT, as well as housekeeping genes including 28S RNA and GAPDH. Primer sequences for both viral and cellular housekeeping genes are provided in table S2. Fold change values were calculated by normalizing each sample to either 28S RNA or GAPDH levels while comparing the results of latent and reactivated samples to uninfected cells (2-∆∆Ct).

Viral DNA was extracted to detect the PRV genome copy number during latency and after reactivation using qPCR. After adding 1 MOI of various pathogenic strains into the latent infection, DNA extraction was performed using a blood/cell/tissue genomic DNA extraction kit (TianGen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 system using a 2 × Premix Ex Taq mix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Each DNA sample was quantified for the copy number of the PRV-gE based on a plotted standard curve. The primer sequences for gE gene are listed in table S2.

RNA seq and analyses

RNA samples were collected and extracted, and RNA integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Total RNA was used for library construction, with mRNA enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads to capture polyadenylate-tailed mRNA. The mRNA was then fragmented and used as a template for library construction. After preparation, the insert size was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer to ensure library quality. Qualified libraries were subjected to Illumina sequencing, generating 150–base pair (bp) paired-end reads. Image data from the high-throughput sequencer were processed using CASAVA base-calling software to generate sequence data (reads), which were stored in FASTQ format. Differential expression analysis between comparison groups for samples with biological replicates was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.20.0), with genes having an P adj. ≤ 0.05 considered differentially expressed. Pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted using clusterProfiler (version 3.8.1), focusing on KEGG pathways.

Construction of CXCL1 knockout cell line using CRISPR/Cas9 technology

Using the online tool CHOPCHOP, we designed two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting the CXCL1 gene (GeneID: 14825), selecting target sites within the coding region (exon). The sequences for sgRNA-1 and sgRNA-2 were synthesized as 5′-CTCGCTTCTCTGTGCAGCGC-3′ and 5′-CACTCAAGAATGGTCGCGAG-3′, respectively. Each was individually cloned into the pYSY-spCas9-sgRNA-Puro vector, generating the constructs pYSY-spCas9-sgRNA1-Puro and pYSY-spCas9-sgRNA2-Puro. The recombinant vectors were transformed into DH5α, and positive clones were screened and verified through sequencing analysis. Endotoxin-free plasmids were extracted from the verified positive clones (Omega Bio-Tek, Georgia, USA). When N2a cells reached 50 to 70% confluency, the two plasmids (1.5 μg each) were cotransfected into N2a cells using Polyplus-transfection reagents (Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France). After 24 hours of transfection, puromycin selection was performed by adding fresh medium containing puromycin (10 μg/ml; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) every 24 hours for 72 hours. Following selection, cells were diluted and seeded into individual wells of a 96-well plates for clonal isolation and cultured for a duration of 7 to 10 days until individual clones could be identified. Genomic DNA was then extracted from each single clone using YSY buffer genomic extraction solution (YSY biotech, Nanjing, China). The target region of the CXCL1 was amplified and sequenced using primers forward: 5′-CCAGTTCCAGCACTCCAGACTCC-3′ and reverse: 5′-GTCAGAAGCCAGCGTTCACCAGAC-3′. Further confirmation of CXCL1 knockout in correctly sequenced single clones was conducted through qPCR analysis with primers forward: 5′-ACAGGACTCAGAGCCTCTAACC-3′ and reverse 1: 5′-GCTGCACAGAGAAGCGAGC-3′, reverse 2: 5′-CCAGACAGGTGCCATCAGAGCAG-3′. In addition, CXCL1-KO was further validated at the protein level using a CXCL1 antibody (1:1000; Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China; RRID: AB_2087568).

Dual-luciferase reporter system for promoter analysis

All gene promoter sequences used in this study were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. Promoter sequences encompassing ~1000- to 2000-bp upstream and 200-bp downstream of the transcription start sites for each respective gene were selected. Firefly luciferase reporter plasmids were constructed using the pGL3-basic vector. The promoter sequences of target genes were cloned and digested, with Kpn I and Xho I serving as the designated restriction sites based on the HuBXY/2018 genome template. Primer sequences for amplification can be found in table S3. The DNA ligation process was performed following the instructions provided in the T4 DNA ligase product manual (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). The ligation products were transformed into DH5α competent cells, and positive clones were selected and sequenced for verification. The constructed reporter plasmids were named pGL3-IE180-promo and pGL3-EP0-promo, respectively. The STAT3 overexpression plasmid was constructed using the pCAGGS-HA vector. The STAT3 gene was cloned from N2a cell cDNA as a template, with Eco RI and Xho I serving as the restriction sites for digestion. The DNA ligation process followed the instructions provided in the T4 DNA ligase product manual (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). Subsequently, the ligation products were transformed into DH5α competent cells, and positive clones were selected and sequenced to confirm their accuracy. The resulting overexpression plasmid was designated as pCAGGS-HA-STAT3. When human embryonic kidney–293T cells reached 50 to 70% confluency, they underwent transfection with the aforementioned promoter reporter plasmids along with the Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid pRL-TK and the overexpression plasmid using jetPRIME transfection reagent (Polyplus-transfection, Illkirch-Graffenstaden, France), followed by incubation for 24 hours. In the ExPEC infection group, cells were exposed to a 100 MOI for 2 hours. In the S3I-201(NSC 74859) (MCE, MedChemExpress, NJ, USA) treatment group, S3I-201 (50 μM) was administered 6 hours before ExPEC infection, followed by a further 2 hours of infection. Subsequently, the luciferase activity of the transfected cells was measured according to the protocol outlined in Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit manual (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Results are presented as FL/RL ratio representing relative firefly luciferase activity levels.

Cleavage Under Target & Tagmentation

To investigate the effect of ExPEC infection on p-STAT3 regulation of the IE180 and EP0 promoters during the reactivation of latently infected PRV, we first established quiescent infection with the HuBXY/2018-mCherry strain in both N2a-WT and CXCL1-KO cells. At 96 hours post-ExPEC infection of the quiescently infected cells, samples were collected for formaldehyde cross-linking. The subsequent experiments were performed using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (TD904) (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The p-STAT3 (Tyr705) antibody (D3A7) (1:100, #9145, Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, USA) was used as the primary antibody, and horseradish peroxidase–conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:1000; Biogragon, BF03008; RRID: AB_3073988) served as the secondary antibody. To control for intergroup variations before qPCR validation, DNA spike-in (forward: 5′-GCCTTCTTCCCATTTCTGATCC-3′; reverse: 5′-CACGAATCAGCGGTAAAGGT-3′) was performed. Last, the fragmented DNA was used for qPCR-based quantitative detection.

Western blot

The cells were lysed in lysis buffer for Western blot following the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, Beijing, China). Subsequently, 1× buffer was added to the lysates, which were then boiled for 10 min. For Western blot analysis, the samples underwent separation through SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transfered onto a nitrocellulose membrane, and probed with specific antibodies (79). In cases where applicable, densitometry was used to quantify the immunoblotting signals.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software. Data are presented as the means ± SEM. Data were analyzed using t test. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
