HIV/AIDS risk factors among young single mothers working in alcohol-serving establishments in Rwanda
Authors: Thierry Claudien Uhawenimana, Theresa S Betancourt, Vincent Sezibera, Ross C Brownson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough prior studies suggest that female bar workers are vulnerable to HIV and engage in high-risk sexual practices, significant data gaps remain on HIV/AIDS risks among young single mothers in alcohol-serving establishments (ASEs), regardless of their roles. To address this gap in literature, this study explored both internal and external contextual factors that may contribute to heightened vulnerability to HIV/AIDS among young single mothers working in informal ASEs in Rwanda.MethodsThis research employed a qualitative multi-method approach. It was conducted in informal ASEs located in both urban and semi-urban areas of Musanze District, Kigali City, as well as in the adjacent emerging semi-urban regions of Kamonyi and Gakenke Districts. The study population consisted of 101 young single mothers engaged in various job roles within pubs, bars, and restaurants, motels, guesthouses, and lodges. A maximum variation purposive sampling was used to sample the study sites. Participants were selected using both purposive and snowball sampling techniques. Data were collected from two distinct sources: YouTube, and key informant interviews. YouTube was searched to retrieve testimonies from female staff who had ever worked in ASEs to garner their experiences on ASEs’ working conditions and how these increased their vulnerability to HIV. An interview guide featuring semi-structured questions was employed to facilitate the interviews. The data were analyzed using a thematic analysis approach supported by Dedoose software.ResultsThe findings suggest that vulnerabilities to HIV/AIDS among young single mothers working in ASEs may be shaped by persistent poverty, inadequate salaries, delays in salary payments, various forms of exploitation, exposure to alcohol, and engagement in transactional or multiple-partner sexual relationships within workplace contexts.ConclusionThis study identified a range of contextual factors that may influence HIV-related vulnerabilities among young single mothers working in ASEs. These findings highlight the potential value of targeted, multi-level interventions to support this population. Such interventions could address work-related stress and the intersecting demands of single parenthood, while promoting economic stability. In addition, programs may benefit from strengthening women’s capacity to negotiate safer sex practices and supporting engagement with HIV prevention and treatment services.Keywords: Alcohol serving establishments, Young single mothers, HIV/AIDS, Vulnerabilities, Rwanda

Introduction

HIV/AIDS remains a significant global public health burden, with Sub-Saharan Africa being disproportionately impacted, particularly among young girls [1]. In 2019, the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) reported that four out of five new HIV infections in the 15–19 age group occurred in young girls [2–4]. 66% of all new infections among adults aged 15 years and older occurred in adolescent girls and young women [1]. In contrast to older individuals, adolescents and young people are less inclined to undergo HIV testing and to establish a prompt connection to HIV care [1, 5].

Young girls in Africa face a heightened risk of engaging in unsafe sexual practices, often in contexts where they have diminished agency over their sexual and reproductive choices [3, 6, 7]. When young girls are confronted with early motherhood, they become victims of increased poverty which leads some of them to seek informal employment to support themselves and their children [8]. Working in certain informal sectors, particularly in ASEs, poses potential risks for adolescent girls and young women (AGYW), including heightened vulnerability to exploitative and transactional sexual relationships and elevated risks of HIV/AIDS [8]. Research indicates that ASEs can increase the risks associated with HIV transmission [9, 10]. AGYW may often experience exploitative sexual relationships, including those who frequent these venues for transactional sex or those employed within them [11–13]. Studies suggest that a considerable proportion of AGYW working in ASEs had ever participated in behaviors that increase the risk of HIV transmission [14–17]. Some studies further suggest that peer pressure, and the organizational structure of the ASEs may lead to substance abuse and risky sexual behaviours that can heighten acquisition and transmission of HIV/AIDS for AGYW working in such settings [16, 17].

Research conducted in various countries, including Cambodia, Cameroon, Ethiopia, South Africa, The Philippines, and Tanzania, suggests that female bar workers, due to their interactions with male clients, are more prone to partake in high-risk sexual behaviors including transactional sex [13, 18–22]. Various motivations drive AGYW to participate in transactional sex, including the pursuit of economic stability, and the need to meet basic needs for themselves and their children [13, 18, 23, 24]. Nonetheless, these studies have focused on the vulnerabilities associated with female staff’s increased risk of HIV/AIDS solely within bars and from a single category of female employees working in these establishments. They have not explored these risks in other informal ASEs, nor have they investigated these vulnerabilities among all female staff, without limiting the scope to bar workers.

While studies have underscored the vulnerability of female bar workers to HIV and the factors that lead them to engage in high-risk sexual activities, there remains a significant lack of data regarding the specific risks associated with HIV/AIDS for young single mothers employed in ASEs in Rwanda. In addition to their identity as women, which places them in a category of vulnerable populations requiring protection, young single mothers in ASEs necessitate focused attention concerning HIV/AIDS prevention strategies. This entails addressing any vulnerabilities that may heighten their likelihood of engaging in risky sexual practices, which could ultimately lead to HIV/AIDS. As single parents, their health and the future well-being of their children are intertwined; if their lives are jeopardized, the health of their children may also suffer. Consequently, it is essential to investigate both inner (e.g., alcohol consumption, low negotiating power, multiple casual partners, limited knowledge about HIV/AIDS) and outer context (e.g., poverty, workplace influences, sexual exploitation and harassment, stigma, limited support systems, and policy enforcement) factors that contribute to their vulnerability to HIV/AIDS. This exploration can aid not only in protecting young single mothers and their children from HIV/AIDS but also in safeguarding potential male partners they may meet, thereby diminishing the risk of new HIV cases within the community.

Methods

All methods employed in this qualitative multi-method study were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study design

This study employed a qualitative multi-method design to generate in-depth insights to inform the development of contextually relevant interventions. The research addressed both individual and structural factors associated with young single mothers’ engagement in risky sexual behaviors and sought to strengthen HIV/AIDS prevention efforts within ASEs in Rwanda and comparable settings.

Study setting

The study was conducted in selected informal ASEs in both urban and semi-urban regions of Musanze district, Kigali City, and neighbouring rising semi-urban places, including Kamonyi, and Gakenke districts. The study was conducted in these settings because they are key centres of informal economic activity in Rwanda and host a high concentration of informal ASEs, largely driven by high population density and wide-spread reliance on informal livelihoods. Furthermore, most ASEs in selected study settings combine alcohol selling and room services.

In Rwanda, there is no formal classification of ASEs. However, from the socio-economic backgrounds, ASEs can be classified as formal or informal based on staff remuneration, work environment, registration status, and organizational structure. Formal ASEs are primarily registered hotels with the Rwanda Development Board (RDB) employing more than 30 staff, offering contracts, departmentalized structures, and defined leadership roles. Informal ASEs, which are the focus of this study, include motels, bars and restaurants, pubs, guesthouses, lodges, and small boutiques selling alcohol alongside roasted foods such as meat, bananas, and potatoes as well as salads. Motels and bars and restaurants provide room services along with selling alcohol, potentially facilitating casual sexual encounters. Most of these establishments typically lack formal departments and may be singlehandedly managed by the owners, have informal management structures, and apply internal regulations (written or unwritten) that could be exploitative toward staff. Informal ASEs were selected because their work environments are less regulated, they have limited labour protections, and expose staff to greater social and occupational vulnerabilities, making them particularly relevant for this study.

A maximum variation purposive sampling method was applied to identify ASEs that met the specific inclusion criteria [25]. These criteria encompassed motels, guesthouses, lodges, bars, and pubs. During the selection process, various factors were considered, including the type of ASEs, their geographical locations (rural versus urban), and their visibility to potential clients (whether they were registered with the RDB, and had signage indicating their status as ASEs or not). Individuals identified through networking sampling provided profile information of ASEs that employed them. Based on these criteria, the research involved 32 establishments serving alcohol.

Study population

In this study, ‘young single mothers’ referred to unmarried AGYW who had given birth and were primarily responsible for their child’s care and financial support [26]. The study included young single mothers working in informal ASEs who held roles including cleaning and housekeeping, serving alcohol, accountancy, bar tending, entertainment, massage, and ensuring security at the venue. Other inclusion criteria included being aged between 18 and 35 years (legal age defining young age in Rwanda) at the time of data collection, to be willing to participate in the study. Young single mothers working in formal ASEs were not included in the study.

Sample and sampling approach

To determine the sample size, previous qualitative multi-method studies involving female bar workers, employing a content analytical approach to provide baseline data for future research were consulted. Earlier studies have reported samples of female bar workers ranging from 8 to 162 participants [13, 27]. The current study was not intended for generalizability but rather gathering diverse perspectives on the vulnerabilities that can potentially lead to HIV/AIDS transmission and acquisition as a result of working in ASEs. For this reason, the study recruited one hundred and four (n = 104) participants and 101 of them completed the study. Participants were sampled utilizing both snowball and maximum variation purposive sampling techniques to ensure variation of roles in the sampled population.

Recruitment process

Initially, business owners received an explanation of the study’s objectives, benefits, anticipated outcomes, and the voluntary nature of participation for their staff. For those ASEs that consented to take part, the study was presented to the employees during staff meetings. These business owners were approached first due to their familiarity with their employees’ profiles, and because the research was relevant to them as business owners. Business owners and supervisors provided access to the site only. Eligible participants decided privately whether to participate, and supervisors or owners were not informed of participation; refusal had no workplace consequences. During subsequent visits, the research team provided additional information to staff members who showed interest in the study. Participants who chose to take part in the study provided contacts of other colleagues they knew to be single mothers employed in ASEs.

Data collection process

Data collection was conducted from October 2024 to 15th February 2025. To gain a more thorough comprehension of the factors that may exacerbate the vulnerabilities to HIV/AIDS among young single mothers employed in ASEs, data were gathered from two sources: (i) Kinyarwanda YouTube channels, and (ii) semi-structured interviews with participants.

Using the search terms “ASEs,” “female staff,” and “HIV/AIDS risks or vulnerabilities” in Kinyarwanda, videos were searched on the YouTube platform. The following guiding question was used: ‘How does the published testimony of a female claiming to have ever worked in an alcohol serving place describe ASEs working conditions and how does this workplace heighten female staff vulnerabilities to HIV?” The retrieved videos were reviewed multiple times to determine whether they contained information answering this question. Five videos were retrieved (3 videos featuring an invitee, 2 of which were from TV news). These videos were transcribed in Kinyarwanda and later translated into English before proceeding to the in-person interview sessions.

The inclusion of YouTube testimonies as a supplementary data source was intended to capture publicly shared narratives that may reflect experiences not easily disclosed in face-to-face interviews, particularly given the sensitivity of the topic. However, this approach presents several methodological limitations. These include potential selection bias, as only individuals willing to publicly share their experiences are represented; uncertainty regarding authenticity, as the identity and background of speakers cannot be independently verified; and limited representativeness, as such testimonies may not reflect the broader population of women working in ASEs. To mitigate these concerns, YouTube data were used primarily for contextual triangulation rather than as standalone evidence. Only videos from identifiable Kinyarwanda-language channels with clear relevance to ASE work contexts were included. The content was critically appraised and compared against themes emerging from in-depth interviews.

A semi-structured interview guide was used to conduct in-person face-to-face interviews with selected participants. This guide included a combination of closed and open-ended questions that addressed sociodemographic information, their revenues, and experiences of working in alcohol serving places. It also comprised questions about risk factors that may increase their vulnerability to HIV/AIDS from their workplace and their status of being single mothers (see Supplementary Information 1). The development of this interview guide was informed by the previous research that has investigated the social and structural determinants influencing HIV/AIDS prevention in alcohol serving venues [13].

Two research assistants with backgrounds in nursing and mental health, both of whom had prior experience interviewing key populations, particularly sex workers, on sexual risk behaviors and sexual and reproductive health issues, including STIs conducted data collection. They received two days of intensive training delivered by the first author, covering recruitment approaches in ASEs, negotiating consent, use of the interview guide, handling participant distress, and techniques to probe for deeper insights. During data collection, the first author accompanied the research assistants to provide guidance in cases where participants had limited comprehension of certain questions, ensuring consistency and completeness of the data.

To ensure consistency across interviewers beyond initial training, the research team implemented several standardization procedures. These included regular field supervision by the first author, daily debriefing sessions to review emerging issues and refine probing techniques, and the use of a shared interview guide with agreed probing strategies. Periodic cross-checking of audio recordings and field notes was also conducted to ensure alignment in interviewing style and depth of inquiry.

Interviews were held with participants based on their convenience and availability given the demanding nature of working in ASEs. Participants were available during off-peak hours, such as mornings and early afternoons, or on their designated days off. Interviews were held in a confidential and private location within the workplace for participants present at their workplaces. For participants who preferred not to be interviewed at their workplace, interviews were held in a private area at a place they preferred to be convenient to them. Each interview lasted between 35 min and one hour. Both written notes and audio recordings were taken after getting participants’ consent.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, which included frequencies and percentages, were computed for participants’ background information including demographic data, financial data, and workplace-related data, such as available support systems, and determinants of young single mothers’ involvement in risky sexual behaviors. The analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 25 [28].

The audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim in both Kinyarwanda and English for the two interviews in which participants utilized English. Following this, the two research assistants who collected the data translated all transcripts from Kinyarwanda into English and the first author and the third author verified the quality of translated versions. The translated documents of the interviews and testimonies sourced from Kinyarwanda YouTube channels were subsequently verified by two additional individuals proficient in both Kinyarwanda and English. The theme identification process was inductive, meaning that themes were derived from the collected data, and thematic analysis was employed for the qualitative data analysis [29]. The transcribed data were thoroughly read and re-read to become acquainted with the collected information, facilitating an understanding of the data and the identification of initial significant sections. The subsequent phase involved a meticulous review of the data from 101 interviews and five YouTube video testimonies to pinpoint recurring patterns and terms, which were then designated as keywords. Each segment of data was assigned short phrases or words (codes) to encapsulate the core message regarding the vulnerabilities of young single mothers to HIV/AIDS. Theme development involved clustering related codes to identify patterns and relationships within the data. This process facilitated the generation of themes and sub-themes reflecting individual and contextual factors that heighten vulnerability to HIV/AIDS among young single mothers working in ASEs. Data analysis was supported by using Dedoose software [30–32].

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was sought from the University of Rwanda, College of Medicine and Health Sciences, Institutional Review Board (No 696/CMHS IRB/2024). Participants were informed about the study objectives, benefits of the study, and what is expected of them. Participation in the study was voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants who agreed to voluntarily participate in the study. Since this study was addressing a sensitive issue of HIV/AIDS, particularly focusing on single mothers employed in ASEs, the research team ensured that participants could engage in the study with a sense of comfort. To support participants in navigating any emotional distress that may arise, we incorporated experienced nursing professionals in the data collection process to provide mental and emotional support in case any participant experienced any distress. The research team assured participants’ autonomy regarding the disclosure of some of their experiences. Strict confidentiality regarding data management and sharing was assured to participants. Narratives regarding the work environment and financial struggles of single mothers might involve recounting challenging life events that could provoke negative emotions. To address this potential issue, the research team paused interviews when such situations arose to provide participants with the necessary time to recuperate.

Participants had complete autonomy to decide whether to engage in the study, free from any pressure from the research team or external parties, and they could withdraw from the study at any time without facing any consequences whatsoever. Participants who chose to participate in the study received a token of appreciation of 10,000 RWF (approximately 7 USD) to compensate for their travel and the time spent with the researchers. Nonetheless, this incentive did not affect participants’ rights to withhold sensitive information or to withdraw from the study if they so desired.

Results

The research included N = 101 young single mothers employed in various informal ASEs, comprising 39 (38.6%) from motels, 37 (36.6%) from pubs, 22 (21.8%) from bars and restaurants, and 3 (3%) from lodges or guest houses.

Participants’ background information

As shown in Table 1, participants undertook diverse roles, with the majority (60.4%, n = 61) working as waitresses. Many participants (67.3%, n = 68) were aged between 26 and 30 during the data collection period. A significant proportion of the participants (n = 99, 96%) identified as Christians, while 72.3% (n = 73) had completed at least secondary education. Approximately half of the participants (n = 51, 50.5%) reported having one parent or being parentless, and three-quarters of the participants (n = 72, 71.3%) lived in a rented residence. Slightly more than half of the participants (n = 52) reported that they did not finish the educational level they were pursuing, particularly those in secondary education, citing various reasons such as financial constraints, pregnancy, and parental separation. Additionally, twenty-two participants indicated that they had cohabited with a male partner for a minimum of six months. Approximately one-third of the participants (n = 38) became pregnant at the age of 18 or younger. Many of these participants reported that they did not receive any assistance from the fathers of their children. More than half of the participants (n = 54) had children under the age of five, 48.5% (n = 49) of stayed with their children and had organized for additional support in caregiving.

Sexual decision making and behaviors

Self-reported sexual behaviors indicated that nearly half of participants (47.5%, n = 48) engaged in penetrative vaginal intercourse during the week preceding the study. Among these, condom use was inconsistent, and some participants reported no protective measures. As presented in Table 2, 27 participants reported sexual encounters with one partner, seven with two partners, six with three partners, four with four partners, three with five men, and one participant having had sexual relations with eight individuals. Among these 48 participants, 17 indicated that they did not employ any protective measures with the men they had sexual relations, while the remaining 31 exhibited instances of inconsistent condom use, particularly among those who reported having more than three sexual partners. Furthermore, 64.4% (n = 65) of participants had sexual relations with more than two male partners during the past year, with 30.7% (n = 32) revealing that they had engaged with four or more male sexual partners. In terms of the nature of the relationships with their sexual partners, 36.6% (n = 37) described their male partners as casual friends, 21.8% (n = 22) identified them as regular friends, 25.7% (n = 26) stated that their partners were boyfriends, and 15.9% (n = 17) reported that their partners were both casual and regular friend categories. In addition, 39.6% (n = 40) of participants reported that they had ever engaged in sexual activities in exchange for material gains. Among the 101 participants, 72.3% reported that they were taking alcoholic beverages, with 34 of them reporting a frequency of drinking between three to seven days per week.

Themes that emerged from the analysis

Six themes regarding the vulnerabilities of young single mothers to HIV/AIDS emerged from the analysis.

Theme 1: exposure to increased forms of exploitation

Women working in informal ASEs are at an increased risk of exploitation, primarily from some employers and significantly from the inappropriate conduct of male patrons. The types of exploitation identified include sexual exploitation, assault, and coercion.

Subtheme 1: sexual exploitation

A large proportion of participants indicated that female employees in ASEs often experience sexual exploitation at the hands of male patrons, a situation that may be exacerbated by the actions of business owners and senior staff. They alluded that some female staff, might find themselves in sexual encounters without their full consent because they fear being dispelled from their employment.

“As I am tasked with renting rooms to clients, it is another risk for me and sometimes the boss pushes me to have sex with clients.” Participant B&R 16, Kigali.

“The boss could order me to sleep with a certain client because they are important to him. Yes, for example, when I worked as a manager in a bar with lodges, the boss would tell me after my shift to take care of a client.” Participant PUB02, Kigali.

Some participants reported that few of the business owners paid a commission fee to female staff who brought in more male customers to sleep in the rooms at the workplaces; something that might heighten these female staff’s risk to HIV/AIDS.

“Yes, depending on the client. If the client gives me 5,000 RWF my boss takes 2,000 RWF and I keep 3,000 RWF.” Participant B&R 21, Kigali.

Subtheme 2: sexual assault and harassment

A significant number of participants indicated that they had encountered various forms of sexual assault and harassment. These experiences encompassed sexually suggestive conversations, forced kissing, non-consensual touch of their body parts such as breasts, buttocks, and intimate areas. A large proportion of participants reported feeling discontent with the flirtatious actions of certain male patrons. They emphasized that the aggressive and inappropriate conduct of male customers coerces female employees into passively enduring sexual assaults, as they aim to prevent being reported to their supervisors for allegedly delivering poor service. Furthermore, they reported that they felt obligated to yield to inappropriate sexual advances from male clients as a means to encourage their return to the workplace, thus ensuring their job security and promoting increased financial benefits for their employers.

“Sometimes they try to touch us, and if we refuse, they report us to the boss, claiming we are being disrespectful.” Participant M04, Musanze.

“You interact with many different men, and you must do everything possible to keep the client satisfied. Some may even touch you inappropriately, but you try not to react angrily.” Participant B&R12, Kigali.

In ASEs that offered alcohol service along with room service, few participants reported that they encountered not only instances of inappropriate touching but also attempted rape during room cleaning or when some male patrons requested drinks and food to be delivered to their rooms.

“A man could rush in, overpower you, and drag you to a room to have sex with you.” Participant B&R40, Kigali.

“Sometimes when you work in rooms, someone might suddenly grab you, and at that moment they may not use protection.” Participant M 01–28, Kigali.

Subtheme 3: sexual coercion

Participants consistently described that female staff employed in ASEs encounter certain male patrons who, with the intent of winning their affection, demonstrate unusual kindness through unsolicited gestures such as providing money, food, and drinks. As these male customers continue to exhibit such generosity, it may lead some female employees to form connections with them, ultimately resulting in sexual encounters as a means of reciprocating the favors received. Furthermore, due to the rapport established, some participants revealed a level of trust that led them to forgo protective measures against HIV/AIDS.

“Sometimes, someone will leave a tip, then come back and buy you fish, or leave you 10,000. They slowly draw you in, and you might start thinking, ‘This man likes me and has money.’ At that point, when he requests you have sex, you understand it quickly even when he cannot use protection.” Participant B&R38, Kigali.

Theme 3: transactional sex facilitated by themselves or often by co-workers

A subset of participants disclosed that, for some female workers in ASEs, the workplace serves as a networking environment, allowing them to meet potential casual sexual partners in exchange for money during their off-duty hours particularly in venues where alcohol was sold alongside room service.

“You understand… sometimes, someone might offer 200,000 RWF for 30 minutes, which is an amount I wouldn’t even make in a month.” Participant B&R 29, Kigali.

Theme 4: alcohol exposure

Many participants suggested that female employees in ASEs faced significant risks when consuming alcohol at work. They noted that excessive drinking could lead to unanticipated sexual encounters, and in such circumstances, they may lack the ability to negotiate for safe sex. They also reported that some male patrons might intentionally intoxicate them to facilitate unprotected sexual activities, particularly when financial incentives are involved.

“Many girls love money, so when clients lure them with cash or alcohol, they get drunk and forget to use condoms, increasing the risk of infection.” Participant PUB 05, Kigali.

“It could be because I drink alcohol and get deceived, or because I accept money and then they force me to do whatever they want.” Participant PUB30, Kigali.

Theme 5: ongoing poverty and financial hardships

Subtheme 1: Limited access to basic needs for themselves, their children, and families

All participants revealed that they cannot consciously choose to engage in risky sexual practices. However, they reiterated that financial difficulties and ongoing poverty they face increase their vulnerability to such behaviors. Specifically, the lack of financial resources to meet essential living expenses such as childcare, rent, food, clothing, healthcare, and support for extended family can significantly impact their decisions regarding their protection against sexual risk factors associated with HIV/AIDS. They alluded that in such challenging financial circumstances, the approach of a male client with a proposition for unprotected sexual relations becomes difficult to resist.

“I can engage in behaviours that increase my risk of contracting HIV, especially when trying to provide for my child—for example, paying school fees and other necessities.” Participant B&R 04, Kigali.

“You might do it because you lack basic things like soap or lotion, and you do it to escape the stress. Once someone pays, you can’t dictate how they act.” Participant M02, Musanze.

Many participants emphasized that the challenges of single motherhood worsened by mental health struggles may lead some young single mothers in ASEs to engage in high-risk sexual behaviors to meet their basic needs.

“For example, a single mother has a lot of responsibilities and may also be dealing with depression. If she meets someone who claims to love her and offers her money, she might not even think about whether that person is healthy or not.” Participant M07, Kigali.

Subtheme 2: inadequate wages, coupled with delays in disbursement and deductions

The majority of participants expressed the view that job-related stressors faced by women employed in ASEs, such as low wages, unexpected deductions, and salary delays may significantly contribute to their engagement in risky sexual behaviors. They underlined that their engagement is often a strategy to compensate for their insufficient salaries.

“Yes, one can do sex with a customer for example, if I am deducted 50,000 RWF and only take home 20,000 RWF out of my 70,000 RWF salary, I may accept an offer for money since it’s quick.” Participant M029, Kigali.

“Sometimes, when payday hasn’t arrived yet, but rent is due, and someone with money comes along, you might think, “Let me agree so I can get that money.” Participant PUB44, Kigali.

Theme 6: societal stigma towards women employed in establishments that serve alcohol

All participants opined that societal perceptions outside their workplace perpetuate stigma against women employed in ASEs. The stereotype that associates female employees in ASEs with prostitution leads some male patrons to frequent these establishments with the expectation of easily finding a female worker for sex.

“For example, when people come from home or clients we work with in this field, many people think we’re involved in sex work. So, some of them, when they come, expect to use the money they brought for what they want. If they find you weak or with other problems at home, they might end up convincing you to have unprotected sex without preparation due to the nature of our job.” Participant M06, Musanze.

A subset of participants suggested that the negative connotations of working in ASEs might drive female staff to engage in high-risk sexual activities to purchase items that are comparable to those of their peers in other industries. Consequently, they conveyed that maintaining safety against HIV poses significant difficulties.

“Because society views this job as low-status, we often wish for a better lifestyle. For example, a girl working in an office might have a nice handbag, and the only way to get such things is by sleeping with someone who can provide them. So, all these things make it difficult to stay safe from HIV.” Participant B&R 38, Kigali.

Discussion

Our study indicated that all young single mothers participating in the study had engaged in sexual intercourse within the previous year, with the majority having sexual encounters with several casual male partners and often failing to employ any preventive measures against HIV/AIDS. The observation that female staff reported having multiple sexual partners can be attributed to their tendency to frequently move from one alcohol-serving establishment to another for greener pastures; which increases their interactions with numerous male patrons. This finding aligns with results from two comparable studies conducted in Tanzania, where female bar workers indicated a lack of stable sexual partners due to the nature of employment in ASEs [19].

One surprising finding from the study was that nearly half of the participants had sexual intercourse in the week preceding the study and 17 out of 101 had not used any HIV/AIDS preventive measures. This situation is concerning regarding HIV/AIDS prevention efforts. This suggests a potential need for collaborative initiatives by the Rwanda Biomedical Center and other stakeholders to intensify health education outreaches on the use of preventive measures in alcohol serving establishments.

Our study suggests that female staff in informal ASEs continue to face sexual exploitation and are not adequately empowered to resist that. The inadequate support systems, especially in informal ASEs, combined with the male-dominated clientele in most of these establishments, exacerbates their vulnerability to HIV/AIDS. This finding corroborates findings from Uganda, Nepal and Ghana, where studies have highlighted the unaddressed sexual exploitation of female staff in bars that serve alcohol and provide room services, establishing unwritten norms that require female workers to cater to both alcohol and sexual demands of male clients [27, 33–35].

Sexual assault and harassment were frequently described by participants across the sampled informal ASEs. The majority of female employees have adapted to endure sexual assault in order to protect their jobs and the business interests of their employers. Furthermore, these instances of sexual assault can be exacerbated by provocative dress codes in some ASEs that facilitate undisciplined male customers in easily invading the personal space of female staff. Our results are consistent with findings from other comparable studies conducted in Nepal, Uganda, Ghana, and Ethiopia, which have documented similar occurrences of sexual assaults [17, 27, 33, 35]. This situation arises because informal ASEs have no adequate protections such as clear policies against harassment, and empowering women against gender dynamics [17, 27, 33, 35–37].

Our study suggests that young single mothers working in informal ASEs frequently experience sexual coercion from certain male partners who provide them with affection and financial support. As a result, these young mothers may engage in sexual activities with these men, sometimes without sufficient protection against HIV/AIDS. The fact that young single mothers are vulnerable to sexual coercion arises from their need for both emotional comfort and financial support. While our study offers a comprehensive overview of the instances of sexual coercion, it does not quantify the extent of the issue. This finding contrasts with a prior study conducted in Ethiopia that reported a prevalence of sexual coercion at 61.7% over the past year in a smaller scale survey [38].

Interestingly, our study suggests that some young single mothers in informal ASEs intentionally establish networks for transactional sex with casual male patrons. In the context of Rwanda, this is a new finding. While this arrangement is not labelled prostitution, it presents a potential risk for HIV/AIDS transmission, as it inherently involves negotiating costs with male partners. As a result, the need to negotiate safe sex may hinge on the emotional state of the male partner as well as the amount of money paid, which can sometimes be impaired by alcohol consumption, leading to inconsistent use of HIV/AIDS protection during these encounters as suggested by studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa [18, 19, 27, 39–41]. While our study suggests that women employed in informal ASEs may participate in risky casual sexual, it is important to approach our findings with caution. This is due to the predominant qualitative nature of our study, which involved a limited sample size that may not accurately represent all women working in such environments.

Alcohol exposure emerged as another factor that participants associated with increased vulnerability. Some described how alcohol consumption whether voluntary or encouraged by clients could reduce their ability to negotiate safer sex practices. This finding corroborates that of previous research conducted in Mexico, the Philippines, Tanzania, Cameroon, and Cambodia, which found that female employees in ASEs reported instances of unprotected sex and ineffective use of condoms while under the influence of alcohol [18, 20, 21, 42–44]. However, the present study did not directly measure levels of intoxication or causal pathways, and these interpretations should therefore be understood as participant-reported associations.

Our study found that young single mothers, although employed in ASEs, which implies they are earning an income, do not receive a salary that aligns with the realities of fulfilling basic needs such as housing, food, clothing, healthcare, and childcare obligations. Our findings suggest that many participants experienced financial hardship, with reported earnings often insufficient to meet basic needs. This aligns with previous studies from Sub-Saharan Africa that have indicated a correlation between elevated poverty levels among single mothers and their socio-economic conditions, including low educational attainment, employment status, and limited financial resources [27, 45]. As a result of their ongoing financial instability, some female staff may often resort to casual transactional sex activities as a last resort [46]. Their choices highlight their status as a vulnerable group that faces an increased risk of acquiring or transmitting HIV/AIDS while seeking financial resources to meet their essential needs. While the findings suggest that economic vulnerability may play an important role, the study design does not allow for causal conclusions regarding poverty and HIV risk.

Our study reveals that inadequate wages, coupled with payment delays and deductions, can greatly exacerbate the vulnerabilities experienced by young single mothers. This situation may lead them to partake in risky sexual activities when presented with enticing financial incentives from men seeking their sexual companionship. Considering that these sexual encounters might occur without the use of condoms or prior HIV/AIDS testing, the risk for HIV/AIDS acquisition and/ or transmission may be high. Notably, female staff may possess a heightened awareness of the risks associated with HIV, yet they often lack the agency to negotiate consistent condom use. Previous studies that examined the vulnerabilities to HIV/AIDS among female staff in informal ASEs across low- and middle-income countries have underscored findings similar to those of our study [13, 33, 35].

In spite of the promotion of gender inclusive policies in Rwanda, our study suggests that society continues to stigmatize women employed in ASEs, branding them as prostitutes. This stigma frequently stems from the dress codes imposed by business owners, such as the requirement to wear miniskirts and to maintain a cheerful demeanor towards customers as part of customer service. The interaction of gender dynamics and economic status reinforces the notion among male clients that their financial power grants them the right to engage in sexual activities, including unprotected sex with any female staff in these ASEs. Our findings corroborate those of prior studies conducted in Rwanda, Uganda, and Eswatini which revealed that being a woman working in an alcohol-serving establishment carries a negative reputation, leading male patrons to abuse and sexually exploit the women employed there [37, 41, 46]. Furthermore, our study supports the conclusions of a prior systematic literature review which established that female staff in Sub-Saharan Africa face social stigmatization as a consequence of their employment in ASEs [13].

Study limitations

This study is the first to identify vulnerabilities and risk factors associated with HIV/AIDS among young single mothers working in informal ASEs in Rwanda. In contrast to earlier research that primarily focused on female bar workers, our study encompassed a varied sample that included waitresses, female bartenders, cleaners, accountants, and receptionists from various informal ASEs. A notable strength of this research lies in its examination of the vulnerabilities faced by these female employees, considering both external and internal contextual factors, and utilizing a range of data sources.

While these findings provide important insights, they are based on a qualitative sample drawn from specific informal ASE contexts and should not be interpreted as representative of all young single mothers or all ASE workers in Rwanda. The patterns described reflect participants’ reported experiences and perceptions, which are shaped by their specific socio-economic and workplace environments.

Moreover, participants were not tested for their HIV/AIDS status, which would have offered insight into the prevalence of HIV/AIDS among female staff in ASEs. Because findings from this study were based on self-reported accounts, participants’ narratives may have been influenced by social desirability, stigma, or concerns about confidentiality, leading to selective disclosure or possible underreporting or overreporting. These methodological considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the transferability and meaning of the findings. Lastly, the study is limited by the exclusion of business owners, which prevented the verification of certain vulnerabilities and risk factors reported by the female employees.

Conclusion

This study identified contextual factors that may explain HIV-related vulnerabilities among young single mothers working in informal ASEs in Rwanda. The external factors included poverty, limited empowerment, inadequate institutional efforts (both governmental and organizational) to safeguard women in ASEs from various forms of sexual exploitation, ASEs’ policing, the existence of rooms within the venues, societal stigma directed at women employed in ASEs, and gender dynamics that continue to objectify women. The individual factors included their limited capacity to negotiate safe sex, deliberate transactional sex (for certain female employees), inconsistent condom usage, and reliance on male patrons who provide tips and other gifts.

Given the qualitative and context-specific nature of these findings, caution is warranted in extending them beyond the study population. Nonetheless, the results point to the potential importance of multi-level interventions that address both structural and individual factors. We propose that interventions be developed to support young single mothers employed in ASEs in coping with the stressors related to their work and the difficulties of single parenthood. Governmental entities such as the Ministry of Family and Gender Promotion, the Ministry of Local Government, along with civil society and Non-Governmental Organizations should strengthen gender protective policies designed to protect women working in informal ASEs. To address potential HIV vulnerability associated with informal ASE contexts, organizations like the Rwanda Biomedical Center should intensify their prevention strategies in informal ASEs and reinforce policies related to HIV/AIDS prevention.
