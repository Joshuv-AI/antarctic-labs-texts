2. The Number-Theoretic Connection

This question can be regarded as a number theoretic one. Note that for
1\leq k\leq n
, the modulus of the complex number
k+i
is
\sqrt{k^{2}+1}
, and every
z\in\mathbb{C}
can be expressed as
z=a+bi
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. If

(5)

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}(k+i)

is purely imaginary, then
z=bi
(i.e.
a=0
), and so
|z|=b
. Consider the product

(6)

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\sqrt{k^{2}+1}}

which is the product of the moduli of the complex numbers
k+i
for all
1\leq k\leq n
. Hence, the problem is equivalent to finding all pairs of natural numbers
\left(b,n\right)
such that

(7)

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}(k^{2}+1)=b^{2}.

We wish to find all solutions to the Diophantine equation in (7), and we assert that
(b,n)=(10,3)
is the only solution. This would conclude that the product in (5) is purely imaginary if and only if
n=3
.

In this paper, we will discuss and expand on a proof given by Cilleruelo. It aims to make his proof more accessible by explaining the parts that were deemed trivial. Moreover, as shown in (79), we will obtain a better bound for our problem, reducing the time taken to seek all positive integer solutions to the Diophantine equation.

We first discuss the following problem from the 17th China Western Mathematical Invitational Competition in 2017. It is from Problem 1 of Day 1 of the competition

Theorem 1
.

Let
p
be a prime and
n
be a positive integer such that
p^{2}
divides

(8)

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}(k^{2}+1).

Then,
p<2n
.

Proof.

Define
P_{n}
to be the above product. Since
p^{2}\mid P_{n}
, then
p\mid P_{n}
. One case to consider is
p^{2}\mid(k^{2}+1)
for some
1\leq k\leq n
. Then,
p^{2}<n^{2}+1
and hence,
p<\sqrt{n^{2}+1}<2n
. Suppose otherwise, then there exists
1\leq a<b\leq n
such that
p\mid(a^{2}+1)
and
p\mid(b^{2}+1)
, and so, taking the difference,
p\mid(b+a)(b-a)
. As
a+b<2n
and
b-a>0
,
b^{2}-a^{2}<2n
. Thus,
p
is a divisor of some number less than
2n
and the result follows.

\Box

Theorem 2
.

For
n>3
,

(9)

\displaystyle P_{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(k^{2}+1)\quad\text{is non-square}.

Suppose on the contrary that for

is square. Then, we can write it as a product of some primes

(10)

\displaystyle P_{n}=\prod_{p<2n}p^{\alpha_{p}},\text{ where }\alpha_{p}\in\mathbb{N}

Note that

(11)

\displaystyle P_{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}(k^{2}+1)>\prod_{k=1}^{n}{k^{2}}=(n!)^{2}.

Consider writing

as

\displaystyle n!=\prod_{p\leq n}p^{\beta_{p}}

since

is a product of all the natural numbers from 1 to itself inclusive, and there are some numbers which are composite and hence, can be written as the product of primes. This expression is apt when we compare it with the expression for

in terms of

claimed in (10).

We have the following as we make use of the property that the logarithm of a product is the sum of that logarithm:

(13)

\displaystyle{{\left(\prod\limits_{p\leq n}{{{p}^{{{\beta}_{p}}}}}\right)}^{2}}

\displaystyle<\prod\limits_{p<2n}{{{p}^{{{\alpha}_{p}}}}}

(14)

\displaystyle 2\log\left(\prod\limits_{p\leq n}{{{p}^{{{\beta}_{p}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle<\log\left(\prod\limits_{p<2n}{{{p}^{{{\alpha}_{p}}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{p\leq n}{{{\beta}_{p}}\log p}

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{p<2n}{{{\alpha}_{p}}\log p}

Corollary 3
.

For

,
\alpha_{2}=\lceil{n/2}\rceil
.

Proof.

Since
k^{2}\equiv 0\text{ or }1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)
, then
k^{2}+1\equiv 1\text{ or }2\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)
. The initial result can be easily derived by considering the cases where

denotes the number of times

If

.

\Box

Before we state and prove an important lemma (Lemma
6
), we define the Legendre symbol and state a major theorem known as the law of quadratic reciprocity.

Definition 4

(Legendre symbol)
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. An integer

is a quadratic residue modulo

if it is congruent to a perfect square modulo

and is a quadratic non-residue modulo

otherwise. The Legendre symbol is a function of

defined as

\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{*{35}{l}}1&\text{if }a\text{ is a quadratic residue modulo }p\text{ and }a\not\equiv 0\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p\right),\\
-1&\text{if }a\text{ is a non-quadratic residue modulo }p,\\
0&\text{if }a\equiv 0\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p\right).\\
\end{array}\right.

Theorem 5

(law of quadratic reciprocity)
.

For distinct odd primes
p
and
q
,

(16)

\displaystyle\left({\frac{p}{q}}\right)\left({\frac{q}{p}}\right)=(-1)^{{\frac{p-1}{2}}\cdot{\frac{q-1}{2}}}.

Lemma 6
.

Let
p
be an odd prime. If
p\text{ }|\text{ }(k^{2}+1)
for
1\leq k\leq n
, then
p\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)
.

Proof.

As
k^{2}\equiv-1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}p)
,
-1
is a quadratic residue modulo

. Here, we set

and so

\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)=1.

By the law of quadratic reciprocity (Theorem

),
p\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)
.

\Box

Lemma 7
.

For any prime
p
,
x^{2}\equiv 0\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}p)
has the unique solution
x\equiv 0\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}p)
.

Proof.

The only zero divisor in the ring
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is 0. As such, if a product is 0, one of the factors must be 0 as well, and the result follows.

\Box

Theorem 8
.

For any prime
p
, each interval of length
p^{j}
contains two solutions to the congruence
x^{2}\equiv 1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j}\right)

[
4
]
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction. First, we solve the base case
x^{2}\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}p)
. Then, our induction hypothesis would be for
j>1
, given a solution to
x^{2}\equiv 1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j}\right)
, we find a solution to
x^{2}\equiv 1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j+1}\right)
. The existence of the two and only two solutions, say
x_{1}
and
-x_{1}
, is a consequence of the law of quadratic reciprocity. For this to occur, we must have

(17)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)=1.

The base case is an immediate consequence of the law of quadratic reciprocity too.

Assume that there exists a solution
x_{0}
such that
x_{0}^{2}\equiv 1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j}\right)
. We wish to find a lift of
x_{0}\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j}\right)
to
x_{1}\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j+1}\right)
that satisfies
x_{1}^{2}\equiv 1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j+1}\right)
. To ensure that
x_{1}\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j+1}\right)
is indeed a lift of
x_{0}\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j}\right)
, consider

(18)

\displaystyle x_{1}=x_{0}+p^{j}y_{0}.

Squaring both sides,

(19)

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=x_{0}^{2}+2p^{j}x_{0}y_{0}+p^{j+1}y_{0}

(20)

\displaystyle\equiv x_{0}^{2}+2p^{j}x_{0}y_{0}\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j+1}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle x_{0}^{2}+2p^{j}x_{0}y_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv 1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}p^{j+1}\right)

Since
x_{0}^{2}-1
is a multiple of
p^{j}
, setting
x_{0}^{2}-1=\lambda p^{j}
, (21) implies
\lambda p^{j}+2p^{j}x_{0}y_{0}
is a multiple of
p^{j+1}
. Since
\operatorname{gcd}(2x_{0},p)=1
, then
\operatorname{gcd}(2p^{j}x_{0},p^{j+1})=p^{j}
and hence,
\lambda+2x_{0}y_{0}
is a multiple of
p
. As such, there exists a unique solution to

(22)

\displaystyle 2x_{0}y_{0}\equiv\frac{1-x_{0}^{2}}{p^{j}}\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}p)

and so

(23)

\displaystyle y_{0}\equiv\frac{1-x_{0}^{2}}{2p^{j}x_{0}}\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}p).

The existence of the two solutions follows by the law of quadratic reciprocity.

\Box

Lemma 9
.

If

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{{\alpha}_{p}}-{{\beta}_{p}}\leq\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left(\left\lceil\frac{n}{{{p}^{j}}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor\frac{n}{{{p}^{j}}}\right\rfloor\right)}+\sum\limits_{\log n/\log p<j\leq\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)/p}{\left\lceil\frac{n}{{{p}^{j}}}\right\rceil}.

Let

\displaystyle S_{1}=\left\{1\leq k\leq n,\text{ }p\text{ prime}:p^{j}\mid(k^{2}+1)\right\}\text{ and }S_{2}=\left\{1\leq k\leq n,\text{ }p\text{ prime}:p^{j}\mid k\right\}

Then, using Theorem

, we can find an upper bound for

and an equation for
\beta_{p}
. That is,

(26)

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}=\sum_{j\leq\log(n^{2}+1)/\log p}|S_{1}|\leq\sum_{j\leq\log(n^{2}+1)/\log p}2\lceil{n/p^{j}}\rceil

and

\displaystyle\beta_{p}=\sum_{j\leq\log n/\log p}|S_{2}|=\sum_{j\leq\log n/\log p}\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rfloor.

Hence,

(28)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{{\alpha}_{p}}-{{\beta}_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{j\leq\log({{n}^{2}}+1)/\log p}{\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil}-\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rfloor}

(29)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil}+\sum\limits_{\log n/\log p\leq j\leq\log({{n}^{2}}+1)/\log p}{\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil}-\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rfloor}

(30)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left(\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rfloor\right)}+\sum\limits_{\log n/\log p\leq j\leq\log({{n}^{2}}+1)/\log p}{\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil}

\Box

We observe a nice property related to the floor and ceiling function (Lemma
10
) before stating an important theorem (Theorem
11
).

Lemma 10
.

Let
C=\lceil{x}\rceil-\lfloor{x}\rfloor
. If
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
C=0
, otherwise,
C=1
.

Proof.

If
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\lceil{x}\rceil=\lfloor{x}\rfloor=x
, and so
C=0
. Suppose
x\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\mathbb{Z}
. By definition,
m<\lceil{x}\rceil\leq m+1
and
m\leq\lfloor{x}\rceil<m+1
, where
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence,
-m-1<-\lceil{x}\rceil\leq-m
, and so
-1<C\leq 1
. Since
C
is an integer,
0\leq C\leq 1
. However,
\lfloor{x}\rfloor\neq\lceil{x}\rceil
, which asserts that
C=1
.

\Box

Theorem 11
.

If
p\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)
, then

(31)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{{\alpha}_{p}}-{{\beta}_{p}}\leq\frac{\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)}{\log p}.

Proof.

The proof hinges on Lemma
9
and Lemma
10
. Note that
\lceil{n/p^{j}}\rceil-\lfloor{n/p^{j}}\rfloor\leq 1\text{ }
for all
j\leq\log n/\log p
. Hence,

(32)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left(\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rfloor\right)}\leq\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}.

It is also clear that for all
\log n/\log p\leq j\leq\log(n^{2}+1)/\log p
,
\lceil{n/p^{j}}\rceil\leq 1
. As such,

(33)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\log n/\log p\leq j\leq\log({{n}^{2}}+1)/\log p}{\left\lceil\frac{n}{p^{j}}\right\rceil}\leq\sum\limits_{\log n/\log p\leq j\leq\log({{n}^{2}}+1)/\log p}.

Combining both inequalities, we have

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{{\alpha}_{p}}-{{\beta}_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}+\sum\limits_{\log n/\log p\leq j\leq\log({{n}^{2}}+1)/\log p}

(35)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j\leq\log(n^{2}+1)/\log p}

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)}{\log p}

and we are done.

\Box

3. Some Results in Analytic Number Theory

We shall make use of two well-known ideas, which are namely the prime-counting function and Bertrand’s postulate (Theorem

Definition 12

(prime counting function)
.

Let
\pi(x)

Theorem 13

(prime number theorem)
.

A well-known asymptotic relation for

is as follows: for large

(37)

\displaystyle\pi(n)\sim\frac{n}{\log n}

Theorem 14

(Bertrand’s postulate)

For every

We make use of the prime-counting function and Theorem

Lemma 17
.

Proof.

We established the inequality

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\leq\sum_{j\leq\operatorname{log}(n^{2}+1)/\operatorname{log}p}2\left\lceil{\frac{n}{p^{j}}}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\operatorname{log}(n^{2}+1)<2\operatorname{log}p,

\displaystyle p^{2}-n^{2}

\displaystyle>1

\displaystyle(p+n)(p-n)

\displaystyle>1

Lemma 18
.

If

, then

(44)

\displaystyle\beta_{p}\geq\frac{n-p}{p-1}-\frac{\operatorname{log}n}{\operatorname{log}p}\geq\frac{n-1}{p-1}-\frac{\operatorname{log}(n^{2}+1)}{\operatorname{log}p}.

Proof.

Recall that (27) yields the equality

(45)

\displaystyle\beta_{p}=\sum_{j\leq\operatorname{log}n/\operatorname{log}p}\left\lfloor n/p^{j}\right\rfloor.

It is worth noting that

(46)

\displaystyle\frac{n-p}{p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{p-1}-\frac{p}{p-1}.

Hence,

(47)

\displaystyle{{\beta}_{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\left\lfloor n/{{p}^{j}}\right\rfloor}

(48)

\displaystyle\geq\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\frac{n}{{{p}^{j}}}}-\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{{}}

(49)

\displaystyle=n\sum\limits_{j\leq\log n/\log p}{\frac{1}{{{p}^{j}}}}-\frac{\log n}{\log p}

(50)

\displaystyle=n\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\log n/\log p\right\rfloor}{{{p}^{-j}}}-\frac{\log n}{\log p}

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{p-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{{{p}^{\left\lfloor\log n/\log p\right\rfloor}}}\right)-\frac{\log n}{\log p}

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{p-1}-\frac{n}{{{p}^{\left\lfloor\log n/\log p\right\rfloor}}\left(p-1\right)}-\frac{\log n}{\log p}

What is left to show is

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{n}{{{p}^{\left\lfloor\log n/\log p\right\rfloor}}\left(p-1\right)}\leq\frac{p}{p-1}.

In other words,

\displaystyle{{p}^{\left\lfloor\log n/\log p\right\rfloor+1}}\geq n,

which is clear because

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{\operatorname{log}n}{\operatorname{log}p}\right\rfloor+1\geq\frac{\operatorname{log}n}{\operatorname{log}p}

Now, we show that

\displaystyle\frac{n-p}{p-1}-\frac{\operatorname{log}n}{\operatorname{log}p}\geq\frac{n-1}{p-1}-\frac{\operatorname{log}(n^{2}+1)}{\operatorname{log}p}.

Working backwards,

\displaystyle\frac{\log({{n}^{2}}+1)}{\log p}

\displaystyle\geq 1+\frac{\log n}{\log p}

(59)

\displaystyle\log({{n}^{2}}+1)

\displaystyle\geq\log pn

(60)

\displaystyle{{n}^{2}}-pn+1

\displaystyle\geq 1

(61)

\displaystyle n\left(n-p\right)

\displaystyle\geq 0

For the quadratic inequality

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{2}\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\log 2+\log({{n}^{2}}+1)\pi(n,1,4)+\sum\limits_{n<p<2n}{\log p}.

As such, a strict upper bound for

(64)

\displaystyle\left(n-1\right)\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}p\leq n\\
p\not\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)\end{smallmatrix}}\frac{\log p}{p-1}-{\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}p\leq n\\
p\not\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)\end{smallmatrix}}}\log({{n}^{2}}+1)

is

(65)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\log 2+\log({{n}^{2}}+1)\pi(n,1,4)+\sum\limits_{n<p<2n}{\log p}

and (39) evaluates to

(66)

\displaystyle\left(n+1\right)\frac{\log 2}{4}+\log({{n}^{2}}+1)\pi(n)+\sum\limits_{n<p<2n}{\log p},

yielding the desired result.

\Box

To wrap up the proof, we exploit Chebyshev’s functions. The first and second Chebyshev Functions are denoted by
\vartheta(n)
and
\psi(n)
respectively.

Definition 20

(von Mangoldt function)
.

For every integer
n\geq 1
, define

\Lambda\left(n\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{*{35}{l}}\log p&\text{if }n={{p}^{m}}\text{ for some prime }p\text{ and some }m\geq 1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\text{.}\\
\end{array}\right.

Definition 21

(first Chebyshev function)
.

For any real number
n\geq 1
,

(67)

\displaystyle\vartheta(n)=\sum_{p\leq n}\operatorname{log}p.

Definition 22

(second Chebyshev function)
.

For any real number
n\geq 1
,

(68)

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)=\sum_{p^{m}\leq x}\operatorname{log}p.

Lemma 23
.

For all
n\geq 1
,
\vartheta(n)\leq\psi(n)

[
6
]
.

Proof.

It is clear by the definitions of
\vartheta(n)
and
\psi(n)
that

(69)

\displaystyle\psi(n)=\vartheta(n)+\vartheta\left(\sqrt{n}\right)+\vartheta\left(\sqrt[3]{n}\right)+\ldots

so the result follows.

\Box

Corollary 24
.

[
5
]

If
p
is prime, then

(70)

\displaystyle\vartheta(n)\leq\pi(n)\operatorname{log}n.

Proof.

Using Lemma
23
, as
\vartheta(n)\leq\psi(n)
, by definition of
\psi(n)
, we have

(71)

\displaystyle\psi(n)=\sum_{p\leq n}\left\lfloor\frac{\operatorname{log}n}{\operatorname{log}p}\right\rfloor\cdot\operatorname{log}p\leq\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{\operatorname{log}n}{\operatorname{log}p}\cdot\operatorname{log}p=\sum_{p\leq n}1\cdot\operatorname{log}n=\pi(n)\operatorname{log}n

This shows that
\vartheta(n)
and
\pi(n)
are closely related.

\Box

Theorem 25
.

If
p
is prime, then

(72)

\displaystyle\sum_{n<p<2n}\operatorname{log}p\leq\vartheta(2n-1).

Proof.

The sum of
\operatorname{log}p
over the interval
n<p<2n
is
\vartheta(2n-1)-\vartheta(n)
, and the result follows.

\Box

Theorem 26
.

[
7
]
For all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
M>0
such that for all
n>M
,

(73)

\displaystyle\pi(n)<(1+\varepsilon)\frac{n}{\operatorname{log}n}.

Theorem 27
.

An upper bound for
\operatorname{log}p/(p-1)
is established
[
2
]
, and it is given by the inequality

(74)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}p\leq n\\
p\not\equiv 1\text{ }(\operatorname{mod}4)\end{smallmatrix}}\frac{\operatorname{log}p}{p-1}<4+\frac{\operatorname{log}2}{4}.

Proof.

(75)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{smallmatrix}p\leq n\\
p\not{\equiv}1\text{ }\left(\operatorname{mod}4\right)\\
\end{smallmatrix}}\frac{\log p}{p-1}

\displaystyle<\frac{n+1}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log 2}{4}+\frac{\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)}{n-1}\pi\left(n\right)+\frac{1}{n-1}\sum\limits_{n<p<2n}{\log p}

(76)

\displaystyle<\frac{n+1}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log 2}{4}+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)}{\log n}+\frac{1}{n-1}\vartheta(2n-1)

(77)

\displaystyle<\frac{n+1}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log 2}{4}+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)}{\log n}+\frac{1}{n-1}(1+\varepsilon)\frac{2n-1}{\log\left(2n-1\right)}\cdot\log\left(2n-1\right)

(78)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log 2}{4}+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot\frac{\log\left({{n}^{2}}+1\right)}{\log n}+(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\frac{2n-1}{n-1}

For large
n
, (78) evaluates to (L’Hôpital’s Rule is useful here)

(79)

\displaystyle\frac{\log 2}{4}+4(1+\varepsilon).

Taking
\varepsilon>0
to be arbitrarily small, the result follows.

It is worth appreciating the following fact about the function

Theorem 28
.

We have

(81)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{\operatorname{log}p}{p-1}=\operatorname{log}n+\mathcal{O}(1),

where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the Big O Notation.

Theorem
28
implies that the execution time of this computation is independent of the size of the input. We provide a proof of this theorem.

Proof.
We have

(82)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{p\leq n}{\frac{\log p}{p-1}}-\frac{\log p}{p}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{p\leq n}{\log p\left(\frac{1}{p\left(p-1\right)}\right)}\ll\sum\limits_{p\leq n}{\frac{\log n}{n\left(n-1\right)}}\ll 1.

Hence,

(83)

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{\operatorname{log}p}{p-1}=\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{\operatorname{log}p}{p}

and this is bounded above by

.
