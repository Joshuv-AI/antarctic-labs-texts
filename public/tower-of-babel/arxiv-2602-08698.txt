Challenges in Translating Technical Lectures: Insights from the NPTEL
Basudha Raje, Sadanand Venkatraman, Nandana TP, Soumyadeepa Das, Polkam Poojitha, M. Vijaykumar, Tanima Bagchi, Hema A. Murthy

Abstract
This study examines the practical applications and methodological implications of Machine Translation in Indian Languages, specifically Bangla, Malayalam, and Telugu, within emerging translation workflows and in relation to existing evaluation frameworks. The choice of languages prioritized in this study is motivated by a triangulation of linguistic diversity, which illustrates the significance of multilingual accommodation of educational technology under NEP 2020. This is further supported by the largest MOOC portal, i.e., NPTEL, which has served as a corpus to facilitate the arguments presented in this paper. The curation of a spontaneous speech corpora that accounts for lucid delivery of technical concepts, considering the retention of suitable register and lexical choices are crucial in a diverse country like India. The findings of this study highlight metric-specific sensitivity and the challenges of morphologically rich and semantically compact features when tested against surface overlapping metrics.

1
Introduction

The domain of higher education is characterized by epistemic access, shaped by the interaction of technological mediation and linguistic plurality. Educational technology, though often conflated with mere combination of instrumental hardware and software manifestations, exhibits an intensive reconfiguration of pedagogy, cognition and participation
(
21
)
. The pursuit of implementing the aforementioned aspects of modern education in a synchronized manner, demands an understanding of the required parameters that contribute to a well annotated dataset for particular domains in academics adhering to the respective discourse setting. Within the scope of this project, the task to create such a dataset that leverages the quality of translation of spontaneous speech in an academic environment considering the domain specificity of the concept in discussion, at the same time maintaining lucidity to democratize accessibility is the prime objective.

To achieve this objective of a high-quality dataset, we must first understand why current tools fail to capture the nuances of academic discourse. This necessitates a close examination of the existing theoretical landscape and technological limitations, which sets the context for our proposed interventions and leads naturally into the review of literature.

1.2
NEP 2020 and Higher Education in India

It is noteworthy that the function of language in a multi-lingual country, is not merely that of a communication conduit but also that of a cognitive facilitator. A substantial body of psycholinguistic and educational research confirms that conceptual transfer and cognitive scaffolding are most efficient when learning occurs in the learner’s first or home language
(
8
,
15
)
. The inverse learning in a linguistically alien medium tends to produce what UNESCO
(
25
)
termed "subtractive bilingualism," wherein linguistic displacement compromises both comprehension and identity formation. Therefore, any applicative integration of technology into education requires language mediation in the primary stage as a core variable as opposed to a logistical afterthought.

In India, this aspect acquires constitutional and cultural significance. The linguistic portfolio, constituting twenty-two scheduled languages and hundreds of regional varieties, renders medium of instruction as an indispensable component in the discussions of equity and epistemic justice. English, though being the global lingua-franca, induces impact of positive nature and otherwise as it ends up entrenching local hierarchies of access towards higher education
(
4
)
especially when the majority of the population is not accustomed to English fluency particularly in marginalized sections of the society. This issue has been addressed in the National Education Policy 2020
(
11
)
which where through its decisive policy shift has emphasized inflection, assertion has been posited upon on the value of proficiency in one’s own language, culture, and traditions that acts as a foundation for cognitive and technological advancement. In its language-policy provisions (4.11–4.22), the NEP advocates that the medium of instruction, wherever feasible, should remain the home or regional language at least through the elementary stages and, progressively, within higher education.

This policy restructures the notion of vernacular medium from being a remedial measure to a strategic developmental goal. It envisions a multilingual knowledge system in congruence with its multi-cultural social setup wherein higher-order cognition, technological literacy and linguistic identity reinforce one another. As a result, there is a significant emphasis on translation, localization and formulation of digital corpora which necessitates that technology become both a vehicle and validator of multilingual pedagogy. In the light of this understanding, the present study aligns itself with the NEP’s agenda by examining the limitations of technology-mediated instruction, specifically, video lectures from the National Programme on Technology Enhanced Learning (NPTEL) can function as an empirical and infrastructural bridge between English-medium content and vernacular learners.

1.3
NPTEL Platform and Multilingualism

NPTEL, a flagship initiative collaboratively developed by the IITs and IISc, is a state-of-the-art MOOC portal that represents one of the most extensive repositories of higher-education content in India. Its gradual multilingual expansion through subtitling, transcription, and translation into several Indian Languages sets the ground for foundational research terrain where technology, pedagogy and policy intersect. These lectures, being spontaneous yet academically curated, provide data that retain both authenticity of spoken discourse and uniformity of disciplinary content. This abomination renders NPTEL not merely a resource that demands refinement but a living corpus that must be regularly updated and upgraded to reflect India’s evolving experiment with digital multilingualism.

From a research-oriented perspective, the structure of NPTEL is portrayed as a measured component that serves as a medium for systematic comparative analysis. The lectures, originally delivered in English, are dubbed into various Indian languages, foregrounding the complex dynamics of multilingual expansion. Originally, the lectures are delivered in English and have been in recent times, being proliferated into vernacular versions. The lectures being dubbed into various languages reflect the multifaceted limitations of multilingual expansion. First, the translation of these videos, where the nature of discourse is spontaneous and yet restricted has to be ensured of maximum lucidity while being translated, and secondly, the lectures that are delivered for various domains, although largely technical, must be ensured to retain the terms specific to whichever course is in question for a particular video. Along with that, one of the objectives of this study is also to deliver annotated datasets for training and evaluating language-technology systems specifically meant for Indian Languages. This proposition resonates with corpus-linguistic traditions inaugurated by the KIT Lecture Corpus (2012, 2014)
(
5
)
, which demonstrated the value of systematically aligned academic speech in examining instructional discourse and enhancing automatic transcription accuracy.

Construction of a multilingual, cross-domain parallel corpus from NPTEL lectures, contributes simultaneously to applied linguistics and language-technology research. On one hand, it investigates how translation in an academically restricted code, spontaneous and yet conceptually dense, is a tool of mediation between course instructors and learners spread throughout the country. Secondly, it generates data that can be used for evaluating the pre-existing model-based metrics and to fine tune the translation models as per the requirement of the current scenario. This prospect in turn paves the way for a context-sensitive evaluation framework that surpasses conventional BLEU or METEOR scores which have also been displayed and discussed in the upcoming sections of this study. In this sense, the corpus functions both as an epistemic artifact and as a technological instrument, embodying the dialogic relationship between linguistic theory and computational praxis (KIT Corpus, 2014; PMAS Nexus, 2023).

1.4
Aims and Objectives

With being in order, the incorporation of the three Indian Languages (ILs) within the scope of this paper is methodologically motivated. The selection of three languages from among the eleven recognized classical languages in India (Press Information Bureau, Government of India, 2025), i.e., Bengali, Malayalam and Telugu, arises from the triangulation of linguistic diversity, pedagogical relevance and representational equity. Bengali, with its rich inflectional morphology and expansive literary register, anchors the eastern Indo-Aryan spectrum; Malayalam, with its complex agglutinative structure and scriptural compactness, represents the southern Dravidian family; and Telugu, situated between inflection and agglutination, serves as a linguistic intermediary.

Therefore, this paper situates itself at the intersection of three key trajectories: pedagogical imperatives of multilingual education under NEP 2020, the potential of NPTEL’s digital lecture platform, and methodology grounded in corpus-based linguistic inquiry. This combination facilitates a research rationale that is policy-relevant and theoretically generative attuned to the linguistic and cognitive realities of India’s learners.

The paper is divided into five sections. The first section introduces the study outlining the rationale and the components selected for analysis. The second section discusses the research methodology and provides a review of the relevant literature. While the third section describes the evolutions of machine translation foregrounding the setting of the study, the fourth section discusses the errors and parameters identified in this study with illustrations from the curated data. The fifth section discusses the results and findings achieved through the metrics along with the future scope of this study and in the sixth section the paper is concluded.

2
Research Methodology

In addressing the persistent challenges in translation accuracy across Indian languages, the present study situates itself at the intersection of language technology research and computational linguistics, emphasizing the significance of corpus quality in enhancing machine translation performance. Despite considerable advancements in neural MT architectures, translation quality for morphologically rich and syntactically divergent languages from the Indian linguistic landscape remains constrained by data scarcity and contextual ambiguity
(
12
,
22
)
. These limitations are more prominent when cross-lingual divergence and domain-specific variations hinder the generation of translations that are semantically accurate and exhibit discourse coherence. Consequently, efforts to refine MT outputs need to address the quality, granularity, and contextual representativeness of the underlying parallel corpora.

Parallel corpora have been considered the backbone of MT research and evaluation as it enables bidirectional alignment between source and target texts, allowing models to learn correspondences beyond lexical equivalence that incorporates syntactic reordering, semantic nuance and discourse-level coherence
(
27
)
. However, as scholars such as Cettolo et al.
(
5
)
and Tiedemann
(
24
)
demonstrate, corpus effectiveness is contingent upon both domain relevance and annotation fidelity. Multilingual corpora, although somewhat abundant when it comes to ILs, often fail to capture the technical register and conceptual density characteristic of academic lecture discourse. Therefore, model predictions tend to over-generalize, producing literal yet contextually irrelevant translations. This observation induces the methodological decision to curate domain-specific parallel corpora: a corrective intervention aligning with data-centric paradigms in contemporary NLP.

Within this framework, the study draws on established lines of research advocating data augmentation and correction cycles as a pragmatic means of minimizing translation errors. Post-editing methodologies
(
2
,
23
)
have underscored the value of human-in-the-loop frameworks, wherein initial machine-generated outputs are iteratively refined by human annotators. Such workflows leverage the computational scalability of MT systems while preserving the interpretive precision of human linguistic judgment. The hybrid model machine generation followed by human correction constitutes an efficient balance between automation and quality assurance. This principle directly informs the present project’s three-stage pipeline: English source text
\rightarrow
MT output (BhashaVerse/SpringLab)
\rightarrow
corrected corpus, where human oversight converts raw translations into a validated, gold-standard dataset.

A substantial body of literature has further argued that corpus correction transcends surface-level rectification; it recalibrates linguistic representation at the model level. For instance, research in adaptive MT
(
7
)
demonstrates that exposure to curated, domain-specific data improves contextual disambiguation and lexical selection accuracy. Similarly, cross-domain adaptation studies
(
9
)
reveal measurable gains when models are fine-tuned on high-fidelity, post-edited corpora. This lineage positions the present methodology not merely as a corrective mechanism but as a contribution to data-driven model optimization. By anchoring the research in corpus-centric refinement, the study aligns with the recent "quality over quantity" discourse that prioritizes dataset integrity over raw scale
(
10
)
.

Equally significant is the project’s focus on Indian-language MT systems specifically, BhashaVerse (IIIT Hyderabad) and SpringLab (IIT Madras) whose architectures embody localized linguistic modeling yet remain susceptible to domain drift when confronted with specialized content such as NPTEL lectures. Prior works highlight the scarcity of pedagogically oriented corpora that capture the syntactic rhythm and conceptual layering of academic instruction. Addressing this lacuna requires corpus design that respects both the semantic depth of technical discourse and the structural particularities of Indian languages. The current methodology, therefore, functions as a data-engineering response to these challenges: bridging computational limitations through human-verified alignment and contextual calibration.

In essence, the literature substantiates a methodological rationale anchored in targeted corpus enhancement. By synthesizing advances in MT adaptation, post-editing, and corpus validation, the study operationalizes a framework that not only corrects existing translation errors but also contributes reusable infrastructure for multilingual educational content. This conceptual orientation underlies the empirical workflow detailed below.

From a workflow perspective, the pipeline’s structure: English source text
\rightarrow
MT translation
\rightarrow
manual correction embodies the principles of feedback-driven optimization. Each stage generates meta-information: alignment scores, correction logs, and linguistic tags that together enable quantitative tracing of error origins. This structured metadata is crucial for explainable MT, an emerging subfield concerned with transparency in neural decision-making
(
26
)
. By documenting how translation hypotheses evolve through correction, the study provides actionable insights into model behavior. Consequently, the methodological design functions not merely as a corpus-building exercise but as a diagnostic apparatus capable of informing both system design and pedagogical resource development.

Minimal theoretical anchoring is warranted to contextualize this practice within translation-quality theory. The act of human correction reintroduces functional equivalence, a concept originating in translation studies but now reinterpreted computationally as semantic alignment preservation. While human translators traditionally pursued equivalence for communicative adequacy, in computational terms it manifests as minimizing divergence between latent semantic representations across bilingual embeddings
(
13
)
. Thus, even the limited theoretical resonance in this work reinforces its technical premise: precision in meaning transfer is achieved not solely through algorithmic sophistication but through the disciplined engineering of verified data.

To achieve this objective, the study incorporated outputs from Machine Translation (MT) tools designed for Indian languages and further customized using NPTEL-specific datasets. The MT tools utilized included BhashaVerse, developed by IIIT Hyderabad, and SpringLab, developed by IIT Madras. It is important to state that BhashaVerse was successfully used to translate both NPTEL and Swayam Lectures, while SpringLab has been explicitly trained on NPTEL lectures to improve the quality of translation of spontaneous speech.

3
Evolution of Neural Translation

Machine Translation (MT) refers to the process that involves computers in achieving translation from one language to another. The development of MT has contributed significantly to conventional translation practices, while the challenge of producing satisfactory output across languages continues to be faced. Early MT systems were Rule-Based Machine Translation (RBMT), relying on linguists to manually encode grammatical and lexical rules. Although these systems offered structural consistency, they were brittle when faced with the ambiguity of natural language and required extensive manual maintenance.

The advent of corpus-based approaches led to Statistical Machine Translation (SMT), which dominated the field from the 1990s until the mid-2010s. Unlike RBMT, SMT systems such as the IBM Models and later Phrase-Based SMT (PBSMT) did not rely on explicit linguistic rules. Instead, they utilized statistical learning theories to derive translation parameters from vast parallel corpora
(
12
)
. Based on the Noisy Channel Model, SMT decomposed translation into a translation model
P(S|T)
(ensuring fidelity) and a language model
P(T)
(ensuring fluency). Although SMT offered superior adaptability and covered a broader vocabulary, it suffered from data sparsity in low-resource languages and often produced syntactically disjointed output because of its localized n-gram focus.

In 2014, the introduction of Neural Machine Translation (NMT) marked a significant paradigm shift through encoder–decoder architectures based on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks
(
6
)
. Unlike SMT, NMT models processed the entire source sentence into a continuous vector representation. While these models improved fluency, they remained limited in contextual scope due to the "bottleneck" problem of compressing varying sentence lengths into a fixed-size vector.

The introduction of the Attention Mechanism addressed this limitation by dynamically focusing on relevant input segments during the decoding process, rather than relying on a single static vector. This innovation ultimately led to the Transformer architecture, which underpins most contemporary large language models. The Transformer utilizes self-attention to weigh the significance of all words in a sentence simultaneously, regardless of their distance from one another. This capability is particularly vital for Indian languages, which often feature complex morphological structures and long-distance dependencies that previous RBMT and SMT systems failed to capture effectively.

4
Data Discussion and Error Analysis

Salesky et al.
(
20
)
address the challenge of translating utterances with disfluent structure in end-to-end MT systems. They demonstrate that conventional MT models trained on well-formed written corpora struggle when translating spontaneous speech, and introduce cleaned "copy-edited" references to train models to generate fluent translations from disfluent input. Moreover, stylistic artifacts characteristic of speech-derived "translationese" have been studied by Bizzoni et al.
(
3
)
, who compare human and machine translations of spoken speech and find that machine outputs often contain non-human translationese patterns that distort meaning and register.

In lieu of the aforementioned literature, this study presents the empirical analysis of multilingual translation outputs generated for spontaneous academic lectures. The dataset comprises text-to-text translations of NPTEL lectures that are essentially transcripts of spontaneous speech in an academic setting, i.e., conventional discourse in a restricted code spanning Humanities, Life Sciences, and Computing domains. Each machine-translated segment (produced by BhashaVerse) was evaluated against the original lecture audio and a manually curated gold reference. Errors were categorized into (i) Over Translation, (ii) MisTranslation, and (iii) Grammatical/Syntactic deviation; cross-linguistic patterns for Bengali, Malayalam, and Telugu were examined.

4.1
Over Translation

When in order to establish faithfulness with the trained set of tokens, translation is generated that disrupts the semantic fluidity of the language instigating decline in overall translation accuracy as opposed to mere structural redundancy, the produced output is categorized to be over translated. This error finds high frequency recurrence especially in phrase based translation as it facilitates ample scope for alignment noise, decoding heuristics and phrase-level probabilities.

Within the scope of this study, domain terms, everyday terms which can be used in English (greetings like good morning/welcome) get translated, and the words emerge from the literary archives of the language. This dismantles the fluidity and at the same time limits the learner from getting acquainted with technical terms that are in English.

4.2
Mistranslation

In cascading speech translation pipelines, that focus on the accuracy of literal translations relying majorly on open source tokens, the polysemous features of Indian languages tend to suffer as they are translated from English to Indian languages. A simple word, for e.g., "well" can be exclamatory (like well! I did not expect that!) or it could refer to the source of water. In spontaneous speech settings we have the recurrence of the former connotation much more than the latter. However, due to lack of data, the machine fails to register that. 4.3
Grammatical Errors

The morphosyntactic system of Indian languages are highly agglutinating (for Dravidian languages i.e., Malayalam and Telugu) and inflectional (Indo-Aryan i.e., Bangla). The languages have intricate syntactic systems and case sensitive agreement structures that are crucial even for spontaneous speech settings. However, this study finds several agreement mismatch and errors, especially for Bangla and Malayalam that suggests the necessity to suffice context by backing the architecture with well-annotated corpora.

Evaluation Metrics and results

The observations made under this study align closely with prior findings in the literature on machine translation of spontaneous speech. For example, Cho et al.
(
6
)
demonstrated that disfluency removal (filled pauses, self-repairs) and sentence segmentation significantly improve MT performance for meeting speech reporting BLEU gains of 2.1-4.9 points which when put into perspective of their conventional values (Papineni et. al.
(
16
)
), ranges from 10-30 for spontaneous conversational speech translations depending on the resource levels and structures built for various domains. Within the scope of this scale, 2-5 BLEU points is considered to be a substantial improvement since BLUE is a logarithmic metric where small improvements reflect large increases in n-gram equivalence (Koehn
(
12
)
). Along the same lines, their approach used a CRF to identify disfluencies and integrated them into the MT decoding lattice, which reduced noise and improved translation quality. Hence, in order to evaluate the accuracy with pre-existing metrics, BLEU, METEOR and COMET are the three metrics that are considered within the scope of this study.

5.1
BLEU

Proposed by Pappineni et al.
(
16
)
, Bilingual Evaluation Understudy operates primarily in formal alignment of intra-language accuracy. Since it is formulated in pursuit of large-scale corpora that allows for literal or near literal lexical overlap, this metric strictly governs through n-gram token match that is rather found to be absent when it comes to Indian Languages.
For instance, consider a Reference sentence: "The cat sat", and a Hypothesis: "Sat the cat". While unigram matching finds "cat" and "sat", the bi-gram "The cat" and "cat sat" fail to match. This rigid sequential dependency penalizes free-word-order languages heavily.
In order to achieve the resulting accuracy scores using this metric, BLEU scores were generated from the Bhashsa Verse generated corpora to the Corrected Corpora.

6
Conclusion

In an attempt to compensate for semantic inadequacy using translation models for specific tasks like text-to-text translation of academic lectures across various domains, the major parameters that can be structured in an uniform taxonomy further leverages the translation output that has been discussed in this study. While modern LLMs are known to translate with much better accuracy when it comes to general concepts and aspects for which the models are backed up with sufficient data, the need to constitute a tool that serves as a translation model that accounts for spontaneous speech parameters and at the same time generating output that is compliant with education being merged with the advent of technology even for rural areas in a diverse country like India still prevails.

Future Scope:
A critical dimension remains the handling of dialectal variations. Standard models often fail on colloquial dialects used in spontaneous speech (e.g., standard vs. colloquial Malayalam dialects). Dialectal variations do not go through varied structures but do differ in terms of lexical choices. For example, ’jol’ standard Bangla becomes ’fani’ in Sylheti which is a dialect of Bangla. In linguistic terms, these lexical variations combined with prosodic pattern of each of these dialects contribute to a diverse phonological implication of each language in terms of concrete realization. Hence, Future research must address this by incorporating dialect-rich datasets to ensure robustness across the linguistic spectrum.
