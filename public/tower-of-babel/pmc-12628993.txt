Prognostic value of combining nutritional inflammatory index trajectories and tumor characteristics in cervical cancer
Authors: Wen Xing, Zhongjie Wang, Yan Zhu, Ge Ge, Ying Zhang, Qicheng Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis investigation seeks to examine how varying longitudinal patterns in nutritional inflammatory index (NII) correlate with clinical outcomes in cervical cancer patients, while developing predictive models for prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 502 surgically treated cervical cancer cases, incorporating comprehensive clinical parameters, laboratory measurements, and longitudinal follow-up data. Three NII components—advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), neutrophil-albumin ratio (NAR), and prognostic nutritional index (PNI)—were evaluated for their temporal dynamics and relationships with overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Machine learning techniques identified critical predictors for constructing nomograms estimating 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival probabilities.ResultsPatients maintaining consistently low ALI/PNI values or elevated NAR levels demonstrated significantly poorer outcomes, manifesting earlier disease progression or mortality (log-rank p < 0.001). Based on the key variables screened by the machine learning algorithm, the OS model integrates variables such as NII (PNI), tumor characteristics (federation of gynecology and obstetrics (FIGO) grade, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, cancer antigen 125 (CA125), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC_Ag)), and surgical intervention, while the PFS model incorporates multi-dimensional factors such as PNI, FIGO stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, diabetes, surgery, and targeted therapy. Predictive accuracy remained robust across timepoints, with OS model AUCs of 0.894 (95%CI:0.853–0.934), 0.917 (0.88–0.954), and 0.925 (0.883–0.967) for 3-, 5-, and 10-year predictions respectively. Corresponding PFS model AUCs were 0.903 (0.868–0.938), 0.902 (0.865–0.939), and 0.905 (0.862–0.948).ConclusionThis study identifies that sustained low ALI/PNI or elevated NAR levels are strongly associated with adverse clinical outcomes in cervical cancer, including earlier progression and mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04125-z.Keywords: Nutritional inflammatory index, Trajectories, Cervical cancer, Nomogram, Overall survival, Progression-free survival

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the most common cancers among women worldwide, especially in developing countries, where its incidence and mortality rates remain high. According to the statistics of the World Health Organization, cervical cancer causes approximately 341,831 female deaths each year, among which 90% of the deaths occur in low - and middle-income countries [1]. Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is regarded as the main cause of cervical cancer, especially HPV types 16 and 18, which are associated with approximately 70% of cervical cancer cases [2]. Although the introduction of HPV vaccines offers hope for the prevention of cervical cancer, the vaccination rate remains low in many countries, especially in areas with limited resources [3]. Studies have shown that certain groups of people, such as elderly women, smokers, those with compromised immune systems (for example, HIV-positive patients), and women with a family history, are all at high risk of cervical cancer [4]. In addition, diabetes also play an important role in the occurrence of cervical cancer. Studies have found that there is a significant association between diabetes and the incidence of cervical cancer in high-risk populations [5]. Significant progress has also been made in the treatment of cervical cancer in recent years. For patients with early-stage cervical cancer, surgical resection remains the main treatment method. The combined use of chemotherapy and radiotherapy can significantly improve the survival rate of patients, especially among high-risk patients [6]. In addition, the introduction of targeted therapy and immunotherapy has provided new treatment options for patients with advanced cervical cancer. For example, targeted drugs against human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) gene mutations have shown good therapeutic effects in some patients [7]. The progress of these treatment regimens has not only increased the survival rate of patients but also improved their quality of life, providing a new direction for the future management of cervical cancer.

Studies have shown that nutritional inflammatory responses are closely related to the prognosis of cancer [8–10]. The advanced lung cancer inflammation index (ALI), as an emerging inflammation assessment tool, has shown its clinical application value in patients with advanced lung cancer. ALI can not only serve as a prognostic indicator, but also assist doctors in assessing the overall health status and treatment response of patients [11]. A study has shown that ALI has been verified as an independent prognostic factor for cervical cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. Compared with patients with high ALI, patients with low ALI have a 2.56 times higher risk of death and a 1.83 times higher risk of recurrence [12]. The neutrophil-to-albumin ratio (NAR), as an emerging biomarker, has attracted extensive attention in tumor prognosis studies in recent years. Studies have shown that patients with NAR ≥ 2.0 have a significantly lower survival rate than those with NAR < 2.0, suggesting that NAR may serve as a useful indicator for prognosis assessment in patients with cervical cancer [13]. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) is widely used in various types of tumors (such as breast cancer, lung cancer and colon cancer) as an important indicator for evaluating the nutritional status and immune function of patient [14]. Studies have shown that PNI is closely related to the prognosis of various cancers. For example, in patients with cervical cancer, the reduction of PNI is associated with poorer survival rate and disease progression [15]. However, most of the existing studies focus on nutritional and inflammatory index (NII) at a single time point, lacking a systematic analysis of their dynamic change processes. The dynamic trajectory model can reveal the changing trends of these indicators of patients during the treatment process, thereby providing a basis for the formulation of individualized treatment strategies. By analyzing the NII at different time points, a more comprehensive understanding of their impact on disease progression and survival period can be achieved.

This study aims to conduct a systematic analysis of NII in patients with cervical cancer by constructing a dynamic trajectory model, in order to explore their relationship with overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Furthermore, we will combine the machine model to screen the key predictive variables in order to construct a robust prognostic model. Through the verification of the model, we hope to improve the predictive ability of the prognosis of patients with cervical cancer, thereby providing a more accurate basis for clinical decision-making. Comprehensively considering clinical characteristics, treatment plans and the dynamic changes of NII will lay the foundation for achieving personalized treatment.

Methods

Research population

This study included gynecological inpatients diagnosed with cervical cancer by histopathology in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University from January 2015 to May 2025. The inclusion criteria are: those aged ≥ 18 years, meeting the clinical diagnostic criteria for cervical cancer and voluntarily signing the informed consent form. Exclusion criteria included patients with missing key laboratory indicator data and those who refused to participate in the study. This study was approved and supervised by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated People’s Hospital of Soochow University (Approval Number: JD-HG-2025-077), and was conducted in accordance with the standards stipulated in the Helsinki Declaration (revised in 2013). Each patient or their relatives participating in this study signed the informed consent form.

Clinical data collection and follow-up

The following data of the patients were included in this study: (1) Age; (2) Body mass index (BMI, collected respectively at the time of disease diagnosis, within one week after the initiation of treatment, and half a year after treatment); (3) Laboratory indicators (standardized tests conducted by an automatic biochemical analyzer at the time of diagnosis, during treatment and half a year after treatment): albumin, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, cancer antigen 125 (CA125), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC_Ag), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA); (4) Underlying diseases: hypertension and diabetes are determined based on clinical diagnostic criteria or medication history; (5) Tumor characteristics: size, pathological type, Federation of gynecology and obstetrics (FIGO) grade, degree of differentiation, lymph node metastasis, HPV infection (16,18, others and negative), treatment methods (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy and surgery, etc.).

The primary outcomes were OS and PFS. OS was defined as the time from the start of treatment to all-cause death, with the last follow-up as the cut-off for lost follow-up. PFS is defined as the pre-existing event from the initiation of treatment to the confirmation of tumor progression or all-cause death by imaging. All laboratory data were double-checked by two people and verified by quality control samples. The imaging and pathological results were independently evaluated by two physicians. In case of disputes, they were decided through discussions among multiple parties. The follow-up was mainly completed through outpatient visits or phone calls (for 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year survival rates) to ensure the reliability and consistency of the data.

Calculation of different NII

To capture the dynamic changes in the nutritional inflammatory status of patients throughout the entire treatment process, this study collected serum samples at three pre-defined time points with clear clinical significance: T0 (at diagnosis): Baseline assessment. The samples were collected within 7 days of starting any definitive tumor treatment. The indicators at this time point reflect the initial physiological state of the patient before treatment. T1 (during treatment): Assessment during treatment. For patients receiving radiotherapy and chemotherapy, the samples were collected on the third week of treatment; for patients undergoing the initial surgery, the samples were collected in the early morning of the day of the surgery (preoperatively). This time point aims to capture the physiological responses and vulnerability of the patient under the stress of treatment. T2 (after treatment): Follow-up assessment after treatment. The samples were collected at the first follow-up visit 6 months (± 2 weeks) after the completion of all major treatments. This time point is used to evaluate the long-term recovery and homeostasis of the patient.

Calculation formula: The NII of ALI, NAR and PNI are calculated as follows: ALI = BMI × albumin/neutrophils to lymphocytes ratio (NLR); NAR = Neutrophil/albumin; PNI = albumin + 5 × lymphocytes.

Construction of dynamic trajectory models of NII (ALI, NAR and PNI)

Proposed by scholars Leffondre et al., trajectory analysis was conducted using package traje in R4.4.2 to determine the dynamic trends based on the detection of NII at different time points, and the best trajectory patterns were identified following relevant parameter standards. Trajectory analysis was conducted based on package traje. ALI, NAR and PNI were divided into different subgroups respectively. Cluster analysis was used to divide patients with similar dynamic changes into the same group. The main reference parameters are: Bayesian information criteria (BIC), Akaike information criterion (AIC), ensuring that each trajectory group contains at least 5% of the participants to maintain statistical significance and odds of correct classification (OOC), etc. Based on the above parameter criteria, the study group selects the best trajectory model.

Statistical analysis

This study was based on the baseline characteristics of patients with cervical cancer (stratified by OS and PFS outcomes). Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as the median (quinterle range), and the differences between groups were analyzed by the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables were described as frequency (percentage), and chi-square test was used for comparison between groups. After grouping the patients through the constructed dynamic trajectory model of the NII, the survival differences were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier curve and the log-rank test. To explore the association between the dynamic trajectory of the NII and prognosis, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was constructed. Subsequently, multi-stage feature screening was adopted to optimize the robustness of the model. In the first stage, four machine learning methods, namely Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression (Liniar L1 regularization), random forest (based on Gini impurity importance ranking), Boruta (based on feature weights), and, GBM (Gradient Boosting Variable Weight) are combined to initially screen the variables. In the second stage, select the top 6 variables ranked by each machine learning method, and further integrate the variables co-selected by at least two methods or those with strong clinical relevance to form a candidate variable pool. In the third stage, a nomogram prediction prognostic model was constructed based on the screening variables. The performance was evaluated through a multi-dimensional validation system, including discrimination (Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC)), calibration (calibration curve combined with Hosmer-Lemeshow test), and classification efficacy (C index). All analyses were performed using R 4.4.2 and STATA 17.0, and the statistical significance was set at two-sided p < 0.05.

Results

Construction of dynamic trajectory models of NII (ALI, NAR and PNI)

The research group used the traje package in R software to conduct trajectory analysis on different NII of ALI, NAR and PNI to determine their dynamic trends at different time points. Following the parameter criteria of BIC, AIC, OOC and the proportion of patients in each group (Table 1), based on the principles of the screening criteria, we ultimately selected different models. ALI was analyzed (Fig. 1A) and divided into 2 groups. Group1: Persistently Low, that is, persistent low ALI levels in patients with cervical cancer; Group2: Persistently High, ALI remained high during disease treatment. AIC and BIC analyses were conducted. The BIC was 3969 and the AIC was 3932. After analysis, the grouping information was obtained. Among them, the sample size ratio of the group1 group was 0.759, and that of the Group 2 group was 0.241. NAR is divided into two groups (Fig. 1B), namely Group1: Persistently Low and Group2: Persistently High. The BIC was 1610 and the AIC was 1572. PNI was divided into three groups (Fig. 1C): Group1: Persistently Low, Group2: Persistently Moderate and Group3: Persistently High. The BIC was 9317 and the AIC was 9258. The posterior probabilities of all groups were > 0.7, and the sample distribution between groups conformed to the clinical observation rules, indicating the stability and biological rationality of the model.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with cervical cancer

A total of 502 patients diagnosed with cervical cancer were included in the study group and grouped according to survival outcomes (Table 2) : OS analysis showed that 418 cases (83.27%) survived and 84 cases (16.73%) died. In the PFS analysis, there were 375 cases (74.70%) of progression-free survival and 127 cases (25.30%) of progression or death. Analysis of OS revealed that the NII of the deceased patients showed significant decreases in albumin, ALI and PNI at all three time points, and a significant increase in NAR (all p < 0.05). Grouping based on the dynamic trajectory model indicated that the risk of death in the Persistently Low group of ALI and PNI and the Persistently High group of NAR was significantly higher than that in the other groups (all p < 0.05). Analysis of tumor characteristics showed that the levels of CA125, CEA and SCC_Ag in the death group were significantly increased. Patients with FIGO stage III-IV (vs. I-II), lymph node metastasis, no radiotherapy, chemotherapy or targeted therapy, low degree of differentiation (vs. moderately to highly differentiated), and diabetes were all prone to death (all p < 0.05). Variables such as age and accompanied hypertension had no significant association with survival (all p > 0.05), which might be related to the limitation of sample size. The inter-group analysis results of PFS were consistent with the OS trend, further verifying the above conclusion (Table 2).

The Relationship between NII (ALI, NAR and PNI) trajectory and the mortality rate of patients with cervical cancer

Based on the dynamic trajectory grouping of ALI, NAR and PNI, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed significant survival differences among the groups (Fig. 2).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of nutritional inflammation index trajectories and mortality in patients with cervical cancer (OS: A: ALI; B: NAR; C: PNI; PFS: D: ALI; E: NAR; F: PNI)

In OS analysis, ALI had the highest all-cause mortality in the Persistently Low group and the most significant survival advantage in the Persistently High group (log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). NAR showed the reverse trend. The survival rate in the Persistently High group was significantly lower than that in the Persistently Low group (log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 2B). The trend of PNI was consistent with that of ALI. The survival rate was the lowest in the persistently Low group and the best in the High group (log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 2C). PFS analysis (Fig. 2D-F) was highly consistent with OS results: The persistently Low group of ALI and PNI, and the persistently High groups of NAR all showed an earlier risk of tumor progression or death (all log-rank p < 0.001).

Multivariate Cox regression further verified the prognostic value of trajectory grouping (Table 3). With the ALI Persistently Low group as reference, The Persistently High group showed significant protective effects in both the unadjusted model (HR = 0.21, 95% CI 0.16–0.53, p < 0.001) and the adjusted age/comorbidity model (HR = 0.32, 0.13–0.71, p = 0.004). However, it lost statistical significance after additional correction for tumor characteristics (HR = 0.83, 0.36–2.09, p = 0.660). NAR Persistently High significantly increased the risk of death in both unadjusted (HR = 5.72, 3.64–9.08, p < 0.001) and fully adjusted (HR = 3.32, 1.94–5.68, p < 0.001) models. The PNI Persistently High group consistently reduced the risk of death throughout the model (HR = 0.17, 0.12–0.31, p < 0.001). PFS analysis also showed a reduced risk of progression in the Persistently High group of ALI and PNI, and an increased risk in the Persistently High group of NAR. However, there was no statistical difference in ALI after multivariate adjustment.

Screen the key factors related to the prognosis of OS and PFS in patients with cervical cancer

To identify the predictor variables most relevant to the target outcome and construct a robust diagnostic model, this study adopts a multi-stage feature screening and verification strategy. The multi-method joint variable initial screening quantified the contribution of candidate variables to the outcome through the following four machine learning methods respectively (Figs. 3 and 4). In the analysis of OS, GBM screened out CA125, CEA, SCC_Ag, tumor size, PNI and age (Fig. 3A); Random forest also screened out PNI, FIGO-Stage, tumor size, lymphatic metastasis, whether surgery was performed and CA125 (Fig. 3B); Boruta screened out PNI, FIGO-Stage, tumor size, lymphatic metastasis, whether surgery was performed and CA125 (Fig. 3C); Lasso regression screened out pathological type, NAR, PNI, tumor size, surgery and FIGO-Stage (Fig. 3D, E and F). Cross-integration and clinical validation, variable intersection extraction, extraction of variables jointly screened by at least two methods and clinical experience. The final screening included PNI, FIGO-Stage, tumor size, lymphatic metastasis, SCC_Ag, whether surgery was performed and CA125, which were determined after discussion by the clinical expert group.

In the analysis of PFS, GBM screened out CA125, CEA, SCC_Ag, tumor size, FIGO-Stage and age (Fig. 4A); Random forest also screened out PNI, FIGO-Stage, tumor size, lymphatic metastasis, targeted therapy and diabetes (Fig. 4B); Boruta screened out PNI, FIGO-Stage, tumor size, lymphatic metastasis, targeted therapy and surgery (Fig. 4C); Lasso regression screened out pathological types, NAR, PNI, diabetes, surgery and FIGO-Stage (Figs. 4D, E and F). Cross-integration and clinical validation, variable intersection extraction, extraction of variables that were jointly screened by at least two methods and clinical experience. The final screening included seven variables: PNI, FIGO-Stage, tumor size, lymphatic metastasis, diabetes, whether surgery was performed, and targeted therapy, which were determined after discussion by the clinical expert group.

Model construction and evaluation

Based on the key variables selected by Lasso regression, random forest, Boruta, and GBM, four machine learning algorithms, a 3-year, 5-year and 10-year prognostic prediction nomogram model for overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) of cervical cancer patients was constructed. Different from the single time-point indicators discussed in the supplementary analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1), the core advantage of the final model lies in integrating the complete dynamic trajectory of nutritional inflammatory indicators (PNI, ALI, NAR) from the time of diagnosis (T0), treatment (T1) to 6 months after treatment (T2), thereby more comprehensively capturing the evolution process of the patient’s nutritional inflammatory status over time and its impact on prognosis.

The OS model integrates the above-mentioned nutritional inflammation dynamic indicators, tumor characteristics (FIGO stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, SCC_Ag, CA125), and surgical intervention variables. The PFS model, on the other hand, incorporates multiple dimensions of factors such as nutritional inflammation dynamic indicators, FIGO stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, diabetes, surgery, and targeted therapy. The supplementary single-time-point ROC analysis provides strong independent efficacy evidence for the indicators included in the model. The analysis shows that the PNI and ALI indicators at the time of treatment (T1) demonstrate outstanding predictive capabilities (for example, the AUC of T1-PNI for predicting 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year OS is 0.854, 0.87, and 0.851 respectively), and their predictive accuracy is significantly superior to the measurements at the time of diagnosis (T0) and after treatment (T2). This not only validates the predictive strength of these indicators themselves, but more importantly, it highlights the key prognostic information contained in the dynamic changes from T0 to T1, which is precisely the core content that our dynamic trajectory model aims to capture.

The model quantified the weights of each variable and generated a nomogram (Figs. 5A and 6A) that can be used to directly assess individual risk. The scores in the graph were positively correlated with the risk of death (the higher the score, the worse the prognosis). The validation results showed that the C-index of the OS and PFS models was 0.742 and 0.757 respectively. The calibration curves closely followed the reference line at 3/5/10 years (the PFS model was more consistent, Figs. 5B and 6B). The ROC curve further confirmed its high discrimination (Figs. 5C and 6C): the 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year AUC of the OS model were 0.894 (0.853–0.934), 0.917 (0.88–0.954), and 0.925 (0.883–0.967), respectively, while the corresponding AUC of the PFS model was 0.903 (0.868–0.938), 0.902 (0.865–0.939), and 0.905 (0.862–0.948). The predictive efficacy of the final multivariate dynamic model (with an AUC > 0.90) was significantly improved compared to the best single indicator (such as T1-PNI), demonstrating the great value and necessity of integrating multi-timepoint dynamic trajectories with clinical multi-dimensional information.

After grouping based on the risk score quartiles (Figs. 5D and 6D), the KM survival analysis showed that the OS and PFS of patients in the low-risk group were significantly better than those in the high-risk group (all log-rank p < 0.001). This model achieved multi-dimensional and cross-time-point precise prediction of the survival outcomes of cervical cancer patients by integrating the dynamic trajectory of nutritional inflammation, tumor biological characteristics, and treatment information. Supplementary analysis indicated that if complete dynamic data cannot be obtained in clinical practice, a single PNI measurement at the treatment time (T1) can also serve as an effective prognostic alternative indicator; while this model provides a more comprehensive and precise risk assessment tool, providing a reliable basis for clinical individualized risk stratification and intervention strategy optimization.

Subgroup analysis

In addition, we also conducted subgroup analyses based on factors such as age (< 65 years old or ≥ 65 years old), BMI (< 25 kg/m or ≥ 25 kg/m), and tumor characteristics (such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, surgery, targeted therapy, etc.) (Table S1). In the subgroup analysis of OS and PFS of ALI, it was found that high ALI was significantly associated with improved OS and PFS in most subgroups, but its prognostic value might be affected by specific clinical factors. Among patients who did not receive targeted therapy, the Persistently High group of ALI had significantly better OS, while the Persistently High group of ALI who received targeted therapy might have been associated with the deterioration of OS, and the interaction was significant (p = 0.007). In patients without lymph node metastasis, the Persistently High group of ALI significantly reduced the risk of death, but this was not associated in patients with metastasis. Subgroup consistency of treatment modalities: In patients who received chemotherapy, radiotherapy or surgery, the Persistently High group of ALI was associated with improved OS, but the interaction was not significant (p > 0.05). The protective effect of the Persistently High group of ALI was stable across different age, BMI, diabetes and hypertension subgroups, and the interaction was not significant (p > 0.05). For HPV infection and histological heterogeneity analysis, it was found that only in patients with “other HPV subtypes”, the Persistently High group of ALI significantly improved OS, while there was no significant association in patients with HPV16/18 or negative.Patients with squamous cell carcinoma benefited significantly, while the trends of patients with adenocarcinoma were similar but not significant. The analysis results of PFS for ALI are basically consistent with the above results. In the analysis of NAR, we found that patients in the Persistently High group of NAR had significantly higher risk of developing OS and PFS. Specifically, this relationship was significant in patients without factors such as diabetes, hypertension, HPV type 18 or other types, or lymphatic metastasis. Analysis of PNI revealed that the basic results were consistent with the trend of ALI.

Discussion

Cervical cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors among women worldwide. Especially in developing countries, its incidence and mortality rates remain relatively high. Although existing studies have provided effective intervention measures, it is still necessary to further explore and optimize the existing prevention strategies and treatment plans to deal with the constantly changing epidemiological characteristics and emerging challenges [3]. OS analysis showed that the mortality rate of cervical cancer reached 16.73%. PFS analysis indicated that progression or death was as high as 25.30%. In this study, by constructing a dynamic trajectory model, a systematic analysis of NII in patients with cervical cancer was conducted to explore its relationship with OS and PFS. Meanwhile, the machine model is combined to screen the key predictive variables in order to construct a robust predictive model for the 3-year, 5-year and 10-year survival rates of cervical cancer.

Analysis of OS suggested that the Persistently Low group of ALI had the highest all-cause mortality, while the Persistently High group had the best survival (log-rank p < 0.001). NAR showed the opposite trend. The survival rate of Persistently High was significantly lower than that of Persistently Low (p < 0.001). PNI was consistent with ALI. The prognosis was the worst in the Persistently Low group (p < 0.001). The PFS results were highly consistent with the OS analysis. Both the low-level ALI/PNI group and the high-level NAR group showed an earlier risk of tumor progression or death (all p < 0.001). ALI is a comprehensive indicator that combines nutritional status and inflammatory response. BMI is used to assess weight status, serum albumin level is an important marker of nutritional status, and NLR is an important indicator of inflammatory response [16]. Inflammatory markers have gradually received attention in the prognosis assessment of cervical cancer. Studies have found that NII is closely related to the occurrence and development of cervical cancer. Specifically, NIIs such as the NLR, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), etc., have been proven to be related to the survival rate of patients with cervical cancer [8]. The increase of NLR is usually associated with the invasiveness of the tumor and the poor prognosis of the patient [17, 18]. ALI is not only an important predictor of the survival rate of cervical cancer, but also an indispensable monitoring tool in clinical practice, which is helpful to improve the treatment effect and survival period of patients [19]. Studies have shown that the inflammatory microenvironment is closely related to the occurrence and development of tumors, especially in cervical cancer, where the infiltration of inflammatory cells such as neutrophils is considered to be closely related to the malignancy and prognosis of the tumor [20]. The increase of neutrophils is often associated with an enhanced inflammatory response in the tumor microenvironment, while the decrease of albumin level, as an indicator of nutritional status, may reflect the invasiveness of the tumor and the overall health status of the patient [21]. Therefore, changes in NAR may provide important clinical information for evaluating the survival rate of patients with cervical cancer. There are significant differences in NAR manifestations among patients with cervical cancer at different stages. Studies have shown that the NAR of patients with early-stage cervical cancer is usually lower than that of patients with advanced cervical cancer, which is closely related to the inflammatory response and nutritional status of the tumor [22]. In a certain study, the median NAR of patients in stage I-II was 1.5, while that of patients in stage III-IV rose to 2.8, indicating that the inflammatory state of patients in the advanced stage was more severe [13]. The advantage of PNI as a prognostic indicator lies in its simplicity, non-invasiveness and easy accessibility. The calculation of PNI is based on serum albumin levels and peripheral blood lymphocyte counts, which can usually be obtained in routine blood tests [23]. Serum albumin is an important indicator reflecting liver function and nutritional status, while lymphocytes are an important component of the immune system and can reflect the immune status of the body [14]. Studies have shown that malnutrition is closely related to the survival rate and treatment tolerance of patients with cervical cancer. A low nutritional status may lead to an increase in treatment-related toxicity and a shortened survival period [24]. Factors such as inflammatory response, oxidative stress and microbiota imbalance are also related to the pathogenesis of cervical cancer. These factors may affect the development of cancer through pathways such as promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting cell apoptosis [25]. A study found that among patients with stage IIIB cervical cancer who received radiotherapy, those with a PNI value lower than 49.5 had significantly lower PFS and OS than those with a higher PNI value [24]. Furthermore, another study pointed out that PNI, as an independent prognostic factor, jointly affects the survival rate of patients with clinical characteristics such as tumor size and the number of lymph node metastases [14]. These research results indicate that PNI is not only an important indicator for evaluating the nutritional status of patients with cervical cancer, but also may be an effective tool for predicting their survival rate.

This study suggests that the risk of progression was reduced in the Persistently High group of ALI and PNI, and increased in the Persistently High group of NAR. However, there was no statistically significant difference in ALI after multivariate adjustment, suggesting that its predictive effect is time-dependent. The consistency of multiple models indicates that maintaining the continuous improvement of ALI and PNI and controlling the level of NAR may become potential targets for precise intervention of cervical cancer.

The OS model integrates variables such as NII (PNI), tumor characteristics (FIGO stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, SCC_Ag, CA125), and surgical intervention, while the PFS model incorporates multi-dimensional factors such as PNI, FIGO stage, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, diabetes, surgery, and targeted therapy. The 3-year, 5-year and 10-year AUC of the OS model were 0.894 (0.853–0.934), 0.917 (0.88–0.954) and 0.925 (0.883–0.967), respectively. The corresponding AUC of the PFS model was 0.903 (0.868–0.938), 0.902 (0.865–0.939), and 0.905 (0.862–0.948). This model achieves multi-dimensional and precise prediction of the survival outcomes of patients with cervical cancer by integrating the dynamic trajectory of NII, tumor biological characteristics and treatment information, providing a reliable tool for clinical individualized risk stratification and optimization of intervention strategies. FIGO staging is not only a tool for disease classification, but also a key indicator for evaluating the prognosis of patients [26]. The survival rate of patients in stage III decreased significantly, especially when para-aortic lymph node metastasis (stage IIIC2) occurred, the prognosis was even worse. Smaller tumors tend to respond better to chemotherapy and radiotherapy, while larger tumors may show drug resistance. Furthermore, the increase in tumor size is also associated with an increased risk of lymph node metastasis, especially when the tumor reaches a certain size, the possibility of metastasis significantly increases, which further affects the prognosis of patients [27]. The level of SCC_Ag is an important predictor of the survival rate of patients with cervical cancer. The SCC_Ag levels before and after treatment were closely related to the survival rate, and the continuously elevated SCC_Ag level was associated with a poor prognosis. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that a SCC_Ag level ≥ 1.2 ng/ml after treatment was an independent predictor of OS in patients with locally advanced cervical cancer [28]. Studies have shown that patients with preoperative CA125 levels ≥ 50 U/mL tend to have larger tumor volumes, more frequent intrauterine tissue invasion and lymph node metastasis, which are associated with poorer survival rates and higher risks of disease progression [29]. Surgical intervention plays an important role in the treatment of cervical cancer, especially for patients with early-stage cervical cancer. The choice of surgical methods (such as simple hysterectomy and radical hysterectomy) has little impact on long-term survival rate, but more conservative surgical methods can reduce postoperative complications. For patients with locally advanced cervical cancer, surgery combined with radiotherapy may offer a better survival rate than chemotherapy and radiotherapy alone [30]. Targeted therapy, especially anti-angiogenic therapy (such as bevacizumab) and immune checkpoint inhibitors, has shown significant effects in improving the survival rate of patients with recurrent, persistent or metastatic cervical cancer [31]. A study pointed out that patients with diabetes have a higher risk of recurrence and death after cervical cancer treatment [32]. Diabetes may increase the risk of cancer recurrence, and this risk persists even after the completion of treatment. Metabolic disorders and immune system changes caused by diabetes may be important factors affecting the prognosis of cervical cancer. High blood sugar levels may promote the growth and spread of tumors [5].

The cervical cancer OS model included SCC-Ag and CA125, while the PFS model did not include them. After cervical adenocarcinoma surgery, a CA125 level ≥ 50 U/mL is an independent factor for OS (HR = 2.27), but it is only marginally significant for Locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS) (p = 0.061), suggesting that it is more sensitive to “long-term death” rather than “early local failure" [29]. Multiple multivariate analyses have shown that SCC-Ag > 3 ng/mL is significantly associated with OS, but in the same model, it loses its independent significance for PFS/relapse-free survival, as the information has been absorbed by “FIGO + tumor size" [33]. The PFS model for cervical cancer included diabetes and targeted therapy, while the OS model did not include these factors. Diabetes and targeted therapy are both “sprint-type” variables. The long-term mortality risk comes from distant metastasis and cardiovascular events. Zheng Y-L et al. conducted a retrospective analysis of 387 postoperative patients with cervical cancer. They found that the 18-month pelvic failure rate for patients with diabetes was 28% compared to 15% (p = 0.003), but the 5-year OS difference was only 3% (p = 0.11), confirming that “the risk of short-term progression is high, but the long-term mortality risk does not increase.“ [34].

The innovation of this study lies in the first construction of a prognostic prediction model for patients with cervical cancer based on dynamic nutritional inflammatory indicators (ALI, NAR and PNI). Compared with previous studies, this study not only systematically analyzed the dynamic changes of these indicators, but also combined the clinical characteristics of the patients, providing a new prognostic assessment tool. But there are still some limitations. Firstly, this study was a single-center retrospective analysis with a relatively small sample size, which might have limited the general applicability of the results and there was a possibility of selection bias. Furthermore, the collection of clinical data relies on patients’ follow-up and self-reporting, which may lead to the absence or inaccuracy of information and thereby affect the reliability of the results. It is particularly important to note that this study lacks an independent validation sample group, which is the main limitation of this research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our longitudinal analysis indicates that the dynamic patterns of nutritional-inflammation indicators are the key determinants of the prognosis of cervical cancer. We found that patients who consistently maintained low ALI/PNI values or had increasing NAR levels had significantly poorer prognoses, manifested by earlier disease progression or higher mortality rates. This highlights the importance of continuous monitoring and management of patients’ nutritional and inflammatory status throughout the disease course. By dynamically tracking ALI, NAR, and PNI indicators, we explored the relationships between these indicators and the prognosis of cervical cancer patients in depth, and established a relatively reliable prognostic model, providing important guidance for clinical decisions. By combining the dynamic changes of NII and key factors such as tumor characteristics, it provides new directions for individualized risk assessment and intervention strategy optimization for cervical cancer patients.
