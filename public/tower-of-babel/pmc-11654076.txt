Impact of nutrition risk index, prognostic nutritional index and skeletal muscle index on early myelosuppression of first-line chemotherapy in stage IV gastric cancer patients
Authors: Juan Wei, WeiFeng Xiang, HangPing Wei, XiaoYan Hu, YiFang Lu, XiaoFang Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn gastric cancer (GC) patients, malnutrition is common and has a negative impact on treatment tolerance, survival, and prognosis. The purpose of this study was to explore the relationship between prechemotherapy nutritional state and early myelosuppression in stage IV GC patients treated with first-line chemotherapy.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with stage IV GC who received first-line chemotherapy between July 2012 and December 2021. Clinical and laboratory data were collected within 1 week before chemotherapy to calculate nutrition risk index, prognostic nutritional index. Pretreatment abdominal computed tomography scans were used to quantify skeletal muscle index (SMI). The main measurable outcome was the incidence of grade ≥ 2 early myelosuppression after chemotherapy.ResultsAmong 102 patients eligible for analysis, 50% were malnourished, 50% were poor prognoses and 49% were sarcopenic at baseline.The side effects were generally well managed, with a 26.5% occurrence of grade 3/4 side effects. Pre-chemotherapy patients with low Nutrition Risk Index (NRI) (p = 0.002), low prognostic nutritional index (PNI) (p = 0.001), and low SMI (p = 0.001) had significantly higher incidences of grade ≥ 2 myelosuppression occurred after the first cycle of chemotherapy. Moreover, the high level of PNI was associated with higher completion rate of chemotherapy (p = 0.01). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that SMI at baseline (p = 0.006) and hemoglobin level (p = < 0.001) were prognostic factors for grade ≥ 2 early myelosuppression.ConclusionStage IV GC patients with low NRI, low PNI and low SMI experienced significantly more grade ≥ 2 early myelosuppression during the first line of chemotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-024-03548-6.

Background

Gastric cancer, a frequently encountered malignancy, ranks third in terms of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Typically, the prognosis for gastric cancer patients is poor; approximately 80% of GC patients are in the advanced stage of the disease at the time of diagnosis [2]. In the case of stage IV GC, there was no difference in survival rates observed between gastrectomy following chemotherapy compared to chemotherapy alone [3]. As a result, palliative chemotherapy has widely become the standard treatment for stage IV GC patients and has been shown to improve outcomes [4].

Malnutrition often occurs in patients with GC, which is related to age, tumor site, and tumor, node, metastasis stage [5, 6]. The tumor itself and associated inflammatory factors may cause anorexia and skeletal muscle depletion. In addition, anticancer treatments such as chemotherapy may cause decreased intake, weight loss, weakness, and malnutrition [7, 8]. Malnutrition is a predictor of cancer survival independent of cancer site, PS, or stage [9]. Malnutrition can not only increase complications, but also prolong hospital stays, and many cancer patients may die from cachexia and malnutrition [10–12].

The NRI was developed by Buzby et al. as a simple and promising tool to evaluate the nutritional status of cancer patients and is based on body weight and serum albumin levels [13]. NRI has also been reported as a sensitive method to predict the prognosis of esophageal squamous carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma and resectable gastric cancer et al. [14–16]. As a simple and feasible nutritional screening tool, the PNI is proved to be associated with the prognosis of various malignancies and is widely used to assess the occurrence of perioperative complications and predict the prognosis [17]. PNI is able to assess not only nutritional status but also the systemic inflammatory reaction of patients.

Muscle loss is another indicator of malnutrition and cachexia, a condition known as sarcopenia. Lee et al. reported a significant reduction in overall survival in patients with sarcopenia [18]. In numerous studies, SMI, which is the normalization of skeletal muscle mass by height, was employed as an index for sarcopenia [19, 20]. However, studies that associate the indicator NRI, PNI and SMI with early myelosuppression after chemotherapy in stage IV gastric cancer patients are still scarce. Therefore, the current retrospective study aims to assess the significance of NRI, PNI and SMI on myelosuppression in patients with stage IV gastric cancer received first-line chemotherapy.

Methods

Patients

This is a retrospective observational study, one hundred and seventeen patients with stage IV gastric cancer received first-line chemotherapy at the Dongyang People’s Hospital during the period from July 2012 to December 2021. Fifteen patients were disqualified from this retrospective study; four for lack of abdominal CT before first chemotherapy in our hospital, six for disease progression before starting chemotherapy, and five for patients underwent chemotherapy at another institution, leaving a total of one hundred and two who met the inclusion criteria: (1) age > 20 years; (2) histopathology diagnosed with gastric cancer depends on the biopsy of gastroscope or puncture pathology of liver metastases; (3) staged as clinical stage IV using contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and positron-emission tomography; (4) Performance Status (PS) score ranging from 0 to 2 before chemotherapy; and (5) neutrophil count exceeding 1,500 cells/µL, haemoglobin exceeding 100 g/L, and platelet count exceeding 100,000/µL.

The nutritional markers were calculated or rated from the peripheral blood tests of each patient within 1 week prior to chemotherapy. The Body Mass Index (BMI) was computed using the formula weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). The NRI was calculated using the formula: 1.519 times serum albumin (g/L) + 0.417 times the present weight divided by the ideal weight (in kilograms), multiplied by 100, the ideal body weight was calculated using the Lorenz equation: Height − 100 - [(Height-150) / 2.5]. Studies have shown that malnourished was defined as an NRI < 97.5 [14]. The calculation formula of PNI was as follows: PNI = serum albumin level (g/l) + 5×total lymphocyte counts (10^9/L) [21]. Various studies have reported that the cutoff values for PNI were usually 40 and 45 [22–24]. Abdominal CT images were used as baseline measurements of sarcopenia prior to chemotherapy. The Slice-O-Matic software (Version 5.0; TomoVision) was used to assess the SMI, which was defined as the total skeletal muscle area at the third lumbar vertebra cross section normalised by height2 (cm 2/m2) [25, 26]. Based on previously established consensus, SMI cut-offs were 34.4 cm2/m2 for women and 45.4 cm2/m2 for men [27].

Treatments

All the patients received SOX, XELOX, S-1 or capecitabine monotherapy regimens according to the physician’s preference. SOX regimen was planned as oxaliplatin at a dose of 130 mg/m2 intravenous 2 h infusion on day 1, while S-1 was administered orally twice daily on days 1–14.

The S-1 dosage was calculated according to the Body Surface Area (BSA) as follows.

: for BSA < 1.25 m2, 80 mg/(m2·d) S-1 was administered; for BSA 1.25-1.50m2, 100 mg/(m2 ·d) S-1 was administered; and for BSA > 1.50 m2, 120 mg/(m2 ·d) S-1 was given. The XELOX chemotherapy protocol involved the oral administration of capecitabine at a dose of 1000 mg/m2 twice daily for 14 days, and oxaliplatin was administered intravenously at a dose of 130 mg/m2 on the first day of treatment. S-1 or capecitabine single-agent chemotherapy dose is calculated as above. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) positive patients were treated with chemotherapy combined with trastuzumab, an anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody. The treatment repeated every 3 weeks until disease progression. The chemotherapy-induced toxicities and side effects were categorized and rated based on the National Cancer Institute Common Toxicity Criteria (NCI-CTC).

Follow-up

In the current study, progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from first chemotherapy administration to objective disease progression or death. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the time of first chemotherapy administration to the time of death by any cause or the last follow-up. The last follow-up was assessed in June 30, 2023. The survival data were obtained from medical records.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze categorical variables, while t-tests were applied to continuous variables. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and the estimated endpoints were PFS and OS. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to identify independent prognostic factors.The connection between patients’ NRI and prognosis was analyzed by calculating 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) and Hazard Ratios (HRs). We utilized logistic regression analysis to explore the risk factors linked to myelosuppression, the Odds Ratios (ORs) were computed with a 95% CI. All data processing and statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS 27 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Statistical significance was considered significant if P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Complications

This study did not result in any unforeseen side effects. However, some patients experienced chemotherapy-related toxicity. The most frequent side effects were hematological toxicity and gastrointestinal reactions. Overall, the toxicity was well tolerated, and most adverse reactions were assessed as grade 1 or 2. The occurrence of neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia of grade 2 or higher was 19.6%, 21.6%, and 10.8%, respectively. Grade 3/4 toxicity occurred in 26.5% of cases.

Of all patients, the incidence of myelosuppression ≥ grade 2 occurred after the first cycle of chemotherapy was higher in the low NRI group compared to the high NRI group (35% vs. 10%; p = 0.002). The incidence was higher in the group with low PNI compared to the group with high PNI, similar to the previous finding (37% vs. 8%; p = 0.001). Similarly, the incidence was higher in the low SMI group compared to the high SMI group (36% vs. 10%; p = 0.001) (Table 3).

We used logistic regression analysis to identify the independent predictors of hematological toxicity.

SMI (P = 0.005) and Hb < 110 (P = 0.002) were identified in univariate analyses as significant risk factors for more than 2 degrees of myelosuppression that occurred after the first cycle of chemotherapy. Furthermore, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified sarcopenia (OR, 5.402; 95%CI, 1.634–17.86; p = 0.006) and Hb < 110 (OR, 13.797; 95%CI, 3.585–53.1; p = < 0.001) as significant and independent predictors (Table 6). The Hb < 110 (P = 0.005) and ECOG score (P = 0.012) were identified as significant risk factors for myelosuppression ≥ grade 2 that occurred after first-line chemotherapy, and Hb < 110 (OR, 5.268; 95%CI, 2.19–12.674; p = < 0.001) and ECOG score (OR, 0.35; 95%CI, 0.129–0.952; p = 0.04) remained significantly in multivariate analyses (Table 7). The NRI (P = 0.028), PNI (P = 0.003) and ECOG score (P = 0.01) were identified as significant risk factors for chemotherapy intensity, and NRI (OR, 0.153; 95%CI, 0.027–0.852; p = 0.032), PNI (OR, 12.128; 95%CI, 2.187–67.255; p = 0.004) and ECOG score (OR, 4.167; 95%CI, 1.544–11.24; p = 0.005) remained significantly in multivariate analyses (Table 8).

Survival

The median OS was 8 months, while the median PFS was 3 months. The OS rates at 1-year and 2-year were 35% and 13% respectively.The 1-year PFS rate was 8.8%.

Using Cox regression analysis for both univariate and multivariate analysis, it was found that low ECOG score was the only factor associated with prolonged PFS ( HR: 0.388, 95% CI: 0.237-0.635, P < 0.001 ) and OS (HR: 0.312, 95% CI: 0.186-0.526, P < 0.001). The complete data is available in Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 1.

Outcomes also included time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) which was defined as the time to either treatment discontinuation or death. The median TTD was 5.0 months (95% CI: 3.9-7.0). Longer median TTD were observed in patients with high NRI versus low NRI (7.0 months [95% CI: 3.8-8.0] versus 4.5 months [95% CI: 3.0-6.0], respectively); in patients with high PNI versus those with low PNI (7.0 months [95% CI: 3.8-8.0] versus 4.5 months [95% CI: 2.3-6.0], respectively) and in patients with high SMI compared to those with low SMI (5.0 months [95% CI: 3.8-6.5] versus 5.6 months [95% CI: 3.0-7.5], respectively) (Supplementary Table 2). In terms of, there were no significant differences among the three groups (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

Gastric cancer has emerged as a prevalent malignant tumor in China, boasting the second highest incidence rate and the third highest mortality rate compared to all other types of malignant tumors [28]. Systemic chemotherapy is the standard first-line treatment for patients with advanced or metastatic gastric cancer that cannot be surgically resected, aiming to enhance survival rates and quality of life [29]. Malnutrition is present in over 80% of patients with metastatic GC [30]. It is recommended to provide nutritional support to patients in both the postoperative and advanced disease settings, as it can have a favorable impact on prognosis, anticancer therapy compliance and quality of life (QOL) [31]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop evaluation strategies that rely on non-invasive, repeatable, and convenient biomarkers to assess the safety of treatment and prognosis of stage IV gastric cancer.

As nutritional factors, we used the NRI, PNI and SMI in this study because they are objective and easily measurable parameters does not require additional measurement. NRI is an assessment tool that has great potential in measuring the impact of nutritional status on cancer patients’ morbidity and mortality rates [32]. Preoperative NRI is a predictor of overall survival in gastric cancer patients with stage 2 or 3 [33]. The PNI has been demonstrated to be a valid indicator of a patient's immunological and nutritional status [34]. Researchers have demonstrated that PNI holds significant value in predicting survival outcomes for various types of cancer [35, 36]. Kouzu et al. conducted a retrospective analysis to investigate the prognostic significance of sarcopenia in patients with recurrent gastric cancer and the results showed that sarcopenia was an independent negative prognostic factor [37]. Recent studies have demonstrated that cancer accelerates sarcopenia, and patients experience persistent muscle loss throughout their treatment, leading to impaired physical function, increased chemotherapy toxicity, poorer QOL, and reduced survival rates [38]. In recent years, exercise and physical activities have received significant attention during cancer treatment, and the therapeutic benefits of exercise interventions for cancer patients have been extensively studied [39, 40].

However, despite this, valuable insights can still be gained, there is little research on the influence of NRI, PNI and SMI on the myelosuppression of stage IV gastric cancer patients.To address a research gap, our study analyzed the clinicopathological characteristics of 102 participants. We were the first to demonstrate the clinical significance of using NRI, PNI, and SMI to assess nutritional risk assessment in stage IV gastric cancer patients. The majority of patients with stage IV gastric cancer were able to tolerate the side effects of first-line palliative chemotherapy. Common adverse effects included hematological and gastrointestinal reactions. Our findings revealed that high levels of NRI, PNI, and SMI were significantly associated with a reduced incidence of early myelosuppression ≥ grade 2. Moreover, the high level of PNI was associated with higher completion rate of chemotherapy. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, baseline SMI and hemoglobin level were prognostic factors for myelosuppression. The ECOG score was the only potential prognostic factor for both PFS and OS, as determined by univariate Cox regression survival analysis. Additionally, the median PFS and OS were found to be longer in the low ECOG score group compared to the high ECOG score group, as determined by the log-rank method, these results are most likely due to the relatively small number of cases in this study. Longer median TTD were observed in patients with high NRI, high PNI. Using NRI, PNI and SMI as predictive indicators and monitoring response to treatment enables timely nutritional and exercise interventions to be implemented to reduce the risk of these complications.

This finding aligns with previous research indicating that prechemotherapy malnutrition can exacerbate chemotherapy toxicity, leading to poorer chemotherapy tolerance and subsequent treatment outcomes [41, 42]. There is an urgent need for nutritional screening, assessment and intervention before chemotherapy to improve nutritional state and to avoid adverse reactions after chemotherapy. Among several significant predictive factors in this study, the prechemotherapy NRI, PNI and SMI can serve as valuable indicators as they may have the potentials to mitigate the side effects after chemotherapy through the enhancement of nutritional status and preservation of muscle mass with intensive exercise prior to chemotherapy. Further studies are needed to confirm whether patients who are predisposed to malnutrition prior to chemotherapy require dose adjustments during treatment.

This study has some limitations. Initially, the investigation was a retrospective analysis conducted at a solitary medical facility with a comparatively small sample size. Secondly, this study solely examined the correlation between nutritional indicators and myelosuppression following chemotherapy, and adverse reactions should also include gastrointestinal reactions, fatigue and other symptoms. Thirdly, there was a lack of dietary records of patients during chemotherapy. Fourth, grouping analysis of chemotherapy regimens was not performed. Therefore, we will continue to collect clinical data and design prospective clinical trials to validate the relationship between nutritional index, safety, therapeutic effect, and prognosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, stage IV gastric cancer patients with low NRI, low PNI and low SMI have significantly more prone to early myelosuppression ≥ grade 2, and the high PNI was associated with higher completion rate of chemotherapy. In patients with stage IV gastric cancer with low NRI, low PNI and low SMI, the side effects of chemotherapy, particularly those associated with myelosuppression, must be carefully managed in subsequent treatment and start early nutritional and exercise interventions so that patients can successfully complete chemotherapy cycles. Prechemotherapy low levels of NRI, PNI and SMI were associated with greater occurrences of early myelosuppression ≥ grade 2 in stage IV patients with gastric cancer treated with first-line chemotherapy.
