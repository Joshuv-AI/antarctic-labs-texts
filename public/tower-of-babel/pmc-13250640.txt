Engineering nanozymes for cancer theranostics: Chemical design, catalytic intelligence, and translational oncology frontiers
Authors: Pankaj Garg, David Horne, Ravi Salgia, Sharad S. Singhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanozymes represent a transformative convergence of chemistry, nanotechnology, and biomedicine, giving rise to a new generation of enzyme-mimicking nanomaterials. Unlike natural enzymes that function optimally within narrow physiological ranges, nanozymes exhibit exceptional structural adaptability, tunable catalytic activity, and superior stability, making them highly promising for translational biomedical and oncological applications. This review focuses on the chemistry-guided design principles that govern nanozyme performance, emphasizing how composition, size, morphology, crystal facets, defect engineering, and surface functionalization collectively influence catalytic behavior. The discussion covers a wide range of enzyme-like activities, such as peroxidase, oxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase mimetics, multi-enzyme and cascade catalytic mechanisms and the underlying chemistry of these activities. Furthermore, this review explores the broad biomedical landscape of nanozymes, spanning biosensing, diagnostics, tumor microenvironment (TME) modulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-mediated cancer therapy, antibacterial and antioxidant interventions, imaging, theranostics, and tissue engineering. Special emphasis is placed on the emerging role of nanozymes in cancer diagnosis, targeted therapy, cancer nanotheranostics, and precision oncology. Despite remarkable progress, challenges persist regarding toxicity, substrate specificity, in vivo stability, and large-scale reproducibility. Advances in rational chemical design, computational modeling, green synthesis, and integration with smart nanomedicine approaches are expected to address these limitations. In essence, this review underscores that chemistry not only drives the creation of nanozymes but also directs their evolution into next-generation oncological and biomedical platforms, enabling precise, multifunctional, and patient-tailored therapeutic strategies for translational cancer medicine.

Introduction

Natural enzymes are extraordinary biocatalysts, crucial in maintaining life by increasing chemical reactions under biologically mild conditions with stunning selectivity. Nevertheless, natural enzymes have several inherent limitations to their biomedical and industrial applications; they readily denatured, their activity only extends to a very small range of temperatures and pHs, and they are costly to manufacture in vivo and to purify in large quantities [1]. Over the last two decades, advances in nanotechnology have inspired the development of nanozymes, a class of nanostructured materials capable of mimicking enzymatic catalysis. The term ‘nanozyme’ was coined in 2004 after scientists discovered that magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles exhibited peroxidase-like activity [2]. Since then, the field has expanded exponentially. Nanozymes are now regarded as a new generation of artificial enzymes that combine the structural versatility of nanomaterials with the functional characteristics of natural biocatalysts [3]. In contrast to natural enzymes, nanozymes do not use amino acid residues or protein folding to produce catalytic activity. Rather, they are reactive because of their chemically tunable properties, including surface atoms, coordination environments, defects, and electron transfer properties. These characteristics opens new avenues for developing catalysts that are not only more efficient but also multifunctional in nature [4]. In general, nanozymes operate in severe conditions in which natural enzymes cannot, such as extreme pH levels, high temperature, and in conditions of oxidative stress. Their remarkable stability, combined with the ability to fine-tune their catalytic behavior through chemical modification, makes nanozymes highly attractive for applications in the life sciences, particularly in diagnostics, therapeutics, and environmental health [5,6].

The interface that has brought nanozymes to attractiveness is that of chemistry and biology. Chemists have the ability to replicate the active centers of the enzymes by controlling the atomic structure, size, and composition of the nanomaterials; biomedical researchers believe that nanozymes can be used in bio-sensing, in curing diseases, and as regenerative medicine [7]. Particularly, iron oxide nanozymes exhibit peroxidase function and are actively studied as a tumor-specific producer of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cancer immunotherapy, whereas cerium oxide (CeO2) nanozymes function as catalase- and superoxide dismutase-like nanozymes, which have antioxidant activity in neurodegenerative disease. Such examples demonstrate how chemistry-directed design principles have been directly applied to biomedical fields [8]. Thus, research on nanozymes goes far beyond the simple replacement of natural enzymes. It signifies a new paradigm in which the chemical engineering of nanomaterials enables precise control over enzyme-like activities, offering remarkable stability, tunability, and multi-functionality. In this review, we briefly discuss the underlying chemistry behind nanozyme design, the catalytic mechanisms that govern their activity, and their promising biomedical applications that place them at the forefront of translational nanomedicine. We also critically assess the current challenges and future directions that determine how nanozymes evolve from laboratory innovations into real-world clinical technologies [9,10].

Nanozymes are nanomaterials that can mimic the catalytic activity of natural enzymes as catalysts. Nanozymes use the catalytic activity of nanomaterials, like transition metals, metal oxides, or carbon-based structures, to catalyze reaction, rather than relying on small organic molecules or metal complexes that resemble an enzyme's catalytic active site, as with the conventional artificial enzymes [11]. Their natural stability due to inorganic or hybrid structure is more favorable over proteins, allowing nanozymes to be used in environments where natural enzymes would otherwise have degraded. It is worth noting that nanozymes can also be specific to various enzymatic activities, and different enzymes, including peroxidase, oxidase, catalase, or even a blend of enzymes, may be imitated by the nanomaterial across different regions, and even cascade reactions may be realized. A combination of heterogeneous and biocatalysis, nanozymes are chemical in nature [12]. They can provide an alternative platform to study essential catalytic events in a nano scale physician combining the long pauses between inorganic materials chemistry and biology functionality. The power of modern synthetic chemistry lies in the possibility to control reactivity by defect engineering, doping engineering, or surface ligand engineering. Many of the biological problems that nanoscience seeks to solve can likewise be addressed by nanozymes. As an example, nanozymes can be applied to replace the enzymes in diagnostic kits that require the use of expensive and sensitive proteins such as horseradish peroxidase [13]. Therapies with the use of nanozymes are also being investigated as an adjuvant or inhibitor of oxidative stress in chronic cancer, and as a ROS producer to kill cancerous cells. Therefore, nanozymes serve as an excellent example of how chemistry not only explains but also drives biological innovation [14].

The concept of artificial enzyme chemistry is not new; it originated from efforts to design small molecule catalysts that mimic enzyme active sites. Such systems have provided data concerning catalysis, and even though these were incapable of matching that of the actual enzymes, they are still stronger and better in nature. The identification of peroxidase-like catalytic activity of NPs marked another milestone in the field, which demonstrated the catalytic property of a shape and physicochemical properties of nanomaterials in absence of traditional enzyme structure. Since then, the number of nanozymes has grown at an exponential rate that includes metals, metal oxides, carbon nanostructures, and porous nanomaterials. Nanozymes are also being used in point-of-care diagnostic, drug delivery systems and theranostics, in addition to being catalytically unique. This is one step towards the growing popularity of nanozyme as a new class of chemical instrument that has biological activity [15].

The purpose of this review is to make a critical discussion about nanozymes, as how chemical principles can be used to design them and how they can be utilized in biomedical applications. It begins by recounting the chemical grounds of nanozyme design (e.g., classes of materials, relations between structure and activity, and approaches to designing surface groups). It delivers detailed descriptions of the mechanisms by which nanozymes catalyze enzyme functions and an overview of representative applications of the nanozymes in the biomedical field such as sensing, treatment, imaging, and tissue engineering. Finally, future prospects are addressed with the description of the limitations for the clinical application of nanozymes and the need for AI-assisted design, personalized medicine, and sustainable synthesis routes for producing such materials [16]. The catalytic programmability, ROS-generating property, and multifunctional surface engineering features of nanozymes are highlighted to advance their use in cancer diagnosis, modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME), targeted therapy, and cancer theranostics and promotion in the field of translational oncology.

This article also attempts to bridge the disciplinary void and to provide a general picture on the current extents to which nanozymes have manifested through a number of chemical and biological investigations so far in the field of life sciences. Although tremendous advances have been made in the development of nanozymes, key hurdles need still be overcome before they can be translated into general clinical practice, such as catalytic specificity, the lack of a comprehensive understanding of structure-activity relationships (SARs), toxicity in the long-term and the lack of standardization in clinical application. Furthermore, several existing reviews were discussed either on material synthesis or catalytic behavior, but they did not sufficiently combine the aspects of chemical engineering with further applications, considering their translation to the field of biomedicine or in oncology [17]. Therefore, the current study aimed to survey the rational chemical design, catalytic intelligence, ROS-mediated therapeutic strategies, and the further translational potential of cancer diagnosis and therapy by perceiving to the research of nanozyme engineering comprehensively and in a mechanism-based manner.

Chemistry of nanozyme design

Nanozymes are intrinsically linked with chemistry applications. Unlike natural enzymes, the catalytic activity of nanozymes is not limited to the folding of amino acids, but is rather determined by the spatial structure, surface properties, electronic structure, and location of cofactors. This allows the nanozymes to be highly tunable systems where remarkable stability and selectivity is obtained by careful selection and design of the chemical components [18]. To enable practical biomedical applications of nanozymes, a critical initial step involves the rational design and optimization of their catalytic properties.

Fundamental principles of enzyme mimicry

In the context of the transition state stabilization and reducing activation energy barriers, the natural enzymes are outstanding. The nanoscale design of nanozymes mimics such behavior where the surface atoms, catalytic centers, and valence states are important. One example of such a case has been already described for the redox reaction Fe2+ / Fe3+ located at the surface of the iron oxide nanoparticles with the activation of the peroxidase activity of the heme groups of horseradish peroxidase [19]. These catalytic centers are different, from the natural enzymes, in that they have no polypeptide framework. Instead, the number, accessibility, and reactivity of such centers can be tuned by synthesizing materials, through the efforts of chemists. Nanozymes are effective, versatile, and can mimic enzyme functions under conditions that would cause natural enzymes to denature rapidly like extreme pH levels, oxidizing conditions and high temperatures, demonstrating their viability to react efficiently [20]. Finally, by comparing to natural enzymes, it can be concluded that the basic axis of designing and optimizing next-generation catalytic nanomaterials is the mimicry of enzymes in a chemical way as far as the design of catalytic centers and the surface reactivity of nanomaterials are concerned.

Material classes used for nanozymes

Nanozymes are not one specific material, but an entire family of materials that have enzyme-like properties. Most metal-based nanozymes (such as Au, Pt, Pd, and Fe) exhibit peroxidase or oxidase activity through electron transfer and redox reactions. Gold (Au) based nanoparticles are applicable to the biosensing platforms and platinum (Pt) nanostructures are good mimics of oxidases. The natural redox properties of metal oxides such as CeO2, MnO2, Fe3O4, and hydroxides have made them catalytically active and are of particular note as such [21]. CeO2 nanoparticles are capable of having high antioxidant potential like catalase and superoxide dismutase in the state of Ce3+ and Ce4+. Other carbon-based nanozymes, such as graphene oxide, carbon nanotubes, carbon dots and fullerenes, have also started to develop biocompatibility and high surface chemistry. The structures of these materials are highly polarized, thus providing an enzyme-like oxidation of sp² systems. They can also be readily modified for use as scaffolds. Examples of notable materials are metal organic frameworks (MOFs) and covalent organic frameworks (COFs). The well-organized and porous structures closely resemble the active site of enzymes, enabling the specific recognition of substrates and cascade catalytic reactions to take place [22]. Hybrid composites combine the desirable properties of various materials. By coupling Fe3O4 nanoparticles with graphene oxide, for example, the biocompatibility and peroxidase activity of the complex is enhanced; by adding noble metals to carbon, the conductivity and signal strength of biosensors are enhanced. Material diversity is one of the advantages of this emerging field, as chemists are able to precisely control the performance of nanozyme precisely by picking appropriate materials combination. Collectively, the wide range of nanozyme materials provides extraordinary flexibility in optimizing catalytic activity, biocompatibility, and biomedical function, which can be used to engineer the properties of nanozyme materials for specific applications.

Structure-activity relationships (SAR)

The catalytic efficiency of nanozymes is not pre-programmed, but can be adjusted by changing the morphology, size, surface chemistry, and elemental composition. Smaller nanoparticles have a higher number of reactive crystal facets due to the surface-to-volume ratio, which may lead to preferential orientation of reactive crystal facets depending on the shape of the nanoparticles (cube, rod, or octahedral). Further, the introduction of structural defects or amorphous domains can create new catalytic sites and can greatly increase the activity [23]. The existence of oxygen vacancies in CeO2 nanoparticles and the catalyzed reversible redox reactions are important markers of stimulated antioxidant activity. Tuning catalytic performance is made even more accurate with other key characteristics present in the nanozyme design toolkit, such as alloying and doping. The inclusion of heteroatoms (e.g., N-doped carbon, Mn-doped oxides) can affect the electronic density and make the catalytic pathways easier [24]. The synergistic effects of heterostructures are also permissible, where two materials can be combined in such a way that they can form a junction that is enriched with electrons, in which electron reaction kinetics will be enhanced. By methodologically reviewing the influence of the various elements of the system in the performance, chemists can bring SAR to life and inform the design of the next generation nanozymes. This information can make trial and error-based synthesis into predictive engineering.

Although the catalytic activity of nanozymes has been well regulated by changing their structure, enzyme-like substrate specificity is still difficult to realize in the field of nanozyme research. However, nanozymes ability to mimic natural enzymes remains a major constraint due to their comparatively low specificity toward substrate. Natural enzymes have three-dimensional structures with finely developed active sites with a definite molecular recognition ability, while nanozymes generally use surface-mediated catalytic and/or redox reactions that have a wide interaction with the surrounding substrates. Consequently, many nanozymes are ‘catalytic promiscuous’ meaning that they catalyze multiple non-specific oxidations and/or reduction reactions with multiple substrates. This inbuilt molecular-level non-selectivity, which is a serious problem when dealing with complex biological mediums where competing biomolecules, proteins, and redox active species abound. Improving the substrate specificity is therefore one of the key challenges for nanozyme engineering in order to realize translation in biomedical applications. In response to these drawbacks, several novel engineering methods have recently appeared such as surface ligand engineering, defect modulation, biomimetic preparation of active sites, and molecular imprinting techniques. Modular substrate binding architecture and molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP)-based nanozymes in particular have shown great improvements in substrate recognition ability and catalytic selectivity for partially mimicking the molecular binding behavior of natural enzymes. These methods could help to narrow the gap between the broad-specific catalytic activity of nanozymes and the catalytic precision of biological enzymes because they simulate the highly selective catalytic function of enzymes. In general, the catalytic activity of nanozymes is highly dependent on their size, shape, charge, composition, and defect structure. Through careful optimization of these SARs, catalytic efficiency and interactions with substrates can also be enhanced, and increased control of selectivity and biological performance can be achieved. Thus, it is important to have a basic understanding of SAR in their rational design for the next generation of biomedical and translational applications with nanozymes [25].

Surface functionalization strategies

As the intrinsic chemistry of a nanozyme defines the core of the catalytically active nanozyme, the interaction of a nanozyme with biological systems is defined by its surface chemistry. Otherwise, on a large scale, a significant portion of nanozyme will be aggregated under physiological conditions or induce an immune response. Surface functionalization is therefore employed for compatibility and targeting chemists to increase stability [26]. Ligand modification incorporates stabilizing molecules, which inhibit aggregation. Hydrophilicity and dispersibility can be tuned by small molecules, polymers, or surfactants. Bioconjugation can be used to target nanozymes to particular targets (e.g., cancer cells or bacterial pathogens) by attaching biological ligands, like antibodies, peptides, or nucleic acids. PEGylating and Zwitter ion coating offer stealth characteristics, which minimize the adsorption of proteins and immune clearance, thereby increasing the effects of circulation in vivo [27]. A modular structure-based approach has been recently developed to improve the catalytic specificity of nanozymes towards the levodopa detection, by constructing artificial substrate binding sites on them. The protein mimetic following this method demonstrated excellent substrate selectivity and visual sensing capabilities, encouraging the use of biomimetic active-site engineering as a key strategy for the development of a new generation of nanozymes [28].

Specific behavior can be built into stimuli-responsive surfaces; degradable under acidic pH or tumor-targeted enzyme-specific polymer-based polymers can be used to activate nanozyme-based activities in disease-modified locations, thereby reducing exposure to off-target effects. Functionalization of the surface is practically considered the language of nanozymes and their ability to interact with biological systems. Catalytic mechanisms of nanozymes

The central question in nanozyme research is how these man-made nanomaterials manage to catalyze reactions in a way similar to natural enzymes. While natural enzymes catalyze reactions with an organized arrangement of amino acids and cofactors, the reactions catalyzed by nanozymes are due to their surface chemistry, electronic behavior, and redox properties at the nanoscale. While the mechanisms vary in specific details with respect to the proteins, they all represent the same idea, that is to say, they stabilize the transition state and make it easier to get there. The researchers can study the catalytic modes of the nanozymes and break them down to know how the nanozymes work, and how to maximize their catalytic activity for biomedical activities [31]. Peroxidase-like activity

One of the pioneers and best studied catalytic activities of nanozymes is their peroxidase-like activity. Like natural peroxidases such as horseradish peroxidase these nanozymes catalyze the production of ROS from hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which oxidizes substrates like TMB (3,3,5,5-tetramethylbenzidine). The Fenton-like activity of the Fe3O4 nanoparticles in the reaction with H2O2 and production of the hydroxyl radicals (OH-) is an example of such catalytic action, with a remarkable catalytic rate [32]. The biomedical relevance of this activity arises from the ability of Fe₃O₄ nanozymes to utilize excess H2O2 present in the TME to generate cytotoxic ROS and selectively killing cancer cells in the therapy. Conversely, peroxidase-like nanozymes that are used in colorimetric detection for diagnostic testing, are relatively inexpensive, more rugged, and more stable, as compared to the natural enzymes. In addition, it should be noted that nanozymes are extremely versatile because they are not only pH and temperature-tolerant like natural peroxidases, but also resistant to widely different environments [33].

Conclusively, peroxidase-like nanozyme represents one of the most developed and clinically effective types of synthetic enzymes. They have the capability to catalytically generate ROS under tumor-specific conditions and have been widely exploited in the field of cancer therapy, antibacterial treatment, and biosensing. With further advances in the catalytic specificity and microenvironment responsive activation, their therapeutic precision can be further improved, while minimizing off-target oxidative damage.

Oxidase-like activity

Another important catalytic activity is oxidase-like activity, in which nanozymes can provide direct oxidation of a substrate in the presence of dissolved O2 as the electron acceptor, without using H2O2. This is similar to the nature of oxidases such as glucose oxidase. Specifically, the nano sheets of manganese dioxide (MnO2) are able to directly oxidize chromogenic substrates with the assistance of oxygen, which can be measured by color alterations [34]. The mechanism has particularly found application in biosensing, and endogenous H2O2 can complicate the detection. It becomes easier with oxidase similar to nanozymes that act in oxygenated physiological conditions. Nanozymes also participate in the metabolism of oxygen during therapeutic conditions (e.g., by local oxygen used to control hypoxia in tumors) [35].

Catalase-like activity

The opposite effect is observed with the catalase-like nanozymes, where the action of peroxidase and oxidase results in the ROS, and catalase-like nanozymes decompose H2O2 to harmless water and oxygen, much like natural catalase. The most suitable materials to fulfil this role are CeO2 and Pt nanoparticles because they have a quick redox cycle. The medical consequences of this antioxidant activity are far reaching. The nanozymes that are catalase-mimicking destroy the harmful H2O2 to reduce oxidative stress in tissues, which implies that the same can be applied in the possible treatment of neurodegenerative disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and inflammatory traumas. Nanozymes can also be used to treat hypoxic tumors to enhance the effect of other therapies, including radiotherapy and photodynamic therapy, by virtue of their oxygen releasing property [36].

Superoxide dismutase-like activity

Nanozymes may expand their catalytic enzymes by becoming superoxide dismutase (SOD)-mimickers, which participate in the dismutation of superoxide radicals (O2-) to oxygen and H2O2. Once again, the nanoparticles of CeO2, in particular, can be singled out with Ce3+/Ce4+ switching, which allows them to scavenge active species effectively. In biology, uncontrolled accumulation of superoxide leads to chronic inflammation, aging, and tissue damage caused by oxidation. Therefore, it has therapeutic potential through the use of SOD-like nanozymes in the role of antioxidants. The redox balance of nanozymes is the reason why they can be relevant in the reperfusion injury treatment of ischemia, traumatic brain injury, and even age-related macular degeneration [37].

Multi-enzyme like and cascade activities

The most interesting direction in nanozyme catalysis is arguably the field of multi-enzyme-like and cascade activities. In contrast to single-function catalysts, a few nanozymes are able to operate in various enzyme-mimicking activities at the same time. As a case in point, CeO2 nanoparticles can turn catalase-like and SOD-like with respect to the local environment and change dynamically to oxidative stress conditions. Cascade nanozymes are even more advanced, in which successive reactions have been designed into a single nanostructure [38]. As an example, a MOF filled with glucose oxidase and combined with peroxidase-mimicking nanoparticles can initially catalyze glucose to H2O2, and then catalyze existing chromogenic substrates using that H2O2, enhancing signals of detection. Such cascade systems can assemble and produce ROS on command in tumors in the presence of metabolic control and cytotoxic activity in the therapeutic setting. Such multifunctional nanozymes bring nanozymes a bit closer to the complexity of natural enzymes and provide versatility not accessible to proteins [39]. It can be concluded that, in concept, the development of multifunctional and cascade nanozyme systems is another important step towards biomimetic catalysis. These systems can be designed to involve several enzymes reaction event(s) to a single nanoplatform and can result in optimized sequential catalytic reaction for high efficiency and therapeutic synergy. Such intelligent catalytic networks, which operate in a similarly integrated manner as natural pathways in biology, have great potential for complex biomedical applications such as precision oncology, multimodal therapy, and intelligent biosensing.

Comparison with natural enzyme catalysis

Although nanozymes mimic the activity of enzymes, they are not as specific as natural enzymes. The specificity of the enzymes is determined by enzyme-substrate interactions including hydrogen bond formation and hydrophobic interactions between the enzyme and substrate, as well as conformational changes. However, Nanozymes are less charge-dependent, less size-exclusion dependent, and are less specific due to their dependence upon chemical affinity. This difference cannot be a drawback. In the complex biological environment, nanozymes can work because of their wide range of activity, however, they cannot work in the simple biological environment, so the greater the ability to act, the larger the scope of application. Moreover, nanozymes are thermally stable, cost-effective in their production and in volume, and can be used to replace and/or supplement the enzymes used in most biomedical applications. More importantly, the catalytic behavior of nanozymes holds a dual advantage; while they mimic the enzymatic strategies found in nature, their chemistry and structural design can be precisely customized through materials science. This unique combination allows nanozymes to merge enzyme-like selectivity with nanoscale tunability, making them versatile and powerful catalytic agents [40,41].

Biomedical applications of nanozymes

The true worth of nanozymes is that they can convert their design, which is chemical, to biomedical practice. Due to a combination of good catalytic activity and surface chemistry control, nanozymes have found their applications in the sphere of diagnostics, therapeutics, imaging, and regenerative medicine [42]. Nanozymes can respond in a controlled fashion in conditions similar to the physiological environment, unlike the case with natural enzymes, which are limited due to stability and environmental factors. Biosensing and diagnostics

Nanozymes have transformed the biosensing platforms by providing strong, low cost, and highly sensitive bio-sensing enzymes. Their peroxidase- and oxidase-like activities are often used in colorimetric, fluorescent, and electronic chemical assays. One such system is the catalysis chromogenic loss of substrates with the use of Fe3O4 nanoparticles in presence of H2O2 to produce a visible coloration that can be measured to detect glucose, cholesterol, or cancer biomarkers. Nanozyme-based sensors are more stable, reproducible, and flexible than the older ones that rely on natural enzymes. Nanozymes can work in harsh conditions, maintain functionality during extended storage, and can be integrated into a point-of-care diagnosis machine [43]. Other applications include pathogen or viral nucleic acid or exosome biomarker detection, where rapid and sensitive detection is required. Furthermore, nanozyme systems can be integrated with either microfluidic systems or paper-based assays to produce portable and field deployable diagnostic platforms [44]. Likewise, molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP)-coupled nanozymes have been developed as a promising platform to enhance the substrate specificity under physiological conditions. A smartphone assisted MIP-nanozyme system was developed which overcomes the limitations of one of the most important applications of existing Fe₃O₄ nanozymes, i.e., glucose detection under neutral pH conditions [45]. To conclude, the nanozyme-based biosensing platforms are more stable than their enzyme counterparts, more cost-effective to produce, tunable catalytic activity, and more durable to use. In addition, their potential for fast and highly sensitive disease detection facilitates the use of colorimetric, electrochemical, and point-of-care detection methods. Nanozymes driven diagnostic systems are expected to play an increasingly vital part in the development and implementation of precision medicine and early Cancer detection, as engineering techniques continue to evolve over time.

Nanozyme clinical potential

Nanozymes have strong possible uses in therapy interventions, particularly in the treatment of cancer and infectious diseases. Using nanozymes, it becomes possible to generate ROS selectively in sick tissues by virtue of their catalysis. As an example, endogenous H2O2 can be transformed into hydroxyl radicals through nanozymes made of iron oxide or Pt, induce oxidative stress, and thereafter cancer cell death in a process referred as chemo-dynamic therapy. Similarly, nanozymes possess a high bactericidal ability. They are able to interfere with the proliferation and survival of bacteria either via the production of ROS or the destruction of biofilm matrix as an alternative to conventional antibiotics. These characteristics may be especially useful when considering multidrug-resistant pathogens [46]. Other than ROS-mediated killing, nanozymes that can be used to control the TME, or microbial metabolism are also known to enhance the efficacy of combination therapies. In addition, nanozymes can be used as antioxidant drugs. The two nanozymes, CeO2 or manganese, destroy superoxide and H2O2, which destroy tissues leading to neurodegeneration, cardiovascular disease, and inflammation. The versatility of nanozymes in therapies results from their ability to generate or quench ROS, depending on their design [47]. Collectively, the findings revealed the considerable translation value of nanozymes to the field of modern therapeutics. The ability to regulate oxidative stress, remodel the TME and selectively produce ROS has made nanozymes appear to be promising agents for the treatment of cancer and the management of inflammatory diseases. Moreover, the combination of catalytic therapy with targeted delivery and multimodal therapeutic approaches may further expand their future clinical applicability in more innovative ways.

Imaging and theranostic opportunities

The use of nanozymes as a universal imaging and theranostic system, or a system that combines a diagnosis and treatment, is gaining popularity. Alloys and metal oxide nanozymes enhance magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) contrast and, at the same time, possess therapeutic characteristics. An example is iron oxide nanozymes, which are applicable as MRI contrast agents and have the ability to generate ROS in tumor tissues. Photoacoustic imaging is another area where nanozymes perform better. Au or Pd nanozymes possess a high light absorption capacity because light images of tissues are very resolute, so these nanozymes can be utilized to target a localized therapy. Nanozymes with catalytic activity combined with diagnostic properties also offer the possibility to monitor the therapeutic activity of drugs in real-time, acquire more control over interventions, and minimize side effects [48]. Overall, all the nanozyme-based theranostic systems open a new promising possibility to combine within a single multifunctional platform, the capabilities of diagnosing, imaging, and therapy of disease. This simultaneous imaging-guided therapeutic ability benefits treatment monitoring, accurate targeting and provides support for personalized therapeutic interventions. These advances can help to improve the care of patients with cancer, in situations where the real-time evaluation of the response to therapy is still a big clinical challenge.

Tissue engineering and regenerative medicine

Beyond their roles in diagnostics and therapy, nanozymes also hold great promise in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, offering new possibilities for repairing and restoring damaged tissues. The incorporation of nanozymes into scaffolds or hydrogels plays a vital role in regulating the oxidative microenvironment, as controlling oxidative stress is crucial for effective tissue repair and cell survival. One such instance is the application of SOD- or catalase-like nanozymes in wound dressings, which minimizes the redox stress leading to delayed healing and decreased inflammation [49]. The concentration of ROS can be controlled when repairing bone and cartilage, which can be established by using nanozyme containing scaffolds that assist in legitimating the multiplication of stem cells. Future potential and future applications

The remarkable versatility of nanozymes continues to inspire their exploration across diverse biomedical applications. Recent studies have highlighted the use of nanozymes in gene-editing applications, where they not only help preserve nucleic acids under degrading conditions but also facilitate their efficient delivery into cells. Moreover, there are other immunomodulators in which ROS-producing or scavenging nanozymes are utilized to regulate immune responses in immunotherapy of cancer or autoimmune disease. Nanozymes can also be incorporated into personalized medicine to enhance the design of chemicals, whereby specific nanozyme systems can be designed to diagnose, monitor, and treat diseases. Nanozymes represent a dynamic interface between chemistry, biology, and medicine, where their design and development are increasingly guided by AI through predictive modeling and high-throughput synthesis [52].

Finally, yet importantly, nanozymes cannot be called as analogs of enzymes; nanozymes are multifunctional chemical agents and their biomedical applications are numerous. The biggest opportunities available to nanozymes are in biosensing, therapeutic intervention, imaging, and regenerative medicine due to their stability and tunable activity and due to their multifunctional properties [53]. The growing potential of nanozymes originates from the way that chemical design principles are turning laboratory innovations into real medical benefits, showing how nanoscale science can truly make a difference in healthcare.

Challenges in clinical translation

Although nanozymes have massive potential, the biggest challenge continues to be the difficulty in translating the potential of nanozymes into clinical use. Another issue of paramount importance is the toxicity and biocompatibility of nanozymes. Though numerous types of nanozymes, such as iron oxide or CeO2 nanoparticles, are regarded as non-toxic in-vitro, their long-term in-vivo behavior is yet to be fully understood. Some of the safety issues that must be taken into consideration are nanozyme accumulation in the body, such as in the liver, spleen, or kidney, and the potential disturbance of normal metabolism. To approve nanozymes for clinical use, experimentation on the immune response to nanozymes, nanozyme biodistribution, and nanozyme clearance over time is necessary [54].

Another issue is permanency and the control of catalytic action in complex biological conditions. Nanozymes must maintain their functional activity with proteins, salts, and varying pH, which can stimulate the aggregation of surfaces, rendering nanozymes ineffective. Their catalytic activities are often broader than those of natural enzymes. While this can be advantageous for certain applications, it also raises the risk of off-target reactions that might harm healthy tissues or disrupt normal biochemical pathways. Another important challenge lies in controlling when and where nanozymes act within the body. Achieving high specificity and selectivity therefore remains a central focus in both the chemical design and engineering of nanozymes. The evolution of natural enzymes has led to the development of active sites that are very selective and that transform specific substrates [55]. On the other hand, nanozymes are usually generally surface-effective or redox-based and are not always as conversant as their molecular analogs specifically as their molecular counterparts. The decrease in specificity compared to natural enzymes might render nanozymes less effective in applications that require extremely specific, targeted action on the substrate (e.g., metabolic regulation or gene-targeted therapies). Overcoming these limitations by developing nanozymes with specific ligands, molecular imprinting, or stimuli-responsive coatings is an active and dynamic area of research [56].

Limitations of nanozymes in cancer research

The concept of nanozymes in oncology is very promising but still there are few limitation factors that restrict the nanozyme application in cancer research and in clinical oncology. A key problem is targeting the tumors to sufficient specifically in highly heterogeneous TME. Unfortunately, most nanozymes depend on the activity of redox reactions that result in ROS formation on the tumor surface around normal tissues and cause oxidative damage to them [57]. Furthermore, catalytic efficiency and therapeutic activity can be changed with different situations of the tumor, including pH, hypoxia, glutathione concentration, and/or metabolic heterogeneity. Another important issue is limited knowledge of the distribution, biodegradability, immunogenicity, and systemic toxicity of nanozymes after repeated administration. Furthermore, inconsistent catalytic activity under physiological conditions and the difficulties concerning production, reproducibility, and regulatory standardization of the catalysts are significant hurdles to the use of these catalysts in the clinical perspective.

To address the issues mentioned above, it is crucial to develop nanozymes capable of targeting the TME and to establish a standardized framework for the translational evaluation of cancer nanotherapeutics, ensuring the development of safer and more effective cancer nanotherapeutic platforms [58].

The challenge in the production and reproducibility of nanozymes is also one of the main issues. Nanozyme size, shape, composition, and surface chemistry have a significant impact on catalytic activity. Gross variations in the activity of nanozymes can be created by even minute changes in manufacture steps and therefore, it is challenging to manufacture in large quantities. Synthesis procedures, batch-to-batch consistency guidelines, as well as quality control procedures must be standardized before the product is regulated and introduced to the clinic. Finally, nanozymes also face regulatory and translational deterrents. Unlike drugs or biologics, nanozymes are neither purely in materials science nor in medicine, so it is challenging to categorize them in vitro when conducting a clinical trial. Preclinical testing is also required to detail the safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy that must be delivered by the regulatory authorities [59]. Furthermore, approval is more complicated because of the potential application of nanozymes as a combination therapy or multifunction platform. In general, nanozymes have massive potential in diagnostics, therapy, and regenerative medicine, but the use of nanozymes in the clinical setting requires special attention regarding their toxicity, specificity, and stability in compliance with regulations. The disciplines of chemistry, materials science, biology, and medicine will have to join their efforts to come up with safe and effective technologies that can utilize nanozymes [60]. Although there has been great progress on the development of nanozymes, several issues need to be addressed for their potential wide clinical applications. Some issues, like long-term toxicity, catalytic specificity, pharmacokinetics, biodegradation, large-scale reproducibility, and regulatory standardization are still not fully answered. In addressing these challenges, closely coordinated interdisciplinary collaboration of chemists, materials scientists, biologists, clinicians, and regulatory scientists is needed to ensure clinically translatable, safe, and effective nanozyme platforms emerge that have the potential to impact clinical settings. Future perspectives

The future of nanozymes is the design and synthesis of technologies based on chemistry and rationality. Nowadays, computational chemistry and AI have reached a stage where scientists can predict how catalytic reactions, enzyme reaction and even simplify the design of nanozyme even before the actual synthesis. The prediction capability could help to speed up the development of more active, more selective, and more biocompatible nanozymes without having to experiment by trial and error [61]. Another possibility being considered is to use nanozymes in synthetic biology and smart nanomedicine. In addition, hybrid systems are possible which take advantage of both nanozyme catalysis and responsive drug delivery and gene circuits that allow for the biological pathways to be controlled in real-time. For instance, disease-specific biomarkers can trigger the release of ROS from nanozymes, thereby achieving therapeutic efficacy when required, and minimizing off-target toxicity [62]. The potential future of nanozyme chemistry also could be shaped by sustainability. The popular alternative to the use of toxic solvents in the environment is the development of environmentally friendly solvents that are prepared using green synthetic procedures, for example, by means of plant extracts, biomolecules or solvents that are ecologically friendly. These strategies could enhance the biocompatibility of nanozymes and may even make them large-scale manufacture possible [63]. Furthermore, tumor-selective, biosafety, catalytic specificity, and clinically scalable manufacturing are important aspects of the future nanozyme systems, which need to be developed with a particular focus on precision oncology application.

Another frontier for the use of nanozymes is in personalized medicine. Because of the potential to chemically alter nanozymes to target specific substrates, tissues, or patient conditions, patient-specific diagnostic and therapeutic platforms can be developed using nanozymes. For instance, consider a nanozyme designed to detect a tumor biomarker in a patient’s blood, generate therapeutic ROS specifically within tumor tissues, and simultaneously monitor treatment progress through imaging, all integrated within a single, chemically engineered nanoplatform [64]. Finally, integrating nanozyme research with emerging technologies such as immunotherapy, regenerative medicine, and wearable biosensors is paving the way for a new generation of versatile biomedical devices. Despite the fact that challenges still exist, the combination of chemical accuracy, engineering on a nanoscale, and biological insights has made the next decade a thrilling one in terms of nanozyme innovation [65] (Fig. 3). Overall, future nanozyme studies will be dominated by the growth of AI-enabled design, the precision engineering of surfaces, smart responsive nanocoatings, and the synthesis of environmentally friendly nanomaterials. Nanozymes currently have a vast number of biological applications, which may be greatly expanded through combinations with immunotherapies, wearable biosensors, regenerative medicine, and personalized oncology. Conclusion

Nanozymes are a paradigm shift in the field of chemistry and life sciences. The chemical tunability of nanomaterials-based catalysts has enabled us to build enzyme-like systems with catalytic precision similar to natural enzymes, with enhanced stability, versatility, and multi-functionality not found in nature. Such engineered nanotubes have shown great potential in various applications such as bio-sensing, therapeutics, imaging, and tissue engineering; they have shown great promise for clinical translation in the real world [66]. The Nanozymes lack certain specificities, stability, toxicity, and are not easy to regulate, although the current trend of rational-designed Nanozymes, surface functionalization, and hybrid systems, overcomes the limitations to a certain extent. With rational design, one can more precisely control the chemical and biomedical properties of nanozymes, thus making the transition to clinical use a step closer [67]. Nanozymes can change the face of diagnostics and therapeutics, and with the ongoing innovations, they will be able to deliver catalytic systems that outperform natural enzymes in terms of power, accuracy, and adaptability. The next revolution in nanoengineering will come from the unifying of materials chemistry, biology, and medicine to bring out-of-the-box scientific concepts into tomorrow's nanomedicine.
