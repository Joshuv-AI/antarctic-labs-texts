Effect of endodontically treated teeth on prosthetically guided orthodontics with clear aligners: a case series
Authors: Zhang-Yu Li, Miao-Zhu Lin, Yue Wang, Xin-Ru Cai, Xiao-Dong Wang, Xue-Qing Huang
Published: 2024

Background

The orderly dentition and appropriate spaces are essential to achieve ideal esthetic rehabilitation [1–3]. When malocclusion exists, a single prosthetic solution alone may demand excessive tooth preparation, and result in compromised esthetic and functional outcomes [4–7]. Therefore, multidisciplinary management including prosthetically guided orthodontics (PGO) is necessary to correct mild malocclusion before prosthetic treatment in complicated cases [4, 5, 8, 9].

Unlike conventional orthodontic treatment, only minor tooth movement is designed in PGO according to the requirement of the following prosthetic rehabilitation, including minimal or no movement of posterior teeth [4–7]. Therefore, the duration of treatment is shortened, which is more acceptable to patients [4–7].

For better appearance during the treatment, clear aligner treatment (CAT) is often preferred over conventional fixed appliance treatment (FAT) with metal brackets [5–7]. Invisalign Go (Align Technology, Santa Clara, California, USA) is a clear aligner system specially designed for general practitioners involved in restorative dentistry [6, 7], which allows a short-term CAT mainly involving the movement of anterior teeth in both arches to treat a specific range of malocclusion, while utilizing posterior teeth as an anchorage and maintain their position in the arch in the condition that their occlusion and appearance are considered acceptable [5–7]. It has been proven that the PGO performed with Invisalign Go can achieve generally satisfactory outcomes and reduce the treatment period [5–7].

However, the efficiency and accuracy of CAT are relatively inferior compared to FAT, which may result in relatively unstable orthodontic tooth movement (OTM), leading to a discrepancy between the predicted and achieved results [10, 11]. Moreover, this discrepancy may also be triggered by specific variables in dental and periodontal conditions, such as endodontically treated teeth (ETT) [12–16]. For conventional CAT, the influence of ETT can be regarded as clinically insignificant [17]. Nonetheless, it is still unclear whether a similar result can be provided by PGO with clear aligners, in which only limited movements are designed. Therefore, this study presented series cases to reveal the potential effect of ETT on PGO with clear aligners.

Case presentation

Clinical case 1

A 29-year-old female complained of the unesthetic color of an upper incisor since childhood and misaligned anterior teeth. Her #21 and #46 (The Fédération Dentaire Internationale tooth-numbering system) received endodontic treatment due to pulpitis before 2 and 12 months, respectively. Physical and radiograph examination revealed no visible fracture line or resorption of the #21 and #46 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The frontal view revealed acceptable facial symmetry, balanced facial thirds, and anterior teeth that were not visible at rest (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). The side view showed a convex profile with a nasolabial angle of 66° (Supplementary Fig. 2C). There were well-defined pitted surfaces and yellowish-brown discoloration on the patient’s #21, along with chalk areas on the other upper anterior teeth intra-orally. The #11 and #21 were retroclined with an anterior deep overbite and normal overjet (Fig. 1A). Both upper and lower anterior teeth were slightly crowded. The #46 was restored with a porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) crown which had normal occlusal contacts (Fig. 1B). The molar relationships were Angle’s Class I on both sides.

Intraoral photographs and digital simulation results in Case 1. (A) Pre-treatment photograph showed discolored #21 with mild malocclusion. (B) The normal occlusal relationship between the #46 and opposite teeth before PGO. (C, D) Digital simulation results. (E) Dentition after PGO. (F) The occlusal contact loss between the #46 and opposite teeth after PGO. (G) Restorations for the #21. (H) Restorations for the #46

Excessive hard tissue would be removed if the malocclusion were corrected by a prosthetic approach alone. Therefore, a single prosthetic solution was considered but excluded. A treatment plan including PGO and prosthetic rehabilitation was suggested and accepted. The following PGO objectives were established: (1) Restore the normal inclination of upper anterior teeth; (2) Correct the anterior deep overbite; (3) Eliminate anterior teeth crowding.

After PGO, the dentition was orderly with a normal anterior overbite, while the #46 and the opposite teeth lost occlusal contacts (Fig. 1C-F). The #46 was intruded, and other posterior teeth maintained their position in the arch (Fig. 1C-F). The patient refused additional orthodontic treatment and wished to replace the PFM crown on #46. Therefore, the #46 and #21 were restored with a zirconia full crown and a lithium disilicate ceramic crown, respectively (Fig. 1G, H). The post-treatment extraoral photographs showed a satisfactory esthetic outcome (Supplementary Fig. 2D-F).

Clinical case 2

A 25-year-old female complained of unesthetic morphology of the upper lateral incisors and spaces among anterior teeth. The space dimension was stable in the past three years. Her #25 received endodontic treatment due to pulpitis 3 years ago. Physical and radiograph examination revealed no visible fracture line or resorption of the #25 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The frontal view revealed acceptable facial symmetry, balanced facial thirds, and anterior teeth that were not visible at rest (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). The side view showed a straight profile with a nasolabial angle of 86° (Supplementary Fig. 4C). There were scatter spaces among maxillary anterior teeth (Fig. 2A). Teeth #12 and #22 were conic-shaped. The #25 showed normal occlusal contacts. (Fig. 2B). The molar relationships were Angle’s Class I on both sides with an anterior deep overbite and normal overjet (Fig. 2A).

Intraoral photographs and digital simulation results in Case 2. (A) Conic-shaped #12 and #22 with scatter spaces among upper anterior teeth. (B) The normal occlusal relationship of left posterior teeth. (C, D) The width-to-length ratios of the #12 and #22 before and after a prosthetic solution alone. (E, F) Digital simulation results. (G) Dentition after PGO. (H) The occlusal contact loss between the #25 and opposite teeth after PGO. (I, J) Restorations for the #12 and #22

The width-to-length ratios of the #11 and #21 (89%) were slightly higher than the generally accepted esthetic range (75%~85%) (Fig. 2C) [18, 19]. It was regarded as acceptable during the consultation with the patient. However, it would increase to 95% if the diastema were closed by veneers, which may not be desirable (Fig. 2D). Therefore, a treatment plan including PGO and prosthetic rehabilitation was suggested and accepted. The following PGO objectives were established: (1) Rearrange the spaces between anterior teeth, including closing the spaces and providing appropriate spaces for porcelain veneers of the #12 and #22; (2) Correct the anterior deep overbite.

After PGO, the midline diastema was closed with a normal anterior overbite (Fig. 2E-H). The spaces between upper lateral incisors and central incisors, and the spaces between upper lateral incisors and canines, were 0.9 mm on both sides (Fig. 2G). While other teeth had been moved to the designed position, intrusion of the #25 and the opposite teeth was detected (Fig. 2E-H). However, the patient was generally satisfied with the current outcome, refused additional orthodontic treatment, and requested to maintain the status quo due to the uncertain prognosis of the #25 with a separated endodontic instrument (Supplementary Fig. 3). Subsequently, the #12 and #22 were restored by porcelain veneers to close the anterior spaces (Fig. 2I, J). The post-treatment extraoral photographs showed a satisfactory esthetic outcome (Supplementary Fig. 4D-F).

Clinical case 3

A 27-year-old male patient complained of swollen and bleeding gingiva on his upper incisors and sought for esthetic rehabilitation. His upper incisors were restored by PFM crowns without endodontic treatment 10 years ago due to caries and midline diastema (Fig. 3A). Persisted swollen and bleeding gingiva were perceptible ever since the restoration and deteriorated recently (Fig. 3A). After removing the PFM crowns, the #11 and #21 showed discolored appearances without pulpal vitality (Fig. 3B). The #11 and #21 were endodontically treated and restored with post-core and temporary crowns (Fig. 3C). Physical and radiograph examination revealed no visible fracture line or resorption of the #11 and #21 (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Photographs in Case 3. (A) PFM crowns on upper incisors with hyperplastic and bleeding gingiva. (B) Discolored #11 and #21. (C) Temporary restoration for the upper incisors. (D) Incisal edge level. (E, F) Unesthetic morphology or scatter spaces with prosthetic solution alone. (G) Dentition after PGO. (H) Final restorations for the upper incisors

The frontal view revealed slight mandibular asymmetry, balanced facial thirds, and anterior teeth that were not visible at rest (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). The side view showed a convex profile with a nasolabial angle of 82° (Supplementary Fig. 6C). There were scattered spaces among maxillary anterior teeth intra-orally (Fig. 3C). The molar relationships were Angle’s Class I on both sides with an anterior deep overbite and normal overjet (Fig. 3C).

The previous restorations on #11 and #21 had width-to-length ratios of 90% and 93%, respectively, whereas crowns with esthetic width-to-length ratios (85%) would inevitably result in scatter spaces when the gingival and incisal edge levels of temporary crowns were designed appropriately (Fig. 3D-F). Therefore, a treatment plan including PGO and prosthetic rehabilitation instead of a prosthetic solution alone was suggested and accepted. The following objectives were established: (1) Close the scattered spaces among upper anterior teeth; (2) Correct the anterior deep overbite.

After PGO, spaces between the anterior teeth were closed with a normal anterior overbite and overjet (Fig. 3G). All the teeth had been moved to the designed position (Fig. 3G). Temporary restorations were subsequently replaced with zirconia full crowns (Fig. 3H). The post-treatment extraoral photographs showed a satisfactory esthetic outcome (Supplementary Fig. 6D-F).

Both conventional orthodontic treatment and PGO were provided in all 3 cases. The patients chose PGO because of the relatively short treatment period, less intervention, and acceptable facial profile. For esthetics and comfort during the treatment, the Invisalign Go system was used. Invisalign provided digital model analyses and generated detailed orthodontic treatment plans. Since PGO in these cases had little effect on the facial profile, cephalometric analysis was not performed.

Removable retainers were delivered to all patients after the prosthodontic rehabilitation. No relapse of malocclusion, root resorption, or prosthesis failure was detected during the 12-month follow-up.

Discussion and conclusions

In the present study, teeth alignment was significantly improved by PGO with clear aligners in all three cases, contributing to the final esthetic and functional outcomes. All the anterior teeth, including ETT, were moved to the designed position successfully. However, posterior ETT did not maintain their position as scheduled. On the contrary, intrusion of ETT was detected unexpectedly, suggesting that ETT may affect the result of PGO with clear aligners.

It is not always easy to maintain teeth position, as the biomechanics of CAT are more intricate compared to conventional FAT [11, 20–22]. Fixed appliances exert orthodontic force on specific points on each tooth’s surface, allowing for more predictable and controllable magnitude and direction of forces [10, 21, 22]. Conversely, the contact point, direction, and amount of orthodontic force are ever-changing during the CAT, making it relatively elusive [11, 12]. It may have an effect on posterior teeth that are not intended to be moved, such as the #46 in Case 1 and the #25 in Case 2. Additionally, no attachment was placed on these teeth, which may make their position less controllable.

CAT is also limited by the properties of clear aligner materials, particularly in terms of stress relaxation [10, 23]. Stress relaxation refers to the time-dependent decrease in stress under constant strain [10]. While maintaining a constant force is desirable in orthodontics, current aligner materials do not achieve this effectively [10]. Unlike a nickel-titanium archwire that remains active for weeks, clear aligners show a significant drop in force [10]. This instability of orthodontic force limits the efficiency and accuracy of CAT [10, 23]. As a result, additional aligners are often needed for further refinement [11].

In present cases, posterior vital pulp teeth (VPT) were not affected by these factors significantly, whereas posterior ETT showed a different result. Numerous studies and clinical practices have demonstrated that ETT can be moved using either FAT or CAT [17]. This interaction results in different effects of orthodontic treatment on ETT and VPT [24–27]. Current studies primarily focus on orthodontic-induced external root resorption, which is associated with prolonged treatment, strong correctional forces, root abnormalities, a history of tooth trauma, and other factors [13]. However, there is a controversy regarding the difference in the amount of root resorption between ETT and VPT [24–26]. Some studies suggest that ETT exhibits less root resorption than VPT, while others show no significant differences [17, 24–26]. In present cases, no significant root resorption was detected, whereas occlusal contact loss of posterior ETT occurred, which also reveals a difference between the OTM of ETT and VPT.

OTM is achieved by remodeling the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone in response to orthodontic force, which can lead to bone resorption on the pressure side and bone formation on the tension side [13–16]. An appropriate inflammatory response is crucial in the process [13–16]. The mechanical force applied by clear aligners or fixed appliances can induce an aseptic inflammatory response in the periodontal tissues [13]. Inflammatory mediators and cells are involved, leading to subsequent bone resorption and apposition, facilitating OTM [13–16]. After endodontic treatment, the inflammatory response of the tooth is altered, and the OTM is affected as well [28]. The #46 in Case 1 and the #25 in Case 2 were both ETT with a history of pulpitis, which potentially affected the inflammatory response, thereby yielding different results in OTM compared to VPT. These effects may be exacerbated by CAT, resulting in the unintended occlusal contact loss of posterior ETT in present cases. Moving the ETT with clear aligners may not be difficult, but keeping still is.

In addition to the possible postulation discussed above, the immediate cause of the unintended movement should not be ignored either. It is still unclear whether there was a discrepancy between the aligner and posterior teeth and what led to it. Inaccurate scanning or aligner manufacturing could lead to such discrepancy [10, 29]. But this discrepancy was not common in these cases, and no unintended intrusion of other teeth was detected. The crown in Case 1 might be a potential influence factor in orthodontic force delivery [30]. However, the open bite also occurred in Case 2, although there was no restoration on #25. Talens-Cogollos L et al. also reported unplanned molar intrusion after Invisalign treatment [31]. Approximately 74.2% of the patients showed molar intrusion after CAT with Invisalign [31]. They believe that the semipermanent and prolonged interposition of plastic material of the aligner and the effect of muscular strength caused this intrusion [31]. Our study provides another possible direction that the endodontic-orthodontic interactions may be the source of it [32, 33]. Moreover, the gingival margin of aligners can affect the biomechanics of CAT. It is possible that the clear aligners’ inherent gingival width and occlusal narrowness result in a gingival-pushing force contributing to the open bite [12, 34]. It must be noted that only limited cases were included in the current study and the impacts of ETT, and CAT have yet to be definitively confirmed. Further studies with more participants, detailed information, longer follow-ups, precise measurement of aligners, and comparisons with teeth are necessary to provide more clinical evidence and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Within the limitations of this current study, it was concluded that posterior ETT showed unintended occlusal contact loss after PGO with clear aligners, which is a potential risk in the treatment. Patients should be informed, and countermeasures should be proposed.
