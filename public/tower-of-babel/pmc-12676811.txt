3D bioprinted microneedle patches loaded with gambogic acid-iron-doxorubicin nanozymes for postoperative glioma in situ therapy via ferroptosis synergistic chemosensitization
Authors: Menghui Liu, Yang Qiao, Sheng Cheng, Ziqing Su, Ziyi Li, Peng Gao, Xianwen Wang, Qiang Cai, Xingliang Dai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative chemotherapy is essential for glioma treatment but faces two major limitations: the blood-brain barrier (BBB) restricts drug delivery, and tumor chemoresistance reduces therapeutic efficacy. To circumvent these hurdles, we engineered a 3D-bioprinted, sustained-release microneedle patches (MNs@GFD) that can be affixed directly to the postoperative tumor cavity, thereby bypassing the BBB and providing localized, long-term drug delivery. The patch incorporates gambogic acid-iron-doxorubicin nanozymes (GAFe@DOX, GFD) that simultaneously remodel the hypoxic tumor microenvironment via multi-enzyme activity and prime tumor cells for doxorubicin (DOX) cytotoxicity through ferroptosis.ResultsComprehensive characterization confirmed successful construction of MNs@GFD and demonstrated potent catalase-, peroxidase- and oxidase-like activities that alleviated intratumoral hypoxia. Multi-omics profiling revealed that GFD orchestrates a transcriptional program that robustly up-regulated pro-ferroptosis genes (ACSL4, COX2) while suppressing anti-ferroptosis guardians (GPX4, FTH1), leading to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial collapse and concomitant apoptosis. Correspondingly, in vitro assays showed rapid and extensive death of glioma cells exposed to GFD, and in vivo studies in a post-surgical orthotopic model demonstrated that MNs@GFD markedly hindered tumor regrowth without systemic toxicity.ConclusionMNs@GFD integrates localized drug release, BBB bypass, hypoxia relief and ferroptosis-mediated chemosensitization into a single platform, establishing a powerful and clinically translatable postoperative chemotherapeutic strategy against glioma.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03790-4.

Introduction

The current treatment for glioblastoma primarily involves maximal surgical resection [1], supplemented by postoperative radiotherapy combined with temozolomide chemotherapy [2]. However, due to the highly invasive [3] and infiltrative growth characteristics of GBM [4], the tumor extensively infiltrates deep regions with indistinct boundaries from surrounding tissues [5], making complete surgical resection challenging. Residual tumors, stimulated by factors such as hypoxia, proliferate aggressively and are prone to recurrence [6]. Chemotherapy is a critical means of suppressing recurrence, but the highly selective nature of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) hinders effective drug penetration to the tumor site, resulting in drug concentrations far below therapeutic levels and significantly limiting treatment efficacy [7, 8]. Even when limited drugs successfully cross the BBB and reach the tumor target area, GBM-driven chemotherapy resistance—mediated by hypoxia-induced HIF-1α signaling activation and iron metabolism reprogramming—poses substantial challenges [9–11]. Therefore, effectively delivering drugs across the BBB and counteracting tumor cell-mediated chemotherapy resistance are key to enhancing chemosensitivity and inhibiting tumor recurrence.

Glioma chemotherapy resistance arises from a complex combination of tumor cell-intrinsic mechanisms and support from the tumor microenvironment (TME) [12]. Tumor cells develop resistance by increasing ABC transporter activity to pump out chemotherapy drugs, activating DNA repair pathways such as MGMT to fix drug-induced DNA damage, altering their metabolism to survive oxidative stress, and displaying adaptive plasticity—especially in glioma stem cells, which enhances their resilience [13, 14]. The surrounding TME further worsens resistance through factors like low-oxygen zones and glioma-associated macrophages, which trigger resistance-related genes and encourage abnormal blood vessel formation [15]. Additionally, disruptions in ferroptosis—a form of cell death—interact with traditional resistance pathways, making treatment even more challenging [16]. To improve outcomes, therapeutic strategies must address these interconnected resistance mechanisms.

In recent years, the role of iron-based nanozymes in tumor chemotherapy sensitization has gradually gained attention [17–20]. Iron-based nanozymes can mimic various enzymatic activities such as peroxidase (POD) and glutathione oxidase, triggering an “iron storm” in the tumor microenvironment [21–23]. This continuously catalyzes the Fenton reaction, overcoming the iron death inactivation defect mediated by tumor chemotherapy resistance. Simultaneously, they deplete glutathione (GSH) and directly inhibit GPX4 activity, blocking the chemotherapy resistance-associated antioxidant defense axis (SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4) [24, 25]. However, their application is limited by several factors: single iron-based nanozymes exhibit limited clearance efficiency for tumor cells with intact DNA repair mechanisms [23] or apoptosis sensitivity [26], making it difficult to eradicate heterogeneous tumor populations [27]. Additionally, the passive targeting efficiency of iron-based nanozymes is low, hindering their penetration through dense barriers to reach the tumor core region [28]. New strategies are urgently needed to address these challenges.

The combination of the DNA-damaging drug Doxorubicin (DOX) with iron-based nanozymes holds promise to overcome the above limitations: DOX unique DNA-damaging capability can compensate for the clearance constraints of iron-based nanozymes while simultaneously upregulating intracellular H₂O₂ levels, providing a sustained substrate for the nanozymes [29, 30]. Meanwhile, the nanozyme-driven ferroptosis induces an oxidative storm, depleting the antioxidant compensation triggered by DOX [31]. Together, they create a unique synthetic lethal effect-DOX causes double-strand breaks in tumor cells, while the nanozymes block oxidative repair pathways, ultimately dismantling the tumor’s dual defense system [31, 32]. Furthermore, we introduce gambogic acid to sensitize this effect. Gambogic acid, a ferroptosis sensitizer, can synergize with iron ions to promote the Fenton reaction [33], further amplifying oxidative stress [34]. As a weakly acid-responsive natural polyphenol, it can form coordination bonds with Fe²⁺ via phenolic hydroxyl groups under normal pH conditions, enhancing nanoparticle stability and enabling targeted, efficient release [35].

Traditional drug delivery methods face challenges such as difficulty penetrating the blood-brain barrier [36, 37] and uneven drug distribution [38], while 3D-bioprinted microneedles effectively address these issues [39]. As an emerging minimally invasive drug delivery platform, 3D-bioprinted microneedle technology demonstrates broad application prospects in glioma treatment due to its advantages in penetrating the blood-brain barrier [40], controlled drug release [41], and high bioavailability [42]. For example, Wang et al. (2022) developed a silk fibroin-based heterogeneous microneedle patch (SMN) capable of sequentially releasing three drugs (thrombin, temozolomide, and bevacizumab) in a spatiotemporal manner, directly delivering them to brain tumor sites for GBM treatment. This patch is biocompatible and biodegradable, enabling rapid postoperative hemostasis and long-term chemotherapy drug release, significantly improving drug distribution in tumor tissues and thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy [43]. Meanwhile, Zhang et al. (2024) proposed a fully degradable implantable microneedle (GMAN@CMN) for localized postoperative delivery of glioma-associated macrophage (GAM)-activating nanoparticles. By blocking Siglec10 and inhibiting OSM secretion to reprogram GAMs, combined with the Toll-like receptor agonist CpG to recruit T cells, this approach reversed the immunosuppressive microenvironment in mouse models and significantly suppressed GBM recurrence post-surgery [44].

However, the clinical translation of microneedle technology still faces several challenges, including the complexity of manufacturing processes, cost issues, and patient acceptance [45, 46]. Nevertheless, with advancements in materials science and nanotechnology, the design and manufacturing of microneedles are continuously being optimized to meet clinical needs. The application of 3D bioprinting [47] and nanocarrier integration technologies [46] has improved drug delivery efficiency. For example, 3D bioprinting technology has garnered widespread attention due to its high-dimensional precision, enabling not only the precise fabrication of microneedle structures but also the customization of microneedle shape and size according to individual needs [48, 49]. This technology makes microneedle production more flexible and allows for the effective delivery of drugs directly into deep brain tissues, thereby enhancing drug bioavailability [43]. Additionally, the integration of nanocarriers has significantly improved drug delivery efficacy [50]. By enhancing the solubility and stability of nanocarriers [51], therapeutic outcomes can be further strengthened.

Through 3D-bioprinted microneedle technology, it is possible to personalize the construction based on the irregular residual tumor cavity, custom-printing conical microneedle arrays to achieve full adhesion to complex cavity walls, addressing the “dead space effect” caused by deep glioma infiltration. The rigid polymer (e.g., PLLA/PEGDA) at the microneedle tips [52] can pierce the pia mater to form microchannels, directly bypassing the BBB and delivering drugs to the tumor site [53]. This “physical penetration-spatiotemporal controlled release” synergistic mechanism enables long-term maintenance of local drug concentrations at effective levels, providing an effective strategy for drug delivery.

Based on this, we designed a gambogic acid-iron-doxorubicin nanozyme (GAFe@DOX, GFD) and loaded it onto 3D-bioprinted microneedles for in situ residual cavity therapy of glioma. The microneedles can directly adhere to the surface of the residual tumor cavity, and their rigid microneedle structure effectively penetrates the dense tumor matrix barrier, achieving in situ sustained drug delivery. GAFe@DOX can pH-responsively dissociate in the tumor microenvironment (pH = 6.5–6.8), synchronously releasing GA, Fe³⁺, and DOX. DOX can induce topoisomerase II-mediated apoptosis by intercalating into DNA double strands, while GA and Fe³⁺ synergistically leverage the advantages of iron-based nanozymes to continuously catalyze the Fenton reaction, inducing oxidative stress and overcoming chemotherapy resistance caused by DOX. The three components work together to create a synthetic lethal effect of “ferroptosis-apoptosis.” Characterization experiments confirmed the successful synthesis of MNs@GFD, and hydrogel performance tests verified its uniform nanostructure. Probe experiments evaluated the excellent multi-enzyme activity of MNs@GFD. In vitro experiments demonstrated that GFD significantly enhanced cell-killing efficiency, while in vivo experiments showed that MNs@GFD effectively inhibited postoperative glioma recurrence with good biosafety. This study provides a novel combination therapy strategy with both high efficacy and safety to overcome postoperative chemotherapy resistance in glioma (Fig. 1).

Experimental methods

Materials

The following reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers: Gambogic acid (GA) and ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl₃·6 H₂O) were obtained from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai). Additionally, methanol, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), doxorubicin hydrochloride, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate, o-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride, reduced glutathione (GSH), Ellman’s reagent (DTNB), and catalase were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai). Moreover, photocrosslinkable gelatin (GelMA) and methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) were obtained from Engineering for Life (Suzhou). Additionally, essential cell culture reagents including phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4), DMEM complete medium, trypsin-EDTA digestion solution, CCK-8 viability assay kit, and DAPI nuclear stain were obtained from SparkJade Scientific Co., Ltd (Shandong). Fetal bovine serum was obtained from Wisent Biotech (Nanjing). Moreover, vital staining reagents (Calcein-AM/PI), apoptosis detection kit (Annexin V-FITC/PI), lipid peroxidation probe (BODIPY 581/591 C11), and autophagic flux marker (MDC) were obtained from Bestbio Biotechnology (Shanghai). Reactive oxygen species indicator (DCFH-DA) and mitochondrial membrane potential probe (JC-1) were obtained from Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology (Shanghai).

Preparation of GFD

GFD nanoparticles were synthesized via a modified one - step self - assembly. First, 20 mg FeCl₃·6 H₂O and 100 mg PVP were dissolved in 10 mL methanol with 10-minute stirring. Then, 10 mg GA and 18 mg DOX in 2 mL methanol each were added dropwise to the iron chloride mixture. The mixture was sonicated for 10 min and stirred for 24 h at room temperature. After the reaction, the suspension was dialyzed for 24 h. The nanoparticles were isolated by 20-minute centrifugation at 10,000 × g, washed with ethanol and water three times each, and lyophilized to get dry powder.

During the GFD synthesis process, the stirring speed was maintained at 500 revolutions per minute. The molecular weight critical value of the used dialysis membrane was 7000 KD, and the dialysis time was at least 24 h. During the freeze-drying process, the sample was pre-cooled at −80 °C for 12 h, and then freeze-dried under a pressure of 0.03 mbar for at least 48 h.

Characterization of GFD nanomaterials

The structural and compositional properties of the synthesized GFD were systematically analyzed using advanced characterization techniques. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) imaging was performed with a JEOL-JEM 2100 F instrument to examine the morphological features at the nanoscale level. Elemental distribution was investigated through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping. The chemical composition and oxidation states were quantitatively assessed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Crystalline structure analysis was conducted using an ARL EQUINOX 3000 X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα radiation. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) measurements on a Nexus 670 spectrometer provided information about functional groups through characteristic vibrational modes. For pharmaceutical evaluation, the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity of both gambogic acid (GA) and doxorubicin (DOX) were quantitatively determined by UV-Vis spectrophotometry using a Thermo Scientific Biomate 160 system, enabling optimization of the formulation parameters.

Ion release rate

Prior studies showed PVP - coated nanoparticles’ pH - dependent release and prolonged drug release. We did ion release experiments on GFD under different pH. First, a 100 mg/mL GFD suspension was digested in aqua regia and analyzed by ICP - OES. For pH - responsive release, dialysis media at pH 7.4 and 6.5 were prepared. 1 mL of 1 mg/mL GFD solution was put in dialysis chambers, dialysate was sampled for ICP - OES, and cumulative ion release profile was determined by quantitative analysis.

Evaluation of TMB and POD activity in GFD

TMB and OPD are commonly used probes for detecting POD enzymes [17]. Taking OPD as an example, under pH = 6.5 PBS conditions, the absorbance at 442 nm was measured by UV - visible spectrophotometer. Absorbance of PBS solutions (1 mL, pH = 6.5) with H₂O₂, OPD, GFD, GFD + H₂O₂, and GFD + H₂O₂+ OPD was measured separately. Also, the absorbance of solutions with equal OPD (100 µg/mL), H₂O₂ (1 mM) and gradient GFD, and those with equal OPD (100 µg/mL), GFD (50 mg/mL) and gradient H₂O₂ was measured. For the TMB assay, it was the same except replacing 100 µg/mL OPD with 100 µg/mL TMB and using λ = 650 nm.

Glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity of GFD

The quantification was performed using DTNB as the chromogenic reagent. The experimental conditions were maintained at pH 6.5, with reaction mixtures containing 100 µM reduced glutathione (GSH) and varying concentrations (10, 20, or 30 µg/mL) of GFD. At predetermined intervals (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 h), aliquots were withdrawn and mixed with 50 µM DTNB solution. The resulting chromogenic reaction was monitored by measuring the optical density at 412 nm using UV-Vis spectroscopy.

CAT activity assay in GFD

In brief, the microplate method was employed to determine CAT enzyme activity by measuring the reduction of H₂O₂. The sample was mixed with the assay reagent, and after a 5-minute reaction, a small aliquot of the mixture was combined with a chromogenic reagent. Then, 200 µL was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance (A) was measured at 510 nm. A standard curve was plotted based on absorbance values to calculate the relative CAT activity. Data were compiled and statistically analyzed.

Microneedle fabrication

Using an electronic balance, 500 mg of lyophilized gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA90, 10% w/v), 50 mg of lyophilized methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA, 1% w/v), 25 mg of photoinitiator (LAP) powder, and 5 mg of UV absorber (UVAW) powder were weighed and placed in a 15 mL amber centrifuge tube. A pipette was used to add 10 mL of PBS solution dropwise into the tube, followed by heating in a 37 °C water bath until complete dissolution. A conical microneedle array model (needle height: 800 μm, base diameter: 300 μm, array spacing: 1 mm) was designed using CAD software. A projection-based stereolithography bioprinter (BP8601 Pro) was used to print the microneedles layer-by-layer under optimized parameters (light intensity: 10, exposure time: 18 s, layer height: 50 μm). After printing, the microneedles were removed and sonicated in PBS for 5 min to remove uncured hydrogel.

For the quality control of the microneedles, we used a digital light projection 3D bioprinter. This type of printer uses light of specific wavelengths to irradiate photosensitive materials, triggering chemical reactions that cause the materials to change from liquid to solid. To ensure dimensional accuracy, we measure the length and diameter of the microneedles using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Additionally, we maintain shape consistency by analyzing SEM images of the microneedles through image analysis software and comparing them against pre-designed shape templates. This comprehensive approach allows us to verify both the dimensional accuracy and morphological consistency of the microneedles throughout the fabrication process.

Microneedle characterization

Perform multidimensional characterization of microneedles: Observe surface morphology and internal pore structure via scanning electron microscopy (SEM, acceleration voltage 5 kV), and reconstruct the three-dimensional spatial distribution of fluorescently labeled drugs using confocal microscopy (CLSM). Utilize a universal material testing machine to compress the microneedles at a rate of 0.5 mm/min to 50% strain, calculate the compressive modulus, and simulate the puncture process (using a PDMS membrane as a biomimetic skin to record the penetration threshold force). Quantify the swelling ratio through swelling experiments (dry weight W₀ immersed in PBS for 24 h to obtain wet weight Wₛ) using the formula: (Wₛ - W₀)/W₀ × 100%.

Drug-loaded microneedle release experiment

To investigate drug-loaded microneedles’ release profile, immerse them in 10 mL PBS (pH 6.4 and 7.4) at 37 °C. PBS with pH 7.4 is a common physiological pH value, which can simulate the normal physiological environment in the human body. And pH 6.4 is close to the slightly acidic environment in some local tissues or under certain pathological conditions. By using these two pH values, we can comprehensively evaluate the drug release behavior of drug - loaded microneedles under different physiological and possible pathological states. Withdraw 1 mL supernatant at different time - points and replace with equal - volume PBS. Measure release by UV spectrophotometry, repeat 3 times. Place a full piece of microneedles in 10 mL PBS (pH 6.4 and 7.4), shake at 150 rpm and 37 °C. Collect 2 mL liquid at intervals (4 h, then 12 h after 24 h, 24 h after 72 h), centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 3 min, and measure the supernatant by UV. Dilute samples two - fold after 24 h to avoid excessive absorbance, and calculate the cumulative release amount.

Rheological measurement

The viscoelastic properties of the multicomponent hydrogel were quantitatively assessed through oscillatory rheological analysis using a rotational rheometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Following UV-induced cross-linking, rheological characterization was conducted under controlled conditions with 1% strain amplitude at physiological temperature (37°C). Frequency-dependent measurements were obtained by applying angular frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 10 radians per second to evaluate both elastic (G’) and viscous (G”) moduli.

Hemolysis assay

To assess GFD’s in vitro hemocompatibility, we did a hemolysis test. Fresh murine whole blood was 10 - fold diluted with PBS, erythrocytes isolated by triple 10 - min centrifugation at 10,000 ×g and resuspended in PBS. 200 µL of erythrocyte suspension was mixed with 800 µL of PBS with different GFD concentrations or microneedle extracts. Deionized water and PBS were controls. After 4 - hour incubation, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 5 min. Hemoglobin release was measured by reading the OD of 100 µL supernatant at 577 nm with a microplate reader.

Cell lines

The GL261 murine glioma cell line and mouse hippocampal neuron cells (HT22) were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Repository. These cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotic mixture under standard culture conditions (21% O₂, 5% CO₂).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic effects of GFD nanocells were assessed in vitro using HT22 cultures. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and allowed to attach overnight. Following incubation, the cultures were exposed to different concentrations of both GFD solution and soluble hydrogel microneedle extracts for 24 h. Cell viability was subsequently quantified through CCK-8 analysis.

Material endocytosis experiment

GL261 cells were seeded in 35 mm confocal dishes at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells per dish and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h until reaching approximately 80% confluency. The material was stained with Cy5.5 dye by adding 5 µL of Cy5.5 dye to 10 mL of 50 µg/mL GFD, followed by stirring for 5 min. The dye solution was then added to the confocal dishes (800 µL per dish), and cells were observed and imaged at different time points using a confocal microscope.

Cell scratch assay

GL261 cells were plated in 6 - well plates at 5 × 10⁵ cells/well. After 12 - hour incubation, they reached confluence. A linear scratch was made for mechanical wounding, detached cells were washed with PBS, and cultures were kept in serum - depleted medium. Four conditions were evaluated: untreated control, GAFe (GF), Doxorubicin (DOX), and combined GAFe + DOX (GFD). Working concentrations were 50 µg/mL for GAFe and GFD, 40 µg/mL for DOX. Cellular migration was assessed 24 h post - treatment by time - lapse microscopy.

Live/Dead assay

To assess the cytotoxic effects of GFD on glioma cells, we employed the Calcein-AM/PI dual fluorescence viability assay. GL261 cells were cultured in 35 mm confocal dishes at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells per well and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator until reaching 80% confluency (approximately 12 h). Following this, cellular treatments were administered for 6 h using identical experimental conditions and dosage regimens as previously described for the wound healing assay. After treatment, cells were incubated with Calcein-AM/PI staining solution under light-protected conditions at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by microscopic analysis using confocal laser scanning microscopy.

Colony formation assay

Log - phase cells from each group were digested into single - cell suspensions with 0.25% trypsin, serially diluted in triplicate. 800 cells/well were seeded in six - well plates, shaken, and 2 mL complete medium added. After attachment next day, materials (same conc. as scratch assay) were added and incubated for 12 h. Medium was replaced with 2 ml fresh medium. Cells were cultured for 2 weeks with medium change every 3 days. Culture ended when colonies formed. Supernatant was discarded, cells washed with PBS, fixed with 5% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet for 30 min, rinsed and air - dried. Colonies were manually counted and data statistically analyzed.

ROS probe detection

Intracellular ROS production was monitored with DCFH - DA. GL261 cells were plated in confocal dishes, adhered for 24 h at 37 °C. After incubation, they received the same 12 - h experimental treatments as in the viability assay. Then, the medium was replaced with 10 µM DCFH - DA - supplemented medium and incubated for 20 min. Fluorescence imaging via a confocal microscope was done to visualize and quantify ROS.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

To assess alterations in mitochondrial transmembrane potential, we employed JC-1 fluorescent dye staining. GL261 glioma cells were plated in confocal imaging chambers and allowed to adhere for 24 h, achieving approximately 80% monolayer coverage. Following various experimental interventions lasting 12 h, cellular samples were processed for JC-1 staining following the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence microscopy was conducted using a laser scanning confocal system, with all staining steps carefully maintained at 4 °C to preserve mitochondrial integrity.

Lipid peroxidation detection

To assess ferroptosis, we utilized C11-BODIPY, a well-established fluorescent indicator for monitoring lipid peroxidation within cells. GL261 cell lines were plated in confocal imaging chambers and allowed to adhere for 4 h, achieving approximately 80% monolayer coverage. Following this attachment period, the cells underwent various experimental treatments for 24 h. Subsequent to treatment, cellular samples were processed following the manufacturer’s protocol provided with the lipid peroxidation detection assay. Fluorescence visualization was performed employing laser scanning confocal microscopy to obtain high-resolution cellular images.

Western blot (WB) analysis

Protein isolation & quantification: Cellular proteins isolated by homogenization in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors, and BCA assay for concentration. Electrophoretic separation & immunoblotting: 30 µg protein samples resolved on 12% SDS - PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes. Immunodetection: Membranes blocked with 5% non - fat dry milk, probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, then secondary antibodies at RT. Washed with TBST between incubations. Signal detection & quantification: Protein bands visualized by chemiluminescence, analyzed by Image Lab, using GAPDH as loading control.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

For immunofluorescence analysis, cell samples underwent fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min) followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min). Non-specific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for one hour. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C using a 1:500 dilution, followed by one-hour room temperature incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies in light-protected conditions. Three phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes were implemented between each procedural step. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for five minutes before applying antifade mounting medium. Fluorescent images were acquired using confocal microscopy, with subsequent quantitative analysis conducted through ImageJ software.

Bio-electron microscopy

To investigate the effects of GFD incubation on mitochondria and the induction of ferroptosis, we performed bio-transmission electron microscopy imaging on GL261 cells in vitro. Cells were seeded in six-well plates, treated with different formulations for 12 h, rapidly fixed, scraped, centrifuged, and then fixed again. Subsequently, samples were imaged using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7800, Japan).

mRNA sequencing analysis

We used GL261 cell cultures to perform RNA - seq analysis for evaluating gene expression under different treatments. After seeding cells and applying compounds, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol. Personalbio Co., Ltd did downstream processing. Gene expression was normalized by FPKM. Computational biology involved KEGG pathway analysis and Gene Ontology annotation to interpret expression changes.

Evaluation of in vivo therapeutic effects

BALB/c nude mice (6 - week - old females, 18–20 g) were used as the animal model, housed in Anhui Med Univ’s Experimental Animal Center under SPF conditions. Experiments were approved by the university’s Ethics Committee. An orthotopic glioma xenograft model was established by intracranial inoculation of Gl261 - luc cells (5 × 105 cells, PBS). Tumor resection was performed on tumor-bearing mice on the 10th day after tumor implantation to create a residual cavity. Mice were divided into four groups (n = 5) and treated with GFD@MNs in the cavity: NC, MNs - only, GAFe@MNs, GFD@MNs. Intracranial recurrence was monitored on surgery day, day 3, 7, and 14. Body weight was recorded every two days, and dead mice were weighed on death day. Remaining mice were euthanized on day 14, dissected, and sections were stained (H&E, Ki67, etc.) and imaged with a Leica DMI8 microscope.

Biosafety evaluation

All animal - related experiments followed Anhui Medical University’s ethical standards and got protocol approval. Hematological and biochemical analyses included RBC, WBC, etc., and markers of hepatic and renal functions. These hematological and biochemical analyses were all conducted 14 days after the in situ tumor surgery in mice. Main organ specimens underwent histopathological examination by hematoxylin - eosin staining.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative results were from at least three samples. Graphs were made with Origin 2024 and GraphPad Prism 10, and statistical data were analyzed. Quantitative data were collected from at least three independent samples. For the comparison of means between two groups, Student’s test was used for statistical analysis. For comparisons of three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A p value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant, and it was marked with an asterisk (*) in the figure (*p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

Synthesis, characterization, and pH-responsive drug release properties of GFD

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) results revealed that the GFD nanomaterials exhibited spherical particles (Fig. 2c, d) with uniform dispersion and a particle size of approximately 20–40 nm (Fig. 2h). The Fe₃O₄ crystalline core displayed a clear and uniform morphology with consistent dimensions, and distinct lattice fringes were observable, indicating excellent crystallinity. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results (Fig. e, Fig. z) showed Fe 2p peaks at 710.8 eV (Fe³⁺) and 713.5 eV (Fe²⁺), confirming the coexistence of Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺. The C 1 s peaks at 284.8 eV (C-C/C-H), 286.5 eV (C-O), and 288.3 eV (C = O) corresponded to the carboxyl groups of GA and the aromatic rings of DOX. The N 1 s peak at 401.2 eV (N-H) originated from the amino group of DOX. The valence states of all elements in GFD matched expectations, ensuring material stability and functionality. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis indicated characteristic peaks at 2θ = 30.1° (220), 35.5° (311), and 43.2° (400), aligning with the standard Fe₃O₄ spectrum (JCPDS No. 19–0629), confirming the magnetic core (Fig. 2f). The diffraction peaks of GFD closely matched theoretical values, further verifying the successful preparation of the nanomaterials and their specific crystal structure. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) revealed peaks at 3440 cm⁻¹ (O-H/N-H stretching vibration, PVP and GA), 1663 cm⁻¹ (C = O stretching vibration, carboxyl groups of GA), 1426 cm⁻¹ (C-H bending vibration, methyl groups of DOX), and 1288 cm⁻¹ (C-N stretching vibration, amine groups of DOX) (Fig. 2g). The appearance of characteristic stretching vibrations for GA and DOX confirmed their successful incorporation into the nanomaterial system. The zeta potential was measured as −12.5 mV in PBS (pH 7.4), −8.7 mV in water, and − 18.3 mV in DMEM medium, indicating a negatively charged surface (Fig. S2). The negative surface charge of the nanoparticles intensified with increasing environmental ionic strength (DMEM contains serum proteins and electrolytes, reducing charge shielding and increasing the absolute potential value). This suggests that the negative charge of GFD generates electrostatic repulsion between particles, preventing aggregation and ensuring stable dispersion in physiological environments.

Reportedly, DOX and GA exhibit characteristic peaks at 480 nm and 360 nm, respectively [54]. Based on this, we calculated and optimized the optimal ratio of DOX to GA. Ultraviolet spectrophotometry results showed an encapsulation efficiency of 83.7 ± 1.9% for GA and a drug loading rate of 11.2 ± 1.1% for DOX, with the optimal molar ratio of Fe³⁺:GA: DOX = 2:1:1.8 (Fig. S3). GA and DOX demonstrated high encapsulation and loading rates in the GFD nanomaterials, and the optimal molar ratio of components was determined. Under different pH conditions, GFD exhibited distinct ion release behaviors. In dialysis fluid at pH 6.5, Fe²⁺ release reached 40% at 12 h, 60% at 24 h, and 82% at 72 h, with faster ion release rates and cumulative release increasing over time. In contrast, at pH 7.4, Fe²⁺ release was 20% at 12 h, 30% at 24 h, and 55% at 72 h, demonstrating slower ion release and sustained-release capability, enabling targeted drug release in the tumor microenvironment.

GFD has excellent multi-enzyme activity

Based on prior reports of iron-based nanozymes’ catalytic properties, we hypothesized that GFD might possess similar multi-enzyme activities and conducted experiments to evaluate this [31]. The OPD probe (λ = 442 nm) revealed: the blank group (H₂O₂ + OPD) had an absorbance of 0.23 ± 0.02, while the 80 µg/mL GAF@DOX group showed an absorbance of 1.78 ± 0.11, a 7.7-fold increase over the blank group, indicating dose dependence (Fig. 3i-j). The TMB probe (λ = 650 nm) showed an absorbance of 1.52 ± 0.09 for the 80 µg/mL group, confirming strong oxidative capacity(Fig. 3k-l). In experiments with OPD and TMB probes, GFD’s absorbance at specific wavelengths significantly varied depending on its reaction with H₂O₂. When GFD coexisted with H₂O₂, absorbance markedly increased, with corresponding systematic changes under varying GFD and H₂O₂ concentration gradients, demonstrating GFD’s peroxidase-mimicking activity.

Elevated levels of GSH in tumor cells can neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), mitigating cellular damage. Therefore, depleting GSH can enhance ROS generation, a catalytic activity known as glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px). After adding varying concentrations of GFD and GSH to a system at pH = 6.5, absorbance at λ = 412 nm was measured at different time points. The DTNB probe (λ = 412 nm) showed that the 30 µg/mL GAF@DOX group exhibited an absorbance of 0.85 ± 0.05 at 6 h, an 8.5-fold increase compared to the control group (0.10 ± 0.01), with a GSH depletion rate of 78% (Fig. 2m-n). The results demonstrated that as GFD concentration increased and reaction time extended, absorbance displayed corresponding dynamic changes, confirming GFD’s glutathione peroxidase-like activity.

Hypoxia leads tumor cells to switch to self-protection, hindering the efficacy of antitumor drugs. Catalase (CAT) can decompose high concentrations of H₂O₂ in the tumor microenvironment (TME) into O₂, alleviating intratumoral hypoxia. We experimentally verified GFD’s CAT-like activity. Using a microplate assay, we measured GFD’s effect on H₂O₂ reduction at different concentrations, plotted a standard curve based on absorbance, and calculated CAT’s relative activity. The H₂O₂ depletion rate was 65 ± 4% within 5 min for the 50 µg/mL GAF@DOX group, significantly higher than the control group (10 ± 2%) (Fig. 2o). Moreover, GFD and H₂O₂ synergistically induced radical generation, exacerbating oxidative damage. The results confirmed that GFD effectively catalyzes H₂O₂ decomposition, exhibiting notable catalase-like activity (Fig. 2p).

The catalase (CAT)-like activity of GFD plays a foundational role by efficiently decomposing intratumoral H₂O₂ into oxygen, with 50 µg/mL GFD achieving a 65 ± 4% decomposition rate within just 5 min. This oxygen generation effectively alleviates tumor hypoxia, a major factor contributing to doxorubicin (DOX) resistance, thereby enhancing DOX’s cytotoxic effects while maintaining the catalytic efficiency of other enzymes. Subsequently, GFD’s peroxidase (POD)-like activity functions as an ROS production engine, utilizing either endogenous or DOX-induced H₂O₂ to generate highly toxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) through the Fenton reaction. Complementing this, the glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px)-like activity acts as an ROS retention switch by depleting antioxidants and inactivating GPX4. This coordinated action of POD-generated ROS combined with GSH-Px-mediated antioxidant depletion creates a powerful ferroptosis response [55]. Importantly, this ferroptosis works in concert with DOX-induced apoptosis to establish a dual cell death pathway, effectively overcoming resistance associated with single-pathway mechanisms and ultimately leading to significantly enhanced chemotherapy sensitization.

Verification of the rheological properties and physical characterization of 3D bioprinted GFD microneedles

The microneedle precursor contains a photoinitiator (LAP), and UV irradiation initiates the crosslinking and curing of GelMA/HAMA (Fig. 3a, Fig. S4). The photopolymerization crosslinking process was verified to ensure the transformation of microneedles from a liquid precursor to a solid structure. The microneedle patches fabricated via 3D bioprinting (BP8601 Pro) exhibited clear base letters and microneedle arrays, consistent with the CAD design (needle height: 800 μm, spacing: 1 mm), confirming printing precision and macroscopic structural integrity, which provided a physical foundation for drug loading and penetration (Fig. 3b). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed conical microneedle arrays with a height of 800 μm, base diameter of 300 μm, and tip curvature radius < 20 μm (Fig. 3e, f). The internal porous structure had pore sizes of 10–50 μm (Fig. 3g, h). The overall morphology of the microneedles was intact, with sharp tips. Three-dimensional reconstruction of fluorescently labeled drugs via confocal microscopy showed uniform drug distribution within the microneedles (Fig. 3c). When the microneedle patch was applied to the brain tissue surface and tissue sections were stained along the longitudinal axis of the microneedles, penetration holes were observed (Fig. 3d), indicating sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate the cerebral cortex and accomplish drug delivery. To further validate penetration capability, the microneedles were applied to brain tissue, and H&E staining showed minimal tissue damage and good biocompatibility.

To investigate the rheological properties of the GelMA/HAMA hydrogel microneedle system, we selected 10% GelMA and 1% HAMA as the concentration ratio for printing microneedles, with 10 mg/mL GFD incorporated. Results showed that in oscillatory frequency and strain sweeps (Fig. 3I, J), all groups exhibited G′ >G′′ with frequency insensitivity, indicating dominant elastic behavior (solid-like properties), consistent with the gel mechanics of microneedles. In photopolymerization modulus-time curves, viscosity-time curves (Fig. 3k, l), and viscosity-temperature curves (Fig. S5), the addition of GFD did not affect the overall rheological properties of the hydrogel. To assess whether the microneedles possessed sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate brain tissue, we measured the fracture force using a tensile-compression tester. The force-displacement results demonstrated that all microneedles could withstand a compressive force of ≥ 0.2 N per needle (Fig. 3p), confirming adequate strength for brain tissue penetration.

Considering that GFD release from microneedles in vivo occurs through simultaneous swelling and dissolution, the microneedles exhibited a swelling ratio of 145 ± 8% in pH 7.4 PBS after 24 h, while at pH 6.5, the swelling ratio was 182 ± 12% (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3m). Drug-loaded microneedles showed faster drug release in an acidic environment (pH 6.4) (Fig. 3n), with a rapid increase in drug release within the first 24 h, resembling a burst release effect due to swelling, followed by gradual stabilization. In contrast, drug release was slower at pH 7.4, demonstrating clear pH-responsive release characteristics, which facilitate targeted rapid drug release in the tumor microenvironment. The 10% GelMA and 1% HAMA system enabled complete degradation of microneedles within approximately 18 days (Fig. 3o, Fig. S8), effectively bridging the therapeutic gap between glioma resection and concurrent chemoradiotherapy.

These results comprehensively validate the physicochemical properties (rheological, mechanical, swelling, and release) of the microneedle drug delivery system, providing critical data to support its clinical application in glioma treatment and ensuring the efficacy and safety of localized drug administration.

The dual-pathway killing effect of GFD in vitro synergistically inducing apoptosis and ferroptosis in glioma cells

The multifunctional enzymatic properties of GFD imply its promising anticancer applications. To assess its biological safety profile, initial hemocompatibility tests were conducted. At a concentration of 400 µg/mL, GFD demonstrated minimal hemolytic activity (2.8 ± 0.4%), well within the acceptable safety range of < 5% (Fig. 4a). Subsequent cytotoxicity evaluations were performed on both murine glioma cells (GL261) and mouse hippocampal neuron cells (HT22) using CCK-8 assays after 24-hour exposure to various GFD concentrations and soluble hydrogel microneedle extracts (the extract was diluted to 10%ཞ100% respectively). The results showed that the 24-hour IC₅₀ of GFD for GL261 was 79.2 ± 2.5 µg/mL. However, for HT22, the survival rate was 83 ± 5% at 80 µg/mL, indicating low toxicity (Fig. 4b, Figure S9a). This suggests that with the increase of GFD concentration, cell viability shows a certain downward trend, but within a certain concentration range, the cells still maintain high activity. Moreover, the microneedle extract has low toxicity to both types of cells (Fig. 4c, Figure S9b). This demonstrates that as the GFD concentration increases, cell viability shows a certain downward trend. However, within a specific concentration range, cells maintain relatively high activity. Additionally, the microneedle extracts exhibited minimal toxicity to both cell types, confirming the biosafety of the microneedles and indicating that GFD’s therapeutic effects are accompanied by relatively controllable toxicity to normal cells.

After successfully verifying the toxicity of GFD to tumors, we introduced a scratch assay to validate the effect of GFD on cell migration through the following four interventions: (1) NC group (serum-free DEMD); (2) GF group (80 µg/mL); (3) DOX group (25 µg/mL); (4) GFD group (40 µg/mL). After 24 h of incubation, it was observed that the migratory ability of glioma cells was progressively inhibited. The healing rate in the control group was approximately 61%, while that in the GFD group was about 18%, with an inhibition rate of 70.5% (Fig. 4d). The healing rates in the GAFe and DOX groups fell between these two values. This indicates that GFD can significantly inhibit the migratory ability of GL261 cells, demonstrating better synergistic effects and showing promising potential in suppressing tumor cell invasion and metastasis.

Subsequently, we further validated its impact on the population dependence and long-term proliferative capacity of GL261 cells through a colony formation assay. The grouping was consistent with the scratch assay. The results showed that the GFD group formed the fewest colonies, while the control group had the highest number (Fig. 4e). The colony numbers in the GAFe and DOX groups were intermediate, indicating that GFD can significantly inhibit the proliferation and colony-forming ability of GL261 cells, exerting a strong inhibitory effect on the long-term proliferation of tumor cells.

We confirmed the successful uptake of GFD by cells through an endocytosis assay. The blue staining represents the nuclei of GL261 cells labeled with DAPI, while the red staining indicates GFD labeled with Cy5.5. Over time, increasing red fluorescence was observed surrounding the blue nuclei. By 2 h, clear encapsulation was visible, demonstrating that the material was successfully taken up by the cells (Fig. 4f).

The cytotoxic efficacy of GFD against tumor cells was quantitatively assessed using dual fluorescence staining techniques. Calcein AM/propidium iodide (PI) co-staining of GL261 cells demonstrated consistent results with prior cytotoxicity assays, revealing GFD as the most potent cytotoxic agent among tested compounds (Fig. 4g). To investigate the underlying apoptotic mechanisms, we performed JC-1 staining to monitor mitochondrial membrane potential alterations, a hallmark of early apoptosis. Quantitative fluorescence microscopy analysis revealed a dose-dependent decrease in JC-1 red fluorescence, accompanied by increased green fluorescence intensity following material treatment (Fig. 5a), indicative of progressive mitochondrial depolarization and consequent apoptotic signaling activation. This disturbance was not obvious in the NC group, gradually increased in the GF and DOX groups, and became significantly more pronounced after DOX addition. The GFD group continued this trend, suggesting that GAFe, DOX, and GFD can all induce apoptosis, with DOX playing a crucial role in apoptosis induction. In the lipid peroxidation probe assay, the intracellular green fluorescence intensity in the GFD-treated group was significantly higher than in other groups, indicating that GFD effectively induced intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Increased ROS typically leads to oxidative stress damage, further promoting apoptosis (Fig. 5b). In the iron ion detection (Ferro Orange), the GFD group exhibited the brightest orange fluorescence (free Fe²⁺), indicating intracellular iron overload (Fig. 5c). Moreover, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to observe the mitochondrial morphology of GL261 cells. The results showed mitochondrial swelling and cristae rupture (red arrows) in the GFD group, which are typical ultrastructural features of ferroptosis (Fig. 5e). Finally, we used the apoptosis detection kit (Annexin V-FITC) to investigate the role of DOX in different stages of apoptosis. With the addition of different materials, the early apoptosis rate (Q2 + Q3) in the GFD group increased to 18.6 ± 1.5%, 3.1 times higher than that of the control group (5.9 ± 0.8%) (Fig. 5d). The gradual increase in apoptosis suggests that GFD also plays a significant role in promoting apoptosis.

The results showed that the GFD group had the highest fluorescence intensity of ACSL4 (green) and COX-2 (red), both of which are core effector proteins of ferroptosis. In summary, GFD comprehensively activates the ferroptosis pathway by elevating ROS, depleting GSH, upregulating ferroptosis effector proteins (ACSL4/COX-2), and downregulating inhibitory proteins (GPX4/FTH1), thereby driving ferroptosis in GL261 cells and explaining its high cytotoxicity toward GL261 (Sect. 2.14, low IC₅₀). Additionally, there is a synergistic therapeutic mechanism: GFD (GAFe + DOX) combines chemotherapy (DOX-induced apoptosis, apoptosis flow cytometry) with ferroptosis (the aforementioned ferroptosis markers), achieving dual-pathway killing. This approach outperforms single-drug therapy, enhances anti-migration and anti-proliferation capabilities, and demonstrates potent tumor-killing efficacy.

In vivo antitumor effects and ferroptosis mechanism validation of GFD-loaded microneedles in an orthotopic glioma model

After confirming the low toxicity and excellent antitumor efficacy of GFD, we evaluated its in vivo antitumor capability through animal experiments. BALB/c nude mice were orthotopically implanted with GL261 murine glioma cells, and an orthotopic xenograft glioma model was established via surgical resection of the tumor (Fig. 7a, b). Based on prior experimental findings, the optimal molar ratio of GA to DOX was established as 1:2, which demonstrated effective tumor-killing efficiency in cell-based studies. To ensure consistency across the research, this same ratio was applied in the subsequent animal experiments. Based on cellular experimental results, we concluded that both GF and GFD exhibit potent antitumor effects. Leveraging the physicochemical properties of the hydrogel microneedles described earlier, GF and GFD (1 mg/mL) were incorporated into the GelMA/HAMA hydrogel system. Drug-loaded microneedles (MNs@GF and MNs@GFD) were fabricated using 3D bioprinting technology and implanted into the residual glioma cavity in mice for treatment (Fig. 7c).

It is necessary to note, however, that glioma is distinguished by its inherent feature of diffuse infiltration into the surrounding normal brain parenchyma, and this characteristic poses an inherent limitation to the single local therapeutic strategy adopted in this study. The drug delivery range of MNs is restricted to the local area they cover; for tumor cells that have diffusely infiltrated into the distant brain tissue beyond the MNs coverage, this local delivery system is difficult to reach and eliminate. These remaining infiltrating tumor cells may become a potential source of subsequent tumor recurrence, which is a critical challenge that needs to be addressed in future research. To overcome this limitation, subsequent studies could explore the combination of MNs-mediated local high-concentration drug delivery with systemic therapeutic strategies (such as targeted drug delivery systems capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier) or multimodal therapeutic approaches (e.g., combining ferroptosis induction with immunotherapy), aiming to achieve comprehensive clearance of both localized residual tumors and diffusely infiltrated tumor cells, thereby further improving the long-term efficacy and clinical transformation potential of glioma treatment.

Transcriptome sequencing and protein validation of GFD-induced ferroptosis in glioma cells reveal core pathway regulation

Based on preliminary experimental results, we have confirmed that GFD exhibits excellent biosafety and potent antitumor effects. To further elucidate the role of ferroptosis/apoptosis in the antitumor process and its underlying mechanisms, we performed transcriptome sequencing on GL261 cell extracts from the NC and GFD groups. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the GFD-treated group were primarily enriched in functional pathways such as apoptosis, oxidative stress response, ferroptosis-related pathways, and cell cycle regulation. This revealed, at the gene expression level, that GFD impacts glioma cells through multiple critical biological processes and signaling pathways, indicating a comprehensive therapeutic mechanism.

DEGs included 1,779 upregulated genes (e.g., ACSL4, COX2, TNF) and 535 downregulated genes (e.g., GPX4, FTH1) (|log2 FC| >1, p < 0.05) (Fig. 9a). KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted pathways related to ferroptosis and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 9b). GO enrichment analysis further demonstrated significant differences between the control and GFD groups. Among these, we identified 16 ferroptosis-related genes, showing that GFD upregulated genes promoting ferroptosis while downregulating inhibitory genes (Fig. 9c, d).

Conclusion

This study investigates the antitumor efficacy of 3D-bioprinted microneedles loaded with gambogic acid-induced ferroptosis and doxorubicin nanoparticles for sustained release in postoperative glioma residual cavities. GFD nanomaterials were synthesized via a one-step self-assembly process, and microneedles were fabricated using projection-based photopolymerization 3D bioprinting technology. A series of characterization experiments confirmed the successful preparation and favorable properties of the nanomaterials and microneedles. In cellular experiments, GFD inhibited glioma cell proliferation and migration while inducing cell death through ferroptosis/apoptosis, demonstrating multidimensional therapeutic effects. The microneedles enable precise in situ drug delivery, bypassing the blood-brain barrier and enhancing postoperative chemosensitivity, thereby achieving greater tumor suppression with lower drug doses. Animal experiments further verified their ability to effectively inhibit tumor recurrence, exhibiting excellent biocompatibility and safety. This study provides an innovative strategy and scientific basis for targeted postoperative glioma therapy, with promising potential for clinical translation, showcasing efficient and safe application prospects in glioma treatment.
