Naringin targets TGF-β1-mediated angiogenesis to enhance the osteogenic effect of induced membrane
Authors: Shuyuan Li, Qunbin Cai, Songyang Wang, Zhanpeng Zeng, Dawen Yang, Qishi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsInduced membrane technique (IMT) is a novel two-stage reconstruction technology for large bone defects. As is widely known, promoting the vascularization of induced membrane tissue is of great importance in accelerating the mineralization process of bone graft. Naringin is a flavonoid compound with anti-inflammatory, anti-osteoporotic, and potential pro-angiogenic effects. The aim of this study is to explore the effect and mechanism of naringin on angiogenesis of induced membrane.MethodsA total of 48 SD rats were randomly divided into a control group, low-dose group (L-Naringin), medium-dose group (M-Naringin), and high-dose group (H-Naringin). All rats underwent the establishment of an IMT model in the right femur. Four weeks after the first-stage surgery, which coincided with the completion of four weeks of treatment with different doses of naringin, the results of angiogenesis and cell proliferation within the induced membrane were evaluated. Eight weeks after the second-stage surgery, imaging and histological methods were employed to assess the healing of femoral bone defects and analyze the impact of induced membranes with varying vascular densities on the mineralization of bone grafts. The naringin-containing serum was collected from rats to cultivate endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) derived from rat bone marrow in vitro. The effects of naringin on the proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)/SMAD signalling pathway of EPCs were assessed at corresponding stages. Meanwhile, the conditioned medium (CM) from EPCs was collected to treat osteoblasts, analyzing the effect of EPCs-derived angiogenic-osteogenic factors on osteoblast mineralization. Finally, naringin binding to TGF-β1 protein was studied by molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation (MDS), and cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA).ResultsCompared with control and L-Naringin groups, the vascular number, cell proliferation rate, and TGF-β/SMAD pathway-related factors (TGF-β1, phosphorylated-SMAD (p-SMAD)2, and p-SMAD3) of the induced membrane were higher in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups. Meanwhile, the richer the blood vessels within the induced membrane, the stronger the mineralization capacity of the bone graft, and the faster the healing rate of the bone defect. In vitro, naringin-containing serum intervention notably increased cell viability, migration, tubular formation, secretion of angiogenic-osteogenic factors, and phosphorylation level of TGF-β/SMAD signalling pathway-related proteins in EPCs (p < 0.05 in the naringin group, vs the control group). Meanwhile, naringin-containing serum reversed the inhibitory effect of small interfering-TGF-β1 (si-TGF-β1) on the function of EPCs (p < 0.05 in the si-TGF-β1+ naringin group, vs the si-TGF-β1 group). The CM experiment demonstrated that angiogenic-osteogenic factors derived from EPCs could promote the formation of calcium nodules in osteoblasts. Additionally, results of molecular docking, myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), and CETSA indicated that naringin could directly bind to the TGF-β1 protein and maintain its thermal stability.ConclusionNaringin could promote angiogenesis of the induced membrane and the mineralization of bone grafts in the later stage in a dose-dependent manner. This effect is partly related to naringin’s targeted binding to TGF-β1 in EPCs, maintaining the stability of TGF-β1, and subsequently enhancing the activation and phosphorylation of the TGF-β1/SMAD signalling pathway.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(5):549–565.Keywords: Bone defect, Induced membrane technique, Naringin, Angiogenesis, TGF-β1, angiogenesis, transforming growth factor-beta 1, bone grafting, Rat model, bone defects, transforming growth factor β, osteoblasts, progenitor cells, blood vessels, serum

Introduction

Regardless of the cause, segmental bone defects pose a execptional challenge in the field of bone reconstruction.1 Induced membrane technique (IMT) is a novel approach for repairing segmental bone defects.2,3 The process primarily involves two stages: in the first stage, after thoroughly debriding the bone defect, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) is implanted to fill the defect.4 After six to eight weeks, PMMA is surrounded by a formed induced membrane, which is rich in blood vessels, growth factors, stem cells, and other components, thereby favouring tissue regeneration. In the second phase, induced membrane is incised, PMMA is removed while keeping the induced membrane relatively intact, and autologous cancellous bone is implanted into the induced membrane, followed by waiting for the bone defect to heal. Currently, this technique has been widely applied in clinical practice, offering advantages such as simplicity of operation, broad indications, and a low incidence of complications.5

Unlike distraction osteogenesis (DO) for repairing large bone defects, the mechanism underlying the repair of bone defects using the IMT is not yet fully understood. However, both membrane formation and bone grafting are essential components of this process.6-8 Numerous in vitro co-culture studies have demonstrated that both induced membranes and induced membrane protein extracts can notably enhance the proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and the expression of osteogenic factors.9-11 Furthermore, the ability of induced membranes to promote stem cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation is fundamentally similar to that of periosteum.12 There are three widely accepted theories explaining the success of IMT.13 Firstly, a membrane serves as a physical barrier, preventing bone resorption factors from entering the bone defect area and absorbing the graft. This barrier, however, is not airtight. Secondly, cells within the membrane, along with their secreted growth factors like transforming growth factor β (TGF-β)1, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP)-2, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), aid in the remodelling of the bone graft. Lastly, the membrane contains a dense vascular network that efficiently fulfills the bone graft’s requirements for oxygen, nutrients, and waste elimination. Therefore, promoting the formation of blood vessels within the induced membrane is crucial for enhancing the mineralization of bone grafts.

In addition to TGF-β1, BMP-2, and VEGF, the induced membrane also contains osteogenesis-related factors such as Runx2, alkaine phosphatase (ALP), and osteocalcin, as well as inflammatory factors including interleukin (IL)-6, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-8, and IL-10.5 TGF-β1, BMP-2, and VEGF are all factors closely related to angiogenesis. In this study, we first analyzed the role of TGF-β1 in intra-membrane vascular formation. The TGF-β superfamily encompasses over 40 distinct members.14 To date, up to 33 TGF-β-linked genes have been found in mammalian genomes, including BMPs, activin/inhibin, and growth and differentiation factors.15 There are three distinct subtypes of TGF-β: TGF-β1, β2, and β3. Among them, TGF-β1 is found in endothelial cells, hematopoietic cells, and connective tissue cells.16-18 During the transduction process of the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, TGF-β1 interacts with the transforming growth factor β receptor II (TβR II), leading to the phosphorylation of the transforming growth factor β receptor I (TβR I). Subsequently, the activated TβR I phosphorylates SMAD2 and SMAD3. The phosphorylated forms of SMAD2 (p-SMAD2) and SMAD3 (p-SMAD3) then form a heterotrimeric complex with SMAD4 and enter the nucleus. This complex activates the expression of downstream target genes, ultimately mediating the biological effects of the TGF-β/SMAD.19 One study has confirmed that the TGF-β/SMAD signalling pathway promotes angiogenesis and vascular remodelling by regulating the proliferation of angiogenesis-related cells, such as vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and endothelial cells.20

Naringin is a flavonoid naturally present in citrus fruits or traditional Chinese medicines (such as Drynariae Rhizoma, Fructus Aurantii Immaturus, and Cassia obtusifolia).21,22 It has exhibited multiple biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumour, and osteoporosis-improving effects.23-25 In recent years, its role in angiogenesis has gradually garnered attention. Naringin could influence angiogenesis through multiple pathways. On the one hand, it has been found to upregulate key factors involved in angiogenesis (such as VEGF, SDF-1, and TNF), enhancing the proliferation, migration, and tube-forming capacity of endothelial cells, thereby promoting neovascularization.26-28 On the other hand, naringin alleviated endothelial cell dysfunction.29 Zhao et al30 discovered that naringin could reverse apoptosis induced by oxidized low-density lipoprotein in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) by inhibiting the Hippo-yes-associated protein (YAP) pathway. Li et al31 demonstrated that naringin enhanced the antioxidant properties of HUVECs and protected them from high glucose-induced damage by binding to C-X3-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CX3CL1) and downregulating its expression. Since the vascular formation within the induced membrane is of vital importance for bone graft mineralization, yet the existing methods to promote angiogenesis within the membrane are limited. Based on the biological properties exhibited by naringin, we hypothesize that naringin could promote angiogenesis within the induced membrane and improve endothelial cell dysfunction. This study primarily aimed to clarify the regulatory role of naringin in angiogenesis within induced membranes during the first phase of induced membrane technique (i.e., the induced membrane formation period), as well as the ‘communication’ relationship between vascularization of the induced membrane and bone graft mineralization.

Methods

Animals and treatment

Based on the random number table method, 48 male SD rats (aged 8 to 9 weeks) were randomly assigned to control group (n = 12), low-dose group (L-Naringin, n = 12), medium-dose group (M-Naringin, n = 12), and high-dose group (H-Naringin, n = 12), with cages as the allocation units. All rats were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment (12-hr on/off light). Within four weeks after the first-stage surgery, rats in the L-Naringin, M-Naringin, and H-Naringin groups were orally administered naringin (MedChemExpress, USA) at doses of of 150, 300, and 450 mg/kg/day, respectively. Meanwhile, the control group received the same volume of normal saline (0.9%). It is worth noting that after the second-stage surgery, the rats did not receive naringin treatment. The number of blood vessels within the induced membrane and the quality of bone graft mineralization mediated by these vessels were taken as the primary observational outcome. The animal sample size was calculated using G•Power 3.1.9.7 software (University of Düsseldorf, Germany). We have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and have included the ARRIVE checklist in the Supplementary Material.

The models of IMT

The surgical procedures were conducted based on the methodology described in Gouron et al’s6 prior research, with minor adaptations. First stage of IMT: in brief, after anaesthetizing the rats with 3% pentobarbital sodium, the right hind limb was shaved, sterilized, and covered with a sterile surgical drape. Subsequently, an incision of approximately 2.5 to 3 cm was made through the skin and subcutaneous tissue along the lateral margin of the femur. A six-hole mini-plate was placed on the lateral margin of the femur, and four cortical screws (1.5 mm in diameter) were employed for the fixation of the plate. A 4 mm segment of bone was resected from the centre of the femur using a wire saw to create a bone defect. The region of bone defect underwent filling with PMMA spacer (Heraeus, Germany). After repeated rinsing with normal saline (0.9%), the incision was sutured with 3/0 silk (Figure 1a). After four weeks, a portion of the induced membrane was collected for detection of relevant indicators.

Second stage of IMT: four weeks after the first-stage surgery, the skin, fascia, and induced membrane were sequentially incised along the original surgical incision. While carefully protecting the integrity of the induced membrane, the PMMA was removed. The rat’s own coccyx was taken, cut into fine bone particles, and filled into the femoral bone defect area. Finally, the skin and subcutaneous tissues were sutured using 3/0 silk. Eight weeks after bone grafting, the rat’s right femur was collected for detection of relevant indicators (Figure 1b).

Radiograph and micro-CT

Eight weeks after bone grafting, the lateral radiographs of the rat’s femur were obtained using an X-ray machine. The repair effect in the bone defect area was evaluated using Lane-Sandhu (LSS) X-ray scoring system (Table I).32 After the X-ray assessment, the rat’s right femur was harvested and placed inside a Micro-CT device (Skyscan 1172; Bruker, Belgium) to acquire images of the femur. The scanning parameters were set as follows: X-ray dose of 502 mGy, resolution of 9 μm, and total reconstruction time of 418 seconds. Afterwards, images were loaded into CTAn software (Bruker) to measure and analyze relevant parameters of bone healing, including trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), cancellous bone volume (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Histological assessment

After embedding the induced membrane or decalcified bone tissue in paraffin, it was sliced using a pathological microtome with a section thickness of 5 μm. Subsequently, the slices were dewaxed using a dewaxing solution and dehydrated with graded ethanol. The samples were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson, or Safranin O-Fast green (SO/FG) staining solution separately. Finally, after sealing with neutral balsam, the microscopic structure of the induced membrane was observed under a microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

After embedding the induced membrane in paraffin and slicing it into 5 μm-thick slices, the slices were dewaxed using a dewaxing solution and dehydrated with graded ethanol. After antigen retrieval using the antigen retrieval solution, 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was applied dropwise to block the membrane tissue for 30 minutes. The slices were incubated with anti-platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) antibody (1:50, Abcam, UK, ab281583), anti-von Willebrand factor (vWF) antibody (1:1,000, Servicebio, China, GB11020), anti-antigen Kiel 67 (Ki67) antibody (1:500, Servicebio, GB121141), anti-TGF-β1 antibody (1:500, Abcam, ab215715), anti-Phospho-SMAD2 antibody (p-SMAD2, 1:100, Abcam, ab188334), and anti-Phospho-SMAD3 antibody (p-SMAD3, 1:100, Bioss, China, bs-5616R). Add the secondary antibody and incubate for 50 minutes. The slices were then developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic solution, followed by counterstaining the nuclei with haematoxylin for five minutes. Finally, after sealing with neutral balsam, the positive-area of the induced membrane was observed under a microscope. The area of the positive regions for the target protein in the images was analyzed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Preparation of the naringin-containing serum

A total of 30 male SD rats (260 to 285 g) were assigned to blank group (n = 15) and naringin group (n = 15). Two groups of rats were orally fed with normal saline and naringin continuously for three days, once every 12 hours. Following the final administration, blood samples were obtained from the abdominal aorta one hour later. The blood was then allowed to stand for two hours, followed by centrifugation to separate the serum from the cellular components. Subsequently, the serum was immersed in a 56°C water bath for 30 minutes to inactivate complements. After filtration through a 0.22 µm micropore filter, the serum was stored at -80°C for future use. For the subsequent cell culture, the Naringin-containing serum or normal saline-containing serum was used for rat bone marrow-derived endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs).

Culture and identification of EPCs

The EPCs used in the experiments were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology (CP-R128, China), with passage 2-3 cells employed throughout the study.

The levels of surface markers CD34 and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) on EPCs were assayed using immunofluorescence. For functional verification, EPCs were exposed to DiI-labeled acetylated low-density lipoprotein(Dil-Ac-LDL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled Ulex europaeus agglutinin I (FITC-UEA-I; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a dark environment. Subsequently, the microscope was used to observe whether EPCs were able to simultaneously uptake both DiL-ac-LDL and FITC-UEA-I.

Transfection of si-TGF-β1

Three small interfering RNA sequences targeting TGF-β1 (si-TGF-β1 #1, si-TGF-β1 #2, si-TGF-β1 #3) and a negative control sequence (si-TGF-β1 NC) were synthesized separately by Sangon Biotech (China). The EPCs were seeded into culture plates, and when EPCs confluence reached 60% to 70%, transfection was performed using Lipofectamin 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty-four hours after transfection, the mRNA and protein levels of TGF-β1 were detected by real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blot, respectively, to evaluate the interfering effects of the three si-TGF-β1 sequences on EPCs. The sequence information for each si-TGF-β1 is presented in Table II.

Grouping of EPCs

According to different interventions, EPCs were divided into control group, si-TGF-β1 group, si-TGF-β1+ naringin group, and naringin group. Each group was given EGM-2 containing 10% volume of naringin-containing serum or normal saline-containing serum. Among them, the si-TGF-β1 and si-TGF-β1+ naringin groups were transfected with si-TGF-β1.

Detection of cell proliferation and viability

After 24, 48, and 72 hours of culture, the cells’ proliferation and viability was detected using the Click-iT EdU-488 kit (Servicebio, G1601) and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China). For the 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining, after climbing, the EPCs were incubated with EdU storage solution (10 mM), washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) one or twice, and fixed with paraformaldehyde fixation solution. EPCs were incubated with 0.5% TritonX-100 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, EPCs were incubated with 100 μL EdU solution in the dark. The nucleus was stained with Hochester staining solution for ten minutes in a dark environment. After sealing, a fluorescence microscope was employed to detect the proliferating EPCs. Regarding the CCK-8, evenly seed EPCs into 96-well plates. After culturing them for 24, 48, and 72 hours in media with si-TGF-β1 or naringin, add CCK-8 reagent to each well. Then put the plates back in the incubator for two more hours. Finally, measure the 450 nm absorbance with a microplate reader.

Detection of migration and invasion

For the scratch wound assay, once the cells in each group had reached confluence in the culture plate, the confluent EPCs were wounded by scratching the monolayer using a sterile pipette tip, followed by removal of detached cells with PBS. Images of the scratch area were captured under a microscope (40×) at 0 and 24 hours post-intervention, and the invasion area of the EPCs was quantified using ImageJ software.

In the transwell assay, cells pre-treated with different methods for 24 hours were seeded (5 × 104 cells/well) into the upper chamber of a 24-well transwell plate, while the lower chamber was filled with 600 µl of EGM-2 medium supplemented with 10% serum. After 24 hours of incubation, migrated cells that passed through the polyestermembrane were stained with crystal violet for 20 minutes. The invasive cell count for each group was then observed and calculated.

Tube formation assay

Following 24 hours of pretreatment with various methods, cells (1 × 105 cells/well) from each group were seeded onto Matrix-coated 24-well plates. After a 12-hour incubation at 37°C, tube formation of EPCs was observed under a microscope, and the total tube length was quantified using ImageJ software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

After 24 hours of pretreatment with naringin or si-TGF-β1 for each group, the culture media from all groups were collected and centrifuged for ten minutes at 1,000 g. The supernatants were then obtained. Following the instructions of the platelet derived growth factor BB (PDGF-BB) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (PP775, Beyotime), VEGF ELISA Kit (PV960, Beyotime), and slit guidance ligand 3 (SLIT3) ELISA Kit (SED353Ra, cloud-clone, China), the concentrations of PDGF-BB, VEGF, and SLIT3 in the EPCs’ supernatants were measured, respectively.

The effects of EPC-conditioned medium on osteoblasts

The supernatant from the aforementioned EPCs was collected and used as conditioned medium (CM) for the subsequent culture of osteoblasts. Rat osteoblasts (Procell, China) were seeded into cell culture plates. After they adhered to the plate surface, the original culture medium was discarded, and the collected CM described above was added for further culture to analyze the impact of angiogenic-osteogenic factors derived from EPCs on the function of osteoblasts.

Alizarin red staining

After 24 hours of treatment with the CM, medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium for a three-week induction culture of osteoblasts. The culture medium was then discarded, and cells were rinsed three times with PBS to remove any residual medium. Subsequently, 4% paraformaldehyde was added to fix the cells for 30 minutes. The fixed cells were completely immersed in an alizarin red solution and incubated for 20 minutes. After rinsing with PBS to remove excess dye, the culture plates were placed under a microscope to observe and record the formation of calcium nodules.

Western blot

After lysing the induced membrane and EPCs using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer, the proteins were isolated from samples. Protein levels were subsequently measured with a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kit. After preparing the separating gel and stacking gel, sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel (SDS-PAGE) was performed. The ethanol-activated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane was gently applied to the separating gel, and then membrane transfer was carried out for 30 minutes at 300 mA. The transferred membrane was then blocked with skimmed milk for 30 minutes. After incubation with the primary and secondary antibodies, enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) development was performed, and the grey values of the target proteins were analyzed. Details on the primary antibodies were provided: TGF-β1 (1:1,000, Abcam, ab215715), SMAD2/3 (1:1,000, Abcam, ab305325), Phospho-SMAD2 (p-SMAD2, 1:1,000, Abcam, ab188334), or Phospho-SMAD3 (p-SMAD3, 1:1,000, Bioss, bs-5616R).

Immunofluorescence phosphorylated-SMAD

Briefly, 3% BSA was added to tissue slices or cells growing on the glass slide and incubated for 30 minutes to seal off. The tissues or EPCs were reacted with Phospho-SMAD2 (p-SMAD2,1:50, Abcam, ab300079) at 4°C overnight. Then, under the dark condition, the second antibody was added to the slices and incubated for one hour. Finally, the nucleus was stained by 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for ten minutes. The p-SMAD2-positive EPCs was observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope.

qRT-PCR

After collecting cells, total RNA from EPCs was isolated using RNA extraction solution (Servicebio, G3013). Following the measurement of RNA concentration and purity with an ultramicro spectrophotometer, RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification was carried out on a quantitative PCR (qPCR) instrument (Bio-rad, USA), following 2× Universal Blue SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Servicebio, G3326) guidelines. TGF-β1 mRNA level was calculated via the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences for TGF-β1 are listed in Table III.

Molecular docking

Compound structure for naringin was obtained from PubChem database, and these SDF files were transformed into protein data bank (PDB) format utilizing Open Babel 2.3.2.33 The receptor protein, TGF-β1, was retrieved from UniProt database and modified using AutoDockTools software (Center for Computational Structural Biology, USA) to remove water molecules, add hydrogen atoms, and balance charges, before being saved in PDB parital charge atom type (PDBQT) format. With naringin serving as ligands, molecular docking of receptor protein with ligands was carried out with AutoDock Vina 1.1. The outcomes of the docking were then visualized through PyMOL (Schrödinger, USA).

Molecular dynamics simulation

Using the complex structure obtained from molecular docking as the initial configuration, we placed it into a cubic water box constructed with the transferable intermolecular potential 3P (TIP3P) water model. An appropriate amount of 0.154 M sodium ions (Na⁺) and 0.154 M chloride ions (Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the system’s charge and simulate physiological ion concentrations. The simulation was conducted using the GROMACS software (GROMACS, Sweden). First, energy minimization of the system was carried out in an absolute vacuum environment to eliminate unreasonable conformations, followed by molecular dynamics simulation. After the simulation was completed, we analyzed the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and the number of hydrogen bonds to evaluate the stability of the complex and the dynamic binding mechanism between the two components.

Cellular thermal shift assay

After pre-treating EPCs with naringin or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for four hours, cells were collected and resuspended in lysis buffer to ensure complete cell lysis. Following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm, the supernatant was collected, representing total protein solution. The total protein solution was then divided equally into eight aliquots and heated for three minutes at gradient temperatures ranging from 42°C to 67°C. The total protein solution was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm again to remove the heat-denatured precipitated proteins. Finally, Western blot was conducted to measure target protein level, and the cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) solubility curve was plotted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of all collected data was performed using SPSS v. 21.0 software (IBM, USA). First, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of data. After confirming that the data conformed to a normal distribution, it was presented as mean (SD). For data showing normal distribution and homogeneity of variance among groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied. Subsequently, if a significant difference was observed, the Bonferroni test was employed for comparing groups. A p-value < 0.05 signified statistical significance.

Results

Angiogenesis and cell proliferation in induced membranes

To evaluate the effect of naringin on vascular formation in the induced membrane of rats, H&E and Masson staining were performed after four weeks of naringin intervention. Rich blood vessels were found in the outer layer of the induced membrane away from the PMMA in each group. Among them, the control group had fewer blood vessels in the induced membrane, while treatment with naringin (L/M/H-Naringin groups) to some extent increased the number of blood vessels, especially in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups (Figure 1c). Both CD31 and vWF were markers of endothelial cells;34,35 we further evaluated the number of CD31-positive vessels and vWF-positive vessels induced by immunohistochemistry and found that the control group had the lowest number of CD31-positive vessels and vWF-positive vessels, while naringin treatment considerably increased the formation of CD31-positive vessels and vWF-positive vessels (p < 0.001 in M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups, vs control and L-Naringin groups) (Figures 1d and 1e). Furthermore, Ki67 immunohistochemistry detection of cell proliferation within the induced membrane revealed that the percentage of Ki67-positive cell area in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups was significantly higher than that in the control and L-Naringin groups (Figure 1f), indicating that medium and high doses of naringin should substantially accelerate the proliferation rate of cells in the induced membrane.

Accelerated repair of large segmental bone defects

To investigate the effect of induced membranes with different vascular densities on bone graft mineralization following naringin treatment, we assessed the mineralization quality of the bone grafts at eight weeks post-implantation, utilizing both radiological and histological approaches. The radiograph scoring results revealed that the bone defect healing scores were significantly higher in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups compared to the control and L-Naringin groups (Figure 2a). Micro-CT analysis indicated that medium and high doses of naringin notably increased BMD, BV/TV, Tb.N, and Tb.Th in the defect area, while reducing Tb.Sp (Figure 2b). The H&E and SO/FG staining results confirmed that the percentages of newly formed bone (NB) and newly formed cartilage (NFC) in the total bone defect were significantly greater in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups compared to the control and L-Naringin groups (p < 0.01 in M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups, vs control group; p < 0.05 in M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups, vs L-Naringin group) (Figure 2c). These findings indicated that naringin-treated induced membranes notably stimulated the rate and quality of bone graft mineralization within the induced membranes. The richer the vascularization within the induced membranes, the faster the bone defects healed.

Upregulated TGF-β/SMAD signalling pathway

Research has shown that there is abundant TGF-β1 in the induced membrane.5 The TGF-β/SMAD signalling cascade serves as a crucial regulatory mechanism in angiogenesis. To corroborate the regulatory influence of naringin on the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, we used western blot and immunohistochemistry to assess the expression of TGF-β1 and its downstream effectors within the induced membrane. The western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of TGF-β1, p-SMAD2, and p-SMAD3 were significantly elevated in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups compared to the control and L-Naringin groups (Figure 3a). The immunohistochemistry result suggested that the positive-staining area of TGF-β1, p-SMAD2, or p-SMAD 3 in the M-Naringin and H-Naringin groups was notably larger than that in control and L-Naringin groups (Figure 3b), which was consistent with western blot, suggesting that the stimulatory effect of naringin on angiogenesis and subsequent bone graft mineralization in the induced membrane could be attributed to its activation of the TGF-β/SMAD pathway.

Transfection efficiency of si-TGF-β1

qRT-PCR was employed, 24 hours after transfection, to detect the expression of TGF-β1 mRNA in EPCs to evaluate the transfection efficiency. The results showed that, compared with the negative control (si-TGF-β1 NC) group, both si-TGF-β1 #2 and si-TGF-β1 #3 sequences showed elavated downregulation of the expression of TGF-β1 mRNA (p < 0.05). Proliferation of EPCs by activating the TGF-β/SMAD pathway

EdU staining was a rapid detecting method for cell proliferation, differentiation, and viability. The EdU staining results indicated that the number of EdU-positive cells was gradually increased over time, indicating that naringin could gradually enhance the proliferation and differentiation of EPCs in a time-dependent manner. At the 24, 48, and 72 hours, compared to the control group, si-TGF-β1 group had significantly fewer EdU-positive cells and the naringin group had more EdU-positive cells, indicating that naringin exhibited an increased stimulatory effect on proliferation and differentiation of EPCs, but si-TGF-β1 could strongly inhibit the proliferation and differentiation of EPCs (p < 0.05 in the si-TGF-β1 group, vs control group). Furthermore, when compared to the si-TGF-β1 group, si-TGF-β1+ naringin group exhibited a significantly higher number of EdU-positive cells, indicating that naringin was capable of reversing the suppressive impact of si-TGF-β1 on the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, ultimately enhancing the proliferation and differentiation of EPCs (Figure 5a). The CCK-8 result also illustrated that the absorbance of EPCs continued to increase within 72 hours. The comparison of absorbance among different groups of EPCs was basically consistent with the EdU staining result (Figure 5b).

Invasion, migration, and angiogenesis of EPCs by activating the TGF-β/SMAD pathway

To assess the impact of naringin on EPC functionality, we initially employed scratch wound and transwell assays to examine its influence on migration and invasion. The scratch wound test revealed that, after 24 hours of culture, the EPCs in the si-TGF-β1 group exhibited a notably smaller invasive area compared to the control group, whereas the naringin group displayed a larger invasive area. Meanwhile, the invasive area in the si-TGF-β1+ naringin group was significantly larger than that in the si-TGF-β1 group (Figure 6a). The transwell assay results showed that the number of migrating cells in the si-TGF-β1 group was significantly smaller than that in the control group, while the number of migrating cells in the naringin group was significantly larger than that in the control group. Naringin could also reverse the inhibitory effect of si-TGF-β1 on the migration of EPCs (Figure 6b). Subsequently, we used a tube formation experiment to detect the effect of naringin on the angiogenic function of EPCs. The results of tube formation assay showed that compared with the control group, the total tube length of EPCs in the si-TGF-β1 group was substantially shorter, while the total tube length in the naringin group was substantially longer. Meanwhile, the total tube length in the si-TGF-β1+ naringin group was substantially longer than that in the si-TGF-β1 group (Figure 6c). The aforementioned findings indicated that naringin has the ability to enhance the invasion, migration, and angiogenesis of EPCs through the TGF-β/SMAD pathway.

Stimulated osteoblast mineralization by activating TGF-β/SMAD pathway in EPCs

The processes of angiogenesis and osteogenesis are tightly intertwined, with endothelial cells capable of simultaneously regulating both vascular and bone formation through the secretion of relevant factors.36 To elucidate the influence of naringin on EPC-derived angiogenic-osteogenic factors, we assessed the levels of PDGF-BB, VEGF, and SLIT3 in the supernatants of EPCs, factors that have been established to possess both angiogenic and osteogenic properties. Our ELISA analysis revealed that the concentrations of PDGF-BB, VEGF, and SLIT3 in the supernatants of cells treated with si-TGF-β1 were significantly lower than those in the control group. Conversely, the concentrations of these three factors in the naringin-treated group were notably higher than those in the control group. Furthermore, naringin was shown to offset the inhibitory action of si-TGF-β1 on the secretion of these factors (Figure 7a).

The formation of calcium nodules was a hallmark of osteoblast differentiation and maturation. After the intervention of EPCs-CM on osteoblasts, alizarin red staining (ARS) revealed that the calcium nodules formed by osteoblasts had been stained orange-red. These angiogenic-osteogenic factors (PDGF-BB, VEGF, and SLIT3) secreted by EPCs had significantly promoted calcium nodule formation in osteoblasts, particularly in the naringin group (p < 0.05 vs control group) (Figure 7b). This indicated that naringin had the potential to stimulate EPCs to secrete angiogenic-osteogenic factors via the TGF-β/SMAD pathway, thereby promoting osteoblast differentiation and mineralization.

Naringin upregulated the TGF-β/SMAD pathway in EPCs

The in vivo experiment has demonstrated that naringin exerts an upregulatory influence on the TGF-β/SMAD pathway within the membrane. To further verify whether naringin can regulate the TGF-β/SMAD signalling pathway in EPCs, we detected the expression of TGF-β/SMAD signalling pathway-related factors from in vitro EPCs after 48 hours of cultivation. Western blot result showed that the protein levels of p-SMAD2 and p-SMAD3 in si-TGF-β1 group cells were significantly lower than those in the control group, while the protein levels of p-SMAD2 and p-SMAD3 in naringin group cells were significantly higher than those in the control group. Meanwhile, the protein levels of these two factors in the si-TGF-β1+ naringin group were significantly higher versus the si-TGF-β1 group (Figure 7c).

To further investigate the impact of naringin on the nuclear transcription level of p-SMAD2 in EPCs, we employed immunofluorescence to detect the localization and quantitation of p-SMAD2. The results demonstrated that p-SMAD2 is primarily located in the EPCs’ nucleus. The number of p-SMAD2-positive cells in the naringin group was significantly larger than that in the control group, and the number of p-SMAD2-positive cells in the si-TGF-β1+ naringin was significantly larger than that in the si-TGF-β1 group (Figure 7d). The findings above indicated that naringin notably boosted TGF-β1 expression, subsequently triggering the phosphorylation of SMAD2 and SMAD3.

MDS analysis

To thoroughly investigate the dynamic interaction between naringin and TGF-β1, we conducted molecular dynamics simulation (MDS). In the root mean square deviation (RMSD) curve of the TGF-β1-naringin system, during the initial simulation stage (0 to 20 ns), the RMSD exhibited significant fluctuations, with a peak value approaching 1.2 nm. After 20 ns, the RMSD gradually stabilized and fluctuated within the range of 0.6 to 1.0 nm, indicating an optimization of the protein-ligand interaction and the formation of a stable binding mode (Figure 8c).

The RMSF curve of TGF-β1 revealed that the RMSF values of most residues were in the range of 0 to 0.4 nm, suggesting high overall rigidity of the protein. However, in the residue ranges of 0 to 50 and 300 to 400, RMSF peaks exceeded 0.6 nm, indicating vigorous movement of residues in these regions (Figure 8d). These highly fluctuating regions were associated with the functions of TGF-β1 and could participate in substrate recognition or ligand binding through conformational changes.

In the radius of gyration (Rg) curve of the TGF-β1-naringin system, during the initial stage (0 to 40 ns), due to the local folding and domain rearrangement of TGF-β1 induced by naringin binding, the Rg decreased from 3.8 nm to around 3.0 nm, corresponding to the conformational adjustment process indicated by the RMSD. After 40 ns, the Rg fluctuated stably within the range of 2.8 to 3.2 nm, suggesting the formation of a relatively stable spatial structure of the complex (Figure 8e).

In the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) curve of the TGF-β1-naringin system, over the simulation period (0 to 100 ns), as the ligand naringin bound, some surface residues of TGF-β1 became buried, resulting in the conformation gradually becoming more compact. The SASA dropped from around 280 nm² initially to the range of 240 nm², and after 40 ns, it fluctuated stably within 230 to 250 nm² (Figure 8f). The stable SASA reflected that during the molecular dynamics simulation of the complex, the degree of protein surface exposure had not changed considerably.

In the hydrogen bond number curve of the TGF-β1-naringin system, the number of hydrogen bonds fluctuated frequently within the range of 0 to 6 throughout the simulation period, showing a highly dynamic hydrogen bonding interaction between the ligand and TGF-β1. Although there were dynamic changes, the number of hydrogen bonds mostly stayed between two and four for most of the time, indicating that hydrogen bonds played an anchoring role in the binding (Figure 8g).

Naringin directly bind to TGF-β1

Finally, the CETSA was conducted to further verify interaction between naringin with TGF-β1 protein. As shown in Figures 8h to 8i, after adding naringin to EPCs, a significant shift in the melting curve of TGF-β1 was observed when heated above 52°C. Analysis of the relative intensity of bands at different temperatures revealed that, following treatment with naringin, the degradation of TGF-β1 decreased and protein expression level increased, indicating that naringin can directly bind to TGF-β1 and enhance its thermal stability.

Discussion

The induced membrane of rats at three to six weeks has been confirmed to contain a three-layer structure.6 The inner layer is in contact with PMMA, which is a thin layer rich in cells. This layer was formed in induced membrane after three to six weeks of PMMA implantation. The intermediate layer of the induced membrane comprises a fibrous network that appears roughly three weeks following PMMA implantation and undergoes gradual maturation during the period between three and six weeks. The outer layer of the membrane is contiguous with the muscle and comprises loosely structured, disorganized tissue. This section is abundant in microvascular structures, which are aligned perpendicular to PMMA. After two weeks of induced membrane formation, vascularization was first observed, with a significant increase in vascular density at four weeks and no further changes at six weeks.37 Liu et al38 observed that the thickness and vascular density of the induced membrane gradually increased within two to eight weeks, and gradually decreased after eight weeks.

As is well known, angiogenesis is of great importance to coupled osteogenesis.39 There is a complex and close communication between the induced membrane’s vessels and the cells in the bone graft area. Currently, some scholars are attempting to use exogenous interventions to promote the quality of induced membrane formation. Yılmaz et al40 investigated the effect of concentrated growth factor (CGF) on the quality of rabbit induced membranes, and found that compared with the control group, CGF can increase the thickness, the proportion of elastic fibers, inflammatory response, cell proliferation, vascular quantity and stem cell quantity in the early (3 weeks) and late (6 weeks) induced membranes. Bilal et al41 studied the effect of platelet rich plasma (PRP) or epidermal growth factor (EGF) on the Masquelet technique in rats. Six weeks after the first-stage surgery, the levels of CD31, TGF, and VEGF in the membrane of the PRP and EGF groups were significantly higher than those in control group without use of growth factors. These studies validated that autologous platelet concentrates (APCs) exhibited efficacy in promoting angiogenesis and growth factor expression within induced membranes. Nevertheless, their procurement from autologous blood often necessitated multiple injections, resulting in some degree of trauma and relatively high treatment costs. Additionally, the absence of standardized preparation protocols for these APCs posed risks such as postoperative infection, inflammation and allergic reactions.42-44 Consequently, there was a pressing need to explore an oral medication for stimulating angiogenesis in induced membranes and facilitating subsequent bone graft mineralization, owing to its ease of administration, cost-effectiveness, and minimal side effects.

Naringin, a natural flavonoid, has been shown to promote angiogenesis by upregulating pro-angiogenic factors and enhancing endothelial function.26-29 The results of this study indicated that naringin not only increased the number of blood vessel formation in induced membranes in a dosage-sensitive manner, but also accelerated the proliferation rate of intramembranous cells. Compared with existing methods for improving the quality of induced membranes,40,41 naringin offers a simple administration approach that requires no injection, is cost-effective, non-invasive, widely available, and has no observed adverse reactions. To avoid potential interference of naringin on bone graft healing outcomes, we did not administer naringin treatment after the second stage of surgery, enabling us to analyze the impact of naringin-treated induced membranes with varying vascular content on bone graft growth. The experimental results demonstrated that a rich vascular network further stimulated the mineralization process of bone grafts in the induced membrane. Consequently, the bone defect healing rates in the medium- and high-dose groups were faster, with more favourable radiological and histological outcomes. The intimate connection between angiogenesis and osteogenesis also underscores the existence of molecular communication between endothelial cells and osteoblasts.35,45 Within the bone microenvironment, various cell types influence each other’s proliferation and differentiation through cell-to-cell connections and the secretion of growth factors.46 Multiple factors and signalling pathways have been identified that concurrently regulate both vasculogenesis and osteogenesis, such as PDGF-BB, VEGF, SLIT3, hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), and the Notch pathway.46 Our research findings demonstrated that naringin stimulated the secretion of PDGF-BB, VEGF, and SLIT in EPCs, suggesting that naringin possessed the capability to promote angiogenesis and osteogenesis even during the phase of induced membrane formation.

TGF-β1 serves as a versatile cytokine, strongly influencing the regulation of angiogenic processes in vascular endothelial cells. SMAD transcription factors are the core of the TGF-β pathway. TGF-β1 triggers both SMAD-dependent and SMAD-independent signalling to fulfill its biological roles.47 Previous research has shown that naringin could promote TGF-β1 expression of induced membrane in a dose-related way in rats, but the regulatory mechanism of naringin on downstream SMAD transcription factors has not been further explored.48 That study suggested that naringin could upregulate the expression of TGF-β1 and its downstream SMAD transcription factors in a dose-dependent manner, thereby promoting vascular formation in induced membranes. As mentioned earlier, the induced membrane contains a rich vascular network, and the formation of blood vessels cannot be separated from EPCs. That in vitro experiment has shown that naringin could promote the proliferation, migration, and angiogenic function of EPCs. Meanwhile, naringin could effectively reverse the inhibitory effect of si-TGF-β1 on the function of EPCs, indicating that naringin should promote the angiogenic function of EPCs by regulating the TGF-β/SMAD signalling pathway.

Finally, to comprehensively verify the direct binding between naringin and TGF-β1 from multiple dimensions, this study conducted a series of experiments including molecular docking, MDS, and CETSA. The initial molecular docking screening revealed potential binding sites between the two. MDS further demonstrated that upon binding to TGF-β1, the system remained stable, with key residues participating in the interaction and exhibiting favourable binding free energy. The CETSA experiment confirmed at the cellular level that naringin treatment enhanced the thermal stability of TGF-β1, making it less prone to denaturation under high temperatures. These results validated the direct binding between naringin and TGF-β1 at different levels, providing directions and new molecular targets for the optimization of IMT. This held promise for the development of more precisely targeted oral medications. Additionally, this study provided a scientific basis for the application of active ingredients of traditional Chinese medicine in the field of bone defects, promoting the development of integrated traditional Chinese and Western medicine in treating bone defects, especially suitable for regions with limited medical resources.

The induced membrane of rats is a continuously developing tissue, and there may be differences in the number of blood vessels and TGF-β1 expression within the membrane at different time periods. The limitation of this study was that it failed to dynamically evaluate the vascular number and the cascade level of TGF-β/SMAD at diverse stages, and the intervention effect of naringin. Furthermore, this study did not include a negative control group containing normal femoral tissue, making it impossible to accurately compare the differences between bone defects repaired using the IMT technique and normal femora. Future studies should add this group to enhance the reliability of conclusions.

To summarize, throughout the phase of induced membrane development, naringin could dose-dependently promote angiogenesis of the membrane and mineralization of bone grafts in the later stage. The underlying mechanism was related to naringin’s ability to target and bind to TGF-β1, upregulate the TGF-β1/SMAD signalling pathway, and thereby stimulate the proliferation, migration, and angiogenic functions of EPCs. This result partly clarified the mechanism of naringin enhancing bone formation in the induced membrane from the perspective of angiogenesis.
