Perinatal Mood and Anxiety in Women Undergoing Open Maternal-Fetal Surgery for Myelomeningocele Closure
Authors: Alexandria Budney, Juliana S. Gebb, Shelly Soni, Nahla Khalek, Julie S. Moldenhauer, Joanna C.M. Cole, Gregory G. Heuer, N. Scott Adzick, Christina Paidas Teefey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPregnant people carrying fetuses with congenital anomalies are at increased risk for perinatal mood and anxiety disorders (PMADs). Limited data exist regarding the impact of maternal-fetal surgery on maternal mental health. This study aimed to evaluate PMAD symptoms in pregnant people who opted for open maternal-fetal surgery (OMFS) for spina bifida (fMMC) closure.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent OMFS for fMMC closure at a level III fetal center. The Postpartum Depression Screening Scale (PDSS) and Impact of Event Scale-Revised (IES-R) were administered at initial evaluation and 1 day post-birth. Demographics, perioperative factors, surgical and delivery outcomes, and type of psychosocial support interactions were collected.ResultsOf 171 pregnant people who underwent OMFS, 91 (50.8%) screened positive for depressive symptoms and 27 (15.1%) for anxiety/traumatic stress at the initial evaluation, prior to OMFS. After delivery, 45 (25.1%) screened positive for depressive symptoms and 2 (0.01%) for anxiety/traumatic stress symptoms. There was a statistically significant decrease in positive screen rates in the postpartum period for depressive and anxiety/traumatic stress (p < 0.001).ConclusionsWomen undergoing OMFS for fMMC closure are at a high risk for PMAD symptoms in the perinatal period. Routine mental health screening in pregnancy and integrated psychosocial support are essential in caring for this unique population.

Introduction

Approximately 3% of fetuses are diagnosed with a congenital anomaly each year [1]. Perinatal mood and anxiety symptoms are common in pregnancy and are often associated with pregnancy-related morbidity [2]. During pregnancy, it is reported that 10–20% of pregnant people experience depressive symptoms [3] and 8–20% experience anxiety symptoms [4, 5]. In expectant parents, the rates of perinatal depression, anxiety, and traumatic stress are further increased in the setting of a congenital anomaly [6, 7].

Myelomeningocele (MMC), the most severe form of open neural tube defect, affects approximately 1 in every 2,875 live births in the USA annually [8]. The open neural tube defect results in ongoing trauma and toxicity to the spinal nerves in the fetus throughout the pregnancy due to amniotic fluid exposure at the level of the vertebral defect and below [9]. The varying degree of injury to the spinal nerves results in sensory, motor, genitourinary, and gastrointestinal sequelae [9]. Uncertainty and concern for short- and long-term well-being and quality of life are prevailing themes that can prompt distress among expectant parents with a prenatal diagnosis of open neural tube defect [10].

Open maternal-fetal surgery (OMFS) for fMMC closure can be offered in certain cases that meet criteria for candidacy with the goal of protecting the exposed central nervous tissue (CNS) from further damage in utero [11, 12]. The Management of Myelomeningocele (MOMS) trial and subsequent studies on outcomes of OMFS for fMMC closure continue to demonstrate significant decrease in the need for ventriculoperitoneal shunting in the first year of life, reversal of hindbrain herniation, and improved motor outcomes for affected children [13, 14].

Expectant parents face psychosocial challenges and increased risk for mood and anxiety symptoms in the setting of a prenatal diagnosis of MMC [15]. Additionally, the option of OMFS prompts consideration of known maternal risk while also raising the question of increased psychological stress burden, as families must consider broader social and environmental factors while also grappling with the ongoing uncertainty surrounding their unborn child’s diagnosis. Furthermore, literature suggests that the parents of children requiring higher levels of medical care are at a higher risk for psychological distress [10, 16]. Despite advances in prenatal diagnosis and in utero therapies, there is a paucity of data on the psychological impact of fetal intervention on affected families. This study describes depressive, anxiety, and traumatic stress symptoms experienced in expectant pregnant patients who have opted to undergo OMFS for fMMC in a well-established fetal therapy center.

Materials and Methods

Evaluation and Eligibility for Fetal Surgery

The study was conducted at a single maternal-fetal therapy center. Patients referred with a fetal diagnosis of an open neural tube defect undergo extensive imaging evaluation including detailed ultrasound, fetal MRI, fetal echocardiography, genetic counseling, and invasive genetic testing prior to being offered OMFS. Expectant families have extensive counseling with a multidisciplinary OMFS team, including maternal-fetal medicine, pediatric neurosurgery, pediatric surgery, neonatology, and perinatal psychosocial team assessment prior to making their decision to proceed with the intervention. Every pregnant patient undergoing OMFS for NTD closure undergoes mental health (MH) screening and social work assessment unless they specifically decline. Based on the social work assessment, an individualized plan is made for each family based on specific needs and resources. Those who screen positive for clinically significant MH symptoms are evaluated by the perinatal psychologist within the fetal center who then determines the need for additional therapy or professional MH consultation. All participants met medical and diagnostic criteria for OMFS as previously described and underwent OMFS for repair of the open neural tube defect, and there were no fetoscopic procedures in this cohort [11]. Based on data from a previous study, OMFS is performed between 23 weeks 0 days and 25 weeks 5 days to optimize the surgical risk-to-benefit profile [17]. Delivery is planned via cesarean at 37 weeks 0 days unless indicated sooner [11].

Study Design

A retrospective cohort study was performed to assess MH symptoms in expectant parents with pregnancies complicated by a fetal diagnosis of open neural tube defect who underwent OMFS [11] between December 2013 and October 2021. MH screening was performed per previously published protocol [6, 18]. All patients are screened at 2 timepoints to assess for depressive risk as well as anxiety and traumatic stress symptoms, first, during pregnancy at the time of fetal diagnosis confirmation and again within 48 h of birth within the fetal center adult inpatient unit. The postpartum screening timepoint rationale includes facilitation of continuity of care teams for the family, addressing immediate concerns prior to discharge and allowing for warm handoff to psychosocial professionals in the specialized neonatal care environment (Fig. 1). Embedded perinatal psychosocial services work alongside families to address the social, emotional, spiritual, and familial support needs for affected families [19].

Antenatal diagnosis and MH screening timeline.

Each pregnant patient’s electronic medical record was reviewed for demographic, obstetric, and MH history. Surgical outcome of the OMFS and pregnancy outcome data were collected. Data on pregnancy-related complications including preterm prelabor rupture of membranes, severe oligohydramnios (AFI <5 cm OR DVP <2 cm on ultrasound imaging), preterm delivery, pulmonary edema requiring treatment, preeclampsia, placental abruption, global chorioamniotic membrane separation, prenatal admission greater than 5 days, and indications for delivery (e.g., scheduled vs. unscheduled) due to various maternal and/or fetal factors were recorded. A composite of these factors was defined as a “complicated pregnancy course.”

MH Screening Measures

Postpartum Depression Screening Scale

To assess depressive symptoms during pregnancy, pregnant patients completed the Postpartum Depression Screening Scale (PDSS) [20], a 35-item self-report measure. Participants indicate their symptoms after hearing about the fetal diagnosis and again after the baby is born using a Likert scale (ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Total scores can range from 0 to 175, and participants’ scores are placed on a continuum of depressive risk: normal adjustment (0–59), significant symptoms of postpartum depression (60–79), and positive screening for major postpartum depression (≥80). This valid and reliable screener allows sensitivity and specificity of seven different subscales: sleeping/eating disturbances, anxiety/insecurity, emotional lability, cognitive impairment, loss of self, guilt/shame, and suicidal thoughts. These subscales allow clinicians to determine specific areas of concern when developing therapeutic interventions. The internal consistency alpha scores for the subscales range from 0.83 (sleeping/eating disturbances) to 0.94 (loss of self) [20, 21].

Impact of Events Scale-Revised

To assess for anxiety and traumatic stress, the Impact of Events Scale-Revised (IES-R) [22] is used with specific instructions to complete the form with respect to learning of their baby’s prenatal diagnosis and then after seeing their baby in the N/ICU postnatally. Total scores range from 0 to 88, and participants’ scores are placed on a continuum of risk for traumatic stress: no risk (0–23), clinical risk (24–32), and high risk (≥33).

Scores above the clinical threshold were followed up in a timely manner with a comprehensive psychological assessment. The screening results were used to inform treatment planning and to address the risks for perinatal mood and anxiety symptoms. The screener utilizes 3 different subscales to aid in determining areas of concern: intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal. Several studies have shown the IES-R’s high internal consistency (alpha = 0.96) as well as a high correlation with the already established PTSD checklist (0.84) [23].

Data Collection and Statistical Analysis

De-identified demographic information, pregnancy-related data, and screener scores were reviewed and entered into a password-protected REDCap [24] electronic database by designated research coordinators. Analyses were conducted using STATA version 16, College Station, TX [25]. Descriptive statistics explored the portion of expectant pregnant patients whose MH screening scores were considered at clinical risk.

Results

During the study period, 179 patients underwent OMFS for neural tube defect closure and completed universal MH screening at both timepoints. Patient demographics are presented in Table 1. The majority of the study population self-identified as Caucasian (80.4%), Christian (74.7%), college-educated (37.4%), and employed (73.2%) with private insurance reported in 86.0%. The majority (67.6%) of patients lived within 50 miles from the study center. The mean gestational age at the time of first MH screener completion was 22 weeks and 4 days gestation (range 17 weeks and 5 days to 32 weeks). Approximately 26% (n = 46) of pregnant patients reported previous or current use of medication to manage mood or anxiety symptoms.

Participant demographics

At the time of the first MH screen, prior to OMFS, clinically significant depressive symptoms were present in 50.8% (N = 91/179) of pregnant patients and 16.2% (N = 29/179) endorsed symptoms of traumatic stress and anxiety (Table 2). At the second timepoint, in the postnatal period, depressive symptoms were reported in 25.4% (45/177) and traumatic stress/anxiety symptoms were reported in 1.1% (2/177) of patients. There was a clinically significant decrease in the rates of positive screening for both symptoms of depression and traumatic stress between the prenatal and postnatal timepoints in the study population (p < 0.001). In subgroup analysis, there was no significant difference in baseline screening scores by education level, employment status, or insurance type for depression (education: p = 0.618; employment: p = 0.103; insurance: p = 0.082) or anxiety/traumatic stress (education: p = 0.527; employment: p = 0.304; insurance: p = 0.194). Additional analysis examining demographic (maternal age, education, insurance status, employment, race, and religion), clinical (history of MH diagnosis and medication usage), and obstetric characteristics (gestational age and gravida) showed no significant differences in baseline depression screening scores. Similarly, no significant differences in depression or anxiety/traumatic stress screening scores were observed by gestational age at initial appointment, time between screening and delivery, or gravida. However, a statistically significant difference in anxiety/traumatic stress symptoms was observed between mothers aged <30 years old and ≥30 years old (p = 0.029).

Within the cohort, 56.4% (101/179) had at least one of the defined factors within the “complicated pregnancy course” composite (Table 4). Pregnancy complications included severe oligohydramnios in 12.3%, preterm prelabor rupture of membrane in 17.9%, and 16.8% were noted to have significant chorioamniotic membrane separation that prompted hospitalization and fetal monitoring or delivery based on gestational age. There were no cases of uterine rupture in the cohort, and therefore, this was not included in the composite.

Discussion

In the USA, between 7 and 20% of pregnant people experience symptoms of depression while 9–21% report symptoms of anxiety or trauma based on previous studies [5, 26]. Previously published studies report that between 22 and 32% of pregnant persons carrying a fetus with a congenital anomaly demonstrate symptoms of perinatal mood and anxiety disorders (PMADs) [27]. PMAD symptoms have been reported in 22–32% of pregnant persons with the current cohort of expectant pregnant people undergoing OMFS for fMMC closure-endorsed depressive symptoms in over 50% and anxiety/traumatic symptoms in over 16% at initial screening [6]. Rates of both depressive (25.4%) and anxiety/traumatic symptoms (1.1%) decreased after birth, although there continued to be heightened depressive symptoms in the postpartum period as parents transition to the neonatal environment, despite prenatal surgery.

Additional risk factors for positive MH screeners in pregnant persons with congenital anomalies are important to consider and expand upon for awareness and support. Hansen et al. [27] identified insurance challenges and a maternal history of MH diagnosis as risk factors for positive screeners for depressive symptoms in the setting of a congenital anomaly [5]. In this fetal surgery cohort, 49 (27.4%) pregnant persons endorsed current or prior MH diagnosis, and 46 (25.7%) reported current or past use of antidepressant medication. There was no significant difference in depression, anxiety, or traumatic stress symptoms in the setting of relocation for care, preexisting MH diagnosis, or treatment of MH diagnosis with medication, in the prenatal or postpartum screening in this fetal surgery population. Interestingly, there was no correlation between screening for depressive, anxiety, or traumatic risk and complicated pregnancy course.

There is a paucity of data to inform the additional psychosocial needs of pregnant patients and families undergoing fetal intervention in the setting of a congenital anomaly. These data emphasize the need for universal perinatal MH screening as well as embedded psychosocial support teams to assess next steps in care for this at-risk population. All patients in the study cohort were referred by their local provider and therefore had various levels of counseling and information prior to additional counseling within the fetal center. It is not possible to know the amount, quality, or context of this counseling as well as the degree of ambiguity in which it was communicated prior to time 1 screening due to the study design.

This individualized psychosocial scaffolding can be built over time throughout the pregnancy and augmented in the postpartum period, when indicated. Based on mean timing of the first MH screener (22 weeks 4 days) and average gestational age at delivery (36 weeks 0 days), there is a window of time where support professionals may be able to propose strategies to mitigate symptoms prior to and following fetal intervention. Our study highlights that pregnant patients with clinically significant symptoms of depression, anxiety, and/or traumatic stress demonstrated higher numbers of psychosocial team interactions (average of 6.8 interactions with a range of 0–53 interactions per family). This study did not further investigate the various diagnoses or treatments in these participants; however, this will be an area of study for our group in the future. As previously published, postpartum screening demonstrates decreased depressive, anxiety, and traumatic stress risk [18]. Long-term screening was not performed in this retrospective study; however, it would be important to assess over time in this group of parents in future research efforts.

The study team acknowledges various challenges with retrospective data. The study did not include patients undergoing a fetoscopic or modified fetoscopic approach to MMC closure to compare open versus minimally invasive approaches. Minimally invasive options for MMC closure may have a different psychosocial screening profile. The results from this study are limited due to the lack of generalizability of the results due to the convenience sample. Another limitation of the study includes the self-reported screening design which allows for a degree of reporting bias. The team also cannot say whether the decrease in screening results from the prenatal to the postnatal period was attributed to the additional psychosocial services as further studies are necessary to understand this potential impact. OMFS for open neural tube defect is a risk factor for PMAD symptoms in the affected pregnant person. The importance of individualized awareness, screening, assessment, and care for expectant families with a prenatal diagnosis of neural tube defect undergoing OMFS is essential. The study also highlights the importance of further studies to define psychosocial intervention models to best support families in the fetal center.
