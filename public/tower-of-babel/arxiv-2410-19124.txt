Magnetic-resonance-induced nonlinear current response in magnetic Weyl semimetals
Ruobing Mei, Chao-Xing Liu

Abstract
In this work, we propose a geometric nonlinear current response induced by magnetic resonance in magnetic Weyl semimetals. This phenomenon is in analog to the quantized circular photogalvanic effect [ 1 ] previously proposed for Weyl semimetal phases of chiral crystals. However, the nonlinear current response in our case can occur in magnetic Weyl semimetals where time-reversal symmetry, instead of inversion symmetry, is broken. The occurrence of this phenomenon relies on the special coupling between Weyl electrons and magnetic fluctuations induced by magnetic resonance. To further support our analytical solution, we perform numerical studies on a model Hamiltonian describing the Weyl semimetal phase in a topological insulator system with ferromagnetism.

In this work, we propose an alternative approach to overcome this obstruction in magnetic Weyl semimetals, in which time-reversal symmetry is broken but inversion symmetry is preserved so that two Weyl nodes with opposite chiral charges appear at the same energy. The CPGE contributions from different Weyl nodes will cancel each other so that we expect a vanishing CPGE for the nonlinear electromagnetic response in this case (Fig.
1
a). The key idea is to introduce the concept of “pseudo-gauge field” via magnetic fluctuation or spin wave in magnetic Weyl semimetals
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
. Here the pseudo-gauge field refers to the perturbation that behaves in a similar manner as a gauge field coupled to the Weyl fermions in the low energy sector of the system. Different from the usual electromagnetic field, this gauge coupling depends on the chirality of Weyl fermions, and has opposite signs when the Weyl fermion chirality reverses. In Sec.
III
, we will study the nonlinear current response in magnetic Weyl semimetals due to the interplay between electromagnetic fields and the magnetic-resonance (MR) induced pseudo-gauge field, in analog to the CPGE purely from the electromagnetic fields. Strikingly, we find two Weyl nodes with opposite chiral charges contribute the same sign to the MR-induced nonlinear current, in sharp contrast to the opposite sign contributions of the normal CPGE. This difference originates from the chirality-dependent gauge coupling form of the pseudo-gauge field. In Sec.
IV
, we will demonstrate that the magnetic fluctuation or spin wave, which can be driven via ferromagnetic resonance, serves as the pseudo-gauge field in magnetic Weyl semimetals. In Sec.
V
, we implemented numerical studies of the magnetic-resonance-induced nonlinear current response in a magnetic Weyl semimetal model that was previously adopted to describe magnetically doped topological insulators, in order to justify our analytical solutions.

II
Model Hamiltonian

We start from a four-band model that describe a topological insulator system with ferromagnetism in Ref.
[
41
]
, which can capture the Weyl semimetal phase when magnetization term dominates over the nonmagnetic band gap. The model Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+H_{1},

(1)

\displaystyle H_{0}={\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k})\tau_{z}+L_{1}k_{z}\tau_{y}+L_{2}(k_{y}\sigma_{x}-k_{x}\sigma_{y})\tau_{x}+m\sigma_{z},

\displaystyle H_{1}=\bm{\nu}(t)\cdot\bm{\sigma}\tau_{z},

where
{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k})=M_{0}+M_{1}k^{2}_{z}+M_{2}(k^{2}_{x}+k^{2}_{y})
,
M_{0,1,2},L_{1,2}
are material dependent parameters, and
m
describes the out-of-plane magnetization and can induce the Weyl semimetal phase when
m>M_{0}
by neglecting the quadratic terms in
{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k})
.
Here we also include the
H_{1}
term that represents the magnetic fluctuation and assume
\bm{\nu}(t)
has temporal dependence.
In Ref.
[
41
]
, additional terms with the form
\bm{\mu}(t)\cdot\bm{\sigma}
can exist for magnetic fluctuations. However, these terms will only slightly modify the form of the pseudo-gauge field discussed below and does not affect the final result. Thus, we drop these additional terms and only focus on the
\bm{\nu}(t)
terms in this work.
H_{1}
is treated as a perturbation below. Next we will show that when the model Hamiltonian is in the magnetic Weyl semimetal phase for
m>M_{0}
and projected to the subspace of two low-energy Weyl fermions, the magnetic fluctuation in
H_{1}
acts like a chiral gauge field which couples oppositely to the Weyl fermions with left and right chiralities
[
41
]
.

The eigen-energy of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
in Eq. (
1
) can be solved analytically as

\varepsilon_{\lambda\mu}=\lambda\sqrt{L_{2}^{2}\left(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}\right)+\left(\sqrt{{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}}+\mu\left|m\right|\right)^{2}},

(2)

where
\lambda,\mu=\pm
, and we denote the eigenstates as
\left|\lambda,\mu\right\rangle
. When
k_{x}=k_{y}=0
, the eigen-energies of the two middle bands are
\varepsilon_{-,-}=-\sqrt{{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}}+m
and
\varepsilon_{+,-}=\sqrt{{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}}-m
, assuming
m>0
. Thus, the gap between the two states
\left|-,-\right\rangle
and
\left|+,-\right\rangle
can be closed at
{\mathcal{M}}^{2}+L_{1}^{2}k_{z}^{2}=m^{2}
if
m>\left|{\mathcal{M}}\right|
. By neglecting the quadratic term
M_{1}k_{z}^{2}
in
{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k})
, namely
{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{k})=M_{0}
, we can find two gap closing points at
\bm{k}=(0,0,\pm K_{0})
with
K_{0}=\sqrt{m^{2}-M_{0}^{2}}/L_{1}
.

At the gap closing point
\bm{k}_{0}=(0,0,K_{0})
, the Hamiltonian can be simplified as

H_{0}(\bm{k}_{0})=M_{0}\tau_{z}+L_{1}K_{0}\tau_{y}+m\sigma_{z},

(3)

and the eigenstates are solved as

\displaystyle\left|-,+\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{-}}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
M_{0}-m\\
iL_{1}K_{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\left|-,-\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{-}}}\begin{pmatrix}M_{0}-m\\
iL_{1}K_{0}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(4)

\displaystyle\left|+,-\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{+}}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
M_{0}+m\\
iL_{1}K_{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\left|+,+\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{+}}}\begin{pmatrix}M_{0}+m\\
iL_{1}K_{0}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

where
N_{\pm}=(M_{0}\pm m)^{2}+L_{1}^{2}K_{0}^{2}
are the normalization factors. The effective Hamiltonian around
\bm{k}_{0}
up to the first order in
\delta\bm{k}
is written as

H^{\prime}=L_{1}\delta k_{z}\tau_{y}+L_{2}(\delta k_{y}\sigma_{x}-\delta k_{x}\sigma_{y})\tau_{x}+\bm{\nu}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\tau_{z}.

(5)

We then project the effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
into the subspace of the basis
\left|-,-\right\rangle
and
\left|+,-\right\rangle
by applying the second-order perturbation

H_{mn}=\left\langle m\right|H^{\prime}\left|n\right\rangle+\sum_{l\neq m,n}\frac{1}{2}\left\langle m\right|H^{\prime}\left|l\right\rangle\left\langle l\right|H^{\prime}\left|n\right\rangle\times\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{l}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}-\varepsilon_{l}}\right],

(6)

which gives the effective Hamiltonian in the low-energy subspace as

\displaystyle H_{eff}^{+}=

\displaystyle-\frac{M_{0}}{m}\nu_{z}+\left(-L_{2}\delta k_{x}+\frac{L_{1}K_{0}}{m}\nu_{x}\right)\sigma_{x}+\left(-L_{2}\delta k_{y}+\frac{L_{1}K_{0}}{m}\nu_{y}\right)\sigma_{y}

(7)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2m}\left(2L_{1}^{2}K_{0}\delta k_{z}+\nu_{z}^{2}-\frac{M_{0}^{2}}{m^{2}}(\nu_{x}^{2}+\nu_{y}^{2}+\nu_{z}^{2})\right)\sigma_{z},

where we keep
\delta\bm{k}
up to the first order and
\bm{\nu}
up to the second order. Similarly, we can perform the projection at the other gap closing point
(0,0,-K_{0})
, and obtain the effective Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{eff}^{-}=

\displaystyle-\frac{M_{0}}{m}\nu_{z}+\left(L_{2}\delta k_{x}+\frac{L_{1}K_{0}}{m}\nu_{x}\right)\sigma_{x}+\left(L_{2}\delta k_{y}+\frac{L_{1}K_{0}}{m}\nu_{y}\right)\sigma_{y}

(8)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2m}\left(-2L_{1}^{2}K_{0}\delta k_{z}+\nu_{z}^{2}-\frac{M_{0}^{2}}{m^{2}}(\nu_{x}^{2}+\nu_{y}^{2}+\nu_{z}^{2})\right)\sigma_{z}.

Combining the two Hamiltonians, we can then write down the low-energy effective Hamiltonian for the two gap closing points or two Weyl points as

\displaystyle H_{eff}

\displaystyle=\left(L_{2}\delta k_{x}\tau_{z}+\frac{L_{1}K_{0}}{m}\nu_{x}\right)\sigma_{x}+\left(L_{2}\delta k_{y}\tau_{z}+\frac{L_{1}K_{0}}{m}\nu_{y}\right)\sigma_{y}

(9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2m}\left(2L_{1}^{2}K_{0}\delta k_{z}\tau_{z}-\nu_{z}^{2}+\frac{M_{0}^{2}}{m^{2}}(\nu_{x}^{2}+\nu_{y}^{2}+\nu_{z}^{2})\right)\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=\hbar v_{f}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\delta\bm{k}\,\tau_{z}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{a}\right),

where
\bm{\sigma}
are Pauli matrices in the spin basis,
\tau_{z}
is in the basis of two Weyl points,
\hbar v_{f}=L_{2}
,
\delta\bm{k}=(\delta k_{x},\delta k_{y},\frac{L_{1}^{2}K_{0}}{L_{2}m}\delta k_{z})
, and
\bm{a}=(a_{x},a_{y},a_{z})
with
a_{x}=\frac{\hbar L_{1}K_{0}}{eL_{2}m}\nu_{x}
,
a_{y}=\frac{\hbar L_{1}K_{0}}{eL_{2}m}\nu_{y}
, and
a_{z}=\frac{\hbar}{2eL_{2}m}(-\nu_{z}^{2}+\frac{M_{0}^{2}}{m^{2}}(\nu_{x}^{2}+\nu_{y}^{2}+\nu_{z}^{2}))
. We may introduce the electromagnetic field by the Peierls substitution
\delta\bm{k}\rightarrow\delta\bm{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{A}
with the vector potential
\bm{A}
. By comparing the coupling form of
\bm{A}
with
\bm{a}
, we find the
\bm{a}
field, which results from the magnetic fluctuation
\bm{\nu}
, couples oppositely to the Weyl nodes at the two gap closing points in a gauge coupling form, and thus we conclude the
\bm{a}
field acts like a chiral gauge field
[
41
]
. Below we will use the terminology, either chiral gauge field or pseudo-gauge field, to refer to the
\bm{a}
field.

Due to the presence of magnetic ordering, the time-reversal symmetry is broken in magnetic Weyl semimetals, and the minimal model of Weyl semimetals with two Weyl nodes can be realized by the model Hamiltonian in Eq. (
1
), as shown by the numerical simulations of the energy bands for Eq. (
1
) in Fig.
2
a.
On the other hand, if only inversion symmetry is broken, the total number of Weyl nodes in the system must be a multiple of four, as the time-reversal symmetry transforms a Weyl node at
\bm{k}
to another Weyl node at
-\bm{k}
with the same chirality and all the chiral charges for the whole Weyl semimetals must cancel
[
42
]
.

III
Magnetic-resonance-induced current response for Weyl fermions

We next consider the nonlinear response for the left-handed and right-handed Weyl fermions described by the Hamiltonian based on the effective Hamiltonian Eq. (
9
)

\displaystyle H_{L}=\hbar v_{f}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\bm{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{A}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{a}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle H_{R}=-\hbar v_{f}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\left(\bm{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{A}-\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{a}\right),

where
\bm{\sigma}
is Pauli matrices,
\bm{A}
is the electromagnetic field, and
\bm{a}
is the chiral gauge field minimally coupled to the left-handed and right-handed Weyl fermions
[
41
]
. We rewrite the above Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H_{L}=H_{L0}+\bm{A}\cdot\bm{J}_{L}+\bm{a}\cdot\bm{J}_{L},

(11)

\displaystyle H_{R}=H_{R0}+\bm{A}\cdot\bm{J}_{R}-\bm{a}\cdot\bm{J}_{R},

where
H_{L0}=-H_{R0}=\hbar v_{f}\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{k}
and
\bm{J}_{L}=-\bm{J}_{R}=ev_{f}\bm{\sigma}
. Let us first focus on the left-handed Weyl fermion
H_{L}
. Following the density matrix formalism in the velocity gauge described in Appendix
B
, we rewrite the Hamiltonian into the second-quantization form as

\hat{H}_{L}=\sum_{n}\int d\bm{k}\varepsilon^{L}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}\left[\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}^{L}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}+\bm{a}_{L}(t)\cdot\bm{J}^{L}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}\right],

(12)

where
\varepsilon^{L}_{n\bm{k}}
is the eigenvalue of
H_{L0}
and
\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}
and
\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}^{{\dagger}}
are the annihilation and creation operators. The Bloch equation of the density matrix
\rho_{nm}(t)
, defined by
\rho_{nm,\bm{k}}(t)=\langle\hat{a}_{m\bm{k}}^{{\dagger}}(t)\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}(t)\rangle
,
is then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{nm}(t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon^{L}_{nm}\rho_{nm}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{n^{\prime}}\left[\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}^{L}_{n^{\prime}n}\rho_{n^{\prime}m}(t)-\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}^{L}_{mn^{\prime}}\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)\right]

(13)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{n^{\prime}}\left[\bm{a}_{L}(t)\cdot\bm{J}^{L}_{n^{\prime}n}\rho_{n^{\prime}m}(t)-\bm{a}_{L}(t)\cdot\bm{J}^{L}_{mn^{\prime}}\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)\right]-\frac{\rho_{nm}(t)}{\tau},

where
\tau
is the relaxation time and
\varepsilon^{L}_{nm}=\varepsilon^{L}_{n}-\varepsilon^{L}_{m}
. We can expand the density matrix in order of the perturbation of dynamic fields
\bm{A}
and
\bm{a}
. The zeroth order of the density matrix is given by
\rho^{(0)}_{nm}=f_{n}\delta_{nm}
where
f_{n}
is the Fermi factor of band
n
. At the first order of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{a}
, we obtain

\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega)=f_{nm}\frac{\left(A^{b}(\omega)+a^{b}_{L}(\omega)\right)(J_{L})^{b}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega)},

(14)

where we define
f_{nm}=f_{n}-f_{m}
,
A^{b}(t)=A^{b}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}
,
a_{L}^{b}(t)=a_{L}^{b}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}
, and
\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}=\varepsilon^{L}_{nm}/\hbar-i/\tau
. Here the Einstein summation rule has been assumed. At the second order of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{a}
, the diagonal component of density matrix can be derived as

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(\omega_{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{m}\left(A^{b}_{\beta}(a_{L})^{c}_{\gamma}+(a_{L})^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}\right)\left[\frac{f_{mn}(J_{L})^{b}_{mn}(J_{L})^{c}_{nm}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta})}-\frac{f_{nm}(J_{L})^{b}_{nm}(J_{L})^{c}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})}\right],

(15)

where
\omega_{\Sigma}=\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}
,
A^{b}_{\beta}=A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})
, and
(a_{L})^{b}_{\beta}=(a_{L})^{b}(\omega_{\beta})
.
Here we only consider the diagonal term in the second order of density matrix
\rho^{(2)}
. This is because we only consider two bands that form the Weyl points here, and the off-diagonal component of
\rho^{(2)}
is zero. It should be noted that if the third bands are involved, the off-diagonal component of
\rho^{(2)}
can exist and a three-band contribution can be important for the CPGE
[
35
]
. Moreover, we only keep the terms involving both
\bm{A}
and
\bm{a}_{L}
, as the other terms quadratic in
\bm{A}
or
\bm{a}_{L}
vanish between the left-handed and right-handed Weyl fermions due to their opposite chiral charges (see Appendix
A
). The corresponding intraband current is derived as

\displaystyle\tilde{j}_{L}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=e\sum_{n}\int_{k}v^{a}_{nn}\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(\omega_{\Sigma})e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\frac{\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{mn}(J_{L})^{b}_{mn}(J_{L})^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}\left(A^{b}_{\beta}(a_{L})^{c}_{\gamma}+(a_{L})^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}\right)e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

where
v^{a}_{nm}=\left\langle n\right|\frac{\partial H}{\partial k_{a}}\left|m\right\rangle
is the matrix element of the velocity operator and
\Delta^{a}_{nm}=v^{a}_{nn}-v^{a}_{mm}
is the velocity shift. We then use
A^{b}(\omega)=E^{b}(\omega)/i\omega
,
a_{L}^{b}(\omega)=\tilde{E}^{b}(\omega)/i\omega
with the pseudo-electric field
\tilde{E}
, and
(J_{L})^{b}_{nm}=\frac{ie}{\hbar}\varepsilon^{L}_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}
with the Berry connection
\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}_{nm}=\left\langle n\right|i\partial_{\bm{k}}\left|m\right\rangle
. We symmetrize all the indices, and take the time derivative of the imaginary part (see Appendix
A
) to obtain

\frac{d\tilde{j}^{a}_{L}(t)}{dt}=\tilde{\eta}^{L}_{abc}(\omega_{\Sigma};\omega_{\beta},\omega_{\gamma})E^{b}_{\beta}\tilde{E}^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}+\tilde{\eta}^{L}_{abc}(\omega_{\Sigma};\omega_{\gamma},\omega_{\beta})\tilde{E}^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

(17)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}^{L}_{abc}(\omega_{\Sigma};\omega_{\beta},\omega_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}(\Omega_{L})^{bc}_{mn}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{nm}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\beta}\omega_{\gamma}}

(18)

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\gamma})+\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\gamma})\right],

with the Berry curvature of the left-handed Weyl fermion
(\Omega_{L})^{bc}_{mn}=i({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})
. For
\omega_{\beta}=-\omega_{\gamma}=\omega
and
\omega_{\Sigma}=0
, we have

\tilde{\eta}^{L}_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)=-\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{2\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}(\Omega_{L})^{bc}_{mn}\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega).

(19)

We rewrite the above equations as (see Appendix
A
)

\tilde{\bm{j}}_{L}=\tau\tilde{\beta}_{L}(\omega)\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\tilde{\bm{E}}^{*}(\omega)+\tilde{\bm{E}}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right],

(20)

where
\tilde{\bm{E}}(\omega)=i\omega\bm{a}
can be regarded as the pseudo-electric field, and the MR-induced current response trace for the left-handed Weyl fermion is

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{L}(\omega)

\displaystyle=i\frac{e^{3}}{2h^{2}}\oint d\bm{S}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{L}=i\beta_{0}C_{L},

(21)

where
\bm{S}
is a closed surface in the momentum space,
\beta_{0}=\pi e^{3}/h^{2}
, and
C_{L}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint d\bm{S}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{L}=1
is nothing but the Chern number (equivalently the chiral charge of the Weyl node).
For the right-handed Weyl fermion, we repeat the above derivation to obtain

\tilde{\bm{j}}_{R}=-\tau\tilde{\beta}_{R}(\omega)\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\tilde{\bm{E}}^{*}(\omega)+\tilde{\bm{E}}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right],

(22)

with

\tilde{\beta}_{R}(\omega)=i\beta_{0}C_{R}=-i\beta_{0}C_{L}.

(23)

Thus, the total nonlinear response current is

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{j}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\bm{j}}_{L}+\tilde{\bm{j}}_{R}=2i\tau\beta_{0}C_{L}\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\tilde{\bm{E}}^{*}(\omega)+\tilde{\bm{E}}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right].

(24)

The current equation (
24
) is the main result of this work, from which we demonstrate that a non-zero total
dc
-current can be induced by the interplay between an electromagnetic field and a chiral gauge field at the nonlinear order for two Weyl nodes with opposite chiral charges, in contrast to the regular CPGE as discussed in Appendix
A
(also see Ref.
[
1
]
). From Eq. (
9
), we can see that the chiral gauge field
\bm{a}
depends on the time-dependent field
\bm{\nu}(t)
that describes magnetic fluctuations and thus we study the magnetic dynamics in magnetic topological insulators below.

IV
Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert equation for ferromagnetic resonance

To see how the magnetic fluctuation can induce a pseudo-electric field, we solve the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation for ferromagnetic resonance in this section. Similar to Ref.
[
38
]
, the LLG equation reads

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}=-\gamma_{0}\bm{M}\times\left(\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}-\eta\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}\right),

(25)

where
\bm{M}=(M_{x},M_{y},M_{z})
is the magnetization. The in-plane magnetization acts as a pseudo-gauge field in terms of
\bm{\nu}=\frac{c}{ev_{f}}g_{M}(-M_{y},M_{x},0)
, with the exchange coupling coefficient
g_{M}

[
38
]
. Therefore, the pseudo-electric field induced by the ferromagnetic resonance is

\tilde{\bm{E}}=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\bm{\nu}}{\partial t}=\frac{g_{M}}{ev_{f}}(\frac{dM_{y}}{dt},-\frac{dM_{x}}{dt},0).

(26)

The LLG equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{0}\bm{M}\times\left[\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}-\eta\left(-\gamma_{0}\bm{M}\times\left(\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}-\eta\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\approx-\gamma_{0}\bm{M}\times\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}-\eta\gamma^{2}_{0}\bm{M}\times(\bm{M}\times\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}),

where
\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}=\bm{B}-\bm{M}_{N}-\frac{KM_{z}}{M^{2}_{s}}\hat{e}_{z}
,
\bm{B}
is the applied magnetic field,
M_{s}=\left|\bm{M}\right|
,
K
is the anisotropy coefficient,
\bm{M}_{N}=(N_{x}M_{x},N_{y}M_{y},N_{z}M_{z})
is the demagnetizing field with
N_{x},N_{y}=0
,
\gamma_{0}=\frac{ge}{2m_{e}c}
is the magneto-mechanical ratio with Landé factor
g
, and
\eta
is Gilbert damping coefficient
[
38
,
43
,
44
]
.
Let us define the dimensionless damping constant
\alpha=\gamma_{0}\eta M_{s}
,
\gamma=\frac{\gamma_{0}}{1+\alpha^{2}}
, and the LLG equation is written as

\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}=-\gamma\bm{M}\times\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}-\frac{\gamma\alpha}{M_{s}}\bm{M}\times(\bm{M}\times\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}).

(28)

Since
dM_{s}/dt=0
, only the direction
\bm{n}=\bm{M}/M_{s}
is time-dependent with

\frac{d\bm{n}}{dt}=-\gamma\bm{n}\times\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}-\gamma\alpha\bm{n}\times(\bm{n}\times\bm{B}_{\text{eff}}).

(29)

We assume the in-plane magnetic field and magnetization are very small, i.e.
\left|n_{x}\right|\sim\left|n_{y}\right|\sim\left|B_{x}/M_{s}\right|\sim\left|B_{y}/M_{s}\right|\ll 1
, and at the first order of these quantities, we have

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{t}+\alpha\tilde{\omega}&\tilde{\omega}n_{z}\\
-\tilde{\omega}n_{z}&\partial_{t}+\alpha\tilde{\omega}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}n_{x}\\
n_{y}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\alpha&\gamma n_{z}\\
-\gamma n_{z}&\gamma\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}B_{x}\\
B_{y}\end{pmatrix},

(30)

where
\tilde{\omega}=\gamma n_{z}B_{\text{eff},z}
and
n_{z}=M_{z}/M_{s}=\text{sgn}(M_{z})
. We then perform the Laplace transformation as

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{i}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{-st}n_{i}(t),

(31)

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{i}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dte^{-st}B_{i}(t).

Eq. (
30
) then becomes

G_{0}(s)\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{n}_{x}(s)\\
tn_{y}(s)\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}n_{x}(0)\\
n_{y}(0)\end{pmatrix}=G_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{B}_{x}(s)\\
\tilde{B}_{y}(s)\end{pmatrix},

(32)

where

G_{0}(s)=\begin{pmatrix}s+\alpha\tilde{\omega}&\tilde{\omega}n_{z}\\
-\tilde{\omega}n_{z}&s+\alpha\tilde{\omega}\end{pmatrix},\ G_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma\alpha&\gamma n_{z}\\
-\gamma n_{z}&\gamma\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

(33)

We consider a general in-plane magnetic field
\bm{B}(t)=B_{0}e^{i\omega t}(a,b,0)
where
a,b
are two complex numbers and describe the polarization of magnetic field components of a microwave. The transformed field reads

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{x}(s)=\frac{B_{0}}{2}\left(\frac{a}{s-i\omega}+\frac{a^{*}}{s+i\omega}\right),

(34)

\displaystyle\tilde{B}_{y}(s)=\frac{B_{0}}{2}\left(\frac{b}{s-i\omega}+\frac{b^{*}}{s+i\omega}\right).

We then perform the inverse Laplace transformation on Eq. (
32
) and obtain

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}n_{x}\\
n_{y}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{B_{0}}{2}\left[ae^{i\omega t}G_{0}^{-1}(i\omega)+a^{*}e^{-i\omega t}G_{0}^{-1}(-i\omega)\right]G_{1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}

(35)

\displaystyle+\frac{B_{0}}{2}\left[be^{i\omega t}G_{0}^{-1}(i\omega)+b^{*}e^{-i\omega t}G_{0}^{-1}(-i\omega)\right]G_{1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

where

The solutions to the above equation are

\displaystyle n_{x}(t)=\frac{B_{0}\gamma}{2D(\omega)}\left[A_{1}(\omega)\cos(\omega t)+A_{2}(\omega)\sin(\omega t)\right]

\displaystyle A_{1}(\omega)

\displaystyle=((\alpha^{2}+1)\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2})\left[(a+a^{*})(\alpha^{2}+1)\tilde{\omega}+i\omega((a-a^{*})\alpha+(b-b^{*})n_{z})\right]

and

\displaystyle n_{y}(t)=\frac{B_{0}\gamma}{2D(\omega)}\left[A_{3}(\omega)\cos(\omega t)+A_{4}(\omega)\sin(\omega t)\right]

\displaystyle=((\alpha^{2}+1)\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2})\left[(b+b^{*})(\alpha^{2}+1)\tilde{\omega}+i\omega((b-b^{*})\alpha+(a^{*}-a)n_{z})\right]

\displaystyle=((\alpha^{2}+1)\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2})\left[i(b-b^{*})(\alpha^{2}+1)\tilde{\omega}-\omega((b+b^{*})\alpha-(a+a^{*})n_{z})\right]

where

We find the resonance frequency satisfying

\displaystyle n_{x}(t)=\frac{B_{0}\gamma_{0}}{2\alpha\omega_{0}}\left[(a_{\text{Im}}+b_{\text{Re}}n_{z})\cos(\omega_{0}t)+(a_{\text{Re}}-b_{\text{Im}}n_{z})\sin(\omega_{0}t)\right],

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{x}=\frac{g_{M}}{ev_{f}}\frac{dM_{y}}{dt}=\frac{g_{M}M_{s}}{ev_{f}}\dot{n}_{y}=\tilde{E}_{0}(b-ian_{z})e^{i\omega_{0}t},

(42)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{y}=-\frac{g_{M}}{ev_{f}}\frac{dM_{x}}{dt}=-\frac{g_{M}M_{s}}{ev_{f}}\dot{n}_{x}=-\tilde{E}_{0}(a+ibn_{z})e^{i\omega_{0}t},

where

\bm{B}(t)=B_{0}e^{i\omega_{0}t}(a,b,0)

(43)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{j}}

\displaystyle=2i\tau\beta_{0}C_{L}E_{0}\tilde{E}_{0}\left[(-a^{*}+ib^{*}n_{z})c-(b^{*}+ia^{*}n_{z})d-(-a-ibn_{z})c^{*}+(b-ian_{z})d^{*}\right]\hat{e}_{z}

Because the MR-induced nonlinear current response is generated by the combination of an electromagnetic field and a pseudo-electric field, the incident light can be linearly polarized, instead of circularly polarized for the regular CPGE. For example, we can choose an incident light with
\bm{E}(t)=E_{0}e^{i\omega_{0}t}(1,0,0)
and a magnetic field
\bm{B}(t)=B_{0}e^{i\omega_{0}t}(0,1,0)

\tilde{\bm{E}}(t)=\tilde{E}_{0}e^{i\omega_{0}t}(1,-i,0)

), the MR-induced nonlinear current is
\tilde{\bm{j}}=-4\tau\beta_{0}C_{L}E_{0}\tilde{E}_{0}\hat{e}_{z}
assuming
n_{z}=1
. It should be noted that although the topological invariant
C_{L}
appears in this current response, the pseudo-electric field
\tilde{E}_{0}
depends on material parameters, not just the fundamental constants, and thus we regard this nonlinear current response to be geometric rather than topological.

V
Magnetic-resonance-induced nonlinear current response in a microscopic model

The above analytical derivation reveals the nonlinear current response induced by MR, in analog to the regular CPGE. In this section, we will further study the nonlinear current response directly from numerical calculations of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
). We emphasize that the purpose of this section is to verify the analytical solutions of the nonlinear current response in the low frequency regime for a more realistic Weyl semimetal model in certain limit. However, the parameters chosen for the calculations of this model are not realistic, so our calculations
cannot
be directly applied to the realistic material systems. A comment of the additional contribution to the nonlinear current response from the remote bands will be discussed at the end of this section.

As shown in Appendix
C
, the MR-induced nonlinear current in the four-band model Eq. (
1
) can be derived as

\tilde{j}_{a}=\tau\tilde{\eta}_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)E_{b}(\omega)\tilde{E}_{c}^{*}(\omega)+\tau\tilde{\eta}_{acb}(0;-\omega,\omega)\tilde{E}_{b}(\omega)E_{c}^{*}(\omega),

(45)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)=i\beta_{0}\sum_{nm}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{2\pi}(\partial_{a}\varepsilon_{nm})f_{nm}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}\Gamma^{c}_{mn}}{\varepsilon_{nm}}\delta(\varepsilon_{mn}-\hbar\omega),

(46)

where
\beta_{0}=\pi e^{3}/h^{2}
,
\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}_{nm}
is the non-Abelian Berry connection of the unperturbed Hamiltonian
H_{0}
,
\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{\sigma}\tau_{z}
, and

is the pseudo-electric field induced by the magnetic fluctuation
\bm{\nu}
,
which comes from the ferromagnetic resonance by solving the LLG equation in Sec.
IV

At low energy, the four-band Hamiltonian can be projected to the effective Hamiltonian describing two Weyl points, and the magnetic fluctuation
\bm{\nu}
appears in the form of the chiral gauge field
\bm{a}
(see Sec.
II
). In this approximation, we can rewrite
\tilde{\bm{E}}(\omega)
as
i\omega\bm{a}
following the definition of Eq. (
20
) and Eq. (
46

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)=i\beta_{0}\frac{\hbar\nu_{c}}{ea_{c}}\sum_{nm}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{2\pi}(\partial_{a}\varepsilon_{nm})f_{nm}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}\Gamma^{c}_{mn}}{\varepsilon_{nm}}\delta(\varepsilon_{mn}-\hbar\omega),

(47)

in terms of
\bm{\nu}
is defined in Sec.
II

2i\beta_{0}
.

As the magnetization in our model is in the
z
direction, only an in-plane pseudo-electric field can be induced by the ferromagnetic resonance
[
38
]
. We assume the incident light propagates in the
z
direction and has in-plane electromagnetic fields, so the indices
b,c
can only be
x
or
y

b\neq c
as the electric fields are along perpendicular directions in CPGE. Furthermore, the four-band model has mirror

, and the in-plane rotational symmetry which imposes
\tilde{\eta}_{zxy}=-\tilde{\eta}_{zyx}
. Therefore, in the following calculations, we focus on the component
\tilde{\eta}_{zxy}
given by

\tilde{\eta}_{zxy}=i\beta_{0}\frac{L_{2}m}{\sqrt{m^{2}-M_{0}^{2}}}\sum_{nm}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{2\pi}(\partial_{z}\varepsilon_{nm})f_{nm}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{x}_{nm}\Gamma^{y}_{mn}}{\varepsilon_{nm}}\delta(\varepsilon_{mn}-\hbar\omega),

(48)

where we have used
a_{y}=\frac{\hbar L_{1}K_{0}}{eL_{2}m}\nu_{y}

and

from Sec.
II
to derive this result.

k_{z}
at

from low to high energies. Two Weyl nodes with opposite charges at opposite

starts to decrease as the effective model of two Weyl fermions is no longer applicable, and thus

Another reason for the rapid decrease in

In our calculation, we only considered two-band transitions (

As long as the other bands are far away enough from the two Weyl bands, we expect the contribution from the three-band transition provides a small correction.

VI
Conclusion and discussion

In conclusion, we demonstrate that a nonlinear current response with an electric field and a MR-induced pseudo-electric field can exist in magnetic Weyl semimetals with two Weyl nodes with opposite charges, in contrast to the regular CPGE. The pseudo-electric field can be induced by the magnetic fluctuation from ferromagnetic resonance, which acts like a chiral gauge field that couples oppositely to the lefthanded and righthanded Weyl nodes. Therefore, the realization of MR-induced nonlinear current does not require a chiral Weyl semimetal in which the two Weyl nodes are located at different energies. By combining this MR-induced nonlinear current response with the normal CPGE measurement, we anticipate a potential approach to extract the contribution from the quantized nonlinear response in realistic materials, which has not been achieved in the current experiments of normal CPGE measurements and will be left for the future work.

with
\tilde{\omega}_{2,nm}=\omega_{nm}-i/\tau_{2}
, we get

\frac{\partial\tilde{p}^{(2)}_{nm}}{\partial t}=\frac{e}{\hbar}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i(\omega_{\gamma}-\tilde{\omega}_{2,nm})t}\left[D^{c},\rho^{(1)}(t)\right]_{nm}.

(66)

We then use the expression for
\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)
in Eq. (
63
) and integrate out
t
to obtain

\rho^{(2)}_{nm}(t)=-\frac{e^{2}\Lambda^{(2)}_{nm}}{\hbar^{2}(\omega_{nm}-\omega_{\Sigma}-i/\tau_{2})}E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

(67)

where
\omega_{\Sigma}=\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}
and

\Lambda^{(2)}_{nm}=\left[D^{c},\frac{\left[D^{b},\rho^{(0)}\right]}{\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\omega_{\beta}}\right]_{nm}.

(68)

We then separate the second-order density operator
\rho^{(2)}_{nm}(t)
in Eq. (
67
) into the diagonal (intraband)
\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(t)
and off-diagonal (interband)
(1-\delta_{nm})\rho^{(2)}_{nm}(t)
parts
[
47
]
. The intraband contribution reads

\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(t)=\frac{e^{2}\Lambda^{(2)}_{nn}}{\hbar^{2}(\omega_{\Sigma}+i/\tau_{2})}E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

(69)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda^{(2)}_{nn}

\displaystyle=\left[D^{c},\frac{\left[D^{b},\rho^{(0)}\right]}{\tilde{\omega}_{1}-\omega_{\beta}}\right]_{nn}

(70)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{c}\frac{\left[D^{b},\rho^{(0)}\right]_{nn}}{\omega_{\beta}+i/\tau_{1}}-i\sum_{m}\left({\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}\frac{\left[D^{b},\rho^{(0)}\right]_{mn}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}-\frac{\left[D^{b},\rho^{(0)}\right]_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta}}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{b}\partial_{c}f_{n}}{\omega_{\beta}+i/\tau_{1}}-\sum_{m}\left(\frac{f_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}-\frac{f_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta}}\right).

The corresponding current is

\displaystyle j^{a}_{\text{intra}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{n}\int_{k}v^{a}_{nn}\Lambda^{(2)}_{nn}E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}v^{a}_{nn}\left(\frac{f_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}-\frac{f_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta}}\right)E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\left(\frac{\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}\right)E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

where
\Delta^{a}_{nm}=v^{a}_{nn}-v^{a}_{mm}
and we take
\tau_{2}\to\infty
and
\partial_{\bm{k}}f_{n}=0
. We then symmetrize the indices
b\beta\leftrightarrow c\gamma
,
\bm{k}\leftrightarrow-\bm{k}
, and
n\leftrightarrow m
to get

\displaystyle j^{a}_{\text{intra}}(t)=\frac{e^{3}}{2\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}\left(\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}+\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\gamma}}\right)E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(72)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{4\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\gamma}}\right)E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}+\omega_{\beta}}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}+\omega_{\gamma}}\right)E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}.

By assuming
1/\tau_{1}
is very small, i.e.
\tau_{1}\to\infty
, we can apply the following identity

\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta}-i/\tau_{1}}=\frac{P}{\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta}}+i\pi\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta}),

(73)

where
P
denotes a Cauchy principle value. The imaginary part of the current is called the injection current or the circular photocurrent
[
47
]
, which reads

\displaystyle j^{a}_{\text{injection}}(t)=[j^{a}_{\text{intra}}(t)]_{\text{Im}}=\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})

(74)

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\gamma})+\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\gamma})\right]E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}.

We then take the time derivative of the current

\displaystyle\frac{d\,j^{a}_{\text{injection}}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=-i\omega_{\Sigma}\times[j^{a}_{\text{intra}}(t)]_{\text{Im}}

(75)

\displaystyle=\eta_{abc}(\omega_{\Sigma};\omega_{\beta},\omega_{\gamma})E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

where

\displaystyle\eta_{abc}(\omega_{\Sigma};\omega_{\beta},\omega_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}\Omega^{bc}_{mn}

(76)

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\gamma})+\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\gamma})\right].

If we consider a monochromatic field
\bm{E}(t)=\bm{E}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}+\bm{E}(-\omega)e^{i\omega t}
, the injection current with
\omega_{\Sigma}=0
is

\frac{d\,j^{a}_{\text{injection}}(t)}{dt}=2\eta_{abc}(0;-\omega,\omega)E^{b}(\omega)E^{c}(-\omega),

(77)

with

\eta_{abc}(0;-\omega,\omega)=-\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{2\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}\Omega^{bc}_{mn}\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega).

(78)

The same result can be also derived from the velocity gauge, as shown in Appendix
B
. We can rewrite Eq. (
77
) as
[
1
,
34
]

j^{a}_{\text{injection}}=\tau\beta_{ab}(\omega)[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)]^{b},

(79)

with

\displaystyle\beta_{ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}(\partial_{a}\varepsilon_{nm})f_{nm}R_{nm}^{b}\delta(\varepsilon_{mn}-\hbar\omega),

(80)

where
\tau
is the lifetime and
\bm{R}_{nm}=(\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}_{nm}\times\bm{{\mathcal{A}}}_{mn})
. We can see that this current can be generated by a circularly polarized light, but not a linearly polarized light, as
\bm{E}\times\bm{E}^{*}
requires the electric fields to be along perpendicular directions, thus the name “circular photogalvanic effect”. Furthermore, the current switches direction when the polarization changes from lefthanded to righthanded.
The CPGE trace
\beta(\omega)
reads

\displaystyle\beta(\omega)=\text{Tr}[\beta_{ab}(\omega)]

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}(\partial_{a}\varepsilon_{nm})f_{nm}R_{nm}^{a}\delta(\varepsilon_{mn}-\hbar\omega)

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{3}}{2h^{2}}\sum_{nm}\oint d\bm{S}_{nm}\cdot\bm{R}_{nm},

where
\bm{S}_{nm}
is a closed surface in the momentum space.
For a two-fold Weyl node with band index
n=1,2
and Fermi energy across at the Weyl node, we use the relation
\bm{\Omega}_{nm}=i\sum_{m\neq n}\bm{R}_{nm}

[
34
]
, and Eq. (
81
) becomes

\displaystyle\beta(\omega)

\displaystyle=i\frac{e^{3}}{2h^{2}}\oint d\bm{S}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{1}

(82)

\displaystyle=i\beta_{0}C,

where
C=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\bm{S}\cdot\bm{\Omega}
is the Chern number and
\beta_{0}=\pi e^{3}/h^{2}
. Therefore, the CPGE trace
\beta(\omega)
is quantized for a Weyl node as proportional to its associated topological charge, and the CPGE current can be written as

\bm{j}=i\tau\beta_{0}C\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right].

(83)

For a two-band Weyl semimetal with mirror symmetry, there are two Weyl nodes with opposite topological charges
C_{L}=1
and
C_{R}=-1
sitting at the same energy (Fig.
1
a). When the Fermi energy is at the Weyl node, the CPGE trace for the two Weyl nodes are
i\beta_{0}C_{L}
and
i\beta_{0}C_{R}
. The CPGE currents from the left and right Weyl nodes are
\bm{j}_{L}=i\tau\beta_{0}C\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right]
and
\bm{j}_{R}=-i\tau\beta_{0}C\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right]
, so the total CPGE current vanishes. If we consider a chiral Weyl semimetal in which the inversion and all mirror symmetries are broken (Fig.
1
b), the left and right Weyl nodes are located at different energies
\varepsilon_{L}
and
\varepsilon_{R}
(measured from the Fermi energy
E_{f}
), respectively. Then in the frequency window
2\left|\varepsilon_{R}\right|<\hbar\omega<2\left|\varepsilon_{L}\right|
, the transition near the left Weyl node is forbidden due to Pauli blocking, and thus, the only contribution to the CPGE current comes from the right Weyl node
\bm{j}_{R}=-i\tau\beta_{0}C\left[\bm{E}(\omega)\times\bm{E}^{*}(\omega)\right]

[
1
]
. Therefore, the quantized CPGE can be realized under such conditions.
The CPGE has recently been observed in chiral Weyl semimetals RhSi
[
29
,
31
]
and CoSi
[
30
]
.

Appendix B
Derivation of CPGE in velocity gauge

In this section, we derive the CPGE in the velocity gauge
[
46
,
49
,
50
]
. The dipole interaction is written as

H_{0}\left(\bm{k}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{A}(t)\right)\approx H_{0}(\bm{k})+\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}=H_{0}(\bm{k})+H_{1},

(84)

where
\bm{J}=\frac{e}{\hbar}\frac{\partial H_{0}}{\partial\bm{k}}
is the current operator. Using the expressions in Appendix
A
, we obtain the second-quantized Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{x}\tilde{\psi}^{{\dagger}}(\bm{x})H_{0}\tilde{\psi}(\bm{x})

(85)

\displaystyle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}d\bm{k}^{\prime}d\bm{x}\psi^{*}_{n\bm{k}}(\bm{x})\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\varepsilon_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\psi_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{x})\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\int d\bm{k}\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}},

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{x}\tilde{\psi}^{{\dagger}}(\bm{x})\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}\tilde{\psi}(\bm{x})

(86)

\displaystyle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}d\bm{k}^{\prime}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\int d\bm{x}d\bm{q}e^{i(-\bm{k}+\bm{q}+\bm{k}^{\prime})\bm{x}}u^{*}_{n\bm{k}}[\bm{A}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\bm{J}]u_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}d\bm{k}^{\prime}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\int d\bm{q}\delta(-\bm{k}+\bm{q}+\bm{k}^{\prime})\left\langle u_{n\bm{k}}\right|\bm{A}(\bm{q},t)\cdot\bm{J}\left|u_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}d\bm{k}^{\prime}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\left\langle u_{n\bm{k}}\right|\bm{A}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime},t)\cdot\bm{J}\left|u_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\right\rangle.

We assume the photon momentum is zero
\bm{q}\approx 0
, i.e.
\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}\approx 0
, as the momentum of light
\bm{q}
is much smaller than that of electrons
\bm{k}
, so the Hamiltonian is as follows

\hat{H}=\sum_{n}\int d\bm{k}\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}},

(87)

where
\bm{J}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}=\left\langle u_{n\bm{k}}\right|\bm{J}\left|u_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}\right\rangle
. The Heisenberg equations of motion for the creation and annihilation operators are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}\right]

(88)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{n^{\prime}}\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H},\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{n^{\prime}}\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{n^{\prime}n\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}\right).

The density matrix is
\rho_{nm,\bm{k}}=\langle\hat{a}_{m\bm{k}}^{{\dagger}}\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}\rangle
, so the optical Bloch equation for the density matrix writes

\frac{\partial\rho_{nm}(t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{nm}\rho_{nm}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{n^{\prime}}\left[\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{n^{\prime}n}\rho_{n^{\prime}m}(t)-\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{mn^{\prime}}\rho_{nn^{\prime}}(t)\right]-\frac{\rho_{nm}(t)}{\tau}.

(89)

Next we expand the density operator in powers of
\bm{E}
as in Eq. (
55
) and the zeroth-order term is
\rho^{(0)}_{nm}=f_{n}\delta_{nm}
.
At the first order,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{n^{\prime}}\left[\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{n^{\prime}n}\rho^{(0)}_{n^{\prime}m}(t)-\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{mn^{\prime}}\rho^{(0)}_{nn^{\prime}}(t)\right]-\frac{\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)}{\tau_{1}}

(90)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}f_{mn}A^{b}(t)J^{b}_{mn}-\frac{\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)}{\tau_{1}}.

Let
\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)=\tilde{p}^{(1)}_{nm}e^{-i\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}t}
with
\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}=\varepsilon_{nm}/\hbar-i/\tau_{1}
, and
A^{b}(t)=\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}}{2\pi}A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})e^{-i\omega_{\beta}t}
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{p}^{(1)}_{nm}}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}}{2\pi}f_{mn}A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})J^{b}_{mn}e^{i(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})t},

(91)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}^{(1)}_{nm}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}}{2\pi}f_{mn}A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})J^{b}_{mn}\frac{e^{i(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})t}}{i(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})},

so the first-order density operator is

\displaystyle\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int dt\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)e^{i\omega t}

(92)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}}{2\pi}f_{mn}A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})J^{b}_{mn}\int dt\frac{e^{i(\omega-\omega_{\beta})t}}{i(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}}{2\pi}f_{mn}A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})J^{b}_{mn}\frac{\delta(\omega-\omega_{\beta})}{i(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{nm}A^{b}(\omega)J^{b}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega)}.

At the second order, the diagonal part (intraband) of the density operator reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{m}\left[\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{mn}\rho^{(1)}_{mn}(t)-\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)\right]-\frac{\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(t)}{\tau_{2}}.

(93)

Let
\rho^{(2)}_{nn}=e^{-t/\tau_{2}}\tilde{p}^{(2)}_{nn}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{p}^{(2)}_{nn}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{m}A^{c}(t)[J^{c}_{mn}\rho^{(1)}_{mn}(t)-J^{c}_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(t)]e^{t/\tau_{2}}

(94)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{m}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}d\omega_{\gamma}}{(2\pi)^{2}}A^{c}(\omega_{\gamma})[J^{c}_{mn}\rho^{(1)}_{mn}(\omega_{\beta})-J^{c}_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega_{\beta})]e^{-i(\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}+i/\tau_{2})t},

\displaystyle\tilde{p}^{(2)}_{nn}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}d\omega_{\gamma}}{(2\pi)^{2}}A^{c}(\omega_{\gamma})[J^{c}_{mn}\rho^{(1)}_{mn}(\omega_{\beta})-J^{c}_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega_{\beta})]\frac{e^{-i(\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}+i/\tau_{2})t}}{\hbar(\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}+i/\tau_{2})}.

Thus, the second-order intraband density operator writes

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int dt\tilde{p}^{(2)}_{nn}e^{i(\omega+i/\tau_{2})t}

(95)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}d\omega_{\gamma}}{(2\pi)^{2}}A^{c}(\omega_{\gamma})[J^{c}_{mn}\rho^{(1)}_{mn}(\omega_{\beta})-J^{c}_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega_{\beta})]\frac{\delta(\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}-\omega)}{\hbar(\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}+i/\tau_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar(\omega+i/\tau_{2})}\sum_{m}\int\frac{d\omega_{\beta}d\omega_{\gamma}}{(2\pi)^{2}}A^{c}(\omega_{\gamma})[J^{c}_{mn}\rho^{(1)}_{mn}(\omega_{\beta})-J^{c}_{nm}\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega_{\beta})]\delta(\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}-\omega).

We then use the expression for
\rho^{(1)}_{nm}
and
A^{b}_{\beta}=A^{b}(\omega_{\beta})
,
A^{c}_{\gamma}=A^{c}(\omega_{\gamma})
, and
\omega_{\Sigma}=\omega_{\beta}+\omega_{\gamma}
to get

\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(\omega_{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{\hbar(\omega_{\Sigma}+i/\tau_{2})}\sum_{m}A^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}\left[\frac{f_{mn}J^{b}_{mn}J^{c}_{nm}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta})}-\frac{f_{nm}J^{b}_{nm}J^{c}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})}\right],

(96)

with the summation over indices
\beta
and
\gamma
implied. The corresponding current is as follows

\displaystyle j^{a}_{\text{intra}}(t)

\displaystyle=e\sum_{n}\int_{k}v^{a}_{nn}\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(\omega_{\Sigma})e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(97)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}v^{a}_{nn}\left[\frac{f_{mn}J^{b}_{mn}J^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}-\frac{f_{nm}J^{b}_{nm}J^{c}_{mn}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta}}\right]\frac{A^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}}{\omega_{\Sigma}+i/\tau_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\frac{\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{mn}J^{b}_{mn}J^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}A^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

where
\Delta^{a}_{nm}=v^{a}_{nn}-v^{a}_{mm}
is the velocity shift. We replace
A^{b}(\omega)
with
E^{b}(\omega)/i\omega

[
49
]
and
J^{b}_{nm}=\frac{ie}{\hbar}\varepsilon_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}
and obtain

j^{a}_{\text{intra}}(t)=\frac{e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\frac{\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{nm}E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\beta}\omega_{\gamma}}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}.

(98)

We then take the imaginary part of the current using the identity Eq. (
73
) and symmetrize all the indices to get

\displaystyle j^{a}_{\text{Im}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{nm}E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\beta}\omega_{\gamma}}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(99)

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\gamma})+\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\gamma})\right].

The time derivative of the above current reads

\displaystyle\frac{dj^{a}_{\text{Im}}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{3}}{8\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})\frac{\varepsilon^{2}_{nm}E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\beta}\omega_{\gamma}}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(100)

\displaystyle\times\left[\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\beta})-\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega_{\gamma})+\delta(\omega_{mn}+\omega_{\gamma})\right]

\displaystyle=\eta_{abc}(\omega_{\Sigma};\omega_{\beta},\omega_{\gamma})E^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}.

For a monochromatic field with
\omega_{\beta}=-\omega_{\gamma}=\omega
and
\omega_{\Sigma}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{3}}{2\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}({\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{nm}-{\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{mn}{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm})\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega)

(101)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{2\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}\Omega^{bc}_{mn}\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega),

which recovers Eq. (
77
), which is the result in the length gauge.

Appendix C
Derivation of MR-induced nonlinear current for the four-band model

Here we drive the MR-induced nonlinear current for the four-band model Eq. (
1
). Following the same procedure in Appendix
B
, we find the second-quantized Hamiltonian as follows

\hat{H}=\sum_{n}\int d\bm{k}\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n\bm{k}}+\sum_{nn^{\prime}}\int d\bm{k}\hat{a}^{{\dagger}}_{n\bm{k}}\hat{a}_{n^{\prime}\bm{k}}\left[\bm{A}(t)\cdot\bm{J}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}+\bm{\nu}(t)\cdot\bm{\Gamma}_{nn^{\prime}\bm{k}}\right],

(102)

where
\varepsilon_{n\bm{k}}
is the eigenvalue of
H_{0}
,
\bm{J}
is the current operator, and
\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{\sigma}\tau_{z}
. We then obtain the first-order and second-order intraband density operator

\rho^{(1)}_{nm}(\omega)=f_{nm}\frac{A^{b}(\omega)J^{b}_{mn}+\nu^{b}(\omega)\Gamma^{b}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega)},

(103)

\displaystyle\rho^{(2)}_{nn}(\omega_{\Sigma})=\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{m}A^{b}_{\beta}\nu^{c}_{\gamma}\left[\frac{f_{mn}J^{b}_{mn}\Gamma^{c}_{nm}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta})}-\frac{f_{nm}J^{b}_{nm}\Gamma^{c}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})}\right]

(104)

\displaystyle+\nu^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}\left[\frac{f_{mn}\Gamma^{b}_{mn}J^{c}_{nm}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta})}-\frac{f_{nm}\Gamma^{b}_{nm}J^{c}_{mn}}{\hbar(\tilde{\omega}_{1,nm}-\omega_{\beta})}\right].

The current reads

\displaystyle\tilde{j}^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\hbar^{2}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\frac{\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{mn}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}\left[J^{b}_{mn}\Gamma^{c}_{nm}A^{b}_{\beta}\nu^{c}_{\gamma}+\Gamma^{b}_{mn}J^{c}_{nm}\nu^{b}_{\beta}A^{c}_{\gamma}\right]e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t}

(105)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar^{3}\omega_{\Sigma}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\frac{\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}}{\tilde{\omega}_{1,mn}-\omega_{\beta}}\left[\frac{J^{b}_{mn}\Gamma^{c}_{nm}}{\omega_{\beta}\omega_{\gamma}}E^{b}_{\beta}\tilde{E}^{c}_{\gamma}+\frac{\Gamma^{b}_{mn}J^{c}_{nm}}{\omega_{\beta}\omega_{\gamma}}\tilde{E}^{b}_{\beta}E^{c}_{\gamma}\right]e^{-i\omega_{\Sigma}t},

where
\tilde{E}^{b}_{\beta}=i\hbar\omega_{\beta}\nu^{b}_{\beta}/e
is the pseudo-electric field. Next we take the imaginary part and the time derivative of the current, and for
\omega_{\beta}=-\omega_{\gamma}=\omega
, we have

\frac{d\tilde{j}^{a}(t)}{dt}=\tilde{\eta}_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)E_{b}(\omega)\tilde{E}_{c}(-\omega)+\tilde{\eta}_{acb}(0;-\omega,\omega)\tilde{E}_{b}(\omega)E_{c}(-\omega),

(106)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{\eta}_{abc}(0;\omega,-\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi e^{2}}{\hbar^{3}}\sum_{nm}\int_{k}\Delta^{a}_{nm}f_{nm}\frac{J^{b}_{mn}\Gamma^{c}_{nm}}{\omega^{2}}\delta(\omega_{mn}-\omega)

(107)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\pi e^{3}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{nm}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}(\partial_{a}\varepsilon_{nm})f_{nm}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{b}_{nm}\Gamma^{c}_{mn}}{\varepsilon_{nm}}\delta(\varepsilon_{mn}-\hbar\omega),

where
\tilde{\bm{E}}(\omega)
is defined as
i\hbar\omega\bm{\nu}/e
.
