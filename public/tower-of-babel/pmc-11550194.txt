Real Track Experiment Dataset for Motorcycle Rider Behavior and Trajectory Reconstruction
Authors: Bastien Vincke, Pauline Michel, Abdelhafid El Ouardi, Bruno Larnaudie, Flavien Delgehier, Rabah Sadoun, Samir Bouaziz, Stéphane Espié, Sergio Rodriguez, Abderrahmane Boubezoul

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dynamics of a two-wheeled vehicle represent a higher level of complexity, with a high variety of rider practices, peculiarly during the bend-taking maneuver. This complexity is one reason why research on motorcyclists’ practiced trajectories is limited compared to cars. Another reason is the low representation of motorcycles in the land motor vehicle fleet.The dataset was collected within the framework of the French ANR research project VIROLO++. The objective was twofold. One concerns the analysis of the interactions between a rider and the motorcycle during a bend-taking maneuver. The second aims at studying the precise trajectory of a motorcycle.Multi-sensor instrumentation of a motorcycle (Honda CBF1000) has been achieved for precise trajectory reconstruction as well as the analysis of the interactions between the rider and the motorcycle during a ride. Real experiments were conducted on the “La Ferté Gaucher'' racetrack. The collected data include the precise location and attitude of the motorcycle and rider. Several sensors providing redundant data have been embedded to validate measurements. Two RTK GPS receivers were installed at the rear of the motorcycle to provide ground truth. A preliminary analysis was performed to validate the consistency of the dataset and the relevance of the sensors according to the aimed study.Keywords: Embedded multisensor system, Sensors data synchronization, Motorcycle instrumentation, Rider behavior

Background

The dynamics of a two-wheeled vehicle represent a higher level of complexity, with a high variety of rider practices, peculiarly during the bend-taking maneuver. This complexity is one reason why research on motorcyclists’ practiced trajectories is limited compared to cars. Another reason is because of the low representation of motorcycles in the land motor vehicle fleet. The dataset was collected within the framework of the French ANR research project VIROLO++. The objective was twofold. One concerns the analysis of the interactions between a rider and the motorcycle during a bend-taking maneuver. The second aims at studying the precise trajectory of a motorcycle.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

To record data, we used an electronic architecture coupling a CAN bus dedicated to low data flow measurements and an embedded computer for high data flow measurements and proprietary systems. This architecture is represented in Fig. 3.

Architecture of the instrumented multi-sensor system.

Several sensors were grouped into measurement nodes. A measurement node (represented by a gray box in Fig. 3) consists of a microcontroller, a CAN interface and one or more sensors. It collects measurements and sends them to the data-logger via the CAN bus.

We recorded the data available on the CAN bus with a VECTOR GL1000 Logger on a SD Card and a custom CAN bus recorder.

Synchronization

All the collected data were locally timestamped. Global timestamps were assigned through post-processing, since some sensors have their own recording system, preventing real-time synchronization.

Sensors data sent on the CAN bus are timestamped at the reception time of the recorder.

The pressure matrix is triggered when the CAN recorder is launched.

A timestamp is generated at the launch of the CAN recorder on the log file of the IMU HGw.

The RTK GPS is synchronized with the time of the GPS signal.

The 360° camera is synchronized with the time of the GPS data because it embeds a GNSS receiver.

Datalogger

Two data-loggers were used: a low-cost data-logger and an industrial data-logger. The main goal was to validate the functioning of our designed recorder.

After analyzing data from the two data-loggers, we did not notice any differences. We therefore chose to provide data from the industrial logger.

Ground truth

Several sensors have been embedded to allow the estimation of parameters in a highly reliable way.

Two RTK GPS have been positioned on the top of the top case at the rear of the motorcycle. They provide the location of the motorcycle in 3 dimensions with centimeter accuracy.

Two laser distance sensors were positioned on each side of the motorcycle pointing downwards to reliably measure the roll angle with respect to the road.

Temporal coherence of the data was verified by displaying the identical data in the same graph, as presented in Fig 4. Overall, the trajectory derived from the four GPS receivers is identical. However, it is noteworthy that the trajectories of the two RTK GPS are the same (they overlap and maintain the spacing between the two receivers). The trajectories of the two low-cost GPS receivers are in line with expectations, with an error of less than 5 meters.

Fig. 5 represents the speed derived from two different types of sensors: encoders fixed on the wheels, as well as the speed measured by the three GPS devices. The speeds are very similar throughout the entire experiment. It will be necessary to delve into the need for using encoders, given the accuracy of the speed obtained from GPS sensors.

Fig. 6 represents the attitude of the motorcycle and its rider. It is important to pay attention to the graphs, as the ordinate scales are not the same.

For the heading, we have seven redundant measurements that yield very similar results. Regarding the roll, the data is consistent across different sensors. It is noticeable that the rider's head tends to smooth out the inclinations. For the pitch, the data from the IMU HG central unit is relatively surprising and deserves further investigation.

Fig. 7 represents linear accelerations from five different sensors. It was necessary to filter the data as it was highly noisy (medfilter1, n=50). Indeed, the 360° camera was mounted on an articulated arm on the motorcycle's handlebars, causing significant variations. After filtering, the data appears consistent across the sensors.

Fig. 8, Fig. 9 represent rotational speeds as well as data from magnetometers. Like the accelerometers, it was necessary to filter the data. After filtering, all the data seems entirely consistent.

Fig. 10 represents the pressures measured at both feet and on the handlebars. Clear differences between the right and left sides during turns are immediately noticeable. Similarly, the use of the two feet is very different. Note that when riding a motorcycle, the right foot operates the rear brake and the left foot the gear selector.

Fig. 11 represents the sum of pressures measured on the right and left sides of the seat and the tank. At the seat level, a clear pressure difference between the two sides is noticeable. This difference varies along the trajectory.

Limitations

We have identified two main limitations. Firstly, some data require further post-processing before they can be fully exploited. The second limitation concerns data collection conditions. They were collected in a controlled environment, on a runway, with no obstacles and no road users. These conditions were chosen to guarantee the proper functioning of our reference sensors but would need to be relaxed to study the various sensors in greater depth.

Ethics Statement

The authors have read and followed the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief. This work involved a human subject. The protocol was not submitted for ethical committee approval. The relevant informed consent was obtained from the participant (the motorcycle rider) prior to the collection of this data.
