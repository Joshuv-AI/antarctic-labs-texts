Letter to the Editor: A commentary on “Transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation accelerates gastrointestinal function recovery after abdominal surgery: a systemic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials”
Authors: Wang Ziyuan, Li Chen
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the systematic review and meta-analysis by Wu et al, which evaluated transcutaneous electrical acupoint stimulation (TEAS) as an adjunct to enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways in patients undergoing abdominal surgery[1]. Drawing on 16 randomized controlled trials and 2300 participants, the authors report that TEAS accelerates time to first flatus, defecation, and oral intake, and reduces postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), pain intensity, length of stay, and overall hospitalization costs[1]. Registered in PROSPERO and conducted in accordance with PRISMA and AMSTAR guidance, this work provides a timely and methodologically robust synthesis of the perioperative TEAS literature in abdominal surgery.

In interpreting these findings, it is useful to view TEAS within the broader context of ERAS programs, which combine multiple pre-, intra-, and postoperative elements to enhance recovery[2,3]. Core ERAS items such as preoperative carbohydrate loading, opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia, early enteral feeding, and early mobilization have independent effects on gastrointestinal motility and length of stay[2,3]. Yet, as summarized in the included trials, the extent to which ERAS components were implemented and perioperative analgesic strategies were standardized varies considerably across studies[1]. Consequently, some of the observed benefits of TEAS may reflect its interaction with more comprehensive ERAS pathways, rather than a uniform standalone effect. Future trials and updated syntheses could be strengthened by more granular reporting of ERAS components and opioid exposure, and by prespecified analyses according to ERAS “intensity” or protocol adherence.

Second, the authors provide a helpful overview of TEAS protocols, showing substantial diversity in acupoint combinations (e.g., PC6, ST36, ST37, and SP6), stimulation frequencies (2–100 Hz), and current intensities and timing, ranging from single intraoperative sessions to repeated stimulation for up to seven postoperative days[1]. This heterogeneity likely mirrors real-world practice, but it also makes it challenging to define a pragmatic TEAS regimen that can be embedded into standard ERAS order sets. Previous work has similarly highlighted variation in TEAS parameters and the need for clearer reporting standards[4]. We would welcome future trials that predefine a small number of standardized, easily deliverable TEAS “packages” tailored to specific surgical contexts (such as major colorectal or pancreatic resections), incorporate fidelity checks, and report stimulation parameters using structured templates. Such an approach could facilitate exploration of dose–response relationships and permit more precise recommendations for guideline developers.

Third, most of the included studies were conducted in China, where TEAS is already familiar to both clinicians and patients, although the authors do include one trial from the United States[1]. Rather than viewing this solely as a limitation, we see an opportunity to use their pooled estimates to prioritize indications for international validation. For instance, TEAS appears particularly promising in settings where postoperative ileus and PONV are common and costly; perioperative meta-analyses focused specifically on these outcomes have reported compatible benefits[1,5]. Pragmatic, multicenter trials in diverse health-care systems that integrate TEAS into existing ERAS pathways and incorporate patient-reported outcomes, recovery quality, and resource use would help to clarify its real-world value.

In summary, Wu et al should be congratulated for delivering the first comprehensive synthesis of TEAS in abdominal surgery within an ERAS framework[1]. Their findings support TEAS as a low-risk, non-pharmacological adjunct that accelerates gastrointestinal recovery and improves several clinically relevant outcomes. We believe that future work which explicitly situates TEAS within standardized ERAS protocols and refines reproducible stimulation regimens will further guide surgeons and anesthesiologists in deciding how best to integrate this modality into routine perioperative care.
