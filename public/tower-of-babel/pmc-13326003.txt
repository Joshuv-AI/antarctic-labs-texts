Characterizing MEFV gene variants in Jordanian patients with Familial Mediterranean Fever
Authors: Wissam A. Alwazani, Nisreen A. Fuqaha, Zekrayat Medras, Yaqeen A. Rjoub, Kholoud Altaany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamilial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) is inherited as an autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disorder caused by mutations in the Mediterranean fever (MEFV) gene and predominantly affects populations from the Mediterranean region. Despite its clinical significance, data regarding the genetic profile of FMF in Jordan remain limited. This study aimed to determine the frequency and pattern of commonly screened MEFV gene variants in a cohort of Jordanian patients clinically diagnosed with Familial Mediterranean Fever.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 366 patients aged 12–18 years who fulfilled the Turkish clinical diagnostic criteria for FMF and were treated at Prince Hamza Hospital between October 2022 and December 2023. The Diagnosis was supported by laboratory investigations and inflammatory markers, followed by genetic screening for most common mutations for the MEFV gene. Ethical approval was obtained, and informed consent was provided by legal guardians. Genetic and clinical data were analyzed using SPSS version 23 and the R Studio program.ResultsOut of 366 individuals tested for MEFV gene mutations, 195 (53.3%) were mutation-positive and met the clinical criteria for FMF. The cohort comprised 75% males and 25% females, with a mean age of 13 ± 3 years and a mean age at symptom onset of 11 ± 4 years. The most frequently identified mutation was E148Q (25.12%), predominantly in the heterozygous state, followed by V726A (21.54%) and M694V (22.05%), which were also mainly heterozygous. M694I demonstrated the highest rate of homozygosity (29.41%), while K695R was detected exclusively in the homozygous form. The I692del mutation was not identified in any patient.ConclusionThis study provides baseline data on the frequency of commonly screened MEFV gene variants in Jordanian adolescents with FMF. E148Q, V726A, and M694V were the most frequent mutations, mainly in the heterozygous state, reflecting genetic heterogeneity in the study cohort. Further large-scale studies are warranted to elucidate genotype–phenotype correlations and refine diagnostic and management strategies for FMF in Jordan.

Introduction

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF), an autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disease, most frequently affects Arabs, Turks, Jews, and Armenians [1–4]. Recurrent episodes of fever (38–40 °C) [5], arthritis, and serositis [6] are the hallmarks of this illness. Positional cloning was used to identify the FMF gene, Mediterranean fever (MEFV), in 1997 (French FMF Consortium 1997; International FMF Consortium 1997) [7]. This gene is located on chromosome 16p 13.3 [8] and has a 2,346 bp coding sequence spanning 10 exons [9], Exon 10 exhibits the most frequent mutations, specifically at codons 694 and 680 [2], followed by mutations found in exons 2, 3, 5, and 9 [3].

The MEFV gene expresses a 781 aa protein known as pyrin or marenostrin [8] that has a regulatory effect on inflammation. Therefore, an anomaly in its production would result in an abnormal inflammatory reaction [9], as seen in FMF, which manifests as recurrent episodes of fever, arthritis, pleuritis, and sterile peritonitis [10]. The most serious side effect of the illness is amyloidosis [4, 11], particularly renal amyloidosis that results in end-stage renal disease [5]. Nowadays, daily colchicine is the primary treatment [12] that has been shown to reduce the severity and frequency of episodes as well as the risk of developing amyloidosis [1].

M694V, E148Q, M680I, M694V, and V726A are the most common mutations among Arabs, Turks, Jews, and Iranians [3, 10, 12–15]. The mutations’ penetrance and correlation with the severity of clinical symptoms vary among the aforementioned populations, with M694V being the most severe and associated with an increased risk of amyloidosis [4, 14, 16] and E148Q having the least penetrance and a milder clinical course [2, 9].

It is well known that FMF mutations are substantially varied among different ethnic groups, whereas the Mediterranean has the highest FMF prevalence carrier rate, ranging from one in three to one in five, according to several studies [13, 14]. A heterozygote advantage in the impacted groups is suggested by this high carrier rate. Although impacted individual-based research is limited, the frequency and distribution of MEFV mutations among Arabs require further investigation [15].

The aim of this study is to characterize the spectrum and frequency of the most common mutations of MEFV gene variants in Jordanian adolescents diagnosed with FMF.

Materials and methods

A retrospective, laboratory-based genetic analysis was conducted on 366 patients who were clinically diagnosed with FMF and followed at the rheumatology clinic of Prince Hamza Hospital between October 2022 and December 2023. Patients’ ages ranged from 12 to 18 years at the time of diagnosis, and all had a minimum follow-up duration of six months.

The diagnosis of FMF was established by the treating physicians based on the Turkish diagnostic criteria, requiring the presence of two or more of the following features: ≥3 attacks with 6–72 hours’ duration of fever (axillary temperature of > 38 °C); abdominal pain; chest pain; oligoarthritis; and positive family history.

For genotyping for the MEFV gene, samples were tested for 10 mutations (E148Q, P369S, M680I, I692del, M694V, M694I, K695R, V726A, A744S, and R761H) using the FMF Strip Assay TM (Vienna Lab Diagnostic GmbH, Vienna, Austria).

Baseline laboratory investigation tests—including complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, routine urine analysis, urine culture, and abdominal ultrasound—were performed as part of routine clinical evaluation to support the diagnosis. In addition, inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, white blood cell count, and urine analysis were assessed during attacks when clinically indicated.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM, USA) and R Studio (2025). Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize the data. P-values were calculated using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test to compare the distribution of heterozygous and homozygous variants within each mutation. The null hypothesis assumed no difference in the distribution between these categories. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). Ethical approval was obtained from Zarqa University (IRB/ZU/2025/37). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants prior to inclusion in the study.

Results

A total of 366 patients were initially enrolled in this study, of whom 195 individuals were found to carry one or more MEFV variants. The frequencies of the ten most common MEFV mutations were analyzed among the 195 mutation-positive individuals. The most prevalent variant was E148Q (25.12%), followed by M694V (22.05%) and V726A (21.54%). In contrast, K695R (0.51%) and A744S (4.10%) were the least frequent variants, while the I692del mutation was not detected in any of the samples (Table 1; Fig. 1). These results demonstrate a variable distribution of MEFV mutations within the study cohort.

Analysis of zygosity revealed that the majority of mutations were present in the heterozygous state (n = 178), whereas homozygous mutations were less frequent (n = 17). The distribution of mutations according to zygosity is presented in Table 1; Fig. 1, highlighting the predominance of heterozygous variants in this cohort.

Statistical analysis comparing heterozygous and homozygous carriers showed that only the M694I mutation demonstrated a statistically significant difference in distribution (p = 0.034). All other mutations did not show statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

FMF is a common autosomal recessive monogenic autoinflammatory disorder. However, numerous studies have demonstrated that individuals carrying MEFV mutation may still manifest clinical features consistent with FMF, suggesting incomplete penetrance and the influence of modifier factors [16]. GWAS studies have identified over 310 MEFV variants, displaying diverse clinical manifestations depending on type of mutation. Some patients are heterozygous, carrying a single mutation, while others lack any known mutations [17, 18]. Additionally, The prevalence and mutation spectrum of FMF vary significantly among ethnic groups, highlighting the importance of population-specific genetic studies [19].

In this study, we characterized the distribution of the most common MEFV gene mutations in a Jordanian cohort. Among the 366 individuals tested, 195 patients had been diagnosed positive based on FMF molecular screening.

The distribution of the ten most common MEFV gene mutations was tested among 195 individuals, revealing a remarkable variation in mutation frequencies. The most prevalent variant was E148Q (25.12%), followed by M694V (22.05%) and V726A (21.54%). In contrast, K695R (0.51%) and A744S (4.10%) were rarely detected, while the I692del mutation was absent in the studied cohort (Table 1). In comparison to other studies conducted in Jordan, E148Q was predominantly prevalent in our study population. Whereas M694V was the most common mutation with a frequency of 50%, followed by V726A at 21.4% and E148Q at 19.4% among 196 FMF children [20]. In contrast, a Jordanian-Lebanese study was performed in 2005. Overall, M694V and V726A were common in both populations, M694I and R761H were significantly more frequent in Lebanese patients, and some MEFV variants (including novel ones) were unique to the Lebanese cohort [18]. These discrepancies highlight regional genetic diversity and founder effects, underscoring tailored MEFV testing.

These findings highlight the value of comprehensive MEFV analysis in understanding mutation patterns in FMF within Arab populations [20].

Statistical analysis in this study demonstrated that M694I showed a statistically significant difference in distribution between heterozygous and homozygous carriers (p = 0.034; Table 1). This finding reflects a variation in distribution within this cohort; however, due to the descriptive nature of the study and the absence of a control group or clinical correlation, this result should be interpreted with caution.

Although the E148Q variant remains one of the most frequently detected MEFV mutations, its clinical significance remains debated, and it has been variably classified as pathogenic, likely benign, or a variant of uncertain significance [21, 22]. Its high frequency in this symptomatic cohort reflects its common occurrence in the studied population, without implying pathogenicity. Similar observations have been reported in other populations, including studies from Turkey and Japan [23].

The M694V mutation was also observed at a considerable frequency in this cohort. However, it has been reported in the literature to be associated with more severe disease phenotypes and complications in FMF patients [2]. The relatively lower prevalence of mutations such as R761H and K695R, as well as the absence of I692del, reflects the distinct genetic makeup of FMF in Jordanians [24].

Most patients in this study carried heterozygous mutations, while homozygous cases were less frequent and mainly involved M694I, M694V, and E148Q. This distribution is comparable to findings from other Arab cohorts, where heterozygosity predominates [25]. The high frequency of heterozygous and compound heterozygous genotypes suggests that additional genetic or environmental modifiers may contribute to disease expression, an observation that has been proposed in previous studies [26].

Compound heterozygous MEFV genotypes, commonly involving E148Q, V726A, and M680I, are prevalent in Jordanian FMF patients, mirroring global patterns while showing local nuances. A 2018 single-center study of 196 Jordanian children reported these in 28.1% of cases, with pairings like E148Q-V726A (3.1%) and M680I-V726A (2%), alongside dominant M694V (50%). Similarities include shared Mediterranean founder effects and high colchicine response (100%), but E148Q ranks higher (19.4%) than in some cohorts, with a 96.4% detection rate exceeding regional averages. Earlier Jordan-Lebanon data (2005) confirmed these mutations’ frequency in 55 Jordanians, underscoring testing needs [18, 20]. These findings reinforce comprehensive MEFV analysis for suspected FMF in Jordan.

Overall, this study expands the current knowledge of MEFV mutation patterns in Jordan and contributes valuable data to the understanding of FMF genetics in Arab populations. The findings underscore the importance of population-specific genetic studies for accurate diagnosis and genetic counseling. Future studies incorporating detailed clinical phenotyping and larger cohorts are needed to further clarify genotype–phenotype correlations and the clinical relevance of debated variants such as E148Q.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a descriptive characterization of the MEFV gene variant spectrum in a Jordanian cohort of FMF patients based on a targeted mutation screening panel. The predominance of E148Q, followed by V726A and M694V, highlights the distribution of commonly screened variants within this population and is consistent with patterns reported in other Middle Eastern cohorts.

These findings emphasize the importance of population-specific genetic data for interpreting MEFV variants, particularly for debated variants such as E148Q. However, due to the descriptive nature of the study, the absence of a control group, and the lack of comprehensive genotype–phenotype correlation, the results should not be interpreted as evidence of pathogenicity or disease association.

Our findings support the role of genetic testing in the diagnostic evaluation of FMF patients in Jordan within the context of the screened variants. Future studies incorporating full-gene sequencing, larger cohorts, and detailed clinical phenotyping are required to better clarify genotype–phenotype correlations and the clinical relevance of these variants.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. It is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings and reduce the statistical power to detect robust associations. In addition, the genetic analysis was restricted to a targeted mutation panel and did not include full MEFV gene sequencing, potentially missing rare or novel variants.

The lack of a control group and the limited availability of standardized clinical data and inflammatory biomarkers further restricted the ability to perform comprehensive genotype–phenotype correlations. The retrospective nature of the study and reliance on existing medical records may also have resulted in incomplete data.

Future multicenter studies with larger, well-annotated cohorts and comprehensive genetic and clinical assessments are needed to validate these findings and to better understand the molecular basis of MEFV-related disease variability.
