Role of Intravenous Pyelography in the Evaluation of Developmental Renal Anomalies
Authors: V Ganesh Gokul, Saranya Ayyadurai, R Sarvesh, Vinoth Thangam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This study aims to examine the role of intravenous pyelography (IVP) in the evaluation of developmental renal anomalies.Materials and Method:A prospective cross-sectional study was conducted on 26 subjects presenting with loin pain and suspicion of renal anomalies, subjected to IVP at ACS Medical College and Hospital, Chennai. Descriptive statistics were used for frequency and percentage.Results:The study population consisted of 61.5% males and 38.5% females, indicating a male predilection for renal anomalies. Horseshoe kidney (31%) and duplex collecting system (23%) were the most prevalent anomalies, underscoring the importance of early detection and tailored management. Less frequent anomalies included malrotation, ectopic kidney, crossed renal ectopia, and unilateral renal agenesis.Conclusion:Despite advancements in imaging technology, IVP remains relevant for detailed anatomical and functional assessment of the urinary tract. This study highlights the importance of proactive management and multidisciplinary collaboration in optimizing outcomes for patients with developmental renal anomalies.

INTRODUCTION

Intravenous Pyelography (IVP) constitutes a valuable diagnostic method used in assessing urinary tract pathology. This technique uses both ionizing radiation and contrast media to visualize the urinary tract. The procedure typically involves acquiring initial control or scout images of the abdomen, focusing on the kidneys and urinary bladder, followed by postcontrast imaging captured at sequential intervals.[1] The assessment and management of developmental renal anomalies constitute a critical aspect of pediatric nephrology and urology. Understanding the structural variations and anomalies within the renal system is paramount for appropriate clinical management and prognosis determination. Among the various diagnostic modalities available, intravenous pyelography (IVP) has historically played a significant role in the evaluation of developmental renal anomalies.

Developmental renal anomalies encompass a spectrum of structural abnormalities affecting the kidneys and urinary tract, ranging from congenital malformations to acquired defects during fetal development. These anomalies pose substantial challenges in diagnosis and management because of their diverse presentations and potential complications, including renal dysfunction and urinary tract obstruction.[2]

Intravenous pyelography, a radiographic imaging technique using intravenous contrast agents to visualize the urinary system, has long been a cornerstone in the diagnostic armamentarium for renal anomalies. By providing detailed anatomical delineation of the kidneys, ureters, and bladder, IVP enables clinicians to identify structural abnormalities, such as renal agenesis, duplex kidneys, horseshoe kidneys, ureteropelvic junction obstruction, and vesicoureteral reflux, among others.[34] Moreover, dynamic imaging during the contrast transit phase offers valuable insights into renal function and urinary flow dynamics, aiding in the assessment of renal perfusion, excretion, and drainage.

Although the utility of IVP in evaluating developmental renal anomalies is well-established, advancements in imaging technology have introduced alternative modalities, including ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and nuclear scintigraphy. These modalities offer distinct advantages such as noninvasiveness, superior soft tissue resolution, and reduced radiation exposure compared with traditional IVP. Consequently, the role of IVP in contemporary clinical practice has evolved, necessitating a reevaluation of its indications and relevance in the diagnostic algorithm for developmental renal anomalies.[4]

By reviewing existing literature, analyzing clinical data, and considering contemporary practices, we seek to elucidate the comparative advantages, limitations, and appropriate indications for IVP in the context of modern imaging modalities.[5] Through this exploration, we endeavor to provide insights that inform clinical decision-making, optimize diagnostic strategies, and enhance the holistic management of patients with developmental renal anomalies. This study aims to critically examine the role of intravenous pyelography in the evaluation of developmental renal anomalies.

MATERIALS AND METHOD

A prospective cross-sectional study was performed in the Department of Radio-diagnosis at ACS Medical College and Hospital, Chennai. A sample of 26 subjects who were admitted with complaints of loin pain and RIF pain with suspicion of developmental renal anomalies on USG, were subjected to IVP. This study was conducted over a period of 4 months. The age group of 10 to 50 years was selected for this study. Patients with renal anomalies and who gave consent are included in this study. Those who are younger than 10 years and older than 50 years old, and with any cancer related to the kidney are excluded from this study. Omnipaque is administered intravenously to the subjects. Dose-1 ml/kg. Serial x-rays were taken in the order of scout film, 2 min nephrogram phase, 7 min pyelogram phase, 15 mins for ureters, prone film, 30 mins for full bladder, and postvoid film. The collected data were entered and interpreted using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, 2019). Descriptive statistics were used frequency and percentage of renal anomalies.

DISCUSSION

Developmental renal anomalies present a spectrum of structural abnormalities affecting the kidneys and urinary tract, posing diagnostic challenges for clinicians. Intravenous pyelography (IVP) has long been a cornerstone in the radiological evaluation of these anomalies because of its ability to provide detailed anatomical information without invasive procedures. The gender distribution among study participants, as depicted in Table 1, reveals that of 26 patients, 61.5% were male, whereas 38.5% were female. This observed gender skew suggests a potential disparity in the susceptibility to developmental renal anomalies, with males being more affected than females in the cohort studied. This finding aligns with existing literature, which often reports a higher prevalence of renal anomalies in males compared with females.[6] Several factors may contribute to this gender discrepancy, including genetic predispositions, hormonal influences, and anatomical variations between male and female urinary systems.

Table 2 and Figure 1 provide a comprehensive overview of the frequency and percentage distribution of specific renal anomalies identified within the study population. The most prevalent anomaly observed was horseshoe kidney, accounting for 31% of cases, followed by duplex collecting system at 23%. These findings are consistent with established epidemiological patterns, where horseshoe kidney and duplex collecting systems rank among the most common congenital renal anomalies encountered clinically.[37] The relatively high incidence of these anomalies underscores the importance of vigilant diagnostic evaluation and management strategies to address potential complications associated with these conditions, such as urinary obstruction, calculi formation, and predisposition to infections.

Most of the anomalies are asymptomatic unless they are associated with other system abnormalities or complicated by superimposed infections or calculi. Duplex collecting system anomalies are divided into single ureter, bifid ureter, double ureter, and duplex kidney based on the level of fusion or lack of fusion. On IVP, the identification of duplex systems grossly is possible in most of the cases.[48] Identification of a specific type is always not possible because of improper technique. In those cases, CT/MR pyelography is helpful. The same rule applies in the identification of subtypes of crossed renal ectopia or crossed fused renal ectopia. In this study, all the findings on IVP are confirmed using CT and USG.

Moreover, the distribution of other renal anomalies, including malrotation, ectopic kidney, crossed renal ectopia, and unilateral renal agenesis highlights the diverse spectrum of structural variations affecting the renal system. Although less frequent compared with horseshoe kidney and duplex collecting systems, these anomalies carry significant clinical implications and may necessitate tailored therapeutic interventions based on individual patient characteristics and associated comorbidities.[9] For instance, ectopic kidneys and crossed renal ectopia may present challenges in surgical planning and renal transplantation due to aberrant anatomical positioning, requiring meticulous preoperative assessment and multidisciplinary collaboration.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study sheds light on the role of intravenous pyelography (IVP) in the evaluation of developmental renal anomalies and provides valuable insights into the gender distribution and spectrum of specific anomalies among the study population. The prevalence of horseshoe kidney and duplex collecting systems highlights the significance of early detection and tailored management strategies to mitigate potential complications associated with these common anomalies. IVP is as accurate as CT/MR pyelography in diagnosing congenital renal anomalies.
