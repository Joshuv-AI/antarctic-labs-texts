Application of fiber bragg grating sensing to thermal variation during dental implant site preparation: an in vitro study
Authors: Shurui Hu, Mingjie Wu, Zhaotian Zhang, Ye Guan, Shurong Ma, Shuyu He, Tingkai Yu, Guoping Cheng, Li Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe objective of this study is to overcome the limitations associated with conventional temperature sensors in assessing the temperature distribution of a surgical implant operation. It achieves this objective by utilizing fiber Bragg grating (FBG) temperature sensors to perform measurements. The performance of these FBG sensors is evaluated by comparison with results reported in previous studies.MethodsAn experimental in vitro model was developed employing bovine rib bones, selected for their physical characteristics analogous to those of human mandibular bone, as test specimens. FBG sensors were placed adjacent to the implant site to monitor temperature fluctuations occurring throughout the surgical procedure. The influence of various factors on temperature variations was measured over seven groups: irrigation and sequential operations (Groups 1–4), drill speed (Group 5), drill diameter (Group 6), and maximum drilling depth (Group 7). Sensor data were analyzed to evaluate the platform’s performance, and statistical analyses were conducted to assess the influence of these factors.ResultsFiber Bragg grating sensors can independently and accurately record temperature variations in both cancellous and cortical bone simultaneously. Statistical analysis indicated that irrigation and intermittent drilling effectively mitigate the risk of temperature exceeding the upper limit of human tissue tolerance, with irrigation playing a predominant role in reducing temperature elevation in our study (p < 0.05). Furthermore, larger drill diameters and increased drilling depths significantly increased the maximum temperature changes (p < 0.05). These findings are largely consistent with those reported in previous studies. Moreover, the use of FBG sensors provided novel insights: increased drilling speed causes higher heat generation in cortical bone (p < 0.05), while temperature changes in cancellous bone remain insignificant. Conversely, deeper drilling causes elevated heat production in cancellous bone (p < 0.05), with minimal effects observed in cortical bone.ConclusionThe advantages of the sensor include high independence and accuracy, low systematic errors due to its small size, biosafety, and cost-effectiveness resulting from multi-point testing within a single optical fiber. These many advantages make the designed FBG temperature sensor a dependable option for real-time temperature monitoring in future in vivo research and clinical surgical procedures.

Background

Successful bone integration is one of the most important factors affecting the success of implant surgery, and controlling the temperature increase during implant site preparation guarantees successful osseointegration [1]. Studies have shown that a bone surface temperature exceeding 47 °C can cause unpreventable thermal injury and bone necrosis, which will finally lead to the failure of implant osseointegration [2]. Temperature variations within the bone are influenced by two primary factors. The first factor concerns the characteristics of the planting equipment, including the geometric design of the implant drilling apparatus, the materials used in manufacturing the drill, and the implant cooling system configuration [3–7]. The second factor is related to clinical procedures, such as the load applied to the drill, the drilling technique, drilling speed, duration of drilling, and the use of piezosurgery and guided surgery systems [8, 9]. Nevertheless, our knowledge of the effect of heat produced during the osteotomy stage of implant placement is still limited [10].

Several sensors, infrared thermocouple, standard thermocouple and negative temperature coefficient (NTC) sensor, have been used to monitor temperature change in an ex vivo studies [11–13]. Infrared testing has the advantage of providing a non-invasive, two-dimensional temperature distribution map. Certain models used the infrared sensor by exposing it to a plane close to the drill bit, which can measure the temperature at its deeper levels [14]. However, when dealing with temperature changes inside bones in three-dimensional space, a two-dimensional surface temperature monitoring method appears unconvincing for such experimental requirements. Any attempt to expose the operation bone area section inevitably alters the original heat dissipation pattern of the material, thereby affecting the experimental accuracy [15, 16]. This disadvantages also means that it can only be used in in vitro simulation experiments and cannot be applied to in vivo experiments through improvements.

Conventional types of standard thermocouple and NTC sensor can only perform one-point measurements. Most experiments require multiple test holes to be prepared at different depths for testing, which increases the workload and introduces errors into the experiments [17]. Some reports have used single thermoprobe equipped with multiple NTC temperature sensors. However, it cannot eliminate heat transfer at the millimeter scale, which causes mutual interference between different points, resulting in significant experimental errors. Also, the sensors need to be specially designed and lack universality [18]. In brief, the primary limitations of these sensors are their inability to effectively facilitate multi-point detection and their failure to accurately replicate the actual clinical treatment environment. Therefore, to achieve precise real-time monitoring of temperature variations during the planting cavity preparation process and to align with the contemporary trend toward intelligent planting systems, it is essential to employ an accurate, efficient, practical and biosafe sensing system.

In this study, an optical fiber Bragg grating (FBG) temperature sensor was utilized, which, to the authors’ knowledge, represents the first application of this technology for monitoring temperature variations during implantation. The FBG is fabricated by periodic axial modulation of the optical refractive index in the core of a standard communication optical fiber, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This technology has been widely utilized in various fields, including data communications, oil and gas security, and healthcare, where it functions effectively as a temperature sensor [19]. Conventional FBGs are considered biosafe due to their silica-based composition. A bare FBG has a temperature sensitivity of 0.1 °C, which meets the accuracy requirements of most testing applications. During operation, light propagates within the core of the optical fiber without affecting the object under examination. The FBG sensors are thin and slender: the radius of the coated optical fiber measures only 125 micrometers, which results in a minimal impact on the structure being measured [22]. A quasi-distributed FBG string temperature sensor can be formed by inscribing multiple FBGs on a single optical fiber, employing wavelength division multiplexing technology to enable simultaneous multi-point environmental monitoring [23, 24]. In this study, the FBG string sensor incorporated two FBGs inscribed within the same optical fiber, which were positioned in the cortical and cancellous bones, respectively, for experimental purposes.

In recent years, researchers have explored various biomedical applications of FBGs, such as wearable optical fiber sensors for medical monitoring, biomedical photonic sensors, and implantable pressure sensors for use within the human body [25, 26]. In stomatology, FBGs have been primarily utilized as strain sensors. Numerous studies examined the alterations in the periodontal ligament caused by complex loading [27–29]. Sensors have been employed to assess the strain field pattern of a maxilla and compare the effectiveness of two orthodontic devices [30], and to analyze stress and strain during the curing process of dental composites [31]. Collectively, these studies highlight the high operability and broad potential of FBGs in dental research. However, their applications in the field of oral implant thermal variation have not been explored by researchers [32].

Given the aforementioned applications of FBG in stomatology, we believe it can demonstrate excellent performance in implant temperature field testing. In this study, FBGs were employed as temperature sensors to evaluate thermal changes that occur during the osteotomy process in oral implant surgery. These changes were caused by various factors, including irrigation, intermittent operation, speed, diameter, and depth, on a bovine rib bone. The objective of this study was to utilize a novel apparatus in an ex vivo implant study to identify the advantages and potential limitations of the use of FBGs. This investigation can provide valuable insights for future research involving FBG sensors in oral experiments, particularly those related to implant machinery.

Methods

This in vitro study built a new test platform based on two main parts: a temperature measuring part and an in vitro dental implant surgery. As Fig. 2a shows, these two parts were assembled by practiced dentists in order to minimize the errors caused by the equipment and inexperience. The temperature of the laboratory settings was maintained at 23℃±2℃, with a relative humidity of 65%.

Temperature measurement part

It is widely accepted that the higher density of cortical bone compared to cancellous bone leads to increased heat generation during surgical procedures [33]. However, effectively delivering irrigating cold fluid to cancellous bone is often challenging, making the comparison of temperature changes between these two bone types particularly significant. Consequently, an optical fiber containing two FBGs, as the right side of Fig. 2b showed, was inserted into a small hole (prepared by slim pilot drill) parallel to the implant hole at a distance of 1 mm. The FBGs were manufactured by Chonghai Information Technology Co., Ltd., China, and the length of each FBG was 1 mm, ensuring that the recorded temperature values correspond precisely to the designated test points. The first FBG, denoted as FBG1, was embedded within the cortical bone, specifically positioned 1 mm beneath the surface of the bovine rib bone. It operated as a temperature sensor to monitor thermal fluctuations in the cortical bone during implant drilling. In contrast, the second FBG, denoted as FBG2, was located within the cancellous bone and served as the temperature sensor for evaluating thermal variations in this area. The 9 mm distance between FBG1 and FBG2 ensured that the temperature could be measured independently by the two sensors. The FBG string was enclosed within a steel tube with an outer diameter of 0.51 mm and an inner diameter of 0.26 mm to prevent any interference caused by pressure during the drilling process. We used the Optical Fiber Sensing Analyzer (Bayan™, China), also called FBG interrogator, to measure the center wavelength changes of the FBG. Its wavelength detection range is 1527 ~ 1566 nm, with a dynamic demodulation rate synchronized at 2500 Hz, a wavelength resolution of 0.1 pm, a wavelength measurement accuracy of ± 1 pm, and a time reading accuracy of 1 µs.

In vitro bovine rib preparation

Fresh bovine rib bones obtained from a cadaver (a 2-year-old bull) were employed to simulate human mandibular bone. This selection was based on previous studies demonstrating that bovine rib bones exhibit similar properties to human mandibular bone with respect to density, external morphology, cortical and cancellous bone thickness, as well as thermal conductivity [34, 35]. All specimens were harvested from the midsection of fresh bovine ribs in sections that measured approximately 100 * 10 * 20 mm [13], as shown in Fig. 2b and d, in order to closely replicate the clinical relationship between cortical and cancellous bones. The processed bone fragments were randomly assigned to each group as samples for the experiment. The samples were carefully cleaned to remove any residual soft tissue. Subsequently, they were embedded in medical-grade super-anhydrite plaster and placed in transparent rectangular polystyrene containers to ensure stabilization during testing. After preparation, all samples were stored at -10 °C for subsequent use. Experimental bones were naturally warmed to room temperature prior to tests.

Experimental groups and operational procedure

A total of seven experimental groups were established (Table 1), covering five clinically relevant variables that potentially influence alterations in heat generation within the bone region during surgical procedures: drill diameter (2.8–3.65 mm), maximum drill depth (12–14 mm), drilling speed (800 or 1400 rpm) and deployment of either irrigation cooling or intermittent operation. This study employed an implant drill (ADIN OS, ADIN®, Israel, Afula) for the implant surgery. Referring to prior studies that conducted analogous in vitro experiments, external irrigation with constant 4 °C saline cooling of 75 ml/min was applied throughout the drilling period [14, 18, 36]. Sequential drilling here refers to a technique characterized by the performance of intermittent and graduated osteotomies (Interval: 4 ± 0.5s, about 5 cycles) for approximately 20 s. This process involves pausing the drilling and retracting the drill from the prepared site to permit the inflow of cool air and irrigation fluid to the region subjected to friction-induced heating. In the absence of intermittent operation, a continuous drilling procedure was completed within 10 s. All experimental procedures were conducted by professional clinical dentists to reduce the likelihood of experimental errors. The distance between difference implant site is always larger than 2 cm to ensure that the operation areas do not interfere with each other. The baseline parameters used in Group 1 were as follows: diameter of 2.8 mm, speed of 800 rpm, drilling depth of 12 mm, and sequential drilling and irrigation procedure.

A total of 210 drilling temperature variation data points were collected across the seven groups, where 30 repetitions were carried out for each group. Results

Detailed thermal variation during operation process

Figure 3a illustrates the temperature variation during drilling for Group 4, where neither irrigation nor intermittent operation was employed. The onset of temperature change in the cancellous bone (point B) was delayed by approximately 3s compared to the cortical bone (point A). The total duration time of the temperature increase in the bone was about 7s, where the time for the cortical bone was about 5s (point C), and the time for the cancellous bone was less than 4s (point D). Subsequently, the bone temperature began to decrease. Notably, during the heat dissipation process, although the highest temperature in the cortical bone was higher than that in the cancellous bone, the heat dissipation rate inside the cancellous bone was lower than that observed in the cortical bone. Consequently, the temperature inside the cancellous bone was higher than that in cortical bone after their temperatures became equal at point E. This finding indicates that FBG1 and FBG2 sensors can independently and accurately record the temperature in the cancellous and cortical bones, respectively.

In Group 4, the maximum temperature increase observed in the cancellous bone was 7.8 °C, while the cortical bone exhibited a maximum increase of 5.77 °C. Temperature increase caused by intense friction indicated a potential risk of thermal damage. Consequently, it is essential to employ irrigation and intermittent operations in order to substantially mitigate temperature elevation. This effect is illustrated in Fig. 3b, which presents the results for Group 1. The temperature rise-and-fall cycles shown confirm the cooling effect of intermittent operation as well as the standardized procedures followed by the operator. It can be observed that the maximum temperature increase was less than 1.6 °C. Unlike the smooth temperature rise curve depicted in Fig. 3a, the temperature rise curves for both cortical and cancellous bones exhibited several pronounced fluctuations, corresponding to the sequential operational intervals.

Comparative analysis of the maximum temperature increase observed within and between seven groups

For the statistical analysis, the maximum temperature increase observed during osteotomy was used as the representative value for each group, as shown in Fig. 4. We compared our results to those obtained in published literature to evaluate the performance of the FBG as a temperature sensor suitable for implant site preparation. First, Fig. 4a shows that irrigation played a decisive role in the heat reduction. This is probably because Group 2, where irrigation was not employed, presented the highest increase in temperature variation both for cortical and cancellous bones compared to all the other solo factor experiments performed on Groups 3, and 5–7. We proposed that, in comparison to other auxiliary cooling technique, irrigation offers a continuous and supplementary delivery of low-temperature fluid, effectively and rapidly dissipating the heat produced during high-speed cutting operations, in accordance with findings from prior research [37]. Second, sequential operation was the second most important factor that affected the temperature variation. Third, due to the higher density and lower thermal conductivity, there was a higher heat production in the cortical bone than in the cancellous bone, as illustrated in Fig. 4a for all groups, consistent with previous studies [18]. Last, excluding the influences of irrigation and intermittent operations, increased drilling speed, larger drill diameter and higher drilling depth contributed to elevated maximum temperature variations, as demonstrated in Groups 5–7 shown in (Fig. 4b and c). These results align with previously reported findings in the literature [16, 38, 39].

Additional findings that were not reported in previous research can be derived from our study. These findings indicate that increasing the drilling speed caused a higher heat generation around the cortical bone, while no significant temperature changes were observed in the cancellous bone, as observed in (Fig. 4b and c, Group 5). This phenomenon may be caused by the more rapid cutting of dense bones, which generated a higher amount of heat, whereas a reduction in bone density diminishes the intensity of this effect. Conversely, drilling deeper resulted in a higher amount of heat production in the cancellous bone, with only minimal effects on the cortical bone (Fig. 4b and c, Group 7). One possible explanation of this phenomenon is that the coolant encounters challenges in reaching deeper regions of bones, thereby impeding the heat dissipation within the inner cancellous bone. These results may help to improve the cooling procedure in clinical operations and guide the future upgrade of surgical instruments.

Discussion

Unlike previous sensors, the primary limitation of FBG sensors utilized in this study is their cross-sensitivity to both temperature and strain. In the proposed design, the steel tube encasing the bare FBGs isolated the strain induced by the drilling process; however, there was a small gap between the FBGs and the implantation site. This gap likely caused the actual temperature rise to be higher than the recorded measurements. In future research, we will further optimize the sensing devices to enhance the testing accuracy. Furthermore, we envisage integrating the FBG sensors into oral surgical instruments to enable temperature monitoring during clinical implant procedures without disrupting the surgical workflow.

However, the cross-sensitivity of the FBG sensor is not always disadvantageous. In our experiments, certain atypical data sets, characterized by rapid impulses with large amplitudes, were identified and subsequently excluded from the statistical analysis, as depicted in Fig. 5. It is possible that these signals may have been caused by unintended pressure applied to the FBGs, as indicated by the observation that the terminal segments of the curves did not return to baseline values following extended heat dissipation. We hypothesize that this unintended pressure was caused by deviations in the preset drilling angle [41, 42], which exerted force on bone regions beyond the intended preparation area. When such pressure-induced strain is transmitted to the sensor, it generates signals that do not solely reflect temperature variations. Nonetheless, this phenomenon may offer a practical advantage by acting as an indicator of potential suboptimal conditions to the clinical operator during the preparation process. This feature is particularly beneficial for clinical dentists, given the persistent challenges associated with achieving microscopic-level precision during dental procedures in actual clinical situations [43]. Thus, the sensor’s capacity to issue a warning represents an additional benefit.

The aforementioned results suggest that the FBG technology constitutes a promising method for complex temperature measurement within the dental field. However, its utilization in dentistry still remains at an early stage, with fundamental mechanisms and operational parameters requiring standardization. Therefore, it is important to involve dental professionals and researchers in further research and investigation to advance the implementation of FBG technology in this field [44].

Conclusion

This study presents an FBG sensor system for monitoring thermal variations during dental implant site preparation, which helps to avoid intraoperative bone thermal damage. The FBG sensors offer advantages including high independence and accuracy, reduced systematic errors due to their small size, biocompatibility, and cost-effectiveness through multi-point testing within a single optical fiber. These attributes make them a reliable option for real-time temperature measurements in in vivo studies and clinical surgeries. In future research, we intend to apply this testing method to implant surgical machines. By utilizing the precision instrumentation integrated within these systems, we aim to accurately regulate experimental parameters, such as the sequential drilling program and irrigation flow rate, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the experimental results. Furthermore, the surgical device will be refined to make it suitable for in vivo experiments, ranging from laboratory animal models to human patients, thus contributing more significant and valuable findings to clinical operation.
