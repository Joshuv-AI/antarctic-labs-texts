Impact evaluation of a population-based ‘Screen and Treat for Anaemia Reduction (STAR)’ strategy: a cluster randomised trial in rural Telangana, India
Authors: Raghu Pullakhandam, Little Flower Augustine, Santosh Kumar Banjara, Teena Dasi, Ravindranadh Palika, Rajesh Majumder, Santu Ghosh, Anju Sinha, Bharati Kulkarni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPopulation level screen and treat approach for anaemia reduction could be beneficial but its feasibility and impact remains to be tested.MethodsThis cluster randomised trial in rural Telangana included 14 clusters randomised (1:1) to either intervention or control arms. A cluster included all participants (6 months to 50 years) served by a frontline health worker. In the intervention arm, participants were screened for anaemia at point of care, using WHO 2011 cut-offs, followed by iron–folic acid (IFA) supplementation as per the national guidelines. The control arm participants were eligible for benefits of an ongoing national programme. The primary outcome was mean difference (MD) in haemoglobin between two arms at 6 months. The trial was registered at CTRI (CTRI/2019/01/016918).ResultsBaseline characteristics of participants in the intervention (n=6131) and control (n=5255) arms were broadly similar. In the intervention arm, 88.6% of eligible participants were screened and intervention was delivered to 97% of participants. There was no difference in population haemoglobin (g/dL) between the arms (MD 0.03, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.12, p=0.464). However, after adjustment for relevant confounders, mean haemoglobin in the intervention arm was significantly higher (MD 0.25 g /dL, 95% CI 0.03 to 0. 48, p=0.028). Anaemia prevalence was lower in the intervention than the control arm in total sample (29.6% vs 32.5%, p=0.002) as well as in adolescent girls (40.7% vs 56.0%, p<0.001) and women of reproductive age (WRA, 52% vs 56.5%, p=0.02). Compliance to IFA (% consumption of prescribed dose) was 32% and 47.5% in those receiving therapeutic and prophylactic doses, respectively.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the feasibility of implementing a population level screen and treat approach. The intervention reduced anaemia prevalence significantly among vulnerable groups, particularly adolescent girls and WRA. Modest improvement in population haemoglobin and anaemia reduction suggests a need for strategies to improve the compliance to IFA.Trial registration numberCTRI/2019/01/016918.

Introduction

In low- and middle-income countries, anaemia continues to be a major public health concern that accounts for ≈9% of all health condition-related disability.1 Repeated rounds of National Family Health Survey (NFHS) have shown persistent high prevalence of anaemia in India,24 with particularly high prevalence in 6–59 months old children (67.1%), adolescent girls (59.1%), pregnant women (52.2%) and non-pregnant women of reproductive age (WRA, 57.0%) as per the NFHS-5.3 Anaemia control remains a priority in India, and iron–folic acid (IFA) supplementation to vulnerable groups was initiated in the 1970s. In the current Anaemia Mukt Bharat (Anaemia free India, AMB) initiative launched in 2018,5 supply chains have been improved, and efforts are underway for strengthening the programme operations through inter-institutional mechanisms. However, despite ongoing anaemia control programmes for almost five decades with high level of government commitment, anaemia prevalence remains high in India.3

A major reason for the persistence of high prevalence of anaemia and lack of programme impact could be that the current anaemia control programmes focus on prophylactic supplementation of IFA to programme beneficiaries. The majority of the anaemic individuals, therefore, remain undiagnosed and receive only prophylactic doses of IFA that are inadequate for recovery from anaemia. In addition, previous assessments of anaemia control programmes have identified some of the important shortcomings of the IFA supplementation efforts, including supply chain constraints resulting in poor last mile delivery and low coverage.612 The AMB programme guidelines also recommend a ‘test and treat’ approach with a focus on pregnant women and 10–19 year-old adolescent girls and boys.13 However, widespread testing and treatment of anaemia in these groups is not yet operational and is limited only to those who access healthcare at a facility. Therefore, addressing the critical gaps in anaemia screening and IFA distribution coverage is expected to improve the programme impact.

In a previous study in a residential setting, in suburbs of Hyderabad city, Telangana, India, screening for anaemia followed by therapeutic or prophylactic IFA supplementation reduced the anaemia prevalence among WRA by 40%, and that significant treatment effects persisted at 9 months follow-up.14 In the present study, we evaluated the impact of a population-based ‘Screen and Treat approach for Anaemia Reduction (STAR)’ as compared with the currently operational anaemia control programme in a cluster-randomised controlled trial. A detailed protocol of the trial has been published earlier.13

Methods

Study design and setting

The current study was a two-arm, open-labelled, cluster randomised trial conducted in 14 villages from Medchal-Malkajgiri district of Telangana State.13 Population served by one frontline health worker called ASHA (Accredited Social Health Activist) in the village constituted a cluster, with cluster size of 1000–3000.

The trial was registered at CTRI (CTRI/2019/01/016918). An independent Data Safety Monitoring Board supervised the study.

Sample size

A sample size calculation was performed for a cluster randomised trial comparing means between two groups (intervention and control). In the absence of information from similar studies in the past, a 0.25 SD difference (Δ 0.65 g/dL) in the population mean haemoglobin between the arms was considered as indicative of public health significance, and a SD of 2.6 (based on previous District Level Household Survey−4 data for the state of Telangana),15 an average cluster size (m) of 1000 and an assumed intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.016,13 with a significance level of 0.05 (two-sided), 90% statistical power and an allocation ratio (AR) of 1, the required sample size was determined to be 7 clusters of size 1000 individuals each in both the intervention and control groups (14 clusters together). This ensured sufficient power to detect the specified effect size under the given study design parameters. However, to validate assumed ICC, a pilot within the study using haemoglobin data from the first three intervention clusters (n=2091; 1018 females and 1073 males) estimated ICC as 0.003. As this estimate was much lower than the previous assumption, the previous sample size estimation was retained.

Randomisation

There were 47 villages listed in the catchment area of the primary health centres (PHCs) of Narapally, Shamirpet and Srirangavaram. These were served by 82 ASHAs (a few of these villages had more than one ASHA). Therefore, a total of 14 villages were randomised (constrained to selected covariates) into intervention and control arms (1:1 ratio) from these 47 villages, by an independent statistician. Among these selected villages, two villages in each of the intervention and control arms had more than one ASHA, respectively. Therefore, only one ASHA was selected randomly from these villages, through computer-generated random numbers by an independent statistician. A covariate constrained cluster randomisation was adopted to make selected clusters comparable across the groups with respect to selected covariates. Hence, the randomisation was constrained by three covariates that could potentially affect the population mean haemoglobin and facility-based screening or intervention: (1) Percentage (%) of children aged 6–59 months in the population (with a maximum allowable difference of 5%), (2) number of households per cluster (with a maximum allowable difference of 500 households) and (3) distance to the closest medical facility (with a maximum allowable difference of 10 km).

Participant enrolment and consent

Using information from ASHA registers, the project staff enumerated household members in each cluster with the help of the ASHA by a door-to-door survey using digital methods as described in the protocol paper.13 All individuals in these clusters meeting the prespecified inclusion and exclusion criteria (figure 1) were enrolled in the study.13 Briefly, all individuals in these clusters belonging to the following age categories: children aged 6–59 months and 5–9 years, adolescent girls and boys (10–19 years), WRA, pregnant women and adult men below 50 years of age and not planning to move out of the village in the next 6 months were included in the study. Individuals with febrile illnesses (except those with malaria) were included by study staff if the fever subsided at the second visit after 7 days. The individuals who reported a history of repeated blood transfusion, diagnosed with haemolytic anaemia, chronic diarrhoea, stomach ulcer, heart surgery/prosthetic valves, cancer, tuberculosis; and those who were on medications like tetracycline or anticoagulants; and women in the first trimester of pregnancy were excluded from the study.

The written informed consent from participants older than 19 years, as well as from parents for their children below 19 years, age-appropriate verbal (7–14 years old) and written assent (14–19 years old) were obtained prior to initiation of the study. The written informed consent was also obtained from ASHAs serving the chosen clusters for their participation in the study.

Data collection

Data on participant characteristics, assets, consumption of iron absorption inhibitors (tea/coffee) or promoters (vitamin C rich fruits) along with meals (on the day of screening to capture habitual consumption pattern), history of exposure to anaemia screening or IFA over the last 1 year, receipt of government programme benefits, morbidity (previous 15 days), dietary diversity and anthropometric data were collected at the household level using structured questionnaires, as detailed elsewhere.13

Screening for anaemia

In the intervention clusters, the participants were mobilised at points near their homes (within a distance of not more than 200 m, where an autoanalyser connected to uninterrupted 3KVA power supply was placed) for haemoglobin estimation with the help of the ASHA and the community leaders. Haemoglobin estimations were done at point of care (POC) by a portable autoanalyser using a pooled capillary blood (PCB) sample, which is previously validated against venous blood estimates.16 Briefly, the phlebotomists collected four to five drops of PCB in K2-EDTA microtainer tubes and analysed the samples using autoanalyzers (ABX Micros ES 60, Horiba, Japan), and the data was transferred electronically in real time to the server using customised software.17 Three levels of quality controls (6, 13 and 18 g/dL) were tested on each day to ensure precision, and in a subsample (n=906), paired venous blood haemoglobin was also estimated. The PCB and venous blood haemoglobin estimates in this subsample are comparable (14.07 g/dL (SD, 2.35) vs 14.14 g/dL (SD, 2.35)), with a bias of −0.07, and 95% limits of agreement ranging from −1.17 to 1.03. Anaemia and its grades were diagnosed using age-specific WHO haemoglobin reference values.13 18

As screening for anaemia was a part of the intervention, baseline haemoglobin concentrations were not measured in the control arm participants. However, to evaluate balance between the two arms, haemoglobin measurements were done in about 10% of households in the control arm followed by referral to primary health centre for treatment for those identified as anaemic.

Intervention delivery

In the intervention arm, mild or moderate anaemics were provided therapeutic and the non-anaemics with prophylactic doses of IFA as per AMB programme guidelines (online supplemental table 1).13 The IFA dosages were delivered by the project staff, instead of ASHAs, as the ASHAs were occupied with the COVID-19 vaccination programme during the trial period. The intervention was delivered directly to an adult household member in three batches (a supply of 30 days at a time) for a total of 90 days. During the first visit, the study team provided one session of in-person counselling regarding IFA usage with detailed information on administration and potential transient side effects. For 6–59 month-old children, caregivers were demonstrated the usage of auto-dispensers for giving appropriate dose of IFA syrup to the children. After 7 days, the study staff re-visited these households and supervised the dosage dispensation by the caregiver to ensure correct administration. In addition, before starting the IFA supplementation, age-appropriate single dose albendazole (tablet or syrup) was provided to all participants except those who reported intake of de-worming medication in the past 6 months.

Phone calls were made by the study team to the participants within 7–12 days after delivery of the first month supply of IFA medications to enquire about side effects. Those who reported side effects were given reassurance on the transient nature of the side effects and were encouraged to continue the medication irrespective of side effects.

Participants diagnosed with severe anaemia were counselled regarding the adverse health implications and referred to the nearby health facility for further management as per the AMB guidelines. Additionally, a list of the severely anaemic participants was given to the medical officer of PHC and ASHA to facilitate their visit to health facility. These participants were also followed up and included in the outcome assessment (haemoglobin at 6 months).

Behaviour change communication

Behaviour change communication (BCC) campaign for improving compliance to intervention focused on three themes: (1) importance of treatment of anaemia with IFA for improving health, (2) enhancing work productivity in adults and (3) cognitive development in children. These messages were communicated iteratively in multiple ways: through counselling during in-person visits by project staff, through an animated movie displayed on the tablet computer and also shared on WhatsApp to those who had smart phones (n=2021 participants). A specially designed calendar which had the local language messages encouraging intake of IFA was also given to each household as a reminder. Additionally, interactive voice messages reminding the consumption of IFA were delivered through mobile phones for the first 3 months.

Follow-up data collection and outcome assessment

The intervention arm participants were followed up monthly during 3 months of IFA supplementation and for a further 3 months when there was no active intervention by the study team. During monthly visits in the first 3 months, information on compliance (including reasons for non-compliance) and side effects was collected using a structured questionnaire. Wherever possible, the IFA blister packs and syrup bottles were verified to ascertain compliance. In the control arm, the participants were followed up once at 3 months after baseline to collect data on the number of haemoglobin tests done/treatment taken, if any, in the previous 3 months.

Outcome assessment was done at 6 months (±2 weeks) in both the arms. A 3-month gap following the completion of 3 months intervention allowed the assessment of the impact of the intervention with short-term sustenance.

Statistical analysis

An intention-to-treat analysis of randomised controlled trial design was employed. Descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentages (%), were used for categorical variables, while mean, median, SD and IQR were reported to summarise continuous baseline and endline characteristics for both arms. Dietary diversity scores were computed by aligning 16 food groups consumed by households with FAO-recommended food groups.19 Anthropometric z-scores were derived using WHO standards.20 21 The potential confounders related to the association of haemoglobin with intervention were compared between the intervention and control group at baseline. The differences in continuous variables between arms and age/sex groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test, and the proportions using χ2 test or by comparing the 95% CIs.

The primary outcome, mean difference in population haemoglobin, was computed for all randomised participants at end line before and after adjustment for relevant confounders specified in the protocol, as well as the cluster as described below. There was no imputation of missing values in the analysis. The secondary outcomes included differences in anaemia prevalence in children (specified a priori). In addition, we have also analysed mean difference in haemoglobin and anaemia (and its grades) prevalence among different age and sex specific groups, considered in the AMB programme. For adjustment, the factors that were not comparable between two groups were adjusted by multiple linear mixed effects regression with cluster being the random intercept to estimate the overall effect of IFA supplementation on haemoglobin change between the control and intervention arms at endline. Age and sex-specific stratified analysis was carried out at univariate as well as multivariate level. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess mean haemoglobin changes and anaemia prevalence at endline after excluding 10% of the control arm participants whose haemoglobin was measured at baseline.

Additionally, longitudinal changes in haemoglobin levels from baseline to endline, stratified by baseline anaemia status and age-sex strata in the intervention arm, were estimated using mean differences with 95% CIs. The association of IFA compliance with haemoglobin was assessed using generalised additive model in the intervention arm. The statistical significance was set at p<0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R, V.4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2023, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The participant enrolment (n=16 062) in the two arms was completed between 25 June 2021 and 8 July 2022. In the intervention arm, a total of 8877 participants (belonging to 2163 households, 7 clusters) were enumerated. Of these, 1538 participants were excluded due to age above 50 years or those who were not staying in the village during the study period; 10 did not consent due to disinterest; 416 were excluded applying prespecified exclusion criteria; and 782 participants were unavailable for either data or sample collection. A total of 6131 participants (from 1872 households) provided required data and blood samples, as required for inclusion in the trial (figure 1).

In the seven control arm clusters, a total of 7185 participants (belonging to 1814 households, 7 clusters) were enumerated; of these, 1204 were excluded due to age above 50 years or not staying in the village during the study period; 12 participants did not consent; 361 were excluded applying prespecified exclusion criteria and 353 were unavailable during data collection. A total of 5255 participants (from 1587 households) provided the required data (figure 1).

Participant characteristics at baseline

Overall, the participant characteristics such as male to female ratio, distribution in different age groups, household dietary diversity score and prevalence of stunting, underweight and wasting (or thinness in children of 5–9 years age group) among children under 10 years of age were comparable between the two arms (online supplemental table 2). Some demographic characteristics had minor differences between the two arms; a higher proportion of households in the uppermost tertile of income had better water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) practices in the intervention arm, while a greater proportion of adults had higher education (online supplemental table 2). The baseline haemoglobin and prevalence of anaemia (intervention compared with 10% subsample of control arm) were comparable between the arms (table 1).

Intervention delivery and compliance to IFA

In the intervention arm, a total of 4485 participants were eligible to receive therapeutic or prophylactic doses of IFA and 136 participants who had severe anaemia needed referral for facility-based treatment as per the AMB guidelines. In the first month, IFA supplements were delivered to 98% of the eligible participants (n=4275), whereas in the second and third months, these proportions were 93% (n=4063) and 90% (n=3927), respectively. Non-availability of at least one adult household member during three attempts of IFA distribution was the major reason for not achieving complete coverage for IFA delivery.

Overall, 65.8% participants (n=2793/4246) reported consumption of IFA at least once and 39.5% (n=1681/4246) reported consuming IFA in all the 3 months. Mean compliance to IFA, defined as percentage of prescribed dose consumed, was on average 47.5% (range 41%–51%) and 32.4% (range 27%–34.7%) in prophylactic and therapeutic regimens, respectively (online supplemental figure 1).

Primary outcome: difference in mean haemoglobin between the two arms at end line

Unadjusted analyses showed no significant difference in the mean difference (MD) population haemoglobin (g/dL) between the two study arms (MD 0.03, 95% CI −0.06 to 0.12, p=0.464) at the end line (6 months). However, after adjustment for relevant confounders, this difference was statistically robust (MD 0.25, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.48, p=0.028) with higher haemoglobin in the intervention compared with control arm (table 2).

In stratified analyses (table 2), the haemoglobin was significantly higher in adolescent girls (MD 0.73, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.93, p<0.001) and WRA (MD 0.19, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.31, p<0.001). Adjustment for potential confounders augmented these differences in adolescent girls (adjusted MD 1.03, 95% CI 0.68 to 1.4, p<0.001) and WRA (adjusted MD 0.39, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.67, p<0.001). Adult men had a reverse pattern of significantly higher haemoglobin in the control compared with the intervention arm in unadjusted estimates (MD −0.16, 95% CI −0.28 to 0.04, p=0.008) but was insignificant in adjusted analyses (MD −0.074, 95% CI −0.42 to 0.27, p=0.645). However, the mean haemoglobin in both arms of adult men was higher than the haemoglobin threshold for anaemia. No statistically significant difference in the mean haemoglobin between the two arms was observed among children (6–59 months and 5–9 years), adolescent boys or pregnant women.

In a sensitivity analysis (excluding the 10% subsample from the control arm in whom the haemoglobin was estimated at baseline), the difference in mean haemoglobin (g/dL) in overall population remained insignificant (MD 0.04, 95% CI −0.05 to 0.14, p=0.389) and the trends in mean differences in haemoglobin between age/sex groups in unadjusted versus adjusted analysis were comparable to the estimates without exclusion of these subjects (online supplemental table 4).

The most commonly reported adverse effects were nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain, predominantly among adolescents and adults (online supplemental table 5). The proportion of adverse effects in the intervention arm was higher in the first month and was reduced in subsequent months (month 1: 3.2%, month 2: 0.9%, month 3: 0.8%).

Difference in anaemia prevalence between the two arms at end line

Compared with control arm, overall prevalence of anaemia (all age/sex groups combined) (29.6% vs 32.5%; p=0.002), anaemia among adolescent girls (41% vs 55%; p<0.001) and WRA (52% vs 57%; p=0.02) was lower in intervention arm, but not in other age/sex groups. Further, the prevalence of moderate anaemia among adolescent girls and WRA was also significantly lower in the intervention arm (figure 2).

Longitudinal changes in haemoglobin and in different grades of anaemia in the intervention arm

The overall mean haemoglobin remained similar at end line compared to base line (13.01±2.44 vs 12.83±2.81). When stratified by age and grade of anaemia at baseline, the increment in haemoglobin at end line was higher in the more severe forms of anaemia across all age groups (table 3). When participants were stratified based on grade of anaemia at baseline, the majority of the non-anaemic participants stayed non-anaemic at endline (80–95%). However, about 20% children (6–59 months), 24% adolescent girls and 30% WRA who were non-anaemic at baseline became anaemic (largely mild anaemia) at endline despite receiving prophylactic IFA supplementation for 3 months. The mean haemoglobin showed significant decline from baseline to endline in the non-anaemic groups (by 0.57 g/dL). Consequent to the therapeutic IFA supplementation, only about 48% mild anaemics and 29.5% moderate anaemics became non-anaemic at endline. However, mild anaemic (19.7%) participants shifted to moderate anaemia (table 3). Further, the increment in haemoglobin per gram of iron consumed was linear in moderate anaemics but plateaued in mild anaemics, beyond 6 g iron. However, in non-anaemics, there is no association of iron dose with haemoglobin response (online supplemental figure 2).

Discussion

In this cluster randomised trial, we demonstrated that population-based POC screening followed by supplementation with prophylactic or therapeutic doses of IFA resulted in no or marginal change in mean population haemoglobin (unadjusted 0.03 g/dL, p=0.464 and adjusted 0.25 g/dL, p=0.028) but reduced the overall anaemia prevalence by 3%. However, the impact was relatively higher among adolescent girls (0.73 g/dL higher haemoglobin and 15.3% lower anaemia) and WRA (0.19 g/dL higher haemoglobin and 4.4% lower anaemia). Further, intervention resulted in an overall shift towards less severe forms of anaemia compared with the base line in the intervention arm. These findings suggest that a population-based screen and treat approach for reduction of anaemia is feasible and efficacious in reducing anaemia among vulnerable population groups, adolescent girls and WRA. However, the magnitude of impact was lower than anticipated, possibly due to inadequate consumption of iron doses in case of both prophylactic (47.5%) and therapeutic regimens (32.4%), as well as lower baseline prevalence of anaemia among 5–9 year-old children (21%), adolescent boys (17%) and adult men (6%); altogether these groups constituted 51% of the intervention arm sample.

The persistence of high prevalence of anaemia in India despite ongoing anaemia control programmes is commonly attributed to both supply and demand side problems.61012 An analysis of IFA supplementation coverage under the AMB programme showed that IFA coverage between 2017 and 2020 was lower than 50% in all beneficiary groups except among pregnant women, where it was ~90%.11 The coverage was particularly low among out-of-school adolescents (23%) and children under 5 years of age (15%).6 Other studies have reported supply side issues such as limited availability of IFA supplements at subcentres of PHCs or with frontline workers.10 In agreement with these estimates, the history of exposure to anaemia screening, treatment or consumption of prophylactic doses of iron at baseline was very low (about 6%), except pregnant women in both study arms. In the present cluster randomised trial, we eliminated these challenges by POC screening (88.6%) near participants’ homes and door-step delivery of IFA doses to >90% of subjects during 3-month intervention, which is higher compared with what is usually achieved by the health system. This could be due to intervention delivery by project staff for all 3 months, instead of ASHA workers as required by the original protocol.13 This modification became necessary because the ASHA workers were occupied with tasks related to community mobilisation for COVID-19 vaccination during the trial period. However, this modification allowed examination of the impact of the ‘screen and treat’ approach in the absence of supply side constraints.

The demographic characteristics, haemoglobin and prevalence of anaemia in different age/sex groups broadly remained similar, indicating the balance between the two arms. The anaemia prevalence among children under 5 years of age (47.4%), children aged 5–9 years (20.5%), adolescent boys (16.8%) and girls (43.6%) was similar to the prevalence estimates of Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey (CNNS) in India.22 The prevalence of anaemia among WRA (48.4%) and adult male (6%) was lower compared with NFHS-5 estimates,3 and this difference could be a ramification of difference in blood sample source (PCB vs drops of capillary blood).16 23 The reasons for no or marginal impact on population increment in haemoglobin or reduction in anaemia prevalence, but a larger impact for few age/sex groups (adolescent girls and WRA), could be due to multiple reasons. A recent study in India among mild-to-moderate anaemic children under 5 years of age has shown ~80% reduction in anaemia when the compliance was >75%.24 Therefore, low compliance to the IFA dosages could potentially limit the impact in this trial; only 27% and 45% of prescribed therapeutic and prophylactic doses, respectively, were consumed by the children. Lack of impact in adolescent boys (17% anaemia, of which 11.5% was mild anaemia) and adult men (6% anaemia, of which 92% was mild anaemia) may also be primarily linked with lower prevalence of anaemia and its severity which may not be attributed to iron deficiency in them. Our previous analyses of CNNS data showed that the prevalence of inflammation-adjusted iron deficiency (ID) was almost three times (11.5% vs 30.4%) lower in adolescent boys than in girls.25 Our previous study among WRA in the state of Telangana showed ~70% ID prevalence.14 Although the current study did not measure ID, a greater impact of the intervention in adolescent girls and WRA is likely to be related to higher prevalence of ID.

It is important to note that the intervention led to significantly lower prevalence of moderate anaemia, particularly among adolescent girls and WRA, which is associated with adverse functional consequences. The majority of the moderate anaemic participants (46.4% of 6–59 month-old children, 69.3% of 5–9 year-old children, 56.1% of adolescent girls and 67.7% of boys, 48.1% of WRA) also showed a positive shift in haemoglobin with IFA supplementation, consistent with our previous analyses of the CNNS data that showed greater ID prevalence in those with moderate anaemia than those with mild anaemia.25 Marginal decline in haemoglobin levels among adult men in the intervention arm cannot be explained but could represent natural variation in haemoglobin in the non-anaemic range. Therefore, overall limited impact of the intervention on population mean haemoglobin and anaemia prevalence despite high coverage of the screening and intervention delivery could be partly explained by a lower baseline prevalence of anaemia and its severity in few age/sex groups, poor compliance and a decline in haemoglobin among men and those who were non-anaemic at baseline.

Previous studies in Indian children,24 26 adolescent girls27 and WRA14 in settings where high compliance was ensured showed large benefits in haemoglobin increments and reduction of anaemia. The haemoglobin increments observed in the current study, although significant, were modest, possibly due to lower baseline prevalence of anaemia in about half of the participant groups as well as low compliance to the treatment across all the age groups. The fact that iron dose consumed was linearly associated with haemoglobin increment in moderate but was plateaued in mild anaemics further substantiates the interaction of iron status (severity of anaemia) with iron dose. The plateaued response in haemoglobin to iron therapy in mild anaemics is expected, as haemoglobin levels reach close to the threshold values via a hypoxia/erythropoiesis axis.28 29 However, our assumption that compliance to IFA would be better if participants become aware of their anaemia status and its potential consequences did not prove to be correct. The BCC campaign targeted at improving the knowledge and adherence to medication and trust-building efforts among participants and the health system throughout the project cycle also appears to be insufficient. Future studies implementing a ‘screen and treat’ approach for anaemia reduction must therefore include additional measures to ensure compliance. The major reasons for lack of compliance were reported as lack of interest, perceived side effects, forgetfulness, illness, being away from the home/village or simply no particular reason for missing the doses (data not shown).

We believe these findings have important implications for the programme efforts for reducing anaemia in India. First, we have demonstrated that implementation of population level screen and treat approach is indeed possible, and it improves the reach of programme to population groups who otherwise do not seek medical care. Second, the digital tools implemented in this trial could be scaled up to improve programme implementation and monitoring at different levels. Third, the anaemia reduction with screen and treat approach showed variable impact in different population groups indicating a need for specific targeting of adolescent girls, WRA and/or regions where moderate anaemia prevalence is higher. Finally, despite substantial efforts through the BCC campaign, compliance with the IFA remained low, which appears to be the most important contributor to the lower than expected impact in this trial. Identifying strategies for improving compliance to IFA supplementation should be an important research priority for addressing the anaemia burden.

The study has several strengths, including a large sample size, use of a validated accurate method of haemoglobin assessment, study design optimised to address the supply-side challenges, IFA regimen aligned with the AMB programme guidelines providing valuable programme-relevant evidence as well as use of novel digital tools for study coordination and data accuracy. Some important limitations of the study should be acknowledged. Lack of baseline anaemia prevalence in the control arm is a significant limitation. However, the anaemia prevalence in the 10% subsample in the control arm was comparable to that in the intervention arm alleviating the concerns about possible baseline differences. Additionally, the intervention delivery by the frontline workers was not possible due to COVID-19-related challenges. Moreover, measures related to iron status were not available to understand whether the lower than anticipated impact was related to causes unrelated to iron deficiency. In addition, generalisability of these findings to diverse settings (i.e. urban and other geographical regions) needs to be tested, as the trial was conducted in a rural area.

In conclusion, the results of this cluster randomised trial demonstrate the feasibility of implementing a screen and treat approach in populations, to effectively address the critical gaps related to supply of IFA. The results also highlight the importance of targeting specific high-risk groups of adolescent girls and WRA for better impact. Modest improvement in haemoglobin and anaemia reduction point towards the need for strategies to improve compliance to the treatment. Finally, complementary strategies are needed to improve other environmental factors related to diets and WASH practices for sustainable impact.
