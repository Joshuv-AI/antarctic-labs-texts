Abstract
. Implicit feedback data, such as user clicks, is commonly used in learning-to-rank (LTR) systems because it is easy to collect and it often reflects user preferences. However, this data is prone to various biases, and training an LTR system directly on biased data can result in suboptimal ranking performance. One of the most prominent and well-studied biases in implicit feedback data is position bias, which occurs because users are more likely to interact with higher-ranked documents regardless of their true relevance. In this paper, we propose a novel control function-based method that accounts for position bias in a two-stage process. The first stage uses exogenous variation from the residuals of the ranking process to correct for position bias in the second stage click equation. Unlike previous position bias correction methods, our method does not require knowledge of the click or propensity model and allows for nonlinearity in the underlying ranking model. Moreover, our method is general and allows for debiasing any state-of-the-art ranking algorithm by plugging it into the second stage. We also introduce a technique to debias validation clicks for hyperparameter tuning to select the optimal model in the absence of unbiased validation data. Experimental results demonstrate that our method outperforms state-of-the-art approaches in correcting for position bias.



1.
Introduction

Learning-to-rank (LTR) systems have become a core component in many online applications, from search engines to recommender systems. These systems typically use implicit feedback data, such as user clicks, as a proxy for user preferences due to its ease of collection compared to costly manual human annotations. While valuable, implicit feedback data is prone to biases
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Yue et al. 2010
)
, with position bias being particularly pervasive. Position bias is the tendency of users to interact more with higher-ranked documents, regardless of their true relevance
(
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Joachims et al. 2007
)
. This skews click data, causing LTR models to overestimate higher-ranked documents and underestimate lower ones. Training on such biased data degrades ranking performance and reinforces a feedback loop that negatively affects user experience.

State-of-the-art methods that address position bias can be broadly categorized as click models, propensity-based approaches, and econometrics-based models. Click models
(
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
;
Wang et al. 2013
)
estimate true relevance of documents by hypothesizing how users browse and maximizing the likelihood of observed clicks. Click models rely on assumptions about complex user click behavior, which sometimes may not hold in practice. Two-tower models
(
Guo et al. 2019
;
Yan et al. 2022
)
, following the position-based click model (PBM)
(
Richardson et al. 2007
)
, attempt to disentangle relevance and bias from clicks by modeling them separately, but may fail when confounding exists between the two. Propensity-based methods treat clicks as counterfactual events and re-weight them using inverse propensity weighting (IPW) to achieve relevance-equivalent training loss
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
. Propensity estimation often relies on result randomization
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
, which can harm user experience
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
. Some methods
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Vardasbi et al. 2020
;
Zhu et al. 2020
)
jointly learn propensities and rankers, but coupling the two raises concerns about the unbiasedness if either model is misspecified
(
Agarwal et al. 2019
;
Fang et al. 2019
;
Tian et al. 2020
)
.
Doubly robust (DR) IPW-based bias correction methods
(
Luo et al. 2023
;
Oosterhuis 2023
)
have also been proposed recently, where the DR estimator combines an IPW estimate with predictions from a regression model and becomes unbiased if either the estimated propensity or the regression model is accurate. Most click modeling and propensity-based methods require modifying the ranker’s loss function, which makes them tightly coupled with a specific LTR algorithm and limits their generalization to other LTR algorithms. Alternative approaches
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
, based on Heckman’s two-stage econometric model
(
Heckman 1979
)
, avoid propensity estimation but assume linear ranking models. However, linear models often struggle to capture complex feature interactions and handle the sparse click data typical in LTR systems.

Addressing data bias has a long tradition in the econometrics literature. A prominent class of approaches for addressing various types of biases is control functions
(
Wooldridge 2015
)
which includes a number of related well-known econometric techniques,
such as Heckman selection
(
Heckman 1979
)
and endogenous switching models
(
Maddala and Nelson 1975
)
; shares the same conceptual foundations with
instrumental variables
(
Reiersøl 1941
)
; and exploits identification via orthogonal moments akin to, the more recent,
double machine learning
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
. Control functions are two-stage methods which model bias through the endogeneity in the error term. In this paper, we introduce a control function for addressing position bias. In the first stage, we model the system’s previous ranking process, which generated the user feedback data based on observable features, to determine document positions in a ranked list for a query.
In the second stage, we leverage the exogenous variation in first-stage residuals as a control function within the ranking model to adjust for position bias. We introduce residual transformation as a hyperparameter in the second stage and suggest several carefully designed transformation options.

A control function approach for addressing position bias in LTR systems offers a number of advantages over existing approaches. Unlike Heckman-based methods
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
, which require linear models in both stages, our control function method allows for any linear or non-linear models in both stages. Additionally, by regularizing the feature space during the modeling of the document ranking process, our method addresses multicollinearity issues with high-dimensional feature spaces that Heckman-based methods do not. While propensity-based methods
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2019
;
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2023
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Vardasbi et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
reweight click data using IPW to account for bias, our method circumvents the need for propensity estimation. We estimate query-document-specific residuals by modeling the system’s previous document ranking process to account for position bias. Our method also does not require result randomization
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
. Unlike click modeling methods
(
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
;
Wang et al. 2013
)
, our method makes no assumptions about user browsing behavior in either of its stages. Moreover, our method addresses position bias without any modification to the ranker’s loss function, which generalizes its application to any pointwise, pairwise, or listwise LTR algorithm.

In addition, our work addresses a gap in the literature: the challenge of hyperparameter tuning for unbiased ranking models in the absence of unbiased ground truth or true relevance of documents. Most existing bias correction methods
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2023
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
either assume the availability of unbiased ground truth data
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2023
)
, which is sometimes unrealistic, or rely on biased click data for hyperparameter tuning
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
. However, learning to rank with biased validation data can exhibit significant declines in performance due to the distribution shift of unbiased test data
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
.
Here, we introduce a technique to debias validation clicks for hyperparameter tuning, ensuring that our method maintains robust performance when true relevance is unavailable.

2.
Related Work

Position bias in LTR systems is mainly addressed through click models, propensity-based methods, and econometrics-based approaches. Several studies
(
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Chuklin et al. 2016
;
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
)
aim to model clicks via user browsing behavior to capture true relevance feedback. Craswell et al.
(
Craswell et al. 2008
)
propose the Cascade model, assuming users view results sequentially. Dupret et al.
(
Dupret and Piwowarski 2008
)
introduce the User Browsing Model (UBM), which estimates examination probabilities based on position and prior clicks, allowing users to skip results. Chapelle et al.
(
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
)
propose a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) model that accounts for search abandonment by modeling user satisfaction by clicks. Wang et al.
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
jointly train a ranking model and estimate examination propensity using a graphical model and an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm.
Guo et al.
(
Guo et al. 2019
)
use a two-tower model based on PBM
(
Richardson et al. 2007
)
model, where one tower models relevance probability and the other models observation bias. While this approach aims to decouple relevance from position bias, it may be ineffective when the two are confounded, leading to biased estimates. A key drawback of click-based models is their reliance on assumptions about user browsing behavior, which may not always hold and can lead to inaccurate relevance estimation.

Propensity-based methods use counterfactual learning, treating bias as a counterfactual factor and debiasing user clicks through IPW. Several approaches
(
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
estimate propensities through result randomization. Wang et al.
(
Wang et al. 2016
)
randomize search results to estimate query-level position bias and apply IPW during training. Joachims et al.
(
Joachims et al. 2017
)
show that, with correctly specified propensities, models trained on click data converge to those trained on true relevance. Ai et al.
(
Ai et al. 2018
)
and Hu et al.
(
Hu et al. 2019
)
propose learning algorithms that jointly learn the propensity model and the ranker. Vardasbi et al.
(
Vardasbi et al. 2020
)
develop a cascade model-based inverse propensity scoring method for estimating propensities under cascade click behavior. Oosterhuis et al.
(
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
)
propose a policy-aware propensity scoring method that uses a stochastic ranking policy to address selection and position bias. Luo et al.
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
show that strong position-relevance correlations can cause existing methods to overestimate propensities. They propose a causal approach that treats relevance as a confounder and use a two-step optimization for unconfounded propensity estimation. Ovaisi et al.
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
develop methods for correcting selection and position bias in LTR systems using the Heckman econometric model
(
Heckman 1979
)
, which do not require propensity estimation. However, Heckman-based approaches require linear models in both stages to satisfy their underlying assumptions.

Position-biased clicks has also been studied beyond LTR. IPW-based bandit algorithms for displaying ads in multiple positions simultaneously have been proposed by Komiyama et al.
(
Komiyama et al. 2015
)
and Lagrée et al.
(
Lagrée et al. 2016
)
. Several works apply DR estimators to position-biased clicks. Saito
(
Saito 2020
)
proposes a DR estimator for post-click conversions by modeling user behavior after a click. For policy evaluation under cascading click behavior, Kiyohara et al.
(
Kiyohara et al. 2022
)
propose a DR estimator. Yuan et al.
(
Yuan et al. 2019
)
develop a DR estimator for ad click prediction, accounting for both shown and unshown documents. Zou et al.
(
Zou et al. 2022
)
propose a unified framework for unbiased relevance estimation by combining counterfactual learning with a DR estimator. However, these methods address different problem settings than unbiased LTR. Unlike in unbiased LTR, where examination propensities are required, general DR estimators rely on action propensity corrections similar to standard counterfactual methods
(
Oosterhuis 2023
)
. Recent studies by Oosterhuis
(
Oosterhuis 2023
)
and Luo et al.
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
introduce bias correction methods for LTR using IPW in a doubly robust way. Unbiased estimation in DR methods can be achieved by combining an IPW estimate with a regression-based prediction, provided that at least one of the two models is correctly specified.

3.
Task Formulation

We first present the formal definition of LTR systems following Ai et al.
(
Ai et al. 2018
)
. Let
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}
be a universal set of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) queries, with each query
q
sampled from the distribution
P(q)
. Given a ranking model
g\in\bm{\mathcal{G}}
, let
l(g,q)
represent the loss incurred for query
q
when all associated documents are annotated. The goal of an LTR system is to find the optimal model
g
that minimizes the total empirical loss
\hat{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}(g)
over all models in
\bm{\mathcal{G}}
.

(1)

\hat{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}(g)=\frac{1}{|\bm{\mathcal{Q}}|}\sum_{q\in\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}l(g,q).

Although human-annotated labels are reliable indicators of true relevance, manually annotating every query-document pair is costly, especially with millions of documents. Instead, LTR systems often rely on implicit feedback, such as user clicks, which is easier to collect and better reflects user intent
(
Joachims 2002
)
.

A document click can be modeled based on its relevance to the query using query-document features.

(2)

C^{y}_{q}=g(\bm{x})+\epsilon^{y}_{q,g}.

C^{y}_{g}
denotes whether document
y
associated with query
q
has been clicked or not.
g()
is a ranking model that predicts click probabilities from which documents can be ranked, and it depends on the query-document features
\bm{x}
, while
\epsilon^{y}_{q,g}
represents the error term for document
y
with query
q
.
g()
can be learned from click data using equation (
2
). However, since the likelihood of clicking a document is also influenced by the document’s position in the ranking,
directly learning from clicks will embed position bias into the ranking model
g()
.

Let us consider a deterministic ranking policy
\pi
, and let
r(y|\pi_{q})
be the position of document
y
in the system’s previous ranking
\pi_{q}
presented for the query
q
. Not accounting for the document position (
r(y|\pi_{q})
) in the click equation (
2
) causes its effect to be absorbed into the error term
\epsilon^{y}_{q,g}
. However, because the document’s position depends on the features and because its unobserved influences are correlated with that of clicks, the residual error in the click model is not independent of the features, i.e.
E(\epsilon^{y}_{q,g}\mid\bm{x})\neq 0
, which violates the standard Gauss–Markov assumption for unbiased estimation
(
Amemiya 1985
)
. One could naively consider directly including the document rank parameter
r(y|\pi_{q})
as a control variable in the ranking model
g()
to account for position bias. However, both document clicks and the document rank depend on the same underlying features
\bm{x}
. Including document rank directly in
g()
as an additional feature would introduce statistical bias in the estimation because a confounder, the features, affects both document ranking and document clicks. Therefore, to achieve unbiased estimation, we require isolating the parts of document rank that are orthogonal to the features. In the following section, we model the system’s previous ranking process to extract the parts of its exogenous variation not explained by the features, in order to account for position bias in the click equation (
2
).

4.
Position Bias Correction Using Control Function

We now introduce our framework for the two-stage Control Function-based Correction (CFC) of position bias in LTR systems.
In the first stage, we model the system’s previous ranking to recover residuals that capture exogenous variation in the unobserved determinants of ranking. In the second stage, we include those residuals as a control function to correct for position bias.

4.1.
Control function for document ranking

In the first stage, we model the previous ranking process that generated the training data, where users were more likely to click on higher-ranked documents, leading to position bias in the clicks. Whether a document is relevant to a query depends on the features of the query, document, and context. These features are the observable attributes that determine the document’s position in the ranked list for that query. Therefore, we can model the previous document rankings as follows:

(3)

r(y|\pi_{q})=m(\bm{x})+\epsilon^{y}_{q,m}.

Here,
m()
is the ranking function that captures the previous ranking process in terms of document position, which depends on the features
\bm{x}
, and
\epsilon^{y}_{q,m}
, the error term for document
y
associated with query
q
. An advantage of a nonparametric control function approach is the use of any linear or non-linear model in the first stage
(
Blundell and Powell 2004
;
Das et al. 2003
)
.
This flexibility also enables us to include model selection via regularization, which leads to more stable parameter estimation.
If the previous ranker that generated the rankings in the training data is known, a similar model could be used to model
m()
.

To disentangle the endogeneity of previous rankings from that of clicks, we exploit heteroskedastic variation in the residuals of the first-stage ranking model
m()

(
Lewbel 2012
)
. This gives us internally generated exogenous variation from the ranking process, which is orthogonal to the observable features and to clicks.

Let
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}=r(y|\pi_{q})-\hat{r}(y|\pi_{q})
be the estimated residual for document
y
associated with query
q
where
\hat{r}(y|\pi_{q})
represents the predicted ranking from
m()
for document
y
, given that users are shown the ranked list
\pi_{q}
for query
q
. Since
m()
captures the effect of features on ranking, the residual
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
is orthogonal to these feature effects. Therefore,
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
represents unobserved factors beyond the features that influence the placement of document
y
in the particular position
r(y|\pi_{q})
within the ranked list
\pi_{q}
.
Examples of such factors include system constraints, biases in prior data, limited training data, or external conditions that are not captured by observable features. These unobserved factors can push more relevant documents to lower positions or less relevant ones to higher positions, which in turn affects their position and likelihood of being clicked.
Following a control function approach, we will use the estimated residuals (
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
) as a “control function” to address the latter bias in ranking each current query-document pair.

4.2.
Position bias correction in LTR model

In this second stage, we train the ranking model incorporating the estimated residuals from the first stage. The ranking model can be written as:

(4)

C^{y}_{q}=g(\bm{x},\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})+\epsilon^{y}_{q,g},

where
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
is an additional variable, serving as the control function, used alongside the features.
This accounts for any exogenous variation that influences the previous ranking process beyond the features and, in turn, affects current clicks, which the residuals capture.

There are different approaches for modeling the residuals. Most studies directly include them in the second stage but a few also show the value of using residual transformations, such as converting the first stage residuals into a likelihood score
(
Rivers and Vuong 1988
)
and estimating a kernel function of the residuals
(
Blundell and Powell 2004
)
.
Instead of deciding on a specific transformation form, we introduce residual transformation as a hyperparameter in the learning of the second stage function
g()
which has not been considered in previous work. This allows us to optimize the transformation for ranking performance using validation data.
We propose four carefully designed transformations of the residuals, each capturing a unique aspect of position bias correction, but our framework can easily incorporate others:

(1)

Min-Max normalization:
We apply min-max normalization to the residuals (
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
), and use the normalized values in the ranking model (equation (
4
)) instead of the raw residuals. This aligns with typical feature normalization and avoids disproportionate impact from the residuals.

(2)

PDF transformation (assuming normal
distribution):
We estimate the probability density function (PDF) of the residual (
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
) for each query-document pair and use the PDF in the ranking model (equation (
4
)) instead of using the residuals directly. The PDF captures how typical or unusual a residual is for a query-document pair relative to the overall residual distribution. In contrast, residuals are absolute measures and do not capture the distributional information of unobserved factors. Therefore, instead of directly using the residuals, the PDF of the residuals can be used as an additional feature. This can improve the consistency of the ranker’s estimation by leveraging distributional information that is not captured by the residuals.

(3)

Hazard ratio
transformation (assuming normal distribution):
This transformation under the normality assumption is also known as inverse Mills ratio (IMR). We transform the residual (
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
) for each query-document pair into a hazard ratio, assuming normally distributed residuals. The hazard ratio is the ratio of the PDF to the cumulative distribution function (CDF). Let
\psi^{y}_{q}
be the hazard ratio of the estimated residual
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
for document
y
associated with query
q
, then the hazard function is defined as:

(5)

\psi^{y}_{q}=\frac{f(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})}{F(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})},

where
f()
and
F()
are PDF and CDF, respectively. We use the estimated hazard ratio or IMR in the ranking model (equation (
4
)) instead of using the residuals directly. We choose IMR because it is typically used in regression models to address non-random missing data.
Documents at lower ranks are less likely to be observed and clicked, even if relevant, leading to missing relevance feedback. This missingness is not random because it depends on the position of the document, which, in turn, is influenced by previous ranking process. Consequently, the observed clicks form a biased and non-random sample, introducing position bias in the click data. The IMR accounts for the non-random missingness of documents by modeling the probability that a document is selected into a sample based on the underlying distribution of residuals. IMR has also been used in Heckman-based methods
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
to model the document selection probability for correcting selection bias, while we apply the IMR of the estimated residuals from document ranking process to correct for position bias. We provide a discussion of the differences between our method and Heckman-based methods in
Appendix
A.4
. The difference between the PDF transformation and this transformation is that the latter is scaled by the CDF.

(4)

Hazard ratio transformation:
Instead of assuming normality as in the PDF and IMR transformations, we use a more general nonparametric Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) to estimate the PDF (
f(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
) of each residual (
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}
).
The estimated PDF of the residual is used to calculate the hazard
ratio using equation (
5
) by scaling with the CDF. We then use the estimated hazard ratio in the ranking model (equation (
4
)) instead of using the residuals directly. Note that, nonparametric control function
methods
(
Blundell and Powell 2004
;
Das et al. 2003
)
do not require parametric assumptions about the distribution of residuals.

Let
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})\in\mathcal{T}
be any transformation of the estimated residuals, with
\mathcal{T}
being the set of all transformations. Now, we can rewrite the final ranking model from equation (
4
) as:

(6)

C^{y}_{q}=g(\bm{x},T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}))+\epsilon^{y}_{q,g}.

We can train the ranker
g()
with an LTR algorithm with features and transformed residuals as inputs and document clicks as target outputs. One of the main advantages of our approach is that
g()
can be any linear or non-linear pointwise, pairwise, or listwise ranking model, making it broadly applicable as bias correction with any state-of-the-art LTR method.

After learning the optimal ranking model
g()
, we can predict click probabilities for all documents with each new query and rank them accordingly.
Since the unbiased ranking model
g()
estimates the unbiased feature effects on clicks, the transformed residual
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
for each query-document pair is no longer required during inference. As a result, we can predict unbiased click probabilities solely based on features and set
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
to zero during inference.
We provide the CFC algorithm in Appendix
A.1
, along with its computational complexity, data requirements, and differences from click model-based methods in Appendix
A.2
,
A.3
, and
A.5
, respectively.

4.3.
Debiasing clicks for hyperparameter tuning

In typical machine learning, train, validation and test data are assumed to come from the same distribution (i.i.d. assumption)
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
. In unbiased LTR, however, training uses biased click data, while proper evaluation relies on unbiased test data. To tune hyperparameters for an unbiased ranking model, it is ideal to use unbiased ground truth data for validation. However, such data is rarely available in real-world scenarios and when the validation set is sampled from the training data, as is typically the case, hyperparameter tuning relies on biased click data, which is suboptimal. We would like to point out that many existing works on unbiased LTR
(
Ai et al. 2018
;
Joachims et al. 2017
;
Luo et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2023
;
Oosterhuis and de Rijke 2020
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
overlook the issue of appropriate validation in the absence of unbiased data, likely because popular benchmark datasets include unbiased validation data. To address the problem of hyperparameter tuning when the true relevance of documents to a query is unavailable for validation, which is likely to occur in real-world LTR applications, we propose a method for debiasing click data.

In equation (
6
), we model the click data as dependent on the features
\bm{x}
and the corresponding estimated transformed residual
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
.
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
accounts for the position bias of document
y
associated with query
q
. To debias the clicks from the position bias effect, we need to partial out the effect of
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
from clicks. To achieve this, we consider
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
as the only independent variable affecting clicks, and learn a function
f()
as follows:

(7)

C^{y}_{q}=f(T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m}))+\epsilon^{y}_{q,f},

where
\epsilon^{y}_{q,f}
is the error term for document
y
associated with query
q
for the function
f()
. Therefore, the effect of the features on a click is absorbed in the error term
\epsilon^{y}_{q,f}
. Using the predicted clicks from the function
f()
, we calculate the estimated residual
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,f}=C^{y}_{q}-\hat{C}^{y}_{q}
for each document
y
associated with query
q
in the validation dataset where
\hat{C}^{y}_{q}
is the predicted value from
f()
for document
y
associated with query
q
. The residual
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,f}
represents the parts of a click that is separated from the position bias effect, capturing only unbiased features’ effect on a click. Thus, when validation data with true relevance is not available, we can use
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,f}
as a proxy for true relevance during validation.

4.4.
Unbiasedness of the CFC method

In selection-type control-function models, to statistically identify the parameters of the outcome (click) equation in the presence of confounding, one typically needs a variable (an instrument) that exogenously affects the endogenous first stage (ranking) process but does not directly affect the outcome (clicks). Then, by conditioning on this variable in the first stage, one can isolate and estimate the part of the residual that is correlated with the outcome residual, leaving only the exogenous variation in the ranking variable. We then control for this estimated residual in the second stage. This breaks the covariance of the errors between the first and second stage equations:
E[\epsilon^{y}_{q,g},\epsilon^{y}_{q,m}]=0
.
However, when no external exclusion restrictions are available, one can follow Lewbel et al.
(
Lewbel 2012
)
who exploit heteroskedasticity in the error terms when applicable—i.e. if the residuals’ variance from the rank equation (equation (
3
)) varies systematically with the features—one can use this as an internal source of exogenous variation in the ranking equation.

We find that heteroskedasticity in the first-stage residuals is true in common LTR benchmark datasets (see Subsection
B.6
in Appendix), and we exploit this residual variation as our source of exogenous shocks in the ranking error. In particular, Lewbel et al.
(
Lewbel 2012
)
show that interacting each demeaned covariate with the ranking residual produces valid control-function variables: they are correlated with the endogenous ranking process but orthogonal to the click-error
(
Lewbel 2012
)
. Since our primary goal is out
-
of
-
sample predictive accuracy rather than formally unbiased parameter estimates, we instead include only the estimated first-stage residual as a single control function. This sacrifices some theoretical unbiasedness but still injects the crucial exogenous variation needed to improve predictive performance.

Datasets and evaluation metrics.
We use three widely used benchmark datasets for LTR: Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge (C14B)

(

)

We provide detailed descriptions of the three datasets in Appendix
B.1
. We evaluate all methods using Expected Reciprocal Rank (ERR) and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG), denoted as
ERR@p
and
NDCG@p
, where
p
is the rank position up to which the performance of a ranked list is evaluated.

Click simulation.
We generate click data using a two-step process following Ai et al.
(
Ai et al. 2018
)
and Joachims et al.
(
Joachims et al. 2017
)
, which is a widely adopted approach in unbiased LTR. First, we train a RankSVM
(
Joachims 2006
)
model on
1\%
of the training data using true relevance to produce initial ranked lists
\pi_{q}
for each query
q
over the remaining
99\%
training data. An initial ranker is used as it outperforms random ranking while leaving room for improvement. In the second phase, we generate synthetic clicks for each ranked list
\pi_{q}
using PBM
(
Richardson et al. 2007
)
, where a document is clicked if it is observed (
O^{y}_{q}
) and perceived as relevant (
R^{y}_{q}
). Hence, the click probability can be written as:
Pr(C^{y}_{q}=1)=Pr(O^{y}_{q}=1|\pi_{q})\ Pr(R^{y}_{q}=1|\pi_{q})
. Following Joachims et al.
(
Joachims et al. 2017
)
, the observation probability can be expressed as:
Pr(O^{y}_{q}=1|\pi_{q})={(\frac{1}{r(y|\pi_{q})})}^{\eta}
, where
\eta
controls the severity of position bias. If not explicitly mentioned, we set
\eta=1.0
, which is considered the standard position bias. According to Chapelle et al.
(
Chapelle et al. 2009
)
, the relevance probability is:
Pr(R^{y}_{q}=1|\pi_{q})=\epsilon+(1-\epsilon)\frac{2^{rel(q,y)}-1}{2^{{rel(q,y)}_{max}}-1}
, where the true relevance for document
y
with query
q
is denoted by
rel(q,y)\in[0,4]
. The maximum relevance
rel(q,y)_{max}
is
4
in all our datasets. The parameter
\epsilon
controls click noise, indicating that irrelevant documents can still have a chance of being mistakenly perceived as relevant and clicked. By default, we set
\epsilon=0.0
unless stated otherwise.
Generating clicks this way for a full pass over the training data is called one sampling pass. Following Ovaisi et al.
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
, we use 10 passes over the training dataset to generate clicks, unless stated otherwise. This method of click generation captures a typical user pattern, where some documents receive several clicks and others may not be clicked at all
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
)
. We use the clicks generated in this step for all our experiments. A detailed explanation of why online evaluation is not feasible and challenging to implement for unbiased evaluation in unbiased LTR is provided in Appendix
B.3
.

The click generation, first-stage ranking, and residual transformations, LambdaMART run on CPU, while DNN-based models are trained on GPU. We provide additional implementation details in Appendix
B.4

B.5

The effect of model choice in estimating the previous ranking process (RQ7).

For question RQ7, we investigate how the choice of machine learning model used to train
m()
—from which the residuals are estimated and included in the second stage—affects the ranking performance of the CFC method. We experiment with four machine learning models to train
m()
: Ridge, Logistic Regression, SVM, and XGBoost, and incorporate the resulting residuals into

. The performance of the CFC method with LambdaMART using each of these models for learning

This may be because the linear structure of Ridge Regression, along with its ability to handle multicollinearity through regularization, is well-suited for modeling residual variation within the control function framework on these

(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
, which require ordinary least squares (OLS) in the first stage to satisfy their statistical assumptions, our control function-based method imposes no such restrictions and allows both linear and non-linear machine learning models in the first stage. Thus, unlike the Heckman-based methods, the CFC method is not tightly coupled with any specific type of learning model in its first stage.

6.
Discussion

The experimental results highlight several key takeaways. First, the CFC method outperforms various baselines across the three benchmark datasets under both true relevance and biased click validation settings. Second, it corrects for position bias with both LambdaMART and DNN rankers, highlighting its flexibility and general applicability. Third, CFC demonstrates robustness under varying levels of position bias severity, sparse click data, and noise. Fourth, its performance improves with more click data, reflecting a favorable bias-variance tradeoff. Finally, CFC maintains comparable performance even with limited annotated validation data and is agnostic to the choice of residual estimation model. While we expected to see better results with kernel-based transformations of the residuals, future work could explore better tuning of these transformations, as well as other parametric and nonparametric transformation techniques.

Our work has broader societal implications. When recommender systems are trained on biased historical data, they risk perpetuating unfair outcomes, particularly for underrepresented users or content
(
Bojic 2024
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
. While our work is a step towards promoting more equitable outcomes, it does not put any guardrails on how this system is used in practice. It is very important that practitioners mitigate any possible negative effects on society by deploying recommender systems responsibly, with careful attention to fairness, privacy, and broader principles of trustworthiness
(
Fan et al. 2023
)
.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce a new control function-based method to correct for position bias in LTR systems. By incorporating the residual variation—which captures the unobserved ranking factors responsible for position bias—from the previous ranking process into current click model, we correct for that bias.
Our method requires no propensity estimation or click model assumptions. It also avoids modifications to the ranker’s loss function, making it applicable for bias correction with any state-of-the-art LTR method. Another innovation of this study is that we also propose a residual-based click debiasing approach for hyperparameter tuning in the absence of annotated validation data. Our evaluation shows that CFC outperforms state-of-the-art methods, while also being robust to varying levels of position bias severity and noisy clicks. The CFC method is flexible and can be extended to incorporate more biases encountered in LTR systems, such as selection bias and trust bias.

The CFC method involves training the previous ranking process

, making it linear. The runtime for training the ranking process
m()
and the current ranking model
g()
depends on the specific algorithm used. Using efficient algorithms (e.g., Ridge, LambdaMART) for both can keep the overall runtime manageable, making it suitable for large-scale applications.

A.3.
Data requirements of our method

Regarding the data requirements of our method, in addition to clicks
(C^{y}_{q})
and feature vectors
\bm{x}
for query-document pairs, our method requires the document rankings (
r(y|\pi_{q})
) that were presented to users and from which the current click data were generated. These rankings are readily available, as users are presented with ranked lists for each query, from which document positions for queries can be extracted. The document rankings are required in the first stage to model the previous ranking process
m()
. Importantly, our method does not require randomized exposures, estimated propensity scores, user browsing assumptions, or linearity assumptions, making it more practical and compatible with existing logged data.

A.4.
Difference with Heckman method

Although the Heckman method is a specific subset of control function techniques, its assumptions and applications differ significantly from other approaches within this family, as do many of the econometric techniques that fall within the control function umbrella.
While we acknowledge works
(
Ovaisi et al. 2020
;
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
for their contributions to Heckman-based bias corrections, our methodology extends beyond these models. The assumptions needed for unbiased estimation of feature parameters within a general control function approach are less stringent than for the specific sub-case of Heckman-based methods. The Heckman model relies on linearity assumptions in both stages for unbiased estimation of feature parameters, which makes it highly unsuitable for LTR tasks that involve high-dimensional, non-linear feature interactions, and highly sparse clicks. On the other hand, our control function approach, which leverages the residual variation from the modeled document ranking process, does not require linearity assumptions in either stage and can handle multicollinearity in high-dimensional feature spaces by selecting flexible models with regularization. Moreover, Heckman-based methods use the same features
\bm{x}
in both stages and estimate the IMR of the first-stage predictions based on features to include it as an additional variable in the second stage. This contributes to the already high likelihood of multicollinearity in a high-dimensional feature space in the second stage. Our CFC method avoids this issue by using residuals, which are orthogonal to the features, thereby evading this multicollinearity.

A.5.
Difference with click models

Basic click models
(
Chapelle and Zhang 2009
;
Craswell et al. 2008
;
Mao et al. 2018
)
typically aim to separate the effects of document position and document relevance (feature effects) and use the relevance component for an unbiased ranking model. Like click models, we can also separate the unbiased feature effects. In equation (
6
), we model the click data as dependent on the features and the transformed residual. To separate the feature effects on clicks, we need to partial out the effect of the transformed residual from clicks. To achieve this, we can consider
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
as the only independent variable affecting clicks to learn a model. The estimated residual from this model will capture the feature effects, which can be used as document relevance. However, extracting document relevance by separating the effect of
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
from clicks or directly incorporating
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
into the ranking model to obtain an unbiased ranking model is statistically equivalent. Since separating the effect of
T(\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,m})
requires training an additional model, we directly use equation (
6
) to obtain the unbiased ranking model
g()
.

B.2.
Baselines

We compare our CFC method with baseline methods commonly used for correcting position bias in LTR systems.

•

UPE:
The Unconfounded Propensity Estimation
(
Luo et al. 2024
)
method corrects position bias with a causal method using document relevance as a confounder and addresses propensity overestimation.

•

MULTR:
The Model-based Unbiased Learning to Rank
(
Luo et al. 2023
)
method uses a context-aware simulator to generate pseudo clicks and addresses the discrepancy between pseudo and actual clicks through IPW with pseudo labels in a doubly robust manner.

•

PIJD:
The Propensity-Independent Joint Debiasing
(
Ovaisi et al. 2021
)
corrects for both position and selection bias using Heckman’s method.

•

PairD:
The Pairwise Debiasing
(
Hu et al. 2019
)
model extends the IPW that jointly estimates position bias and trains a pairwise ranker.

•

PAL:
The Position-bias Aware Learning
(
Guo et al. 2019
)
framework uses a two-tower model to disentangle relevance and bias factors.

•

REM:
The regression-based EM
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
model uses a position bias click model to estimate propensity scores and
trains a ranker.

•

DLA:
The Dual Learning Algorithm
(
Ai et al. 2018
)
jointly learns an unbiased propensity model and unbiased ranker from biased click data.

•

LambdaMART:
Using LambdaMART
(
Burges 2010
)
as the LTR algorithm, we train a model directly on biased click data without any position bias correction. This serves as a baseline benchmark to assess whether our CFC method, which also uses the LambdaMART LTR algorithm as its ranker, can outperform a LambdaMART algorithm that does not correct for position bias.

•

DNN:
We train a Deep Neural Network model directly on biased click data, following the implementation in
(
Ai et al. 2018
)
, without applying any position bias correction. This baseline allows us to evaluate whether our CFC method, which also uses a DNN ranker, can surpass this method that does not account for position bias.

•

Oracle:
This model is trained using human-annotated labels. Because it relies on the true relevance of each query-document pair, its performance represents the upper bound of the ranker.

B.3.
Difficulty of online evaluation

Online evaluation, though often expected, is costly, hard to implement in academic settings, and requires real-time user participation in an existing LTR system. Moreover, in unbiased LTR, unbiased evaluation of methods requires unbiased ground truth or true relevance of documents, which is typically not possible to obtain in real-world data. As a result, unbiased LTR methods depend on benchmark datasets for unbiased evaluation, where true relevance is provided. Following state-of-the-art approaches, we evaluate our method using benchmark datasets and synthetic click data for all experiments. Using the synthetic click generation process, we can simulate clicks with varying experimental conditions that mimic real-world scenarios and user behaviors, including different degrees of position bias, numbers of clicks, and levels of click noise. By doing so, we evaluate the performance and robustness of our method across various scenarios, ensuring that the evaluation aligns with real-world challenges rather than favoring our approach.

B.4.
More implementation details

In the first stage of our method, as formulated in equation (
3
), we train
m()
using a supervised machine learning algorithm. For learning
m()
, we explore various machine learning models, such as Ridge, Logistic Regression, SVM, and XGBoost. While all four classifiers used to train
m()
yield comparable ranking performance on
g()
when residuals from
m()
are incorporated into the ranker, we observe that the Ridge classifier generally leads to better ranking performance on
g()
across all three datasets. Therefore, we report all results using Ridge to learn
m()
in the first stage and incorporate the residuals from this
m()
into
g()
. The impact of using different machine learning models to estimate
m()
is discussed in detail in RQ7. For nonparametric hazard ratio transformation of the first-stage residuals, we use fastKDE
(
O’Brien et al. 2016
)
to estimate the PDF.

For question RQ1, we obtain debiased clicks for hyperparameter tuning and learn the function
f()
with Ridge Regression. We train
f()
using training data and predict on validation data. Next, we calculate the debiased clicks (
\hat{\epsilon}^{y}_{q,f}
) for validation data using the predictions from
f()
. We use these debiased clicks for hyperparameter tuning when we assume that true relevance is unavailable for validation. As previously described, We define two CFC variants based on how we use clicks for hyperparameter tuning: CFC-DC, where the CFC method uses debiased clicks in hyperparameter tuning, and CFC-BC, where it uses biased clicks directly for hyperparameter tuning. For questions RQ2-RQ7, we use the true relevance of the validation dataset for hyperparameter tuning for both our method and the baselines.

B.5.
Training parameters

To facilitate reproducibility, we outline the training parameters for our method. For the LambdaMART ranker, we use the LightGBM
(
Ke et al. 2017
)
implementation, which is a pairwise LTR method. We choose
NDCG
as the optimization metric while setting the minimum number of data points in a leaf node to
2
, and the maximum number of tree leaves to
255
. We keep the number of boosted trees and the learning rate as hyperparameters. The number of boosted trees is tuned between
100
and
500
, while the learning rate
\alpha
is tuned between
0.05
and
0.1
. We leave all other parameters of LambdaMART at their default settings. For DNN-based ranker, we follow the implementation by Ai et al.
(
Ai et al. 2018
)
and integrate DNN with our CFC method. We train the model using a batch size of
256
for
10
k steps, using the AdaGrad optimizer
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
. The neural network architecture consists of three hidden layers with sizes [
512
,
256
,
128
], each using the ELU activation function
(
Clevert et al. 2016
)
. We use softmax cross-entropy loss function, which is a listwise objective function. Additionally, as discussed in section
4.2
, we have a hyperparameter for the transformations of the estimated residuals used in the ranker. We tune this hyperparameter on validation data to select the best ranking model
g()
, whether using LambdaMART or DNN as the ranker.

B.6.
Heteroskedasticity test on first-stage residuals

To validate the presence of heteroskedasticity in the first-stage residuals, we conduct nonparametric variance tests on the Yahoo, MSLR-WEB10K, and Istella-S datasets. Specifically, we use the Fligner–Killeen test
(
Fligner and Killeen 1976
)
, a nonparametric test that does not assume any specific distribution. Our results show statistically significant test statistics with
\bm{p}
-values very close to zero (approximately in the

10^{-6}\sim 10^{-5}
ranges) across all three datasets. Using a significance threshold of
\bm{p}<0.05
, this provides strong evidence of heteroskedasticity in the first-stage residuals of these three benchmark LTR datasets. This finding supports the use of heteroskedastic residuals as a valid source of exogenous variation to correct for position bias in the second stage of our control function framework.
