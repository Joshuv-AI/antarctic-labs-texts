Structural basis for RNA synthesis and inhibition of the orthobunyavirus polymerase
Authors: Jingjing Tang, Wenhua Kuang, Wei Chen, Hao Wu, Yong Wang, Jinyue Li, Zhiming Yuan, Han Xia, Haiyan Zhao, Zengqin Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrthobunyaviruses are segmented negative-sense RNA viruses that encompass several life-threatening human and animal pathogens. However, no licensed antivirals are currently available. The viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) is a multi-domain enzyme critical for genome replication and transcription, representing a promising target for antiviral drug development. Here, we establish robust in vitro enzymatic activity assays for the Ebinur Lake virus (EBIV) polymerase and identify suramin, a century-old drug, as an inhibitor of EBIV polymerase. We further determine cryo-EM structures of the EBIV polymerase in apo, elongation, and suramin-bound states. These structures reveal conformational rearrangements of the polymerase during RNA synthesis, including conformational transitions of the prime-and-realign (PR) loop and a unique β-hairpin that bridges the zinc-binding domain (ZBD) to the RdRp core. The structural observations are correlated with in vitro enzymatic activity and cell-based minireplicon assays. The suramin-bound structure reveals two distinct inhibitory binding sites. One site sterically clashes with the vRNA promoter. The other site directly blocks RNA template strand binding, thereby inhibiting polymerase activity. These findings advance our understanding of orthobunyavirus RNA synthesis mechanisms and offer a structural framework for the rational design and optimization of antiviral drugs.Subject terms: Virus structures, Cryoelectron microscopy, Transcription

Introduction

Bunyaviruses are a large group of segmented negative-sense single-stranded RNA viruses (sNSVs) classified into two viral orders and 16 families1. Among them, the genus Orthobunyavirus encompasses at least 120 viral species capable of infecting a broad spectrum of vertebrate and invertebrate hosts, such as La Crosse virus (LACV), Schmallenberg virus (SBV), and Oropouche virus (OROV)2,3. Despite their significant public health impact, no approved antivirals are currently available against orthobunyavirus infections. The urgency of addressing this unmet medical need is further underscored by recent outbreaks of orthobunyavirus infections, such as the OROV epidemic in the Americas, which had resulted in 9852 confirmed cases by the end of August 20244. Ebinur Lake virus (EBIV) is an orthobunyavirus that was first isolated from Culex mosquitoes in the Ebinur Lake region of northwestern China and has subsequently been detected in bird brain tissues, mosquitoes, and tick pools in Russia5,6. EBIV exhibits high pathogenicity in mice, with inoculation of even one plaque-forming unit (PFU) being lethal7. Moreover, the detection of positive antibody responses to EBIV in human serological surveys implies that it is a potential human pathogen, raising concerns about its threat to human health8.

Orthobunyaviruses possess tri-segmented negative-sense genomes, including large (L), medium (M), and small (S) segments. The L segment encodes the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp, also known as L protein), a multifunctional enzyme essential for viral genome transcription and replication. It contains two functionally conserved enzymatic domains, an N-terminal endonuclease (EN) domain and a central RdRp domain. The RdRp domain and the surrounding domains form the RdRp core to mediate RNA synthesis, while the EN domain, together with a cap-binding domain (CBD) at the C-terminus of L protein, steals host mRNA caps to initiate transcription of the viral genomes via the cap-snatching mechanism9. A characteristic zinc-binding domain (ZBD) is present at the extreme C-terminal region, which is absent in other sNSV polymerases of known structure; however, its precise functional role remains unclear10. Akin to the initiation of influenza virus vRNA synthesis from cRNA, several bunyaviruses also initiate genome replication through a prime-and-realign mechanism10–15. In this process, RNA synthesis initiates internally to produce a short primer, which subsequently realigns to the 3’ terminus before elongation proceeds15. Structural analyses of LACV L protein have provided insights into this mechanism, highlighting the role of a prime-and-realign loop (PR loop) that stabilizes the 3’-vRNA within the active site in the initiation state15. However, the structure-function relevance of this loop and whether this mechanism is conserved among orthobunyaviruses remains to be fully elucidated.

The viral polymerase represents a promising target for antiviral development due to its essential role in viral genome replication and transcription. Broad-spectrum antiviral drugs, such as the viral RNA polymerase inhibitors remdesivir and favipiravir, have shown antiviral activity against diverse RNA viruses16,17. However, systematic evaluation of inhibitors against orthobunyaviruse polymerases remains limited. Given the increasing risk of orthobunyavirus outbreaks and the absence of approved antivirals, there is an urgent need to develop safe and effective antivirals for orthobunyaviruses. This goal may be achieved through novel drug discovery or by repurposing existing compounds. EBIV has a well-established mouse infection model and a robust reverse genetics system7,18, making it a convenient model system for studying orthobunyavirus infections and for developing broad-spectrum antiviral drugs against high-risk members of this genus.

In this work, we focus on the EBIV L protein. We successfully purify the full-length EBIV L protein, establish in vitro RNA synthesis assays, and identify suramin as an inhibitor of EBIV L protein. Furthermore, we determine cryo-EM structures of EBIVL protein in three distinct states: apo, elongation, and suramin-bound states. These structures reveal conformational changes in the PR loop and highlight a critical β-hairpin that bridges the RdRp core and the ZBD, both of which are essential for polymerase function. Suramin inhibits EBIV L by disrupting vRNA promoter binding and occupying the template-product duplex binding site during elongation. Collectively, our findings provide enriched mechanistic insights into orthobunyavirus replication and establish a structural framework for antiviral drug development and optimization targeting the orthobunyavirus polymerase.

Results

Purification and characterization of the full-length EBIV L protein

The Bac-to-Bac baculovirus system facilitates proper protein folding and post-translational modifications, making it ideal for expressing large viral proteins such as the L protein of bunyaviruses. However, its high cost and time-intensive process remain significant limitations. By contrast, the Pichia pastoris expression system offers several advantages, including low cost, ease of scale-up, and stable strain storage, making it an efficient alternative19,20. Notably, the Pichia pastoris expression system has been successfully employed to produce the full-length functional L proteins from bunyaviruses such as Lassa virus (LASV) and Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV)21,22. In this study, we also utilized Pichia pastoris to express the full-length L protein of EBIV, achieving high yields while preserving its structural and enzymatic integrity (Fig. 1a, b). Given the strong endonuclease activity of the EBIV L EN domain23, alanine substitutions at the catalytic residues (D79A and D92A) were introduced to abolish this activity in vitro, thereby preventing nonspecific RNA degradation. Unless otherwise indicated, all proteins used in enzymatic activity assays carry these mutations.

To assess the RNA synthesis activity of the purified EBIV L protein, we designed a 23-nt RNA template devoid of secondary structure to facilitate productive engagement with EBIV L protein. A 4-nt primer was annealed to the template to initiate replication; a similar strategy has been applied to LASV polymerase22. In reactions containing only ATP, a 9-nt product was expected; however, a mismatched 10-nt product was also observed (Fig. 1b, Lane 3). When supplied with ATP and UTP, the EBIV L protein efficiently generated a major 13-nt RNA product (Fig. 1b, Lane 4). Together, these results demonstrate that the recombinant EBIV L protein is catalytically active and suitable for further structural study and inhibitor screening.

Overall structure of the EBIV L protein

To determine the cryo-EM structure of EBIV L, we prepared cryo-EM samples by incubating the purified EBIV L protein with the template that annealed to the 4-nt primer in the presence of ATP, UTP, and Mn²⁺ at 30 °C for 30 min. Data were collected using a CRYO ARM 300 electron microscope equipped with a K3 direct electron detector, and 10,454 movies were collected and processed in cryoSPARC24. After data processing, we obtained two reconstructions, the EBIV L apo state at 3.55 Å and the EBIV L-RNA complex at 3.32 Å (Supplementary Fig. 1). Both structures contain all domains of EBIV L, though certain peripheral regions, such as the ZBD, display weak density (Fig. 1c–e). Given the higher resolution of the EBIV L-RNA complex, we chose this structure for the detailed description.

The overall domain organization and architecture of the EBIV L protein closely resemble those of other reported bunyavirus polymerases (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The RdRp core forms a compact architecture, with the RdRp domain encircled by the core lobe, bridge, ring, and lid domains (Fig. 1d, e). Like other sNSV polymerases, the RdRp domain features a canonical right-hand configuration, comprising fingers, palm, and thumb subdomains (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The C-terminal region, comprising the mid, CBD, and ZBD domains, protrudes outward from the RdRp core. The N-terminal EN and C-terminal CBD domains are positioned close to each other, directly beneath the RdRp core. Notably, a prominent cavity exists between the EN and CBD, enabling their conformational rearrangements during replication and transcription.

Interaction between EBIV L and RNA

Cryo-EM structure of the EBIV L-RNA complex reveals the polymerase in an active elongation state. Upon the addition of ATP and UTP, nine nucleotides are incorporated into the 4-nt primer, with a 10-base-pair duplex (position −9 to +1) observed within the inner cavity of EBIV L protein. The elongation complex adopts a pre-translocated state, with the +1 site occupied by the terminal nucleotide U13 (Fig. 2). Within the inner cavity, the template and product strands are coordinated by conserved motifs A-G of the RdRp domain (Fig. 2a). In addition to these canonical motifs, positively charged and polar residues from the fingers subdomain, lid, and bridge domains engage in interactions with the phosphate backbone and 2′-hydroxyl group of the RNA duplex. Residues D1164 and D1165 in motif C serve as the catalytic residues, while K951 and R959 from motif F flank the proximal end of the product strand to stabilize the RNA (Fig. 2b, c). A sharp kink of the upstream template strand happens between positions +1 and +2, where the template enters, and the nucleotide U8-C10 is accommodated within the entry channel. The distal nucleotide U8 is stabilized by residue K760, located at the junction of the core lobe domain and the fingers subdomain, which forms a hydrogen bond with the base of U8 (Fig. 2b, c). This interaction stabilizes and correctly orients the 5′ end of the template strand. A similar interaction is also observed in the LASV L protein, where the counterpart residue R716 binds to the distal template nucleotide25.

To understand the conformational transitions associated with RNA synthesis, we compared the apo state and the elongation complex structures. Structural superimposition reveals considerable conformational rearrangement of the fingers, ring, and lid domains (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the elongation state, the ring and lid domains shift outward to widen the inner cavity (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d), thereby allowing the accommodation of the RNA duplex. Notably, residue Y1672 within the lid domain engages in π-π stacking with the base of the terminal nucleotide of the product strand (Supplementary Fig. 3c), thereby forcing strand-separation of the RNA duplex, reminiscent of the counterpart residue Y1696 in LACV polymerase10. Furthermore, motif F in the fingers subdomain becomes ordered in the elongation state (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The PR loop also undergoes a significant conformational movement during the transition from the apo to the elongation state, which will be discussed in detail below.

Conformational change of the PR loop during RNA synthesis

The PR loop in the L protein of LACV plays a critical role in initiating RNA synthesis, which adopts two functional conformations, a retracted and an extended state15. However, it remains unclear whether the PR loop serves a similar function in other orthobunyaviruses. To understand the conformational dynamics of the PR loop (residues 983-996) of EBIV L protein, we investigated its conformational changes in different functional states. In the elongation state, the PR loop is extracted away from the active site, adopting a configuration similar to that observed in the LACV elongation state. By contrast, in the apo state, the PR loop exhibits a distinct conformation which is not presented in the reported LACV polymerase structures15.

Specifically, the E981-L985 segment in the EBIV L apo state forms part of a long α-helix, in contrast to a flexible loop observed in the LACV initiation state (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Figs. 4; 5a, b). This helix-to-loop transition spatially permits the PR loop to extend toward the active site, a rearrangement likely induced and stabilized by RNA binding during the initiation state. Furthermore, a cluster of negatively charged residues projects into the solvent, with D988 engaging in an electrostatic interaction network with nearby positively charged residues K1004 and K1005 from the palm subdomain. However, this interaction is disrupted in the elongation state, where D988 moves away from the nearby positively charged residues K1004 and K1005 (Fig. 3a). In addition, residues M990-E993 adopt a more relaxed conformation in the apo structure, while they resemble a curled telephone wire in the elongation state. Structural superimposition of the two states reveals that the PR loop in the apo structure is positioned farther away from the active site than in the elongation state, where it retracts and curls out of the active site (Fig. 3a). This relaxed conformation does not correspond to either the initiation or elongation stage, potentially representing an inactive state.

To investigate the functional contribution of residues within the PR loop of EBIV L, we established an in vitro de novo initiation of RNA synthesis assay in the presence of 5′-vRNA and 3′-vRNA. Notably, a product substantially larger than expected was observed, presumably because the RNA template and its fully complementary product form highly stable RNA duplexes. Six of the seven tested mutants reduced de novo initiation activity, with mutants D988A, E989A, and I991A exhibiting the most severe effects (Fig. 3b). By contrast, these mutants had only a limited impact on primer-dependent extension activity (Fig. 3c).

To further corroborate the structural findings and enzymatic assay results, we evaluated the function of PR loop mutants using a cell-based EBIV minireplicon system. An S RNA analog containing the S-segment untranslated regions (UTRs) and a negative-sense Nanoluciferase reporter gene was subcloned into a vector under the control of a T7 promoter, enabling the generation of a viral genome-like RNA segment. Following co-transfection of this construct with NP and L expression plasmids driven by the CAG promoter, NP recognizes the RNA UTRs and assembles with the minigenome to form functional ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. Subsequent replication and transcription mediated by the L protein result in reporter gene expression. All mutants markedly reduced Nanoluciferase expression, with the E989A, I991A, and S992A substitutions almost abolishing reporter activity (Fig. 3d). Consistently, the RNA levels in these mutants are significantly lower than those in the wild-type (Fig. 3e, f). These results demonstrate that, in addition to the tip residues M990, I991, and S992, which are directly involved in stabilizing the 3′-vRNA distal nucleotides as observed in the LACV initiation state15, residues N986, E987, D988, and E989 also contribute to the functional integrity of the PR loop. Collectively, these findings reveal a previously unresolved conformation of the PR loop and highlight its conformational dynamics as well as its functional significance in the EBIV polymerase.

A β-hairpin of ZBD is important for the polymerase activity

The ZBD (residues 2000-2238), located at the extreme C-terminal region of the EBIV L protein, protrudes from the structure and is a structurally conserved domain among orthobunyaviruses (Fig. 4a). It consists of a set of α-helices with a long protruding β-hairpin. This domain is characterized by its ability to coordinate zinc ions through a specific arrangement of cysteine, histidine, and aspartate residues10. In the EBIV L elongation complex, the β-hairpin within ZBD inserts into the RdRp core, establishing stable interactions with the palm subdomain and core lobe domain (Fig. 4a, b). Structural studies of LACV L have revealed that the C-terminal region, including the mid domain, CBD, and ZBD, undergoes an approximately 20° rotation from the pre-initiation to the putative endonuclease cleavage state15. Notably, the analogous β-hairpin of LACV L remains structurally fixed during this conformational transition, anchored by interactions with the RdRp core, suggesting that the C-terminal region rotates around the β-hairpin, which works as a pivotal hinge. However, the functional significance of the ZBD-RdRp core interaction remains unexplored. We employed both in vitro de novo initiation RNA synthesis and primer-dependent extension assays and a cell-based EBIV minireplicon assay to explore this. To specifically disrupt the interface between the ZBD and the RdRp core, we replaced residues 2060-2080 of the β-hairpin with a flexible GSSG linker, which we named Δβ-hairpin mutant. Following expression and purification under the same conditions as the wild-type EBIV L, gel filtration and SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that this replacement did not affect the overall folding or stability of EBIV L (Supplementary Fig. 6). Both in vitro and cell-based assays revealed that the Δβ-hairpin mutation markedly reduced polymerase activity (Fig. 4c–g). These results underscore the critical role of the ZBD-RdRp core interaction in sustaining polymerase activity, likely acting as a structural anchor that stabilizes the polymerase core during RNA synthesis.

Suramin inhibits the polymerase activity of EBIV L

Suramin, a century-old antiparasitic drug approved for African trypanosomiasis (Supplementary Fig. 7a), has recently gained attention for its broad-spectrum antiviral activity against RNA viruses, including Zika, dengue, Murine Norovirus, Nipah, EBOV, and SARS-CoV-226–31. Here, we demonstrate that suramin also effectively inhibits the polymerase activity of EBIV L both in vitro and in cell-based assays. To assess its impact on RNA synthesis, increasing concentrations of suramin were added to a primer-dependent extension reaction. The results revealed a dose-dependent inhibition of polymerase activity, with 40 μM suramin nearly abolishing polymerase activity in vitro (Fig. 5a). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was determined to be 18.75 μM (Fig. 5b). To evaluate the antiviral efficacy of suramin at the cellular level, BHK-21 cells were infected with a recombinant virus with an eGFP reporter (rEBIV/eGFP/S)18 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 in the presence of serially diluted suramin, and eGFP expression was assessed at 24 h post-infection (hpi). The EC₅₀ of suramin was calculated to be 33.06 μM, indicating its capability of inhibiting EBIV infection in BHK-21 cells (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Notably, suramin exhibited no detectable cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 1 mM (Supplementary Fig. 7d). To determine which stage of infection is sensitive to suramin treatment, a time-of-addition assay was conducted (Supplementary Fig. 7e, f). BHK-21 cells infected with rEBIV/eGFP/S were treated with 300 μM suramin at various time points: 1 h before infection (−1 h), at the time of infection (0 h), or 1, 2, 4, 6, or 8 hpi, and suramin was maintained until 12 hpi except the −1 and 0 h settings. Pre-treatment or co-treatment (−1 h and 0 h) resulted in strong suppression of eGFP expression, with ~30% or nearly no eGFP expression, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7f). Treatment at 1 and 2 h after infection achieved approximately a 40% reduction compared to the non-treated control, whereas treatment at later time points had minimal effect (Supplementary Fig. 7f). These findings indicate that suramin impairs EBIV infection by interfering with both viral entry and replication.

Mechanism of suramin-mediated inhibition of EBIV L

To elucidate the mechanism of suramin-mediated inhibition of EBIV L, we determined the cryo-EM structure of EBIV L-suramin complex at 3.0 Å resolution (Supplementary Fig. 8). The structure reveals two bound suramin molecules at distinct positions (Fig. 5c). Suramin is a symmetrical divalent polysulfonated molecule. In each bound suramin, only one half of the molecule is well resolved, comprising the naphthalene-trisulfonic acid head, two benzene rings, and a urea linker, along with one benzene ring from the other half of suramin. The rest of the other half is poorly defined in the cryo-EM density map. One suramin molecule (referred to as suramin no.1) occupies a pocket of the vRBL domain where it is typically involved in vRNA promoter binding (Fig. 5d). This molecule is stabilized via a hydrogen bond of the sulfonic acid group at position 5 with the side chains of residues N403 and N528. Additionally, residue R411 interacts with the amide bond linker between the naphthalene-trisulfonic acid head and the benzene ring. The second molecule (suramin no.2) spans across the thumb subdomain and the ring domain, with two sulfonic acid groups oriented towards a loop in the ring domain (Fig. 5e). Suramin no.2 is stabilized through interactions with the side chain of residues Q1257 and K1261 in the thumb subdomain and D1556, H1680, K1681, and K1683 in the ring domain. We generated site-specific mutants by introducing combinatorial mutations at key residues involved in each binding site. Microscale thermophoresis (MST) analysis using GFP-tagged EBIV L protein confirmed that the wild-type (WT) EBIV L protein exhibited a high binding affinity to suramin, with a dissociation constant of Kd = 233 nM (Supplementary Fig. 9). Mutations at either binding site resulted in moderate decreases in affinity, with Kd values of 810 nM (site 1 mutation) and 3.021 μM (site 2 mutation), respectively. Notably, the site 2 mutation exerted a greater impact on suramin binding than the site 1 mutation, suggesting a more prominent contribution of site 2 to the overall binding affinity. As expected, the double-site mutant showed a dramatic decrease in binding, with a Kd of 157 μM, a 673-fold increase compared to the WT protein (Supplementary Fig. 9). These results demonstrated that a single site mutation is not sufficient to abolish suramin binding, whereas suramin binding at site 2 is stronger than at site 1.

Comparative structural analysis of the EBIV L-suramin complex with EBIV L elongation state and LACV L initiation state provides insight into the inhibitory mechanism of suramin (Fig. 6a, b). Suramin no.1 occupies the binding site of the 5′/3′-vRNA promoter (Fig. 6c), with its urea linker sterically clashing with the kink between U11 and A12 of the 5′-vRNA promoter. This kink separates nucleotides complementary to the 3′-vRNA from those forming the stem-loop structure of 5′-vRNA, which is important for viral genome replication. Suramin no.2, positioned near the thumb subdomain, occupies the position of the template strand, and one of its sulfonic acid groups points towards the product strand in the EBIV L elongation state (Fig. 6d). Collectively, suramin inhibits EBIV L activity by preventing viral RNA promoter binding and destabilizing the proper positioning of the template and product strands during elongation.

Discussion

The structural and biochemical data presented here provide insights into how the EBIV L protein performs RNA synthesis. In the elongation state, the polymerase accommodates a 10-base-pair template-product duplex in the active site cavity, along with three additional upstream nucleotides ( + 2 to +4) on the template entry tunnel. The RNA duplex interacts with multiple residues lining the cavity through polar interactions. Specifically, residue K760, positioned within the template entry tunnel, contacts the terminal 5’ nucleotide of the template, suggesting a role in guiding and anchoring the incoming template strand during RNA synthesis. The split of the template-product duplex is mediated by the lid domain, where Y1672 forms a π-π stacking interaction with the terminal base of the product strand (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Collectively, these structural features support an active catalytic polymerase and provide a mechanistic framework for the RNA synthesis of orthobunyavirus.

Structural comparison between the elongation complex of EBIV L and the LACV L protein reveals a high degree of structural similarity. While the RdRp core adopts a conserved architecture, a significant difference is observed in the conformation of the EN domain (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Notably, the CBD domain in EBIV L is positioned adjacent to the capped RNA entrance tunnel, while the EN domain is located away from this tunnel, resulting in an open conformation competent for capped RNA engagement. This configuration closely resembles the transcription initiation state of LACV rather than its replication state15. These observations suggest that the transcription-like conformation observed during replication may facilitate a smooth transition between functional states of the L protein. However, without structural resolution of the transcription state of EBIV L, definitive conclusions regarding its switching mechanism cannot yet be drawn.

Structural analysis of LACV L has revealed that the PR loop is located in the palm subdomain, flanked by two long α-helices, and adjacent to the catalytic motif C15. Key residues (M989, I990, and S991) of LACV L were shown to extend to the active site during initiation, interacting with the proximal of the 3′-vRNA and stabilizing the initiation complex. Upon transition to elongation, the PR loop retracts from the active chamber, allowing product strand synthesis to proceed. A comprehensive analysis of the structures and sequences of PR loops from multiple bunyaviruses and influenza viruses revealed that, in all determined structures except for LACV, the PR loop is observed in a retracted conformation15. Whether the extended conformation observed during initiation in the LACV structure also occurs in other sNSVs remains unknown. At the sequence level, the PR loop is not conserved among bunyaviruses or influenza orthomyxoviruses15. Additionally, there is currently no direct evidence that the PR loops of other bunyaviruses actively participate in the prime-and-realign process, as observed in LACV. In EBIV L, we identified two conformations of the PR loop. Its conformation in the elongation state closely resembles the retracted loop observed in elongation states of LACV, HNTV, and SFTSV (Supplementary Fig. 5). By contrast, it adopts a markedly different conformation in the apo state, characterized by a stabilized structure anchored by an electrostatic interaction. This interaction might maintain the loop in an inactive conformation. Given that polymerase activation typically requires binding of the 5′/3′-vRNA promoter32,33, we speculate that promoter engagement may trigger the PR loop activation and reposition it into the active site.

The C-terminal region of sNSVs is highly variable in both sequence and length, exhibiting diverse structural characteristics. Typically, the C-terminal region comprises a bipartite mid-link, a CBD, and a distal domain. The mid-link connects the RdRp core to the C-terminal region and functions as a pivot point during conformational rearrangements. The CBD is relatively conserved across sNSVs, serving to bind host-derived capped RNA. By contrast, the distal domain shows the greatest diversity among sNSVs polymerase, and little is known about their function. For the influenza virus polymerase, the C-terminal 627 domain is critical for host specificity and dimerization, followed by a small nuclear localization signal (NLS) domain essential for nuclear localization34,35. SFTSV L protein features a lariat domain with a large circular structure that packs against the RdRp core36. HTNV L protein displays significantly high flexibility in its distal domain. Structural studies of HNTV polymerase hexamers reveal that the C-terminal region adopts distinct orientations and positions in different protomers35. In EBIV L, the ZBD domain is anchored to the RdRp core via a β-hairpin structure, restricting its flexibility. Deletion of the β-hairpin severely impairs polymerase activity, highlighting its importance for stabilizing the ZBD-RdRp interface. This contrasts with that of other polymerases, such as HNTV, whose flexible distal domains undergo large-scale rearrangements upon dimerization or oligomerization35. The structural constraint imposed by the ZBD in orthobunyaviruses thus represents a unique feature, which may limit the dimerization or higher-order assembly of EBIV polymerase.

Suramin has been shown to target the polymerases of many RNA viruses, with its binding sites revealed in the structures of several viral polymerases (Supplementary Fig. 10). In the SARS-CoV-2 polymerase, suramin binds to two distinct sites within the active center30. The binding of these two suramin molecules blocks the interaction of the RNA template-primer duplex with the active site and prevents the entry of NTP into the catalytic site, thereby inhibiting polymerase catalytic activity. In the Murine Norovirus polymerase, although two suramin binding sites do not overlap with the RNA strands, one of them coincides with the proposed NTP entry channel, indirectly impairing polymerase function28. Suramin also binds to the NTP entry channel of Nipah virus and Ebola virus polymerases, blocking substrate access to the active site29,37.

We demonstrate that suramin effectively inhibits the EBIV polymerase activity by preventing vRNA promoter binding and obstructing the formation of the elongating EBIV L-RNA complex. In EBIV L, the two suramin binding sites are spatially separated. One binding site is located at the vRNA promoter binding site, while the other resembles the binding site in SARS-CoV-2 polymerase, positioned within the active center, thereby hindering RNA duplex binding. Sequence alignment across orthobunyaviruses reveals that suramin no.2 binding residues are highly conserved across orthobunyaviruses that infect humans (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting suramin may represent a potential broad-spectrum inhibitor targeting a conserved site in the orthobunyavirus polymerases. Three positively charged residues, K1261, K1681, and K1683, are the major suramin-interacting residues at site 2. These residues also contribute to RNA coordination. Mutations at this site could impair viral replication and reduce viral fitness, making it difficult for the virus to generate viable escape mutants. The two suramin-binding sites identified in this study do not overlap with the binding sites of nucleoside analogues, which typically occupy the RdRp catalytic active site or become incorporated into the viral genome. Therefore, suramin-derived compounds and nucleoside analogues can simultaneously bind the polymerase, and their combined use would require multiple mutations for the virus to develop resistance.

The dual inhibition mechanism of suramin parallels that of ribavirin against the tomato spotted wilt orthotospovirus (TSWV) polymerase. Ribavirin binds to two distinct sites on the TSWV L protein, one is the 5’-hook binding pocket, which is spatially close to suramin no.1 binding site, and the other is the catalytic center, which differs from suramin no.2 binding site as it lies near the core lobe domain, whereas suramin no.2 binding site is located adjacent to the thumb subdomain (Supplementary Fig. 12)33. This mechanistic similarity suggests that ribavirin may adopt a comparable binding mode with the EBIV L protein, consistent with our previous findings that ribavirin exerts antiviral activity against EBIV18. Our structural analysis, combined with antiviral assays, provides a solid foundation for developing suramin as an antiviral candidate. Although suramin has shown promising antiviral activity against EBIV, it also has limitations. Suramin is highly negatively charged, which hampers cellular uptake. Further drug optimization is needed, including improving compound stability, enhancing membrane permeability, and increasing selectivity toward viral polymerase to maximize antiviral potency and reduce potential off-target effects.

In summary, our study provides a comprehensive structural framework of the EBIV polymerase, capturing the apo state, the elongation complex with a template-product duplex, and the inhibitor-bound complex. Our structures reveal two distinct conformations of the PR loop, offering enriched insights into the regulatory mechanism of orthobunyavirus RNA synthesis. Furthermore, we uncover a critical β-hairpin linking the ZBD and the RdRp core, which is essential for polymerase activity. Finally, we identify suramin as a potent antiviral inhibitor against EBIV. The elucidation of two suramin binding sites in EBIV L reveals a dual inhibitory mechanism that targets both vRNA promoter binding and RNA duplex elongation, providing a structural basis for developing broad-spectrum antiviral agents against orthobunyaviruses. These findings not only advance our understanding of the orthobunyavirus polymerase activity but also lay the groundwork for future antiviral drug development.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

The codon-optimized DNA fragment encoding the full-length EBIV L was subcloned into a modified pPICZ-B vector with a PreScission protease cleavage site and an N-terminal His8 tag or His10-GFP tag for protein expression. The EBIV L mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis. Wild-type EBIV L and mutants were expressed in Pichia pastoris SMD1163H. For purification of wild-type and mutant proteins, yeast cells were harvested and resuspended in a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 2 mM β-Mercaptoethanol, 1 mM PMSF, and DNase I. The cells were disrupted by a high-pressure cell crusher (Union-Biotech) at 700 bar, followed by centrifugation at 30,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and then loaded onto Ni-charged resin FF and washed with wash buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 100 mM imidazole, 10% (v/v) glycerol, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol. The target protein was eluted with a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, 10% (v/v) glycerol, 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and 500 mM imidazole. The eluted protein was subsequently diluted to 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 250 mM NaCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 2 mM β-mercaptoethanol, and then loaded onto a heparin column (HiTrap Heparin HP, Cytiva) equilibrated with the same buffer. The protein was eluted using a linear NaCl gradient and eluted at approximately 600 mM NaCl. Further purification was performed on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT. Purified L proteins were concentrated using Amicon Ultra 100 kDa (Merck Millipore), flash frozen, and stored at −80°C.

Cells and viruses

Baby hamster kidney cells (BHK-21) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. BSR-T7/5 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 1 mg/mL geneticin (G418) (Beyotime). All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The recombinant reporter virus rEBIV/eGFP/S was propagated in BHK-21 cells and titrated using standard plaque assays in the same cell line. All experiments involving infectious viruses were performed in a biosafety level-2 (BSL-2) laboratory.

In vitro primer-dependent RNA synthesis assay

The purified EBIV L at 4 μM in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2 mM Tris-(2-carboxyethyl)-phosphine (TCEP), 150 mM NaCl, 300 μM ATP, 300 μM UTP, and 2 mM MnCl2 was mixed with 6 μM RNA template (5’-GUAUAUCUCCAUUAUUUUUAUCC-3’) annealed with 30 μM pGGAU primer. The mixture was then incubated at a temperature of 30 °C for 30 min in a total volume of 20 μl. An equal volume of stop solution consisting of 95% (v/v) formamide, 20 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 0.02% (w/v) xylene cyanol was added to stop the reaction. The mixture was denatured at 95 °C for 5 min and subsequently analyzed on a 20% (w/v) polyacrylamide-7 M urea gel and stained by Stains-All (Sigma-Aldrich, E9379)38. The suramin inhibition assays used the same primer-dependent RNA synthesis setup described above, except that suramin (Macklin, S832949) was included at final concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, or 80 μM. The RNA ladders used in the enzymatic activity assays are 5’-GG-3’, 5’-ACCACAC-3’, 5’-ACCACACAC-3’, 5’-ACCACACACAC-3’, 5’-ACCACACACACAC, ACCACACACACACAC-3’, 5’-GGGAGAUGAAAAUCUCCAACGAUUUUAUCC-3’, 5’-GCUUUAUCAGAAGCCAGACAUUAACGCUUCUGGAGAAACUCAACGAGCUGGACGCGGAU-3’.

In vitro de novo initiation RNA synthesis assay

The purified EBIV L protein at 4 μM in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 2 mM TCEP, 150 mM NaCl, 300 μM ATP, 300 μM UTP, 300 μM CTP, 300 μM GTP, 6 μM 5′-vRNA, 6 μM 3′-vRNA, and 2 mM MnCl2. The mixtures were incubated at 30 °C for 1 h in a total reaction volume of 20 μL. Reactions were stopped by adding an equal volume of stop solution consisting of 95% (v/v) formamide, 20 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 0.02% (w/v) xylene cyanol. The mixture was analyzed on a 20% (w/v) polyacrylamide-7 M urea gel and stained with Stains-All (Sigma-Aldrich, E9379). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. The 5′-vRNA (5’-ACGAGUGUCGUCCACCU-3’) and 3′-vRNA (5’-AGUUUUAUGCGUGGAGUACACUACU-3’) sequences were derived from the S segment. Four mutations (G2C, U3G, G9C and C10G) were introduced into the 5′-vRNA to decrease the 5′/3′-vRNA complementarity.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

To capture the structural snapshot of EBIV L protein, the purified L protein at 6 μM was incubated with 7.2 μM RNA template that annealed with 4-nt primer for 30 min at 30 °C in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0), 2 mM DTT, 150 mM NaCl, 300 μM ATP, 300 μM UTP, and 2 mM MnCl2. The mixture was then diluted 2-fold, and the NaCl concentration was adjusted to 300 mM. To determine the structure of the EBIV L-suramin complex, 6 μM EBIV L protein was mixed with suramin at a 1:1.5 molar ratio and incubated for 1 h on ice in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.0) and 300 mM NaCl.

For the sample with RNA, 3.5 μL of the mixture was applied to a glow-discharged Au R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grid (200 mesh, Quantifoil). For the EBIV L-suramin complex, 3.5 μL of the mixture was applied to a glow-discharged Cu R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grid (200 mesh, Quantifoil). After incubation for 20 s, the grids were blotted with force 0 for 2 s at 4 °C and 100% humidity and plunge-frozen into liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI Thermo Fisher). Grids were then transferred to a CRYO ARM 300 electron microscope (JEOL, Japan) operating at 300 kV equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan, USA). Cryo-EM images were recorded automatically using Serial-EM software with a super-resolution pixel size of 0.475 Å/pixel at defocus values ranging from −0.5 to −2.5 μm at a calibrated magnification of ×50,000. Data were collected at a frame rate of 40 frames per second. The total electron dose was 40 e-/Å2.

Cryo-EM data processing

10,454 movies were subjected to patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation with cryoSPARC24. Particles were automatically picked with a diameter of 180 Å from 200 micrographs. ~50,000 particles representing good classes with resolution better than 6 Å were used to generate a topaz model for auto-picking39. A total of 4,761,919 particles were picked using the topaz model and subjected to 2D classification. After two rounds of 2D classification, 2,015,549 high-quality particles were selected for ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement, generating six classes. One class representing full-length EBIV L was further refined through an additional round of ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement with 4 required classes. A class corresponding to intact EBIV L was then selected for 3D classification, which produced two subclasses with visualized RNA density. Particles from these two subclasses were combined and further refined using NU-refinement, resulting in a final reconstruction at 3.32 Å resolution. The subclass lacking RNA was also subjected to NU-refinement, yielding an apo-state EBIV L map at 3.55 Å resolution.

For the EBIV L-suramin complex, a total of 2450 movies were processed using patch-based motion correction and patch CTF estimation in cryoSPARC. Particles were automatically selected using Topaz picking, yielding an initial dataset of 646,478 particles. After two rounds of 2D classification, 254,317 high-quality particles with resolution better than 6 Å were retained for further processing. Subsequent ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement were performed with three requested classes. One class (85,378 particles) exhibiting clear secondary-structure features was selected for NU-refinement, resulting in a final reconstructed map at 3.0 Å resolution.

Model building

To build the atomic model, the EBIV L structure predicted by Alphafold40 was docked into the cryo-EM map using UCSF ChimeraX41, and then manually built in COOT42. All the RNAs and the suramin compound were manually built in COOT. Structure refinement was carried out iteratively through manual model building in Coot and automated refinement with PHENIX43. Structural model validation was performed using MolProbity44. Refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

EBIV minireplicon assay

The polymerase activity of EBIV L protein and mutants was assessed using the T7 RNA polymerase-based EBIV minireplicon system. The C-terminal 4 × FLAG-tagged EBIV L and NP genes were cloned into the pCAGGS vector for expression. To generate the reporter plasmid pLCK-EBIV-vS-NLuc, the open reading frame (ORF) of the EBIV-S segment was replaced with the Nanoluciferase reporter gene in an antisense orientation, retaining the 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of the EBIV-S segment. BSR-T7/5 cells were transfected with 0.18 μg pCAGGS-EBIV-L (WT or mutants), 0.36 μg pCAGGS-EBIV-NP, 0.36 μg pCAGGS-T7, and 0.09 μg pLCK-EBIV-vS-Nluc using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The activities of Nanoluciferase were measured using a Nano-Glo® Luciferase Assay System Kit (Promega, N1110) on a Varioskan™ LUX (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

To examine the protein expression levels, BSR-T7/5 cells were lysed 24 h post-transfection in 1% Triton X-100 buffer. Lysates were resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membrane, and probed with mouse anti-FLAG (Abclonal, AE005; 1:3000) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abclonal, AS003; 1:5000). Signals were detected with ChemiScope 6100 (Clinx). β-actin was used as an internal control.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from BSR-T7/5 cells using the Total RNA Extraction Reagent Kit (ABclonal, RK30129). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme). To specifically detect Nanoluciferase-derived vRNA, mRNA and cRNA, strand-specific reverse transcription was performed using tagged primers. The vRNA RT primer (5′-GCTAGCTTCAGCTAGGCACTATTTCGGACGGCCGTATGAAG-3′) and the mRNA and cRNA RT primer (5′-GGCCGTCATGGTGGCGACTCCGTTGATGGTTACTCGGAAC-3′) contain 5′ tag sequences (underlined) to ensure strand specificity during subsequent qPCR amplification45. qPCR was performed with Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on a Quant gene 9600Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bioer). For vRNA detection, primers vRNA-F (5′-TGAAATTTGGCCGAATTATTTTCTC-3′) and vRNA-tag (5′-GCTAGCTTCAGCTAGGC-3′) were used. Notably, the vRNA-F primer does not anneal within the Nanoluciferase coding sequence but instead targets the 5′ UTR sequence of EBIV derived from the reporter plasmid, thereby ensuring specific detection of vRNA. For cRNA and mRNA detection, primers cRNA-tag (5′-GGCCGTCATGGTGGCG-3′) and cRNA-R (5′-ATGGTCTTCACACTCGAAG-3′) were used. Relative expressions of Nanoluciferase vRNA, mRNA and cRNA were normalized to β-actin.

BHK-21 cell-based antiviral assay for suramin

To evaluate the anti-EBIV activity of suramin, BHK-21 cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 96-well plates) were infected with rEBIV/eGFP/S (MOI = 0.1) in the presence of serially diluted suramin. After 1 h, cells were washed thrice with PBS and incubated in fresh medium containing the same concentration of suramin. At 24 hpi, eGFP fluorescence was imaged with Operetta CLS (PerkinElmer) and quantified using ImageJ. EC50 values were calculated using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Time-of-addition assay

BHK-21 cells were seeded in 96-well clusters at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. The next day, the cells were treated with 300 μM suramin during the time intervals as indicated and were infected with rEBIV/eGFP/S at an MOI of 0.1. At 1 hpi, the virus was removed, and cells were washed thrice with PBS to remove the unbound virus. At 12 hpi, eGFP fluorescence was imaged with Operetta CLS (PerkinElmer) and quantified using ImageJ.

Cytotoxicity assay

BHK-21 cells were seeded in 96-well clusters at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well. The next day, the cells were treated with serially diluted suramin. Cell viability was determined using a CCK-8 assay (MCE, HY-K0301-100T) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay

MST experiments were performed using the GFP fluorescence signal of EBIV L as the readout to monitor the interaction between EBIV L and suramin. Three mutants were generated. Site 1 mutation consists of four substitutions (N403A, N528A, H527A, and R411A). Site 2 mutation includes six substitutions (Q1257A, K1261A, D1556A, H1680A, K1681A, and K1683A). Site 1 + 2 mutation incorporates all substitutions from both Site 1 mutation and Site 2 mutation. The expression and purification procedure of the mutants were the same as that used for the wild-type EBIV L. The binding affinity between EBIV L and suramin was measured using the Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper). GFP-tagged EBIV L (200 nM) was incubated with serially diluted suramin in MST buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween-20). After 10 min incubation at room temperature, samples were loaded into capillaries. MST was recorded at 25 °C using 20% LED and medium MST power. Kd values were obtained by fitting the data using MO. Affinity Analysis software.
