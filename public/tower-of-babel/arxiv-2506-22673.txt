Abstract
Studying compact-object binary mergers in star clusters is crucial for understanding stellar evolution and dynamical interactions in galaxies. Open clusters in particular are more abundant over cosmic time than globular clusters, however, previous research on low-mass clusters with ≲ 10 3 ​ M ⊙ \lesssim 10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot} has focused on binary black holes (BBHs) or black hole-neutron star (BH-NS) binaries. Binary mergers of other compact objects, such as white dwarfs (WDs), are also crucial as progenitors of transient phenomena such as Type Ia supernovae and Fast Radio Bursts. We present simulations of three types of open clusters with masses of 10 2 10^{2} , 10 3 10^{3} , and 10 4 ​ M ⊙ 10^{4}~\mathrm{M}_{\odot} . In massive clusters with ≳ 10 4 ​ M ⊙ \gtrsim 10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot} , BBHs are dynamically formed, however, less massive compact binaries such as WD-WD and WD-NS are perturbed inside the star clusters, causing them to evolve into other objects. We further find BH-NS mergers only in 10 3 ​ M ⊙ 10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot} clusters. Considering star clusters with a typical open cluster mass, we observe that WD-WD merger rates slightly increase for 10 3 ​ M ⊙ 10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot} clusters but decrease for 10 2 ​ M ⊙ 10^{2}~\textrm{M}_{\odot} clusters. Since the host clusters are tidally disrupted, most of them merge outside of the clusters. Our WD-WD merger results have further implications for two classes of transients. Super-Chandrasekhar WD-WD mergers are present in our simulations, demonstrating potential sources of Fast Radio Bursts at a rate of 70–780 Gpc -3 yr -1 , higher than the rate estimated for globular clusters. Additionally, we find that Carbon-Oxygen WD-WD mergers in our open clusters (34–640 Gpc − 3 \textrm{Gpc}^{-3} yr -1 ) only account for 0.14–2.6% of the observed Type Ia supernova rate in our local Universe.



I
Introduction

Compact binary mergers have been of specific interest in recent years. For example,
several gravitational-wave signals from neutron star - neutron star (NS-NS), black hole - black hole (BH-BH), and black hole - neutron star (BH-NS) binary mergers have been detected by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA collaboration
(
Abbott et al. 2023a
, e.g.,)
. Furthermore, NS-NS and BH-NS mergers can be used to study short gamma-ray bursts, as they remain the leading progenitors for such explosions
(
Narayan et al. 1992
;
Berger 2014
, e.g.,)
. Meanwhile, white dwarf - white dwarf (WD-WD) merger events could result in magnetars
(
King et al. 2001
;
Kashiyama et al. 2013
, e.g.,)
, one of the leading theories for the creation mechanism of at least some Fast Radio Bursts
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Zhang 2020
;
Yang & Zhang 2018
)
, while carbon-oxygen WD-WD mergers also are believed to be the source of Type Ia supernova

(
Iben & Tutukov 1984
;
Webbink 1984
;
Nomoto 1982
;
Cheng et al. 2020
;
Rosswog et al. 2009
)
. Furthermore, WD-WD mergers can be a source of gravitational radiation
(
Willems et al. 2007
;
Ruiter et al. 2010
)
, and are expected to dominate the events detected by future detectors such as the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna
(
Amaro-Seoane et al. 2023
;
Lamberts et al. 2018
, LISA,)
. WD-WD mergers may further be a pathway to form pulsars in old stellar systems
(
Kremer et al. 2023
;
Kremer et al. 2024
)
, such as PSR J1922+37 which might be associated with the old open cluster NGC 6791
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
. Lastly, WD-NS mergers have been considered for transient events such as but not limited to fast radio bursts
(
Liu 2018
;
Zhong & Dai 2020
, e.g.,)
, gamma-ray bursts
(
Yang et al. 2022
;
King et al. 2007
, e.g.,)
, and subluminous supernova
(
Metzger 2012
;
Fernández & Metzger 2013
)
.

To fully understand compact binary merger rates in galaxies it is important to account for mergers within open clusters. Star clusters contain hundreds to millions of stars, making them an ideal place to study stellar evolution and dynamical interactions, such as these compact binary merger events. Past studies of compact binary mergers typically have focused on the evolution of globular clusters. That being said, there are approximately 150 known globular clusters in the Milky Way, and it is estimated that there are at least 23,000–37,000 open clusters in the Milky Way
(
Portegies Zwart et al. 2010a
)
.
Furthermore, globular clusters were formed 12 to 13 Gyr ago, consisting of old stellar populations. Although open clusters have shorter lifetimes, they can be formed at any age of the Universe. Due to their abundance over cosmic time, open clusters are likely to contribute to the overall compact merger rate in galaxies. Indeed, there have been previous simulation works for BH-BH
(
Banerjee 2017
;
Kumamoto et al. 2020
;
Di Carlo et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, NS-NS
(
Fragione & Banerjee 2020
, e.g.,)
, and NS-BH
(
Fragione & Banerjee 2020
;
Rastello et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
merger events in open clusters. There have also been a few simulations of globular and open clusters to study WD-WD mergers and their implications on Fast Radio Burst and Type Ia supernovae production
(
Kremer et al. 2021a
;
Kremer et al. 2021b
;
Kremer et al. 2023
;
Shara & Hurley 2002
, e.g.,)
. That being said, more work needs to be done for the impact of open clusters on WD-WD and WD-NS systems; our work will comment on such systems in open clusters.

In this paper, we report on compact binary mergers found in N-body simulations of small to large open clusters, while also taking a special interest in high mass WD-WD mergers. Specifically, we test clusters of size
10^{2}\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}
,
10^{3}\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}
, and
10^{4}\ \mathrm{M}_{\odot}
. In Section
II
, we describe our simulation methods, and in Section
III
we show our results. These report on the formation efficiencies, rates, and general properties for NS-NS, WD-NS, WD-WD, BH-BH, and BH-NS mergers in open clusters.
Furthermore, we use our WD-WD mergers to comment on Type Ia and FRB rates in the local universe.
In Section
IV
we conclude our results.

We follow the dynamical evolution of open clusters via the
N
-body simulation code
petar

(
Wang et al. 2020a
)
.
Wang et al. 2020a
demonstrate
petar
utilizing the particle-particle particle-tree method
(
Oshino et al. 2011
;
Iwasawa et al. 2017
)
, highly optimized with FDPS
(
Iwasawa et al. 2016
;
Iwasawa et al. 2020
, Framework for Developing Particle Simulators,)
. Using this method, the force calculation operations in
petar
,
O[N\log{N}]
, is reduced by a factor of
N
compared to that of the direct
N
-body method,
O[N^{2}]
.
petar
also uses the slow-down algorithmic regularization
(
Wang et al. 2020b
, SDAR,)
method to efficiently and accurately evolve the orbits of close interacting systems, including binaries, triples and hyperbolic encounters. This code is known to handle the long-term simulations of massive globular clusters with a large fraction of binaries
(
Wang et al. 2021
, e.g.)
, as well as open clusters
(
Tanikawa et al. 2024
)
. In
petar
we also utilize the single and binary stellar evolution package,
sse/bse

(
Hurley et al. 2002
;
Banerjee et al. 2020a
)
, and the galactic potential package,
galpy

(
Bovy 2015
)
.

Single stars are evolved according to
Hurley et al. 2000
. Massive stars are modeled to collapse to either a BH or NS, and their masses follow the rapid model
(
Fryer et al. 2012
)
with modification of pair instability supernovae modeled by
Belczynski et al. 2016
. Natal kicks of NSs and BHs are isotropic and given by a Maxwellian distribution centered around
265~\textrm{km}~\textrm{s}^{-1}

(
Hobbs et al. 2005
)
. If fallback mass is present, the kick
velocity is reduced by (1-
f_{b}
), where
f_{b}
is the fraction of the
fallback mass
(
Banerjee et al. 2020b
, as demonstrated by)
. For NSs, if a NS forms via an electron-capture supernova (ECS), the kick velocity becomes lower (dispersion
\sim 3
km s
-1
)
(
Podsiadlowski et al. 2004
)
. Stellar evolution includes stellar winds formulated by
Belczynski et al. 2010
. Meanwhile, binary evolution follows the model in
Banerjee et al. 2020a
and contains binary evolution processes such as tidal interaction, wind accretion, stable mass transfer, and common envelope evolution.

We simulate open clusters of masses
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{4}\ \mathrm{M_{\odot}}
; we generate a total 596, 729, and 17 separate clusters for these initial masses, respectively. The initial conditions are generated using
mcluster

(
Küpper et al. 2011
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
and are as follows. Each cluster has a starting metallicity of
Z=0.02
and an initial binary fraction of 100
\%
. We adopt the Plummer model for the initial phase space distribution of stars
(
Plummer 1911
)
, with initial half-mass radii set to 0.1, 0.4, and 1 pc for masses of
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{4}\ \mathrm{M_{\odot}}
, respectively.
The initial mass and half-mass radius of
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
models are similar to those of open and young massive clusters in the Milky Way
(
Portegies Zwart et al. 2010b
)
. For the
10^{2}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
model, the mass and half-mass radius follow the typical size of embedded clusters
(
Lada & Lada 2003
)
. For clusters of
10^{2}
and
10^{3}\ \mathrm{M_{\odot}}
, the Plummer density distribution is also fractalized, motivated by the fractal features of observed open clusters
(
Goodwin & Whitworth 2004
;
Di Carlo et al. 2019
)
. We adopted a fractal dimension of 1.6 following previous studies
(
Di Carlo et al. 2019
)
.

Population Properties of Mergers

As described by Figure
3
, we look at the merger time, distance, mass, and eccentricity distributions for each type of event. The merger time is defined as the elapsed time between the start of the simulation and the time of the merger event. Eccentricity is calculated by
petar
moments before the merger event. Additionally, a summary of the merger efficiencies (numbers per solar mass) can be found in Table
2
.

We look at all mergers produced in the simulation, including those located beyond the cluster tidal radius. All mergers until the end of the simulation are included in our commentary, despite some clusters having been completely dissolved by then. Even if mergers are ejected or the cluster is dissolved, the original cluster may have long-term effects on binary systems.

As we comment on individual merger types, it is important to acknowledge that because of the different initial distributions as highlighted by Figure
1
, the BSE may not agree for different cluster masses. For this reason, we keep in mind how BSE and cluster dynamics differ per mass, and use these results to compare the different cluster masses.

In the following, we describe the results of each binary population. However, in our simulations, we do not observe a large enough number of NS-NS mergers. Therefore, we do not discuss the distribution of NS-NS mergers.

III.2.1
WD-WD mergers

The merger efficiency for the
10^{2}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
BSE case is about half of the
10^{3}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
BSE cases, but this is due to the difference in the simulation time. On the other hand, the difference in merger efficiencies between
10^{3}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
BSE models can be understood by the small difference in the initial eccentricity distribution of binaries.

For our cluster simulations, dynamically formed (non-primordial) mergers make up only about
2\sim 6
% of the total WD-WD mergers between all the cluster masses. For the
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster model,
\sim 70
% of WD-WD mergers occurred outside of the cluster (
>10
pc), while for the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster model, almost 90 % of the mergers occurred inside the cluster. This is expected, as clusters with mass lower than
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
are completely tidally disrupted by the end of their simulations, with most mergers occurring in the tidal tails during their lifetime.

We find that the WD-WD merger efficiencies for the
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster simulations are nearly three times higher than the
10^{2}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. However, there is only a slight increase in WD-WD merger efficiencies between the
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster cases. Furthermore, when we average over all cluster masses and compare the WD-WD merger rate to the merger rate from isolated BSE, there is a slight decrease in WD-WD mergers. This may suggest that WD-WD mergers are slightly suppressed by cluster dynamics. Massive stars and binaries sink to the cluster center due to mass segregation, and binary-binary or binary-single star encounters can excite the eccentricities and break up WD-WD or their progenitor binaries. For example, some of WD-WD progenitors attain high eccentricities through dynamical interactions, and merge before they evolve to WD-WDs.

As shown in Figure
3
, we also find that dynamical interactions in star clusters affect the distribution of WD-WD merger time in lower mass clusters of
10^{2}
and
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
. Cluster dynamics prolong the merger time distribution when compared to isolated BSE. On the other hand, there is no clear difference in the merger time for
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. This is likely because of the initial conditions. As shown in Table
1
, the relaxation time of
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster is more than 30 Myr. In addition, fractal initial conditions immediately collapse locally, and stars experience dynamical interactions earlier compared to spherical initial conditions. Fractalization in the lower mass clusters can allow for pockets of locally clustered stars, but with a spherical distribution, the dynamic activity is more limited to the center of the cluster. The merger eccentricities are further evidence that WD-WD binaries are affected by the dynamical interactions inside our
10^{2}
and
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
star clusters. Without dynamical interactions (BSE runs), the eccentricities of WD-WD mergers are all circular (
\lesssim 10^{-3}
). This is the same for the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. In contrast, the
10^{2}
and
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters have a slightly higher eccentricity distribution than the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters.

Although the merger time and the mass distributions of WD-WD mergers do not largely differ from isolated binary evolution in the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster models, we observe dynamical interactions in these higher mass clusters as well. We find a
\sim 10
% decrease of the WD-WD mergers in these clusters (see Table
2
). We further find that the progenitor binaries are dynamically perturbed by interactions inside the star clusters and evolved to some other compact objects. Unlike less massive clusters, they mostly merge inside star clusters because of the longer survival time of the host clusters.

Lastly, it should also be noted that the higher total mass fraction for WD-WD mergers within the
10^{2}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters is due to the nature of our simulations by stopping the simulations for these lower mass clusters at 500 Myrs. Later time, lower mass WD-WD mergers begin to dominate the distribution (for example, see Figure
4
in Section
III.4
).

III.2.2
WD-NS

Except for our
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters and
10^{3}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
BSE models, the numbers of mergers are less than 10, which is too small to discuss the significance of their distributions. However, the merger efficiencies of cluster runs are lower than those of isolated BSE runs for all cluster masses (see Table
2
). Though our sample size is small, similar to WD-WD mergers, this is likely due to dynamical interactions that break up binaries or excite their eccentricity and cause them to merge earlier in their stellar evolution.
In terms of dynamically formed binaries, our
10^{2}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters produce no mergers that were not already primordial binaries, whereas in our
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, 2 out of 24 total mergers are from non-primordial binaries.

Merger pairs also seem to be affected by the dynamical interaction inside star clusters, though they finally merge outside the cluster as seen by Figure
3
. When looking at
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
cluster models, binaries with smaller total mass (
M_{1}+M_{2}
) tend to decrease due to the dynamical interactions (the number of WD-NS samples is not enough to comment on the other two cluster models). There is also a delay in merger time for WD-NS mergers when compared to isolated BSE.
Our simulations may suggest that cluster dynamics can help prolong WD-WD and WD-NS merger timescales,
providing an avenue in the future to explore ways to generate various transient events and young neutron stars in older open clusters.

III.2.3
BH-BH mergers

BH-BH merger efficiencies have been studied in previous studies for open clusters
(
Kumamoto et al. 2019
;
Di Carlo et al. 2019
;
Rastello et al. 2019
, e.g., )
. Similar to the previous studies, the dynamical BH-BH merger efficiency increases when the cluster mass becomes massive enough. The border cluster mass seems to be between
10^{3}
and
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
.
For
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, there is an approximately 18 times increase in BH-BH mergers when compared to the isolated BSE case. As expected from previous studies of BH-BH dynamics in star clusters
(
Portegies Zwart & McMillan 2000
, e.g.,)
, our BH-BH mergers in the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters are enhanced by dynamical formations in the dense cluster cores. Whereas the BH-BH mergers in
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters are all from primordial binaries, half of the BH-BH mergers in
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters are non-primordial and formed through dynamical interactions (exchanges) in the host cluster.
Kumamoto et al. 2019
performed similar
N
-body simulations for
2.5\times 10^{3}
and
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters without primordial binaries and obtained the merger efficiency of
4.0\times 10^{-5}
and
1.7\times 10^{-5}
, respectively, in contrast to our results. Our BH-BH merger efficiencies for the
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters are nearly 10 times larger than these results, though this can be understood by our initial binary fraction of 100%.

The dynamical formation of BBH in
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters is clear if we see the fraction of BHs in the cluster core.
In Figure
7
in the Appendix, we present the number of BHs in the core per initial cluster mass (similar to an efficiency). The fraction of BHs in
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters is higher than that in
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters.
Binary BHs interact with other stars and BHs, are tightened by dynamical interactions, and merge. As shown in Figure
3
, the majority (
\sim 80
%) of BH-BH mergers occurred inside the cluster for
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
model.

About 30 % of BH-BHs merge with eccentricities
>0.1
for
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. Out of the 6 eccentric BH-BH mergers, only one was formed from a primordial binary. Thus, they are mainly dynamically formed binaries. Eccentric BH-BH mergers have theoretically been suggested
(
Samsing & Ramirez-Ruiz 2017
)
, and the occurrence rate has been estimated with numerical simulations of more massive (
\gtrsim 10^{5}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
) clusters such as globular clusters
(
Samsing et al. 2018
;
Rodriguez & Loeb 2018
)
, active galactic nuclei
(
Tagawa et al. 2021
)
, first star clusters
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
, and young massive clusters
(
Dall’Amico et al. 2024
)
. Our results show that even
\sim 10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters with solar metallicity contribute to eccentric BH-BH mergers.

Figure
3
also indicates the absence of eccentric BH-BH mergers for
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. All of their eccentricities are smaller than
0.01
. BH-BH mergers for
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters originate from primordial binaries and happen outside the clusters. In other words, they merge through isolated binary evolution, not through dynamical interactions.
The absence of eccentric BH-BH mergers is also seen in previous simulations for
10^{3}
–
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
star clusters
(
Kumamoto et al. 2019
;
Di Carlo et al. 2019
)
. Meanwhile,
Rastello et al. 2019
performed simulations of
3\times 10^{2}\sim 10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters and reported some highly eccentric cases, though they initially included BH-BH binaries in the cluster center. Our results instead clearly show that there is a boundary between the presence and absence of eccentric BH-BH mergers, or between dynamically active and inactive clusters in the cluster mass range from
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
to
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
.

III.2.4
BH-NS mergers

In
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, we obtained several BH-NS mergers, which were not seen in
10^{2}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. Four out of nine of the total BH-NS mergers are dynamical, showing that
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters are able to dynamically form BH-NS binaries early in their evolution, to eventually merge. The BH-NS binary mergers occur in the outside (tidal tail) of the
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, and there are two main time periods when these events occur. We see these mergers occurring at early times of
\lesssim 150
Myrs, with a gap in merger activity until
\gtrsim 600
Myrs. However, the sample size is too small to comment on the significance (
<10
), and this gap may be due to a low number of mergers.

Although the number of samples is small, we can calculate the efficiency to be
1.2\times 10^{-5}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}^{-1}
. This value is higher than a previous study for similar cluster mass and metallicity,
1\times 10^{-7}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}^{-1}
for solar metallicity and
2\times 10^{-6}\ ~\textrm{M}_{\odot}^{-1}
for sub-solar metallicity
(
Rastello et al. 2019
)
. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the initial conditions and stellar evolution model. Similar to our result, they also found an increase in BH-NS merger efficiency due to dynamical interactions inside star clusters for the solar metallicity case.

The lack of BH-NS mergers in our high mass clusters specifically agrees with previous studies such as
Fragione & Banerjee 2020
. BHs are more massive than NSs. Therefore, they segregate in the cluster center, dynamically heat up the core, and prevent the formation of BH-NS binaries. As shown in Figure
7
in the Appendix, the number of BH-NS binaries inside clusters for
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters is much smaller than that for
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. Similarly, the cluster cores of
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters are dominated by BHs.

In this section, we focus on WD-WD, WD-NS, NS-NS, BH-BH, and BH-NS merger rates.
We adopt a cosmic star formation rate density as a function of redshift from
Madau & Fragos 2017
:

\psi(z)=0.01\frac{(1+z)^{2.6}}{1+[(1+z)/3.2]^{6.2}}\mathrm{M}_{\odot}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}^{-3},

(1)

which assumes a
Kroupa 2001
IMF. We assume a certain fraction of all stars are formed in a cluster,
f_{\rm cluster}
, giving us a rate density equation of:

\mathfrak{R}(z)=\eta\times\psi(z)\times f_{\rm cluster},

(2)

where
\eta
is the merger efficiency, and
\mathfrak{R}(z)
is the cosmic rate density as a function of redshift. For a Milky Way like galaxy,
f_{\rm cluster}
is
\approx
0.1
(
Misiriotis et al. 2006
;
Piskunov et al. 2007
;
Bastian 2008
)
. However, in order to compare to past studies, we also compute an upper limit of the rate assuming an
f_{\rm cluster}
of 1. We report our rates as a range, using
f_{cluster}=0.1
and
f_{cluster}=1
as the lower and upper bounds, respectively. Table
2
reports on the rate density for the local universe.

In addition to providing separate merger rates for each cluster mass, we calculate a singular merger rate for the local universe. We take a mass dependent cluster formation efficiency
f_{\rm cluster,M}\propto M^{-1}
from a cluster mass function of
dN/dM\propto M^{-2}

(
Portegies Zwart et al. 2010b
)
, and assume all stars are formed in clusters of masses
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
such that
f_{\rm cluster,100}+f_{\rm cluster,1000}+f_{\rm cluster,1000}=1
. Here,
f_{\rm cluster,100}
,
f_{\rm cluster,1000}
, and
f_{\rm cluster,10000}
are the cluster formation efficiencies for
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, respectively. These rate calculations will be referred to as
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{Local}}
in the following text.

We find a
\mathfrak{R}_{\mathrm{BHNS}}
of
2.3
–
23\ \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for clusters of mass
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
; this is in rough agreement with past studies on open clusters and young massive clusters
(
Rastello et al. 2020
;
Arca Sedda et al. 2023
)
, and a couple magnitudes larger than recent estimates of BH-NS mergers in globular clusters
(
Ye et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. This range is also in rough agreement with the limits as calculated by LIGO-Virgo observations
(
Abbott et al. 2023b
,
7.8

We find a

Rodriguez et al. 2016

0.01-1\ \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

)

0.02\ \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

(
Ye et al. 2019
)
. Although our range of rates mostly fall below the LIGO-Virgo range, open clusters could still contribute a non-negligible fraction to the overall NS-NS merger rates.

(
Toonen et al. 2018
)
.

We note that our rates and thus conclusions are highly limited by our sample sizes; this is especially true for mergers that only have a few events, such as the NS-NS mergers. In addition, our three separate cluster cases vary in total mass when summed over all clusters; the
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters consist of a total mass
\sim
4 times greater than the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, and
\sim
12 times greater than the

clusters. In order to have a more accurate understanding of such mergers, more simulations need to be run in the future. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that the

III.4.1
Implications for FRBs

Figure 4:
Left: Sum of WD-WD merger component masses over merger time. Right: WD-WD merger mass-weighted outcome.
Plotted are WD-WD mergers for
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
as an example, as other cluster masses follow similar distributions. Blue points are mergers from the cluster simulations, and red points are from our isolate BSE runs. The dashed line on both plots represents the Chandrasekhar limit. The shaded regions on the right plot indicate a simplified version of which WD-WD mergers we expect to result in WD, NS, and Type Ia supernovae, adopted from
Kremer et al. 2023
and
Shen 2015
.

One of the leading theories for the creation of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) is via magnetars
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Zhang 2020
;
Yang & Zhang 2018
)
, especially since one galactic magnetar has shown FRB-like radio emission
(
CHIME/FRB Collaboration et al. 2020
;
Bochenek et al. 2020
, SGR 1935+2154,)
. These young neutron stars are more likely to arise from core-collapse supernovae in young stellar populations.
However, there has been at least one FRB associated with a globular cluster in M81
(
Bhardwaj et al. 2021
;
Kirsten et al. 2022
)
, and this FRB may be formed via a WD-WD merger
(
Kremer et al. 2021b
)
. If the total mass of the merging WDs exceeds the Chandrasekhar limit, such remnant may form a NS
(
Nomoto & Iben 1985
;
Schwab 2021
)
.
In fact, previous studies of FRB formation in globular clusters have argued that WD-WD mergers are the most plausible formation of young neutron stars in clusters
(
Lu et al. 2021
;
Kremer et al. 2021a
;
Kremer et al. 2023
)
. Here, we investigate the WD-WD merger avenue for young neutron star formation in open clusters.

The left panel of Figure
4
shows the component mass distribution over merger time for all WD-WD events. For all cluster masses, most super-Chandrasekhar mergers occur in the beginning of the cluster’s lifetimes, and after around 500 Myrs (the simulation time for the
10^{2}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters), most of the mergers are below the Chandrasekhar mass limit.
We calculate the formation efficiency of super-Chandrasekhar mergers before 500 Myrs to be
2.3\times 10^{-4}
,
3.5\times 10^{-4}
, and
3.1\times 10^{-4}

\textrm{M}_{\odot}^{-1}
, for
10^{2}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
,
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, respectively. We calculate the formation efficiency of super-Chandrasekhar mergers after 500 Myrs to be
7.1\times 10^{-5}
, and
6.5\times 10^{-5}

\textrm{M}_{\odot}^{-1}
, for clusters of mass
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, respectively.
We estimate the total super-Chandrasekhar WD-WD merger rate range in the local universe to be 78-780, and 70-700
\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for the
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, respectively.

With this said, not all super-Chandrasekhar WD-WD mergers are thought to form NSs. The right panel of Figure
4
shows the detailed primary and secondary mass distribution of WD-WD mergers obtained from our simulations. Similar to Figure 5 of
Kremer et al. 2023
, we also show the expected merger outcome from
Shen 2015
.

From this plot, the NS formation efficiencies during 1000 Myr are
4.2\times 10^{-4}
and
3.8\times 10^{-4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}^{-1}
, for clusters of mass
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, respectively. These values are summarized in Table
3
. Compared to isolated BSE runs, the formation efficiencies of super-Chandrasekhar NS are decreased across all cluster runs.
We also expect the NS formation rate from WD-WD mergers to be 15–150, and 21–210
\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, respectively. For BSE, these rates are 11–110
\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for both the
10^{3}
and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters.

Although the fraction of NSs formed from WD-WD mergers decreases in our star clusters when compared to BSE, the event rate of super-Chandrasekhar WD-WD mergers is larger than that calculated for globular clusters,
\sim 10\ \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

(
Kremer et al. 2021a
)
.
When considering the WD-WD merger scenario to create young neutron stars, our results suggest that it might be more likely to detect an FRB from an open cluster or its remnant. Even if the cluster is possibly dissolved due to tidal stripping, the original cluster dynamics will have prolonged consequences on the stars born in the cluster; open clusters might, therefore, increase the likelihood of detecting FRBs in older stellar populations in the Milky Way and other galaxies.

III.4.2
Implications for Type Ia Supernovae

Simulations suggest that a fraction of WD-WD mergers will instead result in Type Ia supernovae
(
Pakmor et al. 2013
;
Shen & Bildsten 2014
, e.g.,)
, with component mass distributions in accordance to our Figure
4
.
As mentioned in Section
I
, the merging of two Carbon-Oxygen white dwarfs is believed to be a source of Type Ia supernovae, as well as direct, head-on collisions
(
Rosswog et al. 2009
;
Katz & Dong 2012
;
Kushnir et al. 2013
, e.g.,)
. Star clusters are a possible place to generate such Type Ia supernovae, and past studies of open clusters suggested this
(
Shara & Hurley 2002
)
.

Based on our Figure
4
, we can estimate which mergers we expect to result in Type Ia supernovae. From our simulations, we obtain the formation efficiency of Type Ia SN as
1.8\times 10^{-5}
,
3.5\times 10^{-5}
,
2.8\times 10^{-5}
for the
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. Similar to Super-Chandrasekhar NS formation efficiency, the Type Ia SN formation efficiency also is decreased in star clusters when compared to BSE (see Table
3
). As a result,
we calculate a local Type Ia supernova rate of 34–340
\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for clusters of mass
10^{2}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, 64–640
\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
, and 51–510
\mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
for
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. To compare, the observed rate of Type Ia supernovae in the local universe is
(2.5\pm 0.5)\times 10^{4}\ \mathrm{Gpc}^{-3}\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

(
Li et al. 2011
;
Cappellaro et al. 2015
)
. Our simulations of open clusters only account for 0.14–2.6 % of the total observed Type Ia supernova rate in the local universe. These values are similar to the percentages found in simulations of globular clusters from
Kremer et al. 2021b
.

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we performed a series of
N
-body simulations of
10^{2}
,
10^{3}
, and
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
open clusters with a single and binary stellar evolution model. For comparison, we also performed isolated binary evolution simulations for exactly the same binaries evolved in our star cluster simulations.

Relatively massive open clusters with
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
, modeling young massive clusters, survive for our simulation time (1000 Myr). Their evolution is similar to globular clusters, and BH-BH binaries are dynamically formed in their cores. As a result, BH-BH merger efficiency significantly increases in the clusters. Some of them are eccentric. Instead, WD-WD and WD-NS merger rates decrease in the cluster models when compared to isolated binary stellar evolution.

In star clusters with a mass typical for open clusters (
10^{2}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
), BH-BH mergers are suppressed.
Compared to isolate binary stellar evolution, WD-WD merger efficiencies increase in the
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters but decrease in the
10^{2}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. WD-NS mergers decrease in both the
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
and
10^{2}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters.
Furthermore, we found BH-NS mergers only in the
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, although the number of samples is not enough for further discussion.

Our overall volumetric rate for compact binary mergers in the local universe is greater in our open cluster simulations compared to those of globular clusters. When calculating compact binary mergers that can be detected via gravitational waves by LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA, or even LISA, it is important to consider the dynamics of open clusters.

We found a fraction of super-Chandrasekhar WD-WD mergers, which may account for young magnetars and thus FRBs. The merger rate density of WD-WD mergers that collapse into a NS was found to be 11–210 Gpc
-3
yr
-1
, which is larger than that estimated for globular clusters.
Our open clusters further account for at most 2.6 % of the Type Ia supernova rate in the local universe.

We note that our conclusions are limited by the sample sizes of our clusters.
Notwithstanding, this study further shows that opens clusters offer a non-negligible amount to the compact binary populations in galaxies. With there being several more of them when compared to globular clusters, open clusters prove to be an integral part of the stellar evolution and compact merger histories in galaxies.

Appendix B
Binary Counts

In order to understand merger rates, here we discuss the number of compact objects over simulation time in the cores of our clusters. The core is typically the densest region of a cluster, and the dynamical evolution of compact binaries occurs in it.
petar
is able to distinguish the core radius over time following methods in
Casertano & Hut 1985
. Figure
7
shows the average number of the different compact objects in the core per cluster over time for each cluster mass, as well as the number of BH-NS binaries. We do not include plots of the core evolution for
10^{2}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, as they have too few stars to reliably distinguish a core by
petar
.

As shown in Figure
7
, the number of BHs inside the cores of
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters is larger than that of
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. Mass segregation causes the BHs to sink to the center of the
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, causing them to form BH-BH binaries. The higher fraction of BHs in the cores of
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters result in the higher BH-BH merger efficiency of
10^{4}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters compared to
10^{3}\ \textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters. Because BHs are heavier than NSs, the number of NS in the core is an order of magnitude lower than that of BHs. As a consequence, there is no chance of forming BH-NS binaries in the core. The number of BH-NS binaries is also shown in Figure
7
. The number of BH-NS binaries is higher in low-mass clusters because they are tidally stretched before BH-NS binaries sink to the cluster core.

In both
10^{3}
and
10^{4}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
clusters, the number of BHs in the core decreases with time due to the dynamical ejection. The trend for NSs seems to be similar, but the number of NSs in the core is small.
Furthermore, after
\sim
600 Myr, the number of compact objects in the core begins to drop in
10^{3}~\textrm{M}_{\odot}
