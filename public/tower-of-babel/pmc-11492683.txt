Infant BMI trajectories as early risk markers of poor psychosocial health in preadolescence
Authors: Claire Gallagher, George Moschonis, Katrina Lambert, Spyridon Kanellakis, Eva Karaglani, Niki Mourouti, Costas Anastasiou, Bircan Erbas, Yannis Manios
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommon mental disorders often emerge during childhood and adolescence, and their prevalence is disproportionately elevated among those affected by obesity. Early life growth patterns may provide a useful target for primordial prevention; however, research is lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to identify distinct body mass index (BMI) trajectories during the first year of life and to assess their associations with psychosocial outcomes in preadolescence (9–13 years).MethodsData were obtained from n = 1778 Greek children (9–13 years). Infant anthropometric data were obtained from paediatric health records and BMI trajectories during the first year of life were estimated using group-based trajectory modelling. Preadolescent emotional functioning, self-esteem, body image dissatisfaction and dieting behaviours were self-reported via validated questionnaires. Associations were estimated using binary and ordinal logistic regression, adjusted for key confounders.ResultsFour BMI trajectories were identified: low (26.7%), average (41.8%), high (25.2%), and very high (6.4%). Children belonging to the very high trajectory had greater odds of body image dissatisfaction (OR: 1.62, 95%CI: 1.11, 2.38), dieting behaviour (OR: 1.49, 95%CI: 1.01, 2.20) and restrained eating (OR: 1.69 95%CI: 1.14, 2.52) than children belonging to the average trajectory. Body image dissatisfaction was also greater in children belonging to the high trajectory (OR: 1.40, 95%CI: 1.11, 1.76). However, infant BMI trajectories did not significantly predict childhood emotional functioning or self-esteem status.ConclusionInfants with BMI growth in the high reference ranges had poorer psychosocial outcomes in preadolescence. Whilst further research is needed to replicate these findings, monitoring early infant growth trajectories may allow for early stratification of infants at risk of poor psychosocial outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19872-1.Keywords: Infant growth, BMI trajectories, Psychosocial health

Introduction

Mental disorders are recognized as leading causes of disability worldwide and are associated with substantial health, social and economic costs. Depression and anxiety disorders are particularly burdensome, with at least 11% of the global population expected to experience depression in their lifetime [1] and 16% of the global population expected to experience anxiety [2]. Despite affecting all age groups, most mental disorders originate before the age of 25, with 13% of depressive disorders and 20% of anxiety disorders onsetting in mid-adolescence [3]. This has important implications for the course of illness, as an earlier age of onset is associated with increased recurrence throughout adulthood, coupled with a greater severity of symptoms [4]. As such, it is important to identify early determinants of psychosocial distress that may be amenable to intervention.

Obesity in children and adolescents is highly prevalent and is thought to play a role in the development of common mental disorders, with weight-based stigma and discrimination being two of many factors thought to underlie this relationship [5]. Children and adolescents are particularly susceptible to the psychosocial impacts of stigmatization, and obesity during this period can lead to low self-esteem, body image dissatisfaction and unhealthy dieting behaviors [5, 6]. To date, several systematic reviews have confirmed obesity, but not overweight, increases the risk of depression in children and adolescents [7, 8], and findings from a recent review conducted by our research team indicate that, without resolution, the effects of childhood obesity on depression may extend into adulthood [9].

Early infancy is recognized as a critical window for adiposity programming, with increasing evidence indicating that rapid growth rates [10] and higher than average body mass index (BMI) trajectories [11–13] within the first two years of life can predict obesity in childhood, and even adulthood. Currently, little is known about the associations between infant growth patterns and mental health in preadolescence when psychological symptoms typically start to emerge. Given that obesity risk can be identified as early as infancy, growth trajectories during the first year of life may prove useful as an early risk marker of childhood and adolescent heath, allowing early stratification of infants at risk of poor psychosocial health.

Therefore, this study used group-based trajectory modelling methods to identify distinct BMI trajectories from one to 12 months of age and examined their associations with emotional functioning, body image concern, self-esteem, and disordered eating in preadolescence (9–13 years).

Methods

Study design and population

This research utilized data from the Healthy Growth Study, which is a large cross-sectional study comprising n = 2665 schoolchildren aged 9–13 years old, attending the fifth and sixth grades of primary schools located in municipalities within Attica, Aitoloakarnania, Thessaloniki and Iraklio in Greece [14]. Recruitment was conducted between 2007 and 2009. The Healthy Growth Study protocol was approved by the Greek Ministry of National Education and the study was conducted in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. All procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Ethics Committee of Harokopio University of Athens (16/19.12.2006). Recruitment procedures and study methods have previously been described in detail [14]. Briefly, sampling of the schools was random, multi-stage and stratified by parents’ educational level and total population of students attending schools within these municipalities. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents before data were collected. All participating children underwent a physical examination by an experienced paediatrician and children’s health status was assessed using a standardised checklist. Information regarding children’s medical history was collected from their parents via a standardised questionnaire and paediatric records were obtained to acquire data relating to perinatal and postnatal growth. Infants born very preterm (< 32 weeks gestation), and infants born with a very low birth weight (< 1500 g) were excluded from this analysis (n = 35).

BMI and weight status

Weight and height/length data, measured monthly during the first year of life, were retrospectively obtained from children’s paediatric health records. BMI (weight[kg]/Height[m]2) from one to 12 months of age was calculated for inclusion in trajectory analysis. In childhood, height and weight were measured by a trained researcher. BMI was calculated and was categorised as underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese in accordance with the International Obesity Task Force cut-off points [15].

Emotional functioning

Emotional functioning was self-reported via the Dartmouth Cooperative Functional Health Assessment charts/World Organization of Family Doctors (COOPS/WONCA), which measures various aspects of functional status [16, 17]. The COOPS/WONCA was initially translated from English into Greek, followed by a verbatim back-translation into English by two independent translators. The original English version, the translated Greek version, and the back-translated English version were then compared following translation validation methods similar to those outlined by Sperber [18]. A high level of agreement across the three versions was observed in terms of accuracy, consistency and meaning. Emotional functioning was assessed by the following question: “During the past two weeks, how much were you pre-occupied with emotional problems such as feeling anxious, depressed, irritable or downhearted and sad?’. Response options included ‘1 = not at all’, ‘2 = slightly’, ‘3 = moderately’, '4 = quite a bit’ and ‘5 = extremely.’ In accordance with Hoare et al. [19], we defined emotional functioning as ‘good’ if participants responded 1 or 2, and ‘poor’ if participants responded ≥ 3. The COOPS/WONCA charts have been translated into over 19 languages [16], and psychometrically validated in various populations [20, 21]. However, specific validation for the Greek language and population has not been conducted. Despite this, the charts are widely used by researchers and clinicians in Greece to assess children's emotional functioning, owing to their ease of use and simplicity.

Self-esteem

Self-esteem was assessed using the Greek version of the 10-item Rosenberg Self Esteem Scale (RSES), a widely used and validated self-report instrument for Greek school children [22, 23]. Each item is scored on a four-point Likert scale and summed to create a total score ranging from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater self-esteem. Although there is no standardized method of classification, a RSES score < 15 is generally accepted within the literature as identifying individuals with low self-esteem. Further research has shown the sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values of this threshold to be acceptable for use [24]. Therefore, we defined children as having “low” self-esteem if they scored < 15 and “normal” self-esteem if they scored ≥ 15.

Body image and dieting practices

Body image and dieting behaviour was assessed using the Children’s Eating Attitudes Test (ChEAT), which is a validated self-report instrument for measuring one’s level of disordered eating behaviour [25]. This questionnaire consists of 14 items regarding body image perception, dieting practices, food preoccupation, food awareness and social pressure to eat. Each item is scored on a six-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from 1 = never to 6 = always. The current study analysed scores from the body image and dieting subscales only, which range from 3–18, with higher scores indicating greater frequency of body image concern and dieting behaviour. The ChEAT was translated and back translated from English into Greek using the same methods employed for the COOPS/WONCA. The translation was validated in accordance with Sperber [18], achieving a high level of agreement with regard to accuracy, consistency and meaning.

Restrained eating

Restrained eating refers to the intentional restriction of dietary intake to control weight and was assessed using the Greek version of the Dutch Eating Behaviour Questionnaire (DEBQ) for children aged 7–12 years [26]. The DEBQ includes 10 items that measure dietary restraint, such as “how often do you refuse food or drink offered because you are concerned about your weight?” and “when you have eaten too much, do you eat less than usual the following days?”. Each item is scored on a five-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from 1 = never to 5 = very often. Item scores were summed to create a restrained eating score ranging from 10–50, with higher scores indicating greater dietary restraint.

Other variables

Birth weight (grams), gestational age (weeks) and the duration of exclusive breast feeding (months) were obtained from paediatric health records. Maternal years of education were reported by parents and were stratified into three categories: less than 9 years (equivalent to junior high school degree); 9–12 years (equivalent to having a high school degree); and more than 12 years (equivalent to having a college, university, or post-graduate education). Tanner stage, an index of biological maturation, was determined by female paediatricians, who thoroughly inspected breast development in girls and genital development in both girls and boys.

Statistical analysis

BMI trajectory modelling

We used group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM) to delineate and characterize patterns of BMI among participants during the first 12 months of life. Unlike traditional models that typically assume a single, uniform growth trajectory for the entire population, GBTM captures the variability in growth patterns by identifying distinct subgroups with similar trajectories of change. This makes GBTM particularly well-suited for accommodating the inherent heterogeneity in BMI development.

Trajectories were modelled using raw BMI scores rather than standardized z-scores due to the limitations in using z-scores based on cross-sectional reference data to assess longitudinal changes [27–32]. Specifically, changes in BMI z-scores may not accurately reflect physiological growth because they are influenced by the distribution of BMI in the reference population rather than the child's actual growth trajectory. Additionally, within-child variability in BMI z-scores is dependent on baseline adiposity, complicating the comparison of growth among infants with varying baseline BMIs [27, 29–32]. For example, a small increase in BMI can lead to a large change in z-score for an infant with a low baseline BMI, while the same increase results in a much smaller z-score change for an infant with a high baseline BMI. Accordingly, it has been proposed that raw BMI values provide a better indication of longitudinal changes, particularly when analyzing short term-variability and periods of dynamic growth [27, 31]. Therefore, we modeled trajectories during the first year of life using raw BMI values.

After excluding participants with missing or implausible anthropometric data during infancy (n = 774), and those with less than two measures of infant BMI (n = 60), a total of n = 1778 participants remained for inclusion in trajectory analyses. Anthropometric data was considered implausible if the weight-for-age, height-for-age or BMI-for-age z-score of a participant was greater than 5 or -5 at any given time point. Trajectories were modeled in STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas) using a plug-in developed by Jones and Nagin [33]. Using a stepwise approach, we ran models with one to five classes, fitting a quintic (fifth order) polynomial function for each class. If the order of the polynomial was not significant, we reduced the polynomial order until significance was reached (p < 0.05). The optimal model was selected based on the following fit indices: Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC), group posterior probability, entropy, trajectory sample size, and interpretability. Further details regarding model selection are provided in the online supplement.

Descriptive and association analyses

Participant characteristics were compared across trajectory groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons or the chi-square test as appropriate. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and categorical variables are presented as numbers (n) and percentages (%). Emotional functioning and self-esteem were treated as binary outcome and body image dissatisfaction, dieting behaviour and restrained eating outcomes were categorized into tertiles of increasing severity.

Binary and ordinal logistic regression models were used to determine the effects of infant BMI trajectories on emotional functioning, self-esteem, dieting, body image concern and restrained eating in childhood. Adjustments were made for age, sex, birth weight, gestational age, duration of breastfeeding, Tanner stage and maternal education. Sex differences were explored using interaction terms and stratified analyses. Odds Ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) are presented.

Further supplementary analyses estimated cross-sectional associations between childhood weight status and psychosocial outcomes. All analyses were performed in STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas) and statistical significance was defined at a p-value <0.05 for main effects and <0.1 for interaction effects.

Results

BMI trajectories and participant characteristics

Four distinct BMI trajectories were identified, following patterns of progression in BMI comparable to the 15th, 50th, 75th−85th and 97th percentile lines of the WHO infant growth charts (Fig. 1). Accordingly, BMI trajectory groups were respectively labelled: low (n = 468; 26.7%); average (n = 757; 41.8%); high (n = 444; 25.2%); and very high (n = 109; 6.4%).

The characteristics of BMI trajectory groups are summarized in Table 1. Prenatal characteristics differed significantly across groups. Specifically, maternal gestational weight gain was lowest in the low group and highest in the high group (p = 0.04). Children belonging to the high and very high trajectories had slightly longer gestation periods (p = 0.01) and higher birth weights (p < 0.001). No significant differences were seen for age, Tanner stage or maternal education. However, girls were more likely to be members of the low trajectory (63.25%) and the least likely to be members of the very high trajectory (39.45%; p < 0.001). Regarding the psychosocial health of the total sample (Supplementary Table S3, Additional File 1), 25.24% of children had poor emotional functioning and 66.72% had low self-esteem. The greatest proportion of children were in the lower tertile for restrained eating (36.71%) and dieting behaviors (40.52%), but in the middle tertile for body image dissatisfaction (36.67%).

We observed slight differences in participant characteristics between those included (n = 1735) and those excluded (n = 797) from our analysis (Supplementary Table S2, Additional File). Specifically, those excluded from the analysis were older (p < 0.001), were more likely to be in a later Tanner Stage (P = 0.024) and had mothers with lower educational attainment (p < 0.001). Additionally, a slightly greater proportion of children excluded from the analysis were classified as having a normal self-esteem (p = 0.003).

Associations between BMI trajectories and psychosocial outcomes

The proportion of school children with poor emotional functioning and low self-esteem did not differ across trajectories (Supplementary Table S3, Additional File 1). However, a greater severity of body image dissatisfaction, dieting and restrained eating was observed in children belonging to the Very High trajectory during infancy.

In adjusted logistic regression models (Table 2; unadjusted displayed in Supplementary Table S4, Additional File 1), the very high trajectory was associated with a greater likelihood of experiencing increased body image dissatisfaction (ORA 1.62, 95%CI: 1.11, 2.38), dieting behaviors (ORA: 1.49, 95%CI: 1.01, 2.20) and restrained eating (ORA 1.69 95%CI: 1.14, 2.52) when compared to the average trajectory. The high trajectory was also associated with a greater likelihood of experiencing increased body image dissatisfaction (ORA 1.40, 95%CI: 1.11, 1.76) compared to the Average trajectory. Regarding emotional functioning and self-esteem, no associations with infant BMI trajectories were seen.

In supplementary analyses (Supplementary Table S5, Additional File 1), we found strong, cross-sectional associations between childhood weight status (at age 9–13 years) and most psychosocial outcomes, except for self-esteem. Specifically, overweight (including obesity) was associated with poorer emotional functioning (ORA 1.49, 95%CI: 1.15, 1.83), greater body image dissatisfaction (ORA 4.17, 95%CI: 3.42, 5.08), greater dieting (ORA 4.04, 95%CI: 3.32, 4.91) and restrained eating (ORA 4.91, 95%CI: 4.01, 6.02). When overweight and obesity were examined as separate exposures, effect estimates were consistently stronger for obesity; however, confidence intervals were wider due to the smaller sample size of children with obesity (12.19%) vs overweight (32.19%).

Discussion

In this representative sample of Greek school children, we identified four BMI trajectories during the first year of life, which differed in their ability to predict psychosocial outcomes in pre-adolescence. Notably, we found that children belonging to the very high trajectory during infancy, with BMI growth comparable to the 97th percentile curve of the WHO standard growth charts, were more likely to experience body image concern and to engage in disordered eating behaviors at age 9–13 years, when compared to infants belonging to the average BMI trajectory. We also observed greater body image concern in children belonging to the high trajectory, with growth patterns between the 75th and 85th percentile curves. Regarding outcomes of emotional functioning and self-esteem, we found no significant relations with BMI trajectories.

Our study is the first of our knowledge to explore the psychosocial correlates of infant BMI trajectories in pre-adolescence and provides novel evidence demonstrating that infant growth patterns during the first year of life may be early risk markers of poor psychosocial health. Previous research conducted in pediatric populations, ranging from 6 to 18 years of age, focused on the psychosocial impacts of weight status, with evidence from systematic reviews and primary studies consistently showing that children and adolescents with obesity are more likely to be dissatisfied with their body, to engage in dieting behaviors and to restrain their eating [34–39]. In this context, it is unsurprising to find that infants belonging to the very high trajectory were more likely to experience body image concern and disordered eating behaviors in preadolescence, given the known tracking of infant adiposity throughout childhood [10]. This was further evidenced in our study, with members of the very high trajectory exhibiting the highest prevalence of obesity at 9–13 years.

Although the high trajectory was also associated with overweight and obesity in preadolescence, belonging in this trajectory was only found to predict body image concern, but not disordered eating. This may be because members of the high trajectory were more likely to have overweight without obesity in preadolescence and studies conducted in European schoolchildren [37, 38] have demonstrated a dose–response relationship between weight status and dietary restraint, such that those with obesity were more likely to restrain their eating compared to those with overweight.

Body image dissatisfaction and disordered eating play important roles in the development of common mental disorders [40–42], particularly during the adolescent transition, when pubertal development initiates a host of physical, social, and psychological changes which exacerbate this risk [43]. From this perspective, the findings of our study have important implications for the timing of public health interventions to support long-term mental wellbeing, particularly given that adult mental disorders often originate in childhood and adolescence. At present, guidelines in the US and the UK recommend that weight screening commence at 6 years of age [44, 45]. However, this misses an important window of obesity development, as we showed that infants in the very high and high trajectories at 12 months were more likely to have obesity at 9–13 years and consequently, to experience poorer psychosocial health. As the effectiveness of primordial prevention relies on a life course approach, our findings support an increased focus on the monitoring of growth throughout early life to support healthy outcomes.

We found no evidence of an association between infant BMI trajectories and emotional functioning or self-esteem in preadolescence. However, this is not overly surprising, given our cross-sectional analyses found no association between weight status and self-esteem, and associations of overweight/obesity with poor emotional functioning were evident, but weaker in magnitude, than the other outcomes we assessed. Notably, the reported prevalence of low emotional functioning and low self-esteem in the total sample was higher than expected, affecting one quarter of children and two thirds of children, respectively. This may be due to the unique social and economic conditions at the time in which data were collected. Specifically, Greece was one of the most hard-hit countries by the 2008–2009 economic recession, which resulted in high rates of unemployment and job insecurity. Research analyzing the impacts of financial crisis on children and adolescents in Greece reported a dramatic rise in mental health problems [46], accompanied by a decline in self-esteem [47]. It is possible that the socio-economic conditions between 2007–2010 had greater influence over emotional functioning and self-esteem than body size in this sample.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study was that data were drawn from a large, sub-nationally representative sample of school children, with serial anthropometric measurements available from birth to 12 months of age. This allowed the opportunity to develop distinct BMI trajectories during the first year of life and to explore novel relations with psychosocial health in preadolescence.

Limitations of this study include the use of self-reported questionnaires to assess psychosocial outcomes, namely the COOPS/WONA, DEBQ, ChEAT and the RSES. Although these instruments are psychometrically validated and have been adapted for use in various languages, specific validation for the Greek language and population is not available for COOPS/WONCA or ChEAT questionnaires. However, both were translated and back translated from English to Greek with high level of agreement. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility of self-reporting bias. Furthermore, the Healthy Growth Study is cross-sectional in design, and relied on retrospective measures of infant anthropometry for the development of infant BMI trajectories. Although this data could be obtained from maternal and child health records, it was not available for all children, resulting in a reduced sample size. Consequently, the characteristics of participants excluded from the analysis differed slightly from those included, particularly in terms of age, Tanner stage, maternal education, and self-esteem. While this difference may have introduced selection bias, we anticipate that the younger age, earlier stage of development, and higher maternal education within our analytical sample may bias our results towards the null. In addition, whilst our findings can be generalized to wider Greek and Mediterranean populations, they may not be applicable to Western societies. Finally, data was collected during an economic crisis, which, as previously noted, may have impacted the mental health and wellbeing of study participants at this time.

Conclusion

Overall, the results of the present study suggest that early infant BMI trajectories may be a risk marker for preadolescent psychosocial health. Specifically, infant growth from 1–12 months, corresponding to the 97th percentile growth curve, was associated with greater body image dissatisfaction and disordered eating behaviours in school children. This is explained through the tracking of BMI from infancy throughout childhood, highlighting an important window of obesity development. Similarly, infant BMI growth between the 75th and 85th percentile also predicted greater body image dissatisfaction. These findings highlight the importance of primordial prevention and support the monitoring of growth throughout critical stages of early development.
