Size‐specific weighted detectability index for computed tomography image characterization
Authors: Isabelle Fitton, Joël Greffier, Djamel Dabli, Claire Van Ngoc Ty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTechnological developments in computed tomography (CT) have increased the diversity of acquisition and reconstruction strategies available for clinical imaging. Task‐based image quality metrics that account for patient‐size variability are needed to characterize reconstructed image performance and support protocol optimization across clinically relevant imaging conditions.PurposeTo propose and evaluate a novel size‐specific weighted detectability index (SSW‐d’), a task‐based image performance metric (d') that incorporates lesion contrast weighting and water‐equivalent diameter variability for the characterization of CT images acquired at low dose.MethodsFirst, a multi‐sized image quality phantom was scanned on two CT systems: one photon‐counting CT (PCCT, CT1) and one CT equipped with energy integrating detectors (EICT, CT2). Acquisitions were performed with a volume CT dose index (CTDIvol) of 3.2 mGy at 120kV without a tin filter (Sn), at Sn100 and Sn140kV. Second, acquisitions at 3.2 mGy were performed on CT1 using three reconstruction algorithms (FBP, QIR‐2, QIR‐4). Subsequently, five reconstruction kernels (Br32, Br44, Br56, Br68, and Br76) were evaluated at 1.1 mGy. A higher CTDIvol value of 6 mGy, combined with Br68 and QIR‐4, was also tested. The detectability index values (d’) were determined for two 10 mm diameter lesions according to two different levels of contrast, based on iodine and bone equivalent tissue. The SSW‐d’ was calculated for each CT system and for each individual task. Then, SSW‐d’ was expanded to include both tasks, applying contrast weights determined for each of them.ResultsOn CT1, the highest SSW‐d’ values were 19.3 ± 0.3 and 6.9 ± 0.0, at 120 kV for bone and iodine rods, respectively. On CT2, the highest SSW‐d’ results were 19.0 ± 0.3 and 4.9 ± 0.0 at Sn100 kV for bone and iodine rods, respectively. The lowest values were at Sn140kV for both rods and CT systems. When encompassing both tasks, the maximum SSW‐d’ values were 10.05 ± 0.04 and 8.25 ± 0.03, respectively obtained at 120 kV for CT1 and at Sn100 kV for CT2. The results showed that the lowest SSW‐d’ values were at Sn140 kV for both CT systems. Comparison of acquisition and reconstruction parameters showed that the highest SSW‐d′ value was obtained using the Br32 kernel (17.6 ± 0.7), approximately twofold higher than that obtained at 6 mGy (8.6 ± 0.3). The lowest SSW‐d′ value (1.9 ± 0.1) was obtained with FBP, while the highest performance was achieved with QIR‐4 reconstruction.ConclusionsA novel metric based on a size‐specific weighted detectability index (SSW‐d’) was developed and evaluated for the characterization of reconstructed CT image performance across a range of patient‐equivalent diameters and clinically relevant detection tasks. The proposed SSW‐d’ consolidates multiple detectability index measurements into a single descriptor that accounts for lesion contrast weighting and patient‐size variability. The metric was sensitive to acquisition and reconstruction parameters and proved useful for identifying imaging conditions that maximize task‐based performance in ultra‐low‐dose CT. These findings suggest that SSW‐d’ may serve as a practical tool for image‐quality characterization and protocol optimization across clinically relevant imaging scenarios.Keywords: computed tomography, detectability index, size‐specific weighted detectability, task‐based detection, ultra‐low dose, water equivalent diameter

INTRODUCTION

For many years, the evaluation of image quality in computed tomography (CT) has prompted research aimed at achieving a balance between image quality and patient exposure to radiation dose for clinical tasks. The detectability index was introduced to provide a standardized method for benchmarking tasks between different CT systems. 1 , 2 It quantifies the efficiency with which a specified lesion in the human body can be identified, considering parameters such as image noise, contrast, 3 spatial resolution, and object size, given a specific radiation dose. 4 Then, the balance between radiation dose and image quality can be described using a figure‐of‐merit (FOM). 5 Göppel et al. proposed a FOM that characterizes image quality in the presence of low‐contrast structures in relation to radiation dose using a mathematical observer model. 6 Different CT scanners and reconstruction algorithms were benchmarked using this model, which makes it useful for type testing CT systems when varying volume CT dose index (CTDIvol). Several studies emphasized the significance of analyzing task‐based image quality using multi‐sized phantoms, particularly at low doses (LD). 7 , 8 However, this calculation method fails to consider variations in slice diameter and phantom attenuation when comparing protocols at fixed radiation dose values Because fixed‐dose protocols remain common for ultra‐low‐dose (ULD) imaging, comparisons performed at predefined dose levels retain important clinical relevance.

To date, no established protocols at fixed radiation dose values accounts for patient diameter or circumference in specific scanned anatomical regions. CTDIvol selection is currently based solely on patient weight and height. For example, in CT for lung cancer screening, the target CTDIvol is set for a reference patient of standard weight and height, with two additional dose levels for higher and lower BMI values. 9 Conversely, several studies reported that mean body diameter and body circumference are more appropriate parameters than patient weight and height for thoracic and pelvic CT studies. 10 , 11

Technological developments in CT have considerably expanded the range of acquisition and reconstruction options available in clinical practice. These advances have increased the need for task‐based image quality metrics capable of characterizing image performance under varying imaging conditions and across different patient morphologies and enabling diagnoses with low and ultra‐low doses of ionizing radiation. 12 To allow the reduction in patient radiation doses, many methods, such as iterative reconstruction and deep learning‐based reconstruction algorithms, 13 have been implemented. 14 Some CT systems now offer spectral shaping, which involves an additional built‐in tin or silver filter that selectively absorbs low‐energy photons, 15 and thus hardens the X‐ray beam. 16 Furthermore, advanced CT technologies based on semiconductor photon‐counting detectors (PCCT) enable the removal of electronic noise, thereby increasing the weight of the low energy photons in the formation of the image. Whereas the high‐energy photons contribute more to the final image with the energy integrating CT (EICT), thus affecting the contrast. Several comparative studies of task‐generic image quality have been carried out between PCCT and EICT at low doses. 7 , 17 Figure‐of‐merit based studies including a clinically relevant task have shown that, compared with EICT, PCCT reduces image noise and radiation dose, optimizes the contrast‐to‐noise ratio, and enables access to spectral characteristics. 8 , 18 , 19 , 20

The purpose of this study was first to adapt Goppel's weighted detectability index to include variation in water equivalent diameter at a constant dose level, as in ultra‐low dose (ULD) protocols. Based on this approach, a size‐specific weighted detectability index (SSW‐d’) was developed as a metric for CT image characterization across a range of patient‐equivalent sizes. Second, the proposed metric was evaluated using two CT systems employing different detector technologies (PCCT and EICT) in order to investigate its ability to characterize reconstructed image performance for clinically relevant detection tasks. Finally, the influence of dose level, reconstruction algorithm, and reconstruction kernel on SSW‐d’ was investigated for ultra‐low‐dose pelvis CT protocol.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Phantom and determination of the water equivalent diameters

Acquisitions were made on a multi‐sizes phantom (Mercury v.4.0, Sun Nuclear, Melbourne, FL, USA). The three largest sections of the phantom (26, 31, 36 cm in diameter) were used to perform the task‐based image quality assessment (Figure 1a). Each section was composed of a homogeneous ultra‐high molecular weight polyethylene part and a part that contains five rods: solid water, air, 10 mg/mL iodine, polystyrene, and bone. The homogeneous part was used to compute the noise power spectrum (NPS; Figure 1b) and the water equivalent diameters. The part containing the five rods was used for the task‐based transfer function calculation (TTF). In this study, only the iodine and bone rods were used. They were chosen to represent different levels of contrast to evaluate the performance of a CT system. (Figure 1c).

CT scanners and scanning protocols

Low‐dose image quality characterization across two CT systems

Acquisitions were performed on two Siemens Healthineers CT systems, which represent different technical specifications: the NAEOTOM Alpha.Peak (CT1; European Georges Pompidou Hospital), the SOMATOM Edge (CT2; Nîmes University Hospital). Photon‐counting detectors were used in CT1, while CT2 was equipped with energy integrating detectors. Each of them was equipped with a tin filter (Sn) with different thicknesses: 0.4 mm for CT1 and 0.6 mm for CT2 (Table 1).

Acquisition and reconstruction parameters used on the two CT systems.

Acquisitions were performed for all CT systems at Sn100 kV, Sn140 kV, and 120 kV. These three tube voltages were selected as they were specific for optimized protocols. For ultra‐low‐dose protocols, Sn100 kV and Sn140 kV are most commonly used for standard‐sized and obese patients, respectively. 22 A comparison was made between the image quality achieved using a tin filter and a tube voltage of 120 kV, which was used as a reference setting.

For each CT acquisition, the tube current modulation system was disabled. The tube current values were adapted to achieve a CTDIvol close to 3.2 mGy, which is considered in clinical routine as a low‐dose level for lumbar spine and pelvis CT examination. 23

The other parameters of the acquisition are described in Table 1. For each tube voltage and each CT system, ten repeated acquisitions were performed for each setting.

A bone reconstruction kernel used for ultra‐low‐dose lumbar spine CT protocols was studied for each CT system. Each one was specific to each CT system that was studied. The reconstruction kernels were Br68 and Br62 for CT1 and CT2, respectively. Raw data for CT1 were reconstructed using Quantum Iterative Reconstruction (QIR) level 4 and Advanced Modeled Iterative Reconstruction (ADMIRE) level 5 for CT2. These were the highest levels available for each algorithm. All images were reconstructed as polychromatic images (T3D) using data from all detected events above the lowest energy threshold at 20 keV. 24

All CT images used a 380 mm field of view, 512 × 512 pixels matrix size, and 1 mm slice thickness with 1 mm slice increment.

Ultra‐low‐dose protocol definition

Further characterization of the CT1 image quality was performed across varying acquisition and reconstruction parameters to determine the optimal parameters for ULD image quality. Each parameter was then varied individually, while all other parameters were kept constant, to isolate its specific impact on image quality.

A reference protocol, defined in the first part of the study as a low‐dose (LD) protocol (Sn 140 kV, 90 mAs, Br68 kernel, QIR‐4, CTDIvol = 3.2 mGy), was used as the baseline. This protocol was compared with two alternative reconstruction algorithms, Filtered Back Projection (FBP) and QIR‐2, with CTDIvol held constant. In addition, a standard‐dose acquisition (Sn 140 kV, 169 mAs, Br68 kernel, QIR‐4, CTDIvol = 6.0 mGy) was included for comparison. An ultra‐low‐dose protocol (Sn 140 kV, 31 mAs, QIR‐4, CTDIvol = 1.1 mGy) was also evaluated, based on the methodology described by Stern et al. 25 The impact of five reconstruction kernels: Br32, Br44, Br56, Br68, Br76, was assessed for this ULD protocol at 1.1 mGy, all corresponding to standard body kernels. All images were reconstructed as polychromatic images with a 380 mm field of view, 512 × 512 matrix, 1‐mm slice thickness, and 1‐mm slice increment.

Task‐based image quality assessment

A task‐based image quality assessment was performed using the iQMetrix‐CT software (software version: v1.2). 26 NPS, TTF and detectability indexes (d’) were computed for two groups of five consecutive acquisitions performed on each CT system, tube voltage, and diameter.

Task‐based transfer function

The Task‐based transfer function (TTF) was calculated on the iodine and bone rods (Figure 1c) using the circular edge technique. 27 , 28 To minimize the image‐noise effect on the edge spread function, the TTF was computed from two groups of five repeated acquisitions on 100 consecutive axial slices (20 slices for each of the 5 groups of acquisitions). 29 The contrast between the rods and the phantom background material was computed during the TTF calculation process and studied.

To characterize the variability of the detectability index (d′) across phantom diameters and assess its dependence on patient diameter, the mean, minimum, maximum, and inter‐diameter coefficient of variation (CV) of d′ were calculated. The inter‐diameter CV was defined as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean and was used to quantify the relative variability of d′ with patient‐equivalent diameters.

Data analysis

All results were reported as the mean ± expanded uncertainty, with the expanded uncertainty calculated using a coverage factor k = 2. This corresponded to a confidence level of approximately 95%. Descriptive statistics are also reported as median percentages with their interquartile ranges [IQR, 25th–75th percentiles]. Group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired data, with a significance level of 0.05.

The contrast weight values of the detectability index for the two CT systems are shown in Figure 3, considering bone and iodine rods for three tube voltages according to phantom water equivalent diameters.

Weights (Ωj) according to water equivalent diameters for bone and iodine rods. Three tube voltages and two CT systems were considered.

The phantom water equivalent diameter increase had no or very slight impact on contrast weights for both CT system and all the rods. On CT1, identical contrast weights were found at 120 kV and Sn100 kV for each rod. Then, the results showed a very slight decrease in ΩBone, as well as an increase in ΩIodine at Sn140 kV (p < 0.05). On CT2, the ΩBone values decreased from 120 kV to Sn140 kV, while the ΩIodine values increased. The lowest ΩBone values and the highest ΩIodine values were reached at Sn140 kV for all phantom water equivalent diameters on CT2.

The results showed that the contrast weight values were lower for the bone rod than for the iodine rod. Specifically, the ΩBone values were 0.24 ± 0.04 and the ΩIodine values were 0.76 ± 0.04 (p < 0.05) for all tube voltages and diameters and significantly different between the two CT systems (p < 0.05).

Table 2 shows that compared with the reference protocol (3.2 mGy, Br68, QIR‐4), the closest SSW‐d’ at ULD was observed for 1.1 mGy using the Br56 kernel and QIR‐4 reconstruction. The size‐specific weighted detectability in the ULD protocol was 4% lower than that of the reference protocol. The differences for individual rods were ‐2.4 % and ‐6.4 %, for ⟨SSW−d′⟩Bone and ⟨SSW−d′⟩Iodine, respectively. The coefficient of variation of d′ was higher for the reference protocol than for the ULD protocol, with increases of +48.4% and +36.7% for bone and iodine rods, respectively.

The lowest SSW‐d’ value 1.9 ± 0.1 was for the FBP reconstruction algorithm, while the highest performance was achieved with QIR‐4 in the reference protocol.

The highest SSW‐d′ value was obtained at ULD using the Br32 kernel (17.6 ± 0.7), approximately twofold higher than that obtained at 6 mGy: 8.6 ± 0.3. Increasing the radiation dose from 3.2 to 6 mGy had only a limited effect on the SSW‐d′ value compared with the influence of the reconstruction kernel at lower dose levels.

The maximum d′ values for bone and iodine rods were 50.5 and 14.2, respectively, at Br32. However, this configuration showed a high variability across phantom diameters, with an inter‐diameter coefficient of variation ranging from 23% to 26% (a ‐c, Figure 6). In contrast, Br76 yielded a lower maximum d′ (38.0 and 8.2 for bone and iodine rods, respectively), but with improved stability, as indicated by a lower coefficient of variation (6%–9%), resulting in an SSW‐d’ value of 14.0 ± 0.6 (e‐f, Figure 6).

At 1.1 mGy, lower inter‐diameter coefficients of variation were observed for the Br68 and Br76 reconstruction kernels, indicating more uniform detectability values across the investigated phantom diameters and thus reduced diameter‐dependent variability under these acquisition and reconstruction settings.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, SSW‐d′ was introduced as a task‐based metric designed to incorporate phantom diameter into the assessment of CT image quality. To our knowledge, this is the first detectability index‐based metric to incorporate phantom size, as represented by water‐equivalent diameter, into a single task‐based assessment approach. Unlike conventional detectability index analyses, which are performed for specific phantom sizes, SSW‐d′ aggregates measurements across a range of patient‐equivalent diameters and lesion contrast levels. Consequently, SSW‐d′ offers a more comprehensive description of imaging performance at ultra‐low dose, reflecting the combined impact of phantom diameter, acquisition parameters, reconstruction algorithms and kernels, detector technology and task characteristics. The efficiency of the methodology stems from improved interpretability and comparability rather than from a reduction in experimental workload. This approach may be useful for both individual and multi‐task analyses and facilitates optimization of low‐dose and ultra‐low‐dose protocols at fixed radiation dose.

This model was a variation of the FOM suggested by Göppel et al., 6 characterized as a dose‐efficiency index with low contrast structures. While the detectability index is influenced by acquisition and reconstruction parameters, it is also strongly affected in clinical practice by patient‐equivalent diameters and tissue attenuation, through their impact on noise and automatic tube current modulation. 37 This highlights the importance of incorporating patient‐dependent effects, including variations in patient‐equivalent diameter, into objective functions for CT protocol optimization.

Comparative studies in the literature frequently report detectability indices separately for each phantom diameter. However, extrapolating these results to other diameters without accounting for the continuous variation in scatter, attenuation, and noise may lead to oversimplified or potentially misleading conclusions about reconstructed image performance. 8 . 38 . 39 This underscores the importance of the metric that considers phantom diameter when evaluating detectability across imaging conditions.

A similar limitation has long been recognized in CT dosimetry, where CTDIvol alone does not account for patient size and attenuation characteristics. To overcome this issue, the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) introduced size‐specific conversion factors based on water‐equivalent diameter, leading to Size‐Specific Dose Estimate (SSDE). 40 Unlike the physical diameter, the water‐equivalent diameter incorporates both patient size and X‐ray attenuation properties, providing a more representative description of imaging conditions.

The study results showed that detectability index exhibits a similar dependence on phantom diameter: across all imaging conditions, d′ consistently decreased with increasing water‐equivalent diameter, reflecting the combined effects of greater attenuation and scatter, which increase image noise and reduce object contrast. The inter‐diameter coefficients of variation reported in this study quantified the magnitude of these size‐dependent variations in the detectability index. Consistent with these findings, the water‐equivalent diameter had a strong influence on d′ across all imaging conditions. Although the relative contributions varied across reconstruction settings, the resulting variations were similar in magnitude to those associated with changes in reconstruction parameters in several cases. This emphasizes the importance of phantom diameter as a major determinant of reconstructed image performance under low‐ and ultra‐low‐dose conditions. This behavior was consistently observed across the two CT systems and imaging protocols investigated.

The proposed SSW‐d′ metric addresses this dependence by using water‐equivalent diameters combined with weights to adjust the detectability index. The weighting coefficients Ki are explicitly defined by the differences between adjacent water‐equivalent diameters, and contribute proportionally to the interval they represent within the range of phantom diameters. At the extremities of the range, the weights are defined using one‐sided differences, while interior weights are based on symmetric differences between adjacent diameters. This ensures a consistent treatment of boundary points and reflects the higher effective sampling density of intermediate diameters in the discrete integration scheme.

The reconstruction kernel analysis at ULD illustrated this effect. Although the Br32 kernel had the highest detectability index, it was associated with reduced robustness across phantom diameters, as indicated by the high inter‐diameter coefficients of variation. In contrast, kernels such as Br76 provided more stable performance across phantom sizes, albeit with lower peak detectability. SSW‐d′ reduced the influence of exceptionally high d′ values observed at specific diameters by incorporating weights on detectability over the full range of investigated diameters, providing a more balanced assessment of reconstructed image performance. These findings suggest that protocol optimization strategies for ultra‐low‐dose CT based solely on maximum detectability may be suboptimal in clinical practice, where consistent performance across a range of patient morphologies is required. In routine implementation, ULD protocols are typically defined using a reference patient model and subsequently adapted to patient size through adjustments in tube potential and corresponding dose levels. This context highlights that detectability‐based optimization should be interpreted within a morphology‐adaptive framework, rather than as a single global maximum criterion. The SSW‐d′ metric integrates detectability information across all investigated rods using Ωi weighting coefficients that define their relative contributions. Depending on the clinical indication, the single‐task SSW‐d′ formulation may be useful for focused assessments, such as the detection of herniated discs in lumbar spine CT examinations. However, most CT examinations involve multiple clinical tasks, and the SSW‐d′ metric was therefore extended to incorporate task‐specific contrast weighting factors. Clinical indications such as lower back pain, suspected infection, or metastatic disease require adequate image quality across different anatomical structures and contrast conditions. In this context, unweighted aggregation of detectability values may be dominated by high‐contrast rods, such as the bone‐equivalent rod. This could potentially mask the contribution of low‐contrast lesion detection tasks. The proposed weighting scheme mitigates this effect by rebalancing the relative contribution of each task. This preserves sensitivity to low‐contrast detectability within the overall SSW‐d′ metric.

Consequently, the SSW‐d’ provides a task‐based descriptor that more accurately reflects the various contrast requirements encountered in clinical CT examinations across different phantom diameters.

The resulting metric also enables comparison of imaging performance across acquisition and reconstruction conditions. Task‐based image quality assessments were performed on two CT systems for two 10‐mm lesion detection tasks across a range of patient‐equivalent diameters and tube voltages. For both CT systems, detectability decreased with increasing water‐equivalent diameter, consistent with the expected impact of increased attenuation and scatter on image noise and contrast. 37 Differences in SSW‐d′ across acquisition conditions were observed between the two systems; however, these results should be interpreted within the context of the specific reconstruction settings, dose levels, and task definitions used in this study. In this framework, the photon‐counting CT (PCCT) system showed higher SSW‐d′ values than the energy‐integrating CT (EICT) system under the investigated conditions, with optimal performance observed at 120 kV for PCCT and Sn100 kV for EICT. These findings are consistent with previous reports describing improved task‐based performance of PCCT in low‐dose imaging applications. 38 , 39 , 41 Importantly, SSW‐d′ reflects reconstructed image performance resulting from the combined influence of phantom diameter, acquisition parameters, reconstruction settings, detector technology, and task definition, and should not be interpreted as an intrinsic or generalizable ranking of scanner performance. Detectability was found to be strongly dependent on reconstruction kernels and algorithms, with variations of similar order to those observed between CT systems. This suggests that reconstruction strategy may influence task‐based performance to a degree similar to hardware‐related factors.

Overall, the SSW‐d′ metric highlights the potential for optimizing task‐based image quality in ultra‐low‐dose CT protocols, where image degradation due to increased noise and reduced contrast is most pronounced, or alternatively for supporting further dose reduction strategies in such applications. These findings indicate that improvements in detectability are not solely driven by radiation dose but also strongly depend on reconstruction parameters, particularly the reconstruction kernel. In this context, SSW‐d′ provides a compact and clinically relevant framework for jointly assessing the effects of patient‐equivalent diameter, imaging parameters, and reconstruction choices on lesion detectability in CT imaging.

This study has several limitations. First, only a limited number of two rod contrasts and phantom materials was investigated. Second, SSW‐d′ was not evaluated under clinically implemented tube current modulation (TCM). TCM implementations vary across manufacturers and may interact with patient size and non‐linear reconstruction algorithms, potentially affecting noise and dose distributions, particularly in larger and heterogeneous phantoms. The use of fixed mA acquisitions and a simplified cylindrical phantom enabled controlled assessment of size‐dependent effects, however, these conditions do not fully reflect clinical acquisition strategies. 42 Third, noise analysis relied on a 2D NPS approach, which does not account for out‐of‐plane correlations in 3D CT data; a full 3D NPS analysis could not be performed due to software limitations. Fourth, although the ULD acquisition and reconstruction parameters determined using SSW‐d’ showed promising performance in phantom experiments, their clinical generalizability across different patient morphologies remains to be established. While previous studies have demonstrated the clinical feasibility of ULD dose levels, the present work focuses on a proof‐of‐concept optimization framework based on SSW‐d′ under controlled conditions, which will require further validation in patient data. Fifth, while differences between CT systems were observed, these findings cannot be directly generalized to clinical performance. Further work is needed to evaluate SSW‐d′ across a broader range of CT technologies, reconstruction methods, and clinical tasks, in order to better understand the relative contributions of acquisition, detector, and reconstruction factors to overall image quality. Finally, an important next step will be to extend the current water‐equivalent diameters‐specific weighting approach by integrating dose‐related weighting factors. This would allow SSW‐d′ to account simultaneously for variations in phantom diameter and radiation dose, potentially improving its applicability across a wider range of clinical acquisition protocols.

CONCLUSION

A novel size‐specific weighted detectability index (SSW‐d′) was developed to characterize reconstructed CT image performance while accounting for phantom‐diameter variability and task contrast weighting. Applied to PCCT and EICT systems, the metric provided a unified task‐based assessment of image performance across clinically relevant imaging conditions and proved useful for evaluating acquisition and reconstruction strategies in low‐ and ultra‐low‐dose CT.
