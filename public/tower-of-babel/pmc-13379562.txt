Cost-Effectiveness of Biosimilar Denosumab Versus Bisphosphonates in Postmenopausal Osteoporosis
Authors: Jeanine Flanigan, Sarah Kane, Fatemeh Mirzayeh Fashami, Anna Zhou, Lamees Almuallem, Mike Tindal, Marion Schauf, Edward Li, Gary H. Lyman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectivesPostmenopausal osteoporosis (PMO) is a major public health and economic burden in the USA. The objective of this study was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of biosimilar denosumab compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid in treating women with PMO at high risk of fracture from a US payer perspective.MethodsA cost-effectiveness analysis was conducted using a Markov cohort model and a lifetime horizon was applied to capture long-term costs and effects. Treatment effects were based on published network meta-analyses. The cost of biosimilar denosumab was estimated on the basis of assumptions; other costs were obtained from publicly available sources. Main outcomes included quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), costs, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs).ResultsBiosimilar denosumab was associated with greater efficacy and higher costs versus all comparators. Over a lifetime horizon, the ICER was $101,017, $93,544, $100,515, and $144,995 per QALY gained for biosimilar denosumab compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid, respectively. Cost-effectiveness was most sensitive to starting age, discount rate, treatment duration, and biosimilar price. At a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained, biosimilar denosumab was likely to be cost-effective compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid in the treatment of PMO. At a WTP threshold of $100,000 per QALY gained, biosimilar denosumab was likely to be cost-effective relative to risedronate, while ICERs relative to alendronate and ibandronate only marginally exceeded this WTP threshold.ConclusionsBiosimilar denosumab was estimated to be cost-effective compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid at a WTP threshold of $150,000/QALY gained and compared with risedronate at a threshold of $100,000/QALY gained. As a lower-cost alternative to reference denosumab, biosimilar denosumab may enhance patient access to effective PMO treatment in the USA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41669-026-00651-0.

Key Points for Decision Makers

This study is the first cost-effectiveness analysis comparing biosimilar denosumab with bisphosphonates in postmenopausal women at high risk of fracture in the USA.From the US healthcare payer’s perspective, at a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of $150,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gained, biosimilar denosumab was likely to be cost-effective compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid in the treatment of PMO.At a lower WTP threshold of $100,000 per QALY gained, biosimilar denosumab was likely to be cost-effective relative to risedronate, and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios values relative to alendronate and ibandronate only marginally exceeded the WTP threshold of $100,000 per QALY gained.Biosimilar denosumab may offer a more economically favorable treatment option compared with reference denosumab, potentially enhancing patient access to effective treatment for postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMO) in the USA.

Introduction

Postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMO) is a chronic condition characterized by low bone mass and weak bone tissue, which significantly increases fracture risk among women over the age of 50 years [1]. More than 2 million osteoporosis-related fractures occur annually in the USA [2], resulting in substantial morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Direct medical expenses and indirect societal costs of PMO-related fractures in the USA are expected to increase from $57.0 billion in 2018 to more than $95.2 billion by 2040 [3].

In the USA, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved several pharmacological agents to treat PMO among women with high fracture risk, including bisphosphonates (i.e., alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid) and denosumab [4]. Bisphosphonates are a class of medications that inhibit osteoclast activity, thereby inhibiting bone resorption [4], and are approved by the FDA for both the treatment and prevention of PMO [5–8]. Denosumab, a monoclonal antibody that reduces osteoclast activity by inhibiting the receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand, is indicated for the treatment of women with PMO at high risk of fracture [4, 9]. Alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid, and denosumab have demonstrated anti-fracture efficacy and are considered the standard of care for the initial treatment of patients with osteoporosis with high fracture risk in the absence of contraindications [4]. Ibandronate is another bisphosphonate with established clinical efficacy; however, it is less effective in preventing certain fractures compared with other bisphosphonates [4]. A systematic review indicated that persistence with oral bisphosphonates is generally low and shows no significant differences across agents [10]. In addition to highlighting ibandronate’s limited efficacy in reducing non-vertebral fractures and hip fractures (HF), the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists/American College of Endocrinology (AACE/ACE) guidelines classify alendronate and risedronate collectively as broad-spectrum oral bisphosphonates [4]. Despite the availability of multiple treatments, PMO remains significantly undertreated [11]; approximately 70% of patients in the USA receive no treatment (either never commencing treatment or ceasing treatment after commencing) mainly due to poor compliance [10], gastrointestinal (GI)-related AEs [11], and high drug acquisition costs [12].

Studies have shown that adverse events (AEs) associated with bisphosphonates can pose significant barriers to osteoporosis treatment, affecting both initiation and long-term persistence [11, 13, 14]. GI events are common among elderly patients due to the use of chronic non-osteoporosis medications such as nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, which are associated with GI symptoms [15, 16]. GI events significantly reduce the likelihood of patients initiating or continuing bisphosphonate treatment [13, 17, 18]. Patients who have experienced GI events before being diagnosed with osteoporosis are significantly less likely to start taking bisphosphonates compared with other non-bisphosphonate treatments [13, 17, 18]. Denosumab offers incremental efficacy, convenience (as a biannual injection), and improved adherence relative to oral bisphosphonates; however, it is associated with greater drug acquisition costs and since its FDA approval, the price of denosumab has nearly doubled [12], limiting its affordability and access. As such, there is an unmet need for a safe, effective, and affordable treatment for PMO associated with high patient compliance and low GI-related AEs.

The US healthcare system, lacking universal health coverage and marked by high costs and rising expenditures, poses significant affordability challenges [19]. This environment underscores the importance of assessing the value of established therapies when they exclusivity expires. Biosimilars offer the same efficacy as their reference at lower costs, and updated cost-effectiveness analyses will help quantify their value [20]. In March 2024, the FDA approved the first biosimilar denosumab, meeting the agency’s standard for equivalent efficacy and safety relative to reference denosumab [21]. As a lower-cost alternative to reference denosumab, biosimilar denosumab is anticipated to reduce drug acquisition costs and improve patient access [22]. While prior studies have evaluated the cost-effectiveness of reference denosumab relative to the other PMO therapies [23, 24], the cost-effectiveness of biosimilar denosumab relative to bisphosphonates in the treatment of women with PMO has not been established. This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of biosimilar denosumab relative to bisphosphonates in the treatment of women with PMO at high fracture risk from a US payer perspective.

Methods

A cost-utility analysis was conducted according to the FDA Healthcare Economic Information guidance [25] to examine the cost-effectiveness of biosimilar denosumab compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid. Reference denosumab was not included as a comparator because biosimilar denosumab is assumed to provide equal efficacy at a lower cost, making the reference product a dominated comparator. This report aligns with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards statement [26]. The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the US publicly funded healthcare system (Medicare fee-for-service/part B) and only included direct healthcare costs. The model used a 3.0% annual discount rate for costs and effects on the basis of the decision of the panel on cost-effectiveness in health and medicine convened by the US public health service [27, 28]. The patient starting age (72.3 years) used aligned with the mean patient age of the FREEDOM trial population [29].

Model Structure

A Markov cohort model with eight health states was developed using Microsoft Excel® 365 (version 2308; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), aligned with previous models submitted to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and Canada’s Drug Agency/L’Agence des médicaments du Canada (CDA-AMC) for reference denosumab (Fig. 1) [24, 30]. Hip fracture, vertebral fracture (VF), and wrist fracture (WF) were modeled as separate health states due to their high incidence and clinical impact, consistent with common practice in osteoporosis models [31]. All other fracture types were grouped under other osteoporotic fractures (OF) state. The cycle length was 6 months, and a lifetime horizon was applied to capture long-term costs and effects and in line with the recommendation from the US branch of the International Osteoporosis Foundation [32]. This approach ensured that all relevant costs and outcomes were included without introducing unnecessary model complexity [32]. Patients were tracked from the start of treatment until death or 99 years of age. It was assumed that, following treatment discontinuation, patients would not initiate any other osteoporosis medications for the remainder of their lives, except for subsequent therapy after denosumab. Overview of Input Parameters

A targeted literature review was conducted to identify cost-effectiveness studies comparing denosumab with bisphosphonates. Subsequently, the most robust efficacy sources were identified, prioritizing recent network meta-analyses (NMAs) that included all relevant comparators. Baseline fracture risks were derived from existing US models while US-based cost data and all other variables were sourced using recently published literature sources.

The model incorporated a comprehensive set of input parameters reflecting treatment efficacy, disease monitoring, AEs, costs, and health-related quality of life. Treatment efficacy was expressed as risk ratios (RRs) for HF, VF, WF, and OF, with drug-specific values and uncertainty ranges sourced from published NMAs and other relevant literature sources [31, 33–35]. Annual disease monitoring parameters were included to reflect routine care and treatment-related complications [4–8]. Data on the proportion of patients experiencing AEs, as well as AE frequency and duration, were derived from clinical trials and prior cost-effectiveness studies [18, 29, 36, 37]. Cost inputs encompassed drug acquisition [12, 38, 39], administration [8, 38], fracture management [40, 41], and drug monitoring costs [38, 42]. Utility values and decrements accounted for age-specific baseline health states and fracture-related impacts on quality of life [43–47]. Table 1 summarizes all key model inputs and their sources. Fracture Risks and Mortality Risk

The baseline age-stratified annual incidence of HF, VF, and WF were sourced from a US-based study [48]. The annual incidence of OF was derived as the sum of pelvic fractures and other fractures as reported by Burge (2007) [49]. Risk ratios for fracture among patients with osteoporosis relative to the general population were sourced from a US-based cost-effectiveness study [50].

The baseline fracture risk in women with PMO was calculated by multiplying the annual incidence of fracture in the general population of women with the RR of fracture for patients with osteoporosis (Supplementary Table 3). An excess fracture risk after a prior fracture was applied on the basis of values from a Swedish study (Supplementary Table 4) [51].

Mortality risk reflected general population mortality (women) and excess mortality post-fracture (Supplementary Table 5). In alignment with two previously published cost-effectiveness studies [35, 36], it was assumed that 25% of the increased mortality risk post-fracture was associated with HF, VF, WF, and OF.

Persistence

Persistence (i.e., time from the initiation to discontinuation of therapy) was considered for all treatments; data for months 0–60 were sourced from published literature (Supplementary Table 6) [52–57]. Owing to a paucity of long-term persistence data, the 60-month persistence rate [53–56] was applied for the remainder of the 10-year treatment duration. This assumption was also implemented in a recent PMO cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) [35].

Treatment Efficacy and Subsequent Treatment

Treatment efficacy data were obtained from published NMAs or other published literature (Table 1) [31, 33–35]. The AACE/ACE guidelines recommend patients switch to another antiresorptive medication after treatment with denosumab; however, no subsequent treatment is recommended post-treatment with bisphosphonates [4]. Therefore, it was assumed that patients receive alendronate after discontinuing biosimilar denosumab [2] and no subsequent treatment is administered after bisphosphonate discontinuation. Since AACE/ACE guidelines recommend patients discontinue oral bisphosphonates after 5–10 years, it was assumed that 10% of patients discontinue subsequent treatment after biosimilar denosumab (i.e., alendronate) per cycle to prevent patients from receiving treatment beyond 5 years [4]. In alignment with a previously published CEA [35], it was assumed that patients receiving subsequent treatment after biosimilar denosumab maintained the same treatment benefit as when receiving biosimilar denosumab.

Treatment Duration and Offset Time

Treatment duration of 10 years was applied for biosimilar denosumab and oral bisphosphonates and 6 years for zoledronic acid [4]. The treatment effect of bisphosphonates and denosumab declines after discontinuation and reaches zero after a maximum of 5 years and 2 years (offset time), respectively [4, 58, 59]. To avoid overestimation of treatment efficacy once patients discontinued therapy, conservative offset times of 1 year and 2 years were applied for offset time for biosimilar denosumab and bisphosphonates, respectively.

Adverse Events

Similar to other published CEAs [35, 60], severe treatment-related AEs were included in the model, including cellulitis (biosimilar denosumab), GI-related AEs (oral bisphosphonates), and acute phase reaction (APR; zoledronic acid). The incidence and duration of AEs were sourced from published literature (Table 1) [18, 29, 36, 37].

Costs

The model considered only direct costs covered by Medicare part B (Table 1). For simplicity, a uniform 80% payer coverage assumption was applied across all drug costs, consistent with Medicare Part B cost-sharing. While oral bisphosphonates are dispensed under Medicare Part D, the absolute drug acquisition costs for generic oral bisphosphonates are low, and the impact of varying this assumption is expected to be negligible [38]. Fracture-associated costs were derived from Medicare claims data and reflect all costs related to physician visits, hospitalizations, and prescription drugs [4–8, 40, 41]. At the time of the analysis, the price of biosimilar denosumab was not available; therefore, its price was derived on the basis of the price of reference denosumab, assuming a price of 80% of the reference denosumab, and historical biosimilar launch prices [12, 39]. AE management costs were sourced from published US-based CEAs [36, 42]. All costs from published literature were inflated to 2023 US dollars.

Utilities

The utility value for the “well” health state was obtained from age-stratified data of US women using the European Quality of Life Questionnaire-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) [43]. Health state utility multipliers associated with osteoporotic fractures were applied to generate utility values for the fracture health states [44]. After HF or VF, the associated disutility was lowest in the first cycle after the fracture and improved in subsequent cycles, albeit never returning to baseline levels. For WF and OF, disutility was considered only during the first year following the initial fracture. AE disutility values were obtained from published clinical and economic studies (Table 1) [45–47].

Analyses

The base-case analysis compared biosimilar denosumab with each bisphosphonate individually. The main results of the analysis comprised incremental costs (in US Dollars), incremental quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs), and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), defined as the cost per QALY gained. Total life-years (LYs) gained, QALYs gained, costs, and also costs per LY gained were also reported. Exploratory clinical results included number of fractures and also the number needed to treat (NNT) to prevent one additional fracture, calculated as the inverse of the absolute reduction in fracture risk in patients using biosimilar denosumab relative to each comparator.

Model analyses included a deterministic base-case analysis, deterministic sensitivity analyses (i.e., automated one-way sensitivity analyses [OWSAs] and multiple deterministic scenario analyses [DSAs]), a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), and a sequential analysis.

In the automated OWSAs, parameters were independently varied by ± 25% of their base-case value. The starting age varied by only 20%, as the model’s lower bound for starting age is fixed at 65 years. The OWSAs included key parameters such as patient starting age, discount rate, annual fracture incidence in the general population of women with PMO, patients’ baseline fracture risk, excess mortality and fracture risk following an initial fracture, treatment efficacy (expressed as RR for each drug), persistence rates, treatment duration, percentage covered by Medicare, and all cost and utility-related variables.

The DSAs were performed by manually varying key parameters, including starting age, time horizon, discount rate, treatment duration, and offset time. Additional DSAs were conducted by excluding costs and disutilities associated with AEs, long-term care costs, excess mortality following a fracture, and excess fracture risk after an initial fracture. Further analyses explored alternative values for WF efficacy and utility inputs, as well as scenarios using adherence in place of persistence. Details pertaining to the changes in the input values of DSAs are presented in Supplementary Table 7.

The PSA was conducted using 1000 iterations. A range of ±20% was assumed for model inputs without reported uncertainty (i.e., the annual incidence of fractures in women with PMO and all included costs). For model inputs with reported uncertainty, the available standard errors were used to derive 95% confidence intervals, which were then applied in the PSA. The range and distribution of variables included in the PSA are presented in Table 1. In line with the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review and prior published cost-effectiveness analysis in osteoporosis in the USA, the commonly cited willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds of $100,000 and $150,000 per QALY gained were used in this study [35, 50, 61–66].

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of biosimilar denosumab compared with bisphosphonates in the treatment of women with PMO at high risk of fracture in the USA. A recent study reported that more than half of US adults are worried about affording prescription drugs for themselves or for their family members [67]. To address affordability challenges, US payers encourage the most cost-effective use of prescription medications and policymakers increasingly emphasize value for money [68, 69]. In this cost-effectiveness analysis, a Markov cohort model was developed to capture the progression and natural history of women with PMO at high risk of fracture. The analysis was conducted from the US payer perspective (e.g., Medicare) and therefore included only direct medical costs while excluding productivity losses and other indirect costs. A similar approach was used in cost-effectiveness models for health technology assessment submissions for reference denosumab to NICE (2010) and CDA-AMC (2016) [24, 30]. Across these reports, substantial differences in drug acquisition costs for both denosumab and bisphosphonates were noted. Over the past 10–15 years, the increasing price of reference denosumab (which has nearly doubled since 2010) [12, 38] coupled with decreasing prices of bisphosphonates [36] has substantially impacted the cost-effectiveness of reference denosumab. Historically, the trend following launch of biosimilar drugs indicate that their cost gradually decreases over time and eventually stabilizes [70]. In light of this, and considering the continually rising healthcare costs in the USA [71], it is important to understand the value for money of biosimilar denosumab.

The results of this study indicate that compared with alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid, biosimilar denosumab was associated with ICERs of $101,017, $93,544, $100,515, and $144,995 per QALY gained, respectively. Biosimilar denosumab is likely to be cost-effective compared with oral bisphosphonates and zoledronic acid at a commonly employed WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained in the treatment of women with PMO at high fracture risk in the USA [35, 61–65, 72]. When using a WTP threshold of $100,000 per QALY gained, the ICER for denosumab biosimilar compared with risedronate falls below the threshold, while the ICERs compared with alendronate and ibandronate only marginally exceed it [35, 61–65, 72]. While the ICERs for biosimilar denosumab versus bisphosphonates in the current study are generally similar or higher in relation to other published US-based CEAs comparing reference denosumab and bisphosphonates [23, 35, 36], a substantial range in results exists across studies. In Luo (2023), denosumab followed by subsequent treatment with alendronate was associated with ICERs of $13,020 and $12,772 per QALY gained compared with zoledronic acid when treatment was initiated at ages 65 and 70 years, respectively [35]. Le (2019) utilized a discrete event simulation to assess patients at high risk of fracture and estimated an ICER of $9400 per QALY gained for denosumab compared with alendronate [62]. The study further concluded that risedronate is dominated by denosumab and reported that denosumab is less costly while providing equivalent efficacy to zoledronic acid [62]. Parthan (2013) used a Markov model with seven health states and estimated an ICER of $85,100 per QALY gained for denosumab compared with alendronate [60]. The analysis also found that denosumab dominated risedronate and ibandronate [60]. Taken together, in relation to the results published in other US-based CEA, the results described herein represent conservative estimates of the cost-effectiveness of biosimilar denosumab relative to bisphosphonates.

In interpreting the findings of this analysis, it is important to consider the applicability of the input parameters that informed key model assumptions. Several structural assumptions, including the restriction to one fracture per cycle, the duration of residual treatment effects, and patterns of treatment persistence, were based on the best-available evidence but may not fully capture the complexity of real-world patient experiences. In interpreting the results, it is important to consider the applicability of the input parameters that informed key model assumptions. The analyses presented herein utilized a Markov cohort model with eight health states and four fracture types (i.e., HF, VF, WF, and OF), which is not unlike the published CEAs described above. However, notable differences exist in the assumptions employed, including those related to persistence rate, excess mortality after a fracture, subsequent treatment, offset time, and treatment duration. Using fewer health states, simplified transition structures, and the restriction to one fracture per cycle may underestimate the long-term consequences of recurrent fractures and mortality, leading to different cost-effectiveness estimates. Simplifying assumptions regarding recovery from WF, OF, and other minor fractures, which presume a return to the well state after 1 year, may underestimate long-term functional limitations in some patients. Differences may also exist in recovery patterns following VF and post-VF, and these differences are not fully captured in the model. In the absence of long-term (e.g., 10 years) data comparing the efficacy of denosumab and relevant comparators, published 5-year data were used with the assumption that patients who remained persistent through 60 months would continue treatment for the entire treatment duration (i.e., 6 years for zoledronic acid and 10 years for other treatments). Importantly, longitudinal persistence is highly patient-dependent and can vary on the basis of individual experiences, which introduces uncertainty to the model results [73]; however, the assumption used in the analysis likely biases the results in favor of oral bisphosphonates, as their persistence rates decline more sharply compared with other treatments [52]. Overall, some assumptions in this analysis may differ from common clinical practice or the natural history of PMO; however, conservative assumptions were applied to avoid biasing the results to favor biosimilar denosumab. For example, (1) only 25% of post-fracture mortality was attributed to the original fracture [35, 74], (2) it was assumed that patients discontinuing denosumab would switch to alendronate, which has a conservative persistence rate, (3) offset times of 1 year were applied for denosumab and 2 years for bisphosphonates, and (4) treatment duration was the maximum allowed by clinical guidelines rather than shorter periods that would favor denosumab [4]. Generally, although parameters used in this model are consistent with prior cost-effectiveness modeling approaches and current clinical understanding, they may still introduce uncertainty that should be considered when interpreting the results.

Scenario and sensitivity analyses demonstrated that assumptions such as biosimilar denosumab cost, starting age, discount rate, time horizon, treatment duration, and offset time strongly influence cost-effectiveness estimates for biosimilar denosumab compared with bisphosphonates in women with PMO at high fracture risk. A reduction in the price of biosimilar denosumab would lower the ICER; indeed, at a substantial cost decrease (i.e., 80% the cost of reference denosumab), biosimilar denosumab dominates all other comparators. Patient starting age was one of the most influential parameters: ICERs ranged from $164,275 to $214,588 per QALY gained at age 65 years and dropped to $25,255 to $95,420 per QALY gained at age 85 years, suggesting that biosimilar denosumab is not cost-effective compared with bisphosphonates in younger women with PMO. This trend in results is driven by age-related baseline fracture risk and was not unexpected. Discounting also had a substantial effect on results, with ICERs falling to $39,001 to $55,532 per QALY gained with 0% discounting applied to costs and effects and rising to $200,171 to $337,066 per QALY gained with 6% discounting of costs and effects, consistent with expectations. Shorter treatment duration and shorter offset time improved cost-effectiveness, while shorter time horizons reduced it. A shorter treatment duration limits the efficacy difference between denosumab and bisphosphonates to a briefer period before effects decline, while a shorter time horizon precludes consideration of long-term treatment benefits. Excluding excess mortality or post-fracture risk resulted in higher ICERs because these factors impact the overall patient risk of death and subsequent fracture and denosumab is effective when used in high-risk patients. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that the cost-effectiveness results described herein are sensitive to varying particular model parameters; however, this variability is not unexpected given the model structure/assumptions and natural history of patients with PMO with high fracture risk.

Approximately half of patients with osteoporosis who cease their oral bisphosphonate treatment do so due to GI-related AEs [11]. Fear of possible side effects is also commonly reported as a patient-reported reason to discontinue osteoporotic treatment [75]. Furthermore, persistence with oral bisphosphonates is often low due to the requirement of frequent dosing [10]. Difficulty or inability to recall medication-taking instructions is another key factor contributing to poor persistence of osteoporosis medications [76]. Inability to afford the medication is another key factor contributing to non-adherence [12]. Reference denosumab has demonstrated an improved persistence rate, superior efficacy, and a tolerable safety profile relative to bisphosphonates [33, 34]. Despite its demonstrated benefits, reference denosumab is substantially more costly than bisphosphonates, thereby obviating the need for an effective, lower cost alternative therapy [12, 38]. Ensuring patient access to denosumab (reference or biosimilar) is crucial due to its favorable AE profile and higher persistence and efficacy rates relative to bisphosphonates [33, 52]. The availability of biosimilar denosumab will aid in offsetting the price increases observed for reference denosumab over the past decade. By offering better value for money, biosimilar denosumab stands to improve patient access to this effective and safe biologic therapy for women with PMO at high risk of fracture in the USA. Future studies analyzing biosimilar denosumab costs across different baseline fracture risk levels would be valuable, as these two variables have the greatest impact on cost-effectiveness results. While exploratory analyses found that the NNT to prevent one additional HF, VF, WF, or OF for biosimilar denosumab versus bisphosphonate comparators were above the commonly reported clinically meaningful threshold value of 7 [77], the use of tools such as artificial intelligence to better predict patients’ response to biosimilar denosumab is expected to further improve clinical and cost-effectiveness in the future.

Limitations

As with all modeling studies, the current study has limitations to consider when interpreting the results. A key limitation of this analysis is the inherent simplification required in Markov models. It is not feasible for any cost-effectiveness model to capture all possible fracture types, sequences, and clinical complexities without introducing uncertainty. Our model follows a widely accepted structure used in prior health technology assessments, but it does not account for every potential fracture site or recurrent fracture event, which may occur in real-world settings. Owing to the paucity of long-term persistence data, assumptions were required in relation to the persistence rates beyond 5 years; however, this aligned with the approach in a previously published CEA and was expected to have a small impact on model results, since less than 35% of patients remain on treatment at this timepoint [35]. Additional notable limitations pertained to the inclusion of subsequent therapy in the model. Due to modeling constraints, a simplifying assumption was made for patients receiving bisphosphonates after discontinuing biosimilar denosumab, whereby 10% of patients discontinue bisphosphonate treatment per cycle, such that all patients discontinue subsequent therapy after 5 years, in alignment the AACE/ACE guidelines [4]. Furthermore, patients receiving alendronate after discontinuing biosimilar denosumab maintained the treatment effect of biosimilar denosumab, an assumption implemented on the basis of the absence of clinical data but in alignment with a recently published CEA for PMO [35]. All parameters used in the current study were sourced from published literature, and in some cases, data from non-US countries were utilized owing to a lack of US-specific data. Notably, our model used data from a Swedish study to estimate the baseline excess risk of fracture for women with PMO [51], as US-centric information was unavailable; however, this represents best-available data and has been used in previously published US studies [35, 36]. To reduce the impact of applying a disutility multiplier derived from non-US sources, we incorporated average US population utilities [78]. However, we acknowledge that using non-disease-specific US utilities does not fully resolve this limitation, which remains an important consideration when interpreting the results. The clinical efficacy inputs for denosumab and all the comparators were obtained from a global network meta-analysis that included studies conducted in multiple regions, not only in the USA [33]. Adverse event data were also drawn from large multinational clinical trials that enrolled both US and non-US participants [29]. APR for zoledronic acid was sourced from a US-centric study[18], while gastrointestinal adverse event estimates for bisphosphonates were based on a European study [37]. Accordingly, the results may be expected to vary on the basis of genetic, ethnic, and population-specific variations between countries. In addition, this study adopted a payer perspective and did not incorporate societal costs, which is typical of US-centric models. Consequently, it did not account for the important life events and indirect data such as productivity losses. This assumption is conservative, as incorporating a societal perspective (i.e., including productivity losses) would result in substantially lower ICER values for biosimilar denosumab relative to all comparators. Finally, the assumed price of biosimilar denosumab is hypothetical, meaning the actual cost-effectiveness may vary depending on the actual price.

Strengths

This economic evaluation had several key strengths. First, data from NMAs (the highest level of evidence [33, 34]) were used to inform model inputs wherever feasible. Second, the model was designed to comprehensively reflect the disease journey for patients with PMO. To accurately represent quality of life for women with PMO, utility values stratified by age and health state were used. Finally, several DSAs were explored, which may prove useful to payers in the assessment of their coverage policies.

Conclusions

Biosimilar denosumab is likely to be cost-effective compared with oral bisphosphonates and zoledronic acid when considering a WTP threshold of $150,000 per QALY gained. At a WTP of $100,000 per QALY gained, biosimilar denosumab is likely to be cost-effective compared with risedronate. The ICERs for biosimilar denosumab versus alendronate and ibandronate only marginally exceed this threshold. The price of biosimilar denosumab is a key driver of its cost-effectiveness, as demonstrated by DSAs. As a lower cost alternative to reference denosumab, biosimilar denosumab may improve patient access in the USA to effective therapy for women with PMO.
