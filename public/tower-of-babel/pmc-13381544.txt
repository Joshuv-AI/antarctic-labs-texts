A Semi-Markov framework for modeling football possessions and temporal expected threat
Authors: Sebastian Le Coz, Firas Boustila, Frank Imbach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a spatio-temporal semi-Markov framework to model football possessions and evaluate scoring probability within specific time windows. Current possession-value models, such as Expected Threat (xT), typically treat football as a memoryless Markov process where event timing is ignored. Our framework addresses this by jointly representing action type, spatial location, and non-exponential sojourn times. We estimate transition probabilities over a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$16 \times 12$$\end{document} pitch grid and model event durations using Accelerated Failure Time models conditioned on distance and angle to the middle of the goal posts and next event. By using a quantile based criterion to select distributional families, we find that different transitions follow fundamentally different patterns. This contradicts the single-family assumptions used in prior research. We introduce a temporal xT metric that computes the probability of scoring within a fixed time horizon via backward induction. Evaluated on Ligue 1 and EURO event data, our model consistently outperforms a memoryless baseline on AUC and Brier score. We conclude that while infinite-horizon Markov models remain suitable for general play, temporal xT is the essential tool for time-critical decision support where the remaining clock is a binding constraint. This model provides an interpretable and data-driven foundation for analytics that accounts for how action duration directly affects scoring opportunities.

Introduction

Possession-value frameworks are widely used to quantify the impact of football actions1–3. A prominent example is Expected Threat (xT)4, which assigns a value to each field location based on scoring likelihood. In its classic formulation, xT discretizes the pitch into zones and models play as a Markov chain, where each grid cell has a static value representing its scoring potential1. Transition probabilities are estimated from empirical pass and carry patterns and describe how the ball typically moves across zones. This Markovian approach provides an interpretable metric for individual actions but relies on discrete, memoryless transitions and ignores the timing of events entirely. By construction, classic xT assumes exponential holding times, meaning the chance of transitioning from one cell to another depends only on the current location and not on how much time has elapsed. This makes it an effective tool for long-term player scouting but renders it blind to the time constraint. This is a significant limitation because football is an inherently continuous and temporally irregular game5. Additionally, the timing between events can substantially alter the strategic outcome of a play such as shorter delays give defenders less time to recover, thereby increasing the likelihood of a successful attack6,7. While spatial location and the defensive organization at the very instant may be the primary driver of risk8, this study explores how the temporal duration of an action can further refine tactical value estimates. We argue that Expected Threat should not be viewed as a static property of a zone, but as a time-sensitive probability that declines as the window for action closes.

The importance of timing in football has been acknowledged in several studies. Early work by Mendes and al9 used exponential models to characterize touch intervals, while more recent research has employed Cox proportional hazard models or Poisson based stochastic frameworks to describe interaction timing10. Other approaches have modeled possession duration using Gamma distributions11,12, used discrete time Markov chain simulations for match dynamics13, or developed agent based models that capture possession time distributions14. Similarly, Zhao and al15employed a Markov reward framework over a continuous spatio temporal space, thus avoiding spatial discretization but still relying on memoryless transitions16. Nevo and al used a Cox model to relate the timing of goals within a match, though this focuses on match-level event timing rather than a spatial possession-value measure. At the frontier, Narayanan and al17 propose a Bayesian marked point process framework that factorizes the joint distribution of event types and event times, allowing them to be modelled separately, and Yeung and al18 introduce a transformer-based neural point process that explicitly includes temporal intervals between events. These approaches model rich game dynamics but are aimed at predicting event sequences or possession efficiency rather than computing an expected threat value for each possession state. Recent work has also sought to improve the realism of spatial value models. For instance, the Dynamic Expected Threat (DxT) model of Hassani and al19 extends the classical xT framework by incorporating dynamic contextual features such as player positioning and spatial context. While spatial value models have moved toward capturing Pitch Control20 and Expected Possession Value3,21 to reflect the influence of surrounding players and providing a richer representation of the game state, all these models are still defined over event transitions rather than explicitly over continuous time horizons.

A critical limitation shared by many of these frameworks is that they either ignore sojourn time distributions entirely or collapse them into a single temporal component applied uniformly across all event types either through a global Gamma distribution11,12 or a shared temporal module within a neural architecture17,18. In practice, different transition types have fundamentally different timing profiles. Carries are spatially driven and can be sustained over several seconds, while shots are executed in a fraction of a second. Fitting a single distribution across all transitions conflates these behaviours, and the neural approaches additionally limit direct interpretability, which can hinder deployment in operational football analytics.

Although semi-Markov models have been applied in fields such as reliability engineering and genomics22, their use in football analytics has remained limited to macro-level analyses, such as modeling team form23, team compatibility24, or physiological responses25. To our knowledge, no previous work has estimated transition-specific sojourn time distributions for football event sequences and incorporated them into a possession-value model.

To address this gap, we propose a spatio-temporal semi-Markov framework that explicitly models both the type and timing of football events. Unlike traditional Markov models, semi-Markov processes allow the sojourn time to follow an arbitrary distribution, offering a more flexible representation of real-world event sequences. In our model, each on-ball action (pass, carry, shot, etc.) is treated as a state, enriched with continuous spatial covariates like distance and angle from the origin zone to the goal middle of the goal posts, capturing the attacking threat of the current position, and the displacement between origin and destination zone centres, capturing how far the ball travels during the action.

This paper makes the following contributions to football analytics and possession-value modelling. First, we provide a large-scale empirical analysis of event durations demonstrating that the memoryless assumption of standard Markov models is systematically violated and that different transitions follow distinct distributional families. Second, we introduce a spatio-temporal semi-Markov framework that integrates action type, spatial context, and timing. This allows transition likelihoods to depend on both the state and the elapsed time. Third, we generalise the Expected Threat framework to a finite-horizon setting by introducing temporal xT. Unlike standard models that estimate general positional danger, this metric answers the operationally critical question of whether a goal can be scored within a specific remaining time window, enabling a quantitative distinction between actions that are spatially valuable but temporally inefficient and those that are fast enough to beat a closing defensive window or an expiring match clock. Finally, our modular approach separates transition dynamics, duration modeling, and spatial covariates. This preserves interpretability and provides a principled foundation for evaluating players and tactical styles, as validated empirically across time windows.

Semi-Markov model specification

A semi-Markov process generalises a Markov chain by relaxing the assumption that sojourn times are exponentially distributed. Whereas a standard Markov chain requires state transitions to occur after memoryless waiting times, a semi-Markov process retains the Markovian property for the embedded transition sequence while allowing the time spent in each state to follow an arbitrary distribution. This extension is particularly well suited to football event sequences, where actions occur at irregular intervals whose durations are influenced by the spatial context of play.

Transition probabilities

The transition probabilities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p(s'\mid s)$$\end{document} are estimated directly from training data by maximum-likelihood frequency counting. For each observed transition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(s, s')$$\end{document} in the training corpus,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}(s' \mid s) = \frac{Nbr_{s \rightarrow s'}}{Nbr_s}$$\end{document}where Nbr standard for ”number of observations”. This non-parametric estimator assumes that the embedded transition sequence is stationary and first-order Markov. This implies that the next combined state depends only on the current combined state and not on the prior trajectory. In football, tactical context or recent action history may weaken this assumption; we treat it as an approximation and note that higher-order dependencies could in principle be incorporated by augmenting the state with lagged action information, at the cost of increased state-space dimensionality.

Sojourn time modelling

The sojourn time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{a\rightarrow a'}(\boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'})$$\end{document} denotes the duration of action a originating in zone z before the process transitions to an action of type \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a'$$\end{document} in destination zone \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z'$$\end{document}. To incorporate the effect of pitch location and ball displacement on action duration, we adopt the Accelerated Failure Time (AFT) framework29,30. The resulting Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{a\rightarrow a'}(t\mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'}) = \mathbb {P}(T_{a\rightarrow a'}\le t\mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'})$$\end{document} and the survival function is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{a\rightarrow a'}(t\mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'}) = 1 - F_{a\rightarrow a'}(t\mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'})$$\end{document}.

To determine an appropriate distribution for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon _{a \rightarrow a'}$$\end{document}, we evaluated twelve candidate families: Log-normal, Johnson SU, Log-logistic, Mielke, Inverse Gaussian, Generalized Inverse Gaussian, Weibull, Exponential, Generalized Weibull, Generalized Logistic, Generalized Gamma, and Gamma. Expected threat

Expected Threat (xT) quantifies the probability that the current possession results in a goal within a finite time budget T. Formally, given that the process is currently in combined state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s = (a, z) \in \mathcal {S}^+$$\end{document}, we define\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{xT}(s,\, T) \;=\; \mathbb {P}\!\left( \text {Goal scored within } T \text { seconds} \mid \text {current state } s\right) .$$\end{document}Since an absorbing state represents the end of a possession sequence, we extend this definition to absorbing states by the terminal conventions\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{xT}(\texttt {Goal},\,t) = 1, \qquad \textrm{xT}(s,\,t) = 0 \quad \forall \,t \ge 0,\; s \in \mathcal {S} \setminus (\mathcal {S}^+ \cup \{\texttt {Goal}\}\times \mathcal {Z}),$$\end{document}so that states such as Loss or Stoppage, which terminate a possession without a score, contribute zero future threat and are omitted from the recursion without loss of generality.

To evaluate this quantity numerically, we discretise continuous time into steps of width \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta t> 0$$\end{document}, giving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = \lceil T/\delta t\rceil$$\end{document} steps in total. Small values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta t$$\end{document} improve accuracy at the cost of computation and memory; large values may bias hazard estimates when sojourn distributions are concentrated near zero or exhibit heavy tails. In our experiments we evaluate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta t \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$$\end{document} seconds, all of which are small relative to the characteristic sojourn times summarised in Table 2.

At the core of the recursion is the marginal survival \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{S}_k(s)$$\end{document} which is the probability that the process has not yet departed state s by time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\delta t$$\end{document}. Given that the process is still in state s at step \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k-1$$\end{document}, we define the conditional transition probability to successor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$s'$$\end{document} as:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} w_k(s \rightarrow s') = \frac{p(s' \mid s) \left[ S_{a \rightarrow a'}((k-1)\delta t \mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'}) - S_{a \rightarrow a'}(k\delta t \mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'}) \right] }{\bar{S}_{k-1}(s)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{xT}$$\end{document} value is computed via the recursion, initialized at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_0 = 0$$\end{document} and updated for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=1, \dots , K$$\end{document} as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{xT}(s, k, \delta t) = \sum _{s' \in \mathcal {S}} w_k(s \rightarrow s') \textrm{xT}(s', k-1, \delta t) + \frac{\bar{S}_k(s)}{\bar{S}_{k-1}(s)} \textrm{xT}(s, k-1, \delta t). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(S_{k-1} - S_k)_{a \rightarrow a'}$$\end{document} is shorthand for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{a \rightarrow a'}((k-1)\delta t \mid \boldsymbol{x}_{z,z'}) - Baseline model

A central challenge in evaluating the semi-Markov xT model is comparability. A standard infinite-horizon Markov xT model cannot be directly compared to a finite-horizon semi-Markov model because the two quantities answer fundamentally different questions. The infinite-horizon Markov xT estimates the probability that a possession eventually results in a goal, whereas the semi-Markov xT estimates the probability of a goal within a fixed time T. Evaluating both on whether a goal happens within T seconds would unfairly penalise the infinite-horizon model, as it not designed to answer that question.

Summary Statistics for Transition Sojourn Times (Seconds).

The baseline is a timed grid xT model operating on the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_x \times n_y$$\end{document} spatial grid, with transition probabilities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}(s' \mid s)$$\end{document} estimated from the same training data. The sojourn time for each action-type pair \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(a, a')$$\end{document} is approximated by a fixed deterministic delay \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{\tau }_{a \rightarrow a'}$$\end{document}, the empirical mean duration observed in the training corpus. This is equivalent to replacing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{a \rightarrow a'}(t \mid \boldsymbol{x}_z)$$\end{document} by a point mass at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{\tau }_{a \rightarrow a'}$$\end{document}, yielding a degenerate hazard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_k(s \rightarrow s') = \textbf{1}[k = \lfloor \bar{\tau }_{a\rightarrow a'}/\delta t \rceil ]$$\end{document}.

The baseline value surface \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{W} \in \mathbb {R}^{N \times (K+1)}$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{i,k} = \textrm{xT}_{\textrm{base}}(s_i,\, k\,\delta t)$$\end{document}, is built via the same time-indexed recursion as (4), with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{H}_k$$\end{document} replaced by the indicator matrix induced by the deterministic look-back indices:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} W_{i,\,k} \;=\; g_i \;+\; \sum _{j} M_{ij}\; W_{j,\;\max \!\left( k - \lfloor \bar{\tau }_{a_i\rightarrow a_j}/\delta t\rceil ,\;0\right) }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The baseline thereby reduces the semi-Markov model to a Markov chain with deterministic transition delays, serving as a natural ablation that isolates the contribution of the full AFT sojourn time distributions. For the Baseline model we fixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta _t = 0.1$$\end{document}.

Evaluation protocol

The evaluation protocol was designed so that both models answer the same predictive question and can therefore be compared on a common ground truth. To ensure a robust assessment of generalization, the dataset was partitioned into training and test sets using a randomized 80/20 split of the total possession sequences. By splitting at the sequence level rather than the event level, we ensure that the test set consists of entirely unseen attacking moves and prevent data leakage across time-steps. Since the semi-Markov model produces \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{xT}(s, T) = \mathbb {P}(\text {Goal within } T \text { seconds} \mid s)$$\end{document}, the evaluation is cast as a binary classification problem at the event level: for each non-absorbing event \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_j$$\end{document} in a held-out test sequence, the ground-truth label is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_j \;=\; \textbf{1}\!\left[ \exists \; e_k,\; k> j \;:\; \textrm{state}(e_k) = \texttt {Goal} \;\text { and }\; t_k - t_j \le T \right] ,$$\end{document}i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y_j = 1$$\end{document} if and only if a goal is scored within T seconds of event \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_j$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_j$$\end{document} denotes the timestamp of event j. Both models produce a scalar prediction \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_j \in [0,1]$$\end{document} for each event, and the two prediction sequences are evaluated against \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{y_j\}$$\end{document} using three complementary metrics.

We included the original Markovian expected threat with no temporal constraint to evaluate against the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T = 300$$\end{document} second ground-truth label only, as this is the horizon at which the finite-horizon semi-Markov model most closely approximates the stationary regime. This choice is supported by the empirical distribution of sequence durations (Fig. 1), where the median sequence length is 21.75 seconds and the maximum observed duration is 312 seconds.

At shorter horizons the comparison would be ill-posed: the Markov model answers the question of whether a goal will eventually be scored from a given state, whereas the semi-Markov model answers the strictly time-bounded variant of that question.

The Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) measures discriminative power, i.e. whether the model correctly ranks dangerous situations above non-dangerous ones, independently of calibration. The log-loss measures the average negative log-likelihood of the true labels under the predicted distribution, and is particularly sensitive to overconfident predictions.

Sojourn times

Table 3 summarizes the best parametric family selected by QQ-score for the conditional sojourn time of each ordered transition. Overall, sojourns are non-exponential and the selected families reveal a clear structural pattern. The Mielke distribution dominates transitions. The Mielke distribution was selected across all transitions from a Pressure state as well as most transitions from a Pass and from Shot to Goal and Shot to Loss. This reflects the right-skewed, heavy-tailed nature of in-play event timings. Shot continuation outcomes such as Shot to Pass, Shot to Pressure and Shot to Stoppage, are instead best described by the Generalized Inverse Gaussian which is consistent with a more concentrated and moderately skewed timing distribution for post-shot sequences. Stoppage transitions (Carry to Stoppage, Pass to Stoppage) favour the Generalized Gamma, while Carry to Shot is best fitted by Johnson SU and Pass to Pressure by the Log-Logistic. Table 4 reports AUC, Brier score and LogLoss for the baseline model and the semi-Markov model across six time horizons (10 seconds to 300 seconds) and three time step sizes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta t \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0\}$$\end{document} seconds). For Brier score, the semi-Markov model outperforms the baseline at every horizon, and this advantage is statistically unambiguous for all horizons. For AUC, the situation is more nuanced. At short horizons (10, 20, 30 and 60 seconds), the observed AUC of each model falls within the 95% confidence interval of the other, indicating no statistically significant difference in discrimination. Notably, the baseline even achieves a marginally higher point estimate than the semi-Markov at 10, 20 and 30 seconds (0.8544 vs. 0.8456, 0.7314 vs. 0.7236, and 0.6813 vs. 0.6795 at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta t = 0.1$$\end{document} s), though none of these differences are statistically meaningful. The most decisive and consistent advantage of the semi-Markov model is therefore in calibration rather than discrimination.

Discussion

We developed a spatio-temporal semi-Markov framework for possession-value modelling and introduced a temporal Expected Threat (xT) that explicitly accounts for both the type of action and its timing. Our analysis confirms prior findings that the memoryless, exponential sojourn time assumption of standard Markov models is empirically invalid for football event sequences1,16–18,39, shared with earlier models13,15). While the Markov property appears to hold for state transitions, the associated duration distributions deviate substantially from the exponential form. This supports the use of semi-Markov models, which allow more flexible duration distributions, as suggested in other contexts11. In our data, possession-maintaining transitions are best captured by skewed, heavily right-tailed families such as the Mielke distribution. On the other hand, shot continuation outcomes follow more concentrated patterns well described by the Generalized Inverse Gaussian. These results provide strong justification for semi-Markov formulations and highlight their importance in computing short-horizon scoring probabilities. Unlike proportional hazards models40,41, which describe relative event risks, accelerated time to failure29,30 provides a more intuitive interpretation. In an accelerated time to failure model covariates act as time scalers, stretching or compressing the expected duration in a state. For example, actions taken closer to the goal tend to have shorter possession times, while actions farther from the goal allow the ball to be held longer. This reflects a fundamental aspect of football, where the location on the field influences both which actions are taken and their timing.

Our work makes several key methodological contributions. First, we generalise the classic Expected Threat (xT) framework into a semi-Markov model by replacing memoryless holding times with empirically derived AFT sojourn distributions and integrating continuous spatial covariates. Second, our modular approach estimates transitions, sojourn times, and covariate effects separately, enabling independent refinement and uncertainty propagation. This design avoids information loss from spatial discretisation and the limitations of Markovian or simplified stochastic assumptions14. Third, by selecting sojourn distributions using a QQ-score criterion rather than classical goodness-of-fit tests, we ensure that the fitted families accurately represent the tail behaviour of event durations, which is most relevant for finite-horizon scoring probability. The results in Table 4 suggest that the semi-Markov model is particularly well suited to short and constrained time horizons. It offers a clear practical advantage in scenarios where the time available to score is strictly limited, such as injury time at the end of a match. The question answered by the use of the semi-markov xt is not merely will there be a goal, but whether enough time remains to generate and convert a chance. Temporal xT answers precisely that question by coupling spatial position to an explicit sojourn law, making it directly actionable for in-game decision.

This framework has direct applications for professional football. For player and action evaluation, temporal xT offers a finer lens to distinguish temporally efficient actions, such as a rapid penetrative pass that overcomes the press, from spatially similar but slower actions that let defenders recover6,7. This enhances player scouting, on field metrics, and event-level reward attribution. It also supports tactical analysis by allowing coaches to assess both spatial progression and tempo, revealing how different playing styles, such as a high press that forces quick turnovers or a possession based system, influence scoring probability over time. Approaches such as the deep spatio-temporal model by Narayanan and al17 demonstrate the potential of neural methods in this space, but their limited interpretability makes semi-Markov models more practical for football analytics.

Several limitations remain. Instantaneous events and off-ball player tracking data were excluded from the study. This means the model reflects event-stream dynamics rather than the full match context, as critical confounders like defensive positioning are missing. The covariate set is limited to the ball’s spatial position, omitting match state, team identity, player positions, and opponent strength, which could significantly improve predictions if included in the model. Despite evaluating numerous parametric families via AFT, misspecification risk remains, particularly for transitions with few observations. Finally, the model assumes transitions depend only on the current state, ignoring potential longer-term dependencies such as possession history or player fatigue. Looking forward, the most promising extensions involve integrating tracking data to condition on defensive geometry and adding hierarchical structures to capture player and team-specific effects.

The necessity of explicit sojourn modeling depends on the temporal scale of the prediction. At 10 seconds, the baseline actually achieves a marginally higher point estimate (0.8544 vs. 0.8456), though the difference is not statistically meaningful as both values fall within each other’s confidence intervals. In this context, the added complexity of the semi Markov framework offers diminishing returns. However, the semi Markov model provides the most value for finite horizons between 20 and 300 seconds by maintaining superior calibration. While the baseline LogLoss deteriorates from 0.1176 at 20 seconds to 0.2653 at 300 seconds, the semi-Markov model degrades much more slowly, rising from 0.0628 to 0.1300 over the same range. For infinite horizon predictions, the standard Markov xT model is often preferable. In this asymptotic regime, time is no longer a factor and modeling sojourn times can introduce incremental estimation errors that degrade calibration metrics. This is evidenced by the semi-Markov Brier score of 0.0190 being higher than the 0.0152 recorded by the infinite-horizon model. The two models address fundamentally different questions. Standard xT evaluates positional threat under the assumption of unlimited time, quantifying how dangerous a location is in general play. The semi-Markov model, by contrast, accounts for the finite time remaining in a match, making it appropriate when time pressure affects decision-making. If a coach wants to know whether to take a shot from distance or work a better chance with 15 seconds of added time left, temporal xT is the only tool that answers the actual question.

Conclusion

We introduced a semi-Markov framework for football that replaces the standard memoryless time assumption with empirically grounded AFT sojourn distributions, selected via a QQ-score criterion that prioritises tail fidelity over bulk fit. The resulting temporal xT assigns a goal probability to any ball position given an explicit time budget. In terms of calibration, the semi-Markov model outperforms the baseline at every horizon studied, from 10 to 300 seconds, with Brier scores that are statistically distinguishable from those of the baseline at all horizons. In terms of discrimination, the AUC advantage is not statistically significant at short horizons (up to 60 seconds) but becomes clear and consistent from 120 seconds onward. The model is most valuable precisely when time is a binding constraint, such as in the final seconds of added time, where knowing the probability of scoring within a specific window is operationally different from knowing the general danger of a position. For season-level player valuation and contexts where no time limit applies, the original Markov xT remains the appropriate tool. Together, the two models form a complementary pair that covers the full spectrum of analytical needs in professional football, from long-run action quality assessment to real-time, time-critical decision support.
