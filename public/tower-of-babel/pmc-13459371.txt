Molecular detection and low prevalence of Human Herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) among male blood donors in Kassala, Sudan: a preliminary single-center study
Authors: Doaa M. E. Musa, Abdelrhman A. Mohammed, Yasir B. Ahmed, Duaa H. Ibrahim, Leila M. A. Abdelgader, Ghanem M. Mahjaf, Alhaj S. M. A. Ali, Babbiker Mohammed Taher Gorish, Nadir Abuzeid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveHuman herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) is a latent beta-herpesvirus with potential transfusion-transmissible relevance, particularly in immunocompromised recipients. Data on HHV-6 viremia among Sudanese blood donors are extremely limited. This study aimed to determine the molecular prevalence of HHV-6 DNA among blood donors in Kassala, Sudan. A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at Kassala Teaching Hospital. Only male donors were included due to extremely low female donation rates in this setting. Whole-blood samples (n = 180) were screened for HHV-6 DNA using real-time PCR, followed by multiplex PCR for genotyping attempts. Due to the small number of positive cases, comparisons were considered exploratory and descriptive.ResultsHHV-6 DNA was detected in 2.78% (5/180) of donors. No significant associations were found between HHV-6 positivity and age group, region, or ABO/Rh blood groups. Hematological parameters showed no significant differences between HHV-6–positive and negative donors. Genotyping was unsuccessful for all positive samples, likely due to the high cycle threshold (Ct) values (38.7–39.9), indicating low viral DNA levels. HHV-6 viremia was infrequent among blood donors in Kassala. Given the very small number of positive cases (n = 5), failed genotyping, and lack of serological confirmation, these findings are exploratory and preliminary. Larger multicenter studies with more sensitive methods are needed.

Introduction

Blood transfusion plays a critical role in saving millions of lives each year; however, transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) remain a major concern, particularly in low-resource settings [1]. Screening donated blood is therefore essential to reduce the risk of transmitting infectious agents to recipients [2]. Human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) is a double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the Betaherpesvirinae subfamily [3]. First identified by Gallo and colleagues in 1986 in patients with AIDS and lymphoproliferative disorders [4], HHV-6 exists as two distinct species, HHV-6A and HHV-6B [5]. Primary infection typically occurs in early childhood, and HHV-6B is the etiological agent of exanthema subitum (roseola infantum) [6–10]. After primary infection, the virus establishes lifelong latency in various cells—such as monocytes, macrophages, bone marrow progenitors, and T lymphocytes—and may reactivate under conditions of immunosuppression [11].

The immunological impact of HHV-6 is profound, driven by its tropism for CD4 + T cells and its ability to modulate the host immune response. The virus can induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, suppress bone marrow function, and interfere with antigen presentation, thereby exacerbating the clinical condition of recipients with compromised immune systems [12]. While HHV-6 reactivation is usually asymptomatic in immunocompetent individuals, it can cause severe complications such as encephalitis, pneumonitis, and graft rejection in hematopoietic stem cell or solid organ transplant recipients [13–15]. A unique biological feature is its ability to integrate into human chromosomes (ciHHV-6), affecting approximately 1% of the population and resulting in persistently high viral DNA loads that can lead to clinical misinterpretation [16–18].

Despite its potential for transmission via blood transfusion [19, 20], there is a notable scarcity of literature linking HHV-6 infection with ABO and Rh blood group phenotypes. Analyzing blood group distribution is essential to determine whether specific phenotypes correlate with increased susceptibility or viral persistence, as observed with other infectious agents. However, current evidence regarding a direct association between ABO/Rh groups and HHV-6 prevalence remains inconclusive or unexplored, particularly in African populations. Furthermore, assessing hematological parameters alongside viral detection is critical, as HHV-6 is known to induce transient hematological alterations, including thrombocytopenia and leukopenia, which could impact the quality and safety of donated blood units.

In sub-Saharan Africa, blood safety challenges remain significant, and targeted studies on HHV-6 in the transfusion context are scarce [21]. Previous investigations have reported variable HHV-6 prevalence: 6.1% in Burkina Faso [22], 3.49% in Greece [23], 24.3% in Qatar [20], and 16.2% in China [24]. These variations may be attributed to geographical differences, population immunity, methodological approaches, and resource limitations in molecular diagnostics. In Sudan, despite the clinical relevance of HHV-6, no molecular data are available regarding its prevalence among blood donors. This study was therefore conducted to determine the molecular prevalence of HHV-6 DNA among blood donors in Kassala, Sudan, and to attempt genotype differentiation for positive samples.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive, cross-sectional study was conducted at Kassala Teaching Hospital in Kassala State, Sudan. Kassala State serves a population of approximately 2.9 million, with its capital, Kassala City, as the primary urban center [25]. The research was designed as a preliminary single-center study, with the protocol receiving formal approval in 2022 from the Karary University Postgraduate Ethics Committee (Protocol No. KU-PG-2022-011) and the Sudan Ministry of Health Research Ethics Committee (Reference Number MOH-ERC-2022-045). Although recruitment was limited to Kassala Teaching Hospital, the hospital’s catchment area extends to multiple Sudanese regions. Consequently, a small proportion of donors (n = 5, 2.8%) originated from Khartoum, Al-Jazeera, and Darfur. All blood sample collection and data acquisition were performed during the study period in strict adherence to the approved ethical clearance guidelines. The practical and analytical components of the study were carried out through a collaborative effort involving researchers from Alnoor Medical Complex, Karary University, Kassala University, Shendi University, Omdurman Islamic University, and Delta College of Science and Technology.

Study population and rationale for inclusion of male donors

The study population consisted exclusively of male blood donors. In Kassala, female blood donation rates are extremely low due to cultural, social, and physiological factors, resulting in insufficient female donor availability during the study period. Specifically, during the study period, fewer than 2% of presenting donors were female, and the number of eligible female donors was insufficient for meaningful inclusion.

Sample size and sampling technique

As this was a preliminary, single-center, descriptive study, and the expected prevalence of HHV-6 among Sudanese blood donors was unknown, a formal sample size calculation was not performed. Instead, a convenience sample of 180 donors was enrolled based on feasibility, donor availability, and laboratory capacity during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were: male blood donors aged 18–65 years, clinically healthy at the time of donation, meeting WHO donor criteria, and providing informed consent [26]. Exclusion criteria included seropositivity for HBV, HCV, HIV, or syphilis; recent febrile illness; immunosuppressive therapy; and a blood volume of less than 4 mL, which was insufficient to perform both Complete Blood Count (CBC) and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction. Exclusion criteria were verified by rapid serological tests (SD Bioline, Korea) performed per hospital protocol before donation.

Sample collection and processing

Due to the war and the loss of some initial samples, the sample collection process was repeated. All samples included in the final analysis were collected between March 2024 and July 2024, and all collections occurred within the validity period of the approved ethical clearance. Five milliliters of venous blood were drawn into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes. Samples were divided into two parts: one for CBC analysis and the other for DNA extraction. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 5 min and stored at − 20 °C until testing.

Complete Blood Count (CBC) analysis

CBC was performed using the Mindray BC-30s automated hematology analyzer with tri-level quality control. The following adult reference ranges, based on manufacturer specifications and standard clinical laboratory guidelines, were used for interpretation: total white blood cells (4.0–11.0 × 10⁹/L), neutrophils (40–70%), lymphocytes (20–45%), monocytes (2–10%), platelets (150–450 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin (13.0–17.0 g/dL for males), and hematocrit (40–54% for males) [26]. Manual differential assessment for monocytes was performed when required by the analyzer’s flagging system. Hemoglobin levels were assessed solely for predonation eligibility (all donors met the WHO criteria of ≥ 12.5 g/dL for male donors) and were excluded from the comparative analysis because they were not central to the study objectives.

ABO and Rh blood grouping

ABO and Rh blood grouping was performed using the standard slide agglutination method with commercial monoclonal antisera (Bio-Rad IH Blood Grouping Reagents, Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA, a product line available in Sudan).

DNA extraction

Viral DNA was extracted from plasma using a commercial kit (Beijing Origin Gene-Tech Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). An extraction blank was included with each batch, and strict separation was maintained between pre-PCR and post-PCR areas. Filtered pipette tips were used to prevent contamination.

Real-time PCR detection of HHV-6 DNA

HHV-6 DNA was detected using a TaqMan real-time PCR assay targeting a conserved genomic region, as described in a previous study [24]. Macrogen synthesized primers and probes. β-globin amplification served as an internal control to assess extraction efficiency and potential PCR inhibition. The real-time PCR assay has a limit of detection of approximately 100 copies/reaction, as reported by Zheng et al. [24]. The specificity of this assay against other human herpesviruses (including HHV-7, CMV, EBV, and HSV-1/2) was previously validated by Zheng et al. [24]

PCR reaction setup and cycling conditions

Each reaction contained 5 µL of extracted DNA in a total volume of 25 µL, including primers, probe, dNTP mix, MgCl₂, Taq polymerase, and PCR buffer. Reactions were performed on the LineGene 9600 platform with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Samples were run in duplicate, with positive, negative, and extraction controls included in every run. Ct ≤ 40 was defined as positive according to prior studies [27]. Because all positive samples demonstrated Ct values near the assay cutoff threshold, the possibility of very low-level nonspecific amplification cannot be completely excluded.

Multiplex PCR for HHV-6 genotyping

Genotyping was attempted using a multiplex TaqMan assay adapted from a previous study [28], targeting the U57 gene with a FAM-labeled probe for HHV-6A and a HEX-labeled probe for HHV-6B. The same cycling conditions as those used in the real-time PCR assay were employed. Samples without amplification for either genotype were labeled Undetermined.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard error. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess data normality; as the distribution was found to be non-normal (p < 0.05), non-parametric tests were utilized. Specifically, Exploratory non-parametric comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U and Fisher’s exact tests; however, findings were interpreted descriptively because of the limited number of positive cases.

Results

A total of 180 male donors were included in the analysis. HHV-6 DNA was detected in 5 donors (2.78%). Given the small number of HHV-6–positive cases (n = 5), all statistical comparisons reported below are exploratory and should not be interpreted as confirmatory. As shown in Table 1, no apparent associations were observed between HHV-6 detection and age group, region of residence, or ABO/Rh blood groups. Four positive cases occurred among donors aged 19–35 years (2.6%), and one case occurred among donors aged 36–52 years (3.6%). All positive donors originated from Kassala, whereas no cases were detected in donors from other regions; however, the number of donors from these regions was minimal. These findings suggest no clear demographic pattern of HHV-6 positivity in this cohort, although the small number of positive samples limits interpretation.

Association of HHV-6 detection with demographic and blood group characteristics among blood donors

A comparison of hematological parameters between HHV-6 RT-PCR-positive and HHV-6 RT-PCR-negative blood donors showed no apparent differences across all measured indices. Total white blood cell counts were slightly lower in the positive group (4.58 ± 0.59 × 10⁹/L) than in the negative group (5.10 ± 0.13 × 10⁹/L), yielding a small mean difference of − 0.52. Neutrophil counts were lower in positive donors (34.52 ± 3.58%) than in the negative donors (43.27 ± 0.96%), with a mean difference of − 8.75. Lymphocyte levels were also moderately elevated among HHV-6 positive donors (54.98 ± 3.10%) compared with the negative group (46.00 ± 0.93%), resulting in a mean difference of 8.98. Monocyte values were similar between the groups, with a negligible mean difference of − 0.08. Platelet counts were slightly higher in positive donors (247.6 ± 43.19 × 10⁹/L) than in negative donors (236.6 ± 7.0 × 10⁹/L), with a mean difference of 11.0 (Table 2).

Analysis of hematological parameters in HHV-6 positive and negative blood donors

All five HHV-6–positive samples failed to yield type-specific amplification for HHV-6A or HHV-6B in the multiplex assay. This was consistent with their high cycle threshold (Ct) values (38.7–39.9), indicating low viral DNA levels that may be below the sensitivity threshold required for reliable genotype differentiation. Overall, HHV-6 DNA was detected at a low prevalence of 2.78% among male blood donors in Kassala. No clear demographic, geographic, or hematological patterns were observed in association with HHV-6 positivity, and genotyping could not be completed due to low viral load.

Discussion

This study provides the first molecular evidence of HHV-6 DNA among blood donors in Sudan, revealing a low prevalence of 2.78%. Detection of HHV-6 DNA by real-time PCR in plasma indicates viremia; however, this method alone cannot definitively distinguish between active lytic infection, latent virus, or ciHHV-6 [29]. While ciHHV-6 occurs in approximately 1% of the general population and typically results in persistently high viral DNA loads, the low viral loads observed in this cohort are more consistent with low-level viremia than chromosomal integration. This low prevalence must be interpreted within the context of Sudan’s unique epidemiological landscape, where high rates of other co-infections (such as Malaria or Leishmaniasis) and nutritional deficiencies might influence host immune surveillance and viral latency. However, such interactions remain largely unstudied in the local population. Because serological testing for HHV-6 IgG/IgM was not performed, it remains unclear whether the detected DNA represents active replication, latency, or integration. Consequently, the clinical and transfusion-related implications of these findings remain speculative and warrant further investigation.

The current study finding aligns with reports from low-endemic regions such as Greece (3.49%) [23] and Burkina Faso (6.1%) [22], but remains lower than the higher prevalence rates documented in Qatar (24.3%) [20], China (16.2%) [24], and Zambia, where HHV-6A predominated [21]. These variations are likely driven by factors beyond geographical differences; they may stem from differences in the limit of detection (LOD) of various PCR assays and the type of clinical specimen used. For instance, studies using Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMCs) often report higher prevalence than those using plasma, as PBMCs capture the virus’s latent reservoir.

In the current study, HHV-6 detection showed no clear association with donor age, geographic region, or ABO/Rh blood groups. The analysis of ABO/Rh groups was included to explore whether certain carbohydrate antigens serve as receptors or co-receptors for HHV-6, similar to the associations found between ABO blood groups and susceptibility to other viruses, such as SARS-CoV-1 and Norovirus [30]. However, the absence of a correlation here suggests that the CD46 or CD134 receptors used by HHV-6 are likely expressed independently of these blood group antigens. Furthermore, the very small number of positive cases (n = 5) significantly limits the study’s statistical power, making it difficult to identify subtle associations or draw definitive conclusions about demographic risks. This small sample size is a major limitation, leading to wide confidence intervals that may obscure true biological effects.

Hematological parameters, specifically platelet counts, were evaluated because HHV-6 has been associated with transient bone marrow suppression and thrombocytopenia in immunocompromised populations [15]. In this study, HHV-6–positive donors exhibited slightly reduced total white blood cell (WBC) counts and modest lymphocytosis compared with negative donors. Although small differences were observed, they align with transient hematological alterations typically observed during HHV-6 reactivation or low-level viremia [15]. Critically, however, because our study was cross-sectional, we cannot determine whether HHV-6 caused these hematological shifts or whether they were pre-existing conditions in the donors. Given the limited number of positive cases, these results are considered exploratory rather than indicative of a true biological effect.

Genotyping attempts for HHV-6A and HHV-6B were unsuccessful for all five positive samples, most likely due to the low viral loads reflected by high Ct values (38.7–39.9). This highlights a critical technical challenge in donor screening: the viral load in asymptomatic carriers is often near the limit of detection of standard real-time PCR assays. Future research should consider more sensitive molecular approaches—such as droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) or nested PCR—to enable reliable genotype differentiation in low-copy samples.

Although HHV-6 is not routinely screened in blood donation programs, the virus has been recognized as clinically significant in immunocompromised individuals [12–15]. Studies from Egypt and Germany have documented high reactivation rates and clinically significant outcomes in transplant settings [15, 31]. While the present study does not directly assess transfusion transmission, detecting HHV-6 DNA in blood donors highlights a potential concern for high-risk recipients. Accordingly, these results support the need for further research to determine whether HHV-6 viremia in donors may contribute to transfusion-related infection or reactivation in vulnerable patients. Overall, while this study establishes an essential baseline, its findings must be viewed as a preliminary assessment that necessitates larger, multicenter follow-ups to overcome the limitations of the current sample size.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the relatively small number of HHV-6–positive cases (n = 5) limits statistical power and prevents meaningful subgroup analysis. Second, the use of convenience sampling at a single center may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions of Sudan, especially rural areas, because the samples are unlikely to reflect the diverse socioeconomic and healthcare profiles of the broader population. Third, the exclusive inclusion of male donors does not reflect the general population prevalence and may skew results, particularly regarding health and risk factors. Fourth, the inability to genotype HHV-6A and HHV-6B was likely due to the low viral load and limited sensitivity of the multiplex PCR assay used. Fifth, no formal sample size calculation was performed due to the unknown prevalence of HHV-6 in Sudan, which may affect the precision of the prevalence estimate. Finally, the absence of serological testing prevented the distinction between active, latent, and chromosomally integrated HHV-6.

Conclusion

HHV-6 DNA was detected at a low prevalence (2.78%) among male blood donors in Kassala, Sudan. However, the small number of positive cases (n = 5), failed genotyping, and lack of serological confirmation indicate that these findings are preliminary. Larger multicenter studies using more sensitive molecular methods are needed to characterize HHV-6 epidemiology in Sudan.
