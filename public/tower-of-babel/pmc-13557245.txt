Less Is More: Cost-Effective Psoriasis Management via Disease-Driven Dosing of IL-17/IL-23 Inhibitors
Authors: Edoardo Cammarata, Chiara Airoldi, Jacopo Colombo, Andrealuna Ucciero, Luca Mastorino, Veronica Arese, Lorenza Burzi, Franco Castelli, Massimo Chiarpenello, Francesca Graziola, Claudia Leporati, Michela Ortoncelli, Paolo Pella, Ginevra Pertusi, Alessia Pisterna, Pietro Quaglino, Simone Ribero, Gianluca Rossotto, Rossana Tiberio, Paolo Dapavo, Paola Savoia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic plaque psoriasis is commonly treated with IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, which are highly effective but associated with substantial costs. Because many patients achieve long-term disease control, fixed-dose regimens may result in overtreatment.ObjectivesThis study assessed the economic impact of disease-activity-guided dose reduction (DR) strategies using secukinumab, brodalumab, guselkumab, and risankizumab in adults with stable plaque psoriasis.MethodsThis cost analysis was based on data from the PSORED trial, a multicenter prospective open-label study conducted in Piedmont, Italy (2023–2025). Patients with stable disease (PASI ≤3, DLQI ≤3 for ≥9 months) underwent DR trough a 1.5x extension of maintenance dosing intervals. Costs under DR and standard dosing (SD) were compared over 52 weeks using Italian tariff data and real-world treatment patterns.ResultsAmong 156 enrolled patients, 39 discontinued DR, 38 returned to standard dosing, and one switched therapy. DR reduced biologics consumption without increasing follow-up requirements, yielding a 26% decrease in total costs and an average annual saving of €7,303.57 per patient.ConclusionsDR was both clinically effective and cost-saving, maintaining disease control without major safety concerns. These findings support the implementation of DR strategies in routine clinical practice to enhance long-term sustainability while preserving therapeutic efficacy.Keywords: Cost-effectiveness analysis, Dose reduction, Disease activity-guided therapy, IL-17/IL-23 inhibitors, Psoriasis treatment

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the skin, with chronic plaque psoriasis accounting for approximately 90% of all cases. It is frequently associated with comorbidities such as cardiometabolic disorders and psoriatic arthritis. Over the past decade, biological therapies, particularly IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, have significantly advanced the management of moderate-to-severe psoriasis, demonstrating high efficacy [1–4]. However, their substantial cost places a considerable burden on healthcare systems, prompting interest in more sustainable treatment approaches. Although these biologics are generally administered at fixed doses, many patients who achieve sustained disease control may not require continuous standard dosing, potentially resulting in overtreatment. Emerging evidence suggests that dose-reduction (DR) strategies can preserve clinical effectiveness while improving cost efficiency [5–8].

Objectives

The objective of this study was to assess the economic impact of disease activity-guided DR using secukinumab, brodalumab, guselkumab, and risankizumab in adults with stable plaque psoriasis.

Methods

Study Design

This study presents a cost-utility analysis based on the PSORED trial, an observational prospective open-label study evaluating the efficacy and safety of dose-reduction (DR) strategies for secukinumab, brodalumab, guselkumab, and risankizumab in patients with plaque psoriasis. The trial [9] was led by the University of Eastern Piedmont in collaboration with major biologic-prescribing centers in Piedmont region and was conducted from March 2023 to February 2025. It received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Comitato Etico Interaziendale AOU Maggiore della Carità, ASL Bi, ASL NO, ASL VCO; CE 102/2023) and was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The biologics included in this study were selected based on existing evidence supporting DR strategies [5–8]. Eligible patients had stable, low disease activity on an authorized standard dose (SD), defined as PASI ≤3 and DLQI ≤3 for at least nine months. Dose reduction was implemented through dose spacing (DS), extending maintenance dosing intervals by a factor of 1.5, corresponding to 67% of the full standard dose. Drug consumption was recorded at each study visit. This study provides an economic assessment of the PSORED study findings, focusing on a preplanned secondary cost-utility analysis comparing the DR and SD treatment strategies.

Study Population

Patients involved in the PSORED study were recruited in collaboration with dermatologists specialized in biological treatment for psoriasis Each participant received detailed oral and written information from the local investigator and provided written informed consent prior to enrolment. The dosing regimen was individualized according to the specific biological therapy each patient was receiving. Participation was entirely voluntary, and patients were free to withdraw from the study at any time without consequences. Investigators also retained the right to withdraw participants if urgent medical reasons arose. This analysis is based on preliminary data collected during the first 52 weeks of the study [9]. Additional visits were scheduled as needed in response to disease flares occurring outside the planned follow-up timeline.

Statistical Analysis

Data were recorded using Redcap, a validated, secure, web-based data capture system which is compliant with all relevant regulatory requirements. Descriptive statistics for the patient cohorts are reported in Cammarata et al. [9]. For each biologic, the analysis evaluated whether a switch from dose reduction to the full dose or to an alternative therapy was indicated. Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies, while numerical variables are summarized using mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range, depending on their distributions. All analysis were performed using SAS 9.4 and STATA 15.

Individual treatment costs were calculated by multiplying the total number of subcutaneous injections administered over the 52-week study period by the unit price listed in the most recent revision of the Italian national tariff list, based on the official prices published in the Italian Official Gazette. These prices may overestimate actual costs, as regional health authorities in Italy often negotiate discounted procurement costs. All patients included were in the maintenance phase of treatment for stable plaque psoriasis. Under standard dosing regimens, the expected annual number of administrations is 13 for secukinumab, 26 for brodalumab, 7 for guselkumab (rounded from 6.5) and 5 for risankizumab (rounded from 4.33). The dose reduction strategy extended maintenance intervals by 1.5-fold, reducing expected annual administrations to: 9 for secukinumab (rounded from 8.67), 18 for brodalumab (rounded from 17.33), 5 for guselkumab (rounded to 4.33) and 3 for risankizumab (rounded to 2.89 ). These estimates were calculated by dividing the 52-week period by the adjusted dosing intervals and rounding up to the nearest whole number to reflect real-world practice. However, it is important to note that long-term treatment costs under DR regimens are likely even lower.

Economic benefits were assessed by comparing two scenarios: a standard maintenance regimen (Scenario 1), and a dose reduction regimen (Scenario 2). In the DR group, costs calculation reflected the actual treatment sequence. For patients who remained on DR for the full year, total costs were derived from the reduced number of doses multiplied by the unit cost. For those who discontinued DR at 13, 26, or 39 weeks, costs were calculated according to their individualized treatment path. If patients resumed the same biologic, standard maintenance dosing costs were applied for the remaining weeks. In cases of drug switching, both induction and maintenance dosing costs of the new biologic were included. Overall costs were calculated as the sum of individual costs under both scenarios. Scenario 1 represents a counterfactual situation in which all patients followed the standard full-dose regimen, while Scenario 2 reflects the observed treatment patterns, including DR. Cost comparison between the two scenarios are expressed as absolute and relative differences, including percentage reduction. Additional study visits were not associated with significant extra costs and were therefore considered negligible relative to drug expenses. Results are reported as means cost differences and percentage changes, with all costs presented in euros.

Results

A total of 156 patients were included, each with 52 weeks of follow-up. The most frequently used drugs were secukinumab (50 patients, 32.05%) and guselkumab (49 patients, 31.41%), followed by risankizumab (37 patients, 23.72%) and brodalumab (20 patients, 12.82%). Thirty-nine patients discontinued the DR regimen: four at week 13, 27 at the first follow-up (week 26), one at week 39, and seven at the second follow-up visit (week 52). Of these, 38 patients resumed the full drug dose, while only one patient (2.56%) switched therapy, from secukinumab to risankizumab, to regain adequate disease control.

Under Scenario 1, assuming all the patients remained on the full-dose regimens throughout the 52 weeks, the estimated total cost was approximately €4,280,862.62. In Scenario 2, reflecting the observed DR strategy, the total cost was approximately €3,141,505.48, representing a savings of €1,139,357.14 compared to Scenario 1, or a 26% reduction in overall cost. On a per-patient basis, this corresponds to an average annual saving of approximately €7,303.58.

Discussion

While extending the dosing interval of biological therapies can substantially reduce medication-related costs, this approach could potentially increase healthcare utilization, such as more frequent patient consultations or monitoring, partially offsetting the savings. In our study, however, the overall cost-effectiveness of the dose-reduction strategy remained highly favorable. The additional cost of up to five extra visits was minimal and did not meaningfully impact the overall economic budget.

This cost analysis demonstrates that a tightly controlled DR strategy for biologics, and specifically secukinumab, brodalumab, guselkumab, and risankizumab, in patients with stable plaque psoriasis can lead to substantial cost savings. In our study, total saving amounted to €1,139,357.14, representing a 26% reduction in overall treatment cost. On a per-patient basis, the average annual saving over 52 weeks was significant, indicating that DR can meaningfully reduce healthcare expenditures without compromising clinical outcomes. These findings support DR as a cost-effective strategy that balances clinical efficacy with healthcare resource optimization [1]. Previous studies have also explored the economic and clinical implications of DR strategies [10–18]. For example, Atalay et al. [14] reported an average cost saving of €3,820 per patient over 12 months, achieved without significantly affecting quality of life, as reflected by only minimal reductions in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). To our knowledge, this study represents the first prospective economic evaluation in Italy assessing DR for biological therapies in psoriasis using a counterfactual scenario based on standard full-dose care. In our cohort, the cost savings were even greater than those previously reported, likely due to the higher costs of newer biological therapies. Importantly, the DR approach was non-inferior with respect to patient-reported outcomes, including the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) and did not result in persistent disease flares or introduce new safety concerns. These results underscore the potential for DR strategies to be integrated into routine clinical practice as a cost-conscious yet clinically robust alternative to continuous full-dose treatment. The demonstrated cost savings may serve as a compelling incentive for healthcare systems and policymakers to support the broader implementation of personalized, response-guided treatment plans in psoriasis care. This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the observational design may introduce bias. A randomized controlled trial (RCT) would provide a more robust comparison between costs of patients undergoing dose reduction and those continuing full-dose therapy; however, such a trial was not feasible in this context. Consequently, our analysis is based on a counterfactual comparison, and the actual cost differences may be underestimated. For example, some patients on full-dose therapy in routine practice may switch biologics due to loss of efficacy or adverse events–costs not captured in our model. Second, the economic evaluation was conducted over a relatively short follow-up period of 12 months. While appropriate for assessing immediate cost implications and short-term safety, this timeframe may not fully capture the long-term consequences of dose reduction, such as increased reliance on topical therapies or reduced arthropathic and cardiovascular protective effects associated with biologics [13,19–22]. Finally, the generalizability of these finding to other countries is limited. The study was conducted within the Italian healthcare system, and differences in healthcare infrastructure, reimbursement policies, drug pricing, and clinical practice patterns may affect both the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of DR strategies elsewhere. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable real-world evidence on the economic and clinical implications of a DR approach in biological therapy for psoriasis. Future research, ideally incorporating randomized controlled designs and longer follow-up periods, is warranted to validate and expand upon these findings.
