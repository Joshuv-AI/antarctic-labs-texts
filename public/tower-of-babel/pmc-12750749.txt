Association of disability with COVID-19 outcomes in older adults: a prospective analysis of the US health and retirement study
Authors: Wenjie Zeng, Inyoung Jun, Rosha A Loach, Aryaman S Sriram, Nichole E Stetten, Panayiotis V Benos, Nicole M Marlow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately impacted the ageing population and persons with disability. Previous studies have used cross-sectional or descriptive designs, limiting the establishment of robust associations. Our objective was to examine prospective associations between functional disability and COVID-19 outcomes in ageing US adults.MethodsThis study included 9390 Health and Retirement Study participants from the 2018 wave, representing 64 044 633 adults aged 50 years or older, who completed the 2021 Pandemic Mail-in Survey. Baseline functional disability comprised activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental ADL. COVID-19 outcomes included infection, emergency room utilisation, hospitalisation, length of hospital stay and post-COVID conditions (PCC). Multivariate regression and propensity-score weighting were applied to assess prospective associations between baseline functional disability, vaccination and COVID-19 outcomes. Targeted learning was applied to assess the association between functional disability and COVID-19 within each vaccination stratum. Mediation analysis was used to quantify the proportion of effects of functional disability on COVID-19 infection and PCC, mediated through vaccination.Results1460 (16.2%) participants reported infection, 7985 (86.1%) were vaccinated and 178 (11.4%) of COVID-19 infected had PCC. Functional disability was associated with increased risk of infection (OR: 1.28, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.64) and PCC (OR: 2.24, 95% CI 1.05 to 4.47), while vaccination was protective against infection (OR: 0.53, 95% CI 0.42 to 0.67). Vaccination mediated a small portion of the effect of functional disability on infection and PCC, while showing no effect modification.ConclusionsComprehensive strategies beyond vaccination are needed to decrease infections and prevent PCC for vulnerable ageing adults with functional disability. As the first study to prospectively investigate associations between functional disability, vaccination status and COVID-19 outcomes using nationally representative data, these findings provide a robust foundation for future research and policy decisions to protect vulnerable populations during public health emergencies.

Introduction

In the USA, 29.1% and 43.9% of adults aged 45–64 and ≥65 years, respectively, have a disability.1 The COVID-19 pandemic disproportionately impacted persons with disability (PWD).2 3 Although the WHO announced an end to the public health emergency in May 2023, the virus circulates indefinitely. As challenges posed by COVID-19 continue, research addressing specific vulnerabilities of PWD is crucial to ensure vulnerable subgroups receive necessary resources.

Factors associated with functional disability may increase risks of COVID-19 and related outcomes. PWD are more likely to have underlying health conditions and live in congregate settings than persons without disabilities (PWoD).46 Previous studies reported that PWD experienced disproportionately higher rates of COVID-19 incidence, hospitalisation and post-COVID conditions (PCC),710 suggesting disability as a risk factor for COVID-19. However, most studies used cross-sectional7 or descriptive8 9 designs, or did not account for vaccination,10 limiting their ability to establish robust associations between functional disability, vaccination and COVID outcomes.

Leveraging data from respondents in the 2018 wave of the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) who participated in the 2021 HRS Perspectives on the Pandemic Mail-in Survey (2021 Pandemic Survey)11—collected May 2021 to June 2022 and released April 2023—this study fills a critical gap by prospectively examining baseline functional disability as a risk factor of COVID-19 infection and COVID-19-related outcomes (particularly PCC), and by evaluating associations across vaccination strata. Despite more recent temporal patterns in COVID-19 epidemiology, our first aim was to inform the critical need for policies and comprehensive prevention strategies to alleviate adverse COVID-19 outcomes disproportionately affecting ageing persons with functional disability. Additionally, given their longstanding experience of care and outcome disparities, our secondary aim was to identify new disparities facing this vulnerable and underserved population that require increased attention by the clinical community, prior to future public health emergencies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We prospectively analysed HRS data, a nationally representative longitudinal survey involving >37 000 US individuals ≥50 years from >23 000 households.11 12 17 146 participants from the 2018 wave were evaluated, and only those participating in the 2021 Pandemic Survey were included (n=10 459). We excluded participants with missing data on baseline functional disability or covariates (2018), with incomplete/inconsistent COVID-19 outcome or vaccination information (2021 Pandemic Survey), and <50 years. The final analysis included 9309 respondents (representing 64 044 633 ageing adults). This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Exposure

We retrieved information on functional disability from the baseline year (2018). Functional disability (yes/no) was reported based on any difficulty with activities of daily living (ADL) and/or instrumental ADL (IADL).13 14 A series of ‘any difficulty’ questions assessed six ADLs (ie, dressing, ambulating, bathing, eating, transferring, toileting) and five IADLs (ie, preparing hot meals, shopping for groceries, making phone calls, taking medications, managing money). Respondents reporting ‘yes’ or ‘don’t do due to a health or memory problem’ were considered as having difficulty, while those reporting ‘no’ were considered not to have difficulty.

Outcomes

Primary and secondary COVID-19 outcomes were obtained through the 2021 Pandemic Survey. The primary outcome was self-reported COVID-19 infection, and secondary outcomes included COVID-19-related emergency room (ER) utilisation, hospitalisation, length of hospital stay (LOS) and PCC. The questions assessed by outcome included ‘Have you had, or do you now have COVID-19, the disease caused by the novel coronavirus?’ (infection), ‘Did you have to go to an emergency room because of the virus?’ (ER utilisation), ‘Were you admitted to a hospital because of the virus?’ (hospitalisation), ‘How many nights did you spend in the hospital?’ (LOS), and ‘Have you experienced any lingering physical or mental health effects from the virus?’ (PCC). Respondents reporting ‘yes’ or ‘probably yes (I think so)’ were treated as positive for the respective outcome, while those reporting ‘no’ or ‘probably no (I don’t think so)’ were negative.

Vaccination status

The 2021 Pandemic Survey was used to obtain vaccination status: ‘Several vaccines are now available for the coronavirus. Have you gotten the vaccine?’. Respondents responding ‘yes, I’ve had two shots’, ‘yes, I’ve had one shot and am waiting for the second’, or ‘yes, I’ve had one shot and don’t need a second’ were considered vaccinated; those reporting ‘I have an appointment to get one soon’ or ‘no’ were considered not vaccinated; while those who left the question blank or selected multiple choices were treated as missing.

Covariates

We used baseline (2018) covariates of common risk factors between functional disability and COVID-19 (table 1),15 including age (years), gender (male, female), race (White, African-American, other), ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic), educational attainment (no degree, GED/high school diploma, college/associate degree, master’s, doctoral), marital status (married/partnered, separated/divorced, widowed, never married), household size (1, 2, ≥3 persons per household), total annual income (low: <US$52,200, middle: US$52 200 –US$156 600, high: ≥$156,600),16 employment status (employed, sick leave or other leave, unemployed or temporarily laid off, homemaker, retired, disabled), body mass index (BMI), smoking status (current, former, never), drinking status (abstainer, occasional, moderate, heavy), Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression scale (CES-D) score, and number of chronic conditions (sum of the following nine conditions: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric problems, arthritis and sleep disorder. All covariates were self-reported, including sociodemographic characteristics, baseline health and health behaviours.

Disability types

We also collected baseline data on physical, cognitive and sensory limitations. Physical limitation was coded ‘yes’, when respondents reported difficulty with any two or more of the following twelve activities: walking several blocks, jogging one mile, walking one block, sitting for 2 hours, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, climbing a flight of stairs, stooping, reaching arms above shoulder level, pushing or pulling large objects, lifting weights or picking up a dime from a table. A cognitive score was derived for each respondent by summing performance scores across four cognitive tasks: the immediate 10-noun free recall test, the delayed 10-noun free recall test, the serial 7 subtraction test and the backward counting from 20 test. A total score ranging from 0 to 11 (inclusive) was classified as cognitive limitation. When the participant was unable to complete the cognitive assessments themselves, a proxy respondent was asked to provide cognitive evaluations, by rating the participant’s memory (‘excellent’=0, ‘very good’=1, ‘good’=2, ‘fair’=3, ‘poor’=4), reporting the number of IADL limitations they had, and assessing the participant’s difficulty in completing the interview due to cognitive issues (‘no reason to think the respondent has any cognitive limitations’=0; ‘the respondent may have some cognitive limitations but could probably do the interview’=1; ‘the respondent has cognitive limitations that prevent him/her from being interviewed’=2). A proxy-rated cognition score of 0–2 was classified as normal, while a score of 3 or higher was classified as indicative of cognitive limitation. Sensory limitation was evaluated through the following two questions, ‘Is your hearing excellent, very good, good, fair or poor (using a hearing aid as usual)?’ and ‘Is your eyesight excellent, very good, good, fair or poor (using glasses or corrective lenses as usual)?’ Participants who reported ‘poor’ for either hearing or vision were classified as having a sensory limitation, while those who reported ‘excellent’, ‘very good’, ‘good’ or ‘fair’ were classified as not having a sensory limitation. Further incorporating measures of functional disability (ie, ADL/IADL difficulty, defined above), disability type was grouped into one of the following mutually exclusive categories: multiple disabilities (when respondents reported more than one limitation), physical limitation only, cognitive limitation only, sensory limitation only, functional disability only, and no disability.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with R (V.4.2.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Figure 1 illustrates the study flow and analytical pipeline.

Association of baseline functional disability with COVID-19 outcomes

We first conducted regression analysis in the full sample and subsample with COVID-19 infection. We used the 2018 HRS personal-level sampling weights to generate a weighted population. Multivariable logistic regression was conducted on the weighted full sample of 9309 participants (64 044 633 ageing adults) to model the prospective association of baseline functional disability with COVID-19 infection, adjusting for baseline age, gender, race, ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, household size, total annual income, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, CES-D score and number of chronic conditions (sum of the following nine conditions: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric problems, arthritis and sleep disorder). Multivariable logistic or Poisson regression models were then constructed on the weighted subsample of 1460 respondents (representing 10 405 388 ageing adults) to assess the prospective association of baseline complex activity disability with ER utilisation, hospitalisation, LOS and PCC, adjusting for the above covariates.

We further applied propensity score (PS) weighting to ensure balance across the above covariates and infer robust associations between baseline functional disability and COVID-19 outcomes. Following previous research,17 we included 2018 sampling weights in both PS calculation and outcome estimations, resulting in a PS-adjusted estimator resilient to selection bias and more robust than standard regression.18 19 To generate PS-adjusted sampling weights, we first calculated the nationally representative PS on the weighted population, then multiplied the original survey sampling weights by the nationally representative odds (ie, PS/[1−PS]).20 Post hoc balance before and after PS-adjustment was evaluated on the weighted full populations and subpopulations using standardised mean difference,20 where associations between baseline functional disability and COVID-19 outcomes were estimated. OR and 95% CIs were used to report prospective associations.

Association of disability type with COVID-19 outcomes

We further excluded 2023 participants with incomplete information on disability type and conducted logistic regression analysis in the full sample (n=7286 representing 50 113 986 ageing adults) and a subsample population reporting COVID-19 infection (n=1153, representing 8 202 143 ageing adults), respectively. The 2018 HRS personal-level sampling weights were applied to generate a nationally representative weighted study population. Multivariable logistic regression was then conducted on the weighted full sample to examine the prospective association between baseline disability type and COVID-19 infection, and on the weighted subsample with COVID-19 infection, to assess the prospective association between baseline disability type and PCC. Each disability type was compared with the ‘no disability’ category, which served as the reference.

Association of baseline functional disability with COVID-19 outcomes stratified by vaccination status

To assess associations between baseline functional disability and COVID-19 outcomes by vaccination stratum, we employed targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) using R package tmle,21 a machine-learning-based ‘doubly robust’ estimator that relies on estimating the exposure (ie, PS) and outcome models separately. Both the PS and outcome were modelled with the stacking algorithm,22 which combines different algorithms to leverage the strength of each algorithm and avoid strong assumptions required by an individual algorithm. Using the R package SuperLearner,23 we stacked five different algorithms into the TMLE estimator, including standard generalised regression, multivariate adaptive regression splines, random forest and extreme gradient boosting. Baseline functional disability was the exposure, while COVID-19 infection and PCC were the outcomes. All covariates were included, and vaccination status served as the intermediate variable for controlled direct effect estimation in each vaccination stratum. 10-fold cross-validation was applied for all estimations. 2018 HRS sampling weights were used to account for biased sampling and infer nationally representative statistics.

Causal mediation analysis

To test whether baseline functional disability affected COVID-19 outcomes via respondents’ vaccination status and quantify mediated proportions, we performed causal mediation analysis using R package mediation.24 Multivariable logistic regressions were fitted for both mediation and outcome models, adjusting for all covariates. The 2018 HRS sampling weights were employed to account for complex survey design and biased sampling.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate how employment status impacts the association of baseline functional disability with self-reported COVID-19 infection and PCC, respectively, we additionally adjusted for employment status in our multivariable logistic regression models. To further assess how robust the estimated associations between baseline functional disability and COVID-19 outcomes were to unmeasured confounders, we calculated ‘E-value’ conditioning on the measured covariates, defined as the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder needs to have with both exposure and outcome to fully explain away estimated exposure-outcome associations.25

Results

Mean age of respondents was 65.9 years, and 55.0% were female. 1460 (16.2%) reported COVID-19 positive, and 7985 (86.1%) reported having received ≥1 dose of COVID-19 vaccine. Among those who reported COVID-19 positive (n=1460, representing 10 405 388 individuals), 201 (12.2%) reported COVID-19-related ER utilisation, 123 (7.5%) reported hospitalisation, and 178 (11.4%) reported PCC. The median LOS was 4 nights. As compared with persons without functional disability, PWD were significantly older, less likely to be White and more likely to be Hispanic; were less likely to be married or partnered, had lower educational attainment, were more likely to live alone, had lower total annual income and were less likely to be employed. In addition, PWD reported a higher BMI, were more likely to be current or former smokers but also more likely to be abstainers, and presented with higher CES-D scores as well as a greater number of chronic conditions. Table 1 summarises detailed sociodemographic and behavioural characteristics.

In the adjusted full-sample model, baseline functional disability was significantly associated with increased risk of COVID-19 infection (OR: 1.28; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.64), while vaccination was associated with decreased risk (OR: 0.42; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.67). Among the subsample of respondents with COVID-19 infection, baseline functional disability was significantly associated with increased risk of PCC (OR: 2.24; 95% CI 1.05 to 4.77), but not ER utilisation, hospitalisation or LOS. Vaccination was not significantly associated with any secondary COVID-19 outcomes among those reporting COVID positive. Raw regression coefficients, SEs and t-statistics of each independent variable included in the regression analysis of COVID-19 infection and PCC are reported in online supplemental Appendix Table 1 and Table 2, respectively.

Applying PS-adjusted sampling weights ensured covariate balance between persons with and without functional disability, in the full sample (online supplemental Appendix Table 3) and subsample reporting positive for COVID-19 (online supplemental Appendix Table 4). In the full sample, baseline functional disability was significantly associated with increased risk of COVID-19 infection (OR: 1.39 95% CI 1.03 to 1.88), while vaccination was significantly associated with decreased risk of COVID-19 infection (OR: 0.52; 95% CI 0.38 to 0.74). In the subsample reporting COVID positive, baseline functional disability was significantly associated with increased risk of PCC (OR: 2.55; 95% CI 1.24 to 2.56), but vaccination was not (OR: 1.07; 95% CI 0.45 to 2.54). Sensitivity analysis revealed that additionally adjusting for employment status did not significantly affect the prospective association between baseline functional disability and COVID outcomes, which remained significantly and positively associated with risk of self-reported COVID-19 infection (OR: 1.34; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.70), while vaccination remained inversely associated (OR: 0.52; 95% CI 0.41 to 0.66). By employment category, being retired or a homemaker was significantly inversely associated with self-reported COVID-19 infection at p values of 0.002 and 0.037, respectively. In the regression analysis among 1460 COVID-19 infected individuals, baseline functional disability continued to show a significant positive association with risk of PCC (OR: 2.41; 95% CI 1.06 to 5.50), whereas the association between vaccination status and PCC remained insignificant. Employment status also showed no significant association with PCC.

The E-value was 1.52 for the observed association (OR) between baseline functional disability and COVID infection, and 1.05 for the lower bound of 95% CI, indicating that an unmeasured confounder with a RR at least 1.52 with both functional disability and COVID infection could nullify the observed association, and at least 1.05 to nullify the association at the lower bound of 95% CI. The E-value was 2.08 for OR between vaccination and COVID infection, and 1.73 for the upper bound of 95% CI, suggesting that an unmeasured confounder with an RR at least 2.08 with both vaccination status and COVID-19 infection could nullify the protective effect of vaccination, and at least 1.73 to nullify the protective effect at the upper bound of 95% CI. The E value was 2.36 for OR between baseline functional disability and PCC, and 1.19 for the lower bound of 95% CI, indicating that an unmeasured confounder with RR at least 2.36 with both functional disability and PCC could nullify the observed association, and at least 1.19 to nullify the association at the lower bound of 95% CI.

Discussion

Using nationally representative HRS data, we employed multiple advanced confounding adjustment techniques to prospectively examine relationships between baseline functional disability, COVID vaccination and COVID outcomes among US aging adults, during May 2021 to June 2022. Our analysis revealed that baseline functional disability was independently associated with increased risk of COVID-19 infection and PCC, after accounting for confounders and vaccination status. Vaccination was associated with reduced risk of COVID-19 infection but not PCC. Only a small portion of the effect of functional disability on COVID-19 was mediated through vaccination, while no effect modification by vaccination was observed. In the analysis by disability type, the functional disability only category was significantly associated with an increased risk of PCC among individuals who reported COVID-19 infection. This is the first study that prospectively examined associations between disability, vaccination and COVID-19 outcomes, to enlighten strategies for supporting ageing PWD throughout the COVID-19 era and future pandemics.

The COVID-19 pandemic has crippled the global population, and frailty in ageing adults and PWD further exposed them to increased risks. In the USA, 29.1% and 43.9% of adults aged 45–64 and ≥65 years, respectively, have a disability.1 During the pandemic, studies suggest that PWD had higher infection rates and more severe outcomes when infected.3 26 Previous studies reported higher COVID-19 infection rates among PWD residing in residential or long-term care facilities.27 28 However, few studies investigated community-dwelling PWD. One US ecological study reported higher rates of disability were associated with lower infection yet higher mortality rates.29 Another survey study from Italy found that females, PWD and unemployed individuals had significantly higher mean Lockdown Score, an estimate of social distancing and use of personal protective equipment.30 A meta-analysis reported that immunocompromised individuals experience prolonged periods of viral shedding and increased infectivity compared with immunocompetent individuals—a condition that likely affects ageing PWDs.31 To appraise the credibility of observed association and derive nationally representative statistics, it is essential to prospectively investigate a more representative population and assess consistency of findings. Employing methods accounting for complex survey design, incorporating covariates encompassing sociodemographic and behaviour factors, and validating association estimates using PS techniques, our analysis identified baseline functional disability as an independent risk factor for COVID-19 infection, while vaccination was a protective factor. We also showed that the association between baseline functional disability and COVID-19 infection persisted across vaccination strata, emphasising equal importance of effective measures for reducing infection in PWD, regardless of vaccination status.

Studies have reported that PWD experienced not only disproportionately higher rates of COVID-19 incidence, but also of hospitalisation and PCC.79 Healthcare utilisation for reported long-term symptoms was higher among PWD positive for COVID, compared with PWoD who tested positive.7 These findings highlighted disability as a potential risk factor not only for COVID-19 infection but, more importantly, PCC. However, these studies were predominantly cross-sectional7 or descriptive,8 9 limiting their ability to establish a robust prospective association between disability and PCC. Disability may either precede PCC or be a result of PCC. Additionally, most did not adequately account for key confounders or vaccination status.810 Using nationally representative HRS data in a prospective analysis, our study addressed this critical gap and found that, after adjusting for confounders, individuals with baseline functional disability and individuals with baseline functional disability alone faced a higher risk of PCC compared with those without disability.

Previous research suggested that PWD and their caregivers faced health and social inequities preventing them from receiving equitable and timely COVID-19 vaccination.32 Data from the National Immunisation Survey Adult COVID Module revealed that COVID-19 vaccination coverage was lower among PWD vs PWoD, despite PWD being more likely to express a desire to get vaccinated and view vaccines as protective.33 Similarly, we observed significantly lower vaccination rates among ageing PWD (80.5%) compared with ageing persons without functional disability (87.1%). Furthermore, vaccination remained inversely associated with risk of infection, after adjusting for baseline functional disability and age, underscoring its effectiveness in reducing infection rates among ageing adults, irrespective of age or disability status. However, the protective effect of vaccination against PCC was not statistically significant, indicating that vaccination may not provide additional protection against PCC for ageing PWD.

While our study found vaccination to be a significant protective factor against COVID infection among ageing adults, regardless of age and disability, its effectiveness in further reducing infection or PCC risk based on baseline functional disability appeared to be limited. Only a small portion of the effect on COVID-19 infection rates (11.5%) or PCC (1.4%) was mediated through vaccination, suggesting that other factors contributed to the vulnerability of ageing PWD to COVID-19, especially PCC. To effectively reduce these risks, a comprehensive approach addressing multiple factors beyond vaccination is warranted.

We additionally adjusted for employment status and found that the prospective associations between functional disability, vaccination and COVID-19 infection or PCC were largely unchanged. We also noted that individuals who reported ‘retired’ or ‘homemaker’ had a lower risk of COVID-19 infection. This finding aligns with a prior study indicating that people with multiple sclerosis who were unemployed or retired had a reduced likelihood of COVID-19-related hospitalisation.34 However, we did not observe a significant association between employment status and PCC.

We acknowledge the following important limitations of our study. First, most variables were self-reported. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility of information bias and misclassification. Second, despite accounting for all known confounders for functional disability and COVID-19 and using multiple techniques (including double-robust methods), our findings may still be influenced by residual confounding. Thus, we reported E-values, which suggest that an unmeasured confounder needs to be associated with both baseline functional disability and COVID infection or PCC at an RR of at least 1.52 or 2.36, to fully explain away the observed associations. An unmeasured confounder needs to be associated with both vaccination and COVID infection at an RR of at least 2.08, to fully explain away its protective effect. Third, in the analysis by disability type, we observed that the functional disability only category was significantly associated with a higher risk of PCC. However, this category included only 10 individuals, suggesting that most respondents who reported functional disability also experienced other types of disabilities. This limitation raises concerns about potential model overfitting and biased results. To validate our finding on the association between disability types and COVID-19, a larger sample size and more comprehensive study are needed. Fourth, our definition of PCC did not specify the duration of symptoms, while the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention defined PCC as new, returning or ongoing health problems occurring≥4 weeks after being infected with SARS-CoV-2.35 However, it is reasonable to assume that by the time respondents were able to complete the survey, at least 4 weeks had already passed. Finally, due to the observational nature of our study, the inferred associations cannot be finalised as causal.

In conclusion, by prospectively examining nationally representative HRS data and employing diverse analytical techniques to establish robust associations, we identified baseline functional disability as an independent risk factor for both COVID-19 infection and PCC, whose associations persisted across vaccination strata. Vaccination mediated only a small portion of the effect of functional disability on infection and PCC, calling for comprehensive strategies to effectively reduce infection and prevent PCC. This is the first study that prospectively investigated the relationship between functional disability, vaccination and COVID-19 outcomes, in a nationally representative population. Our study provides a robust foundation for future research and policy decisions aimed at protecting the most vulnerable populations during public health emergencies. Nevertheless, important gaps remain, including the need for improved data collection that can validate self-reported COVID-19 infection and vaccination status, as well as extended follow-up to assess the long-term effects of functional disability on COVID-19-related outcomes (especially PCC and long COVID), and evidence from multiple international databases and cross-cohort meta-analyses to ensure generalisability across countries. Further research is essential to develop targeted interventions and address disparities to better support ageing PWD.
