Guided by Trajectories: Repairing and Rewarding Tool-Use Trajectories for Tool-Integrated Reasoning
Siyu Gong, Linan Yue*, Weibo Gao, Fangzhou Yao, Shimin Di, Lei Feng, Min-Ling Zhang

Abstract
Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) enables large language models (LLMs) to solve complex tasks by interacting with external tools, yet existing approaches depend on high-quality synthesized trajectories selected by scoring functions and sparse outcome-based rewards, providing limited and biased supervision for learning TIR. To address these challenges, we propose AutoTraj, a two-stage framework that automatically learns TIR by repairing and rewarding tool-use trajectories. Specifically, in the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage, AutoTraj generates multiple candidate tool-use trajectories for each query and evaluates them along multiple dimensions. High-quality trajectories are directly retained, while low-quality ones are repaired using an LLM (i.e., LLM-as-Repairer). The resulting repaired and high-quality trajectories form a synthetic SFT dataset, while each repaired trajectory paired with its original low-quality counterpart constitutes a dataset for trajectory preference modeling. In the reinforcement learning (RL) stage, based on the preference dataset, we train a trajectory-level reward model to assess the quality of reasoning paths and combine it with outcome and format rewards, thereby explicitly guiding the optimization toward reliable TIR behaviors. Experiments on real-world benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of AutoTraj in TIR. Code is available at https://github.com/GSYY0607/AutoTraj_code .

In the SFT stage

Instead of simply discarding low-quality trajectories, we introduce a trajectory repair mechanism that leverages an LLM to repair and complete their TIR trajectories (LLM-as-Repairer).

\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}

In the RL stage

\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose AutoTraj, a two-stage training framework for tool-integrated reasoning that reuses low-quality trajectories via automatic repair and incorporates trajectory-level rewards into RL, thereby providing more informative supervision and mitigating reward sparsity.

•

We construct two complementary datasets: a high-quality tool-use trajectory dataset for SFT, and a trajectory pair dataset consisting of positive–negative trajectory pairs for training a trajectory reward model. The learned reward model can also provide fine-grained trajectory-level rewards for training other TIR models.

•

Extensive experiments on diverse benchmark datasets, covering mathematical reasoning and knowledge-intensive reasoning, show that the proposed framework consistently achieves substantial improvements in both tool-use capability and reasoning quality.

Related Work

2.1
Tool-Integrated Reasoning

Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR)
(
30
;
27
;
23
;
18
)
has recently emerged as a prominent paradigm for enhancing the complex reasoning capabilities of LLMs by enabling them to autonomously invoke external tools. Existing studies on TIR can be broadly categorized into three lines of research.
The first line of work relies on supervised fine-tuning (SFT-based TIR) over manually annotated reasoning trajectories with explicit tool calls, such as
(
4
;
11
;
22
;
30
)
, which can effectively teach models specific tool-use patterns but suffer from high annotation costs and limited scalability to open-world scenarios.
The second line, RL-based TIR, adopts reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, where the correctness of the final answer or feedback from an external verifier serves as the reward signal, as in ToRL
(
24
)
, ToolRL
(
28
)
, ReSearch
(
3
)
, Search-R1
(
19
)
, ARTIST
(
33
)
, and AutoTIR
(
39
)
. These outcome-only rewards are extremely sparse, offering little guidance for intermediate reasoning steps and resulting in unstable tool-use trajectories.
Finally, the third stream combines SFT with RL, initializing models with synthesized trajectories and then further optimizing them via RL, as in Tool-Star
(
8
)
and ReTool
(
10
)
. Although this hybrid paradigm is effective in practice, it still inherits the dependence on high-quality synthesized trajectories selected by scoring functions and the coarse-grained nature of the outcome-only reward. In contrast, our proposed AutoTraj repairs low-quality synthesized trajectories instead of simply filtering them, and further introduces a trajectory-level reward to provide fine-grained supervision, alleviate reward sparsity, and guide the model toward more reliable TIR.

2.2
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards

The core idea of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is to incorporate rule-based reward signals that can be automatically verified, such as answer correctness and format validity, into the RL framework. In reasoning tasks, RL has been shown to be an effective paradigm for enhancing the multi-step reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Representative works such as DeepSeek-R1
(
12
)
and QwQ
(
35
)
demonstrate that directly optimizing policies with respect to outcome correctness can significantly improve overall reasoning performance. Building on this idea, RLVR has been further extended to tool-integrated reasoning scenarios to learn more stable and effective tool invocation and reasoning strategies (as reviewed in Section
2.1
). However, existing RLVR methods mostly rely on outcome-only verifiable rewards, which results in highly sparse training signals and makes it difficult to effectively constrain intermediate reasoning processes and tool-use trajectories. Therefore, recent studies have begun to introduce process-level or trajectory-level supervision
(
43
;
38
;
7
;
37
)
to encourage models to generate more reliable intermediate reasoning behaviors. However, trajectory-level reward design for TIR remains under-explored and is still insufficient to provide effective supervision over tool-use and multi-step reasoning processes.

3
Problem Formulation

Given a query
q
, its ground-truth answer
y
, and an environment
\varphi(\cdot)
that provides access to a set of external tools, our goal is to train a TIR model
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot)
. The model is expected not only to predict the final answer, but also to generate a corresponding tool-integrated reasoning trajectory
\tau
that explicitly captures the reasoning and tool-use process.
Formally, a reasoning trajectory
\tau
is defined as a sequence of
K
reasoning steps:

\displaystyle\tau={(r_{k},t_{k},e_{k})}_{k=1}^{K},

(1)

where at step
k
,
r_{k}
denotes the natural language reasoning generated by the model,
t_{k}
denotes the invoked tool action, and
e_{k}
represents the execution result returned by the environment
\varphi
after applying
t_{k}
.

The trajectory is generated in an iterative manner. At each step
k
, the model conditions on the input query and the previously generated partial trajectory
\tau_{k-1}
, and produces the next reasoning and tool invocation:

\displaystyle(r_{k},t_{k})=\pi_{\theta}(q\oplus\tau_{k-1}),\quad e_{k}=\varphi(t_{k}),

(2)

where
\oplus
represents concatenation.
This iterative generation loop continues until the model produces a final answer or reaches a predefined maximum context length.

In this paper, we employ a search engine and code execution as the tools, which serve as representative tools to achieve AutoTraj.

4
Framework of AutoTraj

In this section, as shown in Figure
1
, we present the details of AutoTraj, consisting of two stages:

\bullet
Supervised Fine-Tuning via Synthesized Trajectory (SFT Stage): In this stage, we automatically generate and filter TIR trajectories and repair low-quality ones. The resulting supervision dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}
is composed of both the original high-quality trajectories and the repaired ones.
This dataset is then used for supervised cold start. Meanwhile, we create the trajectory pairs by pairing repaired trajectories with their corresponding original low-quality trajectories, forming a dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}
for trajectory preference modeling.

\bullet
Trajectory-Aware Reinforcement Learning (RL stage): In this stage, we first train a trajectory reward model based on
\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}
that can be used to evaluate the quality of reasoning trajectories. We then optimize the TIR model using the RL method, combining the trajectory reward with standard outcome and format rewards, thereby explicitly guiding the model to generate both correct and high-quality tool-integrated reasoning paths.

4.1
Supervised Fine-Tuning via Synthesized Trajectory

In this subsection, as shown in Figure
1
(a), we first describe how to automatically construct tool-integrated trajectories. We then introduce our multi-dimensional metrics for evaluating these trajectories and detail how low-quality trajectories are repaired. Finally, the combination of repaired trajectories and the original high-quality trajectories is used for cold-start supervised fine-tuning.

Figure 1
:

Architecture of AutoTraj. (a) SFT Stage: AutoTraj first synthesizes, evaluates, and repairs tool-use trajectories to construct a high-quality SFT dataset and positive–negative trajectory pairs. (b) RL Stage: A trajectory reward model is then trained on these pairs and integrated with format and outcome rewards under GRPO, providing trajectory-level supervision.

4.1.1
Candidate TIR Trajectory Synthesis

To synthesize an initial set of candidate TIR data, we sample 10,000 question–answer pairs
{(q,y)}
from the Tool-Star dataset
(
8
)
, and discard all annotated tool-use trajectories to ensure independence.
Then, we denote the pretrained trajectory generator as
f_{\psi}(\cdot)
, which is used to generate
n
candidate trajectories based on the given query
q
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\text{cand}}=\{\tilde{\tau}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\sim f_{\psi}(\tilde{\tau}\mid q),

(3)

where each
\tilde{\tau}_{i}=\{(\tilde{r}_{k},\tilde{t}_{k},\tilde{e}_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K_{i}}
represents a candidate sequence of
reasoning-action-observation
tuples.

To enforce a structured and machine-parsable format, we design a constrained prompting protocol (see Appendix
C.1
). Specifically, each trajectory starts with a
<think>
block corresponding to the reasoning variable
\tilde{r}_{k}
. At each step, the model decides whether to invoke a tool
\tilde{t}_{k}\in\{\texttt{code},\texttt{search}\}
, and the resulting observation
\tilde{e}_{k}=\varphi(\tilde{t}_{k})
is inserted between
<result>
and
</result>
tags, forming the TIR trajectoriy
(\tilde{r}_{k},\tilde{t}_{k},\tilde{e}_{k})
. This iterative generation continues until a terminal answer
\hat{y}
is produced or a predefined maximum context length is reached. Finally, the answer is enclosed in
\boxed{}
. In practice, to prevent trajectories from becoming excessively long, we limit each candidate trajectory to at most three tool invocations.

4.1.2
Multi-Dimensional Trajectory Evaluation

To assess the overall quality of TIR trajectories, we design a multi-dimensional evaluation that jointly considers answer correctness, reasoning confidence, trajectory length, and text repetition
1
1

1

We select these metrics based on observations from our preliminary experiments, which show that low-quality trajectories often exhibit low reasoning confidence, suboptimal lengths, or excessive repetition.
. Based on these multi-dimensional criteria, each candidate trajectory
\tilde{\tau}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{cand}}
is assigned a composite quality score and subsequently classified into different quality levels for downstream training.

Specifically, answer correctness is used for initial screening. Trajectories producing an incorrect answer
\hat{y}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})\neq y
are immediately assigned to the low-quality set:
\tilde{\tau}_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{wrong}}.

Only trajectories with correct answers will be proceed to further evaluation.
Then, for each correct-answer trajectory, a multi-dimensional quality score is defined as:

\displaystyle S(\tilde{\tau}_{i})=\lambda_{1}\,S_{\text{conf}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})+\lambda_{2}\,S_{\text{len}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})+\lambda_{3}\,S_{\text{rep}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i}),

(4)

where
S_{\text{conf}}(\cdot),S_{\text{len}}(\cdot),S_{\text{rep}}(\cdot)
denote the confidence, length, and text repetition score, respectively and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}=1
.
Next, we describe how these scores are computed:

\bullet
For the confidence score
S_{\text{conf}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})
, we aim to assess whether a trajectory exhibits hesitation or redundant self-corrections. To this end, we define a binary confidence measure:
if the TIR trajectory contains explicit lexical cues of self-doubt or uncertainty, such as
(maybe, unsure, guess, seems, perhaps, probably, recheck, related terms)
, its confidence score
S_{\text{conf}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})
is set to 0. Otherwise, it is set to 1.

\bullet
For the length score
S_{\text{len}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})
, we aim to quantify how much a candidate trajectory deviates from the ideal TIR length for a given query
q
:

S_{\text{len}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})=\exp(-(\text{len}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})-l_{q})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))
,
where, in practice, the ideal length
l_{q}
is defined as the shortest length among the trajectories in
\mathcal{T}_{\text{cand}}
that produce the correct answer.
This metric is designed to assign lower scores to trajectories that are excessively long.

\bullet
For the repetition score, we aim to assess the degree of repetition in local text within a trajectory. Let
\text{Rep}_{N}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})
denote the
N
-gram repetition rate. We define the repetition score as:

S_{\text{rep}}(\tilde{\tau}_{i})=1-\text{Rep}_{N}(\tilde{\tau}_{i}),

which penalizes low-information or meaningless repeated content.

Finally, we set a quality threshold
\theta_{\text{qual}}
such that a trajectory
\tilde{\tau}_{i}
is considered high-quality if
S(\tilde{\tau}_{i})
exceeds
\theta_{\text{qual}}
, and low-quality otherwise
2
2

2

For detailed implementation, see Appendix
A.4
.
.
Ultimately, all candidate trajectories are divided into three disjoint sets:
\mathcal{T}_{\text{high}}
,
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{correct}}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{wrong}}
.

4.1.3
LLM-based Trajectory Repair

To repair low-quality trajectories, we treat the LLM as a trajectory repairer (i.e., LLM-as-Repairer) to automatically refine low-quality TIR trajectories. Specifically, given a query
q
and its corresponding low-quality trajectory
\tau^{-}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{correct}}
or
\tau^{-}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{wrong}}
, we feed both into the repairer and prompt it to regenerate a candidate repaired trajectory
\tau^{+}
. We then evaluate the quality of
\tau^{+}
using our scoring function. If its score exceeds the predefined high-quality threshold, the repair is regarded as successful. The repaired trajectory is added to the high-quality trajectory set
\mathcal{T}_{\text{high}}
, and meanwhile, the pair
(\tau^{-},\tau^{+})
is stored as a contrastive sample in the self-supervised dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}
.

For different types of low-quality trajectories (
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{correct}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{wrong}}
), we adopt prompt designs with different emphases during the repair process (see Appendix
C.2
), so as to respectively focus on reasoning refinement and error diagnosis.

Finally, based on the above procedure (Sections
4.1.2
and
4.1.3
), we construct two datasets. Among them, the supervised fine-tuning dataset is defined as:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}=\{(q_{i},y_{i},\tau_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},

(5)

where
(q_{i},y_{i})
denotes the
i
-th query–answer pair, and

\tau_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{high}}(q_{i},y_{i})=\{\tau_{i,1}^{+},\ldots,\tau_{i,m_{i}}^{+}\}

is the set of high-quality trajectories (including both originally high-quality ones and successfully repaired ones) associated with
(q_{i},y_{i})
.

The self-supervised dataset is defined as:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}=\{(q_{j},\tau_{j}^{-},\tau_{j}^{+})\}_{j=1}^{M},

(6)

where
\tau_{j}^{-}
is an original low-quality trajectory and
\tau_{j}^{+}
is its successfully repaired counterpart that meets the high-quality threshold. Each element thus forms a low–high trajectory pair for subsequent reward model training.

4.1.4
Cold-Start Supervised Fine-tuning

After constructing the SFT dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}
, we perform cold-start supervised fine-tuning to equip AutoTraj with an initial capability for solving problems using tools. Specifically, given a sample
(q_{i},y_{i},\tau_{i})\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}
, we optimize the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
to
\pi_{\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}
by minimizing the negative log-likelihood:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(q_{i},y_{i},\tau_{i})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{sft}}}\left[\log P_{\theta}((\tau_{i},y_{i})\mid q_{i})\right].

(7)

4.2
Trajectory-Aware Reinforcement Learning

After SFT, in this subsection, as shown in Figure
1
(b), we first train a trajectory reward model. Using this model, we design a comprehensive reward function that integrates format, outcome, and trajectory rewards for RL training.

4.2.1
Trajectory Reward Model

To evaluate trajectory quality during RL, a trajectory-level reward model
r_{\phi}(\cdot)
is required. Since such a model is generally unavailable, we train using our self-supervised dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}
.
Specifically, the reward model is optimized to assign higher scores to high-quality trajectories than to low-quality trajectories for the same query. To this end, we adopt a pairwise ranking objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(q,\tau^{+},\tau^{-})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{self}}}\left[\log\sigma\big(r_{\phi}(q,\tau^{+})-r_{\phi}(q,\tau^{-})\big)\right],

(8)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the sigmoid function, and the input query
q
is included in the prompt fed to the model.

Through this training, the resulting trajectory reward model can effectively distinguish high- and low-quality trajectories and provide fine-grained trajectory-level scores. Compared with sparse answer-level rewards, it delivers denser and more stable supervision signals, which are then incorporated into the RL stage to guide the optimization of TIR behaviors.

4.2.2
Reward Design

To provide stable and fine-grained supervision for TIR, we design a hierarchical reward function composed of three components: a
format reward
, an
outcome reward
, and a
trajectory reward
.

Format Reward.

We first introduce a binary format reward to strictly enforce the predefined output structure.
Given a model-generated response
(\tau_{i},\hat{y}_{i})
, the format reward
R_{\text{fmt}}
is defined as:

R_{\text{fmt}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the output format is valid},\\
-1,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(9)

The detailed format constraints have been described in Section
4.1.1
.

Outcome Reward.

The outcome reward is computed only when the format is valid (
R_{\text{fmt}}=1
).
Specifically, we measure the token-level F1 score
(
8
)
between the predicted final answer
\hat{y}_{i}
and the ground-truth answer
y_{i}
:

R_{\text{ans}}=\text{F1}(y_{i},\hat{y}_{i}).

(10)

Trajectory Reward.

When the outcome reward is positive (
R_{\text{ans}}>0
), we further introduce a trajectory-level reward to assess the overall quality of the reasoning process.
Concretely, the reasoning trajectory
\tau_{i}
is evaluated by a trained trajectory reward model
r_{\phi}(\cdot)
, which outputs a scalar score:

R_{\text{traj}}=\begin{cases}r_{\phi}(q,\tau_{i}),&\text{if }r_{\phi}(q,\tau_{i})>0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(11)

where the trajectory reward is activated only for correct predictions, thereby preventing erroneous reasoning paths from receiving positive reinforcement.

Final Reward.

We combine the format, outcome, and trajectory rewards into a unified final reward:

R=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if }R_{\text{fmt}}=-1,\\
0,&\text{if }R_{\text{fmt}}=1\text{ and }R_{\text{ans}}=0,\\
R_{\text{ans}}+\alpha R_{\text{traj}},&\text{if }R_{\text{fmt}}=1\text{ and }R_{\text{ans}}>0,\end{cases}

(12)

where
\alpha
is a weight that controls the contribution of the trajectory-level reward. In practice, to further stabilize RL, we impose an upper bound
R_{\max}
on the final reward, so that all rewards are clipped into the range
[-1,R_{\max}]
.

4.2.3
RL Training with GRPO

To optimize the policy SFT model
\pi_{\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}
, we adopt Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
31
)
as our RL algorithm, which estimates the baseline using a group of rollouts. Given a group of
G
rollouts
\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}
sampled from the current policy
{\pi}_{\theta}^{\text{old}}
for query
q
, the GRPO objective is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q,\{o_{i}\}\sim{\pi}_{\theta}^{\text{old}}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|o_{i}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|o_{i}|}\min\Bigg(\frac{\pi_{\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}{{\pi}_{\theta}^{\text{old}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}A_{i,t}^{\text{clip}},

(13)

\displaystyle\text{clip}\Big(\frac{\pi_{\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})}{{\pi}_{\theta}^{\text{old}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,<t})},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\Big)A_{i,t}\Bigg)-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi_{\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}||{\pi}_{\text{ref}}]\Bigg],

where
\epsilon
and
\beta
are hyperparameters,
A_{i,t}
is the normalized advantage of the
t
-th step in the
i
-th rollout, and
o_{i,<t}
denotes the partial trajectory before step
t
.
By optimizing this objective, we obtain our final AutoTraj that balances final answer correctness with high-quality reasoning behavior.

5.4
Efficiency of TIR Trajectories (RQ3)

In this section, we evaluate the efficiency of AutoTraj in practical reasoning tasks. Since all baseline methods are fine-tuned from the same Qwen2.5-7B model with identical parameter counts, we measure reasoning efficiency by comparing the average lengths of TIR trajectories. As shown in Figure
2
, we present the average reasoning trajectory lengths of AutoTraj and several baseline methods on the AIME24 and HQA datasets, as well as the average lengths across all 9 datasets. The results indicate that AutoTraj generates significantly shorter trajectories than most baselines, demonstrating higher reasoning efficiency.
Specifically, compared with RL-only TIR methods, AutoTraj exhibits a clear advantage. For instance, ToRL has an average trajectory length of 1,960 tokens across all datasets, reflecting substantial redundancy and over-exploration during reasoning. In contrast, AutoTraj requires only 251 tokens on average, reducing trajectory length by more than a factor of seven. This dramatic reduction greatly improves reasoning efficiency while lowering computational overhead.
Among TIR methods that combine SFT and RL, AutoTraj still achieves superior trajectory efficiency. For example, although Tool-Star demonstrates competitive performance on some datasets, its average trajectory length is around 1,000 tokens, significantly longer than that of AutoTraj, resulting in lower reasoning efficiency. This indicates that AutoTraj can maintain strong reasoning performance while producing more compact and stable trajectories, further enhancing efficiency.

Overall, the results show that AutoTraj learns efficient reasoning patterns by focusing on key reasoning steps and suppressing unnecessary redundant exploration, thereby generating concise, well-structured, and effective reasoning trajectories. It achieves a favorable balance between reasoning efficiency and overall performance.
In Appendix
B.1
, we report the average TIR trajectory lengths for all methods across all datasets, as well as the average lengths for cases where the correct answer is generated. These results further confirm the superior reasoning efficiency of AutoTraj.

5.5
Impact of RL Training Data Scale (RQ4)

In this section, we investigate the impact of RL training data scale on the performance of AutoTraj in Figure

, with the goal of assessing the effectiveness of the proposed trajectory reward in alleviating reward sparsity in RL and improving data efficiency. As shown in Figure

Moreover, as the training dataset size gradually increases, the overall performance of AutoTraj consistently and steadily improves. This trend suggests that the proposed trajectory reward design exhibits good scalability and can continuously guide the model to optimize its reasoning behavior as more training data become available.
In summary, these results show that AutoTraj achieves strong data efficiency in low-resource settings while maintaining stable performance gains in large-scale training regimes, highlighting its practical applicability.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed AutoTraj, a two-stage framework for tool-integrated reasoning that eliminated the reliance on high-quality synthesized trajectories selected by scoring functions and mitigated reward sparsity. Specifically, in the supervised fine-tuning stage, AutoTraj automatically synthesized, evaluated, and repaired tool-integrated reasoning trajectories, providing scalable and high-quality supervision and enabling an effective cold start. In the reinforcement learning stage, AutoTraj leveraged pairs of original low-quality trajectories and their repaired counterparts to train a trajectory reward model, which supplied fine-grained reward signals during optimization. This trajectory-aware reward design explicitly accounted for reasoning quality and effectively alleviated reward sparsity. Extensive experimental results demonstrated that AutoTraj consistently improved tool-integrated reasoning performance across benchmarks, leading to more robust reasoning behaviors.

A.4
Training Settings

In this paper, we use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the backbone model throughout both the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) stages.
Specifically, in Supervised Fine-Tuning via Synthesized Trajectory stage (SFT stage), our goal is to generate reasoning traces that explicitly involve tool invocation. We initially attempted to obtain such trajectories by prompt-based generation using base models such as Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. However, we found that instruction following was often unreliable, and the generated responses frequently ignored the tool-usage protocol, producing trajectories without any tool calls. To reduce the difficulty of trajectory synthesis and ensure structurally valid tool usage, we reproduce the AutoTIR model using reinforcement learning and employ it as a dedicated trajectory generator (
f_{\psi}(\cdot)
), which is able to consistently produce tool-integrated reasoning trajectories. Due to computational constraints, the number of sampled candidate trajectories is set to (n=2) for each query.

In the multi-dimensional trajectory evaluation module (Section
4.1.2
), the weighting coefficients satisfy (
\lambda_{1}
+
\lambda_{2}
+
\lambda_{3}
=1), and we set
\lambda_{1}
=0.4 and
\lambda_{2}
=
\lambda_{3}
=0.3 in all experiments.
During trajectory classification, we set two thresholds
\theta_{\text{qual}}
for correct-answer trajectories, both determined through observations on small-batch trajectory scores. The first threshold,
high quality threshold

\theta^{1}_{\text{qual}}
, is set to 0.86. For trajectories scoring above this threshold on the same query, the highest-scoring trajectory is labeled as high-quality, while the rest are labeled as low-quality. The second threshold is
all high quality threshold

\theta^{2}_{\text{qual}}
, set to 0.9. If all trajectories on a given query score above this threshold, all trajectories on that query are classified as high-quality. This strategy prevents unnecessary downgrading of trajectories with high overall quality and minimal variation, thereby avoiding noise introduction during subsequent reward model training.
Ultimately, all initial trajectories are categorized into three groups: high-quality trajectories with correct answers
\mathcal{T}_{\text{high}}
, low-quality trajectories with correct answers
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{correct}}
, and low-quality trajectories with incorrect answers
\mathcal{T}_{\text{low}}^{\text{wrong}}
. This provides a structured data foundation for subsequent supervised fine-tuning and trajectory reward model training.

For trajectory repair, we adopt Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct as the LLM-as-Repairer. From Table
4
, it can be observed that our repair success rate is over 50%. For trajectories that remain low quality after the first repair, we have not yet conducted further processing in the current work to improve trajectory utilization. In the future, more advanced models could be introduced to attempt a second round of repairs, or these trajectories could be utilized in other potential use cases.

In the Trajectory-Aware Reinforcement Learning stage (RL stage), the trajectory reward model is trained based on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. GRPO is used for policy optimization, where the trajectory reward weight is set to
\alpha
=0.2, and the maximum reward is capped at
R_{\max}
=3. To prevent environment feedback from biasing the policy update, the execution results of tool invocations are explicitly masked during loss computation and excluded from gradient backpropagation.

The SFT stage and the training of the trajectory reward model are implemented using the LLaMA Factory framework
(
44
)
, while the GRPO-based reinforcement learning stage is conducted with the Verl framework
(
32
)
. All experiments are carried out on 4 NVIDIA A800 GPUs, and detailed hyperparameter settings are provided in our released code

Reward Score Analysis

To analyze the role of trajectory rewards in the RL stage, we examine the evolution of validation rewards during GRPO training. As shown in the left of Figure

, after incorporating trajectory rewards, the reward of AutoTraj exhibits a stable and steadily increasing trend over training iterations, indicating a smooth and effective improvement in reasoning capability. In contrast, the right of Figure

In the repaired trajectory, this issue is effectively eliminated. In details, the repairer generates correct code when invoking the code tool and returns the correct result, thereby reaching the correct answer in a smooth and coherent manner.
