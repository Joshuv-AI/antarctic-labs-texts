Glymphatic system dysfunction mediates amyloid deposition and cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease: a PET/MRI multimodality imaging study
Authors: Hanxiao Xue, Sheng Bi, Zhigeng Chen, Yujie He, Wenqi Xu, Xiaoyin Xu, Bixiao Cui, Yujie Hu, Zhigang Qi, Shaozhen Yan, Jie Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbnormal glymphatic system may play a critical role in amyloid-β (Aβ) accumulation in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients. This study aims to use diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) and perivascular space volume fraction (PVSVF) to investigate the aberrant glymphatic functions and the association between Aβ deposition and clinical symptoms in AD spectrum.ResultsThe ALPS index was significantly lower in AD patients compared to MCI and normal controls (NC) groups. Additionally, the AD group showed a significantly higher PVSVF in hippocampus (HP) compared to NC group. No notable variations were observed in the ALPS index or PVSVF across various regions when comparing the MCI group to the NC group. Apolloprotein E (APOE) ε4 + group showed significantly higher PVSVF-HP and PVSVF in basal ganglia compared to APOE ε4 − group. All participants’ HP volume, lower cognitive scores, and higher 18F-AV45 PET SUVr were linked to a lower ALPS index. Enlarged perivascular space was linked to worse cognitive function. Mediation analysis further revealed that the detrimental effects of Aβ pathology and APOE genotype on cognitive decline were mediated by glymphatic dysfunction.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that glymphatic dysfunction is associated with cognitive decline, underscoring the critical roles of Aβ pathology and the APOE genotype in mediating this relationship. Further exploration of glymphatic function holds significantly promise for advancing research on AD pathogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-025-01339-y.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Glymphatic system, Amyloid imaging, PET/MRI, DTI-ALPS, Perivascular space

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a leading cause of dementia in the elderly, presents a major global health challenge [1]. Amyloid-β protein (Aβ), a pathologic hallmark of AD, begins to accumulate approximately 18 years before clinical diagnosis of dementia syndrome [2]. The apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene emerges as a well-established genetic determinant of late-onset AD, with the APOE ε4 genotype strongly associated with increased AD risk and Aβ plaque deposition [3]. Recently, the glymphatic system has attracted considerable interest. This system is hypothesized to clear waste products, including Aβ, from the brain through fluid circulation [4]. It functions by directing cerebrospinal fluid into the brain’s interstitial space, promoting exchange with interstitial fluid and facilitating toxin clearance [5, 6]. This process is particularly relevant to AD, as Aβ accumulation is widely recognized as a key driver of pathology preceding cognitive decline [7, 8]. Therefore, elucidating the function of the glymphatic system in AD is of utmost importance. Clarification of its mechanisms could contribute to improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for AD.

The presence of a glymphatic system has been verified in rodents [9]. In humans, perivascular glymphatic flow can also be visualized with intrathecal gadolinium-based contrast agent [10]. Although this technique directly identify lymphatic function, its application in humans is limited by its invasiveness. Recent studies [11, 12] suggest that diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) and perivascular space (PVS) volume fraction (PVSVF) were two PVS-related magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) indexes to assess glymphatic performance. The ALPS index is derived from diffusivity measurements of deep medullary veins near the lateral ventricular body, utilizing diffusion MRI [11]. The ALPS index has been shown to be lower in AD patients than in both normal controls (NC) and those with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) [12, 13], indicating that glymphatic impairment might initiate during the early phases of AD. ALPS index has been studied in various neurological conditions, including frontotemporal dementia, ischemic stroke, multiple sclerosis and idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus [14, 15]. PVS is a vital part of glymphatic system, which surrounds the perforating artery and facilitated by the vascular endfeet of astrocytes [16]. The dilatation of PVS, commonly observed in white matter (WM), as well as within the basal ganglia (BG) and hippocampus (HP), is thought to result from impaired interstitial fluid drainage [17, 18]. AD patients show a greater prevalence of dilated PVS than NC [19, 20]. Currently, assessment of PVS relies on conventional counting method by vision in most studies. Although this method is convenient and relatively duplicable, it is subject to potential bias and can be challenging when assessing smaller brain structures, like HP, where visual evaluation is more difficult and less precise. To address these limitations, automated methods for measuring PVSVF across the whole brain have been introduced, using isotropic three-dimensional MRI and computational imaging analysis [21, 22]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying PVSVF in each anatomical location and ALPS index in AD patients are not fully understood.

Glymphatic function has also been linked to Aβ deposition on positron emission tomography (PET) images of human brain [13, 23]. Mouse model of Aβ-amyloidosis have shown that the APOE genotype and chronic sleep deprivation alter perivascular polarization of the water channel aquaporin-4 (AQP4) on astrocytic endfeet. Specifically, reduced AQP4 polarization impairs Aβ clearance from the brain [24, 25]. A recent hypothesis proposed by an experiment based on induced pluripotent stem cells suggested that APOE ε4 could generate the premature shrinkage of meningeal lymphatic vessels. This shrinkage results in meningeal lymphatic dysfunctions, which raise the symptoms of AD by decreasing the clearance of Aβ [26]. According to a study conducted on healthy individuals, APOE ε4 carriers have a higher number of PVS [27]. These findings highlight a strong association between APOE and the glymphatic function. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has explored the correlation between glymphatic function and APOE, leaving an important gap in understanding AD pathogenesis.

Although prior studies suggest glymphatic impairment in AD, the pattern of dysfunction across the AD spectrum remains unclear. In this study, we aim to employ ALPS and PVSVF to assess glymphatic function in AD spectrum by examining their associations with cognitive performance and Aβ deposition. Considering APOE ε4 gene is a major risk factor for AD progression, we further examined the impact of APOE genotype on glymphatic function by clarifying the changes of ALPS and PVSVF among different APOE genotypes. In addition, mediation analysis was performed to determine whether the APOE ε4 genotype affects cognitive function and pathological status of AD patients through glymphatic dysfunction.

Methods

Study design and subjects

Between July 1, 2023, and August 31, 2024, 69 subjects were recruited at the Neurology Department of Xuanwu Hospital. This cohort consisted of 20 patients with MCI, 30 AD patients who tested positive on Aβ PET, and 19 NC individuals [28]. All participants received neuropsychological evaluations and brain 18F-AV45 PET/MRI scans. All patients underwent quality control of 18F-AV45 PET/MRI images at enrollment, and all had no 18F-AV45 uptake in the cerebellum. Diagnoses of MCI and AD were established based on core clinical criteria as recommended by the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) [29, 30]. For the enrollment of NC group, participants were included based on normal cognitive scores. NC participants were age and gender-matched to those in the AD and MCI groups, with scores ranging from 27 to 30 on the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), and a Cognitive Drug Rating (CDR) of 0. Participants were excluded if they had current or past psychiatric or neurological disorders, including major depression, stroke, brain tumor, head injury, or cerebrovascular injury known to be associated with cognitive impairment.

All participants underwent neuropsychological tests, which included the MMSE and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). Using Sanger sequencing, we were able to identify the APOE gene’s ε2, ε3, and ε4 variants [31].

PET/MRI data acquisition

A Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant radiosynthesis of 18F-AV45 was performed using a one-pot, two-step procedure on a Trasis Allinone-36 (AIO-36) module (see Supplementary eMethod 1 for details). 18F-AV45 is routinely produced and supplied in multiple patient dose with this fully automated procedure in Xuanwu hospital. The quality control tests, conducted according to the current radiopharmaceutical requirements in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2020 edition), indicate that the 18F-AV45 met all the release criteria for human use (Table S1). The detailed radiosynthesis of 18F-AV45 can be found in Scheme 1 (Supplementary Materials).

Within 7 days following neurocognitive testing, all integrated PET/MRI exams were performed using a simultaneous TOF-enabled hybrid imaging system (uPMR790 TOF, United Imaging Healthcare, China). All participants underwent 18F-AV45 PET/MRI scans. PET images were acquired after injection of 370 ± 21MBq 18F-AV45. The imaging session commenced 50 min into the tracer’s deployment and lasted for 20 min. MRI scans were obtained concurrently during the PET scanning process. The following pulse sequence configuration was used to acquire three-dimensional T1-weighted imaging (3D T1W): flip angle = 10°, voxel size = 1.00 × 1.00 × 1.00 mm3, 192 slices, and repetition time/echo time = 7.2/3 ms. DTI were acquired using with 64 directions, TR/TE = 5700/90.1 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 224 × 224 mm2, matrix size = 112 × 112, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 3.5 mm3, 48 slices, b values = 0 and 1000 s/mm2. A 2-point Dixon scan was used to obtain simultaneous PET data for MRI-based PET attenuation correction. The tomographic reconstruction employed a hybrid iterative framework combining TOF, spatial resolution modeling via point spread function, and ordered subsets optimization. This computational architecture was configured with 32 subset divisions per iteration across 8 reconstruction cycles, implementing 3-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian smoothing during image post-processing.

Post-process of PET data

PET images were preprocessed in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, London, United Kingdom), including spatially normalization and spatial smoothing. With the Harvard–Oxford cortical structural atlas, we pinpointed regions of interest (ROIs), covering both hemispheres from the frontal to the occipital lobes, along with the frontal orbital cortex, angular gyrus, and parahippocampus. The standard uptake value ratio (SUVr) was then determined using PET scans, which involved dividing the average intensity of each region by the average intensity of the entire cerebellum. Next, we averaged the readings from both hemispheres to delve deeper into our analysis.

ALPS index calculation

A robust, semi-automated processing pipeline was used to extract the ALPS index [11]. The Fractional Anisotropy (FA) maps of each participant were first linearly registered and then non-linearly registered to be part of the high-resolution FMRIB58_FA standard space image. The Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus (SLF) and the Superior Corona Radiata (SCR) were determined to be projection and association fibers, respectively, based on the JHU-ICBM-DTI-81 white matter labeled atlas.​ The ROIs were automatically applied to the diffusivity maps of every subject and were described as spheres whose diameter measures five millimeters within the bilateral SCR and SLF areas. All ROIs were manually checked and verified by two trained physicians with extensive clinical experience during placement. Left SLF (128, 110, 99), left SCR (116, 110, 99), right SLF (51, 110, 99), and right SCR (64, 110, 99) were the center coordinates of the ROIs.​ A schematic illustration of the ROIs is provided in Figure S1 of the Supplementary Materials. Cortical segmentation

All T1W images were preprocessed, including subject motion correction, denoising, nonuniform intensity normalization, and skull stripping. Using the preprocessed T1W images, we performed cortical segmentation with FreeSurfer 7.4.1 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/), which yielded segmented masks for multiple brain regions including WM, BG, and HP. The HP volume was then extracted and normalized with total intracranial volume (TIV).

PVS segmentation

Following the procedures used in previous studies, PVS volumes were extracted from T1W scans using an automated quantification approach [21, 22]. The segmentation results of PVS were all manually inspected and verified by two trained physicians with extensive clinical experience. The inter-rater reliability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement. The analysis yielded an ICC value of 0.92. The PVS volume was assessed in the WM, BG, HP regions, as well as in the aggregate of all these structures, denoted as ALL. To normalize for the variations in brain size among individuals, we calculated the PVSVF (PVS volume/ROI volume), followed by PVSVF-WM, PVSVF-BG, PVSVF-HP, and PVSVF-ALL. The detailed calculation method is described in Supplementary eMethod 2.

Statistical analysis

We employed SPSS software, version 26, for all statistical analyses. Baseline characteristics across diagnostic groups were compared using non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests. Spearman correlation to assess associations between the ALPS index and demographic variables. ALPS index group variances were assessed via ANCOVA, with age and gender as control variables. We further employed path analyses to explore the associations among the ALPS index, Aβ accumulation, HP volume, PVSVF, and cognitive function. These analyses were conducted using the SPSS PROCESS macro (version 4.1). Age and sex were included as covariates in the analyses to account for their potential confounding effects. A false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05 was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg approach for correction of multiple comparisons when appropriate.

Association of ALPS index and PVSVF with cognitive scores and 18F-AV45 PET SUVr

Firstly, we analyzed the correlation between ALPS index with cognition and 18F-AV45 PET SUVr of all enrolled subjects. Lower ALPS index was linked to poorer cognitive outcomes, including lower MMSE (r = 0.32, pfdr = 0.048) and MoCA scores (r = 0.38, pfdr = 0.015; Fig. 3a). A reduced ALPS index was associated with higher 18F-AV45 PET SUVr (r = −0.26, pfdr = 0.020; Fig. 3b). Subsequently, correlation analyses between the ALPS index and Aβ deposition in MCI and AD patients showed a moderate correlation in the MCI patients (r = −0.404, pfdr = 0.086) and no correlation in the AD patients (r = −0.013, pfdr = 0.947).

Moreover, higher PVSVF was significantly correlated with poorer cognitive performance, as reflected by both MMSE and MoCA scores in all participants (Fig. 2). Specifically, the strongest association with lower MMSE scores was observed for PVSVF-HP (r = − 0.46, pfdr = 0.004), followed by PVSVF-BG (r = −0.40, pfdr = 0.008), PVSVF-ALL (r = −0.37, pfdr = 0.008; Fig. 3c), and PVSVF-WM (r = −0.35, pfdr = 0.01; Fig. 3e). Similarly, higher PVSVF was correlated with lower MoCA scores, with the strongest association in PVSVF-HP (r = −0.44, pfdr = 0.004; Fig. 3d), followed by PVSVF-BG (r = −0.36, pfdr = 0.01; Fig. 3f), PVSVF-ALL (r = −0.33, pfdr = 0.008), and PVSVF-WM (r = −0.31, pfdr = 0.011). The correlation analysis between PVSVF (PVSVF-ALL, PVSVF-WM, PVSVF-BG and PVSVF-HP) and 18F-AV45 PET SUVr in all participants, as well as across the AD, MCI, and NC, revealed no significant associations (p > 0.05).

The correlation analysis between ALPS index and PVSVF (PVSVF-ALL, PVSVF-WM, PVSVF-BG and PVSVF-HP) in all participants, as well as across the AD, MCI, and NC, revealed no significant associations (p > 0.05).

Smaller HP volume was associated with a reduced ALPS index (r = 0.33, pfdr = 0.003), higher PVSVF-HP (r = −0.47, pfdr = 0.002; Fig. 3g) and higher 18F-AV45 PET SUVr (r = −0.21, pfdr = 0.03; Fig. 3h). There was no significant relationship existed between HP volume and PVSVF (PVSVF-ALL, PVSVF-WM and PVSVF-BG). All correlation analyses were performed with adjustment for gender, age, years of education, WMH burden, and hypertension status, and the resulting p-values were corrected for FDR.

Mediation among glymphatic dysfunction, Aβ deposition and APOE genotype

We further performed statistical mediation compatible with a path model of ALPS index, PVSVF-HP and 18F-AV45 PET SUVr on cognitive scores (MoCA, MMSE) and APOE ε4 genotype (Fig. 4). The association between 18F-AV45 PET SUVr and MoCA scores was partially accounted for by the ALPS index in the path model, with the indirect path representing 24.32% of the total association (total association βtotal = −0.3224, direct association βdirect = −0.2440, indirect association βindirect = −0.0784). AD signature ROI volumes (HP volumes) were found to account for a portion of the association between 18F-AV45 PET SUVr and the ALPS index, with the indirect path representing 23.57% of the total association (total association βtotal = −0.3339, direct association βdirect = −0.2552, indirect association βindirect = −0.0787). In the model examining the association between APOE ε4 genotype and MoCA scores, PVSVF-HP accounted for a substantial portion of this relationship, with the indirect path representing 57.3% of the total association (total association βtotal = −0.674, direct association βdirect = −0.2878, indirect association βindirect = −0.3863).

Discussion

In this study, we employed a multi-dimensional segmentation strategy incorporating the ALPS index and PVSVF to evaluate glymphatic dysfunction across the AD spectrum and investigate its associations with clinical and pathological features of AD. Our findings revealed a significant reduction in the ALPS index and a marked increase of PVSVF across the AD spectrum, indicating impaired glymphatic function in AD patients. Notably, PVSVF in HP and BG were significantly higher in APOE ε4 + than in APOE ε4 − group. In contrast, neither the ALPS index nor 18F-AV45 PET SUVr differed significantly between APOE ε4 + and APOE ε4 − groups. Mediation analyses further indicated that the associations of Aβ pathology and APOE genotype on cognitive decline were mediated by the glymphatic dysfunction.

Glymphatic system, which removes toxic proteins and metabolic waste from the brain, is thought to deteriorate and could be a factor in AD patients’ cognitive decline. ALPS index was considered as a non-invasive method in evaluation of glymphatic function. Prior research has demonstrated that patients with AD and MCI have lower ALPS index [11, 32–34]. And ALPS index is positively correlated with the MoCA and MMSE scores [11, 35–37]. Our results are consistent with these reports. A similar association observed in our study further supports the link between ALPS index and neurocognitive activity in AD. As for Aβ deposition, prior research revealed an inverse correlation between ALPS index and 18F-AV45 PET SUVr or 11C-PiB PET SUVr [13, 38]. Our findings are similar. Considering that glymphatic pathway might exert a crucial influence on the overall clearance of Aβ [39], these results may indicate that the reduced glymphatic clearance is associated with higher Aβ accumulation and the ALPS index might signify glymphatic dysfunction in AD. Mediation analysis model also found that Aβ load, which may contribute to AD-related cognitive decline, was mediated by ALPS index. According to this analysis, glymphatic function might help mitigate the cognitive decline associated with Aβ accumulation. We further discovered that HP volume partially mediates the damage to the glymphatic function caused by Aβ deposition. This underscores the intertwined interactions between Aβ pathology, brain structure changes, and glymphatic dysfunction.

In this research, significantly increased PVSVF-HP can be verified in AD patients compared to NCs, and no significant differences were observed in PVSVF-BG, PVSVF-WM, PVSVF-ALL between AD patients and NCs. This is similar to the mixed results of PVS changes in different brain regions reported in current studies in some degree. One study reported significantly increased PVSVF-ALL, PVSVF-BG, PVSVF-WM in AD patients compared to NCs [12]. While others indicated that AD patients have a higher degree of PVS enlargement in comparison with NCs, especially in the centrum semiovale [19, 40] and HP [41]. However, the overall trend that PVS dilatation was observed in AD patients is consistent in all studies. NCs exhibited the smallest PVS volume in the WM, followed by the MCI patient, while the AD patient demonstrated the largest PVS volume in these regions [12].

In our study, both the ALPS index and PVSVF were considered as biomarkers for assessing the glymphatic function. When using the ALPS index to assess the glymphatic function, significant differences were observed in AD patients compared to MCI patients and NCs patients. As for the PVSVF, there was merely a significant disparity detected in HP between the AD patients and NC. In contrast, MCI patients’ PVSVF did not significantly rise in any of the preselected brain regions when compared to NCs. Based on these findings, ALPS index could be proposed as a more sensitive indicator that reflects early glymphatic system changes along the AD spectrum, whereas PVSVF increase may occur at later stage and serve as an indicator of disease severity. Furthermore, correlation analysis further supported this hypothesis. This association between Aβ deposition and the ALPS index was strongest in the NCs, diminished in the MCI patients with a trend toward significance, and became entirely absent in the AD patients. This decreasing correlation over the course of AD spectrum may be related to that Aβ burden have already reached a plateau in AD patient. Then we explored the correlation between 18F-AV45 PET SUVr and PVSVF across multiple brain regions, but found no association. In recent reports [12, 42], no correlation between PVSVF and Aβ-related CSF biomarker values or PET SUVr was observed in AD patients. Our results were in line with them in some degree. So we can assume that increased PVSVF may contribute to the progression of AD, this change does not lead to Aβ deposition directly or is not as sensitive as ALPS index to reflect the impaired perivascular clearance. These findings further support our hypothesis that PVSVF increase may occur in the later stage of AD spectrum, when Aβ accumulation has already reached a plateau.

Noteworthily, we included the APOE genotypes in this study as a basis for grouping, which was not applied in many previous studies. And an increase in PVSVF-HP was observed in APOE ε4 + group compared to APOE ε4– group, which is consistent with current research findings [27]. However, we did not observe differences in ALPS between different APOE genotypes. Currently, research on the role of APOE in glymphatic system function is only limited to proposed conceptual frameworks or animal and cellular studies. Therefore, future studies need to further expand the sample size, incorporate follow-up investigations, and further explore the relationship between the glymphatic system and the APOE gene in humans. Some researchers believed that enlarged PVS in HP is associated with atrophy; however, they also noted that caution is needed in interpretation, as it is really challenging to assess enlarged PVS in an atrophied HP by visual inspection [43]. To avoid this insufficiency, our study employed quantitative measurements of PVS in HP, which are more reliable than visual assessments. The difference in PVSVF-HP across genetic groups remained statistically significant even after adjustment for a comprehensive set of covariates, including sex, age, WMH, hypertension, TIV, and HP volume. Further stratified analyses revealed that the association between APOE ε4 genotype and PVSVF-HP is highly dependent on Aβ status—significant differences are only observed in the Aβ + status. And this association is not attenuated by adjusting for global or hippocampal atrophy. These results indicated that Aβ accumulation could disrupt the glymphatic system’s clearance function in the hippocampus, and APOE ε4, may further exacerbate PVS dilation only when Aβ is already present, whereas in the absence of Aβ (Aβ- group), this synergistic pathological effect is absent. In AD mouse models, APOE affects Aβ metabolism, and APOE genotypes raise the risk of AD by differentially controlling the brain’s clearance of Aβ [44]. A number of studies indicate that the APOE ε4 genotype causes AQP4, a key glymphatic clearance driver that helps remove Aβ and tau from the brain parenchyma, to disrupt its perivascular polarization, which in turn causes the PVS volume to increase [25, 45]. All things considered, the APOE ε4 + group showed a significantly higher PVSVF-HP than the APOE ε4– group, but there was no discernible difference in the ALPS index. This discrepancy further suggests that APOE genotype and glymphatic dysfunction may be closely linked. In addition, our analysis of the path model suggested that the association between APOE ε4 and cognition decline operates through an indirect path involving increased PVSVF, suggesting that the enlargement of PVSVF could be a pathway through which APOE ε4 affects cognition. As research advances, targeting the APOE genotype may offer new avenues for AD treatment, highlighting glymphatic function as a promising biomarker for advancing our understanding of AD pathogenesis.

There are certain limitations in this study. First, pathophysiological research has not thoroughly and stringently verified the connection between the ALPS index and the glymphatic dysfunction in humans [46]. Thus, utmost caution is warranted when attempting to interpret the relationship between them. Secondly, our relatively small sample size may contribute to the instability of the results. Future studies could address this by increasing the sample size to further validate and expand upon these findings. Thirdly, even though previous research [47, 48] has linked glymphatic dysfunction to sleep disorders and arterial fluctuations, the present study is unable to assess this relationship due to a lack of data. Fourthly, due to the cross-sectional design, our study is limited in its ability to robustly explain the intergroup differences in PVSVF and cannot support causal claims regarding the observed associations. Future research will require further longitudinal follow-up studies to explore differences in the glymphatic system associated with APOE genotypes.

Conclusions

In summary, using the ALPS index and PVSVF measurements, we conducted a preliminary investigation into the relationship between the glymphatic dysfunction and the APOE genotype. Furthermore, these findings highlight the role of glymphatic function as a promising biomarker in assessment of AD, and may further enhance our understanding of AD pathogenesis.
