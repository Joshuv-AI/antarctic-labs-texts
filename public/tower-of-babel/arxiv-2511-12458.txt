Solutions of Three-Dimensional Stationary Gas Dynamics Equations
O.V. Kaptsov

Abstract




This paper examines the three-dimensional stationary equations of a polytropic gas and employs symmetry methods to construct exact analytical solutions. In the Chaplygin gas case, the analysis yields a highly general solution family depending on three arbitrary functions, while the general adiabatic index formulation admits explicit solutions parameterized by several constants.

Keywords:
gas dynamics equations, symmetries, exact solutions

1
Introduction

The role of symmetries in physics is well known; they have proven to be extremely useful in quantum mechanics, special relativity, and field theory.
Comprehensive treatments of symmetry methods for nonlinear partial differential equations can be found in the monographs
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Applications of symmetries to hydrodynamic equations are discussed extensively in the recent monographs
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.
In 1994, L.V. Ovsyannikov
[
7
]
introduced the “Submodel Program,” aimed at studying solutions of reduced hydrodynamic models.
A central goal of this program is the construction of solutions to the equations of gas dynamics.
Despite significant efforts, explicit solutions of nonlinear three-dimensional hydrodynamic equations remain scarce.

This work is devoted to constructing solutions of the three-dimensional stationary equations of gas dynamics.
Section 2 considers potential three-dimensional flows of an ideal gas.
We show that if the entropy and the Bernoulli integral are constant and the flow is sonic, then the corresponding state necessarily satisfies a polytropic law with index
-1
, which corresponds to the Chaplygin gas.
We then derive the symmetry algebra associated with the equation

\operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\right)=0,

which describes the Chaplygin gas in terms of the velocity potential
\phi
.
This algebra is infinite-dimensional; the coefficients of its generators depend on eight arbitrary functions.
Such a structure enables the construction of solutions involving three arbitrary functions.

Section 3 examines stationary vortical flows.
Following the approach of Refs.
[
4
,
8
]
and applying an additional dilatation transformation, we reduce the governing equations in both the axisymmetric and fully three-dimensional settings to a system of ordinary differential equations.
The reduced system possesses a sufficient number of first integrals to further reduce it to a single differential equation.
In a particular case, explicit solutions of this equation are obtained.
This leads to closed-form stationary solutions of the gas dynamic equations expressed in elementary functions, together with the corresponding streamline formulas.

2
Potential Flows and Chaplygin Gas.

We begin with the three-dimensional steady Euler equations for an ideal gas.
In the absence of external forces, the governing equations comprise the
continuity equation, the momentum balance, and the energy relation.
In compact vector form, these equations can be written as

\displaystyle\nabla\!\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\rho\,(\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\!\cdot\nabla p+c^{2}\rho\,\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=0,

where
\rho
is the density,
\mathbf{u}=(u,v,w)
is the velocity field,

p
is the pressure, and
c
is the local sound speed.

Let
q=u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}

denote the square of the flow speed.
We assume that the Bernoulli integral

I_{B}=\frac{q}{2}+\int\frac{c^{2},d\rho}{\rho}

(2.1)

and the entropy remain constant throughout the flow domain.
In addition, we consider the case in which the flow speed equals the local sound speed,

q=c^{2}.

(2.2)

These assumptions imply the relation

\frac{q}{2}+\int\frac{q}{\rho}\,d\rho=k,\hskip 23.49976ptk\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.3)

Because
q
depends solely on
\rho
, differentiation of (
2.3
) with respect to
\rho

yields the ordinary differential equation

q^{\prime}+\frac{2q}{\rho}=0.

Its solution is

q=\frac{a}{\rho^{2}},

(2.4)

where
a>0
is an arbitrary constant.
Substitute this expression into the Bernoulli integral

\frac{a}{2\rho^{2}}+\int\frac{p^{\prime}\,d\rho}{\rho}=k,

and differentiate the resulting relation with respect to
\rho
. This gives the differential equation

\frac{p^{\prime}}{\rho}=\frac{a}{\rho^{3}}.

Its solution,

p=-\frac{a}{\rho}+b,\hskip 23.49976ptb\in\mathbb{R},

(2.5)

defines the equation of state of the Chaplygin gas
[
9
]
.

Thus, the assumptions that the flow speed equals the sound speed and that both the Bernoulli integral and the entropy remain constant imply that the gas must obey the Chaplygin equation of state.

We now turn to potential flows, i.e., we assume the existence of a scalar function
\phi
such that its gradient equals the velocity vector
V=(u,v,w)
. As is well known
[
9
]
, the potential then satisfies

(\rho\phi_{x})_{x}+(\rho\phi_{y})_{y}+(\rho\phi_{z})_{z}=0.

For the Chaplygin gas, this equation reduces to

\operatorname{div}\!\left(\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}\right)=0.

(2.6)

In explicit form, equation (
2.6
) becomes

(\phi_{y}^{2}+\phi_{z}^{2})\,\phi_{xx}+(\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{z}^{2})\,\phi_{yy}+(\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{y}^{2})\,\phi_{zz}-2(\phi_{x}\phi_{y}\phi_{xy}+\phi_{x}\phi_{z}\phi_{xz}+\phi_{y}\phi_{z}\phi_{yz})=0.

(2.7)

It is straightforward to verify that the corresponding equation for characteristics can be represented as

(\phi_{x}\Phi_{y}-\phi_{y}\Phi_{x})^{2}+(\phi_{x}\Phi_{z}-\phi_{z}\Phi_{x})^{2}+(\phi_{y}\Phi_{z}-\phi_{z}\Phi_{y})^{2}=0.

(2.8)

The last equation for the function
\Phi
must hold on solutions of equation (
2.7
). Obviously, if
\phi
is a solution of equation (
2.7
), then
\Phi=\phi
satisfies (
2.8
). Hence, the relation

\phi=\text{const}

defines the characteristics.

Let us proceed to constructing solutions to equation (
2.7
) for the potential. First, we find the symmetries of equation (
2.7
). Applying standard methods of group analysis

[
1
,
3
]
, we find the infinite-dimensional Lie algebra of admissible operators

X=(F_{7}x-F_{5}z-yF_{2}+F_{4})\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+(F_{7}y-F_{6}z+xF_{2}+F_{3})\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+

(F_{5}x+F_{6}y+zF_{7}+F_{8})\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+F_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi},

where
F_{1},\dots,F_{8}
are arbitrary functions of
\phi
.

Take two operators

X_{1}=F_{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+F_{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial y},\hskip 23.49976ptX_{2}=F_{9}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+F_{8}\frac{\partial}{\partial z},

where
F_{9}
is an arbitrary function of
\phi
.
For finding invariants, it is necessary to solve the system of first-order linear differential equations
[
1
]

X_{1}I=0,\hskip 23.49976ptX_{2}I=0.

(2.9)

Obviously,
I_{1}=\phi
satisfies the system (
2.9
). The function
I_{2}=ax+by+cz
is a solution if the functions
a,b,c
satisfy the system of algebraic equations

F_{4}a+F_{3}b=0,\hskip 23.49976ptF_{9}b+F_{8}c=0.

Therefore, any smooth function
G
of
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
is an invariant. Equating
G
to zero, we get a representation for solutions of equation (
2.7
).
By direct calculation, it is not difficult to verify that the formula

ax+by+cz+d=0,

(2.10)

where
a,b,c,d
are arbitrary functions of
\phi
,
indeed defines an implicit solution of the potential equation (
2.7
).
Note that the actual arbitrariness in the solution is three functions.

From representation (
2.10
), one can obtain various explicit solutions.
As an example, consider the potential

\phi=f!\left(\frac{k_{1}x+k_{2}y+k_{3}z+k_{4}}{n_{1}x+n_{2}y+n_{3}z+n_{4}}\right),

(2.11)

where
k_{i},n_{j}
are arbitrary constants, and
f
is any smooth function.
If
n_{1}=\cdots=n_{3}=0
,
n_{4}=1
, then the potential is smooth.

Obviously, the obtained solutions (
2.10
) correspond to characteristic surfaces which are planes. In the two-dimensional case, characteristics are straight lines.
In general, the intersection of characteristic planes (characteristic lines) leads to the formation of shock waves. If the planes intersect along a line, we get solutions of the centered wave type. If the planes do not intersect, solutions of equation (
2.7
) can be smooth.

3
Vortical Flows.

In this section, we first examine stationary adiabatic axisymmetric flows of an ideal gas, which are governed by the system of equations

\begin{cases}u\rho_{z}+v\rho_{r}+\rho\!\left(u_{z}+v_{r}+\dfrac{v}{r}\right)=0,\\[4.0pt]
\rho(uu_{z}+vu_{r})+p_{z}=0,\\[4.0pt]
\rho(uv_{z}+vv_{r})+p_{r}=0,\\[4.0pt]
up_{z}+vp_{r}+\gamma p\!\left(u_{z}+v_{r}+\dfrac{v}{r}\right)=0,\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
u
and
v
are the velocity components,
\rho
is the density, and
p
is the pressure.

It can be shown that the Lie algebra of point symmetries of system (
3.1
)
is generated by the operators

X_{1}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z},\hskip 23.49976ptX_{2}=p\,\frac{\partial}{\partial p}+\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho},\hskip 23.49976ptX_{3}=z\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}+r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r},

together with an infinite family of operators of the form

Y_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}(I_{1},I_{2})\!\left(u\,\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+v\,\frac{\partial}{\partial v}-2\rho\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right),

(3.2)

Here
\mathcal{F}
is an arbitrary function of entropy and the Bernoulli integral

I_{1}=\frac{p}{\rho^{\gamma}},\hskip 23.49976ptI_{2}=\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}}{2}+\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}.

An operator of the form (
3.2
) in the two-dimensional and three-dimensional cases is presented in
[
10
,
11
]
.

Now we assume that the pressure does not depend on
z
. Then the system (
3.1
) admits an additional dilation operator

X_{5}=u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}.

Consider a linear combination of operators
Z=X_{5}+mY_{\mathcal{F}}
, where
m\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{F}=1
. The invariant solution with respect to the operator
Z

must have the form

u=z^{m+1}U,\hskip 11.74988ptv=z^{m}V,\hskip 11.74988pt\rho=z^{-2m}Q,\hskip 11.74988ptp=P,

(3.3)

where
U,V,Q,P
are functions of
r
.
Substituting the representation (
3.3
) into (
3.1
), we obtain a system of ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle(1-m)UQ+VQ^{\prime}+QV^{\prime}+\frac{VQ}{r}=0,

(3.4)

\displaystyle(1+m)U^{2}+VU^{\prime}=0,

(3.5)

\displaystyle mVU+VV^{\prime}+\frac{P^{\prime}}{Q}=0,

(3.6)

\displaystyle VP^{\prime}+\gamma P\left((m+1)U+V^{\prime}+\frac{V}{r}\right)=0.

(3.7)

Rewrite equation (
3.5
) in the form

\frac{U}{V}=-\frac{U^{\prime}}{(m+1)U}.

(3.8)

Divide equation (
3.4
) by
QV
and substitute expression (
3.8
) into the resulting relation.
As a result, equation (
3.4
) can be rewritten as

\frac{m-1}{m+1}(\ln U)^{\prime}+(\ln QV)^{\prime}+(\ln r)^{\prime}=0.

From here we find the first integral

QVrU^{\frac{m-1}{m+1}}=c_{1},\hskip 11.74988ptc_{1}\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, we obtain the representation

Q=\frac{c_{1}U^{\frac{1-m}{m+1}}}{Vr}.

(3.9)

Similarly, if we divide equation (
3.7
) by
PV
and use formula (
3.8
), then (
3.7
) can be written as

\left(\ln\big(PV^{\gamma}r^{\gamma}U^{-\gamma}\big)\right)^{\prime}=0.

From here we obtain the first integral and the representation for the function
P

P=c_{2}\left(\frac{U}{Vr}\right)^{\gamma},

(3.10)

where
c_{2}
is an arbitrary constant.

Next, we divide equation (
3.6
) by
V^{2}
and rewrite it as

-\frac{m}{m+1}\frac{U^{\prime}}{U}+\frac{V^{\prime}}{V}+\frac{P^{\prime}rV}{QV^{2}}=0.

Substituting the representation (
3.9
) into the last expression, we obtain the equation

\frac{(VU^{-\frac{m}{m+1}})^{\prime}}{VU^{-\frac{m}{m+1}}}+\frac{P^{\prime}rV}{c_{1}U^{\frac{1-m}{m+1}}V^{2}}=0.

This equation can be written as

(VU^{-\frac{m}{m+1}})^{\prime}+\frac{P^{\prime}rVU^{-1}}{c_{1}VU^{-\frac{m}{m+1}}}=0.

Using the representation (
3.10
), we find the first integral

\frac{V^{2}U^{\frac{-2m}{m+1}}}{2}+\frac{c_{2}\gamma}{c_{1}(\gamma-1)}\left(\frac{U}{Vr}\right)^{\gamma-1}=c_{3},\hskip 23.49976ptc_{3}\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.11)

Since

V=-(m+1)\frac{U^{2}}{U^{\prime}},

(3.12)

the first integral (
3.11
) gives an ordinary differential equation for the function
U

\frac{(1+m)^{2}}{2}\frac{U^{\frac{2m+4}{m+1}}}{(U^{\prime})^{2}}+\frac{c_{2}\gamma}{c_{1}(\gamma-1)}\left(\frac{-U^{\prime}}{(m+1)Ur}\right)^{\gamma-1}=c_{3}.

(3.13)

To find the streamlines, one needs to integrate the equation

\frac{dz}{u}=\frac{dr}{v}.

(3.14)

Using the representations (
3.3
) and (
3.12
), the equation for the streamlines can be rewritten as

\frac{dz}{z}=\frac{U^{\prime}dr}{-(m+1)U}.

Hence, the streamlines are given by the formula

z=cU^{-\frac{1}{m+1}},

where
c
is an arbitrary constant.

It remains to find the solution of equation (
3.13
). However,
for an arbitrary value of
\gamma
, equation (
3.13
) cannot be solved explicitly when
c_{3}\neq 0
.
Assume that
c_{3}=0
. Then, according to (
3.13
), the function
U
takes the form

U=\left(a\,r^{\frac{2\gamma}{1+\gamma}}+b\right)^{-\frac{(m+1)(1+\gamma)}{2}}.

Here,
b
is an arbitrary constant, and
a
is determined by the constants appearing in equation (
3.13
).

Following
[
8
]
, let us now assume that, in system (
3.1
), the derivative
p_{r}
is equal to zero.
In this case, system (
3.1
) admits an additional scaling operator

Z=r\,\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+v\,\frac{\partial}{\partial v}.

Using a linear combination of the operators
Y
and
Z
, it is easy to obtain the following representation for the solutions
[
8
]

u=r^{m}U,\hskip 11.74988ptv=r^{m+1}V,\hskip 11.74988pt\rho=r^{-2m}Q,\hskip 11.74988ptp=P,

(3.15)

where
U,V,Q,P
are some functions of
z
, and
m\in\mathbb{R}
.
Substituting this representation into the system (
3.1
), we obtain four ordinary differential equations of the type (
3.4
)–(
3.7
).
The system of four equations admits two first integrals
[
8
]
:

QUV^{\frac{2-m}{1+m}}=c_{1},\hskip 11.74988pt\left(\frac{U}{V^{\frac{m+2}{m+1}}}\right)^{\gamma}P=c_{2},

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are arbitrary constants.
Using these integrals, one can obtain a third integral – a differential equation for the function
V

\frac{1}{2(m+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{V^{\frac{m+2}{m+1}}}{V^{\prime}}\right)^{2}+\frac{c_{2}\gamma}{c_{1}(\gamma-1)}\left(-(1+m)V^{\frac{-m}{m+1}}V^{\prime}\right)^{\gamma-1}=c_{3},\hskip 23.49976ptc_{3}\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.16)

To derive this equation, the following relation was used

U=\frac{-V^{2}}{(m+1)V^{\prime}}\ .

Integrating the differential equation (
3.14
), we obtain a representation for the streamlines
in terms of the function
V
:

r=cV^{\frac{-1}{m+1}}\ ,\hskip 11.74988ptc\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us assume that
c_{3}=0
in equation (
3.16
).
Then this equation has two types of solutions.
For
\gamma=3
, the solution takes the form

V=be^{kz},\hskip 23.49976ptk=\frac{-1}{(m+1)}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{3c_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}},\hskip 23.49976ptb\in\mathbb{R},

(3.17)

and for
\gamma\neq 3
,

V=(az+b)^{\frac{\gamma-3}{(\gamma+1)(m+1)}}\ ,

where
b
is an arbitrary constant, and
a
is expressed in terms of the constants in equation (
3.16
).
The solution (
3.15
) corresponding to the function (
3.17
) is smooth.
If one of the streamlines is considered as a solid boundary, then this solution can be interpreted as the flow around a curved wall.

Let us now consider the three-dimensional stationary gas dynamics equations

\displaystyle u\rho_{x}+v\rho_{y}+w\rho_{z}+\rho\left(u_{x}+v_{y}+w_{z}\right)=0,

\displaystyle\rho(uu_{x}+vu_{y}+wu_{z})+p_{x}=0,

\displaystyle\rho(uv_{x}+vv_{y}+wv_{z})+p_{y}=0,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\rho(uw_{x}+vw_{y}+ww_{z})+p_{z}=0,

\displaystyle up_{x}+vp_{y}+wp_{z}+\gamma p(u_{x}+v_{y}+w_{z})=0.

By analogy with the axisymmetric case, it is easy to verify
[
10
]
that the system (
3.18
) admits a symmetry operator of the form

Y_{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{F}(I_{1},I_{2})\cdot\left(u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial v}-2\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\right),

where
\mathcal{F}
is an arbitrary function of the entropy and the Bernoulli integral,

I_{1}=\frac{p}{\rho^{\gamma}},\hskip 23.49976ptI_{2}=\frac{u^{2}+v^{2}+w^{2}}{2}+\frac{\gamma p}{(\gamma-1)\rho}.

If we assume that the pressure
p
does not depend on
x
, then the system (
3.18
) admits
a scaling operator

x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+(m+1)u\frac{\partial}{\partial u}+mv\frac{\partial}{\partial v}+mw\frac{\partial}{\partial w}-2m\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho},

(3.19)

where
m
is an arbitrary constant.
This operator allows us to obtain the following representation for the solutions:

u=x^{m+1}\mathcal{U},\hskip 11.74988ptv=x^{m}\mathcal{V},\hskip 11.74988ptw=x^{m}\mathcal{W},\hskip 11.74988pt\rho=x^{-2m}\mathcal{Q},\hskip 11.74988ptp=\mathcal{P},

where
\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{P}
are some functions of
y
and
z
.
If we substitute this representation into the system (
3.18
), we obtain a system of five equations for these functions.
The resulting system also admits a scaling transformation, which makes it possible to seek a solution of (
3.18
) in the form

u=x^{m+1}y^{n}U,\hskip 11.74988ptv=x^{m}y^{n+1}V,\hskip 11.74988ptw=x^{m}y^{n}W,\hskip 11.74988pt\rho=x^{-2m}y^{-2n}Q,\hskip 11.74988ptp=P,

where
U,V,W,Q,P
are functions of
z
.
Substituting the representation (
3.20
) into (
3.18
), we obtain
a system of five ordinary differential equations

\displaystyle(1-n)QU+(1-m)QV+(QW)^{\prime}=0,

(3.21)

\displaystyle(1+n)U^{2}+mUV+WU^{\prime}=0,

(3.22)

\displaystyle nUV+(1+m)V^{2}+WV^{\prime}=0,

(3.23)

\displaystyle Q(nUW+mVW+WW^{\prime})+P^{\prime}=0,

(3.24)

\displaystyle WP^{\prime}+\gamma((n+1)U+(m+1)V+W^{\prime})=0.

(3.25)

Let us introduce the notation

S=U^{\frac{1}{1+n+m}},\hskip 23.49976ptR=V^{\frac{1}{1+n+m}}.

Then, from equations (
3.22
) and (
3.23
), we obtain

\frac{V}{W}=\left(\frac{S^{n}}{R^{n+1}}\right)^{\prime},\hskip 23.49976pt\frac{U}{W}=\left(\frac{R^{m}}{S^{m+1}}\right)^{\prime}.

(3.26)

As shown in
[
4
]
, the system (
3.21
)–(
3.25
) admits three first integrals

QWR^{2m-1-n}S^{2n-1-m}=c_{1},\hskip 23.49976ptP\left(WS^{-1-m}R^{-1-n}\right)^{\gamma}=c_{2},

(3.27)

W^{2}R^{-2m}S^{-2n}+\frac{2c_{2}\gamma}{c_{1}(\gamma-1)}\left(\frac{R^{n+1}S^{m+1}}{W}\right)^{\gamma-1}=c_{3},

(3.28)

where
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
c_{3}
are arbitrary constants.

Following
[
4
]
, let us introduce additional notation

T=\frac{R^{n+1}S^{m+1}}{W},\hskip 11.74988ptX=S^{n}R^{-n-1},\hskip 11.74988ptY=R^{m}S^{-m-1}.

(3.29)

Then equations (
3.26
) can be rewritten as

\frac{U}{W}=\frac{Y^{\prime}}{Y},\hskip 23.49976pt\frac{V}{W}=\frac{X^{\prime}}{X}.

From the last equations, it is easy to obtain two new relations

Y^{\prime}=XYT,\hskip 23.49976ptX^{\prime}=XYT.

Hence,

Y=X+c_{4},\hskip 11.74988ptT=\frac{X^{\prime}}{X(X+c_{4})},\hskip 23.49976ptc_{4}\in\mathbb{R}.

The first integral (
3.28
) in the new variables takes the form

\frac{1}{2X^{2}(X+c_{4})^{2}T^{2}}+aT^{\gamma-1}=c_{3},

where
a
is expressed in terms of
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, and
\gamma
.
From the last two expressions, we obtain an ordinary differential equation for the function
X

\frac{1}{(2X^{\prime})^{2}}+a\left(\frac{X^{\prime}}{X(X+c_{4})}\right)^{\gamma-1}=c_{3}.

(3.30)

For an arbitrary constant
c_{3}
, it is not possible to find an explicit solution of the equation (
3.30
).
Therefore, we assume that
c_{3}=0
.
If
\gamma=3
, then the differential equation (
3.30
) takes the form

X^{\prime}+A\sqrt{X(X+c_{4})}=0,

where

.
The solution to this equation is

X=\frac{(2e^{-Az+b}-c_{4})^{2}}{8e^{-Az+b}},\hskip 23.49976ptb\in\mathbb{R}.

Now let
c_{3}=c_{4}=0
and
\gamma\neq 3
. Then equation (
3.30
) can be written as

X^{\prime}+AX^{\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}}=0.

Its solution is

X=\left(\frac{A(\gamma-3)(z+b)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{-\gamma-1}{\gamma-3}},\hskip 23.49976pt\forall b\in\mathbb{R}.

Since

Y=X,\hskip 23.49976ptT=\frac{X^{\prime}}{X^{2}},

then, from relations (
3.29

S=R=\frac{1}{X}=\left(\frac{A(\gamma-3)(z+b)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-3}},\hskip 23.49976ptT=A\left(\frac{A(\gamma-3)(z+b)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{4}{\gamma-3}}.

Hence, the functions
U
and
V
are equal:

U=V=S^{1+m+n}=\left(\frac{A(\gamma-3)(z+b)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{(\gamma+1)(1+n+m)}{\gamma-3}}.

According to the first formula in (
3.29
), we obtain

W=\frac{R^{n+1}S^{m+1}}{T}=\frac{z(\gamma-3)}{\gamma+1}\left(\frac{A(\gamma-3)(z+b)}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{(\gamma+1)(1+n+m)}{\gamma-3}}.

The first integrals (
3.27
) allow us to find

P=c_{2}T^{\gamma},\hskip 23.49976ptQ=c_{1}TR^{-2m}S^{-2n}.

Since the streamlines are defined by the equations

\frac{dx}{u}=\frac{dy}{v}=\frac{dz}{w},

in this case these equations reduce to

\frac{dx}{x}=\frac{dy}{y}=\frac{(\gamma+1)dz}{(\gamma-3)z}.

The parametric representation of the streamlines is

x=a_{1}e^{t},\hskip 11.74988pty=a_{2}e^{t},\hskip 11.74988ptz=a_{3}e^{t\frac{(\gamma-3)}{\gamma+1}},\hskip 23.49976pta_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in\mathbb{R}.

4
Conclusion

In this study, new classes of solutions to the three-dimensional stationary gas dynamics equations have been obtained.
Such solutions are exceptionally rare in the existing literature.
For the Chaplygin gas, we derived a family of solutions depending on three arbitrary functions.
It can also be shown that, in the case of an arbitrary spatial dimension
n
, the nonlinear equation (
2.6
) possesses an infinite-dimensional symmetry algebra.
This property makes it possible to construct implicit solutions of the form

a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{n}x_{n}+b=0,

where
a_{1},\dots,a_{n},
and
b
are arbitrary functions of the potential
\phi
.
Such solutions can be obtained more directly using the
B
-determining equation method
[
4
]
