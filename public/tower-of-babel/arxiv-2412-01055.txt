Extension of compressive sampling to binary vector recovery for model-based defect imaging
Wei-Chen Li, Chun-Yeon Lin

Abstract
Common imaging techniques for detecting structural defects typically require sampling at more than twice the spatial frequency to achieve a target resolution. This study introduces a novel framework for imaging structural defects using significantly fewer samples. In this framework, defects are modeled as regions where physical properties shift from their nominal values to resemble those of air, and a linear approximation is formulated to relate these binary shifts in physical properties with corresponding changes in measurements. Recovering a binary vector from linear measurements is generally an NP-hard problem. To address this challenge, this study proposes two algorithmic approaches. The first approach relaxes the binary constraint, using convex optimization to find a solution. The second approach incorporates a binary-inducing prior and employs approximate Bayesian inference to estimate the posterior probability of the binary vector given the measurements. Both algorithmic approaches demonstrate better performance compared to existing compressive sampling methods for binary vector recovery. The framework’s effectiveness is illustrated through examples of eddy current sensing to image defects in metal structures.

Introduction

Some common imaging problems aiming to reconstruct the physical properties of a specimen in 3D space include
MRI
(
MRI
),
MIT
(
MIT
), and
EIT
(
EIT
). In
MRI
, hydrogen nuclei align with an external magnetic field. When exposed to
RF
(
RF
) pulses, these nuclei absorb the
RF
energy, causing their spins to become excited and temporarily disoriented from the magnetic field. As they relax back to their original alignment, they release energy in the form of
RF
waves, which are then detected by antennas. In
MIT
, a time-varying magnetic field induces eddy currents in the specimen. These eddy currents create secondary magnetic fields, which induce an electromotive force in the sensing coils. In
EIT
, currents are injected into the specimen through electrodes placed on its surface, and the resulting voltages are measured at the boundary using the same or different electrodes. Despite their drastic difference in sensing modalities, these three imaging problems can all be formulated as linear inverse problems
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, represented by the equation:

\bm{y}=\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}+\bm{n},

where
\bm{y}
represents the measurements,
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
the measurement matrix,
\bm{x}
the physical properties to be reconstructed, and
\bm{n}
the measurement noise. Additionally, the vector
\bm{x}
is typically sparse.
MRI
images, for example, are sparse in a wavelet basis, whereas in
MIT
and
EIT
, the regions with electrical conductivity anomalies are small compared to the overall specimen size. To recover sparse vectors from underdetermined linear systems, compressive sampling algorithms are often employed.

1.1
Compressive sampling

Compressive sampling or compressed sensing, as coined by
[
4
,
5
]
, enables the recovery of signals using significantly fewer samples than dictated by the Nyquist sampling theorem. Comprehensive surveys of compressive sampling algorithms can be found in
[
6
,
7
]
.
Among these algorithms, two primary classes are frequently employed.

The first class of methods, known as convex relaxation or
\ell_{1}
minimization, relaxes the
\ell_{0}
minimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\|\bm{x}\|_{0}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\end{array}

as the
\ell_{1}
minimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\|\bm{x}\|_{1}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon.\end{array}

The
\ell_{0}
norm counts the number of nonzero entries, but optimizing it is NP-hard
[
8
]
. In contrast, the
\ell_{1}
norm serves as the convex envelope of the
\ell_{0}
norm
[
9
]
, allowing the
\ell_{1}
minimization problem to be efficiently solved using convex optimization algorithms. Theoretical results ensure exact recovery under specific conditions
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Notably, if the vector is sufficiently sparse and the measurement matrix satisfies the
RIP
(
RIP
)–meaning the matrix columns are approximately mutually orthogonal–
\ell_{1}
minimization can yield exact recovery.

The second class of methods is known as
SBL
(
SBL
)
[
13
,
14
]
, which formulates the problem within a probabilistic framework. In
SBL
, a sparse-inducing prior is assigned to the vector
\bm{x}
, and the posterior probability of the vector given the measurements is calculated. The posterior probability is expressed as:

p(\bm{x}|\bm{y})=\frac{p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})p(\bm{x})}{p(\bm{y})},

where
p(\bm{x})
represents the sparse-inducing prior,
p(\bm{y}|\bm{x})
denotes the measurement likelihood, and
p(\bm{y})
is the marginal density of
\bm{y}
. Although
SBL
do not provide any recovery guarantees, they empirically perform better than
\ell_{1}
minimization
[
6
]
. Another advantage of
SBL
is its ability to accommodate more complex structures through prior selection, such as spatial clustering
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
or temporal correlation
[
18
]
. For certain prior choices, the marginal density is tractable, allowing the use of the expectation-maximization algorithm for inference
[
15
,
16
,
18
]
. In other cases, more advanced inference algorithms, such as variational inference
[
17
]
or approximate message passing
[
19
,
20
]
, are necessary.

1.2
Extending to binary vector recovery

The extension of compressive sampling to recover not only sparse but also binary vectors is motivated by certain imaging applications–particularly structural defect imaging–where physical properties take on binary values. For instance, in the inspection of metal structures, a region may exhibit either the nominal electrical conductivity of the metal or zero conductivity, indicating an air-filled crack or void.

While few studies have addressed binary vector recovery, most existing approaches rely on convex optimization methods
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. Common formulations include:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\bm{0}^{\top}\bm{x}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}=\bm{y}\\
&\bm{0}\preceq\bm{x}\preceq\bm{1},\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{x}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}=\bm{y}\\
&\bm{0}\preceq\bm{x}\preceq\bm{1},\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\|\bm{x}-0.5\cdot\bm{1}\|_{\infty}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}=\bm{y}.\\
&\end{array}

These formulations, however, do not account for cases where the measurement
\bm{y}
is noisy, nor do they explore solutions through Bayesian inference methods.

This study extends compressive sampling by focusing on binary vector recovery, with particular application to imaging challenges in structural defect detection. The main contributions are as follows:

•

Convex optimization formulation (
Section

2
).
This study establishes that the native binary-constrained optimization problem is NP-hard. To tackle this, the binary constraint is relaxed to its convex hull, enabling convex optimization techniques to approximate a solution. The tightness of the convex relaxation is proved.

•

Approximate Bayesian inference formulation (
Section

3
).
The study demonstrates that inferring the posterior probability of binary variables within a Bayesian network is also NP-hard. To address this, two approximate inference algorithms are developed, utilizing mean field approximation and approximate message passing.

•

Application to eddy current sensing (
Section

5
).
The study shows how defect imaging with eddy current sensing can be formulated as a binary vector recovery problem. Examples are provided to demonstrate its application to defect imaging in metal plates and pipes.

2
Convex optimization for binary vector recovery

The aim is to find a sparse and binary vector
\bm{x}
that satisfies

\bm{y}=\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}+\bm{n},

(1)

where
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the measured vector,
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
is the measurement matrix, and
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is the
IID
(
IID
) measurement noise. A natural optimization problem to solve is

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\|\bm{x}\|_{1}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\\
&\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N}.\end{array}

(2)

However, this optimization problem is NP-hard. To show this, consider another NP-hard problem.

Definition 1
.

The
SSP
(
SSP
) asks whether, given integers
w_{1},\dots,w_{N}
and a target number
W
, there exists a subset of
\{w_{1},\dots,w_{N}\}
that sums exactly to
W
.

The
SSP
is NP-complete
[
24
]
.

Theorem 1
.

The binary constrained
\ell_{1}
minimization problem (
2
) is NP-hard.

Proof.

To prove that the binary-constrained
\ell_{1}
minimization problem (
2
) is NP-hard, it can be shown that the
SSP
reduces to it.
Consider an instance of the optimization problem where
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}=\bigl[\begin{matrix}w_{1}&\cdots&w_{N}\end{matrix}\bigr]
,
\bm{y}=\bigl[W\bigr]
, and
\epsilon=0
. In this case, the constraint
\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
simplifies to

\Bigl\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}-W\Bigr\|_{2}=0,

which leads to the equation

\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}=W.

The binary constraint
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
directly corresponds to selecting a subset in the
SSP
:
x_{j}=1
means
w_{j}
is included in the subset, while
x_{j}=0
means it is not.
While the
SSP
aims to find a feasible subset, it can be incorporated into the optimization framework by minimizing
\|\bm{x}\|_{1}
, which corresponds to minimization the number of integers selected.
∎

This simple reduction shows that, because of the introduced binary constraint, problem (
2
) is a hard problem. Nonetheless, one might wonder if additional assumptions on the problem could turn the binary constrained problem into a problem that is solvable in polynomial time.

2.1
Relaxing the binary constraint

A possible convex relaxation is to replace the binary constraint
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
with its convex hull. This leads to the following optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\|\bm{x}\|_{1}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\\
&\bm{0}\preceq\bm{x}\preceq\bm{1}.\end{array}

(3)

This relaxed problem can be solved to global optimum in polynomial time using convex optimization algorithms. The question is whether the solution to this relaxed problem coincide with the solution to the original binary-constrained problem.
To address this, the concept of
BRIP
(
BRIP
) is introduced, which measures the ability of a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
to approximately preserve the squared
\ell_{2}
norm of sparse vectors with bounded entries. This property plays a critical role in analyzing whether the relaxed optimization problem yields a solution close to the original binary vector.

Definition 2
.

A matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
satisfies the
BRIP
of order
s
with bounded restricted isometry constant
\delta_{s}^{\mathrm{b}}
if, for all
s
-sparse vectors
\bm{x}
satisfying
\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, the following inequality holds:

(1-\delta_{s}^{\mathrm{b}})\,\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{s}^{\mathrm{b}})\,\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

(4)

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\bm{y}=\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}+\bm{n}
, where
\|\bm{n}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon
. The true vector
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}
is binary and has
s
nonzero entries. Let
\bm{x}^{\star}
denote the solution to the optimization problem (
3
).
If
\delta_{2s}^{\mathrm{b}}(\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}})<\sqrt{2}-1
, then the solution
\bm{x}^{\star}
satisfies

\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}\|_{2}\leq C\epsilon,

(5)

where
C
is a constant.

The proof of
Theorem

2
is provided in
A
.

Theorem

2
states that the solution to the optimization problem (
3
) is close to the true binary vector
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}
in the
\ell_{2}
norm, provided that the bounded restricted isometry constant
\delta_{2s}^{\mathrm{b}}
of the measurement matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
is sufficiently small. In the case where
\epsilon
is sufficiently small such that
\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{x}^{\star}-\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}\|_{2}\leq C\epsilon<0.5
, thresholding the solution vector
\bm{x}^{\star}
can exactly recover the binary vector
\bm{x}_{\mathrm{true}}
.

Remark 1
.

The standard
RIP

[
12
]
with restricted isometry constant
\delta_{s}
requires a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
to satisfy

(1-\delta_{s})\,\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{s})\,\|\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}

for all
s
-sparse vectors, irrespective of their magnitudes. In contrast, the
BRIP
imposes an additional constraint: it applies only to
s
-sparse
vectors
\bm{x}
satisfying
\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. This restriction narrows the set of vectors under consideration to a strict subset of all
s
-sparse vectors. By limiting the scope to vectors with bounded entries, the
BRIP
condition is less stringent than the standard
RIP
. This is because large entries in
\bm{x}
can amplify distortions when the transformation
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
is applied, potentially causing larger values for the restricted isometry constant
\delta_{s}
. Although verifying whether a specific matrix satisfies the
RIP
or the
BRIP
is NP-hard, the bounded nature of the
BRIP
inherently increases the likelihood of a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
satisfying the
BRIP
compared to the
RIP
.

2.2
Multiple measurement vectors with unknown variance

In many real-world scenarios, measurement data is often composed of segments with varying noise characteristics, making (
1
) insufficient. To better represent such cases, the aim is to find a binary vector
\bm{x}
that satisfies

\bm{y}^{(l)}=\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}+\bm{n}^{(l)}\quad\forall l=1,\dots,L,

(6)

where
\bm{y}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are the measured vectors,
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
are the measurement matrices, and
\bm{n}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
are measurement noise vectors with different variances.
Assume that the noise vectors are
IID
Gaussian:

\bm{n}^{(l)}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\bm{0},(\beta^{(l)})^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{I}}\bigr),

(7)

where
\beta^{(l)}
represents the inverse-variance for each noise vector. The problem of recovering the binary vector
\bm{x}
can be formulated as the following convex optimization problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathop{\mathrm{minimize}}&\|\bm{x}\|_{1}\\
\mathop{\text{subject to}}&\|\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{y}}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\\
&\bm{0}\preceq\bm{x}\preceq\bm{1},\end{array}

(8)

where

\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\beta^{(1)}}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{\beta^{(L)}}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(L)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{\bm{y}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\beta^{(1)}}\bm{y}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\sqrt{\beta^{(L)}}\bm{y}^{(L)}\end{bmatrix}.

When
L=1
, this optimization problem is analogous to the one in (
3
). For
L>1
, if a specific channel
l
exhibits high noise, indicated by a small value of
\beta^{(l)}
, the impact of the corresponding measurement matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}
and measurement vector
\bm{y}^{(l)}
on the overall system is reduced due to the scaling applied in the augmented matrices and vectors.
In the optimization problem (
8
), a suitable choice for
\epsilon
is
c\sqrt{ML}
, where
c
represents the number of standard deviations the noise exhibits.

However, what if the noise contents are unknown. One approach is to begin with an initial guess for all
\beta^{(l)}
values and then iteratively refine them based on some heuristic. The process leads the iterative algorithm described in
Algorithm

.

In
Algorithm

1
, the process begins by initializing all
\beta^{(l)}
values to an initial guess
\beta_{0}
. The algorithm then alternates between solving the convex optimization problem (
8
) and updating each
\beta^{(l)}
based on the squared error of the current estimate. While this
\beta^{(l)}
update heuristic may appear arbitrary, the following claim provides a formal justification.

Claim 1
.

Algorithm

1
is an instance of the
EM
(
EM
) algorithm.

Proof.

The
EM
algorithm is an iterative method for finding estimates of parameters
[
25
]
. Given the set of observed variables
\mathsf{Y}
, unobserved variables
\mathsf{X}
, and unknown parameters
\mathsf{\Theta}
, the
EM
algorithm alternates between the E-step and the M-step to estimate the unknown parameters.

-

E-step:
Q(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})=\int p(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{\Theta})\,d\mathsf{X}

-

M-step:
\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{new})}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}Q(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})

For our problem, let
\mathsf{Y}=\{\bm{y}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
,
\mathsf{X}=\{\bm{x}\}
, and
\mathsf{\Theta}=\{\beta^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
. The
EM
algorithm updates
\mathsf{\Theta}
as follows:

\begin{split}\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{new})}&=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\int p(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{\Theta})\,d\mathsf{X}\\
&=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\int p(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})\left[\ln p(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X},\mathsf{\Theta})+\ln p(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{\Theta})\right]\,d\mathsf{X}\\
&\overset{3}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\int p(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})\left[\ln p(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X},\mathsf{\Theta})+\ln p(\mathsf{X})\right]\,d\mathsf{X}\\
&\overset{4}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\int p(\mathsf{X}|\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}^{(\text{old})})\ln p(\mathsf{Y}|\mathsf{X},\mathsf{\Theta})\,d\mathsf{X}.\end{split}

Equality 3 arises because
\mathsf{X}
is independent of
\mathsf{\Theta}
. Equality 4 holds because the second term does not dependent on
\mathsf{\Theta}
. Substituting the specific variables into the expression results in

(\beta^{(l)})^{(\text{new})}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\beta^{(l)}}\int p\Bigl(\bm{x}\big|\bm{y}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{y}^{(L)},(\beta^{(1)})^{(\text{old})},\dots,(\beta^{(L)})^{(\text{old})}\Bigr)\ln p\Bigl(\bm{y}^{(l)}\big|\bm{x},\beta^{(l)}\Bigr)\,d\bm{x}.

The posterior probability of
\bm{x}
can be viewed as a probability mass centered at the convex optimization solution:

p\Bigl(\bm{x}\big|\bm{y}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{y}^{(L)},(\beta^{(1)})^{(\text{old})},\dots,(\beta^{(L)})^{(\text{old})}\Bigr)=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\star}),

where
\delta(\cdot)
denotes the Dirac delta function. Additionally, the measurement likelihood is given by

p\Bigl(\bm{y}^{(l)}\big|\bm{x},\beta^{(l)}\Bigr)=\biggl(\frac{\beta^{(l)}}{2\pi}\biggr)^{M/2}\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(l)}\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\biggr].

Combining these leads to the following expression for the update of
\beta^{(l)}
:

(\beta^{(l)})^{(\text{new})}=\frac{M}{\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}}.

∎

Theorem 3
.

Proof.

It will be shown that the ability to perform

1
. Consider an instance of the Bayesian network where

. Additionally, set

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\{0,1\}}p(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}|y_{1}^{(1)},\beta^{(1)})&=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\{0,1\}}p(y_{1}^{(1)}|x_{1},\dots,x_{N},\beta^{(1)})p(x_{1})\dots p(x_{N})\\

&=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\{0,1\}}-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(1)}\Bigl(y_{1}^{(1)}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{1,j}^{(1)}x_{j}\Bigr)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln p(x_{j})\\
&\overset{4}{=}\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\{0,1\}}-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(1)}\Bigl(W-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\Bigr)^{2}\end{split}

The result of the

If the

L=1
. Set
y_{1}^{(1)}=W
,
\phi_{1,j}^{(1)}=w_{j}
,
p(x_{j})>0
, and let
\beta^{(1)}
be an arbitrarily large number. The posterior probability is given by:

\begin{split}p(x_{1},x_{2}\dots,x_{N}|y_{1}^{(1)},\beta^{(1)})&=\frac{p(y_{1}^{(1)}|x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N},\beta^{(1)})p(x_{1})p(x_{2})\dots p(x_{N})}{p(y_{1}^{(1)})}\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{(1)}}{2\pi}}\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(1)}\Bigl(y_{1}^{(1)}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\phi_{1,j}^{(1)}x_{j}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr]\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(x_{j})\times\frac{1}{p(y_{1}^{(1)})}\\
&=\sqrt{\frac{\beta^{(1)}}{2\pi}}\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(1)}\Bigl(W-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr]\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(x_{j})\times\frac{1}{p(y_{1}^{(1)})}\end{split}

This probability is nonzero if and only if
W-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_{j}x_{j}=0
, because
\beta^{(1)}
is arbitrarily large.
And since

p(x_{1}|y_{1}^{(1)},\beta^{(1)})=\sum_{x_{2}\in\{0,1\}}\dots\sum_{x_{N}\in\{0,1\}}p(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{N}|y_{1}^{(1)},\beta^{(1)}),

the exact conditional inference query
\mathbb{P}(x_{1}=1|y_{1}^{(1)},\beta^{(1)})>0
if and only if there exists a subset of integers from
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{N}\}
that includes
w_{1}
and sums exactly to
W
.
Determining whether such a subset exists is NP-hard because it is equivalent to a
SSP
asking whether there is a subset of
\{w_{2},\dots,w_{N}\}
that sums exactly to
W-w_{1}
.
∎

Theorem

3
and
Theorem

4
shows that inference for the posterior probability of
\bm{x}
on the Bayesian network is a hard problem. Nonetheless, approximate inference techniques could provide reasonable solutions in polynomial time.

3.1
Information theory background

Definition 3
.

Let
p(\mathsf{\Theta})
and
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
represent two joint probability distributions over a set of variables
\mathsf{\Theta}
.
The
KL
(
KL
)-divergence between
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
and
p(\mathsf{\Theta})
is defined as

D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta})\|p(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)=\int q(\mathsf{\Theta})\ln\frac{q(\mathsf{\Theta})}{p(\mathsf{\Theta})}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}.

(11)

Lemma 1
.

The
KL
-divergence satisfies
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta})\|p(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
q(\mathsf{\Theta})=p(\mathsf{\Theta})
.

Proof.

The proof can be found in
[
26
]
.
∎

Lemma

1
shows that
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta})\|p(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)
can serve as a distance metric, and minimizing it amounts to finding a distribution
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
that is close to
p(\mathsf{\Theta})
.

Remark 2
.

The
KL
-divergence is asymmetric, meaning that
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q\|p\bigr)\neq D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)
.
Minimizing
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(p\|q\bigr)
results in a distribution
q
that matches the expected sufficient statistics of the true distribution
p

[
27
]
. However, this projection is often computationally intractable.
When using the other projection, the approximation distribution
q
is typically selected to have a simple form, allowing the minimization of
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q\|p\bigr)
to be computationally tractable.

Proposition 1
.

Consider a set of unobserved variables
\mathsf{\Theta}
and observed variables
\mathsf{Y}
.
Suppose the set
\mathsf{\Theta}
is partitioned into disjoint subsets
\mathsf{\Theta}_{1},\mathsf{\Theta}_{2},\dots
. Assume
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
is chosen as the class of joint distributions that factorizes over these partitions, referred to as mean field distributions
[
25
]
:

q(\mathsf{\Theta})=\prod_{k}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}),

(12)

where
q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})
is any arbitrary probability distribution over the variables
\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}
. By keeping all of
\{q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\}_{k\neq\ell}
fixed, the unique minimizer of
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta})\|p(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{Y})\bigr)
with respect to
q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})
satisfies

\begin{split}\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})&=\int\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})\bigl(\prod_{k\neq\ell}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)+\textit{const}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\prod_{k\neq\ell}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})}\bigl[\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr]+\textit{const}.\end{split}

(13)

Proof.

Start by defining the energy functional
F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)
as

F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)=\int q(\mathsf{\Theta})\ln\frac{p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})}{q(\mathsf{\Theta})}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}.

The following identity always holds:

\begin{split}D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta})\|p(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{Y})\bigr)+F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)&=\int q(\mathsf{\Theta})\ln\frac{q(\mathsf{\Theta})}{p(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{Y})}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}+\int q(\mathsf{\Theta})\ln\frac{p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})}{q(\mathsf{\Theta})}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}\\
&=\int q(\mathsf{\Theta})\ln\frac{q(\mathsf{\Theta})}{p(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{Y})}\frac{p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})}{q(\mathsf{\Theta})}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}\\
&=\int q(\mathsf{\Theta})\ln p(\mathsf{Y})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}\\
&=\ln p(\mathsf{Y}).\end{split}

Since
\ln p(\mathsf{Y})
is independent of
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
, minimizing
D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta})\|p(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{Y})\bigr)
is equivalent to maximizing
F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)
with respect to
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
. Substituting the factorized form of
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
from (
12
) into
F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)
yields

\begin{split}F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)&=\int\bigl(\prod_{k}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\bigr)\bigl(\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})-\sum_{\ell}\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\bigr)\bigl(\prod_{k}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)\\
&=\int\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})\bigl(\prod_{k}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)-\sum_{\ell}\int\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\bigl(\prod_{k}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)\\
&=\iint\Bigl(\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})\bigl(\prod_{k\neq\ell}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)\Bigr)q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell}-\sum_{\ell}\int q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell}\\
&=\iint\Bigl(\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})\bigl(\prod_{k\neq\ell}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)\Bigr)q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell}-\int q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell}-\sum_{k\neq\ell}\int q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\\
&=\int q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\ln\frac{\tilde{p}(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})}{q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})}\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell}-\sum_{k\neq\ell}\int q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\\
&=-D_{\text{KL}}\bigl(q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\|\tilde{p}(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\bigr)-\sum_{k\neq\ell}\int q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\ln q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k},\end{split}

where

\ln\tilde{p}(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})=\int\ln p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})\bigl(\prod_{k\neq\ell}q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{k})\,d\mathsf{\Theta}_{k}\bigr)+\textit{const}.

Obviously,
F\bigl(p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta}),q(\mathsf{\Theta})\bigr)
is maximized with respect to
q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})
when
D_{\text{KL}}\left(q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\|\tilde{p}(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})\right)=0
. According to
Lemma

1
, this implies that
q(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})=\tilde{p}(\mathsf{\Theta}_{\ell})
.
∎

Proposition

1
provides a means for approximating the posterior probability
p(\mathsf{\Theta}|\mathsf{Y})
by another probability density
q(\mathsf{\Theta})
, which factorizes over the subsets of
\mathsf{\Theta}
as given in (
12
). While this mean field approximation cannot capture the correlation between variables in different subsets, the trade-off enables polynomial time algorithms.

3.2
Inference using mean field approximation

To apply
Proposition

1
for inferencing on the Bayesian network illustrated in
Fig.

1
, it is necessary to first specify the prior probabilities.
Gamma priors are chosen for
\beta^{(l)}
:

p(\beta^{(l)})=\mathrm{Gamma}(\beta|c,d)=\frac{d^{c}}{\Gamma(c)}(\beta^{(l)})^{c-1}e^{-d\beta^{(l)}},

(14)

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the gamma function. Parameters
c
and
d
are usually set to zero to obtain non-informative priors
[
13
]
.
To enforce a binary solution vector, a Bernoulli prior is chosen for
x_{j}
:

p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})=\mathrm{Bernoulli}(x_{j}|\pi_{j})=\pi_{j}^{x_{j}}(1-\pi_{j})^{1-x_{j}}.

(15)

The hyperprior of
\pi_{j}
is chosen as a beta distribution since it the conjugate prior of the Bernoulli distribution:

p(\pi_{j})=\mathrm{Beta}(\pi_{j}|a,b)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)}\pi_{j}^{a-1}(1-\pi_{j})^{b-1},

(16)

where
\mathrm{B}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the beta function.
Furthermore, the measurement likelihood formula (
10
) written in vector form is

p(\bm{y}^{(l)}|\bm{x},\beta^{(l)})=\biggl(\frac{\beta^{(l)}}{2\pi}\biggr)^{M/2}\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(l)}\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\biggr].

(17)

The set of observed variables is
\mathsf{Y}=\{\bm{y}^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}
and the set of unobserved variables is
\mathsf{\Theta}=\{\beta^{(l)}\}_{l=1}^{L}\cup\{x_{j},\pi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
. The joint distribution of
\mathsf{Y}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}
is given by

p(\mathsf{Y},\mathsf{\Theta})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}p(\bm{y}^{(l)}|\bm{x},\beta^{(l)})p(\beta^{(l)})\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})p(\pi_{j}).

(18)

The mean field distribution is

q(\mathsf{\Theta})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}q(\beta^{(l)})\prod_{j=1}^{N}q(x_{j})q(\pi_{j}).

(19)

With all the necessary variables and distributions defined,
Proposition

1
can be applied for mean field approximation inference.

Update of
q(\beta^{(l)})

According to the update rule (
13
),

\begin{split}\ln q(\beta^{(l)})&=\mathbb{E}_{\prod_{j=1}^{N}q(x_{j})}\bigl[\ln p(\bm{y}^{(l)}|\bm{x},\beta^{(l)})p(\beta^{(l)})\bigr]+\textit{const}\\
&=\Bigl(\underbrace{c+\frac{M}{2}}_{\tilde{c}}-1\Bigr)\ln\beta^{(l)}-\Bigl(\underbrace{d+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{{q(\bm{x})}}\bigl[\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]}_{\tilde{d}}\Bigr)\beta^{(l)}+\textit{const}\\
&=\ln\mathrm{Gamma}(\beta^{(l)}|\tilde{c},\tilde{d}).\end{split}

(20)

The expected value of the quadratic form is given by

\mathbb{E}_{\prod_{j=1}^{N}q(x_{j})}\bigl[\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\hat{\bm{x}}\|_{2}^{2}+\operatorname{Tr}(\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\bm{x}}\odot(\bm{1}-\hat{\bm{x}}))\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)^{\top}}\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}),

(21)

where
\hat{x}
denotes the mean of
\bm{x}
with respect to
\prod_{j=1}^{N}q(x_{j})
.
The mean of
\beta^{(l)}
, denoted as
\hat{\beta}^{(l)}
, is given by

\hat{\beta}^{(l)}=\mathbb{E}_{q(\beta^{(l)})}\bigl[\beta^{(l)}\bigr]=\frac{\tilde{c}}{\tilde{d}}.

(22)

Update of
q(x_{j})

First define

\bm{y}_{\sim j}^{(l)}=\bm{y}^{(l)}-\sum_{k\neq j}\bm{\phi}_{:,k}^{(l)}x_{k},

(23)

where
\bm{\phi}_{:,k}
denotes the
k
th column of
\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}
.
According to the update rule (
13
),

\begin{split}\ln q(x_{j})&=\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})\prod_{l=1}^{L}q(\beta^{(l)})}\bigl[\ln\prod_{l=1}^{L}p(\bm{y}_{\sim j}^{(l)}|x_{j},\beta^{(l)})p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})\bigr]+\textit{const}\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{q(\beta^{(l)})}\bigl[\beta^{(l)}\bigr]\ \|\bm{y}_{\sim j}^{(l)}-\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)}x_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr)+x_{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}\bigl[\ln\pi_{j}\bigr]+(1-x_{j})\,\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}\bigl[\ln(1-\pi_{j})\bigr]+\textit{const}\\
&=\sum_{l=1}^{L}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\beta}^{(l)}\left(x_{j}^{2}\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)^{\top}}\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)}-2x_{j}\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)^{\top}}\bm{y}_{\sim j}^{(l)}\right)+x_{j}\,\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}\bigl[\ln\pi_{j}\bigr]+(1-x_{j})\,\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}\bigl[\ln(1-\pi_{j})\bigr]+\textit{const}.\end{split}

(24)

The probabilities of
x_{j}
being
1
and
0
are

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x_{j}=1)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}\bigl[\ln\pi_{j}\bigr]\Bigr]\times\prod_{l=1}^{L}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\beta}^{(l)}\Bigl(\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)^{\top}}\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)}-2\bm{\phi}_{:,j}^{(l)^{\top}}\bm{y}_{\sim j}^{(l)}\Bigr)\Bigr],

(25a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x_{j}=0)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Bigl[\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}\bigl[\ln(1-\pi_{j})\bigr]\Bigr].

(25b)

Then, the mean of
x_{j}
with respect to
q(x_{j})
, denoted as
\hat{x}_{j}
, is given by

\hat{x}_{j}=\frac{\mathbb{P}(x_{j}=1)}{\mathbb{P}(x_{j}=1)+\mathbb{P}(x_{j}=0)}.

(26)

Update of
q(\pi_{j})

According to the update rule (
13
),

\begin{split}\ln q(\pi_{j})&=\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{j})}\bigl[\ln p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})p(\pi_{j})\bigr]+\textit{const}\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{j})}[x_{j}]\ln\pi_{j}+\mathbb{E}_{q(x_{j})}[1-x_{j}]\ln(1-\pi_{j})+(a-1)\ln\pi_{j}+(b-1)\ln(1-\pi_{j})+\textit{const}\\
&=(\underbrace{\hat{x}_{j}+a}_{\tilde{a}_{j}}-1)\ln\pi_{j}+(\underbrace{(1-\hat{x}_{j})+b}_{\tilde{b}_{j}}-1)\ln(1-\pi_{j})+\textit{const}\\
&=\ln\mathrm{Beta}(\pi_{j}|\tilde{a}_{j},\tilde{b}_{j}).\end{split}

(27)

The moments of the logarithm of
\pi_{j}
and
1-\pi_{j}
with respect to
q(\pi_{j})
are

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}[\ln\pi_{j}]

\displaystyle=\psi(\tilde{a}_{j})-\psi(\tilde{a}_{j}+\tilde{b}_{j}),

(28a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\pi_{j})}[\ln(1-\pi_{j})]

\displaystyle=\psi(\tilde{b}_{j})-\psi(\tilde{a}_{j}+\tilde{b}_{j}),

(28b)

where
\psi(z)=\frac{d}{dz}\ln\Gamma(z)
is the digamma function.

Following the derivations (
20
) through (
28
), the mean field approximation procedure is summarized in
Algorithm

2
.

\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(L)},\bm{y}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{y}^{(L)}

\hat{\bm{x}}=0.5\cdot\bm{1}

Claim 2
.

converges globally.

Proof.

From
Proposition

1
, sequentially applying the update rule in (

) ensures that the

-divergence

∎

3.3
Inference using approximate message passing

Inspired by expectation propagation
[
28
]
, another approximate inference algorithm can be developed using message passing on cluster graphs
[
27
]
. For convenience, the prior and conditional probabilities from (
15
) through (
17
) are repeated here:

p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})=\mathrm{Bernoulli}(x_{j}|\pi_{j})=\pi_{j}^{x_{j}}(1-\pi_{j})^{1-x_{j}},

p(\pi_{j})=\mathrm{Beta}(\pi_{j}|a,b)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)}\pi_{j}^{a-1}(1-\pi_{j})^{b-1},

p(\bm{y}^{(l)}|\bm{x})=\biggl(\frac{\beta^{(l)}}{2\pi}\biggr)^{M/2}\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{(l)}\|\bm{y}^{(l)}-\bm{\mathbf{\Phi}}^{(l)}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}\biggr].

Here,
\beta^{(l)}
are treated as parameters instead of random variables.

Fig.

2
illustrates a cluster graph corresponding to these probability distributions. The leftmost cluster has an initial potential of
\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(\pi_{j})
. The central cluster has an initial potential of
\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})
. The rightmost cluster has an initial potential of
\prod_{l=1}^{L}p(\bm{y}^{(l)}|\bm{x})
.

Figure 2

The message passing algorithm operates on cluster graphs by facilitating communication between clusters. In general, message passing operates in the following steps:

1.

Each cluster receives messages from its neighboring clusters.

2.

The cluster multiplies its initial potential with all incoming messages, forming the cluster’s belief.

3.

The cluster optionally marginalizes the belief or projects the belief onto another distribution.

4.

Finally, the cluster sends a message to each neighboring cluster. The message is calculated by dividing the cluster’s belief by the incoming message from that neighbor.

All distributions and messages over the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
are represented in the exponential family as follows:

\underbrace{\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-\hat{x}_{j})}_{\frac{1}{Z}}\times\exp\biggl(\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{N}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}}_{\bm{x}^{\top}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\ln\frac{\hat{x}_{1}}{1-\hat{x}_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
\ln\frac{\hat{x}_{N}}{1-\hat{x}_{N}}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\eta}}\biggr),

(29)

where
\bm{x}
is the sufficient statistics,
\bm{\eta}
is the natural parameter,
Z
is the normalization constant, and
\hat{x}_{j}
is the mean of the Bernoulli-distributed variable
x_{j}
.
The natural parameter for the messages
m^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}
and
m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}
are defined as
\bm{\eta}^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}
and
\bm{\eta}^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}
, resulting in

m^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}\propto\exp(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\eta}^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}),\quad m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\propto\exp(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\eta}^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}).

In the cluster graph illustrated in
Fig.

2
, the leftmost cluster’s outgoing message, denoted as
m^{(\pi\to\text{c})}
, is given by

m^{(\pi\to\text{c})}=\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(\pi_{j}).

(30)

The central cluster receives messages
m^{(\pi\to\text{c})},m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}
and multiplies them with its initial potential, resulting in the belief

m^{(\pi\to\text{c})}m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})=m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})p(\pi_{j}).

(31)

This belief, when marginalized over
\pi_{j}
, results in

\begin{split}m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}p(x_{j}|\pi_{j})p(\pi_{j})\,d\pi_{j}&=m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)}\int_{0}^{1}\pi_{j}^{a+x_{j}-1}(1-\pi_{j})^{b+(1-x_{j})-1}\,d\pi_{j}\\
&=m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{B}(a+x_{j},b+1-x_{j})}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)}.\end{split}

(32)

This marginal is then projected onto the exponential family defined in (
29
), and is given by

\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\bm{\eta}}\ D_{\text{KL}}\biggl(m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}\times\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{B}(a+x_{j},b+1-x_{j})}{\mathrm{B}(a,b)}\,\Big\|\,\frac{1}{Z}\exp(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\eta})\biggr)=\bm{\eta}^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}+\ln\frac{\mathrm{B}(a+1,b)}{\mathrm{B}(a,b+1)}\cdot\bm{1}

(33)

Finally, the message passed from the central cluster to the rightmost cluster, denoted as
m^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}
, is computed as follows:

\begin{split}m^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}&\propto\frac{\exp\Bigl(\bm{x}^{\top}\Bigl(\bm{\eta}^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}+\ln\frac{\mathrm{B}(a+1,b)}{\mathrm{B}(a,b+1)}\cdot\bm{1}\Bigr)\Bigr)}{m^{(\text{c}\leftarrow\text{r})}}\\
&=\exp\Bigl(\bm{x}^{\top}\Bigl(\ln\frac{\mathrm{B}(a+1,b)}{\mathrm{B}(a,b+1)}\cdot\bm{1}\Bigr)\Bigr).\end{split}

(34)

The message
m^{(\text{c}\to\text{r})}
is multiplied with the rightmost cluster’s initial potential and projected onto the exponential family defined in (

5
Application to model based defect imaging

A common sensing method for detecting structural defects is eddy current sensing. This section demonstrates how binary vector recovery can be applied to these imaging problems.

5.1
Formulating the defect imaging problem as a binary vector recovery problem

An eddy current system, where the fields oscillate at an angular frequency
\omega
, is described by the following Maxwell’s equations:

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{H}

\displaystyle=\sigma\bm{E}+\bm{J}_{s},

(40a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{E}

\displaystyle=-j\omega\mu\bm{H},

(40b)

where
\bm{E}
is the electric field,
\bm{H}
the magnetic field intensity,
\bm{J}_{s}
the source electric current density,
\sigma
is the electrical conductivity field, and
\mu
is the magnetic permeability field.
The
MFD
(
MFD
) component measured by a magnetic sensor is given by

B=\int_{V}\mu_{0}\bm{H}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}_{\text{sensor}}\,\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{\text{sensor}})\,dV,

(41)

where
\mu_{0}
is the vacuum magnetic permeability,
\hat{\bm{n}}_{\text{sensor}}
is the sensor’s sensing axis,
\bm{r}_{\text{sensor}}
is the sensor’s position, and
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function.

Theorem 5
.

The perturbation in the

\delta\sigma
, and the magnetic permeability field,
\delta\mu

\delta B=\int_{V}\bigl((\bm{E}\cdot\acute{\bm{E}})\,\delta\sigma+(-j\omega\bm{H}\cdot\acute{\bm{H}})\,\delta\mu\bigr)\,dV,

(42)

where
(\bm{E},\bm{H})
represents the electromagnetic field governed by (
40

(\acute{\bm{E}},\acute{\bm{H}})
represents the electromagnetic field governed by

\displaystyle\nabla\times\acute{\bm{H}}

\displaystyle=\sigma\acute{\bm{E}},

(43a)

\displaystyle\nabla\times\acute{\bm{E}}

\displaystyle=-j\omega\mu\acute{\bm{H}}+\mu_{0}\hat{\bm{n}}_{\mathrm{sensor}}\,\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{\mathrm{sensor}}).

(43b)

Proof.

5.2
Imaging defects in metal plates

The first example concerns the imaging of defects in a metal plate. Traditional methods typically involve scanning an eddy current probe across the plate surface, recording the response at each point to form an image, and then using image processing or neural network techniques to identify any defects. However, the resolution of the recorded image is constrained by the Nyquist sampling theorem. By using a model-based approach along with binary vector recovery algorithms, it is possible to significantly reduce the number of samples needed.

The seconds example concerns the imaging of defect in a metal pipe using a bobbin probe. This cylindrical probe, equipped with coils and magnetic sensors, moves through the interior of the pipe. At each position along the pipe, a computed tomography image of any nearby defects is obtained. As the probe advances, these individual images are merged, eventually producing a continuous defect map of the entire pipe length.

To merge the local images obtained at each position, binary Bayes filtering
[
33
]
is used. In this method, each voxel covered by multiple local images is updated by summing the logit values from each recovery. Logit values are clamped to a large number to prevent the probability of defect presence from being exactly 0 or 1, which is a standard practice in Bayesian filtering to avoid irreversible probability states. As the bobbin probe moves along the pipe, each new local image provides updated evidence, which adjusts the voxel’s probability of containing a defect based on both current observations and prior accumulated information. Since summation is commutative, the order in which each local image is merged does not affect the result.

6
Conclusions

This study addresses the problem of recovering binary vectors from linear measurements, an extension of compressive sampling that not only focuses on sparsity but also incorporates the additional constraint of binary values. Two approaches are presented to solve this problem: convex optimization and Bayesian inference.
The convex optimization approach is motivated by the fact that solving the optimization problem with the binary constraint is an NP-hard problem. Therefore, the binary constraint is relaxed to its convex hull. Theoretical results are provided, demonstrating that the convex relaxation is tight when the vector is sufficiently sparse and the measurement matrix satisfies the
BRIP
condition. Additionally, the convex optimization approach can be extended to handle multiple measurement vectors with varying noise variances by augmenting the measurement vectors and matrices with proper scaling.
In the Bayesian approach, a Bayesian network is constructed to represent the linear measurements of a binary vector. It is shown that both
MAP
inference and exact posterior inference on the Bayesian network are NP-hard problems. To address this, an approximate inference algorithm is developed that finds a mean field distribution close to the true posterior in the
KL
-divergence measure. Another approximate inference algorithm is developed using message passing on a cluster graph.
The proposed algorithms are compared with existing compressive sampling methods and demonstrate superior performance in recovering binary vectors. These results suggest that binary recovery algorithms are preferable over sparse recovery algorithms when the binary constraint is applicable.

On the application side, this study demonstrates that the problem of imaging defects in metal structures using eddy current sensing can be formulated as a binary vector recovery problem. This involves analytically linearizing the relationship between the measured
MFD
and the physical property fields. The binary vector recovery algorithms are then applied to perform the imaging, which are able to generate depthwise tomography images of defects in metal plates and pipes.

However, several limitations remain, particularly the challenge of scaling these algorithms to handle larger numbers of vector entries or imaging voxels. In this study, only the mean field approximate inference algorithm exhibits computational complexity that is linear with respect to the number of recovery entries, though it provides biased solutions. In contrast, the convex optimization method offers more accurate solutions but at the cost of higher computational complexity. Future research could focus on optimizing and combining these algorithms to maintain linear complexity while improving recovery performance. Additionally, exploring the application of binary recovery algorithms to other physical measurement systems would be a valuable direction for future work.
