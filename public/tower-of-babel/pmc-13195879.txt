Exercise modality and dose effects on renal function in non–dialysis chronic kidney disease: a Bayesian model-based network meta-analysis
Authors: Wenlong Li, Xiaomeng Lin, Xudong Cai, Minjun Liang, Shirui Shao, Xuanzhen Cen, Yang Song, Guanghui Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global health challenge. Exercise is a key non-pharmacological therapy, but the most effective modality and dose for improving renal function in non-dialysis CKD is unclear.ObjectiveTo compare exercise modalities and quantify dose–response effects on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in adults with non-dialysis CKD.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Central, EBSCO, Embase, and CNKI from inception to July 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adults with non-dialysis CKD. Exercise interventions were classified as aerobic training (AT), resistance training (RT), or mixed physical activity (MPA), which included combined training and other multicomponent interventions. Doses were converted to MET-min/week. A Bayesian dose–response network meta-analysis estimated effects and dose thresholds for eGFR.ResultsTwenty-five RCTs involving 1,827 adults with non-dialysis CKD were included. Studies were published between 2012 and 2024, with sample sizes 20–163 and intervention durations 12–52 weeks. Participants were mostly CKD stages 2–4, with common comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes and obesity. Exercise sessions lasted 30–90 min/week, with doses ranging 208–1,869 MET‑min. Compared with usual care, RT yielded the greatest improvement in eGFR (MD = 5.77 mL/min/1.73 m²; 95% CrI 3.27–8.25), followed by AT (MD = 3.66; 95% CrI 1.25–6.03), whereas MPA showed a smaller, non-significant effect (MD = 1.76; 95% CrI − 0.33–3.79). A non-linear dose–response was observed, with meaningful gains at ~ 264–1,302 MET‑min/week, peaking at ~ 1,200 MET‑min/week. Findings for MPA should be interpreted cautiously and should not be overgeneralized to all specific exercise forms within this category.ConclusionsRT and AT improve eGFR in adults with non-dialysis CKD, with RT showing the greatest effect. A non-linear dose–response indicates moderate weekly volumes offer the most benefit, beyond which gains plateau.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04977-z.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Exercise therapy, Physical activity, Bayesian analysis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive condition defined by a sustained reduction in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or persistent kidney damage for at least 3 months [1]. It affects about 10% of adults worldwide and is among the leading causes of disability and premature death [2, 3]. By 2040, CKD is projected to be the fifth leading cause of global mortality, posing a substantial clinical and economic burden [4]. Current management includes pharmacologic therapies, such as mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, and SGLT2 inhibitors—and nonpharmacological measures like diet and exercise [5]. However, drug therapies often demonstrate limited efficacy and raise safety concerns, particularly in early or non–dialysis CKD [1], highlighting the need for complementary, structured exercise interventions.

Exercise is a key nonpharmacologic strategy that improves physical function and may slow renal decline; it has also been associated with lower mortality in dialysis or transplant populations [6, 7]. In contrast, the evidence for benefit in non–dialysis CKD remains mixed [8], with reviews reaching different conclusions. For example, Karsten et al. reported that aerobic exercise improved eGFR, whereas Liu et al. found no significant effect [9, 10]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in exercise dose (type, intensity, and volume), a central determinant of training response. Yet few studies have systematically characterized dose–response relationships for exercise modality and renal outcomes in non–dialysis CKD. In addition, existing guidelines provide limited practical detail on frequency, and intensity targets, leaving uncertainty for individualized prescriptions [11].

Dose–response meta-analysis allows quantitative evaluation of the association between intervention dose and clinical outcomes, accommodating multiple exposure levels and potential nonlinearity [12]. Bayesian model–based approaches extend this framework by yielding probabilistic estimates with credible intervals and enabling comparisons across dose levels [13, 14]. Given the wide variation in exercise prescriptions for non–dialysis CKD, such methods offer a rigorous way to clarify modality-specific dose–response patterns and to inform evidence-based recommendations.

To address these gaps, we conducted a Bayesian model–based network meta-analysis (MBNMA) of randomized clinical trials to compare exercise modalities and estimate modality-specific dose–response relationships in non–dialysis CKD. We focused on eGFR change and identified weekly dose ranges.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251142071) and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for network meta-analyses guidelines.

Search strategy and data sources

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Central, EBSCO, Embase, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure from inception to July 22, 2025. Based on previous studies [10, 15, 16], we used the search terms “chronic kidney disease*” OR “chronic renal disease*” OR “chronic kidney insufficienc*” combined with exercise-related terms including “exercis*” OR “endurance training” OR “high-intensity interval training” for English databases, while their Chinese counterparts were used in the Chinese database. In addition, we manually screened the reference lists of relevant systematic reviews to identify additional eligible studies [9, 10, 17–22]. The complete search strategy is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were developed based on the Participants, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework. Given the limited number of trials in this field and the clinical diversity of patients with non-dialysis CKD, relatively broad eligibility criteria were adopted to enable a more comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence; however, this approach may also have increased clinical heterogeneity and reduced comparability across studies. The detailed criteria are as follows: (1) Participants: Individuals aged 18 years or older with a definitive diagnosis of CKD, as defined by the “Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of CKD” [23], and not undergoing any form of renal replacement therapy (i.e., dialysis or kidney transplantation). Patients at any stage of non-dialysis CKD (stages 1–5) were included. (2) Intervention: Exercise interventions delivered either alone or in combination with conventional treatments (e.g., pharmacotherapy or dietary management) were eligible for inclusion. Eligible programs had to be planned, structured, and repetitive, with the intent of improving physical fitness or health-related outcomes. Both conventional forms of physical exercise (e.g., aerobic, resistance, or combined training) and mind-body exercise modalities (e.g., Tai Chi, Pilates, yoga) were included. No restrictions were placed on the type, intensity, frequency, setting (e.g., supervised or unsupervised). However, the intervention must involve active exercise sessions conducted over a period exceeding two weeks. (3) Comparisons: Acceptable control conditions included conventional treatment (e.g., pharmacological therapy, dietary advice), routine care, and maintenance of usual lifestyle. (4) Outcomes: The primary outcome of interest was the eGFR, which was used to assess the effects of different exercise modalities and doses on kidney function. (5) Study design: Only RCTs published as full-text, peer-reviewed articles were eligible for inclusion. Opinion articles, congress communications, narrative reviews, single case reports, duplicate publications, and studies published in languages other than English or Chinese were excluded. To minimize language bias and enhance the comprehensiveness of the evidence base, studies published in both English and Chinese were considered. A substantial proportion of clinical trials on exercise interventions for CKD are conducted in China and published in Chinese-language journals. Including these studies improves the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. All included Chinese studies were published in peer-reviewed journals and assessed using the same quality appraisal criteria as English-language studies to ensure methodological rigor.

Study selection

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA) for duplicate removal. Two researchers (WL and XL) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and full texts based on predefined criteria. Disagreements were resolved by a third researcher (GZ). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.84), indicating strong agreement [24].

Data extraction

Two reviewers (WL, YS) independently extracted study characteristics, participant details (sample size, age, sex, comorbidities, CKD stage), intervention features (type, supervision, duration, frequency), and outcomes. Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (XCen). When necessary, corresponding authors were contacted. If the data remained unavailable, they were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer 4.8 (https://apps.automeris.io/wpd4/) based on established settings [25, 26]. Studies without extractable data were excluded. For multi-arm trials, shared comparators were handled to avoid double-counting, and variance was adjusted accordingly.

Data coding and management

A three-level hierarchical coding system was employed to ensure consistency and facilitate the dose-response. At the first level, interventions were broadly categorized as either “Exercise” or “Control” to distinguish active treatments from usual care or placebo. At the second level, exercise interventions were classified by primary modality into aerobic training (AT), resistance training (RT), or mixed physical activity (MPA; including combined training and other multicomponent interventions, such as aerobic plus resistance training), while control groups were categorized as usual care or placebo [27]. This classification was informed by prior exercise dose–response network meta-analytic frameworks and was adopted as a pragmatic analytical strategy in the present study [27]. Because several specific exercise types were represented by only a limited number of trials, broader grouping was necessary to preserve network connectivity and enable stable dose–response modeling. However, this approach was not intended to imply physiological equivalence across all exercise forms within each category. At the third level, interventions were coded by combining the specific type with the estimated weekly energy expenditure, expressed in METs-min/week. This was calculated by multiplying session frequency, duration, and intensity, using standardized MET values from the Ainsworth Compendium [28]. To minimize network fragmentation and facilitate structured comparisons across dose levels, all calculated exercise doses were rounded to the closest convenient pre-specified grouping: 0, 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1250, or 1500 METs-min/week [29]. For example, an intervention delivering 620 METs-min/week was assigned to the 500 group. This stratification strategy enabled distinct modeling of nonlinear dose-effect relationships while preserving a coherent network structure, with each combination of intervention type and dose treated as a unique node in the analysis. Additionally, only the duration of the main intervention was extracted, excluding warm-up and cool-down phases. If a study reported a time frame, its average was calculated for further analysis, while studies lacking this information were excluded [30].

Risk of bias and quality of evidence

Two reviewers (XCai and ML) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [31]. Any discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (SS). Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [32], which classifies evidence as very low, low, moderate, or high. The evaluation considered five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Two authors (WL and YS) conducted the evaluations, which were verified by a third author (GZ) for accuracy.

Data synthesis

Network meta-analysis

All eGFR measurements were harmonized to the unit of mL/min/1.73 m² to ensure comparability across studies. For each trial, we calculated between-group mean differences in change from baseline (change-score MDs) with associated variances. If standard deviations were not reported, they were estimated using available data such as standard errors, 95% confidence intervals (CI), or p values, assuming a correlation coefficient of 0.5 [33]. The r denotes the correlation coefficient between pre- and post-intervention measurements. The calculated mean changes and corresponding standard deviations were subsequently used for Bayesian network meta-analysis.

An MBNMA was conducted using the MBNMAdose and BUGSnet packages in R (version 4.5.1), interfaced with JAGS (version 4.3.2). Four Markov chains were run for 50,000 iterations, discarding the first 10,000 as burn-in. Model convergence was assessed using trace plots and Gelman–Rubin diagnostics (R̂ < 1.05). Relative effects are presented as MDs with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). We evaluated heterogeneity and model fit using the deviance information criterion (DIC) and residual deviance. Consistency was examined by comparing DIC values between the consistency and unrelated mean-effects models, supplemented by node-splitting analyses (Further details are provided in Supplementary File 2). Modalities were ranked by probability of benefit using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA).

Dose-response network meta-analysis

An MBNMA was conducted to assess the relationship between exercise dose and changes in eGFR. Key MBNMA assumptions—network transitivity [34], consistency [35], and connectivity [36]—were satisfied, indicating a coherent evidence structure (Supplementary File 2).

Nonlinear dose–response models (Emax, restricted cubic spline, quadratic, B-spline) were evaluated. The Emax model provided the best fit based on DIC and residual deviance and was selected as the primary model (Supplementary File 3). The B-spline model was used as a flexible alternative to test robustness and reduce model-specific bias. Overlapping significant dose intervals from both models defined the robust effective dose range, representing clinically meaningful effects under varying model assumptions. This dual-model strategy mitigated model misspecification and provided more stable evidence of clinical efficacy [37, 38]. Detailed diagnostics and model comparisons are provided in Supplementary File 6.

The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for eGFR was predefined to assess the clinical relevance of renal changes. For non–dialysis CKD (mainly stages 2–4), the MCID was defined as an improvement or slower decline of ≥ 1.0 mL/min/1.73 m² per year. This threshold was derived from large-scale cohort and simulation studies, which showed that reducing the annual eGFR decline by this amount is associated with approximately a 20% lower risk of kidney failure in this population [39, 40]. Consequently, between-group differences equal to or exceeding this threshold were considered clinically meaningful.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 5,963 potentially relevant records were retrieved from database searches, and an additional 137 were identified through the reference lists of related systematic reviews. After deduplication, title/abstract screening, and full-text review, 25 randomized clinical trials were included. The study selection process is shown in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flowchart of included studies

These studies were published between 2012 and 2024, with 4 in Chinese and the remainder in English. Sample sizes ranged from 20 to 163 participants. Most studies enrolled patients with CKD stages 2–4, commonly accompanied by hypertension, diabetes, or obesity. Participant ages ranged from approximately 47 to 78 years, and the proportion of female participants ranging from 25% to 65%. Exercise interventions included AT, RT, and MPA, with MPA being the most commonly applied modality (n = 13), followed by AT and RT. Each training session lasted between 10 and 90 min, and the total intervention duration ranged from 12 to 52 weeks. The estimated weekly exercise dose ranged from 208 to 1,869 MET-min/week. Baseline and intervention characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Supplementary File 4.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Among the 25 included studies, 5 were judged to have low risk of bias, 16 had some concerns, and 4 had high risk of bias (Fig. 2). Based on the GRADE approach, the overall certainty of evidence ranged from low to moderate. AT and RT were rated as having moderate certainty, whereas MPA was rated as low certainty due to imprecision and risk of bias. Detailed assessments are provided in Supplementary File 5.

Network meta-analysis for eGFR

The NMAs evaluated the effects of AT, RT, and MPA on eGFR compared with usual care. A connected network of direct and indirect comparisons was formed, with most data derived from comparisons with usual care (Fig. 3A). Ranking probabilities (Fig. 3B) indicated that RT had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention, followed by AT and MPA.

Figure 3C summarizes the estimated effects of each exercise intervention on eGFR relative to usual care. RT yielded the greatest improvement (MD = 5.77; 95% CrI: 3.27 to 8.25), followed by AT (MD = 3.66; 95% CrI: 1.25 to 6.03). Although MPA showed a positive trend (MD = 1.76; 95% CrI: − 0.33 to 3.79), the difference was not statistically significant.

Dose-response network meta-analyses

A nonlinear dose–response relationship was observed between total exercise dose and eGFR (Fig. 4A). The posterior median curve showed a rapid increase in eGFR at lower doses, followed by a plateau at higher levels. The effective dose range— defined as the overlap of significant intervals from the Emax and B-spline models, was 264.31–1302.06 METs-min/week. According to the B-spline model, the effect peaked at approximately 1199 METs-min/week, close to the World Health Organization’s recommended upper limit of 1200 METs-min/week for physical activity [41]. Beyond this point, further increases yielded only marginal gains in eGFR, suggesting a plateau and diminishing returns.

Dose–response analyses stratified by exercise modality revealed modality-specific patterns in renal outcomes (Fig. 4B; Supplementary File 6). For AT, the effective dose range was 767.13-1353.79 METs-min/week—the widest among all modalities. The Emax model showed a steep early increase in eGFR, followed by a plateau at higher doses. The B-spline model estimated the peak effect at 1199 METs-min/week, yielding a median improvement of 11.8 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CrI: 3.57–20.25). This value aligns with the WHO-recommended upper limit for weekly physical activity and exceeds the predefined MCID of 1.0 mL/min/1.73 m², indicating a potentially clinically meaningful benefit.

For RT, the effective dose range was 501.67-747.49 METs-min/week. Both models demonstrated consistent dose-response patterns, eGFR increased at lower doses and plateaued thereafter. The B-spline model estimated the peak response at 622.07 METs-min/week, yielding a median gain of 9.23 mL/min/1.73 m² (95% CrI: 5.86–12.64), well above the MCID threshold, with narrower credible intervals supporting the robustness of this finding.

In contrast, MPA showed less consistent dose-response patterns across models. The B-spline model suggested a potential benefit starting at 856.61 METs-min/week, with a peak effect at 998.33 METs-min/week (median gain: 8.99 mL/min/1.73 m², 95% CrI: 3.02–15.02). However, the Emax model failed to identify a statistically credible dose range, indicating model discordance. Moreover, the wide CrI reflects substantial imprecision, limiting confidence in the estimated effect.

Discussion

Using a Bayesian MBNMA, we evaluated how exercise modalities and doses relate to renal function in adults with non–dialysis CKD. Two findings emerged. First, both RT and AT were associated with higher eGFR compared with usual care, with RT showing the largest average effect. Second, the exercise dose–eGFR relationship was nonlinear, rising steeply at lower doses and plateauing at higher levels. The effective dose range—defined as the overlap of high-probability intervals from the Emax and B-spline models—was 264 to 1300 MET-min/week, with a predicted peak near 1200 MET-min/week.

Effects varied by modality. AT had the widest effective range (767.13–1353.79 METs-min/week), while RT conferred benefits at lower doses. For MPA, effect estimates were more heterogeneous and should be interpreted cautiously, given the broad intervention composition within this category and the relatively larger number of included studies. Overall, exercise prescriptions for non–dialysis CKD should consider both total weekly dose and modality.

Previous reviews have investigated the effects of exercise on eGFR in non–dialysis CKD. Consistent with Liu et al. [10], who identified RT as the most effective modality, our study corroborated this finding and further quantified the dose–response relationship. The analysis revealed a nonlinear pattern, with eGFR improvements increasing steeply at lower exercise doses and plateauing thereafter. Specifically, the effective dose range for RT was estimated at approximately 502–747 METs-min/week, with a peak effect near 622 METs-min/week. RT may slow renal decline through multiple biological mechanisms. For example, it can suppress pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, IL-17a, IL-18) and enhance anti-inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-10, IL-15), thereby contributing to systemic inflammatory homeostasis [42]. Concurrently, it can enhance Klotho expression and suppress fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), thereby restoring the Klotho–FGF23 axis, which is associated with renal anti-aging processes and metabolic homeostasis [42]. These adaptations likely contribute to preserving glomerular filtration and slowing CKD progression.

In addition, our results demonstrated that AT significantly improved eGFR in non–dialysis CKD compared with usual care. This finding is consistent with Wyngaert et al. [9], who reported a modest but significant benefit of AT in stage 3–4 CKD. Using a Bayesian framework, this study quantified the AT dose–response relationship, revealing a nonlinear pattern with rapid eGFR gains at lower doses and a plateau thereafter. The effective dose range was approximately 767–1354 METs-min/week, indicating clinically meaningful renal benefits from moderate to high exercise volumes. Mechanistically, AT may enhance renal function through multiple vascular and redox-mediated pathways. It improves systemic redox homeostasis, increases nitric oxide bioavailability, and mitigates oxidative stress—factors that collectively restore endothelial function and promote microvascular vasodilation [43]. These vascular adaptations enhance renal perfusion and glomerular hemodynamics, thereby preserving filtration capacity and slow CKD progression [43]. The plateau beyond approximately 1200 METs-min/week likely reflects a physiological ceiling, where extra training yields diminishing returns.

Compared with the consistent effects of AT and RT, MPA yielded less reliable benefits, with greater variability and wider credible intervals, indicating greater uncertainty in efficacy. Specifically, although the B-spline model identified a possible effective dose range, the Emax model failed to yield credible intervals, further emphasizing uncertainty in the effect estimates. For example, Wu et al. [21] reported significant eGFR improvements after MPA, whereas both this study and Annette et al. [17] found no significant benefits. These discrepancies may result from variations in intervention type, participant characteristics, and the absence of standardized dose quantification. Moreover, the heterogeneity of MPA, which involves varying combinations of AT and RT components, complicates dose specification and may obscure consistent dose–response patterns. The observed inconsistencies between dose–response models underscore the need for more standardized and modality-specific trials to clarify the renal benefits of MPA. The broad modality categories used in this study should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Although necessary for quantitative synthesis, such grouping may conceal important physiological and training-related differences across specific exercise forms, particularly among mind–body and hybrid interventions.

A nonlinear dose–response relationship was identified between exercise and renal function in non–dialysis CKD, with a plateau observed near 1200 METs-min/week. This pattern suggests diminishing marginal benefits beyond moderate to high volumes of exercise. Notably, such a nonlinear association is not unique to CKD. Similar nonlinear dose–response patterns have been observed in other chronic diseases. For instance, higher post-diagnosis physical activity levels have been associated with lower mortality in breast cancer, diabetes, and ischemic heart disease, showing a no-threshold, nonlinear pattern [44]. These findings suggest that although physical activity confers consistent benefits, the incremental gains diminish beyond a certain threshold, highlighting the need to define an optimal and achievable exercise dose.

Current guidelines recommend regular physical activity for non–dialysis CKD, emphasizing that any amount of exercise is better than none [11]. However, this study identified an effective dose range of 264.31–1302.06 METs-min/week that was associated with meaningful improvements in renal function. Clinically, these findings provide guidance for individualized exercise prescriptions. RT yielded notable benefits at lower doses (~ 500 METs-min/week), making it suitable for frail or sedentary individuals, whereas AT exhibited a broader and higher effective range, offering greater flexibility for patients capable of sustaining longer activity.

This is the first study to apply a Bayesian MBNMA to simultaneously evaluate exercise modality and dose in non–dialysis CKD. This framework offers a comprehensive synthesis of heterogeneous evidence and a precise estimation of relative efficacy and optimal dose. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the limited number of trials may have constrained statistical power and reduce generalizability. At the same time, the relatively larger number of studies and participants in the MPA category may have further contributed to the observed heterogeneity and the lack of statistically significant effects. Inconsistencies in MPA classification, along with variations in intervention duration, adherence, and participant characteristics, may have contributed to unresolved heterogeneity. The classification of diverse interventions into AT, RT, and especially MPA may have oversimplified meaningful differences in training characteristics, including movement structure, intensity distribution, and physiological mechanisms. This broad grouping may have contributed to residual heterogeneity and may limit the internal validity of broad-category comparisons. In addition, the relatively broad eligibility criteria, although necessary to ensure a comprehensive evidence base, may have introduced substantial clinical heterogeneity and reduced comparability across studies. Furthermore, although METs-min/week provided a common metric for dose harmonization, the estimation of MET-based dose was partly indirect in some included studies and often required derivation from reported exercise frequency, session duration, prescribed intensity, and intervention type rather than direct measurement. This approach enabled cross-study standardization, but it may have introduced measurement imprecision. Moreover, even when standardized as METs-min/week, exercise dose may not fully capture qualitative aspects of intervention delivery, such as supervision, progression, adherence, or modulation of intensity. Future studies should include more high-quality RCTs to improve robustness and external validity, and to explore how exercise modalities and doses affect both renal and systemic outcomes, incorporating biomarker data and patient-reported outcomes.

Conclusions

This MBNMA synthesized evidence from 25 RCTs involving 1,827 patients with non–dialysis CKD. The interventions examined included AT, RT, and MPA. Both RT and AT significantly improved eGFR, with RT providing the greatest benefit at lower doses. A nonlinear dose-response pattern was identified, with optimal effects observed around 1200 METs-min/week. However, the findings for MPA should be interpreted cautiously and should not be overgeneralized to all specific exercise forms within this category.
