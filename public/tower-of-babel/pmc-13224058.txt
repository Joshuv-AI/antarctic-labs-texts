Robust framework for adaptive field-of-view automatic segmentation in vaginal brachytherapy for endometrial cancer
Authors: Adrià Casamitjana, Ana María Gómez Fresco, Cristian Candela-Juan, Raúl Tudela, Roser Sala-Llonch, Sara Moreno López, Faegheh Noorian, Angeles Rovirosa, Aida Niñerola-Baizán, Antonio Herreros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is one of the few cancers with an increasing rate of mortality – 1.7% per year since mid-2000s [1]. Current clinical treatments involve an initial hysterectomy (i.e., removal of the uterus) through surgical intervention, followed by some adjuvant therapy. Vaginal-cuff Brachytherapy (VBT), with or without external radiotherapy, is an effective postoperative treatment due to its reduced side effects and improved quality of life compared to other similar therapies, with the most important benefit being the reduction in vaginal relapse rates [2], [3]. It consists of the insertion of a cylindrical applicator in the vagina connected to an automatic remote afterloader with a transfer tube that delivers the prescribed absorbed dose to the clinical target volume (CTV) while minimising the side effects to the adjacent neighbouring organs of interest (OOIs). Therefore, VBT requires the accurate delineation of both the CTV and the neighbouring OOIs.

The delineation of the structures of interest is typically carried out manually by a radiation oncologist on pre-treatment Computed Tomography (CT) scans with the applicator in the patient. Generally, this is a tedious and time-consuming process that represents one of the greatest workload burdens in treatment planning. Moreover, it suffers from high intra- and inter-rater variability, large disparities between different levels of expertise and differences in delineation protocols between oncologists, dosimetrists, multiple teams and hospitals, which hinders the reproducibility of the results.

Recently, several groups used automatic segmentation algorithms for CTV and OOI segmentation in radiation therapy. U-Net architectures [4] were initially explored in the context of cervical cancer and endometrial cancer [5] or in general gynaecologic patients [6] showing large overlap between manual and automatic segmentations in relevant organs (uterus, cervix or bladder, among others). Not surprisingly, several commercial tools [7], [8] also appeared and had been analysed and audited in the literature but were surrounded by a lack of transparency on the implementation details. In the specific context of brachytherapy (BT), automatic methods initially leveraged the power of pre-trained 2D networks on large-scale natural images datasets (e.g., ImageNet [9]) to fine-tune them on smaller task-specific medical imaging datasets [10], [11], surpassing 90% overlap between manual and automatic segmentation in relevant OOIs (bladder, rectum, sigmoid), but with poor consistency out-of-plane. A natural extension to those approaches was to train 3D architectures from scratch, such as the work in [12] that reached 80% on the aforementioned OOIs. Upon the appearance of the nnUNet framework [13], which automatically optimises several hyperparameters of the segmentation model, many studies tested it in different contexts [14], [15], [16], [17] reaching larger performances than plain U-Nets and variants thereof.

Although numerous studies have demonstrated promising performance in auto-contouring, most have relied primarily on geometric validation metrics, which alone may be insufficient to establish clinical utility in prospective practice settings [18]. The correlation between the changes in dosimetry calculations and segmentation performance was reported to be small, especially for smaller regions [19]. A more comprehensive evaluation incorporating dosimetric analyses, additional quantitative measures (e.g., workflow efficiency or time requirements), and qualitative assessment was therefore warranted. Moreover, most of the discussed approaches provided independent segmentation for each structure of interest, which posed challenges when merging them. Finally, automatic segmentation in VBT using intracavitary cylinders remained underexplored in the literature.

Hence, in this work we presented a new method for joint CTV and OOI segmentation on pre-treatment CT scans containing the VBT applicator. Our framework was based on nnUnet and followed a specific protocol for segmentation. This study was performed with 289 retrospective postoperative EC patients, and 30 prospective patients, to thoroughly evaluate segmentation performance, analysis of the impact on dosimetry calculations and a clinical transferability assessment.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The dataset used in this work came from EC patients who underwent VBT in the Radiotherapy department of Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (HCB) between 2014 and 2024. Each patient had a pre-treatment CT scan, acquired with either an Aquilion LB (Toshiba) or a Somatom Go.Open Pro (Siemens) CT scanner, using a 1 mm slice thickness. Each patient CT also had the corresponding contour delineation (RTStruct file) for the CTV (treated vagina) and surrounding OOIs (bladder, small bowel and rectum), all of them contoured manually using the Oncentra Brachy v4.6 treatment planning system (TPS). The vaginal CTV was manually delineated as a 3 mm expansion of the applicator and 2.5 cm along the first cylinder with automatic exclusion of the applicator based on Hounsfield number discrimination. The CTV was manually corrected for excess or defect of inappropriate vaginal wall thickness due to generally three systematic adjustments: (i) patient specific variability beyond the applicator cup; (ii) post-surgical suture artifacts at the vagina apex; and (iii) small air gaps as a consequence of the latex sheath covering the applicator. Moreover, for each patient, we had access to the initial treatment plan and dose distribution files. All data available had been properly anonymized. This study was approved by Comité de Ética de la Investigación con medicamentos del Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (CEIm) on the 13/03/2025 with reference number HCB/2025/0202.

Two separate batches of data acquired over disjoint periods of time were defined. In the first batch (D1), we retrospectively collected all data from 7 years of clinical practice (2014–2021). This initial pool of N = 289 patients was used for model development, for which 60% of the cases were used for training (48%, N = 139) and validation (12%, N = 35) and 40% for testing purposes (N = 115). A second batch of data (D2) was built collecting only pre-treatment CT scans on N = 30 patients starting VBT therapy between 08/2023 and 07/2024, contemporary to the development of this work. This dataset was entirely used for evaluation purposes and the corresponding manual contours were made available after model development for evaluation purposes.

Automatic segmentation framework

Our proposed solution works on pre-treatment CT images in DICOM format containing the applicator and outputs the contours of the structures of interest in RTStruct format in order to be imported directly into the TPS. These included CTV (vaginal-cuff) and OOIs (bowel, bladder and rectum).

A general overview of the methodology can be found in Fig. 1. Two dedicated modules were designed for input/output operations using the pydicom library [20]. The CTV and OOI contours needed for the RTStruct format were calculated from the volumetric segmentations using the OpenCV library [21]. The entire framework has been made publicly available in https://github.com/acasamitjana/VBTPlan with clear instructions.Fig. 1General overview. Inference steps of our segmentation framework. The main core of the pipeline consisted of two separate segmentation networks based on the nnUNet framework [13]. The first one provided an initial segmentation of the CTV (Fig. 1, Step 1) on the original images. This initial segmentation was used to automatically define a bounding box centred at the CTV and to align the applicator to the main axes (Fig. 1, Step 2). The second one provided the final joint segmentation of all OOIs and the CTV within the field-of-view (FOV) on the aligned images (Fig. 1, Step 3). A last step was required to register the segmentation back to the original image space using the inverse matrix from the alignment calculated in Step 2. The second step was used to compute the new field-of-view on which CTV and OOI were jointly segmented. This new FOV applied to all patients and included all relevant contours for radiotherapy planning and dose calculations. Specifically, the targeted FOV was defined to contain the original FOV in the left/right (L/R) and anterior/posterior (A/P) but restricted on the direction of the applicator (superior/inferior). This restriction was implemented using a bounding box extending 20 mm above the applicator tip and 20 mm below the first applicator segment. Noticeably, we needed to automatically determine the direction of the applicator. The best way to compute it was to align the applicator direction to the inferior/superior axis. We cast this problem as a registration task by exploiting the applicator symmetry along the L/R and A/P axes. More details on Steps 1–3 can be found in the Supplementary material (Supplementary material A.1–3).

During training, the CTV segmentation (Step 1) was trained on the original data of any resolution, orientation, or field-of-view among other acquisition variables. The joint CTV + OOI segmentation (Step 3) was trained on CT images with the applicator aligned along the left/right (L/R) and anterior/posterior (A/P) directions and restricted to the FOV defined in Step 2.

Evaluation

We compared our segmentation method to three baseline methods (SegResNet, UNetR and Swin-UNetR) implemented in MONAI [22], a vanilla nnUNet [13] and our framework with plain UNet with non-optimised hyperparameters. More details can be found in Supplementary material (Supplementary material A.4). For that, we reported different quantitative evaluation metrics to assess model performance. On one hand, Dice [23] was used to measure the volumetric overlap between the manual and automatic segmentations. Similarly, Hausdorff Distance [24] and Surface Dice [25] metrics were used to evaluate the correspondence between manual and automatic contours. Conversely, we computed the volume-dose distribution with respect to any of the segmented regions and extracted standard dose parameters from both manual and automatic segmentations: D2cm3 on the OOI volumes and D90% on the CTV segmentation. Finally, clinical transferability was assessed using a 1–4 quality scale by an experienced technologist responsible for routine structure delineation and a radiation oncologist (RO) performing manual delineation of the OOIs and CTV, respectively, in an independent and blinded manner. Using the automatic segmentation as base segmentation, we evaluated the quality of the segmentations based on the number of edits and time required for each structure of interest to get a satisfactory result that could be used in treatment planning. More information on evaluation metrics is found in Supplementary material (Supplementary material A.5).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a framework for automatic contouring of pre-treatment CT scans from endometrial carcinoma patients that undergo vaginal cuff brachytherapy (VBT). Our method segmented the clinical target volume (vaginal cuff) and closest OOIs (small bowel, bladder and rectum) with high correspondence and dosimetry differences within the accepted range (±10%). The output was compatible with treatment planning systems.

Compared to manual segmentations, which are done independently in 2D, our AI-based tool provided smoother segmentations out-of-plane due to its 3D nature, which resembles the true underlying organ. We outperformed several baseline methods and nnUNet, a state-of-the-art framework widely used in brachytherapy [17], [26], [27]. The main differences were that we included the small bowel, a complex structure that partially falls beyond what is typically delineated in clinical practice, and we avoided manual initialization as in Prompt-nnUNet approaches [17]. Outliers found in our dosimetry analyses could be attributable to a variety of reasons (e.g., seroma confounder) but in general, we saw higher D2cm3 which were indicative of over-segmentation of the OOIs close to the radiation source.

One limitation of this study is that it used scans from a single institution and contouring protocol. The ability of the method to adapt to different imaging characteristics (resolution, FOV) and the impact on the evaluation metrics is yet to be seen. Another limitation is the exclusion of the sigmoid from our segmentation algorithm. The manual contouring protocol is defined 2 cm above the tip of the applicator and 2 cm below the first cylinder, which excludes the sigmoid for most of the patients. This drastically reduces the number of subjects available for training our AI algorithm risking the overall performance of our solution, and for this reason, we exclude the sigmoid from our study. Finally, a practical limitation of this work, as with related studies in this area, is that the reported metrics serve only as surrogate measures of the true clinical impact of automatic segmentation on patient outcomes, which could ultimately only be established through randomised trials.

A key aspect of our findings relates to the practical applicability of the proposed framework in routine vaginal brachytherapy workflows. The combination of a stable pipeline, robust CTV segmentation, and clinically acceptable dosimetry deviations suggests that the model can serve as a reliable first-pass automatic contouring tool that substantially reduces both manual workload and inter-observer variability. Importantly, the high clinical transferability scores for the CTV, bladder, and rectum indicate that the automatic segmentations can be integrated directly into existing planning pathways. This is particularly relevant for treatment planning time optimization and homogenization of contouring criteria across professionals with varying levels of experience. Furthermore, by aligning the applicator and constraining the field-of-view to the clinically relevant ±20 mm region, the system inherently standardizes the anatomical context presented to the model, which enhances reproducibility and mitigates the heterogeneity typically caused by patient positioning, scanner differences, or variable acquisition protocols.

In many clinical settings, CTV delineation is one of the most time-consuming steps in the planning of postoperative VBT. The ability of our model to produce accurate, smooth 3D contours—often requiring no edits—could help shorten the interval between CT acquisition and treatment delivery, increasing departmental throughput and reducing overall planning time. Additionally, the predictable performance across patients suggests that the method could help harmonize contouring practices across institutions, reducing inconsistencies caused by differing contouring protocols. This is particularly valuable in research settings, where multinstitutional datasets often suffer from substantial variability in contouring guidelines. Finally, the maintained dose metric accuracy underscores that the method preserves the clinically relevant dose metric impact even when geometric deviations occur, making the system suitable not only as an auxiliary tool but also as a potential foundation for semi-automated or fully automated treatment planning in the future.

Future work should explore multi-institutional validation and protocol harmonization to further generalize the model. Nevertheless, our results suggest that robust automatic segmentation in vaginal brachytherapy is feasible and represents a meaningful step toward supporting clinical decision-making, with the potential to contribute to routine practice in gynaecologic oncology as performance is further validated.
