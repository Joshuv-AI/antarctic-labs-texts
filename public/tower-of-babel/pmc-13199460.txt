Elevation-driven influences on soil nutrient dynamics and vegetation structure in temperate subalpine forests of the Western Himalaya
Authors: Renu Rawal, Khashti Dasila, Vikram S. Negi, Lalit M. Tewari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines patterns of soil physico-chemical properties, vegetation attributes, and their interrelationships along an altitudinal gradient (2,100–3,300 m asl) in high-altitude forests of the Western Himalayas. Vegetation was sampled at three locations along 100 m altitudinal intervals within the Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary, Uttarakhand. In each altitudinal belt, three plots (50 m × 50 m) were randomly marked; a total of 105 plots were studied. For tree species, within three plots, 10 quadrates of 10 m × 10 m were laid for trees and saplings, 20 quadrates of 5 m × 5 m for shrubs and seedlings, and 40 quadrates of 1 m × 1 m were laid for herbs. Soil samples were collected from five points along the same transects at two depths (0–15 cm and 15–30 cm) and then composited for physicochemical analysis. Results reveal that soil properties exhibited distinct trends with elevation, with soil moisture content, soil porosity, water-holding capacity, sand content, available phosphorus, and total nitrogen generally increasing, while bulk density, silt content, soil organic carbon, soil organic matter, and C:N ratio declined. Significant differences were observed between upper and lower soil depths, with lower depths exhibiting higher moisture, pH, and clay content, whereas upper depths had higher organic carbon, nutrients, and water-holding capacity. Vegetation patterns also varied with altitude; tree density and species richness declined, shrub richness fluctuated without a clear trend, and herb richness increased. Elevational variation in soil and vegetation was driven by climatic constraints and plant-soil interactions. Higher soil moisture, porosity, and water-holding capacity at higher elevations reflect lower temperatures and reduced evapotranspiration, while declining bulk density indicates reduced compaction. Lower soil organic carbon levels suggest reduced litter inputs from declining tree biomass, whereas higher nitrogen and phosphorus levels reflect reduced nutrient losses. Depth-related patterns result from particle translocation and surface organic inputs. Canonical Correspondence Analysis showed that altitude, soil moisture, bulk density, and silt content were the primary drivers of species composition across life forms. Other soil variables also influenced the composition of specific plant groups in the high-altitude forests of the Himalayan region. The study suggested long-term ecological monitoring of forests for a better understanding of the influence of soil dynamics and vegetation composition in the Himalayan region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42825-0.

Introduction

Mountains are characterised by rapid changes in environmental conditions and plant composition over a short distance along altitudinal and elevation gradients. Both abiotic and biotic factors control the patterns of species diversity and their distribution along altitudinal gradients in the mountains1–5. These changes are indicators of the wide range of micro- and macro-habitat and climatic conditions in this region6,7. The altitudinal gradient provides a natural experimental condition for studying the ecological and evolutionary responses of species to environmental changes in a particular region8. Among the abiotic factors, those that directly influence soil properties also affect plant growth, species diversity, and their composition, as soil is the primary carbon reservoir in the terrestrial biosphere9. It is well reported that plant growth is primarily affected by soil properties6, and the vegetation composition influences the chemical composition of the soil, with a complex interaction in a particular landscape10,11. The concentration of soil nutrients plays a crucial role in contributing to the species diversity of herbaceous plant species in the high-altitude regions of the Himalayas11. The high-altitude limit of forests, commonly known as timberline or treeline, represents one of the most conspicuous physiognomic boundaries, yet it is also one of the most fundamental boundaries12,13, rich in native and endemic species4,14.

The high-altitude forested areas are well recognised for their unique conservation values and richness of economically important biodiversity elements4,15,16. It is well reported that treeline ecotones in high-altitude forests are highly sensitive to climate change and anthropogenic disturbances17,18. Understanding forest dynamics and soil concentration in high-altitude forests along the elevation gradient is vital for conservation planning and management, as these forests require urgent conservation strategies and planning3,4,19. Therefore, researchers are more attentive to understanding the impacts of environmental changes on soil concentration and their effects on vegetation composition in a particular habitat and along gradients20,21. According to Liebig’s law, the most limiting nutrients in a soil are those that regulate plant growth, not the total available amount in a particular habitat. This is the reason that timberline/treeline areas are more likely to have limitations in plant available water and nutrients6 due to (i) harsh climatic conditions22, (ii) extreme cold23, (iii) natural calamities24, (iv) small growing season17,25, (v) anthropogenic disturbances26,27, etc. These factors have a significant impact on soil nutrient concentration and vegetation composition at a local scale within a particular habitat. Adequate knowledge of different forest soils and an understanding of their relationships and interactions with various plant species is of utmost importance, as the above-surface life of a forest directly depends on the underground health of the forest28,11.

Numerous studies on soil nutrient concentrations have been conducted across various regions of the Himalaya, including the North Himalaya11,28–31, Central Himalaya32–36, and Eastern Himalaya37–40. However, it is interesting that only a few studies6,7,11,41 have been conducted in the treeline region of the Himalayas. These studies primarily describe trends in soil nutrient concentrations along altitudinal gradients,however, they lack a comprehensive analysis of species-environment interactions in high-altitude forest ecosystems. In this context, the present study aims to address this gap by conducting a systematic investigation of soil physical and chemical properties in conjunction with vegetation composition and species-environment relationships in the high-altitude forests of the Western Himalaya.

Materials and methods

Study site

The studied sites are part of Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary (KWS), broadly known as the Chopta-Tungnath region (30°30′03.0" to 30°29′23.8" N and 79°09′52.8" to 79°12′42.3" E) in Uttarakhand (Fig. 1). The present study covers an altitude range from about 2100 to 3300 m asl in the high-altitude forested zone in the region. The present study focuses on analysing changes in soil physicochemical properties along the altitude gradient within each 100 m altitudinal belt, from lower closed canopy forests (2100 m) to the subalpine (treeline ecotone) zone (3300 m) in the sanctuary. Sampling approach

Three representative altitude transects (from 2100 to 3300 m) were investigated for generalising compositional patterns of vegetation and soil properties. In general, months of July and August (rainy season) were used for the collection of data on vegetation (herbs, shrubs, and seedlings), while trees and saplings were enumerated from April to September, and the soil samples were collected from April to June (in summer). The study transects were investigated by systematically dividing each transect (from 2100 to 3300 m) into 100 m altitudinal belts. Within each 100 m altitudinal belt, forest vegetation and soil properties were investigated using a systematic random sampling method43. In each altitudinal belt, three plots (50 m × 50 m) were marked randomly,a total of 105 (50 m × 50 m) plots were assessed (Fig. 2). The soil samples were collected from each studied plot (50 m × 50 m) across transects11. For an adequate representation of a given plot, soil samples were collected from 5 different locations, generally one from the centre and four from the corners, in two soil depths (upper, 0–15 cm and lower, 15–30 cm). Later on, a homogenised composite soil was prepared after a thorough mixing process. Around 300 gm soil samples were brought to the laboratory in air-tight polythene bags for physico-chemical analysis. The fresh soil was used for measurements of soil moisture, pH, and bulk density. The remaining collected samples were air-dried and sieved through a 2 mm sieve for further physicochemical analysis.Fig. 2Pictorial diagram of the broad sampling approach used for soil sample collection and vegetation assessment with a 100 m altitudinal belt in the study site.

Physico-chemical properties of soil

The determination of soil moisture content was conducted using the gravimetrical method following Allen44. Soil pH was assessed using a pH meter as described by Black and Allen45. Bulk density and soil porosity were determined per ISO standards46. Water holding capacity was evaluated utilising the Hilgard Cup method47. Soil texture, including sand, silt, and clay content, was analyzed using a density hydrometer, ASTM 152H model, as per the method developed by Bouyoucos48. The organic carbon content was measured by rapid titration, and soil organic matter was calculated using the Walkley and Black49 procedure. Available nitrogen was determined using the macro-Kjeldahl distillation method (Subbiah and Asija50). Available potassium and phosphorus were measured by extraction methods51 and spectrophotometric analysis (UV119), respectively, using the Bray and Olson solution (Olson et al.52). The total nitrogen content was calculated using the standard Kjeldahl procedure53, and the carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio was also determined. The detailed formulas used for calculating various soil parameters are provided in Box 1.

Vegetation assessment

The schematic diagram of the vegetation assessment is presented in Fig. 2. A total of 117 plots (50 m × 50 m) were randomly assessed, resulting in 1050 quadrats for trees and saplings, 2100 quadrats for shrubs and seedlings, and 4200 quadrats for herbs within the entire investigated zone. For tree species, within a 50 m × 50 m plot, 10 quadrates of 10 m × 10 m were laid for trees and saplings, 20 quadrates of 5 m × 5 m for shrubs and seedlings, and 40 quadrates of 1 m × 1 m were laid for herbs, randomly16,54,55 Individuals measuring > 10 cm diameter (diameter at breast height-dbh,130 cm above ground level) were considered as trees (adults), individuals between 3–10 cm diameter as saplings and individuals < 3 cm diameter as seedlings56, and among herbs, angiosperms were enumerated. This quadrat information was pooled for calculating density, frequency, total basal area, and their relative values57, Muller-Dombois and Ellenberg58. The sum of the relative values of density (RD), frequency (RF), and total basal area (RTBA) was considered the Importance Value Index (IVI)59. Following Whittaker60 and Pielou61, species richness was considered simply as the number of species per unit area. For calculating alpha diversity (α), two indices were calculated: (i) Shannon–Wiener information function62 and (ii) Simpson’s Diversity Index63. Species evenness (equability) was determined following64. Considering that beta diversity (β) is an important emergent property, which describes the change in species content from one site or sample to another, it was measured using the Whittaker65 formula as given in Mena and Vazquez-Dominguez66.

Statistical analysis

The normality test, Shapiro–Wilk, was performed in the past version 3 to determine the distribution of the data (normal or not). Since the data did not meet the assumption of normal distribution even after analysing the Z-score (used to normalize) of the data, here we used non-parametric test, Spearman Rank Order Correlation analysis to determine the significant relationship (linear r) between different soil properties and vegetation attributes (phytosociological parameters) using past software to determine the relationship between different soil properties and vegetation attributes (phytosociological parameters). A multiple linear regression model was used to determine the relationship between altitude and soil physico-chemical properties using STATISTICA. The significant difference in soil depths for each soil property was determined using a two-tailed T-test, applied to both dependent variables, and is depicted in a box-whisker plot. Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was performed to examine the interrelationship between environmental variables, and species distribution was determined using the R package vegan (version 3.2.2).

Correlation between soil depths

The correlation analysis of soil properties in relation to depth was presented in S Fig. 1. The soil moisture content (SM) has significant difference in the mean values of both the soil depths; the lower soil depth has significantly (p < 0.01) greater moisture content as compared to the upper soil depth (S Fig. 1a). The upper depth had significantly (p < 0.05) higher water holding capacity (WHC) as compared to the lower soil depth (S Fig. 1d), and the lower soil depth had significantly (p < 0.05) greater pH then the upper soil depth (Fig. 5h). The other soil parameters, such as available nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), available potassium (AK), and total nitrogen (TN) for the upper soil depth, had significantly (p < 0.01) greater proportions than the lower soil depth (S Fig. 1). Vegetation attributes along the altitudinal gradient

Along the altitudinal gradient, tree density decreases and reaches a maximum at altitudes of 2400 to 2600 m, ranging from 215 to 464 individuals per hectare. The sapling and seedling densities also declined, ranging from 0 to 624 individuals per hectare and 160 to 3842 individuals per hectare, respectively. The shrub density ranged from 1003 to 2701 individuals/ha, and the herb density ranged from 62,528 to 342,625 individuals/ha. The tree basal area was maximum (71.79 m2/ha) at the 2600–2700 m altitudinal belt, ranging from 24.36 to 71.79 m2/ha. Tree species richness decreased with altitude, while shrub species richness did not follow any uniform pattern, and herb species richness showed an increasing trend. Correlation between soil and vegetation attributes

The correlation matrix between different variables, including altitude, soil properties (29 variables, comprising 13 physical and 16 chemical properties), and vegetation attributes (31 variables), is depicted in Figure S2 and Table S1. Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was applied for the species-environment (edaphic) relationship at the study site. Considering each life form, trees (35 species), shrubs (44 species), and herbs (212 species) revealed that only four environmental variables (altitude, soil moisture content of upper depth, bulk density of upper depth, and silt content of lower depth) significantly influence the composition and distribution of species across all life forms (Table 3 & Table S2). Three environmental variables, namely, the sand content (SA1), pH1 and the available nitrogen (AN1) of the upper depth (AN1), play a significant role in the composition of tree and herb species (Table 3). However, environmental variables, including bulk density at lower depth (BD2), soil organic carbon at upper depth (SOC1), and available phosphorus from both depths (AP1 and AP2), play a significant role in determining the distribution of shrub and herb species. Soil variables, such as available nitrogen at lower depths (AN2) and total nitrogen at upper depths (TN1), influence the diversity of trees and shrubs. Soil moisture (SM2) and bulk density (BD2) at lower depth, clay content (CL1), soil organic matter (SOM1), soil porosity (SPor1), water holding capacity (WHC1), and available potassium (AK1) at upper depth play a significant role in the distribution of herbs in the study area. Considering the CCA scatter plot for tree, shrub and herb species, four quadrants (clock wise) represents positive and negative (quadrants: I- true positive (+ & +); II-false positive (+ &-); III-true negative (-&-); and IV- false negative (-& +) effects of environmental variables on the individual species occurred in the particular quadrant. The environmental variables occurring in quadrant I affect positively; quadrant II may affect positively or negatively, quadrant III affects negatively on species, and quadrant IV may affect negatively or positively on individual species. The species that occurred in the same quadrant represent species that have more similarity with each other than the species that occurred in other quadrants. Additionally, species that occur in different quadrants but are closer to the orbit exhibit similarities with each other, despite being in different quadrants. The scatter plot for tree, shrub, and herb is depicted (Fig. 5a-f). The species composition and environmental variables affecting the distribution of individual species within each quadrant are depicted (Table S3).

Discussion

Soil nutrient concentration along the altitudinal gradient

It is well reported that climate, topography, and geography profoundly influence the edaphic characteristics along elevations in mountains67,68. Elevational variations influence the pedogenesis, physical, and chemical properties of soil, which subsequently affect the vegetation composition in any particular ecosystem69,70. Temperature is a major controlling factor, and altitude alone accounted for 73% of the variation in soil organic carbon (SOC) storage39,71,72. Our study indicates that nutrient concentrations decrease with increasing soil depth in the study area, primarily due to the decomposition of organic matter that occurs in the uppermost layers10,11. The soil moisture of both depths, water holding capacity of the upper depth, sand content of the lower depth, available phosphorus of both depths, and total nitrogen of the upper soil depth were positively correlated with altitude as reported in previous studies28, Malik et al., 2021). The moisture content and water holding capacity in our study significantly increase along the altitudinal gradient, often due to lower temperatures, snowfall, slow evaporation, and high precipitation9,73,74. The decrease in temperature with elevation is defined as the Temperature Lapse Rate (TLR), and it was reported as -0.58 between 1500 and 2500 m and -0.62 between 2500 and 3360 m for the study area75. It affects vegetation and soil characteristics, especially the moisture content, which decreases with increasing LTR42. The significant decrease in bulk density and increase in porosity are attributed to a high amount of organic matter, which is responsible for expanding pore spaces,this is the reason bulk density also shows a decreasing trend with increasing depth76,33. Lower soil moisture at greater depths, despite higher clay content, is likely due to higher compaction and lower porosity, which restrict water infiltration and storage. The deeper layers also have significantly lower organic carbon and organic matter, which reduces aggregation and effective water retention. In contrast, surface soils receive direct rainfall inputs and benefit from greater biological activity, enhancing moisture retention. Together, these factors override the water-holding advantage of higher clay content at depth.

A slightly acidic nature of the soil, an increase in the proportion of sand, and an increase in organic carbon were recorded along with increasing altitude. The low decomposition of organic carbon at low temperatures and high rainfall is an important attribute that contributes to soil acidity68,77. Soil organic carbon (SOC) exhibited a declining trend along the altitudinal gradient, consistent with observations from earlier studies78. This decline may be attributed to variations in soil texture, reduced litter inputs, and lower decomposition rates, as well as weakened microbial activity under the low-temperature conditions at higher elevations79–81. The available phosphorus was found to be increased, which might be due to the slight decrease in pH with increasing elevation. The growth of trees slows down at higher altitudes, as there is not enough sink for vital nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, resulting in their accumulation in the soil78,82,83. The vital role of soil nutrients in seed germination and tree recruitment is well established84–88. Thus, changes in the soil environment and resources have a significant impact on species dynamics, potentially playing a crucial role in shifting tree species in the treeline zone and towards higher altitudes.

Soil and vegetation correlation

The plant and soil have a complex interrelationship due to their temporal development together over a long period28,77,89. The species richness of herbs has been found to be positively correlated with altitude in the present study, as reported in previous studies from the Himalayan region10,16. Soil moisture content in both soil depths showed a significant correlation with species richness and density of herbs and shrubs, as well as with the concentration of dominance and beta diversity of trees. This pattern suggests that increasing soil moisture along the elevational gradient is associated with higher diversity and density of herb species in high-altitude forests. Soil moisture is reported to be more important for the establishment of seedlings and the growth of tree species at south-facing slopes, while temperature is more important at north-facing slopes90. The lower bulk density is responsible for high soil porosity,if the soil has a greater bulk density, plants may struggle to penetrate the soil and absorb nutrients91. The soil organic carbon (SOC) exhibits a significant positive correlation with organic matter, available phosphorus, C:N, and total nitrogen92,93. This suggests that the easy decomposition of organic matter leads to high concentrations of soil nutrients, as reported in previous studies71,82,83. The soil organic carbon had a significant negative correlation with the density of herbs and species richness, as well as the concentration of tree dominance, beta diversity of trees, saplings, and seedlings. This suggests that the high concentration of SOC in soil is responsible for low herb density and species richness, as well as affecting beta diversity89,94. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies, where elevated SOC levels, often linked to heavy litter deposition and accumulation of organic matter, can limit light penetration, alter nutrient availability, and limit germination of seeds (García‐Palacios et al.19). This can be attributed to the dumping of high litter and organic matter on the soil surface, which restricts the establishment of germinated seeds in the soil. In contrast, higher soil phosphorus concentrations were positively associated with herb species richness and diversity, but negatively associated with tree species richness in upper elevation zones, indicating that P availability may differentially benefit plant functional groups95,96. Furthermore, species dominance patterns appear to be strongly shaped by site-specific physical conditions and microclimatic factors, which collectively regulate species richness within a given habitat97,89,77.

Vegetation-environment interaction

Soil is one of the most significant factors controlling the vegetation of any area, as it serves as a reservoir for all essential nutrients72,89. Variation in edaphic composition leads to the genesis of different forest types, vegetation growth, and species abundance98,99. Canonical correspondence analysis (CCA), an exploratory statistical method, was analysed to assess the influence of environmental variables on species composition100. The analysis revealed that altitude, soil moisture content, and bulk density are the primary environmental variables influencing the overall species composition across all life forms. The CCA scatter plot revealed a positive correlation between altitude and soil moisture at the upper depth, as well as between the composition of tree species and altitude. The sand and silt content of the upper soil depth significantly affected the species composition, likely due to its influence on soil texture, water-holding capacity, aeration, and nutrient availability. The SOC of upper soil depth has a significant impact on the composition of shrubs and herbs,it may also affect the root penetration of herbaceous vegetation (Table S3). The available nitrogen of the upper soil depth showed a significant impact on trees and herbs, while the available nitrogen of the lower soil depth and the total nitrogen of the upper soil depth showed a significant impact on tree species richness. This suggests that the nitrogen content plays a significant role in plant growth and intake by plants in the form of nitrides. Available phosphorus had a significant impact on the growth of trees and shrubs, likely due to its role in root development and reproductive processes in perennial woody species101. In contrast, available potassium had a significant impact on herbaceous species, possibly due to its role in stress tolerance in high-altitude forests. A better understanding of soil dynamics is possible through the establishment of long-term ecological monitoring plots in different forest types in the Himalayan region102,103. Such plots allow researchers to track gradual and often subtle changes in soil physical, chemical, and biological properties over time, rather than relying on short-term snapshots. By comparing monitoring plots in different forest types, such as temperate broad-leaved forests, coniferous forests, subalpine forests, and mixed stands, it becomes possible to evaluate how vegetation composition, litter quality, root dynamics, and microbial communities influence soil processes.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the significant influence of elevation on both soil properties and vegetation composition in the high-altitude forests of the Western Himalaya. The key soil physical and chemical properties exhibited distinct trends along the altitudinal gradient. These changes reflect a shift in edaphic conditions towards more porous, nutrient-available, and moist soils at higher elevations, despite the overall dominance of sandy and acidic soils throughout the gradient. Vegetation analysis revealed that tree species richness and density declined with increasing altitude, while herbaceous diversity increased, indicating a shift in life-forms along the elevation gradient. Shrub species richness showed no consistent trend, suggesting variable responses to environmental gradients. Upper-soil properties such as moisture, bulk density, texture, and nutrient availability disproportionately influence species distribution, especially for herbs and trees, indicating that shallow soil processes drive overall ecosystem functioning. The microbial and biochemical aspects of soil also permit deeper exploration to strengthen ecological inferences, as soil microbial communities play a crucial role in nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, and overall soil health; however, their contributions remain underexplored in the context of altitudinal gradients. The results of the study confirm a strong species-environment relationship in the treeline zone, where both soil depth and elevation-driven variability in soil properties regulate the structure and diversity of the plant community.

Climate-driven changes in temperature and precipitation are likely to alter soil moisture regimes, organic matter dynamics, and nutrient availability. Such shifts may favour fast-growing, nutrient-responsive herbaceous species over slow-growing woody taxa, potentially leading to changes in species dominance. Additional multivariate models and machine learning (ML) approaches could improve predictive accuracy. Frequent sampling across different seasons would provide deeper insights into the effects of climate variability and ongoing environmental changes, like elevation-dependent warming on soil processes. An extensive network of long-term monitoring sites covering a broader altitudinal range is required to understand the impacts of climate on soil dynamics. The present study provides valuable insights into soil-vegetation interactions within a defined elevation range; however, expansion to additional sites with varying climatic and topographic conditions would enhance the generalizability of our findings. Conservation efforts must protect soil structure and organic matter through minimising anthropogenic disturbances, and similar soil-vegetation analyses should be replicated across Himalayan protected areas. Furthermore, incorporating remote sensing and GIS-based analysis can help in assessing large-scale spatial patterns in soil attributes and their influence on vegetation composition in the high-altitude forests of the Himalaya.
