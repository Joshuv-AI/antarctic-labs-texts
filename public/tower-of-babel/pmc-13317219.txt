Plasmodium vivax infection in Duffy-negative populations in Sudan: a systematic review and meta-analysis of host–parasite genetic adaptation
Authors: Mohammed Elfaki, Safaa Ahmed, Musab M. Ali Albsheer, Muzamil Mahdi Abdel Hamid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSudan is one of the few African countries reporting increasing Plasmodium vivax infections despite the high prevalence of the Duffy-negative phenotype, historically considered protective against this parasite. Emerging molecular evidence challenges this paradigm. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to synthesize evidence on the prevalence and geographic distribution of P. vivax infection, Duffy antigen polymorphisms, parasite genetic adaptations, and diagnostic limitations in Sudan.MethodsThis review followed PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025127714). The reports of P. vivax infection in Sudan focusing on host Duffy status and the parasite genetic variability from 2005 to 2025 were identified through systematic searches of PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and EMBASE with predefined Boolean search operators. To determine the risk of bias, the Joanna Briggs Institute prevalence study checklist was employed. To obtain a pooled prevalence estimate in Duffy-negative individuals, a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird estimator) was conducted. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic, while publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test.ResultsA total of sixteen studies conducted between 2005 and 2025, including 5,753 participants from various regions of Sudan, were included. P. vivax infection was reported in both Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative individuals. Meta-analysis of five studies reporting host Duffy antigen status showed a pooled prevalence of P. vivax infection of 11.7% (95% CI 7.2–17.3%) among Duffy-negative individuals. The studies showed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 56%). Substantial genetic diversity was observed in the P. vivax Duffy Binding Protein (PvDBP), including multiple haplotypes and both Malagasy-type and Cambodian-type gene duplications. Most studies were assessed as having low to moderate risk of bias, and funnel plot inspection did not suggest substantial publication bias, although interpretation is limited by the small number of studies.ConclusionsThis systematic review and meta-analysis provide evidence that P. vivax infection occurs among Duffy-negative individuals in Sudan, challenging the long-standing assumption of complete Duffy-mediated protection. The observed parasite genetic diversity highlights adaptive mechanisms that may facilitate infection in Duffy-negative hosts. Strengthening molecular surveillance and integrating host–parasite genomic data into national malaria control programs will be critical to inform malaria elimination strategies and vaccine development in Africa.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05923-y.

Introduction

Plasmodium vivax is one of the most geographically widespread human malaria parasites, responsible for an estimated 4.5–5 million clinical cases globally, particularly in Asia, Latin America, and some countries in East Africa [1]. Historically, P. vivax was considered uncommon in sub-Saharan Africa due to the high prevalence of the Duffy-negative (Fy a–b–) blood group, which lacks the Duffy antigen receptor for chemokines (DARC) essential for parasite invasion of erythrocytes [2].

Previously, it was proposed that Duffy negativity generally provides strong immunity against P. vivax. Consequently, malaria surveillance activities have primarily targeted P. falciparum. This paradigm has been challenged by emerging molecular and epidemiological evidence of P. vivax infections in Duffy-negative individuals in several areas across Africa such as Ethiopia, Madagascar, Cameroon, and Sudan [3, 4]. These observations indicate that P. vivax may employ alternative erythrocyte invasion pathways or utilize host receptors beyond the traditional Duffy-dependent mechanism.

In Sudan, P. vivax infection has been reported in regions previously classified as non-endemic due to high levels of Duffy negativity. More importantly, this underscores the need for further research into transmission dynamics, asymptomatic carriers, and underreporting of disease burden. There may be genetic differences between host binding proteins and the parasite P. vivax Duffy Binding Protein (PvDBP) ligands that contribute to this increased host range, including the presence of polymorphisms and duplications in PvDBP [5, 6].

Although concern is increasing, there remains a widespread research gap in systematic and regional analyses, particularly in Sudan. Most existing studies are based on small data sets, typically in the form of case series or limited molecular studies, with little synthesis across epidemiology, host genetic markers, and parasite diversity. This highlights the need for evidence provided by national and regional malaria programs to address the rising threat of P. vivax among Duffy-negative populations [7, 8]. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim to synthesize available evidence on P. vivax infections in Duffy-negative individuals in Sudan, focusing on prevalence, host genetic background, and parasite diversity, and to assess implications for malaria diagnosis, control, and vaccine development.

Recent molecular studies from Sudan have increasingly documented P. vivax infections among Duffy-negative individuals, indicating that transmission in this population may be more widespread than previously recognized. This emerging evidence challenges traditional assumptions of host resistance and suggests that the burden of P. vivax infection in Sudan may be underestimated, particularly in areas where molecular diagnostic tools have not been widely implemented. Understanding the extent of this hidden transmission is essential for improving malaria surveillance, guiding control strategies, and informing vaccine development in regions where Duffy negativity predominates.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines to comprehensively compile molecular, genetic, and epidemiological data on Plasmodium vivax infections in Sudan, with particular emphasis on Duffy-negative populations. This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement (Supplementary Table S1). A predefined protocol guided the selection criteria, literature search strategy, data extraction process, risk of bias assessment, and analytical approach. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD42025127714). The study was designed to integrate both qualitative synthesis and quantitative estimates of P. vivax prevalence and genetic diversity.

Literature search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search of four electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and EMBASE) to identify studies published between January 2005 and March 2025. The search used predefined keywords, combining all possible variations of "Plasmodium vivax" or "P. vivax" with "Duffy-negative" or "FY*BES, " "Sudan" or "Sudanese, " and terms related to parasite genetic diversity (e.g., "genetic diversity, " "PvDBP, " "Duffy binding protein, " "gene duplication, " or "vaccine"). The reference lists of relevant reviews and regional sources were also manually examined to identify additional eligible studies. The complete database search strategies used for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The search strategy included both epidemiological outcome terms (e.g., prevalence and Duffy antigen status) and parasite genetic diversity within a single query. Although this method was meant to identify the studies that covered both of the objectives, it might have lacked sensitivity in identifying the studies that looked at either of the two epidemiological or genetic sides. This is one of the limitations that are taken into consideration during the interpretation of the findings.

Eligibility criteria

To meet eligibility requirements, studies must be conducted in Sudan or with Sudanese populations. They should include molecular detection of Plasmodium vivax (such as PCR or sequencing) and provide available data on Duffy blood group status, PvDBP diversity, or gene duplications. Only primary research articles, such as cross-sectional, cohort, or case–control studies, were included.

Studies involving non-human subjects, those not confirmed at the molecular level as P. vivax, studies focused on other Plasmodium species, and articles that are not original research (such as reviews or case studies) were excluded. Two independent reviewers evaluated each identified record for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria at the title and abstract levels. The same two reviewers further assessed each record during full-text review to determine inclusion. Disagreements were resolved by consensus, and if necessary, a third reviewer was consulted.

These criteria were applied to ensure that included studies directly addressed the primary objectives of this review, specifically the assessment of P. vivax infection in Duffy-negative individuals and the investigation of parasite genetic diversity. However, this approach may have excluded studies reporting epidemiological data without Duffy stratification or genetic analyses.

Study selection and screening

A total of 729 records were initially identified through database searching and manual reference screening, of which 178 duplicates were removed. The remaining 551 records underwent title and abstract screening, resulting in the exclusion of 508 studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Full-text assessment was conducted for 44 articles, of which 28 were excluded for the following reasons: 12 studies did not report parasite genetic data; 10 studies did not stratify P. vivax infections by host Duffy antigen status despite including Duffy-negative cohorts; and 6 studies did not contain primary data (e.g., reviews or case reports).

Sixteen studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis. Of these, fifteen studies were identified through database screening and full-text assessment, while one additional study [9] was identified through an updated manual search during manuscript preparation. Of the included studies, five that explicitly reported Duffy antigen status among P. vivax–positive individuals were eligible for quantitative meta-analysis, while the remaining studies lacking Duffy genotype stratification were included in the qualitative synthesis only. Data extraction and management

Study characteristics were extracted using a standardized data collection form that included author, year of publication, study setting, population type, sample size, number of P. vivax-positive cases, Duffy antigen status, diagnostic and genotyping methods, and key molecular findings such as PvDBP polymorphisms, haplotypes, and gene duplications. Two reviewers independently performed data extraction, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist on prevalence studies was used to determine the risk of bias. The tool assesses various areas, such as sample representativeness, sampling procedures, adequacy of sample size, measurement of the condition, data analysis, and response rate. Each item was responded to as yes, no, unclear, or not applicable. The proportion of criteria met was used to categorize the studies as low, moderate, or high risk of bias, with higher scores representing lower risk of bias.

For studies reporting P. vivax prevalence in Duffy-negative individuals, pooled estimates were calculated using a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model with 95% confidence intervals. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the meta and metafor packages. Proportions were stabilized using the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic [10]. Sensitivity analysis was performed using a leave-one-out approach [11]. Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression test [12]. Subgroup analyses were conducted where data permitted. All analyses were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05. Detailed descriptions of statistical procedures and model specifications are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Results

The current systematic review and meta-analysis included 16 eligible studies conducted in various geographical regions of Sudan from 2005 to 2025. These studies employed a range of research methodologies, including molecular diagnostics, serological assays, and population-based epidemiological surveys, to investigate P. vivax infections in Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative individuals. Five studies focused on the Duffy antigen genotype and provided data on P. vivax prevalence in Duffy-negative populations for the meta-analysis, while the remaining eleven studies contributed to the qualitative synthesis. The characteristics of the 16 studies included in the qualitative synthesis are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.The findings were organized into the following thematic areas: (1) Geographic distribution of studies, (2) Host genetic diversity, (3) Parasite genetic diversity, and (4) Diagnostic challenges and meta-analytic outcomes.

Geographic distribution of P. vivax studies in Sudan

A spatial analysis of study distribution showed that most investigations of P. vivax in Sudan were concentrated in eastern and central states, particularly Kassala, Khartoum, River Nile, Gezira, and White Nile (Fig. 2A). Kassala emerged as the most consistent hotspot for reporting P. vivax, supported by both clinical and molecular studies [13]. The geographic distribution of studies indicates confirmed P. vivax infections, reports of Duffy-negative cases, and regions where parasite genetic diversity, including PvDBP variation, has been investigated [14].

The map highlights clear regional differences in surveillance coverage. The most frequently studied sites were Kassala (nine studies), Khartoum (five studies), and River Nile (three studies). Additional studies were reported in El Gedaref, Sennar, and North Kordofan, whereas western and southern regions such as Darfur, South Kordofan, and Blue Nile remain substantially under-surveyed due to limited molecular diagnostic capacity.

Among the 16 included studies, only five (31%) reported host Duffy antigen genotype and were therefore eligible for quantitative meta-analysis, while the remaining eleven studies (69%) did not stratify results by Duffy status and were included only in the qualitative synthesis (Fig. 2B). Overall, this spatial overview highlights important gaps in molecular surveillance across Sudan and underscores the need for expanded genomic monitoring to better understand the epidemiology and transmission dynamics of P. vivax.

Host genetic diversity: Duffy antigen expression and P. vivax infection

The Duffy antigen receptor (DARC), encoded by the FY gene, is considered the classical pathway for P. vivax erythrocyte invasion. However, recent studies indicate that this is no longer considered the only pathway for P. vivax invasion. This review examines five key studies in Sudan reporting Duffy phenotypes, which demonstrated significant infection in Duffy-negative patients and found no evidence that the FY*BES/*BES genotype provides absolute protection against P. vivax. Abdelraheem et al. [15] were the first to confirm a Duffy-negative case at 8.3%, based on 48 P. vivax-positive samples. Albsheer et al. [16] reported the highest rate (17.9%), followed by Hoque et al. [17] with a rate of 16.7%, and Ahmed et al. [18] with a rate of 2.1%. More recently, Schroeder et al. Parasite genetic diversity: PvDBP polymorphisms and gene duplications

Genetic diversity of the P. vivax Duffy Binding Protein (PvDBP), especially in domain II, is a major factor that determines erythrocyte invasion and immune evasion. Figure 4 gives a summary of the distribution of PvDBP haplotypes and gene copy number variation in Sudanese isolates. Sudanese isolates revealed 11 haplotypes of PvDBP (H1-H11), and a number of them were common between Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative individuals, reflecting a considerable amount of allelic diversity (Fig. 4A). Along with the haplotypic variation, the duplication of the PvDBP gene was observed in a few isolates, such as Malagasy-type and Cambodian-type duplications. In addition to specific PvDBP studies, genome-wide analysis also demonstrates host-specific adaptation to parasites based on Duffy status. A recent whole-genome sequencing study by Schroeder et al. [9] of Sudan revealed that the nucleotide diversity of P. vivax was significantly higher in isolates of Duffy-positive individuals and was relatively conserved in isolates of Duffy-negative hosts. Although this decreased total diversity, multiple loci related to erythrocyte invasion and immune evasion, such as PvDBP1, PvMSP3.5, and PvRBP2a, were found to be under positive selection (Fig. 5). Broader genetic structure: microsatellite haplotypes and global comparison

Microsatellite genotyping of 113 Sudanese P. vivax isolates by Albsheer et al. [19] identified 91 unique haplotypes and 8 haplotypes shared with other populations worldwide, with expected heterozygosity ranging from 0.74 to 0.86, indicating a very high level of intra-population diversity. The fixation index (FST), which measures genetic differentiation, showed a low level of differentiation between the Sudanese and East African populations and a moderate level of differentiation compared to South Asian isolates. These values suggest a high level of gene flow and connectivity in a historical context between Sudan and the global P. vivax gene pool. Diagnostic and surveillance limitations

Despite the increasing number of reported P. vivax cases in Sudan, the diagnosis of this malaria species still faces significant limitations. Earlier studies relied primarily on light microscopy for parasite detection, which has limited sensitivity for identifying low-density and asymptomatic infections, particularly among Duffy-negative individuals. In contrast, more recent investigations have increasingly incorporated molecular diagnostic approaches such as nested PCR and quantitative PCR (qPCR), enabling more sensitive detection of submicroscopic infections. However, national malaria surveillance systems rarely incorporate parasite genetic diversity or routine host Duffy phenotyping. Consequently, underreporting and incomplete molecular characterization of infections remain major challenges, limiting accurate assessment of the true burden and transmission dynamics of P. vivax in Sudan.

Meta-analytic findings: P. vivax in Duffy-negative individuals

Data from five molecular studies were included in the meta-analysis [9, 15–18], each reporting both P. vivax infection status and host Duffy antigen genotype. The pooled prevalence of P. vivax infection among Duffy-negative individuals was 11.7% (95% CI 7.2–17.3%), indicating that infections occur in populations historically considered resistant. Moderate between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 56%), while Cochran’s Q test approached but did not reach statistical significance (Q = 9.12, df = 4, p = 0.058). The highest prevalence estimates were reported by Albsheer et al. [16] (17.9%) and Hoque et al. [17] (16.7%).

The forest plot summarizing individual study estimates and the pooled prevalence is shown in Fig. 7A. Visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested general symmetry (Fig. 7B), and Egger’s regression test did not indicate strong evidence of small-study effects, although interpretation is limited by the small number of studies included (n = 5). Discussion

This meta-analysis and systematic review demonstrate that Plasmodium vivax is transmitted among both Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative groups in Sudan, challenging the long-standing assumption that Duffy negativity confers complete protection against infection [2]. Malaria surveillance in Sudan has historically relied on microscopy, which has limited sensitivity for detecting low-density and asymptomatic infections, likely leading to underestimation of the true P. vivax burden prior to the adoption of molecular diagnostics. Our quantitative synthesis supports these findings, with a pooled prevalence of 11.7% among Duffy-negative individuals and moderate heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 56%). Although Cochran’s Q test did not reach statistical significance, this likely reflects the limited statistical power of heterogeneity tests when only a small number of studies are included.

The epidemiological results are complemented by emerging genomic evidence that P. vivax populations in Sudan have adaptive diversity. Recent whole-genome sequencing [9] indicated that the nucleotide diversity of isolates from Duffy-positive individuals was higher but isolates from Duffy-negative individuals were found to be relatively more conservative, suggesting the possibility of host-specific selection pressure. This is prematurely observed using meager data on genomes and must be viewed with some care but is in line with larger evidence of parasite adaptation to host genetic backgrounds.

Host genetic factors, especially the polymorphism of the Duffy antigen, are crucial in determining the susceptibility to infection by P. vivax. Although the Duffy-negative phenotype has traditionally been thought to be protective, the rise in the number of P. vivax infections in Duffy-negative people in Sudan implies that this defense is not complete. Diversity in Duffy genotypes and their geographic distribution could play a role in diverse patterns of transmission and the adaptation of the parasite, highlighting the importance of integrating host genetic data into epidemiological studies.

Genetic variation in the P. vivax Duffy Binding Protein (PvDBP) is particularly notable, including several haplotypes found in Sudan, as well as Malagasy- and Cambodian-type gene duplications [18, 19]. The presence of several PvDBP haplotypes and two types of gene duplications, the Malagasy-type and the Cambodian-type, provides strong evidence of substantial genetic diversity, which can result in the adaptation of the parasites and lead to the fact that P. vivax can infect Duffy-negative individuals. These duplications have been identified in parasites from both Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative hosts, suggesting functional redundancy and enhanced invasion efficiency rather than complete dependence on the Duffy antigen, and may contribute to parasite persistence in populations where the Duffy-negative phenotype is common.

The ability of P. vivax to infect Duffy-negative individuals suggests the involvement of alternative erythrocyte invasion mechanisms. While the classical invasion pathway depends on the interaction between PvDBP and the Duffy antigen receptor for chemokines (DARC), P. vivax may utilize additional ligands to invade erythrocytes. Members of the reticulocyte binding protein (RBP) family, such as PvRBP2a, have been implicated in Duffy-independent invasion pathways. Furthermore, PvDBP gene duplication and polymorphism may enhance binding efficiency or provide functional redundancy, potentially enabling parasites to overcome the absence of the Duffy antigen. Although these mechanisms remain incompletely understood, they provide a plausible biological basis for infection in Duffy-negative populations.

Population genetic analyses show that Sudanese P. vivax isolates are closely related to East African populations, with moderate differentiation from South Asian isolates, as expected from historical gene flow and segregation in these regions [19]. Other reports of Duffy-negative infected human cases in Ethiopia, Madagascar, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo [20, 21], support the presence of a geographically connected yet locally adapted parasite population.

Diagnostic limitations also play a significant role in shaping current understanding of P. vivax epidemiology in Sudan. Light microscopy, historically the primary diagnostic tool, has limited sensitivity for detecting low-density and asymptomatic infections, particularly among Duffy-negative individuals. The increasing use of molecular diagnostic methods, such as nested PCR and quantitative PCR, has improved detection of submicroscopic infections and revealed previously unrecognized transmission. However, limited implementation of these techniques and the lack of integration of parasite genotyping and host Duffy phenotyping into national surveillance systems continue to constrain accurate estimation of disease burden.

Overall, the evidence indicates that P. vivax transmission in Sudan involves a genetically diverse parasite population capable of infecting both Duffy-positive and Duffy-negative hosts. Sudan therefore represents an important epidemiological setting for investigating host–parasite adaptation and may serve as a key model for improving P. vivax surveillance and informing vaccine strategies across sub-Saharan Africa. These findings also highlight the importance of integrating molecular diagnostics and host genetic data into national malaria surveillance systems to better capture the evolving epidemiology of P. vivax in Africa [22]. A combined visualization of geographic distribution and pooled prevalence is provided in Supplementary Figure S2 to contextualize the epidemiological significance of Sudan within the broader global evidence of Plasmodium vivax infection in Duffy-negative populations.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is the integration of epidemiological, host genetic, and parasite genomic data within a systematic review and meta-analytic framework, providing both qualitative and quantitative synthesis of P. vivax infection in Sudan. The inclusion of molecular and whole-genome studies strengthens biological inferences regarding Duffy-independent invasion and parasite adaptation. Positioning Sudan within a broader African and global context further enhances the relevance of the findings beyond a single country.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be considered. Only a subset of included studies reported host Duffy antigen status, restricting the meta-analysis to a small number of datasets and contributing to moderate between-study heterogeneity. The use of stringent eligibility criteria may have excluded relevant studies lacking Duffy genotype stratification or genetic data, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the evidence base. Inconsistencies in diagnostic procedures, study designs, and genotyping techniques also hindered direct comparability. Coverage was uneven, with the western and southern regions of Sudan not fully represented, which could affect estimates of prevalence and parasite diversity. Database-specific retrieval counts were not recorded, which may limit reproducibility at the level of individual database comparisons. Furthermore, the limited number of studies available for quantitative synthesis reduced the statistical power to identify heterogeneity and formally evaluate study bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis show that Plasmodium vivax infection occurs in Duffy-negative individuals in Sudan, challenging the long-standing assumption that Duffy negativity provides complete protection against infection. The substantial genetic diversity and recurrent duplication of the P. vivax Duffy Binding Protein (PvDBP) observed among Sudanese isolates suggest adaptive parasite mechanisms that may facilitate alternative erythrocyte invasion pathways. These findings highlight Sudan as an important setting for understanding P. vivax emergence in sub-Saharan Africa and emphasize the need for expanded molecular surveillance and integrated host–parasite genomic research to inform malaria control and vaccine development.
