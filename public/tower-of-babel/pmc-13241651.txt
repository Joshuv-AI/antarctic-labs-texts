Reply to: “Node-level inference and score construction may overstate the diagnostic utility of cervical lymph node ultrasound in Alzheimer’s disease”
Authors: Tae Hyung Kim, Jin Geun Kwon, Jimmy Sung-Chuan Chao, Jae Woo Kim, Changsik John Pak, Hyunsuk Peter Suh, Jae-Sung Lim, Joon Pio Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•GEE sensitivity analyses retained the principal categorical findings.•Continuous nodal measurements remained non-significant after GEE adjustment.•Nested score structure reflects a phenotypic gradient, not independent signals.•AUC of single-variable model (0.770) approached that of the composite (0.808).•Composite score remains exploratory and requires external validation.

To the Editor,

We thank Liu for the careful reading of our recent study on structural and vascular alterations of deep cervical lymph nodes (dCLNs) in amyloid PET-positive Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients.1,2 Two concerns were raised: clustering of nodal observations within individuals, and the nested construction of the Zone 5 composite score. We respond to each below.

We first clarify the units of analysis used in the original report. As specified in the footnote to Table 2 of the original report (Kim et al.1), lymph node counts per participant were analyzed at the participant level (n = 25 vs 25), whereas individual nodal measurements (long axis, short axis, Solbiati index, and SMI intensity) and the corresponding categorical comparisons of node shape, SMI positivity, and morphologic clarity were analyzed at the node level (93 vs 60 Zone 5 nodes). The composite Zone 5 score is, by construction, a single summary value per participant, obtained by combining counts of nodes that meet these node-level criteria within each individual; its ROC analysis (AUC 0.808) therefore compared 25 patients with AD against 25 healthy controls.

Liu correctly identifies, however, that the node-level categorical comparisons of shape, SMI positivity, and morphologic clarity were presented as unadjusted contingency analyses without formal adjustment for within-participant correlation.3 We therefore performed sensitivity analyses using generalized estimating equations (GEE), with participant identifier as the clustering variable and an exchangeable working correlation structure, as recommended for clustered observations in clinical neurology research.4 Shape was analyzed as a binary contrast of oval versus non-oval nodes to simplify clustering-adjusted categorical modeling. After clustering adjustment, the principal categorical findings were retained in direction and statistical significance: morphology visibility (Wald χ² = 5.153, p = 0.023), oval versus non-oval shape (Wald χ² = 14.017, p < 0.001), and SMI positivity (Wald χ² = 4.768, p = 0.029). The continuous nodal measurements (long axis, short axis, Solbiati index, and SMI intensity) remained not significant (Table 1). The direction of these associations was consistent with the descriptive contrasts in the Table 1 of the original report (Kim et al.¹), with fewer oval nodes and lower SMI positivity in patients with AD. We agree with Liu’s second observation regarding the nested construction of the score. The three positively weighted components of the Zone 5 score, namely the count of oval nodes, the count of oval nodes positive for SMI, and the count of oval nodes positive for SMI with intensity ≤ 7, are nested by construction. The stepwise refinement was intended to identify the most discriminative phenotypic gradient, not to attribute independent biological information to each successively narrower subset. In a derivation cohort of 50 participants, the apparent performance of any such composite is vulnerable to optimism.5 The marginal gain of the composite score over the single most discriminative variable, the count of oval Zone 5 nodes per participant alone (AUC 0.770), is modest, indicating that the discriminative signal is concentrated in a single phenotype rather than in the composite structure. Although the goodness of fit test of Hosmer and Lemeshow (χ² = 4.863, df = 7, p = 0.677) did not indicate gross miscalibration in the derivation sample, we recognize that this does not quantify optimism in the apparent AUC. The composite score is therefore not proposed as an externally validated diagnostic instrument.

Our original Discussion anticipated these limitations. We noted that a sensitivity of 64% “suggests that a portion of high-risk cases may be missed” and that the scoring system “may be more suitable for risk stratification or as a supplementary diagnostic tool rather than primary screening,” and that “external validation in independent cohorts will be essential to improve the generalizability and clinical reliability of this model.” We agree with Liu that validation in independent and ideally multicentre cohorts is required before any cervical lymph node ultrasound metric can be regarded as a generalizable biomarker of impaired lymphatic clearance from the brain in AD.6,7 Such validation, together with longitudinal observation and standardized acquisition protocols, will be required to establish the clinical utility of this approach, and future studies incorporating sleep and perfusion correlates may further clarify the underlying biology. The methodological considerations raised here will be valuable in guiding such future work.
