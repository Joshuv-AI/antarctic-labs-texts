Targeting VSMCs to suppress macrophage-like phenotype switching that restrains atherogenesis by immunosuppressive oligodeoxynucleotide (A151) functionalized selenium nanoparticles
Authors: Jingru Wang, Bo Yao, Yutian Zhang, Shuya Liu, Weiming Liu, Yutong Han, Kaiyuan Shi, Wenyi Han, Ruonan Li, Zhihui Cai, Hui Yang, Qinjie Ling, Jingjun He, Fei Liu, Shan Gou, Zhi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMacrophage-like phenotype switching of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) is a crucial mechanism driving atherogenesis. Inhibition of a phenotype switch to macrophage-like cells is a promising strategy to prevent atherosclerosis (AS), and targeted nanotherapeutics represent one approach for implementing this strategy. To this end, we designed immunosuppressive oligodeoxynucleotide A151 functionalized selenium nanoparticles with a spearhead LacNAc (LN-A151-SeNPs) that target macrophage-like VSMCs. Nano characterization showed that the uniformity and stability of nanoparticles were optimized by modification with LacNAc and A151, resulting in an average diameter of 88.90 ± 1.45 nm, Zeta potentials of -21.1 ± 1.5 mV, a A151:Se molar ratio of 1:60 and mass ratio of 1.68:1. The effects of LN-A151-SeNPs on inhibiting VSMCs phenotype switching and attenuation of AS were investigated using ApoE−/− mice fed a high-fat/high-cholesterol diet, combined with a cellular model in which VSMCs treated with cholesterol. Results showed that LN-A151-SeNPs accumulated in aorta and targeted macrophage-like VSMCs in atherosclerotic plaques as assessed by fluorescence tracing and detection of co-localization with Galectin-3. LN-A151-SeNPs inhibited the phenotypic changes of VSMCs into macrophage-like cells by upregulating α-SMA and downregulating CD68 expression, leading to the reduction of plaque formation. Mechanistically, LN-A151-SeNPs inhibited activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, with decreased differentiation of VSMCs into a pro-AS phenotype. Moreover, LN-A151-SeNPs mitigated oxidative stress in VSMCs by triggering selenoprotein synthesis and bioactivity, particularly selenium-dependent enzymes. Thus, these results suggest that LN-A151-SeNPs restrain atherogenesis by targeting VSMCs to suppress phenotype switching and oxidative stress, indicating the translational potential of A151 functionalized selenium nanoparticles for AS therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03925-7.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Selenium nanoparticles, Oligodeoxynucleotide, Vascular smooth muscle cells, Phenotype switching

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a major cardiovascular disease driven by a chronic inflammatory process involving lipid accumulation in the arteries [1–3]. It serves as the predominant underlying cause of myocardial infarction, stroke, and disabling peripheral artery disease [4]. Conventional therapeutic strategies, including statins, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing cholesterol levels and slowing progression in AS. However, a significant number of patients exhibit an inadequate response to these existing treatments or experience considerable side effects [5]. Consequently, developing new pharmacological agents remains a critical step for improving the prevention and management of AS. Central to AS pathogenesis are vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), which constitute the major cellular component of blood vessels and are responsible for regulating vascular tone, structure, and extracellular matrix composition. VSMCs display remarkable phenotypic plasticity and can transition from a contractile state to proliferative and macrophage-like phenotypes during vascular remodeling in AS [6]. The NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, a cytosolic multiprotein complex, represents a compelling therapeutic target [7]. It can be activated by diverse pathogen-associated and damage-associated molecular patterns. Upon activation, the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers caspase-1 activation, which leads to the maturation and secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18. This process amplifies inflammatory responses and promotes the development of AS, a mechanism that is closely associated with phenotypic switching in VSMCs [8]. Given the significant need for new AS therapies and the promise of targeting the critical phenotype switching in VSMCs, novel nanotherapeutics have emerged as a breakthrough approach to mitigate vascular remodeling by directly targeting VSMCs [9, 10].

Selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) have emerged as an attractive nanotherapeutic platform due to their efficient delivery, strong biocompatibility, and relatively low toxicity [11–13]. Their large surface area further enables the functionalization of diverse bioactive ligands, enhancing their applicability in biomedicine [14–16]. The chemical versatility of selenium, which is reflected in its range of bond energies, broad valence states, and distinct redox potentials, underpins the multifunctionality of SeNPs [17]. With a large atomic radius (1.15 Å) and high polarizability, selenium exhibits pronounced redox reactivity. This allows SeNPs in the zero-valence state (Se⁰) to participate in redox cycling across multiple valence states (Se⁻², Se⁺⁴, Se⁺⁶) and to serve as both electron donors and acceptors [18]. Such fundamental properties facilitate a variety of chemical transformations that are essential to the biological functions of selenium. As an essential micronutrient, selenium contributes significantly to numerous physiological processes, largely owing to its anti-inflammatory and potent antioxidant properties [19, 20]. Notably, SeNPs have been shown to mitigate oxidative stress through mechanisms such as upregulating selenium-dependent antioxidant enzymes, scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals to protect against oxidative DNA damage [21, 22]. Studies from our group have further demonstrated the versatile applications of carbohydrate-functionalized SeNPs across multiple inflammatory disease models, underscoring their potential for advancing nanomedicine-based therapies [23–25].

In recent years, several nucleic acid-based drugs have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration for treating a variety of disorders [26–29]. Oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs) are small nucleic acid strands, typically 15–30 base pairs in length, that contain a variety of chemical modifications to improve stability and function [30]. However, establishing nucleic acids as therapies has proven challenging due to nuclease degradation, inherent immune-stimulating properties, and poor cell uptake [31, 32]. This has led to challenges for the development of delivery platform technologies essential for generating safe and efficient nucleic acid therapies [33–35]. Several immunosuppressive ODNs have been developed to alleviate excessive inflammation and immune activation by targeting different factors involved in immune responses [36–38]. The immunosuppressive oligodeoxynucleotide A151, which contains 4 repetitive TTAGGG sequences of the mammalian telomeric, is known to function as a molecular antagonist of specific innate immune sensors [39]. It has recently been demonstrated that A151 can competitively bind to cytosolic nucleic acid-sensing receptors, including Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), cyclic guanosine monophosphate-adenosine monophosphate synthase (cGAS), and the absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome. Through this mechanism of molecular competition with immunostimulatory DNA, A151 effectively inhibits the activation of these critical pathways [40–42]. However, whether this inflammation antagonism by A151 can directly regulate VSMCs phenotypic switching and thereby target the underlying pathological mechanisms of AS still remains poorly defined.

We report herein a nanoplatform, referred to as LN-A151-SeNPs, that has been developed for ODN delivery and exhibits the capacity to regulate VSMCs phenotypic switching and oxidative stress. Specifically, the nanoparticles were synthesized by conjugating SeNPs with A151 and LacNAc (LN), a specific ligand for Galectin-3 (Gal-3), which serves as a targeting moiety. Our results demonstrate that Gal-3, upregulated on the surface of macrophage-like VSMCs in response to cholesterol, facilitates recognition of LN and mediates the docking of LN-A151-SeNPs. Subsequent endocytosis of LN-A151-SeNPs significantly suppressed lipid deposition in the aorta by inhibiting VSMCs differentiation through downregulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activity. In addition, LN-A151-SeNPs reduced ROS production through increased bioactivity of selenium-dependent antioxidant enzymes. Thus, this platform provides a promising strategy for targeted drug delivery to atherosclerotic lesions and modulating the local inflammatory microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of LN-A151-SeNPs

LN-A151-SeNPs were prepared using a method similar to previously described methods. Briefly, 150 µL Na2SeO3 (10 mM) was added to distilled water, and then ascorbic acid solution (40 mM) was added dropwise to the reaction solution. LacNAc (250 µM) and A151 with a phosphorothioate (sequence is 5′-TTAGGGTTAGGGTTAGGGTTAGGG‐3′, 100 µM) were added at room temperature for 3 h. After dialysis, LN-A151-SeNPs were collected and stored at 4 ℃. C151 with a phosphorothioate (sequence is 5′‐TTCAAATTCAAATTCAAATTCAAA-3′, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was used as control. For LN-A151-SeNPs&C6, SeNPs&C6, FAM-LN-A151-SeNPs, C6 or FAM-A151 instead of A151 were added into the reaction system, respectively.

The LN-A151-SeNPs were characterized using a Field Emission Electron Microscope (TEM; JEM 2100 F; JEOL, Showima City, Tokyo, Japan), energy dispersive X-ray (EDS) coupled with TEM, dynamic light scattering (Nano ZS; Malvern Instruments, Great Malvern. UK), UV-visible (UV-Vis) spectrophotometer (Cary 4000; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA), Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FT-IR; Nicolet™ iS™ 10; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Detection of LN-A151-SeNPs targeted to aortic VSMCs

Accumulation of NPs in VSMCs, endothelial cells and macrophages was assessed after 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6 h of treatment with LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 (2 µM) by trypsinizing cells and analyzing with flow cytometry (NovoCyte®; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Additionally, major organs were harvested 6 h after the i.v. administration of LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 to AS mice. Fluorescence imaging was then conducted using a multifunctional biomolecule imaging device (Typhoon FLA9500, General Electric Company, USA).

After activation with H2O2 (100 µM) or LPS (100 ng/mL), cholesterol (50 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added for 24 h to cultured VSMCs that were then incubated with LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 or -LN-FAMA151-SeNPs (2 µM) for 4 h. For distribution of LN-A151-SeNPs in aortic plaque cells, cryosections of aorta roots of AS model mice were blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1.5 h, then incubated with primary antibodies including anti-α-SMA and anti-Gal-3 (1:200; Affinity Biosciences, Changzhou, China) at 4 °C overnight. Cryosections were incubated with secondary antibodies including Alexa-fluor-555 anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Abcam Inc, Toronto, ON, Canada) and Alexa Fluor 647 conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., China) for 1 h at room temperature. After staining with DAPI (1 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 15 min, images were captured by confocal microscopy.

AS model in ApoE−/− mice

For this study, mice included 6-week-old male ApoE−/− mice on a C57BL/6J genetic background (Charles River Labs, Beijing, China) that were randomly divided into 6 groups (n = 6/group) used for AS model induction. Mice in the control group were fed a standard chow while the other groups were fed high-fat and high-cholesterol diets (HFCD) for 12 weeks. The mice in the SeNPs-treated group, the LN-A151-SeNPs-treated group, and the LC151-SeNPs-treated group were i.v. administered with selenium at a dose of 0.10 mg/kg twice/week starting from the 5th and continuing for 8 weeks. The A151-treated group was given the same modified concentration of A151 through the i.v.injection. The mice in the control group and the model group continued to be injected with an equal amount of saline. During this period, the mice were weighed, recorded and analyzed. Selenium concentrations in heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, small intestine and aorta were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; iCAP RQ; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cell treatment

Rat aortic VSMCs (ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ExCell, Suzhou, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (GBCBIO, Guangzhou, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were pretreated with 2 µM SeNPs, LN-A151-SeNPs, LC151-SeNPs or with A151 for 2 h, and then co-treated with cholesterol for 48 h.

Staining of mouse aortas plaques and VSMCs with oil red O (ORO)

Aortas were dissected from the heart to the inferior iliac artery branch and were rapidly preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin. To analyze AS lipid lesions, aortas were completely cleaned of excess lipids and then opened longitudinally to expose the inner surface. The aortas were positioned with dissecting needles and stained with ORO (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). To quantify AS lipid lesions in the aorta, the percentage of ORO-positive areas was analyzed using Image J.

For the detection of foam cell formation in VSMCs, cultured VSMCs were treated with 2 µM SeNPs, LN-A151-SeNPs, LC151-SeNPs or the corresponding concentration of free A151 for 2 h. This was followed by activation with 50 µg/mL cholesterol for 48 h. After three washes with PBS, cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with freshly prepared ORO solution for 30 min and then imaged using optical microscopy. After the images were acquired, the ORO in the cells was extracted with isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 492 nm using a multi-mode microplate reader (CLARIOstar, BMG LABTECH, Germany) to determine the relative ORO concentration.

Detection of blood lipids and inflammatory cytokines

According to the manufacturer’s instructions, serum samples were analyzed for levels of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (T-CHO), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) using commercially available assay kits (NanJing JianCheng Bioengineering Institute, NanJing, China). Levels of IL-1β in the aorta were detected by a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems Inc, USA).

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from VSMCs or aorta tissue using MolPure® Cell RNA Kit and reverse transcribed to cDNA by Hifair® III 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Yisheng, Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Analysis by qPCR was performed using 2×RealStar Green Fast Mixture (Beijing Jianxing Technology Co., Ltd., China) and a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The mRNA levels were calculated by the 2 − ΔΔCT method and normalized to β-actin levels. The target gene primers of VSMCs and aorta were synthesized (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) as listed in Tables S1 and S2.

Western blot

Protein was extracted from cells or aortas using RIPA lysis buffer and applied to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After three washes with TBST, the PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibody (1:1000 dilution) in TBST with 5% BSA overnight at 4 °C on a shaker. The membrane was then incubated with secondary antibody (1:5000 dilution) in TBST with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature after washing with TBST. Finally, the target protein was detected using the Tanon 5200 system (Shanghai, China). The band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to the expression of β-actin. Primary antibodies and secondary antibodies included anti-β-actin (Affinity Biosciences, China), anti-α-SMA (Affinity biotechnology, China), anti-CD68 (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., China), anti-NLRP3 (Proteintech, China), anti-ASC (Proteintech, China), anti-Caspase-1 (Affinity Biosciences, China), anti-IL-1β (Affinity Biosciences, China), anti-GPX1 (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., China), anti-GPX4 (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., China), anti-TXNRD1 (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd., China), anti-TXNRD2 (Affinity Biosciences, China), anti-SPS2 (Proteintech, China), and HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG (Affinity Biosciences, China) antibody.

Immunofluorescence staining

Treated cells or sections were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h. These cells or sections were then incubated with anti-α-SMA, anti-CD68, anti-NLRP3, anti-Caspase-1, and anti-TLR9 (Affinity Biosciences, China) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Abcam Inc, Toronto, ON, Canada) and Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam Inc, Toronto, ON, Canada) at room temperature for 1 h. DAPI (1 µg/mL) was added for 15 min, then anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium was used to seal the sections, and imaging was performed by confocal laser scanning microscope.

Antioxidant capacity test

VSMCs were incubated with cholesterol (50 µg/mL) and nanoparticles (2 µM) for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; 5 µM; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to assess intracellular ROS levels. Representative fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope and quantitatively analyzed with Image J software. In parallel, VSMCs were collected and subjected to flow cytometric analysis for further ROS quantification. Additionally, the supernatants of cell lysates were collected to measure glutathione (GSH) content, along with the enzymatic activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPX) and thioredoxin reductase (TXNRD), using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the mean ± SD by three independent experiments or more. Data were analyzed by Student’s t-test for pairwise comparison and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Preparation and characterization of LN-A151-SeNPs

To synthesize LN-A151-SeNPs, we first optimized the concentration of A151. As shown in Fig. S1A, the smallest nanoparticle size was achieved using 2.75 µM A151. Characterization of the resulting nanoparticles showed a selenium conversion efficiency of 62.64% ± 14.28% and a high A151 loading efficiency of 94.29% ± 3.64% (Fig. S1B), corresponding to an approximate A151 to selenium molar ratio of 1:60 and mass ratio of 1.68:1. As seen by the field emission TEM in Fig. 1A, SeNPs exhibited an aggregated, precipitated form, while LN-A151-SeNPs were monodispersed with homogeneous spherical structures. For SeNPs, the particle size distribution was larger than 200 nm, whereas LN-A151-SeNPs were more transparent and the average particle size was 88.90 ± 1.45 nm (Fig. 1B). Zeta potentials of SeNPs and LN-A151-SeNPs were determined at −10.6 ± 0.3 and − 21.1 ± 1.5 mV, respectively (Fig. 1C). LN-A151-SeNPs exhibited better stability due to the higher absolute value of their zeta potential compared with SeNPs. LN-A151-SeNPs exhibited a blue shift from 268 nm to 260 nm in the UV-Vis spectrum (Fig. 1D). The FT-IR spectrum showed the characteristic C = O peak of A151 at 1686 cm⁻¹ and the C-O/C-C skeletal vibration peaks of LN at 1065 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, EDS analysis revealved the characteristic elements (C, O, N, P) attributable to both LN and A151 (Fig. 1F). These results confirm the successful construction of LN-A151-SeNPs. Signals of a selenium atom (9.26%) from SeNPs were strong, and signals of C (23.74%), O (66.71%), N (0.23%) and P (0.05%) were detected that likely originated from A151 and LN (Fig. 1G). The stability of LN-A151-SeNPs was determined in different media including H2O and 0.9% NaCl as shown in Fig. 1H, with LN-A151-SeNPs maintaining high stability under different storage conditions. LN-A151-SeNPs were found to slightly reduce cell viability at high concentrations in VSMCs (Fig. 1I). The release profile of A151 from LN-A151-SeNPs was evaluated under different pH conditions, and a more efficient release efficiency was observed at pH 5.5 than at pH 7.4 (Fig. S1C-D). For comparative purposes, selenium nanoparticles functionalized with C151, a control sequence for A151, were synthesized in parallel (denoted as LN-C151-SeNPs). Subsequent characterization revealed that the structural properties of LN-C151-SeNPs closely resembled those of the LN-A151-SeNPs (Fig. S1E-H). Taken together, these results indicated successful synthesis of LN-A151-SeNPs with low cytotoxicity that were of appropriate size (< 100 nm) and were highly stable under different physiological conditions.

Accumulation of LN-A151-SeNPs in mouse aorta VSMCs

In order to visualize the accumulation of LN-A151-SeNPs in VSMCs, LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 and SeNPs&C6 were prepared following the same protocol. Both coumarin-6 (C6) labeling on SeNPs and LN-A151-SeNPs were confirmed by fluorimetry (Fig. S2A). The uptake effect of LN-A151-SeNPs by different cell types was assessed to verify the selective uptake by VSMCs. First, uptake of LN-A151-SeNPs by VSMCs was compared to endothelial cells (MAECs) and macrophages (BMDMs) using flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 2A, VSMCs exhibited higher uptake of LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 compared to other cell types, implying targeting binding of LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 skewed toward VSMCs. Moreover, the flow cytometry results suggested effective internalization in a time-dependent manner, with uptake peaking at 4 h with a plateau at 6 h. In addition, the results of flow cytometry and confocal imaging showed that LN-A151-SeNPs were internalized in VSMCs to a greater extent compared to SeNPs among 4 h (Fig. 2B-E). The targeting capability of LN-A151-SeNPs toward atherosclerotic plaques was evaluated using ex vivo fluorescence imaging. After C6-labeled SeNPs and LN-A151-SeNPs were systemically administered via i.v. injection to AS model mice for 6 h, the aortas and major organs were harvested for subsequent fluorescence analysis. In AS mice, aortas from the LN-A151-SeNPs-treated group exhibited markedly stronger fluorescence intensities compared to the other groups (Fig. 2F). Quantitative assessment further confirmed that LN-A151-SeNPs were accumulated at significant levels within aortic tissues (Fig. 2G). Meanwhile, ex vivo imaging and quantitative evaluation of major organs indicated that both free C6 and types of nanoparticles were primarily localized in the liver and kidneys, with no difference in fluorescence signals (Fig. 2H-I). Moreover, selenium levels in different organs of mice were measured by ICP-MS (Fig. S3), and results indicated increased selenium in aortas after LN-A151-SeNPs treatment. These results indicated that functionalizing SeNPs with LN and A151 effectively increased their uptake by aorta VSMCs.

LN-A151-SeNPs targeted to macrophage-like VSMCs

The fluorescet dye FAM was conjugated to A151 to detect the accumulation of LN-A151-SeNPs in cells. Results confirmed that SeNPs were successfully modified with FAM-A151 (Fig. S2B) and FAM modification at the 5’ end of the A151 sequence increased uptake by VSMCs (Fig. S2C). VSMCs alter their phenotype and differentiate into pro-inflammatory macrophage-like cells. This transition is characterized by increased expression of the macrophage marker Gal-3 in vivo [43]. We explored the effect of targeting LN-A151-SeNPs to macrophage-like VSMCs using flow cytometry and confocal imaging. VSMCs were induced to differentiate into a macrophage-like phenotype upon stimulation with H2O2, LPS, and cholesterol, followed by addition of 2 µM of LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 or LN- FAMA151-SeNPs for 4 h. Results showed that the uptake of LN-A151-SeNPs by these macrophage-like VSMCs was increased compared to controls (Fig. 3A-C). In addition, immunofluorescence and co-localization analysis results in macrophage-like VSMCs suggested that LN-A151-SeNPs interacted with Gal-3 (Fig. 3D). Immunofluorescence in the aorta sections also showed that, compared with SeNPs&C6, LN-A151-SeNPs&C6 treatment led to higher fluorescence intensity and increased co-localization with α-SMA+ and Gal-3+ cells (Fig. 3E). These findings collectively show that LN-A151-SeNPs were effectively targeted to macrophage-like VSMCs in atherosclerotic plaques through interactions with Gal-3.

LN-A151-SeNPs delay the progression of AS

We next explored the therapeutic potential of LN-A151-SeNPs in mice using the AS model combined with nanoparticle treatment at a dosage of 0.1 mg/kg by i.v. injection (Fig. 4A). During 8 weeks of treatment, no body weight differences were found between the LN-A151-SeNP group and control group, but increased body weights were observed in mice from the model group (Fig. 4B-C and Fig. S4A). Analysis of sera indicated that LN-A151-SeNPs decreased levels of TG, T-CHO, and LDL-C while increasing levels of HDL-C (Fig. 4D-G). Staining of aorta and aortic roots showed that atherosclerotic lesionsexhibited reduced ORO levels with attenuated AS after treatment with LN-A151-SeNPs compared to the SeNPs, A151, or LN-C151-SeNPs groups (Fig. 4H-K). Overall, LN-A151-SeNPs were effective in preventing aortic plaque formation and reducing atherosclerotic disease. Subsequently, the fitted blood concentration curve of LN-A151-SeNPs obtained through quantitative fluorescence analysis showed a half-life (t1/2) of 16.65 h (Fig. S4B), indicating a favorable circulation time of the LN-A151-SeNPs in vivo. Furthermore, no significant changes were observed in the aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels or in the H&E staining of the liver and kidney following treatment with LN-A151-SeNPs (Fig. S4C-D). Interestingly, LN-A151-SeNPs alleviated splenomegaly caused by HFCD diet, which suggests an additional effect on alleviating immune responses resulting from HFCD (Fig. S4E-F). These results demonstrate that LN-A151-SeNPs possess favorable biocompatibility and biosafety in vivo.

LN-A151-SeNPs inhibit the phenotype switching of VSMCs

We investigated whether LN-A151-SeNPs could inhibit VSMCs differentiation into macrophage-like cells in atherosclerotic mice. RT-qPCR results suggested LN-A151-SeNPs increased levels of mRNAs that promote differentiation of VSMCs phenotype including Acta2, Tagln, and Cnn1 (Fig. 5A). In contrast, LN-A151-SeNPs reduced transcript levels for macrophage-like cells markers such as Cd68, Lgals3, and Ccl2 (Fig. 5B). Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence experiments revealed that LN-A151-SeNPs significantly upregulated α-SMA expression and inhibited inflammatory CD68 expression in the AS mice (Fig. 5C-F). Immunofluorescence experiments of the aortic arch showed similar results (Fig. S5).

VSMCs markers were evaluated through RT-qPCR with results showing that LN-A151-SeNPs, and SeNPs to a lesser extent, upregulated VSMCs phenotypic gene expression while downregulating macrophage-like cells gene expression (Fig. 6A-B). Analyses of CD68 and α-SMA levels by western blot (Fig. 6C-D) and immunofluorescence (Fig. 6E-F) showed that LN-A151-SeNPs reversed the cholesterol induced decrease in α-SMA but increased CD68. We also investigated the effect of LN-A151-SeNPs on phagocytic capacity, with ORO staining showing that treatment of VSMCs with LN-A151-SeNPs reduced foam cell formation induced by cholesterol compared with SeNPs, A151 and LN-C151-SeNPs (Fig. 6G-H). These results were consistent with ORO quantification in VSMCs-derived foam cells assayed via absorbance at 492 nm (Fig. 6I). Phagocytic capacity was also measured using fluorescent beads and results indicated that LN-A151-SeNPs could effectively reverse the increased phagocytosis caused by incubation with cholesterol (Fig. 6J-K). In addition, LN-A151-SeNPs led to a ~ 2-fold increase in HMG-CoA reductase mRNA level (Fig. 6L). These results suggest that LN-A151-SeNPs prevented cholesterol induced phenotypic switching of VSMCs (Fig. 6M).

LN-A151-SeNPs inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation

TLRs are critical for recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns and initiating immune responses. Among them, TLR9, which resides in the endoplasmic reticulum under basal conditions, translocates to endosomes upon stimulation by unmethylated DNA to trigger proinflammatory cytokine production. To evaluate the effects of LN-A151-SeNPs on TLR9-mediated DNA recognition in cholesterol-treated VSMCs, we examined TLR9 colocalization with CpG DNA, a well-defined TLR9 ligand. Following pretreatment with Cy5.5-labeled CpG-DNA, a substantial proportion of the CpG-DNA displayed positive colocalization with TLR9 (Fig. 7A). This interaction was notably suppressed by LN-A151-SeNPs in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 7A-B), indicating a selective inhibitory effect on TLR9 signaling activation.

LN-A151-SeNPs inhibit cholesterol induced NLRP3 inflammasome activation. (A) The inhibition of TLR9 binding to CpG-DNA by LN-A151-SeNPs and (B) its time-dependent effect were detected via immunofluorescence assay. (C) RT-qPCR measurement of mRNA levels for Nlrp3, Asc, Caspase-1, IL-1β. Protein levels of inflammasome factors including NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1 and IL-1β were analyzed in VSMCs by (D-E) western blot, (F-G) immunofluorescence, while (H) IL-1β was measured by ELISA. Data are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

VSMCs phenotype switching upon addition of cholesterol appears to be dependent on the TLR9 downstream NLRP3 inflammasome pathway that promotes IL-1β secretion. To investigate this process in VSMCs, cholesterol induced NLRP3 activation and IL-1β mRNA production were measured and results showed that LN-A151-SeNPs significantly decreased mRNA levels for Nlrp3, Asc, Caspase-1, and IL-1β (Fig. 7C). Western blot analysis showed a decrease in the proteolytic maturation of Caspase-1 and IL-1β after LN-A151-SeNPs treatment (Fig. 7D-E). Immunofluorescence and ELISA results also indicated that LN-A151-SeNPs significantly inhibited cholesterol induced levels of NLRP3 and IL-1β in VSMCs compared to controls (Fig. 7F-H). In addition, isolated aortic tissue showed that the expression levels and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome were significantly inhibited by LN-A151-SeNPs (Fig. S6A-B). Immunofluorescence and ELISA showed similar results (Fig. S6C-G). Collectively, these results suggest that LN-A151-SeNP treatment inhibited the phenotype switching of VSMCs into macrophage-like cells by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

LN-A151-SeNPs reverse oxidative damage by triggering selenoprotein-activation

Oxidative stress represents one of the key pathological mechanisms underlying AS. To mimic the oxidative damage occurring during AS progression, an in vitro oxidative stress model was established utilizing VSMCs, allowing further investigation into the antioxidant properties of LN-A151-SeNPs. The ROS scavenging capacity of LN-A151-SeNPs was initially assessed using the ROS sensitive fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Results showed that treatment with LN-A151-SeNPs markedly suppressed excessive ROS generation in VSMCs (Fig. 8A-B). Exposure to cholesterol led to reduced GSH levels as well as decreased activities of GPX and TXNRD. In contrast, co-administration of LN-A151-SeNPs effectively attenuated these inhibitory effects (Fig. 8C-E).

LN-A151-SeNPs trigger exogenous antioxidant activity and endogenous selenoprotein-activation. (A) The images and fluorescence of intercellular ROS level detected by DCFH-DA. (B) ROS generation was detected by using flow cytometry. (C) GPX activity, (D) TXNRD activity and (E) GSH levels in VSMCs were measured. (F) mRNA level and (G, H) expression level of selenoproteins in VSMCs were measured. Data are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

As an essential trace element, selenium is incorporated into selenoproteins in the form of selenocysteine thereby playing a critical role in maintaining vascular homeostasis. RT-qPCR was performed to determine the mRNA expression of various selenoproteins in VSMCs. The results indicated that LN-A151-SeNPs notably upregulated the expression of multiple selenoproteins, particularly the antioxidant GPX and TXNRD enzymes (Fig. 8F and Fig. S7). In accordance with these findings, western blot analysis was performed to determine protein expression levels in VSMCs following LN-A151-SeNP treatment. As shown in Fig. 8G-H, the expression of GPX1, GPX4, TXNRD1, and TXNRD2 were substantially enhanced. Additionally, selenophosphate synthetase 2 (SPS2), which is involved in selenoprotein biosynthesis, also exhibited a modest increase. Collectively, LN-A151-SeNPs mitigated oxidative damage in VSMCs through direct ROS scavenging concurrent with increased expression and function of key selenoprotein-dependent antioxidant systems.

Discussion

AS is recognized as a chronic inflammatory disease of the vascular system driven by a complex series of cellular events and molecular factors. The differentiation of VSMCs into macrophage-like phenotype through their exposure to atherosclerotic conditions is essential to AS pathology, during which macrophage-like VSMCs and oxidative stress contribute to the development of lesions in artery walls. In this study, we set out to develop LN and A151 coated selenium nanoparticles (LN-A151-SeNPs) to investigate the synergistic effects of SeNPs and A151 in protecting against AS. As expected, LN-A151-SeNPs exhibited an ameliorative effect on the differentiation of VSMCs in vivo and in vitro, by mitigating inflammation and oxidative stress.

Based on the potential of selenium supplements in preventing or treating cardiovascular diseases and the anti-inflammatory effects of A151, we investigated the potential of combining SeNPs with A151, using LN as the targeting ligand. LN-A151-SeNPs were characterized as having a spherical morphology, with an average diameter of 88.90 ± 1.45 nm and a zeta potential of − 21.1 ± 1.5 mV. The molar ratio of A151 to selenium was determined to be 1:60, corresponding to a mass ratio of 1.68:1. Spectroscopic analyses confirmed the successful conjugation of both LN and A151 onto the SeNPs. This surface modification contributed to a smaller particle size and better stability of the LN-A151-SeNPs. Based on these findings, we speculate on the possible structure of LN-A151-SeNPs. Firstly, the spherical SeNPs core provides a high specific surface area, enabling the attachment of LN and A151 via electrostatic adsorption and hydrogen bonding. This may result in a spiky, sea urchin-like structure or a layered core-shell configuration [44, 45]. Owing to the high mass ratio of A151 to selenium, the LN-A151-SeNPs may further self-assemble into more complex biomimetic architectures. LN and A151 are likely arranged in multiple layers enveloping the selenium nano core, forming an onion-like configuration [46, 47]. This unique architecture potentially offers superior drug loading capacity and delivery efficiency, thereby enhancing its overall therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, optimizing the preparation conditions could yield nanomedicines with improved therapeutic outcomes, and further characterization is required to explore and clarify the specific structural manifestations.

VSMCs differentiated into macrophage-like cells upon cholesterol loading, which was accompanied by up-regulated Gal-3 [48]. To develop nanocarriers with the ability to target macrophage-like VSMCs, we designed a multifunctional NP platform containing the Gal-3 targeting molecule LN. Our results show that this platform exhibited smaller size, higher stability, and minimal toxicity. Moreover, the design and synthesis of this LN successfully increased the endocytosis of nanoparticles by VSMCs and transdifferentiated macrophage-like cells. In the atherosclerotic mouse model, LN-A151-SeNPs were found to target the aorta where selenium accumulated compared with other groups. Studies have shown that VSMCs-derived cells are the main component of atherosclerotic plaques [49]. Inflammatory stimuli and oxidative stress can cause phenotypic transition of VSMCs to macrophage-like cells [50]. The phenotypic modulation of VSMCs relies on the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and its regulated cytokine IL-1β [8, 51]. Activating the NLRP3 inflammasome involves two steps: a priming signal that enhances pro-IL-1β/NLRP3 expression and an activation signal that facilitates inflammasome assembly [52, 53]. These investigations have clearly demonstrated the promising therapeutic effects of inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and IL-1β production for reducing plaque formation and AS. Our results showed that not only the mRNA levels of NLRP3 inflammasome components in mice treated with LN-A151-SeNPs were significantly reduced, but the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome and the production of IL-1β were also inhibited through the synergistic effects of A151 and SeNPs. Thus, inhibiting the differentiation of VSMCs can suppress the development of AS.

Oxidative stress is a central pathological factor in AS, contributing to VSMCs' dysfunction and disease progression [54, 55]. Our study demonstrated that LN-A151-SeNPs significantly suppressed ROS accumulation and restored the compromised levels of GSH and the activities of antioxidant enzymes GPX and TXNRD. The role of selenium in cardiovascular protection is largely mediated through its incorporation into selenoproteins [56]. Our findings revealed that LN-A151-SeNPs upregulate the expression of multiple selenoproteins at both mRNA and protein levels, particularly GPX1, GPX4, TXNRD1, and TXNRD2. This enhanced expression aligns with the observed functional recovery of GPX and TXNRD activities, suggesting a mechanistic basis for the antioxidant effects of LN-A151-SeNPs. Notably, the coordinated upregulation of key antioxidant selenoproteins induced by LN-A151-SeNPs may represent a more efficient selenium delivery strategy compared to conventional forms such as selenite or selenomethionine, which are often limited by poor bioavailability or nonspecific incorporation. Nevertheless, we observed a broad upregulation of selenoprotein expression, the precise signaling pathways through which LN-A151-SeNPs modulate this process remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

In recent years, the development of synthetic immunosuppressive ODNs has aimed to mitigate excessive inflammation and immune responses in either a broad or specific manner [57–59]. A151 ODN has demonstrated the ability to metabolically reprogram immune cells and promote immunosuppression through mechanisms involving the TLR9, cGAS, and AIM2 signaling pathways [40]. However, a major challenge in its application for AS treatment lies in reducing the dosage and improving specificity without compromising efficacy. The emergence of various nanomaterials offers a promising strategy to address this issue. In this study, SeNPs were employed to stabilize A151, thereby prolonging its bioreactivity by mitigating degradation. Furthermore, A151 aggregates on the surface of the nanoparticles, which prevents premature dispersion of the drug in the bloodstream, thereby increasing its effective local concentration and allowing for a reduction in the required therapeutic dose. Additionally, the aggregation of A151 combined with the steric hindrance provided by SeNPs effectively suppresses TLR9 activation in response to cytosolic DNA, which suppress inflammation and thereby enhances the therapeutic efficacy. Taken together, this research introduces a promising idea: the LN-A151-SeNPs platform allows ODNs to be delivered to specific cell types in atherosclerotic plaques, offering significant potential for researching and validating treatments for various cardiovascular diseases.

Conclusion

In summary, we designed and synthesized a novel LN-A151-SeNPs system that targets macrophage-like VSMCs as a potential therapeutic agent for alleviating AS. Surface modification with A151 and LN not only enhanced the stability and bioactivity of the SeNPs but also promoted their cellular uptake by VSMCs. Moreover, LN-A151-SeNPs specifically bind to Gal-3 expressed on VSMCs, facilitating their efficient internalization and exerting significant therapeutic effects in vitro and in vivo. Our studies also showed that LN-A151-SeNPs modulated VSMCs phenotypic switching and reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation by suppressing the TLR9/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Furthermore, LN-A151-SeNPs alleviated oxidative damage by directly scavenging ROS and enhancing the expression and activity of selenoprotein-based antioxidant systems. Owing to their targeted delivery, significant therapeutic efficacy, and demonstrated biocompatibility in vivo, LN-A151-SeNPs emerge as a promising candidate for the treatment of AS and potentially other chronic inflammatory diseases.
