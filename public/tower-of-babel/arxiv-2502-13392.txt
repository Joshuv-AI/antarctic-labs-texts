Atomic Proximal Policy Optimization for Electric Robo-Taxi Dispatch and Charger Allocation
Jim Dai, Manxi Wu, Zhanhao Zhang

Abstract
Pioneering companies such as Waymo have deployed electric robo-taxi services in several U.S. cities. Operating such fleets requires coordinating ride matching, vehicle repositioning, and battery charging in a stochastic environment while accounting for limited charging infrastructure. Understanding both how to design efficient, scalable control algorithms for large fleets and how charger deployment shapes system performance is therefore critical for robo-taxi operators. We model the operations of an electric robo-taxi system as a discrete-time Markov decision process with a long-run average reward objective. To efficiently evaluate system performance under different configurations, we develop a scalable deep reinforcement learning algorithm, Atomic Proximal Policy Optimization (Atomic-PPO), which reduces the action space using atomic action decomposition. The resulting training procedure requires only modest computational resources, making it practical to evaluate a large number of system configurations. We calibrate a simulation environment using real-world NYC for-hire vehicle trip records and benchmark Atomic-PPO against heuristic policies and a fluid-based reward upper bound. The results show that Atomic-PPO produces near-optimal fleet dispatching policies and serves as a reliable tool for system evaluation. We conduct extensive numerical experiments to evaluate how charging infrastructure and fleet size affect system performance. Our findings highlight the importance of ridership-based charger placement, balanced investments in charger quantity and charging speed, and joint planning of charging infrastructure and fleet size.



1
Introduction

Robo-taxi services have been deployed in several U.S. cities, such as San Francisco, Phoenix, Atlanta, and Dallas
[
1
]
. Efficient operation of an electric robo-taxi fleet is challenging because trip demand is stochastic and varies across time and space, while vehicles must also be routed to charge under limited charging infrastructure.
Poor coordination between fleet operations and charging infrastructure planning can render vehicles unavailable during peak demand periods, leading to reduced service quality and revenue loss. This creates a joint planning challenge for robo-taxi operators: developing efficient fleet control algorithms for large-scale operations while planning charging infrastructure that is well aligned with fleet size and operational demand.

In this work, we develop a scalable reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm to control large-scale electric robo-taxi fleets for ride matching, vehicle repositioning, and battery charging, and use it to evaluate performance under different configurations of charger deployment and fleet size.
We formulate the fleet operations problem as a Markov decision process (MDP) with discrete state and action spaces. The system state records the spatial distribution of vehicles, their current tasks, active trip requests, and the availability of chargers. Based on the current state, the system dispatches vehicles to fulfill trips, reposition, and charge, earning trip revenues net of repositioning and charging costs. Our objective is to maximize the long run average reward over an infinite time horizon for a given configuration of charging infrastructure and fleet size, which reflects the continuous 24-hour operations of robo-taxi companies.

Computing the fleet dispatching policy is challenging because the state space represents all possible distributions of vehicle statuses and the action space includes all feasible joint vehicle assignments. We develop a scalable deep RL algorithm based on atomic proximal policy optimization (Atomic-PPO), first proposed in
[
2
]
. Atomic-PPO decomposes fleet dispatching into sequential assignments of atomic actions – trip fulfillment, reposition, charge or pass (i.e., no new task) – to individual vehicles, which we refer to as “atomic action decomposition". The size of the atomic action space is independent of fleet size. This avoids enumerating combinations of individual vehicle actions, whose number can grow exponentially with fleet size. We integrate atomic action decomposition into Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
3
]
, which has been effective in policy optimization across various applications
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
We approximate the value function and the policy over atomic actions using neural networks. We further reduce the state dimension by clustering vehicle battery levels and grouping trip requests separately by origin region and by destination region.

Furthermore, we derive an upper bound on the optimal long-run average reward using a fluid-based linear program (LP) (see Theorem
1
). The fluid limit is attained as fleet size approaches infinity, with trip demand and the number of chargers scaling proportionally. This upper bound allows us to assess the trained policy’s optimality gap in numerical experiments.

All numerical experiments are conducted in a simulation environment calibrated using the NYC For-Hire Vehicle dataset

]
for the Manhattan area

To assess the reliability of Atomic-PPO for evaluating system reward, we compare the fleet dispatching policy learned by Atomic-PPO against the fluid-based reward upper bound and two benchmark heuristics. The first heuristic uses randomized rounding to obtain feasible vehicle assignments from the fluid-based linear program, while the second is the power-of-

Atomic-PPO also achieves 89% of the fluid reward upper bound when vehicle repositioning is disabled throughout the day, indicating that its advantage does not arise simply from allowing vehicle repositioning. Rather, Atomic-PPO selectively fulfills trip requests while accounting for their impact on the future spatial distribution of the fleet and trip demand. Its strong performance, both with and without vehicle repositioning, supports using Atomic-PPO to evaluate system reward under different charging infrastructure and fleet configurations. Training a policy for 300 vehicles takes four hours on 30 CPU cores.

We further study how charger deployment and fleet size affect system reward, yielding several managerial insights. First, charger placement matters: 15 chargers (i.e. 5% of fleet size) concentrated in Midtown Manhattan achieve approximately the same average reward as 30 chargers (i.e. 10% of fleet size) uniformly allocated across all regions.
Second, both the number of chargers and the charging speed affect system reward. When either charger quantity or charging speed is low, increasing the other improves reward by allowing vehicles to return to service more quickly. As either becomes sufficiently high, further increases yield smaller improvements. These results suggest that charger quantity and charging speed can partly substitute for one another and should be considered together.
Finally, fleet size and charging infrastructure should be planned together: smaller fleets reach diminishing returns from additional charging capacity at smaller charger quantities or lower outlet rates, whereas larger fleets benefit from further increases in charger quantity or outlet rate.

Our atomic policy framework exploits the structure of fleet dispatching to improve scalability. Instead of conditioning each vehicle’s action on the entire history of preceding actions, it conditions on the updated system state. Each atomic action induces explicit changes in vehicle status, allowing this state update to capture its effects without retaining the action history. Consequently, the input dimension of the atomic policy remains constant and independent of fleet size, while the input dimension of auto-regressive policies that explicitly append preceding actions scales with the number of agents.

Finally, our Atomic-PPO algorithm builds on

Furthermore, we provide a theoretical upper bound on the optimal long-run average reward, which serves as a benchmark for evaluating fleet dispatching policies. Atomic-PPO achieves 91% of this bound in the baseline experiments, supporting its use for evaluating system reward. Its computational efficiency enables extensive experiments on charger deployment and fleet size to obtain managerial insights.

2.1
The ride-hailing system

We consider a transportation network with a set
V
of service regions. In this network, a fleet of electric vehicles with size
N
are operated by a central planner to serve passengers, who make trip requests from their origins
u\in V
to their destinations
v\in V
. For each pair of
(u,v)\in V\times V
, we assume that the battery consumption for traveling from
u
to
v
is a constant
b_{uv}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. The electric vehicles are each equipped with a battery of size
B
, i.e. a fully charged vehicle can take trips with total battery consumption up to
B
. A set of chargers with different charging rates
\delta\in\Delta
are installed in the network, where each rate
\delta
charger can replenish
\delta
amount of battery to the connected vehicle in one unit of time. We denote the number of rate
\delta\in\Delta
chargers in each region
v\in V
as
n_{v}^{\delta}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
.

We model the operations of a ride-hailing system as a discrete-time, average reward Markov Decision Process (MDP) with infinite time horizon. In particular, we model the system as an infinite repetition of a single-day operations, where each day
d=1,2,\dots
is evenly partitioned into
T
number of time steps
t=1,\dots,T
. The system makes a dispatching decision for the entire fleet at every time step of a day, which we refer to as a decision epoch. In each decision epoch
(t,d)
, the number of trip requests between each
u
-
v
pair, denoted as
X_{uv}^{t,d}
, follows a Poisson distribution with mean
\lambda_{uv}^{t}
. The duration of trips from region
u
to region
v
at time
t
is a constant
\tau_{uv}^{t}
that is a multiple of the length of a time step. Both
\lambda_{uv}^{t}
and
\tau_{uv}^{t}
can vary across time steps in a single day, but remain unchanged across days.

When the central planner receives the trip requests, they assign vehicles to serve (all or a part of) the requests. In particular, a vehicle must be assigned to the passenger within the connection patience time
L_{c}\geq 0
, and a passenger will wait at most time
L_{p}\geq 0
(defined as the pickup-patience time) for an assigned vehicle to arrive at their origin. Otherwise, the passenger will leave the system. Both
L_{p}
and
L_{c}
are multiples of the discrete time steps.

The central planner keeps track of the status of each vehicle by the region
v\in V
it is currently located or heading to, remaining time to reach
\eta=0,\dots,\hat{\tau}_{v}
, and remaining battery level
b=0,\dots,B
when reaching
v
. Here,
\hat{\tau}_{v}:=\max_{u\in V,t\in[T]}\tau_{uv}^{t}
is the maximum duration of any trip with destination
v
. We assume that the minimum time cost of all trips
\min_{u,v\in V,t\in[T]}\tau_{uv}^{t}
is larger than
L_{p}
so that no vehicle can be assigned to serve more than one trip request in a single decision epoch.

A vehicle is associated with status
c=(v,\eta,b)
if (1) it is routed to destination
v
, with remaining time
\eta
, and battery level
b
at the arrival; or (2) it is being charged at region
v
, with remaining charging time
\eta
, and battery level
b
after the charging period completes. Here, if
\eta=0
, then the vehicle is idling at region
v
. Additionally, if
\eta>0
, then the vehicle could either be taking a trip with a passenger whose destination is
v
, being repositioned to
v
, or being charged in
v
. Vehicle repositioning refers to sending an idle vehicle to another region without carrying a passenger, where it may subsequently serve trip requests, charge, or idle. We note that the maximum remaining time
\eta
for trip fulfilling vehicles cannot exceed a finite number
\hat{\tau}_{v}+L_{p}
since a vehicle is only eligible to serve a trip if the vehicle can arrive at the origin of that trip within
L_{p}
time and the maximum trip duration is
\hat{\tau}_{v}
. Additionally, a vehicle with status
(v,0,b)
can be charged at region
v
with rate
\delta

.

Markov decision process

We next describe the

State.

We denote the state space of the MDP as
\mathcal{S}
with generic element
s
. The state vector
s^{t,d}=\left(t,\left(s_{c}^{t,d}\right)_{c\in\mathcal{C}},\left(s_{o}^{t,d}\right)_{o\in\mathcal{O}},\left(s_{w}^{t,d}\right)_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\right)\in\mathcal{S}
records the fleet state
\left(s_{c}^{t,d}\right)_{c\in\mathcal{C}}
, the trip order state
\left(s_{o}^{t,d}\right)_{o\in\mathcal{O}}
, and the charger state
\left(s_{w}^{t,d}\right)_{w\in\mathcal{W}}
for each time
t

is the number of vehicles of status
c
,
s_{o}^{t,d}
is the number of trip orders of status

, and
s_{w}^{t,d}
is the number of chargers of status
w
at
(t,d)
. For all
(t,d)
, the sum of vehicles of all statuses equals to the fleet size
N
. That is,

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}s_{c}^{t,d}=N,\quad\forall t\in[T],\ d=1,2,\dots.

(1)

Additionally, the sum of chargers of all remaining charging times for each region
v
and rate
\delta
equals to the quantity of the corresponding charging facility
n_{v}^{\delta}
. That is, for all
v\in V
and all
\delta\in\Delta
,

\sum_{j=0}^{J}s_{(v,\delta,j)}^{t,d}=n_{v}^{\delta},\quad\forall t\in[T],\ d=1,2,\dots.

(2)

Moreover, we note that while the number of new trip arrivals
X_{uv}^{t,d}
can be unbounded, the maximum number of trip orders that can be served before abandonment at any time step is at most
N(L_{c}+1)
. This is because the fleet can serve up to
N

L_{c}
steps before being abandoned. As a result, without loss of generality, we can upper bound
s_{o}^{t,d}
for each status

\mathcal{S}

The policy

State Transitions.

Given the current state and action, vehicle destinations, remaining times to arrival, and battery levels are updated according to the assigned trip-fulfilling, repositioning, or charging actions. Vehicles assigned to charge first travel to the charger, consuming time and battery, and their battery gains are capped at full charge. Vehicles assigned to pass remain idle if they have no ongoing task; otherwise, their remaining task time decreases by one. Trip orders assigned to vehicles leave the queue; unassigned orders age by one time step and are abandoned once their active time exceeds
L_{c}
. New trip orders enter with active time zero, subject to the arrival cap described above. Chargers become available when their charging periods end, while newly assigned charging vehicles occupy available chargers. The complete transition equations are provided in Appendix

2.3
Model limitations

First, to ensure tractability and scalability, the service area is partitioned into regions. This spatial aggregation abstracts away fine-grained geographic details, such as traffic signals, one-way streets, and turning constraints that can be important for exact vehicle-to-request matching.
Our work focuses on higher-level, region-to-region fleet dispatching decisions, including vehicle routing to chargers under limited charging capacity and vehicle repositioning to balance spatial demand. Numerical experiments in Appendix
B
indicate that the algorithm continues to perform well with more regions and modest increases in computational resources; Section
4
discusses its computational complexity.

Second, for notational simplicity in expressing the state transition equations, we model trip durations, charging times, and customer patience times as deterministic, without explicitly modeling heterogeneous patience across customers. The framework can incorporate stochastic versions of these variables or heterogeneous patience. For example, stochastic abandonment can be modeled by allowing each waiting trip request to exit with a certain probability at each time step. Our framework for computing optimal fleet dispatching policies can still be applied in such settings. In the experiments in Appendix
B
, Atomic-PPO achieves comparable average daily rewards under deterministic and stochastic trip durations.

Third, for modeling simplicity, we assume that system parameters such as trip durations and passenger arrival rates are constant across days, omitting day of the week variation and longer term seasonal demand shifts. Day of the week effects can be incorporated by augmenting the state vector with an indicator. With respect to
longer term distribution shifts, our experiments in Appendix
B
show that the trained policy can tolerate moderate month to month changes in demand.

Finally, vehicle repositioning actions are modeled as uninterrupted until completion. In practice, vehicles may dynamically adjust their routes in response to new information or incoming requests, and allowing such interruptions could improve trip fulfillment and system performance. This extension can be incorporated by tracking intermediate vehicle locations in the state, but is left for future work to preserve notational simplicity.

3
Fleet Control Policy Reduction With Atomic Actions

One challenge of computing the optimal control policy lies in the size of the action space
|\mathcal{A}|
. The focus of this section is to address this challenge by introducing a policy reduction scheme, which decomposes fleet dispatching to sequential assignment of tasks to individual vehicles, where the task for each individual vehicle is referred as an “atomic action". We use the name “atomic action policy" because each atomic action only changes the status of a single vehicle. In particular, for any vehicle of a status
c\in\mathcal{C}
, an atomic action can be any one of the followings:

-

\hat{\mathrm{f}}_{o}
represents fulfilling a trip of status
o\in\mathcal{O}
.

-

\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{v}
represents repositioning to destination
v\in V
.

-

\hat{\mathrm{q}}_{\delta}
represents charging with rate
\delta\in\Delta
at its current region.

-

\hat{\mathrm{p}}
represents continuing with its previously assigned actions.

We use
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
to denote the atomic action space that includes all of the above atomic actions, i.e.
\hat{a}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}:=\left\{\left(\hat{\mathrm{f}}_{o}\right)_{o\in\mathcal{O}},\left(\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{v}\right)_{v\in V},\left(\hat{\mathrm{q}}_{\delta}\right)_{\delta\in\Delta},\hat{\mathrm{p}}\right\}
. The atomic action significantly reduces the policy output dimension since
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
does not scale with the fleet size.

We now present the procedure of atomic action assignment. In each decision epoch
(t,d)
, vehicles are arbitrarily indexed from
1
to
N
, and are sequentially selected. For a selected vehicle
n
, the atomic policy
\hat{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\Delta(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
maps from the tuple of system state
s^{t,d}_{n}
before the
n
-th assignment and the selected vehicle’s status
c_{n}
to a distribution of atomic actions. The system state
s^{t,d}_{n}
updates after each individual vehicle assignment, starting with
s^{t,d}_{1}=s^{t,d}
. After the final vehicle is assigned and the new trip arrivals at time
t+1
are realized, the state
s^{t,d}_{N}
transitions to
s^{t+1,d}
.

The total reward for each decision epoch
(t,d)
is the sum of all rewards generated from each atomic action assignment in
(t,d)
, where the reward generated by the atomic action
\hat{a}^{t,d}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}
on a vehicle of status
c^{t,d}:=(u,\eta,b)\in\mathcal{C}
is given by

\displaystyle r^{t}(c^{t,d},\hat{a}^{t,d})=

\displaystyle\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}r^{t}_{f,o}\mathds{1}\left\{\hat{a}^{t,d}=\hat{\mathrm{f}}_{o}\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{(u,v)\in V\times V}r^{t}_{e,uv}\mathds{1}\left\{\hat{a}^{t,d}=\hat{\mathrm{e}}_{v}\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{\delta\in\Delta}r^{t}_{q,\delta}\mathds{1}\left\{\hat{a}^{t,d}=\hat{\mathrm{q}}_{\delta}\right\}.

The long-run average reward given the atomic action policy
\hat{\pi}
and the initial state
s\in\mathcal{S}
is as follows:

\displaystyle R(\hat{\pi}|s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{D\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{D}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\pi}}\left[\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{n=1}^{N}r^{t}(c_{n}^{t,d},\hat{a}^{t,d}_{n})\Bigg|s\right],

\displaystyle\quad\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

where
\hat{a}_{n}^{t,d}
is the atomic action generated by the atomic action policy and
c_{n}^{t,d}
is the status of vehicle to be assigned in the
n
-th atomic step in decision epoch
(t,d)
. Given any initial state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the optimal atomic action policy is given by
\hat{\pi}^{*}=\mathrm{argmax}_{\hat{\pi}}R(\hat{\pi}|s)
.

Our atomic action policy can be viewed as a reduction of the original fleet dispatching policy in that any realized sequence of atomic actions corresponds to a feasible fleet dispatching action with the same reward of the decision epoch. This reduction makes the training of atomic action policy scalable because the output dimension of atomic action policy
\hat{\pi}^{t}
equals to
|\hat{\mathcal{A}}|
, which is a constant regardless of the fleet size.

Remark 1
.

Our atomic policy avoids the growing input dimension of auto-regressive policies that explicitly append preceding actions. Under the atomic policy, the input for the
n
-th vehicle depends only on the current system state
s_{n}^{t,d}
and the selected vehicle status
c_{n}^{t,d}
. In contrast, such auto-regressive policies require augmenting the input with the atomic actions of all preceding vehicles, i.e.
(s_{n}^{t,d},c_{n}^{t,d},\hat{a}_{1}^{t,d},\dots,\hat{a}_{n-1}^{t,d})
, which leads to substantially larger input dimension.

4
Deep Reinforcement Learning With Aggregated States

The adoption of atomic actions has significantly reduced the policy output dimension. However, computing the optimal atomic action policy is still challenging due to the large state space, which scales significantly with the fleet size, number of regions, and battery discretization. In this section, we provide an efficient algorithm to train the fleet dispatching policy by incorporating our atomic action decomposition into the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) framework
[
3
]
. To tackle with the large state size, we use neural networks to approximate both the value function and the policy function, to be specified later. We also further reduce the state size using the following state reduction scheme:

Battery Level Clustering.

We map the state representation of all vehicle statuses into vehicle statuses with aggregated battery levels. We cluster the battery levels into 3 intervals, each of which denotes low battery level
b_{L}
, medium battery level
b_{M}
, and high battery level
b_{H}
, respectively. It is also possible to cluster the battery levels differently. If computing resources allow, we can cluster the battery levels with finer granularity, e.g. into 10 levels instead of 3. We note that the cutoff points need not be chosen as uniform partitions of the
B
battery levels. Instead, they can be selected according to thresholds in the nonlinear charging curve, corresponding to regions where the charging speed changes significantly.

Trip Order Status Clustering.

In the state reduction scheme, trip orders are aggregated by recording only the number of requests originating from or arriving at each region, instead of tracking the number of trip requests for each origin-destination pair.
The clustering of trip order statuses reduces the state dimension from
O(|V|^{2})
to
O(|V|)
.

We emphasize that this state aggregation is used for the neural network inputs, while the full state vector is retained for simulation and policy evaluation. Although the aggregation inevitably removes some information about the exact battery status of vehicles and the detailed trip distribution, our numerical experiments show that the fleet dispatching policy learned under this reduced representation still achieves strong performance (see Section
6
).

We denote the state space after the reduction on the original state as
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
, which we refer to as the “reduced state space".
We use neural network

Reward Upper Bound Provided By Fluid Approximation Model

Before presenting the performance of our atomic PPO algorithm, we construct an upper bound on the optimal long-run average reward using fluid limit. This upper bound will be used to assess the performance of our atomic PPO algorithm in numerical experiments as shown in the next section. We formulate a fluid-based linear optimization program, where the fluid limit is attained as the fleet size approaches infinity, with both trip arriving rates and charger numbers scaling up proportionally to the fleet size. Under the fluid limit, the system becomes deterministic, and the fleet dispatching policy, which is a probability distribution of vehicle flows across all actions, reduces to a deterministic vector that represents the fraction of fleet assigned to each action at each time of the day.

We conduct numerical experiments using the for-hire vehicle trip record data from July 2022, obtained from the New York City Taxi and Limousine Commission (TLC)

We use the power-of-k dispatch policy and the fluid-based policy as our benchmark algorithms. The power-of-
k
dispatch policy considers trip requests in revenue-descending order. For each trip request, it selects the
k
vehicles with the smallest remaining travel time to the trip origin region and assigns the vehicle with the highest battery level. The assigned vehicle must be no more than
L_{p}
time steps away from the trip origin and have sufficient battery to complete the trip and then drive to the nearest charger. Requests for which no vehicle satisfies these requirements are queued or abandoned. After trip assignments, idle vehicles below full charge are assigned to the nearest charger to charge for
J
time steps. All other vehicles remain idle or continue their current tasks. We experimented with
k=1,\ldots,5
and found that
k=2
achieved the highest average reward, so we use
k=2
throughout. The asymptotic service-level guarantee in
[
11
]
, which assumes spatially uniform demand and charger locations, does not imply reward optimality in our spatially heterogeneous setting.

The fluid policy is constructed from the optimal solution of (
Fluid-LP
). For each pair
(v,\eta)
, we aggregate, separately for each action, the LP flows of vehicles with destination region
v
and remaining time
\eta
across all battery levels, and normalize them to obtain the corresponding proportions assigned to each action. At each decision epoch, these proportions are applied to the realized number of vehicles in each status
(v,\eta,b)
to obtain the target number of vehicles of that status assigned to each action. We then convert the resulting fractional assignments into integer vehicle assignments using an adaptation of the bipartite dependent-rounding procedure in
Gandhi et al. [82]
.

The fluid policy is state-independent and does not account for the stochasticity of trip demand. Consequently, its performance can be limited when the fleet size is moderate and stochastic fluctuations remain significant relative to the system scale. In fact, when we scale the fleet size up to 1,200 vehicles, the fluid policy achieves 81% of the fluid upper bound, suggesting that its performance improves as the system approaches the fluid limit.

Power-of-
k
assigns trip requests based on a pre-specified order and does not account for the future spatial distribution of the fleet and trip demand. In contrast, Atomic-PPO can shape the future fleet distribution both through vehicle repositioning and through its selection of trip requests to fulfill. To isolate the effect of vehicle repositioning, which is absent from power-of-
k
, we additionally conduct an experiment in which vehicle repositioning is disabled for Atomic-PPO throughout the day. Even under this restriction, Atomic-PPO achieves 89% of the fluid upper bound, still exceeding power-of-
k
by a large margin, while the percentage of the fleet serving trip requests, the percentage of requests fulfilled, and the average hourly reward remain comparable to those under Atomic-PPO with repositioning enabled across all periods. During evening rush hours, the no-repositioning variant has slightly higher percentages of the fleet serving trips and requests fulfilled, while achieving the same average hourly reward as Atomic-PPO with repositioning enabled. This comparison indicates that Atomic-PPO’s primary advantage comes from selectively fulfilling trip requests rather than vehicle repositioning in this Manhattan experiment.

6.2.2
Charger quantity and outlet rates.

For a fixed fleet size of 300 vehicles, we keep the chargers in Midtown Manhattan and consider deployments of 10, 15, and 20 units and charging outlet rates of 30 kW, 45 kW, 60 kW, and 75 kW. Figure
4
reports the average daily reward achieved by Atomic-PPO for each combination.

When the charging rate is relatively low (30 kW), increasing the charger number yields an approximately linear improvement in average daily reward. At 45 kW or higher, the gain from increasing the charger number from 10 to 15 exceeds that from 15 to 20. For any fixed charger number, increasing the charging rate exhibits diminishing returns, particularly at higher charger quantities.

These patterns suggest that the system transitions from being charging-capacity constrained to vehicle-availability constrained as charger quantity or outlet rate increases. Once charging constraints are largely mitigated, additional capacity has less value. Charger number and charger outlet rate act as partial substitutes over the configurations considered: higher outlet rates can compensate for fewer chargers, and vice versa, up to a moderate level of total charging capacity. Beyond this level, increasing either dimension in isolation yields limited benefit, suggesting that balanced investments across charger quantity and outlet rate are more effective than aggressively scaling one dimension alone.

6.2.3
Impact of fleet size.

To study the joint impact of fleet size and charger deployment on average daily reward, we repeat the numerical experiments from the previous section under different fleet sizes: 200, 250, 300, and 350 vehicles. Trip arrival rates are held fixed at the baseline calibration while fleet size varies. Figure
4
reports the average reward achieved by Atomic-PPO and power-of-
k
for each combination of fleet size, charger quantity, and outlet rate. When charging capacity is limited, additional vehicles provide little improvement: with 10 chargers at 30 kW, rewards under Atomic-PPO remain between $155K and $160K across the four fleet sizes. With 20 chargers at 75 kW, in contrast, the reward increases from $280K for 200 vehicles to $435K for 350 vehicles. For the smaller fleet, rewards remain near $280K once sufficient charging capacity is provided, whereas larger fleets benefit from additional charging capacity.
These findings suggest that the benefits of expanding either the fleet or charging capacity depend on the availability of the other, and hence that fleet expansion and charging deployment should be planned jointly, consistent with
[
11
]
.

The gains from expanding charging capacity also depend on the dispatching policy. Across the same 48 configurations, Atomic-PPO achieves higher reported rewards than power-of-
k
, with daily reward gaps ranging from $10K to $65K. The gap generally increases with charging capacity: for 300 vehicles, it grows from $10K with 10 chargers at 30 kW to $55K with 20 chargers at 75 kW. Thus, Atomic-PPO extracts a larger reward gain from the same increase in charging capacity than power-of-
k
, indicating that the operational value of charging infrastructure depends in part on how the fleet is dispatched.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this article, we develop a scalable Atomic-PPO algorithm for the efficient control of large-scale electric robo-taxi fleets, including trip matching, vehicle repositioning, and battery charging.
Our approach integrates atomic action decomposition into Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), using an atomic action space whose size is independent of fleet size and significantly lowering the complexity of policy training.
Our state reduction technique substantially decreases the input dimension of the neural networks, improving runtime and memory usage. The Atomic-PPO algorithm makes it practical to compute near-optimal fleet dispatching policies under a wide range of charging infrastructure and fleet size configurations.

The computational efficiency of Atomic-PPO enables a large set of numerical experiments on how charger location, quantity, outlet rate, and fleet size jointly affect long-run system performance. Placing chargers according to observed ridership patterns yields higher system reward than distributing chargers uniformly across regions. For a fixed fleet size, increasing either charger quantity or outlet rate yields smaller reward gains when the other is already high. Larger fleets continue to benefit from additional charging capacity even when the same increase yields little improvement for smaller fleets, highlighting the need to plan fleet expansion and charging infrastructure together.

Future work includes incorporating heterogeneous rider patience and abandonment behavior, allowing vehicle repositioning to be interrupted by newly arriving trip requests, incorporating carpooling decisions into fleet control, and capturing more refined spatial and temporal granularity while preserving scalability.

A.2
System Parameters

We consider a robo taxi ride hailing system operating continuously over a 24 hour horizon. The service area is partitioned into 10 regions (see Figure
2
), and the fleet consists of 300 vehicles. System level decisions are made every 5 minutes, resulting in 288 decision epochs per day. We assume a homogeneous fleet calibrated using the Chevrolet Bolt EUV model with a nominal driving range of 260 miles. Autonomous vehicles are estimated to consume roughly 50% of their battery capacity on onboard computation and sensing, which effectively halves the usable driving range after a full charge. Accordingly, we discretize each vehicle’s battery into 130 evenly spaced levels, where one battery level is depleted for every mile traveled.

We use 75 kW DC fast chargers as the default outlet rate in all experiments unless otherwise specified
[
80
,
81
]
. Each charging period lasts 15 minutes, and the number of battery levels gained is determined according to Table
2
. When chargers with different outlet rates are considered, the battery gain per charging period is adjusted by scaling the charging time in each battery percentage bucket of Table
2
according to the ratio between 75 kW and the corresponding charger outlet rate. The charging cost incurred is set to be $0.39 per kWh consumed at all times of the day
[
83
]
.

The direct repositioning cost is set to zero. Repositioning consumes battery and vehicle time, with electricity costs incurred when the battery is recharged. The full-day comparison in Table
3
assesses performance without vehicle repositioning between regions, including overnight; travel to chargers within a vehicle’s current region remains allowed.

We calibrate trip durations and trip rewards using the NYC TLC dataset
[
9
]
. For each origin destination region pair and each hour of the day, we compute the average trip duration and average trip fare across all observed trips, and round the average duration to the nearest 5 minutes. At each 5 minute decision epoch, the simulator uses the trip duration and reward corresponding to the hour in which that epoch occurs. In Appendix
B
, we conduct additional experiments using stochastic trip durations sampled from the empirical distribution. For each origin destination region pair and each hour of the day, we calibrate this empirical distribution by first rounding all observed trip durations to the nearest 5 minutes and then computing the fraction of trips in each 5 minute bin. During simulation, the trip duration is sampled according to these fractions.

For the trip demand profile, we first compute the average number of trips for each origin destination pair at each hour of the day, averaged over all Mondays to Thursdays in July 2022. We then scale these average arrivals by the ratio between the chosen fleet size (300 vehicles) and the estimated actual fleet size (12.8k vehicles), where the latter is inferred from the maximum number of simultaneous trips observed throughout the day. At each decision epoch, the simulator generates trip requests for every origin destination pair by sampling from a Poisson distribution whose mean equals the scaled average arrival rate corresponding to that pair and the hour in which the decision epoch occurs.

A.3
Atomic-PPO Training Procedure

We use deep neural networks to approximate the policy and value functions. Both networks are implemented as three-layer feed forward architectures. The numbers of neurons in the hidden layers are 500, 64, and 64 for both the policy and value networks. The activation functions for the policy network are tanh, tanh, and tanh, while the value network uses tanh, relu, and tanh. The network input consists of the reduced state vector (defined in Section
4
) together with an embedding of the time of day. The time embedding is obtained by passing the time index through an embedding layer with dimension 24.

The initial clipping rate
\epsilon_{0}
of Atomic-PPO is set to
0.1

\gamma=0.97
across policy iterations. Specifically, the clipping rate at policy iteration
m=1,2,\dots
is given by
\max\{\epsilon_{0}\cdot\gamma^{m},0.01\}
. In each policy iteration, we roll out 30 independent trajectories, each spanning 8 operation days, with vehicles initialized at half charge and uniformly distributed across all regions. Using the collected trajectories, we update the value network and the policy network according to Algorithm
1
. Both networks are trained using mini-batch updates. At each gradient step, we compute gradients from a batch of 1024 data points randomly sampled from the collected dataset and update the network parameters using Adam. The learning rates are set to
3\times 10^{-4}
for the value network and
5\times 10^{-4}
for the policy network. The numbers of gradient updates are 100 for the value network and 20 for the policy network. Table

Appendix B
Robustness Check of Atomic-PPO

We evaluate the robustness of Atomic-PPO along three complementary dimensions that reflect realistic sources of model mismatch in ride-hailing systems: temporal shifts in trip demand profiles, stochasticity in trip durations, and increased state–action complexity induced by finer spatial partitions. Together, these experiments assess whether Atomic-PPO generalizes beyond the specific modeling assumptions and training instances used in the main experiments.

Distribution shift of trip demand profile.

To assess the generalizability of Atomic-PPO under demand distribution shifts, we compare the long-run average daily reward achieved by Atomic-PPO trained on July’s demand profile with that achieved by Atomic-PPO retrained on each corresponding month’s demand profile, for June, August, and September (see Table
9
). This experiment reflects realistic non-stationarity in ride-hailing demand arising from seasonal variation, changes in travel patterns, and external factors.

Specifically, we first construct the demand profile for July 2022 following the procedure described in Section
6
. This involves computing the trip demand volume for each origin–destination pair at each time of day, averaged across all workdays in July 2022, and then uniformly scaling the demand volumes according to the ratio between the chosen fleet size (300 vehicles) and the actual fleet size in Manhattan, defined as the maximum number of vehicles simultaneously serving trips over a day. We then construct the demand profiles for June, August, and September by applying the same uniform scaling factor to the corresponding average demand volumes for each origin–destination pair at each time of day.

Table
9
shows that Atomic-PPO trained on July’s demand profile achieves nearly identical long-run average daily reward to Atomic-PPO trained on the corresponding month’s demand profile across all three months, attaining as high as 91% of the fluid-based upper bound for June and September and 95% for August. These results indicate that Atomic-PPO maintains strong performance under month-to-month demand shifts, even without retraining on the updated demand profile.

Larger number of regions.

To assess how Atomic-PPO scales with the number of regions, we partition Manhattan into 10, 15, and 20 regions, retrain Atomic-PPO for each instance, and compute the corresponding fluid-based revenue upper bound when feasible. Increasing the number of regions substantially enlarges the state and action spaces, making this a stringent test of scalability.

Across all three instances, Atomic-PPO’s total training time remains similar (within 4 hours), while peak memory usage increases from approximately 200 GB to 300 GB and 400 GB as the number of regions grows from 10 to 15 and 20. In contrast, a naive implementation of the fluid-based LP solves the 10-region instance in under 2 hours, requires roughly 14 hours for 15 regions, and fails with an out-of-memory error for 20 regions (exceeding 1 TB).

Table

shows that for the 10- and 15-region settings, where the fluid LP remains tractable, Atomic-PPO attains 91% and 90% of the fluid upper bound, respectively. Although the fluid upper bound cannot be computed for the 20-region instance, Atomic-PPO continues to achieve stable performance, with only a moderate reduction in reward. These results highlight that Atomic-PPO can scale to finer spatial partitions while maintaining strong performance, even in regimes where fluid-based benchmarks become computationally infeasible.
