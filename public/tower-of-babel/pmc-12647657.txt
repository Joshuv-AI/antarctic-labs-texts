Squat lobster latitudinal life habitat shifts and metabolic response to combined temperature and oxygen conditions in the Humboldt Current System
Authors: María de los Ángeles Gallardo, Kurt Paschke, Katherina Brokordt, Marcel Ramos, Martin Thiel, Matias Pizarro-Koch, Beatriz Yannicelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe examined how a species inhabiting a latitudinal gradient, from warm oxygenated surface waters to cold oxygen-limited subsurface waters along the Eastern South Pacific (ESP) shelf, responds to latitudinal temperature shifts at low-oxygen isopleths. We combined temperature-oxygen sections from the World Ocean Database, historical records of pelagic/benthic Grimothea monodon occurrence across latitude, models with these data, and laboratory experiments assessing juveniles’ routine and postprandial metabolism under realistic temperature-oxygen conditions. The life habits (pelagic or benthic) of squat lobsters were related to temperature at the 2 mL O2 L−1 oxygen isopleth. At temperatures > 15 °C near the upper oxygen minimum zone isopleth, mostly pelagic individuals were observed, suggesting restricted vertical migration. The physiological performance of juveniles (main migratory stage) was negatively affected by high temperature-hypoxia interaction. Routine metabolic rates decreased by 60% under hypoxia at 21 °C, and postprandial metabolism (as Specific Dynamic Action) was also strongly reduced under those conditions. Grimothea monodon can shift between pelagic and benthic habitats across a range of ESP conditions, maintaining the intergenerational ability to alternate habitats. Introduction

Global climate change is altering oxygen and temperature conditions in the ocean, representing challenges for many marine species1. Ocean oxygen depletion and temperature rise are expected to restrict the suitable habitat of marine invertebrates in the near future2 due to their metabolic thresholds1,3. Such changes are expected to affect individual growth and reproduction, population dynamics, and the spatial distribution of many species4. The effects of temperature and oxygen on the physiology of ectotherms have been studied mainly in species inhabiting coastal environments5, but are scarce in organisms inhabiting oxygen minimum zones (OMZ)6 that already display complex physiological adaptations to overcome oxygen-poor, but mainly cold conditions7. Few species are reported as having a geographic distribution extending across normoxic/hypoxic conditions (as prevailing in OMZs) and pronounced temperature gradients. A methodological approach to infer the future shifts of OMZs on population parameters and distribution is to evaluate the current distribution and life cycle characteristics of species that occupy contrasting temperature and oxygen conditions together with their ecophysiological and metabolic characteristics. This approach should enable the assessment of potential physiological and metabolic responses under future physicochemical scenarios.

Hypoxia may affect the routine metabolic rates and aerobic scope of metazoan organisms (within species-specific tolerance thresholds), limiting the supply of cellular oxygen and ultimately affecting the production of energy (ATP) by aerobic metabolic pathways8. OMZ organisms usually keep oxygen provision independent from ambient concentrations down to very low levels (oxyregulation)9. Nevertheless, routine metabolic demands increase with temperature, which leads to (i) an elevation of critical oxygen (Pcrit), i.e. the threshold below which organisms can no longer maintain a stable routine metabolic rate (RMR), and (ii) a reduction in aerobic scope, defined as the difference between maximum oxygen uptake and RMR, which serves as an indicator of aerobic performance and overall fitness5. Therefore, temperature variability associated with the upper OMZ boundary (0.5–2.0 mL O2 L−1) is expected to have strong metabolic and biological effects on organisms10 as demonstrated by studies on temperature–oxygen interactions influencing individual fish performance11.

Grimothea monodon (H. Milne Edwards, 1837), a decapod crustacean (Munididae) previously referred to as Pleuroncodes monodon until its recent taxonomic reassessment12, is distributed along the Pacific coast of America, from ~ 15° N to ~ 40° S13. Its capacity to withstand low oxygen concentrations have been proven from field campaigns14,15 and laboratory experiments16,17, although severe hypoxia induces sublethal metabolic, physiological and behavioral effects16–18. Thus, it can be considered an ideal model species for examining responses to latitudinal gradients in temperature and oxygen concentrations within its distribution range. From Peru to southern Chile, the OMZ becomes progressively narrower and deeper with latitude19, while sea surface temperature diminishes (> 23 °C to 12 °C)20. For example, at ~ 7 °S large pelagic populations of G. monodon inhabit surface waters above the oxycline, both day and night21,22. South of 25 °S, adult populations support an important benthic trawl fishery on the hypoxic continental shelf (e.g. ~ 34–37 S)23. Additionally, limited evidence suggests that adult vertical migration may occur along the Peruvian margins17.

The high phenotypic plasticity of G. monodon24 is also reflected in the smaller adult size and earlier maturity observed in northern pelagic populations25,26 compared to southern benthic ones. Adult life in benthic habitats is reached by southern populations after a juvenile stage that performs daily migrations from surface waters to the benthos27, and to the best of our knowledge, there are no reports of pelagic ovigerous females at southern latitudes. So far, a qualitative literature review points to both temperature and hypoxia among the potential drivers of plastic shifts in life-history traits of the species28. However, neither the latitudinal gradient of the upper OMZ temperature over the continental shelf of the Humboldt Current System (HCS), nor its relationship with the life habits of G. monodon have been described. The physiological and ecological challenges that juveniles face when migrating from warm, oxygen-rich surface waters to deep, colder, hypoxic layers could be key determinants of the life habits and adult population parameters.

We hypothesize that the observed segregation of adult life habits (pelagic/benthic) within the latitudinal range of our study (5 °S to 35 °S, Fig. 1A) is associated with the latitudinal variability of temperature-oxygen oceanographic conditions over the continental shelf, which varies between 10 to 100 km in width (Fig. 1B). Moreover, naturally occurring combinations of temperature-oxygen levels within this distribution range impose metabolic constraints on juveniles. In order to evaluate our hypothesis, we first reconstructed the mean latitudinal distribution of water temperature and oxygen characteristics over the continental shelf. Second, we conducted an exhaustive literature review of the occurrence of pelagic/benthic G. monodon throughout the same latitudinal range. Third, we related the presence/absence of each life habit (pelagic or benthic) with the latitudinal oceanographic reconstruction. Finally, we experimentally determined metabolic responses of juveniles to combined temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) conditions within natural ranges on routine and post-prandial metabolic rates (specific dynamic action, SDA) and metabolic enzyme activities.

Results

Latitudinal oceanography

Based on World Ocean Database (WOD) oceanographic profiles (1969–2008) for the Eastern South Pacific (ESP) continental shelf (Fig. 1A,B), hypoxic waters are colder than more oxygenated surface waters. The mean temperatures associated with the OMZ boundaries, defined by the 2, 1, and 0.5 mL L−1 oxygen isopleths, decreased with latitude (Fig. 1C). At 14–16 °S, temperatures were 16.0 °C, 14.9 °C and 14.1 °C, respectively, while further south at 23–37 °S, they decreased to 12.3 °C, 12.1 °C and 11.8 °C, for each corresponding isopleth. Isopleth depths do not trend monotonically over the continental shelf (Fig. 1D). In general, oxygen-deficient waters (< 1 mL L−1) also exhibited a latitudinal decline in temperature: at 15 °S, 12–18 °C; 22 °S, 12–14 °C and at 35 °S, 10–11.5 °C (Fig. 1E). A coastal alongshore section of the ratio of oxygen/temperature derived from World Ocean Atlas (WOA-2023) climatology shows this pattern, although less pronounced. This is also reflected in the temperature latitudinal distribution along several oxygen isopleths (Supplementary Fig. S1 online).

Mean surface temperatures over the continental shelf ranged from ~ 11 °C in the subtropical region (35–37 °S) to ~ 20 °C in the northern HCS (Fig. 1E; 2A), and the largest latitudinal surface temperature gradient was observed between 27 and 20 °S. Vertical temperature profiles and temperature at isopleth display a wider range in northern latitudes. The 2 mL L−1 isopleth deepened south of 18 °S from ~ 15 to 50 m, while the 1 and 0.5 mL L−1 isopleths also deepened north of 8 °S (Figs. 1D; 2B). The depth of the 0.5 mL L−1 isopleth did not exhibit a monotonic trend with latitude nor shelf width latitudinal variability (Figs. 1B,D, 2B).

Presence/absence of Grimothea monodon in pelagic and benthic zones

The latitudinal distribution of G. monodon adult habitat shows a break between 20 and 23 °S (Fig. 2C). Out of 37 demersal/benthic studies between 1965 and 2015 between 5 and 38 °S (Table S1), 16 studies reported consistent occurrence of benthic G. monodon either north of 9 °S and south of 20–23 °S (Fig. 2C). North of 9 °S, benthic G. monodon appeared between 1996 and 2014 in low densities (Supplementary Table S1 online) as bycatch and food item of Peruvian hake29. South of 20–23 °S, G. monodon sustains large benthic biomasses (targeted by trawl fisheries; bycatch and food item of Chilean hake30). Benthic densities in Chile have been one to two orders of magnitude higher than those ever recorded in Peru.

Important biomasses of pelagic (adult) G. monodon have been documented during small pelagic fish assessments between 6 and 18 °S, overlapping with the Engraulis ringens distribution over the Peruvian continental shelf from 1988 onwards22. North of 9 °S, benthic and pelagic forms coexist. In Chile, small pelagic assessments have revealed G. monodon pelagic forms only between 20 and 18 °S, as anchovy bycatch31, but never south of those latitudes even though such assessments extend to 40 °S (Table S1).

The Peruvian Institute of the Sea (IMARPE) undertook campaigns (1970–1972) from 12 to 18 °S dedicated to explore potential fisheries of benthic crustaceans. During the 1972 El Niño, G. monodon was found in low densities at a single 40 m depth station at 16.5 °S (Table S1). Two records of benthic G. monodon were found at 12.2 °S: (i) Seven individuals caught at 160 m depth during 1965 (El Niño)32, and (ii) 11 individuals at 71 and 145 m depth in 2013, neutral oceanographic conditions17, and hypoxic waters ~ 1 mL L−1.

Temperatures associated with the boundaries of the OMZ (i.e. the depths of the 0.5, 1 and 2 mL L−1 oxygen isopleths) significantly contributed to explaining the shift in G. monodon habitat use. The Random Forest model, which included all predictor variables, achieved a classification accuracy of 90.91%, with an OOB prediction error of 9.09%, indicating a strong predictive ability of habitat type (benthic, pelagic, or co-occurrence) based on environmental variables (Supplementary Table S2). After excluding variables with lower importance (shelf width and depth of 0.5 mL L−1 isopleth), the model’s classification accuracy increased to 93.90%. Benthic habitats were predicted with 100% accuracy, associated with low temperature at the OMZ boundaries (< 12.3 °C) and deeper OMZ location (> 50 m). Pelagic habitats were predicted with 91.7% accuracy under conditions of high temperatures (> 15 °C) and shallow OMZ boundaries (< 50 m). Co-occurrence of benthic and pelagic habits was predicted with 66.7% accuracy, associated with deeper OMZ boundaries (1 and 0.5 mL L−1 isopleths below 90 m) and wider continental shelf areas (> 50 km) (Figs. 1 and 2).

Ecophysiology of Grimothea monodon

Metabolic rates of G. monodon juveniles were significantly influenced by temperature, oxygen partial pressure (kPa), and their interaction, under both fasting and postprandial conditions. Routine metabolic rate (RMR) increased with temperature, with oxygen consumption nearly tripling from 11 to 21 °C under normoxia conditions (Fig. 3A). At 11 °C, RMR was low (0.13 ± 0.03 mg O2 h−1 g−1; mean ± SD) and independent of oxygen partial pressure across the range evaluated, suggesting no evident oxygen limitation threshold at this temperature. RMR was significantly higher at 21 °C (p < 0.0001). Using a generalized additive model (GAM) to assess the metabolic response to decreasing oxygen partial pressure, we identified an inflection point at approximately 7 kPa (21 °C), which we interpret as a proxy for an oxygen tolerance threshold under high temperature. This threshold does not represent a formal Pcrit, but may reflect the point where oxygen availability begins to constrain RMR under warm conditions. At this temperature, RMR decreased from a mean of 0.32 ± 0.07 mg O2 h−1 g−1 to 0.23 ± 0.05 mg O2 h−1 g−1 (mean ± SD) across the oxygen range, representing a 60.3% reduction in RMR (Fig. 3A).

The apparent specific activity of LDH (Lactate dehydrogenase) in G. monodon juveniles increased with rising temperature and decreasing oxygen levels. LDH activity remained relatively stable in juveniles maintained at 11 °C (4.45 ± 1.06 UI LDH g−1; mean ± SD) across the range of DO analyzed. At 21 °C, the apparent specific activity of LDH increased from 5.22 to 7.23 UI LDH g−1, at the lowest oxygen level considered (Fig. 3B).

Temperature significantly affected RMR in postprandial metabolic experiments, depending on oxygen concentrations. RMR increased with temperature at normoxia and at 2.9 mg L−1, but at 1.4 and 0.7 mg L−1, maximum RMR was observed at 15 °C (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table S3 online). Consistent with previous experiments, at 11 °C juveniles maintained stable RMR across all DO levels analyzed (Fig. 3A and 4A). At 21 °C, RMR decreased with decreasing DO; with reduction from normoxia to 2.9 mg L−1 similar to that seen in previous experiments, and further reduced at 1.4 and 0.7 mg L−1, although no significant difference in oxygen consumption was detected between the latter two treatments (Fig. 4A). At 15 °C, RMR under normoxia was significantly higher only when compared with the lowest oxygen treatment (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Table S3 online).

The peak of the postprandial metabolic curve was modulated by temperature and DO (Fig. 4B). Overall it decreased with decreasing oxygen concentrations at all temperatures, but with different slopes and shapes. The decrease was steeper at 21 °C, where no peak was observed at 0.7 mgL−1, than at 15 °C and 11 °C. At 11 °C, significant differences were observed only between normoxia-2.9 and 1.4–0.7 mg L−1 treatments. An increase in the postprandial peak with temperature was observed only under normoxic conditions. In general, the postprandial metabolic scope increased with oxygen concentration at all temperatures and greater at low temperatures, regardless of DO level (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Table S3 online). Postprandial metabolic duration decreased with declined oxygen levels, and a steeper reduction at higher temperatures. At 11 °C and 15 °C, duration decreased from normoxia to 2.9 mg L−1 and remained constant in more hypoxic treatment, whereas at 21 °C it decreased across all hypoxia levels. Under normoxia conditions, the duration was marginally longer at 21 °C, while at 0.7 and 1.4 mg L−1 it decreased significantly with increasing temperature (Fig. 4D).

The accumulated energy expended during the postprandial metabolic response (SDA) was significantly influenced by interaction between temperature and DO. SDA decreased by 85–90% from normoxia to 2.9 mg L−1 for all temperatures. The reduction below 2.9 mg L−1 was steepest at 21 °C, approaching zero at 0.7 mg L−1, and was not significant at 15 °C and 11 °C. This pattern suggests that at 21 °C, animals likely operated below the oxygen threshold needed to sustain the elevated metabolic rate required for digestion, consistent with the concept of Pcrit for SDA (Fig. 4E; Supplementary Table S3 online).

For LDH activity, a significant effect of both temperature (p = 0.001) and DO concentration (p = 0.007) was observed. At 21 °C, LDH activity remained low and stable (~ 3 UI g−1) across all DO concentrations. At 15 °C, LDH activity only increased at 0.7 mg L−1 (mean 9.3 UI g−1), while at 11 °C, high LDH activities was observed from 2.9 mg L−1 and lower. LDH activity decreased with increasing temperature, indicating a higher reliance on anaerobic metabolism at lower temperatures under hypoxia (Fig. 5A). HSP70 concentration was significantly affected by DO (p = 0.0143), but not by temperature (p = 0.67). The highest HSP70 levels were observed at 0.7 mg L−1 (mean = 1.3 HSP70 mg protein−1), indicating a cellular stress response under more severe hypoxia (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

The shallow depth of the oxycline had previously been identified as the main cause for the shift of adult Grimothea monodon habitat use-from benthic habitats in southern-central Chile to pelagic habitats off southern Peru22. Nevertheless, the question remained: why do adults avoid low-oxygen waters at low latitudes, while inhabiting hypoxic bottom waters at higher latitudes?

Our study shows that the most conspicuous adult habitat shift is associated not only with the depth of the upper OMZ, but also with its temperature. Warm and hypoxic waters therefore appear to restrict G. monodon to areas above the 2 mL O2 L−1 isopleth. We alsodemonstrate that juveniles experience metabolic constraints under hypoxic and warm conditions, as evidenced by the significant reductions in SDA (accumulated energy). Juveniles are known to migrate daily between oxygenated surface waters and cold hypoxic demersal waters in northern-central and southern-central Chile14,27. RMR show no significant decline at low oxygen concentrations at 11 °C, but do decrease at 15 °C and particularly at 21 °C. Additionally, postprandial metabolism is limited under warm hypoxic conditions. These findings suggest that the physiological constraints squat lobsters begin to experience during the juvenile phase may determine their future life habit (benthic or pelagic), and contribute to the observed latitudinal shift in habitat use.

Throughout the latitudinal range considered, oxygen concentrations below 1 mL L−1 and 0.5 mL L−1 were frequently observed in subsurface waters over the continental shelf. The upper boundary of the OMZ has previously been described as deepening with latitude, generally reaching depths greater than 100 m towards 37°S33,34. However, when focusing exclusively on oxygen profiles over the continental shelf (< 200 m), the presence of a shallower OMZ becomes evident along the Chilean continental shelf, as described for the Concepción area35. Low-oxygen conditions are usually associated with “cold waters”14,17, but the latitudinal variability of such “cold” conditions had not been previously assessed, despite the well-established interactive effect of temperature and oxygen concentration on the ectotherm metabolism8 and their broad importance for metabolic and geochemical processes. The latitudinal variation in temperature at the upper boundary for the OMZ ranges from 4 to 7 °C, while the thermal gradient across the oxycline varies from 2 °C in the south to 6 °C at low latitudes (Supplementary Fig. S2 online).

Our review revealed that latitudinal variations in temperature and oxygen are significantly linked to changes in adult life habits of G. monodon. Several members of the family Munididae exhibit bentho-pelagic life histories, either with (Grimothea gregaria)36,37 or without (Grimothea planipes38 and Grimothea quadrispina39) distinct morphology differences between pelagic and benthic adult forms, although benthic individuals tend to attain significantly larger sizes. In the case of G. monodon, the use of different habitats is explained by latitudinal variation in the structure of temperature-oxygen profiles, while life history traits (e.g., longevity, maximum size, and age/size at first sexual maturity) are likely shaped by temperature gradients across latitude (Figs. 1 and 2). In Peru, adult pelagic squat lobsters are captured at temperatures between 16 and 23°C40, whereas in Chile they are caught in the benthos at 11–12 °C15. The size at sexual maturity (CL–Carapace Length) differs by ~ 6–8 mm between pelagic (Peru, CL 10 mm) and benthic individuals at sexual maturity (Concepción, Chile, CL > 18 mm)41,42. Our observations are consistent with the expected temperature-size relationship in ectotherm taxa43,44. Moreover, temperature shows a negative correlation with longevity and size at first sexual maturity, and maximum adult size, as development rates are more temperature-dependent than growth rate43.

Meeting the oxygen requirements necessary to support the expected increase in metabolic rates (RMR, postprandial metabolism) from 11 °C to 15–18 °C appears to be limited for juveniles at the oxygen concentrations characteristic of the upper OMZ. Additionally, rising temperatures reduce oxygen solubility, further diminishing oxygen availability under hypoxic conditions5. Together, these warm and hypoxic conditions likely constrain both habitat suitability and maximum attainable body size43.

Further evidence supporting temperature–oxygen isopleth restriction comes from field observations: benthic adults of G. monodon are consistently found between 6 and 7 °S, and only occasionally at 11 °S and 16 °S17,32. The area between 6 and 7 °S coincides with a deepening of the OMZ, due to influence of the oxygen- and nutrient-rich Cromwell Current45. In contrast, the few benthic individuals recorded further south were collected during warm El Niño years (e.g., 1972, 1967, 2002), when the OMZ retreats offshore and deepens, temporarily improving oxygen conditions on the seafloor. In both scenarios, the typical configuration of a shallow, warm, low-oxygen isopleth is disrupted.

Our experimental results on RMR, inflection point, and postprandial metabolism across ecologically realistic temperature and oxygen scenarios are consistent with latitudinal patterns of habitat use and plasticity observed in the field. As expected, temperature and oxygen interacted to shape the metabolic response in juvenile G. monodon. Specifically, RMR increased with temperature from 11 °S and 21 °C under normoxic conditions, matching the thermal range of natural populations. An inflection point in RMR was detected at high temperatures (7 kPa), but not at 11 °C within the range of evaluated partial pressures, regardless of feeding state (starved or postprandial). The RMR values observed are consistent with those predicted by the log–log relationship between weight-specific respiration and individual mass across the life cycle of the species (Supplementary Fig. S1 online)46, as well as with previous estimates for G. monodon17. This is coincident with expected results for ectotherms that within species-specific ranges show higher RMR at higher temperatures47, and also with observations of other squat lobster species, such as Galathea strigosa48 and adult Grimothea planipes (ex Pleuroncodes planipes49,50). The inflection point, used here as an approximation of Pcrit to assess hypoxia tolerance, was higher in juveniles than the values previously reported for adult G. monodon (0.5 kPa)17,18 and for benthopelagic adults of G. planipes49. This difference may be explained by the fact that juveniles exhibited RMR approximately twice as high as those of adults (see Fig. S1).

Below the Pcrit threshold, the capacity of uptake and transport oxygen through hemolymph becomes insufficient to sustain basic aerobic metabolic demands, forcing the organism to rely on anaerobic pathways for ATP production5. However, some species can downregulate their metabolic rate prior to initiating anaerobic metabolism, allowing them to temporarily maintain aerobic scope even as oxygen availability declines50. Our results indicate that at 21 °C, the decline in oxygen consumption below 7 kPa coincided with a significant increase in LDH activity, suggesting the onset of anaerobic metabolism. LDH is a well-established proxy for anaerobic potential, particularly in crustaceans, where it is the key enzyme of the glycolytic pathway51, and its activity can respond rapidly to changes in DO. Nevertheless, this anaerobic support can only sustain metabolic function for short periods of time, being unable to allow the permanence of an organism for prolonged periods in hypoxia9. Consequently, high temperature and low oxygen conditions would be avoided by most species throughout their life cycle.

For juveniles kept at 11 °C, no inflection point was observed in our experiments, as they exhibited full oxyregulation down to 1.4 kPa. This patterns is further supported by the constant apparent specific activity of LDH measured in starved organisms at 11 °C across different oxygen partial pressures. These results are consistent with previous observations in bento-pelagic adult G. planipes from the northeastern Pacific50. Under natural conditions, many benthic juveniles of G. monodon can be found at concentrations below 0.7 mg O2 L−1, and down to 0.2 mg O2 L−1 (~ 30–37 °S)14,15 in waters of about 11 °C52.

Postprandial metabolism in juveniles of Grimothea monodon was strongly influenced by the temperature–oxygen interaction. While postprandial oxygen consumption reflects multiple processes—mechanical digestion, nutrient absorption, and assimilation—the majority is related to protein synthesis53,54, making it a proxy for growth-related metabolic costs56,57. Total SDA depends on both the increase in oxygen consumption above RMR and its duration53. Under normoxia at 11 °C, juveniles exhibited a 2- to threefold increase in oxygen consumption over RMR, consistent with expected values for invertebrates53. However, at 21 °C, the peak SDA increased only 1.2-fold over RMR, indicating a constrained postprandial metabolic response under warm conditions. Although SDA energy was higher at elevated temperatures, the reduced SDA-to-RMR ratio suggests limited aerobic scope in juveniles under warm hypoxic stress. These results likely reflect the early ontogenetic stage analyzed, as the capacity to compensate SDA expenditure at increasing temperature develops progressively during ontogeny54. Overall, these findings indicate that juvenile metabolic constraints under combined temperature and oxygen stress may shape subsequent growth and life-history traits, linking physiological performance to habitat use and latitudinal distribution.

Low DO conditions might restrict the peak of postprandial SDA if the maximum oxygen uptake capacity is structurally limited, and thus SDA scope, as oxygen becomes limiting, particularly at elevated temperature8. In this case, the SDA scope increased as temperature decreased, and this trend persisted with the decrease in DO. The reduction in SDA scope at 11 °C under the two lower DO concentrations indicates that, although juveniles maintained stable RMR, they were unable to sustain postprandial oxygen uptake rates comparable to those under normoxia. Nevertheless, they maintained total SDA energy across hypoxic conditions.

A key step in metabolic adaptations and evolution is the organism’s ability to uptake and deliver oxygen to tissues, cells and mitochondria45. The maximum oxygen delivery capacity during routine metabolism may reflect physiological constraints on fitness, commonly evaluated through the scope for activity, growth, and SDA scope58. If the postprandial SDA peak would reflect this maximum uptake and delivery capacity, enabling the organism to take advantage of food resources, then a duration of postprandial activity may act as a compensatory mechanism, helping to maintain a stable energy gain despite reduced peak uptake.

However, in the juvenile stage, when growth is critical, the oxygen delivery hypothesis may not fully account for the lack of compensation between high and low oxygen conditions for 11 °C observed in this study55. Given that juveniles are known to grow and persist under similar environmental conditions in the field (30–37 °S; < 0.7 mg O2 L−1 and ~ 11 °C), a more likely explanation is the adjustment of ATP-demanding processes in response to varying temperatures and oxygen concentrations.

Hypoxia at 21 °C allowed for an increase above RMR during postprandial metabolism, except at the lowest DO level tested (Figs. 3 and 4). This is a common response in crustaceans under moderate hypoxia50. In such conditions, internal oxygen demand at the organ level is expected to determine whole-body oxygen uptake rate. However, under severe hypoxia and higher temperature (0.7 mg DO and 21 °C), no postprandial peak was observed, suggesting that oxygen uptake had reached its physiological limit and digestion/assimilation was no longer viable. At 21 °C and 0.7 mg L−1, G. monodon would be unable to meet post-feeding energy demands, and therefore these conditions should be ecologically avoided.

We expect that a plastic species such as G. monodon may not restrict its latitudinal distribution in climate change scenarios, but instead adjusts its vertical distribution and life-history traits. Climate change projections indicate the future intensification of OMZs (expanding vertically, latitudinally, and longitudinally)9,59,61. The SE Pacific is expected to be among the regions least affected by deoxygenation59. Nevertheless, changes in oxygen patterns will likely impact the vertical distribution of coastal aerobic species due to the shoaling oxycline62. Meanwhile, the SE Pacific water column has slightly cooled (− 0.2 °C), a trend linked to stronger southern winds, changes in coastal circulation61,62, and possibly due to multidecadal oceanic-atmospheric oscillatory modes63. Global climate models suggest a slower warming rate over the SE Pacific compared to other basins, primarily due to wind-driven circulation. However, a shallower oxygen isopleth could bring the upper OMZ boundary into contact with warmer temperatures, particularly north of 24 °S60. If this trend persists, two scenarios could be expected: (i) a contraction of the oxygenated pelagic habitat of G. monodon in Peru, or (ii) an extension of the pelagic habitat to G. monodon to the south of 20 °S.

Latitudinal changes in habitat use would likely have ecosystem-wide impacts, as this species plays a central role in both benthic and pelagic food webs, including interactions with commercially important species (e. g. horse mackerel and hake). Based on our results, we predict that in a scenario of surface warming and expansion of hypoxia, the proportion of G. monodon populations adopting pelagic life habits will increase. In contrast, species lacking this life-history plasticity are expected to retreat into regions where oxygen and temperature conditions remain favorable. Our results suggest that differential species responses to warming and deoxygenation could lead to a reassembly of pelagic and benthic communities associated with the OMZ, with potential consequences for food web structure, species interactions, and the resilience of Eastern South Pacific ecosystems, and future expansion/retraction of fishing grounds.

Limitations and future directions

Although our experiments were conducted on juvenile G. monodon (0–30 m depth), this stage represents the critical phase for benthic recruitment. The observed metabolic constraints provide insight into environmental factors shaping the species’ depth distribution. Results should be interpreted with caution, as they apply only to juveniles. Future research should include pelagic adults (Peru and northern Chile), which may exhibit different physiological responses depending on reproductive state. This study offers a hypothesis on habitat shifts under climate change, opening the door to Individual-Based Modeling (IBM) analyses to assess individual physiological and ecological responses under current and future scenarios.

Methods

Bio-physical metadata compilation and analysis

The latitudinal variability (meridional section) of mean temperature and dissolved oxygen concentrations over the continental shelf of western South America was characterized after extracting available temperature/oxygen profiles from the World Ocean Database (WOD, www.nodc.noaa.gov), using all available records from 1970 to 2018. These profiles spanned from 5 to 35 °S and from the coast to offshore distances corresponding to station depths of 200 m. The data were spatially averaged in 0.5° latitude grids, from the coast to the 200 m depth contour, with the aim of representing characteristic conditions over the continental shelf. After checking the quality of temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) values, these were averaged over time every 1° of latitude at standard depths. Afterwards the depths and temperature of the limits of the OMZ were identified for each averaged profile, considering three values of oxygen isopleths 0.5, 1 and 2 mL O2 L−1 (Fig. 1). Finally, the width of the continental shelf (distance in km from the coast to the 200 m isobaths) was estimated from the database GEBCO 2014 Grid, and also averaged for each degree of latitude.

A database for Grimothea monodon presence/absence was constructed after reviewing surveys conducted along the continental shelf of western South America (5–37 °S) between 1965 and 2015 (Supplementary Methodology and Table S1 online); a total of 56 published papers and unpublished reports were used. The matrix of presence/absence of G. monodon in pelagic and benthic habitats for every degree of latitude was constructed using direct fishery assessment reports of free-living individuals of this species as well as its presence in the diet of pelagic/demersal predators. Three types of life habits were distinguished by latitudinal degree: 1-benthic adults, 2-pelagic adults, and 3-both benthic and pelagic adults combined. To evaluate the environmental drivers associated with the distribution of these life habits across the southeastern Pacific, we used a Random Forest (RF) classification model. This non-parametric machine learning method is particularly suitable for handling complex ecological datasets with potential nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictor variables.

The RF analysis was conducted to relate the occurrence of Grimothea monodon’s life habits (benthic, pelagic, and both combined) to a set of environmental predictors, including: shelf width (as a proxy for available benthic habitat), depth of the OMZ boundary, defined by three thresholds of dissolved oxygen (0.5, 1 and 2 mL O2 L−1), and temperature recorded at each corresponding OMZ boundary depth. The analysis was implemented in R using the randomForest package64 within RStudio (R Core Team, 2024)65. To assess the model’s performance and the relative importance of each predictor, we examined metrics such as the out-of-bag (OOB) error rate and variable importance scores based on the mean decrease in accuracy. This approach enabled us to identify key environmental thresholds that may drive shifts in habitat use and life-history strategies in G. monodon across latitudinal gradients.

Ecophysiological experiments

Juveniles of G. monodon were obtained from plankton sampled near Concepción Bay in southern Chile (36° 28.4′ S, 73° 00.8′ W) on March 19, 2014. Specimens were captured during the night, between 0 and 30 m, with a bongo net (300 µm), and immediately placed at 11–12 °C to be transported to the Laboratory of Crustacean Ecophysiology (LECOFIC) at the Universidad Austral de Chile, Chile (Puerto Montt), where all the ecophysiological experiments were conducted. Before the experiments were set up, the collected juveniles (large cephalothorax: 7.40 ± 1.12 mm, mean ± SD; weight: 0.20 ± 0.04 g, mean ± SD) were maintained in large aquaria with recirculating micro-filtered seawater, under normoxic contions and at temperatures between 10–12 °C. They were fed every other day with Artemia spp. nauplii.

We evaluated the relationship between routine metabolic rate and oxygen partial pressure at two temperatures (11 and 21 °C) by constructing metabolic response curves. For each temperature, individuals were incubated at eight initial dissolved oxygen (DO) levels (0.7, 1.4, 2.9, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5, 6.5, and ~ 8 mg O2, corresponding to a range of 1.5 to 24.3 kPa). Juveniles were first individually transferred to 1 L Schott bottles and maintained under normoxic conditions without feeding for 24 h prior to the experiment. Experimental bottles were prepared with water adjusted to the target temperature and DO levels, and the previously separated juveniles were individually introduced into the bottles one hour before the start of the measurements. Oxygen concentration was determined at the beginning and after 5 h of incubation. Six replicates and six controls (without juveniles) were included for each treatment. DO treatments were achieved by bubbling seawater with N2 gas. Temperature was maintained using a thermostatic bath in which bottles were fully submerged. Initial and final DO concentrations were measured using an optical sensor connected to a Microx TX3 AOT oxygen meter (PreSens, GmbH, Germany), and oxygen consumption was calculated accordingly. At the end of each experiment, individuals were weighed, immediately frozen, and stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analyses.

This experimental design was used as an alternative methodological approach to the classical critical oxygen tension (Pcrit), which requires continuous oxygen consumption measurements in individual organisms exposed to progressive hypoxia. In our case, routine metabolic rate was measured in different individuals exposed to fixed oxygen concentrations, allowing us to detect changes in the slope of the kPa–oxygen consumption relationship. These inflection points can be interpreted as a proxy for the physiological oxygen tolerance threshold, which is particularly useful when standard Pcrit protocols are not feasible.

Postprandial metabolism (peak, time, scope and SDA, details in Supplementary Material) was determined at four DO levels (0.7, 1.4, 2.9 and ~ 8 mg O2 L−1, equivalent to 0.5, 1, 2 mL L−1 and normoxia, respectively) for three temperatures (11 °C, 15 °C, and 21 °C). As in the RMR experiments, juveniles were individually reared and fasted for 24 h prior to the trials. One hour before the start of the experiment, they were transferred into 100 mL Schott bottles connected to the respirometry system, where they remained unfed until the feeding event began. They were then fed for 30 min with freshly hatched Artemia nauplii (~ 1500 nauplii/100 mL). Non-consumed nauplii were removed after the 30 min feeding period. Juveniles were kept individually in Schott bottles (100 mL) connected to an open system, where the input is connected to peristaltic pumps (maintaining constant flow: ~ 0.19 L h-1) and the output connected to a Sensor Dish Reader SDR (PreSens, GmbH, Germany) of 24 sensor spot (recording DO every 15 s). Postprandial metabolism was measured for 24 h. The DO treatments were achieved by bubbling seawater with nitrogen, while the temperature was maintained by fully submerging the bottles in a thermoregulated bath. Each day, measurements were performed for two DO concentrations at a single temperature. Thereafter, each individual was weighed and immediately frozen and stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analyses.

To characterize the postprandial metabolism, oxygen consumption curves were constructed (mg O2 per g WW−1) as a function of time, for each combined treatment of temperature-DO. As described by Secor (2009)53, five descriptors of postprandial metabolism were extracted from each curve: (i) Routine metabolic rate (RMR), (ii) SDA Peak, (iii) SDA scope, (iv) total SDA (energy), and (v) SDA duration (time).

Abdominal muscle (20 mg) of deep-frozen replicates of juvenils G. monodon from both experimental set-ups were separated for the determination of Lactate dehydrogenase apparent specific activity (LDH activity). The cephalotorax of juveniles (30 mg) frozen after postprandial experiments were used to determine Heat Shock Protein 70 (HSP70) and total protein concentrations, following Brokordt et al. (2015)66. All analyses were carried out using an EPOCH microplate spectrophotometer (BioTeck).

Ecophysiological data analysis

All the analyses described below were conducted in R software (R Core Team, 202465). A generalized additive model (GAM) was fitted to evaluate the relationship between oxygen consumption rate (mg O2 g−1h−1) and oxygen partial pressure (kPa), with temperature (11 °C and 21 °C) included as a categorical predictor, using mgcv package67. The model included a smooth term for oxygen partial pressure interacted with temperature (s(kPa, by = Temperature, k = 6)) and a parametric term for temperature. An additional GAM was then fitted for the subset of data at 21 °C, also using k = 6, to better characterize the shape of the relationship between oxygen availability and metabolic rate under warmer conditions. As an approximation of potential physiological thresholds in metabolic response to oxygen, we calculated the derivatives of the smooth term using the derivatives function from the gratia package68. The first sign change in the derivative curve was identified and used as a proxy for the physiological inflection point, interpreted here as an indirect estimate of the oxygen tolerance threshold. This approach was used as an alternative to the classical method for determining Pcrit, which requires continuous measurements of oxygen consumption in individual organisms under progressively decreasing oxygen conditions. The model and response curve were visualized using ggplot2 package (version 3.5.169), allowing clear representation of smooth terms, 95% confidence intervals, and estimated inflection points. The same procedure (GAM fitting, derivative calculation, and visualization) was applied to evaluate the relationship between apparent specific activity of LDH and oxygen partial pressure (kPa).

The postprandial results were represented as mean ± standard deviation (library Rmisc, version 1.570). Postprandial parameters, LDH activity and HSP70 concentration were compared between treatments (DO/temperature) using factorial two-way ANOVA, followed by a Tukey post-hoc test (HSD Tukey, library agricolae, version 1.2–471). Previously, normal distribution (Normal Q-Q plot) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) were checked (library Car version 2.1–472). The figures were made with the library ggplot2 (version 3.5.169).
