Weed-derived allelochemicals enhance maize performance through multidimensional modulation of growth and stress physiology
Authors: Mukul Machhindra Barwant, Tejasvi Chavan, Mojtaba Kordrostami, Mehdi Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Allelopathic interactions between plants offer promising opportunities for developing sustainable weed management and biostimulant strategies in agriculture. This study evaluated the effects of aqueous leaf extracts from five common weed species (Amaranthus retroflexus, Parthenium hysterophorus, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Cynodon dactylon, and Trichodesma indicum) on maize (Zea mays L.) under field-relevant conditions. Extracts were applied at 5%, 10%, and 20% concentrations using four application methods: seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation. Across treatments, maize germination decreased by 15–55% relative to the control (p ≤ 0.05), while shoot and root lengths were reduced by up to 40% and 50%, respectively. Chlorophyll a and b contents declined by 20–60% in highly inhibitory treatments, whereas moderate treatments maintained pigment levels comparable to the control. Oxidative stress markers increased significantly, with malondialdehyde and electrolyte leakage rising by 30–120% (p ≤ 0.05), accompanied by a 1.5–2.0-fold increase in antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, POD). Proline content increased by up to 115%, while abscisic acid (ABA) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) levels increased by 2–fourfold in several treatments, indicating coordinated stress and growth regulation. Multivariate analyses (PCA, clustering, and radar profiling) identified Trichodesma 20% foliar spray, Parthenium 10% seed soaking, and Alternanthera 5% rhizosphere simulation as the most effective treatments, exhibiting improved stress tolerance and partial growth maintenance. These findings demonstrate that weed-derived allelochemicals can exert both inhibitory and adaptive effects on maize, highlighting their potential utility in eco-friendly crop management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54476-2.

Introduction

Allelopathy is a biological phenomenon wherein certain plants release bioactive chemicals, known as allelochemicals, into their environment. These substances have the potential to influence a wide range of physiological processes in neighboring plant species, affecting their germination, growth, and reproductive success1,2. Historically recognized and utilized in traditional agriculture, allelopathy has gained renewed attention in modern crop management due to the rising demand for sustainable and eco-friendly agricultural practices3–5. As synthetic herbicides and other chemical inputs face growing scrutiny over environmental and public health concerns, understanding allelopathic interactions offers a promising avenue for developing natural and less invasive strategies for weed control and crop enhancement6,7.

Among cereal crops, maize (Zea mays L.) stands out as one of the most important globally, serving as a key source of food for humans, animal feed, and industrial raw materials8. Due to its high yield potential, nutritional value, and adaptability, maize production has a direct impact on global food security and economic stability. However, like many crops, maize productivity is often compromised by the presence of weeds. Weeds not only compete with crops for vital resources—such as nutrients, water, sunlight, and space—but can also exert allelopathic pressures, thereby intensifying their harmful impact9–11.

Numerous weed species are known to produce allelochemicals that can inhibit crop growth and development, yet our understanding remains limited regarding how specific, commonly encountered weeds influence maize physiology through allelopathy, particularly under realistic field conditions rather than in controlled laboratory setups12,6). While several studies have focused on direct competition between weeds and crops, fewer have examined the subtler yet potentially more insidious effects of allelochemicals on maize growth parameters. These knowledge gaps hinder the development of integrated weed management strategies that optimize resource use efficiency, reduce reliance on chemical herbicides, and maintain ecological balance7.

Addressing this gap is crucial, as allelopathic weed management has the potential to lead to more sustainable agricultural systems. By exploiting natural plant-plant interactions, farmers and agronomists can devise weed control methods that are more environmentally benign, cost-effective, and compatible with integrated pest management frameworks13,14. Such an approach aligns with global trends toward reducing chemical inputs, preserving soil health, and promoting biodiversity in agroecosystems3.

The weed species selected for this study—Amaranthus retroflexus, Parthenium hysterophorus, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Cynodon dactylon, and Trichodesma indicum—are among the most prevalent and competitive weeds in maize-growing regions of South Asia and other subtropical agroecosystems15. These species are frequently associated with yield losses in cereal crops due to their aggressive growth habits, persistence in cultivated fields, and capacity to interfere with crop establishment16. Importantly, several of these weeds have been previously reported to exhibit allelopathic or phytotoxic activity. For example, Parthenium hysterophorus is well known for producing sesquiterpene lactones such as parthenin that inhibit germination and seedling growth of multiple crops17, while Cynodon dactylon and Amaranthus species have been reported to release phenolic compounds capable of altering crop growth and physiological processes18,19. Alternanthera species have also demonstrated inhibitory effects on seed germination and early seedling development20, whereas Trichodesma indicum, despite its widespread occurrence, remains comparatively underexplored with respect to its allelopathic potential21.

Although these species have been individually examined in laboratory or pot-based studies, comparative evaluations under field-relevant exposure routes and across multiple physiological, biochemical, and hormonal traits remain limited, particularly in maize. The present study was therefore designed to address this gap by systematically comparing the allelopathic effects of these dominant weeds using multiple extract concentrations and application modes, thereby providing a more integrative and agronomically relevant assessment of weed–crop chemical interactions.

The present study aims to improve our understanding of allelopathic interactions between weeds and maize. We focused on five common and agriculturally significant weed species—Amaranthus retroflexus, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Cynodon dactylon, Trichodesma indicum, and Parthenium hysterophorus. By applying aqueous extracts from these weeds to maize seeds and monitoring the resulting growth and biochemical parameters under field conditions, we aimed to determine the extent to which allelochemicals affect maize performance. Specifically, we hypothesize that exposure to these weed extracts will reduce maize germination rates, alter root-shoot balance, limit leaf expansion and biomass accumulation, and ultimately lower yield potential.

This research not only advances our understanding of allelopathic weed-crop interactions but also provides practical insights for integrating allelopathy into weed management programs. The findings from this study may guide the development of biologically based strategies that enhance maize resilience, improve resource utilization, and contribute to a more sustainable agricultural landscape.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Maize (Zea mays L.) seeds used in this study were of the hybrid variety PDKV Arambh (BMH-18-2), a widely cultivated medium-maturity hybrid recommended for the peninsular region, including Maharashtra, under rain-fed and irrigated conditions. The seeds were procured from the local agricultural supplier in Kopargaon. This hybrid was chosen due to its local adaptability, uniform germination, and relevance to maize cropping systems in the study area. All experiments were conducted using seeds from the same seed lot to ensure consistency across treatments and the control.

Soil and substrate characteristics

Maize was grown in a uniform field soil collected from the experimental site at Sanjivani Arts, Commerce and Science College, Kopargaon, in the Ahmednagar district of Maharashtra, India. Soils in this region predominantly consist of black cotton and alluvial-derived materials, with textures ranging from sandy loam to clay loam, and moderate organic matter content and water retention capacity typical of fertile agricultural soils in the Godavari basin. Such soils are generally neutral to slightly alkaline in reaction (pH ~7.0–8.5) under natural conditions in the Ahmednagar and surrounding Rahata–Kopargaon area, and exhibit moderate electrical conductivity and organic carbon content typical for rain-fed cereals. The same soil substrate was used consistently across all treatments and the control, minimizing variability due to soil properties.

Experimental design and plant material

This study was conducted to evaluate the allelopathic effects of five dominant weed species—Amaranthus retroflexus, Parthenium hysterophorus, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Cynodon dactylon, and Trichodesma indicum—on maize (Zea mays L.) seed germination and early seedling growth. All weed species were taxonomically identified using standard regional floras, and scientific names were verified against accepted nomenclature to ensure consistency throughout the manuscript. The experiment followed a completely randomized factorial design, incorporating multiple treatment factors to ensure a comprehensive assessment. The design included five weed species, three aqueous extract concentrations (5%, 10%, and 20% w/v), and four distinct modes of application (seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation), along with a non-treated control group. Each treatment combination was replicated five times, resulting in 305 individual experimental units (5 species × 3 concentrations × 4 methods + 1 control) × 5 replicates. Maize seeds used in the study were sourced from a widely cultivated local hybrid known for its consistent germination and moderate stress tolerance under subtropical conditions. All treatments and control plants received the same irrigation regime, and soil moisture was maintained uniformly throughout the experiment to minimize irrigation-driven differences in allelochemical leaching or accumulation. While irrigation volume was not quantified instrumentally, watering was applied consistently to keep the substrate adequately moist and to avoid visible waterlogging or excessive drainage.

Preparation of aqueous weed extracts

Fresh, mature leaves of the five weed species were collected from the university’s experimental field, thoroughly washed under running tap water, and then shade-dried at room temperature (approximately 25°C) for 5–7 days until completely desiccated. The dried material was ground into a fine powder using an electric grinder. To prepare the aqueous extracts, 50, 100, or 200 grams of leaf powder were mixed with 1 liter of distilled water to obtain extract concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 20% (w/v), respectively. Each mixture was left to soak at ambient temperature for 24 hours with occasional stirring to ensure uniform extraction. The mixtures were then filtered first through double-layered muslin cloth and subsequently through Whatman No. 1 filter paper to obtain clear filtrates. Extracts were stored in sterilized containers at 4°C and used within 48 hours to preserve allelochemical stability and prevent microbial degradation.

Application methods

Four distinct application methods were employed to simulate different routes of allelopathic exposure under field-relevant conditions: seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation. Each method was designed to represent a specific mode through which weed-derived allelochemicals may interact with maize plants in agroecosystems.

Seed soaking

Maize seeds were surface-sterilized using 1% sodium hypochlorite for 2 min and thoroughly rinsed with sterile distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then soaked in the respective aqueous weed extracts (5%, 10%, or 20%) for 4 h at room temperature. After soaking, seeds were immediately sown in germination trays containing sterile, moistened sand.

Soil incorporation

Air-dried and finely ground weed leaf residues were mixed uniformly with topsoil at a rate of 1% (w/w). The amended soil was moistened and incubated for 24 h to allow the release of allelochemicals before sowing maize seeds. Control soil received no weed residues.

Foliar spray

Maize seedlings were grown until the emergence of the third true leaf. At this stage, aqueous weed extracts at the designated concentrations were applied uniformly to the foliage using a hand-held sprayer. Foliar applications were conducted every two days for a total of three sprays, ensuring complete leaf surface coverage while avoiding runoff.

Rhizosphere simulation

To simulate belowground allelopathic interactions, maize and weed seedlings were grown in the same container but separated by a nylon mesh barrier that prevented direct root contact while allowing chemical diffusion. In addition, 20 mL of freshly prepared weed root leachates were applied directly to the maize root zone at three-day intervals to mimic continuous allelochemical exposure under natural field conditions. During rhizosphere simulation, irrigation was applied carefully to maintain uniform moisture while minimizing drainage, thereby reducing the likelihood of allelochemical leaching from the root zone.

Growth and physiological trait measurement

Germination and seedling growth parameters were recorded at 7 and 14 days after sowing (DAS). Germination percentage was calculated based on the number of germinated seeds out of the total sown. Root length and shoot length were measured using a ruler from the base of the stem to the root tip and the shoot apex, respectively. The number of leaves per plant was counted manually, and total biomass was recorded by drying the seedlings at 70°C for 48 hours and weighing them to the nearest 0.01 g using an analytical balance. Chlorophyll a and b contents were quantified following Arnon’s method (1949), which involves extraction in 80% acetone and spectrophotometric measurement at 645 and 663 nm, respectively. Oxidative stress was assessed by determining malondialdehyde (MDA) levels via the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction, and electrolyte leakage was measured by immersing leaf discs in deionized water, recording initial and final conductivity, and calculating percentage leakage.

Biochemical and hormonal analysis

Antioxidant enzyme activities were determined using spectrophotometric assays. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assayed based on its ability to inhibit the photoreduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT), catalase (CAT) activity was measured by monitoring the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide at 240 nm, and peroxidase (POD) activity was quantified using guaiacol as a substrate. Proline content was determined following the acid ninhydrin method described by Bates et al. (1973), where the absorbance of the toluene-extracted chromophore was read at 520 nm. Endogenous phytohormone levels—abscisic acid (ABA) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)—were measured using ELISA-based quantification kits (Agdia®, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with values expressed as ng/g fresh weight (FW).

Statistical and EC₅₀ analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R statistical software (R Core Team, version 4.x). Data were subjected to factorial analysis of variance (ANOVA), and treatment means were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering, were conducted using appropriate R packages. EC₅₀ (effective concentration for 50% inhibition of germination) values were calculated using a non-linear logistic regression model fitted to germination data across concentrations for each weed and method combination. The logistic function used was of the form y = c / (1 + exp(-a(x - b))), where b represents the EC₅₀ value. Results

Statistical overview and treatment effects

Factorial analysis of variance revealed that weed species, extract concentration, and application method significantly influenced maize germination, growth, physiological, biochemical, and hormonal traits (Supplementary Table S1). Significant main effects of weed species were observed for all measured traits (p ≤ 0.001), indicating species-specific allelopathic responses. Extract concentration exerted a strong dose-dependent effect on most traits, particularly germination, root and shoot growth, biomass accumulation, chlorophyll content, oxidative stress markers, antioxidant enzyme activities, osmolyte accumulation, and endogenous hormone levels. Application method also significantly affected maize responses, highlighting differences in allelochemical exposure dynamics among seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation treatments.

In addition to main effects, significant two-way and three-way interactions among weed species, concentration, and application method were detected for all traits (Supplementary Table S1), demonstrating that the magnitude and direction of allelopathic effects depended on specific combinations of these factors. These interactions underscore the complexity of maize responses to weed-derived allelochemicals and justify the use of multivariate approaches to integrate trait-level responses.

Treatment-wise means (± SE) for all growth, physiological, biochemical, and hormonal parameters are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Across treatments, maize germination and early growth were generally reduced with increasing extract concentration, although the extent of inhibition varied markedly among weed species and application methods. Moderate treatments maintained relatively higher growth and biomass accumulation compared with high-concentration treatments, consistent with the observed interaction effects. Changes in chlorophyll content, oxidative stress indicators, antioxidant enzyme activities, proline accumulation, and hormone levels further reflected treatment-specific stress and adaptive responses.

Estimated EC₅₀ values for maize germination inhibition differed among weed species and application methods (Supplementary Table S3), indicating variation in allelopathic potency and exposure efficiency. Lower EC₅₀ values were generally associated with application methods that facilitated greater contact between allelochemicals and the germinating seeds or root zone, whereas higher EC₅₀ values reflected comparatively weaker inhibitory effects under certain exposure routes. Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), heatmaps, and hierarchical clustering, were employed to visualize overall response patterns and relationships among traits and treatments. These analyses are presented as exploratory tools and are supported by the statistically significant differences identified through ANOVA and DMRT (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Effects of weed extract treatments on germination and early growth traits of maize

The allelopathic influence of various weed leaf aqueous extracts—Amaranthus, Parthenium, Alternanthera, Cynodon, and Trichodesma—at concentrations of 5%, 10%, and 20% under four application methods (seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation) was assessed through five key traits (Fig. 2): germination percentage, root length, shoot length, leaf number, and total biomass. The control treatment (no extract) consistently exhibited the highest values across all measured parameters, with a germination rate of 92.48%, root and shoot lengths of 11.79 cm and 18.97 cm, respectively, a leaf count of 6.76, and the maximum total biomass at 3.41 g. In comparison, all allelopathic treatments significantly reduced maize performance, although the extent of this reduction varied by weed species, extract concentration, and method of application. Among the Amaranthus treatments, the 10% foliar spray yielded a relatively high root length (11.27 cm) and moderate shoot elongation (12.85 cm), but biomass remained suppressed. The 20% foliar spray, in contrast, significantly improved shoot length (16.29 cm), albeit with lower germination and biomass, suggesting potential stimulatory effects on shoot growth under moderate allelopathic stress. Parthenium extract treatments displayed inconsistent but notable trends. The 10% soil incorporation yielded high germination (71.53%) and a relatively long shoot (14.83 cm), despite having the lowest root length (4.63 cm), suggesting a possible shift in root-to-shoot allocation. The 5% foliar spray had a positive impact on biomass (2.34 g), while 20% rhizosphere simulation maintained a balance between root growth and biomass (10.57 cm and 2.35 g respectively), indicating resilience to phytotoxic stress under simulated field conditions. In the case of Alternanthera, the 5% rhizosphere simulation treatment showed the highest germination among all weed treatments (76.49%), accompanied by substantial root length (11.01 cm) and biomass (2.32 g), suggesting that lower concentrations may stimulate early vigor. Conversely, 20% foliar spray sharply reduced germination (45.56%) but did not greatly suppress shoot growth (12.6 cm), implying selective inhibitory effects. Cynodon treatments generally showed a concentration-dependent response. The 5% soil incorporation treatment enhanced root length (10.85 cm) and biomass (2.41 g), comparable to the control, while the 20% foliar spray treatment maintained impressive shoot elongation (16.01 cm) and the highest biomass among all treatments (2.85 g). However, germination was compromised, indicating delayed or selective seedling development. Trichodesma extracts, particularly at 5% concentration, demonstrated the most balanced response. Foliar application at this level yielded long shoots (16.04 cm) and high biomass (2.91 g), while rhizosphere simulation and seed soaking also supported root growth (8.01 cm and 7.43 cm) and germination (>60%). At 20%, seed soaking and foliar spray treatments still maintained appreciable shoot lengths (13.44 and 11.78 cm respectively) with biomass values above 2.3 g, highlighting potential biostimulant effects at higher doses under certain application modes. Overall, the most promising treatments combining high biomass and shoot elongation included Trichodesma 5% foliar spray, Cynodon 20% foliar spray, and Alternanthera 5% rhizosphere simulation. These findings suggest that certain allelopathic interactions, especially under foliar or rhizospheric exposure, may stimulate rather than inhibit plant growth depending on concentration and weed species.

Oxidative damage and membrane stability under weed extract treatments

The evaluation of oxidative stress indicators—malondialdehyde (MDA) content and electrolyte leakage—revealed significant variation across treatments involving aqueous extracts of five weed species applied at different concentrations and through diverse methods (Fig. 4). The control plants exhibited the lowest levels of MDA (2.65 nmol/g FW) and electrolyte leakage (14.06%), indicating optimal membrane integrity and minimal oxidative damage under normal conditions. Among all treatments, the highest MDA content was recorded in plants treated with Trichodesma 10% rhizosphere simulation (5.67 nmol/g FW), followed closely by Cynodon 10% seed soaking (5.33 nmol/g FW) and Trichodesma 20% seed soaking (5.13 nmol/g FW). These elevated values suggest enhanced lipid peroxidation and oxidative stress. In contrast, several treatments exhibited MDA levels comparable to or slightly higher than the control, such as Parthenium 10% seed soaking (2.26 nmol/g FW) and Alternanthera 5% seed soaking (2.93 nmol/g FW), suggesting minimal membrane damage. Electrolyte leakage—a key indicator of membrane disruption—was significantly higher in several treatments. The greatest leakage was observed in Amaranthus 10% foliar spray (44.32%), Parthenium 20% seed soaking (42.46%), and Trichodesma 20% seed soaking (40.98%). These findings highlight the deleterious impact of high-concentration allelopathic extracts on membrane permeability. Hormonal and osmotic adjustments in response to allelopathic stress

To assess the physiological and biochemical responses of maize to weed-derived allelopathic stress, the levels of proline (as a key osmoprotectant), abscisic acid (ABA, a stress-responsive hormone), and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, a key growth regulator) were analyzed across diverse treatments involving extracts from five weed species (Amaranthus, Parthenium, Alternanthera, Cynodon, and Trichodesma) (Fig. 6). The treatments varied by extract concentration (5%, 10%, and 20%) and mode of application (seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation), offering a detailed view of plant adaptive mechanisms under chemically induced stress. A marked elevation in proline content was observed in almost all treatments compared to the control (5.12 μmol/g FW), reflecting the initiation of osmotic stress signaling and protective metabolic pathways. The highest proline level was observed in plants treated with Trichodesma 5% via soil incorporation (11.06 μmol/g FW), suggesting potent allelochemical stimulation of osmolyte biosynthesis. Other treatments that strongly induced proline accumulation included Parthenium 5% rhizosphere simulation (10.62 μmol/g FW) and Parthenium 10% soil incorporation (10.09 μmol/g FW). Interestingly, several low-concentration treatments performed comparably or better than their higher-concentration counterparts, hinting at a hormetic effect of allelochemicals, where mild stress promotes adaptive benefits. ABA levels were significantly influenced by both the weed species and the mode of application. Control plants showed an ABA level of 14.26 ng/g FW, while treatments led to sharp increases. The Alternanthera 10% foliar spray recorded the highest ABA level (60.47 ng/g FW), implying a pronounced activation of stress-related signaling pathways. Close behind were Trichodesma 20% rhizosphere simulation (51.46 ng/g FW) and Amaranthus 5% rhizosphere simulation (51.39 ng/g FW). Foliar spray and rhizosphere simulation frequently induced higher ABA levels than seed soaking, indicating that surface-level and root-zone exposure to allelochemicals may more efficiently trigger water-deficit-like hormonal cascades. The auxin hormone IAA, critical for cell division and elongation, also displayed considerable fluctuation. While the control maintained an IAA level of 23.10 ng/g FW, several treatments resulted in dramatic increases. The most pronounced IAA accumulation was noted in Parthenium 5% seed soaking (82.82 ng/g FW), followed by Amaranthus 20% foliar spray (73.76 ng/g FW) and Cynodon 5% rhizosphere simulation (71.57 ng/g FW). EC₅₀ determination of weed extracts on maize germination

To assess the allelopathic potential of the selected weed species, a dose-response analysis was conducted using germination percentage as the primary response variable (Fig. 7). The effective concentration required to inhibit 50% of seed germination (EC₅₀) was calculated for each weed species using a sigmoidal logistic regression model fitted to the observed germination data across extract concentrations. The calculated EC₅₀ values revealed differential allelopathic intensities among the five weed species tested. Notably, Alternanthera exhibited the strongest allelopathic effect with the lowest EC₅₀ value of 2.25%, indicating that even low extract concentrations substantially suppressed maize germination. Parthenium followed closely, with an EC₅₀ of 2.40%, which aligns with its well-documented allelopathic aggressiveness through the release of sesquiterpene lactones like parthenin. Cynodon and Amaranthus showed relatively moderate allelopathic effects, with EC₅₀ values of 2.44% and 3.23%, respectively. These results suggest a somewhat milder inhibition of maize germination by these species under similar extract concentrations. The control (distilled water) was included as the non-inhibitory reference treatment for germination comparisons; EC₅₀ estimation was performed only for weed extracts. This reference point enabled consistent curve fitting across treatments. The dose-response trends exhibited characteristic sigmoidal curves, wherein germination decreased non-linearly with increasing extract concentration. This pattern underscores a concentration-dependent suppression, suggesting a potential threshold beyond which allelochemical toxicity sharply impairs seed viability. The derived EC₅₀ values offer a quantitative basis for comparing allelopathic potency, with Alternanthera and Parthenium emerging as the most phytotoxic among the tested weeds. These insights provide critical guidance for the development of allelopathic weed management strategies and for identifying species whose residues may pose a risk to crop establishment when incorporated into soil.Fig. 7Heatmap representation of EC₅₀ values for maize germination under allelopathic influence of different weed species and application methods. It is important to note that EC₅₀ values were estimated from fitted dose–response models and therefore represent the overall concentration–response behavior of each weed extract across the tested concentrations. In contrast, germination percentages reported for individual treatments correspond to single-point means at a specific concentration and application method. Because exposure intensity and allelochemical availability differ among application routes (seed soaking vs. soil incorporation vs. rhizosphere simulation vs. foliar spray), germination at a given concentration may not strictly follow the potency ranking suggested by EC₅₀ values. Additionally, non-linear responses, including mild stimulation at low concentrations (hormesis), can further contribute to apparent discrepancies between model-based EC₅₀ estimates and individual treatment means.

Discussion

The present study provides an in-depth examination of the allelopathic effects of aqueous extracts from five dominant weed species—Amaranthus retroflexus, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Cynodon dactylon, Trichodesma indicum, and Parthenium hysterophorus—on the growth and physiological traits of maize (Zea mays L.). Through a factorial design encompassing multiple application modes (seed soaking, soil incorporation, foliar spray, and rhizosphere simulation) and extract concentrations, a comprehensive array of traits was analyzed to evaluate the influence of allelopathic stress from morphological, physiological, biochemical, and molecular viewpoints. It is acknowledged that irrigation regime can influence allelochemical concentration in the rhizosphere through dilution and leaching22. However, because irrigation was applied uniformly across all treatments and the control, the observed relative differences among treatments primarily reflect weed extract effects rather than irrigation variability.

Germination percentage and vigor were significantly reduced in allelopathically treated maize, with values falling from 92.48% in the control to below 60% in some treatments. This aligns with reports from2 and12, indicating that allelochemicals such as phenolics and terpenoids may inhibit water imbibition or disrupt cellular respiration and mitosis, thereby affecting early developmental events. Shoot and root lengths were also adversely affected. Control plants reached up to 18.97 cm in shoot length and 11.79 cm in root length, while many treated groups fell below 15 cm and 8 cm, respectively. The reduction in root-shoot development could be attributed to disrupted auxin signaling or oxidative imbalance, reducing cellular elongation and division3,23. Consequently, leaf number and total biomass also declined. Treated plants often produced 2–3 fewer leaves and up to 50% less biomass than the control, reaffirming the view that allelochemicals impede the source–sink balance and resource allocation essential for leaf development and biomass accrual10,9.

The apparent mismatch between some single-treatment germination values and EC₅₀ rankings reflects differences between point estimates and curve-derived potency metrics. EC₅₀ integrates the overall response trend across concentrations24 while individual treatment germination outcomes are influenced by method-specific exposure kinetics (e.g., duration of contact, adsorption/desorption in soil, dilution effects, and diffusion in the rhizosphere)25. Therefore, a treatment showing comparatively higher germination at one concentration does not necessarily contradict a lower EC₅₀ value for that weed species when the full concentration–response relationship is considered.

The photosynthetic machinery was notably impaired, as reflected in reduced chlorophyll a and b levels. Control plants maintained chlorophyll a and b contents of 1.28 mg/g and 0.53 mg/g, respectively, which dropped to as low as 0.61 and 0.18 mg/g in treated groups. This loss is likely caused by allelochemical-induced inhibition of chlorophyll biosynthesis enzymes or increased degradation via oxidative pathways, as suggested by26 and27. Since chlorophyll pigments are critical for harvesting light energy, their reduction corresponds to lower carbon assimilation, directly impairing plant growth and productivity28,29.

Biochemical stress was evident in the elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) content and electrolyte leakage among treated plants. MDA levels rose from 2.65 nmol/g in the control to above 4.8 nmol/g in some treatments, indicating lipid peroxidation of membrane structures under oxidative stress. Similarly, electrolyte leakage exceeded 35% in certain treatments compared to 14.06% in the control, a sign of compromised membrane integrity. These findings are consistent with3, who explained that reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation under allelopathic stress damages lipid bilayers and protein channels, weakening cellular homeostasis. Interestingly, antioxidant enzyme activities such as SOD, CAT, and POD were elevated in allelopathically stressed plants. For instance, CAT activity rose from 95.37 to over 170 U/mg in many treatments. This enhancement represents a defensive upregulation in response to ROS overproduction. The antioxidant system, though active, was insufficient to counterbalance the oxidative damage entirely, as evidenced by persistently high MDA and leakage levels30. These enzyme trends also suggest that maize plants attempt to mitigate stress via induced antioxidative defenses, aligning with prior observations by31,32 on sorghum allelopathy.

Although enhanced oxidative stress is commonly associated with increased activity of antioxidant enzymes, the relationship between reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and enzymatic defense is not strictly linear33. In the present study, antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, CAT, and POD were generally elevated under allelopathic treatments, particularly at moderate extract concentrations, indicating activation of ROS-scavenging mechanisms. However, treatments associated with high malondialdehyde accumulation and electrolyte leakage did not always exhibit proportionally higher enzyme activities. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the intensity and duration of allelopathic stress. Under severe stress conditions, excessive ROS production may overwhelm the antioxidant defense system, leading to membrane damage despite partial enzymatic activation34. In such cases, antioxidant enzymes may become functionally insufficient or metabolically constrained, resulting in elevated oxidative damage markers even in the presence of increased enzyme activity35. Moreover, allelopathic compounds are known to interfere with protein synthesis, enzyme stability, and redox homeostasis, which may further limit the effectiveness of antioxidant defenses under high-stress regimes36. Similar non-linear antioxidant responses under allelopathic or abiotic stress have been reported in previous studies, where moderate stress induced enzymatic protection, while severe stress led to oxidative injury despite defense activation37. Variability between root and shoot growth and total biomass accumulation can also be attributed to stress-induced alterations in carbon allocation and growth efficiency38. While shoot or root elongation may be partially maintained under certain allelopathic treatments—often driven by hormonal adjustments such as increased IAA—biomass accumulation depends on sustained photosynthetic efficiency, assimilate partitioning, and cellular integrity39. Reduced chlorophyll content, increased membrane damage, and elevated ABA levels observed in several treatments likely constrained carbon assimilation and dry matter deposition, resulting in lower biomass even when elongation growth appeared relatively less affected40. These findings highlight that under allelopathic stress, morphological growth parameters alone may not reliably predict biomass production41.

An important observation in this study was the simultaneous increase in abscisic acid (ABA) and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) under several allelopathic treatments. While ABA is classically associated with stress perception and growth inhibition42 and IAA with cell elongation and growth promotion43 their concurrent elevation suggests a coordinated hormonal adjustment rather than antagonistic signaling. Under allelopathic stress, increased ABA likely functions as a primary stress signal, regulating stomatal behavior, redox balance, and stress-responsive gene expression44. At the same time, elevated IAA may contribute to the maintenance of root and shoot elongation, enabling partial growth continuity despite stress conditions45. This dual hormonal response may reflect an adaptive strategy in which maize balances stress tolerance with growth plasticity, allowing limited development while activating protective mechanisms. Similar co-regulation of ABA and auxin has been reported under various abiotic and chemical stress conditions, where growth is modulated rather than completely suppressed. The observed ABA–IAA interplay therefore underscores the complexity of hormonal cross-talk governing maize responses to weed-derived allelochemicals.

At the molecular level, the rise in proline content—up to 8.88 μmol/g FW in treated plants versus 5.12 in controls—indicates an osmoprotective response. Proline accumulation serves not only to stabilize proteins and membranes but also acts as a molecular signal modulating stress-responsive gene expression5,12. In parallel, endogenous hormone levels showed marked alterations. Abscisic acid (ABA), a key stress hormone, increased significantly in treated groups (up to 51.39 ng/g FW), possibly triggering stomatal closure and enhancing stress tolerance pathways. Conversely, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), crucial for growth and elongation, fluctuated across treatments but generally declined in low-performing treatments, suggesting a suppression of growth-promoting signals due to allelochemical interference with auxin biosynthesis or transport. These hormonal dynamics align with studies by Inderjit & Duke1 and Gulzar (2016), which proposed that allelopathy-induced hormone imbalance is a central mechanism in growth inhibition. The increase in ABA may be a downstream consequence of chlorophyll degradation, ROS generation, and nutrient stress—an integrated stress signature observed in many allelopathic systems.

Radar chart and PCA analysis clearly grouped the best-performing treatments (e.g., Amaranthus 5% - Soil Incorporation, Alternanthera 10% - Seed Soaking) with the control, confirming their relatively lower allelopathic potential. These treatments retained higher chlorophyll and hormone balance, lower MDA and leakage, and improved shoot-root development. In contrast, treatments such as Parthenium 20% - Rhizosphere Simulation showed the most significant reductions across multiple traits. Cluster analysis reinforced this categorization, indicating clear phenotypic divergence driven by the weed species, extract concentration, and mode of application.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This study provides compelling evidence that aqueous extracts from five common weeds—Amaranthus retroflexus, Parthenium hysterophorus, Alternanthera philoxeroides, Cynodon dactylon, and Trichodesma indicum—exert significant allelopathic effects on maize, modulating a broad spectrum of morphophysiological, biochemical, and hormonal traits. The factorial design encompassing multiple concentrations and modes of application revealed that certain treatments, particularly those involving Parthenium 10–20%, Alternanthera 10–20%, and Trichodesma 10–20%, led to notable increases in antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, POD), phytohormone levels (ABA, IAA), and stress markers (proline), while maintaining or enhancing growth parameters such as shoot length, biomass, and chlorophyll content. Multivariate analyses including PCA, cluster analysis, and radar profiling collectively highlighted the ability of specific weed extracts to act as bioelicitors rather than mere stress agents. The integrative insights gained here not only deepen our understanding of weed-plant interactions but also open promising avenues for sustainable crop management through the use of plant-derived biostimulants. Further field-scale evaluations and mechanistic studies will be essential to validate these findings and develop eco-compatible formulations for broader agricultural use.
