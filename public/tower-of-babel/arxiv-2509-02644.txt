Needlets and foreground removal for SKAO hydrogen intensity maps
Carmelita Carbone

Abstract
Intensity Mapping (IM) of the 21-cm line of the neutral hydrogen ( Hi ) has become a compelling new technique to map the large-scale structure of the Universe. One of the main challenges is the presence of strong foreground emissions of several orders of magnitude larger than the Hi signal. Here, we implement a version of the Principal Component Analysis, a blind component-separation technique, based on a kind of spherical wavelets called needlets. These functions exploit double localization both in real and in harmonic space. We test Need-PCA performances on a set of maps that simulates the SKA MID radio telescope in the AA4 configuration. We compare our results with other component separation methods such as Generalised Morphological Component Analysis (GMCA) and Generalized Needlet Internal Linear Combination (GNILC). All the methods have comparable results, recovering the Hi signal within 10% accuracy across the frequency channels, in the multipole range 30 ≲ ℓ ≲ \lesssim\ell\lesssim 136. We also test our pipeline in the presence of systematics such as polarization leakage. We find that the cleaning methods are insensitive to the presence of such systematic, yielding the same results as in the leakage-free case.

2
Foreground cleaning methods

In this section we review some of the most used and studied methods for the 21-cm foreground cleaning. All methods rely on the assumptions that the foreground emissions are slowly varying with frequency, i.e. they are highly correlated, and are order of magnitude larger than the
Hi
signal, which, in contrast, is uncorrelated in frequency. Thus, we can identify the dominant foreground contributions as a set of smooth functions of frequency to be subtracted from the data in order to isolate the
Hi
signal. We start by defining the sky brightness temperature along a given direction in the sky
\hat{\bm{n}}
and at frequency
\nu
as
[
36
]

T(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})=\sum^{N_{\rm fg}}_{k=1}f_{k}(\nu)S_{k}(\hat{\bm{n}})+T_{\rm cosmo}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})+T_{\rm noise}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})\,,

(2.1)

where
N_{\rm fg}
is the number of foreground sources to subtract,
f_{k}(\nu)
are the smooth functions of frequency,
S_{k}(\hat{\bm{n}})
are the foreground sky maps,
T_{\rm cosmo}
is the cosmological signal and
T_{\rm noise}
is the instrumental noise contribution. Thus, Eq. (
2.1
) can be written as a set of linear equation computed at
N_{\nu}
frequencies:

\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{A}\mathsf{S}+\mathsf{r}\,,

(2.2)

where
X_{i}=T(\nu_{i},\hat{\bm{n}})
,
A_{ik}=f_{k}(\nu_{i})
, and
r_{i}
is the sum of the contribution from the cosmological signal and the instrumental noise
r_{i}=T_{\rm cosmo}(\nu_{i},\hat{\bm{n}})+T_{\rm noise}(\nu_{i},\hat{\bm{n}})
. Being largely uncorrelated across frequencies, the
Hi
signal and the instrumental noise are coupled and recovered together.

The scope of foreground cleaning is to determine
\mathsf{A}
, which is called the mixing matrix, and
\mathsf{S}
in order to accurately recover the target signal as
\mathsf{r}=\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{A}\mathsf{S}
.

Foreground removal techniques can be categorized as either
blind
, when they rely only on general foreground characteristics such as spectral smoothness, or
non-blind
, when they require a more detailed and specific model of the foregrounds or the cosmological signal.

Principle Component analysis (PCA)

The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is one of the most used
blind
foreground removal techniques. In fact, PCA exploits the main properties of the foregrounds, i.e. the smoothness in frequency and the large amplitude, to compute both the source components
\mathsf{S}
and the mixing matrix
\mathsf{A}
.

It can be demonstrated that the covariance between the frequency channels of a highly correlated data-set has a peculiar eigensystem: most of the information is stored in a few number of very large eigenvalues, called principal components, while the other one are negligibly smaller. Thus, we can attempt to subtract the foreground system from the data-set by looking at the largest N
fg
eigenvalues and eliminating the corresponding eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The PCA algorithm can be summarized as follow:

•

Compute the covariance matrix for different frequency channels by averaging over all the available line of sight, corresponding to the number of pixels N
pix
of the observed temperature map
\mathsf{X}
:

{\mathsf{Cov}_{\rm ch,ch^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\rm N_{pix}}\sum_{i=1}^{\rm N_{\rm pix}}\rm X^{ch^{\prime}}_{i}X^{ch^{\prime}}_{i}};

•

Compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
{\rm Cov}_{\rm ch,\,ch^{\prime}}
and diagonalize the matrix:

\hat{\mathsf{U}}^{T}\mathsf{Cov}\hat{\mathsf{U}}=\hat{\mathsf{\Lambda}}\equiv{\rm diag}(\lambda_{1},\,...\,,\,\lambda_{N_{\nu}})

•

We identify the N
fg
eigenvalues corresponding to the foregrounds which are larger than the rest. We build the matrix
\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg}
which columns are the eigenvectors corresponding to the N
fg
largest eigenvalues, i.e. the principal components. The foreground components
\mathsf{S}
are found by projecting the observations
\mathsf{X}
on
\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg}
, yielding

\mathsf{S}=\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg}^{T}\mathsf{X}

and, combined with Eq. (
2.2
), we estimate the foreground from the data as

\mathsf{X}_{\rm fg}=\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg}\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg}^{T}\mathsf{X}\,.

The cosmological signal is then recovered as:

\mathsf{X}_{\textsc{Hi}}=\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{X}_{\rm fg}\,.

The choice of N
fg
is the key to optimize the estimation of the foreground from the data and thus remove it. As discussed above, due to their spectral properties the foreground emissions can be represented by few number of eigenvalues. On the contrary, the
Hi
cannot be compressed to a few number of components, thus a strong cleaning process, i.e. a large N
fg
, can remove part of the cosmological signal together with the foreground, typically large-scale line of sight modes
[
27
]
. Of course, if N
fg
is too small, fraction of the foreground emission will not be removed, dominating the
Hi
signal. Thus, a fine balance on the choice of the number of components to remove is critical.

In a simulation-based approach, working with pure-
Hi
and pure-foreground maps, we can calculate the contribution of these components left on the residuals after the cleaning process.
This leakage in the residuals are computed by projecting the mixing matrix onto the pure-
Hi
and pure-foreground as

\displaystyle\mathsf{X}_{\rm leak\ fg}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{X}_{\rm fg}-\mathsf{U}_{\rm fg}\mathsf{U}_{\rm fg}^{T}\mathsf{X}_{\rm fg}

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathsf{X}_{\rm leak\ \textsc{Hi}}

\displaystyle=\rm\mathsf{U}_{\rm fg}\mathsf{U}_{\rm fg}^{T}\mathsf{X}_{\textsc{Hi}}\,.

(2.4)

The first equation defines the foreground that leaks into the cosmological signal plus noise, while the second one defines the cosmological plus noise signal that leaks into the estimated foreground. The cleaning process is successful when the power spectra of the foreground and
Hi
plus noise leakage are negligible compared to that of the cosmological signal
[
25
]
.

Generalized Morphological Component Analysis (GMCA)

GMCA is a blind source separation algorithm that assumes that the target components can be represented sparsely in a suitable signal domain, such as the Fourier or wavelet domains
[
37
]
. A signal is considered sparse if most of its coefficients are zero. For instance, periodic signals are typically sparse in the Fourier domain, as they can be described using only a few coefficients. This sparsity assumption is crucial, as it significantly enhances the contrast between different components, facilitating their separation.

GMCA imposes sparsity by solving the following optimization problem
[
25
]
:

\{\tilde{\mathsf{A}},\tilde{\mathsf{S}}\}=\min_{\mathsf{A},\mathsf{S}}\sum^{N_{\rm fg}}_{i=1}\lambda_{i}||\mathsf{S}_{i}||_{1}+||\mathsf{X}-\mathsf{AS}||^{2}_{F}\,,

(2.5)

where the first term is a sparsity constraint term and the second is a data-fidelity term. Indeed,
||\cdot||_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
norm defined by
||\mathsf{Y}||_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|\mathsf{Y}_{i,j}|
. And
||\cdot||_{F}
is the Frobenius norm defined by
||\mathsf{Y}||^{2}_{F}=\mathrm{Trace}(\mathsf{YY^{T}})
. Specifically, the
\lambda_{i}
terms act as regularization coefficients — sparsity-thresholds — that are crucial for ensuring robustness against noise in the problem, which is, in our problem, the difference in intensity between the foregrounds and the cosmological signal. TGMCA initially estimates these coefficients using the median absolute deviation (MAD) method and gradually reduce them to a final level determined by the noise characteristics.

As PCA, GMCA is a
blind
method, as the only input needed is the number of sources N
fg
, while both the mixing matrix
\mathsf{A}
and the sources
\mathsf{S}
are not modelled. In the
Hi
IM context, the wavelet domain has been demonstrated to be optimal for a sparse description of the astrophysical foregrounds
[
25
]
, hence, we perform the analysis also with a wavelet-decomposition of the maps before performing the minimization in Eq. (
2.5
). Originally, the wavelet dictionary used is that of the isotropic undecimated wavelet, also known as starlets due to their success in efficiently describe astrophysical images
[
38
]
. Here, we will perform contaminant separations with GMCA either applied on spherical harmonics- (Standard-GMCA) and needlet-decomposed (Need-GMCA) maps.

Generalized Needlet Internal Linear Combination (GNILC)

The Internal Linear Combination (ILC) method was first used in the analysis of CMB data from the WMAP mission
[
39
]
. It extracts one or more components from observations with known spectral properties by applying a vector of weights
\bm{W}
that enforces a unit response to the desired signal while minimizing the total variance of the residual foregrounds.

The data can be modelled as
[
40
]

\bm{x}(\hat{\bm{n}})=\bm{a}s(\hat{\bm{n}})+\bm{n}(\hat{\bm{n}})\,,

(2.6)

where
\bm{x}(\hat{\bm{n}})
is a vector of observed maps for each frequency channels,
s(\hat{\bm{n}})
is the map of the component of interest,
\bm{a}
is a known mixing vector which scales
s
across the frequency channels. The term
\bm{n}(\hat{\bm{n}})
includes all other components such as noise and foregrounds.
The ILC estimates
s
as a linear combination
\hat{s}=\bm{W}^{T}\bm{x}(\hat{\bm{n}})
. A key strength of the ILC method is that it does not require any assumptions about the foreground models. However, it is limited to recovering components that have a fixed frequency scaling across the sky.

GNILC aims to reconstruct emission which can not be modelled as a single template scaling in a known way with frequency, e.g. the foreground emission. It is an extension of the ILC method into needlet space that compensates for the lack of information on the frequency dependence of the target signal by some
prior
information on its power spectrum
[
29
]
. The needlet decomposition allows to constrain the emission on localized areas of the sky and defined angular space.

GNILC has been successfully applied to CMB data analysis by the Planck collaboration (
[
41
]
) and later extended to 21-cm data analysis by
[
30
]
and the BINGO collaboration
[
42
]
.

The key ingredients for the GNILC algorithm are the theoretical
Hi
21-cm power spectrum across the frequency bins and the number of foreground components, N
fg
. The value of N
fg
is estimated via a constrained PCA using the covariance matrix of the
Hi
signal, evaluated from the theoretical
prior
. This estimation is performed independently at each needlet scale using the Aikake Information Criterion (AIC)
[
43
]
. For each location of the sky and range of needlet scales, the number N
fg
is found by minimizing the AIC,

\rm AIC(N_{\rm fg})=2nN_{\rm fg}-2\log{\mathcal{L}_{max}(N_{\rm fg})}\,,

(2.7)

where
n
is the number of modes in the needlet domain considered and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm max}
is the maximum likelihood solution of the data covariance matrix given a model of N
fg
independent foreground components (see Appendix A of
[
30
]
for more details). Once the number N
fg
is known, one can compute the ILC weight matrix
\mathsf{W}
at each needlet scale and reconstruct the
Hi
signal from the observations.

Gaussian and log-normal simulations

3.1
Cosmological signal

After reionization,
Hi
is distributed inside galaxies, self-shielded from the ultra-violet background
[
44
]
. Thus, the
Hi
distribution is a (biased) tracer of the underlying dark matter field distribution. The simulated
Hi
maps are obtained from the lognormal approximation of the matter field, according to the prescription of
[
28
]
, using the code described therein, CRIME. According to this model, the intensity in an observed frequency bin
\delta\nu
coming from the 21-cm emission of n object at a redshift

with neutral hydrogen mass
M_{\textsc{Hi}}
, subtending a solid angle
\delta\Omega
is given by

I(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})=\frac{3h_{p}A_{12}}{16\pi m_{H}}\frac{1}{((1+z)r(z))^{2}}\frac{M_{\textsc{Hi}}}{\delta_{\nu}\,\delta_{\Omega}}\nu_{21}\,,

(3.1)

where
A_{12}=2.876\times 10^{-15}
Hz is the Einstein coefficient corresponding to the emission from the 21-cm hyperfine transition,
h_{p}
is the Planck’s constant and
m_{H}=1.6733\times 10^{-24}
g is the hydrogen atom mass. Hera,
r(z)
is the comoving curvature distance
r(z)=c\sinh{H_{0} \sqrt{|\Omega_{k}|}\chi(z)c}/(H_{0}\sqrt{|\Omega_{k}|})
and
\chi(z)
is the radial comoving distance
\chi(z)=\int_{0}^{z}\frac{cdz^{\prime}}{H(z^{\prime})}
. We can write the intensity
I(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})
in terms of a black-body temperature in the Rayleigh-Jeans approximation
T=Ic^{2}/(2k_{B}\nu^{2})
, where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann’s constant. From this we can obtain the mean brightness temperature coming from redshift

and its fluctuations in terms of the neutral hydrogen density:

T_{21}(z,\hat{\bm{n}})=(190.55~\mathrm{mK})\frac{\Omega_{\textsc{Hi}}h(1+z)^{2}}{E(z)}(1+\delta_{\textsc{Hi}})\,,

(3.2)

where
h=H_{0}/100
km sec
-1
Mpc
-1
,
E(z)=H(z)/H_{0}
, and
\Omega_{\textsc{Hi}}
is the
Hi

\delta_{\textsc{Hi}}=b_{\textsc{Hi}}\delta_{M}
.
The

b_{\textsc{Hi}}(z)=0.3(1+z)+0.6
in agreement with observations at redshift
z\lesssim 0.8

[

]
, and
\Omega_{\textsc{Hi}}(z)=4(1+z)^{0.6}10^{-4}
, following

The cosmological model is fixed to

{\Omega_{m},\Omega_{\Lambda},\Omega_{b},h}={0.3,0.7,0.049,0.67}

3.2.1
Astrophysical foregrounds

The astrophysical foregrounds in the MHz range considered in our simulations are of galactic and extragalactic origins. Here we describe the models that have been implemented to build the simulted maps of the astrophysical emissions. They are described in detail in
[
25
]
.

Synchrotron emission

The synchrotron emission in our Galaxy arises from charged electrons accelerating in the magnetic field. It is the brightest emission at the radio frequencies. Its brightness temperature can be modelled as a power-law as a function of frequency, in each pixel
p
, with amplitude
T_{s}
and spectral index
\beta_{s}
:

T(\nu,p)=T_{s}\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu_{0}}\right)^{\beta_{s}(\nu,p)}\,.

(3.3)

The full-sky amplitude as well as the synchrotron spectral index are provided by the Planck Legacy Archive FFP10 simulations. The amplitude is computed from the FFP10 simulations at 217 GHz.

The synchrotron spectral index varies across pixels and its map aisre taken from
[
47
]
. Since the resolution of the maps is around 5 deg, to account smaller scales we adapted the small-scale fluctuations from
[
48
]
. The mean value over the pixels of the spectral index is
\beta_{s}\sim
3.

Free-free emission

Another diffuse galactic emission is the free-free or Bremsstrahlung. It arises from the interaction of free electrons with ions in the galactic medium. It is mostly concentrated in the Galactic plane, unlike the synchrotron radiation.

As for the synchrotron, we model the free-free using a power-law for the brightness temperature, with the spectral amplitude and spectral index provided by the FFP10 simulations. The spectral index is kept fixed across the pixels and frequencies with value
\beta_{s}
=-2.13.

Extragalactic point sources

Extragalactic point sources—including active galactic nuclei (AGN), radio galaxies, quasars, and others—can be categorized into a diffuse distribution of unresolved continuum sources and a population of bright, isolated point sources.

The simulated maps are generated according to the empirical model described in
[
19
]
. They obtain the differential source count
dN/dS
, which is the number of sources per steradian
N
per unit flux S, from an empirical fit of several continuum surveys at 1.4 GHz. The mean temperature of the continuum populations is given by integrating
dN/dS

[
49
]

T_{ps}=\Big(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2k_{B}}\Big)\int^{S_{\rm max}}_{0}S\frac{dN}{dS}dS\,,

(3.4)

where
\lambda
is the wavelength of the observed radiation and
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant. We assume that all the sources with
S>S_{\rm max}
are removed from the data, with
S_{\rm max}
= 1 Jy.

The contribution of isolated point sources comes from a clustering and a Poisson component; these are calculated from the angular power spectrum from which is generated the pixel map by using the
HEALPix synfast
routine. Point sources for
S_{\rm max}\lesssim
0.01 Jy are randomly injected into the map as fully resolved with mean random temperature
[
49
]

T_{ps}(\mathrm{1.4\ GHz},p)=\Big(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2k_{B}}\Big)\Omega^{-1}_{\rm pix}\sum^{N}_{i=0}S_{i}\,,

(3.5)

where
S^{i}
is the flux of the point sources
i
at 1.4 GHz,
N
is the number of sources in the pixel

and
\Omega_{pix}
is the pixel area.

To scale this estimate at different frequencies, we assume a power-law dependence,
T_{ps}\propto\nu^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is randomly chosen for each pixel of the simulated map according to a Gaussian distribution. We choose a mean value of -2.7 a standard deviation of 0.2
[
50
]
.

3.2.2
Polarization leakage

The galactic synchrotron radiation is the most intense diffuse emission in the frequency range considered in this work. Thus, it is fundamental to properly model it as realistic as possible. The linearly polarized part of such emission changes its polarization angle as it crosses the magnetic fields within our Galaxy’s interstellar medium
[
28
]
. Unfortunately, the Faraday effect is non-spectrally smooth effect, i.e. it depends on the frequency channels. If any polarization fluctuation leaks into the total intensity for some instrumental issue, it would be difficult to subtract it without losing part of the unpolarized
Hi
signal. In fact, a miscalibration of the instrument could yield a leakage of some percentage of the Stokes Q and U parameters of the synchrotron emission into the Stokes I parameter
[
28
]
. Thus, the polarization leakage is not constant along the frequencies since the Faraday rotation changed the Stokes Q/U parameters across the channels.

In literature, there are two models of the polarization leakage in the frequency range relevant for the 21-cm emission. These models are described in
[
28
]
and
[
24
]
. The two models use the same data-set (
[
51
]
) but produce map with a qualitatively different structure in pixel space, due to the different assumptions
[
27
]
.
We use the model adopted by
[
28
]
as it is more conservative than the one proposed by
[
24
]
, in terms of non-smooth contaminant
[
25
]
. This model assumes that all the leakage comes from just the Q Stokes component and not U, which is not totally realistic. They produce with the code CRIME a set of simulated maps of the Stokes Q emission at each frequency. The intensity of the simulated polarization leakage maps is thus:

T_{\rm leak}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})=\epsilon_{P}T^{Q}_{\rm synch}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})\,,

(3.6)

where
T^{Q}_{\rm synch}(\nu,\hat{\bm{n}})
is the Stokes Q maps of the synchrotron emission and
\epsilon_{p}
is the polarization leakage fraction. We set
\epsilon_{P}=
0.5% for this study
[
25
]
, yielding to a contribution of one order of magnitude higher than the cosmological signal, as seen in Fig.
2
, for region close to the Galactic plane.

3.3
Instrumental effects

To mimic a realistic single-dish experiment, we include the smoothing effect by the telescope beam and the uncorrelated thermal noise.

We consider a symmetric Gaussian beam with a frequency-dependent width

\theta_{\rm FWHM}=\frac{c}{\nu D}\,

(3.7)

where
c
is the speed of light and
D
is the telescope dish diameter. Note that in this case the maps in different frequency range have different resolution. We also test the foreground cleaning strategies with a constant Gaussian beam for all the frequency channels, computing Eq. (
3.7

We assume the thermal noise to be Gaussian, with variance per pixel
[
52
]

\sigma=\frac{T_{\rm sys}}{\sqrt{N_{\rm dish}\tau\Delta\nu}}\,

(3.8)

where
\tau

\Delta\nu
is the width of each frequency channel,
N_{\rm dish}
is the number of dishes in the array and
T_{\rm sys}
is the system temperature.
The integration time per pixel is related to the observation time as

\rm t_{\rm pix}=\frac{\Omega_{\rm beam}}{4\pi f_{\rm sky}}t_{\rm obs}\,,

(3.9)

where

\Omega_{\rm beam}=1.33\theta_{\rm FWHM}^{2}
is the beam solid angle.

The system temperature

is calculated as

T_{\rm sys}=T_{\rm rx}+T_{\rm spl}+T_{\rm CMB}+T_{\rm gal}\,

(3.10)

where

T_{\rm gal}\approx 25\,\rm K(408\,\rm MHz/\nu)^{2.75}
is the contribution of our own galaxy, and
{T_{\rm rx}}
is the receiver noise temperature
[
53
]
. For SKAO, we assume
[

]

T_{\rm rx}=15\,\rm K\,+\,30\,\rm K\,\left(\frac{\nu}{\rm GHz}-0.75\right)^{2}.

(3.11)

4
Needlets

Needlets are a kind of spherical wavelets introduced for statistical analysis by
[
54
]
; their properties are discussed in
[
33
,
35
]
. Their first application in cosmology was for CMB data analysis
[
34
,
32
]
. The main advantage of the needlets is the double localization in the harmonic and in the real space.

Needlets exhibit several notable features. They do not rely on any tangent plane approximation and allow for a simple reconstruction formula. Due to their harmonic localization, they are computationally efficient and easily implemented using the standard
HEALPix
pixelization scheme
[
55
]
. For any fixed angular position, needlet coefficients decay quasi-exponentially in scale space, and it can be shown that the coefficients become asymptotically uncorrelated at fixed angular separation as the scale in harmonic space increases. This property enables them to be treated as approximately independent at small scales.

The spherical needlet system
\psi_{\{jk\}}
is constructed by convolving spherical harmonics
a_{\ell m}
with a suitable weight function,
w_{\ell}(\cdot)
and is defined as

\psi_{jk}(\hat{\bm{n}})=\sqrt{\lambda_{jk}}\sum^{\left[D^{j+1}\right]}_{\ell=\left[D^{j-1}\right]}w{\Big(\frac{\ell}{D^{j}}\Big)}\sum^{\ell}_{m=-\ell}Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\bm{n}})Y_{\ell m}(\xi_{jk})\,,

(4.1)

where
\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the integer part,
{\xi_{jk}}
and
\lambda_{jk}
are the cubature points and the weights at frequency
j
and location
k
. The weight functions are usually binned in the harmonic space, with each bin labelled by the index
j
. The parameter
D
controls the bandwidth: smaller values
D
correspond to tighter localization in harmonic space, while larger values improve localization on the sphere.

In the
HEALPix
implementation,
k
corresponds to a pixel index, the locations
\xi_{jk}
are identified with the pixel centers, and the cubature weights
\lambda_{jk}
can be approximated as
4\pi/N_{\rm pix}
, where
\rm N_{\rm pix}
is the number of pixels.

The needlet coefficients
a_{jk}
of a scalar field
T(\hat{\bm{n}})
are obtained by projecting the field onto the needlet basis
\psi_{jk}(\hat{\bm{n}})
:

\begin{split}a_{jk}&=\int_{S^{2}}d\Omega T(\hat{\bm{n}})\psi_{jk}(\hat{\bm{n}})\\
&=\sqrt{\lambda_{jk}}\sum_{\ell m}a_{\ell m}w\Big(\frac{\ell}{D^{j}}\Big)Y_{\ell m}(\xi_{jk})\,,\end{split}

(4.2)

where
a_{\ell m}
are the spherical harmonic coefficients of the field
T(\hat{\bm{n}})

a_{\ell m}=\int_{S^{2}}d\Omega T(\hat{\bm{n}})Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{\bm{n}})\,,

(4.3)

At each frequency
j
, the needlet coefficients
a_{jk}
are represented by an
HEALPix
map.

In this work, we implement the so-called
standard
needlets functions. The weight function for standard needlets is shown in Fig.
5
for a maximum frequency index
j_{\rm max}=
4. These functions are compactly supported in harmonic space, with support in the range
\frac{\ell}{D^{j}}\in\left[1/D,D\right]
and involve only a finite number of multipoles. This property guarantees an exact reconstruction of the field after decomposition:

T(\hat{\bm{n}})=\sum_{jk}a_{jk}\psi_{jk}(\hat{\bm{n}})\,.

(4.4)

Figure 5
:
Weight functions for needlets with
j_{\rm max}=4
,
\ell_{\rm max}=383
. The gradient of colors changes from green to blue as the band index
j
increases from
0
to
4
. The solid line corresponds to the weight function of the standard needlets, the dashed line of the cosine ones.

Fig.
5
also shows the weight function for
cosine
needlets, which are built on a different weight function, but share the same properties as the standard needlets. The GNILC algorithm uses the
cosine
needlets to decompose the field and calculate the ILC weights.

Another class of needlets, known as
Mexican
needlets
[
56
]
, differ in that their weight functions are not compactly supported in harmonic space. Instead, each frequency bin includes contributions from the full range of multipoles, which prevents exact reconstruction of the input field.

In this work, we adopt standard needlets with
j_{\rm max}=
4. For completeness, in Appendix
A
we explore the case with
j_{\rm max}=
12 and in Appendix
B
we test our pipeline using the
Mexican
needlets.

5
The pipeline

This section describes the pipeline we implement to obtain the power spectrum of the
Hi
signal.

5.1
Need-PCA

We implement a new version of PCA which, instead of using the spherical harmonics as the
standard
PCA, exploits the decomposition with standard needlets, the
Need
-PCA.

We expand the data cube
\mathsf{X}
in needlet coefficients X
jk
and we apply PCA to each map corresponding to each coefficient
j
. We compute the covariance matrix as

\mathsf{Cov}_{j,\,ch,\,ch^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\rm N_{\rm pix}}\sum^{N_{\rm pix}}_{k=1}X_{jk}^{\rm ch}X_{jk}^{\rm ch^{\prime}}\,,

(5.1)

and its eigenspectrum to identify the principal eigenvalues. Once set the number of components to remove N
fg
, we compute the foreground residual maps as
\mathsf{X}_{\rm fg,\,j}=\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg,\,j}\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg,\,j}^{T}\mathsf{X}_{\rm j}
, where
\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg,\,j}
is the mixing matrix at the needlet scale
j
. Thus, the cleaned
Hi
maps at scale
j
is recovered as

\mathsf{X}_{\textsc{Hi},\,\rm j}=\mathsf{X}_{j}-\mathsf{X}_{\rm fg,\,j}=\mathsf{X}_{\rm j}-\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg,\,j}\hat{\mathsf{U}}_{\rm fg,\,j}^{T}\mathsf{X}_{\rm j}\,.

(5.2)

As a final step, we reconstruct the
Hi
signal by convolving the spherical harmonics coefficients of each X
{}_{\textsc{Hi},\,\rm jk}
maps with the needlet weight functions and then summing up the resulting maps.

5.2
Need-GMCA

As for PCA, we implement a version of GMCA which uses needlets (Need-GMCA). As described in Sec.
5.1
, we decompose the data in needlet space and apply GMCA
2
2

2

https://github.com/isab3lla/gmca4im/tree/master
to each needlet coefficient. We thus reconstruct the residual maps of the
Hi
signal for each frequency channel.

5.3
GNILC

As discussed in Sec.
2
, GNILC require
prior
maps of the
Hi
signal as input to evaluate the
Hi
dimension in the PCA step. These
Hi
maps are generated from the theoretical angular power spectrum calculated in each frequency channel.
We extract from the set of
Hi
plus noise simulated maps the angular power spectra using the routine
anafast
from the
HEALPix
package. We pass them through a Savitzky–Golay filter
3
3

3

https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.signal.savgol_filter.html
and generate from them a set of maps with the
synfast
routine of
HEALPix

3.3
. Finally, we apply the mask to the simulations.

We assess the robustness of this choice by comparing the angular power spectrum of the recovered
Hi

In Appendix
C
we also evaluate the impact on the recovery of the
Hi
from the data, by comparing the recovered signal from GNILC using a "wrong" choice of the prior.

7
Conclusions

The aim of this work is to assess the performance of different foreground removal methods in recovering the
Hi
signal from simulated data. We use a set full-sky sky maps in the frequency range
\nu\in\left[900.5-1004.5\right]
MHz, corresponding to the redshift range
z\in\left[0.41-0.58\right]
. The simulations include the cosmological signal, galactic and extragalactic foregrounds, and a polarization leakage component. To mimic observations with the SKAO-MID telescope in the AA4 configuration, each map is smoothed with a Gaussian beam, affected by thermal noise and masked over 50% of the Galactic plane. The data-cube consists of 105
HEALPix
maps with a frequency resolution of 1 MHz.

We implement a novel PCA algorithm based on spherical wavelets known as needlets. Unlike spherical harmonics, needlets are double localized in both harmonic and pixel space, making them well-suited for spherical data analysis. We apply PCA with needlets (Need-PCA), GMCA, GMCA with needlets (Need-GMCA), and GNILC to our dataset. Our main findings are as follows:

•

When polarization leakage is excluded, all methods recover the angular power spectrum with approximately 10% precision for
\ell\gtrsim 30
, averaged over the frequency channels, as shown in the upper panels of Fig.
10
. For Need-PCA, PCA, Need-GMCA, and GMCA, we fix the number of foreground components to N
fg
=3. In contrast, GNILC estimates N
fg
locally at each needlet scale and spatial region using a statistical criterion. GNILC, as a
semi-blind
method, requires
prior
information on the
Hi
power spectrum, while the others are
blind
and make no assumptions about the cosmological signal. We find same results for
blind
methods without any assumptions on the cosmological signal.

•

With respect to the other methods considered in this work, GNILC is affected by lower foreground residuals at large angular scales, i.e.
\ell\lesssim 30
, since it varies dynamically the number of sources to remove,
N_{\rm fg}
, for different scales and regions of the sky.

•

All methods are insensitive to polarization leakage, yielding the same results as in the leakage-free case, and fixing the same N
{}_{\rm fg}=
3, as shown in the bottom panels of Fig.
10
. This is because the leakage primarily affects the Galactic plane, which is masked in our analysis. Therefore, we conclude that polarization leakage–––modelled as in
[
28
]
—has a negligible impact on
Hi
recovery in SKAO-MID AA4-like surveys.

•

We repeat the analysis using a constant Gaussian beam across all frequency channels, set to
\theta_{\rm FMWH}=
1.3 deg (the largest beam size in the band). The results are consistent with those obtained using a frequency-dependent beam, indicating that resolution differences across frequencies do not significantly affect the cleaning process.

In conclusion, out findings provide strong support for the application of these techniques for the analysis of SKAO-MID IM future data.

Appendix C
GNILC with different priors

To run GNILC, we use a smooth prior extracted from the
Hi
plus noise simulations for each frequency channels. Then, we synthetize the prior maps with
synfast
and smooth them with the proper beam width. We test the effects of having an overestimated and an underestimated smooth prior. In particular, we run the code with a smooth prior overestimated by a factor 2.5 and a smooth prior underestimated by a factor 2, following
[
30
]
. The results in Fig.
17
show that overestimating the prior does not affect the accuracy of recovering
C_{\ell}^{\textsc{Hi}}
. On the other hand, we are not able to properly recover the
C_{\ell}^{\textsc{Hi}}
when the smooth prior is underestimated. A more detailed discussion is found in
[

]
