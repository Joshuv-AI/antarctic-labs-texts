Water sorption and bond strength of graphene oxide-added universal adhesives
Authors: Muhittin Ugurlu, Fatmanur Sari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effect of adding graphene oxide (GO) to HEMA-containing and HEMA-free universal adhesives on water sorption/solubility and bonding effectiveness to dentin.Materials and methodsGO was incorporated into universal adhesives (Scotchbond Universal and G-Premio Bond) at three weight percentages (0.5, 1, and 3%) and characterized by SEM. Clearfil SE Bond was used as the reference adhesive. Disk-shaped specimens were prepared to evaluate water sorption and solubility (n = 10). Microspecimens were prepared from ninety extracted human third molar. The µTBS of half of the specimens was tested after 24 h, and the remaining specimens were aged for 6 months. Failure mode was analyzed using a stereomicroscope. Statistical analyses were performed with one-way ANOVA, Duncan, and paired t-tests (p = 0.05).ResultsAdding 3% GO increased water sorption of the adhesives (p < 0.05). Clearfil SE Bond exhibited the lowest water sorption and solubility (p < 0.05). 1% GO significantly increased the immediate µTBS of the adhesives, whereas 3% resulted in a significant decrease (p < 0.05). 0.5 and 1% GO significantly increased the aged µTBS of Scotchbond Universal, while only 1% improved the aged µTBS of G-Premio Bond (p < 0.05). 3% GO reduced the aged µTBS of the adhesives (p < 0.05). The highest immediate and aged µTBS were recorded for Clearfil SE Bond (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThe bonding performance of universal adhesives improved with 0.5% and 1% GO additions, whereas 3% GO had a detrimental effect on both bond strength and water sorption.Clinical significanceAdding GO to adhesives may improve the long-term durability of bonded restorations.

Introduction

The longevity of composite restorations primarily depends on the integrity of the resin–dentin bonding interface [1, 2]. The acid-etch technique can achieve a satisfactory long-term adhesion to enamel; however, bonding to dentin through hybridization remains more challenging [2, 3]. The long-term durability of the adhesion between dentin and the material is affected by the stability of both the adhesive and hybrid layers [2, 4]. Universal adhesives are accepted as the latest generation of bonding agents. They can be applied using different strategies, including the etch-and-rinse, self-etch, and selective enamel etching techniques [3]. Nevertheless, there are some concerns about the long-term bonding performance of universal adhesives [2, 4]. Their thin film thickness may reduce light-curing efficiency and the ability to absorb interfacial stress [2]. In addition, their high hydrophilicity makes them more susceptible to hydrolytic degradation through increased water sorption [2].

One reason for the hydrophilicity of universal adhesives is the presence of the monofunctional hydrophilic monomer 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), which is still frequently incorporated into them [5]. HEMA acts as a co-solvent for other monomer components and facilitates resin infiltration into the demineralized dentin [6–8]. HEMA also enhances the wettability of the dentin surface due to its low molecular weight [7]. However, its highly hydrophilic nature, attributed to the hydroxyl group, may adversely affect the long-term bond durability [4, 8]. HEMA does not polymerize efficiently, thereby weakening the mechanical properties of the polymer matrix [4, 5, 7]. It keeps water at the adhesive-dentin interface and increases water uptake; therefore, harming interfacial strength [4, 9]. Moreover, it has allergenic and cytotoxic side effects [10].

Previous studies have shown that incorporating inorganic fillers into adhesive formulations can enhance bonding performance by improving the mechanical properties of the adhesive layer and reducing water sorption and solubility [11–16]. Graphene has recently been utilized to enhance the properties of dental adhesives. Owing to its remarkable chemical, physical, and mechanical characteristics, graphene has the potential to improve resin–dentin bonding [12, 15, 17]. Graphene oxide, the oxidized derivative of graphene, contains various oxygen-based functional groups that enable interactions with organic polymers [18]. While graphene itself is hydrophobic, graphene oxide exhibits hydrophilic behavior due to its oxygen-containing functional groups. The functional groups of graphene oxide can enhance interfacial interactions by altering the surface properties [17]. The hydrophilic groups of graphene oxide can promote the growth of hydroxyapatite crystals by interacting with calcium ions [18]. Previous studies have demonstrated that adding graphene oxide to adhesives may enhance bond strength and durability through various mechanisms [13, 15, 17, 19, 20]. However, these studies are limited, and the optimal amount of graphene oxide to be added to adhesives remains unclear. The influence of graphene oxide addition on the water sorption and solubility of adhesives has not yet been investigated. Furthermore, no study investigates the effect of graphene oxide on both HEMA-containing and HEMA-free universal adhesives.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effect of adding graphene oxide with different proportions to HEMA-containing and HEMA-free universal adhesives on the water sorption/solubility and bonding effectiveness to dentin. The null hypotheses tested were that (1) the incorporation of graphene oxide nanoparticles into universal adhesives would not affect water sorption and solubility, and (2) the incorporation of graphene oxide nanoparticles into universal adhesives would not influence the bonding effectiveness to dentin, either immediately or after aging.

Materials and methods

Preparation of experimental adhesives

The schematic illustration of the experimental study design is shown in Fig. 1. A HEMA-containing universal adhesive (Scotchbond Universal Adhesive, 3 M Oral Care; Seefeld, Germany) and a HEMA-free universal adhesive (G-Premio Bond, GC; Tokyo, Japan) were used in this study. A two-step self-etch adhesive (Clearfil SE Bond, Kuraray Noritake; Tokyo, Japan) was also employed as a reference. The composition and manufacturer’s directions of the adhesives is shown in Table 1. Single-layer graphene oxide with 99.5% purity and 1 nm size (Fig. 2) was used (Nanografi, Ankara, Turkey). Graphene oxide was incorporated into the universal adhesives at different weight proportions (0.5, 1, and 3 wt%). The universal adhesive was inserted into a 1 ml black glass vial and weighed with an accuracy of 0.001 mg using a calibrated analytical balance (Precisa XB 205 A, SCS; Zürich, Switzerland). The graphene oxide was weighed and added to the universal adhesives in predetermined proportions. The mixtures were mechanically blended using a high-speed mixing device (Vortex, IKA-Werke; Staufen, Germany) for 3 min to obtain a homogeneous mixture. The prepared adhesives were used immediately and re-homogenized with an ultrasonic homogenizer before each use—the universal adhesives without graphene oxide served as the control groups. All experimental procedures were made by a single operator to ensure procedural standardization.

SEM/EDS analysis of the experimental adhesives

The presence of graphene oxide within the universal adhesives was determined using SEM (Scanning Electron Microscopy) and EDS (Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy). Five samples of the experimental adhesives were photo-polymerized on glass slides with a dental light-curing unit (Valo, Ultradent; South Jordan, UT, USA) at a light output of 1000 mW/cm². The specimens were mounted on aluminum stubs with conductive carbon tape and examined under a scanning electron microscope (Quanta FEG 250, FEI; Eindhoven, Netherlands) with secondary electrons at varying voltages and magnifications. EDS analyses were performed simultaneously with SEM observations.

Water sorption and solubility

Disk-shaped specimens were prepared from the adhesives using Teflon molds (10 mm in diameter and 1 mm in thickness; n = 10). To ensure optimal polymerization, the disks were light-cured for 60 s on each side with a light-curing unit (Valo, Ultradent). The specimens were then polished with P600 and P1200 SiC papers (Struers; Ballerup, Denmark) to smooth their surfaces. The height and diameter of each disk were measured using a digital caliper (Digimatic Caliper, Mitutoyo; Tokyo, Japan) to calculate the disk volume (V). The disks were then incubated at 37 °C for 22 h, followed by incubation at room temperature (23 °C) for 2 h. They were placed in a glass desiccator and weighed on an electronic analytical balance every 24 h until a constant mass was reached. It was recorded as m1. The disks were then immersed in 10 mL of distilled water and incubated at 37 °C for 1 week. After storage, the specimens were reweighed (m2). The disks were dried again in the glass desiccator until a constant mass was achieved. The final mass was noted as m3. Specimen preparation and bonding procedure

Ninety non-carious human third molars were collected after receiving ethical approval (ref no: 2022/8). The teeth were cleaned of soft tissue residue using a scaler and disinfected with 0.5% chloramine trihydrate solution for 48 h at 4 °C. The teeth were stored in distilled water and utilized within one month following extraction. The occlusal third of the teeth was sectioned using a precision-cutting machine (Minitom, Struers; Ballerup, Denmark) after mounting the teeth in acrylic blocks. The exposed dentin surfaces were further polished with wet #600 grit silicon carbide paper for 60 s to create a standardized smear layer. The sound dentin surfaces were examined for the presence of enamel and pulp tissue under a stereo-microscope (S4E, Leica Microsystems; Wetzlar, Germany).

The teeth were randomly assigned to nine groups (n = 10). In all the groups, the adhesives were applied exclusively in the self-etch mode and polymerized using a light-curing unit (Valo, Ultradent), following the manufacturer’s directions. After applying the adhesive, a nanofilled resin composite (Estelite Σ Quick, A2 shade, Tokuyama; Tokyo, Japan) was incrementally applied in 2-mm layers to build up a composite layer 5–6 mm in thickness, with each layer light-cured using the curing unit, following the manufacturer’s directions.

The composite-bonded tooth specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h. The specimens were then cut into sections using the precision cutting machine (Minitom, Struers) to acquire composite–dentin sticks with a cross-sectional area of approximately 1 mm² (± 0.1 mm²). One composite–dentin stick was selected from each experimental group for SEM analysis.

Microtensile bond strength testing (µTBS)

The ten microspecimens were collected from the central region of each tooth. Half of the sticks were used for the immediate (24-hour) µTBS testing. The remaining half was stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 6 months to evaluate the aged (6-month) µTBS, following the same testing protocol. The water was changed periodically (weekly).

The stick dimensions were measured exactly using the digital caliper (Digimatic Caliper, Mitutoyo) before µTBS testing. The sticks were fixed to a custom-made micro-tensile testing apparatus with cyanoacrylate glue (Loctite Super Glue, Henkel; Bopfingen, Germany). The apparatus was embedded in a universal testing device (Autograph AGS-X, Shimadzu; Kyoto, Japan), and the sticks were stressed at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until failure. The µTBS was calculated in MPa by dividing the load at the fracture (in N) by the bonded area (in mm²). The mean µTBS values for beams from the same tooth were calculated, treating the average bond strength for each tooth as a distinct statistical unit. The µTBS of the specimens that failed before testing (PTFs) was recorded as 0 MPa, included in the calculation of the mean µTBS.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS program (version 30.0; IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variances were verified with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Levene’s tests, respectively (data shown). The data on water sorption and solubility were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The µTBS data were analyzed using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA. Duncan’s post hoc test was applied for pairwise comparisons between groups, and paired-samples t-tests were used to compare the means between storage times. A significance level for all tests is 0.05.

SEM evaluation of adhesive-dentin interface

The beams were wet-polished, rinsed, and ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water. Subsequently, they were examined under the scanning electron microscope (Quanta FEG 250, FEI).

Failure analysis

The failure modes were thoughtfully examined using the stereomicroscope (S4E, Leica) across a range of magnifications. The failure modes were categorized into four distinct types: adhesive failure, cohesive failure in dentin, cohesive failure in resin composite, and mixed failure.

Discussion

The clinical success of composite restorations is closely associated with the properties of the dental adhesives. The adhesives should provide sufficient bond strength and maintain it over time. Ongoing research has focused on improving their physicochemical and mechanical properties. This study aimed to assess the effect of adding graphene oxide into the universal adhesives on their water sorption/solubility and bond strength.

The composite restorations and their adhesive interfaces are perpetually exposed to a wet environment in the oral cavity [5]. An optimal adhesive should maintain both chemical and thermal stability and remain undissolved under oral conditions [4, 21]. Unfortunately, the polymer resin network of adhesives absorbs water. It releases chemical components, such as uncured monomers, into the environment, which negatively affect the mechanical and chemical characteristics of the adhesive layer by impairing its structure [21]. The hydrolytic effects of water uptake substantially degrade the bonds [9]. Therefore, water sorption and solubility are important parameters that affect the bonding performance of adhesives [11].

Water sorption and solubility are dependent on the material’s chemical composition [21]. The latest generation of dental adhesives, known as universal adhesives, has become popular in dentistry. These adhesives exhibit comparatively high hydrophilicity due to the presence of hydrophilic monomers, which are combined with hydrophobic components in a single bottle [4, 14]. HEMA is the most commonly used monomer and contains hydrolytically susceptible ester groups [22]. The existence of hydroxyl, carboxyl, and phosphate groups further increases its hydrophilicity, making it more prone to water uptake [21, 22]. The HEMA-containing adhesives have been reported to exhibit higher water sorption and solubility than HEMA-free adhesives [5, 23–25]. The two universal adhesives investigated in the present study were Scotchbond Universal Adhesive and G-Premio Bond. In graphene-free groups, the water sorption of HEMA-containing universal adhesive, Scotchbond Universal Adhesive, was higher than that of HEMA-free universal adhesive, G-Premio Bond. The results of this study are in agreement with previous studies [21, 23–25]. The non-solvated and hydrophobic adhesives show less water uptake [21]. In this study, only the adhesive resin of Clearfil SE Bond without mixing primer was used for water sorption and solubility tests. Therefore it exhibited the least water sorption and solubility in this study, consistent with previous research [5, 21]. Mixing the adhesive resin with primer may increase the water sorption and solubility [5].

Several studies have attempted to reduce the water uptake of adhesives by modifying their composition [5, 7, 11]. The present study investigated the effect of the effect of incorporating graphene oxide into universal adhesives on water sorption and solubility. Graphene oxide may enhance the water resistance of adhesives; however, higher concentrations could adversely affect the properties of adhesives [15, 17, 19, 20]. In the current study, adding 0.5% and 1 graphene oxide to the tested universal adhesives did not affect the water sorption of the materials. Nevertheless, adding 3% graphene oxide dramatically increased the water sorption of the tested universal adhesives.

The quality of the polymer network formed during polymerization has a significant influence on water sorption, as the presence of cross-links between polymer chains reduces the polymer’s water uptake [21, 22]. Filler particles in adhesives can reduce light transmission by acting as scattering barriers, particularly as their concentration increases. It may adversely affect the polymerization process [26]. Previous studies have reported that increasing the graphene content in adhesive formulations can lead to the formation of agglomerated structures, which may reduce the conversion degree [15, 19, 20]. The highest water sorption observed in the 3% graphene oxide-added groups may be attributed to adverse effects on the polymerization process and a lower degree of conversion. However, the present study did not evaluate the conversion degree. In the current study, the addition of graphene oxide did not affect the solubility of the universal adhesives. The outcome may be related to the fact that solubility occurs primarily from the resin matrix rather than the filler particles [11]. Consequently, the first null hypothesis-that the incorporation of graphene oxide nanoparticles into universal adhesives would not affect water sorption and solubility-was partially rejected.

Resin monomers in adhesive materials can absorb water and release components into the surrounding environment, leading to the formation of water-filled channels within the polymer matrix and consequent weakening of the polymers [27]. Therefore, water sorption and solubility exert deleterious effects on adhesive properties, particularly bond strength [21, 27].

The penetration of adhesive resin monomers into demineralized dentin surfaces forms resin tags [2]. Graphene oxide, being a hydrophilic component, may facilitate the infiltration of these monomers and promote the formation of deeper resin tags, thereby providing stronger mechanical support to the hybrid layer [12, 15, 17, 20]. The two-dimensional structure of graphene oxide, characterized by its high specific surface area, contributes to the reinforcement of the material’s mechanical properties [28, 29]. The graphene oxide filler particles may fill in the voids inside the adhesive layer, thereby enhancing its mechanical resistance [12, 17, 20, 30]. In addition, graphene oxide may provide shock-absorbing capacity to the adhesive layer, facilitating the distribution of stresses induced by occlusal loads across the interface [16, 31].

In the current study, the addition of 0.5% graphene oxide did not statistically affect the immediate µTBS of the two universal adhesives and the aged µTBS of G-Premio Bond. Incorporating 1% graphene oxide significantly increased the immediate and aged µTBS of the universal adhesives. This increase in bond strength may be attributed to the formation of deeper resin tags and a mechanically stronger adhesive layer. According to previous studies, incorporating 0.5% graphene into an adhesive did not significantly affect the immediate [32] and aged µTBS [17, 32]. Other studies have reported that adding 0.5% and 2% graphene oxide nanoparticles improved both the immediate and aged µTBS, although the 2% concentration reduced the degree of conversion [15]. Furthermore, incorporating 5% graphene oxide/calcium phosphate nanoparticles was also found to enhance the aged shear bond strength [12].

The proportion of added nanoparticles is crucial for optimizing the properties of the dental adhesive [16, 20]. The particle size and percentage affect the rheological properties of the materials [33]. Higher concentrations increase the material’s viscosity due to an enlarged surface area and induce void formation within the hybrid layer through agglomeration [15, 16]. The high viscosity also hinders the formation of resin tags, thereby reducing the bond strength [15, 16, 20, 32, 34]. Moreover, previous studies found that higher concentrations of graphene nanoparticles led to lower conversion degree due to agglomeration [12, 15, 19, 20]. In this study, the incorporation of 3% graphene oxide reduced both the immediate and aged µTBS of the universal adhesives. The concentrations of graphene oxide were determined according to previous reports, and the present findings are consistent with those studies [12, 15, 30, 32]. Ultimately, the second null hypothesis-that the incorporation of graphene oxide nanoparticles into universal adhesives would not influence the bonding effectiveness to dentin, either immediately or after aging-was partially rejected.

In the present study, artificial aging caused bond weakening. The incorporation of 0.5 and 1% graphene oxide significantly hindered the reduction in the µTBS of the universal adhesives. Water sorption is responsible for bond degradation during aging [15, 21]. The hydrophilic property of graphene oxide enhances the water resistance of adhesives, as its amphiphilic nature directs water from hydrophilic sites toward hydrophobic carbon sites [15, 17, 20, 29]. Therefore, the addition of graphene oxide to an adhesive may help reduce deterioration in dentin bond strength [17]. In this study, the addition of 3% graphene oxide did not prevent the aging-related reduction in bond strength. It may have resulted from increased water sorption.

Clearfil SE Bond is considered the gold standard self-etch adhesive due to its outstanding performance in numerous laboratory studies and long-term clinical research; hence, it was employed as a reference in this study. In the current study, the most frequent failures were adhesive interfacial failures for all experimental groups. Aging did not substantially change the distribution of failure modes. The present study evaluated water sorption and bond strength but did not assess the degree of conversion; therefore, no conclusions could be drawn regarding the effects of graphene oxide incorporation on the polymerization process, which influences bond strength.

Dentin is a living and dynamic tissue, but it loses this characteristic after the tooth is extracted. Therefore, the outcomes of the present study may not fully reflect clinical adhesive performance. Laboratory studies may not accurately replicate the dynamic loading and restorative conditions of the oral environment. Further studies simulating the clinical oral environment are warranted. In this study, the experimental adhesives were applied in self-etch mode. Using them in etch-and-rinse mode could potentially affect bond strength due to their influence on resin tag formation. Moreover, only three concentrations of graphene oxide were evaluated in the present study.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of thsate present study, it can be concluded that the addition of 3% graphene oxide caused an increase in the water sorption of the universal adhesives. Incorporation of 0.5% and 1% graphene oxide improved the bonding performance of universal adhesives to dentin, whereas 3% graphene oxide had a detrimental effect. Excessive graphene oxide loading may compromise the adhesive properties. The optimal proportion of graphene oxide in adhesives remains an important topic for future research.
