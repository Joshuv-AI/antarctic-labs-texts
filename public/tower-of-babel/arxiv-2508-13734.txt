Entanglement witnesses for stabilizer states and subspaces beyond qubits
Jakub Szczepaniak, Owidiusz Makuta, Remigiusz Augusiak

Abstract
Genuine multipartite entanglement is arguably the most valuable form of entanglement in the multipartite case with application for instance in quantum metrology. In order to detect that form of entanglement in multipartite quantum states, one typically uses entanglement witnesses. The aim of this paper is to generalize the results of [G. Tóth and O. Gühne, Phys. Rev. A 72 , 022340 (2005)] in order to provide a construction of witnesses of genuine multipartite entanglement tailored to entangled subspaces originating from the multi-qudit stabilizer formalism—a framework well known for its role in quantum error correction, which also provides a very convenient description of a broad class of entangled multipartite states (both pure and mixed). Our construction includes graph states of arbitrary local dimension. We then show that in certain situations, the obtained witnesses detecting genuine multipartite entanglement in quantum systems of higher local dimension are superior in terms of noise robustness to those derived for multiqubit states.



I
Introduction

Entanglement is considered to be one of the most characteristic features of quantum theory that has become a resource for interesting applications such as quantum key distribution
Ekert 1991
or quantum teleportation
Bennett et al. 1993
(see also Ref.
Horodecki et al. 2009
for a review). It is also the key phenomenon giving rise to Bell nonlocality
Bell 1964
, which is another form of nonclassical correlations that quantum theory exhibits. In the multipartite setting, the most valuable form of entanglement is considered to be the so-called genuine multipartite entanglement, which, among others, is vital for the possibility of achieving the Heisenberg limit in quantum metrology
Tóth 2012
;
Hyllus et al. 2012
.

However, the full exploitation of entanglement as a resource requires efficient tools that allow its detection in composite quantum states. Arguably, one of the most powerful and at the same time most complete methods is that based on entanglement witnesses (EWs), first introduced for bipartite quantum states in the seminal paper
Horodecki et al. 1996
, and later extended to the multipartite case in
Horodecki et al. 2001
; notice that the term itself was coined only later in Ref.
Terhal 2000
. Entanglement witnesses are Hermitian observables whose expectation values can be used to detect entanglement in composite quantum states. Specifically, their expectation values are nonnegative for all separable states
Werner 1989
, while certain entangled states yield negative expectation values, signaling the presence of entanglement. At the same time, for any entangled state, there exists an entanglement witness which detects entanglement of that state
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 2001
. Importantly, entanglement witnesses can also be used to detect genuine multipartite entanglement (see, e.g., Refs.
Tóth and Gühne 2005a
;
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
).

For these reasons, entanglement witnesses have been intensively studied in the literature
Lewenstein et al. 2000
;
Augusiak et al. 2011
;
Hansen et al. 2015
and many constructions of them have been devised to detect entanglement in various bipartite and multipartite quantum states, just to mention Refs.
Tóth and Gühne 2005a
;
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
;
Chruściński et al. 2009
;
Sperling and Vogel 2013
;
Aloy et al. 2019
;
Chruściński et al. 2018
(cf. also the reviews
Gühne and Tóth 2009
;
Chruściński and Sarbicki 2014
). However, the majority of these efforts have focused on the simplest case of multipartite systems composed of qubits, whereas systems with higher local dimensions have received a lot less attention. At the same time, significant progress has recently been made in the preparation and manipulation of various multiqudit quantum systems (see e.g. Refs.
Wang et al. 2018
;
Reimer et al. 2019
;
Ringbauer et al. 2022
), providing strong motivation to develop suitable entanglement witnesses capable of detecting genuine multipartite entanglement in such systems. Despite the fact that a number of entanglement criteria have been designed up to date, the problem is far from being completely solved.

The main aim of this work is to introduce constructions of witnesses of GME for quantum states originating from the stabilizer formalism
Steane 1996
;
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Gottesman 1997
;
Laflamme et al. 1996
in Hilbert spaces composed of many qudits. While this framework is most known for its utility in designing quantum error correction codes such as the five-qubit
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Laflamme et al. 1996
and Kitaev toric
Kitaev 2003
codes, it also provides a very efficient and convenient-to-handle representation of a broad class of multipartite states (both pure and mixed) composed of many qudits. In fact, the stabilizer formalism encompasses the well-known graph states
Hein et al. 2004
;
Hein et al. 2006
corresponding to one-dimensional stabilizer subspaces, but also classes of genuinely entangled mixed states defined on certain multidimensional stabilizer subspaces
Makuta et al. 2023
.

In this work, we build upon the results of Refs.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
;
Tóth and Gühne 2005a
, which introduced entanglement witnesses for multiqudit graph states. Specifically, we generalize these results to higher-dimensional subspaces arising from the multiqudit stabilizer formalism. As a particular case of our construction, we also recover entanglement witnesses tailored to graph states of arbitrary prime local dimension. Robustness to white noise of the obtained EW’s is then studied. Interestingly, we observe that the robustness of a given witness tailored to a particular graph state grows with the local dimension
d
, and also that, entanglement witnesses for genuinely entangled subspaces are in general superior in terms of robustness to white noise over witnesses tailored to states contained in those subspaces, which is a consequence of the fact that EW’s for subspaces involve less stabilizing operators as compared to states. We finally provide a construction of a witness detecting GME tailored to a three-qubit two-dimensional subspaces, which does not originate from the stabilizer formalism, for which the stabilizing operators are non-local.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we provide basic information about stabilizer formalism and genuine multipartite entanglement.

II.1
Genuine multipartite entanglement

Let us consider an
N
-partite Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\textrm{tot}}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes...\otimes\mathcal{H}_{N}
, where we assume that
\mathcal{H}_{i}=\mathbb{C}^{d}
for any
i
. We call a bipartition
Q|\overline{Q}
a partition of the set of parties
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
into two, disjoint, and nonempty sets such that
Q\cup\overline{Q}=[N]
. Naturally, a bipartition
Q|\overline{Q}
translates into a splitting of the total Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}^{\textrm{tot}}
into Hilbert spaces corresponding to both sets, that is,
\mathcal{H}^{tot}=\mathcal{H}_{Q}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\overline{Q}}
.

The notion of bipartition is central to the definition of
genuine multipartite entanglement
(GME): we say that a mixed state
\rho
is biseparable if it admits the following decomposition.

\rho=\sum_{Q\subset[N]}p_{Q}\sum_{i}q_{i,Q}\rho_{Q}^{i}\otimes\rho^{i}_{\overline{Q}},

(1)

for some
\rho_{Q}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{Q})
,
\rho_{\overline{Q}}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{{Q}})
,
\{q_{i,Q}\}_{i,Q}
and
\{p_{Q}\}_{Q}
, where
\{q_{i,Q}\}_{i,Q}
and
\{p_{Q}\}_{Q}
are probability distributions. We then call a state
\rho
genuinely multipartite entangled if it is not biseparable, that is, it does not admit the decomposition (
1
).

Note that in the case of pure states, the above definition simplifies to the following: a state
|\psi\rangle
is GME if for any bipartition
Q|\overline{Q}
there are no
pure states defined on
Q
and
\overline{Q}
such that

|\psi\rangle=|\psi_{Q}\rangle\otimes|\psi_{\overline{Q}}\rangle.

(2)

In other words, a pure GME state is one that cannot be written as a tensor product of any two other pure states. Lastly, the definition of pure GME states can directly be extended to entire subspaces of
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{tot}}
: we say that a subspace
V
is GME if every state
|\psi\rangle\in V
is GME.

An example of a pure GME state is the well-known
N
-qubit
W
state

|W\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(|10\ldots 0\rangle+|01\ldots 0\rangle+\ldots+|00\ldots 1\rangle),

(3)

which is a symmetric combination of all kets in which one qubit is
in the excited state whereas the remaining
N-1
qubits are in the ground state. Then, an example of a GME subspace would be one spanned by the
W
state and the "negated"
W
state

|\overline{W}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(|01\ldots 1\rangle+|10\ldots 1\rangle+\ldots+|11\ldots 0\rangle),

(4)

which is obtained from
W
by flipping all bits
0\leftrightarrow 1
.

II.2
Qudit stabilizer formalism

In this work, we focus on quantum states—both pure and mixed—and subspaces that arise from the stabilizer formalism. Accordingly, we begin by introducing the fundamental concepts of this formalism that will be used throughout the paper. Let us consider a qudit Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, with the requirement that
d
is prime.
Then, let us consider the Weyl–Heisenberg matrices
\omega^{i}X^{j}Z^{j}
, where
i,j,k\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
and
X,Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
are
d
-dimensional generalizations of the Pauli matrices
and are given by

\displaystyle X=\sum_{i=0}^{d}|i+1\rangle\langle i|,\qquad Z=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\omega^{i}|i\rangle\langle i|,

(5)

where the addition is done modulo
d
,
\omega=\operatorname{exp}(2\pi\mathbb{i}/d)
, and
X,Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. Importantly, they satisfy the following conditions

\displaystyle X^{d}=\mathbb{1},\quad Z^{d}=\mathbb{1},

(6)

\displaystyle XX^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1},\quad ZZ^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1},

i.e., they are unitary and their eigenvalues are
d
th roots of the unity. Moreover, they also satisfy the following commutation relation,

ZX=\omega XZ.

(7)

Now, the set of all
N
-fold tensor products of the Weyl-Heisenberg matrices endowed with the matrix multiplication forms the so-called generalized Pauli group
\mathbb{P}_{N,d}
. A subgroup of
\mathbb{P}_{N,d}
is called a stabilizer
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{P}_{N,d}
if
a\mathbb{1}\in\mathbb{S}
implies that
a=1
. For each such
\mathbb{S}
, one can identify a subspace
V_{\mathbb{S}}\subset\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{tot}}
which is stabilized by all elements of
\mathbb{S}
in the following sense.

\forall_{S\in\mathbb{S}}\quad S|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad|\psi\rangle\in V_{\mathbb{S}}.

(8)

As with other groups, it is convenient to represent a stabilizer
\mathbb{S}
with its generators
\mathbb{S}=\langle G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\rangle
, i.e., such elements that any other element of a group can be represented by their product.

One of the use-cases for the generators is the computation of the dimension of
V_{\mathbb{S}}
. Given that
d
is prime and the number of generators equals
k
, we have that
\dim V_{\mathbb{S}}=d^{N-k}

Gheorghiu 2014
.

II.3
Graph states

An important subclass of stabilizer subspaces is the one-dimensional ones, known as stabilizer states. Among them, graph states form a notable subset, characterized by additional constraints derived from a graph
\mathcal{G}
. To formally define graph states, let us first introduce the concept of graphs.

A graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
is an ordered pair, where
V
is a set of vertices, and
E
is a set of edges, i.e., two-element, unordered sets of vertices. If we allow multiple copies of the same edge in
E
then such a graph is called a multigraph. While in this paper we will mostly focus on multigraphs, for simplicity, we will refer to them as graphs.

With each graph, we can associate an adjacency matrix
\Gamma
whose entry
\Gamma_{i,j}
corresponds to the number of edges connecting vertices
i
and
j
. On Fig.
1
, we provided some examples of graphs.

Figure 1:
Exemplary graphs leading to four-partite graph states.
Graph (a) represents the GHZ state, (b) the cluster state, and (c) is an example of a graph with chromatic number
K=3
.

Let us finally introduce the concept of graph colorability and the related notion of chromatic number. A
k
coloring of a graph
G
is a division of the vertices
V
into
k
disjointed subsets
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
. A proper coloring of
G
is a coloring for which
v_{1},v_{2}\in C_{i}
only if
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}\notin E
. We say that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is
k
-colorable if there exist a proper
k
-coloring of
\mathcal{G}
. Then, the chromatic number
K
of a graph
\mathcal{G}
is the smallest
k
for which we can find a proper
k
-coloring of
\mathcal{G}
.

Finally, let us define the graph states. Consider a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with an adjacency matrix
\Gamma
. A graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
is a unique state stabilized by the following generators

G_{i}=X_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{N}Z^{\Gamma_{ij}}_{j},

(9)

where
X_{j},Z_{j}
are operators that act on the qudit
j
with operators
X
and
Z
respectively, while acting with
\mathbb{1}
on every other qudit.

It is worth noting that any graph state can be expressed
in the computational basis as
Raissi et al. 2024
:

|G\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d^{N}}}\sum_{\vec{j}}\omega^{\tau(\vec{j})}|\vec{j}\rangle,

(10)

where
\vec{j}\in(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{\otimes N}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{d}=\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
, the sum is over all possible strings in
(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{\otimes N}
, and the function
\tau(\cdot):(\mathbb{Z}_{d})^{\otimes N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is given by

\tau(\vec{j})=\sum_{r,s=1}^{N}\mu_{r,s}j_{r}j_{s},

(11)

where
\mu_{r,s}
equals the number of times the edge
\{r,s\}
appears in
E
.

In this paper, we also consider particular stabilizer states: Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) state
Greenberger et al. 1989
and linear cluster states. The generators of these states are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle G_{1}^{GHZ}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}X_{i},

(12)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{GHZ}=Z_{k-1}Z^{\dagger}_{k},\quad k=2,3,\ldots,N,

and

\displaystyle G_{1}^{C}=X_{1}Z_{2},

(13)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{C}=Z_{k-1}X_{k}Z_{k+1},\quad k=2,3,\ldots,N-1,

\displaystyle G_{N}^{C}=Z_{N-1}X_{N}.

Let us finally notice that the graph states are all genuinely multipartite entangled.

II.4
Entanglement witnesses

In order to detect genuine multipartite entanglement of a given state, one can employ a plethora of methods. Among them, one of the most commonly utilized and, at the same time, the most powerfull, is that based on the concept of
entanglement witness
. A witness
\mathcal{W}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\textrm{tot}})
designed to detect GME is a Hermitian operator acting on
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{tot}}
whose expectation value
on biseparable states is non-negative,

\forall_{\rho_{\mathrm{bisep}}}\,\,\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{W}\rho_{\mathrm{bisep}})\geq 0,

(14)

and there exists a GME state
\sigma
for which

\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{W}\sigma)<0
. In other words,
\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{W}\rho\right)<0
constitutes a sufficient condition for genuine multipartite entanglement of
\rho
.

II.5
Measures for entanglement witnesses

To assess the viability of a given entanglement witness for experimental implementations, one needs to consider its robustness to noise. To this end, we consider a state
\rho(p_{noise})
defined as

\rho(p_{\mathrm{noise}})=p_{\mathrm{noise}}\frac{\mathbb{1}}{d^{N}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{noise}})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|,

(15)

which is a probabilistic mixture of a target state
|\psi\rangle
with white noise. Then, the noise robustness of a witness can be assessed by analyzing the amount of white noise we can add to a target state that still leads to a detection of entanglement. To this end, let us recall a useful notion of threshold probability. It is an upper-bound on the probability
p
for which the witness
\mathcal{W}
detects the entanglement of
\rho(p)
, i.e.,

\operatorname{Tr}[W\rho(p_{\mathrm{limit}})]=0.

(16)

From simple algebra, we obtain

p_{\mathrm{limit}}=\frac{-\langle\psi|\mathcal{W}|\psi\rangle}{d^{-N}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal{W})-\langle\psi|\mathcal{W}|\psi\rangle}.

(17)

Another metric that one can use to assess the experimental viability of a given witness is the complexity of its experimental realization. For this task, usually the concept of local measurement settings (LMSs) is used, but first, we need to introduce a notion of local commutation
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
. Consider the following operators

K=K^{(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes K^{(N)},\quad L=L^{(1)}\otimes\ldots\otimes L^{(N)}.

(18)

We say that
K
and
L
commute locally if
[K^{(n)},L^{(n)}]=0
for every
n=\{1,\ldots,N\}
. Returning to LMSs, we simply say that 2 operators are measurable in the same LMS if they commute locally. Understandably, the metric in question is the number of LMSs needed to measure a given witness
\mathcal{W}
. This notion captures the intuition that in an experimental setup, where we can perform measurements on each party separately, on the same site, we can measure only operators that commute, so the operators for a whole system have to commute locally.

As was observed in Ref.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
, in the case of LMSs for entanglement witness tailored to a graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
has a very elegant interpretation: it corresponds to the chromatic number
K
of the graph
\mathcal{G}
.

The idea of LMSs is central to the construction of entanglement witnesses in Ref.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
. For example, the entanglement witness tailored to the
N
-qubit GHZ state is given by

\mathcal{W}^{(GHZ)}\coloneqq 3\mathbb{1}-2\left(P_{1}^{GHZ}+\prod_{i=2}^{N}P_{i}^{GHZ}\right),

(19)

where
P_{i}=(\mathbb{1}+G_{i}^{GHZ})/2
is the projector onto the eigenspace of
G_{i}^{GHZ}
associated to the eigenvalue
+1
. Notice that this witness can be measured using only two LMSs: one for
P_{1}^{GHZ}
and another for
\prod_{i=2}^{N}P_{i}^{GHZ}
. In this way, one maximizes the number of GME states detected by this witness while minimizing the number of LMSs.

III
Witnesses of GME for Graph States

The main goal of this paper is to generalize the results of Ref.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
in two ways: first, we construct GME witnesses for graph states of any prime local dimension, and, second, we expand the construction of EWs to stabilizer subspaces of dimension higher than one.

As a first result of this work, we will construct a projector-based witness for a graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
.

Theorem 1
.

Let us consider a graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
. The following operator

\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}-|\mathcal{G}\rangle\!\langle\mathcal{G}|

(20)

is an entanglement witness detecting GME in the vicinity of
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
.

Proof.

To show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is a witness of GME, we need to show that the condition
in Eq. (
14
) is satisfied. At the same time, it is not difficult to observe that
\langle\mathcal{G}|\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}|\mathcal{G}\rangle=-(d-1)/d
.

To prove that (
14
) holds in this case, let us consider a bipartition
Q|\overline{Q}
.
It is direct to realize that it is enough to prove that

\operatorname{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\rho_{Q}\otimes\rho_{\overline{Q}}\right)\geqslant 0

(21)

for any pair of density matrices
\rho_{Q}
and
\rho_{\overline{Q}}
because by linearity of the trace, the inequality will also follow for an arbitrary state which is separable across the bipartition
Q|\overline{Q}
.
One then observes that the above inequality will follow if

\max_{Q|\overline{Q}}\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\Phi_{Q|\overline{Q}}}|\langle\phi|\mathcal{G}\rangle|^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{d},

(22)

where
\Phi_{Q|\overline{Q}}
is the set of product states across
Q|\overline{Q}
. Notably, this fact follows directly from
(
Makuta et al. 2025
, Corollary 1)
which states that for all GME stabilizer subspaces
V_{\mathbb{S}}
, the generalized geometric measure of entanglement
E_{GGM}(\cdot)

Gour and Wallach 2007
equals

E_{GGM}(V_{\mathbb{S}})=1-\max_{Q|\overline{Q}}\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\Phi_{Q|\overline{Q}}}\langle\phi|\Pi_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}|\phi\rangle=1-\frac{1}{d},

(23)

where
\Pi_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}
is the projector onto
V_{\mathbb{S}}
. Taking
\Pi_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}=|\mathcal{G}\rangle\!\langle\mathcal{G}|
, one obtains (
21
).
To complete the proof, we note that since the equation holds for any bipartition, the condition is also fulfilled by the linearity of the trace.
∎

Let us now consider the experimental viability of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
. First, after simple computations, one can show that the threshold probability
p_{\textrm{limit}}
equals

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{d-1}{d}\frac{1}{1-d^{-N}}.

(24)

Next, to determine how many LMSs are needed to experimentally implement
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
, we use the fact that a projector of any stabilized state can expressed as follows

|G\rangle\!\langle G|=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}G_{j}^{i},

(25)

where
G_{k}
are generators of a stabilizer of a given
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
. With the aid of this decomposition, it was shown in Ref.
Gühne et al. 2007
that in the case of
d=2
, the number of LMSs needed to implement experimentally projector-based witnesses grows linearly with the number of qubits
N
. Thus, although this witness offers high robustness against noise, it is impractical for larger systems. This fact is what motivates the search for other witnesses that offer easier experimental implementation.

Although projector-based witness (
20
) involves a sum over all stabilizing operators of a given graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
, to uniquely identify the graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
it is sufficient to check the stabilizing relations of
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
on generators
N
of the corresponding stabilizer. Therefore, it should be possible to limit the number of stabilizing operators contributing to the witness and so also the number of required LMSs.

Taking this idea to its limits, one can construct a witness that consists of only generators of a stabilizer for a given
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
.

Theorem 2
.

Consider a graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
and the corresponding stabilizer
\mathbb{S}=\langle G_{1},\ldots G_{N}\rangle
. Then the following operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\biggl\{N-\frac{d-1}{d}\biggl[1-\cos\biggl(\frac{2\pi}{d}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr\}\mathbb{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(G_{k}+G_{k}^{\dagger})

(26)

is a witness detecting GME in the vicinity of
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
.

To formulate the proof of the above theorem, we will have to use Lemma from
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
.

Lemma 1
.

If
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is an entanglement witness and there exists
\alpha>0
such that the following operator inequality

\mathcal{W}\geqslant\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}

(27)

is satisfied, then
\mathcal{W}
is also an entanglement witness.

Proof.

Eq.(
27
) implies that
\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{W}\rho\right)\geq\alpha\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\rho\right)
and since
\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\rho\right)\geq 0
for all separable states and
\alpha>0
, then also
\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{W}\rho\right)\geq 0
so
\mathcal{W}
is an entanglement witness.
∎

With this lemma, we can now proceed to the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is as follows. We start with the operator

\mathcal{W}=c\mathbb{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(G_{k}+G_{k}^{\dagger}\right)

(28)

and, taking
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
to be projector-based witness from Theorem
1
, we make use of Lemma
1
. Then, by construction, we show that for
\alpha=1-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{d}\right)
and
c=\bigl\{N-\frac{d-1}{d}\bigl[1-\cos\bigl(\frac{2\pi}{d}\bigr)\bigr]\bigr\}

\mathcal{W}\geq\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

Substituting Eq. (
20
) and (
28
) to Lemma
1
we get

c\mathbb{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(G_{k}+G_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\geqslant\alpha\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}-|\mathcal{G}\rangle\!\langle\mathcal{G}|\right).

(29)

The projector
|\mathcal{G}\rangle\!\langle\mathcal{G}|
can be decomposed as a sum of stabilizing operators. Since all of the stabilizing operators mutually commute, they share a common eigenbasis; hence, the above inequality can be reduced to estimating the lowest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{W}-\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
and choosing such
\alpha>0
that the lowest eigenvalue is non-negative. A general expression for the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{W}-\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
reads

\lambda=c-\frac{\alpha}{d}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\omega^{q_{k}}+\omega^{-q_{k}}\right)+\alpha\prod_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{jq_{k}}.

(30)

There are two cases when
\lambda
is the lowest: when all
\omega^{q_{k}}=1
or when all
\omega^{q_{t}}=1
expect one
\omega^{q_{t}}\neq 1
. If we make sure they are positive, the others will be as well. Those two cases lead to

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=c-\frac{\alpha}{d}-N+\alpha

(31)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=c-\frac{\alpha}{d}-N+1-\text{Re}({\omega^{q_{t}}}).

\lambda_{2}
can be estimated further by noticing that
\text{Re}({\omega^{q_{t}}})
is the highest for
q_{t}=1
. We want both
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
. Solving this gives us optimal
c=N-\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\left(1-\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{d}\right)\right)
and
\alpha=1-\cos(\frac{2\pi}{d})
. Notice that
\forall_{d}

\alpha>0
Thus, we proved that

\mathcal{W}=\biggl\{N-\frac{d-1}{d}\biggl[1-\cos\biggl(\frac{2\pi}{d}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr\}\mathbb{1}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(G_{k}+G_{k}^{\dagger})

(32)

detects GME in the vicinity of
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
.
∎

This witness has the minimal number of LMS required to detect GME since it measures only generators of a stabilizer, but this number depends on the size of the target graph state. On the other hand, one can quickly check that
p_{\textrm{limit}}=1/N
, so for larger
N
any amount of noise will make this witness unable to detect GME.

IV
Optimal Witnesses for Graph States

The witnesses discussed so far were either hard to implement experimentally or their robustness against noise decreased unboundedly as the size of the system grew. In this section, we provide witnesses with minimal LMSs required to detect GME and the highest robustness against noise that can be obtained by Lemma
1
. We prove it in general for a graph state and then consider examples such as GHZ and cluster state.

Theorem 3
.

Consider a graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
, corresponding stabilizer
\mathbb{S}=\langle G_{1},\ldots,G_{N}\rangle
and the associated graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
with chromatic number
K
and the corresponding coloring
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. Then, the following operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}=[(K-1)d+1]\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{j}^{n}

(33)

detects GME in the vicinity of
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
.

Proof.

For a graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
associated with the graph
\mathcal{G}
whose chromatic number is
K
, we need at least
K
LMSs to detect GME. Let us consider the common eigenbasis of generators
G_{i}
of the stabilizer of
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
, which we denote by
\{|\vec{g}\rangle\}_{\vec{g}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}}
such that

G_{i}|\vec{g}\rangle=\omega^{g_{i}}|\vec{g}\rangle,

(34)

where
g_{i}
is the
i
’th entry of
\vec{g}
. Note that this is not a computational basis: in fact many of the states
|\vec{g}\rangle
are entangled.

Next, let us consider the following entanglement witness

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}=c\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{j}^{n},

(35)

where
c\in\mathbb{R}
is an arbitrary coefficient which we determine below. Notice that in the basis
\{|\vec{g}\rangle\}_{\vec{g}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}}
this witness can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}

\displaystyle=c\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}\sum_{\vec{g}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}}\omega^{ng_{j}}|\vec{g}\rangle\!\langle\vec{g}|

(36)

\displaystyle=c\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{\vec{g}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}}\left(\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\delta_{g_{j},0}\right)|\vec{g}\rangle\!\langle\vec{g}|,

where
\delta_{g_{j},0}
is the Kronecker delta. This form of
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}
allows us to easily compute its trace as well as the expectation value in the graph state
|\mathcal{G}\rangle
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}(\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}})

\displaystyle=cd^{N}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}d^{N-|C_{i}|},

(37)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}|\mathcal{G}\rangle

\displaystyle=c-dK.

Now, using Eq. (
17
) we can calculate the threshold probability as a function of
c
, giving

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{dK-c}{dK-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}d^{-|C_{i}|}}.

(38)

Clearly, if we wish to increase the noise robustness of the witness, we need to maximize
p_{\textrm{limit}}
, and so we need to find the minimal
c
.

To this end, let us examine for which values of
c
, the operator
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}
is a witness of genuine multipartite entanglement. For this purpose we consider the following inequality

\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}-\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\geqslant 0,

(39)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is the projector-based witness (
20
) and
\alpha
is real-valued parameter and, apply Lemma
1
. The eigenvalues of the operator on the left-hand side of (
39
)
are given by

\lambda_{\vec{g}}=c-\frac{\alpha}{d}+\alpha\delta_{\vec{g},\vec{0}}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\delta_{g_{j},0},

(40)

where
\vec{0}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is a zero vector. There are two ways in which we could achieve the minimal eigenvalue. First, by achieving the absolute minimum of the last term in the above sum, which is the case for
\vec{g}=\vec{0}
. However, this also means that we have a positive contribution from the term
\alpha\delta_{\vec{g},\vec{0}}
. The second option is to minimize the last term, but under the condition
\alpha\delta_{\vec{g},\vec{0}}=0
. This is achieved, if
g_{j}=0
for all
C_{i}
except for one.
All together, these two conditions give us

\displaystyle c-\frac{\alpha}{d}+\alpha-dK

\displaystyle\geqslant 0,

(41)

\displaystyle c-\frac{\alpha}{d}-d(K-1)

\displaystyle\geqslant 0.

The minimal
c
is achieved when both of these bounds coincide, which implies
\alpha=d
and so minimal
c
equals

c=(K-1)d+1,

(42)

which ends the proof.
∎

Notice, that since
c=(K-1)d+1
, the threshold probability
p_{\textrm{limit}}
of
\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{G})}_{\mathrm{opt}}
equals

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{d-1}{d}\frac{1}{K-\sum_{i=1}^{K}d^{-|C_{i}|}}.

(43)

With these results we can consider what state would allow for the most robust detection of GME in this construction. From the form of
p_{\textrm{limit}}
it is clear that states that require more LMSs are also less robust against noise. Since the minimal number of LMSs required to detect any entanglement is
K=2
, then the optimal state

for GME detection with

has to have
K=2
. Moreover, the maximal
p_{\textrm{limit}}
for

is achieved when
|C_{1}|=1
and
|C_{2}|=N-1

The graph state associated to a star graph is equivalent, up to local unitary transformation, to the GHZ state. The witness
\mathcal{W}^{(GHZ)}_{\mathrm{opt}}
tailored to the GHZ state is given by

\mathcal{W}^{(GHZ)}_{\mathrm{opt}}=(d+1)\mathbb{1}-d\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{1}^{n}+\prod_{i=2}^{N}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{i}^{n}\right),

(44)

where
G_{i}
are the generators of the GHZ stabilizer (

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{d-1}{2d-1-d^{2-N}}.

(45)

To compare those results with other states, we may consider the cluster state. The graph corresponding to the cluster state also has chromatic number
K=2
, but both colors have either the same number of generators or differ by one generator, for even and odd

\mathcal{W}^{(C)}_{\mathrm{opt}}=(d+1)\mathbb{1}-d\left(\prod_{\textrm{even }i}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{i}^{n}+\prod_{\textrm{odd }i}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{i}^{n}\right),

(46)

where
G_{i}
are generators of the cluster state stabilizer (
13
). As compared to the witness tailored to the GHZ state, this one is slightly less robust against white noise because the
critical value of
p
reads

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\begin{cases}&\displaystyle\frac{d-1}{2d-2d^{-N/2+1}}\quad\text{for even $N$},\\
&\displaystyle\frac{d-1}{2d-d^{-N/2+3/2}-d^{-N/2+1/2}}\quad\text{for odd $N$}.\end{cases}

(47)

of those states in 4 different cases.

V
Optimal Witnesses for GME stabilizer subspaces

In this section we first generalize the notions that we used for graph states to GME stabilized subspaces. Moreover, we show a method to determine the minimal number of LMSs needed to detect GME for every state in GME stabilized subspaces, analogous to the method for graph states. Then, we construct witnesses, including the most general results of this work - entanglement witnesses detecting GME for every state in the GME stabilized subspace and its vicinity.

V.1
Measures for entanglement witnesses for GME subspaces

Before we move to the construction of the optimal EW for a GME stabilized subspace, we first need to generalize the notion of critical probability
p_{\mathrm{limit}}
introduced in the previous sections to subspaces.

A natural generalization in the case of robustness against noise would be to consider the following noise model

\rho(p_{\textrm{noise}})=p_{\textrm{noise}}\frac{\mathbb{1}}{d^{N}}+(1-p_{\textrm{noise}})\rho_{V_{\mathbb{S}}},

(48)

where
\rho_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}
is an arbitrary state supported on
V_{\mathbb{S}}
.
p_{\textrm{limit}}
corresponding to this noise model is

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{-\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}\mathcal{W}\right)}{d^{-N}\text{Tr}\left(\mathcal{W}\right)-\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}\mathcal{W}\right)}.

(49)

Since, in general, such a value could in principle depend on the specific state, one can also define

p_{\textrm{limit}}(V_{\mathbb{S}})=\min_{|\psi\rangle\in V_{\mathbb{S}}}p_{\textrm{limit}}(|\psi\rangle).

(50)

However, it is not difficult to observe that as long as witnesses are constructed solely from a sum of stabilizing operators,
p_{\textrm{limit}}
is the same for any state
\rho_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}
, and so this issue does not manifest. Thus, in what follows, we consider a
p_{\textrm{limit}}
for a particular state, namely the maximally mixed state in the subspace
V_{\mathbb{S}}
:
\rho_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}=1/d^{N-k}P_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}
, where
P_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}
is a projector onto the
V_{\mathbb{S}}
and
k
is the number of generators of the stabilizer
\mathbb{S}
.

V.2
Determining number of LMSs for GME subspaces

We can also say something about determining LMSs for measuring stabilizer operators of a subspace. In the case of graph states, this problem was equivalent to finding the chromatic number of the corresponding graph. It turns out that a similar equivalence can be made in the case of a subspace.

First, we need to introduce the commutation matrices formalism, first proposed in Ref.
Englbrecht et al. 2022
. Consider a stabilizer
\mathbb{S}=\langle G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\rangle
. Recall that each
G_{i}=A_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes A_{N}
, where
A_{i}
are the Weyl-Heisenberg matrces that satisfy
A_{i}A_{j}-\omega^{\tau_{ij}}A_{j}A_{i}=0
, where
\tau_{ij}\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
is determined by the exact form of
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
. Now, with each site we associate a matrix
C^{\alpha}\in M_{k\times k}(\mathbb{Z}_{d})
such that
(C^{\alpha})_{ij}=\tau_{ij}
of
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
at site
\alpha
of
G_{i}
and
G_{j}
respectively. Let us then consider

\tilde{C}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}C^{\alpha},

(51)

where, importantly,
\tilde{C}\in M_{k\times k}(\mathbb{Z})
, i.e., the above sum is not modulo
d
. It is direct to see that if
(C)_{ij}=0
, then
g_{i}
and
g_{j}
commute locally.

With matrix
\tilde{C}
we can associate an adjacency matrix of the graph
\Gamma
such that if
(\tilde{C})_{ij}\neq 0
then
(\Gamma)_{ij}=1
and 0 otherwise. By analogy to the graph state case, it is clear that the chromatic number of this graph will correspond to the minimal number of LMSs needed to measure all generators, and that vertices in the same color correspond to stabilizer operators that are measurable in the same LMS. Indeed, in the case of qubit graph states
\tilde{C}/2=\Gamma
. Note, however, that this method has the same problem as in the case of graph states. Many graphs can be associated with the same stabilizer subspace and, in principle, their chromatic number
K
may differ. So to find a proper lower bound on the number of LMSs needed to detect GME, we should minimize
K
over all graphs that give rise to the same stabilized subspace.

To illustrate this formalism let us present two simple examples. First, let us consider the three-qubit GHZ state. We want to show that, indeed,
\tilde{C}/2=\Gamma
holds in this case.

The commutation matrices for each site for the GHZ state are given by

C^{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad C^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad C^{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\
1&0&0\end{bmatrix},

(52)

and their sum amounts to,

\tilde{C}=\begin{bmatrix}0&2&2\\
2&0&0\\
2&0&0\end{bmatrix}=2\Gamma_{\mathrm{GHZ}}.

(53)

The second example concerns a stabilizer subspace corresponding to the five-qubit code
Knill et al. 2001
. The generators of its stabilizer are given by

\displaystyle G_{1}=X_{1}Z_{2}Z_{3}X_{4},\qquad G_{2}=X_{2}Z_{3}Z_{4}X_{5},

(54)

\displaystyle G_{3}=X_{1}X_{3}Z_{4}Z_{5},\qquad G_{4}=Z_{1}X_{2}X_{4}Z_{5}.

In this case the matrix
\tilde{C}
equals

\tilde{C}=\begin{bmatrix}0&2&2&2\\
2&0&2&2\\
2&2&0&2\\
2&2&2&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(55)

Note that here it is also possible to associate a graph to this stabilizer by
\Gamma=\tilde{C}/2
, but that is not a general rule for all stabilizer subspaces.

V.3
Entanglement witnesses for subspaces

After considering the above, we can proceed to construct GME witnesses for GME stabilized subspaces. As in the case of graph states, let us start with projector-based witness, which are for given stabilizer subspace
V_{\mathbb{S}}
can be stated as

\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{1}-P_{V_{\mathbb{S}}},

(56)

where
P_{V_{\mathbb{S}}}
is the projector onto
V_{\mathbb{S}}
. The proof that this is a witness of GME follows the same lines as the proof of Theorem
1
, and therefore we skip it here.

Similarly to the projector-based witness for a graph state, this witness has

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{d-1}{d}\frac{1}{1-d^{-N+k}},

(57)

and the number of LMSs needed to detect GME with it grows linearly with the number of qudits
N
.
In order to lower the number of LMSs, we could construct a witness as in Theorem
2
, but let us move to the following construction.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a GME stabilized subspace with a corresponding stabilizer
\mathbb{S}=\langle G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(V_{\mathbb{S}})}_{\mathrm{opt}}=[(K-1)d+1]\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{j}^{n}

(58)

detects GME around an arbitrary GME stabilized subspace, where
K
is the chromatic number of a graph associated with the subspace in a way described in the previous section, and
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
is the corresponding coloring. Furthermore, this witness is the most robust against the noise among all witnesses
\mathcal{W}
that need a minimal number of LMSs and fulfil
\mathcal{W}\geq\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

Proof.

This proof follows the same idea as the proof of Theorem
3
, and so here we only mention its major steps, while highlighting the modifications needed to adapt it for stabilizer subspaces.

First, we have to slightly adapt the definition of the basis

\{|\vec{g}\rangle\}_{\vec{g}\in(\mathbb{C^{d}})^{\otimes N}}
as
the number of generators
k
can now be lower than
N
, which implies that
the relation

G_{i}|\vec{g}\rangle=\omega^{g_{i}}|\vec{g}\rangle,

(59)

does not identify states
|\vec{g}\rangle
uniquely. To fix that, we simply take
|g_{k+1},g_{k+2},\ldots,g_{N}\rangle
to be arbitrary orthonormal states.

With this, we can rewrite the witness

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(\mathcal{V_{\mathbb{S}}})}_{\mathrm{opt}}=c\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{n=0}^{d-1}G_{j}^{n},

(60)

as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(V_{\mathbb{S}})}_{\mathrm{opt}}=c\mathbb{1}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{\vec{g}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}}\left(\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\delta_{g_{j},0}\right)|\vec{g}\rangle\!\langle\vec{g}|.

(61)

Then, the threshold probability equals

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{dK-c}{dK-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}d^{-|C_{i}|}}.

(62)

Determining the minimal
c
via Lemma
1
leads us to the non-negativity condition of the following eigenvalues

\lambda_{\vec{g}}=c-\frac{\alpha}{d}+\alpha\delta_{\vec{g}_{k},\vec{0}_{k}}-d\sum_{i=1}^{K}\prod_{j\in C_{i}}\delta_{g_{j},0},

(63)

where
\vec{g}_{k}
is the vector consisting of the first
k
entries of
\vec{g}
. From this condition, we derive

c=(K-1)d+1,

(64)

which ends the proof.
∎

It follows from the above that

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{d-1}{d}\frac{1}{K-\sum_{i=1}^{K}d^{-|C_{i}|}},

(65)

so it may seem that there is no advantage in detecting entanglement around a subspace than around a state. However, this is not necessarily the case since
\sum_{i=1}^{K}|C_{i}|=k\leqslant N
, and so there is a potential for an increase in
p_{\textrm{limit}}
coming from the smaller number of generators of a stabilizer.

The simplest example in which this advantage can be witness is the stabilizer of the subspace of
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes d}
(i.e.,
N=d
) generated by

G_{1}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}X_{i},\quad G_{2}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}Z_{i}.

(66)

By
(
Makuta et al. 2023
, Theorem 1)
, this subspace is GME. For
d=2
, this is a stabilizer of a Bell state, which could be also seen as a GHZ state for
N=2
, and so there is no advantage. However, for any
d>2
the witness tailored to this subspaces produces a higher noise robustness than the one for GHZ, as for this subspace

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{1}{2}.

(67)

Note that, as opposed to any witness for a graph state, the noise robustness of this witness does not scale with
N
or
d
, and in fact, it achieves the maximal value of
p_{\textrm{limit}}
possible in this witness construction. However, there is a big caviat to this claim as this witness only works for subspaces of
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes d}
.

For the general case of
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes N}
, this construction can be generalized to the stabilizer generated by

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}X_{i},

(68)

\displaystyle G_{j}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=(d-1)(j-2)+1}^{(d-1)(j-1)+1}Z_{i}\quad\textrm{for }j\in\{2,\ldots,k-1\}

\displaystyle G_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=(d-1)(k-2)+1}^{N-1}Z_{i}\right)Z_{N}^{r},

where
r=d-1-(N-2\mod d-1)
and
k=\lceil(N-1)/(d-1)\rceil
. Clearly, in this case, the number of required LMSs remains two, but now the number of qudits
N
is independent of
d
. Moreover, for the case
N=d
, this stabilizer is equal to the one generated by (
66
). Interestingly, for
d=2
this construction reproduces the GHZ tailored witness, implying that the advantage over graph state witnesses is achieved only for
d>2
.

The threshold probability for the witness tailored to this stabilizer equals

p_{\textrm{limit}}=\frac{d-1}{2d-1-d^{-\lceil(N-1)/(d-1)\rceil+1}}.

(69)

While we cannot claim that this is the highest
p_{\textrm{limit}}
one can achieve with this witness construction, this is certainly a good candidate for such a maximum.

Entanglement witnesses beyond the stabilizer formalism

In this section, we slightly deviate from the main object of our work - qudits. Here, we limit ourselves to qubits to ease the computations, but we do not see any problems in generalizing the results below to higher local dimensions. Our aim here is to further develop the approach of Ref.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
, where it was shown that the construction of entanglement witnesses can be extended beyond the stabilizer formalism by considering non-local stabilizing operators. In the following, we define the
nonlocal stabilizer
of a subspace
V
, denoted
\mathbb{S}
, to be a group generated by
k
generators constructed from the sum of tensor products of Weyl-Heisenberg/Pauli matrices, such that

\forall_{|\psi\rangle\in V}\quad\forall_{S\in\mathbb{S}}\quad S|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle.

(70)

It might be confusing to refer to this group as non-local
stabilizer
since it is not a subgroup of the generalized Pauli group, but we decided to use this term because some of the properties of the stabilizer group relevant for our purpose carry over to the case of non-local operators.

To illustrate this method, let us consider the state
|0\ldots 0\rangle
whose stabilizer is
\mathbb{S}=\langle Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\rangle
, where
Z_{i}
is the (generalized)
Z
Pauli matrix acting on site
i
. Consider then a multipartite state
|\psi\rangle
that does not originate from the stabilizer formalism. One of the approaches to construct the non-local stabilizer of this state is to find a unitary matrix
U
such that

|\psi\rangle=U|0\ldots 0\rangle.

(71)

Then, it follows that the non-local stabilizer of
|\psi\rangle
is
\mathbb{S}=U\langle Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N}\rangle U^{\dagger}=:\langle S_{1},\ldots,S_{N}\rangle
. Note that the key point of this method is to allow non-local stabilizer operators, and the above is just one way to obtain them.

In the case of the
N
-qubit
|W\rangle
state, we found a class of unitary matrices fulfilling (
71
), with a simple decomposition into tensor products of Pauli matrices. Below, we present examples of these matrices in both the odd- and even-qubit case. In the first case, the matrix reads

\displaystyle U^{W_{N=2j+1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}(X\otimes\underbrace{Z\otimes\ldots\otimes Z}_{j}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{j}+

(72)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{1}\otimes X\otimes\underbrace{Z\otimes\ldots\otimes Z}_{j}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{j-1}+\ldots+

\displaystyle+Z\otimes\ldots\otimes Z\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{1}\otimes X).

Notice that each next element in this sum can be obtained by applying the permutation

\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\ldots&N\\
2&3&\ldots&1\end{pmatrix}

(73)

to the previous element. This, together with the fact that the first operator in the sum consists of
X
on the first site,
(N-1)/2

Z

N/2
or
N/2-1
in the case of even
N
. There are 2 choices because they both lead to
Z
matrix appearing

times in the whole operator, which is one of the conditions in this class of unitary matrices and
\mathbb{1}
at the rest of the sites, characterizes the class of unitaries we mentioned above.

For even
N
, the following unitary matrix fulfills
(
71
):

\displaystyle U^{W_{N=2j}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}[X\otimes\underbrace{Z\otimes\ldots\otimes Z}_{j-1}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{j}(Z)+

(74)

\displaystyle+Z\otimes X\otimes\underbrace{Z\otimes\ldots\otimes Z}_{j-1}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{j-1}(\mathbb{1})+\ldots+

\displaystyle+\underbrace{Z\otimes\ldots\otimes Z}_{j-2}\otimes\underbrace{\mathbb{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathbb{1}}_{j}\otimes Z\otimes X(Z)].

Notice that in each operator in the sum, there are
N+1
matrices. The (
N+1

73
) to the previous one, we have to do the following: we create a string of
N+1
elements (so including the operator in parentheses), and, to obtain the next string, we use permutation (
73

N

74

N
operators from each string.

In the case of an odd number of qubits
N
, we obtain the following generator of the
W

\displaystyle S_{1}^{W_{N=2j+1}}=\frac{1}{N}[2YY\mathbb{1}\ldots\mathbb{1}Z\mathbb{1}\ldots\mathbb{1}+

With these operators, we can construct non-local stabilizer witnesses as in previous sections. For instance

Theorem 5
.

Consider the
N
-qubit
W
state, where
N
is an odd number, and its non-local stabilizer
\mathbb{S}=\langle S_{1},\ldots,S_{N}\rangle
. The following operator

\mathcal{W}^{W_{N}=2j+1}=\frac{N^{2}-1}{N}\mathbb{1}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}S^{W_{N=2j+1}}_{k}

(76)

detects GME around the
N
-qubit
W
state.

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of Theorem
2
. We first consider
\mathcal{W}^{W_{2j+1}}=c\mathbb{1}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}S^{W_{2j+1}}_{k}
and, we find such
c
for which the inequality
\mathcal{W}^{W_{N=2j+1}}\geq\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}^{W_{N}}
holds for some positive
\alpha
and

\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}^{W_{N}}=\frac{N-1}{N}\mathbb{1}-|W^{N}\rangle\!\langle W^{N}|.

(77)

The above was proven to be a projector-based EW in
Tóth 2007
. Then, using Lemma
1
, we finish the proof.

To begin, the general expression for the eigenvalue of
A=\mathcal{W}^{W_{N}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}^{W_{N}}
is

\lambda=c-\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}(-1)^{q_{k}}+\alpha\prod_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}[1+(-1)^{q_{k}}].

(78)

There are two distinct cases when
\lambda
is the lowest. When
q_{k}=0
for all
k=1,\ldots,N
, or when some
q_{t}=1
and the rest of
q_{k}=0
for
k=1,\ldots t-1,t+1\ldots,N
. Those cases give us

\lambda_{1}=c-N-\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}+\alpha,\quad\lambda_{2}=c-N+1-\alpha\frac{N-1}{N}.

(79)

Imposing that both
\lambda_{1}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{2}\geq 0
, we obtain the optimal
c=(N^{2}-1)/N
.
∎

Unfortunately, this EW does not give us any advantage over the projector-based witness for the
W
state. It detects states mixed with white noise for any
p<1/N^{2}
, so it performs poorly even in small systems. Computing the number of LMSs required to use this witness is challenging, but for the 3- and 5-qubit examples, it proved less efficient than the projector-based witness. Since the operators are non-local, it no longer holds that the minimal number of LMSs required to detect GME is the number of LMSs required to measure all the generators of the stabilizer, e.g., by considering products of generators, we may obtain a witness that requires fewer LMSs. A perfect example is EW from
(
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
, Theorem 8)
, where, although it has still lower
p_{\textrm{limit}}
than the projector-based witness, it requires only three LMS, compared to 5 LMSs required by the projector-based EW.

It is also possible to construct an EW for subspaces in this formalism. In the case of a subspace spanned by two states
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}\rangle
, we have to find a unitary matrix
U
fulfilling
U|000\rangle=|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
U|001\rangle=|\psi_{2}\rangle
. For example, consider a subspace
V
spanned by the 3-qubit
\overline{W}
state and 3-qubit
W
state. Since we already have the non-local stabilizer of
|W\rangle
, we do not have to start from the beginning again. It can be checked that
V
is stabilized by
\mathbb{S}=\langle S^{W_{3,2}}_{1}S^{W_{3,2}}_{2},S^{W_{3,2}}_{1}S^{W_{3,2}}_{3}\rangle=:\langle S_{1}^{W,\overline{W}_{3,2}},S_{2}^{W,\overline{W}_{3,2}}\rangle
. With these operators, we can construct an EW similar to the one from
(
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
, Theorem 8)
, but first, we need to obtain a projector-based witness for this subspace.

Theorem 6
.

Consider a subspace
V
spanned by the 3-qubit
W
state and the 3-qubit
\overline{W}
. The following operator

\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}^{W,\overline{W}_{3,2}}=\frac{2}{3}\mathbb{1}-P_{V}

(80)

detects GME for every state in
V
and its vicinity.

Proof.

To prove that the above operator detects GME, we have to show that

c=\max_{Q|\overline{Q}}\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{P}}\langle\phi|P_{V}|\phi\rangle=\frac{2}{3}.

(81)

In
Demianowicz and Augusiak 2019
;
Branciard et al. 2010
it was proven that the above is equivalent to

c=\min_{|\psi\rangle\in V}\max_{Q|\overline{Q}}\max_{|\phi\rangle\in\mathcal{P}}|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^{2}=\frac{2}{3}.

(82)

From
Bourennane et al. 2004
it follows that the first maximum in the above is equivalent to the square of the maximum Schmidt coefficient
\lambda_{i}
of
|\psi\rangle
. Furthermore, both the GHZ and the
W
state are symmetric under swapping of qubits; thus, we can limit ourselves to considering just one particular bipartition. After these remarks, this proof reduces to showing that

c=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\psi\rangle=a|\overline{W}\rangle+b|W\rangle,\\
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1\end{subarray}}\max_{i}\lambda_{i}=\frac{2}{3}.

(83)

This problem can be solved analytically. First, we compute the Schmidt coefficients of
|\psi\rangle
and take the maximal one. Then, we minimize it over
a
and
b
with the constraint that
|a|^{2}+|b|^{2}=1
. Indeed, after these computations, one obtains
c=2/3
.
∎

With this result, we can construct a non-local stabilizer witness for the subspace
V
.

Consider the subspace
V

Our examples show that the problem with stabilizer EWs with non-local stabilizer operators is not that they are hard to construct. Indeed, we showed many examples that were not hard to obtain, and most of them are easy to generalize further. The problem is that the stabilizer witnesses were a promising alternative to projector-based EWs because they required fewer LMSs to implement, with worse, but still acceptable, noise robustness. In general, allowing for non-local stabilizer operators makes it harder to obtain an easy-to-implement EW. Yet, examples such as 3-qubit
W
state suggest that there still might be a place for them, but it would require a method to find the unitary matrices with the simplest decomposition into tensor products of Pauli/Weyl-Heisenberg matrices. Once the optimal non-local stabilizer operators are found, a method to find an EW with the fewest LMS would be needed, since, in the case of non-local stabilizer operators, it no longer holds that the lowest number of LMSs required to detect GME is the number of LMSs required to measure all generators of the stabilizer.

VII
Conclusions

In summary, we have generalized the genuinely multipartite entanglement witnesses with high noise robustness introduced in Ref.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
for multiqubit graph states in two directions: (i) to systems with arbitrary prime local dimension
d
, and (ii) to stabilizer subspaces of dimension greater than one.
In the first case, we have constructed GME entanglement witnesses tailored to graph states of higher local dimension than two. We have also shown that the best robustness to white noise is achieved by considering a witness tailored to the GHZ state.

For the latter, we showed how the construction can be extended to the cases in which the stabilizer does not specify a state, but rather a whole subspace of states. We have also shown that considering witnesses tailored to the stabilizer subspaces offers a clear advantage in noise robustness as compared to their graph state counterparts. In fact, for the case where the number of qudits equals the local dimension of a Hilbert space, we have formulated the most noise robust witness that can be created via this construction. For more general case of arbitrary local dimension and number of qudits, we have proposed a witness that offers a noise robustness higher than the one achieved by the graph state witness for any local dimension higher than two.

This work opens several avenues for further research. The most immediate is to identify, within this framework, entanglement witnesses that exhibit optimal noise robustness. While one candidate would be the stabilizer subspace and its corresponding witness which we proposed here, we have not provided a proof of optimality. Another promising line of research, explored already in Ref.
Tóth and Gühne 2005b
for some particular states, involves extending the method of constructing witnesses from tensor products of Pauli/Weyl-Heisenberg matrices to entangled quantum states beyond the stabilizer formalism, by considering sums of operators from the generalized Pauli group.
