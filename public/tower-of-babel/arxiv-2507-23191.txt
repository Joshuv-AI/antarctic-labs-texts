Tractable Responsibility Measures for Ontology-Mediated Query Answering
Meghyn Bienvenu, Diego Figueira, Pierre Lafourcade

Abstract
Recent work on quantitative approaches to explaining query answers employs responsibility measures to assign scores to facts in order to quantify their respective contributions to obtaining a given answer. In this paper, we study the complexity of computing such responsibility scores in the setting of ontology-mediated query answering, focusing on a very recently introduced family of Shapley-value-based responsibility measures defined in terms of weighted sums of minimal supports (WSMS). By exploiting results from the database setting, we can show that such measures enjoy polynomial data complexity for classes of ontology-mediated queries that are first-order-rewritable, whereas the problem becomes # ​ 𝖯 {\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{\#P}} -hard when the ontology language can encode reachability queries (via axioms like ∃ R . A ⊑ A \exists R.A\sqsubseteq A ). To better understand the tractability frontier, we next explore the combined complexity of WSMS computation. We prove that intractability applies already to atomic queries if the ontology language supports conjunction, as well as to unions of ‘well-behaved’ conjunctive queries, even in the absence of an ontology. By contrast, our study yields positive results for common DL-Lite dialects: by means of careful analysis, we identify classes of structurally restricted conjunctive queries (which intuitively disallow undesirable interactions between query atoms) that admit tractable WSMS computation.

Introduction

The question of how to explain query answers has received significant attention in both the database
and ontology settings. Qualitative notions of explanation, based e.g. on minimal supports of fact or proofs,
have been more extensively explored, particular in the ontology setting, cf.
[
\citeauthoryearBorgida, Calvanese, and
Rodriguez-Muro2008
,
\citeauthoryearAlrabbaa et al.2022
,
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Bourgaux, and
Goasdoué2019
,
\citeauthoryearCeylan et al.2019
,
\citeauthoryearCeylan et al.2020
]
.
However, there has been recent interest in quantitative notions of explanation based upon
responsibility measures
, which assign scores to the dataset facts
to quantify their respective contributions to obtaining a given answer.
Prior work on responsibility measures for query answers has predominantly focused on the so-called ‘drastic Shapley value’
[
\citeauthoryearLivshits et al.2021
,
\citeauthoryearDeutch et al.2022
,
\citeauthoryearKhalil and Kimelfeld
,
\citeauthoryearKara, Olteanu, and
Suciu2024
,
\citeauthoryearReshef, Kimelfeld, and
Livshits2020
,
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2024b
,
\citeauthoryearKarmakar et al.2024
,
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2024a
]
.
The drastic Shapley value is defined as the Shapley value of the 0/1 modeling of the (Boolean) query. It was motivated by the appealing theoretical characterization of the Shapley value as a concept in cooperative game theory, which is the only distribution of wealth among players respecting certain guarantees, known as ‘Shapley axioms’
[
\citeauthoryearShapley1953
]
. The choice of modelling the query as a 0/1 cooperative game, however, has not been justified.

Unfortunately, the computation of the drastic Shapley value is generally intractable (
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{\#P}}
-hard in data complexity),
even in the absence of ontologies and for very simple (conjunctive) queries
[
\citeauthoryearLivshits et al.2021
,
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2024b
]
.
Furthermore, it has recently been argued in
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2025a
]
that:
(i) not all Shapley axioms yield desirable properties when translated into the query answering setting, and
(ii) the genuinely desirable properties for responsibility measures of query answers do not pinpoint a single best
score function.

In light of this,
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2025a
]
has very recently proposed a family of responsibility measures, based on
weighted sums of minimal supports
(
WSMS
), where the score of a fact is defined as a weighted sum of the sizes of the query’s minimal supports containing it.
The cited work shows that WSMS satisfy several desirable properties and that they enjoy more favourable computational properties compared to the drastic Shapley value in the database setting. Further, WSMS can also be defined as the Shapley value of suitable cooperative games.

The positive results for WSMS in the database setting motivate us to investigate the complexity of computing WSMS responsibility scores in the more challenging setting of
ontology-mediated query answering
(
OMQA
)
[
\citeauthoryearPoggi et al.2008
,
\citeauthoryearBienvenu and
Ortiz2015
,
\citeauthoryearXiao et al.2018
]
. For this first study of WSMS
in the ontology setting, we focus on description logic (DL) ontologies
[
\citeauthoryearBaader et al.2017
]
,
paying particular attention to DLs of the DL-Lite family
[
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2007
]
, which are the most commonly adopted in OMQA,
due to their favourable computational properties.
We thus consider
ontology-mediated queries (OMQs)
of the form
(\mathcal{T},q)
,
where
\mathcal{T}
is formulated in some DL and
q
is either a conjunctive query (CQ) or atomic query.

Contributions

Our results show that the good computational behaviour of WSMS in the database setting exhibited in
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2025a
]
extends to some relevant classes of ontology-mediated queries.
This is in sharp contrast to the intractability of the drastic Shapley measure considered in the database
[
\citeauthoryearLivshits et al.2021
,
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2024b
]
and ontology
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2024a
]
settings.

More precisely, we observe that
WSMS computation is tractable in data complexity
for any UCQ-rewritable OMQ. In particular, this covers the class of OMQs
(\mathcal{T},q)
consisting of a DL-Lite ontology and CQ
q

(
Theorem

4
). We show in fact that WSMS computation for such OMQs can be implemented using relational database systems via simple SQL queries (
Theorem

5
&
Corollary

8
).
We further define a class of ‘well-behaved’ OMQs composed of DL-Lite ontologies and bounded
treewidth
CQs
for which we establish tractability also in combined complexity (corollary of
Theorem

15
).

We also identify DL constructs that render WSMS computation intractable.
In particular, we show that the data complexity becomes
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{\#P}}
-hard for classes of OMQs
exhibiting reachability behaviour, e.g. admitting axioms
\exists R.A\sqsubseteq A
(
Corollary

10
).
Furthermore,
the presence of concept conjunction, present in lightweight DLs like as
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{EL}}
and Horn dialects of DL-Lite,
leads to

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{\#P}}
-hardness in combined complexity (
Proposition

11
).
Furthermore, we show that while UCQ-rewritable OMQs enjoy tractable data complexity,
this is not the case for combined complexity,
even if the rewriting falls within a very restrictive fragment of UCQs (namely, acyclic and self-join free) (
Proposition

13
).

Organization

After reviewing basic terminology and notation,
we recall in
Section

3
the different responsibility measures, in particular, the recently introduced WSMS,
explain how they can be applied in the OMQA setting, and define the WSMS computation task.

Section

4
focuses on (in)tractability results on data complexity. The remaining sections consider combined complexity.

Section

5
presents (in)tractability results for OMQs based upon atomic queries.

Section

6
shows that UCQs are generally intractable for WSMS computation.
Finally,
Section

7
exhibits a condition ensuring polynomial-time tractability.
We finish with some concluding remarks in
Section

8
.

Preliminaries

We recall key definitions and notation concerning description logics
and ontology-mediated query answering.

Description Logic Knowledge Bases

A
description logic
(
DL
)
knowledge base
(
KB
)
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})
consists of an
ABox

\mathcal{A}
and a
TBox

\mathcal{T}
,
constructed from mutually disjoint sets
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{N_{C}}}
of
concept names
(unary predicates),
\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{N_{R}}}
of
role names
(binary predicates),
and
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
of
individual names
(constants).
An
inverse role
has the form
r^{-}
, with
r\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}
,
and we use
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{N}_{R}^{\pm}}={\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}\cup\{r^{-}\mid r\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}\}
for the set of
roles
.
The
ABox

is a finite set
of
concept assertions
of the form
A(c)
with
A\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{C}}},c\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
and

role assertions

r(b,c)
with
r\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}},b,c\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
.
We use
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
for the set of
individual names
in
\mathcal{A}
,
and
we write
R(b,c)
to mean
r(b,c)
if
R=r\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}

and
r(c,b)
if
R=r^{-}
.
The
TBox
(ontology)
is a finite set of
axioms

whose form depends on the DL in question.

Many of our results concern lightweight DLs of the
DL-Lite
family.
We shall in particular consider the
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}}}
dialect, whose TBox
axioms take the form of
concept inclusions

B\sqsubseteq C

and
role inclusions

R\sqsubseteq S
,
built according to the following grammar

\displaystyle B:=A\mid\exists R\quad C:=B\mid\neg B\quad S:=R\mid\neg R

where
A\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{C}}}
and
R\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N}_{R}^{\pm}}
.
The logic
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathsf{core}}}}
is obtained from
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}}
by disallowing role inclusions.
Another prominent lightweight DL is
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathcal{EL}}}
, whose
TBoxes consist of concept inclusions
D_{1}\sqsubseteq D_{2}

between
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{EL}}
-concepts
built as follows:

D:=\top\mid A\mid D\sqcap D\mid\exists r.D\qquad A\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{C}}},r\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}

The semantics of DL KBs is defined using

interpretations

\mathcal{I}=(\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},\cdot^{\mathcal{I}})
,
where the
domain

\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
is a non-empty set
and the interpretation function
{.}^{\mathcal{I}}
maps each
a\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
to
a^{\mathcal{I}}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
,
each
A\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{C}}}
to
A^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
,
each
r\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}
to
r^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\times\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
.
The function
\cdot^{\mathcal{I}}
is extended to complex concepts and roles:
\top^{\mathcal{I}}=\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
,

(\exists R)^{\mathcal{I}}=\{d\mid\exists e\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},(d,e)\in R^{\mathcal{I}}\}
,

(r^{-})^{\mathcal{I}}=\{(e,d)\mid(d,e)\in r^{\mathcal{I}}\}
,
(C\sqcap D)^{\mathcal{I}}=C^{\mathcal{I}}\cap D^{\mathcal{I}}
.
An interpretation
\mathcal{I}

satisfies an assertion

A(a)
(resp.
r(b,c)
) if
b^{\mathcal{I}}\in A^{\mathcal{I}}
(resp.
(b^{\mathcal{I}},c^{\mathcal{I}})\in r^{\mathcal{I}}
).

\mathcal{I}

satisfies a (concept or role) inclusion

G\sqsubseteq H
if
G^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq H^{\mathcal{I}}
.
We call
\mathcal{I}
a
model of a KB

\mathcal{K}
, denoted
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{K}
, if
\mathcal{I}
satisfies all axioms in
\mathcal{T}
(written
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{T}
) and all assertions in
\mathcal{A}
(written
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{A}
).
It will also be convenient to introduce notation
\mathcal{K}\models\exists R(a)
(with
R\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N}_{R}^{\pm}}
) to indicate that
a^{\mathcal{I}}\in(\exists R)^{\mathcal{I}}
in every model
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{K}
.
We use
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{Mod}}}(\mathcal{K})
for the set of models of
\mathcal{K}
.
A KB
\mathcal{K}
is
consistent
if it has a model.

Databases

While our main interest is in DL KBs, we shall import definitions and techniques from the database literature,
so we briefly introduce some notation and terminology for databases. Formally, a
(relational)
database

\mathcal{D}

is a finite set of relational
facts

P(\vec{a})
, where
P
is a relational predicate of arity
k\geq 1

and
\vec{a}
is a
k
-ary vector of
constants
(which we may assume drawn from
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
).
The
signature
of
\mathcal{D}
is the set of predicates that occur in the facts of
\mathcal{D}
,
and we speak of a binary
signature
if only unary and binary predicates are used.
We will use
\alpha\in\mathcal{D}
to indicate that fact
\alpha
occurs in
\mathcal{D}
.

Every
database

\mathcal{D}
can be equivalently viewed as a finite first-order interpretation
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}

whose
domain
is the set of constants in
\mathcal{D}
, which interprets every constant from
\mathcal{D}
as itself,
and which interprets each predicate
P

as follows:
P^{\mathcal{I}}=\{\vec{a}\mid P(\vec{a})\in\mathcal{D}\}
.
Moreover, it will sometimes prove convenient to treat an ABox
\mathcal{A}
as a
database
and to consider the associated finite interpretation
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Queries

An (abstract, non-numeric)
query
of arity
k\geq 0
is a function that
maps every first-order interpretation
\mathcal{I}
to a set
q(\mathcal{I})
of
k
-tuples of
domain
elements.
We will mostly work with
Boolean queries
, of arity 0, for which

q(\mathcal{I})
can only take two values:
\emptyset
which we interpret as ‘false’, and
\{()\}
which we interpret as ‘true’.
In the latter case, we write
\mathcal{I}\models q
.
Queries may also be (and in fact are usually) evaluated over databases,
in which case
q(\mathcal{D})
returns a set of tuples of constants from
\mathcal{D}
(called
answers
)
and
\mathcal{D}\models q
means that
q
evaluates to ‘true’ in
the associated interpretation
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{D}}
.

We consider various concrete classes of

first-order queries
(
FO
-queries for short),
which are defined as first-order logic formulas.
Note that when querying DL KBs, the relational atoms in queries
will use predicates from
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{C}}}\cup{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}
, whereas in the database context,
the atoms will use the available database predicates.
The most prominent class of
FO
queries is
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{CQ}}}
, the class of
conjunctive queries
, which are defined by formulas of the form
q(\vec{x})=\exists\vec{y}.\alpha_{1}\land\dots\land\alpha_{n}
where the
\alpha_{i}
are relational
atoms
that can contain
constants
and/or
variables
in
{\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\textit{vars}}}(q)\vcentcolon=\vec{x}\cup\vec{y}
, where
\vec{x}
is the vector of
free variables
of the formula.
A
CQ

q
can be partitioned into is
connected components
which are the inclusion-maximal
connected
subqueries of
q
.
When a
CQ
has no
free variables
it is naturally
Boolean
.
We shall also consider the subclass
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{AQ}}}
of
CQs
with a single
atom
called
atomic queries
(AQs),
the class
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{UCQ}}}
of
unions of conjunctive queries

defined as finite disjunctions of
CQ
s with the same set of free variables, and the class

\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{(U)CQ}^{\neq}}{\immediate}
of
(U)CQ

with inequality atoms.

We will often treat
CQs
as sets of atoms and use notation like
\alpha\in q
to indicate that atom
\alpha
is a conjunct of the CQ
q
.
It is well known that when
q
is a constant-free
Boolean

CQ
,
\mathcal{I}\models q
iff there is a homomorphism
h:q\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\xrightarrow{\smash{\textit{ \!hom}}}}}\mathcal{I}
, i.e. a function
h
from
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(q)
to
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
P(\vec{x})\in q
implies that
h(\vec{x})\in P^{\mathcal{I}}
(or
P(h(\vec{x}))\in\mathcal{D}
if we
evaluate
q
over a database
\mathcal{D}
). This characterization extends to
Boolean

CQs
with constants by adding the requirement that

h

maps every constant
c
to
c^{\mathcal{I}}
(or to
c
itself, if we work with databases).
Note that one can define in the same manner homomorphisms between two queries or between two interpretations.

Ontology-Mediated Query Answering

We say that a
Boolean query

q
is
entailed from a KB

\mathcal{K}
,
written
\mathcal{K}\models q
, if
\mathcal{I}\models q
for every
\mathcal{I}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{Mod}}(\mathcal{K})
.
The
certain answers
to a non-
Boolean

k
-ary query
q(\vec{x})

w.r.t.
a
KB

\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})

are the tuples
\vec{a}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})^{k}
such that
\mathcal{K}\models q(\vec{a})
,
with
q(\vec{a})
the
Boolean query
obtained by substituting
\vec{a}
for

\vec{x}
.

Alternatively, we can
treat
\mathcal{T}
and
q
together as constituting a composite
ontology-mediated query
(
OMQ
)
Q=(\mathcal{T},q)
,
in which case we will write
\mathcal{A}\models(\mathcal{T},q)

to mean
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})\models q
.
The notation
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{Q)}
will be used to designate the class of all OMQs
(\mathcal{T},q)
consisting of a TBox formulated in the DL
\mathcal{L}

and a query
q\in\mathcal{Q}
.

A common approach to computing certain answers (or checking query entailment) is to rewrite an
OMQ
into another query that can be directly evaluated using a database system. Formally, we call a query
q^{*}(\vec{x})
a
rewriting of an OMQ

(\mathcal{T},q)
if for every
ABox

\mathcal{A}
and candidate answer
\vec{a}
:

\mathcal{A}\models(\mathcal{T},q(\vec{a}))\quad\text{ iff }\quad\mathcal{A}\models q^{*}(\vec{a})

If we restrict the above definition by requiring that
q^{*}
belong to a class of queries

\mathcal{C}
(
e.g.

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{FO}}
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{UCQ}^{\neq}}
), we call it a
\mathcal{C}
-rewriting
.
Several dialects of DL-Lite, including
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}}
, are known to guarantee the existence of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{FO}}
-rewritings,
meaning that every OMQ from
({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})
possesses an FO-rewriting. Moreover, such rewritings
in fact can be expressed in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{UCQ}}
(or in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{UCQ}^{\neq}}
, if the DL admits functional roles or number restrictions).

In Horn DLs, like
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{EL}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}}
, every
consistent

KB

\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})
admits a
canonical model

\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}

with the property that for every model
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{K}
, there is a homomorphism
h:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\xrightarrow{\smash{\textit{ \!hom}}}}}\mathcal{J}
that is the identity on
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
.
Importantly, if
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})
admits a
canonical model

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
, then for every

CQ

q
and candidate answer tuple
\vec{a}
:

\mathcal{K}\models q(\vec{a})\quad\text{ iff }\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}\models q(\vec{a})

The precise definition of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
depends on the particular Horn DL. In the case of
\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}
,
the
domain

\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}}
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
consists of all words
aR_{1}\ldots R_{n}
(
n\geq 0
) such that
a\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
,
R_{i}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N}_{R}^{\pm}}
, and:

•

if
n\geq 1
, then
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})\models\exists R_{1}(a)
and there is no
b\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
such that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})\models R_{1}(a,b)

•

for
1\leq i<n
,
\mathcal{T}\models\exists R_{i}^{-}\sqsubseteq\exists R_{i+1}
and
R_{i}^{-}\neq R_{i+1}
.

Elements in
\Delta^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}}\setminus{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
will be
called
anonymous elements
.

Responsibility Measures & Shapley Value

In this section, we recall the notion of responsibility measure, which provides
quantitative explanations of query answers,
and some concrete Shapley-value-based responsibility measures.
We also explain and illustrate how these notions, defined for databases, transfer to the OMQA setting.

3.1
Responsibility Measures for Query Answers

Although we shall be interested in employing responsibility measures
to quantify the contribution of facts to obtaining an

answer

\vec{a}

to a query
q(\vec{x})
,
it will actually be more convenient to consider the equivalent task of quantifying contributions to
satisfying
the associated Boolean query

q(\vec{a})
(obtained by instantiating the
free variables

\vec{x}
of
q
with
\vec{a}
).
For this reason, in the remainder of the paper,
we shall w.l.o.g. restrict ourselves to
Boolean queries
.

We shall further focus on
monotone
Boolean queries, defined in the database setting
as queries
q
such that
\mathcal{D}_{1}\models q\Rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{2}\models q
whenever
\mathcal{D}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}
.
Such queries notably include the class of homomorphism-closed queries,
which covers most well-known classes of
OMQ

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{UCQ}^{\neq}}

Note that a natural qualitative approach to explaining why a
monotone

Boolean query

q

\mathcal{D}
is to consider the set
{\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{MS}_{q}}}(\mathcal{D})

minimal supports
of

in
\mathcal{D}
, defined as
inclusion-minimal subsets

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
s.t.
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\models q
.

Our focus will be on providing
quantitative
explanations in the form of

responsibility measures
, which are functions that assign a score to every
fact
in the data, reflecting their contributions to making the query hold.
Such measures have been formally defined, in the database setting,
as ternary functions

database

\mathcal{D}

(Boolean) query

3.2
Shapley-Based Responsibility Measures

The
responsibility measures
we consider in this paper are based on the ‘
Shapley value
’. Originally defined in
[
\citeauthoryearShapley1953
]
, it takes as input a cooperative game consisting of a finite set
P
of players and a
wealth function

\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathbf{\xi}}:2^{P}\to\mathbb{Q}
that assigns a value to each coalition (
i.e.
, set) of players, with
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}}(\emptyset)=0
. The
Shapley value
then assigns to each player
p\in P
a value
\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathrm{Sh}}(P,{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}},p)
that should be seen as a fair share of the total wealth
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}}(P)
of the game that should be awarded to
p
based on the respective contributions of all players.
By “fair share” we mean that the
Shapley value
is, provably, the only wealth distribution scheme that satisfies a very natural set of axioms
[
\citeauthoryearShapley1953
, Axioms 1 to 3]
.

To obtain a
responsibility measure
from the
Shapley value
, one
needs to
model the input instance
(\mathcal{D},q)
as a
cooperative game

(P,\xi)
. The set
P
contains
the elements that will receive a score, hence it should naturally be the set
\mathcal{D}
itself. As for the
wealth function
, it must assign a numerical score to every database, reflecting in some way the satisfaction of the query.
Formally, one needs to provide a
wealth function family

\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\Xi}^{\star}\vcentcolon=({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}}^{\star}_{q})_{q}
which associates a
wealth function

\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathbf{\xi}}^{\star}_{q}
with each query
q
. A
responsibility measure
can be straightforwardly obtained by applying the
Shapley value
to the game
(\mathcal{D},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}}^{\star}_{q})
:

\phi(\mathcal{D},q,\alpha)\vcentcolon={\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathrm{Sh}}(\mathcal{D},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}}^{\star}_{q},\alpha)

The first
wealth function family
that was considered in the literature is
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\Xi}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\textsf{dr}}}
, defined by:
\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathbf{\xi}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\textsf{dr}}}_{q}(\mathcal{D})\vcentcolon=1
if
\mathcal{D}\models q
and
0
otherwise
[
\citeauthoryearLivshits et al.2021
]
, which gives rise to the
drastic Shapley value

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathrm{Sh}}(\mathcal{D},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathbf{\xi}}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsf{dr}}}_{q},\alpha)
. In fact,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\Xi}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsf{dr}}}
was until recently the only
wealth function
used to define Shapley-based responsibility measures for Boolean queries.
2
2

2

drastic Shapley value
is simply called ‘Shapley value’ in many papers.

Very recently, however, a new family of

called

\immediate\immediate\phi_{\mathsf{wsms}}^{w}(\mathcal{D},q,\alpha)\vcentcolon=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{MS}_{q}}(\mathcal{D})\\

It has been shown that all WSMSs can be equivalently defined via the Shapley value: for every

naturally build upon existing results from the database setting

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\Xi}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsf{s}}}

4.1
Tractability Result for Rewritable OMQs

With this lemma at hand, we can forget about the particular weight function
w
and
concentrate on the conceptually simpler task
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{Q}^{\textsf{fms}}}
of counting the number of minimal supports, per size.
This can be achieved in polynomial time (in data complexity) whenever there is a data-independent bound on the
size of minimal supports,
since we can then simply iterate over all bounded-size subsets of the ABox. In particular, this holds for OMQs that can be rewritten into a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{UCQ}^{\neq}}
, yielding the following tractability result:

Theorem
4
.

(Corollary of
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2025a
, Theorem 5.2]
)

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textup{SVC}^{w}_{Q}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{FP}}
for every

tractable weight function

w
and every
Boolean

OMQ

Q
that is
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{UCQ}^{\neq}}
-rewritable

\mu_{\alpha}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\alpha)\to{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(f)}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\circ}}
,
\mu_{\beta}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\beta)\to{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(f)}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\circ}}
, we cannot have both
(\{f\},\mathcal{T}){\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\models}_{\mu_{\alpha}}\alpha
and
(\{f\},\mathcal{T}){\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\models}_{\mu_{\beta}}\beta
, unless
(\alpha,\mu_{\alpha})=(\beta,\mu_{\beta})
.
We denote by
\immediate\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0.3633,0.0781,0.0859}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}

the class of all
interaction-free

OMQs
in
({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})
.

We believe
that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
is a practically relevant class of OMQs: 8 of the 14 queries from the well-known LUBM benchmark
[
\citeauthoryearGuo, Pan, and
Heflin2005
]
correspond to
interaction-free
OMQs and the remaining 6 are
interaction-free
after removing obviously redundant atoms.
In the case where

\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
, the above condition can only be violated by two distinct atoms
\alpha,\beta
and two assignments
\mu_{\alpha}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\alpha)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}},\mu_{\beta}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\beta)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
(with no
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{anon}}
in their image) such that
\mu_{\alpha}(\alpha)=\mu_{\beta}(\beta)
. As it turns out, this corresponds to saying that
\alpha
and
\beta
are ‘
mergeable
’ in the jargon of
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Figueira, and
Lafourcade2025a
, §6.3]
.
The notion of
interaction-free
thus generalizes the notion of queries with no
mergeable
atoms (corresponding to
self-join width
0)
, which includes all
self-join free

CQs
.

Example 14
.

1.

The
OMQ

(\emptyset,\exists x.r(c,x)\land r(d,x))
,
with distinct
c,d\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{I}}}
, is
interaction-free
despite its
self-join
, because no fact can satisfy both
r(c,x)
and
r(d,x)
.

2.

The OMQ
(\{\exists r\sqsubseteq A;\exists r^{-}\sqsubseteq A\},\exists x.A(x))
isn’t
interaction-free
despite the query having a single atom, because the fact
r(c,d)
satisfies it in two different ways (
x\mapsto c
and
x\mapsto d
).

3.

The OMQ
(\exists x,y.A(x)\land r(x,y),\{A\sqsubseteq\exists r\})
isn’t
interaction-free
either, because the fact
A(c)
would satisfy both atoms thanks to the ontology.

∎

7.2
Theorem Statement and Proof Idea

As previously mentioned,
we aim to reduce the problem of counting the
minimal supports
of the input OMQ to counting the minimal supports of its component atomic OMQs. Formally, we prove:

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a subclass of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
such that the set of queries
\{q\mid(\mathcal{T},q)\in\mathcal{C}\}
has bounded
treewidth
.
Then
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{\mathcal{C}}^{\textsf{fms}}}\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\leqslant_{\mathsf{P}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{AQ}})}^{\textsf{fms}}}
. Further,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{\mathcal{C}}^{\textsf{fms}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{FP}}
.

For the proof of
Theorem

15
, we focus on the reduction, since the tractability will readily follow from it and
Proposition

12
. Before diving into the details, we first give the following incomplete but informative formula:

\immediate\immediate{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q)}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})\approx\sum_{\mu:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(q)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})}\prod_{\alpha\in q}{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},\mu(\alpha))}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})

(2)

Intuitively, this formula enumerates every possible assignment
\mu
, computes the number of
minimal supports
associated with
\mu
by multiplying the number of possibilities for each atom,
then sums everything up. This formula does not give the correct result for all OMQs in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
(hence the
\approx
), for reasons that will be explained and addressed in
Section

7.4
, but it does work in many cases thanks to the following consequence of the absence of
interactions
.

Lemma
16
.

Let
(\mathcal{T},q)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
, and
\mathcal{A}
be an
ABox
. Then for every assignment
\mu:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(q)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
:

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},\mu(q))}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})=\prod_{\alpha\in q}{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},\mu(\alpha))}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})

Even when
Equation

2
holds, it has two issues:
(a) it does not directly yield a polynomial-time procedure
as it sums over an exponential number of mappings
\mu
(this will be addressed in
Section

7.3
); and
(b)
it computes the value of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#^{\textsf{ms}}}
while we actually need
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#^{\textsf{fms}}}
. However, (b) is not actually a problem: as we observed in the proof of
Lemma

16
,
\mathcal{T}

has singleton supports
and
q
is
interaction-free
, so the
minimal supports
for
(\mathcal{T},q)
all have the same size as
q
.

7.3
Efficient Summation Over Assignments

Observe that in
Equation

2
we can ignore all assignments
\mu
such that
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}){\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\not\models}_{\mu}q
since the summand is 0. In other words,
Equation

2

is a sum over all
homomorphisms

h:q\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
whose image is contained in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
.
As it turns out, efficient summation over
homomorphisms
has been studied in the context of databases annotated with semirings, or

weighted databases
. These are defined as

\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}^{\dagger},\omega)
, where
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}
is a
database
and
\omega:\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}\to\mathbb{N}
assigns, to each
fact
, a ‘weight’.
The weight
q(\mathcal{D})\in\mathbb{N}
associated to the evaluation of a

Boolean

CQ

q=\exists\vec{x}.\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}
to a
weighted database

\mathcal{D}
is

q(\mathcal{D})\vcentcolon=\sum_{h:q\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\xrightarrow{\smash{\textit{ \!hom}}}}}\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\omega(h(\alpha_{i}))
.

Equation

2

can thus be seen as

q
applied to the
weighted database

\mathcal{D}^{q}_{\mathcal{A}}\vcentcolon=(\mathcal{D}^{\dagger},\{\beta\mapsto{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},\beta)}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}})
for
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}=\{\mu(\alpha)\mid\alpha\in q,\mu:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\alpha)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})\}
.
Since each atom of
q
contains at most 2 variables,

\mathcal{D}^{q}_{\mathcal{A}}
has at most
|q|\cdot|{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})|^{2}

facts
.
For each
fact

\beta\in\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}
, the weight
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},\beta)}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})
can be computed by a call to the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{AQ}})}^{\textsf{ms}}}
oracle.
Overall,
\mathcal{D}^{q}_{\mathcal{A}}
can be built in polynomial time with the oracle. The last step is to compute

q(\mathcal{D}^{q}_{\mathcal{A}})
,
which can be done in polynomial time for any class of
CQs
with bounded
treewidth
.
7
7

7

This is a trivial adaptation of the algorithm of
[
\citeauthoryearFlum and Grohe2004
, Proposition 3.5]
for counting homomorphisms.
It is also a basic case of the more general tractability results of
[
\citeauthoryearKhamis, Ngo, and Rudra2016
,
\citeauthoryearJoglekar, Puttagunta, and
Ré2016
]
.

7.4
Variables Mapped Outside the ABox

As mentioned earlier,
Equation

2
is inaccurate with respect to
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}}
, as evidenced by the following example.

Example 17
.

Take
\mathcal{T}=\{A\sqsubseteq\exists r;\exists r^{-}\sqsubseteq B\}
,
q=\exists x.B(x)
and
\mathcal{A}=\{A(c)\}
. Then the left-hand side of
Equation

2
equals
1
because
\mathcal{A}
is a minimal support for
(\mathcal{T},q)
, but the right-hand side equals 0 because the only possible
\mu
is
x\mapsto c
, but
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T})\not\models B(c)
.
∎

The issue highlighted by
Example

17
is that some
minimal supports
may only be witnessed by homomorphisms
that map some variable to an
anonymous element
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
rather than an ABox
individual
. Such minimal supports will thus
be missed by
Equation

2
.
However, by exploiting the structure of canonical models in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}}
, we can show that the
interaction-free
condition ensures that all

shared variables

(i.e. variables
x\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(q)
that appear in multiple atoms of
q
)
are necessarily mapped to ABox individuals:

Lemma 18
.

Let
Q=(\mathcal{T},q)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
and
x
be a
shared variable
of
q
. Then, for every
homomorphism

h:q\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\xrightarrow{\smash{\textit{ \!hom}}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
,
we have
h(x)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Assume
for a contradiction that
h:q\mathrel{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\xrightarrow{\smash{\textit{ \!hom}}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
and
h(x)\not\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
for some shared variable
x
.
From the definition of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
, we know that
h(x)=aR_{1}\ldots R_{n}
for some
a\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
and roles
R_{i}
.
For convenience we suppose
R_{1}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N_{R}}}
, but the proof is analogous if
R_{1}
is an inverse role.
As
x
is shared, it must appear in at least two distinct atoms
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
of
q
.
If
\alpha_{i}=A(x)
, then we must have
h(x)\in A^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}}
.
If
\alpha_{i}=s(x,y)
or
\alpha_{i}=s(y,x)
, then
h(y)
must either be equal to the unique ‘predecessor’ of
h(x)
, which is
aR_{1}\ldots R_{n-1}
,
or to some immediate ‘successor’ of
h(x)
, which must have the form
aR_{1}\ldots R_{n}R_{n+1}
for some role
R_{n+1}
. This follows from the way roles are interpreted in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
.
Thus, all terms in
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
must be mapped by
h
either to
a
or to some anonymous element with prefix
aR_{1}
.

The existence of
aR_{1}\ldots R_{n}
in the domain
means there is some assertion
f\in\mathcal{A}
such that
(\{f\},\mathcal{T})\models\exists R_{1}(a)
and
(\{f\},\mathcal{T})\not\models R_{1}(a,b)
for any individual
b
.
It follows that

{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\{f\},\mathcal{T}}}
will also contain
aR_{1}
, and in fact all of the anonymous elements of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
having prefix
aR_{1}
, and it will interpret concept and role names on these elements in precisely the same way as in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{T}}}
. Thus,
h
witnesses the satisfaction of
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}

in
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathcal{I}_{\{f\},\mathcal{T}}}
. But this means that we can use
h
to define
assignments
\mu_{1}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\alpha_{1})\to{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(f)}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\circ}}
,
\mu_{2}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(\alpha_{2})\to{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(f)}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\circ}}

such that both
(\{f\},\mathcal{T}){\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\models}_{\mu_{1}}\alpha_{1}
and
(\{f\},\mathcal{T}){\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\models}_{\mu_{2}}\alpha_{2}
.
This is impossible as
\alpha_{1}\neq\alpha_{2}
and
(\mathcal{T},q)
is
interaction-free
.
∎

We
now have three kinds of query atoms to consider. First, those that contain no
shared variables
,
which can be treated by a separate call to the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{AQ}})}^{\textsf{ms}}}
oracle, due to the following easy lemma:

Lemma
19
.

Let
(\mathcal{T},q)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
with
q=q_{1}\land q_{2}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(q_{1})\cap{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{vars}}(q_{2})=\emptyset
. Then for any
ABox

\mathcal{A}
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q)}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})={\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q_{1})}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})\times{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q_{2})}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})
.

Next, we have the atoms that contain only
shared variables
. By
Lemma

18
, their variables must
be mapped to
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
, hence such atoms are already accounted for by the
weighted database

\mathcal{D}^{(\mathcal{T},q)}_{\mathcal{A}}
built in
Section

7.3
.

The third class of atoms are
the role atoms which contain one
shared variable
and one
unshared variable
.
These will be addressed by constructing a
weighted database

\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{(\mathcal{T},q)}_{\mathcal{A}}
that extends

\mathcal{D}^{(\mathcal{T},q)}_{\mathcal{A}}
with some extra facts.
Consider one such atom
\alpha=R(x,y)\in q
, with
x
and
y
being
shared
and
unshared
respectively, and
R\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{N}_{R}^{\pm}}
(since the
unshared variable
could come first).
Again by
Lemma

18
,
we know that
x
must necessarily be instantiated by an
individual

c\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
, but

y
might be mapped to an
anonymous element
. We thus add to
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{(\mathcal{T},q)}_{\mathcal{A}}
a fact
R(c,c_{\alpha})
, with
c_{\alpha}
a fresh
individual
, for every
individual

c\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textit{ind}}(\mathcal{A})
such that, for some

f\in\mathcal{A}
,
(\{f\},\mathcal{T})\models\exists R(c)
but
(\{f\},\mathcal{T})\not\models R(c,d)
for all
d\in\mathcal{A}
, and set the weight of this
R(c,c_{\alpha})
to be the number of such
\{f\}
.

Lemma
20
.

Let
(\mathcal{T},q)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{itf\text{-}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{CQ}})}
such that
q
is
connected
and
|q|\geqslant 2
. Then
q\left(\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{(\mathcal{T},q)}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)={\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q)}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})
.

7.5
Putting everything together

The construction has been presented in a progressive manner for ease of understanding.
We now
recapitulate the argument in a more direct fashion.

Proof of
Theorem

15
.

The algorithm goes as follows. For every
connected component

q_{c}
of
q
with at least 2 atoms we:
(1) build the
weighted database

\mathcal{D}^{q_{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}
described in
Section

7.3
using the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{AQ}})}^{\textsf{ms}}}
oracle;
(2) extend
\mathcal{D}^{q_{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}
into
\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{q_{c}}_{\mathcal{A}}
as described in
Section

7.4
;
(3) compute
q_{c}(\overline{\mathcal{D}}^{q_{c}}_{\mathcal{A}})
using standard
weighted database
algorithms.

By
Lemma

20
, this yields the value of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q_{c})}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})
.
The remaining
connected components
consist in a single atom, so the corresponding value can then be obtained by a direct call to the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{({\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\text{DL-Lite}_{\mathcal{R}}},{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{AQ}})}^{\textsf{ms}}}
oracle.
Once all the values are obtained, we finally multiply them all together, which yields the desired
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\#_{(\mathcal{T},q)}^{\textsf{ms}}}(\mathcal{A})
by
Lemma

19
.

Regarding the consequence that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\textsc{eval-}\#_{\mathcal{C}}^{\textsf{fms}}}\in{\color[rgb]{0,0.2344,0.2773}\mathsf{FP}}
, this is a direct application of
Proposition

12

to the above.
∎

8
Conclusion and Future Work

Our work explores the recently introduced class of Shapley-based responsibility measures, known as WSMS, in the context of ontology-mediated query answering.
Our complexity analysis
pinpoints sources of intractability but also identifies relevant classes of OMQs for which WSMS computation is tractable in data (and sometimes also combined) complexity and can moreover be computed using standard database systems. It would be interesting in future work to test out the approach in practice
and try to generalize the ‘interaction-free’ condition to identify further tractable cases.

While we focused on DLs,
many results extend to other ontology formalisms such as existential rules. In particular, the data tractability result extends to UCQ-rewritable rulesets, and the tractability result for atomic queries
extends to bounded-arity linear existential rules because they satisfy the conditions of
Proposition

12
. An interesting future step would be to see if a useful notion of ‘interaction-free’ could be defined in order to obtain tractability in combined complexity for linear existential rules with CQs.
