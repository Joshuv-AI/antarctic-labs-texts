Development and evaluation of a novel voice-enabled prototype to support consistent application of surgical safety checklists: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Pedro Miguel Inácio Medroa, Marcel Saltan, Sandro-Michael Heining, Kerstin Denecke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical safety checklists are essential for reducing errors and improving outcomes, but consistent, full-phase adherence remains suboptimal. Traditional paper-based systems can disrupt workflow and limit team engagement. Voice-enabled solutions offer potential to improve compliance while preserving sterile conditions and communication flow in the operating room (OR).MethodsThis study aimed to develop and evaluate a prototype voice-controlled digital assistant for surgical checklists - referred to as ‘VoiceCheck’ – with a focus on usability, technical performance, and feasibility in real-world clinical settings. VoiceCheck guides clinical teams through the “Sign In”, “Time-out”, and “Sign Out” phases of the surgical checklist using voice commands and speech transcription. The system operates offline, incorporating Rhasspy for intent recognition and Whisper for speech-to-text transcription. Twelve surgical team members participated in a field evaluation, completing structured tasks alongside routine workflows. Usability was assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and a custom questionnaire. Technical evaluations tested Whisper V2 and V3 under quiet and noisy conditions, and Rhasspy’s intent and wake word recognition across 1’125 commands in two environments (noisy, quiet).ResultsParticipants rated usability positively (SUS median score 76.04). Whisper V3 achieved 90.7–97.3% transcription accuracy, outperforming V2 in noisy settings. Rhasspy recognized intents with 92.8–94.8% accuracy and maintained a low false-positive rate. VoiceCheck functioned reliably offline and was preferred with a conference microphone for multi-user interaction.ConclusionVoiceCheck demonstrates feasibility for voice-assisted checklist execution in surgical settings. It was well accepted by users and performed reliably under realistic conditions. Further research should explore clinical integration, workflow impact, and multilingual capabilities.

Background

Surgical care, while essential and often lifesaving, remains a high-risk domain within healthcare. Globally, over 310 million surgical procedures are performed each year, and a significant proportion of in-hospital adverse events—up to 50% in some studies—are directly related to surgical interventions [1, 2]. These events can lead to severe patient harm, increased healthcare costs, and diminished trust in healthcare systems. In response to these challenges, the World Health Organization (WHO) developed the Surgical Safety Checklist (SSC), a structured tool aimed at improving the safety and consistency of perioperative care. This 19-item checklist is designed to be used at three critical junctures during surgery—before induction of anesthesia (“Sign In”), before incision (“Team Time-out”), and before the patient leaves the operating room (“Sign Out”)—to enhance team communication and ensure that essential safety steps are not overlooked [3].

The impact of the SSC has been well documented: multiple studies and meta-analyses have shown significant reductions in postoperative complications, surgical-site infections, and mortality following its implementation [3, 4]. However, its real-world adoption and consistent use have proven more challenging to sustain. In Switzerland, national efforts such as the “Sichere Chirurgie” initiative [5] have promoted high adoption rates, with 99% of surgeries using at least part of a checklist. Full and consistent execution across all three phases remains suboptimal, with complete adherence achieved in only 79% of cases [6]. This gap underscores the need for systems that promote not only checklist completion but also meaningful engagement by surgical teams. In many cases, only partial completion is recorded, and the checklist is often reduced to a rote or superficial exercise, with limited impact on actual surgical practice [7]. These shortcomings reveal a critical gap between the availability of an evidence-based safety tool and its practical, reliable execution in the complex and time-pressured environment of the operating room.

From a medical informatics perspective, this gap presents a compelling opportunity. To fully realize the benefits of surgical checklists, there is a need for intelligent, context-aware technologies that support, rather than disrupt, clinical workflows. Voice-based systems offer a particularly promising avenue for innovation. Voice interfaces can enable hands-free operation, facilitate real-time team communication, and provide dynamic checklist tracking—features that align closely with the needs of surgical teams who operate in sterile and high-concentration environments.

Recent advances in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and speech technologies have introduced new opportunities for voice-driven interaction in clinical environments. Speech recognition systems, coupled with intent recognition and natural language understanding, can support hands-free operation and enable real-time feedback without diverting attention from patient care [8]. Voice assistants are increasingly integrated into consumer and enterprise settings, and early implementations in healthcare suggest they may support workflow efficiency, documentation, and safety [9–11]. In vascular surgery, several applications of virtual assistants have been described such as synthesis and transcription of electronic health record information, conduct of administrative tasks or facilitation of diagnosis and management of vascular diseases [12]. A voice user interface has also been tested to perform tasks in laboratory environments (e.g. setting a timer, asking for information on procedures) [13]. Siegert et al. reviewed usability of voice-based interaction in therapies in healthcare [14] and Duong et al. assessed usability of voice assistants in healthcare [15]. However, few studies have rigorously evaluated the implementation of voice assistants for surgical safety processes in real-world clinical settings. The potential to enhance checklist adherence, streamline intraoperative communication, and reduce documentation burden remains largely unexplored.

Methods

Objectives

This study addresses the above-mentioned gap by introducing and evaluating a voice-enabled checklist assistant – referred to as VoiceCheck - designed specifically for use in the operating room, designed to improve adherence to WHO checklist protocols in operating rooms. The system leverages state-of-the-art speech recognition and intent processing technologies, operating fully offline to meet healthcare data privacy requirements. The approach emphasizes usability, integration with existing surgical workflows, and real-time feedback mechanisms to foster checklist fidelity and team accountability. Its evaluation focuses on usability, reliability, and integration potential within surgical workflows, contributing to the evidence base for voice interaction in safety-critical clinical applications. By bridging implementation science and informatics, this work aims to contribute to the development of safer, more innovative surgical environments.

Description of the technology

VoiceCheck is a modular, voice-enabled digital assistant designed to support surgical safety protocols by guiding clinical staff through all three phases of the surgical safety checklist: “Sign In”, “Time-out”, and “Sign Out”. The Swiss adaptation of the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist was implemented. The system allows users to record patient identification, clinical observations, and team communications via voice commands or manual input.

The system architecture consists of integrated frontend and backend components, operating entirely offline to ensure data privacy (see Fig. 1). Speech processing comprises two components: structured voice command recognition using the open-source Rhasspy voice assistant, and free-form speech transcription using OpenAI’s Whisper model. All data are securely stored in a local MongoDB database, and completed checklists are archived as PDF documents for audit and compliance purposes.

Study setting and design

The evaluation was conducted in a surgical department of a partner hospital in Switzerland, under real-world conditions in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study design was reviewed by the ethics committee of the Canton of Bern, which confirmed that no ethics approval was required (Req-2025-00205). VoiceCheck was used in parallel with the hospital’s standard paper-based checklist workflow. No patient-identifiable data were recorded, and no direct comparisons or timing metrics were collected. The goal was to assess usability, feasibility, and technical performance under routine clinical conditions.

Twelve surgical team members participated in the evaluation, including anesthesiologists, surgeons, and operating room (OR) nursing staff. Following hands-on use of the system, participants completed the System Usability Scale (SUS) and a custom application-specific questionnaire to assess usability.

Speech technology evaluation

The technical evaluation consisted of two parts:

Speech Recognition (Whisper):Whisper Large V2 and V3 were evaluated using ~ 10’000 words recorded by five native German speakers. Recordings were conducted under both quiet (< 40 dB) and noisy (> 70 dB) conditions, using a headset and a conference microphone. Transcriptions were evaluated using word error rate (WER), with error types (insertions, deletions, substitutions) and processing latency recorded for each sentence.Intent Recognition (Rhasspy):A test set of 45 spoken commands (34 checklist-related, 11 system commands) was used to evaluate Rhasspy’s performance. Each command was repeated 25 times in quiet and noisy conditions, totaling 2’250 wakeword and 2’250 intent recognition attempts (1’125 attempts per environment). A 24-hour background noise test was conducted to measure false positive activations. Recognition performance and reliability were analyzed using RStudio.

Results

Study results

Among the twelve participants who filled the questionnaire, SUS scores ranged from 55 to 92.5, with a mean score of 76.04 (SD = 11.61), which, according to Bangor et al. (47), corresponds to good usability (Fig. 2).

Usability test results: Distribution of SUS-score (n = 12)

Application-specific questions regarding VoiceCheck’s contribution to patient safety, support for consistent checklist completion, and tablet suitability received mean scores between 3.67 and 4.27, with medians of 4 or 5 for all three items.

Thematic analysis of open-ended responses revealed several key findings, among them the following perceived benefits: Participants valued the system’s support for consistent checklist execution across all three phases, particularly the ability to document “Team Time-out” and add integrated notes. The “hands-free documentation” feature was highly appreciated, along with anticipated time savings and reduced document loss risk with future hospital system integration. Users recognized enhanced workflow transparency and clearer role definitions for team members.

While verbal interaction was well-received, some participants preferred tactile input options (e.g., clicking yes/no). Concerns included small tablet interface elements, noise interference, battery life, device hygiene, system startup delays, and privacy implications of voice recording in the operating room.

Additionally, we made observations during the field testing related to process support, technical performance, and usability. VoiceCheck effectively supported structured checklist completion on tablets, with particularly efficient performance during “Team Time-out” phases. Even passive use (reading only) proved helpful. However, the hectic “Sign In” phase yielded limited evaluable data due to minimal testing in noisy conditions. WLAN connectivity and tablet pairing functioned reliably. The Rhasspy speech recognition system provided adequate feedback but required extended initialization periods. External Bluetooth microphones occasionally experienced signal disruptions and range limitations. The user interface was generally intuitive with minimal instruction requirements, though the wake word function needed explanation and sometimes caused operational delays. Both manual input and voice commands were utilized effectively. Conference devices were preferred during “Team Time-out”, enabling multiple team members to contribute from various positions while capturing direct transcriptions. Additional checklist entries were successfully transcribed with acceptable accuracy, though response times were not systematically measured.

Key areas for improvement include the need for more direct voice recognition, better integration of wake word functionality, and optimization for noisy OR environments. Workspace organization and clear sterile zone delineation require further consideration. The tablet was deemed suitable overall, with no major handling issues observed, though interface readability could be enhanced. Overall, VoiceCheck was perceived as a useful documentation tool that demonstrates potential for enhancing safety and workflow consistency in surgical settings, particularly during structured phases like “Team Time-out”.

Technical evaluation results

Tests of Whisper language models

The results showed that both Whisper Large V2 and V3 achieved high transcription accuracy under quiet conditions, with accuracy above 96% for both models (Table 1). However, under simulated operating room noise conditions, Whisper Large V3 demonstrated superior robustness, maintaining a lower WER compared to V2, especially when using the conference microphone. Device selection also influenced performance: the conference microphone yielded lower WERs in noisy environments compared to the headset, particularly with Large V3. This suggests that both the choice of automatic speech recognition model and the recording hardware are critical to achieving reliable voice control in the complex acoustic environment of the operating room.

Results for Rhasspy speech and intent recognition

The performance of the open-source speech assistant Rhasspy was evaluated with a focus on the recognition accuracy of both wake words and intents under two distinct acoustic conditions: a quiet environment (< 40 dB) and a noisy environment (> 70 dB). System reliability was further assessed by monitoring false-positive activations. In total, 1’125 wake word trials and 1’125 intent trials were conducted under each condition. Each intent was tested 45 times, providing a robust statistical basis for analysis.

In quiet conditions, the wake word was correctly recognized in 94.8% of cases, while recognition accuracy decreased to 92.8% in the noisy environment (Table 4). The number of false negatives increased from 59/1’125 in the quiet setting to 81/1’125 in the noisy setting, despite an identical number of attempts.

Intent recognition showed a more pronounced sensitivity to background noise. In the quiet environment, the success rate was 96.8% (36/1’125 unrecognized intents), whereas in the noisy environment, the success rate dropped to 89.3% (121/1’125 failures). This indicates a notable decline in performance when exposed to simulated operating room noise. A detailed summary of results is presented in Table 4. Figure 3 visualizes the success rates for each tested intent, separated by environment, using a grayscale heatmap. In quiet conditions, most intents achieved a perfect (100%) recognition rate, with the exception of “Teams okay,” which had a lower rate of 76%. In noisy conditions, the reduction in recognition accuracy was more pronounced for certain intents, with “Präparate okay” (“medication ok”) at 68% and “Anästhesie okay” (“anesthesia okay”) at 72%. Most other intents in the noisy environment-maintained success rates above 80%, with the majority ranging between 88% and 96%.

Discussion

This study evaluated VoiceCheck, a voice-controlled checklist application designed to support surgical safety protocols. Field testing in real-world operating room environments demonstrated that the system is broadly acceptable to clinical staff, with particularly strong support from anesthesiologists and perioperative nursing teams. Usability feedback indicated that the tablet-based interface was perceived as intuitive, and the voice interaction enabled a structured and distraction-free execution of checklists. Notably, the effectiveness of checklist completion improved significantly when paper-based workflows were completely replaced, underscoring the importance of full digital integration into clinical routines.

There is only limited related work to compare to. One existing digital solution, OP-Check (https://www.op-check.eu/funktionen), supports the surgical safety checklist across all three procedural phases and is already integrated with the electronic health record. Our voice assistant could complement this system by enhancing interaction during the critical “Sign In” and “Sign Out” phases. VoiceCheck as hands-free, voice-controlled interface would be especially advantageous for maintaining sterility and improving workflow efficiency in the operating room [16]. Intelligent checklists have been tested successfully in intensive care where patient-specific checklists were generated by a tool using rules and patient data [17]. The compliance with best clinical practice was improved by these intelligent checklists. We used a standardized checklist for implementation in VoiceCheck. Patient-specific checklists in the “Sign in” phase could remove redundant items from the list.

In their literature review, Kiefel et al. [18] identified a tablet-based client-server system integrated with the electronic health record as the most promising digital approach for implementing surgical safety checklists. While their proposed interface does not incorporate voice interaction, they highlight several advantages of tablet use, such as enabling patient identity verification via wristband scanning. In contrast, we opted for a voice-based interface to provide a more intuitive user experience and to avoid interrupting clinician–patient interaction through manual input on a computer or touch device. By leveraging voice recognition technology, our system aims to overcome common barriers to checklist adherence and seamless workflow integration. Evaluation results are still pending to validate whether voice interaction is more readily accepted than traditional paper-based methods. Supporting this direction, Pati et al. [19] found that technology-assisted checklist implementation can enhance checklist completeness, and prior studies have shown that voice assistants can improve operating room workflow [16]. Uppot et al. tested the accuracy and user acceptance of a system that loudly verbalized clinical information from the electronic health record in the form of a time-out checklist [20]. They could demonstrate that the tool would be accepted by most staff. As our study only involved 12 healthcare professionals from one hospital, we cannot generalize whether the tool would be accepted. An acceptance study involving multiple hospitals would be required.

Whisper Large V3, the automatic speech recognition model deployed in VoiceCheck, demonstrated strong recognition rates across varying noise levels and microphone types. It substantially outperformed Whisper V2 in noisy conditions, confirming its suitability for high-acuity settings. However, performance limitations were observed with domain-specific terms, compound words, and phonetically complex expressions—issues commonly linked to regional accents (e.g., Swiss Standard German). These results highlight the importance of continued optimization, including domain-specific fine-tuning and phonetic adaptation.

Rhasspy also delivered unexpectedly high performance. Intent recognition rates exceeded 92% in most scenarios, with the lowest rate (89.4%) still considered acceptable under high-noise conditions. Its low false-positive rate (e.g., only eight misfires in 24 h of background conversation) further underscores its reliability. These findings suggest that Rhasspy, when combined with stable hardware and communication architecture, is a viable option for clinical voice interfaces.

VoiceCheck’s potential impact extends beyond usability. The application may improve documentation consistency, support legal defensibility through audio-confirmed steps, and reduce communication errors during transitions. The ability to display checklist progress in real-time supports transparency and shared team awareness. Importantly, some users expressed interest in automatic report generation from voice input—indicating that secondary documentation benefits could enhance adoption.

The VoiceCheck application may prove valuable in various highly standardized takeover situations throughout the patient journey, from the emergency room to rehabilitation. Concrete, high-yield deployment targets include prehospital-to-trauma bay handovers, operating room–to–ICU transitions, and interfacility transfers such as acute-care hospital to rehabilitation. In these settings, a dual-input model—structured checklists coupled with preserved free‑text narratives—can maintain standardization while capturing the nuanced clinical context that often drives decision-making.

Strengths of this study include its real-world deployment, multi-professional feedback, and testing under realistic acoustic and procedural constraints. Limitations include the lack of objective clinical outcome data (e.g., error rates or time savings), restricted prototype scope, and limited patient pathway coverage. Additionally, full integration with hospital information systems remains to be implemented. Besides technical quality, we studied only usability. Future work should assess the impact of the tool on its implementation. The Checklist Performance Observation for Improvement (CheckPOINT) tool [21] could be applied in future work to study implementation fidelity of VoiceCheck in terms of checklist adherence, communication effectiveness, attitude and engagement.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of deploying voice-enabled systems to support safety-critical processes in surgical environments. The prototype effectively guided multidisciplinary clinical teams through structured safety checklists, with favorable usability ratings and strong acceptance among anesthesia and OR staff. Technical evaluations confirmed that the underlying speech processing components—Whisper Large V3 and Rhasspy—performed robustly in noisy environments, with recognition rates suitable for clinical deployment. These findings suggest that voice-assisted checklist systems may enhance documentation consistency, workflow standardization, and team communication in the operating room.

At the same time, this work highlights several challenges and opportunities for future research. Objective data on time savings, error reduction, and workflow impact remain to be gathered in broader clinical pilots. Additionally, the integration of VoiceCheck into hospital information systems, ideally using interoperable standards such as HL7 FHIR, could significantly enhance its utility and scalability. Early integration into the preoperative patient pathway (e.g., on the ward) may further support standardization and completeness of safety protocols.

Key design and implementation considerations have also emerged. These include intuitive user interfaces, low-latency offline performance, role-based access control, and alternative authentication methods suited to time-critical OR environments. Multilingual support, user-specific personalization, and the potential use of large language models to improve flexibility and natural interaction present promising avenues for further enhancement.

While VoiceCheck remains a prototype, it serves as a functional foundation for exploring the broader role of voice interaction in clinical informatics. Future studies should evaluate its effectiveness at scale, assess clinician adoption over time, and explore how voice technologies can be reliably embedded into surgical workflows without increasing cognitive or documentation burden. A structured change management process, including interprofessional training, will be critical to successful clinical implementation.
