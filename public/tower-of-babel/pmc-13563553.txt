Development and validation of a human-supervised AI-augmented living oncology evidence platform: a breast cancer pilot study☆
Authors: H Rugo, A Forsythe, D Flora, S Glück, S Grieve, R Campden, RJ Liu, M Musat, JM Rege, PA Kaufman, RL Coleman, P Tarantino, J O’Shaughnessy, L Schwartzberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOncologists face increasing difficulty staying current with rapidly evolving clinical data, guidelines, and regulatory updates. Building and maintaining an annotated clinical trial evidence library is time- and labor-intensive. To address this challenge, we developed and validated a living oncology evidence platform (Living-OEP) for breast cancer (BC), powered by an agentic artificial intelligence (AI) system that supports daily, human-conducted, AI-augmented systematic literature review (SLR).Materials and methodsThe agentic AI system, incorporating GPT-4.1 and o3 (OpenAI) and Claude [Anthropic, PBC, San Francisco, CA] Sonnet-4 (Anthropic), was designed to emulate expert-led, Cochrane-compliant SLR workflows. Guided by a human-developed annotation manual, the system decomposes tasks, self-debugs, and validates outputs. Training data included 29 236 clinical trial abstracts across BC, lung, and prostate cancer, each annotated with four review and 32 extraction variables. Structured data were integrated with guideline-based treatment pathways, forming a real-time, evidence-linked OEP. Accuracy was benchmarked against 1997 human annotations. Living-OEP evidence quality was compared with AI chatbots (ChatGPT [OpenAI, San Francisco, CA], Perplexity [Perplexity AI, Inc., San Francisco, CA], Consensus [Consensus, Boston, MA], and OpenEvidence [OpenEvidence Inc., Miami, FL]) using six criteria across eight BC treatment scenarios.ResultsThe agentic AI review accuracy ranged from 95.1% to 97.2%. Extraction accuracy ranged from 51.1% to 99.4%, with three variables still undergoing iterative refinement. Compared with other AI tools, the Living-OEP provided more comprehensive and accurate evidence and linked all data to original publications and Food and Drug Administration labels.ConclusionsA human-conducted, AI-augmented living SLR integrated with guidelines and regulatory data can provide real-time evidence support. Future studies are required to evaluate its impact on physician workflows, clinical decision-making, and implementation in oncology practice.Key words: agentic artificial intelligence, breast cancer, clinical trial annotation, evidence synthesis, systematic literature review

Introduction

The volume and complexity of oncology data are increasing rapidly, driven by ongoing clinical trials, rapidly evolving evidence, frequent updates to clinical practice guidelines, and new regulatory approvals.

In the first 6 months of 2025, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network issued four guideline updates in breast cancer (BC),1 the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) issued one,2 and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) issued one.3,4 The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted two new BC approvals during the same period.5,6 In PubMed alone, 3424 articles related to BC were published in the first half of 2025, corresponding to an average of ∼19 new articles indexed daily (3424 articles over 180 days). These figures are further compounded by the volume of new data presented at major international conferences: 549 abstracts were presented on BC at ASCO and 447 at ESMO in 2025.

The treatment of cancer is further complicated by the interplay of genetic, molecular, and clinical factors. In BC, treatment selection must account for germline genetic status, tumor estrogen and progesterone receptor expression, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, tissue-derived and liquid biopsy-based tumor genomic profiles, risk of visceral crisis, and prior lines of therapy.

The average 5-year survival rate for BC in the United States is 91.7% across all stages, decreasing substantially to 32.6% among patients with metastatic disease.7 In Europe, the 5-year survival rate is ∼83% overall, with substantial intercountry variability ranging from 89% in Iceland to 74% in Lithuania.8 In the United States, annual medical costs for BC reached ∼$29.8 billion in 2020, representing 14% of cancer-related spending.9 In Europe, BC was the second most costly malignancy in 2009 at €15 billion, accounting for 12% of total cancer-related costs.10 Patients frequently incur indirect costs, contributing to financial toxicity that correlates with poorer quality of life, increased depression and anxiety, and impaired physical and functional well-being.11,12

Physicians require access to a trustworthy, up-to-date evidence library maintained under rigorous quality control (QC). Manual curation of this evidence for clinical decision support is labor-intensive and often lags behind the latest advances. Recent artificial intelligence (AI) developments offer the potential to automate and augment systematic literature review (SLR) processes but robust validation and integration with clinical workflows remain limited.

AI tools may accelerate evidence retrieval and synthesis. However, the application of AI in oncology demands rigorous validation to ensure reliability, accuracy, and clinical credibility. Although conversational AI tools are increasingly used in health care, their performance in oncology remains insufficiently validated, particularly regarding the quality and credibility of referenced sources, including peer-reviewed journals, regulatory approvals, and recognized clinical practice guidelines. Some chatbots reference high-impact sources such as the New England Journal of Medicine and the Journal of the American Medical Association but may under-represent the broader clinical trial literature.

We developed and validated a living (updated daily) oncology evidence platform (Living-OEP) for BC, powered by an agentic AI system that supports daily, human-conducted, AI-augmented SLR, integrated with clinical practice guidelines, regulatory approvals, and ongoing clinical trial information. Importantly, the Living-OEP is intended to support structured oncology evidence synthesis and retrieval and is not designed to function as an autonomous clinical decision-making system.

The Living-OEP was compared against four publicly available AI tools commonly used by clinicians: ChatGPT (OpenAI), Perplexity AI, Consensus, and OpenEvidence. Technical characteristics of each comparator are summarized in Table 1. ChatGPT is a general-purpose large language model (LLM),23 Perplexity AI is a retrieval-augmented search engine,24 Consensus is an AI-powered academic search engine,25 and OpenEvidence is a clinical AI platform supporting medical decision-making.26,27 SLRs are structured evidence synthesis methods that use predefined search strategies, eligibility criteria, and data extraction procedures to identify, evaluate, and synthesize relevant evidence while minimizing bias.

Materials and methods

The Living-OEP employs a Real-Time AI-assisted Living Systematic Literature Review (REAL-SLR)28 methodology. REAL-SLR consists of both an agentic AI system (part A) that performs daily data review and extraction annotations, as well as a human-led workflow enabled by a data management system (DMS) (part B) that performs QC on the AI annotations, interprets evidence, groups publications, analyzes treatment pathways, and integrates other sources of clinical evidence such as guidelines, clinical registries, and regulatory approvals. The study did not include confidential individual patient-level data. All data used for training the agentic AI system, included in the Living-OEP, and used for validation are publicly accessible clinical trial data presented in the abstracts of published articles or conferences.

Agentic AI system construction: part A

We developed a multimodel agentic AI system utilizing GPT-4.1 and o3 (OpenAI) and Claude Sonnet-4 (Anthropic). The AI system functions as an autonomous agent designed to emulate expert-led, Cochrane-compliant SLR workflows.

The SLR workflow involves search strategies constructed by human scientists and executed in PubMed. The complete search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100770). Search results were first screened by applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Publications meeting inclusion criteria were then evaluated and relevant data were extracted into a structured database.

Human scientists documented review and extraction procedures in an annotation manual. The manual defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, extraction variables, expected formats, representative reporting variations, and guidance for identifying key information within clinical trial abstracts. It also incorporated foundational clinical research concepts to support AI system training. The annotation manual was decomposed into structured prompt templates incorporating task-specific instructions, decision trees for ambiguous cases, and few-shot examples drawn from previously validated annotations. Through each iteration, the annotation manual was updated along with the relevant AI system components based on human feedback on the AI’s performance in the previous iteration. Iterations continued until review variables achieved above 95% accuracy with <1% false negative rate and trained extraction variables achieved above 90% accuracy. Iterative refinement of extraction accuracy is ongoing for several variables, including treatment path (61.8%), interventions (51.1%), and observation time (34.0%); these variables are currently annotated by trained human scientists pending further AI model optimization.

Data and annotation process for training the agentic AI system: part A

The annotation manual was developed by human scientists using a corpus of 29 236 clinical trial abstracts across BC, lung cancer, and prostate cancer published from 2019 to the present. Out of the total abstracts, 9887 were full manuscripts, 19 349 were abstracts-only publications from major oncology clinical conferences. Among these, 9800 were abstracts related to BC. Each abstract was annotated with four review variables (population, intervention/comparator, outcome, and study design) and 32 extraction variables, including TNM29 (tumor–node–metastasis) staging, histology, biomarkers, risk factors, treatment line, intervention, study design, sample size, follow-up duration, outcomes [overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), overall response rate (ORR), and quality of life], subgroup data, and toxicity. All 36 variables were defined with annotation examples and extraction guidance. The iterative training process involves human scientists reviewing the AI annotations, making corrections where needed, and updating the annotation manual to improve clarification, then finally retraining the agentic AI system on the updated annotation manual.

DMS engineering for integration and evidence-linking: part B

A DMS was engineered to manage both the initial construction of the BC database and the daily ingestion of new abstracts. The DMS automatically conducts PubMed searches, deduplicates results, processes new abstracts through the agentic AI system for annotation, and presents each abstract alongside its AI predictions for human quality review. The system then assists in human-conducted study grouping and ultimately creates a master entry for all publications reporting data from the same clinical trial.

Every AI annotation was reviewed by a human scientist, including annotations generated during both publication relevance screening and data extraction. In cases where an AI annotation was determined to be incorrect, the human annotation replaced the AI output. Agentic LLM was not involved in trial-level interpretation, only publication-level annotation of data. All trial-level cross-checking interpretations between published data and regulatory/guideline status were done by human scientists. An expert-trained scientist then reviewed the master entries for each study and assigned appropriate treatment paths matching the BC treatment algorithm. Each study’s experimental intervention was cross-referenced against treatment guidelines and regulatory approvals and tagged accordingly. The system was updated daily with new publications, guideline changes, and regulatory updates.

In addition to abstract-level review and extraction, the Living-OEP incorporated continuous stepwise human-supervised reconciliation and cross-referencing of data variables across (i) full-text peer-reviewed publications, (ii) clinical trial registry records, (iii) regulatory approval labels, and (iv) clinical practice guidelines linked to ensure completeness and accuracy of trial characteristics and outcomes. Detailed reconciliation and data currency adjudication are described in Supplementary Materials (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100770).

Validation of the agentic AI system: part A

The agentic AI system’s review and extraction accuracy was benchmarked against 1997 human-annotated BC publications independent of the training dataset. This set was selected as they were newly added to the Living-OEP since the training of the agentic AI system. The validation set was locked before the final accuracy confirmation. The primary benchmark for the validation of the agentic AI system was predefined to be the accuracy point estimate. Accuracy was defined as the percentage of AI annotations exactly matching human annotations in both content and format. A false negative rate was included as an exploratory outcome and was defined as the percentage of AI annotations that were falsely excluded during relevance screening, calculated over all abstracts reviewed.

Validation of the Living-OEP against commercially available AI chatbots

The Living-OEP’s evidence quality was evaluated in eight clinically realistic BC scenarios using six criteria: (i) completeness of available treatments with published efficacy and safety data in peer-reviewed journals, (ii) guideline concordance, (iii) inclusion of pivotal new trials (defined as practice-changing or phase III interventional trials), (iv) presentation of specific outcome data (PFS, OS, and ORR), (v) provision of source references linked to original publications, and (vi) inclusion of regulatory approval links.

Comparisons were made against ChatGPT (GPT-4o model, accessed 24 June 2025), Perplexity (continuous rolling improvements with no versioning, accessed 24 June 2025), Consensus (continuous rolling improvements with no versioning, accessed 24 June 2025), and OpenEvidence (v2.0, accessed 19 July 2025). Although there was a gap in assessment dates between ChatGPT, Perplexity, Consensus, and OpenEvidence, no substantive updates were released for any of these chatbots during the intervening period.

Although the GPT-5 model was available at the time of evaluation, preliminary testing demonstrated no meaningful improvement in treatment completeness or accuracy versus GPT-4o; therefore, GPT-4o was selected for comparative analysis.

Responses were evaluated by a single trained researcher using a binary scoring rubric (criterion met versus not met) for each of the six criteria. The assessment was not blinded to the source of the response. Accuracy for each criterion was defined as the percentage of questions evaluated as positive across all eight scenarios.

The validation of Living-OEP was designed to be a developmental and descriptive validation. The primary outcome of the validation study is the descriptive comparison between Living-OEP and the commercially available AI chatbots. The specific comparison was determined post hoc instead of preplanned. Accuracy for each criterion is an exploratory outcome.

The following eight clinical scenarios were used to evaluate the performance of the Living-OEP versus commercial chatbots. All queries were submitted verbatim to each platform without modification. Additional explicit prompts were provided to the chatbots to include ‘all original references, ongoing trials, and present any available OS and PFS data, as well as if the treatments are approved by FDA (provide link to FDA package insert), and recommended by USA and European guidelines (provide link to guidelines)’.

Results

Review accuracy of the agentic AI (part A of the Living-OEP)

The agentic AI system accurately recognized key clinical concepts and SLR tasks. The system identified study populations with high accuracy, achieving 99.82% concordance with human annotations based on iterative prompt refinement.

Intervention/comparator classification achieved 98.75% accuracy after iterative prompt and annotation manual refinement. Outcomes classification achieved 93.73% accuracy after refinement to distinguish study protocols from results-reporting abstracts. Study design classification achieved 98.03% accuracy after refinement of systematic review and meta-analysis identification.

Minimizing false negatives was prioritized because incorrectly excluded abstracts are less likely to undergo human review. Accordingly, system sensitivity was adjusted to favor inclusion when certainty was low.

Extraction accuracy of the agentic AI (part A of the Living-OEP)

The agentic AI system generally carried out well on variables with less heterogeneity in reporting format. Within the first three iterations of training, the system achieved 100.0%, 100.0%, 95.2%, 100.0%, 94.4%, and 90.4% accuracy for the following study characteristic variables: randomization, phase, clinical trial name, clinical trial registration, follow-up time, and sample size, respectively.

Eight iterations were required for the system to achieve above 80% accuracy for patient characteristic variables. This reflected four principal challenges: (i) the reporting format for patient characteristics is highly heterogeneous across publications, (ii) full comprehension of patient characteristics requires understanding of cancer pathology and systematic organization into a structured framework, (iii) certain variables are interdependent (e.g. the treatment path should not be classified as adjuvant if the clinical stage is metastatic), and (iv) some variables combine multiple concepts (e.g. treatment path encompasses both the number of prior lines of therapy and the specific prior treatments received). Within 10 iterations, the system achieved 87.2%, 99.4%, 87.8%, 93.6%, 80.9%, 61.8%, 51.1%, and 74.5% accuracy for clinical stage, histology, biomarker, subpopulation, risk, treatment path, interventions, and intervention category, respectively.

Accuracy for the results variables continues to undergo iterative refinement. Current accuracy values are as follows: OS measure (93.6%), median survival (83.6%), landmark survival (91.0%), observation time (34.0%), primary progression measure (89.4%), median primary progression (65.8%), landmark primary progression (84.2%), other progression measures (60.8%), median other progression (64.4%), landmark other progression (76.6%), response measured (91.5%), response data (76.1%), quality-of-life measure(s) (97.9%), quality-of-life data (85.1%), and safety data (78.7%). Variables with accuracy <80% remain under active iterative development.

Three variables have yet to be incorporated into the AI training pipeline: analysis type, subgroup names, and subgroup data. These variables are currently annotated manually by trained scientists within the Living-OEP. Accuracy data for these variables will be reported in subsequent publications.

Accuracy metrics are reported as point estimates. As this study represents a descriptive benchmarking validation, formal statistical testing with confidence intervals and prespecified sample sizes will be incorporated in future evaluations.

Efficiency of the agentic AI system (part A of the Living-OEP)

The agentic AI system annotated 1997 publications in 7.25 h in a single continuous session without interruption. Based on institutional benchmarks, we estimated that human scientists review ∼80 abstracts per hour and extract data from approximately four abstracts per hour, with an inclusion rate of 30% following title/abstract screening. These estimates were derived from internal time-tracking data collected during the development of the annotation manual. Under these assumptions, human scientists would require an estimated 174.7 h to complete the same tasks, representing a 95.9% reduction in annotation time.

Comparative performance of the Living-OEP against AI Chatbots

The Living-OEP provided more comprehensive structured evidence outputs compared with ChatGPT, Perplexity, Consensus, and OpenEvidence in completeness and precision of evidence across all six clinical criteria in eight BC scenarios. The Living-OEP consistently included guideline-recommended treatments, pivotal new trials, and actionable clinical data, whereas other AI tools frequently omitted recent therapeutic advances or failed to specify guideline categories and evidence levels.

Evidence currency

Twenty-three percent of evidence included in the Living-OEP derived from recently published, potentially practice-changing pivotal trials, highlighting the system’s capacity to rapidly incorporate emerging data into the decision support framework.

Discussion

Our Living-OEP demonstrates that human-conducted, AI-augmented daily SLR can deliver highly accurate, up-to-date, and evidence-linked clinical information support in oncology. The agentic AI system achieved high concordance with human annotations for key trial annotation tasks and dramatically reduced the time required for evidence integration. Comparative analysis revealed clear advantages over leading AI chatbots in guideline concordance, completeness, and inclusion of pivotal new data—factors that are critical for real-world clinical decision-making.

The integration of structured trial data, guidelines, and regulatory updates enables evidence-linked recommendations that are continuously updated.

Quality assurance and human oversight

Although several extraction variables have not yet achieved target accuracy through AI annotation, every AI-generated annotation is quality-checked by a human scientist before inclusion in the Living-OEP database. This human oversight ensures accurate data presentation regardless of AI performance on individual variables.

AI is intended to remain assistive, with clinical interpretation and treatment decisions remaining the responsibility of physicians.

Comparison with existing AI tools

We acknowledge a fundamental asymmetry in design and intended use between the Living-OEP—a purpose-built, continuously curated decision support system—and general-purpose AI chatbots. However, given that physicians increasingly use chatbots as de facto clinical decision support tools, this comparison reflects real-world usage patterns and highlights the limitations of chatbot-derived clinical information. Notably, the Living-OEP consistently provided guideline-concordant, evidence-graded treatment recommendations with traceable source references, whereas all comparator chatbots failed to systematically identify guideline recommendation status and evidence levels for most scenarios tested.

Potential sources of bias

Several sources of potential bias merit consideration. The current search strategy is limited to PubMed and may miss relevant evidence indexed in Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, or regional databases. Additionally, restricting the search to English-language publications may underrepresent evidence from non-English-speaking regions. Because the AI system is trained on human-generated annotations, any systematic biases present in the training data may be propagated and amplified. Future iterations will incorporate multidatabase search strategies and bias auditing protocols.

Regulatory considerations

It is important to note that the Living-OEP is intended as an informational tool to support, not replace, clinical judgment. It has not been evaluated or approved by any regulatory authority as a clinical decision-making device and its outputs should be interpreted within the context of individual patient circumstances and physician expertise.

To support transparency, the Living-OEP distinguishes interim, primary, and follow-up data within each clinical trial. Updated publications are incorporated into a master study entry, whereas earlier publications are retained with annotations describing publication date, data maturity, and links to the original source. Data maturity, regulatory status, and provisional evidence are defined within the interactive platform.

Limitations

Evidence maturity remains an important consideration in oncology evidence synthesis. Early-phase studies, interim analyses, conference abstracts, and post hoc subgroup analyses require careful contextual interpretation and should not be weighted equivalently to mature randomized evidence or guideline-endorsed standards of care. For this reason, all evidence included within the Living-OEP undergoes human review and contextual reconciliation before integration.

The present system primarily evaluates abstract-level evidence, including conference presentations that may precede full peer-reviewed publication. Because conference abstracts frequently omit subgroup analyses, mature safety data, and methodological details, certain extraction variables continue to require expert review and reconciliation across full-text publications, guidelines, regulatory documents, and trial registries.

Although trained and validated primarily in BC, the Living-OEP is being expanded to additional tumor types. The Living-OEP currently focuses on medical oncology clinical trials, with future work including broader implementation, prospective clinical utility studies, and electronic health record integration.

The comparison between the Living-OEP and AI chatbots was not blinded and was assessed by a single researcher. We acknowledge that this design does not permit inter-rater reliability assessment and future studies should incorporate blinded, multiassessor evaluation with a validated scoring instrument.

Although database searches included publications from 2019 onward, guidelines and regulatory approvals were cross-checked to include critical-path studies referenced in regulatory labels, guideline recommendations, and major conferences regardless of publication date. This approach enabled continuity of the evidence base while supporting expansion to additional tumor types. Technical descriptions of comparator chatbots were drawn from publicly available sources.

Although the Living-OEP is updated daily, this analysis evaluates the evidence base retrospectively. Prospective validation of its ‘living’ capabilities is planned.

By combining daily, human-conducted, AI-augmented SLR with guideline and regulatory integration, the Living-OEP delivers real-time, evidence-linked clinical information support for oncology. This approach offers oncologists a scalable framework for structured oncology evidence synthesis and retrieval. Integration with electronic health record systems to enable point-of-care access is being explored.
