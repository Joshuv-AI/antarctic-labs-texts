Associations of Genetic Variants in Uric Acid Transporter Genes With Salt Sensitivity, Longitudinal Blood Pressure Changes, and Incident Hypertension in Chinese Adults
Authors: Ming‐Ke Chang, Ling‐Yun Kong, Zhuo‐Ran Zhang, Shi Yao, Xin Wang, Hao Li, Yang Wang, Wei‐Hua Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUric acid transporters mediate renal and extrarenal urate handling and may contribute to blood pressure (BP) regulation. This study examined the associations of common single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in key urate transporter genes (SLC2A9, ABCG2, SLC17A3, SLC22A7, SLC22A6, SLC22A11, SLC22A12, and ABCC4) with salt sensitivity, longitudinal BP changes, and incident hypertension. Data were derived from the Baoji Salt‐Sensitivity Study, a family‐based cohort in which 514 Chinese adults completed a controlled dietary sodium intervention and were followed prospectively for 14 years. After multivariable adjustment and multiple‐testing correction, ABCG2 rs2054576 and rs4491984 were associated with DBP response to low‐salt diet; SLC22A6 rs4149170 with SBP and DBP responses; ABCG2 rs12505410 and SLC22A12 rs7932775 with DBP and MAP responses; and ABCC4 rs1189466 and rs17189390 with SBP, DBP, and MAP responses. During high‐salt intake, SLC2A9 rs3733591 was associated with SBP and MAP responses; and SLC22A11 rs3759053, ABCC4 rs17189390 and rs9590211 were associated with SBP, DBP, and MAP responses. Over 14 years of follow‐up, SLC2A9 rs3733591 and SLC17A3 rs1165165 were associated with longitudinal systolic BP (SBP) change; ABCG2 rs2054576, SLC22A7 rs2270860, SLC22A12 rs79226484, and ABCC4 rs1189466 were associated with diastolic BP (DBP) and MAP change; and SLC22A6 rs4149170 and ABCC4 rs9590220 and rs7322318 were associated with change in SBP, DBP and MAP. Additionally, ABCC4 rs7982809 and rs869951 were associated with incident hypertension over the 14‐year follow‐up. These findings suggest that genetic variation in urate transporters may contribute to salt sensitivity, long‐term BP progression, and hypertension risk, supporting a possible role for urate‐transport pathways in BP regulation.

Introduction

Salt sensitivity of blood pressure (BP) is a heritable trait associated with elevated risks of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and premature death [1, 2]. Although the heritability of this phenotype is well established [3], the specific genetic mechanisms modulating BP response to sodium intake remain incompletely understood. Beyond classical renal sodium‐handling pathways, uric acid (UA) has emerged as a potential mediator of hypertension and renal dysfunction [4]. Elevated serum UA has been linked to endothelial impairment [5], oxidative stress [6], renal microvascular injury [7] and increased tubular sodium reabsorption [8], processes that may impair sodium homeostasis and promote a salt‐sensitive state. Experimental models further demonstrate that diet‐induced hyperuricemia can lead to a sustained salt‐sensitive hypertensive phenotype, even after UA levels normalize [9]. Epidemiological studies have also consistently associated serum UA levels with hypertension incidence [10]. Despite this converging evidence, the genetic underpinnings linking UA homeostasis to salt sensitivity in humans remain insufficiently defined.

Renal handling of UA is a precisely regulated process mediated by a coordinated system of transport proteins in the proximal tubule [11]. The reabsorptive pathway is initiated by the apical transporter urate transporter 1 (SLC22A12), which takes up urate from the tubular lumen [12]. Intracellular urate is then shuttled to the bloodstream via the basolateral transporter glucose transporter 9 (SLC2A9) [13]. Conversely, urate secretion involves basolateral uptake by organic anion transporter 1 (SLC22A6) [14], followed by efflux into the lumen via the apical transporters including ATP binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2) [15], sodium phosphate transporter 4 (SLC17A3) [16], and multidrug‐resistant protein 4 (ABCC4) [17]. Additional transporters such as organic anion transporter 4 (SLC22A11) and organic anion transporter 2 (SLC22A7) further modulate this balance [18].

The physiological importance of this transporter network is underscored by genetic disorders of urate homeostasis. Loss‑of‑function variants in SLC22A12 and SLC2A9 cause renal hypouricemia, a condition characterized by impaired tubular urate reabsorption and often associated with endothelial dysfunction [19, 20]. Reduced UA elimination due to ABCG2 dysfunction, caused by pathogenic genetic variants, is a common mechanism leading to hyperuricemia [21]. Beyond rare variants with large effects, common SNPs in urate transporter genes may also modulate urate handling and consequently influence BP regulation. These observations establish a biologically plausible link between urate transporter dysfunction and vascular pathology relevant to BP regulation [22, 23]. However, whether common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in these genes are associated with hypertension remains inconclusive, and their relationship with salt sensitivity of BP has not been fully investigated.

The current study aimed to examine common SNPs in key urate transporter genes in relation to BP responses to dietary sodium, long‐term BP changes, and incident hypertension. We evaluated variants in SLC2A9, ABCG2, SLC17A3, SLC22A7, SLC22A6, SLC22A11, SLC22A12, and ABCC4 within a well‐characterized family‐based cohort of Han Chinese participants who underwent a controlled dietary sodium intervention and prospective long‐term follow‐up.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This study was based on the Baoji Salt Sensitivity Cohort, which was initiated in 2004. The cohort design, along with its inclusion and exclusion criteria, is summarized in Figure 1 and has been described in detail previously [24, 25, 26]. In brief, this longitudinal family‐based investigation enrolled 514 adults belonging to 124 families across seven villages in Baoji City, Shaanxi Province, China. For safety considerations, the parents of the 181 probands were not included in the dietary intervention phase. Therefore, 333 non‐parent participants completed a controlled dietary sodium trial designed to examine the association between SNPs in UA metabolism‐related genes and BP responses to dietary sodium. The trial protocol comprised three consecutive dietary phases: A 3‐day usual diet, followed by a 7‐day low‐salt diet (51.3 mmol sodium/day), and then a 7‐day high‐salt diet (307.8 mmol sodium/day). Throughout the intervention, all meals were prepared and provided by a central study kitchen, and daily potassium intake was kept constant. This chronic salt‐loading intervention protocol has been described in detail in our previous studies [27, 28, 29].

Flowchart of the Baoji salt‐sensitivity study cohort.

To assess the long‐term associations of urate transporter SNPs with BP progression and incident hypertension, all 514 cohort participants were eligible for follow‐up assessments in 2009, 2012, and 2018. A total of 102 participants were lost before the 2009 visit, with an additional 56 lost before 2012 and 59 before 2018, leaving 412, 356, and 297 attendees, respectively. At each visit, standardized BP measurements and demographic, clinical, medication, and biochemical data were collected. Hypertension status and antihypertensive medication use were ascertained using standardized questionnaires, consistent with previous reports from this cohort [24, 25, 26].

The study protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University. All participants gave written informed consent at enrollment and at every subsequent follow‐up visit. The research was carried out in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov under identifier NCT02734472.

Measurement of BP and Definitions

To maintain measurement accuracy and consistency, all research staff received standardized training prior to data collection at each phase. BP was measured with participants in a seated position using a calibrated random‐zero sphygmomanometer (Hawksley & Sons, Lancing, UK), following established protocols [24, 25, 26]. Participants were instructed and supervised to rest quietly for at least 5 min before measurement, and to abstain from smoking, alcohol, or strenuous activity. BP readings were taken daily during the 3‐day baseline period, on the final three days of each 7‐day dietary intervention, and at each follow‐up visit. Hypertension was defined as systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication [25]. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated as MAP = DBP + (SBP—DBP)/3.

Given the controlled dietary sodium conditions in both intervention periods, the difference in BP from the low‑salt to the high‑salt phase provides a reliable measure of salt sensitivity. The low‑salt response reflects the BP‐lowering effect of sodium restriction, and identifying genetic factors that influence this response has direct clinical and public health relevance for dietary intervention strategies. The high‑salt response represents the BP elevation induced by sodium loading, which is the core phenotype for assessing salt sensitivity and has been the primary focus of prior genetic studies on salt‐sensitive hypertension. Therefore, in accordance with prior methodology [24, 30], BP responses were calculated using the following formulas: Low‑salt response = (mean BP on low‑salt diet) − (mean baseline BP); High‑salt response = (mean BP on high‑salt diet) − (mean BP on low‑salt diet). For descriptive purposes, participants were classified as salt‑sensitive or salt‑resistant based on the change in MAP from the low‑salt to the high‑salt diet. Salt sensitivity was defined as an increase in MAP ≥ 5 mmHg, and salt resistance as an increase < 5 mmHg [31].

Biochemical Assays

On the final day of each dietary phase, fasting venous blood samples were obtained after an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min, and serum or plasma aliquots were stored at −80°C until analysis. Twenty‐four‐hour urine was collected at baseline and on the final day of each intervention period to assess adherence. Triglycerides, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, serum creatinine, and urinary sodium and potassium were measured using an automated biochemistry analyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Serum uric acid was available only at the 2018 follow‐up and was therefore used solely in exploratory mediation analyses.

SNP Selection and Genotyping

SNPs in the UA transporter gene region were selected based on public genomic databases, including the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) SNP database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/projects/SNP) and the Genomic Variation Server (http://gvs.gs.washington.edu/GVS147/). Database‐based selection prioritized variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05 in the reference population and tagSNP coverage at pairwise linkage disequilibrium r 2 ≥ 0.8. Hardy‐Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was assessed as a post‐genotyping quality‐control criterion in the parental generation. Thirty SNPs were genotyped. SLC2A9 rs6820230 and ABCC4 rs2274410 deviated from HWE and were excluded from association analyses; the following 28 SNPs were retained: SLC2A9 (rs4621429, rs2280205, rs11722228, rs3733591, and rs74812044), ABCG2 (rs2054576, rs12505410, rs4491984), SLC17A3 (rs942379, rs1165165), SLC22A7 (rs2270860), SLC22A6 (rs11231287, rs4149170), SLC22A11 (rs3759053), SLC22A12 (rs79226484, rs3825017, rs11231825, rs7932775), and ABCC4 (rs1189466, rs1729760, rs3765534, rs17189390, rs6492771, rs9590211, rs9590220, rs7322318, rs7982809, and rs869951). All genotyping procedures were conducted by CapitalBio Corporation (Beijing, China) [24, 30].

Statistical Analysis

Genotype quality control was performed using PLINK, including checks of Mendelian consistency, minor allele frequency, and Hardy‐Weinberg equilibrium in the parental generation. Associations of individual SNPs with BP responses to dietary sodium and with longitudinal BP changes were examined using linear mixed‐effects models under additive, dominant, and recessive genetic models. Generalized linear mixed‐effects models were used to evaluate associations with incident hypertension; 51 participants with hypertension at baseline were excluded from incidence analyses. All primary models included baseline age, sex, body mass index, fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and serum creatinine as fixed effects, with family included as a random intercept. Bonferroni adjustment was applied to the prespecified family of independent comparisons within each phenotype‐specific analysis, and adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses replaced serum creatinine with eGFR calculated using the CKD‐EPI equation and separately excluded participants with baseline eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Exploratory mediation analyses were performed using the mediation R package to examine whether UA measured at the 14‑year follow‑up mediated the associations between urate transporter SNPs and long‑term BP changes and hypertension incidence. The analysis included individual SNPs as independent variables, 2018 UA levels as the mediator, and 14‑year changes in SBP, DBP, and MAP, as well as incident hypertension, as outcome variables. The mediation effects were estimated using a quasi‑Bayesian Monte Carlo method based on 1,000 simulations, with bias‑corrected and accelerated bootstrap confidence intervals for the indirect effects. All models were adjusted for baseline age, sex, BMI, FBG, TC, TG, and serum creatinine as covariates. Because UA and the outcome variables were measured at the same follow‑up visit, this analysis was exploratory and was not intended to establish temporal or causal mediation.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and BP Responses to Dietary Sodium Modulation

As shown in Table 1, SBP, DBP, and MAP decreased significantly in all four participating family groups during the low‐salt intervention (all p < 0.05). During the high‐salt intervention, SBP increased significantly in all four groups; MAP increased significantly among probands, siblings, and spouses; and DBP increased significantly among probands and siblings (all p < 0.05). Participants with hypertension had greater reductions in SBP, DBP, and MAP during the low‐salt intervention (p = 0.024, p = 0.001, and p = 0.019, respectively; Table S1). Adherence to the dietary interventions was assessed by 24 h urinary sodium excretion (Table S2). Urinary sodium excretion varied consistently with the intervention phases, reaching its lowest level during the low‐salt period and its highest level during the high‐salt period, indicating good adherence to the study protocol.

Table S3 summarizes the genotype distribution of the UA metabolism‐related gene SNPs. With the exception of rs6820230 in SLC2A9 and rs2274410 in ABCC4, which showed deviation from HWE (p < 0.05), all remaining SNPs were in HWE (p > 0.05), supporting the overall quality of genotyping. The two SNPs that deviated from HWE were excluded from the association analyses.

Table 2 summarizes the associations of SNPs in UA transporter genes with BP responses to dietary sodium interventions. During the high‐salt intervention, SLC2A9 rs3733591 was associated with SBP and MAP responses, whereas SLC22A11 rs3759053, ABCC4 rs17189390 and rs9590211 were associated with SBP, DBP, and MAP responses. Association of UA Transporter SNPs With Longitudinal BP Changes

Table 3 summarizes participant characteristics at baseline (2004) and the 2009, 2012, and 2018 follow‐up visits. Across the observed visits, mean SBP, DBP, and MAP were 21.2, 7.9, and 12.3 mmHg higher, respectively, in 2018 than at baseline. At the 2018 follow‐up, 160 incident hypertension cases were reported among participants included in the incidence analysis. Salt‐sensitive participants had higher absolute BP levels at baseline and follow‐up and a higher incidence of hypertension than salt‐resistant participants (65.2% vs. 36.1%; Table S4); however, the 14‐year changes in SBP, DBP, and MAP did not differ significantly between the groups.

During 2004–2009, SLC22A11 rs3759053 was associated with changes in SBP; ABCC4 rs1189466, rs3765534, and rs869951 with changes in DBP; and SLC2A9 rs74812044 and SLC17A3 rs1165165 with changes in both SBP and MAP. In sensitivity analyses using eGFR as a covariate in place of serum creatinine, and after excluding participants with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, the results remained broadly consistent with the primary findings (Tables S5 and S6).

Discussion

In this family‐based cohort with a controlled dietary sodium intervention and long‐term follow‐up several variants in urate transporter genes were associated with BP responses to low‐ or high‐salt intake, longitudinal BP changes, or incident hypertension. Specifically, SNPs in SLC2A9, ABCG2, SLC22A11, SLC22A12 and ABCC4 were associated with MAP responses to chronic salt diets; SNPs in SLC2A9, ABCG2, SLC17A3, SLC22A7, SLC22A6, SLC22A12, and ABCC4 were associated with longitudinal BP changes over 14 years; and SNPs in ABCC4 were associated with incident hypertension over the 14‐year follow–up. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive report examining SNPs of key urate transporter genes in relation to salt sensitivity and long‑term BP progression, and incident hypertension in humans. The findings may contribute to a better understanding of the role of urate transporter pathways in the development of hypertension, particularly given the marked population‐specific variation in urate transporter genetics that informs the interpretation of our results in a Han Chinese cohort.

Previous studies suggest that UA may be involved in salt sensitivity of BP. Proposed mechanisms include UA‐induced activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system, renal arteriolopathy, endothelial dysfunction, impaired nitric oxide bioavailability, and reduced sodium excretion [32, 33]. Together, these pathways may amplify BP responses to dietary sodium. This mechanistic framework is consistent with observations from urate transporter disorders and supports the biological plausibility of our findings. More than 50 coding variants have been described in SLC22A12 [34], with clear population specificity, as variants such as p.W258X and p.R90H are found at appreciable frequencies in East Asian populations (2.30%–2.37% and 0.40%, respectively) but are virtually absent in European and African populations, suggesting a founder effect in Asia [35]. Common SNPs identified in our study may influence BP responses through subtle alterations in renal urate handling, endothelial function, or tubular sodium reabsorption, although the effect sizes are likely smaller than those caused by rare pathogenic variants. Animal models treated with uricase inhibitors demonstrate that hyperuricemia is associated with a salt‐sensitive phenotype characterized by BP elevation during high‐salt challenge [9]; however, direct human evidence linking UA transporter variants to this intermediate phenotype has been limited. A key Mendelian randomization study by Parsa et al. [22]. identified the SLC2A9 gene in this pathway, showing that a UA‐lowering SNP was associated with reduced BP, with a larger reduction during high sodium intake (2.2 mmHg) than during low sodium intake (1.5 mmHg), suggesting a possible gene‐environment interaction. Building upon this, our study evaluated multiple UA transporter genes within a controlled sodium‐intervention setting and suggests that the relationship between urate transport and salt sensitivity may extend beyond SLC2A9.

Genetic evidence has increasingly pointed to an association between urate transporter genes and BP regulation, though the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. A polymorphism in SLC2A9 has been associated with higher clinic SBP [36], while SNPs in ABCG2 have been linked to BP measures and composite cardiovascular risk scores [37]. Approximately 45 non‐synonymous variants of ABCG2 have been identified, many of which exhibit marked ethnic differences in allele frequency. The most common dysfunctional variant, rs2231142, reduces transporter function by decreasing protein expression levels [38], significantly influences the age of onset of gout, and is strongly associated with a positive family history [39]. In contrast, the V12M variant does not significantly affect ABCG2 expression or urate transport activity. Another variant, Q126X, is relatively common in the Japanese population but rare elsewhere [40]. These variants are associated with a markedly increased risk of gout, with effect sizes that vary by variant and population, and may exert a stronger influence on hyperuricemia risk than common environmental factors such as obesity or alcohol consumption [41]; their contribution to disease susceptibility thus appears to be substantial. Although rs2231142 was not among our tested SNPs, our observation that other ABCG2 variants were associated with salt sensitivity and BP changes suggests that the ABCG2 locus may influence BP through urate‑related mechanisms. Furthermore, large‐scale trans‐ancestry genome‐wide association studies have identified SLC22A7 as a BP‐related locus [42], reinforcing the potential importance of renal transporter pathways. The rs2231142 variant has been associated with a trend toward lower BMI and C‑reactive protein levels, and higher estimated glomerular filtration rate [43], suggesting that its cardiovascular effects may extend beyond UA alone. Our longitudinal data provide a temporal perspective on these associations by showing that multiple SNPs in SLC2A9, SLC17A3, ABCG2, SLC22A7, SLC22A6, SLC22A12, and ABCC4 were associated with BP progression over 14 years. To further explore whether UA might mediate the observed genetic associations, mediation analyses were performed using UA levels measured at the 14‑year follow‑up.

Regarding hypertension incidence, two ABCC4 variants were associated with the risk of developing hypertension during follow‑up, suggesting a role for this transporter in the transition from BP progression to overt hypertension. The associations observed for SLC22A12, ABCC4, SLC22A6, SLC22A7, and SLC17A3 are physiologically plausible given their established roles in urate handling. Notably, the highest prevalence of dysfunctional SLC22A12 variants has been reported in the European Roma population, with p.T467M occurring at 5.6% and the deletion variant p.L415_G417 at 1.9% [44], underscoring the marked ethnic differences in allele frequency that can influence urate homeostasis and its downstream effects. Because the genetic architecture of urate transport is complex, our findings suggest that a broader network of transporters beyond SLC22A12, SLC2A9, and ABCG2 may influence BP regulation. This pattern is consistent with findings from drug‐targeted Mendelian randomization studies, which suggest that inhibition of urate transporters may affect continuous BP traits. For example, one study reported that genetic proxies of SLC2A9 inhibition were associated with a 0.03 mmHg reduction in DBP (95% confidence interval [CI] −0.05 to −0.01, p = 0.005), and SLC22A12 inhibition with a 0.07 mmHg reduction (95% CI −0.13 to 0.00, p = 0.048) [45]. Taken together, these observations suggest that multiple SNPs may modulate gradual BP changes through pathways involving renal sodium handling, urate metabolism, or vascular function, and that ABCC4 variants may be particularly relevant to the risk of developing hypertension. The development of overt hypertension likely involves the convergence of multiple genetic and environmental factors over an extended period, with distinct genetic loci contributing to different stages of the hypertensive process.

In our mediation analysis using UA measured at the 14‑year follow‑up, no significant indirect effects were detected. This may reflect the limited temporal data, as UA measurements were only available at the 2018 follow‑up and not at baseline or earlier time points, which prevented us from capturing dynamic changes in UA over the 14‑year follow‑up period. The relationship between UA and long‑term BP progression has been well established in epidemiological studies, with a meta‑analysis showing that hyperuricemia was associated with a significantly increased risk of hypertension (risk ratio [RR] 1.41, 95% CI 1.23–1.58) [46], and another systematic review and meta‑analysis further confirming that hyperuricemia was significantly associated with an increased risk of new‑onset hypertension (RR 1.36, 95% CI 1.16–1.59) [47]. Prospective cohort studies have demonstrated that baseline UA levels predict both the onset and maintenance of hypertension [48]. Mechanistically, UA contributes to BP elevation through multiple converging pathways. It activates the renin‑angiotensin‑aldosterone system [33, 49], reduces nitric oxide bioavailability by inhibiting endothelial nitric oxide synthase phosphorylation [33], induces oxidative stress and vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation via mitogen‑activated protein kinase activation and cyclooxygenase‑2 stimulation [50], and enhances renal sodium reabsorption via epithelial sodium channel activation [49]. These mechanisms collectively establish a coherent framework linking elevated UA to sustained BP elevation.

Separately, the relationship between UA and salt sensitivity has been specifically investigated. Wang et al. [51]. proposed a two‐phase model in which UA‐induced renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system activation initiates renal arteriolopathy, endothelial dysfunction, and perivascular inflammation in the early phase—a stage that is still reversible with urate‐lowering therapy. The second phase is characterized by irreversible preglomerular vascular disease, including afferent arteriolar thickening, collagen deposition, and lumen stenosis, which impair pressure‐natriuresis and dysregulate sodium homeostasis. Once this structural damage is established, salt sensitivity persists despite normalization of UA levels. Experimental evidence supports this model: Mild hyperuricemia in rats acutely increases BP through a renin‐dependent mechanism under low‐salt conditions, while chronic hyperuricemia induces salt sensitivity through the development of preglomerular vascular disease [9]. Clinically, elevated UA has been associated with salt sensitivity in both normotensive and hypertensive individuals [51], and higher UA levels have been reported in young non‑dipper hypertensive patients [52]. A previous intervention study demonstrated that plasma UA levels are dynamically regulated by dietary sodium intake and are significantly associated with salt sensitivity status [53]. However, the lack of UA measurements during the dietary intervention phase of the present study precluded a direct examination of whether the identified SNPs influence salt sensitivity through UA‐mediated pathways. These observations suggest that a single time‐point UA measurement is insufficient to fully capture the complex role of UA in either long‐term BP progression or salt sensitivity, and future studies with repeated UA measurements across multiple time points are needed to more rigorously examine the mediating role of UA.

This study has several strengths. We employed a family‐based design in a rural region of northwest China, which reduced, but did not eliminate, heterogeneity in genetic ancestry and environmental exposures. The rigorously controlled, chronic salt‐loading intervention, following an established protocol, enabled a precise assessment of the effects of dietary sodium on BP phenotypes, with subject compliance further verified by 24 h urinary sodium excretion. Additionally, BP was measured nine times across baseline and all intervention phases, significantly reducing measurement variability and enhancing result reliability.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size of 514 participants is modest for a genetic association study, which may increase the risk of both false‑positive and false‑negative findings despite multiple‐testing correction. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating and require replication in larger independent cohorts. Second, the study population consisted exclusively of Han Chinese individuals from a family‐based rural cohort, which may restrict generalizability to other ethnic groups or populations with different environmental exposures. This limitation is particularly relevant because allele frequencies of functionally important urate transporter variants differ markedly across ancestries. Third, the candidate‐gene approach focused on pre‑specified urate transporter genes and therefore cannot capture the broader genetic architecture of BP regulation; future genome‑wide or sequencing‐based studies may identify additional loci beyond those examined here. Finally, functional validation of the identified variants was not performed. Further mechanistic investigations, including transporter assays and animal or cellular models, are warranted to clarify the functional role of these variants in BP regulation.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that common SNPs in UA transporter genes are associated with salt sensitivity, long‐term BP progression, and incident hypertension in Chinese adults. These results support the potential involvement of urate‐transport pathways in BP regulation and provide a rationale for further replication and mechanistic studies to clarify how urate handling, renal sodium balance, and vascular function interact in the development of salt‐sensitive hypertension.
