Editorial: vocal communication in corvids
Authors: Claudia A F Wascher, Valérie Dufour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCorvids are one of the most prominent avian taxa for the study of animal behaviour and cognition, yet their vocal communication remains comparatively understudied. This special collection synthesizes current research on the ontogeny, mechanisms, functions, and evolutionary trajectories of corvid vocal systems through the lens of Tinbergen’s four questions. Contributions highlight early emergence of vocal individuality and socio-contextual plasticity in vocal behaviour across development. Corvid vocal sequences often conform to linguistic laws like Menzerath’s law, while also exhibiting context-sensitive modulation. Experimental work demonstrates advanced vocal flexibility and cognitive control: rooks can learn human verbal commands by attending primarily to auditory cues, and perceive rhythmic variations and flexibly adjust vocal timing in response to tempo and metrical changes, even without full entrainment, positioning corvids as promising models for exploring the evolutionary roots of rhythm and musicality. Field-based experiments show that female jackdaws recognize mates’ contact calls under natural conditions, supporting social coordination and pair-bond stability. Phylogenetic analyses demonstrate how ecological pressures and social complexity shape acoustic structure across the Corvidae family, while novel biologging and machine-learning approaches promise to overcome methodological challenges in studying vocal communication in corvids. Together, these findings position corvids as a powerful model for exploring the evolution of communication systems and their cognitive underpinnings.Keywords: Corvids, Ontogeny, Proximate mechanisms, Phylogeny, Tinbergen’s 4 whys, Vocal communication

Introduction

Understanding key aspects of vocal communication, such as deciphering the information encoded in calls or understanding how individuals interpret different call-types, is essential for advancing animal cognition research. It provides a window into the cognitive processes underlying how animals produce, perceive, and interpret signals (Schwartz et al. 2020; Seyfarth et al. 2010). Vocal communication can involve decision-making, perspective-taking, and emotional expression and animals can flexibly adjust their signals based on social and environmental cues (Kasumovic and Brooks 2011; Proppe et al. 2011). Investigating these processes offers insights into the evolution of language and the neural and cognitive mechanisms involved. Ultimately, understanding vocal communication also informs broader questions about the origins and diversity of communication systems across species (Castellucci et al. 2022; Suzuki et al. 2020).

Research on vocal communication has traditionally centred on songbirds, due to their remarkable vocal learning abilities (Jarvis 2019; Nowicki and Searcy 2014), and non-human primates, given their close evolutionary relationship to humans (Fischer 2017; Hage 2024). Interestingly, despite being a main model system in animal behaviour and cognition, many aspects of corvids vocal communication remain surprisingly understudied (Taylor, 2014; Wascher, 2018). Due to their high adaptability to diverse ecological and social environments, corvids present an ideal study system to investigate evolutionary drivers of vocal complexity. By expanding research on corvid vocal communication, we can gain deeper insights into the evolution of social cognition, the flexibility of animal communication systems, and the parallels between avian and mammalian communication. Recent studies have demonstrated that corvids possess flexible, cognitively controlled vocalizations, including volitional control and context-dependent vocal output (Brecht et al. 2019; Liao et al. 2024; Tomasek et al. 2023), bridging the gap between birdsong research and cognitive neuroscience (Liao et al. 2025). The main aim of this special collection is to synthesize current knowledge on the ecological, social, and cognitive drivers of vocal communication in corvids and to highlight their value as a model group for understanding the evolution of vocal communication. We suggest studying corvid vocal communication through Tinbergen’s four questions: ontogeny, mechanisms, function, and evolution, which offers a comprehensive framework for understanding how and why complex vocal systems evolved (Bateson and Laland 2013). We aim to inspire future research that bridges gaps in species coverage and methodological approaches to advance the broader field of animal cognition.

Ontogeny

Developmental aspects of animal behaviour and cognition are often poorly understood, and this includes questions around how corvid vocalizations develop over an individual’s lifetime.

Gidl et al. (2025) provide the first systematic investigation of how vocal individuality develops during early life in carrion crows (Corvus corone). Tracking five hand-raised chicks from the nestling to fledgling stage, the study shows that calls are individually distinctive from as early as eleven days post-hatching and can be classified to the correct individual above chance across all age classes. Identity cues were encoded by multiple acoustic parameters, such as duration, amplitude, and spectral features, whose relative importance remained stable over time, suggesting a robust multi-parameter coding system. Importantly, vocal dissimilarity between individuals increased with age, indicating that individuality becomes more pronounced. These findings highlight that vocal identity in corvids emerges early and strengthens through development.

In their study of captive common ravens (Corvus corax), Damini et al. (2025) investigate the developmental trajectory and social-contextual plasticity of mobbing behaviour across two key stages of offspring development: shortly after fledging and near independence. Using a potentially threatening human as a predator stimulus, they tested twelve family groups and found that adult ravens exhibited significantly more mobbing early in the offspring’s life, while juvenile ravens showed increasingly active engagement and were less prone to ignore the threat as they matured. These results suggest a developmental shift in parental anti-predator investment, which diminishes as offspring gain cognitive and motoric competence. The authors propose that the observed individual variation in mobbing engagement may mark the emergence of behavioural individuality, offering a promising avenue for future research.

Proximate mechanisms

As oscine passerines, corvids possess specialized brain regions for vocal learning and production, allowing them to acquire, modify, and control their vocal output based on experience and environmental cues (Liao et al. 2025). Understanding the underlying cognitive and neural mechanisms of vocal behaviour in corvids can help to trace the evolutionary origins and diversity of cognitive abilities across species. Kuspiel et al. (2025), in this special collection, use a playback setup to test responses of non-territorial Eurasian magpies (Pica pica) to the general ‘chatter’ alarm call. Individuals responded faster to longer chatter calls. The differential responses of receivers indicate that chatter calls are not treated as acoustically equivalent but instead appear to encode information about the caller’s motivational state or perceived threat level.

Jackdaws (Corvus monedula) provide evidence that individual identity is reliably encoded in vocalisations. Szipl et al. (2025) analysed over 600 contact calls and revealed two structurally distinct variants, a harmonic and a noisy form, both of which encoded individual identity. Using spectrogram-based classification, the study demonstrated high accuracy in identifying callers, particularly for harmonic calls, which exhibited greater acoustic variability.

In the same species, Lee et al. (2025) conducted a field-based playback experiment to test whether female jackdaws distinguish their partner’s contact calls from those of a familiar neighbour and an unfamiliar individual. Incubating females responded significantly more quickly to playbacks of their mate’s calls, compared to those from neighbouring or stranger jackdaws. These findings demonstrate that female jackdaws can recognize individual mates via vocal cues under natural conditions. Such vocal recognition likely supports social coordination and stable pair bonds crucial for reproductive cooperation and colony life. The study provides robust experimental evidence of mate-specific vocal discrimination in a wild corvid, shedding light on the cognitive mechanisms underpinning vocal communication and social bonding in group-living birds.

Recent studies highlight how corvid vocal communication reflects both universal efficiency principles and context-sensitive modulation. Wascher and Youngblood (2025) demonstrated that vocal sequences in captive carrion crows adhere to Menzerath’s law, with shorter elements occurring in longer sequences, a pattern consistent with compression and energetic optimization observed across taxa. Complementing this, (Liao et al., 2026) examined crows performing a numerically cued vocal production task and found that Menzerath’s law emerged under externally imposed sequence lengths, but with reduced strength compared to wild sequences. Moreover, crows exhibited final lengthening, progressive extension of calls toward sequence boundaries, suggesting cognitive planning strategies when sequences are goal-directed. Strikingly, humans performing the same task showed the opposite pattern, underscoring that such temporal laws are not rigid universals but shaped by species-specific cognition, motor constraints, and communicative context. Together, these findings illustrate that corvid vocal systems balance efficiency with flexibility, offering a powerful model for investigating how cognitive and ecological pressures interact to shape the structure and function of vocal communication.

Rooks (Corvus frugilegus) demonstrated the ability to learn and follow human verbal commands without relying on domestication or extensive human interaction. Cornero et al. (2025) describe how one rook successfully learned three verbal commands by attending primarily to auditory cues, not to human gaze or subconscious experimenter signals, and quickly adapted to the absence of visual lip movements. Two additional rooks also showed command learning over a shorter period. These findings suggest that sensitivity to human cues and command learning in animals can arise from general cognitive abilities rather than domestication or enculturation. The results also shed further light on general associative learning abilities of corvids and how these cognitive mechanisms can be relevant in the context of vocal communication.

Martin et al. (2026) tested whether rooks could spontaneously entrain their vocalizations to non-biologically relevant rhythmic stimuli without prior conditioning. While the birds did not synchronize their vocal output to the beat in a predictive or reactive manner, several individuals modulated aspects of their song, such as inter-onset intervals and vocalization duration, in response to changes in tempo and metrical structure. These findings suggest that rooks perceive rhythmic variations and can flexibly adjust vocal production, even in the absence of reinforcement. Such plasticity may reflect underlying cognitive processes linked to vocal learning and volitional control, traits that are rare among non-human animals. Although full entrainment was not observed, the ability to modify vocal timing in response to external rhythms positions corvids as promising models for exploring the evolutionary roots of musicality and the building blocks of rhythm perception across species.

Ultimate functions

Corvid vocalizations serve a range of adaptive functions for example territory defence or predator avoidance, allowing corvids to navigate complex social and ecological environments (Wascher and Reynolds 2025). For example, some corvids use functionally referential calls to distinguish between different types of threats, enabling group members to respond appropriately to specific dangers (Griesser 2008, 2009). Vocalizations also facilitate group cohesion and coordination, supporting the maintenance of social bonds (Dibnah et al. 2022).

Research on the meaning of corvid calls lags behind compared to other taxa, for example primates (Fischer and Price 2017). This gap is largely due to methodological challenges, such as identifying which vocal units carry meaning, accurately linking calls to specific contexts, and the limitations of traditional playback experiments when dealing with corvids’ large and flexible repertoires. Salis et al. (2025), in this special collection, highlight emerging research avenues, stemming from studies in other songbirds and mammals that could help address these challenges and advance our understanding of meaning in corvid vocalisations. They also provide suggestions for developing innovative experimental protocols that move beyond classical playback methods to better capture the complexity and flexibility of corvid communication.

Phylogeny

Corvid vocalisations are shaped by ecological and social factors. Empirical evidence shows that corvid vocalizations have evolved in response to a wide range of habitats, trophic niches, and social systems, with adaptations such as calls encoding information about predator type and vocalizations supporting group cohesion and coordination (Wascher and Reynolds 2025).

In this special collection, Kovářová et al. (2025) conducted a comparative study on alarm-call structure across the Corvidae family, analysing recordings from 66 species spanning 21 genera. They measured four acoustic parameters, mean peak frequency, frequency modulation, harmonicity, and call duration, and examined their relationship to phylogeny, morphology, and ecology. Results showed strong phylogenetic signals for peak frequency, modulation, and harmonicity, while call duration evolved more independently. Larger-bodied species produced lower-frequency calls, and habitat type influenced acoustic structure: species in open environments exhibited greater frequency modulation, whereas forest-dwelling species produced more tonally stable calls, consistent with the acoustic adaptation hypothesis. The study highlights how alarm-call variation in corvids reflects both evolutionary lineage and ecological pressures, offering insights into the adaptive shaping of vocal communication systems.

Wascher et al. (2025) collate evidence for vocal mimicry in 39 out of 128 species. Using Bayesian modelling, they suggest that many corvid species may be ‘hidden mimics’ meaning vocal mimicry is likely more widespread than currently documented. The study provides specific predictions for which less well studied species are most likely to be mimics, aiming to guide future research and fieldwork.

Gaps and future directions

Recent research and contributions in this special collection has significantly advanced our understanding of ontogeny, mechanisms, function and phylogeny of corvid vocal communication. However, most studies have focused on a limited number of well-known species, such as American crows (Corvus brachyrhynchos) and common ravens (Corvus corax), leaving the majority of corvid species poorly studied (Wascher and Reynolds 2025). A distinct lack of standardized observational and experimental paradigms makes direct comparisons across species and contexts difficult (Wascher and Reynolds 2025). Further, corvids generally present challenging model organisms, for example due to graded calls, which makes them difficult to categorize (Martin et al. 2024) and neophobic nature, which makes it difficult to record data in the wild (Miller et al. 2022). Emerging technological tools can help to overcome challenges. For example, Baglione et al. (2025) introduce a novel biologging approach to investigate vocal communication in wild carrion crows. Over three breeding seasons they equipped 52 adult crows with lightweight “MiniDTAG” devices, integrating a microphone, accelerometer, magnetometer, and pressure sensor. Utilizing a custom machine-learning model called Voxaboxen, the researchers processed the recordings to detect and categorize over 127,000 vocalizations. By combining acoustic data with accelerometer signals, they also inferred specific behaviours—such as anti-predator mobbing events, adding vital behavioural context to vocalizations. Importantly, video monitoring across 22 crow groups confirmed that deploying these tags had negligible impact on feeding rates or reproductive success. The study highlights the MiniDTAG’s potential to reliably capture the full repertoire of close-range and context-dependent vocalizations in free-living corvids, offering a powerful tool to examine cooperative communication and social cognition in natural environments.

Conclusion

Research compiled in this special collection advances our understanding of corvid vocal communication by integrating developmental, mechanistic, functional, and evolutionary perspectives. Evidence for early vocal individuality, flexible signal production, and adherence to efficiency principles such as brevity laws highlights the cognitive sophistication underlying corvid communication. At the same time, comparative and phylogenetic studies reveal that vocal systems are shaped by ecological constraints and social demands, reflecting adaptive pressures across evolutionary timescales. Despite these advances, significant gaps remain, particularly in species coverage and standardized methodologies. Emerging technologies, such as biologging and automated acoustic analysis, offer promising avenues to address these challenges and capture the complexity of corvid vocal repertoires in natural contexts. By bridging behavioural ecology, cognitive science, and bioacoustics, future research on corvids will not only deepen our understanding of avian communication but also provide critical insights into the evolutionary roots of language and social cognition.
