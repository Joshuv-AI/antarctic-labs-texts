Green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles and calcined biowaste for sustainable electrodes in asymmetric supercapacitors
Authors: Serra Ersoy, Mohamad Hasan Aleinawi, Emre Erdem, Figen Kaya, Cengiz Kaya, Bahattin Koc
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen synthesis is increasingly popular due to the focus on sustainability and biowaste recycling. This study explores zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) synthesized using broccoli extract, with carbon powder derived from calcined broccoli pulp as an alternative to commercial carbon in electrochemical slurries. Powders were characterized, and slurries were prepared in a three-electrode system with 6 molar (M) potassium hydroxide (KOH) electrolyte. Results showed that biowaste carbon in 50–70 nm ZnO NPs slurry increased specific capacitance by 74.64 Farad/gram (F/g). The ZnO NPs/biowaste carbon powder retained 64% stability after 500 cycles, with 400 degrees Celsius (°C) identified as the optimal calcination temperature for asymmetric supercapacitors. Thanks to this study, the energy applications of materials produced by recycling biowaste in a sustainable and non-toxic way have been examined in detail for the first time.Keywords: Transition metal oxides, Supercapacitors, Eco-friendly synthesis, Sustainability, Biowaste recyclingSubject terms: Electrochemistry, Nanoscale materials

Introduction

As the global population has grown, so too has the need for energy. There is a growing interest in renewable, sustainable, and clean energy systems since non-renewable energy sources cannot keep up with the growing demand, could be costly, and could hurt the environment1. Research on high performance energy storage has been very successful because of its consistent, dependable, and cost-effective benefits, especially in the areas of renewable energy systems such as portable and flexible electronics, hybrid electric vehicles, power supply, wind/solar powering, implantable healthcare, defense and aerospace, and other electric utilities2. There are two important energy storage systems which are supercapacitors and batteries. When compared to batteries, supercapacitors offer higher cycle stability, quick charging and discharging, higher power density and safe operation3. However, their cost and environmental impact are still problems. Additionally, factors such as low energy density, voltage limitations, self-discharge rates, and cost, which still need to be developed for supercapacitors, must be considered. Ongoing developments are focused on improving energy density, cycle life, and cost-effectiveness across these technologies, aiming to meet the evolving demands of modern energy storage solutions4. For this reason, the aim of this study was to develop an electrode material in order to understanding the behaviours of green electrode via sustainable materials and increase the energy density and cycle life. Electrode materials can be grouped as electric double-layer capacitors (EDLCs) such as carbon, pseudocapacitors (PCs) such as metal oxides and conductive polymers, and composite (hybrid) materials where the two groups are combined and there are benefits- drawbacks of these materials3. For example, carbon materials have advantages in terms of surface area, conductivity, stability, and economy, while metal oxide electrodes have high specific capacitance values3. In addition, EDLCs store charge electrostatically with the Helmholtz layer principle, while PCs store charge electrochemically with the Faradaic redox principle3. In order to eliminate the negative aspects of each other due to their working mechanisms and electrochemical properties, this research article aims to develop composite electrode materials in which carbon-based materials and metal oxide materials are used as hybrids with sustainable materials and methods. Additionally, supercapacitors are classified into two varieties based on their electrodes which are symmetric (which use the same materials for both the anode and the cathode) and asymmetric (which use different materials for both the anode and the cathode)3. Carbon electrodes with a PC substance formed of conductive polymers or metal oxides/nitrides make up an asymmetric supercapacitor in the same device3. In this asymmetric arrangement, EDLC is used for one electrode and pseudocapacitive materials for the other in the study. This design significantly increases the specific energy of supercapacitors without significantly compromising cycling stability. The composite electrode design is a significant advancement in supercapacitor technology that offers a solution to problems with cycling stability and energy-power density5.

As a PC electrode, compared to other metal sources6, the use of metallic zinc anodes in supercapacitors has become widespread due to their advantages such as low redox potential, abundant resources, and excellent chemical stability in aqueous solutions7. ZnO is non-toxic and the particles have a high surface-to-volume ratio8. There are chemical, physical, and biological methods to synthesize ZnO NPs. Compared to traditional methods, nanoparticles produced by biological green synthesis have become quite attractive due to certain features such as cost-effectiveness, non-toxicity and environmental friendliness9. However, the potential of ZnO NPs made by green synthesis in the field of supercapacitors has not been sufficiently investigated, and electrochemical analysis has been shown to be very lacking in the studies that have been done. For example, in the literature, ZnO NPs synthesis was carried out using green tea leaf extract and only superficial electrochemical analyses of the 54 nm particle size powder were performed10. In another study, ZnO NPs synthesized with Almond Gum were found to be 35 nm and showed good electrical conductivity11. Another study emphasized that zinc oxide is a known energy storage device with high energy density of 650 Amper/gram (A/g) but it is a material that may lead to a decrease in life cycle due to dendrite growth during consecutive cycling and showed that activated carbon/ZnO composite electrode has high capacitance value, but charge-discharge, cycling stability and device measurements have never been investigated12. Therefore, this study aimed to produce ZnO using waste broccoli extract and to produce carbon source from broccoli pulp for detailed electrochemical analysis and device construction. Brassica oleracea (broccoli), which belongs to the Brassicaceae (Cruciferae) family with six species, was selected as plant sourse because it is easily accessible, its extract is acidic, and more final product is obtained when dried13. Plant sources use as stabilizing and reducing agents reduce metal salts and enable them to turn into metal nanoparticles14. In the literature, NPs production using broccoli extract has been carried out for biological applications, but none of them have been done for supercapacitor applications13. Moreover, as a EDLC electrode, carbon-based electrode materials are useful for energy storage devices because they are inexpensive, have high conductivity, and are resistant to heat and chemicals15 but there is no record of an electrode system that utilizes metal nanoparticles produced from broccoli and a carbon source derived from burning broccoli at high temperatures.

ZnO NPs and powdered calcined biological waste-derived carbon were created, described, and their electrochemical and physicochemical characteristics investigated at different calcination temperatures for the first time in the literature. Prior to being tested in asymmetric device designs, these ZnO NPs and carbon powders were independently electrochemically characterized. The study first examined the viability of substituting carbon from recovered food waste for commercially available carbon black in order to establish the ideal calcination temperature for energy storage performance. The asymmetric device was built after ZnO NPs were characterized as PC electrodes and calcined broccoli powder as EDLC electrodes. ZnO NPs served as the cathode (positive electrode) and biochar (calcined broccoli powder) as the anode (negative electrode) in the asymmetric configuration. The carbon side, working in the opposite manner, is projected to enlarge the window by acting in an EDLC mechanism, whilst the metal oxide side, behaving in a Faradaic way, stores charge in a wide potential window. This study enhances the body of knowledge concerning the application of green synthesis in energy storage systems. The efficacy of green synthesis in the energy sector, particularly amid the growing dependence on renewable energy sources, has been extensively explored, alongside investigations focused on its biological applications. This research, which emphasizes sustainability and recycling, underscores the critical environmental and economic significance of utilizing household food waste for energy-related applications.

Method

Materials

ZnO NPs were synthesized using distilled water (DW), broccoli extract (BE) derived from broccoli purchased from Istanbul greengrocers, and zinc acetate dihydrate ((CH3COO)2Zn·2H2O, CAS No. 5970-45-6, EMSURE®). The broccoli extract was also utilized in preparing electrode slurry. The slurry, formulated with N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP), conductive carbon black (C-nergy Super C65), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder from Nanografi, was applied onto a 1 × 2 cm² nickel foam substrate for three-electrode electrochemical analyses. The electrochemical properties of ZnO NPs, calcined broccoli powders, and fabricated devices were evaluated using KOH pellets from AFG Bioscience as the electrolyte.

Broccoli extract production

Broccoli purchased from a local grocery store was thoroughly washed with DW and dried in an air fryer at 250 °C for 6 h. Subsequently, 5 g of dried broccoli was extracted in 50 ml of DW at 60 °C for 1 h. The extract was filtered using Whatman filter paper (110 milimeter diameter), cooled, and stored at 4 °C until use. The BE exhibited a pH of 5.21 and oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) values of 327mV and 106 mV, measured using an pH meter to assess electron activity during oxidation-reduction reactions13. Also, It was observed that ORP and mV values come from biomolecules as reactive materials and are effective in the formation of ZnO NPs.

Green synthesis of ZnO NPs

Zinc oxide nanoparticles were synthesized using the green synthesis method and BE. 10 ml of prepared BE were added dropwise to 90 ml of DW. Then, the solution was mixed with a magnetic stirrer at room temperature. 0.1 M zinc acetate dihydrate was added to the beaker aqueous as a zinc salt raw source and BE solution. The solution was agitated at 70o C for 3 h for bioreduction mechanism. To remove the solvents, it was dried at 100° C for 24 h in the oven. After that, when it had dried, it was ground with a mortar into a fine powder. The last heat treatment, calcination, was performed at 500° C for 1 h. After the calcination procedure, the powdered ZnO NPs were once again pulverized in a mortar before being placed within tubes13.

Calcination of dried broccoli

The broccoli pulp, a byproduct of extract production, was re-dried at 250 °C for 6 h. The dried broccoli was then calcined in a crucible at temperatures of 300, 350, 375, 400, 450, and 500 °C for 1 h each. The resulting broccoli powders were ground into fine particles using a mortar. Analytical studies were conducted to assess the impact of calcination temperature on the powder properties, and the powder calcined at 400 °C for 1 h (B400) was identified as exhibiting optimal performance based on characterization results. This powder was selected for use as a carbon electrode in electrochemical analyses. The primary focus was on the color of the powder. For example, Fig. 1(a) evaluated the colors of the broccoli powder obtained at each temperature. The presence of green broccoli indicated that the temperature was not high enough for combustion. The black color in the powder results from the carbonization (burning) of the vegetable’s organic components and is generally associated with prolonged cooking or high sugar content, typically between 200 and 350 °C. At temperatures above 400 °C, everything organic burns away completely, leaving behind mineral oxides leftovers like calcium and potassium oxides, which makes the powder turn white or gray. Because this study aimed to use alternative natural wastes instead of commercial carbon, a high carbon content temperature was selected. Calcination temperatures of 300, 350, 350, and 500 °C were initially eliminated due to the color of the broccoli powder16–19.

(a) Green synthesis of ZnO NPs with the help of the broccoli extract and producing steps of broccoli carbon powders from waste broccoli at different calcination temperatures, and (b) preparation of the slurry using PVDF and NMP to coat ZnO NPs and B400 powders onto nickel foams and electrochemical analysis in the three-electrode system [BioRender].

Electrode and electrolyte preparations

This study aimed to evaluate the potential of calcined broccoli powder (B400) as a substitute for conductive carbon black to enhance the electrochemical performance of ZnO nanoparticles (NPs). To prepare electrodes, nickel foams were cut into 1 × 2 cm² pieces, cleaned in 2 M hydrochloric acid (HCl), ultrasonicated for 1 min, rinsed with DW and ethanol, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 24 h. The slurry was prepared by mixing 5% PVDF binder, 10% conductive carbon black, and 85% active material, with 0.5 ml NMP added, and stirred for 24 h. Each nickel foam was coated with 30 µL of the homogeneous slurry, dried at 80 °C in a vacuum oven, and weighed before and after coating. The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 6 M KOH in DW and stirring until cooled. For surface activation, the coated nickel foam, 6 M KOH solution, and one drop of Triton-X-100 (Polyethylene Glycol Octyl Phenyl Ether) were placed in a beaker and stored in a desiccator overnight before electrochemical analysis20. Then, electrochemical analysis of the powders was carried out in a three-electrode system. Additionally, the same procedure was repeated with calcined broccoli powder (B400) instead of 10% conductive carbon black and 85% ZnO NPs as an active material. All experimental steps were sketched in Fig. 1.

Characterizations

This work includes the characterizations of ZnO NPs, BE, calcined broccoli, three- and two-electrode electrochemical studies, as well as electrochemical tests for asymmetric devices. Supercapacitor performance is determined by the electrode’s chemical composition, electrolyte, temperature, crystal structure and crystallinity, morphology, pore structure and specific surface area, and electrode thickness21. Therefore, the analyses in this study were conducted to examine these factors.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses

SEM and TEM analyses were performed to interpret the morphology, size and elemental mapping of the produced ZnO NPs and calcined broccoli with EDS analysis. All particles were suspended in 1 ml of ethanol and air-dried on silicone surfaces. The observation was carried out with a nitrogen-cooled Zeiss Ultra 55 Gemini SEM outfitted with an EDAX detector with accelerating voltages of 10 kilovolt (kV). The particles’ morphology was also investigated using a JEOL JEM-ARM200CFEG UHR-TEM (fitted with STEM and EDS). The particle size of the produced powders was determined from SEM and TEM pictures using the Image-J analysis software (Image-J v1.54/ https://imagej.net/ij/download.html ), which is freely available in the public domain13.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The ZnO and calcined broccoli particles’ crystalline structures were studied using a Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer at 40 kV and 40 mA (miliamper), using CuKa radiation at 1.5418 Ångström (Å). The crystallinity values were determined, and the material’s amorphous and crystalline properties were examined. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (IRAffinity-1 S Shimadzu) was used to examine the chemical bonds of ZnO nanoparticles, broccoli juice, calcined broccoli, inks at various concentrations, and gel electrolyte in the 600–4000 cm− 1 range13.

Thermogravimetry differential thermal (TG/DTA) analysis

To assess the thermal degradation of zinc oxide powder and dried broccoli powder before calcination, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA-Exstar 6300) was carried out in air at a heating rate of 5 ° C min− 1. In addition, differential thermal analysis (DTA-Netzsch STA 449 C) was carried out on the same powders from room temperature to 800° C in an oxygen (O2) environment at a heating rate of 5° C min− 1.

Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) analysis

The effects of annealing temperature on surface area and porosity were examined using BET on a Micrometrics ASAP 2020 apparatus. The samples were degassed at 150° C for 24 h under 0 to 950 millimeters of mercury (mmHg) pressure using the Micrometrics VacPrep061 Sample Degas System in nitrogen (N2 ) atmosphere3.

Raman analysis

Raman spectroscopy is one of the most non-destructive and effective techniques for extracting structural information from particles23. For Raman analysis, a hybrid Renishaw Raman apparatus was employed. The surface of the three different calcined broccoli powders were smoothed after they were added to the microscope section. 10% laser power was used for the Raman analysis, which covered the 100–3200 cm− 1 range.

Electrochemical analyses

The three-electrode system, was typically used for identifying the electrochemical properties of powders for the electrodes which are ZnO NPs and calcined broccoli, and the two-electrode system was used to test the asymmetric devices3. In the three-electrode system, there was a working electrode (WE: ZnO and calcined broccoli powders at 400 °C), a reference electrode (RE: Mercury/mercury oxide-Hg/HgO), and a counter electrode (CE: Platinum), whereas the two-electrode system has positive and negative electrodes3. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS), Galvanostatic Charge/Discharge (GCD), energy density, power density, and specific capacitance values were used to study the electrochemical responses of ZnO NPs, calcined broccoli, and devices3. CV studies were carried out in 6 M KOH with a potential window range of -0.6 V to + 0.6 V and scanned at different rates (5-200 mV/s). A Metrohm/Autolab potentiostat was used to acquire the voltammograms, which were coupled inside the 6 M KOH electrolyte23. Electrochemical analysis measurements were measured with INTELLO 1.5 and Methrom Autolab B.V. Nova 2.1.7 (https://www.metrohm.com/tr_tr/products/electrochemistry.html) software programs. Furthermore, measurements were conducted in a three-electrode system by covering nickel foams with the PC material ZnO NPs and using carbon source calcined broccoli powder as the EDCL material. The effect of the slurry produced with calcined broccoli rather than conductive carbon black was then investigated. ZnO NPs and calcined broccoli were also used in asymmetric device analyses. ZnO NPs and brocoli powders at different temperatures in 6 M KOH electrolyte were used for asymmetric devices to understand the behaviours of calcined broccoli as a carbon content instead of conductive carbon black. Results

Characterizations of ZnO NPs

Figure 2(a) shows the SEM image of ZnO nanoparticles at 4.00, 10.00 and 20.00 kX magnifications, TEM image at 200 nm, and EDS analysis. SEM and TEM pictures revealed that ZnO NPs were generated in a uniformly distributed, conical-rod-like configuration. The ZnO NPs formed in this study had a rod-like structure, similar to the chemical technique, however those made via green synthesis were found to be spherical in comparison to other investigations. Even NPs between 5 and 20 nm could be synthesized using BE, according to a review of ZnO NPs made with BE and zinc chloride heptahydrate source in the literature30. Green synthesis produced nanoparticles that were at a more nano level than ZnO NPs made by chemical and physical methods22. An average crystallite size of 50–70 nm was determined during this investigation. Figure 2(b) shows the XRD analysis of ZnO NPs31. At 2θ values of 31.96°, 34.59°, 36.49°, 47.81°, 56.73°, 63.02°, 66.6°, 68.11°, 69.38°, 72.81°, and 77.19°, ZnO patterns displayed distinct peaks that could be attributed to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), (201), (004), and (202) planes of hexagonal wurtzite phase ZnO. The powder diffraction standard reported for the hexagonal phase of ZnO nanoparticles (JCPDS 36-1451) is in good agreement with the diffraction peaks32. In addition, crystallinity ratio and ZnO NPs crystallite size were calculated with Debye–Scherrer’s Eq. (1). It was observed that the particles were 84.86% crystalline and 15.14% amorphous30. Furthermore, Fig. 2(c) displays the prepared ZnO NPs’ FTIR spectra. The stretching frequency of the hydroxyl group (O–H) in the ZnO NPs made with BE was 3227 cm− 1. The alkene group’s (C = C) asymmetric stretching frequency originated at 1680 cm− 1. At 1521 cm− 1, the N–H bending was detected. The Zn-O stretching bands are responsible for the peaks observed at about 600 cm− 1. Other peaks may show the existence of metabolites including alkaloids, flavonoids, polyphenols, and carboxylic acids, based on the FTIR data. Zinc ions were reduced to ZnO NPs with the aid of these substances, primarily flavonoids and other phenolics22. The presence of free amino and carboxylic groups interacting with the zinc surface is most likely what gives the produced ZnO NPs their stability. Furthermore, the medium’s proteins stabilize ZnO NPs by covering them with a sheath that stops them from aggregating. Previous research on this topic suggests that the molecules’ phenolic groups are responsible for the reduction process, while the durability of ZnO nanoparticles may be due to free amino and carboxylic groups22. As seen in Fig. 2(d), a thermogravimetric examination was performed to ascertain the heat stability of green produced ZnO NPs. As the dried powder of ZnO NPs was heated from room temperature to 800° C, the weight loss was recorded. The heating process was conducted at a temperature scanning rate of 5° C/min in an oxygen atmosphere31. Using TGA analysis, it was demonstrated that green produced ZnO NPs had excellent heat stability. In the temperature range between room temperature and 800° C, a progressive weight loss of about 14.8% of the actual weight was assumed to be caused by the loss of organic matter and moisture content. The degradation of organic groups involved in the production of ZnO NPs utilizing plant extract, on the other hand, was responsible for the notable weight loss (~ 50%) that was noted between 350 and 600° C, as demonstrated by the research in the literature31. Additionally, endothermic and exothermic reactions and their temperatures in DTA analysis are shown in Fig. 2(d), respectively. DTG analysis is also included in the same table. The highest weight loss values of the material were shown in this analysis as 84.1o C at 7.56th minute and 305.7o C at 28.42nd minute.

Characterizations of broccoli

To promote sustainability and eco-friendliness, the study focused on converting biowaste into carbon without chemical activation. Visual analysis indicated that powders calcined at 300, 375, and 400 °C exhibited optimal properties. Low-temperature calcination preserved the green color of broccoli, while high-temperature calcination led to oxidation, resulting in gaseous or glassy material loss. Broccoli powder calcined at 400 °C (B400) was selected as the carbon source for electrode production due to its superior surface area and pore structure, as determined by comparative analyses with powders calcined at 300 °C (B300) and 375 °C (B375). The use of biowaste broccoli as a carbon source, particularly through calcination at various temperatures, is novel and unexplored in the literature, prompting reference to studies on similar biowastes for calcination development. SEM with EDS analyses, presented in Fig. 3(a), revealed that increasing calcination temperature significantly reduced the carbon-to-oxygen ratio in the powder structure. Additionally, higher temperatures resulted in smaller particle sizes, thereby increasing surface area, which is advantageous for achieving higher capacitance values in electrochemical applications33. B400 powder was selected as the most suitable powder. In Fig. 3(b), the XRD results of the powders are shown. From the XRD graph, crystallinity was calculated as approximately 1% for B300 powder, 6% for B375 powder and 16% for B400 powder. The diffraction patterns in the XRD result were found to be similar to the peaks corresponding to the standard JCPDS file (41–1487) for the (002), (100) and (101) crystal planes34. Figure 3(c) shows the FTIR analysis result of BE. The FTIR spectra of BE revealed bands at 3300 cm− 1, 2100 cm− 1, 1634 cm− 1, and 664 cm− 1. The 3300 band was assumed to represent a -NH stretching of the amide (II) band. Furthermore, the peak at 1634 cm− 1 revealed the O-H stretching peaks of alcohols and phenols35. Because of the bio-extract present in the sample, the C-Br-stretching vibration was assumed to constitute a chemical bond in the band gap seen at 664 cm− 1 The C-C bond stretching of the alkene produced the peak at 2100 cm− 1. The plane bending vibration of aromatic primary amines was also seen to be generated by the C-H bond, according to literature36. Figure 3(d) also includes the FTIR analyses of dried (DB), and calcined B300, B375 and B400 powders, respectively. The graph, which matches well with the FTIR analysis of porous carbon in the literature, consists of hydroxyl group, aliphatic (-CH), aromatic and -CH bonds, respectively. Differences in calcination temperature were observed, especially in the fingerprint region. It was observed that the aromatic C-O bonds in the 1115 cm− 1 band gave the most peak in B400 powder37.

The structural characteristics of carbon compounds generated from broccoli that were calcined at various temperatures were assessed using Raman spectroscopy. Characteristic D (~ 1350 cm⁻¹) and G (~ 1580 cm⁻¹) bands were seen in every sample, as shown in Fig. 3. Planar vibrations of sp2 hybridized carbon atoms are represented by the G band, whereas flaws in the carbon structure and its amorphous nature are represented by the D band. For samples B300, B375, and B400, the corresponding IG/ID ratios were determined to be 1.165, 1.174, and 1.158. These ratios show that the orderliness of the carbon structure is significantly impacted by the calcination temperature. The sample with the most ordered graphitic structure, specifically the one calcined at 375 °C, had the highest IG/ID ratio. The low ratio at 400 °C implies that high temperatures lead to structural degradation and an increase in flaws, while the lower temperature of 300 °C indicates that carbonization has not advanced far enough. According to these findings, the ideal temperature for achieving an optimal carbon structure is 375 °C. Furthermore, a broad, low-intensity band measuring roughly 2500–3000 cm⁻¹ was seen in every sample. The 2D (or G′) band, which is often the second harmonic component of the G band in carbon structures, is represented by this region. Second-order Raman scattering, which is especially noticeable in graphene and multilayered graphitic materials, is linked to the 2D band. Although the resulting carbon compounds are far from highly organized crystalline graphene structures, the broad and low intensity of the observed band suggests that they do possess some graphitic features. Additionally, this area may potentially contain overtone signals that arise from the combination of the D and G bands. The idea that biochars made from broccoli contain a heterogeneous carbon structure with amorphous and semi-graphitic characteristics is supported by this discovery38–41 .

Additionally, The D and G intensities in the graphs correspond to the structure of activated carbon described in the literature42. The degree of graphitization of a carbon material is often determined by the IG/ID value in Raman spectra, where IG represents the sp2-bonded carbon atoms in the hexagonal graphite ring and ID represents the vibrations of the sp3-bonded carbon atoms of defects and disorder.

As demonstrated in Fig. 3(e), the IG/ID ranking of carbon materials is B400 > B300 > B375, implying that B400 would have the highest adsorption selectivity coefficient and increase hydrogenation43.

Figure 3(f) shows the TG/DTA analysis of dried broccoli from room temperature to 800o C. The TG curve demonstrates three weight loss phases at 0∼200, 200∼400, and 400∼800o C. These indicate the loss of physically absorbed water and crystal water, the generation of CO2 and the degradation of biomolecules into carbon. The DTA curve revealed three major endothermic peaks at 64.3, 296, and 456.5o C, which corresponded to the procedure outlined above. Weight loss becomes minimal above 450o C. However, because the carbon content reduced as the temperature climbed, it was assumed that the optimal calcination temperature would be 400o C 42.

Electrochemical analyses of ZnO NPs, calcined broccoli and devices

After characterization of ZnO NPs, first powders were added to slurry prepared with conductive carbon black, PVDF, and NMP and coated on nickel foams for electrochemical analysis in a three-electrode system. ZnO NPs measured in 6 M KOH electrolyte were used as the working electrode, the platinum plate counter electrode, and the Hg/HgO reference electrode in a three-electrode system. In addition, NPs were measured in the 0-0.6 voltage range. EIS, CV, and GCD analyses were performed, respectively, and specific capacitance values were calculated.

The Nyquist plot for EIS of ZnO NPs is explained in Fig. 4(a). It showed a small semi arc at very high frequency and continued as a straight line like the pseudocapacitor materials at low frequency44. The connecting point is on the Z-axis, which determines the electrochemical resistance (Rs). The semicircle diameter indicates the high-frequency range charge transfer resistance (Rct), whereas the linear line explains the Warburg impediment in the low frequency range44. The Rs value for ZnO NPs was found to be 0.002325 ohm (Ω). The Rs value describes the kinetics of the material and indicates faster charge transfer44. For three-electrode systems, CV records the voltage between the reference and working electrodes, whereas for two-electrode systems, CV records the cathode and anode current between positive and negative. In supercapacitors, EDLC electrodes have a fully rectangular CV, whereas PC electrodes have a quasi-rectangular CV due to faradic responses3. As specific capacitance (Csp) grows, Csp gradually declines as current density and scan rate increase; thus, at low current density and scan rate, electrolytic ions penetrate the electrode’s entire surface more steadily. High scan rates hinder the movement of electrolytic ions, negatively impacting redox and energy storage capacity44.

Electrochemical analyses of ZnO NPs powder in a three-electrode system: (a) EIS, (b) CV at different scan rates in the 0-0.6 voltage range, (c) GCD plots at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 A/g current density values, and (d) specific capacitance values calculated from the CV plot.

Figure 4(b) adds the CV analysis at different scanning speeds for ZnO powder. In CV analysis, as the scan rate increases, the area under it increases, but the specific capacitance values decrease because the ions are not allowed to penetrate sufficiently.

While the specific capacitance was found to be 75.176 F/g at 5 mV/s, it was calculated as 43.012 F/g at 100 mV/s scan rate (Fig. 4(d)). For more accurate measurements, the GCD graph was used3. In CV analysis, oxidation and reduction peaks were also seen. Oxidation (anodic) peaks increased as positive current as scan rate increased. Similarly, reduction (cathodic) peaks decreased as negative current as scan rate increased. GCD analysis uses factors such as capacitance, ohmic resistance (iR drop), and peak voltage (V0) of the working electrode to calculate the energy and power density of the devices3. While the GCD graph in EDLC electrodes is in the shape of an inverted V, the graphs of PCs are more curved.

Discussions

Green synthesis has been extensively researched for biological applications due to its biocompatibility and non-toxicity in the literature. However, new research has begun to investigate the use of plant-extract-synthesized ZnO in asymmetric supercapacitors and other energy storage devices 46,47,. Notably, there has been no direct study on ZnO NPs synthesized specifically with broccoli extract for use in asymmetric systems. Carbon production from biomass wastes such as broccoli has attracted attention in recent years as a sustainable and environmentally friendly approach. However, additionally there is no widely accepted application of carbon specifically obtained from broccoli as a commercial carbon substitute and broccoli has only been used for photocatalytic activity30 and biological applications 48,49. In this study, ZnO nanoparticles were first synthesized using broccoli extract (Fig. 1a). Compared to existing literature, the synthesized material exhibited higher purity (83.36% Zn) and more homogeneous nanoparticle distribution, resulting in improved crystal structure (Fig. 2a). Also, XRD analysis confirmed high crystallinity (Fig. 2b), while the distinct Zn–O band in Fig. 2c and a 50.2% mass loss up to 756.7 °C (Fig. 2d) suggested enhanced thermal stability30,48,49. Moreover, broccoli waste carbons calcined at different calcination temperatures were compared with carbon sources obtained from rice husk, banana peel50 and tea waste51 since no such studies have been found in the literature. Among the samples, B400 and B475 showed more homogeneous and porous morphology, resembling the micro- and mesoporous architecture of activated carbon. As shown in Fig. 3, the homogeneous and porous structure of B400 powder was observed to be closer to the micro- and mesoporous structure of activated carbon. The (101) and (002) peaks of B400 were more pronounced than the others, indicating that the graphitic carbon content may be higher. Raman analysis showed that commercial carbon and activated carbon were generally characterized by IG/ID>1, indicating a dominant graphitic structure. The IG/ID ratio of B400 was found to be close to the others, but the literature reported that this ratio optimizes around 400 °C during biowaste carbonization. BET analysis (Table 1) showed a surface area of 3.3512 m²/g for B400, below that of commercial carbon but improved in terms of porosity and graphitic features 52,53. Following powder characterization, electrochemical analyses were carried out using 6 M KOH in a three-electrode system. Initially, ZnO NPs and B400 powders were integrated with commercial carbon black for slurry preparation. Performance metrics were evaluated via EIS, CV, GCD, and specific capacitance analyses (Figs. 4 and 5). Subsequently, a ZnO NPs slurry was prepared using PVDF and NMP, using B400 instead of commercially available carbon black, and compared with the commercially prepared ZnO NPs electrode (Fig. 6). According to the results, the WEZ value for ZnO was approximately 1500 Ω, while this value decreased to around 1000 − 500 Ω for the B400-ZnO combination. B400 was found to increase the electrical conductivity of ZnO, as a lower WEZ value indicates better conductivity and faster ion diffusion. The combination with B400 increased the current density due to the conductive contribution of carbon, indicating an expansion of the electrochemically active surface area for ZnO. It also increased the specific capacitance at low scan rates, and the B400-ZnO maintained a capacitance stability of 64.05% after 500 cycles. In this study, three promising biowaste calcined carbon powders (B300, B375 and B400) were used in an asymmetric device constructed in a two-electrode system using 6 M KOH electrolyte (Fig. 7). B400 exhibited the best conductivity with the lowest WEZ value (400 Ω). OCV analysis also showed that it was the least susceptible to rapid corrosion. Furthermore, it demonstrated good stability with approximately 75% stability for 1000 cycles. It was observed to have the second-highest energy density with a long discharge time, but its power density was lower compared to the other powders. In Fig. 8, B400 gave the best result as the most symmetrical and long-lasting charge-discharge profiles. Furthermore, when examining Fig. 9, B400 had the best Q3 and a3 (surface homogeneity) values.

Thanks to this study, green synthesis was compared with the electrochemical analysis results of nanoparticles synthesized by traditional methods. The results presented here reveal that powders made by green synthesis are more cost-effective than commercial alternatives, have intriguing potential for energy storage applications, and exhibit equivalent or superior specific capacitance at low scan rates when derived from recycled biowaste. These findings demonstrate that powders created by green synthesis are less expensive than commercial equivalents, have considerable potential for energy storage applications, and provide comparable or superior specific capacitance at low scan rates when obtained from recycled biowaste.

On the other hand, several limitations were identified in this study. Despite promising conductivity and stability comparable to commercial carbon black, the ZnO/B400 system still suffers from limited surface area, constraining specific capacitance performance. While it could replace commercial carbon black with its sustainability advantages, it was determined that surface area optimization was necessary for practical applications. Because surface area was thought to be the reason for the inability to achieve high specific capacitances, it was considered that the surface could be activated and used in future studies. It is also thought that ZnO NPs should be manufactured at a nanoscale range. For this aim, it is proposed that alternate plant extracts, rather than broccoli, be used in synthesis trials for future investigations. Following synthesis, the BET analysis, which was only performed on calcined broccoli powders in this study, should be applied to ZnO powders to determine their surface area and porosity. It is expected that if an extract source capable of producing high surface area results can be identified using BET analysis of ZnO NPs synthesized with various plant extracts, the electrochemical findings would improve. When the reasons for the low electrochemical results were investigated, it was determined that there may be other effects. Another parameter that affects the results is the electrode width and diameter. According to research in the literature, the parameters that yield the best results are listed as follows. The ideal electrode size in supercapacitor devices is determined by a balance of ion transport kinetics and active material consumption. Electrode thicknesses between 50 and 200 μm are recommended for optimal ion diffusion and rate performance. Excessively thick electrodes can impede diffusion, increase internal resistance, and cause polarization loss. Similarly, the electrode diameter must be carefully matched to the cell configuration; for example, coin-type cells typically use electrodes with diameters ranging from 12 to 16 mm, whereas larger pouch or cylindrical cells require scaled geometries to ensure uniform current distribution and minimize edge effects. Thus, electrode size should be tuned based on device architecture to maximize capacitance and energy density while ensuring consistent power delivery and long-term cycling dependability265455. It is thought that the electrochemical results can be improved if the electrode is prepared by paying attention to these measurements in future studies.

Nonetheless, this study shows for the first time in the literature that domestic food waste such as broccoli, may be transformed into carbon in a cost-effective manner, and that recycling can be converted into energy. Furthermore, the complete electrochemical evaluation of high-purity, extremely crystalline ZnO nanoparticles manufactured using green methods in both three- and two-electrode systems provides a solid foundation for future research. The findings highlight the importance of sustainable materials in advancing energy technology and encourage further research in this area.

Conclusions

This study employed a green synthesis strategy to reduce ZnO nanoparticles (NPs), using zinc acetate dihydrate as a zinc source and leftover broccoli juice as both a stabilizing and reducing agent. Asymmetric supercapacitor devices were fabricated using carbon sources derived from broccoli waste calcined at various temperatures (300, 350, 375, 400, 450, and 500 °C), and ZnO NPs synthesized through heat treatments at 100 °C for 24 h and 500 °C for one hour. First, physicochemical analyses were conducted independently for both the calcined biowaste broccoli and the ZnO NPs. In a three-electrode setup, 30 µL of 49–70 nm rod-like ZnO NPs were deposited onto 1 × 2 cm² nickel foam and immersed in 6 M KOH electrolyte for electrochemical evaluation. Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) measurements at 0.5 A/g revealed a discharge time of 36.999 s, while cyclic voltammetry (CV) at 5 mV/s yielded a specific capacitance of 75.176 F/g. Calcined waste broccoli powders at 300, 375, and 400 °C showed optimal carbonization color and avoided transformation into organic oxides. Raman spectroscopy identified B400 as having the best graphitic structure and crystallinity. Electrochemical evaluation of B400 in the three-electrode system showed a specific capacitance of 143.67 F/g (CV at 5 mV/s) and a discharge time of 19.999 s (GCD at 0.5 A/g). The addition of ZnO NPs significantly enhanced specific capacitance when paired with calcined broccoli-derived carbon materials, surpassing the performance of sludge prepared using commercial carbon. Furthermore, EIS, OCV, CV, GCD, and capacity retention tests were conducted on asymmetric devices assembled using three different broccoli-derived powders. Among them, the ZnO/B400 device exhibited the longest discharge time (0.072 s), while the ZnO/B375 device demonstrated superior stability, highest specific capacitance, and an energy density of 8.1 Wh/kg at 10 mV/s. This study demonstrates that recycled waste materials have significant potential compared to commercial alternatives by presenting, for the first time, a comprehensive analysis of the energy storage mechanisms in powders synthesized via green methods. With its cost-effective, environmentally friendly, and non-toxic characteristics, green synthesis is expected to be further investigated in future energy-related research and to contribute to the advancement of recycled energy technologies.
