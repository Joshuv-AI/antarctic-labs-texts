In Response
Authors: Elio Adib, Amin H Nassar, Florence K Keane
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We thank Dr. Feigenberg et al. for their interest in our article and sincerely appreciate their thoughtful perspective on the adoption of definitive radiation therapy-based approaches after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in patients with locally-advanced NSCLC who did not proceed to surgical resection.1 Their commentary adds important clinical nuance to an area in which the evidence base is still emerging.

As the authors astutely note, this is a rapidly evolving field, and considerable caution is necessary before strategies described in retrospective analyses are incorporated into routine practice. We fully share the authors’ concerns regarding the increasing number of patients in whom surgery is abandoned, particularly outside the structured framework of clinical trials, in which the rigorous selection criteria and multidisciplinary oversight that govern trial enrollment are absent. Although we reported survival outcomes and adverse event rates in this population, it is indeed inappropriate to draw direct comparisons with patients who proceed to surgical resection, as these groups represent fundamentally different clinical baselines, performance trajectories, and tumor biology profiles. This distinction must be made explicit when interpreting our findings.

Our cohort underscores this complexity through its marked heterogeneity. As our data demonstrate, patients in whom surgery was aborted because of surgical contraindications (such as local progression, critical structure invasion, etc.) experienced meaningfully worse outcomes compared with those in whom an acute intercurrent medical event precluded resection at the time of planned surgery. Although the granular details of multidisciplinary tumor board deliberations were not uniformly available across all participating institutions, this pattern highlights a subpopulation for whom more rigorous upfront selection may have yielded more favorable risk stratification and treatment allocation.

We also share our colleagues’ concerns regarding pneumonitis rates in this setting. The immunologic priming introduced by checkpoint inhibitor-based induction regimens may meaningfully alter the landscape of pulmonary toxicity, and the risk of clinically significant pneumonitis must be carefully weighed against the anticipated oncologic benefit in each individual case.2

Radiation therapy in this context may indeed provide meaningful disease control when surgery is no longer feasible, but we must first ensure, to the greatest extent possible, that the neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy approach is itself the most appropriate strategy for the patient at the outset. Several prospective trials offer a critical lens through which these questions may ultimately be answered. Early-phase induction chemoimmunotherapy data from the Atezolizumab Plus Induction Chemotherapy Plus CT-radiotherapy (APOLO) trial, the PACIFIC-BRAZIL trial, the Induction Chemotherapy and Toripalimab Followed by Chemoradiotherapy for Large-volume Local Advanced NSCLC (InTRist) trial, and other studies suggest that prechemoradiation chemoimmunotherapy may be a reasonable approach, though larger randomized trials are needed before definitive conclusions can be drawn.3, 4, 5 In the meantime, we urge our multidisciplinary colleagues to resist the tendency to position radiation as a rescue strategy and, instead, to engage these patients proactively to ensure that the pathway selected from the outset reflects a deliberate, evidence-informed decision.
