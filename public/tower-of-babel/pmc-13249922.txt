Anhydrous stannous fluoride toothpaste in dentin hypersensitivity: clinician-assessed and patient-reported outcomes from two controlled trials
Authors: Jianhong Qiu, Jonathan E Creeth, Abeera Mohammad, Kimberly R. Milleman, John Gallob, Jeffery Milleman, Monica Pradhan, Jimmy Qaqish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo confirm anti-sensitivity efficacy of a new anhydrous 0.454% stannous fluoride (SnF2) toothpaste formulation compared to a standard fluoride toothpaste after 28 and 56 days of twice-daily brushing, using clinician-assessed measures and patient-reported outcomes.Materials and methodsTwo independent, examiner-blind, parallel studies enrolled healthy adults with dentin hypersensitivity (DH). Participants were randomized to brush twice-daily with either the SnF₂ toothpaste (Test) or a standard fluoride toothpaste (Reference). DH was assessed by clinician via evaporative (Schiff scale) and tactile (Yeaple probe) stimuli. Participant-reported outcomes were measured using the Labelled Magnitude Scales (LMS) (for DH pain), and the DH Experience Questionnaire (DHEQ-48) (for impact on oral health-related quality-of-life).Results104 participants in Study 1 and 113 participants in Study 2 (18–65 years) were randomized. In both studies, the Test treatment showed a statistically significant decrease in Schiff sensitivity scores, an increase in tactile threshold, and an improvement in LMS domain scores from baseline to Day 28 and 56. The improvements in all three measures were greater than those observed for the Reference treatment (all, p < 0.05). Additionally, Study 1 showed significantly greater improvements in DHEQ scores in the Test treatment versus the Reference at Day 56. Toothpastes were generally well-tolerated.ConclusionsTwice-daily use of the 0.454% SnF₂ toothpaste significantly and progressively reduced examiner-assessed and perceived DH over 56 days versus standard fluoride toothpaste.Clinical RelevanceThe studies confirm the efficacy of the novel 0.454% SnF₂ toothpaste in delivering meaningful relief from DH, supporting its role as a patient-centred treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00784-026-06942-4.

Introduction

Dentin hypersensitivity (DH) is a persistent condition characterized by short, sharp dental pain of transient duration, arising from the exposure of dentin due to loss of overlying enamel or gingival recession [1, 2]. Exposure of the dentin surface means external stimuli (thermal, tactile, osmotic, chemical or evaporative) can cause fluid movement within dentinal tubules, which activates pain receptors originating within the pulp [3, 4]. While the pain typically subsides soon after removal of the triggering stimulus, it can significantly affect patients’ quality of life, disrupting routine activities such as eating, drinking, speaking, and toothbrushing [5].

Estimates of the prevalence of DH vary widely [2]. A recent large study by West and coworkers in a range of European countries found approximately 50% of participants experienced hypersensitivity pain when exposed to evaporative stimulation in the clinic [6]. In comparison with related earlier data, it suggests prevalence may be increasing [7].

Daily use of anti-sensitivity toothpastes is the first-line treatment for DH: they are easily accessible, cost-effective and compatible with conventional oral hygiene regimens [8]. Of the two treatment strategies used by toothpastes — including ingredients that either occlude dentinal tubules or desensitize nerves – tubule occlusion approaches are increasingly used [5]. Amongst these, toothpastes that treat DH with 0.454% stannous fluoride (SnF₂) are widely available with extensive clinical efficacy and in vitro laboratory supporting evidence, together with safety and tolerability data confirming suitability for long-term use [9–13].

The active component here is the stannous ion. This has both an affinity for negatively charged dentin surfaces, and an intrinsic instability in aqueous, aerobic conditions [14]. This means that when brushed on the teeth, the ions tend to react with salivary components and toothpaste constituents, and precipitate on the surface of dentin [14, 15]. These insoluble deposits can block tubule openings, insulating the tubule from external stimulation, effectively reducing DH.

The instability of stannous ion, key to its efficacy, makes careful formulation critical. One approach has been to formulate without water, using instead a glycerine-based continuous phase. Such formulations have proven successful in previous long-term clinical studies [9–11]. The formulation tested in this study has been modified versus those previously tested (in reference 9–11) aiming to enhance non-therapeutic benefits. New ingredients have been added to improve cosmetic benefits including tooth stain removal and organoleptic qualities, thus providing a better user experience. The results from the present DH clinical investigation aim to confirm that the updated formulation continues to deliver the same level of DH efficacy, with these additional enhancements. Therefore, to provide a definitive assessment of the efficacy of this new experimental formulation, and by extension, update the clinical efficacy package for the anhydrous, glycerin-based SnF2 toothpaste formulation family, two 56-day DH studies were undertaken. To accurately reflect expected usage according to the pack label instructions, the investigation compared two ‘regimens’: a regimen in which the new anhydrous stannous fluoride formulation was applied via brushing directly onto sensitive areas as part of the brushing routine, compared to a treatment regimen in which a standard fluoride toothpaste was brushed without specific instruction on technique. Efficacy of the Test treatment regimen would be confirmed if statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in DH were observed in both studies compared to the Reference treatment regimen.

These two studies had identical design, conducted in two separate sites with different group of populations, and involved conventional clinician-assessed DH after evaporative [16] and after tactile [17] stimulation [18]. A key advance of the present methodology was to further understand the perception and impact of DH relief in study participants. This was undertaken via use of a validated Labeled Magnitude Scale (LMS) [19–22], together with use of a validated DH Experience Questionnaire (DHEQ-48) to determine the impact of any changes in their DH experience on their oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) [23], . Taken together with the clinical efficacy evidence, recording these participant-assessed measures aimed to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the therapeutic benefits of this novel anhydrous formulation.

Materials and methods

Two single-center, 56-day, randomized, controlled clinical studies were conducted to evaluate interventions in healthy adults with DH. Both studies employed a parallel design with two treatment arms, examiner blinding, and overwrapping of all products with fully opaque adhesive labels to conceal their identity from participants and study staff. Study 1 was conducted at Salus Research in Fort Wayne, Indiana, United States of America (USA), and Study 2 was conducted at All Sum Research Center Ltd in Melbourne, Florida, USA. Both the studies received prior approval from independent research ethics committees (Veritas IRB Inc., via two separate submissions) and were registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Study 1: NCT06359028; Study ID: 300103 and Study 2: NCT06354270; Study ID: 300104).

Participants

Healthy adults aged 18 to 65 years were enrolled in the studies, with potential participants recruited from the sites’ internal database of local individuals who had previously expressed interest in, or participated in, clinical research studies. Key eligibility criteria required participants to have at least 20 natural teeth and a self-reported history of DH lasting from 6 months to 10 years. Additionally, participants were required to have at least two accessible, non-adjacent sensitive teeth, (incisors, canines, or premolars), as indicated by a qualifying tactile stimulus threshold of ≤ 20 g and a Schiff sensitivity score ≥ 2 at both screening and baseline assessments. These needed to be situated in different quadrants, and demonstrate erosion, abrasion, and/or facial/cervical gingival recession (EAR), a Modified Gingival Index (MGI) score of 0 [24] and no clinical mobility [25].

Participants were excluded if they were pregnant or breastfeeding, allergic to any test ingredients, had used anti-sensitivity products within eight weeks, or failed LMS training (participants who were unable to respond appropriately to LMSs after the training section based on investigator’s evaluation). Further exclusions were use of pain-altering medications, antibiotics within the previous two weeks, dental prophylaxis within four weeks, bleaching within eight weeks, scaling within three months, gross periodontal disease, or orthodontic appliances. Dentition-specific exclusions for test teeth included current or recent caries or treatment for decay within 12 months, used as denture abutments, had crowns, veneers, orthodontic bands, cracked enamel, or sensitivity not due to EAR and unlikely to respond to over-the-counter toothpaste, as assessed by the investigator.

Procedures

The studies adhered to the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines [26]. At screening visit (Visit 1), following written informed consent, participants’ medical history, demographic data, and current or prior medication were recorded. Oral soft tissue (OST) and oral hard tissue (OHT) examinations were performed, and dentition was assessed for eligibility as described above. Participants needed to have at least two teeth meeting the inclusion/exclusion criteria and confirmed sensitivity (a tactile threshold of ≤ 20 g and a Schiff sensitivity score > 2). Participants also completed LMS training for accuracy and consistency of sensitivity reporting.

Eligible participants were provided with a regular fluoride toothpaste (Colgate Cavity Protection, 1000 ppm fluoride; Colgate-Palmolive, New York, NY, US; USA marketed product) and a soft-bristled toothbrush (Oral-B Sensi-Soft, Procter & Gamble, Cincinnati, OH, US, USA marketed product). Following first use under supervision at the study site, participants used this toothpaste twice daily during a 14 to 28-day acclimatization phase between the screening and baseline visits.

At the baseline visit (Day 0), the participant’s continued eligibility for the study was verified. During this visit, participants completed the DHEQ-48 questionnaire (Supplementary Information) to evaluate their OHrQoL. After the OST examination, eligible teeth identified during the initial screening were reassessed using both a tactile stimulus (Yeaple probe) and an evaporative (air) stimulus (Schiff sensitivity score). LMS was conducted immediately after Schiff scoring. From the teeth with confirmed sensitivity at both screening and baseline visit, the examiner selected two non-adjacent hypersensitive teeth, which were designated as the test teeth to be assessed for the duration of the study.

Qualified participants were stratified by maximum baseline Schiff score of the two test teeth and randomized to receive either the Test treatment or the Reference treatment, along with a new toothbrush. Following a supervised brushing session, participants used the assigned product twice daily for 56 days. Visits 3 and 4 (after 28 and 56 days) included compliance checks, DHEQ-48, OST, DH assessments, and LMS questionnaires; Visit 3 had supervised brushing and Visit 4 added a full OHT examination.

Before study visits 2–4, participants avoided oral hygiene for 8 h, eating/drinking for 2 h, and excessive alcohol for 24 h. Only small sips of room temperature water were allowed up to 1 h before visits for medication or thirst. During the study, gum and dental products other than those provided, including sensitive teeth treatments, were prohibited. Dental floss could be used for impacted food, and non-emergency dental treatment was postponed until after the study.

Intervention

The toothpaste for the Test treatment regimen contained 0.454% w/w SnF₂, (1100 ppm fluoride ion). The toothpaste for the Reference treatment regimen was a standard fluoride formulation in the US marketplace (Crest Cavity Protection, Procter & Gamble, Cincinnati, OH, USA; 1100 ppm fluoride as sodium fluoride). All participants were instructed to brush for at least one minute twice daily, covering the full brush head with toothpaste; those using the Test treatment were also instructed to focus on the sensitive areas of the test teeth first. To ensure participants remained unaware of which toothpaste they received, each toothpaste tube was wrapped in white vinyl to conceal its identity and hide any branding on the Reference toothpaste.

Assessments

Both clinician-assessed and patient-reported outcomes were incorporated into the study to evaluate the efficacy of the SnF2 toothpaste in reducing DH and improving OHrQoL.

For the clinician assessment, two methodologies were utilized with distinct stimuli. Firstly, tactile sensitivity was evaluated by applying a constant-pressure probe to the tooth surface. The force started at 10 g and increased in 10 g increments. If a participant reported discomfort or pain in response (a “yes” response indicating the stimulus caused pain or discomfort) to the same force on two consecutive occasions [17], that force was recorded as their tactile threshold. A higher tactile threshold therefore reflects lower tooth sensitivity. During the screening and baseline visits, the maximum force applied was 20 g; at Visits 3 and 4, the upper force limit was 80 g. Secondly, after a minimum five-minute recovery period, evaporative sensitivity was measured using a one-second air blast from a dental syringe positioned 1 cm from the exposed dentin. The participant’s response was rated using the Schiff sensitivity scale (ranging from 0 = no response to 3 = severe pain with a request to stop [16]). All clinical examinations were conducted by qualified, experienced, and trained examiners.

Two patient-reported outcome measures were used: the LMS and the DHEQ-48. For the LMS, participants evaluated their response to the air stimulus at baseline (Visit 2), Day 28 (Visit 3), and Day 56 (Visit 4) using four 100-mm LMS scales (where 0 = No pain and 100 = Worst imaginable), capturing intensity, duration, tolerability, and qualitative description immediately after Schiff scoring. Importantly, participants were not made aware of the Schiff sensitivity scores given by the examiners, and the examiners were blinded to the participants’ LMS responses. Participants also completed the DHEQ-48 questionnaire at baseline (Visit 2), Day 28 (Visit 3), and Day 56 (Visit 4) prior to clinical assessments. The DHEQ-48 is a condition-specific tool designed to assess OHrQoL in individuals with DH [23, 27]. The DHEQ is divided into two main sections: Sect. 1 evaluates the impact of DH on everyday life; Sect. 2 assesses five distinct domains: restrictions, adaptation, social impact, emotional impact, and identity. Scoring for each domain provides detailed insight into which aspects of OHrQoL are affected and how participants experience improvement. Additionally, Sect. 2 includes specific questions about how tooth sensations influence the participant’s overall oral health and the effect on their life as a whole.

Statistical analysis

Efficacy analyses were performed on the Modified Intent-to-Treat (mITT) population [28], defined as randomized participants who completed at least one dose of the study product and completed at least one post-baseline DH efficacy assessment. The primary and main secondary endpoints for both studies included the change from Baseline in Schiff sensitivity score and change from baseline in tactile threshold, at Days 28 and 56, comparing the Test treatment with the Reference treatment. Other endpoints included changes in LMS parameters and changes in DHEQ-48 scores across five domains at Days 28 and 56.

Descriptive statistics were reported for continuous variables and categorical variables were summarized by the number and percentage of participants. Mixed model with repeated measures (MMRM) analyses were performed for both within and between group comparisons and were presented as least square mean (LSM) changes from Baseline for each interventional product at Day 28 and Day 56 with p-values presented at the 5% significance level. Participants were included in the model as a repeated measure with an unstructured covariance matrix, and Kenward-Roger’s approach [29] to degrees of freedom was applied. Adjusted mean changes from baseline, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and two-sided p-values were reported for each treatment group and for between-group differences. Model assumptions, such as normality and homogeneity, were assessed, and non-parametric analyses were provided to support MMRM results. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 or higher.

Safety analysis

Safety and oral tolerability of the study products were assessed for all randomized participants who received study toothpaste via recording treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) from the initial use of the acclimatization toothpaste until 5 days after the last use of study toothpaste. Monitoring included review of TEAEs, findings from OST and OHT examinations, and participant self-reports.

Sample size determination

Both studies screened enough participants to randomize approximately 110 participants, to ensure approximately 100 completed the trial, approximately 50/treatment arm, allowing for approximately a 10% dropout.

The studies were sufficiently powered to demonstrate statistical superiority of the Test treatment regimen compared to the Reference treatment regimen for the change from Baseline in the Schiff sensitivity score and tactile threshold, after 56 days of treatment [12, 30, 31]. A sample size of 50 evaluable participants per treatment group provided at least 90% power to detect a mean difference of 0.4 units (SD = 0.6) in change from Baseline in Schiff sensitivity score after 56 days of treatment, using a 2-tailed 2-sample t-test with a 5% significance level. The sample size calculation was performed using PASS software version 23.0.1.

The treatment difference, dropout rate, and SD estimates are based on the results of DH efficacy studies of similar design and duration evaluating 0.454% SnF2 toothpastes [12, 29–31].

Impact on oral health-related quality-of-life (DHEQ-48)

Study 1 showed a statistically significant reduction from baseline within the Test treatment group for the total score (all domains in Sect. 2) and in the restrictions, adaptation, emotional impact domains, and effect on life overall score at both Day 28 and Day 56 as well as in social impact, identity, and global oral health score at Day 56 (Table 6).

Study 2 demonstrated a statistically significant reduction from baseline within the Test treatment group for the total score and for the restrictions, adaptation, social impact, and emotional impact domains at Day 56 only as well as for effect on life overall score at both Day 28 and at Day 56. There were no statistically significant reductions observed for any Sect. 2 domain at Day 28, as well as no significant reductions for identity and global oral health score both at Day 28 and Day 56 (Table 7).

Between-treatment comparisons for DHEQ scores in Study 1 showed statistically significant improvements in the Test treatment group versus Reference at Day 56 across Sect. 1 (impact on everyday Life), DHEQ total score and all the individual domains (restrictions, adaptation, social impact, emotional impact, identity) as well as effect on life overall, and global oral health (Table 6). No significant differences were observed at Day 28. In study 2, no statistically significant differences were found between the Test and Reference treatments for any of the sections at Day 28 or Day 56 (Table 7).

Safety

Overall, both products were well tolerated, with no serious adverse events reported in either study. In Study 1, a total of 9 treatment-related TEAEs were reported: 8 in the Test treatment group (4 participants) and 1 in the Reference treatment group (1 subject). (Supplementary Information). One participant in the Test treatment group with three severe treatment-related TEAEs led to the participant’s withdrawal. The remaining events were mild. All adverse events were resolved by the end of the study.

There were no treatment-related TEAEs reported in study 2.

Discussion

The present studies provide robust, consistent evidence that this anhydrous 0.454% w/w SnF₂ toothpaste delivers both better clinician‑measured and patient‑perceived relief from DH amongst sufferers over 56 days of use, than a standard fluoride toothpaste (the Reference). Both examiner assessments and patient-reported outcomes were included in the studies to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the Test treatment’s therapeutic benefit – including quality-of-life benefits - relative to the Reference toothpaste. By integrating both external assessor and participant measures and examining a range of outcomes, the studies align with current standards in clinical dental research.

In both studies, by clinician assessment, participants who used the SnF₂ toothpaste experienced statistically significant relief from DH compared to the Reference. These benefits were observed at both Day 28 and Day 56. The Reference toothpaste, which did not contain any anti-DH ingredients, performed as expected, with only minor changes that could be attributed to placebo and/or Hawthorne effects [32]. Clinically meaningful differences between the SnF₂ and the Reference treatments, as defined by the American Dental Association’s criterion of a minimum 20% difference between treatments [33], were already apparent by Day 28, and increased through Day 56. The clinician-measured objective results confirm previous research demonstrating the DH relief benefits of this anhydrous SnF₂ toothpaste formulation family [10, 11].

A key strength of these studies is the use of patient-reported outcomes, the LMS and DHEQ. In both studies, the Test treatment demonstrated a statistically greater reduction from Baseline in all LMS endpoints compared to the Reference treatment at both Day 28 and Day 56. The LMS was developed specifically to discriminate between different levels of DH pain, and have been piloted, validated, and utilized in observational and interventional clinical DH studies [19–22]. It provides detailed insights into pain response to an evaporative stimulus across four endpoints: intensity, duration, tolerability, and description. These LMS results align with findings from a previous monadic study of anhydrous 0.454% SnF2 toothpaste for LMS at Day 28 and Day 56 [22], supporting a meaningful decrease in perceived symptoms of DH following continued use of the Test treatment: the present study shows for the first time a superior reduction versus a Reference treatment. As in these studies, Schiff sensitivity score and LMS are measured following the same single stimulus, the alignment between these two endpoints over time shows that the clinical indicator of DH (Schiff sensitivity score) is linked with perceived pain or discomfort (LMS).

In addition to LMS, the two studies reported here also used robust measures of OHRQoL. DH is known to negatively affect daily activities, causing discomfort during routine tasks like eating, drinking, and brushing. Severe symptoms may lead individuals to change behaviours and lifestyles [34]. The DHEQ-48 is widely recognized for assessing OHRQoL in people with tooth sensitivity, and has shown reliability and validity in both general and research settings [27, 35–38]. In both present studies, there was a statistically significant overall improvement in OHrQoL in the Test treatment after 28 and 56 days. In both studies, responses to DHEQ Sect. 1 questions, which assess the physical impact of DH, showed notable improvements: participants reported sensations were less intense, less bothersome, and more tolerable over time. Similarly, both studies observed positive trends in “The Effect on Life Overall” and “The Global Oral Health score.” For DHEQ Sect. 2, which examines the broader impact of DH on daily life, significant improvements were seen in domains such as Restrictions, Adaptation, Social Impact, Emotional Impact, and Overall Effect on Life, after 56 days use of the SnF2 toothpaste. Both studies’ results were consistent with previous studies [22, 36], confirming overall improvement in OHrQoL with the use of the SnF2 toothpaste. The patient-assessed (subjective) improvements support the clinically measured endpoints.

However, while Study 1 demonstrated statistically significant differences in DHEQ-48 between the Test and Reference treatments—indicating the Test treatment’s superior effectiveness on improving the OHRQoL—Study 2 found no significant difference between groups (the Reference also showed improvement). Given the strong evidence of efficacy from the clinically measured endpoints and LMS, as well as Study 1 DHEQ results, this suggests a placebo effect in Study 2 [23]. As participants are involved in a study with multiple formal clinic visits, their subjective evaluations of the product’s DH benefits may be biased—they might expect positive results, which could enhance their psychological sense of well-being and contribute to the measured effect [1, 7, 32, 36]. DHEQ is reliable and responsive; however, between-treatment discrimination may be limited in short trials or when placebo effects are prominent. Further, the DHEQ response differences between the two studies may be due to variations in baseline characteristics or study population lifestyles. To address these limitations and enhance generalizability in future research, increased or stratified sampling can be considered.

In terms of potential limitations of these studies, one notable factor is the difference in brushing instructions between the Test and Reference treatments. Participants in the Test treatment group were directed to carefully brush the sensitive areas of their two designated ‘Test teeth,’ in addition to brushing their entire dentition, while participants in the Reference treatment group were only instructed to brush their whole mouth. This distinction was made to be consistent with the label instructions. In the U.S. anti-sensitivity toothpastes advise brushing directly onto sensitive areas — this is the ‘focused brushing’ technique [39]- which was done with the Test product only. In contrast, standard fluoride anti-cavity toothpastes (such as the one used as the reference in the present investigation) - do not provide this recommendation. Adhering to labelled directions, thereby closely simulating typical consumer practice, we believe would help ensure that the clinical study outcomes accurately reflect the real-world performance of the products when used as intended by consumers. This approach would also enhance comparability of these two studies to previous DH studies of similar formulations [39–43].

Taken together, the studies confirm that long-term use of the experimental SnF2 toothpaste progressively alleviates physical symptoms associated with DH. Further, use of the product positively influences quality of life.

Conclusion

In summary, these two new clinical studies show that using the experimental anhydrous toothpaste with 0.454% stannous fluoride twice daily leads to greater relief from DH than a standard fluoride toothpaste measured by Schiff sensitivity score and tactile threshold after 28 days of use (and further improvement at day 56). These examiner-assessed benefits were also reported by the participants themselves, in terms of consistently improved LMS scores. This alignment highlights that the demonstrable clinical efficacy of the anhydrous SnF₂ toothpaste in managing DH will give meaningful benefits to users, supporting its role as a first-line option in routine dental care for individuals with the condition.
