Planning and evaluating an integrated clinical exercise oncology service: an exploratory mixed-methods study
Authors: Ciaran M Fairman, Shana E Harrington, Alec R Schumpp, Alex M Brooks, Morgan J Jones, Julian Kim, Mary A Kennedy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis project aimed to design and evaluate the potential to integrate an exercise oncology service into clinical care in a local healthcare system. The goal was to inform the design of an implementation strategy to promote its sustainable use in standard care.MethodsThis two-phase, exploratory study used a mixed-methods approach. First, qualitative measures were used to understand the context for exercise integration into oncology care by clinicians and administrators in the healthcare system. Next, the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework guided a comprehensive evaluation of the exercise service. Reach and Adoption were measured via number of patients enrolled in the program relative to those treated. Adoption was measured via referrals from stakeholders. Implementation was described using the program structure and delivery. Maintenance was described using hospital funding decisions. Effectiveness was assessed using the Bellarmine Norton Assessment Tool (BNAT) and Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy – Fatigue Scale.ResultsPhase 1 context analysis suggested critical elements to guide program delivery, including limiting participants to post-primary treatment (surgery, chemotherapy and radiation) and streamlining referral process. The Phase 2 evaluation demonstrated suboptimal program reach (2%); significant program effectiveness (improved physical function (BNAT; p = 0.05 ) and decreased fatigue (p < 0.05)); receptiveness to program adoption (75%); and strong potential for program maintenance.ConclusionsTraditional approaches to exercise oncology research trials are not easily integrated into healthcare systems. Designing an exercise program for meaningful integration and sustainment requires understanding the context where the program will be delivered, followed by intentional and continuous engagement with key stakeholders to ensure the program continues to meet the needs of the system. Initial exploration of the settings is critical to inform a comprehensive implementation strategy.Trial registrationclinicaltrials.gov: NCT06039488. Background

A mounting body of evidence demonstrating the benefits of exercise on a variety of cancer health-related outcomes (fatigue, quality of life, and physical function) has resulted in governing bodies across the world developing evidence-based exercise guidelines for individuals with cancer [1–3]. As a result, there have been numerous calls to integrate exercise as a part of standard care for individuals with cancer, particularly as a result of evidence demonstrating the association with exercise and reduced recurrence/mortality [1, 4–7]. Despite this, there remains a paucity of effectively implemented evidence-based exercise oncology programs in clinical and community settings. This is supported by the recognition that efficacy studies alone are insufficient to improve public health, and strategies that address barriers and facilitators of implementation in real-world settings are critical to closing the translation gap by moving research into practice [4, 8–12].

The study of dissemination and implementation of exercise oncology interventions/programs is a critical component for increasing our understanding on how to increase the access to care for people with cancer, and ultimately increase the number of individuals engaging in regular exercise [4, 6–8]. Implementation of interventions in real-world settings requires careful consideration of the intervention components (who delivers the intervention, how they are trained, how the intervention is offered in terms of location, days and times, etc.), the setting in which it will be employed (culture, resources, infrastructure, etc.), along with contextual factors (funding, readiness, attitudes, etc.) influencing the long-term viability for integration and sustainability in practice [4, 8, 11–15].

The use of implementation science strategies in exercise oncology can complement the research supporting the efficacy of exercise for various cancer treatment-related impairments and accelerate the translation of exercise oncology evidence into practice [8, 11]. However, while this is a burgeoning area of research, few studies to date have specifically reported on the implementation of exercise oncology interventions in real-world settings [8]. Additionally, to ensure equitable access, it is important to utilize implementation strategies across a variety of settings in cancer care (i.e. academic medical centers, urban vs. rural hospital settings) to understand how to develop and deliver exercise oncology initiatives safely to all individuals with cancer [10, 16]. The same effort needs to be made to inform development of implementation strategies as is used to inform effectiveness studies. As such, preliminary work to explore the context for implementation is an important first step to inform the development of a sustainable implementation strategy [17].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to design and evaluate the integration of an exercise oncology service into clinical care for people receiving oncology care in a community-based private hospital setting in the United States to understand the potential for implementation into standard practice. In the short term, this work will lead to the development of an implementation strategy. In the long term, the work will help to inform future efforts to embed exercise into routine oncology care.

Methods

This mixed methods study was conducted in collaboration with two local oncology institutions, Prisma Health – Midlands (PHM) and South Carolina Oncology Associates (SCOA) that comprise the majority of cancer care for adults in the Midlands region of South Carolina. Exercise participants signed a written consent prior to study activities. The study was comprised of two phases, (1) context analysis and (2) evaluation. The first provided insight into the design of the program with an aim to create a sustainable partnership. The second evaluated key points of the program’s implementation and effectiveness. The study authors come from both exercise science and oncology backgrounds. Most (CMF, SEH, ARS, AMB, MJJ, MAK) have extensive training in exercise science or physical therapy and have a range of experience prescribing and delivering exercise to people living with and beyond cancer. One author (JK) is a medical oncologist working within the study’s healthcare system. Additionally, one author (MAK) is trained as an implementation scientist. All study activities were reviewed and approved by Prisma Health Midlands Institutional Review Board (Pro00109975).

Setting

The Midlands of South Carolina represents a unique model of cancer care to understand factors surrounding the implementation of exercise oncology interventions in community-based, private hospital settings. The majority of cancer care in the Midlands region is split between a large hospital system, PHM, (providing diagnostics and surgery) and a private cancer center, SCOA, (providing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy, immunotherapy, and allied health services). Though there was interest for an exercise service at PHM, there was no infrastructure to run clinical trials for exercise oncology when this study began. However, the exercise program utilized the physical therapy clinic associated with PHM. This is a fully equipped physical therapy space with the necessary equipment (free weights, resistance bands, benches, treadmills, cycle ergometers etc.) to deliver the intervention.

Funding

The work was funded through a collaborative seed grant between PHM and the University of South Carolina. This funding was primarily used for salary support for the PI (Fairman), along with additional study supplies (flyers, t-shirts for participants, etc.)

Stakeholder advisory group

A stakeholder advisory group (SAG) was established to develop and guide the project. The SAG included four members: exercise oncology researchers from University of South Carolina (n = 2; authors CF, SH), a rehabilitation clinician and the operations manager of the physical therapy department at the partner oncology organization. SAG members were chosen to provide a mix of people knowledgeable about exercise oncology programming and those familiar with the needs of the local oncology organizations. The SAG met monthly throughout the development phase of the program and at the conclusion of each intervention wave during the implementation phase, a total of seven times during the study (Fig. 1). Detailed meeting minutes were recorded by study author CF.

Exercise intervention description

The exercise program was ~ 60 min, delivered 2 days per week for 8 weeks. The intervention was delivered by graduate and undergraduate exercise science students, under the supervision of lead investigator CF, an exercise physiologist who has extensive experience in the design and delivery of exercise programs for individuals with cancer. Students were required to complete a combination of theory-based learning and practical workshops for coaching individuals with cancer prior to delivering the program. A sample of the exercise programming and progression can be found in Supplementary file 1. Exercises were individually tailored and modified according to each participant’s physical conditioning, mobility, and cancer-related characteristics (i.e. presences of metastatses) or impairments (fatigue, dyspnea etc.) Outside of supervised exercise sessions, participants were encouraged to be as active as possible. Three 8-week cycles of the exercise program were delivered over the 10-month study period and the program was adapted after each wave as described below (Fig. 1).

Phase 1: context assessment

Participants

The SAG identified key people within the PHM and SCOA to provide perspective on the organizational needs and logistical realities of creating an exercise service for people with cancer. Participants (n = 5) included potential referrers and administration staff with decision making abilities.

Data collection and analysis

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with organizational decision makers. Interview questions were designed using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to elicit the perspectives on the essential contextual factors that may influence implementation outcomes for the service [18, 19]. Importantly, this study used constructs and domains from the original CFIR, prior to its update [20]. The interview guide was developed specifically for this study and can be found in Supplementary File 2. Interviews were designed to be completed within ~ 30 min in recognition of the known time constraints of clinicians and hospital administrators. Questions were chosen to focus on factors that could be influenced within the healthcare system in future implementation efforts and included nine constructs across three domains of the CFIR framework [12]. Participants received an email invitation to participate, with a one-page description of the study and background about the study team. Author CF conducted the interviews via telephone, which lasted ~ 20–35 min and were digitally recorded (Zoom, San Jose, CA) and transcribed (Descript, San Francisco, CA). The interviewer (CF) was aware of the need to listen without reacting or interjecting with any personal views given his extensive experience delivering programs for people with cancer. Each interview was transcribed verbatim. An inductive qualitative descriptive analysis was used to ensure the results are easily understood by practitioners and useful in healthcare practice [21]. Analysis focused on identifying common concerns for integrating exercise into clinical oncology care. Open-coded themes were developed using a stepped process where key phrases that related to the research question were extracted and coded, grouped into sub-themes and subsequent major theme categories. These theme categories were then mapped to according to the constructs and domains of the CFIR framework that were explored in the interviews. Together the themes tell the story of the factors relevant to development of an exercise service within the organization. Data analysis was led by study author MAK. 40% of the data were independently coded and categorized by a second author (CF) to demonstrate trustworthiness of the process [22]. MAK is an experienced implementation science researcher without direct contact with the study site or participants. CF was specifically chosen as a second reviewer given his position within the SAG and his direct contact with all participants, which ensured interpretations of the data were an accurate reflection of the setting. Results are reported descriptively, in-line with each domain of the CFIR framework.

Phase 2: evaluation

Participants

People who had completed active treatment (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy) for any cancer type at either PHM or SCOA, were ≥ 18 years of age, and received a diagnosis of stage I-IV cancer were eligible to be referred to the exercise program. Individuals receiving hormone therapy were eligible to participate. Importantly, individuals who were receiving active treatment and/or were stage IV were initially excluded as a result of data collected during initial interviews with stakeholders (see “Table 2. Design Quality”). However, following the success of the first 8-week program, and feedback to the oncology community, a collective decision was made by the SAG to expand the eligibility to include individuals receiving active treatment, and those with a stage IV diagnosis. People not willing to complete exercise sessions at the on-site exercise clinic; with neuromuscular, cardiovascular, or psychological conditions that precluded safe exercise; or unable to read or understand English were not referred.

Data collection and analysis

The RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance) framework was chosen to guide the evaluation because it offers a practical framework that is well understood by clinicians and members of an organizational team and can serve as a template for future implementation efforts [23]. Outcomes align with RE-AIM constructs as described in Table 1. Importantly, as this was an exploratory study, we did not define what we would consider as successful outcomes per the RE-AIM framework a priori. Rather, the goal was to complete a preliminary exploration (i.e., context analysis) of the healthcare system and its willingness / ability to adopt exercise into the organizational culture.

Exercise program records

All exercise participants completed health information questionaires for demographic, medical and cancer related history. Additionally, exercise assessments, validated for people with cancer [24, 25], were selected with consideration given to space limitations in the facility, minimizing participant burden, and the need to fit within a fully operational physical therapy clinic. Exercise assessments were completed within one week of program initiation and completion, respectively.

SAG meeting minutes

The SAG met seven times during the study. Meeting minutes were recorded by study author CF and formed a data set.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe demographic and medical characteristics of participants. Mean and standard deviations are reported for all exploratory outcomes at baseline and 8-week follow up for all participants. Pre-post changes were evaluated using paired t-tests and Cohens d effect sizes were calculated for each outcome. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5) and large (d = 0.8) [26]. Data analyses were conducted using RStudio on a per protocol basis. Qualitative data from interviews and the SAG meeting minutes were analyzed using a descriptive approach designed for practitioners and policy makers whereby standard language is used to describe facts without the need for abstract theorizing [25]. A deductive approach to analysis was used, focused on identifying common issues related to the relevant RE-AIM construct. Descriptive summaries were created through interpretation of interview transcripts, and SAG meeting minutes. Data analysis was led by MAK with review and input from the other authors. Results are reported descriptively, in-line with each construct of the RE-AIM framework.

Results

Phase 1: context assessment

Five participants from the partner oncology institutions were interviewed between May and August 2021. One representative from each of the following disciplines participated: social work, physician assistant, oncologist, administrator, data manager. Culture

Participants described the organization as having a strong commitment to improving the cancer care experience and a willingness to introduce new programs that support this goal.

Several points of referral were suggested (e.g., chemotherapy education nurses prior to treatment, offboarding process with social workers, consults with oncologists); however, all participants expressed the need for a streamlined process that clearly defined eligibility criteria and program details.

Cost

While participants were excited a research grant would cover costs of the exercise program for patients, they questioned the program’s potential as a long-term solution for integration without a clear funding stream.

Outer setting

Patient needs and resources

Concerns were raised about patients’ ability to access the program due to transportation challenges.

Information gathered through the interviews informed the program’s referral practices and participant eligibility. Specifically, four referral sources were identified: chemotherapy education nurses, nurse practitioners and/or medical oncologists, social workers, and physical therapists. The physical therapists specialized in lymphedema and offered services that were considered a routine part of cancer care and had access to patients through tumor board meetings. Each referral group received program flyers to post in their offices and provided contact details for the study staff to share with patients. Participant eligibility was initially limited to people who had completed primary treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation) to account for concerns expressed by the clinicians in the interviews.

Phase 2: evaluation

Reach

During the 9-month recruitment period, 1,275 people received a diagnosis at PHM. Of the 30 participants who were enrolled and completed baseline assessments, 23 completed the exercise program. This represents an overall reach of 2% and a retention rate of 70%. Reasons for withdrawal included personal reasons (3), travel time (1), and unrelated medical concerns (1). One participant withdrew due to muscle soreness following their first exercise session.

Key demographic and medical characteristics of the program participants are outlined in Table 3. The age of participants ranged from 38 to 76 years old. The majority (96%) of exercise participants were female and had a diagnosis of breast cancer (88%). 32% (n = 8) of participants were Black and 8% (n = 2) were Asian.

Adoption

Referral information revealed three of the four stakeholder groups initially identified adopted the program (i.e. the program received referrals from three of the four groups) (75%), with physical therapists as the most common pathway to the program, representing 60% (n = 18) of all referrals. Additional pathways included oncologists (20%; n = 6), flyers in their oncologist’s office (10%; n = 3), and “other” (10%; n = 3). Notably, the referral pathways became more diverse with each wave. Wave 1 participants (n = 9) were primarily referred by their physical therapist (n = 8; 89%), with only one participant reporting “other” (11%). Of the 11 participants enrolled and baselined during Wave 2, 73% (n = 8) were referred by their physical therapist, while the remaining 27% (n = 3) were referred directly by their oncologist. Ten participants were enrolled in Wave 3. 30% (n = 3) were referred by their oncologist, 30% (n = 3) were recruited by flyers in their doctor’s office, 20% (n = 2) were referred by their physical therapist and the remaining 20% (n = 2) reported “other”.

Implementation

Program funding and delivery: The project was solely funded from a small grant funded through a partnership with the hospital and University. Program decisions were made by the SAG to ensure the program offerings were feasible within the financial constraints of the grant. Specifically, the program was offered using the existing infrastructure of the organization’s physical therapy facilities at two time points in the day: 6.30am – 7.30am and 5.30pm – 6.30pm, Monday through Thursdays. These time slots were chosen to minimize the impact of the program on current clinical operations in the physical therapy clinic. The capacity of this space allowed for four participants per hour. As mentioned above, the principal investigator of the grant (CF), designed and supervised the program. The program was delivered by undergraduate and graduate research assistants supervised by one of the PIs (CF), and who went through training in the area of exercise oncology were responsible for day-to-day program delivery with patients. The funding supported the program to be offered in 3-waves of 8-weeks across 9-months.

Program structure: The program structure (8 weeks; 2 sessions/week) remained consistent across all three waves. However, the model of delivery was changed between waves 1 and 2 in response to participant feedback. The initial program model was delivered in a 1:1 format, with each participant working through their program individually with an exercise professional. Participants expressed a desire for more interaction with other participants. As a result, SAG meeting minutes note a decision to update the delivery model to a group format. Waves 2 and 3 employed a group exercise format, whereby participants were encouraged to exercise together, alternating exercises and stations. In waves 2 and 3, exercise prescription was still individualized and monitored by on-site exercise professionals.

Participant eligibility: Initial program eligibility required that participants had completed primary treatment for their cancer based on concerns raised by clinicians during the context assessment phase. SAG meeting minutes clarified that study staff were integrated into the breast tumor board meetings, which took place monthly during the intervention period. Updates about the exercise program were presented to medical oncologists and nurse practitioners at each meeting. Following the second meeting (November 2021), requests were made and subsequently actioned to extend program eligibility to people on primary treatment.

Maintenance

The grant funding support for the exercise program ended in June 2022, after completion of Wave 3. The program could not continue without additional funding. Meeting minutes from the SAG noted the results of a presentation by the study PI (CF) of the program’s preliminary findings to the breast tumor board on January 25th, 2022. The presentation resulted in a decision by the breast tumor board to fund a PhD student to ensure the program could continue. This funding is provided on an annual basis, pending progress reports to the tumor boards, and shifts in funding budget/priorities. Under the supervision of the study PI (CF), this funded PhD student would oversee a team of graduate and undergraduate students responsible for program delivery.

Discussion

In this study, we used a two-phased approach to design and offer an exercise program for people receiving cancer care in the Midlands region of South Carolina. Our aim was to explore the potential for providing exercise as standard care within the organization and be sustained beyond the research trial. In Phase 1, we conducted an assessment guided by the CFIR framework to understand the context for exercise integration. The results of the assessment were used to design an exercise service to align with the needs of our partner cancer care organizations so it could be introduced into the patient care pathway. In Phase 2, we conducted an evaluation of the effectiveness and implementation of the exercise program. The evaluation was guided by the RE-AIM framework, spanned 9 months, and included three rounds of an 8-week exercise program. The results of this exploratory study will inform the development of a comprehensive implementation plan to improve the adoption and utilization of the service. The combined focus on implementation and effectiveness from the start of the project emphasizes the opportunity researchers have to leverage funding to design sustainable solutions for integrating exercise into standard oncology cancer care.

Traditional approaches to clinical research trials are not sufficient to make exercise standard of care, in part because clinical trials have a limited capacity to serve people in the target population [27, 28]. Over nine months our 3-wave trial reached just 2% of the target population. This result is similar to efforts in Australia by Dennett et al. [29] and Newton et al. [30] where their exercise trials, similarly co-located within cancer treatment centers, achieved a reach of 10% and 12% respectively. A key reason for this low penetration is that trials are designed based on the needs of researchers, not clinicians or organisations. Traditional clinical research trial design requires strict eligibility criteria to ensure internally valid outcomes. The requirement to target only a select group of people automatically limits the population that can access an exercise service. Moreover, research trials are designed based on grant contracts. Grant limitations often do not allow researchers the funding or time necessary to perform the foundational steps of understanding the needs and capacity of an organization to adopt new programs or practices. As a result, research trials often result in one-off projects and the project groundwork laid is left unused.

Integration of a new program or practice into organizational systems requires deliberate planning [31]. As this trial was the first introduction of exercise research to the Midlands cancer care organizations, several barriers needed to be addressed before patient recruitment could begin. The SAG [32] provided the research team ongoing access to organizational experts to help navigate the system, including identification of key stakeholders to include. The planned context analysis phase of the project, guided by the CFIR framework [18], provided a comprehensive description of the most critical barriers to adopting exercise into the system. Recognizing the barriers at the outset al.lowed for the program design to address those challenges. For example, clinician interviews revealed hesitancy to refer specific groups of people (e.g., on active treatment, with co-morbidities) to exercise, which informed the initial study approach to focus on “acceptable” populations. Knowledge of this hesitancy prompted the research team to prioritize opportunities to overcome the noted trust gap between physicians and exercise professionals [33], such as incorporating the research team into tumor board meetings to provide regular updates about the exercise program. These meetings cultivated collective learning and mutual trust between the researchers and clinicians [34], resulting in diverse referrals to the program and set the research team up to secure organizational support to continue the program beyond the initial grant. Cultivating relationships between researchers and organizations offers the potential for long-term collaboration; however, it requires on-going efforts that can be difficult for researchers to prioritize as they are not typically a central focus of once-off projects/trials.

Incorporating implementation questions into traditional effectiveness trials [35] in exercise oncology provides an opportunity for researchers to think beyond the current trial and understand the potential for integration from the outset of a project. The effectiveness outcomes of this trial add to the evidence-base describing the positive impact of exercise for people living with and beyond cancer [1].However, the addition of implementation-focused questions allowed the research team to co-design an exercise service with potential long-term sustainability. This hybrid approach is emerging within the field of exercise oncology. The first example was provided by Beidas et al. in 2014 [36], though implementation research in the field has gained momentum [10, 14] as calls to provide exercise as a routine part of cancer care have strengthened [6, 7, 37]. Incorporating implementation approaches in exercise oncology research can help researchers leverage short-term funding for longer-term, sustainable projects by providing an understanding of organizational needs [38]. This study is an exemplar. While integration was not achieved, the foundation was laid to develop future collaborations critical for implementation.

Not every exercise oncology research trial requires a dedicated implementation focus; however, all projects can benefit from understanding the context where they are intended to be delivered. A context assessment to understand the needs and readiness of a health care setting to integrate an evidence-based program is a valuable preliminary step to set a project up for potential implementation and dissemination and is critical to guide the targeted development of implementation plans. Researchers should include an implementation expert on the research team from the beginning of a project to achieve this goal [39].

A major strength of this study was its hybrid approach, which allowed for the integration of organizational needs into program planning. Additionally, the work was guided by the CFIR and RE-AIM frameworks and used a mixed-methods approach to provide context to the quantitative data. However, the results are limited to one healthcare setting. Important differences may be present in other settings (i.e. academic, community, rural etc.). Additionally, patients with lived experience were not included on the SAG or as participants in the context analysis phase, though their perspectives were included during the evaluation phase and incorporated into adaptations. In line with the above, a noteworthy limitation of this study is that we did not garner information on participant (i.e. individuals treated for cancer) perspectives on barriers and facilitators to the program. Though not the primary focus of this study, the inclusion of individuals with lived experience in SAGs could provide a more comprehensive understanding of what is needed to develop a sustainable exercise oncology program.

Conclusion

Traditional approaches to exercise oncology research trials are not sufficient to close the research to practice gap preventing exercise from moving into standard practice. Integrating into healthcare systems requires deliberate planning. Hybrid implementation-effectiveness research design approaches that include a thorough context analysis allow for targeted implementation planning. This comprehensive approach presents an opportunity to leverage research funding to design sustainable solutions for integrating exercise into cancer care.
