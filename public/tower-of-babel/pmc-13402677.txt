Sulfur-engineered rhenium single-atoms on borides for tunable syngas and lactic acid co-production
Authors: Weikang Ling, Qiong Liu, Wei Quan, Jimei Li, Yushun Han, Tsz Woon Benedict Lo, Jiliang Ma, Runcang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The simultaneous and selective production of syngas and value-added chemicals from biomass-derived feedstocks is fundamentally restricted by sluggish multi-electron-proton transfer and the lack of precisely defined active sites capable of stabilizing reactive intermediates. Here, we report a sulfur-mediated coordination reconstruction strategy that transforms metastable rhenium species on two-dimensional chromium boride into uniformly dispersed Re–S4 single atoms. The electronic environment of Re–S4 optimizes the d-band center, stabilizing the key intermediate glyceraldehyde for selective C-C bond cleavage. In this work, the synergistic combination of atomic-scale engineering and reactant kinetics modulation yields a total syngas rate of 34.08 mmol g−1 h−1 with a wide-ranging tunable H2/CO ratio (0.1 to 14.4), alongside a lactic acid yield of 90.8%. The system’s robustness is further validated via large-scale outdoor sunlight-tracking tests, demonstrating its potential as a scalable, sustainable biorefinery technology for the concurrent production of gas-phase fuels and liquid-phase platform chemicals.

Introduction

Syngas serves as a versatile and strategic feedstock for modern energy and chemical industries, underpinning the production of low-carbon fuels, commodity chemicals, and sustainable high-value intermediates1–3. Beyond its established role, precise regulation of the H2/CO ratio is critically important, as it governs the selectivity and efficiency of diverse downstream transformations, such as methanol synthesis and Fischer-Tropsch processes4,5. Traditional fossil-based routes are inherently constrained by thermodynamic limitations, making tunable gas compositions difficult to achieve. In contrast, biomass-derived syngas offers a renewable and carbon-neutral alternative. The varied chemical composition and flexible reaction pathways of biomass enable greater potential for tailoring the H2/CO ratio to meet specific industrial needs. However, conventional biomass gasification requires high thermal energy input and generates significant CO2 emissions, highlighting an urgent need for more energy-efficient and environmentally friendly strategies6.

Photocatalytic biomass reforming coupled with water splitting has recently emerged as a promising strategy. By linking the oxidative cleavage of biomass C–C bonds with the photoreduction of water under ambient conditions, this approach simultaneously produces syngas and value-added chemicals. This paradigm directly addresses the limitations of conventional processes, offering significant potential for carbon-neutral syngas production. However, widespread implementation is hindered by inherently slow charge-transfer dynamics and a lack of precisely engineered active sites, which together constrain photocatalytic efficiency and selectivity7.

To overcome these limitations, two-dimensional (2D) transition metal borides (MBenes) have emerged as a particularly promising platform due to their exceptional electrical conductivity and highly tunable surface chemistry8–10. Unlike the widely studied MXenes, MBenes exhibit structural stability and broader functional diversity. Typically synthesized via selective etching of Al layers from MAB-phase precursors11, allow precise control over surface terminal groups, which critically influence their electronic structure12. Building on this conventional approach, notable progress has recently been made in developing fluoride-free and environmentally friendly routes for MBenes synthesis13,14. Despite these advantages, challenges such as ultrafast exciton annihilation and a low density of structurally well-defined active sites continue to constrain their potential15. Integrating metal co-catalysts, such as rhenium (Re), is an effective strategy to enhance charge utilization and introduce additional active centers. Re is particularly attractive due to its flexible oxidation states and capacity to mediate multi-electron-proton transfers16. However, achieving high-density Re single-atom dispersion remains challenging; loading above ~5 wt% often leads to Ostwald ripening and aggregation into clusters17,18. This is further compounded by Re’s classification as a “soft acid” under Hard–Soft Acid–Base (HSAB) theory, which results in weak interactions with the “hard base” oxygen atoms typically found on support surfaces19,20. While MBenes offer abundant –O/–OH terminal groups, the steric and geometric constraints of O-coordination inherently limit the achievable density of stabilized Re SAs.

Herein, we report a sulfur-mediated coordination reconstruction strategy that transforms metastable Re species on 2D chromium boride (CrB) MBenes into uniformly dispersed Re–S4 single atoms. Leveraging the HSAB principle—where the “soft acid” Re prefers the “soft base” sulfur—we achieve a coordination reconstruction that fundamentally alters the interfacial charge dynamics. The Re–S4 configuration (Fig. 1a) provides an electron-rich environment that precisely regulates the adsorption energy of glyceraldehyde (GAD), a notorious bottleneck intermediate in carbohydrate oxidation. By stabilizing this key species, we bridge the gap between high-rate syngas production and selective liquid-phase chemical synthesis. We demonstrate that this sulfur-mediated tuning enables continuous modulation of the H2/CO ratio across a broad industrial range (0.1–14.4) within a single platform. Advanced microscopy and spectroscopy confirm the well-defined Re–S4 coordination, while ultrafast charge dynamics, in-situ characterization, and density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal accelerated interfacial charge transfer (Fig. 1b). This configuration dramatically enhances syngas production to 34.08 mmol g−1 h−1 and promotes lactic acid yields to 90.8%. Furthermore, we validate the system’s scalability and operational stability through outdoor sunlight-tracking experiments, providing a robust pathway for sustainable biorefinery technology (Fig. 1c).Fig. 1Schematic illustration and design concept of this study.a Structural advantage of Re–S coordination vs drawbacks of Re–O coordination. b Charge-transfer dynamics of photogenerated electrons. c Proposed catalytic reaction pathway and mechanism over the Re/S@CrB catalyst.

Results

Synthetic strategy and structural characterizations

The Re/S@CrB composite was synthesized via a three-step procedure (Fig. 2a), comprising selective etching of Al from Cr2AlB2 to generate CrB MBene, S incorporation through solid-state diffusion, and subsequent anchoring of Re SAs via incipient wetness impregnation. This approach avoids the harsh etching conditions of conventional methods and enables the construction of stable S-modified CrB MBene supports for Re SA dispersion (Supplementary Table 1). Additional synthetic details are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1–7 and Supplementary Table 2. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed bulk morphologies for both Cr2AlB2 and the floating layer (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 3), while the suspended CrB exhibited an accordion-like structure (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4), indicating successful delamination. All functionalized materials retained thin lamellar morphologies without structural degradation (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9), suggesting that S and Re incorporation preserves the fundamental CrB framework. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis revealed a 1.4-fold increase in specific surface area compared with pristine Cr2AlB2 (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 3), attributed to the expanded accessible surface provided by the accordion-like morphology. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) identified lattice fringes with spacings of 0.278 nm and 0.202 nm, corresponding to the (110) and (111) planes, respectively (Fig. 2e–g and Supplementary Fig. 11). Elemental mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of Cr, Al, B, O, S, Re, and Cl throughout the composite (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).Fig. 2Synthetic strategy and morphology characterization.a Schematic illustration of the fabrication strategy of Re/S@CrB; SEM images of b Cr2AlB2 and c CrB; d SEM image of Re/S@CrB and corresponding elements mapping (Cr, Al, B, O, S, Re, Cl); e–g HRTEM images of Re/S@CrB (insets in e correspond to the regions indicated by the rectangles in f, g); Atom-corrected HAADF-STEM of h Re/S@CrB (insets in h correspond to the regions indicated by the two rectangles) and i Re@CrB; DFT calculation of formation energy values for Re clusters on the surfaces of j sulfur (S@CrB) and k oxygen (CrB).

The Re species in Re/S@CrB are identified as bright spots in aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (AC HAADF-STEM) images (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 14), with an atomic radius of approximately 1.5 Å, consistent with the theoretical size of Re SAs21 (Supplementary Fig. 15). No Re-based NPs were detected, confirming monatomic dispersion of Re on the S@CrB support. In contrast, in the absence of S atoms, Re clusters/NPs with a larger size of 9.1 Å are clearly observed (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 16). Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) measurements indicated a Re loading of 5.98 wt% in Re/S@CrB, slightly higher than that in Re@CrB (5.77 wt%). Although the absolute difference appears modest, it is significant for maximizing Re SA dispersion in catalytic materials17,22. Our results suggest that S-terminated surfaces, with lower electronegativity, reduce the critical threshold for Re aggregation. To elucidate the role of S atoms, DFT calculations were performed to evaluate the formation energies of Re SAs and NPs on differently terminated surfaces (Fig. 2j, k, Supplementary Table 4, and Supplementary Data 1). The formation energy of Re NPs was lower on O-terminated surfaces (−2.83 eV) than on S-terminated surfaces (−2.22 eV), whereas Re SAs exhibited lower formation energy on S-terminated surfaces (S: −6.28 eV, O: −4.15 eV). This energy-driven selectivity highlights the critical role of S incorporation in modulating Re species configuration and promoting atomic dispersion. These findings suggest that S facilitates the formation of Re–S bonds, suppressing Re-Re bond formation and subsequent clustering, thereby favoring the stabilization of Re SAs. This conclusion is further corroborated by radial distribution function (RDF) analysis, which provides direct evidence for the formation of a highly ordered Re–S coordination environment (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The chemical compositions of the samples were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The chemical states of Cr2AlB2 before and after etching were first examined (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Table 5), followed by analysis of the S/Re co-modified systems (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Figs. 19–21). Compared with S@CrB and Re@CrB, Re/S@CrB exhibited positive binding energy shifts in the S 2p peaks and negative shifts in the Re 4f peaks (Fig. 2a, b), indicating electron transfer from S to Re and resulting in localized charge accumulation23. Notably, the Re 4f spectrum of Re@CrB was deconvoluted into four peaks corresponding to different Re species: metallic Re0 from Re-Re NPs and Reδ+ from Re–O coordinated Re SAs. This observation aligns with AC HAADF-STEM results, which confirmed the presence of metallic Re NPs in Re@CrB24. The chemical speciation and local coordination environment of Re were further investigated using X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS). Analysis of the Re L3-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 3c) showed that both Re@CrB and Re/S@CrB exhibited white-line intensities intermediate between those of Re foil and Re oxides, indicating a mixed-valence state of Re species (0 <δ+ <+4), consistent with XPS results25. Notably, the Re L3-edge white-line intensity in Re/S@CrB was higher than in Re@CrB. Given that Re@CrB contains a substantial fraction of zero-valent (Re0) metallic Re particles, the lower average Re oxidation state in Re@CrB relative to Re/S@CrB aligns with these observations. To accurately determine the valence states, derivative analysis was performed. The valence states of Re in Re@CrB and Re/S@CrB are +0.450 and +2.568, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 22). In the k3-weighted Re L3-edge Fourier-transformed extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra (Fig. 3d), Re foil and Re oxides displayed characteristic Re-Re and Re–O scattering peaks at approximately 2.6 Å and 1.5 Å, respectively26. For Re@CrB, the EXAFS spectra were dominated by Re–O bonding features, with a minor Re-Re peak observed. In contrast, Re/S@CrB exhibited a distinct scattering feature at approximately 2.0 Å, attributed to Re–S bonds27. Wavelet transform (WT) analysis of the Re L3-edge EXAFS spectra further confirmed these observations (Fig. 3g–i), validating the formation of Re–S coordinated SAs in Re/S@CrB and highlighting the critical role of S in stabilizing Re SAs.

EXAFS fitting analysis indicated average coordination numbers of approximately four for the center of Re SAs in both Re–S (Re/S@CrB) and Re–O (Re@CrB) scattering paths (Fig. 3e, f, Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24, and Supplementary Table 6), suggesting that Re atoms are primarily adsorbed on S- or O-terminated surfaces. Structural analysis revealed distinct bonding configurations: Re–O4 coordination exhibited two Re–O bond lengths (2.05 Å and 1.98 Å), whereas Re–S4 coordination displayed uniform Re–S bonds (2.45 ± 0.04 Å) with higher symmetry, contributing to enhanced thermodynamic stability28. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, this specific Re–S4 coordination has not been previously reported. DFT calculations provided atomic-scale insight into Re coordination (Fig. 3j, Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Data 1). The adsorption energy of Re in a four-coordinate O environment was −2.59 eV, while progressive S substitution reduced the adsorption energy monotonically, reaching −4.78 eV for the Re–S4 configuration, indicating a strong thermodynamic preference for Re–S coordination29. These results confirm a preferential four-coordinate bonding environment between Re atoms and S species on the CrB surfaces. Structural optimization of Re/S@CrB further identified a Re–S4 coordination geometry centered within the S site matrix (Supplementary Fig. 25), consistent with EXAFS fitting results.

Efficient photocatalytic performance of Re/S@CrB

To investigate the structure-activity relationships of the synthesized materials, their performance was systematically evaluated in visible-light-driven photocatalytic biomass conversion coupled with water splitting for the co-production of syngas and lactic acid in a KOH aqueous system (Supplementary Fig. 26). Detailed quantitative information is provided in Supplementary Figs. 27, 28. Photocatalytic H2 evolution showed negligible activity in the absence of hole sacrificial agents. Addition of triethanolamine (TEOA) enhanced H2 evolution threefold (Supplementary Fig. 29), confirming the necessity of electron donors. However, TEOA resulted in inefficient consumption of photogenerated holes30. To address this, a cooperative reaction system was developed, replacing traditional sacrificial agents with ad-xylose oxidation pathway. Comparative catalyst testing revealed that Re/S@CrB exhibited the highest reactivity (Supplementary Fig. 30). Subsequent optimization of reaction conditions further enhanced the photocatalytic performance of Re/S@CrB (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). The apparent quantum yield (AQY) at 420 nm was calculated to be 3.93%. Control experiments under various conditions confirmed that light irradiation, the catalyst, air, and an alkaline environment are all essential for the reaction to proceed (Supplementary Fig. 33). The origin of the tunable H2/CO ratio has been systematically elucidated through controlled experiments (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 34–37). d-Xylose concentration exhibits a strong and monotonic correlation with product distribution (Fig. 4a), whereas electron-hole competition, competitive adsorption, and pH/ionic strength variations contribute only marginally. Under simulated sunlight irradiation, bubble evolution on the Re/S@CrB surface was visually observable within just 3 min (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Movie 1). Both 1 L (Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Movie 2) and 5 L systems (Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Movie 3) exhibited sustained bubble generation, confirming the high activity and practical scalability of Re/S@CrB for syngas production. Comparative analysis showed that Re/S@CrB exhibited competitive performance relative to MBene-based and advanced single-/dual-atom photocatalysts in maximum syngas evolution, tunable H2/CO ratio range, lactic acid yield, and long-term stability (Supplementary Figs. 40 and 41 and Supplementary Tables 8 and 9), demonstrating its effectiveness for simultaneous syngas and lactic acid production.

The photocatalytic performance of Re/S@CrB was further evaluated using various pentoses, hexoses, and disaccharides (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 10). Re/S@CrB exhibited significant syngas and lactic acid production across all substrates. H2 evolution remained largely consistent, confirming the effectiveness of biomass-derived sugars as electron donors. However, CO evolution and lactic acid yields were lower for monosaccharides other than d-xylose. To understand these differences, DFT calculations were performed (Fig. 4d–f and Supplementary Figs. 42–45). Adsorption energy calculations on S sites indicated that d-xylose had the most favorable adsorption energy (Supplementary Fig. 42), consistent with its high CO evolution and lactic acid yield. Notably, the distinct spatial configuration of the C2 hydroxyl group in d-arabinose, compared with d-xylose, led to a higher energy barrier for the isomerization step during CO and lactic acid production. During the oxidation of d-glucose and d-fructose, aldose-ketose interconversion occurs via isomerization. However, cleavage of d-fructose into GAD and dihydroxyacetone (DHA) involves a higher energy barrier31. In l-rhamnose, methylation at the C6 position introduces steric hindrance, further reducing CO and lactic acid production. For d-cellobiose, cleavage of the β-1,4-glycosidic bond in alkaline aqueous solution yields two glucose molecules, which subsequently convert to lactic acid32. This structural feature enhances productivity, as each disaccharide provides two reactive glucose units per hydrolysis event, increasing the availability of substrates for downstream oxidative conversion to lactic acid.

Given the high activity of Re/S@CrB, its photocatalytic performance under natural sunlight was evaluated using a laboratory-scale simulated sunlight concentrator (Supplementary Fig. 46a) and an outdoor sun-tracking photocatalytic reactor (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Movie 4). In the laboratory system, gradual color changes in the reaction solution after 1 h indicated progressive d-xylose conversion, achieving syngas evolution of 7.77 ± 0.03 mmol g−1 h−1 (CO: 2.62 ± 0.02 mmol g−1 h−1, H2: 5.15 ± 0.01 mmol g−1 h−1) with a 70.9 ± 0.05% lactic acid yield (Supplementary Fig. 46b–d). In outdoor sun-tracking tests, a solar radiation sensor and an automated solar-tracking gimbal system were used to maximize solar energy harvesting. Under direct sunlight across varying weather conditions (real-time optical power densities 800–1000 W/m2), an average syngas evolution of 42.21 ± 3.59 mmol g−1 h−1 and lactic acid yield of 81.3 ± 4.44% were achieved (Supplementary Figs. 47 and 48). In addition, it achieves an STH efficiency of 0.28% under outdoor sun-tracking conditions, highlighting its potential for practical application.

Finally, the cycling stability of Re/S@CrB was evaluated over 200 consecutive photocatalytic cycles spanning 233 days under laboratory conditions (Fig. 4h). The system exhibited high stability, retaining over 88% of its initial activity after 200 cycles (H2: 88.4%, CO: 89.1%, and lactic acid: 90.7%). Post-cycling characterization, including SEM, XRD, and XPS, revealed no significant structural changes compared with the pristine catalyst (Supplementary Figs. 49–51). Furthermore, Re/S@CrB maintained excellent photocatalytic performance after 365 days of storage. These results unequivocally confirm the long-term stability and robust cycling durability of Re/S@CrB (Supplementary Fig. 52).

Investigation of band structure and charge-carrier dynamics

The electronic band structures of the synthesized catalysts were investigated using ultraviolet-visible-near infrared diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-vis-NIR DRS), UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), and Mott-Schottky measurements (Supplementary Figs. 53-56). These analyses revealed suitable band edge positions and band gaps for Re/S@CrB, providing sufficient driving force for simultaneous syngas and lactic acid production. To probe the photocatalytic reaction pathway and elucidate the roles of S/Re species, a series of in-situ characterization techniques was employed. Surface electrical properties and photoelectron transfer dynamics were analyzed using in-situ irradiated Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) (Supplementary Fig. 57). Upon illumination, both Re@CrB and Re/S@CrB exhibited surface photovoltage (SPV) signals, with contact potential differences (ΔCPD) of 58.6 mV and 86.2 mV, respectively. The observed decrease in surface potential, correlating with elevated surface electron concentration, confirms enhanced accumulation of photoelectrons on Re/S@CrB33. In-situ XPS measurements under controlled light illumination were performed to probe light-induced electronic dynamics in Re/S@CrB. The Re 4f and S 2p spectra exhibited significant and opposing binding energy shifts: S 2p peaks shifted positively by 0.12 eV, while Re 4f peaks shifted negatively by 0.38 eV (Supplementary Fig. 58). This counter-directional behavior indicates redistribution of electron density, reflecting electron transfer from S to Re atoms34. Combined analysis of KPFM and in-situ XPS demonstrated that photogenerated electrons preferentially localize at Re sites via a transfer pathway mediated by Re–S bonds. This finding was further corroborated by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 59). Photoelectrochemical measurements additionally confirmed the exceptional charge-carrier separation and migration efficiency of Re/S@CrB (Supplementary Figs. 60–64).

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) was employed to investigate photogenerated carrier dynamics. Under 350 nm excitation, transient absorption spectra exhibited a positive band at 600–710 nm, attributed to excited-state absorption (ESA) arising from electron transitions between low- and high-energy states associated with electron accumulation at Re centers (Fig. 5a, d)35. At the same time, the ESA intensity is significantly higher in Re/S@CrB, suggesting that S incorporation modulates the local electronic structure and enhances electron population or stabilization at the excited state. Re@CrB displayed rapid signal decay over the probe delay time (Fig. 5b), whereas Re/S@CrB exhibited significantly slower decay (Fig. 5e). Kinetic fitting at 665 nm revealed two decay components (Fig. 5c, f). The fast component (τ1) corresponds to the decay of the excited-state signal, reflecting the initial relaxation of photogenerated electrons. The slow component (τ2) is attributed to carrier migration, representing the process of electron transfer toward catalytically active Re centers36. The observation of biexponential decay without additional components excludes significant contributions from trap states. The prolonged average lifetime in Re/S@CrB (2099.9 ps) compared to Re@CrB (1019.6 ps) demonstrates that the Re–S4 coordination environment stabilizes photoexcited electrons and promotes their efficient migration, contributing to the high photocatalytic performance37. Electron localization function (ELF) and differential charge density analyses were employed to elucidate charge-transfer dynamics and bonding characteristics in Re/S@CrB. Typically, distinct colors represent varying degrees of electron localization. ELF values (VELF) range from 0 (high delocalization, blue) to 1 (full localization, red)38, with intermediate values (≈0.5, green) indicating abundant free electrons39. For Re@CrB, VELF at the Re–O interface was 0.31 (Fig. 5g). Substitution of coordinating O atoms with S in Re/S@CrB increased VELF to 0.49 (Fig. 5h), reflecting enhanced interlayer free electron density. These results indicate that S atoms act as interfacial bridges between Re and the support, establishing efficient charge transport pathways and improving electron mobility. Differential charge density calculations further corroborated these findings (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 66). Specifically, S atoms act as electron transfer mediators, directing electrons toward Re active sites via Re–S pathways. This facilitates rapid electron delivery to adsorbed *H intermediates on Re, accelerating H2 evolution during syngas production. Additionally, crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (COHP) analysis showed that the integrated ICOHP value of the Re–S bond in Re/S@CrB (−3.763 eV) is more negative than that of the Re–O bond in Re@CrB (−2.356 eV) (Fig. 5j, k), indicating stronger bonding and more stable electron-donor orbitals in Re/S@CrB40.

Mechanism of the synergistic production of syngas and lactic acid

EPR spectroscopy was used to probe the concentration dynamics of reactive species on Re/S@CrB, which were higher than those observed for S@CrB and Re@CrB, confirming its excellent redox capability (Supplementary Figs. 67–69). Additionally, poisoning experiments demonstrated that the generated active species (e−, h+, ·O2−, and 1O2) contribute positively to the reaction efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 70).

In-situ surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) was employed to investigate the ultrahigh sensitivity of Re/S@CrB toward surface-adsorbed molecules and its mechanism for enhancing d-xylose oxidation and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) performance. Figure 6a–f shows the in-situ SERS spectra of S@CrB, Re@CrB, and Re/S@CrB during photocatalytic reactions with different illumination times of 0–90 min. The VS-O vibrational mode at 978 cm−1 confirms selective O-terminal adsorption of d-xylose at S sites42. The isotopic labeling experiment of in-situ Raman spectroscopy was performed by replacing H2O with D2O. This substitution led to the complete disappearance of the signals attributable to H2O and the emergence of new bands at 896 cm−1 (D–O–D bending), 1466 cm−1 (Re–D stretching), and 2200–2800 cm−1 (interfacial D2O vibrations) (Supplementary Fig. 71). Critically, the complete disappearance of the Re–H signal at 1897 cm−1 and the concomitant appearance of the Re–D signal at 1466 cm−1 provide unambiguous evidence that the H protons of H2 originate from water rather than d-xylose. This evidence directly confirms the water-splitting mechanism and excludes the possibility of H2 evolution via oxidative dehydrogenation of d-xylose.

Additionally, in-situ FTIR spectra were collected under reaction conditions to monitor the evolution of surface species (Supplementary Fig. 72). Detailed examination of the FTIR spectra reveals multiple distinct absorption bands attributable to key intermediates and products. The characteristic absorption bands at 1027 cm−1 and 1197 cm−1 correspond to the ν(C–O) stretching vibration of DHA and lactic acid, respectively46. Additional bands at 1298 cm−1 and 1532 cm−1 arise from the δ(C–H) bending and ν(–C=O–) stretching vibrations of GAD, respectively47. Furthermore, lactic acid displays characteristic δ(C–H) bands at 1358 and 1381 cm−1, along with ν(–C=O–) bands at 1637 and 1708 cm−148. A distinct band at 1941 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of *CO, confirming the evolution of CO during the reaction. Two additional positive bands appear at 1231 cm−1 and 1676 cm−1, assigned to d-xylose and the bending vibration of H2O (H–O–H), respectively, indicating the progressive consumption of d-xylose and H2O49.

DFT calculations were performed to simulate the energetics of reactant adsorption, intermediate diffusion, and product desorption for H2 evolution and d-xylose oxidation, providing mechanistic insights into the excellent photocatalytic performance of Re/S@CrB. The Gibbs free energy of hydrogen adsorption (ΔGH) was calculated to evaluate HER activity50. According to the Sabatier principle51, both highly negative and highly positive ΔGH values are unfavorable for HER kinetics, whereas ΔGH ≈ 0 indicates optimal catalytic potential. As shown in Fig. 7c, Cr2AlB2 exhibited the highest ΔGH, reflecting its limited HER capability. Removal of Al layers to form CrB significantly lowered ΔGH, and further improvements were achieved upon incorporation of S and Re, with Re sites outperforming S sites. In Re/S@CrB, synergistic Re–S electron transport pathways enriched electron density at Re active centers, achieving near-optimal HER activity (ΔGH = −0.15 eV). Subsequent DFT calculations were performed to investigate the d-xylose oxidation pathway, including conformational analysis to identify the most stable adsorption configuration of d-xylose on Re/S@CrB surfaces. The adsorption energies of d-xylose on S, Cr, and B sites were systematically compared, confirming that S atoms serve as the primary adsorption sites for d-xylose oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 73). The geometries and atomic configurations of intermediate reactants and products during d-xylose oxidation are illustrated in Fig. 7a, b. The reaction initiates with proton transfer from the C2 α-hydroxy group (C2–OH) of d-xylose to surface S atoms. This proton rearrangement induces keto-enol tautomerism, converting the C1 aldehyde group to a C2 ketone and isomerizing d-xylose to xylulose (*C5H10O5), analogous to the Lobry de Bruyn-Alberda van Ekenstein (Ldb-AvE) rearrangement52. Re/S@CrB then abstracts a proton from the C4 hydroxy group (C4-OH), reducing electron density and weakening the C3–C4 bond. Subsequent deprotonation forms conjugated double bonds between C3 and C4, facilitating cleavage of the C3–C4 bond. In the d-xylose-to-lactic acid conversion pathway (Fig. 7d), DHA undergoes intramolecular dehydration, with the C1 hydroxy group oxidized to an aldehyde, forming pyruvaldehyde (*C3H4O2). Subsequent hole-mediated oxidation at S sites converts the C1 aldehyde to a carboxyl group, while the C2 ketone is reduced to a hydroxyl group, yielding lactic acid (*C3H6O3). For CO evolution (Fig. 7e), DHA (*C3H6O3) and GAD (*C3H6O3) undergo keto-enol isomerization, producing GAD. Under alkaline conditions and the synergistic catalytic effect of Re/S@CrB, the terminal aldehyde and adjacent hydroxy groups of GAD are protonated, triggering intramolecular dehydration followed by C1-C2 bond cleavage to release CO54. GAD is thus a key intermediate in d-xylose-to-CO conversion, as substitution of d-xylose with GAD still produces substantial CO (Supplementary Figs. 74 and 75).

The complete d-xylose oxidation pathways over S@CrB, Re@CrB, and Re/S@CrB are summarized in Fig. 7d, e, with the corresponding ΔG changes for each reaction step shown in Fig. 7f. The variations in energy barriers can be attributed to the following factors: (i) d-Xylose preferentially adsorbs on S atoms in S@CrB and Re/S@CrB. Compared with O adsorption sites, the lower electronegativity and higher polarization capability of S atoms effectively promote d-xylose deprotonation. (ii) In Re/S@CrB, the formation of an additional Re–S electron transfer pathway directs electrons from S to Re atoms, enriching S-terminated surfaces with holes and enhancing oxidative capacity, thereby lowering ΔG for d-xylose oxidation. Analysis of the key intermediate GAD revealed its most favorable adsorption on S sites of Re/S@CrB, indicating that hole-enriched S atoms strengthen hydroxyl group adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 76). This observation is further supported by temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) experiments (Supplementary Fig. 77)55.

The d-band center position is a key determinant of reactant adsorption on catalysts. To elucidate the electronic origin of the optimized adsorption behavior and the role of sulfur, frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis and projected density of states (PDOS) calculations were performed (Fig. 7g, Supplementary Figs. 78 and 79, and Supplementary Data 1). As presented in Fig. 7g, S incorporation induces a downshift of the LUMO of GAD (0.36 eV) and narrows the HOMO-LUMO gap (0.40 eV), indicating enhanced electronic coupling between the intermediate and the catalyst surface56. Importantly, S atoms actively participate in the electronic structure: strong Re–S coupling redistributes charge density and modifies the d-orbital configuration of Re.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a sulfur-mediated coordination reconstruction strategy for Re species on 2D CrB MBenes. By leveraging the HSAB principle, we developed dispersed Re–S4 motifs on 2D CrB MBenes through a sulfur-mediated coordination reconstruction strategy. This structural evolution enhances photocatalytic performance through various synergistic effects: (i) the Re–S4 motif establishes efficient electron-transfer channels, promoting the accumulation of photogenerated electrons at Re sites while localizing holes at S sites. This spatial segregation of redox-active centers significantly suppresses charge combination; (ii) the reconstructed active sites fundamentally improve reactant activation. Specifically, the adsorption energy of the key GAD intermediate was optimized from −1.62 eV to −2.86 eV, effectively lowering the energy barrier for biomass conversion and water splitting; (iii) we achieved continuous control over syngas composition, with H2/CO ratios tunable from 0.1 to 14.4 via substrate concentration. This flexibility, coupled with high-rate syngas production (34.08 mmol g−1 h−1) and lactic acid selectivity (90.8%), demonstrates the platform’s industrial potential. Overall, this work provides atomic-scale insights into how coordination reconstruction can regulate SA dispersion and substrate interactions, offering a robust strategy for the design of new-generation catalysts capable of the sustainable co-production of tunable syngas and high-value chemicals.

Methods

Synthesis of CrB

CrB was synthesized by selective etching of Al atomic layers from Cr2AlB2. In a typical procedure, 1 g of Cr2AlB2 was added to 50 mL HCl solution (1 M) and stirred at room temperature (200 rpm) for 10 days. Following stirring, lamellar materials floating on the liquid surface were skimmed off. The suspension was subsequently filtered and washed repeatedly with deionized water. The resulting powder was finally freeze-dried to yield CrB.

Synthesis of S@CrB

A small porcelain boat (20 mm × 20 mm × 10 mm) containing CrB was positioned centrally within a larger covered porcelain boat (60 mm × 30 mm × 20 mm). Sulfur was loaded bilaterally around the small boat. This assembly was transferred to a tube furnace and annealed under an Ar atmosphere at 350 °C for 3 h, with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. After calcination, the resulting powders were uniformly ground and denoted as S@CrB.

Synthesis of Re@CrB and Re/S@CrB

Re/S@CrB photocatalysts were synthesized via incipient wetness impregnation. Specifically, 400 mg of S@CrB and 20 mg of NH4ReO4 were combined with 10 mL of deionized water. The mixture was subjected to ultrasonic dispersion, followed by freeze-drying to achieve a homogeneous dispersion. The obtained powder was annealed at 300 °C for 1.5 h under 10 vol% H2/Ar atmosphere with a ramping rate of 5 °C min−1. After natural cooling to room temperature, the resulting powder was thoroughly ground and designated Re/S@CrB (Re content of approximately 5 wt%). For comparison, Re@CrB was prepared analogously using CrB as precursor.

Photocatalytic simultaneous production of syngas and lactic acid

Photocatalytic biomass-derived feedstocks selective oxidation coupled with water splitting for the simultaneous production of syngas and lactic acid was conducted in a 50 mL sealed Flat quartz reactor on a Beijing PerfectLight PCX-50C Multi-channel photochemical reaction system equipped with a 10 W white LED Slight panel. Recycling experiments were systematically conducted over 200 consecutive runs. Computational details

First‑principles calculations were carried out with the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) code using projected augmented wave (PAW) potentials for core-valence interactions61. Two functionals were employed for structure relaxation: Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), and PBE augmented with Grimme’s D3 dispersion correction (PBE + D3). A plane‑wave cutoff of 480 eV and a total‑energy convergence criterion of 10-5 eV were applied. All atomic positions and lattice parameters were fully relaxed until the maximum force fell below 0.01 eV Å−1, using a Gamma‑centered 5 × 5 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k‑point mesh. While DFT simulations provide valuable insights into catalytic mechanisms, all conclusions should be cautiously interpreted due to approximations in modeling real catalytic environments.
