Lack of an association between spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection in cancer patients: a nested case-control study
Authors: Ibuki Kurihara, Hajime Yamazaki, Sakura Kato, Noriko Oyama-Manabe, Hitoshi Sugawara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe spleen is a key organ in preventing pneumococcal infection, especially in patients with immunocompromised condition such as those with cancer. Previous studies have shown that a small spleen volume in pneumococcal pneumonia patients is associated with severe disease course. However, it is unknown whether a small spleen increases risk of pneumococcal infection. We investigated the association between spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection.MethodsThis study was a retrospective cohort study using a nested case-control design and involved adult patients with malignancy who underwent chest and/or abdominal CT scans from January 1, 2008, to September 30, 2020, at a tertiary care center in Japan. Exclusion criteria comprised patients diagnosed with hepatic cirrhosis, leukemia, lymphoma, and/or post-splenectomy. From the cohort group that met all selection criteria (n = 22475), we identified all incident cases of pneumococcal infection (pneumococcal pneumonia and/or invasive pneumococcal diseases) and matched them with four controls by age, sex, and follow-up duration. Odds ratios (ORs) for the association between spleen volume and pneumococcal infection were estimated using conditional logistic regression models adjusted for body surface area, performance status, Charlson comorbidity index, and metastatic cancer.ResultsThe median spleen volume was 85.8 (interquartile range, 65.8–120.8) cm3. Over a median follow-up of 4.95 (interquartile range, 1.54–9.25) years, 60 patients were diagnosed with pneumococcal infection (20 with invasive pneumococcal disease and 40 with pneumonia without invasive pneumococcal disease) and matched with 240 controls. Spleen volume reduction (per 10 cm3) did not increase risk of pneumococcal infection in a crude analysis [OR 1.04 (95% CI 0.98–1.11)]. The outcome remained unchanged in the multivariable analysis (OR 1.01 [95% CI 0.95–1.08]).ConclusionsSmall spleen volume did not increase risk of pneumococcal infection in cancer patients.Keywords: Streptcoccus pneumoniae, Splenic volume, Neoplasm, Malignancy, Computed tomography

Background

Community-acquired pneumonia is a leading cause of death among the elderly in developed countries, with Streptococcus pneumoniae being the most common pathogen causing bacterial pneumonia [1]. Streptococcus pneumoniae infections are known as pneumococcal infections, with severe cases being invasive pneumococcal infections where the mortality rate ranges from 15 to 20% [2]. A condition prone to invasive pneumococcal infection is cancer. Cancer patients are 20 times more likely to develop invasive pneumococcal infections than healthy individuals [3]. Thus, it’s crucial to identify risk factors for pneumococcal infection development in cancer patients.

The spleen plays a key role in regulating immune homeostasis by linking innate and adaptive immunity and protecting against infections [4]. There are few case reports of invasive pneumococcal infections in patients with small spleen volumes. It was also reported that a small spleen volume was associated with the severity of pneumococcal infection among patients who developed pneumococcal infection [5, 6]. However, it is unknown whether individuals with a small spleen volume are susceptible to developing pneumococcal infection.

Thus, the present study aimed to investigate the association between spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection in cancer patients at high risk for pneumococcal infection.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a nested case-control retrospective study. This approach leads to efficient sampling by cutting down on the need for costly measurements across a large sample of control subjects, while still using information on all cases [7].

This study involved adult patients with malignancy who underwent chest and/or abdominal CT scans from January 1, 2008, to September 30, 2020, at Jichi Medical University Saitama Medical Center. Patients diagnosed with hepatic cirrhosis, leukemia, lymphoma, and/or post-splenectomy were excluded. We used ICD-10 codes (i.e., C00-96 for malignancy, K74 for hepatic cirrhosis, C91-96 for leukemia, C81-88 for lymphoma, and D730 for post-splenectomy status) were used to include and exclude patients, and the accuracy of these diagnoses were verified using medical notes.

Case-control selection

We used medical records to identify all incident cases of pneumococcal infection during follow-up from January 1, 2008, to December 31, 2022. For each case, we defined the registration date of the cancer diagnosis as the observation start date. Four controls were randomly selected from the case risk set by matching age, sex, and follow-up duration. Active follow-up is defined by the date of the last medical record. From the cohort group that met all selection criteria (n = 22475), we identified 60 patients with streptococcal infection (20 with invasive pneumococcal disease and 40 with pneumonia without invasive pneumococcal disease) and 240 matched control patients (Fig. 1).

CT, Computed Tomography

Spleen volume measurement

Non-contrast CT images with each slice ranging from 5 to 10 mm in thickness were acquired using a multi-slice helical scanner. We used the earliest available CT scan from within three months prior to the observation start date up to the end of follow-up. No CT scans taken after the outcome occurrence were included. If a patient has undergone CT more than once, we used the image from the date closest to the observation start date.

We used Inobitec PRO software (Inobitec Software FZ-LLC, Dubai, UAE). Using this software, a physician (I.K.) was blinded to information beyond the CT image and measured the spleen volume using a region growing method [8]. Starting from a seed point, a growing region was included on all 3D vowels with a CT value between 0 and 100 Hounsfield units. The physician checked and reviewed the spleen contours on each CT slice, and the Inobitec PRO software automatically calculated the spleen volume.

To assess spleen volume measurement reliability, 30 patients were randomly selected from the entire patient cohort. In addition to the region growth method by a physician (I.K.), an independent radiologist (S.K.) manually measured spleen volume slice-by-slice (i.e., planimetry method) using SYNAPSE VINCENT software (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) [9]. The radiologist was blind to clinical data, except for CT images. Spleen boundaries were manually outlined on a slice-by-slice basis, and SYNAPSE VINCENT automatically calculated the spleen area within the manually defined contours on each CT slice. The sum of the calculated areas multiplied by the slice thickness provided the total spleen volume. A previous study indicated that spleen volume measurement using the planimetric method is accurate, with an average error of less than 3.7% of the actual spleen volume [10]. The intraclass correlation coefficient between the spleen volume measured by the physician using the region growing method and the volume measured by the independent radiologist using the slice-by-slice method was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.98–0.99). To assess reproducibility, a physician (I.K.) repeated spleen volume measurement using the region growing method 3 months after the initial measurement. The intraclass correlation coefficient between measurements was 0.99 (95% CI: 0.99–0.99).

Pneumococcal infection

Pneumococcal infection is defined as pneumococcal pneumonia and/or invasive pneumococcal disease. Pneumococcal pneumonia was defined when all the following criteria were met: symptoms consistent with pneumonia, isolation of Streptococcus pneumoniae from sputum culture or positive urinary antigen test for Streptococcus pneumoniae, and new pulmonary infiltrates on chest X-ray or CT scan consistent with pneumonia [11]. Chest X-rays and CT scans were reviewed by two radiologists blinded to the clinical data, who independently determined the presence of new pulmonary infiltrates. If the two decisions did not match, they would discuss and determine whether there were new pulmonary infiltrates or not. The Cohen’s kappa coefficient for the two radiologists’ assessment was 0.84. Invasive pneumococcal disease was diagnosed by isolating Streptococcus pneumoniae from a normally sterile site (e.g., blood, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, peritoneal fluid, pericardial fluid, surgical aspirate, bone, or joint fluid) [12].

Statistical analyses

For the primary analysis, we used conditional logistic regression to estimate odds ratios and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals for the association between spleen volume and pneumococcal infection. In the multivariable analysis, we adjusted for body surface area (BSA), Charlson comorbidity index (0–2, 3, > 4), performance status (0–1, > 2), and metastatic cancer. Performance status is a scale used to assess a patient’s level of functioning, and higher performance status indicates a worse performance status, meaning greater impairment in daily function [13]. We conducted multiple imputation for missing adjusted variables.

For the sensitivity analysis, we used spleen volume categories defined by tertiles instead of continuous spleen volume data and repeated the primary analysis. The three resulting groups (i.e., tertile-defined group) included patients categorized as having small, moderate, and large spleens. Furthermore, we categorized spleen volume in 40 cm³ increments and used the highest volume category as the reference group to repeat the primary analysis [6].

Spleen volume

Figure 2 shows the spleen volume distribution in this study. The median spleen volume for all patients was 85.8 (interquartile range: 65.8–120.8) cm3, that for cases was 80.0 (54.9–113) cm3, and that for controls was 88.3 (66.3–122) cm3. The minimum spleen volume in cases was 10.5 cm3, and in controls, it was 20.3 cm3. In this study, 90% of patients had a spleen volume ranging from 33.3 to 184.5 cm3 in cases and ranging from 39.3 to 205.7cm3 in controls.

Spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection

Table 2 shows spleen volume and odds ratios for pneumococcal infection. In the crude analysis, a smaller spleen volume did not increase risk of pneumococcal infection (OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.98–1.11; P = 0.16). After adjusting for potential confounders, the results remained similar (OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.95–1.08; P = 0.54). In the sensitivity analysis, the tertiles (cutoff points) used to categorize spleen volume were 71.8 and 108 cm3 respectively. Thus, each patient’s spleen was categorized as small (< 71.8 cm3, n = 100), moderate (71.8–108 cm3, n = 100), and large (> 108 cm3, n = 100). Pneumococcal infections occurred in 26 patients with small spleens, in 17 patients with moderate spleens, and in 17 patients with large spleens, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, compared to large spleen, a small spleen did not increase risk of pneumococcal infection (OR, 1.28; 95% CI, 0.57–2.88; P = 0.53). In the analysis categorizing spleen volume in 40cm3 increments, each patient’s spleen was categorized as 0–40 cm³ (n = 18), 40–80 cm³ (n = 111), 80–120 cm³ (n = 93), 120–160 cm³ (n = 41), and > 160 cm³ (n = 37). Pneumococcal infections were observed in 5 patients with a spleen size of 0–40 cm³, 25 patients with 40–80 cm³, 17 patients with 80–120 cm³, 8 patients with 120–160 cm³, and 5 patients with > 160 cm³, respectively. The 0-40cm3 category did not increase risk of pneumococcal infection compared to the > 160cm3 category after adjusting for potential confounders (OR, 1.11; 95%CI, 0.21–5.73 P = 0.89).

Discussion

In this CT-based study with spleen volumes mostly over 40 cm3, small spleen volumes did not increase risk of pneumococcal infections in cancer patients. This result remained unchanged in our sensitivity analysis using both tertile-defined cutoff points and a 40cm3 increment for spleen volume. Our findings did not support the notion that a small spleen volume is a risk factor for pneumococcal infection.

There is a discrepancy between previous studies and the current study. Previous studies suggested that a small spleen volume is linked to worsened pneumococcal infection [5, 6, 14], whereas this study found no association between spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection. The discrepancy between the previous and current study may be due to the outcome (i.e., infection severity vs. incidence) and the timing of spleen volume assessment (i.e., post-infection vs. pre-infection). The spleen volume may be affected by infection and subsequent shock state [5, 15–17]. Patients with sepsis often have an enlarged spleen marked by congestion and neutrophilic infiltration in the red pulp [5]. The spleen can store 20–30% of the total blood volume [16], and its volume decreases during hypovolemic shock, aiding in maintaining hemoglobin concentration [15]. It was also suggested that in septic shock, adrenaline stimulation can cause spleen contraction due to increased sympathetic nervous system activity [17]. Thus, we believe that spleen volume can vary with infection severity or patients’ overall condition, and a reduced spleen size post-infection may indicate infectious disease severity. On the contrary, this study, which assessed spleen volume prior to infection, suggests that spleen volume does not increase risk of pneumococcal infection.

In the current study, 90% of patients had a spleen volume ranging from 38 to 202 cm3, and only 2.3% of patients had a spleen volume of 30 cm3. The spleen volume associated with risk of pneumococcal infection might be less than observed in this study. Corazza et al. reported that in patients with partial splenectomy, those with a residual spleen volume of less than 22 cm3 have a similar pitted erythrocyte count to those who underwent total splenectomy, suggesting that a minimum spleen volume of 20–30 cm3 is necessary to restore sufficient splenic function for protection against pathogens [18]. Anai et al. reported that spleen volume of < 40cm3 was an independent association factor for mortality in patients with pneumococcal pneumonia [6]. When examining the association between spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection, it may be essential to involve a significant number of individuals with spleen volumes less than 40 cm3.

This study only investigated cancer patients. Tumor infiltration into the spleen can increase spleen volume [19]; however, this study did not estimate tumor infiltration into the spleen. Although splenic metastases occur in less than 1% of all metastases [20], it cannot be ruled out that splenic metastases may have influenced spleen volume in this study. Moreover, cancer patients are over 20 times more likely to develop invasive pneumococcal infections than healthy individuals [3], and the relationship between spleen size and pneumococcal infection may vary between cancer patients and the general population. A further study is needed to investigate the association between spleen volume and risk of pneumococcal infection in the general population. Given that the incidence of pneumococcal infection is around 2 per 1,000 person-years [21], an ideal study would be a population-based cohort study including over 10,000 individuals with varying spleen sizes.

A strength of this study is that it measured spleen volume before the infection occurred. As stated above, spleen size can change due to infection and subsequent circulatory status. However, the present study has several limitations. First, the present study was a single-center retrospective study, and the results may not be generalizable to different clinical settings. Secondly, we lacked information on pneumococcal vaccination history. The pneumococcal vaccine effectively reduces pneumococcal infection incidence [21, 22], which may have influenced the current study’s results. Third, the present study did not measure spleen function parameters, such as pitted erythrocyte count or IgM memory B cells. Fourth, we lacked information on immune status. Fifth, both the primary analysis and sensitivity analysis exhibited wide confidence intervals, indicating that the statistical power of this study may have been insufficient, which could lead to the oversight of relevant effects. Finally, this study may be subject to detection bias due to the lack of specific criteria for ordering microbiological tests.

Conclusions

Small spleen volume did not increase risk of pneumococcal infection in cancer patients with predominantly over 40 cm3 spleen sizes. Further population-based studies involving a significant number of individuals with a spleen volume less than 40 cm3 are needed to assess if an extremely small spleen volume raises risk of pneumococcal infection.
