Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) premedication for pain-free inferior alveolar nerve block: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohammad A. Sabeti, Tara Boroumand, Seyed Mohammadreza Masoumi, Nicka Aghamohammadi, Mohammad Hosein Amirzade-Iranaq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated whether oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) premedication increases pain-free inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) success compared with placebo in adults with mandibular posterior teeth diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP). Randomized controlled trials comparing oral NSAID premedication with placebo before IANB in adults with mandibular posterior teeth and SIP were included. MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched from inception to October 20, 2025. This review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251171592). Success required absence of patient-reported intraoperative pain at any recorded procedural step after the initial IANB and no supplemental anesthesia. Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2, certainty of evidence was assessed using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation, and pooled effects were assessed using random-effects meta-analysis. Eleven trials involving 737 participants were analyzed. Oral NSAID premedication increased pain-free IANB success compared with placebo (292/452 [64.6%] vs 81/285 [28.4%]; risk ratio, 2.06; 95% confidence interval, 1.48-2.86). Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 63%), and the prediction interval crossed no effect (0.83-5.10). Sensitivity analyses, excluding high-risk trials and access-only studies, remained significant, with no detected heterogeneity. Diclofenac and ketorolac showed significant and consistent effects, whereas ibuprofen favored benefits but remained imprecise and heterogeneous. Oral NSAID premedication increased pain-free initial IANB success compared with placebo in mandibular posterior teeth with SIP; however, low certainty and between-study variability limit the predictability of this benefit. Oral NSAID premedication may be considered when not contraindicated; however, clinicians should still anticipate supplemental anesthesia because pain-free success after the initial IANB is not predictable.

INTRODUCTION

Fear of pain during root canal treatment is a major component of patient anxiety and is associated with greater intraoperative pain and deferral or avoidance of care [123]. This concern is supported by clinical experience and evidence showing that supplemental anesthesia is often required during endodontic treatment, particularly for inflamed pulps [45].

Achieving profound pain-free initial pulpal anesthesia remains a major clinical challenge, particularly in patients diagnosed with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis (SIP) [678]. SIP is a clinical diagnosis of a vital inflamed pulp that is incapable of healing and typically produces spontaneous or lingering thermal pain [910]. For mandibular posterior teeth with SIP, routine inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) frequently fails to provide adequate endodontic anesthesia [67811]. This failure rate is linked to the underlying inflammation: the acidic tissue environment in SIP reduces the effective non-ionized fraction of local anesthetic, whereas inflammatory mediators, such as prostaglandins, sensitize peripheral nociceptors, thereby reducing IANB efficacy [41213]. In contrast, IANB is typically more successful in cases of asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis (AIP) [14], whereas in SIP, preoperative pain predicts lower block success, consistent with nociceptor sensitization [15].

Given that inflammation in SIP increases prostaglandins derived from cyclooxygenase (COX), preoperative administration of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which inhibit COX, is a commonly recommended strategy to attenuate prostaglandin-mediated sensitization and potentially improve IANB effectiveness [161718]. However, clinical trials evaluating NSAID premedication before IANB for SIP have reported conflicting results. For example, some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported no significant improvement in IANB success with NSAID premedication [1920], whereas other RCTs have reported significantly higher success with specific NSAID regimens [21222324]. The observed differences may reflect variables such as the drug class, dose, and timing of administration before IANB, all of which are plausible effect modifiers that warrant structured evaluation.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses generally support oral NSAID premedication as an adjunct to improving trial-defined IANB success in irreversible pulpitis [2526272829]. The remaining issue is that pooled trial-defined success may not estimate pain-free initial anesthesia in symptomatic mandibular posterior teeth, an endpoint relevant to urgent endodontic treatment. Earlier syntheses identified heterogeneity that directly affects this estimate, particularly differences in baseline pain and how success was measured, with outcomes based on sensibility testing in some trials and patient-reported pain during access, instrumentation, or pulp removal in others [2529]. Including asymptomatic cases may raise apparent IANB success because AIP has higher block success than SIP [14], and grouping mild pain with no pain can inflate pain-free estimates by counting the remaining intraoperative pain as a success [2328], despite pain-free treatment being the aim of endodontic anesthesia [3031].

Therefore, this review evaluated whether preoperative oral NSAID premedication increases pain-free IANB success compared with placebo in adults with mandibular posterior teeth diagnosed with SIP. We hypothesized that oral NSAID premedication would increase the likelihood of pain-free pulpal anesthesia after initial IANB without supplemental anesthesia.

Studies on primary dentition, maxillary teeth, non-SIP diagnosis, AIP, non-oral or combination premedication, irrelevant/confounded designs, animal/microbiological studies, ex vivo/in vitro studies, case reports, review articles, and trials without extractable pain-free IANB success were excluded. Trials specifying diagnosis as “irreversible pulpitis” or “acute irreversible pulpitis” were included only when eligibility criteria described symptomatic vital mandibular posterior teeth consistent with SIP.

1. Search methods for the identification of studies

Three reviewers developed the search terms in consultation with a librarian and searched MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Web of Science Core Collection, and the Cochrane Library using the search terms or equivalent Medical Subject Headings (Supplementary Table 1) from database inception through October 20, 2025. Supplementary identification included screening the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, ClinicalTrials.gov, Google Scholar, backward reference checking, and forward citation searching to identify potentially eligible records and corresponding full-text peer-reviewed publications. The records were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) [34] and de-duplicated. Eligibility was limited to English language, full-text, peer-reviewed, randomized trials. Unpublished studies were excluded from the analysis.

2. Screening and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts and subsequently assessed the full texts. Calibration was conducted by jointly reviewing the first 20 records. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when needed, adjudication with a third reviewer who also verified the final inclusion list. Data from the included studies were independently extracted by two reviewers and verified by a third reviewer. The extracted variables included study design and setting, SIP diagnostic criteria, participant characteristics, intervention and comparator details (agent, dose, and timing), IANB local anesthetic protocol, outcome definition and timing, and anesthetic success and failure counts. Adverse events reported after premedication were extracted, when available, and summarized.

To make outcome harmonization prespecified and reproducible, we created a study-level outcome-mapping form before quantitative synthesis. For each trial, we recorded the original definition of anesthetic success, pain threshold used, procedural step(s) assessed, whether success required the absence of supplemental anesthesia, and whether explicit no-pain counts were directly reported or derived. In this review, success was defined as the absence of patient-reported intraoperative pain at any recorded procedural step after the initial IANB and no supplemental anesthesia. Direct no-pain counts were extracted when reported. If a broader category, such as “no or mild pain” was reported, only explicit no-pain counts were used, and the study contributed to the meta-analysis only when those counts could be directly obtained from the publication. If pain was assessed in multiple steps, success required no pain at any of the reported steps before rescue or supplemental injection. Any participant requiring rescue or supplemental anesthesia before the completion of the reported procedural endpoint was classified as a failure. No numerical imputation of no-pain counts was performed. Supplementary Table 2 lists the full-text exclusions, including studies excluded because strict no-pain data were not separately extracted from broader reported success categories, and Supplementary Table 3 summarizes study-level mapping decisions.

3. Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias for each included randomized clinical trial was assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) across five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process, (2) bias caused by deviations from intended interventions, (3) bias caused by missing outcome data, (4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (5) bias in the selection of the reported result [35]. Two reviewers independently performed RoB 2 assessments, and disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. Domain-level judgments were recorded as low risk, some concerns, or high risk, and an overall risk-of-bias judgment was assigned as follows: “low risk” if all domains were low risk, “some concerns” if at least 1 domain raised some concerns, and “high risk” if at least one domain was judged high risk. Trials rated as having a high risk of bias were retained in the primary analysis and evaluated using a prespecified sensitivity analysis.

4. Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for the primary outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework across 5 domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [36]. Because all the included studies were randomized trials, certainty began at high levels and was downgraded as appropriate based on domain-specific concerns. Two reviewers independently performed the GRADE assessments, and disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. A Summary of Findings table was prepared to report the pooled effect estimates and certainty ratings with justification for any downgrading decisions.

5. Quantitative analyses

Meta-analyses were performed using RevMan Web (Cochrane, London, UK) [37]. The primary effect measure was the risk ratio (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), where RR > 1 indicates a higher probability of anesthetic success with NSAID premedication than with the placebo. For trials with a zero cell count, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied. Effect estimates were pooled on a logarithmic scale using inverse-variance methods. A random-effects model was selected to estimate the average effect across studies, given the expected clinical and methodological variability among NSAID, doses, anesthetic protocols, and trial conduct [38]. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood, and 95% CIs for the pooled effect were calculated using the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman approach [39]. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using the chi-square test and quantified using I2 and τ2. For the primary pooled analysis, a 95% prediction interval was calculated [40]. Forest plots were used to display the individual studies and pooled effect estimates.

1) Multi-arm trials

In trials evaluating more than one eligible NSAID arm against a shared placebo group, NSAID arms were combined within the study for pooled (any NSAID) comparison by summing events and totals across the NSAID arms to avoid double-counting the placebo group.

2) Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup meta-analyses were conducted using an NSAID agent when an agent was evaluated in at least three trials. Agents represented by fewer trials were summarized narratively. For the agent-specific subgroup meta-analyses, each NSAID arm contributed only to its corresponding subgroup model, and the shared placebo groups were not double-counted in a single analysis. Subgroup findings were interpreted descriptively and not used to infer comparative superiority across agents.

3) Sensitivity analysis

The robustness of the primary pooled estimate was assessed by repeating the meta-analysis after excluding trials judged to have a high overall risk of bias. When substantial heterogeneity or marked variation in control event rates was observed, additional sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the influence of the individual trials. Because the procedural assessment windows differed across the trials, an additional sensitivity analysis restricted the primary pooled comparison to trials that assessed pain beyond access preparation, including access plus instrumentation or pulp extirpation. The same random-effects framework was retained for sensitivity analyses to ensure the consistency of the inference.

4) Timing and dosing patterns

Timing, dose, and local anesthetic protocols were summarized descriptively at the study arm level. As none of the included trials directly compared timing intervals, doses, or local anesthetic protocols with the same NSAID, no comparative meta-analysis of timing, dose, or anesthetic protocol was performed.

5) Publication bias

For the primary pooled analysis, funnel plot inspection was used to explore publication bias, with cautious interpretation, given the limited power of funnel plots and the influence of between-study heterogeneity.

RESULTS

1. Results of the search process

Database searches and other methods identified 347 records (340 from databases and 7 from other methods). After the removal of duplicates, 184 records underwent title/abstract screening, and 44 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. At the full-text stage, 33 articles were excluded, leaving 11 randomized clinical trials included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1). The reasons for full-text exclusion are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Studies frequently cited in prior reviews but not included here are individually listed in Supplementary Table 2, with explicit reasons for exclusion, most commonly failure to report separately extractable strict no-pain outcomes or inclusion of noneligible diagnostic groups.

2. Characteristics of included studies

Eleven randomized clinical trials published between 2010 and 2024 were included, comprising 737 participants [2021222324414243444546]. All studies evaluated preoperative oral NSAID administration before an inferior alveolar nerve block, compared with a placebo, in mandibular posterior teeth with SIP. In this review, anesthetic success was harmonized with the strict endpoint of no pain without supplemental anesthesia. Several trials were multi-arm studies evaluating more than one NSAID against a shared placebo arm. Across trials, NSAIDs included diclofenac (50 mg) [22244243], ketorolac (10 mg) [2223414446], ibuprofen (400-600 mg) [2021414445], and, in one trial each, lornoxicam (8 mg) [42], piroxicam (20 mg) [43], naproxen (550 mg) [43], aceclofenac [46], and flurbiprofen [46]. All trials used a conventional IANB: seven used lidocaine [22234142434446], two used mepivacaine [2145], and two used articaine [2024]. The key characteristics of the included trials are summarized in Table 1.

3. Outcome harmonization for the primary endpoint

Supplementary Table 3 shows how each trial’s original definition of success was mapped to the pain-free endpoints. Only trials that directly reported or derived no-pain counts were included in the meta-analysis. When broader outcomes such as no or mild pain were reported, only the explicit no-pain category was accepted. If pain was assessed at multiple procedural steps, the harmonized outcome required no pain at any of the reported steps before supplemental anesthesia. Because procedural windows differed across trials, the pooled estimate represented pain-free success across each trial’s reported intraoperative assessment period rather than a single uniform procedural endpoint.

4. Risk of bias

The overall risk of bias varied across studies (Table 2). Using RoB 2, four trials were judged as low risk, three trials had some concerns, and four trials were judged as high risk. Across domains, the most common limitations involved the randomization process (domain 1), reflecting incomplete reporting of randomization details, and measurement of the outcome (domain 4), reflecting subjective pain assessment, and variation in success definitions across trials. RoB 2 judgments informed the sensitivity analysis.

5. Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for the primary pooled comparison of any oral NSAID premedication versus placebo was rated low. Although the pooled effect favored NSAIDs, certainty was downgraded one level for risk of bias because four trials were judged to be at high overall risk of bias and one level for inconsistency because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 63%) and the prediction interval crossed the line of no effect (0.83-5.10). Although the sensitivity analysis, excluding high-risk trials, yielded a similar effect estimate with no observed heterogeneity, it did not eliminate concerns about variability in the full evidence base. Therefore, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as evidence of an average benefit across trials and not as evidence that NSAID premedication is beneficial in every clinical setting. Certainty was moderate for diclofenac owing to imprecision, moderate for ketorolac owing to the risk of bias, and very low for ibuprofen owing to the risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision (Table 3).

6. Quantitative synthesis

1) Primary (pooled) analysis

For any NSAID + IANB versus placebo + IANB, all 11 trials contributed to the pooled comparison (Fig. 2A). Across the studies, anesthetic success occurred in 292 (64.6%) of 452 participants receiving NSAID + IANB and 81 (28.4%) of 285 participants receiving placebo + IANB. Random-effects meta-analysis estimated a higher average likelihood of pain-free IANB success with NSAIDs compared with placebo (RR = 2.06; 95% CI, 1.48-2.86; P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 63%; τ2 = 0.14). The prediction interval was wide (0.83-5.10) and crossed no effect, indicating that the magnitude, and possibly the presence, of benefit may vary across settings and study conditions.

2) Sensitivity analysis: excluding high risk-of-bias trials

A sensitivity analysis excluding studies with a high risk of bias showed consistent findings (Fig. 2B). The pooled effect remained statistically significant with a similar magnitude (RR = 2.28; 95% CI, 1.84-2.82; P < 0.001; Fig. 2B). No heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0.00), suggesting that observed variability in the primary analysis may be driven by trials at higher risk of bias or methodological differences captured in risk-of-bias judgments.

Sensitivity analysis: trials assessing pain beyond access A sensitivity analysis excluding access-only studies showed consistent findings (Fig. 2C). This analysis included six trials that assessed pain during access plus instrumentation or pulp extirpation [222342444546]. The pooled effect remained statistically significant with a similar magnitude (RR = 2.27; 95% CI, 1.81-2.85; P < 0.001; Fig. 2C). No heterogeneity was detected (I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0.00), suggesting that the primary finding was not driven by access-only endpoint assessment.

3) Sensitivity analysis: influence of individual trials

Because Sharma et al. [20] and Rodrigues et al. [24] reported the highest and lowest placebo success rates, respectively, separate sensitivity analyses excluded their studies from the primary pooled analysis. After excluding the study of Sharma et al. [20], the pooled effect remained statistically significant (RR = 2.31; 95% CI, 1.89-2.83; P < 0.001), with no heterogeneity detected (I2 = 0%; τ2 = 0.00). After excluding the study of Rodrigues et al. [24], the pooled effect also remained statistically significant (RR = 2.01; 95% CI, 1.44-2.81; P = 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 64%; τ2 = 0.14).

5) Timing and dosing patterns across trials

NSAID regimens varied according to the agent, dose, and timing before IANB. Most trials administered the study medication approximately 30-60 minutes before the block; however, the timing was not compared directly within the trials. Ibuprofen was evaluated at 400-600 mg, diclofenac at 50 mg, and ketorolac at 10 mg; other NSAIDs were present less frequently (Table 1). As the agent, dose, timing, local anesthetic protocol, and trial quality varied simultaneously, the available evidence does not allow the isolation of an optimal timing or dosing regimen.

6) Adverse-event reporting

Adverse-event reporting was limited: 3 of the 11 included trials explicitly reported adverse event information. Noguera-González et al. [21] reported no adverse effects or flare-ups, and Saha et al. [22] reported no adverse events. Hegde et al. [23] reported gastritis in one participant receiving ketorolac. The remaining trials did not report adverse events as discrete outcomes; therefore, adverse events were not pooled.

7) Publication bias

No clear funnel plot asymmetry was observed; however, with only 11 trials and substantial heterogeneity, the funnel plot inference was underpowered and should be interpreted cautiously (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

This systematic review analyzed 11 RCTs that evaluated oral NSAID premedication before IANB in mandibular posterior teeth with SIP using a strict pain-free definition of anesthetic success. Overall, NSAID premedication increased the average probability of pain-free IANB success without supplemental anesthesia. The association remained after excluding trials with a high risk of bias; however, the primary prediction interval crossed no effect, indicating that the magnitude and presence of benefits may vary across clinical settings.

The findings of this review are directionally consistent with those of previous systematic and umbrella reviews [2526272829] that reported that NSAID premedication may improve IANB success in patients with irreversible pulpitis. The contribution of the present review is methodological and interpretive; the analysis was restricted to oral NSAID versus placebo trials involving mandibular posterior teeth with SIP and used a strict pain-free endpoint requiring no reported intraoperative pain and no supplemental anesthesia. By excluding broader “no or mild pain” success categories unless explicit no-pain counts were available, the review provides a more conservative estimate aligned with the clinical goal of pain-free treatment [3031].

The prediction interval for the primary pooled analysis crossed the line of no effect, indicating that the magnitude of the benefit may vary across clinical settings [40]. Variability in drug selection, dose, timing before injection, local anesthetic solution, operator technique, and baseline pain intensity [15] may all contribute to the differences in the observed effects. Baseline pain was reported using different scales and summary formats and could not be quantitatively evaluated across studies. Preoperative pain duration and anatomical factors affecting IANB are generally unreported. Lornoxicam, piroxicam, naproxen, aceclofenac, and flurbiprofen were each evaluated in one trial, and only two trials used articaine, and two used mepivacaine; therefore, reliable subgroup analyses of these factors were not possible. These differences may also contribute to the variability across agent-specific subgroup estimates. The overall pooled estimate remained significant after excluding the studies by Sharma et al. [20] or Rodrigues et al. [24]. From a biological perspective, SIP is characterized by the release of inflammatory mediators and sensitization of nociceptors [413]. COX inhibition reduces prostaglandin synthesis [18] and may partially attenuate peripheral sensitization before local anesthetic delivery, increasing the likelihood of profound pulpal anesthesia [417].

The available trials did not establish the optimal timing or dose of NSAID for pre-medication. Most regimens were given 30-60 minutes before IANB, consistent with the intended preemptive anti-inflammatory mechanism; however, no included trial directly compared the timing intervals. Similarly, ibuprofen was studied at 400-600 mg, but the evidence did not establish whether a single dose was superior. Therefore, the present findings support the concept of oral NSAID premedication when not contraindicated. However, they do not justify a specific recommendation regarding 30 versus 60 min or ibuprofen 400 versus 600 mg.

The ibuprofen subgroup deserves particular caution because ibuprofen is the most clinically accessible NSAID and may be the agent most likely to influence clinical practice. In the primary subgroup analysis, ibuprofen did not reach statistical significance, and the heterogeneity was substantial. Although the point estimate favored ibuprofen, the certainty of the evidence was very low, and the evidence was less reliable than that for diclofenac or ketorolac. The exploratory exclusion of one outlier trial with 100% placebo success that used articaine [20] resolved heterogeneity and produced a statistically significant result; however, this should not be interpreted as confirmatory. Its influence may reflect both a statistical ceiling effect and variations in the background anesthetic protocol. Therefore, the overall class-level effect of NSAIDs should not be assumed to establish a robust ibuprofen-specific effect.

Clinically, oral NSAID premedication represents a simple adjunct that may increase the average probability of pain-free IANB success in mandibular posterior teeth with SIP. The GRADE absolute effect estimate was 301 pain-free IANB successes per 1000 patients with NSAID premedication (95% CI, 136-529) with low certainty. However, premedication does not guarantee anesthetic success, and clinicians should anticipate the need for supplemental techniques when indicated. When premedication is considered and no contraindications exist, the current evidence supports oral NSAID premedication as a class, whereas the differences between individual agents remain uncertain because subgroup comparisons were indirect. Because safety reporting was incomplete and the sample sizes were small, these data cannot establish comparative safety between NSAID agents. Clinical use should be individualized based on the patient’s medical history, anticipated block difficulty, and contraindications such as NSAID hypersensitivity, gastrointestinal ulceration or bleeding risk, renal impairment, anticoagulant use, and cardiovascular risk.

This systematic review has several limitations. The included trials varied in terms of diagnostic criteria, local anesthetic protocols, dosing regimens, and timing of administration. Because the procedural assessment windows differed across trials, some endpoint heterogeneity remained despite strict no-pain harmonization. Several otherwise relevant trials reported anesthetic success only as a combined no/mild pain category. Because the pain-free subgroup could not be separated, these trials were not eligible for strict no-pain meta-analysis. Several trials were small, four were judged to have a high risk of bias, and the overall certainty of evidence was low for the main pooled comparison. Some subgroup estimates were imprecise, and adverse events were inconsistently reported, preventing a quantitative safety synthesis. With the available number of studies, publication bias could not be confidently excluded. Eligibility was restricted to English-language reports, which may have introduced language bias. In addition, unpublished studies were excluded, which may have increased susceptibility to dissemination bias. Nevertheless, this review has notable strengths, including focused diagnosis eligibility criteria, a strict pain-free success definition, handling of multi-arm trials in pooled analyses, and sensitivity analyses examining the influence of high-risk-of-bias trials.

Future studies should standardize SIP diagnostic criteria, pain-free outcome definitions and timing, and adverse event reporting. Trials directly comparing commonly used NSAIDs, dose and timing regimens, and postoperative pain outcomes would help define which patients benefit the most and reduce reliance on rescue injections.

Preoperative oral NSAID premedication increases the average pain-free IANB success rate compared with placebo in adults with mandibular posterior teeth diagnosed with SIP. However, the prediction interval crossed no effect, indicating that the magnitude and presence of benefits may vary across settings. Oral NSAID premedication may be considered when not contraindicated; however, clinicians should continue to anticipate the need for supplemental anesthesia.
