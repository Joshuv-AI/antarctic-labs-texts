Rethinking Quantum Repeaters: Balancing Scalability, Feasibility, and Interoperability
Javier Rey-Domínguez, Mohsen Razavi

Abstract
Quantum repeaters are enabling technologies for long-distance quantum communications. Despite the significant progress in the field, we still not only face implementation challenges but also need theoretical solutions that better meet all the desired design criteria. Preliminary solutions for quantum repeaters often do not scale well, while the most advanced solutions are so demanding that their implementation may take a long time and require substantial changes to current telecom infrastructure. In this paper, we propose a compromise solution that is not only scalable in the mid-to-long term but also adapts well to the realities of the backbone networks in the current Internet infrastructure. The key ideas behind our solution are twofold. First, we use a connectionless approach to entanglement swapping, allowing our solution to benefit from the same features as packet-switched networks. Second, we employ simple error detection, rather than more complicated error correction, techniques to make our solution sufficiently scalable in the face of errors. This is achieved without requiring overly demanding specifications for the physical devices needed in the network. We test this idea in a quantum key distribution (QKD) setting over a repeater chain and demonstrate how trust-free continental QKD can be achieved through several stages of development.



I
Introduction

Quantum technologies are witnessing unprecedented investment and development
Gibney 2019
;
Brooks 2019
, with quantum communications emerging as a cornerstone of this revolution.
The long-term objective in this domain is the realization of large-scale quantum networks
Elliott et al. 2005
;
Peev et al. 2009
;
Salvail et al. 2010
;
Sasaki et al. 2011
;
Dynes et al. 2019
;
Joshi et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Alshowkan et al. 2021
and, ultimately, the quantum Internet
Wehner et al. 2018
;
Illiano et al. 2022
;
Kozlowski et al. 2023
.
However, achieving long-distance quantum communications remains a formidable challenge.
In this paper, we address this problem from a pragmatic perspective, seeking a solution that meets essential performance criteria while avoiding unnecessary complexity and adapting more effectively to the realities of existing telecommunications infrastructure.

Quantum repeaters are the key solutions to the challenges of long-haul terrestrial quantum communications, for which we have to deal with the exponential increase in channel loss with distance
Briegel et al. 1998
;
Munro et al. 2015
;
Muralidharan et al. 2016
.
Historically, the primary criterion in designing quantum repeaters has been scalability: achieving efficient and reliable quantum data transfer over large distances without prohibitive resource overheads.
To address this issue, existing repeater approaches fall into two broad categories.
The first aims to distribute high-fidelity entanglement for quantum teleportation and includes:
(a) probabilistic repeaters
Duan et al. 2001
, which are not the best in terms of resource efficiency but are easier to implement with current technology;
(b) deterministic repeaters
Briegel et al. 1998
, which improve performance by using deterministic quantum gates for entanglement swapping
Żukowski et al. 1993
and conventional distillation
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Deutsch et al. 1996
but remain constrained by two-way communication and memory decoherence;
and (c) encoded repeaters
Jiang et al. 2009
, which employ quantum error correction (QEC) to overcome decoherence limits by correcting errors during entanglement connection.
All these rely on repeat-until-success (RUS) entanglement distribution across elementary links.
The second category directly encodes an unknown quantum state into a strong QEC code capable of correcting both loss and computational errors
Munro et al. 2012
;
Muralidharan et al. 2014
, enabling one-way, long-distance quantum transmission.

While scalability has been the dominant design criterion for quantum repeaters, it has often overshadowed other considerations critical to their practical deployment.
Transitioning from probabilistic to one-way repeater architectures, for example, entails a substantial increase in hardware quality requirements to realize gains in rate and performance
Muralidharan et al. 2016
.
Certain networking constraints have also been overlooked.
For instance, long-haul fiber infrastructure typically places nodes tens of kilometers apart
Hazell and Little 2002
;
Feuerstein 2005
;
Inc. 2024
, whereas one-way repeaters may require spacing of only a few kilometers.
Resource allocation presents an additional challenge: many repeater proposals implicitly assume that all intermediate nodes between communicating users are exclusively dedicated to their use, enabling the optimization of operations such as entanglement swapping and distillation
Briegel et al. 1998
;
Chang and Xue 2022
;
Pathumsoot et al. 2024
.
While such assumptions can reduce memory decoherence and error propagation for participating users, they neglect fairness concerns in multi-user settings and may be incompatible with some of the core tenets underlying modern packet-switched networks
Peterson and Davie 2007
;
Kurose and Ross 2017
.

In this paper, our objective is to design practical quantum repeaters holistically by addressing scalability, feasibility, and interoperability within the constraints of existing telecom infrastructure and its operational realities.
To achieve this vision, it is useful to reflect on some of the techniques and concepts commonly used in classical networks.
One such concept is that of statistical multiplexing
Peterson and Davie 2007
;
Kurose and Ross 2017
, the key paradigm enabling efficient and fair resource sharing in packet-switched networks.
Under this paradigm, communication resources along each transmission channel are dynamically allocated to incoming requests if possible, rather than reserved to specific users.
Packets of data received during busy periods are temporarily stored, and then forwarded as soon as resources become available.
This mechanism, often known as store-and-forward, allows multiple users to share infrastructure without requiring persistent reservations, and supports diverse communication profiles with minimal coordination overhead.

In the context of quantum networks that rely on end-to-end entanglement, the natural translation of store-and-forward seems to be sequential entanglement generation (SEG)
Aparicio and Meter 2011
.
Rather than reserving entire paths for end-to-end entanglement distribution, SEG allows entanglement to be generated and swapped hop-by-hop as soon as resources become available.
This is the first step towards a repeater solution that is better aligned with existing infrastructure, hence offering a better chance at commercial success.

Some initial studies have already explored the topic of SEG
Aparicio and Meter 2011
;
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Kamin et al. 2023
;
de Andrade et al. 2024
.
The insights from these papers suggest that some sort of resource multiplexing strategy is indeed desirable for quantum networks
Aparicio and Meter 2011
, and that SEG in particular might outperform reservation-based protocols in short-range networks
Xiao et al. 2023
.
Furthermore, rigorous analyses based on probability theory have been conducted
Kamin et al. 2023
, offering valuable insights into the potential impact of buffering on the performance of SEG protocols
de Andrade et al. 2024
.
Nevertheless, most of these works omit the critical issue of error propagation, which must be addressed in order to enable scalable quantum networks.

In this work, we propose a connectionless quantum repeater architecture that handles errors without imposing substantial increases in resource requirements or quality demands.
Our solution relies on encoded repeaters
Jiang et al. 2009
, but instead of using QEC to correct errors, we use them solely for error detection, aborting an SEG round upon the detection of an error.
This approach is motivated by two factors.
First, focusing on error detection enables the usage of simpler codes, making implementation easier.
Second, previous studies
Jing et al. 2020
;
Jing and Razavi 2021
have already shown that encoded repeaters using error detection can achieve acceptable performance when used for quantum key distribution
Scarani et al. 2009
;
Lo et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Pirandola et al. 2020
(QKD) applications.
In particular, they show that the rounds in which an error is detected may not contribute much towards the overall system performance.
In the context of networks, this has the implication that, by aborting the SEG protocol early upon detection of an error, we effectively release the resources to other users in the network.
As we will see, this can offer a more efficient resource allocation scheme.

To benchmark our SEG with error detection (SEG-ED) repeater scheme, we calculate the achievable secret key generation rate for an entanglement-based QKD system run on such a repeater chain.
Here is a summary of our findings:

•

Under realistic mid-term hardware assumptions, our proposed strategy could support communication ranges approaching 2000 km, enabling continental-scale quantum connectivity;

•

Our solution adapts well to node spacing variations of up to tens of kilometers;

•

We observe improvement in resource utilization in high-traffic scenarios, when compared to traditional reservation-based (connection-oriented) approaches; and

•

Our solution offers a middle-ground for simplicity versus scalability, addressing the networking issues that facilitate early commercial deployment of quantum networks.

II
Sequential entanglement generation with error detection

Let us explain the core idea and motivation behind our proposed setting in this section.
Suppose two users, say Alice and Bob, wish to distribute a shared entangled state across a quantum network, as shown in
Fig.

1
.
To do so, they employ a quantum repeater protocol that coordinates and exploits the available network resources, such as physical channels and quantum memories.

Figure 1:

A schematic of a quantum repeater network, with a certain path of seven repeater nodes connecting two users.
Solid lines represent quantum and classical channels and circles represent nodes.

In conventional repeater protocols, the network allocates a path to Alice and Bob, e.g., via nodes
\mathsf{R}_{1},\mathsf{R}_{2},\dots
, in
Fig.

1
, and reserves the required resources at the corresponding nodes.
Then,
in parallel
, the nodes attempt to generate entanglement across all elementary links, and they subsequently execute entanglement swapping
Żukowski et al. 1993
at the intermediate nodes.
This strategy, which we refer to as parallel entanglement generation (PEG), generally reduced waiting times, minimizing the effects of quantum memory decoherence.
Moreover, it allows the repeater protocol to optimize the order of any subsequent operations
Chang and Xue 2022
;
Pathumsoot et al. 2024
, for instance choosing a nested swapping strategy
Briegel et al. 1998
when dealing with probabilistic repeaters or swapping as soon as possible if deterministic Bell-state measurements (BSMs) are available.

The PEG approach can be likened to the classical circuit switching approach
Peterson and Davie 2007
;
Kurose and Ross 2017
, a connection-oriented strategy in which a physical path, and the corresponding necessary resources, is reserved and allocated to each pair of communicating users.
Much like circuit switching, PEG may not be appropriate for large networks with bursty traffic.
In such networks, many users may wish to distribute quantum information simultaneously, so making a fair and efficient use of the network resources is critical.

An alternative to PEG, which in principle solves these problems, is SEG.
In SEG, Alice first generates entanglement with the first hop in the path, i.e.,
\mathsf{R}_{1}
in
Fig.

1
.
Once successful,
\mathsf{R}_{1}
starts generating entanglement with
\mathsf{R}_{2}
, after which entanglement swapping is performed at
\mathsf{R}_{1}
to distribute entanglement between Alice and
\mathsf{R}_{2}
.
This procedure goes on, distributing the entanglement hop-by-hop along the path and releasing the resources of intermediate nodes after swapping, until Alice and Bob become entangled.
Under this approach, only one channel is used at a time to generate entanglement, while the remaining channels are free to be used by other users in the network.
Therefore, SEG can allow for fairer resource allocation and may be more appropriate for enabling future quantum data traffic, better adapting to the practical preferences of network operators.

Nevertheless, entanglement distribution using SEG faces several challenges.
Notably, the achievable rate of an SEG repeater system using probabilistic swapping with success probability
p
scales with
p^{N_{r}}
for
N_{r}
swaps.
A connection-oriented protocol like PEG, however, can use a nested swapping strategy to improve this scaling to
p^{\log(N_{r})}
.
Therefore, the sequential approach may not be useful unless deterministic swapping is available.

Another key challenge for all quantum repeater protocols is that of mitigating error propagation.
A typical technique used for this purpose is entanglement distillation
Bennett et al. 1996
;
Deutsch et al. 1996
.
Conventional distillation techniques are, however, generally probabilistic, which again could create rate scaling issues, making it a less appealing option in the context of SEG protocols.
More importantly, such distillation techniques do not fully resolve memory decoherence issues
Razavi et al. 2009
.
Since such issues are especially harsh in SEG protocols, due to its sequential approach to entanglement distribution, conventional entanglement distillation may be unsuited for such schemes.

An alternative solution to error handling in quantum repeaters is to take advantage of QEC by employing encoded repeaters
Jiang et al. 2009
.
This is a completely deterministic approach to error mitigation, but requires increasingly complex codes in the face of growing errors, which makes implementation less feasible in the short term.

Accounting for the issues above, here we propose a strategy that offers a feasible solution to error mitigation, all the while maintaining the features and benefits of SEG protocols.
This strategy relies on using SEG on encoded repeaters but, instead of correcting errors at each step, we use the underlying QEC to only detect errors.
We refer to this SEG with error detection scheme as SEG-ED.
Essentially, we generate an encoded (logical) entangled state at each hop, which we then extend sequentially using a logical swap operation.
At each swap, the structure of the distributed logical entangled state allows us to detect errors using the employed code.
If we detect an error, the distribution is immediately aborted, and all resources are released.

Our proposal has several advantages, namely:
(1) detecting errors is easier than correcting them, allowing us to use simpler codes and significantly relaxing the implementation demands of encoded repeaters;
(2) the resources that are released after abortion can be reused by other users in the network, which might offer an advantage in terms of efficient resource utilization;
and (3) its architecture is similar to that of the Internet and other modern networks, facilitating integration with existing infrastructure.

That being said, the regimes of operation in which we can benefit from what SEG-ED offers are unclear.
First, the error resilience of the protocol needs to be assessed.
In particular, if the equipment used in the repeater nodes is too noisy, the frequency of abortion might render SEG-ED unusable.
Secondly, we need to determine the rate and reach that our solution can achieve, and compare it with alternative solutions able to cover similar distances.

In this paper, we assess the feasibility of the SEG-EG strategy by exploring its achievable performance under different hardware assumptions.
Specifically, we place our focus on a QKD setup, thoroughly described in the following section.
The choice of QKD for benchmarking allows us to obtain results that are both relevant and easy to understand.
Moreover, it enables some simplifications in the analysis.
Finally, we remark that previous work on QKD systems using error detection
Jing et al. 2020
;
Jing and Razavi 2021
has demonstrated promising performance on PEG repeaters.
This provides us with a robust starting point to analyze the SEG-ED solution.

III.1
Quantum memories and quantum operations

We consider that every node in the chain is equipped with two types of quantum memories capable of storing quantum states.
Specifically, some of the quantum memories can be used in conjunction with the quantum channels to, through some link-level mechanism, distribute entanglement between neighboring nodes.
We refer to these specific memories as communication qubits, and refer to all other memories as data qubits.

We assume that each node contains
{N_{\text{mux}}}
communication qubits for each connected channel, that is, one set (two sets) of
{N_{\text{mux}}}
communication qubits at each user (repeater) node.
Each communication qubit is paired with a corresponding qubit in the neighboring node via a quantum channel, enabling pairwise entanglement generation between them.
We assume that each node has at least the same number of data qubits as communication qubits; that is, the total number of quantum memories in each user (repeater) node is greater than or equal to
2{N_{\text{mux}}}
(
4{N_{\text{mux}}}
).

Note that, in a full network setting, buffering is a key element of protocols based on statistical multiplexing, and therefore we implicitly assume that each node possesses some additional data qubits used for this purpose.
This assumption allows us to use a pipeline approach to entanglement distribution, where each subsequent round is initiated as soon as possible, as explained in
Section

III.5
.
By assuming that we have sufficient data qubits at each node, we can ignore any issues that may appear in practical networks due to limited-size buffers, such as additional delays or blocked requests.
We remark that, in the case of QKD, this assumption is not necessary for Alice because she can measure her own data qubits immediately.
This is not necessarily the case in more general quantum communications applications, in which she may need to store the logical entanglement until she confirms its distribution to Bob, making the number of required quantum memories quickly increase as additional rounds are initiated.

We assume that all nodes are capable of performing deterministic, albeit noisy, gates.
This includes applying any necessary single-qubit operations and measurements on any of the quantum memories.
We also assume that each communication qubit is paired with a specific data qubit for applying two-qubit gates in a deterministic way.
In general, certain platforms may allow for mixing and matching data and communication qubits, but this is not the case for all emerging quantum memories.
In particular, our restrictive assumption makes our results applicable to a promising class of memories based on color centers
Acosta and Hemmer 2013
;
Fuchs et al. 2011
;
Sukachev et al. 2017
, where electron and nuclear spins are paired together in each physical memory.
Nevertheless, we allow data qubits to interact among each other as necessary through two-qubit gates.
In terms of timing, we assume that any local operations, including gate and measurements, are performed with negligible delay, as it is often the case in long-haul quantum communications that propagation delays across elementary links amount to the main source of latency.

III.2
Link-level entanglement generation

In this subsection, we explain the method that we consider for the distribution of entanglement between pairs of communication qubits in adjacent nodes
\mathsf{R}_{i}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
.
We assume that LLEG relies on a two-photon interference scheme
Simon and Irvine 2003
;
Simon et al. 2007
;
Moehring et al. 2007
;
Bernien et al. 2012
.
In this scheme, single photons are entangled to communication qubits at each node and subsequently interfered, pairwise, in optical BSM modules.
The results of such BSMs are shared among the involved nodes using classical communication.
If a BSM is successful, the corresponding memories are assumed to be projected onto an entangled state.
This occurs with a probability

p_{\text{gen}}=p_{\text{cou}}^{2}\frac{\eta_{D}^{2}}{2}10^{-\alpha_{\text{ch}}L_{0}/10},

(1)

where
p_{\text{cou}}
is the probability of entangling a photon with a communication qubit and coupling it to a fiber optic channel after any necessary frequency conversion,
\eta_{D}
is the quantum efficiency of the detectors used in the BSM,
\alpha_{\text{ch}}
is the attenuation coefficient of the fiber, and we account for the fact that BSMs implemented with linear optics can only distinguish two of the four Bell states
Calsamiglia and Lütkenhaus 2001
.

While, in the above scheme, it is common to assume that the BSM module is located half way between
\mathsf{R}_{i}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
, here we assume that the BSM is performed at node
\mathsf{R}_{i}
.
This is to avoid having an extra node, albeit not with full functionality, in the network, which may cause us deviate from our objective of better adapting quantum repeaters to existing infrastructure and its node spacing.
This approach is also known as ‘receiver-sender” architecture
Jones et al. 2016
.
For the sake of simplicity, we assume that LLEG is initiated on-demand only.
That is, node
\mathsf{R}_{i}
starts the procedure with a classical message sent to
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
.
We will then use proper timing of events to ensure synchronized interference of photons at the BSM module in node
\mathsf{R}_{i}
.

III.3
Logical entanglement generation

Here, we explain how a logical (encoded) entangled state
\ket{\Phi^{+}_{L}}=(\ket{0_{L}}\ket{0_{L}}+\ket{1_{L}}\ket{1_{L}})/\sqrt{2}
, with
\ket{0_{L}}
and
\ket{1_{L}}
representing the encoded versions of respective states
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
, can be distributed between adjacent nodes
\mathsf{R}_{i}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
.
We assume that we use a QEC code of size
n_{\text{code}}
, and that
n_{\text{code}}
quantum memories are used to represent the states
\ket{0_{L}}
and
\ket{1_{L}}
.

To create
\ket{\Phi^{+}_{L}}
, we first use the LLEG scheme in
Section

III.2
to entangle as many pairs of communication qubits as needed at
\mathsf{R}_{i}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
.
To do so, we repeatedly apply, in parallel, the LLEG procedure on all available communication qubits (initially
{N_{\text{mux}}}
) in the two nodes.
This can be achieved in conjunction with an appropriate multiplexing technique.
We continue until we have the required number of entangled pairs.
If an excessive number of pairs are generated during the last round of LLEG, for simplicity, we discard them, such that all
{N_{\text{mux}}}
communication qubits are available when next logical entangled states are needed.

Once we have a sufficient number of entangled pairs, node
\mathsf{R}_{i}
prepares
n_{\text{code}}
of its data qubits in the state
\ket{+_{L}}=(\ket{0_{L}}+\ket{1_{L}})/\sqrt{2}
, while
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
initializes the corresponding data qubits in
\ket{0_{L}}
.
At that point, the entanglement shared by the communication qubits can be used to implement a logical CNOT gate remotely
Jiang et al. 2009
, using the qubits in
\mathsf{R}_{i}
as control and those in
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
, as target.
Notably, this procedure generates some classical information, which must be exchanged between the nodes so that the Pauli frame of the state can be adjusted, using single-qubit gates.

III.4
Logical entanglement swapping, error detection, and decoding

Suppose
\mathsf{R}_{i}
shares a logical entangled state with
\mathsf{R}_{0}
(through the SEG-ED protocol, as we explain next) and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
.
We can then perform a logical swapping operation (LSWP) to distribute encoded entanglement between
\mathsf{R}_{0}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
.
The information obtained during LSWP can, in general, be used to correct some errors.
In our scheme, however, we only use this information to detect errors.
In general, this classical information needs to be transmitted to the involved nodes, which will use it for error detection/correction and to adjust the Pauli frame.

In our setup, we consider that the nodes employ a three-qubit repetition code (3QRC)
Jiang et al. 2009
;
Jing et al. 2020
.
The main appeal of 3QRC is its simplicity when it comes to implementation, as it is the smallest code (
n_{\text{code}}=3
) that can correct a bit-flip.
It is particularly useful when the sources of error are mainly of one type (if most errors are phase-flips, a simple rotation of the code can be used), while being an easy-to-analyze choice for our benchmarking purposes.
Logical operations using 3QRC can be implemented by applying the equivalent gates on the physical qubits, row-by-row
Jiang et al. 2009
;
Jing et al. 2020
.
Specifically, this means that the encoding procedure consists of three remote CNOT gates, therefore we only need to
n_{\text{code}}=3
pairs of entangled communication qubits.
Moreover, the typical circuit for the LSWP for 3QRC consists of three BSMs.
Thinking of each BSM as a CNOT gate followed by measurements in
\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathcal{X}
bases, an error is detected by 3QRC if the
\mathcal{Z}
-basis measurements do not all agree.

Two users sharing a logical entangled state can use a decoding operation
Jiang et al. 2009
to ideally convert it back to the unencoded entangled state
\ket{\Phi^{+}}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}
, which can then be used for the application of interest.
In the case of QKD, however, we can shortcut this process by extracting key bits directly from the logical entangled state
Jing and Razavi 2021
.
This can be done, as we assume in our SEG-ED setup, by directly measuring the data qubits storing the logical entanglement, and then performing the decoding operation in the classical domain
Jing and Razavi 2021
.
More specifically, we consider the QKD-specific decoder introduced in Ref.
Jing and Razavi 2021
, where the decision in the
\mathcal{Z}
basis is taken by majority vote.
It is noteworthy that this decoder has some classical error correction capabilities, but these are not necessarily in contrast with SEG-ED’s principles, since they take place in the user nodes rather than in the repeater nodes.

In general, we consider standard circuits for both the encoding and LSWP procedures, where in the former we assume that the state
\ket{+_{L}}
is prepared using deterministic but imperfect gates
Bratzik et al. 2014
.
The in-depth analysis of these procedures is provided in
Appendix

A
.

entangled pairs, they immediately apply the encoding operation for 3QRC, and exchange the resulting classical information.
Without waiting to receive the classical information from
\mathsf{R}_{1}
, Alice measures her data qubits according to the QKD decoder described in
Section

III.4
to obtain a raw key bit,
b_{\text{A}}
, as shown in
Fig.

3
(a).
Measuring as soon as possible ensures that her data qubits suffer little memory decoherence, and any adjustment indicated by the result of
\mathsf{R}_{1}
’s encoding can be applied in the classical domain.

After receiving the encoding results from Alice, the data qubits in
\mathsf{R}_{1}
are projected into a quantum state determined by the value of
b_{\text{A}}
.
From here on, this quantum state is logically teleported hop-by-hop in the following manner, as shown in
Fig.

3
(b), at every intermediate node.
First,
\mathsf{R}_{1}
uses the procedure in
Section

III.3
to share a logical entangled state with
\mathsf{R}_{2}
.
After the encoding operations are locally applied,
\mathsf{R}_{1}
immediately performs the LSWP operation on its data qubits.
The information obtained from the encoding and the swapping operations is transmitted to the next node, which uses it to determine whether an error is detected.
If it is, the distribution round is aborted.
Otherwise,
\mathsf{R}_{2}
performs the appropriate Pauli-frame adjustments and moves on to the next hop.

Eventually, assuming no error is detected, the distribution round reaches Bob.
At the last hop, depicted in
Fig.

3
(c), the repeater node
\mathsf{R}_{N_{r}}
acts as any other intermediate node.
Bob, on the other hand, acts in a similar manner to Alice, and measures his data qubits immediately after encoding to obtain his own raw key bit
b_{\text{B}}
.

Once Bob receives the classical information from
\mathsf{R}_{N_{r}}
, he can determine whether any error was detected and, if not, perform any necessary adjustments to
b_{\text{B}}
.
Importantly, we choose to consider that Bob discards the distribution round if he detects an error.
While in practice this may not offer any advantage in resource utilization, since all network resources have already been used at this point, this assumption simplifies our analysis without having a significant impact on the protocol’s performance.

At the end of a successful round, Alice and Bob are left with one raw key bit each, which will be used by the subsequent postprocessing steps of the QKD protocol, conducted over an authenticated classical channel.
We assume Alice and Bob use the efficient version of BBM92, that is, they use the
\mathcal{Z}
basis most often, allowing them to sift most raw key bits for key extraction.

One key advantage of our SEG-ED approach is that we do not need to wait for a distribution round to be complete before starting the next one.
Instead, we assume that Alice starts every round as soon as possible, that is, as soon as the previous round has completed its first hop.
Since we have assumed that sufficient buffer space is present in the nodes, this creates a pipeline of distribution rounds from Alice to Bob.
This pipeline approach is assumed for the key rate analysis in
Section

IV
.

III.6
Error models

We introduce here the error models considered for our analysis.
In particular, we consider that each quantum memory decoheres according to a depolarization channel specific to that memory.
That is, if the relevant part of the system is described by density matrix
\rho
, the impact of an arbitrary qubit
\mathsf{Q}
decohering for some time
\tau
is modeled by the following transformation:

\rho\to(1-\gamma_{\tau})\rho+\gamma_{\tau}\trace_{\mathsf{Q}}(\rho)\otimes\frac{\mathds{I}_{\mathsf{Q}}}{2},

(2)

where
\mathds{I}_{\mathsf{Q}}/2
is the maximally mixed state in qubit
\mathsf{Q}
, and

\gamma_{\tau}=1-e^{-\tau/T_{\text{coh}}}

(3)

is the probability of depolarization, with
T_{\text{coh}}
denoting the coherence time of the quantum memory.

We consider that the coherence time of all quantum memories is identical.
While this may not always be true in practice, especially in heterogeneous systems in which communication qubits and data qubits are different in nature, most of the storage times will take place in data qubits (as we will see later), and so the effects of reduced coherence in communication qubits are not expected to offer vastly different insights.

We assume that the entangled state projected onto a pair of communication qubits after a successful BSM during the LLEG procedure can be described by a Werner state with fidelity
F_{0}
.
That is, denoting the density matrix of the two entangled qubits by
\rho_{W}
, we have that:

\rho_{W}=F_{0}\ket{\Phi^{+}}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{\Phi^{+}}+(1-F_{0})\frac{\mathds{I}_{4}-\ket{\Phi^{+}}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{\Phi^{+}}}{3},

(4)

where
\mathds{I}_{4}
corresponds here to the identity operator in the joint Hilbert space of the two entangled qubits.

Regarding quantum circuits, we consider that all single-qubit gates can be applied without introducing any errors.
This is justified by the fact that error rates in practical single-qubit gates are typically much lower than error rates in two-qubit gates
Brown et al. 2011
;
Harty et al. 2014
;
Harty et al. 2016
, and therefore their contribution is comparatively small.
On the other hand, we consider that two-qubit gates have a probability
\beta
of depolarizing the qubits they are acting upon.
That is, a two-qubit gate acting on qubits
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
of a system with density matrix
\rho
is modeled by the following quantum operation:

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{U}}_{\mathsf{AB}}(\rho)=(1-\beta)\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{AB}}\rho\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{AB}}^{\dagger}+\beta\trace_{\mathsf{AB}}(\rho)\otimes\frac{\mathds{I}_{\mathsf{AB}}}{4},

(5)

where
\mathrm{U}_{\mathsf{AB}}
is the unitary operator representing the ideal operation of the gate, and
\mathds{I}_{\mathsf{AB}}/4
represents the maximally mixed state in qubits
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
.

Finally, we assume that single-qubit measurements have an error probability
\delta
when measuring in either of the
\mathcal{X}
or
\mathcal{Z}
bases.
This symmetry arises from our assumption that single-qubit gates are ideal, which allows us to implement measurement in the
\mathcal{X}
using a Hadamard gate followed by a measurement in the computational basis (or vice versa).

We remark here that the error models introduced in this section are quite generic, and therefore applicable to a wide range of hardware platforms.
What is more, the depolarization channel is a relatively pessimistic assumption for our particular setup, since arbitrary errors cannot be detected using repetition codes.
In view of this, it is reasonable to expect that a practical implementation of SEG-ED, which employs more appropriate codes for its underlying technology, may be even better performing than our results shown in
Section

V
.

IV
Performance analysis

A fundamental performance metric for a QKD protocol is the secret fraction
Scarani et al. 2009
,
r_{\infty}
, which can be interpreted as the amount of secret information that can be extracted from a sifted bit in the asymptotic regime.
For a BBM92 protocol with an efficient (asymmetric) choice of basis, the secret fraction can be computed as

r_{\infty}=\max\{0,1-\binentropy(e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{Z}}})-\binentropy(e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{X}}})\},

(6)

where
\binentropy(p)=-p\log_{2}(p)-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
and
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{Z}}}
(
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{X}}}
) is the error rate in the
\mathcal{Z}
(
\mathcal{X}
) basis.

In this section, we simulate the QKD setup in
Section

III
by assuming that there is no eavesdropper.
This allows us to compute the error rates
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{Z}}}
and
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{X}}}
from the theoretical description of the end-to-end entangled quantum state
\rho_{\text{end}}
virtually shared between Alice and Bob.
Crucially, the error rates are calculated with respect to the QKD measurements, and therefore
\rho_{\text{end}}
refers to the quantum state of the data qubits in the users at the moment of decoding, where we are taking into account that Alice’s decoding is performed as soon as possible.

In order to compute
\rho_{\text{end}}
, we exploit the recursive nature of the SEG-ED protocol.
Specifically, we define
\rho_{i}
as the quantum state shared between the data qubits of Alice at the time of decoding (as explained above) and the data qubits of
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
right after the logical swap in
\mathsf{R}_{i}
, provided no error was detected until this point.
By definition, it follows that
\rho_{\text{end}}=\rho_{N_{r}}
.
Moreover, we can write the recursive expression for
\rho_{i}
as:

\rho_{i}=\mathcal{E}^{\text{LSWP}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}\left(\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}(\rho_{i-1})\otimes\rho_{0}\right)\quad i=1,\ldots,N_{r},

(7)

where
\mathcal{E}^{\text{LSWP}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}
denotes the logical swapping operation in
\mathsf{R}_{i}
, provided that no errors are detected;

\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}
corresponds to the memory decoherence process suffered by the data qubits in
\mathsf{R}_{i}
during the generation of the logical entanglement between this node and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
;
and
\rho_{0}
is the logical entangled state shared between
\mathsf{R}_{i}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
right after encoding.
A thorough description of
\mathcal{E}^{\text{LSWP}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}
is provided in
Appendix

A
.

Importantly, the description of
\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}
and the value of
\rho_{0}
depends on the probabilistic nature of the entanglement generation procedure between any two adjacent nodes.
However, for the sake of simplicity, we focus here only on the average waiting times of the LLEG procedure.
This simplification also justifies our notation for
\rho_{0}
, as this state, while representing different quantum systems at each iteration, keeps its form in
Eq.

7
.

Based on the above discussion, we are interested in finding out the average waiting time at each hop,
{\tau}_{\text{hop}}
, as well as the average time
{\tau}_{g_{j}}
elapsed from the start of the logical entanglement generation process until the generation of the
j
-th pair of entangled communication qubits, with
j=1,2,3
.
The former quantity,
{\tau}_{\text{hop}}
, corresponds to the storage time in the data qubits of
\mathsf{R}_{i}
, and is therefore crucial to the description of
\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}_{\mathsf{R}_{i}}
.
The parameters
\rho_{g_{j}}
, on the other hand, determine the memory decoherence suffered by the communication qubits that become entangled earlier than the others.
Altogether, we have that
{\tau}_{\text{hop}}=\rho_{g_{n_{\text{code}}}}
, as we execute all local operations (encoding and LSWP or decoding) as soon as
n_{\text{code}}
entangled pairs are generated.

To calculate the above time parameters, we define
G_{j}
as the random variable corresponding to the number of LLEG rounds required to generate
j
pairs of entangled communication qubits.
Then, the average time from the start of the procedure until the
j
-th generation event,
{\tau}_{g_{j}}
, can be computed as:

{\tau}_{g_{j}}=(2\expected\left\{G_{j}\right\}+1)\frac{L_{0}}{c},

(8)

where the factor 2 appears due to the reattempt delay in the receiver-sender LLEG arrangement,
\expected\{G_{j}\}
is the expected value of
G_{j}
and
c
is the speed of light in the transmission channel.

To compute
\expected\left\{G_{j}\right\}
, we find that we can model the distribution of
G_{j}
as the
j
-th order statistic for
{N_{\text{mux}}}
realizations of a geometric distribution with success probability
p_{\text{gen}}
.
Therefore, its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is:

\begin{split}&\operatorname{CDF}_{G_{j}}(k)=\\
&\sum_{l=j}^{{N_{\text{mux}}}}\binom{{N_{\text{mux}}}}{l}\left[1-(1\!-\!p_{\text{gen}})^{k}\right]^{l}(1\!-\!p_{\text{gen}})^{{N_{\text{mux}}}-l},\end{split}

(9)

and its expected value can be shown to be:

\begin{split}&\expected\{G_{j}\}=1\!+\!\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\frac{(1\!-\!p_{\text{gen}})^{{N_{\text{mux}}}+l-k}}{1-(1\!-\!p_{\text{gen}})^{{N_{\text{mux}}}+l-k}}.\end{split}

(10)

Now that the waiting times
{\tau}_{g_{j}}
are calculated, we can compute the decoherence suffered in the communication qubits during the logical entanglement generation.
Specifically, we have that the depolarizing noise acting on a Werner state reduces the state’s fidelity but does not change the shape of its probabilistic distribution, i.e., it remains a Werner state.
Therefore, we can simply describe each state
\rho_{g_{j}}
, which we define as the
j
-th Werner state distributed by the LLEG procedure, from its fidelity
F_{g_{j}}
, computed as:

F_{g_{j}}=\left[1-\gamma_{({\tau}_{\text{hop}}-{\tau}_{g_{j}})}\right]^{2}F_{0}+\frac{1-\left[1-\gamma_{({\tau}_{\text{hop}}-{\tau}_{g_{j}})}\right]^{2}}{4},

(11)

for
j=1,\ldots,n_{\text{code}}
.

Finally, we can now describe the initial state in
Eq.

7
,
\rho_{0}
, as

\rho_{0}=\mathcal{E}^{\text{ENC}}\left(\rho_{\ket{+_{L}}}\otimes\ket{0_{L}}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{0_{L}}\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n_{\text{code}}}\rho_{g_{j}}\right)\right),

(12)

where
\mathcal{E}^{\text{ENC}}
represents the encoding operation and
\rho_{\ket{+_{L}}}
represents the imperfectly prepared state
\ket{+_{L}}
.
Both of these are described in detail in
Appendix

A
.
Putting everything together, we can finally calculate
\rho_{\text{end}}
using
Eq.

7
.

The secret fraction gives us an idea of the secrecy in rounds where the distribution has been completed, that is, no errors have been detected.
However, for our protocol to be practical, we are more interested in the rate at which secret bits are generated, that is, the secret key rate (SKR).
We compute the SKR,
K
, as:

K=R_{\text{bit}}r_{\infty},

(13)

where
R_{\text{bit}}
denotes the raw key bit distribution rate from Bob’s perspective.

Due to the fact that Alice initiates each subsequent round of SEG-ED as soon as possible, and thanks to our assumption that enough data qubits are employed for buffering (see
Section

III.1
), we can compute the distribution rate
R_{\text{bit}}
by considering a pipeline of entangled states.
Therefore, we have that:

R_{\text{bit}}=\frac{p_{\text{end}}}{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}},

(14)

where
p_{\text{end}}
is the probability that a distribution round is completed, that is, no errors are detected, which can be computed as:

p_{\text{end}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N_{r}}p_{\text{LSWP},{i}},

(15)

with
p_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
denoting the probability of not detecting an error in the
i
-th LSWP operation.
We derive the value of
p_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
in
Section

A.5
.

Finally, we introduce another interesting metric for the analysis of quantum repeater schemes: the normalized cost,
C_{K}
.
For QKD applications, cost is a metric that relates the number of qubits employed by a protocol to the key rate it achieves.
This is useful for normalization purposes, as it accounts for the number of resources used to achieve a certain level of performance, enabling us to compare different solutions more fairly.

In our particular case, however, we are also interested in somehow measuring the resource allocation efficiency in the SEG-ED protocol and compare it to that of reservation-based alternatives.
Consequently, we define
C_{K}
as the ratio between the average number of qubits used by the repeater chain at any given point and the secret key rate.
The motivation for this is that, after a distribution round is aborted, the resources of any subsequent repeater node in the chain will be unused by the users for a period of time.
In a network with other active users, these resources could potentially be used by other entanglement distribution sessions, increasing the overall performance of the network.

The value of
C_{K}
can be obtained using the following expression:

C_{K}=\frac{4{N_{\text{mux}}}}{K}\left[2+\sum_{i=2}^{N_{r}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}p_{\text{LSWP},{j}}\right],

(16)

where we have accounted for
4{N_{\text{mux}}}
quantum memories used for each hop, and we are disregarding the additional data qubits that may be used for buffering.

V
Results

In this section, we present and discuss the numerical results of our simulations regarding the performance of the protocol proposed in
Section

III.5
, and compare it with some alternative solutions.
In particular, we consider two other sequential schemes in which no encoding is employed, that is,
n_{\text{code}}
is 1 and
\rho_{0}
is a Werner state with fidelity
F_{0}
.
The first of these unencoded SEG schemes, which we call SEG-noED, implements the swapping operation using deterministic but noisy quantum gates.
Since no error detection is employed, no distribution rounds are aborted, and consequently a raw key bit is successfully distributed to Bob at the end of each round.
However, the correlation between this key bit and Alice’s is not necessarily high, as the distributed quantum state has accumulated errors along the repeater chain.
The second unencoded SEG variant, which we refer to as SEG-prob, implements the swapping operation using an optical BSM with success probability
\eta_{D}^{2}/2
, but the devices used in the BSM modules are otherwise assumed to be error free.
Lastly, we also compare our results with the best-case scenario of a PEG scheme using 3QRC for error detection, which we denote by PEG-ED.
Here, by best-case scenario we mean that, after the entanglement generation procedure is initiated in parallel at every hop, we assume that it is successfully completed at approximately the same time for all nodes, minimizing decoherence.
The details regarding the analysis of these alternative protocols are included in
Appendix

B
.

While in practice this is also likely to change as technology evolves, its impact is relatively small when kept within reasonable bounds, so we simplify the discussion by keeping it a constant.

V.1
Impact of the hop length on the performance

One of the key factors that determine how well quantum repeaters can be integrated with existing fiber optic infrastructure, crucially affecting their commercial prospects, is the inter-node distance or hop length,
L_{0}
.
In today’s long-haul fiber networks, this distance is often on the order of tens of kilometers
Feuerstein 2005
;
Inc. 2024
, even reaching lengths over 100 km for undersea deployments
Hazell and Little 2002
.
It is important to see how each repeater solution conforms to this reality of optical networks, as deploying additional nodes and splitting deployed fiber requires additional investments and maintenance costs.
Interestingly, the most promising repeaters in terms of achievable rates, i.e., one way repeaters, are the worst in this regard, often requiring the hop length to be only a few kilometers long
Muralidharan et al. 2014
;
Muralidharan et al. 2016
.

In the particular case of entanglement-based repeater schemes, the increased waiting times leading to memory decoherence are the main factor restricting the hop lengths.
In fact, when considering ideal repeaters, which introduce no errors, it is always beneficial to use shorter channels by increasing the number of intermediate nodes, as this reduces the waiting times in optical fiber setups exponentially with
L_{0}
.
However, if repeater nodes are noisy it may instead be more desirable to use longer hop lengths to minimize the errors introduced in the swapping stage.

In this section, we explore this performance trade-off with respect to the hop length to find out the acceptable values of
L_{0}
that enable long-distance quantum communications.
We are especially interested in the hop lengths between 40 km and 120 km, which correspond to typical channel lengths in deployed long-distance fiber networks
Hazell and Little 2002
;
Feuerstein 2005
;
Inc. 2024
.
We focus on two metrics: the range of the QKD system,
L_{\text{max}}
, which we define as the maximum distance between the users such that
r_{\infty}>0
; and the achievable secret key rate
K
.

In
Fig.

4
(a), we plot
L_{\text{max}}
against
L_{0}
for the hardware parameters in
Table

1
.
We observe that, for stage 1, the most pessimistic configuration in our study, the range of all protocols considered is quite poor, as they can only distribute secret bits up to about 200 km, with the optimal value of
L_{0}
ranging from 15 km to 35 km.
However, if technology on par with the parameters in stage 2 is available, our protocol can distribute secret bits across distances close to 2000 km, which would allow continental-scale communications across Europe.
This is achieved when the distance between repeater nodes
L_{0}
is around
40\text{\,}\mathrm{km}
.
While this is on the shorter side for typical long-distance fibre systems, it is manageable and could allow practical integration, especially when considering that communication over 1000 km could still be possible even for hop lengths as long as 80 km.
Finally, if near-perfect equipment such as that described by stage 3 is available, terrestrial quantum communications at arbitrary distances can be achieved, as the maximum range of our protocol is on the order of the Earth’s circumference.

To put these results into perspective, we compare them with the alternative schemes introduced in the beginning of this section.
It can be seen that the range of the protocols not using error detection, namely SEG-noED and SEG-prob, is notably shorter than SEG-ED when considering stage 2 and 3 parameters.
In stage 2, these protocols reach only up to around 700 km, and the gap between them and SEG-ED becomes even larger when we consider the parameters in stage 3.
The reason is that encoded repeaters employ a larger number of gates and memories, so more precise equipment is more beneficial to them.
As for PEG-ED, we unsurprisingly see that it outperforms our sequential protocol.
Nevertheless, it is interesting that the advantage of this resource-consuming approach over our proposal is not very large, which suggests that the two strategies might actually be very comparable in practice.
For instance, in stage 2, the range is around 1800 km for SEG-ED and 2100 km for PEG-ED at
L_{0}=$40\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$
.

It must be noted that the value of
L_{0}
that maximizes the range of the QKD application might not be the value that maximizes the secret key rate at every distance
L
.
The reason is that the range depends only on
r_{\infty}
, that is, on the quality of the distributed states, while the SKR calculation also includes the raw key bit distribution rate
R_{\text{bit}}
.
In general, one can always reduce the average waiting time at each hop by shortening
L_{0}
and, due to our pipelining strategy, this in turn can increase
R_{\text{bit}}
.
Therefore, the optimal value of
L_{0}
that maximizes the SKR is, generally, shorter than the one that maximizes
L_{\text{max}}
.

To verify the claim above, we plot the value of
K
in
Eq.

13
versus
L_{0}
at
L=$1000\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$
and
L=$1500\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$
in
Fig.

4
(b).
The upper plot in that graph, corresponding to
L=$1000\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$
, shows us that the hop length that maximizes
K
in stage 2 is now lower than the optimal values in
Fig.

4
(a), which confirms our previous explanation.
This effect is even more pronounced when focusing on stage 3, as the repeater nodes introduce even less errors and therefore it is beneficial to increase the number of nodes.
At
L=$1500\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$
, depicted in the lower graph of
Fig.

4
(b), we now see that the optimal value of
L_{0}
gets closer to that in
Fig.

4
(a), especially in the case of stage 2.
This is expected, as now shorter hop lengths would require even more intermediate nodes, and therefore their impact on the error rates increases.
Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the difference in key rate calculated at the optimum value of
L_{0}
that maximizes the range and the one that maximizes
K
is within one order of magnitude in stage 2.
This suggests that the SEG-ED strategy is not extremely sensitive to variations of
L_{0}
.
In general, we notice a trend where, as
L
gets larger, the quality of the distributed entanglement becomes more critical, causing the optimal values of
L_{0}
maximizing either metric, range or SKR, to approach each other.

The first observation that we make is that both SEG-ED and PEG-ED schemes are capable of surpassing the PLOB bound

Comparing our protocol to the alternative schemes considered here, we make the following observations.
In stage 2, only the schemes relying on error detection are able to beat the PLOB bound.
Specifically, PEG-ED slightly outperforms our protocol, SEG-ED, but the latter offers an acceptable performance up to roughly 1800 km.
Additionally, the figure shows that we can still achieve a good SKR without error detection if the distance between the users is rather short.
What is more, at such short distances, the SEG-noED scheme surpasses schemes that use encoded repeaters.
This is because it allows for a faster repetition rate, since a single entangled pair needs to be generated at each hop.
SEG-prob, on the other hand, achieves rates comparable to those of SEG-noED at very short distances, but its performance scales badly with

This finding suggests a shift in the approach to quantum network design as technology transitions from stage 2 to stage 3.

Lastly, we are interested in exploring the cost associated to the key rate achieved with the repeater protocols considered in our work.

Figure

, as defined in
Eq.

16

Once again, we set the hop length to be
L_{0}=$50\text{\,}\mathrm{km}$
.

The most notable aspect of this simulation is the relationship between the costs for the two schemes that use encoded repeaters.
From previous results, the PEG strategy consistently achieves larger secret key rates, but as we alluded to earlier, this could be at the cost of using more resources.

Figure

6
sheds some light into this issue.
In fact, our sequential scheme seems to very slightly outperform PEG-ED at some intermediate distances.
In particular, the normalized cost of SEG-ED using stage 2 hardware is smaller than that of PEG-ED when
L
is between 500 km and 1000 km.
The advantage becomes larger, and over a wider range of distances, when stage 3 hardware is considered.

This finding supports our claim that sequential swapping with error detection may allow for a more efficient resource utilization in some scenarios.
The intuition here is the following.
At short distances, the cost for both SEG-ED and PEG-ED is similar, similar to their relation in terms of
K
.
However, as the distance grows larger, the probability of detecting an error increases at all hops.
In the case of SEG-ED, this means that some distribution rounds are aborted.
Those abortions that occur towards the start of the distribution then result in resources further down the repeater chain to be temporarily released, reducing the actual cost of the repeater chain and leading to a more efficient resource utilization.
However, as
L
increases further and nears the maximum range of the system, the errors rapidly accumulate and the rate of decrease of the SKR ramps up, rapidly increasing the normalized cost
C_{K}
.
The rate of change in
K
is faster at shorter distances in SEG-ED when compared to PEG-ED due to the memory decoherence, and therefore the reduction in resource consumption cannot compensate for it.
All in all, this is a promising result and necessitates a network wide analysis to better explore the resource efficiency in quantum networks.

VI
Conclusion

In summary, we have proposed a practical quantum repeater scheme, termed SEG-ED, that relies on a sequential entanglement generation strategy combined with error detection through encoded repeaters.
The sequential approach enhances compatibility with existing classical networks, based on statistical multiplexing, but we have also shown that it may lead to a more efficient use of the resources in certain scenarios.
Furthermore, our findings suggest that this scheme may offer scalability in the near to mid-term future, even when using relatively simple error correction codes.
We have also shown that acceptable performance can be achieved when considering a realistic topology, in terms of node spacing, for the underlying structure, facilitating integration with telecommunication infrastructure.

Further investigations are needed to complete the study of SEG-ED.
Importantly, while our study has focused on a single path between two users, i.e., a repeater chain, without external interference, the main appeal of our protocol lies in its practicality for multi-user networks.
Therefore, a more advanced network-level analysis is needed to benchmark its performance under high-traffic scenarios.
In particular, such analysis would also be interesting to reveal the potential fairness and cost efficiency advantage of our protocol.
Additional aspects of the LLEG procedure, such as the trade-offs between on-demand and advanced generation models
Li et al. 2024
, remain to be explored.
Investigating more powerful error detection codes is also a promising direction, as this could enable the inclusion of more diverse noise models.
Another open question concerns the integration of our protocol into hybrid quantum networks that combine terrestrial and satellite links, which are expected to form the backbone of the quantum Internet.
Finally, platform-specific implementations should be investigated to pave the way for practical deployment.

A.1
Analysis framework

We start this by making the observation that the logical entangled state for an
n
-qubit repetition code corresponds to the
2n
-qubit GHZ state
Greenberger et al. 1989
,
\ket{\text{GHZ}_{2n}}=(\ket{0}^{\otimes 2n}+\ket{1}^{\otimes 2n})/\sqrt{2}
.
In fact, the class of entanglement
Bennett et al. 2000
;
Vidal 2000
to which the GHZ belongs is also shared by the logical entangled states under repetition codes.
Therefore, it is useful to define a basis that incorporates the GHZ state, as well as other entangled states of the same class.
Specifically, let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be defined as the following basis for
n
-qubit states:

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\left\{\Ket{\Phi_{s}^{(n)}}|\ 0\leq s\leq 2^{n}-1\right\},

(17)

with

\Ket{\Phi_{s}^{(n)}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[(-1)^{\texttt{MSB}(\mathtt{s})}\ket{\mathtt{s}}+\ket{\overline{\mathtt{s}}}\right],

(18)

where
\mathtt{s}
is the
n
-bit binary representation of
s
,
\overline{\mathtt{s}}
denotes the inverse of
\mathtt{s}
and
\texttt{MSB}(\mathtt{s})
is the most significant bit of
\mathtt{s}
.

As it turns out, many of the relevant states throughout our analysis can be written as probabilistic mixtures of pure states in the basis above when the error models in
Section

III.6
are considered.
For this reason, we introduce next the notation for a specific framework that simplifies our analyses of such probabilistic mixtures.

We say that a quantum state is diagonal in
\mathcal{B}
, or
\mathcal{B}
-diagonal, with
\mathcal{B}=\left\{\ket{b_{0}},\ket{b_{1}},\dots,\ket{b_{D-1}}\right\}
a basis for a Hilbert space of dimension
D
, if it can be expressed as a probabilistic mixture of the basis states in
\mathcal{B}
.
That is, a quantum state with density matrix
\rho
is
\mathcal{B}
-diagonal if
\rho
can be written as:

\rho=\sum_{s=0}^{D-1}\lambda_{s}\Ket{b_{s}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{b_{s}},

(19)

with
\lambda_{s}\geq 0
and
\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}=1
.

Alternatively, we can describe a
\mathcal{B}
-diagonal quantum state using a
D
-element vector representation
\vec{\Lambda}
, such that:

\vec{\Lambda}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{0}&\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{D-1}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},

(20)

where the elements
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\dots
, correspond to the weights of the sum in
Eq.

19
, that is,
\lambda_{s}=\bra{b_{s}}\rho\ket{b_{s}}
.

Some quantum operations can be conveniently represented by taking advantage of the vector notation above.
Consider, for instance, an arbitrary quantum operation
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{M}}
, and bases
\mathcal{B}=\{\ket{b_{0}},\ket{b_{1}},\dots,\ket{b_{D_{B}-1}}\}
and
\mathcal{V}=\{\ket{v_{0}},\ket{v_{1}},\dots,\ket{v_{D_{V}-1}}\}
, each corresponding to a Hilbert space with dimension
D_{B}
and
D_{V}
, respectively.
Suppose that, when
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{M}}
is applied to a basis state in
\mathcal{B}
, it outputs a
\mathcal{V}
-diagonal state.
Then, it is clear that the output of this operation acting on a
\mathcal{B}
-diagonal state should be
\mathcal{V}
-diagonal.
In fact, we have that, for an input with a vector representation
\vec{\Lambda}_{B}
with respect to
\mathcal{B}
, the vector representation of the output is:

\vec{\Lambda}_{V}=\mathrm{M}\vec{\Lambda}_{B},

(21)

where
\vec{\Lambda}_{V}
is the vector representation of the output with respect to
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathrm{M}
is a linear operator with the following matrix representation:

\mathrm{M}=\begin{bmatrix}m_{0,0}&m_{0,1}&\dots&m_{0,D_{B}-1}\\
m_{1,0}&m_{1,1}&\dots&m_{1,D_{B}-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
m_{D_{V}-1,0}&m_{D_{V}-1,1}&\dots&m_{D_{V}-1,D_{B}-1}\end{bmatrix},

(22)

for

m_{i,j}=\bra{v_{i}}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{M}}(\ket{b_{j}}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{b_{j}})\ket{v_{i}},

(23)

where the condition
\sum_{i}m_{i,j}\leq 1
must be met, with equality for all
j
when the operation is trace-preserving.

A.2
Error models under the new framework

We focus now on rewriting our error models using more appropriate notation for the framework introduced in the previous section.
Firstly, let us define the bit-flip and phase-flip noisy channels, which will be a useful building block in the remainder of the appendix.
We have that, for some quantum state with density matrix
\rho
, the bit-flip (phase-flip) channel acting on system
\mathsf{Q}
with a probability
p
, denoted by
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{X}({p})}_{\mathsf{Q}}
(
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{Z}({p})}_{\mathsf{Q}}
), induces the following transformation:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{X}({p})}_{q}(\rho)&=(1-p)\rho+p\mathrm{X}_{q}\rho,\\
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{Z}({p})}_{q}(\rho)&=(1-p)\rho+p\mathrm{Z}_{q}\rho,\end{split}

(24)

Immediately, we make the observation that these noise channels are useful to model the errors in our measurements.
Specifically, it turns out that a noisy measurement in the
\mathcal{Z}
(
\mathcal{X}
), with error probability
\delta
, has the same measurement statistics as a bit-flip (phase-flip) channel followed by the ideal measurement in that basis.
For instance, the POVM element corresponding to measuring
\ket{0}
with error probability
\delta
is:

\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{0}=(1-\delta)\ket{0}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{0}+\delta\ket{1}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{1}.

(25)

Therefore, the probability of obtaining this result when measuring qubit
\mathsf{Q}
in a quantum system with density matrix
\rho
can be written as:

\begin{split}\trace\left\{\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{0}\rho\right\}&=(1-\delta)\bra{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}\rho\ket{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}+\delta\bra{1}_{\mathsf{Q}}\rho\ket{1}_{\mathsf{Q}}=(1-\delta)\bra{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}\rho\ket{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}+\delta\bra{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\rho\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\ket{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}\\
&=\bra{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{X}({\delta})}(\rho)\ket{0}_{\mathsf{Q}}=\trace\left\{\mathrm{P}_{0}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{X}({\delta})}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\rho)\right\},\end{split}

(26)

where
\mathrm{P}_{0}
is the ideal projector
\ket{0}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{0}
.
Similar results can be trivially obtained for measuring in the
\mathcal{X}
basis.

We make an additional observation regarding the bit-flip and phase-flip channels.
We see that the output of Pauli-X and Pauli-Z operations on a basis state in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
corresponds to a different state in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
Therefore, for diagonal states in these bases, we can describe the action of the noise channels with a single linear operator.
Specifically, given a
\mathcal{G}_{n}
-diagonal quantum state with vector representation
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
, the vector representation of the output of the bit-flip (phase-flip) channel acting on system
\mathsf{Q}
with probability
p
,
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{bf}}
(
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{pf}}
) is:

\begin{split}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{bf}}&=(1-p)\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}+p\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}=\left[(1-p)\mathds{I}+p\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\right]\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}},\\
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{pf}}&=(1-p)\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}+p\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}=\left[(1-p)\mathds{I}+p\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}\right]\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}},\end{split}

(27)

where
\mathds{I}
is the identity operator, and
\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}
(
\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}
) denotes the linear operator representing the Pauli-X (Pauli-Z) on
\mathsf{Q}
from
\mathcal{G}_{n}
-diagonal to
\mathcal{G}_{n}
states.
An equivalent result can be found for the computational basis.

We now move on to the quantum depolarizing channel.
We have that the action of a depolarizing channel with probability
p
on single qubit
\mathsf{Q}
of a system with density matrix
\rho
can be written as:

\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({p})}_{\mathsf{Q}}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+\frac{p}{4}(\mathds{I}+\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}+\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}+\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}})\rho(\mathds{I}+\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}+\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}+\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}).

(28)

Taking into account our previous observation, it is easy to see that the output of depolarizing channel acting on a state in the
\mathcal{G}_{n}
(computational) basis will be diagonal in the same basis.
Specifically, provided that the vector representation of the state in that basis be
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
, the vector representation of the output can be obtained as:

\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{dp}}=\left[(1-p)\mathds{I}+p\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{Q}}\right]\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}},

(29)

where we define the linear operator describing the depolarization of qubit
\mathsf{Q}
as:

\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{Q}}=\frac{\left(\mathds{I}+\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{Q}}\right)\left(\mathds{I}+\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{Q}}\right)}{4}.

(30)

Importantly, the single-qubit quantum depolarizing channel is useful to us as a means to describe the effects of memory decoherence.
In particular, provided some qubits of a state have been stored in quantum memories for some time
\tau
, the resulting state can be obtained considering the action of a depolarizing channel
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({\gamma_{\tau}})}
on each of these qubits.
Nevertheless, we need to extend the definition in order to model the errors in two-qubit gates.
We define a multi-qubit depolarizing channel with probability
p
, acting on
k
systems
\mathsf{Q_{1}Q_{2}\dots\mathsf{Q}_{k}}
such that, with probability
(1-p)
, the initial state is left intact and, with probability
p
, the systems
\mathsf{Q_{1}Q_{2}\dots}
all suffer joint depolarization.
Specifically, the action of such a noise channel,
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({p})}_{\mathsf{Q}_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{2}\dots\mathsf{Q}_{k}}
, on a state with density matrix
\rho
, can be written as:

\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({p})}_{\mathsf{Q}_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{2}\dots\mathsf{Q}_{k}}(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\left[\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({1})}_{\mathsf{Q}_{1}}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({1})}_{\mathsf{Q}_{2}}\circ\dots\circ\mathsf{Q}_{k}\right](\rho),

(31)

with
\circ
denoting composition.

It is easy to see that, for the case of a diagonal state, the composition operation above can be reduced to a simple matrix multiplication on the corresponding linear operators.
That is, the action of the same channel on a diagonal state with vector representation
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
can be rewritten as:

\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({p})}_{\mathsf{Q}_{1}\mathsf{Q}_{2}\dots\mathsf{Q}_{k}}(\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}})=\left[(1-p)\mathds{I}+p\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{D}_{i}\right]\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}},

(32)

where
\mathds{I}
is the identity operator in the joint basis of the quantum state, the operators
\mathrm{D}_{i}
correspond to the depolarizing operators in each qubit
i
for their corresponding quantum bases.

Now that we have defined the multi-qubit quantum depolarizing channel, we can rewrite the model for noisy two-qubit gates.
Specifically, we focus on the CNOT gate, since this gate is sufficient to implement every circuit that we consider in this appendix.
Let us, then, consider that we apply some CNOT gate to a quantum state described by density matrix
\rho
using system
\mathsf{C}
as the control and system
\mathsf{T}
as the target.
Then, we can rewrite the transformation of the noisy gate,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CX}}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}
, in terms of the noise channels introduced above.
Specifically, we write the following:

\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CX}}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}(\rho)=(1-\beta)\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}\rho\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}+\beta\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({1})}_{\mathsf{CT}}(\rho),

(33)

where
\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}
represents the ideal operator of the CNOT gate and
\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({1})}_{\mathsf{CT}}
is the multi-qubit depolarizing channel on systems
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{T}
as defined in
Eq.

31
.

We use dashed borders to represent general (non-unitary) quantum operations.
Additionally, the black dot in the noisy CNOT operation (first diagram to the left) represents the control qubit.

We make two final observations regarding the model above.
Firstly, we notice that any unitary operation, say
\mathrm{U}
, acting on a maximally mixed state (that is, a state whose density matrix is proportional to the identity) results in another maximally mixed state, since
\mathrm{U}\mathds{I}\mathrm{U}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathrm{U}\mathrm{U}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathds{I}
.
Secondly, we also notice that any unitary operation preceding the complete depolarization of the quantum system it affects also has no effect.
Therefore, the noisy CNOT gate can be expressed in terms of the ideal CNOT operator and a two-qubit depolarizing channel in two different ways, depicted in
Fig.

7
.
Formally, we can rewrite the quantum operation
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CX}}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}
as:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{CX}}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}(\rho)&=\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({\beta})}\big(\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}\rho\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}\big)\\
&=\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({\beta})}(\rho)\mathrm{C}_{{\mathsf{C}}\to{\mathsf{T}}}.\end{split}

(34)

Conveniently,

It is easy to see that
\ket{0_{L}}
is a

-diagonal state.

\rho_{g_{j}}

The encoding procedure comprises three remote CNOT gates.
Each of these uses the entanglement between a pair of communication qubits
\mathsf{a_{j}b_{j}}
to perform an effective CNOT gate between two data qubits
\mathsf{A_{j}}
and
\mathsf{B_{j}}
, with the former as control.
This is implemented with two CNOT gates, followed by the measurement of the communication qubits in the
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Z}
bases, as shown in
Fig.

8
(b).
The result of this measurements is then used to adjust the Pauli frame, which completes the operation.

Considering our error models, we can express each noisy CNOT gates as a quantum depolarizing channel followed by the ideal gate.
Then, we notice that the effect of an error on the measurement in the
\mathcal{X}
(
\mathcal{Z}
) results in a phase-flip (bit-flip) in a data qubit of the same row.
Overall, we can model the operation of the encoding circuit using three building blocks, which we depict in
Fig.

8
(c) for a single row:
(1) a quantum operation describing the depolarizing effects due to gate errors,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{F}_{\text{ENC}}}
, which is characterized by the probability
\beta
;
(2) the ideal circuit for the remote CNOT,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{G}_{\text{ENC}}}
, including the measurements and adjustments, which we can express as controlled quantum operations as shown in
Fig.

8
(c);
and (3) a quantum operation containing the phase-flips and bit-flips which might occur due to measurement errors,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}}
, as determined by the value of
\delta
.

It is straightforward to derive the operator for the first operation in the encoding procedure,
\mathrm{F}_{\text{ENC}}
, expressed in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{ENC}}
as defined in
Eq.

38
.
Specifically, the operator can be computed as:

\mathrm{F}_{\text{ENC}}=\prod_{j=1}^{3}\left[(1-\beta)\mathds{I}+\beta\left(\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{A_{j}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{3}}\otimes\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{a_{j}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}}\right)\right]\left[(1-\beta)\mathds{I}+\beta\left(\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{B_{j}}}^{\mathcal{Z}_{3}}\otimes\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{b_{j}}}^{\mathcal{G}_{2}}\right)\right],

(40)

where we use superscripts in the linear operators representing the depolarization to indicate that they are acting on different bases.

As we have explained, the ideal action of the remote CNOT gate is to perform an effective CNOT between the data qubits, after which the communication qubits are consumed.
That is, provided an arbitrary basis state in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{ENC}}
, we have that the output will be a state in
\mathcal{G}_{6}
.
We define
\mathrm{G}_{\text{ENC}}
as the operator corresponding to the transformation
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{G}_{\text{ENC}}}
, mapping
\mathcal{B}_{\text{ENC}}
-diagonal states to
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal states.
This operator is trivial to derive from the circuit representation in
Fig.

8
(c), using the definition in
Eq.

23
.

Moving on to the third part in the encoding procedure, we have that the state after
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{G}_{\text{ENC}}}
is
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal.
Since this section of our model is composed only by bit-flip and phase-flip channels, we have that its corresponding linear operator,
\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}
, is an operator from
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal to
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal states.
This operator may be computed as:

\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}=\prod_{j=1}^{3}\left[(1-\delta)\mathds{I}+\delta\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{A_{j}}}\right]\left[(1-\delta)\mathds{I}+\delta\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{B_{j}}}\right].

(41)

Finally, provided the input to the encoding procedure is a
\mathcal{B}_{\text{ENC}}
-diagonal state with representation
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
, the vector representation of the
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal output state is:

\vec{\Lambda}_{0}=\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}\mathrm{G}_{\text{ENC}}\mathrm{F}_{\text{ENC}}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}.

(42)

A.4
Memory decoherence

We explain in
Section

III.5
how Alice measures her data qubits as soon as possible.
That is, the operation
\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}
acts only on the data qubits of the intermediate nodes.
Specifically, we consider that the storage time corresponds to the average waiting time at each hop,
{\tau}_{\text{hop}}
, and therefore each qubit decoheres independently with some probability
\gamma_{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}}
.

As we mentioned in
Section

A.2
, quantum depolarizing channels acting on a
\mathcal{G}_{n}
-diagonal state can be described using a
\mathcal{G}_{n}
to
\mathcal{G}_{n}
operator.
In this specific case, we define
\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}
as the linear operator from
\mathcal{G}_{6}
- to
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal states describing the overall decoherence operation.
This operator can be computed as:

\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}=\prod_{i=1}^{3}\left[(1-\gamma_{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}})\mathds{I}+\gamma_{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}}\mathrm{D}_{3+i}\right].

(43)

The result of this measurements, say
r

Since we are considering that the distribution is aborted upon the detection of an error, we can focus on analyzing the results
r

For simplicity, we consider our reference result to be that corresponding to obtaining

\mathcal{Z}
, and
\ket{+}
in those in the
\mathcal{X}
basis, since this result allows us to disregard the adjustment of the Pauli frame.
We shall see that state after the
i
-th swapping procedure can be obtained as:

\rho_{i}=\frac{\trace_{\mathsf{BC}}\left\{\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}\left(\rho_{\text{LSWP},{i}}\right)\right\}}{p_{\text{ref},{i}}},

(44)

where
\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}
is to the noisy measurement operator corresponding to the reference measurement,

\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}
denotes the transformation applied by the noisy CNOT gates in
Fig.

9
(b),

\rho_{\text{LSWP},{i}}=\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}(\rho_{i-1})\otimes\rho_{0}
is the input to the
i
-th swapping procedure,
and

is the probability of obtaining the reference measurement result in the said procedure, specifically:

p_{\text{ref},{i}}=\trace\left\{\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}\left(\rho_{\text{LSWP},{i}}\right)\right\}.

(45)

We have learned from

and
Section

A.4
that the states shared between Alice and
\mathsf{R}_{i}
, and between
\mathsf{R}_{i}
and
\mathsf{R}_{i+1}
, can each be written as a probabilistic mixture of basis states in
\mathcal{G}_{6}
.
Therefore, we can express
\rho_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
as a probabilistic mixture of states in the quantum basis
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LSWP}}=\mathcal{G}_{6}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{6}
.
Specifically, the vector representation of
\rho_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LSWP}}
,
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
, is computed as:

\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{LSWP},{i}}=\left(\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}\vec{\Lambda}_{i-1}\right)\otimes\vec{\Lambda}_{0}.

(46)

Importantly, both
\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}
act on the systems in
\mathsf{R}_{i}
, that is,
\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{C}
.
Therefore, we are interested in rewriting the basis states in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LSWP}}
in a more convenient format for joint operations in these qubits.
With this goal in mind, we make the following observation.
Let
a,b,c,d\in[0,7]
, and
s:=512a+64b+8c+a
.
Then, we can prove that:

\begin{split}\Ket{\Phi_{(8a+b)}^{(6)}}_{\mathsf{A}\mathsf{B}}\otimes\Ket{\Phi_{(8c+d)}^{(6)}}_{\mathsf{C}\mathsf{D}}=\frac{(-1)^{\varphi(s)}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{4}(-1)^{\psi_{k}(s)}\Ket{\Phi_{n_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}_{\mathsf{AD}}\otimes\Ket{\Phi_{m_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}_{\mathsf{BC}},\end{split}

(47)

where
\varphi(s)\in[0,1]
is some unimportant global phase,
\psi_{k}(s)\in[0,1]
and

\begin{split}n_{1}(s)&=\begin{cases}8a+d&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(c-\frac{7}{2}\right)>0,\\
63-8a-d&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(c-\frac{7}{2}\right)<0,\end{cases}\\
n_{2}(s)&=\begin{cases}8a+7-d&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(c-\frac{7}{2}\right)>0,\\
56-8a+d&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(c-\frac{7}{2}\right)<0,\end{cases}\\
n_{3}(s)&=63-n_{2}(s),\\
n_{4}(s)&=63-n_{1}(s),\end{split}\qquad\begin{split}m_{1}(s)&=\begin{cases}8b+c&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{7}{2}\right)>0,\\
63-8b-c&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{7}{2}\right)<0,\end{cases},\\
m_{2}(s)&=\begin{cases}8b+7-c&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{7}{2}\right)>0,\\
56-8b+c&\text{if }\left(a-\frac{7}{2}\right)\left(b-\frac{7}{2}\right)<0,\end{cases}\\
m_{3}(s)&=63-m_{2}(s),\\
m_{4}(s)&=63-m_{1}(s).\end{split}

(48)

Taking this into account, we can rewrite
Eq.

44
as:

\rho_{i}=\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{4095}\lambda^{\text{LSWP},i}_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\sum_{l=1}^{4}\Ket{\Phi_{n_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{n_{l}(s)}^{(6)}}\trace\left\{\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}\left(\Ket{\Phi_{m_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{m_{l}(s)}^{(6)}}\right)\right\}}{4p_{\text{ref},{i}}},

(49)

where
\lambda^{\text{LSWP},i}_{s}
represents the
s
-th element of
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
.

It can be shown that any cross-product,
\trace\left\{\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}\left(\Ket{\Phi_{m_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{m_{l}(s)}^{(6)}}\right)\right\}
for
k\neq l
, is equal to 0.
Therefore, we rewrite the equation above once again as:

\rho_{i}=\frac{\sum_{s=0}^{4095}\sum_{k=1}^{4}\lambda^{\text{LSWP},i}_{s}q_{s|k}\Ket{\Phi_{n_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{n_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}}{p_{\text{ref},{i}}},

(50)

with

where we define

as:

q_{s|k}:=\frac{1}{4}\trace\left\{\mathrm{P}_{\text{ref}}\mathcal{E}^{\text{CNOTS}}\left(\Ket{\Phi_{m_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{m_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}\right)\right\}.

(52)

It is clear, therefore, that the probability
p_{\text{ref},{i}}
may be computed as a vector product, such that:

p_{\text{ref},{i}}=\sum_{k=1}^{4}\vec{Q}_{\text{LSWP},k}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{LSWP},{i}},

(53)

where we define
\vec{Q}_{\text{LSWP},k}

\vec{Q}_{\text{LSWP},k}:=\begin{bmatrix}q_{0|k}&q_{1|k}&\dots&q_{4095|k}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(54)

What is more, it turns out that we can also compute the vector representation
\vec{\Lambda}_{i}
from
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{LSWP},{i}}
.
Specifically, we have that:

\vec{\Lambda}_{i}=\frac{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{4}\mathrm{N}_{\text{LSWP},k}\diagonal\left\{\vec{Q}_{\text{LSWP},k}\right\}\right)\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{LSWP},{i}}}{p_{\text{ref},{i}}},

(55)

where
\diagonal\{\vec{v}\}
represents a matrix with
\vec{v}
as its main diagonal and 0 in all other elements, and
\mathrm{N}_{\text{LSWP},k}
is a matrix operator defined as:

\mathrm{N}_{\text{LSWP},k}:=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{\Lambda}_{n_{k}(0)}&\vec{\Lambda}_{n_{k}(1)}&\dots&\vec{\Lambda}_{n_{k}(4095)}\end{bmatrix},

(56)

with
\vec{\Lambda}_{n_{k}(s)}
denoting the vector representation of
\ket{\Phi_{n_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}
in
\mathcal{G}_{6}
.

Similarly, we can define the vector representation of
\ket{\Phi_{m_{k}(s)}^{(6)}}
in
\mathcal{G}_{6}
as
\vec{\Lambda}_{m_{k}(s)}
.
Then, to derive the values of
q_{s|k}
, we can focus on exploring the effect of the swapping circuit when applied to
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal states, and apply this knowledge to
\vec{\Lambda}_{m_{k}(s)}
.
Specifically, we can model noisy operations in
Fig.

9
(b) by substituting the noisy CNOT gates by depolarizing channels followed by ideal CNOTs, and the noisy measurements in
\mathcal{Z}
(
\mathcal{X}
) by bit-flip (phase-flip) channels followed by the ideal measurements, as depicted in
Fig.

9
(c).
Specifically, we express the circuit in four stages:
(1) a quantum operation describing the depolarizing effects due to gate errors,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}}
, which is characterized by the probability
\beta
;
(2) the ideal operator for the CNOT gates,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{G}_{\text{LSWP}}}
;
(3) a quantum operation containing the phase-flips and bit-flips which might occur due to measurement errors,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}}
, as determined by the value of
\delta
;
and (4) the ideal measurement of the reference result.

The action of the first operation,
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}}
, on a
\mathcal{G}_{6}
can be described by the following linear operator:

\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}=\prod_{j=1}^{3}\left[(1-\beta)\mathds{I}+\beta\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{B}_{j}}\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{C}_{j}}\right].

(57)

In regards to the second operation, we observe that the output of
\mathcal{E}^{\mathrm{G}_{\text{LSWP}}}
when applied to a
\mathcal{G}_{6}
-diagonal state is a probabilistic mixture of states in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LBSM}}
, where we have defined the quantum basis as
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LBSM}}:=\mathcal{G}_{3}\otimes\mathcal{Z}_{3}
.
Conveniently, by considering an appropriate ordering of the states in
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LBSM}}
, we can write the vector representation of the state after this stage using the same vector as for the stage’s input.

After that, we may obtain the final vector representation before measurement by applying
\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}
, the linear operator on
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LBSM}}
that describes measurement errors.
This operator is computed as:

\mathrm{E}_{\text{ENC}}=\prod_{j=1}^{3}\left[(1-\delta)\mathds{I}+\delta\mathrm{X}_{B_{j}}^{\mathcal{G}_{3}}\right]\left[(1-\delta)\mathds{I}+\delta\mathrm{Z}_{C_{j}}^{\mathcal{Z}_{3}}\right],

(58)

where we have used the
\mathcal{G}_{3}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{3}
superscripts to remark that the operators corresponding to the errors are expressed in different bases.

Finally, to compute the probability that we obtain the reference result when measuring some arbitrary
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LBSM}}
-diagonal state, we can use the fact that a state in
\mathcal{G}_{3}
can be rewritten in terms of the joint basis
\mathcal{X}_{3}
, such that:

\Ket{\Phi_{(4x+2y+z)}^{(3)}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{H}^{\otimes 3}\left(\mathds{I}+\mathrm{Z}_{2}\mathrm{Z}_{3}+\mathrm{Z}_{1}\mathrm{Z}_{3}+\mathrm{Z}_{1}\mathrm{Z}_{2}\right)\ket{\mathtt{xyz}},

(59)

where
x,y,z\in[0,1]
,
\mathtt{xyz}
is the three-bit binary representation of
4x+2y+z
,
\mathrm{H}^{\otimes 3}
denotes a Hadamard gate acting on every qubit and
\mathrm{Z}_{q}
denotes a Pauli-Z gate acting on the
q
-th qubit.

Following the above observation, we define
\vec{P}_{\text{ref}}
as:

\vec{P}_{\text{ref}}:=\frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&1&0&1&1&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0&0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},

(60)

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

Putting everything together, we find that:

q_{s|k}=\vec{P}_{\text{ref}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{E}_{\text{LSWP}}\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}\vec{\Lambda}_{m_{k}(s)},

(61)

and therefore we can compute
\vec{Q}_{\text{LSWP},k}
as:

\vec{Q}_{\text{LSWP},k}=\vec{P}_{\text{ref}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathrm{E}_{\text{LSWP}}\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}\mathrm{M}_{\text{LSWP},k},

(62)

with

\mathrm{M}_{\text{LSWP},k}:=\frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix}\vec{\Lambda}_{m_{k}(0)}&\vec{\Lambda}_{m_{k}(1)}&\dots&\vec{\Lambda}_{m_{k}(4095)}\end{bmatrix}.

(63)

B.1
SEG-noED: unencoded chain with gate-based swapping

In this section we discuss our analysis of the SEG-noED protocol, which implements sequential swapping using gate-based BSMs without error detection.
Since this protocol does not require an encoding procedure, the vector representation of the
\mathcal{G}_{2}
-diagonal quantum state at the first hop,
\vec{\Lambda}_{0}
, can be computed as:

\vec{\Lambda}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}F_{0}&\frac{1-F_{0}}{3}&\frac{1-F_{0}}{3}&\frac{1-F_{0}}{3}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(67)

The memory decoherence operation, on the other hand, corresponds to a single-qubit quantum depolarizing channel.
Therefore, the linear operator describing this process,
\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}
, is:

\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}=(1-\gamma_{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}})\mathds{I}+\gamma_{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}}\mathrm{D}_{2},

(68)

where we remind the reader that the average waiting time at each hop is computed with respect to the number of entangled pairs generated,
{\tau}_{\text{hop}}={\tau}_{g_{n_{\text{code}}}}={\tau}_{g_{1}}
.

Figure 11:

Standard quantum circuit for a gate-based BSM, used in protocol SEG-noED.
(a) State before the
i
-th swapping procedure in the virtual PEG-ED protocol where Alice delays her QKD measurement until the end of the round.
Here, squares denote data qubits and twisted lines denote entanglement.
(b) Ideal circuit.
(c) Model for the noisy circuit.
In all figures, dotted lines represent physical separation between distant nodes.

After the necessary entanglement has been distributed to the next hop (see
Fig.

11
(a)), the current repeater node executes the swapping operation.
In SEG-noED, this is implemented using a CNOT gate, followed by measurements in the
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Z}
bases to determine a Pauli-frame adjustment, as depicted in
Fig.

11
(b).

Importantly, since this protocol does not use error detection, the probability that the swapping operation is successful is 1, that is,
p_{\text{LSWP},{i}}=1
for
1\leq i\leq N_{r}
.
The implication of this is that the swapping circuit in
Fig.

11
is a trace-preserving operation.
In fact, we have that the vector representation of the
\mathcal{G}_{2}
-diagonal state after the
i
-th swapping along the chain can be computed as:

\vec{\Lambda}_{i}=\mathrm{S}_{\text{LSWP}}\left(\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}\vec{\Lambda}_{i-1}\otimes\vec{\Lambda}_{0}\right),

(69)

where
\mathrm{S}_{\text{LSWP}}
is the linear operator that represents the swapping procedure.

We now derive the expression for
\mathrm{S}_{\text{LSWP}}
.
Firstly, we make the observation that the depolarization of one of the two qubits in a Bell state (this is, a state in
\mathcal{G}_{2}
) leads to the depolarization of the entire Bell state.
That is, for some two-qubit system in a Bell state, e.g.,
\ket{\Phi^{+}}=\ket{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}
, the depolarization of its, say, first qubit has the following effect:

\mathcal{E}^{\textrm{D}({1})}_{1}\left(\Ket{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}\right)=\trace_{1}\left\{\Ket{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}\hskip-2.84526pt\Bra{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}\right\}\otimes\frac{\mathds{I}_{1}}{2}=\frac{\mathds{I}_{2}}{2}\otimes\frac{\mathds{I}_{1}}{2}=\frac{\mathds{I}_{12}}{4},

(70)

where
\mathds{I}_{1}/2
,
\mathds{I}_{2}/2
and
\mathds{I}_{12}/4
represent the maximally mixed state in the first, second and both qubits, respectively.

From the observation above, it can be deduced that a depolarizing error in the initial CNOT gate of the swapping circuit would result in the entire joint state to depolarize.
Since the swapping procedure acting on a maximally mixed state results simply in another maximally mixed state, we can model the noisy CNOT gate with an ideal gate at the input of the circuit, plus a quantum depolarizing channel with probability
\beta
at its output, as we draw in
Fig.

11
(c).
Errors in the measurements, on the other hand, lead to bit-flips or phase-flips, and can therefore be modeled as such noise channels.
Finally, the classically controlled operations can be substituted by controlled quantum gates, which with the noise channels above result in the model depicted in
Fig.

11
(c).

Taking into account the considerations above and the model from
Fig.

11
(c), we can split the circuit in three main parts:
(1) the ideal swapping circuit, which can be described by the linear operator
\mathrm{G}_{\text{LSWP}}
from
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LSWP}}
-diagonal states to
\mathcal{G}_{2}
-diagonal states,
where
\mathcal{B}_{\text{LSWP}}=\mathcal{G}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{G}_{2}
is the input basis to the swap procedure;
(2) the noise channels for the measurement errors, determined by linear operator
\mathrm{E}_{\text{LSWP}}
;
and (3) a quantum depolarizing channel which corresponds to the error in the noisy CNOT gate, with linear operator
\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}
.
That is, we have that:

\mathrm{S}_{\text{LSWP}}=\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}\mathrm{E}_{\text{LSWP}}\mathrm{G}_{\text{LSWP}}.

(71)

The expression for
\mathrm{G}_{\text{LSWP}}
can be trivially derived from the circuit diagram in
Fig.

11
(c), while the operators for the other two parts are:

\begin{split}\mathrm{E}_{\text{LSWP}}&=\left[(1-\delta)\mathds{I}+\delta\mathrm{Z}_{\mathsf{D}}\right]\left[(1-\delta)\mathds{I}+\delta\mathrm{X}_{\mathsf{D}}\right].\\
\mathrm{F}_{\text{LSWP}}&=(1-\beta)\mathds{I}+\beta\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{A}}\mathrm{D}_{\mathsf{D}},\end{split}

(72)

Finally, the error rates
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{Z}}}
and
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{X}}}
can be computed from the density matrix of the end-to-end entangled state
\rho_{\text{end}}
as
Jing et al. 2020
:

\begin{split}e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{Z}}}=\>&\left[(1-\delta)^{2}+\delta^{2}\right]\left(\Bra{\Phi_{1}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{1}^{(2)}}+\Bra{\Phi_{2}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{2}^{(2)}}\right)\\
+&2\delta(1-\delta)\left(\Bra{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}+\Bra{\Phi_{3}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{3}^{(2)}}\right),\\
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{X}}}=\>&\left[(1-\delta)^{2}+\delta^{2}\right]\left(\Bra{\Phi_{2}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{2}^{(2)}}+\Bra{\Phi_{3}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{3}^{(2)}}\right)\\
+&2\delta(1-\delta)\left(\Bra{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{0}^{(2)}}+\Bra{\Phi_{1}^{(2)}}\rho_{\text{end}}\Ket{\Phi_{1}^{(2)}}\right).\end{split}

(73)

Since we have computed the vector representation of the end-to-end state,
\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{end}}=\vec{\Lambda}_{N_{r}}
, we can now compute the error rates as:

\begin{split}e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{Z}}}&=\mathrm{S}_{\text{QKD},\mathcal{Z}}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{end}},\\
e_{\mathcal{\mathcal{X}}}&=\mathrm{S}_{\text{QKD},\mathcal{X}}^{\mathsf{T}}\vec{\Lambda}_{\text{end}},\end{split}

(74)

where

\begin{split}\mathrm{S}_{\text{QKD},\mathcal{Z}}=\begin{bmatrix}2\delta(1-\delta)&(1-\delta)^{2}+\delta^{2}&(1-\delta)^{2}+\delta^{2}&2\delta(1-\delta)\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}},\\
\mathrm{S}_{\text{QKD},\mathcal{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}2\delta(1-\delta)&2\delta(1-\delta)&(1-\delta)^{2}+\delta^{2}&(1-\delta)^{2}+\delta^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.\end{split}

(75)

B.2
SEG-prob: unencoded chain with probabilistic swapping

Similarly to the SEG-noED, analyzed in the previous section, the SEG-prob protocol generates and swaps entanglement sequentially without considering error detection.
However, the swapping is now implemented using linear optics.
Specifically, we consider that the BSM relies on two-photon interference
Simon and Irvine 2003
;
Simon et al. 2007
;
Moehring et al. 2007
;
Bernien et al. 2012
, this is, two single-photon detectors are used.
In such setup, both detectors need to “click” at once to herald the success of the BSM, which may only detect two out of the four Bell states.
Therefore, the probability of success in the swap is

p_{\text{LSWP},{i}}=\frac{\eta_{D}^{2}}{2},

(76)

for
1\leq i\leq N_{r}
.

Here, we consider that, despite its probabilistic nature, the swap procedure does not introduce any errors.
This key difference with other protocols motivates us to use a different framework for our analysis.
In particular, we note that a Werner state with density matrix
\rho_{W}
and fidelity
F
may be rewritten in terms of the Werner parameter
w
as:

\rho_{W}=w\ket{\Phi^{+}}\hskip-2.84526pt\bra{\Phi^{+}}+(1-w)\frac{\mathds{I}_{4}}{4},

(77)

where the parameter
w
is:

w=\frac{4F-1}{3}.

(78)

Using this alternative formulation, we first remark again that a Werner state remains Werner after memory decoherence.
In particular, for an initial Werner parameter
w
, the new Werner parameter after one of the qubits is stored for a time
\tau
is
\gamma_{\tau}w
.
What is more, it is easy to see that swapping two Werner states using an ideal circuit, that is, a circuit which introduced no errors, results in another Werner state with a Werner parameter defined as the product of the Werner parameters of the inputs.
With this observations, and reflecting on the recursive expression in
Eq.

7
, we can deduce that the end-to-end state
\rho_{\text{end}}
is also a Werner state, whose Werner parameter
w_{\text{end}}
is:

w_{\text{end}}=(w_{0}\gamma_{{\tau}_{\text{hop}}})^{N_{r}}.

(79)

From the result above, it is trivial to obtain the error rates using either
Eq.

73
or
Eq.

74
.

B.3
PEG-ED: parallel entanglement generation with error detection

In order to compare the SEG-ED chain to a non-sequential strategy, we have introduced PEG-ED as a PEG protocol using the same error decoding techniques as SEG-ED.
Specifically, we assume that the LLEG procedure is initiated at the same time at every hop, and that it is completed after a constant wait
{\tau}_{\text{hop}}
every round, minimizing decoherence.
It is easy to see that, in this case, the analysis of the error rates follows that in
Appendix

A
, with the only difference being the decoherence suffered between each hop.
Specifically, we consider that
\mathcal{E}^{\text{MEM}}(\rho_{i})=\rho_{i}
, that is,
\mathrm{S}_{\text{MEM}}=\mathds{I}^{\mathcal{G}_{6}}
.

Naturally, this assumption is not realistic at all.
In a practical network, the LLEG procedure will finish at different times at every hop.
Moreover, even if we assume a swap-as-soon-as-possible approach, the specific order in which logical entanglement is generated across the chain may affect the end rate
Kamin et al. 2023
.
What is more, the raw key bit distribution rate would also be affected, as the next round cannot be initiated until the completion of the previous one has been heralded.

Nevertheless, we consider this highly simplified scenario to give us an idea of the upper bound, in terms of performance, that the SEG-ED protocol could aim at.
In fact, if considering a more practical analysis of the PEG strategy, it is to be expected that the two protocols would show much more similar results, with SEG-ED possibly surpassing the key rate of PEG-ED in some regimes of parameters.
