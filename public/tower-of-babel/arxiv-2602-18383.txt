Design-based inference for generalized causal effects in randomized experiments
Xinyuan Chen, Fan Li

Abstract
Generalized causal effect estimands, including the Mann-Whitney parameter and causal net benefit, provide flexible summaries of treatment effects in randomized experiments with non-Gaussian or multivariate outcomes. We develop a unified design-based inference framework for regression adjustment and variance estimation of a broad class of generalized causal effect estimands defined through pairwise contrast functions. Leveraging the theory of U-statistics and finite-population asymptotics, we establish the consistency and asymptotic normality of regression estimators constructed from individual pairs and per-unit pair averages, even when the working models are misspecified. Consequently, these estimators are model-assisted rather than model-based. In contrast to classical average treatment effect estimands, we show that for nonlinear contrast functions, covariate adjustment preserves consistency but does not admit a universal efficiency guarantee. For inference, we demonstrate that standard heteroskedasticity-robust and cluster-robust variance estimators are generally inconsistent in this setting. As a remedy, we prove that a complete two-way cluster-robust variance estimator, which fully accounts for pairwise dependence and reverse comparisons, is consistent.



Keywords:
design-based inference, finite-population central limit theorem, pairwise comparisons, randomized experiments, U-statistics

Introduction

Randomized experiments increasingly evaluate treatments using complex outcomes, including ordinal responses
(
20
)
, prioritized composite endpoints
(
12
)
, multivariate clinical measures
(
13
)
, and outcomes for which distributional shifts are more meaningful than mean differences
(
4
)
. In such settings, treatment effects can be summarized through pairwise comparison estimands that contrast outcomes across counterfactual conditions. Examples include the probabilistic index
(
17
)
and the causal net benefit estimands
(
1
)
. Beyond the classical average treatment effect (ATE), these quantities provide flexible, interpretable summaries of treatment superiority that extend beyond linear contrasts and are particularly suited to non-Gaussian or multi-component outcomes.

In this article, we develop a unified design-based framework for inference on a broad class of generalized causal effect (GCE) estimands in randomized experiments, specifically accommodating non-Gaussian, multivariate, and composite outcomes. Mathematically, let
\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{Q})^{\top}
denote the
Q
-dimensional (
Q\geq 1
) outcomes, where each component outcome
Y_{q}
(
q=1,\ldots,Q
) is unconstrained and can be of any type. Under the finite-population potential outcomes framework, the GCE estimand is defined as

\displaystyle\lambda(a,1-a)=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}w\{\bm{Y}_{i}(a),\bm{Y}_{j}(1-a)\},

(1)

where
N
is the total number of units in the experiment,
\bm{Y}_{i}(a)=(Y_{1,i}(a),\ldots,Y_{Q,i}(a))^{\top}
is the potential outcome vector under treatment condition
a=0,1
, and
w
is a contrast function encoding the comparison rule between two potential outcome vectors. Here, the summation averages contrasts over all distinct ordered pairs of units, representing a between-unit comparison in the finite population. This formulation accommodates arbitrary outcome structures, including multivariate, prioritized, or composite outcomes, and encompasses a wide range of nonlinear treatment contrasts (Section
2.1
). For example, when
Q=1
and
w(y_{1},y_{2})=\mathbbm{1}(y_{1}>y_{2})+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}(y_{1}=y_{2})
, (
1
) reduces to the probabilistic index underlying Mann-Whitney type effects
(
17
;
4
)
; when
Q=1
and
w(y_{1},y_{2})=y_{1}-y_{2}
is a linear contrast, (
1
) reduces to the classical ATE given by
\lambda(a,1-a)=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\{Y_{i}(a)-Y_{i}(1-a)\}
. The GCE, therefore, provides a unification for both classical linear estimands and modern nonlinear contrast functionals. Combining
\lambda(a,1-a)
and
\lambda(1-a,a)
leads to the causal net-benefit summaries such as
\tau(a)=\lambda(a,1-a)-\lambda(1-a,a)
, which quantify the advantage of allocation
a
relative to its complement
1-a
; see, for example,
1
and
14
.

For randomized experiments with a univariate outcome (
Q=1
) and
w(y_{1},y_{2})=y_{1}-y_{2}
,
11
demonstrated that covariate adjustment via linear regression can improve asymptotic efficiency under complete randomization when treatment-covariate interactions are included, and the heteroskedasticity-robust variance estimation can be used for asymptotically conservative inference. Importantly, this result does not rely on the correct specification of the linear outcome model; rather, regression adjustment serves as a model-assisted estimator whose efficiency gains do not compromise the ATE estimand. In comparison, the design-based theory for estimating the GCE has been elusive, despite a few prior efforts. For example, for a nonlinear
w
with univariate outcomes (
Q=1
),
19
and
14
have suggested that similar efficiency gain for estimating
\lambda(a,1-a)
in (
1
) through the use of the probabilistic index model
(
17
, PIM,)
—a model that resembles linear regression but specifically exploits the relationship between the probabilistic index and covariates. However, neither
19
nor
14
provided detailed analytical discussions of the impact of covariate adjustment on estimation efficiency and of the consequences of different regression model specifications. In addition and perhaps more importantly, design-based theory for variance estimation with a nonlinear contrast function remains largely unexplored in randomized experiments. We therefore provide a comprehensive treatment for model-assisted analysis of the GCE under the design-based framework, along with asymptotically valid variance estimators.

Specifically, for estimating
\lambda(a,1-a)
in randomized experiments, we consider two classes of estimators using different levels of data: individual pairs and per-unit pair averages. For each class of estimators, we study three regression model specifications: the Neyman-type
(
15
)
, regressing the outcome on the treatment indicators, the Fisher-type
(
5
)
, on the treatment indicators and covariates, and the Lin-type
(
11
)
, on the treatment indicators and treatment-covariate interactions. We prove that these estimators are consistent and asymptotically normal for the GCE even when the working models are arbitrarily misspecified. Leveraging the theory of U-statistics, we compare the leading terms in the Neyman finite-sample randomization variances (hereafter referred to as Neyman variances) of these estimators. Our results demonstrate a fundamental departure from the classical ATE setting. That is, under nonlinear contrast functions, covariate adjustment admits no universal guarantee of asymptotic efficiency gains, regardless of the choice of model-assisted estimators. This result points to the intrinsic limits of regression adjustment for the GCE, including the probabilistic index estimands. However, covariate-adjusted estimators remain more efficient empirically when the covariates are prognostic for the outcome, as shown in the simulation studies. Of note, the theoretical development underlying the technical results is non-trivial. This is because all estimators are functions of finite-population U-statistics arising from pairwise contrasts, which induce complex dependence structures through shared units and reverse comparisons. Establishing consistency and asymptotic normality, therefore, requires extending finite-population central limit theorems
(
8
)
to this nonlinear comparison setting.

Finally, for inference, we demonstrate that, surprisingly, neither the heteroskedasticity-robust (HR)
(
7
)
nor the cluster-robust (CR) variance estimator
(
10
)
is consistent, as neither correctly accounts for the correlation structure. Instead, the two-way (TW) cluster-robust variance estimator
(
2
)
is consistent for the variance of
\widehat{\lambda}(a,1-a)
, but not for the covariance between
\widehat{\lambda}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}(1-a,a)
and may, therefore, be anti-conservative for the final variance of the summary estimand
\widehat{\tau}(a)
. As a solution, we propose a complete two-way (CTW) cluster-robust variance estimator that also accounts for the within-pair covariance, i.e., between
w\{\bm{Y}_{i}(a),\bm{Y}_{j}(1-a)\}
and
w\{\bm{Y}_{j}(1-a),\bm{Y}_{i}(a)\}
, which consistently estimates the variance of
\widehat{\tau}(a)
in randomized experiments.

2
Regression estimators for generalized causal effect

2.1
Notation and framework

We consider a completely randomized experiment with
N
units, and let
A_{i}=a\in\{0,1\}
denote the treatment indicator for individual
i=1,\ldots,N
. We assume
N_{a}
units are randomly assigned to treatment
a
, with
N_{0}+N_{1}=N
. Let
\pi_{a}=N_{a}/N
for
a=0,1
be the assignment proportion. Define
\bm{X}_{i}
as the
d_{x}
-dimensional pre-treatment covariate vector, and
\bm{Y}_{i}=(Y_{1,i},\ldots,Y_{Q,i})^{\top}
as the
Q
outcomes of interest, where
Q\geq 1
. Under the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), the observed outcome is
\bm{Y}_{i}=A_{i}\bm{Y}_{i}(1)+(1-A_{i})\bm{Y}_{i}(0)
. The observed data for unit
i
is
(\bm{Y}_{i},A_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
. We pursue the design-based causal inference framework such that
(N_{a},\pi_{a})
are known in the experiment planning phase, and the potential outcomes
\bm{Y}_{i}(a)
and covariates
\bm{X}_{i}
are considered fixed. The sole source of randomness comes from the treatment assignment process; that is, only the treatment assignment
A_{i}
is treated as random. This notation allows us to define the GCE estimand in equation (
1
). Below, we offer two key remarks on this target estimand.

Remark 1
(Further examples of target estimand)

With multiple outcomes (
Q\geq 2
), we write
Y_{q}(a)\in\mathscr{Y}_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathscr{Y}}=\times_{q=1}^{Q}\mathscr{Y}_{q}
. The contrast
w:\boldsymbol{\mathscr{Y}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathscr{Y}}\mapsto\mathscr{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
may be constructed in at least the following ways. In the non-prioritized setting, where no consensus exists on relative clinical importance, one may use a dimension-wise aggregation
w(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})=\mathcal{A}\{w_{1}(y_{1,1},y_{2,1}),\ldots,w_{Q}(y_{Q,1},y_{Q,1})\}
, where
w_{q}(y_{q,1},y_{q,2}):\mathscr{Y}_{q}\times\mathscr{Y}_{q}\mapsto\mathscr{W}_{q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a contrast function for the
q
-th outcome, e.g., the Heaviside step function
w_{q}(y_{q,1},y_{q,2})=\mathbbm{1}(y_{q,1}>y_{q,2})+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}(y_{q,1}=y_{q,2})
, and
\mathcal{A}:\times_{q=1}^{Q}\mathscr{W}_{q}\mapsto\mathscr{W}
is an aggregation operator. A notable example is the non-prioritized average, where
\mathcal{A}=\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\omega_{q}w_{q}
with
\omega_{q}\geq 0
and
\sum_{q=1}^{Q}\omega_{q}=1
. In the prioritized setting, where outcomes can be ranked by clinical importance, a joint lexicographic rule may be defined as
w(\bm{y}_{1},\bm{y}_{2})=\mathbbm{1}(\bm{y}_{1}\succeq_{L}\bm{y}_{2})
, where
\bm{y}_{1}\succ\bm{y}_{2}
if the first component on which
\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{y}_{2}
differ favors
\bm{y}_{1}

(
1
)
.

Remark 2
(An alternative estimand)

The estimand (
1
) averages contrasts over distinct ordered pairs and therefore corresponds to a U-type GCE estimand. However, an alternative, equally valid causal estimand includes diagonal terms and can be written as

\lambda^{\dagger}(a,1-a)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}w\{\bm{Y}_{i}(a),\bm{Y}_{j}(1-a)\}.

(2)

We refer to (
2
) as the V-type GCE estimand. The two estimands differ only by

\displaystyle\lambda^{\dagger}(a,1-a)-\lambda(a,1-a)=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}w\{\bm{Y}_{i}(a),\bm{Y}_{i}(1-a)\}-\frac{\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}w\{\bm{Y}_{i}(a),\bm{Y}_{j}(1-a)\}}{N^{2}(N-1)},

which is of order
O(N^{-1})
under mild boundedness conditions on
w
. Hence, under standard finite-population asymptotics with
N\to\infty
, the U- and V-type estimands share the same limiting value and coincide with the corresponding super-population contrast when potential outcomes are viewed as independent draws. From a randomization-based perspective, the estimators developed in this article are asymptotically unbiased and consistent for both the U- and V-type estimands. For a definitive structural similarity, we therefore focus on the U-type formulation throughout, noting that all asymptotic results extend directly to the V-type counterpart.

To proceed, we define the indicator function
\mathbbm{1}_{i}(a)=\mathbbm{1}(A_{i}=a)
. For a pair units
i
and
j
, define the potential contrast as
W_{i,j}(a,a^{\prime})=w\{\bm{Y}_{i}(a),\bm{Y}_{j}(a^{\prime})\}
for
a,a^{\prime}=0,1
. Thus, the observed contrast is
W_{i,j}=w(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})=\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{a^{\prime}=0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}_{i}(a)\mathbbm{1}_{j}(a^{\prime})W_{i,j}(a,a^{\prime})
. Since
\lambda(a,a)
is not practically meaningful, we only focus on cases where
a^{\prime}=1-a
. Note that although
\lambda(a,a)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
,
\lambda(a,a)
is not necessarily zero in a finite population of size
N
. In general, the contrast function
w
is asymmetric, and therefore
W_{i,j}\neq W_{j,i}
, implying that there is a pair of observed contrasts
(W_{i,j},W_{j,i})
for each pair of
(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})
. Thus,
(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})
contributes to the estimation of both
\lambda(a,1-a)
and
\lambda(1-a,a)
if
A_{i}\neq A_{j}
. For notational simplicity, we further define
\bm{X}_{i,j}=\bm{X}_{i}-\bm{X}_{j}
. Thus,
\bm{X}_{j,i}=-\bm{X}_{i,j}
and
\sum_{1\leq i\neq j\leq N}\bm{X}_{i,j}=\bm{0}
, i.e.,
\bm{X}_{i,j}
is a centered covariate vector.

2.2
Regression adjustment using individual pairs

To estimate the GCE in randomized experiments, we first introduce regression estimators that enable baseline covariate adjustment using per-unit pair averages. Define
\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)=\{(i,j)|A_{i}=a,A_{j}=1-a,1\leq i\neq j\leq N\}
, and a natural nonparametric estimator for
\lambda(a,1-a)
in (
1
) is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)}W_{i,j}.

(3)

Hence, we consider the regression model

\displaystyle W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{\top}\equiv(A_{i}(1-A_{j}),(1-A_{i})A_{j}).

(4)

Then,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(1,0)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(0,1)
in (
3
) are coefficients of
A_{i}(1-A_{j})
and
(1-A_{i})A_{j}
from the ordinary least squares (OLS) fit of the saturated regression model (
4
). This is the pairwise or dyadic extension of the Neyman difference-in-means estimator
(
15
)
for the classical ATE in randomized experiments. Since this estimator is built upon averaging individual pairs, we include the subscript ‘
\mathrm{I}
’ to signify this feature.

To potentially improve efficiency through covariate adjustment,
5
proposed a regression estimator for the ATE in randomized experiments. Here, we consider its analogy in the pairwise setting as

\displaystyle W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}\equiv(A_{i}(1-A_{j}),(1-A_{i})A_{j},\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}),

(5)

which can be considered as an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model, but now for pairwise comparisons. The ANCOVA estimator using individual pairs,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1,0)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(0,1)
, are coefficients of
A_{i}(1-A_{j})
and
(1-A_{i})A_{j}
from the OLS fit of (
5
), respectively. Finally, based on
5
and
6
,
11
developed a regression estimator that includes treatment indicators and treatment indicator-covariate interactions. In the pairwise comparison setting, its analogy is given by

\displaystyle W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{{\mathrm{adj}}\top}\equiv(A_{i}(1-A_{j}),(1-A_{i})A_{j},A_{i}(1-A_{j})\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top},(1-A_{i})A_{j}\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}).

(6)

Therefore, the covariate-adjusted estimator using individual pairs,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1,0)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(0,1)
, are coefficients of
A_{i}(1-A_{j})
and
(1-A_{i})A_{j}
from the OLS fit of (
6
), respectively.

2.3
Regression adjustment using per-unit pair averages

Based on the pairwise comparison observation
W_{i,j}
, regression adjustment using individual pairs is not the only possible approach for estimating the GCE. Here, we present an alternative approach that operates upon per-unit pair averages. First, one can rewrite
\lambda(a,1-a)
in (
1
) as

\displaystyle\lambda(a,1-a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}W_{i,j}(a,1-a)+\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i:i\neq j}W_{i,j}(a,1-a)

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)+\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\right\},

where the second equality defines the per-unit pair averages as

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}W_{i,j}(a,1-a),~~\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i:i\neq j}W_{i,j}(a,1-a).

Of note, the
1/2
on each term in the first equality of (
7
) is for symmetry, which can be replaced by any non-negative normalized weights. Equation (
7
) further implies that one can estimate
\lambda(a,1-a)
using per-unit pair averages or, equivalently, per-unit pair totals. That is, we can define
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}=N_{1-A_{i}}^{-1}\sum_{j:A_{j}=1-A_{i}}W_{i,j}
and
\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}=N_{1-A_{i}}^{-1}\sum_{j:A_{j}=1-A_{i}}W_{j,i}
as the observed per-unit pair averages, and consider the two separate regression models

\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{\top}\equiv(A_{i},1-A_{i}),\quad\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},\cdot,i}^{\top}\equiv(1-A_{i},A_{i}).

(8)

Then the coefficient of
A_{i}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{\top}
and the coefficient of
1-A_{i}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},\cdot,i}^{\top}
give the exactly equivalent estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}(1,0)
for
\lambda(1,0)
, because they both use the same pairs in
\mathcal{S}(1,0)
under different index labels. Similarly, we have
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}(0,1)
for
\lambda(0,1)
. The subscript ‘
\mathrm{A}
’ is added to signify that the estimator is obtained using per-unit pair averages. It is straightforward to show that the estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}(a,1-a)
is algebraically equivalent to
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
.

Similar to (
5
), the paired ANCOVA models based on using per-unit pair averages are

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}\equiv(A_{i},1-A_{i},\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{A\top}),\quad\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},\cdot,i}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}\equiv(1-A_{i},A_{i},\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{A\top}),

(9)

where
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{A}=N_{1-A_{i}}^{-1}\sum_{j:A_{j}=1-A_{i}}\bm{X}_{i,j}
and
\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{A}=N_{1-A_{i}}^{-1}\sum_{j:A_{j}=1-A_{i}}\bm{X}_{j,i}
represent the per-unit pair averages of baseline covariates. Importantly, the final ANCOVA estimator using per-unit pair averages for
\lambda(1,0)
is the weighted average of the coefficient of
A_{i}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}
and the coefficient of
1-A_{i}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},\cdot,i}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}
, since these two coefficients are not algebraically equivalent due to baseline covariate adjustment. Similar observations apply to the estimator for
\lambda(0,1)
. The optimal weights can be determined using the Neyman variances of the coefficients and their covariance, which requires involved estimation as we detail in Section
4
. Therefore, in practice, we recommend selecting the coefficient with the smaller estimated variance as the final estimator. For simplicity of exposition, in the remainder of the article, we label the estimator only using coefficients from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}
as
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
and the corresponding estimator from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},\cdot,i}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}
as
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
, and focus our theoretical discussions on these estimators.

Finally, to allow for treatment-by-covariate interactions using per-unit pair averages, we consider the following regression analogues of the
11

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{{\mathrm{adj}}\top}\equiv(A_{i},1-A_{i},A_{i}\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{A\top},(1-A_{i})\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{A\top}),

(10)

\displaystyle\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},\cdot,i}^{{\mathrm{adj}}\top}\equiv(1-A_{i},A_{i},(1-A_{i})\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{A\top},A_{i}\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{A\top}).

The final covariate-adjusted estimator using per-unit pair averages
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1,0)
is the weighted average of the coefficient of
A_{i}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{A},i,\cdot}^{{\mathrm{adj}}\top}
, and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1,0)

Remark 3
(Connection with rank regression)

Under specific contrast functions, e.g.,
w(\bm{Y}_{1},\bm{Y}_{2})=\mathbbm{1}(\bm{Y}_{1}\succeq_{L}\bm{Y}_{2})
, models in (
8
)-(
10
) are unit-level rank-average or aggregated pairwise rank regressions
(
3
)
applied to randomized experiments. The per-unit average
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)
is the proportion of wins for unit
i
in treatment group
a
against all units in treatment group
1-a
, or the average rank of
\bm{Y}_{i}(a)
among all
\bm{Y}_{j}(1-a)
, with
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}^{A}
being the observed version of
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)
. Under
w(\bm{Y}_{1},\bm{Y}_{2})=\mathbbm{1}(\bm{Y}_{1}\succeq_{L}\bm{Y}_{2})
, the estimand
\lambda(a,1-a)=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)\to\mathbb{P}\{\bm{Y}(a)\succeq_{L}\bm{Y}(1-a)\}
as
N\to\infty
, which is the causal Mann-Whitney estimand under a super-population framework.

3
Asymptotic properties of regression estimators

3.1
Preliminaries

To study the asymptotic properties of the regression estimators under arbitrary model misspecification, we adopt the finite-population asymptotic regime of
8
, considering a sequence of finite populations with
N\to\infty
. All quantities depend on
N
, and we suppress this dependence in notation for simplicity.
The following regularity conditions are required to establish these results.

Condition 1

\pi_{a}
has a limit in
(0,1)
for
a=0,1
, and
d_{x}
is finite and independent of
N
.

Condition 2

\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}W_{i,j}(a,1-a)^{4}=O(1)
for
a=0,1
.
\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\times\sum_{i\neq j}\|\bm{X}_{i,j}\|_{\infty}^{4}=O(1)
and
\max_{i,j:i\neq j}\|\bm{X}_{i,j}\|_{\infty}^{2}=o(N)
.

Condition 3

The limit of
\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}\bm{X}_{i,j}\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}
is positive definite, and the limit of
\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}\bm{X}_{i,j}W_{i,j}
is finite.

Conditions
1
-
3
are typical regularity conditions invoked in design-based causal inference; see, for example,
11
and
8
. Condition
1
requires that the number of units under the two treatments is not too imbalanced, and restricts the covariates to a fixed number. Condition
2
restricts the moments of the potential outcomes and covariates, removing highly influential/outlier observations and preventing the magnitude of the covariates from growing too rapidly. Condition
3
requires the components in the design matrices to be well-behaved.

We introduce additional notation in preparation for the main results. Let
\overline{W}(a,1-a)=\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}W_{i,j}(a,1-a)
. Define
\epsilon_{i,j}(a,1-a)=W_{i,j}(a,1-a)-\overline{W}(a,1-a)
, with
\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\epsilon_{i,j}(a,1-a)=\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)-\overline{W}(a,1-a)
and
\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\epsilon_{j,i}(a,1-a)=\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)-\overline{W}(a,1-a)
. Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)=0
by definition. Let
\mathcal{E}_{i}(a,1-a)\equiv\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a),\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
be pairs of quantities with
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)=0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)=0
. Motivated by the form of the Neyman variance, we further define

\displaystyle V_{c}\{\mathcal{E}_{i}(a,1-a)\}=\frac{1}{\pi_{a}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi_{1-a}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)^{2},

\displaystyle V\{\mathcal{E}_{i}(a,1-a)\}=V_{c}\{\mathcal{E}_{i}(a,1-a)\}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)+\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\right\}^{2}.

Also, for the covariances, we define

\displaystyle CV(\mathcal{E}_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi_{a}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(1-a,a)+\frac{1}{\pi_{1-a}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(1-a,a)\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)+\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\right\}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(1-a,a)+\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(1-a,a)\right\}.

Finally, for the Neyman variance of the causal net benefit estimators, we also define

\displaystyle V_{c}(\mathcal{E}_{i})=\frac{1}{\pi_{a}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)-\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(1-a,a)\right\}^{2}+\frac{1}{\pi_{1-a}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(1-a,a)-\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\right\}^{2},

\displaystyle V(\mathcal{E}_{i})=V_{c}(\mathcal{E}_{i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)-\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(1-a,a)\right\}+\left\{\mathcal{E}_{i,\cdot}(1-a,a)-\mathcal{E}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\right\}\right]^{2}.

3.2
Regression estimators using individual pairs

Let
\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(a,1-a)=\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a),\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)\}
. We have the following result for
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
.

Theorem 1

Under complete randomization and Conditions
1
and
2
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)=\lambda(a,1-a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(a,1-a)\}\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)-\lambda(a,1-a)\}/\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)=V\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(a,1-a)\}+O(N^{-1})
. For the covariance, if
CV(\overline{\epsilon}_{i})\nrightarrow 0
, then
\nu_{\mathrm{I}}=N{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a),\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(1-a,a)\}=CV(\overline{\epsilon}_{i})+O(N^{-1})
.

Theorem
1
presents the consistency and asymptotic normality of the nonparametric estimator
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
. The condition
\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}W_{i,j}(a,1-a)^{4}=O(1)
can be relaxed to
\{N(N-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}W_{i,j}(a,\allowbreak 1-a)^{4}=o(N)
for
a=0,1
. The key to obtaining results in Theorem
1
is to apply the Hoeffding decomposition
(
18
, §11.4)
to
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
, which is an order-two U-statistic. This yields a Bahadur representation of the U-statistic that provides the form of a linear summation of Hàjek projections and an uncorrelated degenerate remainder that is of order
o_{\mathbb{P}}(N^{-1/2})
. Thus, the asymptotic properties of
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
can be obtained by investigating the dominant linear summation term, which is also linear in
A_{i}
. Then, under Conditions
1
and
2
, the consistency is obtained by Chebyshev’s inequality, and the asymptotic normality is obtained via applying the finite-population central limit theorem for simple random sampling
(
8
, Theorem 1)
to the linear terms. We obtain leading terms of the Neyman variances of
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(1-a,a)
, as well as their covariance, rather than their full exact expressions, where the
O(N^{-1})
terms in
\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
and
\nu_{\mathrm{I}}
are from the uncorrelated degenerate remainder.

Next, let
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
denote the coefficient from the theoretical OLS fit of
\epsilon_{i,j}(a,1-a)
on
\bm{X}_{i,j}
, which is the finite-population probability limit of
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
, the coefficient of
\bm{X}_{i,j}
from the OLS fit of (
6
). Define
r_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\epsilon_{i,j}(a,1-a)-\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
r_{\mathrm{I},j,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\epsilon_{j,i}(a,1-a)-\bm{X}_{j,i}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
, with
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i,\cdot}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}r_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},\cdot,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}r_{\mathrm{I},j,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
, where
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\bm{X}_{i,j}
and
\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}\bm{X}_{j,i}
. Let
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i,\cdot}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a),\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},\cdot,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
. We have the following result for
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
.

Theorem 2

Under complete randomization and Conditions
1
-
3
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\lambda(a,1-a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)-\lambda(a,1-a)\}/\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}+O(N^{-1})
. For the covariance, if
CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}})\nrightarrow 0
, then
\nu_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=N{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a),\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)\}=CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}})+O(N^{-1})
.

Theorem
2
presents the consistency and asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
, under arbitrary model misspecification. Interestingly, the form of
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
suggests that
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
may be less efficient than
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
, as
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
is not the coefficient of
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)
on
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}
. This phenomenon was first identified by
11
for estimating the ATE in individual randomized experiments. A related result is also provided by
16
, who expanded the results of
11
to cluster randomization, where the Neyman variance of estimators based on individual-level data does not incorporate the “correct” regression coefficient, whereas estimators constructed from scaled cluster totals do. Consequently, the latter is guaranteed to be at least as statistically efficient as the corresponding unadjusted estimators.

Finally, let
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}
denote the coefficient from the theoretical OLS fit of
\sum_{a=0}^{1}\sum_{a^{\prime}=0}^{1}\pi_{a}\pi_{a^{\prime}}\times W_{i,j}(a,a^{\prime})
on
\bm{X}_{i,j}
. Define
r_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=\epsilon_{i,j}(a,1-a)-\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}
and
r_{\mathrm{I},j,i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=\epsilon_{j,i}(a,1-a)-\bm{X}_{j,i}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}
, with
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i,\cdot}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}r_{\mathrm{I},i,j}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
and
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},\cdot,i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=(N-1)^{-1}\sum_{j:j\neq i}r_{\mathrm{I},j,i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
. Let
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i,\cdot}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a),\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},\cdot,i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a))
. We have the following result for
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
.

Theorem 3

Under complete randomization and Conditions
1
-
3
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=\lambda(a,1-a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)\}\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)-\lambda(a,1-a)\}/\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)=V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)\}+O(N^{-1})
. For the covariance, if
CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})\nrightarrow 0
, then
\nu_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}=N{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a),\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1-a,a)\}=CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})+O(N^{-1})
.

Theorem
3
presents the consistency and asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
, under arbitrary model misspecification. Similar to
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
, a noteworthy finding is that
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
may be also less efficient than
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
. In the classical setting of estimating the ATE with linear contrasts,
11
established that covariate adjustment under a fully interacted specification does not result in asymptotic efficiency loss under complete randomization. Our result clarifies that, although consistency holds under arbitrary model misspecification, this efficiency property does not extend to GCEs based on nonlinear contrast functions. The departure arises from the non-separable structure of the GCE, under which regression adjustment alters the leading term of the Neyman variance in ways that need not reduce variability. This difference points to a fundamental limitation of regression adjustment beyond linear estimands and clarifies that efficiency guarantees for covariate adjustment can be estimand-dependent when moving to nonlinear contrast functionals. We offer more details on this discussion in the ensuing subsection.

It is worth noting that in some cases, the contrast functions can be anti-symmetric such that
W_{i,j}=C-W_{j,i}
for some constant
C
. Some examples include
w(Y_{i},Y_{j})=Y_{i}-Y_{j}
, where
C=0
, and
w(\bm{Y}_{i},\bm{Y}_{j})=\mathbbm{1}(\bm{Y}_{i}\succeq_{L}\bm{Y}_{j})
, where
C=1
. Some intrinsic connections among estimators using individual pair data emerge under such contrast function specifications.

Proposition 1

If the contrast function
w
satisfies anti-symmetry, i.e.,
W_{i,j}=C-W_{j,i}
for some constant
C
, then, for
a=0,1
, (i)
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)
; (ii)
V\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(a,1-a)\}=V\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(1-a,a)\}
,
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}=V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)\}
, and
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)\}=V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1-a,a)\}
; and (iii)
CV(\overline{\epsilon}_{i})=-V\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(a,1-a)\}=-V\{\overline{\epsilon}_{i}(1-a,a)\}
,
CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}})=-V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}=-V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)\}
, and
CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})=-V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)\}=-V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1-a,a)\}
.

Proposition
1
states that if the contrast function satisfies anti-symmetry, then
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)
. For variances,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(1-a,a)
have the same leading term of the Neyman variances, and the same property holds for
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)
, as well as
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1-a,a)
. The leading term of the covariance between
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(1-a,a)
is the negative of the leading term of the variance, and the same property holds for the covariance between
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)
, and the one between
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1-a,a)
.

3.3
Regression estimators using per-unit pair averages

Next, for regression estimators using per-unit pair averages, we focus the discussions on
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a,1-a)
since
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
are algebraically equivalent. We first state the following regularity conditions tailored for per-unit pair averages.

Condition 4

N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)^{4}=O(1)
for
a=0,1
.
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}\|_{\infty}^{4}=O(1)
and
\max_{i}\|\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}\|_{\infty}^{2}=O(1)
.

Condition 5

The limit of
N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{\top}
is positive definite, and the limit of
N^{-1}\times\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}
is finite.

Let
\{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1,0),\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(0,1)\}
and
\{\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1,0),\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{adj}}(0,1)\}
denote the coefficients of
A_{i}\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{A}
and
(1-A_{i})\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{A}
, and
(1-A_{i})\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{A}
and
A_{i}\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{A}
from the OLS fit of models in (
10
), respectively. Let
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
denote the coefficient from the theoretical OLS fit of
\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)
on
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}
, and
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
from the theoretical OLS fit of
\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)
on
\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}
, which are finite-population probability limits of
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
, respectively. For
\mathfrak{o}=1,2
, define
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i,\cdot}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},\cdot,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)-\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}^{\top}\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
. Let
\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i,\cdot}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a),\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},\cdot,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
. We have the following result for
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
for
\mathfrak{o}=1,2
.

Theorem 4

Under complete randomization and Conditions
1
,
4
and
5
, for
\mathfrak{o}=1,2
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\lambda(a,1-a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)-\lambda(a,1-a)\}/\sigma_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}+O(N^{-1})
. For the covariance, if
CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i}^{\mathrm{adj}})\nrightarrow 0
, then
\nu_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}=N{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}}\{\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a),\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1-a,a)\}=CV(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i}^{\mathrm{adj}})+O(N^{-1})
.

Theorem
4
presents the consistency and asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
for
\mathfrak{o}=1,2
, under arbitrary model misspecification. Similar to the regression estimators using individual pairs, the form of
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o},i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
suggests that
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
may also be less efficient than
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{I}}(a,1-a)
. Next, for
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},1,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
,
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
is the coefficient of
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{\epsilon}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)
on
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}
, but not the coefficient of
\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}
from the OLS fit of
\overline{\epsilon}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)
on
\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}
, whereas the right coefficient is
\bm{\gamma}_{\mathrm{A},2}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
. Similarly, vice versa for
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},2,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
.

An intuitive interpretation of
V\{\overline{r}_{\mathrm{A},1,i}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)\}
is that it is the Neyman variance of the ANCOVA estimator in a hypothetical setting of estimating the ATE with potential outcomes
\overline{W}_{i,\cdot}(a,1-a)
under treatment
a
and
\overline{W}_{\cdot,i}(a,1-a)
under
1-a
. Therefore, by
11
, the relative efficiency ordering between
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},1}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A}}(a,1-a)
is not definitive and can depend on the data generating process. Specifically, let
\overline{W}_{\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)}=|\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)|^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbbm{1}_{i}(a)\mathbbm{1}_{j}(1-a)W_{i,j}
and
\overline{\bm{X}}_{\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)}=|\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)|^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbbm{1}_{i}(a)\mathbbm{1}_{j}(1-a)\bm{X}_{i,j}
. By the property of the OLS, we have
\widehat{\lambda}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)=\overline{W}_{\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)}-\overline{\bm{X}}_{\mathcal{S}(a,1-a)}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{A},\mathfrak{o}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a,1-a)
for
\mathfrak{o}=1,2
. Under regularity conditions, it is shown in the Supplementary Materials that

3.4.2
Estimators from the probabilistic index models

An alternative approach for directly estimating the causal net benefit is based on PIMs
(
17
;
14
)
. Although PIMs have been previously proposed, their design-based properties under complete randomization have not been formally characterized since their introduction. Under randomization, we prove that the coefficient from a linear PIM with individual pair data is always consistent for the causal net benefit estimand, even when the working PIM is arbitrarily misspecified. This result affirms that the linear PIM estimator is model-assisted in the same sense as the regression estimators studied above. To formalize this connection, define
D_{i,j}=A_{i}-A_{j}
, and consider the regression models

\displaystyle W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{P},i,j}^{\top}\equiv D_{i,j},\quad W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{P},i,j}^{{\mathrm{acv}}\top}\equiv(D_{i,j},\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}),

\displaystyle W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{P},i,j}^{{\mathrm{int}}\top}\equiv(D_{i,j},D_{i,j}\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}),\quad W_{i,j}\sim\bm{Z}_{\mathrm{P},i,j}^{{\mathrm{adj}}\top}\equiv(D_{i,j},\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top},D_{i,j}\bm{X}_{i,j}^{\top}).

Let
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}
,
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}
,
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{int}}
, and
\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}
be the respective coefficients of
D_{i,j}
from OLS fits of the above models. Then, PIM estimators for
\tau(1)
are
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}(1)=2\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P},(1)}
,
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(1)=2\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}
,
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{int}}(1)=2\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{int}}
, and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(1)=2\widehat{\beta}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}
. We then obtain the following results.

Theorem 6

Under complete randomization and Conditions
1
-
3
, (i)
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}(a)=\tau(a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V(\overline{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{I},i})\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}(a)-\tau(a)\}/\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}=V(\overline{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{I},i})+O(N^{-1})
. (ii)
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a)=\tau(a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a)-\tau(a)\}/\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}=V(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})+O(N^{-1})
. (iii)
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{int}}(a)=\tau(a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V(\overline{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{I},i})\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{int}}(a)-\tau(a)\}/\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}=V(\overline{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{I},i})+O(N^{-1})
. (iv)
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a)=\tau(a)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
; if further
V(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})\nrightarrow 0
, then
N^{1/2}\{\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a)-\tau(a)\}/\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
with
\sigma_{\tau,\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}=V(\overline{r}_{\mathrm{I},i}^{\mathrm{acv}})+O(N^{-1})
.

Theorem
6
summarizes the consistency and asymptotic normality of linear PIM estimators for
\tau(a)
. The results first show that
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}(a)
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{I}}(a)
are equivalent. Second,
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}(a)
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{int}}(a)
are asymptotically equivalent, and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a)
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a)
are also asymptotically equivalent. That is, the adjustment of
D_{i,j}\bm{X}_{i,j}
is not meaningful asymptotically. The intuition is that
D_{i,j}\bm{X}_{i,j}
forces the coefficient of this term to be in the opposite sign for groups with
D_{i,j}=1
and
-1
, which, coupled with
\overline{\bm{X}}_{i,\cdot}=-\overline{\bm{X}}_{\cdot,i}
, cancels out the related terms in the leading terms of the Neyman variances. Lastly, both
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a)
and
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{P}}^{\mathrm{adj}}(a)
are asymptotically equivalent to
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{I}}^{\mathrm{acv}}(a)
. These findings provide a theoretical ground for justifying the linear PIM as a model-assisted estimator for the causal net benefit.

Remark 4

The CTW variance estimator provides an explicit, design-based representation of the generic sandwich variance estimator originally discussed in
17
for PIMs. However,
17
developed their variance expressions under a superpopulation, sampling-based framework and did not pursue a finite-population randomization perspective. In contrast, our CTW formulation explicitly characterizes the randomization variance induced by known treatment assignment, accounting for the two-way dependence structure arising from shared units and reverse comparisons.
The standard two-way (TW) clustering variance estimator
(
2
)
is consistent for the marginal variances of
\widehat{\lambda}(a,1-a)
and
\widehat{\lambda}(1-a,a)
, but omits their covariance. Therefore, it is not consistent for the variances of the PIM estimators. The heteroskedasticity-robust (HR) variance estimator
(
7
)
is neither consistent for either the marginal variances nor the covariances because it omits the correlation among pairs and the reverse effect. The (one-way) cluster-robust (CR) variance estimator
(
10
)
is also not consistent for either the marginal variances or the covariances because it omits half of the correlations and the reverse effect. We prove these results in Section S5 of the Supplementary Materials.

5
Simulation studies

5.1
Simulation design

We conduct simulation studies to illustrate the finite-sample behavior of the proposed regression point estimators and variance estimators. Although our theoretical results demonstrate that covariate adjustment under nonlinear contrast functions does not always admit a universal efficiency guarantee, we design scenarios to show that empirical efficiency gains can nonetheless arise when baseline covariates are prognostic for the outcomes. At the same time, we also construct counterexamples in which covariate adjustment fails to improve, or may even reduce, efficiency, thereby providing practical insight into the limits identified by our theory. We further evaluate the empirical performance of the proposed CTW variance estimator and compare it with commonly used HR and CR alternatives in finite samples. Throughout, our primary focus is on the U-type GCE estimand defined in (
1
); additional simulation results for the V-type GCE estimand are provided in the Web Table S8 and S9 in Section S7 of the Supplementary Materials, where we confirm that the asymptotic equivalence established in Section 2 is reflected in finite-sample performance.

Data example

We illustrate the proposed GCE estimators using data from the Best Apnea Interventions for Research (BestAIR) trial, an individually randomized, parallel-group study designed to evaluate the effect of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment on health outcomes among patients with obstructive sleep apnea and elevated cardiovascular risk but without severe sleepiness
(
21
)
. Participants were recruited from outpatient clinics at three medical centers in Boston, MA, and randomized to receive either CPAP-based therapy or conservative medical therapy. In the analytic sample considered here, there are 169 participants, with 83 assigned to the CPAP group and 86 assigned to the control group. A set of baseline patient-level covariates was collected prior to treatment assignment, and clinical outcomes were measured at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months.

For illustration, we consider estimating the treatment effect of CPAP on two outcomes measured at 6 months. We conduct two analyses. The first one focuses on the objective outcome (
Y_{1}

Y_{1}

For covariate adjustment, we consider a total of 10 baseline covariates, including demographics (age, gender, race, ethnicity), body mass index, Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI), average seated radial pulse rate (SDP), trial site, and baseline outcome measures (baseline average blood pressure and ESS). 7
Concluding remarks

In this article, we develop a unified design-based theory for inference on GCE estimands defined through pairwise contrast functions in randomized experiments. By representing probabilistic index effects, the causal net benefit, and related nonlinear summaries within a common finite-population framework, we extend classical regression adjustment theory beyond the classical linear ATE. We establish that regression estimators based on both individual pairs and per-unit averages remain model-assisted and asymptotically normal under arbitrary misspecification. At the same time, we discuss a fundamental departure from the linear setting: for nonlinear contrast functions, covariate adjustment does not admit a universal efficiency guarantee. This clarifies that efficiency gains from regression adjustment are inherently estimand-dependent when moving beyond additive contrasts. We further provide a randomization-based variance theory tailored to pairwise dependence and introduce complete two-way cluster-robust variance estimators that are consistent. Finally, we have pursued complete randomization in this work as a starting point, and
extending the present framework to rerandomized designs would require characterizing the joint asymptotic distribution of randomization-based U-statistics under correlated assignment mechanisms
(
9
)
. Understanding the interplay between rerandomization and analytic covariate adjustment can help further elucidate the potential for potentially more efficient estimators for estimating the GCE estimand.
