DP-SPRT : Differentially Private Sequential Probability Ratio Tests
Thomas Michel, Debabrota Basu, Emilie Kaufmann

Abstract
We revisit Wald’s celebrated Sequential Probability Ratio Test for sequential tests of two simple hypotheses, under privacy constraints. We propose DP-SPRT , a wrapper that can be calibrated to achieve desired error probabilities and privacy constraints, addressing a significant gap in previous work. DP-SPRT relies on a private mechanism that processes a sequence of queries and stops after privately determining when the query results fall outside a predefined interval. This OutsideInterval mechanism improves upon naive composition of existing techniques like AboveThreshold, potentially benefiting other sequential algorithms. We prove generic upper bounds on the error and sample complexity of DP-SPRT that can accommodate various noise distributions based on the practitioner’s privacy needs. We exemplify them in two settings: Laplace noise (pure Differential Privacy) and Gaussian noise (Rényi differential privacy). In the former setting, by providing a lower bound on the sample complexity of any ε \varepsilon -DP test with prescribed type I and type II errors, we show that DP-SPRT is near optimal when both errors are small and the two hypotheses are close. Moreover, we conduct an experimental study revealing its good practical performance.



1
Introduction

Sequential hypothesis testing offers an elegant solution when data collection is costly or time-consuming, allowing decisions to be reached with fewer observations on average compared to fixed-sample approaches. The Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT), developed by
Wald, 1945
, is the foundational algorithm in this field, achieving optimal performance for simple hypothesis testing with independent and identically distributed observations.

Sequential testing procedures are evaluated based on their error rates (Type I and Type II errors) and expected sample size (or
sample complexity
) under each hypothesis.
Wald, 1948
proved that SPRT minimizes sample complexities subject to error probability constraints—a remarkably strong optimality result in statistical decision theory.
Despite its theoretical optimality and applications in clinical trials
(
Armitage, 1950
;
Armitage, 1954
)
, quality control, and manufacturing
(
Wald, 2004
;
Siegmund, 2013
)
, SPRT faces a significant modern challenge:
privacy
. When applied to sensitive data, such as patient records in medical trials, financial information, or personal behavior patterns, sequential testing procedures risk exposing confidential information. Privacy concerns are particularly acute for the most recent observations, as the decision to terminate data collection can reveal information about them.

Consider a clinical trial where patients sequentially receive treatments and their responses are recorded. With the classical SPRT, if the test concludes that a drug increases the chance of observing an undesirable effect, then we can deduce the effect actually happened to the last participant in the study. More sophisticated attacks could deduce many other pieces of private information about participants throughout the trial. Even if individual patient responses are kept confidential, the pattern of treatment assignments and the stopping decision itself can leak sensitive information about participants. This privacy risk requires a formal framework for privacy-preserving sequential testing.

Related Work.
Differential privacy (DP)
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
has emerged as the standard framework for addressing privacy concerns in statistical analyses. By ensuring that the presence or absence of any single data point cannot significantly affect the outcome, DP offers a principled method to protect individual data while enabling meaningful statistical inference. Researchers have explored DP hypothesis testing– primarily in the fixed-sample setting. For example,
Gaboardi et al., 2016
studies private chi-squared testing.
Canonne et al., 2019
and
Couch et al., 2019
investigate on optimal private tests for simple hypotheses, and nonparametric hypothesis testing with DP, respectively. These approaches, however, are not directly applicable to sequential testing due to the adaptive nature of sample collection.

Wang et al., 2022
explored the privatization of SPRT by adding Laplace noise to thresholds and using the exponential mechanism for the final decision. However, due to their specific use case, their approach provides a weaker notion of privacy that only requires that critical privacy failures do not happen only for a subset of plausible datasets. In contrast, our work maintains DP throughout the testing process while working to minimize stopping time.

Zhang et al., 2022
developed PrivSPRT, a private version of the SPRT using Gaussian noise with Renyi DP guarantees
(
Mironov, 2017
)
. The authors provide upper bounds on the error probabilities given fixed thresholds and noise level, but do not specify how to calibrate these thresholds to achieve desired Type I and Type II error rates while maintaining a desired privacy guarantee. In practice, this forces to rely on Monte Carlo simulation to determine appropriate thresholds, a process that may itself leak privacy in certain scenarios
(
Papernot and Steinke, 2021
)
. In this work, we overcome this limitation and provide an explicit threshold calibration to guarantee specified upper bounds on the errors as well as the desired privacy guarantees. Additionally, we provide a more general method supporting multiple noise mechanisms, and subsampling methods.

Another related line of work is Differentially Private Best Arm Identification (DP-BAI) in bandits
(
Sajed and Sheffet, 2019
;
Kalogerias et al., 2021
;
Azize et al., 2023
;
Azize et al., 2024
)
. BAI is a generalization of sequential hypothesis testing, where we simultaneously test means of multiple distributions to identify the largest one.
(
Azize et al., 2023
;
Azize et al., 2024
)
design algorithms for
\varepsilon
-DP-BAI whose sample complexity has irreducible problem-dependent constants compared to the lower bound, keeping the optimal algorithm design open. Thus, we focus on the archetypal setup of testing two hypotheses, and aim to explicate the privacy-utility trade-offs.

Contributions.
(1) We introduce a general framework for differentially private SPRTs, called
DP-SPRT
, that supports
various noise mechanisms
while providing privacy guarantees and error control. We propose a generic sample complexity analysis of
DP-SPRT
for Bernoulli observations and particularize it to Gaussian noise (for Renyi DP) and Laplace noise (for
\varepsilon
-DP). (2) This framework is based on the novel
OutsideInterval
mechanism for differentially privately comparing a sequence of queries to two thresholds. This might be of independent interest for applications for DP problems with or without continual observations. (3) We further prove a lower bound on the sample complexity of any
\varepsilon
-DP algorithm that achieves prescribed type I and type II errors. This lower bound reveals two distinct regimes: one where privacy has minimal impact on sample complexity, and another where higher privacy requirements substantially increase the expected sample size. It also shows that
DP-SPRT
with Laplace noise is near optimal in a regime of small errors and when the two hypotheses are close
. (4) On the practical side, we use subsampling to propose a practical improvement of
DP-SPRT
with better privacy-utility trade-off, especially in high privacy regimes.

Our analysis of
DP-SPRT
is conducted for Bernoulli observations, which model binary outcomes present in applications such as clinical trials, quality control, and A/B testing. Bernoulli distributions form a one-dimensional exponential family with bounded support in
[0,1]
, making them suitable for DP mechanisms requiring bounded sensitivity. This setting provides an illustration of privacy-utility trade-offs while maintaining mathematical tractability.

Preliminaries: Sequential Tests, Differential Privacy, and SPRT

Sequential hypothesis testing
allows collecting data incrementally and making decisions as soon as sufficient evidence accumulates. Given a stream of i.i.d. observations
X_{i}
from some parametric distribution
\nu_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\Theta
, we consider two simple hypotheses
\mathcal{H}_{0}:(\theta=\theta_{0})
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}:(\theta=\theta_{1})
, where
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
are two distinct parameters in
\Theta
. We let
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
be the probability space under which the sequence
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is i.i.d. under
\nu_{\theta}
.

A sequential test consists of a
stopping rule
that determines when to stop collecting data, and a
decision rule
that selects one of the hypotheses. Specifically, a sequential test yields a pair
(\tau,\hat{d})
, where
\tau
is a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t})
, and the final decision
\hat{d}\in\{0,1\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable. Given prescribed error levels
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
, we seek to design tests that have a type I error smaller than
\alpha
and a type II error smaller than
\beta
. These tests should have a small
sample complexity
,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{i}}[\tau]
, under either hypotheses.

Definition 1

((
\alpha,\beta
)-Correct Sequential Test)
.

A sequential test
(\tau,\hat{d})
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-correct if it satisfies
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\hat{d}=1)\leq\alpha
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(\hat{d}=0)\leq\beta
.

Differentially Private Sequential Tests.
Differential Privacy (DP)
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
is usually defined for a randomized mechanism based on a dataset of fixed size. It is expressed as a constraint on the randomness used by the mechanism: the distribution of its output cannot change too much if a single entry in its input (the dataset) is modified. It is possible to extend DP to sequential tests by considering as input an
infinite observation sequence

\underline{X}:=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots)
. The mechanism
\mathcal{M}(\underline{X}):=(\tau(\underline{X}),\hat{d}(\underline{X}))
outputs the stopping time and decision of the sequential test run on the data stream
\underline{X}
(regardless of the distribution of that stream). We formalize below for sequential tests different notions of privacy commonly used in the literature.

Definition 2

(Neighboring Observation Sequences)
.

Two observation sequences
\underline{X}:=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots)
and
\underline{X}^{\prime}:=(X^{\prime}_{1},X^{\prime}_{2},\ldots)
are neighboring if they differ in at most one observation, i.e., if there exists at most one index
i
such that
X_{i}\neq X^{\prime}_{i}
.

Definition 3

(Differentially Private Sequential Test)
.

A sequential test with associated randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}(\underline{X}):=(\tau(\underline{X}),\hat{d}(\underline{X}))
satisfies:

(i)

(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
if for all neighboring observation sequences
\underline{X}
and
\underline{X}^{\prime}
, and for all possible outputs
(t,d)\in\mathbb{N}\times\{0,1\}
,

\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\underline{X})=(t,d)]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{M}(\underline{X}^{\prime})=(t,d)]+\delta\;.

We say that the test satisfies
\varepsilon
-pure DP if
\delta=0
.

(ii)

(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-Rényi DP
if for all neighboring observation sequences
\underline{X}
and
\underline{X}^{\prime}
:

D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}(\underline{X})\|\mathcal{M}(\underline{X}^{\prime}))\leq\varepsilon

where
D_{\alpha}(P\|Q)=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{x\sim Q}\left[\left(\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right)^{\alpha}\right]
is the Rényi divergence of order
\alpha
.
A test has an
RDP
profile
\tilde{\varepsilon}(\alpha)
if it satisfies
(\alpha,\tilde{\varepsilon}(\alpha))
-
RDP
for all
\alpha>1
.

Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP)
(
Mironov, 2017
)
provides a useful alternative framework for privacy analysis that often leads to tighter composition bounds than standard differential privacy.
(\alpha,\varepsilon^{\prime})
-RDP can be converted to approximate
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
(
Dwork et al., 2014
)
using standard conversion formula, i.e., for
\varepsilon>\varepsilon^{\prime}
,
\delta=\exp(-(\alpha-1)(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}))

(
Abadi et al., 2016
)
.

-correct tests with privacy guarantees, expressed either in DP or in RDP. To do so, it is natural to rely on an optimal (non-private)

3
OutsideInterval
Mechanism

Now, we introduce a novel private mechanism that forms the foundation of our approach to differentially private sequential testing. Let

\Delta

This design is directly motivated by SPRT, which monitors whether a test statistic falls outside an interval defined by two thresholds, rather than crossing a single threshold.

This mechanism extends AboveThreshold
(
Dwork et al., 2006

4
Differentially Private Sequential Probability Ratio Test

Building on the threshold mechanism presented in Section
3

DP-SPRT

General Construction.

We consider the sequential hypothesis test between
\mathcal{H}_{0}:(\theta=\theta_{0})
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}:(\theta=\theta_{1})
(with
\theta_{0}<\theta_{1}
) based on i.i.d. samples
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
from
\nu_{\theta}

\theta

(\tau,\hat{d})
that (i) satisfies differential privacy, (ii) controls type I and type II error probabilities at specified levels
\alpha

\beta

The idea of our method is to privatize the classical SPRT by adding carefully calibrated noise to both the thresholds and test statistic.

and
\mathcal{D}_{Z}

and a parameter

\tau:=\min(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
with

\displaystyle\tau_{0}

\displaystyle:=\inf\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{Y_{n}}{n}\leq\mu_{0}+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})-\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\beta}\right)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}-\tfrac{1}{n}Z-C\left(n,(1-\gamma)\beta\right)\bigg\},

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle:=\inf\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{Y_{n}}{n}\geq\mu_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}})-\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\alpha}\right)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}+\tfrac{1}{n}Z+C\left(n,(1-\gamma)\alpha\right)\bigg\}

for queries with sensitivity

satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}(D)\|\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha-1/2}{\alpha-1}\varepsilon_{Z}(2\alpha)+\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)+\frac{\log\left(2\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\hat{d})\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|z]^{2}]\right)}{2(\alpha-1)}\;.

(2)

\bm{(ii)}
Correctness.
We establish correctness of
DP-SPRT
with the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(Correctness of
DP-SPRT
)
.

For any error allocation
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
DP-SPRT
is
(\alpha,\beta)
-correct if the noises and correction function satisfy:

\displaystyle\forall\delta\in(0,1),\ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>C\left(n,\delta\right)\right)\leq\delta\;.

(3)

The main idea is to decompose the error probabilities using a union bound into the error from the standard SPRT (bounded via a change-of-measure argument) and the error from privacy noise (controlled through the correction function). The parameter
\gamma
allocates the total error budget between these two sources. See Appendix
D
for the full proof.

\bm{(iii)}
Sample Complexity.

The following theorem provides a generic upper bound on the expected sample complexity for any noise distribution satisfying appropriate conditions.

Theorem 4

(Sample Complexity of
DP-SPRT
)
.

Let us assume (
3

\gamma\in(0,1)
and

satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+(1-\gamma)\beta+\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{(\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}}))^{4}}{2(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})^{2}}\right)}+N(\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\beta,\gamma)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\tau]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+(1-\gamma)\alpha+\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{(\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}}))^{4}}{2(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})^{2}}\right)}+N(\theta_{1},\theta_{0},\alpha,\gamma)

Comparison with a Lower Bound.

In Appendix
F
, we prove the following lower bound on the sample complexity of any
(\alpha,\beta)
-correct test that is
\varepsilon
-DP. This lower bound is valid for any distributions
\nu_{\theta}
, and follows from techniques used to prove lower bounds under DP constraints in the multi-armed bandit literature (e.g.,
Azize et al., 2023

Azize, 2024

Theorem 5

(Lower Bound for Private Sequential Tests)
.

Let
(\tau,\hat{d})
be an
\varepsilon
-differentially private test with
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\hat{d}=1)\leq\alpha
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(\hat{d}=0)\leq\beta
. Then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\geq\frac{\mathrm{kl}(\alpha,1-\beta)}{\min\left(\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}}),\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\right)}\text{ and }\ \mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\tau]\geq\frac{\mathrm{kl}(\beta,1-\alpha)}{\min\left(\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}}),\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\right)}

where
\mathrm{TV}
is the total variation distance between distributions and
\mathrm{kl}(x,y):=\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{B}(x),\mathcal{B}(y))=x\log(x/y)+(1-x)\log((1-x)/(1-y))
is the binary relative entropy.

We now compare this lower bound to our upper bound for Laplace
DP-SPRT
, in the asymptotic regime in which
\alpha
and
\beta
go to zero, for which we have
\mathrm{kl}(\alpha,1-\beta)\simeq\log(1/\beta)
and
\mathrm{kl}(\beta,1-\alpha)\simeq\log(1/\alpha)
. Combining Theorem
4
with Corollary
1
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{\beta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\tau\right]}{\log(1/\beta)}\leq 48\max\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})},\frac{(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})}{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\varepsilon}\right)\,,

where
\nu_{\theta_{i}}
is the Bernoulli distribution with natural parameter
\theta_{i}
and mean
p_{i}=\tfrac{e^{\theta_{i}}}{1+e^{\theta_{i}}}
. Using the simple expression of the TV for Bernoulli distributions in Theorem
5
and
p_{1}>p_{0}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\beta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\tau\right]}{\log(1/\beta)}\geq\max\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{kl}(p_{0},p_{1})},\frac{1}{(p_{1}-p_{0})\varepsilon}\right).

By definition, we have
\mathrm{kl}(p_{0},p_{1})=\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})
. As for the second term, using the expression of KL divergence for exponential families (see Eq. (
4
) in Appendix
A

{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})}/{(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})}=(b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0}))/(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})-p_{0}
where

is the log-partition function, which goes to
b^{\prime}(\theta_{1})-p_{0}=p_{1}-p_{0}
when
p_{1}
goes to
p_{0}
. Hence, when
p_{1}
and
p_{0}
are close, the upper and lower bounds are matching, up to a multiplicative constant. By symmetry, we can get similar nearly matching upper and lower bounds on
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\tau]/\log(1/\alpha)

with Laplace noise near-optimal in the regime of small errors and close hypotheses.

Privacy Amplification by Subsampling.

DP-SPRT
with Laplace noise can be further enhanced using subsampling
(
Steinke, 2022
)
. That is, in each round the empirical mean is computed based on a random subset of the available observations instead of all observations. This approach allows adding less noise for the same privacy guarantee, and in turn, a smaller correction factor. This is particularly effective in high privacy regimes as demonstrated in the experiments. Details of the implementation of this approach are provided in Appendix
G
.

7
Discussion and Future Work

We introduced
DP-SPRT
, an adaptation of the classical SPRT to incorporate various types of privacy protections in sequential tests of simple hypotheses. This general framework supports different noise mechanisms, such as Laplace noise for
\varepsilon
-differential privacy and Gaussian noise for Rényi differential privacy. Its design is based on a specialized threshold mechanism,
OutsideInterval
, that concurrently handles both decision boundaries instead of treating them separately. Notably,
DP-SPRT
with Laplace noise is the first
\varepsilon
-DP sequential test that can be calibrated to attain prescribed type I and type II errors without any hyper-parameter tuning. Moreover, for Bernoullis, its sample complexity is close to the lower bound that we have derived for the minimal sample complexity of any
\varepsilon
-DP test. In future work, we will seek to strengthen these optimality guarantees beyond Bernoulli, and extend our findings to composite hypothesis testing, for which
OutsideInterval
could also be useful.

Appendix A
A Primer on SPRT for Exponential Families

A one-dimensional canonical exponential family is a class of distributions

\mathcal{F}=\{(\nu_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}:\nu_{\theta}\text{ has a density }\ f_{\theta}(x)=\exp(\theta x-b(\theta))\text{ w.r.t. }\nu\},

where
\nu
is the reference measure,
\Theta
is the natural parameter space and
b:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a convex, twice differentiable function called the log-partition function.

For exponential families, it can be shown that
\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu_{\theta}}[X]
satisfies
\mu(\theta)=b^{\prime}(\theta)
, and
\sigma^{2}(\theta)=\mathrm{Var}_{X\sim\nu_{\theta}}[X]
satisfies
\sigma^{2}(\theta)=b^{\prime\prime}(\theta)
. As a consequence, the mean is an increasing function of the natural parameter, and there is a one-to-one mapping between mean and natural parameter.

We recall the expression of the KL divergence in an exponential family (see, e.g.,
Cappé et al., 2013
). For all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, we have

\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta},\nu_{\theta^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu_{\theta}}\left[\log\frac{f_{\theta}(X)}{f_{\theta^{\prime}}(X)}\right]=\mu(\theta)(\theta-\theta^{\prime})-b(\theta)+b(\theta^{\prime})\;.

(4)

Specific form of the SPRT.

The general SPRT

\tau=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})}{f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})}\notin\left(\beta,1/\alpha\right)\right\}

particularizes to

\tau=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\exp\left((\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-n(b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0}))\right)\notin\left(\beta,1/\alpha\right)\right\},

which can be re-written
\tau=\min(\tau_{0},\tau_{1})
with

\displaystyle\tau_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-n(b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0}))<\log(\beta)\right\},

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-n(b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0}))>\log(1/\alpha)\right\}.

Dividing by
\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}>0
and re-arranging the terms, we get

\displaystyle\tau_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}<\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log(1/\beta)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}\right\},

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}>\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}+\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log(1/\alpha)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}\right\}.

The expression given in Section
2
can be obtained by noting that
b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})=\mu_{0}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})+\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})
and
b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})=\mu_{1}(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})-\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}})
.

However, the above expression is also interesting, as it shows that the two thresholds

T_{0}^{n}=\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log(1/\beta)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}\text{ and }T_{1}^{n}=\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}+\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log(1/\alpha)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}

to which the empirical mean is compared indeed satisfies
T_{0}^{n}\leq T_{1}^{n}
.

DP-SPRT can thus be alternatively written

\displaystyle\tau_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{Y_{n}}{n}<\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log(1/\gamma\beta)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}-C(n,(1-\gamma)\beta)-\frac{Z}{n}\right\},

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{Y_{n}}{n}>\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}+\frac{\frac{1}{n}\log(1/\gamma\alpha)}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}+C(n,(1-\gamma)\alpha)+\frac{Z}{n}\right\}.

Bernoulli distributions.

Bernoulli distribution form a one-dimensional exponential family if we set the natural parameter of the Bernoulli distribution with probability of success
p_{i}
to be
\theta_{i}=\log\frac{p_{i}}{1-p_{i}}
. The log-partition function is
b(\theta)=\log(1+e^{\theta})
.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{1}

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[X_{i}\log\frac{p_{1}}{p_{0}}+(1-X_{i})\log\frac{1-p_{1}}{1-p_{0}}\right]_{-A}^{A}+Y_{n}^{2}<-a+Z^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{0}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\tau}_{1}

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}+\frac{Y_{n}^{2}}{n(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})}<\frac{b(\theta_{1})-b(\theta_{0})}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}-\frac{a}{n(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})}+\frac{Z^{2}}{n(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})}\right\}.

The differences with

are the following: (1) PrivSPRT uses

and

. (3) The noise is not scaled in the same way in both approaches.

We will use the following two lemmas in the proof of Theorem

Lemma 1

Let

and any neighboring datasets

e^{-\tilde{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\in S]^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\leq\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D)\in S]\leq e^{\tilde{\varepsilon}(\alpha)\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{M}(D^{\prime})\in S]^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha}}.

(5)

This second lemma is a direct consequence of the definition of differential privacy for noise-adding mechanisms with continuous noise.

Lemma 2
.

Let
M(D)=f(D)+Z
be a mechanism that adds noise
Z\in\mathbb{R}
with probability density function
p_{Z}
to the output of a query
f
. Let
\Delta
be the sensitivity of

\Delta=\max_{D\sim D^{\prime}}|f(D)-f(D^{\prime})|

denotes that

and
D^{\prime}
are adjacent datasets. If

\forall z\in\mathbb{R},\ {p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}\leq e^{\varepsilon}{p_{Z}(z)}\;.

Proof.

By definition,
M

\varepsilon
-differentially private if for all adjacent datasets
D\sim D^{\prime}
and all measurable sets

\Pr[M(D)\in S]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D^{\prime})\in S].

This must hold for all measurable sets

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ p_{M(D)}(x)\leq e^{\varepsilon}p_{M(D^{\prime})}(x)\;.

Since
p_{M(D)}(x)=p_{Z}(x-f(D))
and
p_{M(D^{\prime})}(x)=p_{Z}(x-f(D^{\prime}))
, we have, for all query
f
of sensitivity
\Delta
and for all neighboring
D
and
D^{\prime}

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ p_{Z}(x-f(D))\leq e^{\varepsilon}p_{Z}(x-f(D^{\prime}))\;.

Consider the worst case where
f(D)=f(D^{\prime})+\Delta
. Substituting we get

\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\ p_{Z}(x-f(D^{\prime})-\Delta)\leq e^{\varepsilon}p_{Z}(x-f(D^{\prime}))\;.

Letting

yields

\forall z\in\mathbb{R},\ p_{Z}(z-\Delta)\leq e^{\varepsilon}p_{Z}(z)\;.

\mathcal{A}
) satisfies:

\mathbb{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}(D)\|\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime}))\leq\frac{\alpha-(\gamma-1)/\gamma}{\alpha-1}\varepsilon_{Z}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\alpha\right)+\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)+\frac{\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[\mathbb{E}[\tau|z]^{\gamma}]\right)}{\gamma(\alpha-1)},

for any
\gamma>1
.

The proof is adapted from the one by
Zhu and Wang, 2020
for GeneralAboveThreshold.

Consider an arbitrary output of the algorithm
\mathbf{a}=(\bot,\dots,\bot,\top_{d})
with
\bot
appearing
k-1
times and
d\in\{0,1\}
indicating whether the
k
-th query falls below the lower threshold (
d=0
) or exceeds the upper threshold (
d=1
). Let

\displaystyle g_{i}(D,z)

\displaystyle=\Pr[f_{i}(D)+Y_{i}>T_{0}^{i}-z\land f_{i}(D)+Y_{i}<T_{1}^{i}+z],

\displaystyle h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\Pr[f_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]&\text{if }d=1\\
\Pr[f_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\leq T_{0}^{k}-z]&\text{if }d=0\end{cases},

where the randomness in
\Pr
is on the distribution of the
Y
s only. Letting
p_{Z}
be the pdf of
Z
, we have

\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{Z}(z)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D,z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z)\,dz\;.

Step 1: Bounding output probabilities.

Using a change of variable
z\to z-\Delta
:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{Z}(z-\Delta)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D,z-\Delta)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\,dz

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}p_{Z}(z)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D,z-\Delta)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\,dz.

Note that
p_{Z}(z-\Delta)/p_{Z}(z)
is well-defined for DP or RDP mechanism, as from Lemma
1
or Lemma
2
it can be shown that the density has to be supported on
\mathbb{R}
.

For queries before
k
, we establish an upper bound on
g_{i}(D,z-\Delta)
through inequality analysis. For any
i<k
, we have:

\displaystyle g_{i}(D,z-\Delta)

\displaystyle=\Pr[f_{i}(D)+Y_{i}>T_{0}^{i}-(z-\Delta)\land f_{i}(D)+Y_{i}<T_{1}^{i}+(z-\Delta)].

Since
f_{i}
has sensitivity
\Delta
, we know that
|f_{i}(D)-f_{i}(D^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
. This gives us two inequalities:

\displaystyle f_{i}(D)

\displaystyle\leq f_{i}(D^{\prime})+\Delta,

\displaystyle f_{i}(D)

\displaystyle\geq f_{i}(D^{\prime})-\Delta.

For the first inequality condition, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr[f_{i}(D)+Y_{i}>T_{0}^{i}-(z-\Delta)]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[f_{i}(D^{\prime})+\Delta+Y_{i}>T_{0}^{i}-(z-\Delta)]

\displaystyle=\Pr[f_{i}(D^{\prime})+Y_{i}>T_{0}^{i}-z].

For the second inequality condition:

\displaystyle\Pr[f_{i}(D)+Y_{i}<T_{1}^{i}+(z-\Delta)]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[f_{i}(D^{\prime})-\Delta+Y_{i}<T_{1}^{i}+(z-\Delta)]

\displaystyle=\Pr[f_{i}(D^{\prime})+Y_{i}<T_{1}^{i}+z].

Since both conditions in the conjunction are upper-bounded, we obtain:

\displaystyle g_{i}(D,z-\Delta)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[f_{i}(D^{\prime})+Y_{i}>T_{0}^{i}-z\land f_{i}(D^{\prime})+Y_{i}<T_{1}^{i}+z]

\displaystyle=g_{i}(D^{\prime},z).

Therefore:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}p_{Z}(z)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\,dz

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\right]\;.

(6)

With our notation, we can also write

\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)\right]\;.

(7)

We will now use the properties of the private mechanism to relate the two probabilities. First we will prove the results when the mechanism is
\varepsilon
-DP.

B.1
\varepsilon
-DP Guarantee

Proof of Theorem
1
(i).

We have established in Equation (
6
) that for any output
\mathbf{a}=(\bot,\ldots,\bot,\top_{d})
with
\bot
appearing
k-1
times:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\right].

Step 2: Bounding the density ratio and stopping time query.

Since the noise-adding mechanism corresponding to
\mathcal{D}_{Z}
satisfies
\varepsilon_{Z}
-DP for queries with sensitivity
\Delta
, by Lemma
2
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Z}}\;.

For
d\in\{0,1\}
, we need to establish a bound on
h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)
in terms of
h_{k}^{(d)}(D^{\prime},z)
. We first consider the case
d=1
(upper threshold crossed).

Let us define a new query:

\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime\prime})=\begin{cases}f_{k}(D)+\Delta&\text{if }D^{\prime\prime}=D\\
f_{k}(D^{\prime})&\text{if }D^{\prime\prime}=D^{\prime}\end{cases}.

To establish the sensitivity of
\tilde{f}_{k}
, we note that since
f_{k}
has sensitivity
\Delta
, we have
|f_{k}(D)-f_{k}(D^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
. Thus:

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}_{k}(D)-\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|f_{k}(D)+\Delta-f_{k}(D^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{k}(D)-f_{k}(D^{\prime})|+\Delta

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+\Delta=2\Delta.

Therefore,
\tilde{f}_{k}
has sensitivity
2\Delta
. Now, we can rewrite:

\displaystyle h_{k}^{(1)}(D,z-\Delta)

\displaystyle=\Pr[f_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+(z-\Delta)]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D)-\Delta+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+(z-\Delta)]

\displaystyle=\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z].

Similarly,
h_{k}^{(1)}(D^{\prime},z)=\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]
.

Since the noise-adding mechanism corresponding to
\mathcal{D}_{Y}
satisfies
\varepsilon_{Y}
-DP for queries with sensitivity
2\Delta
, we can apply the privacy guarantee:

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}}\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z].

Therefore:
h_{k}^{(1)}(D,z-\Delta)\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}}h_{k}^{(1)}(D^{\prime},z)
.

A similar analysis for
d=0
(lower threshold crossed) would define
\tilde{f}_{k}(D)=f_{k}(D)-\Delta
and
\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})=f_{k}(D^{\prime})
, yielding the same bound:
h_{k}^{(0)}(D,z-\Delta)\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}}h_{k}^{(0)}(D^{\prime},z)
.

Step 3: Deriving the final DP bound.

Using these inequalities in our original expression:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[e^{\varepsilon_{Z}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[e^{\varepsilon_{Z}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot e^{\varepsilon_{Y}}h_{k}^{(d)}(D^{\prime},z)\right]

\displaystyle=e^{\varepsilon_{Z}+\varepsilon_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D^{\prime},z)\right].

From Equation (
7
), we recognize that the expectation in the last line equals
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]
, giving us:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Z}+\varepsilon_{Y}}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}].

By symmetry of the definitions of neighboring datasets, we can also show that
\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Z}+\varepsilon_{Y}}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]
.

Since this bound holds for any output
\mathbf{a}
, we have established that
OutsideInterval
satisfies
(\varepsilon_{Z}+\varepsilon_{Y})
-differential privacy.
∎

B.2
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-Rényi DP Guarantee

Proof of Theorem
1
(ii).

Starting again from Equation (
6
), we proceed with proving the RDP guarantee.

Step 2: Computing the Rényi divergence and applying Jensen’s inequality.

Let us consider the expectation in the definition of Rényi divergence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{O}}\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\right]^{\alpha}}{\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)\right]^{\alpha-1}},

where we obtain the inequality using Equations (
6
) and (
7
).

Let us consider the bivariate function
\varphi_{\alpha}:\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as
\varphi_{\alpha}(x,y)=\frac{x^{\alpha}}{y^{\alpha-1}}
. Notice that for any
\alpha>1
,
\varphi_{\alpha}
is jointly convex over
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. By Jensen’s inequality, we have
\varphi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{E}[X],\mathbb{E}[Y])\leq\mathbb{E}[\varphi_{\alpha}(X,Y)]
for any positive random variables
X
and
Y
. Applying this inequality to our case:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d\in\{0,1\}}\frac{\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)\right)^{\alpha-1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\hat{d})\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\left.\frac{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\tau-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h_{\tau}^{(\hat{d})}(D,z-\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{\tau-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h^{(\hat{d})}_{\tau}(D^{\prime},z)\right)^{\alpha}}\right|Z=z\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\hat{d})\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\left.\frac{\left(h_{\tau}^{(\hat{d})}(D,z-\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(h^{(\hat{d})}_{\tau}(D^{\prime},z)\right)^{\alpha}}\right|Z=z\right]\right].

Step 3: Applying RDP guarantees and relating to stopping time.

We now establish a relationship between
h_{k}^{(d)}(D,z-\Delta)
and
h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)
under Rényi DP for any
d\in\{0,1\}
and
k>0
. We prove that

\displaystyle\frac{\left(h^{(d)}_{k}(D,z-\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)\right)^{\alpha-1}}\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)(\alpha-1)}\;.

(8)

To prove it for
d=1
(the upper threshold case) we introduce the query

\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime\prime})=\begin{cases}f_{k}(D)+\Delta&\text{if }D^{\prime\prime}=D\\
f_{k}(D^{\prime})&\text{if }D^{\prime\prime}=D^{\prime}\;.\end{cases}

To establish the sensitivity of
\tilde{f}_{k}
, we note that since
f_{k}
has sensitivity
\Delta
, we have
|f_{k}(D)-f_{k}(D^{\prime})|\leq\Delta
. Thus:

\displaystyle|\tilde{f}_{k}(D)-\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=|f_{k}(D)+\Delta-f_{k}(D^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|f_{k}(D)-f_{k}(D^{\prime})|+\Delta

\displaystyle\leq\Delta+\Delta=2\Delta.

Therefore,
\tilde{f}_{k}
has sensitivity
2\Delta
. Applying the indistinguishability property of Rényi DP (Lemma
1
) to the noise-adding mechanism with noise distribution
Y_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(\Pr[\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]\right)^{\alpha-1}}\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)(\alpha-1)}.

Since
\tilde{f}_{k}(D)+Y_{k}=f_{k}(D)+\Delta+Y_{k}
and
\tilde{f}_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}=f_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\Pr[f_{k}(D)+\Delta+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(\Pr[f_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]\right)^{\alpha-1}}\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)(\alpha-1)}.

Since
f_{k}(D)+\Delta+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z
is equivalent to
f_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+(z-\Delta)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\Pr[f_{k}(D)+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+(z-\Delta)]\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(\Pr[f_{k}(D^{\prime})+Y_{k}\geq T_{1}^{k}+z]\right)^{\alpha-1}}\leq e^{\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)(\alpha-1)},

which yields (
8
) for
d=1
. The proof for
d=0
follows the same lines.

Using Equation (
8
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\hat{d})\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\left.\frac{\left(h_{\tau}^{(\hat{d})}(D,z-\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(h^{(\hat{d})}_{\tau}(D^{\prime},z)\right)^{\alpha}}\right|Z=z\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{(\tau,\hat{d})\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\left.\frac{e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}}{\left(h^{(\hat{d})}_{\tau}(D^{\prime},z)\right)}\right|Z=z\right]

\displaystyle=e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d\in\{0,1\}}\mathbb{P}_{(\tau,\hat{d})\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}(\tau=k,\hat{d}=d|Z=z)\frac{1}{h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)}

\displaystyle=e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{d\in\{0,1\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)\cdot\frac{1}{h^{(d)}_{k}(D^{\prime},z)}

\displaystyle=2e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z).

To relate this sum to an expectation, we note that
\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)
represents the probability that the machanism
\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})
outputs
\bot
for each of the first
k-1
queries, which means the stopping time
\tau
is at least
k
. Therefore:

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}(\tau\geq k|Z=z).

Using that for a positive integer-valued random variable
X
, we can express its expectation using
\mathbb{E}[X]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pr(X\geq k)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g_{i}(D^{\prime},z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}(\tau\geq k|Z=z)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z].

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\left.\frac{\left(h_{\tau}^{(\hat{d})}(D,z-\Delta)\right)^{\alpha}}{\left(h^{(\hat{d})}_{\tau}(D^{\prime},z)\right)^{\alpha}}\right|Z=z\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z].

Step 4: Combining results and applying Hölder’s inequality.

Now we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[2\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z]\cdot e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}\right].

We apply Hölder’s inequality to separate the terms involving
Z
and
\tau
. For any
\gamma>1
, let
\gamma^{*}
be its Hölder conjugate such that
\frac{1}{\gamma^{*}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}=1
. Let
X=\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha}
and
Y=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z]
. By Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[X\cdot Y]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[X^{\gamma^{*}}]\right)^{1/\gamma^{*}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[Y^{\gamma}]\right)^{1/\gamma}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha\gamma^{*}}\right]\right)^{1/\gamma^{*}}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[\mathbb{E}[\tau|Z=z]^{\gamma}]\right)^{1/\gamma}.

Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}\left[\frac{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D)=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}{\Pr[\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})=\mathbf{a}]^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha\gamma^{*}}\right]\right)^{1/\gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z]^{\gamma}]\right)^{1/\gamma}.

The first expectation term is related to the RDP guarantee of the noise-adding mechanism with distribution
\mathcal{D}_{Z}
. Specifically, since this mechanism satisfies
\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\gamma^{*})
-RDP for queries with sensitivity
\Delta
, we have:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}\left[\left(\frac{p_{Z}(z-\Delta)}{p_{Z}(z)}\right)^{\alpha\gamma^{*}}\right]\right)^{1/\gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(e^{(\alpha\gamma^{*}-1)\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\gamma^{*})}\right)^{1/\gamma^{*}}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\alpha\gamma^{*}-1}{\gamma^{*}}\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\gamma^{*})}.

Substituting
\gamma^{*}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}
(from the Hölder conjugate relation), and simplifying the exponent through algebraic manipulations:

\displaystyle e^{\frac{\alpha\gamma^{*}-1}{\gamma^{*}}\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\gamma^{*})}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\alpha\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}-1}{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1})}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{(\alpha\gamma-\alpha+1)(\gamma-1)}{\gamma}\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1})}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{(\alpha-1)\gamma-\alpha+1}{\gamma}\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1})}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{(\alpha-1)-\frac{\alpha-1}{\gamma}}{\alpha-1}(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1})}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{\alpha-(\gamma-1)/\gamma}{\alpha-1}(\alpha-1)\varepsilon_{Z}(\alpha\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1})}.

Taking logarithms and dividing by
(\alpha-1)
, we obtain the bound:

\displaystyle{D}_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}(D)\|\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime}))\leq\frac{\alpha-(\gamma-1)/\gamma}{\alpha-1}\varepsilon_{Z}\left(\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\alpha\right)+\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)+\frac{\log\left(2\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z]^{\gamma}]\right)}{\gamma(\alpha-1)}.

In particular, if we choose
\gamma=2
then we have:

D_{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}(D)\|\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime}))\leq\frac{\alpha-1/2}{\alpha-1}\varepsilon_{Z}(2\alpha)+\varepsilon_{Y}(\alpha)+\frac{\log\left(2\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{Z}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})}[\tau|Z=z]^{2}]\right)}{2(\alpha-1)}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1
(ii).
∎

Appendix C
The Variants of
DP-SPRT
: Laplace and Gaussian Noises

C.1
DP-SPRT
with Laplace Noise

For this variant, we use the following noise distributions and correction function:

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\text{Lap}\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right),

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\sim\text{Lap}\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right),

\displaystyle C(n,\delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{n\varepsilon}.

We now prove that this choice satisfies the condition in Equation (
3
):

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>C\left(n,\delta\right)\right)\leq\delta.

First, we rewrite the inequality inside the probability:

\displaystyle\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}

\displaystyle>\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{n\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow Y_{n}-Z

\displaystyle>\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}.

Using the union bound, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}-Z>\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}>\frac{4\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(Z<-\frac{2\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right).

For Laplace random variables, we have the tail bounds:

\displaystyle\text{If }X\sim\text{Lap}(b),\text{ then }\mathbb{P}(X\geq t)=\mathbb{P}(X\leq-t)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-t/b}.

Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}>\frac{4\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{4\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\delta}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2n^{s}\zeta(s)}.

Similarly:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z<-\frac{2\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{2\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{-\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{2n^{s}\zeta(s)}.

Combining these results:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}-Z>\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}{n\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{2n^{s}\zeta(s)}+\frac{\delta}{2n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}.

Summing over all
n
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>C\left(n,\delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\delta,

where we used the definition of the Riemann zeta function:
\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}
.

C.2
DP-SPRT
with Gaussian Noise

For this variant, we use the following noise distributions and correction function:

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{Y}^{2}),

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{Z}^{2}),

\displaystyle C(n,\delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}}{n}.

We now prove that this choice satisfies the condition in Equation (
3
):

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>C\left(n,\delta\right)\right)\leq\delta.

Since
Y_{n}
and
Z
are independent Gaussian random variables, their difference is also Gaussian. Specifically:

\displaystyle Y_{n}-Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2}).

The probability we need to bound is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>\frac{\sqrt{2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}}{n}\right).

Multiplying both sides by
n
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}-Z>\sqrt{2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}\right).

For a Gaussian random variable
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
, we have the standard tail bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X>t)\leq e^{-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}.

Applying this bound with
X=Y_{n}-Z
,
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2}
, and
t=\sqrt{2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{n}-Z>t)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t^{2}/2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})}

\displaystyle=e^{-2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)/2(\sigma_{Y}^{2}+\sigma_{Z}^{2})}

\displaystyle=e^{-\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}.

Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>C\left(n,\delta\right)\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}.

Summing over all
n
:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z}{n}>C\left(n,\delta\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\delta\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\frac{1}{\zeta(s)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}

\displaystyle=\delta\cdot\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=\delta.

This demonstrates that the Gaussian noise implementation with the specified correction function satisfies condition (
3
).

Appendix D
Correctness of
DP-SPRT
for Exponential Families: Proof of Theorem
3

In this appendix, we provide the detailed proof of Theorem
3
, which establishes the error guarantees of our
DP-SPRT
. This result is central to our paper as it formally demonstrates that the algorithm achieves the desired privacy-utility trade-off while maintaining statistical validity.

Proof.

We prove the bound on type I error; the type II error follows analogously. Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
be an arbitrary parameter that allocates the error probability.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\big(\hat{d}=1\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\big(\tau_{1}<\infty\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\bigg(\exists n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}-\mu_{1}+\frac{Y_{n}}{n}\geq

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{-\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}})+\log(1/(\gamma\alpha))/n}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}+\frac{Z}{n}+C\left(n,\frac{(1-\gamma)\alpha}{3}\right)\bigg).

By the union bound, we can separate this into two events:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\big(\hat{d}=1\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\bigg(\exists n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\ \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}-\mu_{1}\geq\frac{-\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}})+\log(1/(\gamma\alpha))/n}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\bigg(\exists n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\ \frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z_{1}}{n}\geq C\left(n,(1-\gamma)\alpha\right)\bigg).

For the first term, we can recognize the probability of eventually rejecting
H_{0}
in the non-private SPRT. Let us define
\tau_{\text{SPRT}}
as the first time the likelihood ratio exceeds the threshold for rejecting
H_{0}
:

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{SPRT}}=\inf\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\log\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})}{f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})}\bigg)\geq\log\bigg(\frac{1}{\gamma\alpha}\bigg)\bigg\}.

For exponential families, this is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\tau_{\text{SPRT}}=\inf\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{n}-\mu_{1}\geq\frac{-\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}})+\log(1/(\gamma\alpha))/n}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}\bigg\}.

Thus, the first term equals
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty)
, which is the probability under
\theta_{0}
that we eventually reject
H_{0}
in the non-private SPRT. Let
L_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})}{f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})}
be the likelihood ratio.

Applying Wald’s likelihood ratio identity (i.e., a change-of-measure argument) to this specific stopping event, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}\left[\frac{1}{L_{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty\}}\right].

By definition of
\tau_{\text{SPRT}}
, on the event
\{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty\}
, we have
L_{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}}\geq\frac{1}{\gamma\alpha}
, which implies
\frac{1}{L_{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}}}\leq\gamma\alpha
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}\left[\frac{1}{L_{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}\left[\gamma\alpha\cdot\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma\alpha\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(\tau_{\text{SPRT}}<\infty)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\alpha.

For the second term, using the condition on the correction function and a union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\bigg(\exists n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:\ \frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z_{1}}{n}\geq C\left(n,(1-\gamma)\alpha\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\bigg(\frac{Y_{n}}{n}-\frac{Z_{1}}{n}\geq C\left(n,(1-\gamma)\alpha\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\gamma)\alpha}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}\zeta(s)}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\alpha.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}\big(\hat{d}=1\big)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\alpha+(1-\gamma)\alpha

\displaystyle=\alpha.

∎

Appendix F
Lower Bound: Proof of Theorem
5

As the proof could generalize to other than Bernoulli distributions, we denote by
\nu_{\theta_{0}}
(resp.
\nu_{\theta_{1}}
) the distribution of each
X_{i}
under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
(resp.
\mathcal{H}_{1}
) and by
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
the probability space under which
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is an iid stream distribution under
\nu_{\theta_{0}}
(resp.
\nu_{\theta_{1}}
).

We let
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}}^{Y}
denote the distribution of a random vector
Y
under the probability model
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}}
. To ease the notation, we will sometimes denote by
X_{1:t}
the random vector
(X_{1},\dots,X_{t})
.

We establish the lower bound by combining information-theoretic inequalities with privacy constraints. Let
(\tau,d)
be a
(\alpha,\beta)
-correct test. We assume that
\tau
is almost surely finite under both
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}
, otherwise any lower bound on its expectation is trivially true.

Step 1: Lower bounding a KL divergence for any
(\alpha,\beta)
-correct test.

Using the data processing inequality, one can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{\mathds{1}(\hat{d}=1)},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{\mathds{1}(\hat{d}=1)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\hat{d}=1),\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(\hat{d}=1)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}(\alpha,1-\beta),

where the last step uses the correctness of the test and monotonicity properties of the Bernoulli KL divergence,
\mathrm{kl}(x,y)=x\log(x/y)+(1-x)\log((1-x)/(1-y))
.

This argument is quite common to prove lower bounds on regret or sample complexity in the multi-armed bandit literature, and was popularized by, e.g.
Garivier et al., 2019
;
Garivier and Kaufmann, 2016
.

Step 2: Computing the KL for any test.
Still using standard arguments, we can establish the following equality:

\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\;.

(9)

For completeness, we provide the proof of this result.
Assuming
\nu_{\theta_{i}}
has a density
f_{\theta_{i}}
with respect to a common reference measure, we can express:

\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\log\prod_{i=1}^{\tau}\frac{f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})}{f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\log\frac{f_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})}{f_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})}\right].

Let
Z_{i}=\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(X_{i})}{\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(X_{i})}
. Since the observations are i.i.d., each
Z_{i}
has mean:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[Z_{i}]=\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})
. By Wald’s identity
Wald, 1945
(as
\tau
has finite expectation), we have

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}Z_{i}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[Z_{1}]=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}},\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}})\;,

and we obtain (
9
).

Step 3: Upper Bounding the KL for Private Tests.
Using the privacy constraints, we now prove that for an
\varepsilon
-DP test we further have

\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}\right)\leq\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\;.

(10)

The argument is similar to the proof of Theorem 4.9 from
Azize, 2024
, which applies to a more complex bandit model but with a finite horizon
T
. The idea is to build a coupling between the data collected under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}
.

Specifically, we let
\underline{\nu}
be the maximal coupling between
\nu_{\theta_{0}}
and
\nu_{\theta_{1}}
:
(X,X^{\prime})\sim\underline{\nu}
is such that the marginal distribution of
X
is
\nu_{\theta_{0}}
, the marginal distribution of
X^{\prime}
is
\nu_{\theta_{1}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{(X,X^{\prime})\sim\underline{\nu}}[\mathds{1}(X\neq X^{\prime})]=\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})
. We now define the probability space
\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}
in which we collect iid pairs
(X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i})\sim\underline{\nu}
and let
(\tau,\hat{d})
be the stopping and decision rules of the test based on the iid stream
X_{i}
(distributed under
\nu_{\theta_{0}}
) and
(\tau^{\prime},\hat{d}^{\prime})
be the stopping and decision rules of the test based on the iid stream
X^{\prime}_{i}
(distributed under
\nu_{\theta_{1}}
).

We observe that the marginal distribution of
(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})
in the probability space
\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}
is
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}
while that of
(X^{\prime}_{1:\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime},\hat{d}^{\prime})
is
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}
. Using again the data-processing inequality,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}\right)\leq\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\underline{\nu}}^{(X_{1:\tau},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\underline{\nu}}^{(X_{1:\tau^{\prime}},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime},\hat{d}^{\prime})}\right)\;.

To compute this last KL-divergence, for clarity we will consider the special case of Bernoulli distributions that is the main focus of this paper. In that case the distribution of
(X_{1:\tau},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})
and
(X_{1:\tau^{\prime}},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime},\hat{d}^{\prime})
are discrete and we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\underline{\nu}}^{(X_{1:\tau},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\underline{\nu}}^{(X_{1:\tau^{\prime}},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime},\hat{d}^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T>0,d\in\{0,1\}\\
x_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\\
x^{\prime}_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(X_{1:T}=x_{1:T},X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T},\tau=T,\hat{d}=d\right)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(X_{1:T}=x_{1:T},X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T},\tau=T,\hat{d}=d\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(X_{1:T}=x_{1:T},X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T},\tau^{\prime}=T,\hat{d}^{\prime}=d\right)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T>0,d\in\{0,1\}\\
x_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\\
x^{\prime}_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(X_{1:T}=x_{1:T},X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T},\tau=T,\hat{d}=d\right)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(\tau=T,\hat{d}=d|X_{1:T}=x_{1:T}\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(\tau^{\prime}=T,\hat{d}^{\prime}=d|X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T}\right)}.

Using the fact that the test is
\varepsilon
-DP we obtain by the group-privacy property that

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(\tau=T,\hat{d}=d|X_{1:T}=x_{1:T}\right)}{\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(\tau^{\prime}=T,\hat{d}^{\prime}=d|X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T}\right)}\leq e^{\varepsilon\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathds{1}(x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i})},

hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\underline{\nu}}^{(X_{1:\tau},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\underline{\nu}}^{(X_{1:\tau^{\prime}},X^{\prime}_{1:\tau^{\prime}},\tau^{\prime},\hat{d}^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}T>0,d\in\{0,1\}\\
x_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\\
x^{\prime}_{1:T}\in\{0,1\}^{T}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}_{\underline{\nu}}\left(X_{1:T}=x_{1:T},X^{\prime}_{1:T}=x^{\prime}_{1:T},\tau=T,\hat{d}=d\right)\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{T}\mathds{1}(x_{i}\neq x^{\prime}_{i})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\nu}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\mathds{1}(X_{i}\neq X_{i}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\nu}}\left[\tau\right]\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\left[\tau\right]\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}}),

where the last but one step uses Wald’s equality and the fact that
\mathbb{E}_{\underline{\nu}}\left[\mathds{1}(X_{i}\neq X_{i}^{\prime})\right]=\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})
by definition of the coupling. This proves (
10
).

Step 4: Putting things together.

Combining the lower bound with (
9
) and the private upper bound (
10
), we have

\mathrm{kl}(\alpha,1-\beta)\leq\mathrm{KL}\left({\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{0}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})},{\mathbb{P}}_{\theta_{1}}^{(X_{1:\tau},\tau,\hat{d})}\right)\leq\min\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}}),\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\right).

Rearranging, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau]\geq\frac{\mathrm{kl}(\alpha,1-\beta)}{\min\left(\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}}),\varepsilon\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\right)}\;.

The lower bound on
\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{1}}[\tau]
follows in the same way by swapping the roles of
\theta_{0}
and
\theta_{1}
.

Appendix G
Enhancing
DP-SPRT
with Subsampling

Privacy amplification by subsampling
(
Steinke, 2022
)
provides an approach to enhance the privacy-utility trade-off in adifferentially private algorithms. When a dataset is randomly subsampled before applying a private mechanism, the effective privacy cost can be reduced roughly proportionally to the sampling rate for pure differentially private mechanisms.

In our DP-SPRT context, subsampling allows us to improve privacy for a given noise level, or equivalently, to maintain the same level of privacy while requiring less noise. At each step when a new observation
X_{i}
arrives, we implement Bernoulli subsampling by including it in our computation with probability
r\in(0,1)
, independent of all previous decisions. We maintain a running sum
S_{n}
and count
M_{n}
of the included observations:

1.

For each arriving observation
X_{i}
, include it with probability
r
(draw
B_{i}\sim\text{Bernoulli}(r)
independently);

2.

Update
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}X_{i}
and
M_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}
;

3.

Compute the subsampled empirical mean
\bar{X}^{r}_{n}=S_{n}/M_{n}
when
M_{n}>0
.

We chose this Bernoulli subsampling approach over alternatives like sampling without replacement because it requires only constant space and time complexity at each time step and thus can directly handle streaming observations with limited memory, providing significant computational advantages for long trials or challenging problem instances.

We incorporate subsampling into our
DP-SPRT
with Laplace noise by using noise scaled for
\varepsilon/r
-DP, since subsampling with rate
r
amplifies privacy by this factor, resulting in
\varepsilon
-DP overall. The subsampling mechanism is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tau^{\varepsilon,r}

\displaystyle=\min(\tau_{0}^{\varepsilon,r},\tau_{1}^{\varepsilon,r})

\displaystyle\tau_{0}^{\varepsilon,r}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bar{X}^{r}_{n}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\frac{Y_{n}}{n}\leq\mu_{0}+\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})-\log(1/(\gamma\beta))/{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}M_{n}}}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\frac{Z}{n}-{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/(1-\gamma)\beta)}{n\varepsilon}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\tau_{1}^{\varepsilon,r}

\displaystyle=\inf\bigg\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}:{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bar{X}^{r}_{n}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\frac{Y_{n}}{n}\geq\mu_{1}+\frac{-\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{1}},\nu_{\theta_{0}})+\log(1/(\gamma\alpha))/{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}M_{n}}}{\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\frac{Z}{n}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}r}\frac{6\log(n^{s}\zeta(s)/(1-\gamma)\alpha)}{n\varepsilon}\bigg\},

where
M_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}
is the actual count of included observations,
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\bar{X}^{r}_{n}=S_{n}/M_{n}}
is the subsampled empirical mean, and
Y_{n}\sim\text{Lap}(4/\varepsilon)
,
Z\sim\text{Lap}(2/\varepsilon)
as in standard DP-SPRT. The decision rule remains
\hat{d}^{\varepsilon,r}=i
if
\tau^{\varepsilon,r}=\tau_{i}^{\varepsilon,r}
. The terms in
red
are the modifications compared to the standard
DP-SPRT
algorithm.

Privacy and Correctness:
The subsampled DP-SPRT maintains
\varepsilon
-differential privacy and satisfies the error bounds
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}(\hat{d}^{\varepsilon,r}=1)\leq\alpha
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{1}}(\hat{d}^{\varepsilon,r}=0)\leq\beta
. The privacy follows from the subsampling amplification applied to the standard DP-SPRT analysis, while correctness uses appropriately scaled thresholds.

Sample Complexity:
For the asymptotic regime where
\alpha,\beta\to 0
, the expected stopping time will be of the form:

\displaystyle\limsup_{\beta\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}[\tau_{0}^{\varepsilon,r}]}{\log(1/\beta)}\leq 48\max\left(\frac{1}{r\cdot\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})},\frac{r\cdot(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})}{\mathrm{KL}(\nu_{\theta_{0}},\nu_{\theta_{1}})\varepsilon}\right).

This bound reveals a fundamental trade-off: smaller subsampling rates
r
lead to worse statistical efficiency—the first term
1/(r\cdot\mathrm{KL})
increases due to higher variance of the subsampled empirical mean—but provide better privacy amplification as the second term
r\cdot(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})/(\mathrm{KL}\cdot\varepsilon)
decreases, reducing the impact of privacy noise. The optimal sampling rate depends on which effect dominates, determined by the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
and how hard the hypotheses are to distinguish. When
\varepsilon
is small and high privacy is required, aggressive subsampling with small
r
can be beneficial as privacy amplification becomes more valuable. When
\varepsilon
is large and privacy is relaxed, less aggressive subsampling is preferable to maintain statistical efficiency. As demonstrated in our experiments (Section
6
), this trade-off is particularly pronounced in high-privacy regimes.

For practical applications, the bound suggests setting the sampling rate as
r\approx\min(1,\sqrt{\varepsilon/(\theta_{1}-\theta_{0})})
to balance the statistical and privacy terms. However, in our experiments we used the arbitrary choice
r=\min(1,\sqrt{\varepsilon/10})
, which already worked very well in practice for the range of parameter values we considered.

H.4

Our theoretical correction function

guarantees desired error probabilities but may be conservative. We explored a tuned version by scaling the function by the factor that maintains error probability targets, estimated through simulation.

The tuning process involves finding a scaling factor
\kappa\in(0,1)
to replace
C(n,\delta)
with
\kappa\cdot C(n,\delta)

\varepsilon=1
and
\alpha=\beta=0.1
, we found
\kappa\approx 0.5
maintains error probabilities below target. However, like PrivSPRT’s threshold, this value must be recomputed for new parameters.

H.5
Summary of Findings

Our complementary experiments reveal several important insights:

1.

The advantage of our
DP-SPRT
methods becomes more pronounced as problem difficulty increases, making them particularly valuable for challenging testing scenarios with parameters that are close to each other.

2.

All
DP-SPRT
variants exhibit lower variance in stopping times compared to PrivSPRT, leading to more predictable performance.

3.

In asymmetric scenarios (e.g., Instance 3 with parameters near boundaries), decisions under different hypotheses require substantially different sample sizes for PrivSPRT. Our method is less sensitive to this asymmetry.

4.

The subsampling approach provides substantial benefits in high-privacy regimes (
\varepsilon=0.1
).

5.

Our theoretical correction function is conservative, and a tuned version scaling the function by a factor
\kappa\approx 0.5
can significantly improve sample complexity while maintaining error guarantees.
