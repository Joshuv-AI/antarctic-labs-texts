Bifidobacterium breve207-1 protects against later-life colitis following early-life antibiotic exposure by enhancing intestinal barrier and immune homeostasis
Authors: Xiaolin Xu, Liang Li, Xiaoting Li, Yunyi Wang, Minghao Huang, Huajiao Wu, Huijing Liang, Xiaolei Ze, Fang He, Xi Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe early colonization of bacteria has a significant impact on immune system development. The disruption of gut microbiota by antibiotics in early life is a major risk factor for pediatric inflammatory bowel disease. This study explores how Bifidobacterium breve 207-1 modulates gut microbiota and immune responses after early antibiotic exposure, and whether such intervention mitigates DSS-induced colitis later in life.MethodsNewborn mice were administered with normal saline, ceftriaxone, or B. breve 207-1 from birth to day 21. The Ceftri + 207-1 + DSS group continued to receive B. breve 207-1 thereafter. Starting at day 42, all “-DSS” groups received 3% DSS drinking water to induce colitis.ResultsDSS increased inflammation scores versus the NS-Water group. The scores of the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS and Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS (the group receiving B. breve 207-1 continuously from birth to day 46) groups were lower than the Ceftri-DSS group (P < 0.0001). Gut microbiota analysis showed the relative abundance of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes was significantly higher in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group (P < 0.05). Long-term use of B. breve 207-1 from early life enhanced the relative abundance of beneficial gut bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus spp. (P < 0.05). Compared to the NS-DSS group, the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group exhibited significantly higher concentrations of butyric and valeric acids (P < 0.05). Treatment with B. breve 207-1 during early life and its long-term usage resulted in increased levels of SCFAs (P < 0.05). Furthermore, these treatments enhanced the indices related to changes in the mucosal barrier (including mRNA for KI67 and MUC2) and colonic mechanical barrier (including mRNA for Claudin-1, ZO-1, and Occludin). With regards to systemic immune changes induced by B. breve 207-1 administration during early life, the anti-inflammatory cytokines (particularly IL-10 and TGF-β) were elevated (P < 0.05), while the pro-inflammatory cytokines (mainly IL-6 and TNF-α) were reduced (P < 0.05).ConclusionsB. breve 207-1 enhances immune development after antibiotic exposure, mitigates the severity of DSS-induced colitis later in life, likely through early remodeling of the gut microbiota and enhancement of intestinal barrier function. Our results suggest that the early postnatal period represents a strategic opportunity for probiotic intervention to confer protection against later-life colitis in the context of early antibiotic exposure. Notably, B. breve 207-1 may influence the composition of early-stage gut microbiota and subsequently affect local and systemic immune development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04953-9.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that affects the gastrointestinal tract and extra-intestinal organs, encompassing various clinical-histological variants including ulcerative colitis (UC), Crohn’s disease (CD), and indeterminate colitis [1–3]. The prevalence of IBD has risen globally, with nearly 30% of cases occurring in children and adolescents [4], leading to significant financial burdens on healthcare systems [5, 6]. In China, the age-standardized incidence of IBD varies from 1.77 per 100,000 to 3.14 per 100,000 [7, 8], while the incidence of IBD in Chinese children has risen from 5 per 100,000 in 2001 to 6 per 1,000,000 in 2010 [9]. Children with IBD are primarily treated via the induction and maintenance of remission [10]. Nevertheless, the pathogenesis underlying IBD remains poorly understood. Thus, further investigation into the development and treatment of IBD is warranted.

The establishment of the gut microbiota in early life plays a pivotal role in shaping IBD risk [11], yet this process is highly vulnerable to disruption by antibiotics, particularly in neonates frequently exposed to broad-spectrum agents [12–17]. A nested case-control study found that 58% of children with IBD received antibiotics in their first year of life, compared to only 39% of controls [18]. This association underscores the susceptibility of the developing gut microbiota during a critical window that antibiotics such as ceftriaxone may profoundly disturb, potentially leading to long-lasting immune dysregulation. Ceftriaxone was selected for this study due to its widespread pediatric use and well-documented disruptive effects on the early-life gut ecosystem.

Bifidobacterium species can alleviate IBD through immunomodulation, oxidative stress reduction, and barrier fortification [19, 20]. Various B. breve strains exert protective effects via distinct mechanisms: CNCM I-403 activates innate immunity through TLR2/9 [21] and TLR5/9; C50 suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines by inhibiting AP-1 and NF-κB [22]; M-16 V attenuates MAPK-driven chemokine production [23]. Despite these documented immunomodulatory properties, the efficacy of probiotics in counteracting early-life antibiotic-induced dysbiosis is highly strain-specific. It remains unclear whether B. breve 207-1, a strain isolated from the healthy infant gut, can restore immune homeostasis following early antibiotic exposure and thereby confer long-term protection against colitis.

To address this, we utilized an animal model of IBD involving early antibiotic exposure followed by subsequent treatment with B. breve 207-1. Our findings substantiate that B. breve 207-1 can enhance immune development post antibiotic exposure, thereby reducing the severity of DSS-induced colitis later in life and strengthening intestinal resistance. Importantly, B. breve 207-1 may influence the composition of the initial gut microbiota and subsequently modulate local as well as systemic immune development. These findings hold immense significance for effectively preventing IBD subsequent to early antibiotic exposure in children.

Materials and methods

Mice

Fourteen-day-old pregnant Balb/c female mice (n = 25) were obtained from SPF Beijing Biotechnology Co., Ltd. [animal license number: SCXK (Jing) 2019-0010] and housed at the Experimental Animal Center of West China School of Public Health, Sichuan University (license number: SYXK 2023-0011) in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment.

These pregnant Balb/c mice underwent natural birth, resulting in a naturally distributed sex ratio among the offspring. The average litter size ranged from 6 to 8 pups. Newborn mice were administered normal saline, ceftriaxone, or B. breve 207-1 via oral gavage from birth to 21 days of age, with the dosing volume and method adapted to body size: 10 µL per pup using a pipette tip during the first week, 100 µL per pup using an 8-gauge straight stainless-steel feeding needle (rounded tip) during the second week, and 200 µL per pup using the same 8-gauge feeding needle from the third week onward. After 21 days, Ceftri + 207-1-207-1-DSS group continued to receive B. breve 207-1 intervention. Starting at 42 days of age, the drinking water for all “-DSS” groups was replaced with a solution containing 3% DSS to induce colitis. The specific grouping is as follows: the control group (NS-NS, n = 30), DSS group (NS-DSS, n = 30), ceftriaxone-DSS group (Ceftri-DSS, n = 30), ceftriaxone + B. breve 207-1 for 21 days-DSS group (Ceftri + 207-1-DSS, n = 30) and ceftriaxone + B. breve 207-1 for 46 days-DSS group (Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS, n = 30). All newborn pups were raised at the animal center under controlled conditions (ambient temperature of 23 °C, humidity ranging from 50% to 70%, and a 12-hour alternating light-dark cycle) with free access to water and food.

A separate control group receiving ceftriaxone followed by saline gavage from day 22 to 46 was not included in the current experimental design. Therefore, comparisons between the short-term and long-term probiotic interventions should be interpreted with caution, as the potential effects of prolonged probiotic administration cannot be fully dissociated from the natural recovery of the gut ecosystem post-antibiotics.

All the animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Fourth Hospital and West China School of Public Health of Sichuan University [Approval number: Gwll2021081].

Experimental materials

Ceftriaxone (Aladdin Shanghai Biochemical Technology) was dissolved in saline at a dosage of 100 mg/kg body weight. B. breve 207-1 (with an active bacteria count of 1.72 × 1010 CFU/g) was isolated from the intestines of healthy infants in China. Based on the previous research findings from our team [24], it was observed that the strain exhibited a significant health-promoting effect on the body at a dosage of 109 CFU. Taking into consideration the tolerance level of offspring rats, we incrementally administered an appropriate range of dosages for the strain through gradient gavage. The daily intake of probiotics ingested by mice during the first week was 1 × 107 CFU/mouse, increasing to 1 × 108 CFU/mouse during the second week, and finally reaching 1 × 109 CFU/mouse from the third week onwards. The gavage volume administered to each mouse during the first week was 10 µL each time, which increased to 100 µL/time in the second week and then to 200 µL/time from the third week onwards. The probiotic group received probiotics 2 h after ceftriaxone gavage. Dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; M.W: 36 − 50 kD), purchased from MP Biomedicals, LLC., United States was dissolved in sterilized ultra-pure water at 3 g DSS/100 mL pure water (3% DSS).

Treatment

Depending on the experimental group, all neonatal mice were administered either saline, ceftriaxone alone, or a combination of ceftriaxone and B. breve 207-1 from birth to 21 days. Half of the mice in each group were euthanized to obtain their organs and fecal samples. In the Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS group, mice were continuously administered B. breve 207-1 via gavage from 22 to 46 days of age. From 42 to 46 days of age, the drinking water of all “-DSS” groups was replaced by 3% DSS to induce colitis. After the experiment, the remaining mice were euthanized. All mice in each group were euthanized by cervical dislocation to obtain their organs and fecal samples.

Histopathological analysis

After the experiment, colon tissues were harvested from the euthanized mice and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Solarbio, Beijing) for 48 h. These tissues were dehydrated with ethanol, paraffin-embedded, frozen at − 18 °C, and de-molded. Each sample was sectioned and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The sample sections were finally examined using a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the pathological condition of colon tissues was assessed by experts.

The inflammatory pathology of the colon tissues was assessed based on the following criteria: inflammation severity (grade 0, absent; grade 1, mild; grade 2, moderate; and grade 3, severe); extent of crypt damage (grade 0, intact crypts; grade 1, loss of the bottom third; grade 2, loss of the bottom two thirds; grade 3, complete loss with surface epithelium intact; and grade 4, complete loss with surface epithelium); percentage of inflamed area (grade 1, 1%–25%; grade 2, 26%–50%; grade 3, 51%–75%; and grade 4, 76%–100%); percentage of crypt damage (grade 1, 1%–25%; grade 2, 26%–50%; grade 3, 51%–75%, and grade 4, 76%–100%); and depth of inflammation involvement (grade 0, normal; grade 1, mucosa; grade 2, submucosa; and grade 3, transmural). The final pathology score for each colonic tissue was calculated as the sum of these component scores [25].

mRNA expression in the colon and spleen

Spleen and colon tissues (10–15 mg each) were placed in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, and 50 mg of grinding beads (Tiangen, China) and Buffer L1 (Foregene, Chengdu, China) were added. After grinding these tissues twice at a speed of 4 M/s for 20 s using a tissue homogenizer (MP Biomedicals, United States), RNA was extracted using the Animal Tissue RNA Isolation Kit (Foregene, Chengdu, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, United States). The reverse transcription procedure consisted of consecutive incubations: first at 25 °C for 5 min, then at 46 °C for 20 min, and finally at 95 °C for 1 min. Real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on the cDNA using a SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). The qPCR protocol involved an initial denaturation step at 98 °C for 30 s, followed by amplification with 39 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at a temperature of 60 °C for 30 s. The mRNA expression levels of cytokines in the spleen and colon were normalized to β-actin and GAPDH, respectively. The cytokines quantified in the mouse colon and spleen tissues included anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, IL-13, and TGF-β) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-5, IL-6, IL-12 (p40), IL-17a, TNF-α, and IFN-γ). Additionally, the expression of proteins associated with intestinal tight junctions (ZO-1, Claudin, and Occludin) was assessed in colonic tissues. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (Table 1).

Quantification of serum cytokines

Blood samples were collected from mice on days 21 and 46 using ocular blood sampling and then centrifuged at 2,000 g for 15–20 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant obtained from the previous experiment underwent an additional centrifugation step at 2,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain the sample for testing. The concentrations of IL-5, IL-6, IL-10, IL-13, IL-17a, and TNF-α in the serum samples were analyzed with a Mouse Magnetic Luminex® Assays (Bio-Techne Corporation, United States) and measured using a Luminex 200TM multiplexing instrument (Merck Millipore, United States) and none of the samples were diluted. Serum TGF-β concentrations were analyzed with a TGF-β 1 ELISA Kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China) and the samples were diluted 30-fold.

16 S rRNA sequencing

The fresh fecal samples were collected from mice at 21, 42 and 46 days of age and subsequently frozen at − 80 °C. Fecal genomic DNA was extracted from each time point using the TIANamp Stool DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene was then amplified by PCR using the extracted DNA as a template with the barcoded forward primer 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). High-throughput sequencing was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the PE250 paired-end strategy. The raw sequencing data was then spliced and filtered to obtain the clean data. Subsequently, denoising was performed using the DADA2 algorithm in QIIME2, and sequences of frequencies below 5 were filtered out to obtain the final ASVs.

The representative sequences of each amplicon sequence variant (ASV) were annotated against the SILVA 138 database to obtain species information and the corresponding species-based abundance distribution. Simultaneously, the abundance and alpha diversity of the ASVs were analyzed to provide insights into species richness and homogeneity within samples. Furthermore, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances and beta diversity calculations were performed using the R package ‘vegan’ to evaluate differences in community structure among samples or groups. LEfSe was employed to assess the significance of differences in species composition and community structure from clustered samples in investigating variations in the community structure among grouped samples.

Analysis of short-chain fatty acids

Stool samples (50 mg) from the mice were homogenized with water (500 µL) and glass beads (100 mg) for 1 min and then centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Then, the supernatant (200 µL) was extracted using phosphoric acid solution (15%, 100 µL), IS solution of 4-methylvaleric acid (375 g/mL, 20 µL), and ether (280 µL). After vortexing for 1 min, the samples were centrifuged again at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was transferred to a vial before gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis.

GC analysis was conducted using a Trace 1300 gas chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The GC system was equipped with an Agilent HP-INNOWAX capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm) using helium as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injection was performed in split mode at a ratio of 10:1 with an injection volume of 1 µL and an injector temperature set at 250 °C. The ion source and the MS transfer line temperatures were maintained at 300 °C and 250 °C, respectively. The column temperature was programmed to ramp from an initial temperature of 90 °C, followed by a gradual increase to 120 °C at a rate of 10 °C per minute, then to 150 °C at a rate of 5 °C per minute, and finally to reach 250 °C at a rapid rate of 25 °C per minute, which was maintained for 2 min.

MS conditions: Metabolite detection using MS was conducted with an ISQ 7000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in electron impact ionization mode. The single ion monitoring mode was employed, and the electron energy level was set at 70 eV.

Determination of the secretory immunoglobulin A level

The feces (50 mg) were soaked in 200 µL of PBS, homogenized, and then centrifuged at 1,000 g for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was used for further analysis. The sample supernatant was diluted 2,500-fold at 21 day and 10,000-fold at 46 day. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was performed using the Mouse sIgA (secretory immunoglobulin A) ELISA kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, absorbance at 450 nm was recorded with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China). The data were fitted with a four-parameter logistic function.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). The results were presented as means ± SDs. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for multiple comparisons, followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis H-test was used, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test or Bonferroni correction as appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. All statistical tests were two-tailed.

Results

Colonic inflammation after DSS induction at day 46

At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and their colon tissues were harvested and stained with HE for histopathological scoring. In this study, according to the time and type of early intervention, different groups of neonatal mice were given normal saline (NS), ceftriaxone (Ceftri), and Ceftri + B. breve 207-1 by gavage from birth. The first phase of treatment was stopped on day 21, and colitis was induced with DSS on days 42 to 46.

After DSS induction, compared with the NS-Water group, the inflammation scores in all “-DSS” groups increased apparently (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A). The scores of the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group were lower than the Ceftri-DSS and NS-DSS groups (P < 0.05 and 0.01, respectively, Fig. 1A), while the Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS group scored higher than the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group (P < 0.05; Fig. 1A).

On day 46, the NS-DSS group had more severe inflammatory infiltration and crypt damage than the NS-Water group (Fig. 1B). The Ceftri + 207-1-DSS and the Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS groups showed milder inflammatory damage than the NS-DSS group (Fig. 1B). The villi of the NS-DSS and Ceftri-DSS groups were shorter and sparser than those of the other groups. Meanwhile, compared to the Ceftri-DSS group, the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group had more intact colonic mucosal epithelial cell structure.

Changes in gut microbiota after DSS induction at day 46

The gut microbiota composition was analyzed at 46 days. At the phylum level (Fig. 2A), the NS-DSS group exhibited a decreased relative abundance of Bacteroidetes (23.57% vs. 37.07%) and an increased relative abundance of Proteobacteria (5.02% vs. 0.45%) compared to the NS-Water group. The relative abundance of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group was the highest, while that of Verrucomicrobia in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group was the lowest among all five groups. According to the NS-Water, NS-DSS, Ceftri-DSS, Ceftri + 207-1-DSS, Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS order, the contents of Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Verrucomicrobia were 0.52%, 0.85%, 1.14%, 3.13%, 0.93% and 46.57%, 45.17%, 27.31%, 56.81%, 46.08% and 11.28%, 15.97%, 45.30%, 6.06%, 19.15%, respectively. The relative abundance of Methanobacterium and Bacteroides at the genus level was higher in the NS-DSS group compared to the NS-Water group (3.40% vs. 0.00% and 8.72% vs. 2.40%, respectively) (Fig. 2B). In addition, compared to the Ceftri-DSS group, the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group exhibited a significantly higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Rikanella (2.26% vs. 0.18% and 2.69% vs. 0.62%, respectively) (P < 0.05), and significantly lower relative abundance of Akkermansia spp. (5.86% vs. 45.22%, P < 0.05). Similarly, the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus spp. was the highest, while that of Enterococcus was the lowest in the Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS group (according to the NS-Water, NS-DSS, Ceftri-DSS, Ceftri + 207-1-DSS, Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS order, the contents of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus spp. and Enterococcus were 0.12%, 0.43%, 0.14%, 0.39%, 9.58% and 12.76%, 7.48%, 3.21%, 4.31%, 16.86% and 2.03%, 0.13%, 0.14%, 0.02%, 0.02%, respectively). In terms of α diversity (Fig. 2C), the ACE and Chao1 indices of the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS and Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS groups were significantly higher than those of the NS-Water, NS-DSS, and Ceftri-DSS groups (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, compared to the NS-DSS group, the Ceftri-DSS group exhibited lower Shannon and Simpson indices after successfully establishing DSS. Compared to the NS-Water, NS-DSS, and Ceftri-DSS groups, the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS and Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS groups exhibited significant differences in the distribution at the phylum and genus levels, and α diversity of gut microbiota. The concentrations of propionic, isobutyric, and isovaleric acids in the intestinal metabolites showed no significant differences between the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group and other groups (all p > 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S1A–E). However, compared to the NS-DSS group, the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group exhibited significantly higher butyric and valeric acid levels (P < 0.05).

Changes in the intestinal mechanical barrier and the short-chain fatty acid (SCFAs) levels at day 46

Figure 3 shows the changes in the colonic mucosal barrier on day 46 after colitis. The mRNA expression levels of KI67, Claudin-1, and Occludin numerically lower following DSS induction compared to the NS-Water group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance P > 0.05 for all comparisons; Figs. 3A, D, and E). In comparison with the Ceftri-DSS group, the mRNA expression levels of KI67, MUC2, Claudin-1 and Occludin, and sIgA levels were augmented after B. breve 207-1 intervention in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group (Figs. 3A–F). Regarding changes in the mucosal barrier, the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group exhibited significantly higher levels of MUC2 mRNA expression and slgA than the NS-DSS group (P < 0.05 and 0.01, respectively; Fig. 3B, C). Additionally, significantly elevated levels of sIgA were observed in the Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS group compared to the NS-DSS group (P < 0.05; Fig. 3C). In the case of colonic mechanical barrier changes, the mRNA expression of Claudin-1 was significantly higher in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group compared to the NS-DSS group (P < 0.01, Figs. 3D). The mRNA levels of Occludin in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group were significantly higher than in the Ceftri-DSS group (P < 0.05, Figs. 3F). Discussion

The global prevalence of IBD is increasing, particularly among pediatric populations [26]. Although treatment is significant for patients with pre-existing IBD, severe side effects, such as intestinal obstruction and Clostridium difficile infection, along with exorbitant treatment costs, often impose significant physical and psychological distress on patients. These factors significantly hinder the effectiveness and long-term sustainability of pharmacological interventions [27–30]. Therefore, implementing preventive measures for pediatric IBD is more cost-effective than treatment options. The early-life prevention of IBD in children is crucial as the period between fetal development and child growth is critical for acquiring adaptive responses to various exposures with both short- and long-term health implications [31–33]. Early antibiotic exposure is commonly observed in Chinese newborns, which might exert a certain anti-inflammatory effect in combating DSS-induced colitis in the long run, as shown in our previous study. However, antibiotic usage can still compromise immune development [34, 35]. Therefore, within the context of simulating early antibiotic exposure, this study primarily aimed to investigate whether B. breve 207-1 administered during the antibiotic period could enhance immune development and reduce the severity of DSS-induced colitis later in life. This research provides a theoretical foundation for preventing the long-term adverse effects of early-life antibiotic exposure.

The colonic inflammation score is the most direct indicator of the severity of colitis, while the structural changes in the colon can be assessed using the histopathological H&E Sections [36]. In this study, we utilized the inflammation score at day 46 to determine the level of inflammation in the DSS-induced colitis mice model. Following DSS induction, the NS-DSS group exhibited significantly higher inflammation scores and more severe crypt injury, indicating successful modeling of colitis. The colitis score after antibiotic exposure during early life was higher than that in the NS-Water group but lower than that in the NS-DSS group, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that early-life antibiotic exposure can modulate long-term susceptibility to experimental colitis [37]. Notably, the purpose of antibiotic exposure here was to disrupt early-life gut microbiota assembly rather than to directly assess colitis exacerbation. Importantly, B. breve 207-1 administered during the antibiotic exposure period significantly alleviated subsequent DSS-induced colitis and preserved intestinal structure, demonstrating its anti-inflammatory and barrier-protective effects independent of whether antibiotics directly worsened colitis severity. Based on these findings, we continued to use B. breve 207-1 without antibiotics to investigate its long-term use on colitis, which demonstrated good remission in colitis models. However, its effect was not as pronounced as when administered solely during early life. Therefore, we speculate that short-term use of B. breve 207-1 following early antibiotic exposure serves as a preventive measure for long-term colitis, while prolonged use might inhibit gut microbiota recovery and fail to restore complete intestinal microenvironment homeostasis. Interestingly, our results demonstrated that short-term early-life intervention (21 days) exhibited superior protective effects compared to continuous long-term administration (46 days). This paradox suggests that the early postnatal period serves as a pivotal opportunity for microbial ‘education’ of the immune system. Based on these results, we suggest that early-life administration of B. breve 207-1 following antibiotic exposure can effectively prevent intestinal inflammatory damage caused by subsequent DSS challenge, even after cessation of the intervention.

Recently, the relationship between gut microbiota and IBD has garnered increasing attention. While a definitive summary of IBD pathogenesis remains elusive, most scholars posit that the aberrant immune response in genetically susceptible hosts triggered by an imbalance in homeostasis between host and gut microbiota can contribute to this [38]. Joossens et al. conducted a comparative analysis of the gut microbiota in 68 patients diagnosed with CD and 84 unaffected relatives without intestinal infection, revealing significant dysbiosis in the gut microbiota in CD patients [39]. These findings indicate that, compared to the gut microbiota observed in patients with IBD, healthy individuals exhibit a decrease in bacteria possessing anti-inflammatory properties and an increase in the abundance of bacteria associated with pro-inflammatory responses [40]. The most consistent finding indicates that patients with IBD exhibit a diminished diversity of gut microbiota and significantly lower levels of Firmicutes than healthy individuals. However, there is ongoing debate regarding the extent of alterations in the abundance of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes [41]. The abundance of F. prausnitzii, Blautia faecis, Roseburia inulinivorans, Ruminococcus torques, and Clostridium lavalense is significantly lower in patients with CD than in healthy individuals [42, 43]. Additionally, the number of F. prausnitzii has been correlated with the risk of relapse of ileal CD after surgery [44]. Growing evidence suggests that an imbalance in the indigenous gut microbiota can compromise intestinal barrier integrity and trigger immune dysregulation, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of IBD. Previous reports have also indicated a consistent pattern, wherein individuals with CD exhibit lower proportions of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (specifically Clostridium) but higher proportions of Proteobacteria (specifically Enterobacteriaceae) in comparison to healthy individuals [41]. The gut microbiota analysis data conducted in this study revealed that, compared to the NS-Water group, the NS-DSS group showed a decreased proportionate presence of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes but an increased proportionate presence of Proteobacteria. Previous studies have also reported similar findings indicating that individuals with CD exhibit lower proportions of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes (specifically Clostridium) while displaying higher proportions of Proteobacteria (specifically Enterobacteriaceae) compared to healthy individuals [45]. The differential impact of DSS-induced colitis in adulthood intervention following early-life antibiotic exposure on the composition of gut microbiota is concerning, as it can significantly alter the relative abundance of various gut microbiota. Specifically, the relative abundance of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Euryarchaeota was notably decreased at the phylum level and Dubosiella and Mathanobrevibacter at the genus level. These changes might be due to the antimicrobial spectrum of ceftriaxone. Antibiotics can exhibit both bactericidal and bacteriostatic effects, leading to a reduction in the concentration and diversity of gut microbes. However, it is noteworthy that the diversity of gut microbiota is decreased in individuals with IBD [46]. The exposure to antibiotics did not have a significant impact on the microbial diversity of mice, as indicated by the ACE and Chao1 indices. However, there was an observable decrease in the evenness of the microbial community in mice treated with antibiotics, as evidenced by the Shannon and Simpson indices, compared to the NS-DSS group. Therefore, we focused on identifying specific microorganisms that exhibited an increased relative abundance within the Ceftri-DSS group. The proportion of Akkermansia was significantly higher than in the NS-DSS group. Previous studies have demonstrated the crucial role of Akkermansia in preventing and improving IBD. Akkermansia possesses the potential to alleviate mucosal inflammation through microbe-host interactions, leading to a downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and protection of gut barrier function. Additionally, it promotes an upregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokine expression while simultaneously maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [47]. However, the elevated abundance of Akkermansia in the antibiotic-exposed group appears paradoxical. This may represent a compensatory expansion following antibiotic-induced depletion of other commensals, rather than a primary protective effect. Strain-level functional differences, unresolved by 16 S rRNA sequencing, could also contribute to this discrepancy. However, the Ceftri-DSS group exhibited an increase in pathogenic bacteria (Enterococcus and Pathogenic Enterohaemorrhagic Escherichia coli), possibly due to the broad antibacterial spectrum of ceftriaxone, which eradicated most commensal bacteria and facilitated the proliferation of enteropathogens [48]. Based on this premise, we initially employed B. breve 207-1 and observed that the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group exhibited a relatively higher abundance of Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidetes compared to the antibiotic group. Furthermore, the levels of Lactobacillus and bacteria from the Riken genus were also higher. The metabolic byproducts of Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus, such as acetic acid, propionate, and butyrate, have been extensively investigated for their capacity to inhibit intestinal mucosal inflammation [49, 50]. Compared to the ceftriaxone-treated group, the content of organic acids, propionic, isovaleric, and valeric acids was higher in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group, indicating that the simultaneous administration of antibiotics and B. breve 207-1 during early life effectively promotes the colonization and maturation of beneficial bacteria. Crucially, these microbiota and metabolite changes are functionally linked to the subsequent enhancement of intestinal barrier integrity. Our hypothesis suggests that differences in establishing early-life gut microbiota might alter the intestinal microbial composition 46 days after the birth of newborn mice. Analysis of the gut microbiota on day 21 revealed a decreased relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and Akkermansia in the Ceftri-DSS group, indicating disruption of gut microbiota caused by early-life antibiotic usage. Additionally, a higher relative abundance of Enterococcus and Staphylococcus was observed compared to the NS-Water group. Intervention with B. breve 207-1 significantly increased the levels of Actinobacteria and Bifidobacterium. The beneficial effects of B. breve 207-1 were also reflected in the significantly elevated levels of acetic, propionic, butyric, isobutyric, isovaleric, and caproic acid levels. The clearer separation of microbiota composition at day 21 reflects the acute antibiotic-induced disruption and concurrent probiotic modulation, whereas by day 46 substantial natural recovery had diminished these intergroup differences.

In conclusion, early-life exposure to antibiotics can cause substantial damage to gut microbiota development. However, this study did not find evidence that this early disruption leads to a persistently deteriorated gut microbiota in later-life colitis. Intervention with B. breve 207-1 following early-life antibiotic exposure mitigated the antibiotic-induced disruptions at the early time point (day 21), as evidenced by the enhanced relative abundance of Bifidobacterium and increased SCFA levels. These early microbiota improvements likely contributed to the enhanced intestinal barrier integrity and balanced immune responses observed in adulthood following DSS challenge. These findings indicate that addressing disruptions in the gut microbiota caused by early-life antibiotic usage has significant preventive implications for long-term colitis, resulting in prolonged protective effects.

These observed microbiota improvements, including increased SCFA production, are positioned to underpin the enhancement of intestinal barrier function. Microscopic examination of the intestinal tissues in patients with IBD revealed destruction of the mucus barrier and alterations in mucus composition. Using animal models, genetic deletion of the mucin MUC2 has been shown to induce the development and progression of inflammatory enteritis in mice, suggesting that dysfunction of the intestinal epithelial barrier and impaired tight junction function contribute to the pathogenesis of IBD [51]. The expression of cell proliferation-related outer membrane proteins (KI67, Occludin and MUC2, Claudin 1, ZO-1) was analyzed to determine their close connection and assess the intestinal barrier function. The levels of SlgA, the principal guardian of the intestinal mucosal immune barrier against exogenous pathogens [52] were significantly different between the Ceftri-DSS and Ceftri + 207-1-DSS groups on day 46, indicating that antibiotics administration in early life suppressed the expression of Claudin-1 and Occludin mRNA. Furthermore, intervention with B. breve 207-1 exhibited a positive tendency towards enhancing the mRNA levels of KI67, MUC2, slgA, and ZO-1. Previous research demonstrated the crucial role of the ZO-1 protein in its interaction with the transmembrane protein Occludin [53]. The mRNA expression of ZO-1 was significantly reduced in the Ceftri-DSS group, indicating that early-life antibiotic usage can impede the interaction between Occludin and ZO-1 proteins within intestinal epithelial cells, compromising the integrity of tight junction structure. KI67 is a cell surface marker closely linked to cellular proliferation [54]. MUC2 is secreted by goblet cells and is considered the main component of intestinal mucus [55]. The observed decrease in KI67 and MUC2-positive cells in the Ceftri-DSS group also suggests that antibiotics might disrupt the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells in mice, thereby impairing the regulation of abnormal mucus secretion. These findings indicate that, despite the lack of statistical significance, intervention with B. breve 207-1 can potentially mitigate severe damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier caused by early-life antibiotic exposure, offering valuable insights for future prevention or management of colitis. A more robust intestinal barrier is critical for regulating subsequent immune responses. However, no discernible distinctions between the Ceftri-DSS and Ceftri + 207-1-DSS groups were observed on day 21. Peng et al. observed that the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier was significantly enhanced after a similar intervention with another strain of Bifidobacterium bifidum BD-1 after 21 days, which contradicts the observations in our study using B. breve 207-1 [56]. Therefore, separate verifications should be conducted to assess the impact of different strains. In the Ceftri + 207-1-207-1 + DSS group, prolonged administration of B. breve 207-1 also failed to enhance intestinal barrier function, potentially contributing to the observed lack of reduction in enteritis scores within this group. The precise role of strain B. breve 207-1 in promoting the development of intestinal barrier tissue remains uncertain. However, we hypothesize that prolonged administration of B. breve 207-1 following early antibiotic exposure might enhance the structural integrity of intestinal tissues via the local and systemic pathways of intestinal immunity. When the integrity of the mucus barrier is compromised, antigens originating from the external environment and those associated with the gut microbiota stimulate an immune response [57]. Therefore, our main objective was to evaluate changes in pro- and anti-inflammatory factors within the adaptive immune response. Thus, the barrier function modulated by early-life interventions serves as a key interface influencing the final immune outcome. At day 21, prior to DSS challenge, the local immune cytokine levels within the colon were not significantly altered among groups. Notably, the immune response on day 21 was comparatively weaker than on day 46. The systemic immunity showed no significant differences among the groups on day 21, with one exception: serum IL-10 levels were significantly lower in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group compared to the Ceftri-DSS group. This reduction may reflect the broader anti-inflammatory effect of B. breve 207-1, which suppresses overall inflammatory tone—possibly involving Treg-mediated pathways—thereby diminishing the stimulus for compensatory IL-10 production. In contrast, antibiotic exposure alone does not confer such global suppression, and IL-10 levels in the Ceftri-DSS group remained similar to those of the NS-Water group. However, the mRNA levels of IL-10, IL-13, and TGF-β, representing the local immune response in the spleen, were notably increased in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group. A significant decrease was observed in IL-12 (P40) mRNA expression, representing the local colonic immunity, within this group, indicating the need to employ a Th1/Th2 approach to elucidate this phenomenon further. Interleukin (IL)-6, -23, TNF-α, and IL-12 (P40) are commonly considered pro-inflammatory cytokines in DSS-induced colitis and are frequently associated with apoptosis. They can stimulate lymphocytes and activate other immune cells. Conversely, IL-10, -13, and TGF-β are widely recognized as anti-inflammatory factors in DSS-induced colitis. For instance, IL-13 suppresses Th1 cell activity and cellular immune responses while inhibiting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. Furthermore, it regulates tight junction protein expression within the intestinal barrier and modulates epithelial cell apoptosis [58]. At day 46, following DSS-induced colitis, serum cytokine levels, including IL-5, 6, 17 A, and TNF-α, were significantly reduced in the group treated with B. breve 207-1. Additionally, the expression of serum cytokines, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, was significantly higher than the Ceftri-DSS group. This likely reflects a reduced need for systemic anti-inflammatory compensation, as the overall inflammatory burden was substantially lower in the probiotic-treated mice. Although the local immune cytokine levels within the colon of the Ceftri-DSS group were not significantly altered, these levels were relatively improved within the spleen. The diverse biological effects of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are primarily mediated by its secretion from activated mononuclear macrophages. TNF-α can activate NF-kB, MAPK, and multiple apoptosis signaling pathways to exert its immunomodulatory effects [59]. IFN-γ can induce proliferation and differentiation of immune cells, actively participate in immune regulation, demonstrate antiviral properties, and enhance NK cell activity. These two cytokines are considered archetypal examples of Th1 cytokines. Through collaborative efforts, IL-4 and IL-10 effectively regulate dendritic cell (DC) tolerance by inducing a Th2 response while simultaneously inhibiting the Th1 response. Furthermore, IL-6 can activate T cells and facilitate NK cell lysis. These three cytokines represent typical characteristics associated with Th2-type cytokine responses [60]. The levels of local splenic immune factors TNF-α and IFN-γ in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group were comparable to those observed in the NS-Water group, indicating similar expression patterns for Th1 cytokines. Additionally, the local colon immune factor IFN-γ in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group approached normal values in the NS-Water group. Regarding the Th2 cytokines, the IL-6 and IL-10 levels in the Ceftri-DSS group were similar to those observed in the NS-Water group. Additionally, the levels of the local immune cytokine IL-10 in the spleen of the Ceftri-DSS group were comparable to that of the NS-Water group, while the colon-localized immune cytokine IL-6 in the Ceftri + 207-1-DSS group approached normal values similar to those seen in the NS-Water control. These findings suggest that probiotics have a beneficial impact on maintaining immune homeostasis by rectifying the imbalance between Th1 and Th2, which leads to immune disorders. These results demonstrated significant regulatory effects of early-life antibiotics on IL-10 and IL-6, while B. breve 207-1 exhibited even more pronounced regulatory effects on TNF-α and IFN-γ, promoting immune cell proliferation and differentiation and enhancing NK cell activity. This ultimately shifts from a strong Th2 response towards a Th1 response, achieving immunological balance. This study suggests that the early effects of B. breve 207-1 are mainly through its characteristic pathway to regulate immunity.

The lack of a control group receiving ceftriaxone followed by saline from day 22 to 46 limits our ability to definitively interpret the effects of long-term versus short-term probiotic intervention. While our data suggest that prolonged administration of B. breve 207-1 did not confer additional protection compared to short-term early-life intervention, it remains unclear whether this reflects a true plateau or interference effect, or merely the natural resilience of the recovering gut microbiota. Future studies incorporating this control are warranted to clarify this issue.

In summary, our findings delineate a potential mechanistic pathway: early-life intervention with B. breve 207-1 reshapes the disrupted gut microbiota and increases beneficial SCFAs, which subsequently contributes to the reinforcement of intestinal barrier integrity. This enhanced barrier function, in turn, helps to modulate a more balanced local and systemic immune response, ultimately reducing the severity of DSS-induced colitis later in life. In conclusion, early-life antibiotic exposure is a prevalent health risk in Chinese children. B. breve 207-1 can augment its impact on immune development after antibiotic exposure, thereby mitigating the severity of later-life colitis and enhancing intestinal resistance. Importantly, B. breve 207-1 might influence the composition of early-stage gut microbiota and subsequently impact local and systemic immune development.
