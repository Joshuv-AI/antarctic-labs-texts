Preemptive biofilm colonization blocks microbial metal corrosion
Authors: Peiyu Ma, Di Wang, Weixin Kong, Dake Xu, Derek R Lovley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable materials are needed to address the serious economic and safety risks of microbial metal corrosion. Colonizing metal surfaces with biofilms of noncorrosive microbes was previously shown to reduce aerobic, abiotic corrosion. However, the ability of biofilms to thwart highly corrosive anaerobic microbes is untested. Here we report on a strain of Escherichia coli genetically modified for enhanced metal adherence and adaptively evolved to tolerate sulfide. The E. coli biofilms effectively inhibited all known major routes for anaerobic microbial iron corrosion, including proton and sulfide attack, as well as the highly aggressive corrosion of electroactive microbes that directly extract electrons from Fe0. The E. coli biofilms prevented corrosion much better than biofilms of other microorganisms previously reported to reduce aerobic, abiotic corrosion. The results highlight the possibility of tailoring biofilm properties to function as effective sustainable, self-healing coatings to safeguard critical metal infrastructure.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

With the exception of infectious disease, no other microbial activity is as economically damaging as microbial corrosion. The costs associated with corrosive biofilms dwarf all other negative biofilm impacts1. Microbial involvement in corrosion has been known for over 100 years, but effective prevention strategies have remained elusive2–4. Conventional methods for combating corrosive biofilms include physical cleaning, biocide application, and material modification5,6. High costs and stringent environmental regulations limit the application and effectiveness of these approaches.

Many studies have shown that biofilms of non-corrosive microbes pre-grown on metal surfaces can reduce rates of abiotic corrosion under aerobic conditions6–23. Cells, along with their extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and mineral precipitates, can act as barriers to impede access of oxygen or other corrosive agents to the metal surface7,9,10,12–14,16,17.

However, microbial metal corrosion is typically most severe and costly under anaerobic conditions2. Even when bulk conditions are aerobic, oxygen consumption within biofilms typically results in anaerobic conditions at the biofilm/metal interface7,10,13. An important reaction associated with anaerobic corrosion is Fe0 oxidation coupled with the production of H2:

This reaction takes place abiotically, but microbial activity can accelerate it. For example, corrosive sulfate-reducing microbes like Desulfovibrio vulgaris and Desulfovibrio ferrophilus consume H2 with the reduction of sulfate to sulfide24–27. The sulfide produced enhances H2 production, accelerating corrosion in a positive feedback cycle25.

where X includes electron acceptors such as nitrate, Fe(III), sulfate, organic acids, or carbon dioxide24,29,31–34. Electroactive microbes available in pure culture that are capable of this electrobiocorrosion2 include species of Geobacter, Shewanella, and Methanosarcina28,29,32–34. Electrobiocorrosion is an especially aggressive corrosion mechanism. Electroactive microbes can even extract electrons from ‘corrosion resistant’ metals like 316 L stainless steel, which has a chromium-oxide rich passive layer on its surface that prevents H2 production29,32.

In a previous study, a biofilm of a Vibrio species grown on carbon steel partially inhibited carbon steel corrosion by a hydrogenase-deficient strain of the electroactive microbe, Geobacter sulfurreducens18. However, the implications of the study were limited because: 1) incubations were not under strict anaerobic conditions; 2) the medium contained high concentrations of a complex mixture of organics with unknown corrosion impacts; 3) the utilization of H2, a key electron donor for corrosive microbes, was artificially inhibited; and 4) the ability of the biofilm to protect against sulfide production, an important corrosive metabolite, was not evaluated.

In this study we employed strict anaerobic conditions and simple, defined media to rigorously investigate whether a biofilm could be developed that effectively prevented anaerobic microbial iron corrosion. Initial analysis demonstrated that biofilms of microbes previously shown to be effective against aerobic, abiotic corrosion provided limited protection against anaerobic microbes. This included biofilms of Tenacibaculum mesophilum D-6, which produces a robust EPS barrier14,15. and Pseudoalteromonas lipolytica, which generates a biomineralization barrier9,12. However, Escherichia coli MBD, which has been genetically modified to express curli fibers with a metal-binding motif to enhance attachment to metal23, effectively prevented H2 production and electrobiocorrosion, and once adaptively evolved to tolerate sulfide, also prevented the sulfide-based corrosion of a sulfate-reducing microbe. These results demonstrate that biofilms designed with the appropriate characteristics can effectively mitigate microbial metal corrosion.

Results

E. coli biofilms prevent H2 production from carbon steel

All three microbes evaluated (T. mesophilum strain D-6, P. lipolytica, and E. coli MBD) produced confluent biofilms on carbon steel (Fig. 1a–c). As expected from previous studies12,19,23, the P. lipolytica biofilm was mineralized with calcium and magnesium (Fig. S1). Abiotic anaerobic corrosion, as estimated by weight loss of the steel, was less with all three biofilm coatings (Fig. 1d). Bare carbon steel steadily generated H2 from abiotic proton reduction (Fig. 1e), as expected from previous studies25,27. T. mesophilum biofilms were no impediment to H2 production (Fig. 1e), demonstrating that they would not be effective in deterring the growth of corrosive microbes that feed on H2. P. lipolytica only somewhat limited H2 production (Fig. 1e). In contrast, E. coli biofilms completely inhibited H2 production (Fig. 1e), demonstrating the biofilm’s potential to eliminate this important energy source for corrosive microbes.

Impact of biofilms on abiotic corrosion of carbon steel.

Scanning electron micrographs of biofilm formation by a T. mesophilum D-6, b P. lipolytica, and c E.coli MBD. Weight loss d and H2 production e in absence and presence of biofilms. Results are the mean and standard deviation of triplicate incubations for each treatment. ***P < 0.001.

Inhibition of electrobiocorrosion

Although they were not isolated from corroding steel surfaces, the marine microbe Shewanella algae and the freshwater microbe G. sulfurreducens strain ACL are commonly employed as model electroactive microbes in corrosion studies. They aggressively corrode iron via electrobiocorrosion, which requires direct contact between the microbes and the metal surface28–30,32. There was substantial weight loss and pitting in incubations of untreated carbon steel with S. algae or G. sulfurreducens. (Fig. 2). Pregrown biofilms of T. mesophilum or P. lipolytica notably reduced the weight loss from carbon steel attributable to S. algae or G. sulfurreducens (Fig. 2a), but weight loss was higher than in abiotic controls (Fig. 1a) or carbon steel colonized with T. mesophilum or P. lipolytica, but not exposed to S. algae or G. sulfurreducens (Fig. 2a). More importantly, the T. mesophilum or P. lipolytica biofilms only slightly diminished the pitting that could be attributed to S. algae and G. sulfurreducens (Fig. 2b, c, e, f). This is significant because pitting is typically the primary concern when determining the corrosion damage most meaningful from an engineering and safety standpoint.

Impact on G. sulfurreducens electrobioccorrosion of pre-colonization with e T. mesophilum D6, f P. lipolytica, or g E. coli MBD biofilms. Images of representative pitting for panels b-g available as supplemental data (Fig. S3-S5). Weight loss data is the mean and standard deviation of three independent incubations for each treatment. Pit data shows 10 randomly selected pits for each treatment.

In contrast, the E. coli biofilms were highly effective in preventing electrobiocorrosion. In the presence of S. algae or G. sulfurrreducens the weight loss and pitting of E. coli colonized carbon steel was minimal and comparable to that observed with just the E. coli biofilm alone (Fig. 2d, g; Fig. S5a–d). These results suggested that the E. coli biofilm can block electroactive microbes from accessing the metal surface and extracting electrons.

In order to further evaluate E. coli biofilm competitive exclusion of electroactive microbes, E. coli MBD and S. algae were modified to express the superfolder green fluorescent protein and the red fluorescent protein m-Cherry, respectively. Confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed extensive biofilm formation when S. algae was incubated with bare carbon steel for seven days (Fig. 3a). However, when the carbon steel surface was preemptively colonized with E. coli prior to exposure to S. algae the low numbers of S. algae that adhered to the biofilm after seven days were restricted to upper layers of the biofilm (Fig. 3d, e). Thus, the E. coli biofilm effectively prevented S. algae from establishing contact with the metal surface, which is a prerequisite for electrobiocorrosion.

Electrochemical analysis further confirmed that the E. coli biofilms blocked electrobiocorrosion. For example, the open circuit potential (EOCP) of the carbon steel colonized with the E. coli films was more positive than for carbon steel exposed to S. algae or G. sulfurreducens (Fig. 4a, d). A higher EOCP indicates that material is less prone to corrosion30,32,35,36. The corrosion polarization resistance (Rp), which is inversely proportional to the corrosion rate30,37, was significantly higher for steel colonized with E. coli than for steel directly exposed to electroactive microbes (Fig. 4b, e). The corrosion current density (icorr), which quantifies the rate of metal corrosion, was high in incubations of untreated steel with just S. algae or G. sulfurreducens, but when the steel was pre-colonized with E. coli the icorr in the presence of S. algae or G. sulfurreducens was the same as when pre-colonized steel was incubated in the absence of the electroactive microbes (Fig. 4c, f).

Electrochemical analysis of the effect of pre-colonizing carbon steel with E.coli MBD biofilms on S. algae and G. sulfurreducens electrobiocorrosion.

a, d Open circuit potential (OCP). b, e Polarization resistance (Rp). c, f Corrosion current density (icorr) after seven days of incubation as determined from potentiodynamic polarization data (Supplemental Fig. S9). The results are the mean and standard deviation of triplicate incubations for each treatment.

Preventing attack by sulfate reducers and sulfide

As detailed in the Introduction, sulfate reducers produce sulfide, which enhances Fe0 oxidation with the production of H2, accelerating corrosion with a positive feedback cycle as the sulfate reducers consume the H2 with additional sulfide production. D. vulgaris is a model microbe for this process25,27,. Weight loss measurement indicated that carbon steel corrosion in the presence of D. vulgaris (Fig. 5a), was ca. 10-fold slower than with the electroactive microbes (Fig. 2a) but still significantly faster than in carbon steel pre-colonized with the biofilm-forming microbes (Fig. 5a). None of the three biofilm types initially investigated inhibited the corrosion attributed to D. vulgaris sulfide production (Fig. 5a–d). Not only was there no substantial impact on pitting (Fig. 5b–d), but in some instances weight loss of pre-colonized steel in the presence of D. vulgaris was greater than in the absence of the pre-colonized biofilms (Fig. 5a). Progressive blackening of the carbon steel surfaces due to the accumulation of ferrous sulfide was visually apparent in incubations with D. vulgaris.

Only biofilms of a strain of E. coli MBD adapted to tolerate sulfide could protect against steel corrosion associated with D. vulgaris.

Weight loss a and pit distribution b–e. Weight loss data is the mean and standard deviation of three independent incubations for each treatment. Pit data shows 10 randomly selected pits for each treatment. Images of representative pitting available as supplemental data for panels b-d (Fig. S6-S8) and panel e (Fig. S11).

The inability of the E. coli MBD biofilms to protect the carbon steel from the sulfide produced by D. vulgaris was unexpected because the biofilms were highly effective in preventing protons reacting with Fe0 (Fig. 1e). However, H2 was produced when sulfide was added to carbon steel colonized with E. coli MBD biofilms (Fig. 6a). Crystal violet analysis of the biomass of the E. coli biofilms on the steel surface before and after exposure to sulfide indicated that sulfide greatly reduced the attached cell mass (Fig. 6b). As little as 1.25 mM sulfide inhibited E. coli MBD anaerobic growth (Fig. 6c). When sulfide was added the robust biofilm matrix (Fig. 1c) was destroyed with only scattered cells and minerals on the carbon steel surface (Fig. 6d). Thus, the sulfide toxicity was the likely explanation for the inability of E. coli MBD biofilms to prevent corrosion in the presence of D. vulgaris corrosion.

Impact of sulfide on E. coli MBD biofilms before and after adaption to sulfide.

a H2 production following sulfide addition to carbon steel colonized with unadapted or sulfide-adapted strains of E. coli MBD. b Crystal violet analysis of biofilm biomass for both strains before and after exposure to sulfide. c Sulfide impact on anaerobic planktonic growth. d Scanning electron micrograph of unadapted E. coli biofilm after exposure to 1.25 mM sulfide. e, f Biofilms of sulfide-adapted E. coli biofilms before and after exposure to 3 mM sulfide.

In an attempt to improve E. coli MBD’s resistance to sulfide, E. coli MBD was adapted over time to grow in successively higher concentrations of sulfide until it could grow in medium containing 3 mM sulfide (Fig. S10), a concentration commonly encountered in corrosive environments such as oil pipelines and produced waters38,39. The sulfide-adapted E. coli MBD biofilms (Fig. 6e) were not destroyed with exposure to sulfide (Fig. 6f); prevented H2 production upon sulfide exposure (Fig. 6a); and prevented corrosion associated with D. vulgaris as evidenced by weight loss (Fig. 5a) and pitting (Fig. 5e) comparable to levels observed in controls without D. vulgaris.

Discussion

The performance of the sulfide-adapted E. coli MBD demonstrates that biofilms with the appropriate characteristics can protect against even the most aggressive forms of anaerobic microbial corrosion, not only preventing protons and sulfide from interacting with the carbon steel, but also blocking the intense corrosion of electroactive microorganisms. Protection against multiple major anaerobic corrosion pathways highlights the potential of this strategy in real world environments, where steel surfaces are typically exposed to complex and dynamic microbial communities that may simultaneously employ diverse mechanisms of iron corrosion. Preemptively colonizing metal surfaces with a corrosion-protective biofilm is an attractive sustainable solution to the pervasive problem of microbial metal corrosion of critical infrastructure. Unlike conventional coatings and chemical corrosion inhibitors, ‘green’ biofilm coatings require no hazardous chemicals for synthesis or deployment and offer the possibility of self-regeneration.

In the proof-of-concept studies reported here a genetically engineered strain outperformed natural biofilm-forming strains. However, targeted enrichment and selection strategies should enable recovery of sulfide-resistant, strongly adherent strains from environmental consortia, removing barriers associated with regulatory constraints on the environmental release of genetically modified organisms. Future studies should also evaluate biofilm corrosion mitigation under dynamic fluid flow to more accurately simulate the shear force conditions common in industrial environments that may influence biofilm formation and stability.

Methods

Flow chart of the experimental design.

Microbial strains

Pseudoalteromonas lipolytica (SCSIO 04301; Genbank 16S rRNA Number, JDVB00000000) and Shewanella algae (MCCC 1A11468; Genbank 16S rRNA Number, CP129401) were obtained from the South China Sea Institute of Oceanology and the Marine Culture Collection of China, respectively. Tenacibaculum mesophilum D-6, which was from our laboratory culture collection was isolated from seawater near Dongshan Island, Fujian Province, China40. Desulfovibrio vulgaris strain JW710 is a markerless Δupp deletion mutant derived from D. vulgaris Hildenborough, constructed using an SOE-PCR–assembled deletion cassette and selected on 5-fluorouracil41. Strain JW710 serves as the parental strain enabling subsequent markerless genetic exchange through upp-based counterselection and has been routinely employed in previous corrosion studies27,42. It was a gift from Valentine V. Trotter and Adam M. Deutschbauer of the Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory. Geobacter sulfurreducens strain ACL, which is a genetic modification of G. sulfurreducens strain PCA in which the gene for an ATP-dependent citrate lyase was introduced to enable autotrophic growth43 was obtained from our laboratory culture collection. Escherichia coli strain MBD was obtained from our laboratory culture collection. It was previously engineered from E. coli K-12 MG1655 by introducing the OmpR234 mutation to enhance curli expression, deleting the native csgA gene via λ-Red recombination, and expressing a plasmid-borne csgA-MBD fusion constructed by In-Fusion cloning to enable strong metal adhesion23.

A fluorescent E. coli strain was generated by chromosomally integrating a superfolder green fluorescent protein (sGFP) cassette into the previously described23 E. coli OmpR234ΔCsgA host background, using the homologeous recombination method of dual-selective marker44–46. To enable genomic integration, the host was first made recombination competent by introducing the thermosensitive plasmid pSIM647, which expresses the λ-Red recombinase system (gam, bet, exo) and is subsequently cured at a non-permissive temperature. The sGFP cassette was driven by a constitutive promoter of CP2548. Briefly, a Kan-ara-ccdB1_attB cassette, PCR-amplified from plasmid Kan-ara-ccdB1, was integrated into the chromosome of E. coli OmpR234ΔCsgA/pSIM6, generating E. coli OmpR234ΔCsgA/pSIM6-Kan-ara-ccdB1_attB. This cassette was then replaced with the CP25-sGFP_attB fragment, amplified from plasmid CP25-sGFP, via electroporation, yielding an E. coli strain expressing sGFP. The plasmid pCA24N-CsgA–MBD23,49,50, which constitutively encodes curli fibers fused to a metal-binding domain, was introduced by electroporation. The resulting strain, E. coli MBD-GFP, constitutively produces both chromosomally encoded sGFP and plasmid-encoded curli fibers with a metal-binding domain.

A fluorescent S. algae strain was obtained by heteroexpression of a mCherry plasmid, in which the mCherry gene is driven by the constitutive PLtetO-1 promoter51,52. Briefly, competent S. algae cells were first prepared and then transformed with plasmid pZS-Ptet-mCherry-Chl carrying the mCherry expression cassette. Transformants were selected on LB agar plates supplemented with tetracycline (100 µg/mL) and incubated at 30 °C for 14 h. Positive colonies were confirmed and propagated in liquid LB medium under the same conditions to establish the strain S. algae/mCherry.

Carbon steel incubations

X60 carbon steel (composition wt%: C, 0.12; Si, 0.25; Mn,1.50; S, 0.006; P, 0.02; N, 0.07; Ti, 0.012; Nb, 0.03; Al, 0.025; V, 0.08; and Fe, 97.997) was cut into square coupons (10 mm wide × 10 mm long × 5 mm thick), polished with silicon carbide paper to 1000 grit, ultrasonically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol for 20 min, air-dried, and sterilized under UV light for at least 30 min. For electrochemical measurements, a copper wire was soldered to one side of the coupon to serve as a working electrode.

To colonize steel coupons with biofilms, coupons were incubated in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 30 mL of sterile growth medium, sealed with gas-permeable film. Biofilms were grown aerobically in previously described medium as follows: T. mesophilum D-6 biofilms 3 days at 30 °C19; P. lipolytica for 14 days at 37 °C12; and E. coli MBD for 3 days at 37 °C23.

Corrosion experiments were performed in 50 mL anaerobic vials containing 30 mL of sterile anaerobic medium. The specific anaerobic defined mineral media used were: a simulated seawater medium (g L-1: NaCl 23.476, KCl 0.664, CaCl2·6H2O 1.469, Na2SO4 3.917, KBr 0.096, H3BO3 0.026, NaHCO3 0.18, Na2CO3 0.41, MgCl2·6H2O 10.61, SrCl2·6H2O 0.04) for S. algae, NBAF medium for G. sulfurreducens28, and NBLS medium for D. vulgaris25. All media were degassed by bubbling with N2/CO2 (80:20) for 5 min without a stopper, followed by 10 min with a thick butyl rubber stopper, and an additional 3 min in the headspace. The biofilm-colonized carbon steel coupons were transferred into the vials in an anaerobic glove bag (Coy Laboratory Products, MI, USA; 7% H2, 20% CO2, 73% N2). The vials were resealed with the butyl rubber stoppers, removed from the glove bag and the headspace was flushed with sterile N2/CO2 (80:20) for 10 min. Cultures of S. algae, G. sulfurreducens, or D. vulgaris were inoculated aseptically via pre-flushed with oxygen-free gas syringes to a final concentration of 5% (v/v) and incubated for 7 days at 30 °C for corrosion evaluation.

Biofilm and Planktonic Growth Characterization

Biofilms were observed with scanning electron microscopy (EVO10, Zeiss, Germany). Samples were rinsed with a pH 7.4 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution, fixed with 4% (v/v) glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol (50–100%), sputter-coated with gold, and imaged at 20 kV.

Biofilms of microbial strains expressing fluorescent proteins were examined with confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM 900, Zeiss, Germany). Superfolder green fluorescent protein was excited at 488 nm (Fig. S2a), and mCherry was excited at 587 nm (Fig. S2b). Z-stack images were acquired to reconstruct the three-dimensional architecture of the biofilms on the carbon steel coupon surfaces.

Biofilm biomass was quantified with crystal violet staining. Coupons were rinsed with PBS, air-dried, stained with 1% (w/v) crystal violet for 20 min, washed to remove excess dye, and destained with 33% acetic acid. Absorbance at 570 nm (OD570) was measured using a microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek).

Growth of planktonic cells in sulfide-adaption studies was monitored by inserting the anaerobic pressure tubes directly in a spectrophotometer (Model Genesys 50, Thermo Scientific, USA) and measuring absorbance at 600 nm (OD600).

Corrosion measurements

To estimate iron loss from the carbon steel coupons, they were washed with Clarke’s solution to remove corrosion products following ASTM G1-03 standards53 prior to weighing the coupons. Pit topography of the cleaned coupons was analyzed with confocal scanning laser microscopy in bright-field mode. Data were processed with Mountains ConfoMap Premium (Zeiss). H2 in the headspace of carbon steel incubations was measured with a gas chromatograph equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (Trace 1310, Thermo Scientific, USA).

Electrochemical measurements were conducted in a 500 mL three-electrode glass cell. A platinum sheet (10 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm) served as the counter electrode, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode, and a carbon steel coupon with an exposed area of 1 cm2 as the working electrode. All tests, including open-circuit potential (EOCP), linear polarization resistance (LPR), and potentiodynamic polarization (PDP), were performed using a Gamry Reference 600 potentiostat (Gamry Instruments, USA). LPR was measured by scanning from ‒10 mV to +10 mV relative to EOCP at a rate of 0.167 mV/s. The polarization resistance (Rp) was calculated from the linear slope of the potential-current curve within ±5 mV of the EOCP. PDP curves were recorded by scanning from ‒0.3 V to +0.3 V relative to EOCP at a rate of 0.333 mV/s. The corrosion current density (icorr) was determined by Tafel extrapolation of the PDP curves.
