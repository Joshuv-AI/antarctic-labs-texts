Prognostic Factors and Treatment Effect Modifiers for Acupuncture Response in Patients with Neurogenic Claudication of Degenerative Lumbar Spinal Stenosis: Secondary Analysis of a Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Xinyu Shen, Shuai Gao, Jun Liang, Jiufei Fang, He Chen, Wei Wang, Zhishun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAcupuncture has shown its efficacy in improving pain-specific disability among patients with degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) who predominantly present with neurogenic claudication pain symptoms. It is still unclear which group of the patients have better response to acupuncture. This secondary analysis of a randomized controlled trial aimed to identify potential prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers of response to acupuncture in DLSS patients with neurogenic claudication.Patients and MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted based on a multi-center randomized controlled trial, where 196 DLSS patients were randomized to acupuncture or sham acupuncture and 178 completed the 6-week intervention. Responders were defined by a 2-point reduction in the modified Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) score from baseline to week 6. Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Seventeen baseline characteristics were considered as potential prognostic factors of response and subjected to logistic regression analysis.ResultsAmong the 178 patients included in this secondary analysis, 119 were defined as responders. Treatment assignment (OR 2.0977; 95% CI 1.0300 to 4.2721; p = 0.0412), age (OR 1.0684, 95% CI 1.0176 to 1.1217; p = 0.0078), and Swiss Spinal Stenosis Questionnaire (SSSQ) symptom severity score (OR 1.1270; 95% CI 1.0077 to 1.2606; p = 0.0363) were positively associated with patient response. Patients with a shorter duration of the current episode (OR 0.9384; 95% CI 0.9020 to 0.9764; p = 0.0017) might specifically benefit more from acupuncture.ConclusionDLSS patients with receiving acupuncture, older age, higher SSSQ symptom severity score, and shorter duration of current episode may be more likely to be responder. Duration of current episode modifies the response of acupuncture. These findings provide preliminary evidence to inform patient selection and warrant validation in future studies.

Introduction

Degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) is a common spinal disorder commonly caused by disk protrusion, facet joint osteophytes, ligamentum flavum buckling and hypertrophy, and spondylolisthesis.1 DLSS is a worldwide prevalent disease. According to statistics, there are approximately 103 million people with DLSS worldwide and more than 200 thousand people to be affected annually in the United States alone.2,3 Additionally, about 20% of individuals older than 60 years are found to have lumbar spinal stenosis based on imaging data.4 Neurogenic claudication is the cardinal symptom of DLSS. Patients typically present with gradually worsening low back, buttock, and lower extremity pain, numbness, and other discomfort induced by lumbar spinal stenosis when the spine is extended, such as during standing upright or walking, whereas the symptoms are relieved or disappear when sitting or bending forward. This phenomenon is attributed to a reduction in the cross-sectional area of the central spinal canal and neural foramina during spinal extension.5 In later stages, patients may exhibit decreased mobility, including balance disorders and wide-based gait.4 Moreover, DLSS often imposes a substantial economic burden. Data indicated that the 2-year cost of nonoperative management of lumbar spinal stenosis was as high as $9,892 per person, and the total surgical expenditure in the United States reached $15 billion in 2018. Accordingly, more appropriate management strategies for lumbar spinal stenosis are needed.3,4

Nonsurgical treatments of DLSS causing neurogenic claudication are limited.6–8 Due to the lack of specific treatment, the guidelines recommend using nonpharmacological therapies first for DLSS causing neurogenic claudication.6 Our previous network meta-analysis found that acupuncture was associated with clinically important improvements in pain and function compared with placebo.9 In contrast, no other interventions showed clear benefits over placebo for pain or function. These findings suggest that acupuncture is considered as one of the potential options for DLSS-related neurogenic claudication. In a high-quality randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted by our group, 18-session acupuncture effectively reduced DLSS-related disability.10 However, the response rate to acupuncture was only 54.4%, and nearly half of the patients were below the threshold for minimal clinically important difference (MCID).10 Acupuncture is a complex intervention with multiple contributing components. The clinical effect of acupuncture may depend on patient and context-related factors, which partly explains why some patients gain greater benefit than others.11–13 As response to acupuncture varies substantially across individuals, identifying response-related factors is critical for refining clinical decision-making. Current evidence regarding which patients are most likely to benefit from acupuncture remains uncertain. An individual patient data meta-analysis found no evidence for the existence of exceptional acupuncture responders.14 However, other studies have proposed that baseline severity of pain or functional disability, as well as temporal summation of pain rather than treatment expectations, are prognostic factors of acupuncture treatment response.15–17 In addition, no studies have examined prognostic factors of acupuncture response specifically in patients with neurogenic claudication due to DLSS. Therefore, we carried out this secondary analysis to evaluate the associations between potential prognostic factors (including age, gender, etc) and response, and treatment effect modifiers for acupuncture response, aiming to identify factors that might influence response among patients with neurogenic claudication and informing clinical decision-making for doctors and patients.

Methods

Overview of the Original Trial

The original trial is a multi-center RCT conducted at five hospitals in China.10 The protocol was approved by the institutional review committees or ethics committees at each participating site, and it was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03784729). Every participant signed the informed consent before participation.

Detailed methods have been described in our published article.10 In brief, this trial consisted of a 1-week run-in period, a 6-week intervention with either acupuncture or sham acupuncture (SA), and a 24-week post-treatment follow-up. Primary outcome was patient-reported disability, manifested by the change in the Chinese-version modified Roland-Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ) score from baseline to week 6.18,19 Secondary outcomes included buttock and/or leg pain and lower back pain on standing, walking, or spinal extension measured by the numerical rating scale (NRS);20 general DLSS symptoms, physical function, and satisfaction toward treatment as measured by the Swiss Spinal Stenosis Questionnaire (SSSQ);21–23 the severity of anxiety and depression measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).24,25 In total, 263 patients from March 2019 to August 2021 were selected, and 196 eligible patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the acupuncture group (n = 98) or the SA group (n = 98). Eighteen patients discontinued treatment, and 178 patients completed the 6-week treatment course and the end-of-treatment assessment.

Secondary Analysis Design

This secondary analysis was designed to evaluate the data from patients who received acupuncture or SA and completed the 6-week treatment. Responders were defined as participants who achieved a 2-point reduction from baseline in the modified RMDQ score at week 6.6,26 All other participants were classified as non-responders.

The potential prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers comprised all baseline variables prospectively collected in the original trial. These baseline variables included demographic characteristics such as treatment assignment, age, gender, race, body mass index (BMI), and some clinical characteristics associated with DLSS, including duration of symptoms, duration of the current episode (The longer of duration of current back pain episode or duration of current buttock and/or leg pain episode), anterior and posterior diameter of the narrowest part of lumbar central canal, modified RMDQ score, NRS of buttock and/or leg pain, NRS of back pain, SSSQ score, HADS score, prior acupuncture treatment experience, comorbidities, participants’ expectations of acupuncture and participants’ expectations of acupuncture in DLSS at baseline.

Statistical Analysis

Only participants with available end-of-treatment data were included in the secondary analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2).27 Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. For continuous variables, normally distributed data were expressed as means and standard deviations (SD), whereas non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians along with interquartile range (IQR). For categorical variables, counts and percentages were adopted. Binary univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify candidate predictors. Variables with p-value <0.1000 were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis.28 The statistical significance level for logistic regression was determined as a two-sided p-value <0.05. Before the multivariate regression analysis, collinearity among predictors was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). A VIF exceeding 5 was indicative of collinearity. Interactions between treatment assignment and each predictor included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis were evaluated. The assumption of linearity in the log-odds for continuous variables in multivariate logistic regression analysis was evaluated using restricted cubic splines (with 4 knots). Model complexity was quantified using the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio. Model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap approach (using 1000 bootstrap samples of all the data).

Results

Study Population

A total of 178 participants who completed the 6-week treatment were included in our secondary analysis. According to the prespecified responder definition, among the 178 participants who had available end-of-treatment outcome data, 119 (66.85%) were classified as responders and 59 (33.15%) as non-responders at week 6. This rate is higher than the 54.4% response rate reported in the original trial under the intention-to-treat principle. In univariate analysis, baseline characteristics such as age (p = 0.0730), gender (p = 0.3381), race (p = 0.7935), BMI (p = 0.9176), duration of symptoms (p = 0.3727), the narrowest anterior and posterior diameter of lumbar central canal (p = 0.2406), NRS score (p > 0.1000), HADS score (p = 0.6069), prior acupuncture treatment (p = 0.8540), comorbidity (p = 0.3438) and participants’ expectations (p > 0.1000) were not found to be predictors of treatment response. A longer duration of current episode (OR 0.9503; 95% CI 0.9171 to 0.9848; p = 0.0050) was associated with a decreased rate of treatment response. Conversely, a higher RMDQ score (OR 1.1485; 95% CI 1.0450 to 1.2622; p = 0.0041) was correlated to a higher rate of treatment response.

Variables with p <0.1000 (n = 5) were considered as candidate predictors and included in multivariable modeling. No collinearity was observed among the variables (VIFs < 1.2376). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, 4 predictors were significantly associated with acupuncture response. Duration of current episode (OR 0.9384; 95% CI 0.9020 to 0.9764; p = 0.0017) remained as a predictor of treatment response. Patients with a shorter duration of current episode tended to achieve greater improvement. After controlling for other variables, participants receiving acupuncture had 2.0977 times higher odds of being responders than those receiving SA (OR 2.0977; 95% CI 1.0300 to 4.2721; p = 0.0412). Age of patient (OR 1.0684, 95% CI 1.0176 to 1.1217; p = 0.0078) also showed a correlation with treatment response, as the likelihood of response increased by 6.84% with each one-year increase. Moreover, symptom severity score of SSSQ (OR 1.1270; 95% CI 1.0077 to 1.2606; p = 0.0363) was associated with response, for each 1-point increase, the odds of response increased by 12.70%. Restricted cubic spline analyses did not indicate significant nonlinear relationships for age (P for nonlinearity = 0.7224), duration of the current episode (P for nonlinearity = 0.1772), RMDQ (P for nonlinearity = 0.1276) and SSSQ symptom severity (P for nonlinearity = 0.5577).

The interaction term between treatment assignment and duration of current episode was statistically significant (OR 0.9097; 95% CI 0.8297 to 0.9975; p = 0.0441). The model-predicted probability of response across duration of current episode was shown in Figure 1, indicating that the rate of response in acupuncture group decreased with the extension of duration of current episode. Discussion

This secondary analysis investigated prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers for acupuncture response in DLSS patients with neurogenic claudication. Older age, higher SSSQ symptom severity score and shorter duration of current episode were associated with higher response rates. Shorter duration of the current episode showed a significant interaction with treatment assignment and might act as a treatment-effect modifier, suggesting that patients with shorter duration of the current episode may specifically benefit more from acupuncture. Other baseline characteristics, including gender, race, BMI, disease duration, anterior and posterior diameter of the narrowest part of the lumbar central canal, modified RMDQ score, NRS buttock and/or leg pain score, NRS back pain score, HADS score, prior acupuncture treatment experience, comorbidities, and participants’ expectations, were not correlated with response to acupuncture in this analysis.

DLSS patients receiving acupuncture had a higher treatment response rate than those with SA. This is consistent with our previous report that acupuncture outperformed SA in patients with DLSS. However, this finding should be accepted with caution. Our interaction analysis suggested that the specific benefit of acupuncture over sham acupuncture was duration-dependent. Patients with a shorter duration of the current episode derived greater additional benefit from acupuncture, whereas this advantage attenuated with prolonged duration of the current episode, with the predicted probability curves intersecting at approximately 14 months. This finding supported the consideration of earlier initiation of acupuncture treatment. However, given that the confidence interval for this interaction term approached 1.0, this observation is of marginal statistical significance and should be regarded as exploratory.

In our analysis of candidate factors, independent regression analyses were conducted to avoid the influence of treatment allocation.

Duration of current episode showed a significant difference between responders and non-responders. For each additional month of current episode, the rate of response decreased by 6.16%. Consistently, a previous primary care study on low back pain reported that a longer duration of current episode was associated with a higher Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) score at 1 year (OR 2.36) and 5 years (OR 1.79).18 These results support the prognostic relevance of the duration of current episode. Pathophysiological studies have suggested that neurogenic claudication is primarily explained by ischemic mechanisms and venous congestion. Inoue et al reported that acupuncture improved symptoms by increasing the blood flow around the sciatic nerve.29 In addition, structural and biological changes around the spine might accumulate as the duration of current episode increases. Other studies revealed that hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum and intervertebral disc degeneration upregulated the chronic inflammatory response-related cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6); increased IL-6 contributed to inflammatory edema and further promoted ligamentum flavum hypertrophy by increasing collagen deposition and fibroblast activity, leading to increased neural compression with obvious symptoms.30,31 Mechanistic studies of acupuncture for lumbar spinal stenosis suggested that acupuncture might improve symptoms by alleviating edema and muscle spasm, inhibiting the release of proinflammatory factors and modulating spinal cord synaptic plasticity.32 However, there is currently no established theory to support that acupuncture can reverse intervertebral disc degenerative lesions or reduce ligament hypertrophy. A longer duration of current episode may present with more potential serious pathological changes that cannot be improved by acupuncture, which may partly explain the poor responsiveness to acupuncture. Research also showed that factors resulting from a prolonged duration of lumbar spinal stenosis symptoms, such as biomechanical changes and muscle atrophy, also led to a reduced rate of treatment response.33 Collectively, the worsening of low back pain is not only a source of short-term discomfort, and patients with a longer duration of current episode may be less likely to achieve improvement, highlighting the need for additional clinical studies to identify effective treatment strategies for this subgroup.

Previous findings about the role of age as a prognostic factor of response are inconsistent. In a retrospective cohort study where low back pain was considered as the major symptom in patients, the acupuncture-associated decrease in lumbar surgery rate was more evident in older age groups.34 Conversely, there was a statistically significant additional benefit observed from acupuncture treatment in younger participants with low back pain in a systematic review.35 In the present study, older patients were more likely to be responders than younger patients, and the probability increased by 6.84% with each year increase in age. However, the role of patient age as a prognostic factor of response to acupuncture remains unclear. The possible explanations are the inconsistent type of diseases and that age may be an indirect measure of other factors that may affect the response.

As expected, higher scores of SSSQ at baseline were identified as a potential response prognostic factor, which was consistent with previous study.36 A higher baseline SSSQ score was associated with an increased chance of improvement, which may be due to the greater room for reduction among patients with higher baseline scores.36,37 Notably, the RMDQ score and the NRS score were not significant in logistic regression analysis. RMDQ was significantly associated with treatment response in univariable analysis but lost significance after multivariable adjustment. This may reflect partially overlapping prognostic information between RMDQ and SSSQ symptom severity. RMDQ is a generic measure of low back pain related disability, whereas SSSQ captures disease-specific symptom severity in lumbar spinal stenosis. When both measures were entered into the model, SSSQ appeared to provide stronger and more disease-specific prognostic information, thereby reducing the independent effect of RMDQ.38,39

According to the above results, we might infer that the response of DLSS might not be influenced by baseline characteristics, including gender, BMI, psychological stress, expectations, imaging characteristics, and comorbidities. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that the reliability may be decreased due to the small sample size. With respect to race, this trial was conducted only in China, where ancestry differences are relatively small, and only 7 non-Han patients were included in the study. Findings should be interpreted primarily within the context of Han Chinese patients with DLSS. As a result, external validity to other racial groups and populations is limited. Additional multi-regional studies and more ethnically diverse studies are needed to validate the findings across different populations and clinical practice settings.

Imaging characteristics did not predict response in this analysis. Weber et al reported that Imaging characteristics were unrelated to disability and pain in DLSS, suggesting that imaging findings might not reflect clinical severity.40 This was consistent with our finding that the anterior and posterior diameter of the narrowest part of the lumbar central canal was not a prognostic factor of efficacy for neurogenic claudication. In addition, although the Schizas and Lee systems were used to grade lumbar spinal canal stenosis, they assessed the relationship between the cerebrospinal fluid space and the cauda equina, which was not evaluated in this study.41 Therefore, future studies may be needed to determine whether baseline Schizas or Lee grades influence response to acupuncture.

Unexpectedly, depression, anxiety and expectations were not significant prognostic factors in our study, although they are components in biopsychosocial model.42–44 Possible explanations for this discrepancy might be that our study population was not entirely the same, in addition, the type of intervention and the time of follow-up differed.

Acupuncture is commonly used to manage patients with chronic pain. However, prognostic factors and treatment effect modifiers for acupuncture response have not been widely investigated. Further exploration is needed to identify baseline characteristics that can affect the response. It is of great significance to conduct a secondary analysis of a large-scale RCT for DLSS patients with neurogenic claudication. The present study examines prognostic factors in a highly generalizable sample of DLSS patients with neurogenic claudication receiving acupuncture. Thus, the findings contribute to the field by providing preliminary evidence to help identify patients who may be more likely to benefit from acupuncture and may support initial pre-treatment prognosis assessment to inform therapeutic decision-making.

However, this secondary analysis has several limitations. First, candidate variables were restricted to variables captured from the original trial, and potentially relevant factors, such as detailed imageology parameters, walking distance limitations, physical activity status, socioeconomic indicators (eg, household income and job occupation), were not included. Second, this study was conducted in China and included only Chinese participants. Generalizability of findings to other populations should be interpreted with caution. Third, the accuracy of the results may be decreased given the small sample size. Fourthly, this secondary analysis was restricted to participants who completed the 6-week intervention, which might introduce bias.

Conclusions

DLSS patients with older age, a higher SSSQ symptom severity score, and shorter duration of current episode may be more likely to be responder, whereas those with a shorter duration of the current episode may specifically benefit more from acupuncture. The reliability and significance of this conclusion remain to be substantiated through further studies.
