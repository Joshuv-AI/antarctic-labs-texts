The patterns and determinants of healthcare utilisation of older adults in rural Odisha, India – a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohanasundaram Mourougan, Arvind Kumar Singh, Abhisek Mishra, Binod Kumar Behera, Binod Kumar Patro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndia’s population is ageing rapidly, with an estimate that one in every five Indians will be 60 years or older by 2050. Ageing with the ongoing epidemiological transition increases the burden on already strained healthcare systems of the country. Older adults face health related, financial and social vulnerabilities including access to healthcare facilities, especially in rural areas. The study aims to find the patterns and determinants of healthcare utilisation of older adults especially in rural areas.MethodsThis community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from two blocks in the Khordha district, Odisha, India from March 2023 to April 2024. Households containing older adults were included in the study. The study used a 30 × 15 cluster sampling design with villages as primary sampling units(clusters) selected by probability proportional to size. 15 households from each cluster were selected by simple random sampling. We also compared the healthcare utilisation of older adults with younger family members with health expenditure.ResultsThe study collected information from 440 households and 604 older adults. More than half of the older adults (52.5%) had outpatient visits in the previous month. Though the majority (69.7%) visited doctors with MBBS degrees and specialists for consultation, more than two-fifths (22.3%) visited pharmacies for outpatient care and consulted pharmacists. Preference for private health facilities (41.6%) was seen among older adults. 8.1% of older adults were hospitalised in the previous year, predominantly (67.3%) in the private health facilities. The median distances to health facilities visited for outpatient visits and hospitalisations were 2 km and 20 km, respectively. The multivariable analysis showed that utilisation of public health facilities was higher among the poorest quintiles (AOR: 2.57; 95% CI: 1.09–6.08; p = 0.032) and poorer quintiles (AOR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.01–4.42; p = 0.047) of monthly per capita consumption expenditure. On comparison with younger family members, the older adults had more outpatient visits with pharmacists and AYUSH practitioners.ConclusionThe study shows that higher healthcare utilisation among older adults, but public healthcare utilisation remains low. The public healthcare utilisation, mostly by poorer population, highlights equity concerns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06706-x.Keywords: Older adults health, Rural population, Healthcare utilisation, Public and private health facilities

Background

The global population is ageing, with the population worldwide aged 65 years and above is expected to increase from 10% in 2022 to 16% in 2050 [1]. India, the most populous country in the world, is experiencing a demographic shift, resulting in a growing older adult population. By 2050, one in every five Indians is expected to be 60 years or older [2].

Countries face significant challenges in adapting their health and social systems to the demands of demographic shift [3]. The combination of demographic and epidemiological transition burdens the strained health systems of developing countries, especially due to the rise of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). The Longitudinal Ageing Study in India(LASI) reported that the older adults experienced a disproportionately higher disease burden compared to those under 60 years [4]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, NCD-related deaths in India have risen, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) increase with age, from 1.1 in the 45–49 age group to 4.5 in those 85 and older [5]. The Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health (SAGE) revealed that 60.7% of people aged 70 and above have multiple chronic diseases compared to only 12.3% of those aged 18 to 49 years [6]. The LASI study also noted that older adults required more inpatient and outpatient care than those aged 45 to 59 years [4].

According to the 75th round of the National Sample Survey(NSS), only 28% of older adults in rural India and 33% in urban India were economically self-sufficient [7]. Only around one-third of India’s older adults use public healthcare, with the rest opting for private facilities [8]. The rising need for healthcare and its costs, along with financial dependence on other family members, pose a huge challenge to older adults. Due to their financial vulnerability, older adults may not seek healthcare when required, further worsening their health-related morbidities.

Moreover, the healthcare utilisation in rural areas of the country, where two-thirds of the population resides, was lower than in urban areas [9, 10]. The rural areas of the country face challenges such as inadequate infrastructure, inequitable distribution and the shortage of health workforce, requiring special attention [11–14]. Odisha, the state where the study is conducted, is also witnessing a consistent increase in its older adult population, who are expected to double between 2011 and 2036 [15]. Studies have also shown the population to have high morbidity but low care-seeking behaviour [16–19]. Additionally, there were mixed findings among health care utilisation and health seeking behaviour among older adults in comparison to younger population [20, 21].

The LASI Phase 1 study [4], conducted in 2017-18, yielded important national-level insights into ageing in India. However, there remains a gap in primary evidence concerning healthcare utilisation among older adults in rural regions, particularly in the period following the LASI study. This study seeks to address this gap by exploring the patterns and determinants of healthcare utilisation among older adults and comparing the patterns with those of younger family members to see the vulnerabilities among the older adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted this community-based cross-sectional study from March 2023 to April 2024 in the Khordha district of Odisha. The state of Odisha is located in the eastern part of India, with a population of 42 million and comprises 30 districts. Figure 1 shows the location of Khordha district in Odisha, India. Khordha district has 10 community development blocks. According to the 2011 Census, the district has a population of 2,251,673, including 1,534 villages. Older adults (aged 60 years and above) represent 9.09% of the total population, accounting for 204,845 people [22]. Two blocks, namely Tangi and Bhubaneswar, were purposively selected for the study as the community health centres (CHC) in these blocks were the rural field practice areas of the Department of Community Medicine & Family Medicine, AIIMS Bhubaneswar. CHC provides secondary-level care at the block level in India.

The current study is a part of a larger research focused on estimating health expenditure among the older adults. The sample size for the main study was determined based on the incidence of Catastrophic health expenditure at the 10% threshold level in 2018 in Odisha was 24.3% [24]. With an absolute precision of 5% and a design effect of 1.5, the sample size is calculated as equal to 424. Assuming a 5% non-response rate, the sample size came out to be 446. It was rounded and 450 households were considered for the study.

A 30 × 15 two-stage cluster sampling design was used in the study, adapted from the World Health Organisation’s 30 × 7 EPI cluster survey method, which is widely used in community-based research [25]. The design was chosen due to its operational feasibility and ability to provide geographical representativeness. In the first stage, 30 clusters were selected using Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) [26]. As the study was planned to be conducted in rural areas, villages were chosen as the clusters and were the primary sampling units. The list of all the villages was obtained from the block hospitals. The villages with populations over 500 individuals were considered as clusters, while villages with populations less than 500 were combined with the next adjacent villages if selected as clusters. However, during sampling, none of the selected villages had populations below 500, so no combinations were necessary.

In the second stage, 15 households containing at least one older adult (aged ≥ 60 years) in each cluster were selected by simple random sampling. The line list of households from the selected cluster was obtained from the registers maintained by community health workers of the cluster. From the list, households containing older adults were listed out and numbered sequentially. Fifteen households were selected randomly using Google’s random number generator. The choice of 15 households per cluster was based on the calculated sample size requirement of 450 households while maintaining feasibility for collection from 30 clusters. Figure 2 gives the summary of the sampling strategy followed in the study. Households were included in the study if they had at least one older adult (age ≥ 60 years) who had been residing in the selected village for more than six months at the time of data collection. Households were excluded if the older adult had severe cognitive, hearing, visual, or other impairments that made it impossible to understand or respond to the interview, even with appropriate support.

Sampling strategy and the study procedure followed in the study

Data collection

A semi-structured questionnaire, developed based on our comprehensive literature review, was used for data collection. The questionnaire is attached in the supplementary file. It captured sociodemographic details and details of healthcare utilisation. The caste to which the family belonged was asked, as the caste is a hereditary marker of social hierarchy and status in the Indian context [27]. The socioeconomic status of the household was assessed using Udai Pareek and G. Trivedi’s socioeconomic status scale. The scale is widely used in community-based research in rural settings in India to capture multidimensional aspects of rural socioeconomic status. In addition, as we had also collected the information on consumption expenditure, as part of expenditure estimation, Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure(MPCE) quintiles were computed for comparisons with national surveys. The below poverty line (BPL) status was assessed using the presence of priority ration cards. The poorest of BPL households possessed Antodaya Anna Yojana cards [28]. The eligibility for Biju Swasthiya Kalyan Yojana (BSKY), the state sponsored health assurance scheme, was assessed by the presence of a BSKY card [29]. Healthcare utilisation was assessed by asking whether the older adults had experienced any health-related ailment in the past year and if treatment was sought. To reduce recall bias, further data on outpatient visits and hospitalisations were taken for the last month and last year as the reference period, respectively. Questions focused on the type of healthcare provider and facility visited, reasons for the visit, distance and travel time to the facility, and overall satisfaction with the healthcare experience. To compare the patterns of utilisation, the data on outpatient visits and hospitalisation were also collected from other family members of the household with any outpatient visit or hospitalisation. Prior to the survey, a pre-test was conducted in December 2022 with 15 households each in two villages in one of the study blocks to check the clarity and relevance of the questions and ensure its appropriateness for the study context. The villages were excluded from final sampling during PPS cluster selection. Based on pretest findings, modifications were made to improve question wording and sequencing. The questionnaire used was developed in English and translated into the local language (Odia) for use in the field.

Data collection and ethical considerations

The selected households were identified with the help of a community health worker in the cluster. The ethical approval was taken from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Reference number IEC/AIIMS BBSR/PG Thesis/2022-23/54). The written informed consent was taken in the local language. For participants who were unable to read or write, the consent form was read aloud and thumb impression of the older adults were taken instead of the signature. For participants with mild visual or hearing impairments, the consent was taken after saying the contents slowly and clearly or reading aloud as needed to ensure understanding of the process. The face-to-face interviews were conducted in the houses of older adults to ensure privacy and comfort. The interviews were conducted by MM, a trained doctor, with the help of people who were well-versed in the local language. The questions were asked in the local language after sufficient training. Each interview spanned from 20 to 30 min.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data was collected through Epicollect v5.1.52 [30]. Statistical analysis was done using the Stata version 14.2 from StataCorp. Categorical variables were presented by proportion or percentages with a 95% confidence interval, and continuous variables were presented by mean with standard deviation and median with an interquartile range for non-parametric variables. Associations were carried out by appropriate statistical tests of significance. A binary logistic regression model was used to identify the determinants of public healthcare facilities usage for outpatient services. The dependent variable used was healthcare facility type (public vs. others). The independent variables were selected based on the literature, including gender, marital status, education, caste, employment status, socio-economic classification (Udai Pareekh scale), and MPCE quintiles. The variables that came out significant (p < 0.2) were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. The adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Model diagnostics including multicollinearity and model fit (pesudo R², Hosmer–Lemeshow test) were examined.

Results

A total of 450 households with at least one older adult (≥ 60 years) were approached for data collection. Out of them, ten households did not meet the study’s eligibility criteria, resulting in a final sample of 440 households with older adults.

Sociodemographic characteristics of the households

The household characteristics and sociodemographic characteristics of the study population are given in Table 1. Most households belonged to the Hindu religion (433 households, 98.4%) and the general caste category (260 households, 59.1%). Socioeconomic classification based on the Udai Pareek Scale showed that 279 (63.4%) households belonged to the lower middle class. The majority of households, possessed a ration card (367 (83.4%)), indicating that they belonged to the below poverty line. Among the older adults, the mean age of the older adults was 70.3 years. Among the 571 older adults for whom work status was available, 84 (14.7%) individuals were currently employed, 229 (40.1%) individuals were currently non-employed, and 258 (45.2%) individuals had never been employed in income-generating work or were homemakers. Among those who were currently employed or previously employed, the majority had elementary occupations (205 individuals, 65.5%), which included agricultural farm labourers, building construction labourers, manufacturing labourers and street vendors. Information on self-reported diagnosed chronic health conditions was available for 559 older adults. Out of 604 elderly individuals, 520 (86.1%) experienced a health-related illness in the past year, with 507 (97.5%) of these visited a healthcare facility. The most commonly reported conditions were hypertension (41.5%), musculoskeletal disorders (28.4%), and diabetes mellitus (14.3%), followed by gastrointestinal problems, chronic respiratory diseases, stroke and cataract. A detailed breakdown of chronic conditions reported by older adults is provided in Annexure Supplementary Figure 1.

Healthcare utilisation of older adults - outpatient visits in the last one month

Out of 604 older adults, 317 (52.5%) had outpatient visits in the past month. Table 2 provides the characteristics of outpatient visits in the last one month and hospitalisation of the last year of the survey of the older adults. Among those with outpatient visits, most consultations occurred with doctors with MBBS degrees or specialists (69.7%), with 41.6% visiting private health facilities, 34.7% using public facilities, and 23.7% using other types. Public Primary Health Centres were the most visited (50.0%), while private clinics were predominant among private options (62.9%). Pharmacies accounted for 94.7% of other facility visits. Hypertension (42.9%), bone/joint problems (30.3%), and diabetes (18.3%) were the main reasons for visits. A detailed account of the type of health facilities visited and the reasons are provided in Supplementary Table 1 in annexure. The median distance to the facility was 2 km, with a median travel time of 10 min. Common transport modes included motorcycles/scooters (32.1%) and walking (30.0%). Satisfaction was high, with 81.7% satisfied and 8.2% very satisfied.

In the past year, 49 out of 604 older adults were hospitalized, with 47 admitted once and 2 admitted multiple times. The median stay during their last visit was 5 nights. Most utilised private facilities (67.3%), while 24.5% used public hospitals and 8.2% visited NGO-run hospitals. The primary reasons for hospitalization were sickness (87.8%) and injury/accidents (12.2%). Common conditions included surgery for ophthalmic causes (28.5%), respiratory illnesses (10.2%), and surgeries for abdominal causes (8.2%). The median distance to the facility was 20 km, with a median travel time of 40 min, mostly by autorickshaw (49%) and car/taxi (32.7%). Satisfaction with treatment was high, with 28.6% very satisfied and 59.2% satisfied.

Determinants of public healthcare facility usage for the outpatient visits of older adults

The determinants of public healthcare facility utilisation (given in Table 3) showed that gender, marital status, socioeconomic class and Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure (MPCE) quintiles appeared significant during bivariate analysis. In the multivariable regression model, socioeconomic class (Udai Pareekh scale) and MPCE quintiles remained significant. In comparison to the upper middle and middle classes, the older adults belonging to the lower middle and lower classes of Udai Pareekh scale had 1.70 times higher odds of using public healthcare facilities (AOR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.00–2.61.00.61; p = 0.05), with the association becoming marginally significant. Similarly, older adults in the poorest MPCE quintile were 2.57 times (AOR: 2.57; 95% CI: 1.09–6.08; p = 0.032); and poorer quintiles 2.11 times (AOR: 2.11; 95% CI: 1.01–4.42; p = 0.047). more likely to use public healthcare compared to the richest quintile.

Discussion

We collected the data of 440 households containing 604 older adults in the study. Of them, 86.1% experienced a health-related illness in the past year, with 83.9% having at least one healthcare visit in the preceding year of interview. This was slightly higher than the WHO Study on global AGEing and adult health (SAGE) survey, which reported 81% having a healthcare visit in the preceding year of the survey [6]. 97.5% of those with illness had a healthcare visit in our study. This was consistent with the analyses of 71 st and 75th round of National Sample Survey (NSS) [31, 32]. Our study aligns with the regional findings, such as India Health Systems Reform Project in Odisha by Yip et al., and the Assessment of Health Status of the Elderly in Tigiria using Syndemic approach (AHSETS study, (2019-20)) which indicated that approximately 90% and 98.5% seeking care following ailment [16, 33]. While the NSS and other studies used a shorter recall period (15 days or 30 days) and our study had a time frame of one year for this variable, the overall patterns of high utilisation was consistent.

More than half of the older adults (52.5%) surveyed had outpatient visits in the previous month. This was higher than the first wave of the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI) (2017-19), which reported 29.3% of older adults in rural areas and 24.5% in Odisha sought outpatient care the month prior to the survey [4]. In the AHSETS study, 24.14% of the older adults in their study suffered from some form of illness, and nearly all sought care [16]. In our study, most visits were to private health facilities (132 individuals, 41.6%), followed by public health facilities (110 individuals, 34.7%) and other facilities like pharmacies (75 individuals, 23.7%). A preference for private health facilities for outpatient care was seen among older adults. Ranjan and Muraleedharan, based on the 75th round of NSS, reported that share of proportion of ailing persons receiving outpatient care was 39.7% in the rural population, which is closely aligning with our finding [8]. The LASI study also reported the public facility utilisation to be 39.4% [4]. Similar preferences to use private facilities(53.3%) over government facilities (33.8%) were seen among the younger family members who had health expenditures, as reported by Haakenstad et al. in another study conducted in Odisha [34]. The higher finding on public healthcare utilisation in our study in comparison to the WHO SAGE and LASI surveys could be because the study was conducted after the implementation of health system strengthening measures such as Ayushman Bharat Health and Wellness centres [35].

The determinants for the utilisation of public health facilities for outpatient visits indicated that older adults belonging to lower and lower middle class utilised public healthcare facilites, 1.70 times more than upper middle and middle class(AOR: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.00–2.61.00.61; p = 0.05). The finding was consistent with another cross sectional study conducted in Odisha by Sarit Rout, which also said people from lower socioeconomic status used public facility more than private facility [36]. The utilisation was higher among the poorest MPCE quintile, with them having 2.57 times higher odds and poorer quintile 2.11 times higher odds of using public health facilities compared to the richest MPCE quintile. This finding aligns with our understanding and highlights the underlying inequities in healthcare access. In the LASI study, the poorest and poorer wealth quintiles were significantly more likely to use public facilities (OR: 1.491, p ≤ 0.001 and OR: 1.264, p ≤ 0.001, respectively) [10]. The socially and economically disadvantaged populations are dependent on public health facilities for healthcare, likely due to the affordability of services. While the wealthier individuals seek care in private facilities and pay out-of-pocket, the poorer population tend to utilise the government-sponsored public health facilities. The findings also highlight the need for continued investment in quality and health system strengthening of public health infrastructure.

Though the majority (69.7%) visited doctors with MBBS degrees and specialists for consultation, more than one-fifth (22.3%) visited pharmacies and consulted pharmacists for outpatient care. Similar results were reported by Kalita et al. in Odisha. In their study, 17% of households use private pharmacies for outpatient treatment which was same as the proportion of individuals using public primary health care facilities [37]. However, in the AHSETS study, pharmacy visits for outpatient care in their study at 10.85% [16]. The lower proportion in their study could have been due to difference in the study setting. The study by Bose et al. examined the relationship of private pharmacies in Odisha and the health facilities in the state. Their findings showed that private pharmacies in rural areas of the state perform the substitutive caregiving role of the public health facilities, primarily due to a shortage of healthcare workers [11]. Consistency of our findings to these results emphasises the accessibility and availability issues within the formal health system.

In our study, the top five self-reported chronic conditions were hypertension (41.5%), musculoskeletal problems (28.4%), diabetes mellitus (14.3), gastritis and other gastrointestinal problems (11.3) and chronic respiratory diseases (10.6%). The pattern was similar to chronic conditions in older adults, as reported in the LASI report. The LASI has reported hypertension (32%), diabetes mellitus (14.2%) and chronic respiratory diseases (8.3%) [38]. The similarity with the national trends reinforces the need for integrated chronic care models, such as the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Non-Communicable Diseases (NP-NCD), to be strengthened. Though our study did not capture the multimorbidities, the findings are important as the AHSETS study notes the prevalence of multimorbidity to be 48.8% in a similar setting [39].

The main reasons for outpatient visits by the older adults in our study were non-communicable diseases like hypertension (42.9%), bone or joint problems (30.3%), diabetes (18.3%), gastritis (10.4%) and chronic respiratory problems (9.7%). Similar findings were seen in the study by Ranjan and Muaraleedharan, which reported hypertension (32.0%), diabetes (22.5%), musculoskeletal conditions (13.9%), infectious diseases (10.0%), and respiratory ailments (7.3%) as the top five conditions for seeking outpatient care among the older adults in the last 15 days [8]. A study among rural older adults in the Bargarh district of Odisha in 2011-12 reported that arthritis (52.4%), chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases (20.0%) and high blood pressure (19.3%) were the three leading causes of self-reported chronic diseases in the study [18]. The differences noted in our study could be due to increased awareness of NCDs currently and the programmes targeting NCDs include health promotion and awareness generation as one of its key strategies. Another study conducted in 2013-14 from ten districts of Odisha reported acid peptic disorder (31%), hypertension (16.4%) and arthritis (15.4%) as the leading causes for primary care visits [40]. The difference could be due to differences in the age of the study population and subsequently the morbidities.

In our study, 49 individuals (8.1 per cent) had hospitalisation in the last year. This proportion is comparable with other national surveys. 8 per cent of hospitalisation for the older adults was also found in wave 1 of the LASI survey, and analysis by Ranjan and Muraleedharan based on the 75th round NSS showed hospitalisation was 7.7% in rural areas in older adults during the last year of the survey [4, 8]. The older adults, on average, spent 8.5 days in the hospital in the LASI survey, whereas the median hospital stay in our study was 5 days [4]. Cardiovascular diseases (18.1%), infectious diseases (16.6%), eye ailments (8.4%), psychiatric or neurological conditions (8.2%), and injuries (7.9%) were the top five reasons for hospitalization in the last 365 days in 75th round of NSS. In our study, top five reasons were admission for eye-related complaints (2%), surgery for other causes (17%), cardiovascular diseases (14%), chronic respiratory diseases (10%), vector borne diseases like dengue (8%) and injuries (8%). The change observed in our study could be because of fewer hospitalisation(n = 49) which could have introduced bias in representation. The other reason for increased surgery especially for cataract and other causes could be because of wider availability of health insurance in our study in comparison to studies based on NSS and LASI as the study was conducted after the roll out of BSKY health insurance scheme in Odisha. This highlights a positive impact of recent health financing reforms in the state.

The median distances of health facilities and time taken to reach them were 2 km and 10 min for outpatient visits and 20 km and 40 min for hospitalisations, respectively. This finding was similar to the study by Yip et al., which reported that the public health services were accessible within 30 min for most of their households surveyed [33].

Important age-related differences were observed in healthcare utilisation between older adults and their younger family members with health expenditures. The reasons for visits were non-communicable diseases among older adults and acute ailments in younger members. The comparison showed that older adults visited pharmacies and AYUSH practitioners more while younger members visited MBBS and specialists. Also, the younger family members preferred visiting private facilities more than older adults. The differential access could be due to various factors like education status, accessibility and priority given to healthcare. The mode of transportation also significantly differed, with the older adults preferring walking, suggesting proximity and round-the-clock availability of pharmacies without any waiting time, but also potential mobility limitations or affordability barriers [34, 37]. Also, despite mobility issues, the older adults preferred to go to health facility by walking while the other group preferred motorcycles for outpatient visit and subsequently able to go for further distance for seeking care. The unmet transport needs and mobility challenges may warrant policymakers considering supportive transport strategies or older adults-focused targeted outreach programs. Several Indian states have introduced innovative models to bridge this gaps. Tamil Nadu’s Makkalai Thedi Maruthuvam and Kerala’s Vayomitram initiatives offer door-to-door medical care can be considered [41, 42]. Strengthening and implementation of geriatric clinics to provide comprehensive geriatric care at primary, secondary and tertiary levels, as envisaged by the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly, should be considered.

Strengths and limitations

The study followed a cluster sampling framework similar to the WHO EPI strategy, which enhances the credibility and comparability of findings with similar studies. The usage of probability proportional to size ensured proportional representation across varying village sizes, reducing sampling bias. We used simple random sampling instead of stratified random sampling used by the WHO EPI methods, which also reduces selection bias. to select households, which ensures the representativeness of the findings. Many results of the study were similar to the national surveys, like LASI and NSS, which increases the comparability of the findings. We compared the older adults with younger family members with expenditure to identify if any differential care was followed for older adults.

The study has few limitations. Due to the cross-sectional design used prevents us to draw causal inference between the determinants assessed and healthcare utilisation outcomes. The usage of cluster sampling may reduce statistical efficiency due to intra-cluster correlation. To address this design effect was used during sample size calculation. The data on certain variables, like morbidity profile and healthcare utilisation, were self-reported and are subject to recall bias. The small number of hospitalisation cases, despite proportional to the sample size and consistent with the national surveys, prevented us from examining the associations and determinants related to hospitalisation.

Conclusion

The study provides a comprehensive analysis of healthcare utilisation of the older adults. A significant proportion of elderly individuals in the study area face barriers to healthcare access; however, the extent and nature of these barriers vary based on factors such as income, education, and geographic location. Increased private healthcare utilisation despite the improvements in public healthcare remains a concern. Moreover, the utilisation of public healthcare is more among poorer population raising the inequity concerns in healthcare utilisation. There is a need for focusing on health system strengthening and proper implementation of national programmes like the National Programme for Health Care for the Elderly (NPHCE) to increase the public healthcare utilisation of the older adults.
