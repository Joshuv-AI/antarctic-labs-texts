Ly6E on tumor cells impairs anti-tumor T-cell responses: a novel mechanism of tumor-induced immune exclusion
Authors: Lan Hailin, Chen Yiting, Wu Yue, Li Lijun, Zhang Renlu, Chen Yunhan, Zhu Yanyang, Zhang Qiuyu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLymphocyte antigen 6 complex, locus E (Ly6E) has been initially demonstrated to involve in T cell activity and impair viral infectivity. Recently, high expression levels of Ly6E have been reported in tumor microenvironment (TME) of various types of cancers. However, the immunoregulatory mechanism of Ly6E manipulating TME remains unknown.MethodsTCGA database and Kaplan–Meier plotter database were used to evaluate the correlation between Ly6E expression levels and cancer patient survival. After analyzing Ly6E expression levels in human breast cancer tissues and tumor cell lines, we generated Ly6E knockout (KO) and overexpression (OE) mouse cell lines. Cell proliferation ability in vitro and the ability of growth and metastasis in mouse tumor models were compared between KO/OE and wild-type tumor cells. On day 7 after tumor implantation, tumor tissues were separated for flow cytometric assay, bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing (ScRNA-seq). The role of Ly6E-expressing tumor cell on macrophage was analyzed in vitro.ResultsOur result surprisingly found that high Ly6E expression levels were associated with CD8+ T cell exclusion in tumor tissues and resistance to immunotherapy. Our data showed that knockout of Ly6E in tumor cells prompts tumor regression and inhibits tumor metastases, and Ly6E-OE tumor cells vice versa. The enhanced anti-tumor effect of Ly6E knockout in tumor cells was dependent on T cell response and formed long-lasting memory. The increase in the CD8+ T-cell infiltration into the tumor islet of Ly6E-KO tumors confirmed the role of Ly6E on T cell exclusion. ScRNA-seq analysis showed that M2 macrophages are particularly abundant in the Ly6E-expressing tumor tissues, especially M2-4 macrophage cluster identified by high levels of Arg-1, indicates that Ly6E-expressing tumor cells might restrict T cell infiltration via M2 macrophages. Moreover, in vitro assay showed that cell culture media derived from Ly6E-positive tumor cells promoted macrophage migration and M2 polarization.ConclusionOur study illuminated that Ly6E-expressing tumor cells facilitated the accumulation of M2 macrophages in TME, which contributes to CD8+ T cell exclusion and provides new insights for improving efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-024-03851-x.Keywords: Ly6E, Breast cancer, CD8+ T cells, Immune exclusion, Macrophage

Background

In recent years, immunotherapy targeting the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1 has revolutionized cancer treatment, offering promising outcomes in various malignancies. Despite its notable success, a considerable number of patients do not respond to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy [1]. The mechanisms of resistance to this therapy involve complex interactions within the tumor microenvironment (TME), such as changes in antigen presentation, T cell exclusion, lack of PD-L1 expression and the proliferation of immunosuppressive cell populations [2]. Particularly, the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, in terms of both quality and quantity, plays a pivotal role in determining the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitor [3]. Exploring the factors that influence CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation in the TME shows great potential for overcoming resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and enhancing clinical outcomes.

Lymphocyte antigen 6 complex, locus E (Ly6E), also known as stem cell antigen 2 (SCA2) and thymic shared antigen-1 (TSA-1), belongs to the lymphostromal cell membrane Ly6 superfamily. Initially recognized for interferon inducing gene, Ly6E plays a role in T cell activation [4, 5]. Recently, Ly6E has attracted attention for its various functions in tumorigenesis, metastasis and therapeutic resistance [6–8]. The development of Ly6E-targeting antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) has shown promising results in inhibiting tumor growth both in vitro and in vivo. This highlights Ly6E as a potential target for ADCs therapy in solid tumors with unmet medical needs [9–11]. However, the distribution of Ly6E expression in the tumor microenvironment and its precise immunoregulatory mechanism are still not fully understood, which greatly limits the translational application of targeting Ly6E.

In this study, we analyzed the distribution of Ly6E expression in tumor tissues and cell lines to assess its expression pattern at the tumor site and its correlation with CD8+ T cell distribution. We further investigated how Ly6E-expressing tumor cells affect CD8+ T cell immune response using Ly6E knockout/overexpression mouse tumor models, single-cell RNA sequencing and in vitro function assays of macrophage.

Methods

Acquisition of gene expression profiles from datasets

RNA-seq data for tumor tissues in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were downloaded through UCSC Xena (http://xena.ucsc.edu/). The expression of genes analyzed in normal breast tissues was collected from the Genome Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. The abbreviations and full names of TCGA cancers are listed in Additional file 1: supplementary Table S1. Cancer patients were divided into high and low groups according to the median gene expression level of Ly6E. The survival difference between high- and low-gene expression group in different types of cancer patients was estimated by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Immunotherapy data were obtained from http://kmplot.com/analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were also generated in the patients receiving anti-PD-L1, anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 antibodies. Overall survival probability was analyzed based on cutoff values of the median ratio.

Patients and sample preparation

A total of 45 patients with breast cancer who underwent surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University from 2017 to 2019 were included in this study. None of these patients received preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy before surgery. The clinical pathological features of the patients are listed in Additional file 2: supplementary Table S2. Relevant clinical data were collected by retrospective review of the patients’ files, and follow-up data were available for all patients.

Mice and cell lines

Female BABL/C and C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River (Beijing, China). C57BL/6 Rag-1−/− transgenic mice were bred in-house. Human breast cancer cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), including SK-BR-3, MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-453, T-47D and BT-474. Murine tumor cell lines 4T1, CT-26 and B16-F10 were also purchased from ATCC, and GL261-luc, ID8-luc, E0771 and MC38-OVA were obtained from the laboratory of Dr. Lieping Chen (Yale University). ANA-1 mouse macrophages were obtained from the Kunming Institute of Zoology. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 or DMEM supplemented 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

The construction of tumor cell lines with Ly6E gene knockout or overexpression

Ly6E gene knockout cells (4T1-KO, B16-KO and ID8-KO) were generated using the CRISPER/Cas9 system by infecting a custom-made lentivirus vector containing the Cas9 gene and sgRNA targeting the sequence of mouse Ly6E, and then, single-cell clones were isolated by serial dilutions. Ly6E-overexpressing tumor cells were prepared by transfection of the pcDNA-Ly6E plasmid encoding full-length mouse Ly6E into MC38-OVA cells. G418 (Corning, NY, USA) was used in antibiotic selection to obtain stable clones. The positive cell clones were identified by flow cytometry.

Mouse tumor models and treatment

BABL/c mice or C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 tumor cells (4T1-WT/KO or B16-WT/KO) in the right flank. Tumor length and width were measured with a caliper every other day to calculate tumor diameter using the equation (length + width)/2. To analyze lung metastasis in 4T1 metastatic tumor model, lung tissues were harvested on day 24 after tumor implantation. C57BL/6 mice were injected with 1 × 106 MC38-Mock/OE via the tail vein to establish lung metastasis. C57BL/6 mice were intraperitonealy injected with ID8-luc (WT/KO) tumor cells to generate abdominal tumor model. C57BL/6 mice were injected with 1 × 105 GL261-luc (WT/KO) tumor cells in the right cerebral hemisphere of brain using a standard stereotaxic instrument (RWD Life Science, USA). Tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging every 3–7 days using a Lumina XR Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). The survival of the mice was monitored in all the tumor models. Tumor tissues were isolated on the indicated days for further analysis.

Immunohistological assays

Human breast tissues were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded using standard procedures. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections were immunolabeled with an anti-Ly6E polyclonal antibody (#ab300399; Clone EPR26038-105, Abcam, USA) or anti-CD8 antibody (#MA5-16345, Clone SP16, Thermo Fisher, USA), followed by processing with an anti-rabbit peroxidase kit (ImmPRESS™, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For immunofluorescence staining, mouse sections were immunolabeled with an anti-CD4 (#ab183685, Clone EPR19514, Abcam, USA), anti-CD8 antibody (#98941, Clone D4W2Z, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) or anti-F4/80 (#ab6640, Clone CI:A3-1, Abcam, USA) as the primary antibody, followed by treatment with goat anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa488 (#A-A11034, Thermo Fisher, USA), goat anti-mouse IgG-Alexa594 (#A-11007, Thermo Fisher, USA) and goat anti-rat IgG-Alexa647 (#A-A21244, Thermo Fisher, USA), respectively. Finally, the slides were counterstained with DAPI to visualize cell nuclei (#D9542, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). All slides were scanned using EVOS ™ FL Auto Imaging System (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Flow cytometry

Ly6E expression levels on mouse cell lines were detected by flow cytometry using anti-Ly6E (#NBP1-52176, Novus Biologicals, USA). The Ly6E expression on human breast cancer cell lines was detected using anti-Ly6E (#SAB4501790, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) by flow cytometry and immunofluorescence. To analyze tumor-infiltrating immune cells, single-cell populations were isolated from mouse tumor tissues using a Gentle MACS mechanical dissociator in the presence of lysis buffers (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and enriched according to the Lymphocyte-M manufacturer’s recommendations (Dakewe biotech, China). Cells were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 mAbs (TruStain fcX, USA) and subsequently stained with mAbs against mouse CD45, CD4, CD8a, CD11b, CD11c, F4/80, Ly6C/Ly6G (Gr-1), CD19, CD49b, CD25, CD206, MHC-II, IFN-γ, Granzyme B and a death marker as well as matched isotype controls depending on the experiment. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm buffer (BD Biosciences, USA) after surface marker staining. These antibodies were obtained from Thermo Fisher, Biolegend or BD Biosciences. Stained samples were run on a BD FACSVerse™ and analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences, USA).

RNA sequencing, gene functional enrichment analysis and immune cell analysis

RNA sequencing was performed on tumor tissue samples (4T1-WT or 4T1-KO) isolated 7 days after implantation. A total amount of 2 μg total RNA per sample was prepared to construct sequencing libraries using the VAHTSTM mRNA-seq V2 Library Prep Kit for Illumina® according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. Paired-end sequencing of the libraries was performed on HiSeq XTen sequencers (Illumina, USA). Normalized and log2-transformed TPM values from RNA-Seq based on the expectation maximization data. The algorithm of sample gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was based on 36 immune gene sets from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) database or published gene signatures. We employed the GSEA algorithm via R packages (GSVA, GSEABase and limma) to comprehensively assess the immunologic characteristics of tumor tissue samples (4T1-WT or 4T1-KO). GSEA (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp) was applied to analyze signaling pathway enrichment in 4T1-KO and 4T1-WT using the KEGG database in MSigDB (version 7.3). The enriched pathways were arranged in the order of their normalized enrichment scores (NESs), and p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25 were considered statistically significant in the GSEAs.

ScRNA sequencing and analysis

Tumor tissue samples (4T1-WT or 4T1-KO) were isolated 7 days after implantation and digested with MACS lysis buffers to generate single-cell suspension. Cells were stained by V450 conjugating anti-CD45 antibody and a LIVE/DEAD marker, and then sorted by BD Aria III (BD Biosciences, USA). ScRNA-seq libraries were prepared with Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent v3 Kits (10 × Genomics, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Seurat software was used for dimensionality reduction, clustering and obtaining cluster markers [12]. After applying these QC criteria, 25,000 single cells were included in downstream analyses. Library size normalization was performed with the NormalizeData function in Seurat. The specific marker genes for each cluster identified by Seurat were determined using the “FindAllMarkers” function. P value < 0.05 and |log2foldchange|> 0.5 were set as the threshold for significantly differential expression. Gene ontology (GO) and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were performed using R based on the hypergeometric distribution. Cell–cell communication analysis between immune cells of TME was performed by “CellChat” R package [13].

Macrophage migration assay

The paired tumor cell lines (MC38-WT/MC38-OE or B16-WT/B16-KO) were seeded in 24-well culture plate at 2 × 105 cells/well, and cell culture media (CM) were collected after culture for 2 days. ANA-1 cells were stained with 3 µM 5,6-carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and then seeded in the upper chamber of an 8.0-μm transwell insert in a 24-well culture plate (Corning, USA). The lower chamber comprised CM from different tumor cells. After culture for 8 h, cell number in lower chamber was counted using EVOS ™ FL Auto Imaging System (Thermo Fisher, USA) and evaluated by Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Five visual fields were randomly selected for each chamber.

Macrophage polarization assay

Bone marrow-derived monocytes (BMDMs) were isolated from the bone marrow of C57BL/6 mice and seeded in ultralow attachment 24-well U-bottomed plates (Corning, USA) at 1 × 105 cells/well, following by culture with 10 μg/ml M-CSF for 4 days to induce into monocytes. Subsequently, monocytes derived from C57BL/6 mice were cocultured with CM from MC38-WT/MC38-OE or B16-WT/B16-KO, respectively. After coculture for 24 h, macrophage surface markers were analyzed by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-q PCR).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-q PCR)

RT-q PCR were used to detect the expression of Ly6E gene in tumor cell lines and that of marker genes in CM-treated BMDMs. Total RNA was isolated from tumor cells or BMDMs, and then, it was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript TM RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-q PCR) was performed using a TB Green®Premix Ex Taq™ II Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Primers are listed in Additional file 2: supplementary Table S3. Target gene expression was standardized to the expression levels of the Gapdh gene using the 2−△△Ct method. RT-q PCR analyses were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 9.0 was used to generate plots and perform additional statistical analyses. All data are presented as the mean ± SEM or mean ± SD of a single experiment, representative of two or three independent experiments. A two-tailed unpaired t test or two-way ANOVA was used to determine the statistical significance of intergroup difference. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson coefficient analysis. Statistically significant results were defined as follows: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Ly6E expression level was negatively associated with patient prognosis and clinical outcomes of the immunotherapy

To assess the association of LY6E gene expression with the cancer progress, we compared LY6E gene expression in 31 types of tumor tissues from the TCGA databases and corresponding normal tissues from GTEx databases. The data showed that the mRNA expression levels of LY6E were higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues across a broad spectrum of human cancers, especially in 17 types of cancer (Fig. 1A). The differential expression also exist between the same tumor and adjacent normal tissues of several tumor types (Additional file 4: S1A). Next, the patients were divided into two groups based on median expression levels of LY6E (high- and low-LY6E expression groups). The data showed that LY6E expression level was negatively associated with overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) of tumor patients in 17 different types of cancer, including GBM, PAAD, UVMU, etc. (Fig. 1B and Additional file 4: S1B). Notably, based on Kaplan–Meier plotter for immunotherapy dataset analysis (http://kmplot.com/analysis), we found that LY6E gene expression was negatively associated with patient prognosis after treatment with anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 antibodies in melanoma and with anti-PD-L1 antibodies in urothelial cancer. Consistent with this result, the patient survival rates were negatively related to the ratio of LY6E/CD8 gene expression level in tumor tissues before receiving treatment with anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-L1 antibodies (Fig. 1B and Additional file 4: S1C). These findings indicated that Ly6E might impaired T cell response and the patients with low LY6E gene expression may benefit from immunotherapy.

High level of Ly6E protein was detected in breast tumor tissues and on the surface of tumor cell lines

Given that the reported aberrant overexpression of Ly6E was in breast cancer tissues, we examined Ly6E expression levels in breast cancer tissue and tumor cell lines. According to the TCGA BRCA database, a gradual increase in LY6E gene expression levels was found in breast cancer patients with stage II (n = 620) or stage III (n = 250) as compared to patients with stage I (n = 182), while there is no significant correlation between LY6E gene expression levels and patient survival rates. Furthermore, the highest LY6E gene expression levels were found in the basal-like subtype of breast carcinoma as opposed to non-basal-like subtypes (luminal A, luminal B and HER2) (Additional file 5: Fig. S2A, B and C). Immunohistochemical staining revealed a high level of Ly6E expression in tumor cells rather than immune cells, with 37 out of 45 cases of IDC (82.2%) being positive for Ly6E protein expression. The clinical pathological features of 45 patients with breast cancers are detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Importantly, in the TME with high Ly6E expression levels, CD8+ T cells were found to be excluded from tumor cell nest (Fig. 2A). Immunofluorescent staining revealed Ly6E protein on the surface of several human breast cancer cell lines (Additional file 5: Fig. S2D), a finding confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we investigated Ly6E expression in mouse tumor cell lines. As shown in Fig. 2C, Ly6E expression was detected on the surface of 4T1, E0771, GL261, B16-F10 and ID8 cells, whereas it was not present on MC38-OVA and CT-26 cells.

Ly6E promoted tumor growth and metastasis in several mouse tumor models

To determine the biological impact of Ly6E on tumor progress, we generated Ly6E gene knockout mouse tumor cell lines (4T1-KO, GL261-KO, ID8-KO and B16-KO) and Ly6E gene overexpressing mouse tumor cell lines (MC38-OE) (Fig. 3A and Additional file 6: S3A). When comparing with control cell lines (4T1-WT, GL261-WT, B16-WT, ID8-WT and MC38-Mock), we found no proliferation differences in vitro in Ly6E gene knockout or overexpressing mouse tumor cell lines (Fig. 3B and Additional file 6: S3B). However, in comparison with control tumor cells, tumor growth in vivo was significantly inhibited in 4T1-KO, GL261-KO, ID8-KO and B16-KO cells (Fig. 3C, F, and Additional file 6: S3C, D, F), resulting in an increase in survival rate and the number of tumor-free mice (Fig. 3G, H, and Additional file 6: S3E and G). In 4T1 models with spontaneous lung metastases, lung metastatic lesions were found in mice injected with 4T-WT, whereas no metastases were observed in mice injected with 4T1-KO cells (Fig. 3D). For established lung metastasis of MC38 injection via the tail vein, body weight of MC38-OE-bearing mice was sharply decreased on day 35 after tumor cell injection compared to MC38-Mork-bearing mice (Fig. 3I). Consistent with the results from 4T1 models, MC38-OE-bearing mice displayed prominent lung metastatic lesions compared to control mice (Fig. 3J, K). Histological quantification analyses further confirmed 4T1 and MC38 metastases in the lung (Fig. 3E, L). Additionally, these findings suggest that the significant impact of Ly6E promotes tumor growth and metastasis in vivo, although the alteration of Ly6E expression did not affect the cell growth of mouse tumor cells in vitro.

The enhanced anti-tumor effect of Ly6E knockout in tumor cells was dependent on T cell response

To evaluate the impact of Ly6E knockout on T cell response, we investigated tumor progress between GL261-KO and GL261-WT in the absence of CD4+ T cells or CD8+ T cells. Bioluminescence imaging revealed that the suppression of tumor growth in Ly6E knockout GL261 was compromised after CD8+ T cell deficiency. Similarly, Ly6E knockout improved survival rates and increased number of tumor-free mice in immunocompetent mice but not in those CD8+ T-cell-deficient mice (Fig. 4A, B). When GL261-WT and GL261-KO cells were implanted into syngeneic C57BL/6 mice and Rag-1-deficient mice, we observed that Ly6E gene knockout inhibited tumor growth in immunocompetent mice but not in Rag-1-deficient mice (Additional file 7: Fig. S4A and B). Consistent results were also observed in 4T1 tumor models, where the anti-tumor effect of 4T1-KO tumor cells was compromised after depletion of CD8+ T cells, leading to increased tumor growth, decrease in survival rates and fewer number of tumor-free mice (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, tumor-free immunocompetent mice from 4T1-KO were monitored for 90 days and then rechallenged with 4T1-KO and 4T1-WT tumors, resulting in significantly inhibited tumor growth in these mice (Fig. 4D), indicating that long-lasting anti-tumor effects are induced by Ly6E knockout in tumor cells.

The level of CD8 + T cell infiltration and activity was comparatively elevated in 4T1-KO tumors compared to those observed in 4T1-WT tumors

To further characterize the immunologic mechanisms behind the anti-tumor effects of Ly6E knockout in tumor cells, tumor-infiltrating immune cells from 4T1-WT and 4T1-KO-bearing mice were analyzed on day 7. Flow cytometric analysis showed that the frequency of CD3+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and effector CD8+ T cells (CD8+GzmB+ and CD8+IFN-γ+) in lymphocytes was significantly increased in 4T1-KO tumors compared to 4T1-WT tumors. Conversely, the frequency of CD4+Foxp3+Treg cells was decreased. However, the frequency of suppressive immune cells MDSC (CD11b+Gr-1low) and M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (CD11b+CD206+) in the total tumor-infiltrating immune cell population (CD45+ live cells) was reduced in 4T1-KO tumors compared to 4T1-WT tumors. Additionally, there was no significant difference in the frequency of CD19+ B cells and NK1.1+ NK cells between both groups of mice (Fig. 5A and Additional file 8: S5A). Immunofluorescent staining confirmed the differential infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and F4/80+ tumor-associated macrophages between 4T1-WT and 4T1-KO tumors. Notably, CD8+ T cells in 4T1-WT tumors were distributed around the tumor cell nests, while CD8+ T cells in 4T1-KO tumors infiltrated into the center of tumor cell nests (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, we performed RNA sequencing to analyze the different distribution of 36 immune cell subsets between 4T1-KO and 4T1-WT tumors using an online tool named the Immune Cell Abundance Identifier (immuCellAI-mouse). Consistent with the results from flow cytometry, in 4T1-KO tumor tissues, the infiltration of several CD8+ T cell subsets was increased, including Tc, Tem, Tcm and Tex, while the infiltration of Treg cells was decreased. Additionally, M1-like tumor-associated macrophages was increased whereas pDC was decreased (Fig. 5C). We then performed the analysis of GO and KEGG pathway from GSEA to elucidate the enrichment of hallmark pathways. The data revealed that the expression of related genes in T cell receptor signaling pathway and chemokine pathway was upregulated in 4T1-KO tumors (Fig. 5D and Additional file 8: Fig. S5B, C). Overall, our results indicated that knocking out Ly6E in tumor cells could potentially reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment. This reversal might lead to enhanced infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumor nest.

Ly6E-mediated immune suppression was associated with the polarization of M2-like macrophage

To better understand how Ly6E-expressing tumor cells exclude T cells, we compared the composition of immune cells and their functional diversity in 4T1-KO tumors with those in 4T1-WT tumors using single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. In the TME of 4T1-WT, over 90% cells formed myeloid clusters, with only a few cells assigned to the lymphoid lineage. Consistent with the flow cytometric analysis results, the proportion of T cells and dendritic cells was increased in 4T1-KO tumors (21% and 38%) compared to 4T1-WT tumors (8% and 11%). Additionally, the prevalent cell population, M2-like macrophages, was significantly decreased in 4T1-KO tumors (13%) compared to 4T1-WT tumors (64%) (Fig. 6A, B, and Additional file 9: S6A). The re-clustering analysis of M2 macrophages, NK cells and T cells revealed 16 subcluster cell proportions. Interestingly, among 4 subclasses of M2-like macrophages, cluster 4 of M2-like macrophages (M2-4) showed the most significant differences in two TME with high expression of immunosuppressive genes like arginase-1 (Arg-1) and tumor metastasis-related genes like MMP9, MMP12, MMP13, etc. On the other hand, among the 10 subclasses of T cells, the CD8+ T cluster-expressing Cd8 gene showing the highest increase in 4T1-KO tumors compared to 4T1-WT tumors. Conversely, the cluster-expressing genes related to M1-like macrophages were increased in 4T1-KO tumors (Fig. 6C, D and Additional file 9: S6B). Discussion

Ly6E is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored protein belonging to the Ly6/uPAR family [14]. The Ly6 family of proteins plays a significant role in tumor progression, with various members demonstrating both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive effects. For instance, Ly6D, Ly6E, Ly6H and Ly6K have been found to be overexpressed in various cancers and are associated with a poor prognosis [7]. Ly6D and Ly6K are mainly expressed in tumor cell to facilitate tumorigenesis and metastasis [6, 15–17]. Conversely, certain members of the Ly6 family, such as Ly6C and Ly6G, are mainly expressed in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells. Ly6C+ cells often exert immunosuppressive functions, while Ly6G+ cells exhibiting both pro- and anti-tumor activities [18, 19]. Ly6E was reported to express primarily on the surface of hematopoietic cells, including lymphocytes and myeloid cells [20, 21]. Traditionally, Ly6E has been implicated in various immune processes, such as T cell activation [20, 22] and T cell development [4, 23]. Recently, Ly6E was considered as an anti-viral restriction factor and confers immune control of viral disease [24–26]. However, emerging evidence suggests a more nuanced involvement of Ly6E in the context of cancer biology. Notably, Ly6E has been shown to promote tumor cell proliferation through PTEN/PI3K/AKT pathway [27]. Additionally, Ly6E has been implicated in promoting PD-L1 and CTLA-4 expression, resulting in increased tumor-infiltrating T regulatory cells (Treg) and decreased natural killer (NK) cell activation in breast cancer [6]. Moreover, its association with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) underscores its significance in driving metastatic dissemination and therapy evasion by modulating the TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway in NSCLC [28]. These findings suggested that Ly6E might mediate immune escape in tumor microenvironment (TME). However, its expression profiles and molecular mechanisms remain elusive.

Based on the analysis of TCGA datasets, the Ly6E gene was found to be highly expressed in tumor tissues, and its expression levels showed a negative correlation with patient survival across a total of 17 types of tumors. Importantly, we observed that Ly6E gene expression levels were inversely related to the survival of patients undergoing immunotherapy, which included treatments with anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies. These findings suggest that Ly6E may hinder T cell infiltration and impact the effectiveness of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapies. Subsequently, we examined the expression of the Ly6E protein in breast cancer tissues. Consistent with earlier findings, high levels of Ly6E protein were detected in breast cancer cases with various molecular phenotypes [6, 9]. Furthermore, our study further confirmed that Ly6E protein was predominantly expressed in tumor cells rather than in immune cells present in the tumor microenvironment. Intriguingly, T cells were located in the vicinity of tumors exhibiting high Ly6E expression, while T cells infiltrated the center of tumors with low or negligible Ly6E expression. These results suggest that Ly6E may play a role in shaping an immune-excluded tumor microenvironment, presenting a unique challenge in the context of cancer immunotherapy.

TME plays a pivotal role in cancer progression and therapeutic response. Among the diverse TME phenotypes, the immune exclude type was characterized by the presence of effective T cells at the periphery of the tumor mass, with limited T cell infiltration into the core [29]. To verify the phenomena observed in the human TME and investigate its immune regulation mechanism of Ly6E, we generated Ly6E gene knockout and overexpression mouse tumor cell lines, such as 4T1-KO, B16-KO and MC38-OE. There was no significant change in tumor cell proliferation in vitro after Ly6E gene knockout, which contradicted with previous reports on human tumor cells [6, 8]. However, tumor growth and metastasis were significantly inhibited in immunocompetent mice bearing Ly6E gene knockout tumor cells, including 4T1-KO, B16-KO, ID8-KO and GL261-KO. The promotion of tumor growth by Ly6E was confirmed in immunocompetent mice bearing Ly6E gene overexpressing MC38-OE cells. The anti-tumor effect of Ly6E-knockout in tumor cells was impaired in immunodeficient mice or in mice treated with anti-CD8 antibodies, indicating that Ly6E-mediated T cell dysfunctions in TME. Similar to the results from human breast cancer, the distribution of CD8+ T cells at the periphery of the tumor islets was observed in the 4T1-WT mouse model, and increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the tumor islets was detected in the 4T1-KO mouse model. The data from bulk RNA-seq revealed that the proportion of six CD8+ T cell subsets and the T cell signaling pathway were increased in the 4T1-KO mouse tissues. Nevertheless, comparing with Ly6E-WT tumor cell, Ly6E-KO tumor cell showed no difference in T cell functional activity of cytotoxicity (data not shown). These findings suggest that Ly6E expressing in tumor cells might indirectly hinder CD8+ T cell functional activity, preventing effective anti-tumor responses.

To further elucidate immune evasion mechanisms of Ly6E, we applied scRNA-seq methods to study CD45+ cells isolated from 4T1-KO and 4T1-WT tumors. By comparing the transcriptomic profiles of 4T1-KO, we found M2-like macrophages enriched in 4T1-WT tumor tissues, in addition to the increased proportion of T cells. Here we identified four distinct M2-Like macrophage subsets, and the proportion of M2-4 cell subset was the highest, expressing two marker genes: CD11b and Arg-1. Emerging evidence suggests that M2-like macrophage-mediated T cell exclusion decreased tumor surveillance by CD8+ T cells and impedes tumors responsive to anti-PD-1 treatment [30–33]. Our findings indicated that Ly6E as a potential molecular candidate for therapeutic targeting in multiple types of cancers, particularly those exhibiting resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Knockout of Ly6E in tumor cell can restore the function of exhausted T cells, promote T cell activation and enhance the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI). Recently, Ly6E(hi) phenotype of neutrophils was found in mouse tumor models that responsiveness to ICI therapy, in which tumor cells was mutagenized using the chemical mutagen 1-methyl-3-nitro-1-nitrosoguanidine (MNNG) [34]. Although the results of this study are derived from genetically mutated tumor cell lines, it inspired us to further investigate the different expression profiles and immune regulatory roles of Ly6E in different types of tumor microenvironments.

Conclusion

Our study elucidated the complex interaction between Ly6E-expressing tumor cells and immune cells in the TME, despite previous reports of high expression of Ly6E in immune cells. Our findings indicate that Ly6E-expressing tumor cells facilitate the polarization of M2-like macrophages, leading to the exclusion of T cells from the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 7). Based on our observations, we propose that targeting Ly6E could be a promising strategy to overcome resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in different cancer types. Further research and clinical trials are necessary to confirm its effectiveness and safety in cancer treatment.
